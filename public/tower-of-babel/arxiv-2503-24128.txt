Perfect circle-valued Morse functions on hyperbolic 6 6 -manifolds
Giovanni Italiano, Matteo Migliorini

Abstract
. We build the first example of a hyperbolic 6 6 -manifold that admits a perfect circle-valued Morse function, which can be considered as the analogue of a fibration over the circle for manifolds with non-vanishing Euler characteristic. As a consequence, we obtain a new example of a subgroup of a hyperbolic group which is of type ℱ 2 \mathcal{F}_{2} but not ℱ 3 \mathcal{F}_{3} .



1.
Introduction

A peculiar phenomenon that has been widely studied in the previous decades is the existence of many hyperbolic
3
-manifolds that are also fibre bundles over the circle
S^{1}
. The first example of such a manifold was constructed by Jørgensen
[
21
]
in 1977, and quickly captured the interest of many topologists, as some behaviours of these examples are quite paradoxical, as noted in
[
9
]
.

After Jørgensen’s example, Thurston classified which diffeomorphisms of surfaces yield mapping tori that carry hyperbolic metrics
[
30
]
, providing a simple tool to construct many such fibring hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.
A celebrated theorem of Agol and Wise
[
2
,
31
]
shows how such a phenomenon is actually ubiquitous; every finite-volume hyperbolic
3
-manifold has a finite cover that fibres over
S^{1}
.

Despite the omnipresence of fibring manifolds in dimension
3
, the first progress in building similar examples in higher dimensions is much more recent. The first example in higher dimension is due to Martelli and the authors
[
18
]
, and consists of a
5
-manifold tessellated by some copies of a particular right-angled polytope.

In this paper, we look at dimension
6
. Hyperbolic
6
-manifolds cannot fibre due to an Euler characteristic obstruction; the natural generalisation is the notion of
perfect circle-valued Morse function
. We prove the following.

Theorem A
.

There exists a hyperbolic
6
-manifold
M^{6}
that admits a perfect circle-valued Morse function.

To prove the theorem, we construct an explicit manifold
M^{6}
equipped with a Morse function onto the circle whose critical points are all of index
3
. The manifold
M^{6}
is cusped, and as was the case for the fibring
5
-manifold
M^{5}
in
[
18
]
, the techniques used in this paper cannot produce a closed manifold in dimension
n\geq 5
due to the lack of compact right-angled hyperbolic polytopes
[
27
]
.

As an analogue of
[
4
]
*Corollary 3, we get the following.

Corollary B
.

There are infinitely many finite-volume (cusped) hyperbolic
6
-manifolds
M
admitting a handle decomposition with bounded numbers of
1
-,
2
-,
4
- and
5
-handles. In particular, there are infinitely many
M
with bounded Betti numbers
b_{1}(M)
,
b_{2}(M)
,
b_{4}(M)
,
b_{5}(M)
and bounded rank of
\pi_{1}(M)
.

Perfect Morse functions

Let
M
be an even-dimensional hyperbolic manifold. Given a Morse function
f\colon M\to S^{1}
, the alternating sum of the number of critical points of each index yields the Euler characteristic
\chi(M)
. It follows that the number of critical points for such a function is at least
\lvert\chi(M)\rvert
. In particular, a necessary requirement for
f
to be a fibration is that the Euler characteristic of
M
vanishes.

However, the Chern-Gauss-Bonnet formula implies that the Euler characteristic of
M
is proportional to its volume and never zero; therefore
M
cannot fibre over the circle. The natural generalisation of the fibring problem in this setting is whether
M
admits a Morse function with a minimal number of critical points.

Definition 1.1
.

A Morse function
f\colon M\to S^{1}
is said to be
perfect
if it has exactly
\lvert\chi(M)\rvert
critical points.

In the odd-dimensional case
\chi(M)=0
, so perfect Morse functions are actually fibrations.
Perfect circle-valued Morse functions have already been studied in this context by Battista and Martelli
[
4
]
. In particular, they constructed several hyperbolic
4
-manifolds, both closed and cusped, that admit such a function.

Finiteness properties of groups

The manifold
M^{6}
can be used to construct subgroups of hyperbolic groups with exotic finiteness properties.

Recall that a group is said to be of type
\mathcal{F}_{n}
if it admits a classifying space with finite
n
-skeleton, and of type
\mathcal{F}
if the classifying space has finitely many cells. This generalizes classical notions of finiteness, as being of type
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is equivalent to being finitely generated, and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
to finitely presented. Hyperbolic groups are of type
\mathcal{F}_{n}
for every
n
.

A natural question is under which conditions a subgroup of a hyperbolic group is necessarily hyperbolic itself. Rips
[
28
]
in 1982 presented a construction that produces many examples of finitely generated subgroups of hyperbolic groups that are not finitely presented, and therefore not hyperbolic; finding finitely presented counterexamples turns out to be a much harder task. Except for the group built in
[
18
]
, which is of type
\mathcal{F}
, all the known examples rely on the failure of some higher finiteness property to obstruct hyperbolicity.

A strategy for constructing a subgroup of a hyperbolic that is finitely presented but not
\mathcal{F}_{3}
was first proposed by Gromov
[
17
]
. In fact, Bestvina noticed that the ambient group could not be hyperbolic.

Later Brady
[
8
]
, building on Gromov’s idea, managed to construct a working example. This remained the only known finitely presented, non-hyperbolic subgroup of a hyperbolic group until much more recently, with constructions by Lodha
[
26
]
and Kropholler
[
22
]
.
An example of a subgroup of type
\mathcal{F}_{3}
not
\mathcal{F}_{4}
was found by Llosa Isenrich, Martelli and Py
[
23
]
starting from the fundamental group of the hyperbolic
8
-manifold constructed in
[
19
]
; more generally, Llosa Isenrich and Py
[
24
]
constructed subgroups that are type
\mathcal{F}_{n}
not
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
, using methods from complex hyperbolic geometry.

As consequence of
Theorem

A
, we construct a finitely presented subgroup of a hyperbolic group that is not
\mathcal{F}_{3}
, that adds to this now growing class of examples.
To the best of our knowledge this is the first example of this class that is produced using real hyperbolic geometry.

Main techniques

Jankiewicz, Norin, and Wise
[
20
]
described an algorithm, starting from a so-called
system of moves
and an
initial state
, that produces a cube complex
C
with a Bestvina-Brady Morse function
f\colon C\to S^{1}
.
When certain combinatorial conditions are satisfied, then
f
has connected ascending and descending links and by
[
7
]
it is an algebraic fibring.

Getting a topological fibration, or more generally a perfect Morse function, is a much harder task. A fibration of a hyperbolic
5
–manifold was eventually found in
[
18
]
, but the authors needed to relax the condition on the moves, defining a notion of
set of moves
that allows some degeneracy on certain codimension–
2
faces, called
bad ridges
. This degeneracy was controlled by choosing a suitable subdivision of the cube complex, and it allowed to construct a function with
collapsible
ascending and descending links.

In this paper, we generalise this by introducing the notions of
bad
and
good
faces of a polytope (see
Definition

2.16
), that depend only on the set of moves.
Since bad faces can now appear in every codimension, we are only able to define the Bestvina-Brady Morse function on the barycentric subdivision of
C
.
This has the drawback that we introduce many more vertices for which we have to check ascending and descending links. We solve this issue with
Theorem

3.1
, where we give a combinatorial condition to check their collapsibility.

We then apply this general strategy to the polytope
P^{6}
, which belongs to a remarkable family of polytopes introduced in
[
1
]
and studied in
[
27
]
. Since we are in even dimension, we cannot get a fibration, so some critical points are expected. Indeed, we get some ascending and descending links that are not collapsible, but they collapse to a sphere. After taking care of the cusps, a smoothing argument leads to the proof of
Theorem

A
.

Structure of the paper

The paper is structured as follows.

•

In
Section

2
we recall the construction described in
[
20
]
, while introducing
good
and
bad

•

In
Section

we show how to construct a Bestvina-Brady Morse function

of the cubulation

, such that the ascending and descending links of

•

In

we describe the combinatorics of the polytope

, while focusing on a particular subgroup of its symmetries, and we choose a set of moves and an initial state. By using the results in

3
we construct a Bestvina-Brady Morse function

with ascending and descending links that are either collapsible, or collapse to a

•

In

we embed

, and we extend

.

•

.

•

.

2.
Preliminaries

Jankiewicz, Norin and Wise described in
[
20
]
a combinatorial game that, given a right-angled Coxeter group equipped with some combinatorial data, produces a
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
cube complex
C
equipped with a piecewise-linear map
f\colon C\to S^{1}
. The aim of this section is to recall the notions of Bestvina-Brady Morse theory on which the game is based on, and then present the game in a slightly modified form, which can be seen as a generalisation of the procedure employed in
[
18
]
.

2.1.
Bestvina-Brady Morse theory

Given a cell complex
X
, and two cells
\sigma,\tau
of
X
, we say that
\sigma
is a
face
of
\tau
, or equivalently that
\tau
is a
coface
of
\sigma
, if
\sigma\subseteq\tau
. We add the adjective
proper
if the inclusion is strict.

The following definitions are taken from
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
affine cell complex
is a cell complex where every cell
\sigma
is equipped with a characteristic function
\chi_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\to P_{\sigma}
, which is a homeomorphism to an affine polytope
P_{\sigma}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that whenever
\tau
is a face of
\sigma
, with inclusion map
i\colon\tau\hookrightarrow\sigma
, then
\chi_{\sigma}\circ i\circ\chi_{\tau}^{-1}
is the restriction of an affine homeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Affine cell structure have a natural piecewise-linear structure, inherited by the affine structure on the cells.
Examples are simplicial complexes and cube complexes, where characteristic functions are identifications with the standard simplex or cube. Note that every subdivision of an affine cell complex is naturally an affine cell complex.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
X
be an affine cell complex, and let
f\colon X\to S^{1}
be a map. We say that
f
is a
circle-valued Bestvina-Brady Morse function
if for every positive-dimensional cell
\sigma
the restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{\sigma}}
is affine and nonconstant.

In the definition above,
affine
means that there is an affine map
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f=\pi\circ F\circ\chi_{\sigma}
, where
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}\to S^{1}
is the standard covering map. Since we mostly work with circle-valued functions, we will drop the
circle-valued
adjective, and use the term
real-valued Bestvina-Brady Morse function
when the codomain is
\mathbb{R}
.

Bestvina-Brady Morse functions are the piecewise-linear analogue of their more widespread smooth counterpart. There is also a piecewise-linear notion that mimics the concept of regular and critical point; this is given by the
ascending and descending links
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
v
be a vertex of an affine cell complex
X
, and let
f\colon X\to S^{1}
be a Bestvina-Brady Morse function. The
ascending (descending) link
of
v
, denoted by
\link_{\uparrow}(v;f)
(resp.
\link_{\downarrow}(v;f)
), is the subcomplex of
\link v
spanned by cells
\sigma
such that
v
is the minimum (maximum) of any lift of
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{\sigma}}\colon\sigma\to S^{1}
to a real-valued function.

If the function
f
is clear from the context, we just write
\link_{\uparrow}(v)
and
\link_{\downarrow}(v)
.

Descending links control how the topology of sublevels of
f
change when crossing a vertex, as explained in the following proposition.
We refer to
[
29
]
for the definition of
collapsing
.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
7
]
)
.

Suppose that
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is a real-valued Bestvina-Brady Morse function on an affine cell complex
X
, and let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

If there are no vertices of
X
with value in
(a,b]
, then
X_{\leq b}
collapses to
X_{\leq a}
. If there is a single vertex
v
with value in
(a,b]
, then
X_{\leq b}
collapses to
X_{\leq a}
with a cone attached over
\link_{\downarrow}(v)
, where
\link_{\downarrow}(v)
embeds naturally inside
f^{-1}(a)
.

We will see in
Section

6
that any vertex with collapsible (i.e. that collapses to a point) descending link can be smoothened to a regular value for a smooth Morse function. On the other hand, a vertex with descending link that collapses to a
(k-1)
-sphere can be replaced with an index-
k
critical point. This will be crucial for the proof of
Theorem

A
.

2.2.
Jankiewicz, Norin, and Wise’s algorithm

We start by fixing the terminology regarding the main objects of this paper, which are hyperbolic polytopes.

Definition 2.5
.

A
hyperbolic
n
-polytope

P
is a finite volume intersection of finitely many hyperspaces of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. A
face
of
P
is either
P
itself, or the non-empty intersection of
P
with the boundary of a hyperspace containing it. A face of
P
is
proper
if it is different from
P
.

We refer to faces of dimension
n-1
,
n-2
,
1
, and
0
as
facets
,
ridges
,
edges
, and
(finite) vertices
respectively. An
ideal vertex
is a point of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{n}
contained in the closure of
P
, and it is not considered a face of the polytope.

Remark 2.6
.

In contrast to the usual convention, we exclude the empty set from the faces of a polytope. On the other hand, it is convenient to consider the polytope as a face of itself.

Let
P
be a hyperbolic right-angled
n
-polytope, meaning that the dihedral angle between any two adjacent facets is
\pi/2
.
Let
c
be the number of facets of
P
, denoted by
F_{1},\dots,F_{c}
(after choosing an arbitrary ordering).

The group generated by the reflections along the faces of
P
is a right-angled Coxeter group
W
with
c
generators. Its commutators subgroup
W^{\prime}
is a torsion-free subgroup of index
2^{c}
.

So the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/W^{\prime}
is a finite volume hyperbolic manifold
M
, tessellated in
2^{c}
copies of
P
. A concrete description of
M
is the following. For every
v\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
consider a copy of
P
denoted by
P_{v}
; then we glue
P_{v}
to
P_{v+e_{i}}
along the facet
F_{i}
with the identity map, for all
v
and for every
i\in\{1,\dots,c\}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The above construction, originally introduced by Löbell
[
25
]
, coincides with the one described in
[
4
,
19
,
18
]
in terms of colourings, in the particular case where we choose the “inefficient colouring” in which all facets have distinct colours. This is not restrictive, as the heavy work is done by the set of moves that we define below; changing the colouring yields virtually the same result.

The dual of the tessellation is a cube complex
C
, since
P
is right-angled; this can also be seen as the quotient of the Davis complex of
W
by the action of
W^{\prime}
by left-multiplication.

When
M
is compact, then
C
is homeomorphic to
M
; otherwise, the cube complex
C
is just a deformation retract of
M
, also called a
spine
. We will see in
Section

5
that
M
admits a cube complex structure
\bar{C}
in which
C
embeds nicely.

The aim is to define a map
f\colon M\to S^{1}
, and we first define it on
C
. To do so, we need the following two combinatorial objects.

Definition 2.8
.

A
set of moves
is any partition of the facets. The sets of the partition are called
moves
. If the facets belonging to each move are pairwise disjoint, the set of moves is called
sparse
.

Similarly to
[
18
]
, we allow non-sparse set of moves, which is in contrast with the definition of
system of moves
given in
[
20
]
. This will be crucial for the concrete example we examine in this paper.

Definition 2.9
.

A
state

s
is any labelling of the facets of
P
with
I
(for In) or
O
(for Out). The label on a facet
F
is called the
status
of
F
and is denoted by
s(F)
.

A state induces a bipartition on the vertices of the polytope
P^{*}
dual to
P
. Since
P
is right-angled and therefore simple, then
P^{*}
is a simplicial complex. We consider the full subcomplexes whose vertices are the vertices of
P^{*}
with label
O
and
I
; denote these subcomplexes by
s_{O},s_{I}
respectively.

Definition 2.10
.

A state is:

•

legal
if both
s_{O}
and
s_{I}
are connected;

•

m
-legal
if both
s_{O}
and
s_{I}
are
m
-connected;

•

totally legal
if both
s_{O}
and
s_{I}
are collapsible.

Moves act on states naturally: if
m
is a move, then
m
acts by inverting the status of all the facets belonging to
m
, while preserving the status of all the others.

The objective is to define a state on all copies
P_{v}
. To do so, we start by fixing a state
s_{0}
on
P_{0}
, called the
initial state
. We then define an action of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
on the states, by saying that the element of the basis
e_{i}
acts by applying on each state the move that contains the facet
F_{i}
. Since the actions of moves commute with each other, this defines an action of the whole
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
.
We use this action to propagate the initial state
s_{0}
of the polytope
P_{0}
to all other polytopes of the tessellation; to do so, we assign to the polytope
P_{v}
the state
s_{v}=v\cdot s_{0}
.

In particular, the state
s_{v+e_{i}}
is obtained from
s_{v}
by acting with the move containing
F_{i}
. This can be summarized with the slogan:

whenever we cross a facet
F
,

we invert the status of
F
and all other facets that are in the same move as
F
.

We use the states on the polytopes to define an orientation on each edge of the cube complex
C
that is dual to the tessellation in copies of
P
.
Every vertex of the dual cube complex
C
is dual to a polytope of the tessellation. We use the same letter
v
for both the vectors of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
and the vertices of
C
, since there is a natural correspondence between them. So for
v\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
we also denote by
v\in C
the vertex dual to the polytope
P_{v}
.

Let
F_{i}
be a facet, and let
e
be the edge connecting
v
with
v+e_{i}
. If the status
s_{v}(F_{i})
is
O
, we orient
e
from
v
to
v+e_{i}
; otherwise we put the opposite orientation.
Note that the status
s_{v}(F_{i})
of the facet
F_{i}
inside
P_{v}
is always the opposite of the status
s_{v+e_{i}}(F_{i})
of the facet
F_{i}
inside
P_{v+e_{i}}
. This simple fact follows from how the state propagation law is defined, and guarantees that the orientation on every edge of
C
is well-defined.

Denote with
C^{(1)}
the
1
-skeleton of
C
. We define a map
f^{(1)}\colon C^{(1)}\to S^{1}
as follows: every oriented edge is canonically identified with the interval
[0,1]
, which we map to the circle via the usual quotient
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}=S^{1}
. We would like to extend this map to the whole
C
.

Note that choosing an orientation for every edge naturally produces a cellular
1
-cochain, which maps every oriented edge to either
1
or
-1
, depending on whether the orientations coincide. To admit a continuous extension to
C
, we need this
1
-cochain to be a cocycle, meaning that every square should have two edges oriented clockwise and two oriented counter-clockwise.

However, for our purpose we also need the map to be piecewise linear. This motivates the following definition, given in
[
20
]
.

Definition 2.11
.

A square of
C
is
coherent
if parallel edges are oriented in the same direction.

Figure 1
.
The two possible orientations on a square producing a
1
-cocycle, up to rotation.

If the set of moves is sparse, then every square is coherent, and we can extend
f^{(1)}
on every cube via a diagonal map, as done in
[
20
]
. Here, however, the situation is more complicated.

Let
P_{v}
be a copy of
P
, and let
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
be two adjacent facets. Their intersection is dual to a square, as in
Fig.

1
. We have three cases:

•

If they belong to different moves, then the square is coherent, as in
Fig.

1
, left.

•

If they belong to the same move, and they have the same status, then the square is as in
Fig.

1
, centre (up to a rotation).

•

If they belong to the same move, and they have different status, then we get a square as in
Fig.

1
, right.

Note that if we have squares of the third kind, then the
1
-cochain is not a
1
-cocycle, so there is no hope to extend
f^{(1)}
to the whole
C
. The following definition ensures that we avoid this case.

Definition 2.12
.

A state
s
is
compatible
with a set of moves if whenever
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
are adjacent and belong to the same move, then
s(F_{i})=s(F_{j})
.

Remark 2.13
.

If a state
s
is compatible, than the whole orbit under the actions of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
via the moves is made of compatible states. This ensures that it is sufficient to check compatibility of the initial state.

From the analysis above, we get the following simple consequence.

Lemma 2.14
.

The map
f^{(1)}\colon C^{(1)}\to S^{1}
extends continuously to
C
if and only if the initial state is compatible.

However, to extend it to a Bestvina-Brady Morse function, we need a subdivision of the cubes. This had already been done in
[
18
]
, by subdividing the squares that are not coherent into four triangles: since here the situation is more complicated, we will work more abstractly and perform a full barycentric subdivision. We will explain in
Section

3
how to define a Bestvina-Brady Morse function on this subdivision.

2.3.
Bad faces and state inheritance

After performing the subdivision, a new vertex appears at the barycentre of every cell of the tessellation. To study the ascending and descending links at these new vertices, we need to introduce two definitions of combinatorial nature on the faces of
P
: the notion of
good
and
bad
face, and the
inherited state
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
P^{\prime}
be a face of
P
of codimension
\ell
. We call the facets of
P
that contain
P^{\prime}
the
defining facets of
P^{\prime}
.

Note that
P^{\prime}
is the intersection of all its defining facets. Let
P^{\prime}
be a face of
P
, with defining facets
F_{i_{1}},\dots,F_{i_{\ell}}
.

Definition 2.16
.

We call the face
P^{\prime}

good
if there is a move that contains exactly one of its defining facets. We call it
bad
otherwise.

A state on the polytope
P
induces states on all its faces, as follows.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
P
be a polytope equipped with a state
s
, and
P^{\prime}
be a face. For a facet
F^{\prime}
of
P^{\prime}
, consider the facet
F
of
P
such that
F^{\prime}=F\cap P^{\prime}
.

The
inherited state
on
P^{\prime}
assigns to
F^{\prime}
a status as follows:

•

it assigns
s(F)
if
F
is in a different move than all the defining facets of
P^{\prime}
;

•

it assigns status
O
otherwise, independently of the status of
F
.

Even though this definition could seem arbitrary, the reason for which we need to sometimes assign status
O
will become apparent in
Section

4
.

Remark 2.18
.

If
P^{\prime}=P
, then the inherited state coincides with the usual state. Moreover,
P
is a bad face of itself (since the set of defining facets is empty).

For now, we considered
P^{\prime}
to be a face of an abstract copy of the polytope
P
; there are however several copies of
P^{\prime}
inside the tessellation of
M
. Given a certain copy of
P^{\prime}
, we can choose some copy
P_{v}
of
P
that contains it. In particular, this copy of
P^{\prime}
is the intersection of all the copies of
P
of the form
P_{v+w}
, where
w\in\langle e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{\ell}}\rangle
. There is therefore a natural correspondence between copies of
P^{\prime}
and vectors of
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{c}/\langle e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{\ell}}\rangle
, so we will denote a copy of
P^{\prime}
by
P^{\prime}_{[v]}
, where
[v]
is the projection to the quotient of
v\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
.

We assign to
P^{\prime}_{[v]}
the inherited state from
P_{v}
.

Lemma 2.19
.

The inherited state
s_{[v]}
is well-defined, that is, if
v,w
are two vectors that project to the same element
[v]=[w]
in the quotient, then
s_{[v]}=s_{[w]}
.

Proof.

Consider a facet
F^{\prime}=P^{\prime}_{[v]}\cap F
of
P_{v}
. If
F
belongs to a move that contains one of the
F_{i_{j}}
, then
s_{[v]}(F^{\prime})=s_{[v]}(F^{\prime})=O
. This is independent of
v
, since it is defined using only data from the abstract polytope
P
.

Otherwise, suppose that
F
does not belong to the same move as any of the defining facets
F_{i_{1}},\dots,F_{i_{\ell}}
. In this case, the action of the defining facets does not change the status of
F
. Since
w
can be obtained from
v
by adding some
e_{i_{j}}
, and doing so does not change the status of
F
by what we just assumed, we get that
s_{v}(F)=s_{w}(F)
, and therefore by definition
s_{[v]}(F^{\prime})=s_{v}(F)=s_{w}(F)=s_{[w]}(F^{\prime})
.
∎

Note that the lemma above would not hold if we had defined the inherited state just by looking at the status of the corresponding facets of
P
, without taking the moves into account.

3.
The barycentric subdivision

Let
P
be a hyperbolic right-angled polytope, equipped with a set of moves and a compatible initial state. By using the Löbell construction described in the previous section, we obtain a manifold
M
tessellated into copies
P_{v}
of
P
, where
v\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
. Dual to the tessellation of
M
, we have a cube complex
C
.

Given any cell complex
X
, denote by
\sd X
the barycentric subdivision of
X
.

The aim of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
P
and
C
as above. There exists a Bestvina-Brady Morse function
f\colon\sd C\to S^{1}

such that the following holds for any copy
P^{\prime}_{[v]}
of some face
P^{\prime}
of
P
.

•

If the inherited state on
P^{\prime}_{[v]}
is
m
-legal for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then the ascending and descending link of
f
at the barycentre of
P^{\prime}
are
m
-connected.

•

If either
P^{\prime}
is good
or
the inherited state is
\infty
-legal, the ascending and descending links are collapsible.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that if we only consider sparse set of moves, we get the original theory of Jankiewicz–Norin–Wise: all the proper faces of
P
are good, so the vertices of
\sd C
that were introduced by the barycentric subdivision have collapsible ascending and descending link. A vertex
v
of the original cubulation corresponds dually to a copy
P_{v}
of
P
, so the ascending and descending links are determined by the state on
P_{v}
.

In the previous section, we used the states to build a Bestvina-Brady Morse function
f^{(1)}\colon C^{(1)}\to S^{1}
defined on the
1
-skeleton of
C
.
Starting from
f^{(1)}
, we define a Bestvina-Brady Morse function
f\colon\sd C\to S^{1}
on the barycentric subdivision of
C
.

Fix a small
\varepsilon>0
. We start by letting
f(v)=0
for every vertex
v\in\sd C
that comes from the original cubulation. Then we identify each oriented edge with
[0,1]
, where the barycentre corresponds to
\frac{1}{2}
, and we define
f
on that edge via the map
[0,1]\mapsto[0,1]/\sim
that sends
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
to
[0,\varepsilon]
and
[\frac{1}{2},1]
to
[\varepsilon,1]
linearly.

Suppose that we have already defined
f
on the barycentric subdivision of the
(k-1)
-skeleton
C^{(k-1)}
of
C
. Let
Q
be a
k
-cube of
C
, and let
\widehat{Q}
denote the barycentre of

. We may lift
f\colon\sd(Q^{(k-1)})\to S^{1}
to a map
\tilde{f}\colon\sd(Q^{(k-1)})\to\mathbb{R}
; here we are using that
f^{(1)}
comes from a cocycle when

.
We define

\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q})=\min\left\{{\left.\kern-1.2pt\tilde{f}\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial Q}}\right\}+k\varepsilon,

and we extend it linearly to all the simplices of
\sd C
. Then we pass it to the quotient
f\colon Q\to S^{1}
: it is easy to see that the resulting

does not depend on the choice of the lift. A concrete example of the construction can be seen in
Fig.

2
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that, in the previous definition, the minimum is always attained at a vertex of
Q
.

Figure 2
.
On the left, a square
Q
with its edges oriented; the number on the vertices are the values of the lift of

to

. On the right, the values of the lift

This procedure defines inductively a Bestvina-Brady map

.
To show that

satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem

3.1
, we employ a well-known decomposition of links in barycentric subdivision into a join of two simplicial complexes, as follows (compare for example
[
12
]
*Lemma 2.1).

Let
Q
be a cube of
C
. The link of its barycentre
\widehat{Q}
is the full subcomplex of
\sd C
whose vertices are all the barycentres of the proper faces and cofaces of
Q
.

This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

We call
face link of
Q
, and denote with
\flink Q
, the full subcomplex of
\sd C
whose vertices are barycentres of proper faces of
Q
. Similarly, we call
coface link of
Q
the full subcomplex of
\sd C
whose vertices are barycentres of proper cofaces of
Q
; we denote it with
\coflink Q
.

Remark 3.5
.

The face link
\flink Q
coincides with the barycentric subdivision of
\partial Q
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The link
\link(\widehat{Q},\sd C)
is the join
\flink Q*\coflink Q
.

Proof.

Follows directly from the definition of barycentric subdivision.
∎

We can obtain a similar statement for the ascending and descending links by intersecting them with the decomposition given above.

Definition 3.7
.

The
ascending
(resp.
descending
)
face link
of
Q
is the intersection of
\flink Q
with the ascending (resp. descending) link of
f
at
\widehat{Q}
.s The
ascending
(resp.
descending
)
coface link
of
Q
is defined analogously by intersecting
\coflink Q
with the ascending (resp. descending) link at
\widehat{Q}
.

We denote the ascending and descending face links with
\flink_{\uparrow/\downarrow}Q
, and the ascending/descending coface links with
\coflink_{\uparrow/\downarrow}Q
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The ascending link
\link_{\uparrow}(\widehat{Q};f)
is the join
\flink_{\uparrow}(Q)*\coflink_{\uparrow}(Q)
. Analogously, the descending link
\link_{\downarrow}(\widehat{Q})
is the join
\flink_{\downarrow}(Q)*\coflink_{\downarrow}(Q)
.

This way, to show that the ascending link of
\widehat{Q}
is collapsible, it suffices to show that at least one of
\flink_{\uparrow}(Q)
and
\coflink_{\uparrow}(Q)
is collapsible. We give an algorithmic way to compute both: we will show that the goodness of a face controls the face link, while the inherited state controls the coface link.

3.1.
Ascending and descending face links

Let
Q
be a
k
-cube of
C
. This is dual to some cell of the tessellation of
M
into copies of
P
; in particular, it is a face
P^{\prime}_{[v]}
of some
P_{v}
, where
v
is a vector of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}^{c}
. We denote by
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
the set of defining facets for
P^{\prime}
, so
P^{\prime}=\bigcap\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
.

Our aim is to compute ascending and descending face links of
Q
. To do this, we need to study the restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{Q}}\colon Q\to S^{1}
; since
Q
is simply connected, it is more convenient to study the lift
\tilde{f}\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
. We conventionally choose the lift such that it has minimum at
0
.

Let us start by analyzing the simple case where all the facets of
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
belong to the same move. We call such a cube
monochromatic
. By the compatibility of the state, all the edges coming out of a vertex of
Q
are all either oriented inward or all oriented outwards, so we get the pictures in
Fig.

3
.

Remark 3.9
.

All edges of
C
are by definition monochromatic
1
-cubes.

Figure 3
.
On the left, a monochromatic square; on the right, a monochromatic cube. The numbers on the vertices denote the values of
\tilde{f}
on the vertices.

We may explicitly compute the value of
\tilde{f}
at the vertices of the barycentric subdivision of a monochromatic cube
Q
. On the vertices of the original cube, the function assumes values
0
and
1
in a checkerboard-like pattern. Every positive-dimensional face
Q^{\prime}
of
Q
contains a vertex
v
such that
\tilde{f}(v)=0
, so
\tilde{f}
assumes the value
h\varepsilon
at the barycentres of all
h
-faces of
Q
, for all
1\leq h\leq k
.

We get the following lemma.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
Q
be a monochromatic
k
-dimensional cube. Then the ascending face link consists of
2^{k-1}
disjoint points, while the descending face link is obtained from
\sd\partial Q
by removing the open stars of
2^{k-1}
vertices of
Q
.

In particular, ascending and descending face links of
1
-cubes are collapsible, while ascending and descending face links of a monochromatic square collapse to
S^{0}
(i.e. a disjoint union of two points).

Proof.

Since
\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q})=k\varepsilon
, the only barycentres of faces on which
\tilde{f}
assumes a higher value are the vertices of the cube on which
f
assumes value
1
. So the ascending face link contains exactly half of the vertices of
Q
. The descending face link is the full subcomplex of
\sd\partial Q
with vertex set consisting of all vertices that do not belong to the ascending face link: so it is obtained by removing from
\sd\partial Q
the open stars of vertices in the ascending link.
∎

We now turn our attention to the general case. Given a cube
Q
, we can always decompose it as a product
Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q_{\ell}
, where every factor is monochromatic. This decomposition is obtained by looking at how the moves partition the set
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
; in particular, a
2
-dimensional face of
Q
is coherent if and only it is not contained in one of the factors.

Consider a product
Q=Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
of two monochromatic cubes. If
v,v^{\prime}
are vertices of
Q_{2}
, the orientation of the edges on
Q_{1}\times\{v\}
and
Q_{1}\times\{v^{\prime}\}
coincide, so
f(x,v)=f(x,v^{\prime})
for all
x\in Q_{1}
. For this reason, whenever we have a product
Q=Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q_{\ell}
of monochromatic cubes, we have a well-defined restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{Q_{i}}}
, obtained by restricting
f
to any face of the form
\{v_{1}\}\times\dots\times Q_{i}\times\dots\times\{v_{\ell}\}
, where
v_{j}
is a vertex of
Q_{j}
for all
j\neq i
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
Q
be a cube of
C
that decomposes as a product of monochromatic cubes
Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q_{\ell}
. Then

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{Q_{i}}}(x_{i})

for every
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})\in Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q_{\ell}
.

Proof.

Lift every
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{Q_{i}}}
to a map
\tilde{f}_{i}\colon Q_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
, in such a way that their minimum is
0
. Lift also
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{Q}}
to
\tilde{f}\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
, with the same condition on the minimum. We claim that
\tilde{f}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{f}_{i}(x_{i})
; once proved this, the lemma follows by passing to the quotient.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}
be vertices of
\sd Q_{1},\dots,\sd Q_{\ell}
. Choose an
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, and let
x^{\prime}_{i}
be another vertex of
\sd Q_{i}
.

Then we have that

\tilde{f}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{\ell})-\tilde{f}(x_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{i},\dots,x_{\ell})=\tilde{f}_{i}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i}).

This implies that the two members of the claimed equality coincide up to an integer constant. By the condition on the minimum, the constant must be
0
, so we conclude that the claim holds whenever the
x_{i}
are vertices of the barycentric subdivision.

Since we are taking the unique affine extension on each simplex of
\sd Q
, the claim holds for every point in
Q
.
∎

We now translate
Definition

2.16
to the dual cube complex.

Definition 3.12
.

We call a
k
-cube
Q

good
if it is dual to a good face of
P
(as per
Definition

2.16
), and
bad
otherwise.

Remark 3.13
.

As noted in
Remark

2.18
, the polytope
P
is always bad; therefore all vertices of
C
are bad, since that are dual to some copy of
P
.

A
k
-cube is therefore good if and only if there is a move containing exactly one facet of
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
; in other words, when at least one of its monochromatic factors is
1
-dimensional.

In
Fig.

4
we have an example of a product of a bad square with an interval, which is a good cube.

Figure 4
.
On the left, the product of a bad square with an interval. On the right, the ascending (blue) and descending (red) face links. The descending link is a join of the face links of a bad square and of an interval, while the ascending link only collapses on a join (drawn with a thick blue line).

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
Q
be a cube of
C
, and let
Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q_{\ell}
be its decomposition into monochromatic factors. The descending face link of
Q
is a subdivision of the join of the descending links of the
Q_{i}
.

Proof.

To see clearly the join decomposition, it is more convenient to look at the dual of the cubes, which are cross-polytopes. This way, we have that
\partial Q^{*}
is the join of the
\partial Q_{i}^{*}
. Recall also that the barycentric subdivision of a polytope is isomorphic to the barycentric subdivision of its dual.

By the definition of
f
, the barycentre of some face
Q^{\prime}
of
Q
belongs to the descending face link of
Q
if and only if
Q^{\prime}
contains a minimum for
\tilde{f}\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
. If
Q^{\prime}
decomposes as a product of
Q^{\prime}_{1}\times\dots\times Q^{\prime}_{\ell}
, requiring that
Q^{\prime}
contains a minimum for
\tilde{f}
is equivalent to requiring that
Q^{\prime}_{i}
contains a minimum for
\tilde{f}_{i}
for all
i
, which is in turn equivalent to the barycentre of
Q^{\prime}_{i}
belonging to the descending face link of
Q_{i}
. By looking dually, this proves that the descending face link of
Q
is the join of the descending face links of the
Q_{i}
.

Similarly, the barycentre of
Q^{\prime}
belongs to the ascending link if and only if at least one of the barycentres of the
Q^{\prime}_{i}
belong to the ascending link of
Q_{i}
. This means that the ascending link is a regular neighbourhood of the join of the ascending links of the
Q_{i}
, and so it collapses on that join (for details on the definition of regular neighbourhood, we refer to
[
29
, Chapter 4]
).
∎

Proposition 3.15
.

If
Q
is a good cube, then the ascending and descending face links of
Q
are collapsible.

If
Q
is a
2k
-cube, and every move contains either zero or two facets of
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
, then the ascending and descending face links of
Q
collapse to the barycentric subdivision of the boundary of a
k
-dimensional cross-polytope (which is PL-homeomorphic to
S^{k-1}
).

Proof.

In the first case, one of the factors of
Q
is an interval, whose ascending and descending face link is a point. By
Lemma

3.14
, the ascending and descending links of
Q
collapse on a cone, which is in turn collapsible.

In the second case, all the monochromatic factors of
Q
are squares, so by
Lemma

3.10
the ascending and descending link collapse on the join of
k
copies of
S^{0}
, i.e. the boundary of a
k
-dimensional cross-polytope.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

For vertices of the cubulation, the face link is empty, and in particular non-collapsible. This does not contradict
Proposition

3.15
as vertices are
bad
by definition.

Corollary 3.17
.

The ascending and descending links of
f
at the barycentre of a good cube are collapsible.

Proof.

Follows by combining
Proposition

3.15
with
Lemma

3.8
.
∎

3.2.
The coface link

Let us switch our focus to the coface link. Let
Q
be a cube of
C
; we are mostly interested in the case when
Q
is bad, otherwise we already know that the ascending and descending links at
\widehat{Q}
are collapsible.

Remark 3.18
.

To better understand this section, it is convenient to keep in mind the simple case where
Q
is
0
-dimensional, i.e. it is a vertex
v
. In this case,
Q
is the barycentre of
P_{v}
, and the coface link is the whole link.

We employ the same notation used for the study of the face link, so we let
Q
be dual to some cell
P^{\prime}
of the tessellation of
M
into copies of
P
.
Let
v
be a vertex of
Q
, so that
P_{v}
contains
P^{\prime}
, and let
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
denote the facets of
P_{v}
that intersect in
P^{\prime}
.

The coface link of
Q
, being the dual notion of the face link, is the barycentric subdivision of the boundary of
P^{\prime}
. Its vertices are in correspondence with all the proper cofaces of
Q
; to compute the ascending and descending coface link, a priori we would need to compute for each coface of
Q
whether the corresponding barycentre is in the ascending or descending link. The following lemma allows us to restrict our analysis to cofacets, i.e. cofaces of dimension exactly one higher than
\dim Q
.

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
Q\subset Q^{\prime}
be two cubes of
C
.
Then
\widehat{Q^{\prime}}\in\coflink_{\uparrow}(Q)
if and only if for every cofacet
Q^{\prime\prime}
of
Q
, with
Q^{\prime\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
, we have
\widehat{Q^{\prime\prime}}\in\coflink_{\uparrow}(Q)
.
Conversely, we have that
\widehat{Q^{\prime}}\in\coflink_{\downarrow}(Q)
if and only if there is some cofacet of
Q
contained in
Q^{\prime}
whose barycentre is in
\coflink_{\downarrow}(Q)
.

Proof.

Lift
f\colon Q^{\prime}\to S^{1}
to a function
\tilde{f}\colon Q^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
such that its minimum is
0
. Then
\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q^{\prime}})=\varepsilon\cdot\dim Q^{\prime}
, and
\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q})<\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q^{\prime}})
if and only if
\tilde{f}
assumes the value
0
on
Q
. In this case, for every cofacet
Q\subset Q^{\prime\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
we have that
\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q^{\prime\prime}})=\varepsilon\cdot\dim Q^{\prime\prime}>\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q})
, so all cofacets of
Q
contained in
Q^{\prime}
have their barycentre in
\coflink_{\uparrow}(Q)
.

Vice versa, decompose
Q^{\prime}
as a product
Q^{\prime}_{1}\times\dots\times Q^{\prime}_{\ell}
of its monochromatic factors, and define
Q_{i}=Q^{\prime}_{i}\cap Q
, so that
Q=Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q_{i}
.

Suppose that
\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q})>\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q^{\prime}})
. Then by using
Lemma

3.11
there must be a
Q_{i}
with
\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q_{i}})>\tilde{f}(\widehat{Q^{\prime}})
; so
Q_{i}
does not contain a minimum of
\tilde{f}\colon Q^{\prime}_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
. The only subcubes of a monochromatic cube that do not contain a minimum are the vertices where
\tilde{f}
attains maximum. Therefore, the cube
Q_{i}
is
0
-dimensional.
If we let
Q^{\prime\prime}_{i}
be any edge such that
Q_{i}\subset Q^{\prime\prime}_{i}\subseteq Q^{\prime}_{i}
, we obtain a cofacet

Q^{\prime\prime}=Q_{1}\times\dots\times Q^{\prime\prime}_{i}\times\dots\times Q_{\ell}

of
Q
, whose barycentre is contained in
\coflink_{\downarrow}(Q)
, as requested.
∎

Cofacets of
Q
correspond dually to facets of
P^{\prime}
. If
P^{\prime}=F_{1}\cap\dots\cap F_{k}
, then the facets of
P^{\prime}
are in natural correspondence with the facets
F
of
P
that are adjacent to all
F_{i}
, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
; the correspondence is given by
F\mapsto F\cap P^{\prime}
.
Recall that
P^{\prime}
inherits a state from
P
, by assigning to the facet
F\cap P^{\prime}
of
P^{\prime}
an inherited status as follows:

•

if
F
is in the same move as at least one of the
F_{i}
, then we assign status
O
;

•

else, we assign the same status as
F
.

We are finally able to motivate this definition with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
Q
be a
k
-cube of
C
, and let

be one of its vertices. Consider the copy

dual to

, and let

be the face of

.

Let

be a cofacet of
Q
, and let

be the facet of

such that

is dual to

.

Then
\widehat{Q^{\prime}}\in\coflink_{\uparrow}(Q)
if and only if

has inherited status

in

.

.

4.
The polytope
P^{6}

In this section, we introduce the
6
-dimensional polytope
P^{6}
to which the Löbell construction will be applied, and describe its combinatorial structure. In the latter part of the section, we present an initial state, and the set of moves that allow us to construct the Bestvina-Brady function using the theory described in Section
3
.

The polytope
P^{6}
belongs to a particular sequence of polytopes, and shares many properties with the other members of its family. For this reason, we start by describing this sequence, in a wider context.

4.1.
Potyagailo-Vinberg polytopes

There is a remarkable sequence of right-angled hyperbolic polytopes
P^{n}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
, for
n=3,\dots,8
, which was first considered by Agol, Long, and Reid
[
1
]
. They are obtained by considering the star of a vertex in a tessellation of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
by copies of a suitable Coxeter simplex. The combinatorics of these polytopes was later studied by Potyagailo and Vinberg
[
27
]
, and Everitt, Ratcliffe, and Tschantz
[
13
]
; in particular, it was shown that they are dual to some semi-regular Euclidean polytopes discovered by Gosset
[
16
]
in 1900. These polytopes were already used in
[
19
]
, to construct algebraic fibring of some hyperbolic manifolds, and in particular the polytope
P^{5}
is the one used in
[
18
]
to construct a hyperbolic
5
-manifold fibring over the circle.

We summarize in the next paragraphs the properties of the polytopes that are needed for this paper, and we refer to the previous sources
[
13
,
27
]
for more detailed information about the polytopes, and
[
18
]
for the particular use in this context.

It is worth noting that each
k
-dimensional face of
P^{n}
is isometric to the polytope
P^{k}
, so these polytopes are closely related to each other. For this reason, even though the main protagonist of our construction is
P^{6}
, we start our analysis from the
5
-dimensional polytope
P^{5}
.

4.2.
The polytope
P^{5}

The polytope
P^{5}
has
16
facets,
16
real vertices and
10
ideal vertices. Each facet is isometric to the polytope
P^{4}
, and each real vertex is opposed to a facet. It is dual to the Gosset polytope
1_{21}
.

It is possible to visualize
P^{5}
inside the Klein model for the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{5}
, as the intersection of the
16
half-spaces

\pm x_{1}\pm x_{2}\pm x_{3}\pm x_{4}\pm x_{5}\leq 1

having an even number of minus signs. The
10
ideal vertices are precisely the points

(\pm 1,0,0,0,0),\ (0,\pm 1,0,0,0),\ (0,0,\pm 1,0,0),\ (0,0,0,\pm 1,0),\ (0,0,0,0,\pm 1);

while the real vertices are the points of the form
\left(\pm\frac{1}{3},\pm\frac{1}{3},\pm\frac{1}{3},\pm\frac{1}{3},\pm\frac{1}{3}\right)
with an odd number of minus signs.
Each real vertex is opposed a facet.

In this simple description, all its isometries are the maps of the form

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\mapsto(\pm x_{\sigma(1)},\pm x_{\sigma(2)},\pm x_{\sigma(3)},\pm x_{\sigma(4)},\pm x_{\sigma(5)})

where
\sigma\in S_{5}
is any permutation, and the number of minus signs is even. The isometry group
\Isom(P^{5})
has therefore order
2^{4}\cdot 5!=1920
.

Since there is an even number of minus signs, each isometry is determined by the first four coordinates of the images. We look at
\mathbb{R}^{5}
as
\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}
, and interpret
\mathbb{R}^{4}
as the quaternion space
\mathbb{H}
. This point of view allows us to better describe two useful subgroups of
\Isom(P^{5})
, already considered in
[
18
]
, that are
Q_{8}
and
R_{16}
. Here we summarize their definitions and properties.

The group
Q_{8}
is the quaternion group
\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}
. It acts on the quaternion space
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{4}
by left multiplication, and this action can be extended trivially on
\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}
. Since it preserves all the half-spaces defining
P^{5}
, it preserves the polytope, so it can be seen as a subgroup of
\Isom(P^{5})
.

Among the isometries of
P^{5}
, we consider the involution
\iota
given by

\iota\colon(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\mapsto(x_{1},-x_{2},-x_{4},-x_{3},-x_{5})

One may verify that
\iota
normalizes the subgroup
Q_{8}
, and that the group
R_{16}
generated by
Q_{8}
and
\iota
has order
16
. Moreover,
R_{16}
acts transitively on the facets of
P^{5}
.

Finally, we are interested the binary tetrahedral group
Q_{8}\cup\{\frac{1}{2}(\pm 1\pm i\pm j\pm k)\}
, which has
24
elements. While it could be seen as a subgroup of
\Isom(P^{5})
in the same way
Q_{8}
can, we will not need it in this paper. In fact, it will be just used as a set of labels, to encode some combinatorial properties of the polytopes; to simplify the notation, we will use
T_{24}=Q_{8}\cup\{(\pm 1\pm i\pm j\pm k)\}
, omitting the

. The action of
R_{16}
on
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}
restricts naturally to an action on
T_{24}\subset\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}
.

4.5.
States

We now choose an initial state for

Definition 4.3
.

A state is called

if it assigns the same status to

,
B
, and

, and if for every other move it assigns status

to one of the two triples of pairwise adjacent facets, and

to the facets of the other triple.

In particular, a balanced state satisfies the property that, given two facets with label in

and in the same move, they are adjacent if and only if they have the same status. In particular, every balanced state is compatible.

Lemma 4.4
.

If the initial state is balanced, then the orbit consists precisely of all balanced states.

Proof.

Since inverting the status of all facets in a move preserves the balancedness of a state, the orbit contains only balanced states. Let
s
be the initial state, and

be another balanced state. Moreover, let

be a set of facets containing exactly one facet for every move.

By acting with the moves, we can send

to another state

which coincides with

on

. Since a balanced state is uniquely determined by its restriction to

, and the action is therefore transitive on the balanced states.
∎

Therefore, we choose as initial state any balanced state, as the resulting orbit does not depend on the chosen balanced state.

5.
The extended cubulation

In the previous section we have constructed a Bestvina-Brady Morse function

with well-behaved ascending and descending links, as described in

4.6
. We would like to promote this to a perfect circle-valued Morse function on

By truncating

, and is tessellated into copies of the truncated polytope

[

Let

that contains all the cubes intersecting

We extend

Remark 5.1
.

This extension produces the same result as the procedure in
[

Another possibility was to consider directly the extended cubulation in the previous section, orienting all its edges as done in
[

later, we avoid having to perform a full barycentric subdivision of the collars, which are now only subdivided into prisms over simplices of

To prove that the

6.
Smoothing

The aim of this section is to promote the Bestvina-Brady Morse function on
\bar{C}
to a smooth one.
In the following, we employ the same terminology and definitions given in
[
29
]
.

Let
M
a PL
n
-manifold with a compatible structure of an affine cell complex. Given a Bestvina-Brady Morse function
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define an analogue of the concepts of regular and critical point that are defined for smooth Morse functions.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
M
be an affine cell complex that is a compact PL manifold, and let
f\colon M\to S^{1}
be a Bestvina-Brady Morse function. We call a vertex
v

regular
if the descending link is collapsible. We call it
critical of index
i
if its descending link collapses to a PL
(i-1)
-sphere.

This analogy can be made precise by the following statement.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
M
be an affine cell complex that is a compact PL manifold of dimension
n\leq 6
, with possibly non-empty boundary, and
f\colon M\to S^{1}
be a Bestvina-Brady Morse function.

Suppose that every vertex
v
is either regular, or is critical of index
i_{v}
and is contained in the interior of
M
. Suppose moreover that the restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial M}}
has only regular vertices.

Then there exists a unique smooth structure on
M
compatible with the piecewise-linear one, and there exists a smooth Morse function
g\colon M\to S^{1}
with the same amount of critical points for every index as
f
.

Remark 6.3
.

The bound on the dimension is a technical condition under which the smooth and the PL categories coincide. In dimension
7
there are PL manifolds admitting multiple compatible smooth structures, while in higher dimension there might be no compatible smooth structures. We refer the interested reader to
[
5
, Section 1.3]
.

The core of the proof of
Proposition

6.2
is a lemma of local nature. Before stating it we need to recall the definition of shelling.

Definition 6.4
.

An
elementary shelling
of a PL
n
-manifold
M
is the removal of
\interior B\cup(B\cap\partial M)
, where
B
is a PL
n
-ball such that
B\cap\partial M
is a
(n-1)
-ball; we say that
M
shells to
N\subseteq M
if
N
can be obtained by a sequence of elementary shellings. Note that, in contrast to collapses, shelling does preserve PL homomorphism type (so if
M
shells to
N
, then
M
and
N
are PL-homeomorphic). For more details, see
[
29
, Chapter 3]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
M
be a PL
n
-manifold with boundary, and let
B
be a PL
n
-ball. Let
C\subseteq\partial B,\,C^{\prime}\subseteq\partial M
be PL
(n-1)
-submanifolds with (possibly empty) boundary, with a PL homeomorphism

7.
The infinite cover and finiteness properties

Given the manifold
M
equipped with the perfect Morse function
f\colon M\to S^{1}
, we consider the infinite cyclic cover
\widetilde{M}
associated with
\ker f_{*}
. Analogously to
[
4
, Corollary 5]
we obtain the following.

Corollary 7.1
.

The infinite cyclic cover
\widetilde{M}
is a geometrically infinite hyperbolic manifold that is diffeomorphic to
F\times[0,1]
with infinitely many
3
-handles attached on both sides, where
F
is the interior of a
5
-manifold with toric boundary, and has nontrivial
\pi_{2}(F)
.

Moreover,
\widetilde{M}
has fundamental group that is finitely presented but not type
\mathcal{F}_{3}
, and has
b_{3}(\widetilde{M})=\infty
.

Note that there is a key difference when compared to the
4
-dimensional case. The regular fibres obtained in
[
4
]
are all hyperbolic
3
-manifolds; this cannot happen in dimension
6
, as they are not aspherical.

Proof.

Lift
f\colon M\to S^{1}
to a Morse function
\tilde{f}\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
F
be the preimage of a regular value
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since
\tilde{f}
has infinitely many critical points, all of index
3
, by Morse theory we can obtain
\widetilde{M}
from
F\times[t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon]
by attaching infinitely many
3
-handles.
From this, it follows that
b_{3}(\widetilde{M})
is infinite.

Attaching
3
-handles does not change the fundamental group, so
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{M})\cong\pi_{1}(F)
is finitely presented. Since
\widetilde{M}
is aspherical, it is a classifying space for
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{M})
, so
b_{3}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{M}))
is also infinite. Therefore the fundamental group cannot be of type
\mathcal{F}_{3}
.

It remains to prove that
\pi_{2}(F)
is nontrivial. If
\pi_{2}(F)=0
, then we could construct a classifying space for
\pi_{1}(F)
by attaching to
F
cells of dimension
\geq 4
, that contradicts
b_{3}(\pi_{1}(F))=b_{3}(\widetilde{M})=\infty
.
∎

Since
M
is not compact, its fundamental group is not hyperbolic.
However, it is possible to obtain a hyperbolic group by considering an appropriate
filling
of the cusps, similarly to what was done in
[
18
, Section 3]
.

By removing a small horoball at each cusp of
M
, we obtain a compact manifold
\bar{M}
, with boundary components diffeomorphic to the
5
–torus. By possibly passing to a finite cover, we may assume that each boundary component has systole larger than
2\pi
.

The Morse function
f
restricts to a fibration on each
5
–torus; we may assume, up to an isotopy, that this fibration has geodesic
4
-tori as fibres. Denote by
\hat{M}
the space obtained by coning every
4
-torus to a point, so that
f
extends to a map on
\hat{M}
. Let
\hat{F}
be the preimage of a regular value
t
inside
\hat{M}
,
G\coloneqq\pi_{1}(\hat{M})
, and
H\coloneqq\pi_{1}(\hat{F})
.

Proposition 7.2
.

The group
G
is Gromov-hyperbolic and torsion-free, and the subgroup
H
is finitely presented but not of type
\mathcal{F}_{3}
.

This resembles closely the construction of a group of type
\mathcal{F}_{3}
not
\mathcal{F}_{4}
in
[
23
]
, based on a Morse function defined on a hyperbolic
8
-manifold
M^{8}
. Since the Morse function on
M^{6}
is perfect, which is a much stronger condition than the one on
M^{8}
, the proof here is much simpler.

Proof.

The filling is a
2\pi
–filling, and by
[
15
]

\hat{M}
admits a negatively curved metric, so its fundamental group is Gromov-hyperbolic and torsion free.

The group
H
is finitely presented, since it is the fundamental group of a compact pseudo-manifold. The infinite cyclic cover
X
of
\hat{M}
associated with
H
is aspherical, and can be obtained from
\hat{F}\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
by attaching infinitely many
3

This implies that

, so

is not of type
\mathcal{F}_{3}

∎

8.
Related results and some questions

The main inspirations for this paper are the combinatorial game in
[
20
]
, and some of its applications
[
4
,
19
,
18
]
.
A natural direction of investigation is to continue pursuing this line towards higher dimensions.

Question 8.1
.

Do the manifolds
M^{7}
and
M^{8}
in
[
19
]
admit a perfect circle-valued Morse function?

In the case of
M^{7}
such a function would be a fibration. Following the intuition of this paper, one could try to partition the
56
facets of
P^{7}
into
7
moves with
8
faces in each, and use balanced states to define a Morse function. However, the resulting function is necessarily null-homotopic on some cusp of
P^{7}
, as it does not satisfy
Lemma

5.2
, so it cannot be a fibration. The reason is that
P^{7}
has
126
ideal vertices, but there would be at most
4\cdot 4\cdot 7=112
pairs of facets in the same move and with opposite status.
The problem relative to the manifold
M^{7}
has also been studied in
[
14
]
with more algebraic techniques.

In
[
4
]
some regular and singular fibres of perfect Morse functions on hyperbolic
4
–manifolds were computed.
They all are hyperbolic
3
-manifolds, and therefore aspherical.

In
Corollary

7.1
, we have shown that regular fibres of
6
-dimensional hyperbolic manifolds cannot be aspherical. It would be interesting to know more about the regular and singular fibres of the example we constructed.

Question 8.2
.

What are the regular fibres of the map
f\colon M^{6}\to S^{1}
? What are the singular fibres?

Finally, we focus on the following question.

Question 8.3

(
[
4
]
, Question 27)
.

Does every hyperbolic manifold have a finite cover that admits a perfect circle-valued Morse function?

Since perfect Morse functions lift to finite covers,
Theorem

A
shows that
Question

8.3
has positive answer for all manifolds commensurable to
P^{6}
.

Passing to a finite cover is necessary, as it is not hard to build a
6
-manifold that does not admit a perfect Morse function. In fact, two obvious obstructions are the vanishing of the first Betti number, or the presence of one cusp that does not fibre over
S^{1}
. Indeed, both orientable
6
–manifolds built in
[
13
]
satisfy both obstruction properties; they have
b_{1}=0
and also a cusp section with vanishing
b_{1}
.
Regarding closed manifolds, one possible obstruction would be the vanishing of
b_{1}
, however the authors do not know any example of a closed hyperbolic
6
–manifold
M
with
b_{1}(M)=0
(there are explicit examples in dimension
5

[
10
]
and
7

[
6
]
).

Lastly, we point out that there have been also some findings that could suggest a negative answer to
Question

8.3
, at least in a more generalised context: Avramidi, Okun and Schreve
[
3

constructed a closed, aspherical
