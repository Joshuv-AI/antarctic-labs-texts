Vaginal Weissella confusa MV0113 suppresses pathogens and restores microbiota to alleviate bacterial vaginosis: integrated genomic and in vivo evidence
Authors: Cuiling Luo, Xue Dong, Jiarong Song, Yi Li, Qingqing Zhang, Yu Pang, Bisong Yue, Tao Guo, Zhenxin Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bacterial vaginosis (BV), the most common vaginal infection in women of reproductive age, shows high recurrence after antibiotic therapy, highlighting the need for novel probiotic approaches. This study aimed to evaluate the probiotic properties of the vaginal isolate Weissella confusa MV0113 and its potential role in preventing and managing BV. MV0113 remained sensitive to clinically relevant antibiotics and produced high levels of lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide. Its cell-free supernatant inhibited Candida albicans and Gardnerella vaginalis biofilms, showing greater activity than the reference probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG). Strong adhesion of MV0113 to HeLa cells was observed, suggesting preliminary potential for mucosal colonization. Whole-genome sequencing indicated functional potential for carbohydrate metabolism and bacteriocin production. In a mouse model of BV, MV0113 treatment alleviated vaginal inflammation, modulated cytokine expression, and restored microbial balance by increasing Lactobacillus abundance and reducing pathogens. By integrating genomic, in vitro, and in vivo analyses, our findings suggest that MV0113 is a promising probiotic candidate for BV management, warranting further clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04552-w.

Introduction

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is one of the most common vaginal infections globally, particularly affecting women of reproductive age. Epidemiological data indicate that BV accounts for approximately 40%–50% of all vaginitis cases and is one of the leading causes of female reproductive tract infections [1, 2]. Metagenomic studies have revealed that the healthy female vagina is typically dominated by Lactobacillus species, which maintain a low pH environment and inhibit pathogen colonization by producing lactic acid, hydrogen peroxide, and antimicrobial peptides [3]. However, when Lactobacillus levels decline and anaerobes such as Gardnerella and Atopobium proliferate abnormally, dysbiosis occurs, which can trigger BV, vulvovaginal candidiasis, urinary tract infections, infertility, and other reproductive tract conditions [4, 5]. This microbial imbalance has been associated with increased risks of HIV infection, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and failure of assisted reproductive technologies [6–8]. Therefore, BV represents a major public health concern.

Currently, the standard treatment for BV relies on oral or intravaginal administration of antibiotics such as metronidazole and clindamycin. Although these treatments provide transient symptom relief, recurrence rates remain high, with approximately 50–60% of women experiencing relapses within weeks to months [9]. This high recurrence rate is partly attributed to Gardnerella-dominated biofilms, which impede antibiotic penetration and persist after therapy [10, 11]. Moreover, the broad-spectrum bactericidal activity of antibiotics disrupts beneficial microbial communities, leading to further vaginal dysbiosis. Repeated antibiotic use also promotes the emergence of resistant strains, exacerbating treatment failure and causing systemic side effects such as nausea, dizziness, and gastrointestinal disturbances [12]., which further reduce patient compliance. Therefore, there is an urgent need for alternative therapies that are more targeted, ecologically compatible, and less prone to relapse.

Although various probiotic products aimed at improving female vaginal health are already available on the market, most commercial Lactobacillus formulations are derived from intestinal strains and may show limited ecological adaptation. Oral supplementation with these strains has failed to reduce infections or achieve vaginal colonization in a multicenter RCT [13]. Vaginal-dominant Lactobacillus species differ significantly from gut-derived strains in terms of genetic background, metabolic capabilities, colonization potential, and immune-regulatory effects [14–16]. Vaginal and intestinal Lactobacillus crispatus (L. crispatus) strains display distinct phenotypes in glycogen metabolism, glycosylation, and both anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial functions [17, 18]. Vaginal-dominant Lactobacillus species have demonstrated better colonization potential and therapeutic efficacy in preventing BV recurrence and promoting vaginal health due to their unique metabolic and immune-regulatory functions [19–21]. However, the availability of vaginal-origin strains is limited, particularly those with strong lactic-acid–producing and antimicrobial capabilities.

Weissella confusa (W. confusa) has been underexplored for vaginal applications despite its lactic-acid–producing and adhesive potential [22]. Genomic analysis reveals that W. confusa possesses genes related to exopolysaccharide (EPS) production, stress responses, and antimicrobial activity, further supporting its potential as a probiotic strain [23]. Additionally, its genome shows the ability to metabolize a wide range of carbon sources, particularly its capacity to utilize various sugars during fermentation, which enhances its adaptability in diverse fermentative environments [24]. These characteristics make W. confusa a promising candidate for the BV management and related conditions. This study aimed to systematically evaluate a vaginal isolate, W. confusa MV0113—via in vitro functional assays, genome analysis, and an in vivo BV mouse model—to determine its probiotic potential for clinical application. Whole-genome sequencing was also conducted to investigate its functional genetic basis. Finally, a murine model of bacterial vaginosis was established, and histopathological analysis along with vaginal microbiota 16 S rRNA sequencing was employed to assess its in vivo therapeutic effects. Our study provides a rare and clinically promising candidate strain for the development of vaginal probiotic preparations.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Mid–vaginal secretions were collected in July 2022 from healthy volunteers at West China Second University Hospital’s gynecology outpatient clinic (Sichuan University) using sterile cotton swabs. Samples were stored at 4 °C until processing. The study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (2022-054) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria: age 24–55 years; sexually active; regular menstrual cycles; no antibiotics or estrogen in the past 30 days; no vaginal medications or douching in the past 7 days; no cervical procedures in the past 3 months.

Strain isolation and culture conditions

W. confusa MV0113 was isolated from vaginal secretions and identified by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Stocks are maintained at the Key Laboratory of Wildlife Resources Protection, Sichuan University. Gram staining was performed using a commercial kit (Guangdong Huankai Biotech). MV0113 was cultured in MRS medium (Guangdong Huankai Biotech) at 37 °C for 18 h. Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, CMCCB 26003) were obtained from Yijia Biotech and grown in TSB (Guangdong Huankai Biotech) at 37 °C for 18 h. Gardnerella vaginalis (Thermo Fisher Scientific)(G. vaginalis, ATCC 14018) was cultured in NYC III medium (5% CO₂, 37 °C, 18 h). Candida albicans (C. albicans, ATCC 10231) was grown in Sabouraud dextrose medium (Guangdong Huankai Biotech) at 30 °C for 24 h.

In vitro probiotic characterization

Hemolysis and antibiotic susceptibility

MV0113 was streaked on Columbia blood agar and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h to assess hemolysis; S. aureus served as the β-hemolysis control. Antibiotic sensitivity was measured by the disk diffusion method, testing chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, ceftriaxone, clindamycin, clarithromycin, gentamicin, and ampicillin (Shanghai Source Leaf Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Results were interpreted per Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) (where applicable) for fastidious organisms; breakpoints for Weissella were considered with caution.

Antagonism against C. albicans and G. vaginalis

Following Zhang [25] with modifications, C. albicans cells were harvested (4 °C, 8000 rpm, 10 min), washed, and adjusted to 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. MV0113 cell-free supernatant (CFS) was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with C. albicans suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Cultures were serially diluted (10⁴–10⁶) and plated for viable counts. MRS and Sabouraud media served as negative and positive controls, respectively.

For G. vaginalis biofilms, 100 µL of G. vaginalis (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was added to 96-well plates and incubated 24 h (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Wells were treated with 100 µL MV0113 CFS and incubated an additional 24 h. Biofilms were washed, fixed in methanol, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, washed, destained with 33% acetic acid. The biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring the absorbance at OD₅₉₀ using a microplate reader.

H₂O₂ and lactic acid production

MRS agar was supplemented with 0.01 mg/mL Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.25 mg/mL Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Sigma-Aldrich), poured into plates, cooled, then inoculated with MV0113. After anaerobic overnight culture at 37 °C, plates were exposed to air and timed for color development, indicating H₂O₂ production. Lactic acid was quantified by a commercial assay kit per the manufacturer’s protocol (Sigma-Aldrich).

HeLa cell adhesion

HeLa cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were routinely cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientifi) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were authenticated and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination prior to use. For adhesion assays, HeLa cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. MV0113 was harvested (8000 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min), washed twice with PBS (Beijing Zhongshu Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd), and resuspended to 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. After removing the culture medium and washing the cells with PBS, 0.1 mL of bacterial suspension was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were then washed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich), Gram-stained, and examined under oil immersion. Adherent bacteria were counted in 45 randomly selected fields (5 cells per field), and each assay was performed in triplicate. HeLa cells were used as a standardized epithelial substrate for preliminary adhesion screening because of their wide availability and reproducibility across assays.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted and sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform. Reads were assembled with SPAdes and gap-closed using GapCloser. Assembly quality was evaluated by assembly-stats and QUAST. Phylogenetic analysis was performed with OrthoFinder; genomic similarities were assessed via fastANI and CompareM. Gene prediction used Prodigal; Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations were assigned via eggNOG-mapper. Antibiotic resistance genes were identified with the CARD database; carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were annotated by DIAMOND against CAZy, and secondary metabolite gene clusters predicted using antiSMASH.

Murine bacterial vaginosis model

Mice were purchased from Chengdu Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (license number SCXK(Chuan) 2020-030). All procedures involving animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Life Sciences, Sichuan University (No. SCU230303001) and were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Mice were euthanized under deep isoflurane anesthesia (3% induction; 1.5% maintenance), followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death under unconsciousness. This protocol complied with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Following Zhou et al. [26], mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 9/group): blank control (BC), model (M), preventive (P), preventive-therapeutic (PT), and therapeutic (T). Estrus was induced every three days by subcutaneous injection of 0.2 mg β-estradiol (Sigma-Aldrich) valerate in 100 µL sesame oil.

From days 1–7, groups P and PT received daily intravaginal W. confusa MV0113 (1 × 108 CFU/100 µL), whereas BC, M, and T received saline. On days 8–9, all groups exceptBC were intravaginally infected with G. vaginalis (1 × 108 CFU/100 µL). From days 10–17, groups PT and T received daily MV0113, whereas BC, P, and M received saline. Vaginal lavage for CFU counts was collected at baseline (day 0, pre-infection) and on days 3, 6, and 9 post-infection. Clinical scoring of vulvar swelling and vaginal discharge was performed on days 2, 6, and 9 post-infection. At each sampling time point, three mice/group were euthanized; vaginal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stored at − 80 °C for histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC). A flowchart of the experimental protocol is shown in Fig. S1.

Clinical scoring and microscopy

Vulvar swelling (erythema/edema) and vaginal discharge were scored on days 2, 6, and 9 post-infection according to predefined criteria (0–3; Supplementary Tables S2–S3) by two blinded assessors. Bacterial colonization was assessed by (i) Gram-stained vaginal smears collected on days 0 and 6 post-infection and (ii) serial dilution of vaginal lavage followed by plate counting of CFU on appropriate agar.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Day-9 vaginal tissues from 3 samples per group were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) (objective ×40; total ×400; scale bar = 100 μm). Histological assessment was performed by two independent, blinded investigators. For IHC, sections underwent deparaffinization, heat-mediated antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0), endogenous peroxidase quenching, and blocking with goat serum (Bioswamp Biological Co., Ltd). Primary antibodies against IL-1β (Bioswamp, BS-0812R), IL-10(Bioswamp, BS-0698R), and TLR4(Proteintech,66350-1-LG) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio, GB23301) at 37 °C for 30 min. 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Beijing Zhongshu Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was used for visualization, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. DAB-positive area fractions were quantified on day-9 sections using HALO (images acquired at objective ×40; total ×400; scale bar = 10 μm for IHC).

Vaginal microbiome analysis

Lavage DNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (paired-end 250 bp). Reads were merged (FLASH), quality-filtered (fastp), and de-chimerized using a SILVA-based reference. Taxonomic profiling and diversity analyses (alpha and beta) were performed using standard bioinformatics pipelines.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For normally distributed data, group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The LEfSe analysis was conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test to identify differentially abundant features, followed by Dunn’s post hoc correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was then applied to assess the significance of the identified features. Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1).

Results

Strain identification

Over 100 Weissella isolates were obtained from human vaginal secretions. Isolates underwent a sequential funnel screening: (i) exclusion of β-hemolysis; (ii) prioritization of antibiotic susceptibility to clinically relevant agents; (iii) selection for higher lactic acid and H₂O₂ output (relative to batch median); and (iv) enrichment for epithelial adhesion in the preliminary assay. The detailed performance of the top 15 isolates, together with MV0113, is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. MV0113 was identified as W. confusa by PCR amplification and sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene, showing 99% sequence homology with W. confusa strain 6418 GenBank accession MT229713.1. The full alignment results are provided in Supplementary Material (Text S1). Colonies of MV0113 were creamy-white, opaque, and smooth, and cells were Gram-positive (blue-violet), rod-shaped, and catalase-negative—consistent with the typical phenotype of W. confusa.

In vitro probiotic performance

Hemolysis and antibiotic sensitivity

MV0113 exhibited γ-hemolysis (no clear hemolytic zone). It was sensitive to tetracycline, ampicillin, ceftriaxone, and clarithromycin; resistant to gentamicin and ciprofloxacin; and showed intermediate susceptibility to chloramphenicol and clindamycin. The complete antibiotic resistance profile is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

The cell-free supernatant (CFS) of MV0113 significantly inhibited the growth of C. albicans in co-culture assays, with OD₆₀₀ values remaining consistently lower than those in the control groups (Fig. 1 A). The inhibition was stronger compared to the probiotic reference strain LGG. Hydrogen peroxide and lactic acid production

MV0113 demonstrated rapid hydrogen peroxide production, with a color-change time of 189 ± 17.3 s, significantly faster than LGG (P < 0.0001). Lactic acid production was 17.55 ± 0.22 µmol/mL, which was significantly higher than LGG’s 8.40 ± 0.27 µmol/mL (P < 0.01). These results highlight the strong metabolic activity of MV0113, contributing to its probiotic properties. Detailed data for hydrogen peroxide and lactic acid production are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Adhesion to HeLa cells

MV0113 demonstrated strong adhesion to HeLa cells, with 45 ± 7 CFU per cell, significantly higher than LGG’s 13 ± 2 CFU per cell (P < 0.0001). This suggests that MV0113 has strong adhesion capabilities.

Genomic analysis of MV0113

The assembled genome of W. confusa MV0113 comprised 2.19 Mb with a GC content of 44.9%. Phylogenetic analysis placed MV0113 within clade D, closely related to strains isolated from food and animal sources, confirming its taxonomic position as W. confusa (Fig. 2A). Functional annotation revealed that genes related to carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism were particularly abundant, followed by pathways for transcription, replication, and repair (Fig. 2B). Consistently, COG classification showed that metabolic functions, translation, and DNA repair accounted for a major proportion of the genome (Fig. 2 C). GO analysis further supported these findings, with catalytic and binding activities identified as dominant functional categories (Fig. S2B).

Carbohydrate-active enzyme annotation identified 118 CAZy-related genes, mainly glycoside hydrolases (GH) and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBM) (Fig. 2D; Fig. S2C). Antibiotic resistance analysis detected only vanT and vanY (vancomycin resistance), a pattern consistent across all 18 reference genomes, and no quinolone resistance genes were found. Finally, antiSMASH prediction revealed a bacteriocin biosynthetic locus containing a type III polyketide synthase (T3PKS) cluster (Fig. 2E).

Together, these genomic features provide a comprehensive overview of MV0113, highlighting both its close relatedness to other W. confusa strains and its functional repertoire relevant to metabolism, enzyme activity, and secondary metabolite production.

Effects of MV0113 in a mouse BV model

Gram-stained vaginal smears obtained at baseline and during infection showed a loss of Gram-positive bacilli in the model (M) group, whereas P, PT, and T retained abundant Gram-positive populations similar to BC (Fig. 3 A). Vaginal Lactobacillus CFU declined after infection and, by the final assessment, recovered most in P, followed by PT; T exhibited a modest increase, while M remained low (Fig. 3B). Clinical scores for vulvar swelling and vaginal discharge peaked at the intermediate assessment and decreased by the end of observation, with the largest reduction in T (Fig. 3C–D). H&E staining of terminal tissues showed severe epithelial damage and inflammatory infiltration in M, whereas lesions in T were mild and resembled BC (Fig. 3E). IHC performed at the terminal time point revealed higher IL-1β and TLR4 but lower IL-10 in M compared with BC, while MV0113-treated groups displayed the opposite pattern, with T most closely resembling BC (Fig. 3 F). Impact on vaginal microbiota

16 S rRNA sequencing showed Proteobacteria and Firmicutes as the predominant phyla in vaginal samples (Fig. 4 A). At the species level, hierarchical clustering revealed that infection decreased probiotic taxa in M, including Lactobacillus intestinalis, Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) and Vagococcus fluvialis. In contrast, T recovered beneficial species such as W. confusa, Lactobacillus murinus, and Pediococcus pentosaceus, while reducing Streptococcus dysgalactiae (Fig. 4B).

Additional analyses confirmed these differences at finer taxonomic levels: Enterobacteriaceae expanded in M but declined with a parallel increase in Lactobacillaceae in T, and LEfSe identified commensals enriched in BC, opportunists in M, and a mixture of antagonists and commensals in T (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Discussion

W. confusa MV0113, a lactic acid bacterium isolated from the vaginal tract of a healthy female, exhibits intrinsic ecological adaptability that may provide specific advantages in the treatment of bacterial vaginosis (BV). As a site-specific probiotic, MV0113 displays enhanced adherence and metabolic compatibility within the vaginal niche—traits consistent with prior observations [27–29]. In vitro experiments using HeLa cell monolayers revealed that MV0113 exhibits significantly higher adhesion than Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG), although HeLa cells are not fully representative of vaginal epithelium. Moreover, its lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide production capacities surpass those of control strains, suggesting that MV0113 can help establish stable colonization while maintaining the acidic vaginal barrier. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate W. confusa in a BV animal model, combining genomic analysis with in vivo validation, although further work is required to confirm translational relevance. The CAZyme system plays a crucial role in competitive colonization by probiotics. Genomic analysis of MV0113 revealed an abundance of GH and CBM families, which may enable degradation and utilization of complex polysaccharides in the vaginal microenvironment [30]. Specifically, GH43 and GH13_31 families may contribute to the breakdown of host glycoproteins and glycans associated with Gardnerella-dominated biofilms, thereby supporting a ‘nutrient-depletion’ competitive mechanism [31, 32]. CBMs facilitate binding to glycoconjugates on host epithelial surfaces, enhancing probiotic adhesion and host immune interactions [33, 34]. This CAZyme profile not only supports MV0113’s ecological fitness but also gives it a competitive edge in disrupting biofilms and nutrient access of pathogenic species such as G. vaginalis.

In addition, MV0113 may exert strong inhibitory effects against C. albicans and G. vaginalis through combined secretion of acidic metabolites and bacteriocins [35]. However, these activities were only qualitatively evaluated in this study and thus require further quantitative validation. Genomic analysis identified a type III polyketide synthase (T3PKS) gene cluster in MV0113. T3PKS enzymes, commonly found in actinobacteria and vaginal lactobacilli, suggesting potential production of bioactive compounds that can inhibit pathogens by disrupting membrane integrity, interfering with iron metabolism, and modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) [36, 37].

Restoration of Lactobacillus abundance is a key strategy in BV management. In a murine model of BV, MV0113 intervention led to an increase in lactobacilli and restructuring of the vaginal microbiota as reflected by 16 S rRNA profiling. High-throughput 16 S rRNA sequencing revealed elevated Firmicutes and reduced Proteobacteria in the treatment group, indicating that MV0113 effectively restored microbial homeostasis and suppressed pathogenic overgrowth [38, 39].

Lactic acid bacteria also contribute to inflammation resolution through immune modulation. Studies have shown that they downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, while upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10, thereby alleviating local inflammation associated with BV [40, 41]. In our study, MV0113 significantly downregulated expression of pro-inflammatory markers including IL-1β and TLR4, and upregulated IL-10, suggesting partial restoration of mucosal immune homeostasis, potentially via TLR/NF-κB modulation [42–44].

Despite its promising anti-BV potential, several limitations remain in the current study. First, the precise molecular mechanisms of MV0113 require further elucidation. The murine BV model used here does not fully replicate the human vaginal microbiome or immune response, highlighting the need for more humanized models(e.g., VK2/E6E7) in future studies. Regarding Weissella species are associated with opportunistic infections, further toxicological testing and long-term safety studies are needed to confirm its safety of MV0113 in humans. In this study, LGG was used as a reference strain to provide a well-characterized probiotic benchmark; however, as an intestinal isolate, it may not fully represent the characteristics of vaginal-resident lactobacilli. While several potential advantages of MV0113, this study did not directly compare it with widely studied vaginal probiotic strains such as L. crispatus. Future studies should further compare MV0113 with these extensively researched probiotics to better assess its probiotic effects.

Additionally, challenges such as the stability and dosage formulation of probiotic preparations must be addressed. Optimizing delivery systems and exploring combination therapies will be essential for successful clinical translation. Future studies should also focus on mechanistic validation to better understand how MV0113 interacts with the vaginal microbiota, paving the way for its use in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the probiotic potential of W. confusa MV0113 for the prevention and management of bacterial vaginosis through in vitro assays, whole-genome analysis, and a mouse model of bacterial vaginosis. MV0113 demonstrated strong inhibitory activity against C. albicans and G. vaginalis, robust hydrogen peroxide production, high lactic acid output, and excellent adhesion to HeLa cells—hallmarks of an effective probiotic. Genomic insights revealed key metabolic pathways and intrinsic resistance genes, deepening our understanding of MV0113’s functional capabilities. In vivo, MV0113 treatment significantly alleviated inflammation in the murine BV model and promoted restoration of the vaginal microbiota, outperforming several control groups. These integrated findings support MV0113’s biofunctional basis and in vivo activity, positioning it as a promising candidate for BV management. Further rigorous safety evaluation and preclinical and clinical validation are warranted.
