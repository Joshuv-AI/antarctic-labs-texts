Assessment of spatial and temporal overlap between wildcats (Felis silvestris) and domestic cats (Felis catus) in northeastern Iberia
Authors: Ignasi Fernández-Cabello, Marc Vilella, Eric Serratosa, Ariadna Sanglas, Nerea Fernandez-Arrieta, Santiago Palazón, Joan Real, Salvador Salvador, Roger Puig-Gironès, Ferran Sayol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPresence of domestic species can cause multiple impacts on natural ecosystems, especially when wild close relatives are also present. The European Wildcat is an excellent example, facing major conservation threats due to the presence of domestic cats in natural environments. Their interactions can result in varying levels of hybridization, disease transmission, and ecological competition across different regions. Here, we studied spatiotemporal overlap of both species using over 300 camera traps located in more than 30 plots across Catalonia (NE Spain). Occupancy and detectability of the wildcat were not significantly influenced by the relative abundance of domestic cats. However, we observed a high spatial segregation between both species, which could help to explain the relatively low hybridization rate in this area. Daily activity patterns of the 2 cat species showed higher overlap during the mating period than during the non-mating period. Domestic cat activity patterns shifted in those areas with presence of wildcats only during the non-mating period, while the presence of domestic cats had no effect on wildcat activity. Thus, wildcat estrus could influence the activity times of domestic cats, whose active hours show increased overlap with those of wildcats during the mating period. Wildcat conservation measures could focus on enhancing spatial segregation in those regions where hybridization is more persistent, reducing domestic cat incursions in natural areas, while monitoring wildcat distribution and range expansion into areas with a high risk of encounters with domestic cats.

The presence of domestic animal species in natural environments represents a growing conservation challenge, particularly when they coexist with closely related wild counterparts (Twardek et al. 2017; Rodríguez-Rodríguez et al. 2022). Such coexistence can introduce conservation threats including hybridization (i.e., genetic introgression, such as between livestock and wild ungulates; Randi 2007; Feulner et al. 2013), disease transmission (e.g., from domestic dogs to wild canids; Marino et al. 2017; Jori et al. 2018), and competition for resources among different ecological guilds (Prins 2000; Vanak and Gompper 2009). These impacts are most severe when wild populations are small (Gordon et al. 2015) or already threatened prior to the domestic arrivals (Miller et al. 2012) and can sometimes lead to depletion or local extinction of the wild species (Besser et al. 2021).

Wild species can respond to the presence of domestic types, potentially reducing or increasing encounters and associated impacts (Martinez et al. 2013; Costanzi et al. 2021). Some examples include spatial segregation between wild ungulates and livestock in some areas of Nepal (Bhandari et al. 2022), or temporal avoidance between foxes and domestic dogs in Chile and India (Hernández et al. 2021; Ramesh et al. 2025). However, not all species respond in the same way to the presence of domestic counterparts (Franchetto et al. 2025). In addition, reproductive cycles can exacerbate the consequences of these interactions through hybridization (Hindrikson et al. 2012). Therefore, a better understanding of the spatiotemporal overlap between domestic and wild species could aid in managing human-associated animals in and around natural habitats.

Among the various species potentially affected by domestic interactions is the European Wildcat Felis silvestris von Schreber, 1777 (wildcat, hereafter), a small feline native to Europe. Despite not being at risk of extinction (Gerngross et al. 2023), their populations are considered to be largely depleted (Ambarli et al. 2025), and it is considered threatened or near threatened in regions including Spain (López-Martín et al. 2007), Portugal (Cabral et al. 2005), and Scotland (Gerngross et al. 2023). The wildcat has coexisted with the human-introduced domestic cat F. catus Linnaeus, 1758 for centuries (Sunquist and Sunquist 2002). The domestic cat is a descendant from the African Wildcat F. lybica Forster, 1780, which was domesticated in the Near East 9,000 ya (Driscoll et al. 2007). Since approximately 2,000 ya, it has spread alongside human settlements into Europe (De Martino et al. 2025), posing significant conservation concerns on the wildcat including hybridization (Beaumont et al. 2001; Pierpaoli et al. 2003; Hertwig et al. 2009), disease transmission (Kellner et al. 2018; Alves et al. 2023), and potential resource competition (Biró et al. 2005; Germain et al. 2009).

Differences in wildcat conservation status across regions are well exemplified by hybridization rates, which vary significantly (Tiesmeyer et al. 2020), being higher in Scotland, lower in Central Germany and Western Europe, and moderate in the Iberian Peninsula. Other conservation concerns derived from domestic cat presence in wildcat ranges are resource competition (Biró et al. 2005; Germain et al. 2009) and disease transmission (Kellner et al. 2018; Alves et al. 2023; Gil-Sánchez et al. 2025), which again vary depending on the region. However, domestic cats tend to have a broader diet breadth (Biró et al. 2005; Germain et al. 2009). Feeding habits, such as prey mass, seem to slightly differ between wildcats and domestic cats, so partial resource partitioning is possible, at least in some areas (Biró et al. 2005). Some of these conservation threats might occur more frequently in areas where the wildcat is found at low densities, likely due to other conservation problems (Lozano and Malo 2012).

Spatial patterns of wildcats and domestic cats can also vary depending on the area of study. Beutel et al. (2017) found that home ranges of wildcats and domestic cats highly overlapped in a protected area of Bavaria (southern Germany), while Gil-Sánchez et al. (2015) and Rodríguez et al. (2020) found a strong spatial segregation between them in areas from southern and northern Spain, partially due to differences in habitat preferences between the 2 species. Nevertheless, few studies have simultaneously measured the spatial and temporal overlap between wildcats and domestic cats. Germain et al. (2008) found similar activity rhythms and low spatial overlap between the 2 species. More recently, Gruber et al. (2025) observed a high overlap in the daily activity patterns of the 2 species in Hungary, with significant differences detected only in spring. This result could imply that seasonal cycles, such as the mating period, might have an impact on the behavior of the cats, as it had previously been described (Lozano 2017). However, more studies are needed to provide insights into both spatial and temporal overlap, as well as to test the effect of mating period in interactions between the 2 species.

In this study, we aim to understand the spatiotemporal overlap between wildcats and domestic cats (Fig. 1) by exploring (i) the degree of spatial overlap between wildcats and domestic cats; (ii) if daily activity patterns of wildcats and domestic cats differ depending on the presence of the other species in the same areas; and (iii) how the mating period influences the spatiotemporal overlap between the 2 species. We hypothesize that wildcats and domestic cats will tend to segregate both spatially and temporally, but that this segregation might be reduced during the wildcat mating period.

Methods

Study area

Our study area is Catalonia (northeastern Iberian Peninsula; Fig. 2), a 32,100 km2 region characterized by significant environmental heterogeneity due to its climatic and topographical gradients. The wettest and coldest areas are found at higher elevations in the northern and western zones, especially in the Pyrenees. The driest and hottest regions are located in the southern and eastern areas, characterized by lower elevations (Puig-Gironès et al. 2017) and high human population density (Wildlife Conservation Society 2020). Human presence has greatly impacted landscape and species distribution since prehistoric times, and human settlements are widespread all over the region (Ruiz-Olmo and Aguilar 1995; Cervera et al. 2019; Ruiz-Olmo and Camps 2023). Nowadays, the region is primarily covered by forests (31%), evergreen shrublands (29%), and agricultural land (33%; Vallecillo et al. 2013). Although most of our study sites are located within natural protected areas—which cover 32% of Catalonia (Generalitat de Catalunya 2022)—some landscape heterogeneity still exists within them (Fernández-Cabello et al. 2025).

Map of the Permanent Survey Plots for Carnivores (plots) across Catalonia, classified according to cat species detections during the 2 study campaigns altogether. In the map, the European Wildcat distribution range can also be observed (IUCN 2023).

Catalonia is located between 2 biogeographical realms: the Greater European Mixed Forests in the northwest, including most of the Pyrenees and the Mediterranean realm. The latter is divided into 2 ecoregions: the Iberian Sclerophyllous and Semi-Deciduous Forests in the central and western areas, and the Northeastern Spain and Southern France Mediterranean Forests along the coast (Burkart 2023). Within the study area, the wildcat is mainly present in the northern part of the region, along the Pyrenees and Pre-Pyrenees mountain ranges and also in continental Catalonia. In addition, another population can be found in the south (Gerngross et al. 2023; Fig. 2).

Camera trapping surveys

We obtained data on the presence, activity, and relative abundance of wildcats and domestic cats from a network of camera traps used for the Catalan Mesocarnivore Monitoring Scheme. This citizen science project began in 2020, establishing a network of Permanent Survey Plots for Carnivore monitoring (plots, hereafter) across Catalonia (Fig. 2). Since then, the network has expanded from 29 plots surveyed in the 2021–2022 campaign to 34 in the 2022–2023 campaign; we used data from both campaigns in this study. Each plot included at least 12 cameras, spaced 1.5 ± 0.5 km apart, covering a mean area of 63.63 ± 12.13 km2, based on a 2 km-radius buffer around the cameras. Camera placements were selected to maximize the likelihood of detecting large and medium-size mammal species, considering structural features such as animal trails. Lures or baits were not used to avoid influencing the activity of the species. Cameras were set to operate continuously for at least 90 d, programmed to take three-photo bursts when the sensor was triggered.

To avoid overestimating animal activity, repeated detections of the same species by the same camera were considered independent only if separated by at least 1 h. This approach, commonly used in camera-trapping, maximizes the number of theoretically independent contacts and provides a more accurate estimation of daily activity patterns (Azevedo et al. 2018).

Although individualization was not possible from the images, and therefore the exact number of individuals could not be determined, the use of well-distributed sampling sites, was assumed to be placed in distinct individual territories. This, combined with the one-hour independence threshold, mitigated the risk of repeated counts of the same individual. Sex identification was not possible either (see implications in the Discussion).

All research conducted on live animals followed the American Society of Mammalogists guidelines (Sikes et al. 2011).

Initial data processing

We filtered camera trap data to include only wildcat and domestic cat detections, classified using well-known previous morphological criteria (Ragni and Possenti 1996). As the presence of “cryptic hybrids”—hybrids with morphological characteristics of wildcats—(Devillard et al. 2014) cannot be ruled out, here when we refer to wildcats we are referring to putative wildcat individuals, as genetic analyses were not performed. Nevertheless, monitoring programs in the study area have found a low percentage of hybridization (4.3%), concentrated in 3 of the 32 plots (Serratosa et al. 2023). Therefore, it is unlikely that results are highly influenced by hybrid presence. Furthermore, domestic cats could be free-roaming (cats with owners that are allowed to roam outside their homes), stray (cats with no owner, but that rely upon human food, at least partially), or feral (cats with no owner that never rely on human food, very uncommon in Europe). With our data, we are not able to distinguish between these types of cats, and we refer to all of them as domestic cats.

For each detection, we recorded the species, plot, site, date, and time of detection. Cameras active for less than 30 d were excluded, as this is the minimum time period required to detect a species in temperate regions (Kays et al. 2020). Furthermore, we discarded from the analysis 6 detections that could not be classified to species level.

As the mating period might influence behavior of a cat (Lozano 2017), we classified detections by reproductive period. Detections between 1 January and 31 March, corresponding to the maximum estrus period of wildcats (Nowell and Jackson 1996; Lozano 2017), were classified as “mating,” while those from 1 April to 31 December were classified as “non-mating.” These “mating” and “non-mating” periods refer specifically to the reproductive cycle of the wildcat, as domestic cats have a longer and more continuous reproductive period throughout the year (Liberg 1983; Daniels et al. 2002; Blottner and Jewgenow 2007). Even though later reproductions in wildcats exist, they are thought to be rare (Ruiz-Olmo et al. 2018; Ruiz-Villar et al. 2023), and thus they will probably not impact our results. Additionally, we excluded from further analyses for a certain reproductive period data from cameras that recorded less than 30 d within that specific period.

For the temporal overlap analyses, we categorized each plot based on the presence or absence of each species. To avoid bias from marginal detections, the relative proportion of detections for each cat species was calculated as: the number of detections of the species divided by the sum of detections of wildcats and domestic cats. When this proportion was low (i.e., higher than 0 but lower than 0.05), we excluded the plot from further analyses, as it likely indicates minimal presence and provides limited information about potential species interaction. Four plots (3 from the 2021–2022 campaign, and 1 from the 2022–2023 campaign) were excluded from further analyses using this criterion. We ran the same tests without using this exclusion criterion, and the main conclusions of the study remained unaltered.

To better understand interactions between wildcats and domestic cats, we analyzed both spatial and temporal overlap between the 2 species. Spatial overlap was assessed applying dynamic occupancy models to evaluate the effect of domestic cat abundance on wildcat occupancy and vice versa. We then calculated 2 different indices to quantify spatial overlap between the species. Temporal overlap was assessed by comparing daily activity patterns between areas where both species co-occurred and areas where only 1 was detected. We conducted all the analyses in RStudio version 2023.06.01 (RStudio Team 2023), using the “ggplot2” R-package (Wickham 2016) for plotting.

Spatial overlap analyses

Occupancy models

We assessed spatial overlap between wildcats and domestic cats using dynamic occupancy models conducted with the “camtrapR” (Niedballa et al. 2016) and “unmarked” (Fiske and Chandler 2011; Kellner et al. 2023) R packages. These models can estimate key parameters such as the probability of initial occupancy (Ψ), site colonization probability (γ), site extinction probability (ε), and species detectability (p; MacKenzie et al. 2003). Here, we have focused on assessing initial occupancy (the probability of a site being occupied by a species in the first place) and detectability (the probability of detecting a species when it is present in a site). We used each camera as a sampling site, with the 2 monitoring campaigns (2021–2022 and 2022–2023) treated as primary sampling periods. We excluded plots with no wildcat detections in either campaign, as these sites are likely outside its current distribution range (Fig. 2). To maintain spatial consistency across campaigns, we only considered cameras that were active during the 2 campaigns and located within less than 500 m from the place where they recorded during the first season.

Running occupancy models requires the creation of detection histories, which consist of a sequence of sampling occasions. We set the first day of the first camera (17 November 2021) as the beginning of the first sampling occasion, with subsequent occasions defined as non-overlapping 21-d period to reduce temporal autocorrelation (Goldstein et al. 2024). Subsequently, we grouped each camera’s detections based on their activation dates. Commonly, active periods of cameras did not align exactly with the 21-d blocks. Therefore, the initial and/or final occasion could be shorter than 21 d. These partial occasions were included, maintaining consistent occasion lengths across sites while preserving all valid detection data.

Based on the biology of the species (Klar et al. 2008; Martín-Díaz et al. 2018; Oliveira et al. 2018; Ruiz-Villar et al. 2023; Cushman et al. 2024), we included several environmental factors as explanatory variables in the occupancy models: (i) forest proportion; (ii) forest–open edge proportion; (iii) altitudinal difference, all within a 2 km-radius buffer around the camera location; (iv) Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI); and (v) Human Footprint Index (HFI) for the camera location. We chose a radius of 2 km to calculate variables (i), (ii), and (iii) because it covers an area similar to the mean home range of wildcat males (i.e., the largest ranges) in regions similar to the study area (Monterroso et al. 2009).

We calculated forest and edge proportions using the 2018 CORINE Land Cover map (Copernicus Land Monitoring Service 2018) at 100 m resolution. Forest proportion was the ratio of broad-leaved, coniferous, and mixed forest area to the total terrestrial area within the buffer. We calculated edge proportion after simplifying the CORINE map into 3 categories: (i) forest; (ii) open areas; and (iii) others (see Supplementary Data SD1 for details). We then defined edge proportion as the number of pixels at the forest-open areas interface. We calculated elevation range as the difference between the maximum and minimum elevation within each buffer, using a 5 m resolution Digital Elevation Model from the Cartographical and Geological Institute of Catalonia (ICGC), based on data from 2009 to 2018 (Institut Cartogràfic i Geològic de Catalunya 2018). Furthermore, we obtained NDVI values from 2021, used as a proxy of rodent prey abundance (Owen 1988; Chidodo et al. 2020), from ICGC at a 1 m resolution (Institut Cartogràfic i Geològic de Catalunya 2021). Lastly, HFI data came from Wildlife Conservation Society (2020) at a 300 m resolution. We standardized all explanatory variables before running the analyses.

Subsequently, we conducted an initial occupancy model for the wildcat and for the domestic cat (hereafter “no-RAICAT” and “no-RAISIL” models, respectively) to assess which environmental variables could affect occupancy of each species at the site level, independently of presence of the other species. To this aim, we conducted a model selection using the dredge function from R-package “MuMIn” (Bartoń 2023), including all environmental variables as predictors. We also included mating period (as a categorical variable, mating/non-mating) as an explanatory variable of the detectability. We did not include any explanatory variables as determinants of colonization or extinction probabilities to avoid overparameterization. Then we selected all environmental variables with a sum of weights (sw) higher than 0.6, as this threshold is commonly used to identify variables with at least moderate importance in the model (Kennedy et al. 2013; Chiaradia et al. 2016).

Then, we compared the “no-RAICAT” and “no-RAISIL” models with a model including the same selected environmental variables plus the relative abundance index (RAI) of the other cat species at a site level, used as an explanatory variable for both occupancy and detectability (“RAICAT” and “RAISIL” models, hereafter). For these models, we calculated the RAI as the number of independent detections for 100 survey days, as a surrogate for relative abundance (Bengsen et al. 2011). We compared the “RAICAT” and “no-RAICAT” models for the wildcat, as well as the “RAISIL” and “no-RAISIL” models for the domestic cat, using both ΔAIC and a likelihood-ratio test. This procedure allowed us to determine whether differences were statistically significant, which would indicate that the RAI of the other cat species is an important variable influencing the occupancy and/or detectability of the respective species.

We repeated this comparison 3 times using: (i) all detections; (ii) mating; and (iii) non-mating detections. The distinction between these periods was based on the hypothesis that mating could affect both the behavior and the spatial overlap between species. Therefore, we only included mating as a predictor of the detectability in the analyses that used all detections. We obtained detection histories for the mating and non-mating periods by modifying the full detection history of the study period: we removed sampling occasions totally outside the mating period for the mating analysis, and those entirely within the mating period for the non-mating analysis. We conserved sampling occasions that included days both within and outside the mating in both period analyses.

Spatial overlap indices

We calculated 2 indices to estimate the spatial overlap between the 2 species, using a presence/absence matrix of both species at site and campaign level (without further sampling occasion). Here, we included only those sites where at least 1 of the 2 species was detected during the campaign. The first index was the Species Interaction Factor (SIF; Richmond et al. 2010) calculated as:(Proportion of sites with detections of both wildcats and domestic cats)/ (Proportion of sites with detections of wildcats × Proportion of sites with detections of domestic cats).

(Proportion of sites with detections of both wildcats and domestic cats)/ (Proportion of sites with detections of wildcats × Proportion of sites with detections of domestic cats).

A SIF = 1 indicates that the 2 species occur independently, while an SIF < 1 indicates a co-occurrence lower than expected by chance (signifying spatial segregation), and an SIF > 1 indicates a co-occurrence higher than expected by chance (signifying spatial overlap) (Richmond et al. 2010).

The second index was the Cole index (Cole 1949), calculated as:[(2 × Sites with detections of both wildcats and domestic cats)/(Sites with detections of wildcats + Sites with detections of domestic cats)].

[(2 × Sites with detections of both wildcats and domestic cats)/(Sites with detections of wildcats + Sites with detections of domestic cats)].

The Cole index ranges between 0 (total spatial segregation) and 1 (maximum spatial overlap) (Cole 1949). To assess whether the observed overlap was significantly different from random expectations, we generated a null distribution by randomizing the presence/absence data 1,000 times. In each iteration, species values were reassigned across camera sites to break spatial associations between species while maintaining the number of occupied sites. We then compared the observed Cole index to this null distribution to calculate a P value. We repeated this analysis for all detections, as well as separately for the mating and non-mating periods. To assess spatial overlap between reproductive periods, we calculated differences in SIF and Cole index between the mating and non-mating periods. 10,000 bootstrap resamplings were performed for each period, calculating the SIF, Cole index, and their difference between periods to create a null distribution. We then compared the observed differences to this null distribution to estimate a 2-sided P value.

Temporal overlap analyses

To assess temporal overlap between the 2 cat species, we classified the plots into 3 categories based on the presence or absence of wildcats and domestic cats during each campaign. The categories were defined as follows: “silvestris” (plots where only wildcats were detected during the campaign); “catus” (plots where only domestic cats were detected); and “mixed” (plots where both species were detected during the same campaign).

Based on these categories, different activity pattern curves were estimated using kernel density functions with R-packages “camtrapR” (Niedballa et al. 2016), “chron” (James and Hornik 2023), “overlap” (Meredith et al. 2024), and “activity” (Rowcliffe 2023). We transformed detection times into radians to fit the density function (Ridout and Linkie 2009). We then compared all activity patterns pairwise, calculating the overlap coefficient (Δ4), which ranges from 0 (complete temporal segregation) to 1 (full overlap) and is considered the most adequate index for sampling sizes with over 50 detections (Ridout and Linkie 2009). 95% confidence intervals (CI) for Δ4 were calculated by bootstrapping 1,000 samples per kernel function, and calculating overlap coefficients for each iteration pair (Ridout and Linkie 2009). We calculated a P value by comparing the observed overlap with the null distribution from bootstrapping 1,000 samples. Significant values indicate that the activity patterns are less overlapped than expected by chance, indicating temporal segregation.

The patterns analyzed by paired comparisons were (i) overlap between wildcat and domestic cat activity patterns in “mixed” plots in each reproductive period; (ii) domestic cat activity overlap depending on the presence/absence of wildcat in the plot (“catus” vs “mixed”) in each reproductive period; and (iii) wildcat activity overlap depending on the presence/absence of domestic cat in the plot (“silvestris” vs “mixed”) in each reproductive period. Finally, we compared the time intervals between consecutive detections of the 2 species (wildcat after domestic cat and domestic cat after wildcat) in sites where both were recorded using Welch’s 2-sample t-tests, separately for mating and non-mating periods. Consecutive detections of the same species were not included in these analyses.

Results

Overall, wildcats and domestic cats were detected in 230 and 383 sites (i.e., different camera traps), respectively for each of the campaigns. Wildcats were generally much more detected than domestic cats (Supplementary Data SD2). There were only 12 sites during the 2021–2022 campaign, and 7 during the 2022–2023 campaign where both species were recorded in the same site (see Supplementary Data SD2 for more details).

Patterns of spatial overlap

We used data from 189 sites from 19 plots for overall occupancy models, while 107 and 129 of these were also included in the mating and non-mating periods models. Of these, 30% to 40% of sites detected only wildcats, while both species only co-occurred in less than 12% of the sites. Both species were relatively more abundant during the mating period than during the non-mating period (Supplementary Data SD3).

After the model selection with all explanatory variables, we selected 4 key variables for the wildcat based on their total sum of weights (sw): elevation range (sw = 0.99), human footprint index (0.76), proportion of forests (0.68, all explanatory variables for occupancy), and mating period (0.67, as explanatory variable for detectability). On the other hand, the proportion of edges between forest and open areas (sw = 0.49) and NDVI (0.45) were discarded. Model selection for the domestic cat showed that none of these explanatory variables was relevant for determining its occupancy and detectability, as none had a sum of weight higher than 0.30 (Supplementary Data SD4). When testing the effect of domestic cat abundance on wildcat occupancy (“RAICAT” models), we found that both elevation range and proportion of forests were significant factors in determining wildcat occupancy in the general analysis and mating but not in the non-mating period (Table 1; Fig. 3). Human footprint index, although potentially related to wildcat occupancy, was non-significant in all cases (Table 1).

Regarding the spatial overlap indices, results indicated that the overlap between wildcats and domestic cats was significantly lower than expected by chance in all reproductive periods of both campaigns (P value < 0.001; Fig. 4), suggesting a spatial segregation of the 2 species. Both the SIF and Cole indices were consistently higher during the 2021–2022 campaign in all 3 analyses conducted. Specifically, SIF values were 0.50 and 0.41 for the general analyses, 0.45 and 0.32 for mating, and 0.53 and 0.41 for non-mating; while Cole values were 0.21 and 0.11 for the general analysis, 0.22 and 0.10 for mating, and 0.20 and 0.09 for non-mating (Fig. 4). Patterns of temporal overlap

A total of 1,031 detections were used for the elaboration of the activity pattern curves. These detections occurred between 2 periods: 554 during the mating period, and 477 during non-mating. During the mating, we recorded 383 wildcat (93 in “silvestris” plots and 290 in “mixed” plots) and 171 domestic cat (91 in “catus” plots and 80 in “mixed” plots) detections. On non-mating, we recorded 324 wildcat (172 in “silvestris” plots and 152 in “mixed” plots) and 153 domestic cat (58 in “catus” plots and 95 in “mixed” plots) detections.

Overall, a high overlap in activity patterns was observed in all comparisons (Δ4 values ranging from 0.66 to 0.92; Fig. 5; Supplementary Data SD5). This result aligns with high activity levels during the night or cathemeral hours (i.e., sunset and sunrise). However, we detected significant differences in activity patterns in 2 of the comparisons, both during the non-mating.

In plots where both species were present, domestic cats tended to be more active in the afternoon, after midnight, and following sunrise (especially during the mating), whereas wildcats were more active before midnight and sunrise. In these “mixed” plots, we found significant differences in activity patterns during the non-mating (Δ4 = 0.78, P value = 0.04; Supplementary Data SD5B) but not during the mating (Δ4 = 0.92, P value = 0.92; Supplementary Data SD5A). During the mating, both species exhibited more evenly distributed activity throughout the day, resulting in near-complete temporal overlap. However, the differences in activity patterns outside the mating were only marginally significant.

When examining wildcat activity patterns, the presence or absence of domestic cats did not result in any significant change, either during mating (Δ4 = 0.87, P value = 0.18; Fig. 5A) or the non-mating (Δ4 = 0.85, P value = 0.08; Fig. 5B), with overlap coefficients remaining similarly high in both cases. During the mating, wildcats were more active during the early hours of the night when domestic cats were present. In their absence, wildcat activity was more evenly distributed throughout the day, particularly during the early afternoon (Fig. 5A). A similar but opposite pattern was observed in the non-mating, with wildcats being more nocturnal when domestic cats were absent. Nonetheless, the main peaks of wildcat activity during the non-mating still occurred at night and before sunrise (Fig. 5B). Again, none of these differences in wildcat activity patterns were statistically significant.

Regarding domestic cat activity patterns, we detected significant differences depending on the presence of the wildcat but only during the non-mating (Δ4 = 0.66, P value = <0.001; Fig. 5D). For instance, the highest activity peak for domestic cats (detection densities between 0.08 and 0.10) occurred at around 5 to 6 PM when the wildcats were present, whereas it occurred to 10 to 11 PM in their absence. Similarly, activity peaks typically observed before sunrise and mid-morning in the absence of wildcats occurred just after midnight and post-sunrise when wildcats were present (Fig. 5D). In contrast, during the mating (Δ4 = 0.86, P value = 0.49; Fig. 5C), main activity peak of domestic cats occurred consistently at around 8 to 9 PM regardless of wildcat presence.

When comparing the time (i.e., delay) between wildcat and domestic cat detections after the other species was detected, we found that the delay to detect a wildcat after a domestic cat tended to be higher (12.3 d for the overall, 6.4 for the mating period, and 27.7 for the non-mating period) than the delay to detect a domestic cat after a wildcat detection (6.4, 5.7, and 5.7 d, respectively). However, these differences were not statistically significant in the whole dataset (Welch’s 2-sample t-tests, P value = 0.16) or neither for the mating period (P = 0.85) or for the non-mating period (P = 0.09) alone.

Discussion

Our results suggest that the presence of 1 cat species is not influenced by the abundance of the other. Both cat species segregate spatially, while domestic cats also present significantly different activity patterns depending on the presence of wildcats in the area during non-mating period.

Relative abundances of each species did not significantly improve occupancy or detectability predictions for the other, beyond key habitat variables. All these findings suggest that a low abundance of wildcats could be enough to influence behavior of domestic cats. This was expected, as the wildcat is a territorial species usually found at low densities (Anile et al. 2012; Gil-Sánchez et al. 2015; Gil-Sánchez et al. 2020).

Habitat variables such as altitude heterogeneity, forest coverage, and human impact are known to influence wildcat occupancy (Klar et al. 2008; Martín-Díaz et al. 2018; Oliveira et al. 2018; Otgontamir et al. 2024). Wildcats preferred specific environmental conditions such as flatter and more forested areas, whereas domestic cats showed no clear associations, suggesting a more opportunistic habitat use. Although domestic cats are often linked to highly anthropized areas (Ferreira et al. 2011; Morin et al. 2018; Anile et al. 2025), we found no association between HFI and their abundance. One possible explanation is that our cameras were placed in forestry and natural areas to maximize wild-living animal detections. In these areas, domestic cats are probably free-roaming or stray cats that live or roam near isolated houses, rather than in more urbanized areas. Alternatively, the relatively low number of domestic cat detections may have limited our ability to identify associations between their abundance and environmental variables.

Spatial overlap indices were significantly lower than expected in both study campaigns and all reproductive periods, likely reflecting differences in habitat preferences between the species (Ruiz-Olmo et al. 2023). This apparent spatial segregation is consistent with results found by previous studies in different regions (Gil-Sánchez et al. 2015; Beutel et al. 2017; Rodríguez et al. 2020; Gil-Sánchez et al. 2025). Segregation probably reduces conflict risk, given apparent competitive disadvantage of domestic cats in natural landscapes (Gil-Sánchez et al. 2015), due to the lack of complete adaptation to these areas compared to the wildcat or other predators. The observed segregation, strong but not complete, may also explain the low hybridization rates found in our study area, which is estimated at 4.3% according to recent technical reports (Serratosa et al. 2023).

Landscape differences might play a key role in shaping this segregation. For instance, higher hybridization rates in northeastern France compared to the Pyrenees have been suggested to result from a more fragmented forest-agricultural landscape (Beugin et al. 2020). In contrast, the Pyrenees present a larger extension of continuous forest, potentially reducing contact between both species (Beugin et al. 2020). This pattern may also apply to our study area, where forest cover was a significant factor influencing wildcat presence.

Regarding activity patterns, both species generally exhibited a high degree of temporal overlap, as previously described by other studies (Anile et al. 2025; Gruber et al. 2025). However, we observed distinct activity peaks during the non-mating, when overlap was notably lower. Wildcat activity was broadly consistent with Lazzeri et al. (2022), and aligns with the activity of nocturnal small rodents (Vilella et al. 2020). In contrast, domestic cats—with a broader diet (Biró et al. 2005; Germain et al. 2009)—may be less dependent on these prey and feed more on passerines and pet food, which could explain their more diurnal activity. Future dietary studies within the study area would help clarify the ecological mechanisms behind the observed temporal segregation.

During the non-mating period, domestic cats seemed to adjust their activity pattern, becoming more diurnal in areas with wildcat presence. We also found that domestic cats tended to be more spaced in time after wildcat detections, although the differences were not significant. Therefore, clear temporal avoidance cannot be confirmed, although the low number of sites where both species were detected prevented a powerful statistical analysis. The higher temporal overlap and the lack of changes in domestic cat activity in response to wildcat presence during mating suggest that female wildcats, which show higher fecal estradiol levels during estrus (Piñeiro et al. 2020), may attract male domestic cats in this period of the year. The reverse is less likely, as domestic cats have a more continuous estrus cycle (Liberg 1983; Daniels et al. 2002; Blottner and Jewgenow 2007). Nonetheless, this increased temporal overlap does not match an increase in spatial overlap. This pattern might reflect a more aggressive behavior of male wildcats during mating, when they are more prone to fighting (Lozano 2017), reinforcing territorial exclusion of domestic cats, and compensating a potential male–female attraction. Changes in wildcat home ranges during mating could also influence our results by affecting habitat use or interspecific relationships.

Despite previous findings, we cannot rule out the effect of environmental variables on activity patterns. Areas where only the domestic cat was present tended to be more anthropized, and generally presented a slightly lower forest cover and productivity. Although none of these variables were found to be significant in the occupancy models of the domestic cat, this does not necessarily mean they do not affect their activity patterns. Furthermore, these variables could also be related to the presence and abundance of other mesocarnivores, such as red foxes Vulpes vulpes (Linnaeus, 1758), whose behavior could also have an impact on cat behavior (Rodríguez et al. 2020). Even if the Red Fox was present in all surveyed areas and appeared in a vast majority of the sites, differential effects cannot be ruled out, and should be further studied in the future.

Our results reflect general patterns and do not account for individual or sex-specific behaviors. Sex differences in space use (Beugin et al. 2016; Jerosch et al. 2017; Oliveira et al. 2018) or activity (Ferretti et al. 2022; Lazzeri et al. 2022) have been reported. While individual identification is possible using camera traps (Burton et al. 2015; Caravaggi et al. 2017), sexing is challenging due to low sexual dimorphism (Anile et al. 2012; Maronde et al. 2020), which may affect our interpretation of spatial and temporal dynamics. For example, male avoidance (Lozano 2017) could be masked by male–female tolerance or aggregation during the mating period (Beugin et al. 2016). Future studies should incorporate individual and sex identification methods, such as radio telemetry or GPS tracking.

Our study highlights the importance of examining both spatial and temporal overlap between species to understand their interactions and conservation implications. Our findings indicate that wildcats and domestic cats tend to spatially segregate and select different territories naturally, provided suitable habitats for wildcats—which seem more selective—are preserved. However, the increased temporal overlap during mating should be interpreted as a warning sign for hybridization risk. To mitigate this, conservation efforts should focus on protecting high-quality wildcat habitats while minimizing domestic cat presence in natural areas (Biró et al. 2005; Germain et al. 2009; Horn et al. 2011), for example, by keeping domestic cats indoors or by strictly regulating stray cat colonies in these areas. This is particularly important in regions where wildcats face additional conservation threats (Lozano and Malo 2012). Even in regions with recovering or expanding wildcat populations, continued monitoring is essential to prevent increased contact with domestic cats (Nussberger et al. 2014; Nussberger et al. 2018; Quilodrán et al. 2019). Ultimately, ensuring spatial segregation remains key to safeguarding the genetic integrity and long-term viability of wildcat populations.
