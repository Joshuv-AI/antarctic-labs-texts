Efficient points in a sum of sets of alternatives
Anas Mifrani

Abstract
The concept of efficiency plays a prominent role in the formal solution of decision problems that involve incomparable alternatives. This paper develops necessary and sufficient conditions for the efficient points in a sum of sets of alternatives to be identical to the efficient points in one of the summands. Some of the conditions cover both finite and infinite sets; others are shown to hold only for finite sets. Examples are provided that illustrate these findings.

Decision problem, Finite set, Mathematical optimization

We shall also not try to work out applications, although it might be pointed out from the outset that the abovementioned Yu-Ehrgott result has helped elucidate the relationship between multiple objective and single-objective optimization problems. Indeed, it is well established that for a mathematical maximization problem involving

So far as we can detect, efficiency in sum sets has been the subject of only a handful of publications.
Klamroth et al. ((2024))

Under (P1)-(P3), if

Remark 1
.

We remarked in Section

Proposition 5

Assume (P1), (P3) and (P5). Let

&bI{0_{G}},\end{aligned}\right.

Suppose system (

S
4

which appears on the left-hand side of the comparison

This sequence says that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a^{j_{1}}P(a^{j_{0}}+b),\\

&\dots\\
&a^{j_{n}}P(a^{j_{n-1}}+b),\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Now each element in the sequence ranges in

and culminating in

forms a cycle. Indeed, because this subsequence means

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a^{j_{r+1}}P(a^{j_{r}}+b),\\

&\dots\\
&a^{j_{m}}P(a^{j_{m-1}}+b),\\
\end{aligned}\right.

holds, and inasmuch as

. We infer from this that (

Theorem 9
.

Suppose

is finite. Under (P1), (P3) and (P5), if

is stable and there exists

such that

and

, then (

) fails.

Assume

for some

incomparable with

, and write

for

.

For the sake of contradiction, let us suppose that equality (

) holds. Since

for each

(because

), (

) says that all the points in

distinct from the

are dominated in

. Such points do exist, because
A+B\neq A
.
Put otherwise, there exist in

at least one pair of indices

for which

. Two situations may materialize.

•

Situation 1:

a^{i}\neq a^{j}+b
for each

.

Let

,

, be any member of
A+B
. We have assumed here that

is different than
a^{j}
for each

. Furthermore,

because

. This establishes that

, and hence, by Theorem
6
, the existence of an index

such that

. Property (P3) tells us that since

, the comparison

—and by extension

— does not hold, so that

. Repeating this argument for all

,

, we construct a set of indices

Situation 2:

such that

It will prove useful to work with the set

. It follows that for each such

. In this situation, we have assumed that there exists an integer (possibly several)

. The net result is a mixed system of comparisons and equalities of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a^{i_{1}}P(a^{j_{1}}+b),\\
&\dots\\
&a^{i_{k}}P(a^{j_{k}}+b),\\
&a^{i_{k+1}}=a^{j_{k+1}}+b,\\
&\dots\\
&a^{i_{n}}=a^{j_{n}}+b,\\
\end{aligned}\right.

for some sets of indices

,

,

and

(with

. To this end, it helps to borrow some of the ideas of Proposition
5
. Let

be the permutation that maps each

,

. Fix some initial index

and consider the sequence of indices

The pigeonhole principle assures us that at least one integer appears twice in this sequence (see the proof of Proposition

for the full argument), so that there exist

for which

. Denote this integer

, and consider the subsequence that begins in

This subsequence is cyclical in the sense that

We have seen that either situation leads to a logical contradiction, whence the falsity of the initial premise that

4
Summary and discussion

The purpose of this paper was to develop conditions for the validity of set equality (
E
). The problem originated in a theorem in
Yu ((1985))
and a proposition in
Ehrgott ((2005))
asserting the equality for specific subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
and a specific choice of relation. This work was an effort at generalizing (
E
) to situations where the sets considered are arbitrary, possibly non-Euclidean, and where the relation used for comparing alternative points in those sets need not constitute an order relation.

In Theorems
1
and
2
, we obtained conditions sufficient for (
E
) to hold. Theorem
2
subsumes the Yu-Ehrgott result. Theorems
3
,
4
and
7
-
10
describe situations in which (
E
) is false. Although this was not made explicit, contraposition of these theorems provides necessary conditions for the validity of the equality. Theorem
5
states a necessary and sufficient condition in case
A
is finite and stable and
B
contains a single point.

Theorems
3
and
4
are each predicated on some efficient set—
\mathscr{E}(A)
in one case and
\mathscr{E}(A+B)
in the other—being nonempty. Efficient points are guaranteed to exist in a finite set provided
R
is transitive (Theorem
6
). The situation tends to be more intricate where infinite sets are involved.

A number of promising research programs follow from these conclusions.

Consider first the scope of some of our results. Theorems
1
-
4
cover both finite and infinite sets. Theorems
7
-
10
deal strictly with finite sets, while the counterexamples in Remarks
3
,
6
,
7
and
8
stress the necessity of this assumption for the four theorems. It remains to be seen whether these theorems have counterparts in cases when
A
is infinite,
B
is infinite, or both. This shall be the object of future study.

Next, while the foregoing treatment was entirely theoretical, it would be worthwhile inquiring about its algorithmic implications in light of recent advances in the computation of efficient points in Euclidean sum sets.
Kerbérénès et al. ((2023))
,
Klamroth et al. ((2024))
and
Funke et al. ((2025))
devised methods for enumerating the efficient subset of a finite sum set which, across a broad range of experiments, appeared to expend significantly less computation time than exhaustive search. It would be interesting to compare the performance of these methods in determining
\mathscr{E}(A+B)
with that of a simple enumeration of
\mathscr{E}(A)
in situations where equality (
E
) holds.

A further interesting direction would be to extend the analysis beyond groups to, say, semigroups, lattices or cones, where the absence of additive inverses may lead to new conditions for equality validity. A raft of stochastic and combinatorial objects familiar to decision theorists and practitioners—such as lotteries, nonnegative random variables (as typified, for example, by payoffs and costs) and paths on graphs—live in such algebraic structures.
