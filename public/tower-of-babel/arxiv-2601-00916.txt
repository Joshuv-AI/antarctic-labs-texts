Variants of the Damascus inequality


Abstract
In 2016, Dannan and Sitnik established the celebrated Damascus inequality, which features a symmetric structure under a multiplicative constraint. This study comprises of two directions. First, we consider the natural generalisation of this inequality by characterising all positive integers m m and n n such that the inequality ∑ j = 1 m x j n − 1 x j n + 1 + 1 ⩽ 0 \sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x_{j}^{n}-1}{x_{j}^{n+1}+1}\leqslant 0 holds for any positive real numbers x 1 , … , x m x_{1},\ldots,x_{m} with ∏ j = 1 m x j = 1 \prod_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}=1 . For the cases where the inequality fails, we also investigate the topological properties of the set of non-solutions. Second, we construct new symmetric inequalities under the constraint x + y + z = 1 x+y+z=1 . Our approach employs the GA-convexity concept, the Sturm’s sequence, and the ABC method.

Introduction

Inequalities form the backbone of mathematical analysis and optimisation, with symmetry and convexity often determining both their structure and sharpness. Classical examples illuminate these principles: the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality (AM-GM) states that for nonnegative real numbers

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\geqslant\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{1/n},

with equality if and only if
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}

[
2
,
6
]
.
Similarly fundamental results—including the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
[
13
]
, Jensen’s inequality for convex functions
[
7
]
, and Chebyshev’s sum inequality for ordered sequences
[
12
]
—share common threads of symmetry, convexity, and majorisation that continue to guide modern developments in inequality theory.

Within this rich landscape, the Damascus inequality emerged in 2016 as a surprising result. Proposed by Dannan from Damascus, Syria, it states that for positive real numbers
x
,
y
, and
z
satisfying
xyz=1
,

\displaystyle\frac{x-1}{x^{2}-x+1}+\frac{y-1}{y^{2}-y+1}+\frac{z-1}{z^{2}-z+1}\leqslant 0,

with equality if and only if
(x,y,z)=(1,1,1)
. Despite its elementary appearance, this inequality resisted straightforward verification and sparked considerable interest. Dannan and Sitnik
[
4
]
eventually established it using Lagrange multipliers, cyclic inequalities, and geometric methods, while Kalinin and Pankratova
[
11
]
provided an alternative proof employing geometric means, logarithmic means, and GA-convex functions.

Building on these foundations, Kalinin and Pankratova posed a natural generalisation: for which positive integers
n
does the inequality

\frac{x^{n}-1}{x^{n+1}+1}+\frac{y^{n}-1}{y^{n+1}+1}+\frac{z^{n}-1}{z^{n+1}+1}\leqslant 0

hold for positive real numbers
x
,
y
,
z
with
xyz=1
? Moreover, when does the inequality become an equality? They explicitly resolved only the cases
n=1,2
, leaving the general question open. Questions of exponent extension as such appear widely in inequality theory, often revealing deeper structural properties and delimiting the scope of particular techniques. A systematic characterisation of all valid exponents not only completes the theoretical picture but also provides practical guidance for applications in optimisation and analysis.

In this paper, we address two main objectives. First (Section
2
), we provide a complete answer to the question posed by Kalinin and Pankratova concerning this extension. In fact, we introduce and fully resolve a related question in a more general setting. Particularly, we characterise positive integers
m
and
n
such that the inequality

S_{n}^{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x_{j}^{n}-1}{x_{j}^{n+1}+1}\leqslant 0

(1)

holds on the
(m-1)
-dimensional smooth constraint hypersurface

\mathcal{H}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in(0,+\infty)^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}=1\right\}.

Furthermore, we study the structure of the violation set under the original constraint, proving it is bounded and separated from certain planes for
m=3
.
Second (Section
3
), we construct new inequalities of this type, but the constraint is changed from
xyz=1
to
x+y+z=1
. Our proofs employ several analytical techniques, including the concept of GA-convexity, Sturm sequences, and the ABC method.

For the second case, it suffices to only verify the cases

s,w\in(0,117/500)

\displaystyle h(s)+h(w)+h(x)+h(y)+h(z)\leqslant 2h\left(\frac{117}{500}\right)+3\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)<0.

Proof of Theorem
2.13
.
Fix
n\in\mathbf{N}_{\geq 4}
. For convenience, define

f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{x^{n}-1}{x^{n+1}+1}

for

\mathcal{I}_{n}^{3}
is unbounded. Then for every

(x_{c},y_{c},z_{c})\in\mathcal{I}_{n}^{3}

where we assume without loss of generality that

•

\displaystyle\frac{(\tilde{c}-\tilde{c}^{n})(\tilde{c}+1)}{(\tilde{c}^{n+1}+1)(\tilde{c}^{2}+1)}\geqslant 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(\tilde{c})=\frac{\tilde{c}^{n}-1}{\tilde{c}^{n+1}+1}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{c}-1}{\tilde{c}^{2}+1}.

•

For
\tilde{c}>1
,

f(\tilde{c})=\frac{\tilde{c}^{n}-1}{\tilde{c}^{n+1}+1}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{c}^{n}}{\tilde{c}^{n+1}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{c}}.

In addition, a routine calculus argument shows that the map
\tilde{c}\mapsto(\tilde{c}-1)/(\tilde{c}^{2}+1)
is increasing on
(0,1)
, and the map
\tilde{c}\mapsto 1/\tilde{c}
is decreasing on
(1,+\infty)
.

By Theorem
2.12
, there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that for every

, none of the coordinates lies in
(1-\delta,1+\delta)
. Since

, there are two cases to consider.

, and from

Using the bounds above and the monotonicity properties, we obtain

(7)

As

such that any triple

.

y_{c}>1
. Then by Theorem

and

Thus

We now investigate how the sets
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{3}
evolve as the exponent

Conjecture 2.16
.

For any

, we have
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{3}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{n+1}^{3}
.

In other words, this conjecture states that if a triple
(x,y,z)
fails to satisfy (
1
) for some
n

. This implies that the sequence of sets of non-solutions is nested and increasing. Establishing this conjecture in complete generality appears to be highly nontrivial; nevertheless, if true, it would offer a more refined picture of the structure and progression of non-solutions to the generalised Damascus inequality. We therefore leave this question for future investigation.

In addition to the above, it is natural to ask whether analogous structural properties hold for
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{m}
when

is arbitrary. Can one prove or disprove that

\mathcal{I}_{n}^{m}
(i) stays uniformly away from

.
We additionally note that the proof of Theorem
2.12

when

, a feature that fails for other values of

, as shown in the previous sections.
Because of this, we may likely think that the answer to question (ii) is negative as well. However, we do not know whether the converse “Theorem

” is true for any

. Moreover, we have not been able to construct an explicit counterexample yet. If it surprisingly turns out to be true, then it would require a new technique to prove. Thus this question, together with question (iii), remains open. Clarifying these properties of

for

would therefore be an interesting direction for further study, which we leave to the interested reader.

Modifications

In this section, we will be constructing new inequalities that are similar to the Damascus inequality. The main characteristic of this inequality lies in its symmetric structure. To be more precise, an inequality

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\leqslant 0

is said to be
symmetric

[
8
]
if for any permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=p(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})\leqslant 0.

Indeed, we can easily see that the Damascus inequality is symmetric since permuting
x,y,z
does not alter it.

3.1
Preliminaries

For notational clarity, we introduce the following conventions. The constraint set is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbf{R}^{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x+y+z=1\}.

Additionally, we employ the standard elementary symmetric polynomial notation as follows.

\displaystyle u=x+y+z,\quad v=xy+xz+yz,\quad w=xyz

We now introduce a main tool known as the ABC method, which is employed for optimising symmetric polynomials. It is well-established that any symmetric polynomial in three variables
x
,
y
,
z
can be completely expressed as a polynomial in
u
,
v
,
w
. The essence of this lies in fixing the values of
u
and
v
, thereby treating the given polynomial as a univariate function of
w
. This method has several versions, but we will only state the ones we will be using. For details, one may consult
[
3
]
, chapter 16.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f(x,y,z)
be a symmetric polynomial. Suppose
f(u,v,w)
is a linear function with variable
w
. Then
f
attains its maximum and minimum on
\mathbf{R}
if and only if
(x-y)(y-z)(z-x)=0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f(x,y,z)
be a symmetric polynomial. Suppose
f(u,v,w)
is a quadratic function with variable
w
. Then
f
attains its maximum on
\mathbf{R}
if and only if
(x-y)(y-z)(z-x)=0
.

Remark 3.3
.

It is important to note that the ABC method cannot be directly applied to problems involving the constraint
xyz=1
(for instance, the usual Damascus inequality). Instead, one must employ an appropriate change of variables to reformulate the problem.

Let us also state a typical fact in real analysis.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{R}^{3}\to\mathbf{R}
be a continuous function and assume that

\lim_{\|(x,y,z)\|\to+\infty}f(x,y,z)=-\infty,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm. Then
f
attains a global maximum on
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.
Note that the affine plane
\mathcal{S}
is closed in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
. By the hypothesis on
f
, choose a point
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{S}
(e.g.
\mathbf{p}=(1,0,0)
) and pick
R>0
such that for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{R}^{3}
with
\|\mathbf{x}\|>R
, we have
f(\mathbf{x})<f(\mathbf{p})-1
.

Now consider the set
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathcal{S}\cap\overline{B(\mathbf{0},R)}
. Since
\mathcal{S}
is closed and
\overline{B(\mathbf{0},R)}
is closed and bounded,
\mathcal{K}
is closed and bounded in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
. Hence it is compact by Heine-Borel theorem. The restriction
f|_{\mathcal{K}}
is continuous on a compact set, so it attains a maximum on
\mathcal{K}
from the extreme value theorem. Let

M\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}f(\mathbf{x})

and pick
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{K}
with
f(\mathbf{x}^{*})=M
. If
y\in\mathcal{S}
satisfies
\|\mathbf{y}\|>R
, then by the choice of
R
, we have
f(\mathbf{y})<f(\mathbf{p})-1
. Because
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{K}
, we have
M\geqslant f(\mathbf{p})
, hence for such
\mathbf{y}
,

f(\mathbf{y})<f(\mathbf{p})-1\leqslant M-1<M.

Thus every point of
\mathcal{S}
outside
\mathcal{K}
gives a strictly smaller
f
-value than
M
. Therefore
M
is the global maximum on
\mathcal{S}
, attained at
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
.
\square

3.2
New inequalities

Our novel results here are derived by modifying the constraints we impose and restructuring the expressions within the inequalities. The motivation for these inequalities stems from the Purkiss principle
[
14
,
15
]
, which roughly states that, in most cases, when optimising a symmetric function under a symmetric constraint, the extremum is attained when all the variables are equal
2
2

2

We must note that the precise formulation of the Purkiss principle guarantees only the existence of a local extremum at the point where all variables are equal. However, this local extremum need not necessarily constitute a global extremum, as demonstrated in the preceding section concerning the generalised Damacus inequality.
.
