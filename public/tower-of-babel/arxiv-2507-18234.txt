Superconvergence relations for structure functions emerging at the Siegert point
Vladimir Pascalutsa

Abstract
The Burkhardt–Cottingham sum rule is an exact superconvergence relation for a spin-structure function, derived from general principles of light absorption and scattering, and valid at any momentum transfer Q 2 Q^{2} . I illustrate how a class of such relations emerges from the Siegert point, an unphysical kinematical point where both the probe and the target are at rest. From light-by-light scattering, a new sum rule for γ ∗ ​ γ ∗ \gamma^{\ast}\gamma^{\ast} fusion is emerging, valid for arbitrary photon virtualities. When one of the photons becomes real, this sum rule reproduces the known relations for the photon spin-structure functions. Regarding the convergence of these relations, I present a simple argument for the suppression of longitudinal photon polarizations at high energy. Among its consequences is the prediction of σ L / σ T → 0 \sigma_{L}/\sigma_{T}\to 0 at high energy, for the ratio of unpolarized nucleon photoabsorption cross sections.

Introduction

The celebrated Burkhardt-Cottingham (BC) sum rule
Burkhardt and Cottingham 1970
is
a superconvergence relation,

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,g_{2}(x,Q^{2})=0,

(1)

for one of the two spin-structure functions
g_{1,2}(x,Q^{2})
of a target particle
probed by a virtual
photon with energy
1
1

1

More specifically, the photon with 4-momentum
q
is absorbed by a target particle with 4-momentum
p
, with kinematic invariants given by:
\nu=p\cdot q/M
,
p^{2}=M^{2}
,
q^{2}=-Q^{2}
, using the “mostly-negative” Minkowski metric and natural units.

\nu=Q^{2}/(2Mx)
and virtuality
Q^{2}
;
M
is
the particle mass.
Derived from the general principles of causality (analyticity), unitarity (optical theorem),
Lorentz symmetry, and the electromagnetic gauge invariance, manifested in the process of photon absorption and scattering (see, e.g.,
Lampe and Reya 2000
;
Drechsel et al. 2003
;
Pascalutsa 2024
), it provides an exact constraint, valid at any
Q^{2}
(up to possible convergence issues, cf., e.g.,
Jaffe 1990
).
The BC is applicable to any particle with spin, albeit mainly
used in the context of nucleon and nuclear structure
Kuhn et al. 2009
;
Deur et al. 2019
, and is particularly useful in the evaluations of the nuclear effects in the hyperfine splitting of hydrogen-like atoms, cf.
Zheng et al. 2021
;
Antognini et al. 2022
;
Ruth et al. 2024
for recent developments.

The present paper shows that a broad class of such relations
can be obtained from dispersion relations for transverse (
T
) and longitudinal (
L
) Compton amplitudes, by using the equivalence of these amplitudes (
T=L
) at the Siegert point:

\nu^{2}=-Q^{2}
. This equivalence is a manifestation of the fact that, at this unphysical kinematical point, both the target and the photon are at rest (photon momentum
|\vec{q}\,|=\sqrt{Q^{2}+\nu^{2}}=0
), and hence, longitudinal and transverse polarizations are indistinguishable. I shall refer to it as the ‘‘LT-degeneracy” at the Siegert point, for lack of a better name in the literature.
2
2

2

In Ref.
Biloshytskyi et al. 2024
, we called it the
Siegert theorem
— but, strictly speaking, it isn’t. In retrospect, it may be viewed as a lemma to the actual theorem
Siegert 1937
which, in addition, exploits the current conservation, multipole expansion, the extreme non-relativistic (static) approximation, etc.; see e.g.,
Friar and Fallieros 1984
for details.

The LT-degeneracy is, of course,
correlated with the spin of the target particle, since
the total helicity is conserved.

II
High-energy suppression of longitudinal versus transverse components

On the question of UV-convergence, it is important to realize that
the longitudinal amplitudes and cross sections must be suppressed with energy, as compared to their transverse counterparts.
The suppression factor is roughly
1/\nu
, per longitudinal photon.
This is because in the limit
\nu\to\infty
, just as
for
Q^{2}\to 0
, the photon becomes real.
Indeed, the virtual-photon velocity is the ratio of photon momentum to its energy (in natural units):

v_{\gamma^{\ast}}=\frac{\sqrt{Q^{2}+\nu^{2}}}{\nu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nu\to\infty}}{{\to}}1.

(2)

and the longitudinal components will ultimately decouple.
This is also true in the Bjorken limit: fixed
x
, large
\nu
and
Q^{2}
.

This suppression is automatically ensured by the electromagnetic gauge invariance.
3
3

3

Consider a typical matrix element,
\epsilon_{L}\cdot J
, where

\epsilon_{L}=1/Q(|\vec{q}\,|,0,0,\nu)

is the photon polarization 4-vector in the lab frame. Note that it is singular
in the limit of
Q\to 0
or
\nu\to\infty
. However, thanks to gauge-invariance,

q\cdot J=0=\nu J^{0}-|\vec{q}\,|J^{3},

and the factors are turning up-side-down:

\epsilon_{L}\cdot J=1/Q\,\big(|\vec{q}\,|J^{0}-\nu J^{3}\big)=(Q/\nu)J^{3}.

This agrees with the decoupling of longitudinal components, and also explicitly shows why they are accompanied by a factor of
Q/\nu
.

Yet, it is often missed in assessments of the Regge behavior
at large
\nu
.
In particular,
the asymptotic Regge behavior of the unpolarized cross sections
of total-photoabsorption on the proton,
\sigma_{T}
and
\sigma_{L}
,
is often assumed to be the same in DIS fits aimed at
the extraction of unpolarized structure functions (
F_{1}\sim\sigma_{L}
and
F_{2}\sim\sigma_{T}+\sigma_{L}
), see, e.g.,
Alwall and Ingelman 2004
;
Gao et al. 2018
and references therein.
In reality, where the electromagnetic gauge invariance is exact,
\sigma_{L}
should be suppressed by extra powers of
1/\nu
, to ensure
that it vanishes in the real-photon limit of
\nu\to\infty
.

This becomes apparent in any simple model with gauge invariance. For instance, in the naive parton model, one has:

\sigma_{L}=\frac{Q^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\sigma_{T}\,.

(3)

We have verified the suppression in other simple examples
Biloshytskyi 2024
; first of all, in
the leading-order QED, where the photoabsorption cross-sections
are given by tree-level Compton scattering. In this case, the polarized cross sections happen to exactly follow this trend.
4
4

4

Note that the helicity-difference cross-section
\sigma_{TT}=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\sigma_{1/2}-\sigma_{3/2})
is not positive definite. The full QED expressions can be found in Ref.
Hagelstein and Pascalutsa 2018
and references therein.

\sigma_{LT}(\nu,Q^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nu\to\infty}}{{\to}}-\frac{Q}{\nu}\sigma_{TT}(\nu,Q^{2}).

(4)

This feature is, of course, crucial in assessing the convergence of
the relations discussed below.

The specific power of suppression may vary from theory to theory, but it is clear that

\sigma_{L}(\nu,Q^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nu\to\infty}}{{\to}}0,

(5)

and this is true for any cross section of longitudinal-photon absorption.
For the proton, this means, given the rising
\sigma_{T}^{p}
, that the ratio of longitudinal
and transverse cross sections must vanish:

\sigma_{L}^{p}/\sigma_{T}^{p}\to 0
, for
\nu\to\infty
.

III
Virtual Compton scattering at the Siegert point

The forward doubly-virtual Compton scattering (VVCS) on a spin-1/2 target, such as the nucleon, can be described by
four scalar amplitudes: two unpolarized (or, spin-independent):

T_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})
and
T_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})

and two polarized (or, spin-dependent):

S_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})
and
S_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})
, see

Drechsel et al. 2003
;
Hagelstein et al. 2016
for further details
and notations. In this section, I focus on the unpolarized case for simplicity.

The optical theorem relates the discontinuity of these amplitudes to the structure functions, e.g.,
\im T_{1,2}(\nu,Q^{2})\sim F_{1,2}(x,Q^{2})
, the unpolarized. The amplitude
T_{1}
is purely transverse, whereas the purely longitudinal one is given by the following linear combination
of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
:

T_{L}(\nu,Q^{2})=\Big(-T_{1}+\frac{\vec{q}^{\,2}}{Q^{2}}T_{2}\Big)(\nu,Q^{2}),

(6)

with
\vec{q}^{\,2}=\nu^{2}+Q^{2}
. From this expression it is immediately clear that, at the Siegert point where
\vec{q}=0
,
T_{L}=-T_{1}
, and hence the LT degeneracy. If we would assume
unsubtracted dispersion relations for both
T
and
L

amplitudes, with the corresponding optical theorem,

\displaystyle\im T_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\,\sigma_{T}(\nu,Q^{2}),

(7a)

\displaystyle\im T_{L}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\,\sigma_{L}(\nu,Q^{2}),

(7b)

we then obtain:

0=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{\nu^{2}}{\nu^{2}+Q^{2}}\left[\sigma_{T}(\nu,Q^{2})+\sigma_{L}(\nu,Q^{2})\right].

(8)

Of course, this cannot work, because both cross sections are positive-definite.
There is also a convergence problem here. According to our gauge-invariance argument, the
T
- and
L
-cross-section do not
go with the same power of
\nu
—
\sigma_{L}
is suppressed.
The
T
-term in this relation is unlikely to converge by itself, as is also
known empirically for the nucleon from the Regge behavior of hadronic cross sections.

It makes more sense to use a subtracted dispersion relation for

T_{1}
amplitude,

T_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})=T_{1}(0,Q^{2})+\frac{2\nu^{2}}{\pi}\int_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\frac{\sigma_{T}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime\,2}-\nu^{2}}

(9)

where the amplitude at
\nu=0
is
the “subtraction function”, sometimes regarded as a nuisance — most prominently in calculations of the nuclear-polarizability effect in muonic atoms (see, e.g.,
Pohl et al. 2013
;
Carlson 2015
;
Antognini et al. 2022
for reviews).

The BC analogue, written in Eq. (
8
), then becomes
the sum rule for the subtraction function
Gasser et al. 2015
;
Hagelstein and Pascalutsa 2021
;
Biloshytskyi et al. 2024
:

\displaystyle T_{1}(0,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{\nu^{2}}{\nu^{2}+Q^{2}}\left(\frac{Q^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\sigma_{T}-\sigma_{L}\right)(\nu,Q^{2}),

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{M}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{2}}\,F_{L}(x,Q^{2}),

where in the second line,
F_{L}=F_{2}-2xF_{1}
is the longitudinal structure function, obtained by using

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}\alpha}{M}F_{1}(x,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\,\sigma_{T}(\nu,Q^{2}),

(11a)

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}\alpha}{\nu}F_{2}(x,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu\,Q^{2}}{\nu^{2}+Q^{2}}\sigma_{T}(\nu,Q^{2}).

(11b)

The integrals look dangerously singular in
the limit of high energy
\nu
or low
x
.
Certainly the first term, containing
\sigma_{T}
,
is known to be rapidly convergent, as it essentially gives the Baldin sum rule
Baldin 1960
. The second term may also
converge, if indeed
the longitudinal photon (here squared) comes with the suppression factor of
Q/\nu
.
At least, in simple examples it converges, cf. Ref.
Biloshytskyi et al. 2024
.

To summarize, in the unpolarized case, one does not obtain a superconvergence relation
at the Siegert point, but rather a sum rule for the subtraction function
T_{1}(0,Q^{2})
. If convergent for the nucleon, the resulting relation may still be useful for
a data-driven determination of the nucleon polarizabilities and their effect
in the Lamb shift of hydrogen-like atoms.

IV
Derivation(s) of Burkhardt-Cottingham sum rule

Let us now turn the attention to the spin-dependent case, described
by VVCS amplitudes
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.

The BC sum rule is derived from a dispersion relation
for
S_{2}
, in the limit
\nu\to 0
. One first assumes
an unsubtracted dispersion relation for
\nu S_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})
,
and then requires
S_{2}
to have no pole at 0, i.e.:

\lim_{\nu\rightarrow 0}\nu\,S_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})=0.

(12)

This derivation seems somewhat arbitrary. In particular, it is
not very clear why one starts with the dispersion relation
for
\nu S_{2}
, and not simply
S_{2}
. In the latter case, we would not have the BC sum rule. One argument
is that (the Born contribution to)
S_{2}
, in the limit of
Q^{2}\to 0
, does have a pole at
\nu=0
,
which would invalidate the dispersion relation.
Hence, it makes sense to consider
\nu S_{2}
. However, if there is a pole, why can we demand (
12
)?
Rather than struggling with these questions, we take the alternative, more straightforward path.

Consider the spin amplitudes corresponding to
T
or
L
photon polarizations, expressed as a linear combination of original amplitudes as:

\displaystyle S_{TT}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Q}{M}\!\left[\frac{\nu}{Q}S_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})-\frac{Q}{M}S_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})\right]\!,\qquad

(13a)

\displaystyle S_{LT}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Q}{M}\!\left[S_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})+\frac{\nu}{M}S_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})\right]\!.

(13b)

We assume that these amplitudes satisfy the unsubtracted dispersion relations:

\displaystyle S_{TT}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\nu}{\pi}\int_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\frac{\nu^{\prime}\sigma_{TT}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime\,2}-\nu^{2}},

(14a)

\displaystyle S_{LT}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\frac{\nu^{\prime\,2}\sigma_{LT}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime\,2}-\nu^{2}},

(14b)

where
\nu_{\mathrm{el}}=Q^{2}/2M
is the elastic threshold, and we have used the optical theorem,
5
5

5

Sometimes the amplitudes
S_{TT}
and
S_{LT}
are denoted as
g_{TT}
and
g_{LT}
, respectively (e.g., Ref.
Drechsel et al. 2003
).
Also, because of the ambiguity of defining flux factor of the virtual photon, a different phase-space factor (which multiplies the cross section) can be used in the optical theorem; e.g., many use
|\vec{q}\,|
instead of
\nu
(e.g.,
Biloshytskyi et al. 2024
). However, the expressions in terms of structure functions are independent of these conventions.

\im S_{i}(\nu,Q^{2})=\nu\,\sigma_{i}(\nu,Q^{2})
to relate to the longitudinal-transverse (
i=LT
) and the transverse-transverse (
i=TT
) photoabsorption cross sections.
Note that convergence of these dispersion relations is warranted by convergence of, respectively, the GDH
Gerasimov 1966
;
Drell and Hearn 1966
and Schwinger
Schwinger 1975
;
ar Rashid 1976
sum rules, which schematically read as:

\displaystyle S_{TT}(\nu\to 0,0)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\varkappa^{2}\sim\int\frac{d\nu}{\nu}\sigma_{TT}(\nu,0)

(15a)

\displaystyle\frac{S_{LT}(0,Q^{2})}{Q}\Big|_{Q\to 0}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\varkappa\sim\int\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{\sigma_{LT}(\nu,Q^{2})}{Q}\Big|_{Q\to 0},

(15b)

where
\varkappa
is the anomalous magnetic moment. Here
it is important to emphasize that, as argued above, a longitudinal
photon brings in an extra
1/\nu
at high energy, see Eq. (
4
).
Hence the convergence of the two dispersion relations is correlated.

The second step of our derivation is the Siegert point (
\nu=iQ
), where we have an equivalence of the
T
- and
L
-amplitude:

-iS_{TT}(iQ,Q^{2})=S_{LT}(iQ,Q^{2}).

(16)

This is a manifestation of the fact that, at this kinematical point, the photon is at rest, its momentum
\vec{q}=0
, and hence longitudinal and transverse polarizations are indistinguishable.

Plugging in here the dispersion relations of Eq. (
14
), we find
the BC sum rule:

0=\frac{2Q}{\pi}\int_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{\nu}{\nu^{2}+Q^{2}}\left[\frac{\nu}{Q}\sigma_{LT}(\nu,Q^{2})-\sigma_{TT}(\nu,Q^{2})\right],

(17)

which can be cast in its standard form (
1
) by using: (i) the relation between the spin structure function
g_{2}
and the photoabsorption cross sections,

\displaystyle\im S_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}\alpha M}{\nu^{2}}\,g_{2}(x,Q^{2}),

(18a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M^{2}\nu}{\nu^{2}+Q^{2}}\left[\frac{\nu}{Q}\sigma_{LT}-\sigma_{TT}\right](\nu,Q^{2}),\qquad

(18b)

and (ii) a change of the integration variable from
\nu
to
x=Q^{2}/2M\nu
.

V
A new sum rule for
\gamma^{\ast}\gamma^{\ast}
fusion

A photon by itself can be viewed as the target,
probed by a virtual photon via the light-by-light (LbL) scattering. In this case, the Siegert point is tricky. One may say it does not exist, because both photons cannot be put to rest, if one of them is real. However, one can start with a doubly-virtual
(forward) LbL scattering, which is characterized by two virtualities, say
q_{1}^{2}=-Q_{2}^{2}
and
q_{1}^{2}=-Q_{1}^{2}
, and a crossing-symmetric invariant
\nu=q_{1}\cdot q_{2}
. The Siegert point is then defined as:
6
6

6

Initial steps to study the LbL scattering at this point were undertaken in a PhD thesis
(
Biloshytskyi 2024
, Sec. 5.4)
.

\nu^{2}_{S}=Q_{1}^{2}Q_{2}^{2}

(19)

It is interesting that this kinematical point is now real, albeit still unphysical (the total energy,
\sqrt{s}=iQ_{1}\pm iQ_{2}
).

At the Siegert point the photon four-momenta are orthogonal to any of their polarization vectors:

q_{i}\cdot\epsilon_{j}=0,\quad\mbox{$\forall\,i,j=1,2$.}

(20)

Hence, the Lorenz structure of the doubly-virtual LbL amplitude has only 3 terms, just as for the real LbL
Pascalutsa and Vanderhaeghen 2010
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}}(q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A(\nu_{S})\,g^{\mu_{4}\mu_{2}}g^{\mu_{3}\mu_{1}}

(21)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B(\nu_{S})\,g^{\mu_{4}\mu_{1}}g^{\mu_{3}\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C(\nu_{S})\,g^{\mu_{4}\mu_{3}}g^{\mu_{2}\mu_{1}}.

The LT-degeneracy is hereby immediately exposed: out of the 8 amplitudes needed
to describe the forward-doubly-virtual LbL scattering, only 3 needed for real-LbL
remain at the Siegert point.

Therefore, we have 5 inter-relations among the amplitudes, which one can easily derive by contracting Eq. (
21
) with various
polarizations. For brevity, I will only consider the one involving the spin structure functions. In the notation of Budnev
et al.

Budnev et al. 1975
(slightly adjusted in
Pascalutsa 2024
), I obtain the following superconvergence relation:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\nu_{\mathrm{el}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{\left[\tau_{TT}^{a}-\mbox{$\frac{\nu}{Q_{1}Q_{2}}$}\tau_{LT}^{a}\right](\nu,Q_{1}^{2},Q_{2}^{2})}{\sqrt{\nu^{2}-Q_{1}^{2}Q_{2}^{2}}}\,,

(22)

where
\nu_{\mathrm{el}}=(Q_{1}^{2}+Q_{2}^{2})/2
, and the
\gamma^{\ast}\gamma^{\ast}
-fusion
response functions
\tau_{TT}^{a}

and
\tau_{LT}^{a}
are quite analogous to, respectively:
\sigma_{TT}
and
\sigma_{LT}

response functions considered in VVCS.

Let me emphasize that this relation is valid
for any spacelike virtualities
Q_{1}^{2}
and
Q_{2}^{2}
, and is a first relation of this kind.
We can take the limit of one of the virtualities going to 0, i.e., one of the photons is real. In this limit, the Siegert point is at
\nu=0
, i.e., the same limit in which the LbL sum rules are normally derived via the low-energy expansion (see, e.g.,
Pascalutsa and Vanderhaeghen 2010
;
Pascalutsa et al. 2012
.
In this case, each of the two terms in Eq. (
22
) vanishes separately, leading to the
known superconvergence for both spin-structure functions of the (real) photon,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,g_{1}^{\gamma}(x,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(23a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,g_{2}^{\gamma}(x,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(23b)

Thus, besides the BC sum rule (second line), we have reproduced the sum rule
for
g_{1}^{\gamma}
, cf.
Bass et al. 1998
and references therein. At least one additional sum rule, identified in Ref.
Pascalutsa et al. 2012
, belongs to this class (i.e., valid at all
Q^{2}
) and should be recoverable by this approach. A detailed discussion will appear elsewhere.

VI
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we revisit the exact sum rules which follow from general principle of photon scattering and absorption on a target particle with spin.
The emphasis is on two basic principles:

•

the “LT-degeneracy” at the Siegert point, i.e., the equivalence
of the transverse and longitudinal photon polarizations at an unphysical kinematical point,

•

decoupling of longitudinal photon polarizations in the high-energy limit,
\nu\to\infty
, which should be ensured by the
electromagnetic gauge invariance and unitarity bounds.

We have seen how the Burkhardt-Cottingham (BC) sum rule naturally follows from the LT-degeneracy, and that there are further relations that fall in the same class defined as: model-independent constraints on (moments of) structure functions, valid at any
Q^{2}
. In the unpolarized case, it leads to the sum rule for the subtraction function
T_{1}(0,Q^{2})
.

The case of light-by-light scattering is the most interesting here, as it leads to a novel superconvergence relation for
\gamma^{\ast}\gamma^{\ast}
-fusion
at arbitrary virtualities,
see Eq. (
22
). This is the central result of this paper.
It is a first relation of this sort, and can, for instance, be used to
constrain the doubly-virtual meson transition form factors, similar to the single-virtual case
Dai and Pennington 2017
;
Danilkin and Vanderhaeghen 2017
.
In the limit when one of the photons is real, this relation yields the two superconvergence relations for the photon spin-structure functions,

g_{1}^{\gamma}
and
g_{2}^{\gamma}
.

It is interesting to extend this approach to other spin targets.
In the case of a target with spin 1 and higher — such as the deuteron — the four tensor structure
functions
b_{1},\dots,b_{4}(x,Q^{2})
begin to appear. Here, insofar, we only
know the Close-Kumano sum rule
Close and Kumano 1990
:

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,b_{1}(x)=0,

(24)

shown to hold in the naive parton model.
The question whether one can extend it to any
Q^{2}
in
model-independent way is still open.

One can extend this method to even higher spins, starting with the spin 3/2, which would be very interesting in the context
of the many studies of the
\Delta(1232)
isobar and other decuplet baryons (see
Pascalutsa et al. 2007
;
Geng 2013
;
Ramalho and Peña 2024
for reviews).

Furthermore, similar relations are expected to arise
for the gravitational structure functions, which describe the target
probed by a graviton rather than a photon
Polyakov and Schweitzer 2018
;
Cotogno and Freese 2022
.
