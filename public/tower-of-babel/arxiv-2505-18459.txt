Bridging reaction theory and nuclear structure in π ± \pi^{\pm} - 48 Ca scattering
Viacheslav Tsaran, Francesco Marino, Sonia Bacca, Francesca Bonaiti, Marc Vanderhaeghen

Abstract
We extend the pion-nucleus multiple-scattering framework to include detailed second-order rescattering dynamics for nuclei with non-zero isospin. To account for intermediate charge-exchange and nucleon spin-flip effects, we develop a scattering potential that depends on the one- and two-body densities of the target nucleus. We compute one-body densities from coupled-cluster theory and two-body densities within the Hartree-Fock approximation. To estimate theoretical uncertainties, we employ modern nuclear Hamiltonians derived from chiral effective field theory. While the sensitivity to nuclear structure details is mild, second-order corrections are found to be sizeable and essential for accurately reproducing differential cross sections measured in π ± \pi^{\pm} - 48 Ca elastic scattering within the Δ ⁡ ( 1232 ) \Delta(1232) -resonance region.

II
Multiple-scattering formalism

The hadronic component of the amplitude describing pion-nucleus elastic scattering is given by the scattering amplitude
F
, which encapsulates the interactions between the incoming pion and the target nucleus.
These interactions include multiple scattering processes, resonance formations, and nuclear-medium effects such as pion absorption.
In our approach, we build upon the Kerman-McManus-Thaler formulation of multiple-scattering theory
[
30
]
, where the elastic scattering amplitude
F
is found as solution of the Lippmann-Schwinger-type integral equation

F(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=V(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})\\
-\frac{A-1}{A}\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{V(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})F(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})}{k_{0}^{2}-{k^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+i\,\varepsilon},

(1)

where
\bm{k}
and
\bm{k}^{\prime}
are the initial and final pion momenta in pion-nucleus center-of-mass (c.m.) frame, respectively,
k_{0}
is the on-shell pion momentum,
A
is the number of nucleons in the target nucleus, and we use the convention
\hbar=c=1
.
The solution of Eq. (
1
) accounts for pion rescattering on the nucleus in its ground state (g.s.) to all orders in perturbation theory, while the dynamics of nuclear excitation is encapsulated within the scattering potential
V
.
In this way, the pion-nucleus scattering potential incorporates contributions from all possible intermediate excited states of the target nucleus.

In our approach, we approximate
V
using the first two terms of the corresponding scattering series, leading to
[
17
]

\displaystyle V(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})\approx V^{[1]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})+V^{[2]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})

(2a)

\displaystyle V^{[1]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=-A\frac{\sqrt{\mathscr{M}(k^{\prime})\mathscr{M}(k)}}{2\pi}\langle\pi(\bm{k}^{\prime}),\Psi_{0}|\hat{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}}|\pi(\bm{k}),\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(2b)

\displaystyle V^{[2]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=-A(A-1)\frac{\sqrt{\mathscr{M}(k^{\prime})\mathscr{M}(k)}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \times\langle\pi(\bm{k}^{\prime}),\Psi_{0}|\hat{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}}_{2}\hat{G}\hat{P}_{\emptyset}\hat{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}}_{1}|\pi(\bm{k}),\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(2c)

where
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is the g.s. wave function of the target nucleus and
\hat{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}}_{i}
represents the transition amplitude operator for pion scattering on the
i
th nucleon within the nucleus.
For simplicity, the subscript will be omitted when it is not necessary.
The projection operator
\hat{P}_{\emptyset}=\hat{\mathds{1}}-|\Psi_{0}\rangle\langle\Psi_{0}|
ensures that intermediate states exclude the nuclear g.s., and
\hat{G}
is the pion-nucleus Green’s function.
Here,
\mathscr{M}
denotes the off-shell analog of the relativistic reduced mass of the pion-nucleus system in the pion-nucleus c.m. frame, which is given by

\mathscr{M}(k)=\frac{[E(k_{0})+E(k)][\omega(k_{0})E_{A}(k_{0})+\omega(k)E_{A}(k)]}{2\left(E^{2}(k_{0})+E^{2}(k)\right)},

(3)

where
\omega(k)
and
E_{A}(k)
are the relativistic energy of the pion and the nucleus, respectively, as functions of the c.m. momentum
k=\absolutevalue{\bm k}
, and
E(k)=\omega(k)+E_{A}(k)
.

).

Figure

illustrates pion-nucleus scattering within the multiple-scattering formalism.
The upper panel depicts the all-order rescattering of the outgoing pion off the nucleus in its ground state, corresponding to the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
).
The middle and lower panels depict the contributions of first and second order to the scattering potential
V
, corresponding to Eqs. (
2b
) and (
2c
), respectively.
The lower panel specifically illustrates the second-order contribution, where the pion first scatters off one nucleon, exciting the nucleus, then propagates and undergoes a second scattering event that de-excites the nucleus.
This two-nucleon mechanism facilitates charge exchange and/or spin flip between nucleons via the intermediate pion, ultimately bringing the nucleus back to its ground state.

To evaluate the matrix elements in Eqs. (
2
), we employ the
impulse approximation

[
31
]
,
assuming that
\hat{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
is related to the pion-nucleon scattering amplitude in the pion-nucleon c.m. frame as

\langle\pi(\bm{k}^{\prime}),N(\bm{p}^{\prime})|\hat{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}}|\pi(\bm{k}),N(\bm{p})\rangle\approx{-(2\pi)^{4}}\delta(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{k}-\bm{p})\\
\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{p}^{\prime})\mu(\bm{k},\bm{p})}}\,\hat{f}(\bm{k}^{\prime}_{\text{2cm}},\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}),

(4)

where
N(\bm{p})
denotes a single nucleon with momentum
\bf p
,

\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}
is the pion momentum in the pion-nucleon c.m. frame defined by the Lorentz transformation and
\mu(\bm{k},\bm{p})={\omega(k)E_{N}(p)}/{W(\bm{k},\bm{p})}
in terms of the nucleon energy
E_{N}(p)
and the invariant mass
W(\bm{k},\bm{p})
of the pion-nucleon system.
The amplitude
\hat{f}
is an operator in nucleon spin-isospin space analogous to the free-space pion-nucleon scattering amplitude.
However, in the nuclear medium, it undergoes additional modifications due to the presence of numerous inelastic channels (see Section IV.C in Ref.
[
17
]
).

While the c.m. momentum
\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}
and the phase factor
\mu
depend explicitly on the nucleon momentum
\bm{p}
, we follow the standard
optimized factorization approximation

[
32
,
33
]
, evaluating these quantities at the effective initial and final nucleon momenta

\bm{p}_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\bm{q}}{2}-\frac{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{k}}{2A},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{p}_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}=-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}-\frac{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{k}}{2A},

(5)

where
\bm{q}=\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{k}
.

Under these assumptions, the first-order potential in momentum space takes the form
[
34
]

V^{[1]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=\mathscr{W}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})\Tr\left[\hat{f}\left(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}\right)\rho(\bm{q})\right],

(6)

where the nuclear form factor
\rho(q)
is normalized to
\Tr\rho(0)=A
, and

\mathscr{W}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=\sqrt{\frac{\mathscr{M}(k^{\prime})\mathscr{M}(k)}{\mu(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{p}_{\text{eff}}^{\prime})\mu(\bm{k},\bm{p}_{\text{eff}})}}

(7)

is a purely kinematic factor.
Here and further,
\Tr
represents the summation over all nucleon spin and isospin projections, which are omitted for simplicity.
Similarly, the “2cm” subscript in the arguments of the scattering amplitude
f
is omitted but remains implicit.

Considering the expectation value in Eq. (
2
) only over the nuclear ground state
\ket{\Psi_0}
, the structure of this contribution can be decomposed into two distinct terms

\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{f}_{2}\hat{G}\hat{P}_{\emptyset}\hat{f}_{1}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=-\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{f}_{2}|\Psi_{0}\rangle\hat{G}\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{f}_{1}|\Psi_{0}\rangle\\
+\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{f}_{2}\hat{G}\hat{f}_{1}|\Psi_{0}\rangle.

(8)

Here, the first term involves the product of two expectation values of the pion-nucleon scattering amplitude.
Each matrix element
\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{f}_{2}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is related to the first-order potential given in Eq. (
6
) (see Ref.
[
17
]
for details).
As a result, in momentum space, the part of the potential corresponding to the first term in Eq. (
8
) takes the form

\tilde{V}^{[2]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=\frac{A-1}{A}\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{k_{0}^{2}-{k^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+i\varepsilon}\\
\times V^{[1]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})V^{[1]}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k}).

(9)

Unlike the first term, the second term in Eq. (
8
) cannot be reduced to a product of one-body matrix elements
as it represents the expectation value of a two-body operator
\hat{f}_{2}\hat{G}\hat{f}_{1}
.

In momentum space, this term can be expressed as

\langle\pi(\bm{k}^{\prime})\Psi_{0}|\hat{f}_{2}\hat{G}\hat{f}_{1}|\pi(\bm{k})\Psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{A(A-1)}\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}G(k^{\prime\prime})\\
\times\Tr\left[\hat{f}_{2}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})\hat{f}_{1}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})\rho(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}-\bm{k})\right],

(10)

where
\rho(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})
is the two-body nucleon density matrix in momentum space, normalized to
\Tr\rho(0,0)=A(A-1)
.
In coordinate space, the latter is defined as

\rho(\bm{r}_{1},\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2};\bm{r}_{1}^{\prime},{\sigma_{1}}^{\prime},{\tau_{1}}^{\prime},\bm{r}_{2}^{\prime},{\sigma_{2}}^{\prime},{\tau_{2}}^{\prime})\\
=\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{r}_{1},\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{r}_{2},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})\\
\times\hat{\psi}(\bm{r}_{1}^{\prime},{\sigma_{1}}^{\prime},{\tau_{1}}^{\prime})\hat{\psi}(\bm{r}_{2}^{\prime},{\sigma_{2}}^{\prime},{\tau_{2}}^{\prime})|\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(11)

where
\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{r},\sigma,\tau)
and
\hat{\psi}(\bm{r},\sigma,\tau)
are the creation and annihilation operators for a nucleon at position
\bm{r}
with spin projection
\sigma
and isospin projection
\tau
.
General definitions and properties of the one- and two-body density matrices are considered in Appendix
A
.
Since we are considering target nuclei with zero angular momentum, we can trace over spin projections and define, for later convenience, the following spin-independent density matrix

\rho(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2};\tau_{1},\tau_{2};{\tau_{1}}^{\prime},{\tau_{2}}^{\prime})=\sum_{\sigma_{1,2}}\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{1}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{2}\,e^{-i(\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}_{1}+\bm{q}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}_{2})}\\
\times\rho(\bm{r}_{1},\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2};\bm{r}_{1},\sigma_{1},{\tau_{1}}^{\prime},\bm{r}_{2},\sigma_{2},{\tau_{2}}^{\prime}),

(12)

The general form of the potential described in this subsection was employed in Ref.
[
17
]
to derive the scattering potential for spin-zero nuclei with equal numbers of protons and neutrons, achieving a good description of experimental data for
\pi^{\pm}
scattering on
12
C,
16
O,
28
Si, and
40
Ca.
For these nuclei, isospin invariance was assumed, enabling a simplified treatment of the spin-isospin summation in Eq. (
10
) without the need to distinguish between proton and neutron distributions. This no longer holds for nuclei with non-zero isospin, such as
48
Ca.

II.1
The non-zero isospin case

A comprehensive description of spin and isospin transitions during rescattering requires a detailed treatment of the spin-isospin structure of both nuclear structure functions and the pion-nucleon scattering amplitude.
The latter can be expressed as the decomposition
[
1
]

\hat{f}=\hat{f}^{(0)}+\hat{f}^{(1)}\ \hat{\bm{t}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\left(\hat{f}^{(2)}+\hat{f}^{(3)}\ \hat{\bm{t}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{n},

(13)

where
\hat{\bm{t}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
are the pion and nucleon isospin operators,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is the nucleon spin operator, and
\bm{n}
is the unit normal to the scattering plane.
We adopt the convention that protons (neutrons) have positive (negative) isospin projection.

Substituting the expansion given by Eq. (
13
) into Eq. (
6
), yields the first-order potential

V^{[1]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=\mathscr{W}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})\left(f^{(0)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})\left[\rho_{n}(q)+\rho_{p}(q)\right]\right.\\
\left.-e_{\pi}f^{(1)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})\left[\rho_{n}(q)-\rho_{p}(q)\right]\right),

(14)

where
\rho_{n}(\bm{q})
and
\rho_{p}(\bm{q})
are the neutron and proton form factors, while
e_{\pi}
is the pion charge.
The form factors are normalized to the number of the corresponding nucleons, such that
\rho_{p}(q=0)=Z
and
\rho_{n}(q=0)=N
.

To simplify the cumbersome derivation of
V^{[2]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})
, we will break down the key steps rather than directly substituting Eq. (
13
) into Eq. (
10
) in momentum-space representation.
The primary distinction from the isospin-zero case considered in Ref.
[
17
]
lies in the summation over isospin projections for nuclei with non-zero isospin.
To address this, we first examine the isospin structure involved in this summation. Specifically, we consider

\Tr_{\tau}\left[\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{2}+\hat{f}^{(1)}_{2}\ \hat{\bm{t}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{2}\right)\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{1}+\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}\ \hat{\bm{t}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{1}\right)\rho\right]=2\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{2}\hat{f}^{(0)}_{1}-\hat{f}^{(1)}_{2}\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}\right)\rho_{np}\\
+\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{2}+e_{\pi}\,\hat{f}^{(1)}_{2}\right)\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{1}+e_{\pi}\,\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}\right)\rho_{pp}+\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{2}-e_{\pi}\,\hat{f}^{(1)}_{2}\right)\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{1}-e_{\pi}\,\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}\right)\rho_{nn}+2\hat{f}^{(1)}_{2}\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}C_{\text{(ex)}},

(15)

where
\rho_{np},\rho_{pp}
, and
\rho_{nn}
are the diagonal elements of Eq. (
12
) defined by setting
\tau_{1}^{\prime}=\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}^{\prime}=\tau_{2}
.
In this regard, we define
\rho_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=\rho(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2};\tau_{1},\tau_{2};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
, where
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}=pp,nn,np
specifies the isospin projections of the nucleon pair.
The corresponding terms in Eq. (
15
) describe pion scattering on a pair of nucleons while preserving the individual nucleon isospin.
In contrast, the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
15
) accounts for the intermediate charge-exchange process, which swaps the isospin projections in the
np
pair.
The associated density matrix is off-diagonal in isospin, and we refer to it as the exchange correlation function, defined as

C_{\text{(ex)}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=-\rho(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2};n,p;p,n),

(16)

with normalization
C_{\text{(ex)}}(0,0)=\text{min}(N,Z)
.
The factor of 2 in the second term of Eq. (
15
) arises from the symmetry of this term under the exchange of
p
and
n
.

Although we are considering nuclei with zero spin, the intermediate spin-exchange dynamics results in a nontrivial outcome for the spin summation
[
17
]
.
Due to spin invariance, the summation with the spin-dependent part of the amplitudes can be factorized, allowing us to consider separately

\displaystyle\Tr_{\sigma}\left[\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{2}+\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2}\ \hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\right)\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{1}+\hat{f}^{(2)}_{1}\ \hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ =2\left(\hat{f}^{(0)}_{2}\hat{f}^{(0)}_{1}+\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2}\hat{f}^{(2)}_{1}\,\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\right),

(17)

where
\bm{n}_{1}=\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}\times\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}^{\prime\prime}/|\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}\times\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}}^{\prime\prime}|
and
\bm{n}_{2}=\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}^{\prime}}^{\prime\prime}\times\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}^{\prime}}^{\prime}/|\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}^{\prime}}^{\prime\prime}\times\bm{k}_{\text{2cm}^{\prime}}^{\prime}|
, with the subscript ”2cm” (”
\text{2cm}^{\prime}
”) corresponding to the c.m. system of the pion and the first (second) nucleon.

Combining Eqs. (
15
) and (
17
) to derive the full second-order part of the potential in momentum space, we obtain

V^{[2]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k})=\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{\mathscr{W}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})\mathscr{W}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})}{k_{0}^{2}-{k^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+i\varepsilon}\\
\times\left(\varv_{\text{(ex)}}+\varv_{nn}+\varv_{pp}+2\varv_{np}\right)-\frac{1}{A-1}\tilde{V}^{[2]}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}),

(18)

where the charge-exchange part of the potential is given by

\varv_{\text{(ex)}}=2\left[f^{(1)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})f^{(1)}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})\right.\\
\left.+f^{(3)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})f^{(3)}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})\,\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\right]C_{\text{(ex)}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}),

(19)

and the terms
\varv_{nn}
,
\varv_{pp}
, and
\varv_{np}
, which preserve individual nucleon isospin, are defined by

\varv_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}=\left[\left(f^{(0)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})+e_{\pi}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}f^{(1)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})\right)\left(f^{(0)}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})+e_{\pi}\varepsilon_{\tau_{2}}f^{(1)}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})\right)\right.\\
\left.+\left(f^{(2)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})+e_{\pi}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}f^{(3)}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime\prime})\right)\left(f^{(2)}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})+e_{\pi}\varepsilon_{\tau_{2}}f^{(3)}(\bm{k}^{\prime\prime},\bm{k})\right)\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\right]C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}),

(20)

with
\varepsilon_{p}=1
and
\varepsilon_{n}=-1
.
Here, we have introduced the correlation functions for a nucleon pair, as given by

C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=\rho_{\tau_{1}}(q_{1})\rho_{\tau_{2}}(q_{2})-\rho_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}),

(21)

which are normalized to
C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(0,0)=\delta_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}A_{\tau_{1}}
.
We are using the spin-independent operator Eq. (
12
), so the factor 2 in Eq. (
17
) is already accounted for in the correlation functions.
The last term in Eq. (
18
) accounts for the distinction between the processes described by
\varv_{(\text{ex})}
and
\varv_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}
, from the point of view of the Pauli exclusion principle.

The resulting second-order potential for pion scattering on spin-zero nuclei, derived with explicit treatment of the intermediate spin-isospin dynamics, is given by Eqs. (
14
) and (
18
)–(
20
).
For nuclei with zero isospin, assuming isospin invariance and the Slater determinant form for the nuclear ground-state wave function, the two-nucleon correlation functions satisfy
C_{pp}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=C_{nn}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=C_{\text{(ex)}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})
and
C_{np}=0
.
Under this condition, Eqs. (
18
)–(
20
) simplify to Eq. (52) from Ref.
[
17
]
.
Note that in Eq. (
18
),
\varv_{np}
vanishes under the Slater determinant assumption employed in the following, as
C_{np}
is zero in this case (see Section
II.1
).

The calculation of the second-order potential requires two types of inputs: the scattering amplitudes
f^{(i)}
and the nuclear structure functions
\rho_{\tau}(q)
and
C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2},(\text{ex})}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})
.
The scattering amplitudes on a single nucleon are universal across all nuclei but include free parameters that account for their modification within the nuclear medium.
In our approach, the free-space amplitudes are taken from the phase shift analysis (WI08) by the SAID Collaboration
[
35
]
, while their in-medium modifications are characterized by three real energy-independent parameters.
The three parameters consist of the real and imaginary parts of the
\Delta(1232)
self-energy, which modify the resonant
P_{33}
channel, and the slope parameter of the imaginary part of the
s
-wave isoscalar amplitude.
These free parameters were determined in Ref.
[
17
]
by fitting
\pi^{\pm}
-
12
C scattering data in the pion laboratory kinetic energy range of 80–
180\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
, which shows a strong sensitivity to the
\Delta
-resonance properties.
These parameters are adopted unchanged in the present analysis.

In contrast to the single-nucleon scattering amplitudes, the nuclear structure functions are specific to each nucleus.
Rather than being fitted to pion-nucleus scattering data, they are either extracted from experiments or determined through theoretical predictions.
In Ref.
[
17
]
, under the assumption of isospin invariance, nucleon form factors were determined from the measured charge form factors, while two-body correlation functions were derived using a non-interacting harmonic oscillator (HO) shell model.

Here, as follows from Eq. (
14
), constructing the potential for nuclei with non-zero isospin requires separate knowledge of both point-proton and point-neutron form factors.
In particular, the distributions of neutrons and protons inside the nucleus differ. While the proton density can still be extracted from experimental charge densities, a precise, model-independent determination of neutron densities from experiment is challenging or accompanied by poorly controlled uncertainties
[
36
]
.
As a result, theoretical predictions are required not only for obtaining the correlation functions but also for the point-proton and point-neutron form factors. In this work, we take this input from modern nuclear structure theory.

III
Nuclear structure

The foundation of
ab initio
nuclear structure calculations
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
lies in the choice of the model for the interaction among nucleons and of an appropriate many-body method.
Regarding the many-body technique, several different methods can be used
[
38
]
. Here, we work with coupled-cluster (CC) theory
[
19
,
21
,
20
]
and approximations thereof. CC theory stands out for the excellent accuracy at a moderate computational cost, allowing access to medium-mass or even heavy nuclei
[
40
]
, as well as electroweak observables
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
.
In terms of interactions, Hamiltonians obtained from chiral effective field theory (
\chi
EFT)
[
22
,
23
,
46
,
47
]
, including nucleon-nucleon and three-nucleon contributions, are employed.

\chi
EFT provides a systematic approach to derive nuclear Hamiltonians consistent with the symmetries of the underlying theory of QCD.
The guiding principle is a low-momentum expansion of the interactions in powers
(Q/\Lambda_{\chi})^{\nu}
, where
Q
is a typical low-momentum scale characterizing nuclear physics and
\Lambda_{\chi}\approx 1\,\rm{GeV}
is the chiral symmetry breaking scale within QCD.
Chiral forces depend on a set of parameters, called low-energy constants (LECs), that are constrained by experimental data or theoretical results.

In particular, we will use two interactions at the next-to-next-to-leading order in the
\chi
EFT expansion (i.e., including terms up to
\nu=3
), namely
\rm{NNLO_{sat}}(450)

[
48
]
and the
\Delta
-full model
\Delta\rm{NNLO_{GO}}(394)

[
49
]
, with the associated momentum cutoff (in MeV/c) shown in parenthesis. For
\rm{NNLO_{sat}}(450)
the LECs were adjusted to reproduce nucleon-nucleon (NN) scattering data, as well as energies and radii of selected light and medium-mass nuclei up to
{}^{24}\rm{O}
, while
\Delta\rm{NNLO_{GO}}(394)
has been fitted to properties of both light nuclei and nuclear matter in addition to NN phase shifts.
Both models are able to accurately describe bulk observables (such as charge radii and densities) in the calcium region relevant for this work, see e.g.
[
43
,
40
,
49
,
50
]
.
By comparing the results obtained with these two different chiral forces, we can appreciate the effect of including explicit delta isobars in the chiral expansion, as well as of varying the cutoff and the optimization procedure employed for the LECs.

To discuss the many-body approach, we start from Hartree-Fock (HF) theory, which provides the simplest approximation to the g.s. wave function, assumed to be a single Slater determinant

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi_0}=\mathcal{A}\{\ket{p_1}\otimes...\otimes\ket{p_A}\},

(22)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the antisymmetrization operator and the single-particle (s.p.) orbitals

are determined self-consistently.
In the following, we will focus on closed-shell nuclei with g.s. angular momentum and parity
J^{\pi}=0^{+}
.
In this case, a spherical HF solution provides a reasonable starting point for a more advanced many-body calculation
[
19
]
.

In CC theory
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
the g.s. wave function

of a given nucleus is determined from the following exponential parametrization of the many-body g.s. ,

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_0}=e^{\hat{T}}\ket{ \Phi_0 }.

(23)

Dynamical correlations are built on top of the reference state
\ket{ \Phi_0 }
by the action of

\hat{T}
is expanded as a combination of
n

n
-hole (
n

n

We employ a common truncation of the cluster operator, known as coupled-cluster at the singles and doubles level (CCSD), including terms in

up to

2h

\displaystyle\hat{T}=\sum_{ai}t^{a}_{i}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{abij}t^{ab}_{ij}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{b}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}\hat{c}_{i}+...,

(24)

where

(
\hat{c}^{\dagger}

and

denote single-particle states that are occupied (holes) and unoccupied (particles) in the reference state
\ket{ \Phi_0 }
, respectively.
The amplitudes

We also investigate the effect of including leading

3h

[

The HF state is built from a model space consisting of 15 major HO shells with frequency

(22 MeV) for

\rm{NNLO_{sat}}

These frequencies are optimal for describing charge radii and densities with these potentials (see

\alpha\equiv\{n;ljm\tau\}

III.1
One-body densitites

From the CC wave function, expectation values of observables can be determined as described in
[
19
,
53
]
.
For our purposes, we focus on the one-body density matrix, whose matrix elements in the HO basis are given by

\displaystyle\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\matrixelement{\Psi_0}{ \hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\alpha} \hat{c}_{\beta} }{ \Psi_0}.

(26)

In spherical nuclei,
\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}
is block-diagonal for any choice of spherical s.p. states, since conservation laws of angular momentum, parity
\pi=(-1)^{l}
, and isospin projection enforce
[
54
,
55
]

\displaystyle\rho^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\delta_{j_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\delta_{m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}}\delta_{l_{\alpha}l_{\beta}}\delta_{\tau_{\alpha}\tau_{\beta}}\rho_{j_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}}^{(n_{\alpha}n_{\beta})}.

(27)

That is, the one-body density matrix mixes up states of the spherical s.p. basis that differ by the principal quantum number only.
The corresponding real-space point-neutron and point-proton densities
\rho_{\tau}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})
are given by
[
54
,
29
]

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha}(x)^{\dagger}\rho_{\beta}^{\alpha}\phi_{\beta}(x^{\prime})\delta_{\tau_{\alpha}\tau}\delta_{\tau_{\beta}\tau}

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{jln_{1}n_{2}}\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\rho_{jl\tau}^{(n_{1}n_{2})}R_{n_{1}l}(r)R_{n_{2}l}(r^{\prime})P_{l}(\hat{\bm{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{r}}^{\prime}),

where
P_{l}
are the Legendre polynomials of order
l
.
The spherical number densities
\rho_{\tau}(r)
(with
r=\absolutevalue{\bm r}
) are readily obtained by setting
\bm{r}=\bm{r}^{\prime}
and
P_{l}=1
in Eq. (
28
).
The Fourier transform of
\rho_{\tau}(r)
defines the form factor
\rho_{\tau}(q)

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau}(q)=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\,e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\rho_{\tau}(\bm{r})=4\pi\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}rr^{2}j_{0}(qr)\rho_{\tau}(r),

(29)

with
j_{0}(qr)=\sin(qr)/(qr)
.

We obtain intrinsic one-body densities by following the prescription of Refs.
[
56
,
57
,
42
]
.
The CC wave function factorizes approximately into an intrinsic part times a Gaussian c.m. wave function. Thus, a deconvolution with respect to the Gaussian wave function allows finding the intrinsic one-body density
\rho_{p,n}^{\rm in}(q)
from
\rho_{\tau}(q)
in the laboratory frame.
Finally, the elastic charge form factor
\rho_{\text{ch}}(q)
is found by folding
\rho_{p,n}^{\rm in}(q)
with the individual proton and (small) neutron electric form factors,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{ch}}(q)=G_{p}(q)\rho_{p}^{\rm in}(q)+G_{n}(q)\rho_{n}^{\rm in}(q).

(30)

As in Ref.
[
58
]
, we use the Kelly’s parametrizations
[
59
]
for

The charge density can be determined through the inverse transform,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{ch}}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q\,q^{2}j_{0}(qr)\rho_{\text{ch}}(q).

(31)

III.2
The two-nucleon correlation functions

As demonstrated in Sect.
II
, the nucleon-nucleon correlation functions and, correspondingly, the two-body density matrix play a key role in describing the second-order pion-nucleus optical rescattering.
In this work, we determine the two-body densities for
48
Ca within the simplifying assumption of an HF wave function.
This approach offers a good balance between accuracy and simplicity.
As shown in Fig.
2
, the HF framework reasonably describes one-body form factors.
Therefore, we expect the error related to using the HF state instead of the CC one in the two-body densities to be relatively small.
At the same time, computations at the HF level are significantly less demanding than a CC calculation of two-body density matrices (see, e.g.,
[
53
,
21
,
61
]
).

Within the HF approach, the two-body density operators defined in Eq. (
11
) are evaluated on the reference Slater determinant state
\ket{\Phi_0}
.
As a consequence of Wick’s theorem, the two-body density matrix
\rho(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2};\tau_{1},\tau_{2};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
in this case can be expressed entirely in terms of the one-body density matrix, as shown in Appendix
B
.
As a result, the correlation functions that enter the second-order potential, namely
C_{(\text{ex})}
and
C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}

D_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}

\displaystyle D_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\tau_{1}}(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2})\rho_{\tau_{2}}(\bm{r}_{2},\bm{r}_{1}).

(32)

Explicitly, the relationships among these quantities are given by

\displaystyle C_{(\text{ex})}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=D_{np}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}),

(33a)

\displaystyle C_{pp,nn}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=D_{pp,nn}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}),

(33b)

\displaystyle C_{np}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=0,

(33c)

Upon applying a double Fourier transform analogous to Eq. (
12

in momentum space takes the form

\displaystyle D_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})=\sum_{L}(2L+1)D^{(L)}_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}(q_{1},q_{2})P_{L}(x),

(34)

where
x=\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{q}_{2}/(q_{1}q_{2})

Here, the partial-wave components are given by

0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}\\
\times\chi_{l}\chi_{l^{\prime}}Q^{(L)}_{n_{1}ln_{1}^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(q_{1})Q^{(L)}_{n_{2}ln_{2}^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(q_{2})\,\rho_{jl\tau_{1}}^{(n_{1}n_{2})}\rho_{j^{\prime}l^{\prime}\tau_{2}}^{(n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime})},

(35)

where
\begin{pmatrix}l&l^{\prime}&L\\

for the outermost shell, when only its subshell

V
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we have developed a second-order momentum-space potential for pion scattering on nuclei with non-zero isospin, extending the framework introduced in Ref.
[
17
]
.
Our approach provides a detailed treatment of intermediate charge-exchange processes in isospin-asymmetric nuclei by explicitly including
pp
,
nn
, and exchange
np
correlations.
These second-order effects are expressed through corresponding two-nucleon correlation functions entering the second-order part of the potential.
The nuclear structure inputs for the pion-
48
Ca potential were derived employing the state-of-the-art CC method for the proton and neutron one-body density and the HF approach for two-nucleon correlation functions.
By employing different chiral EFT nuclear interactions, we were able to estimate the sensitivity of the scattering model to variations in the microscopic nuclear structure input.
The pion-nucleon scattering amplitudes modified within the nuclear medium were adopted from the fit to
\pi^{\pm}
-
12
C scattering data presented in Ref.
[
17
]
.

The developed model successfully reproduces the differential cross sections for
\pi^{\pm}
-
48
Ca elastic scattering in the
\Delta(1232)
-resonance energy region, with only mild sensitivity to the choice of the
ab initio
nuclear interaction.
In particular, the correlation functions
C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2},(\text{ex})}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})
derived within the HF framework using the
\Delta\text{NNLO}_{\text{GO}}(394)
and
\text{NNLO}_{\text{sat}}(450)
interactions exhibit good agreement, indicating their low sensitivity to the specific choice of interaction.

The results obtained here for
48
Ca corroborate our earlier findings in Ref.
[
17
]
, confirming that the inclusion of the second-order part of the potential is essential for achieving a consistent description of pion-nucleus scattering across the
12
C-
48
Ca range in the
\Delta(1232)
resonance energy region.
As the impact of the second-order contribution increases at lower energies, we anticipate that the choice of nuclear interaction will become a more significant source of theoretical uncertainty in the low-energy regime.

As a next step, we plan to extend our analysis to low-energy pion scattering on calcium isotopes, aiming to refine the in-medium modifications of the isoscalar and isovector
s
-wave pion-nucleon amplitudes.
Given the universality of our approach to both nuclear pion scattering and photoproduction processes (see Ref.
[
18
]
), we expect the
C_{\tau_{1}\tau_{2},(\text{ex})}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})
correlation functions developed in this work to be directly applicable for computing second-order pion rescattering corrections in coherent pion photoproduction on
48
Ca in order to compare with forthcoming data
[
15
]
.
In future work, we also aim to investigate the sensitivity of coherent pion photoproduction and scattering observables to the neutron skin thickness of
48
Ca.

Finally, pion scattering and photoproduction on
40
Ar are of particular interest for modern long-baseline neutrino oscillation experiments
[
6
,
5
]
, where pion production is an important resonant process.
Motivated by this, we furthermore plan to apply our formalism to
40
Ar.
As a doubly open-shell nucleus,
40
Ar presents additional challenges, which can be efficiently addressed using the double-charge-exchange equation-of-motion technique
[
67
,
43
]
, or through the recently developed deformed coupled-cluster framework
[
68
]
.

Appendix A
One- and two-body density matrices

Density matrices offer a powerful framework for describing and analyzing many-body quantum mechanical systems, particularly atomic nuclei, covering the structural properties and correlations of nucleons within a nucleus
[
69
]
.
In this Section, we focus on one- and two-body density matrices of a system of
A
nucleons in a nucleus.
In coordinate space, the ground state of this system is described by a normalized antisymmetric
A
-body wave function
\Psi_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{A})
, where
x_{i}=\{\bm{r}_{i},s_{i},\tau_{i}\}
encompasses the position (
\bm{r}_{i}
), spin (
s_{i}
), and isospin (
\tau_{i}
) degrees of freedom of the
i
-th particle.
The one-body and two-body density matrices associated with the wave function
\Psi_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{A})
are defined in coordinate space as
4
4

4

Note that the factor
A(A-1)
in Eq. (

) is twice as large as that in another commonly used definition.

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x_{1};x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv A{\int}\left\{\prod\limits_{i=2}^{A}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{i}\right\}\Psi_{0}^{\dagger}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{A})\Psi_{0}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2},\ldots,x_{A}),

(38a)

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv A(A-1){\int}\left\{\prod_{i=3}^{A}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{i}\right\}\Psi_{0}^{\dagger}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{A})\Psi_{0}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3},\ldots,x_{A}),

respectively.

and

\int\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,\ldots=\sum_{s_{i}}\sum_{\tau_{i}}\int\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{i}\,\ldots.

Similarly, an arbitrary
n

A-n

The density matrix formalism allows for the straightforward derivation of the expectation values of any physical quantity
Q
associated with a many-body system.
In its most general form, the operator
\hat{Q}
includes all possible interactions, from single particles to all particles interacting simultaneously, which can be represented as

\hat{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{A}\hat{Q}^{(1)}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{A}\sum_{j\neq i}^{A}\hat{Q}^{(2)}_{ij}+\sum_{i=1}^{A}\sum_{j\neq i}^{A}\sum_{k\neq j}^{A}\hat{Q}^{(3)}_{ijk}+\cdots,

(40)

where
\hat{Q}^{(n)}_{i}
are operators acting on
n

The expectation value of
\hat{Q}
can be expressed in terms of the density matrices as follows

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{Q}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\bar{Q}_{1}+\bar{Q}_{2}+\ldots,

(41a)

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{1}=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{\prime}\,\rho_{1}(x_{1};x_{1}^{\prime})\left\langle x_{1}^{\prime}\left|\hat{Q}^{(1)}\right|x_{1}\right\rangle,

(41b)

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{2}=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{\prime}\,\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left\langle x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\left|\hat{Q}^{(2)}\right|x_{1},x_{2}\right\rangle,

(41c)

where the pattern continues similarly for higher-order terms involving more interacting particles.
Thus, the density matrices defined by Eqs. (

In this regard, they play a key role in deriving the potentials describing nuclear pion scattering and photoproduction.

As follows from their definitions, the density matrices are Hermitian

\begin{split}\rho_{1}(x;x^{\prime})&=\rho_{1}^{*}(x^{\prime};x),\\
\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})&=\rho_{2}^{*}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime};x_{1},x_{2}).\end{split}

(42)

They are related by the formula

\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2})=(A-1)\rho_{1}(x_{1};x_{1}^{\prime}).

(43)

The diagonal elements of the density matrices,

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\rho_{1}(x;x),

(44a)

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1},x_{2}),

(44b)

referred to as “densities” or “density distributions”, are of special importance because they represent the probability densities of finding particles in specific states and positions.
For an infinitesimal volume
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{1}

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{1}\rho_{1}(x_{1})/A
provides the probability of finding a nucleon with spin
s_{1}
and isospin
\tau_{1}
in the volume
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V_{1}
around the coordinate
\bm{r}_{1}
when other nucleons have arbitrary positions, spins, and isospins.
Similarly,
\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})/A(A-1)
gives the probability density for finding two nucleons with coordinates, spins, and isospins specified by
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.
The density distributions, Eqs. (
44

\rho_{2}
is symmetric in its coordinates:
\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho_{2}(x_{2},x_{1})
.
The normalization conditions for the densities are

\displaystyle\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\rho_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=A,

(45a)

\displaystyle\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=A(A-1).

(45b)

Since nucleons obey Fermi-Dirac statistics, the total wave function of the nuclear system,
\Psi_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{A})

This antisymmetry also applies to the density matrices

\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})=-\rho_{2}(x_{2},x_{1};x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})=-\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{2}^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime}).

The one-body density matrix in coordinate space can be generally defined as the expectation value of the density operator

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}(x,x^{\prime})=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\psi}(x^{\prime}).

(47)

Correspondingly, the non-diagonal one-body density operators for protons and neutrons take the form

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\tau}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{\sigma}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{r}\sigma\tau)\hat{\psi}(\bm{r}^{\prime}\sigma\tau).
