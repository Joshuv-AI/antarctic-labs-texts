Burn Rehabilitation Exercise Adherence and Its Influencing Factors: A Cross‐Sectional Study Based on Social Cognitive Theory
Authors: Yilin Fan, Qing Zhou, Chenyuan Liu, Yunyun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo investigate the level of functional exercise adherence in patients during burn rehabilitation and identify its key influencing factors based on social cognitive theory (SCT). A cross‐sectional study was conducted among 220 patients undergoing burn rehabilitation, selected from three tertiary hospitals using convenience sampling. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, a functional exercise adherence scale, an SCT‐related variables scale for patients with burns, the visual analogue scale, the Self‐Rating Anxiety Scale and the Self‐Rating Depression Scale. Descriptive statistics, group comparison analysis, correlation analysis and multiple linear regression analysis were performed to analyse the data. The total score of functional exercise adherence in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation was 32.68 ± 6.72 points, with 31.8% (70 cases) showing low adherence, 52.3% (115 cases) showing moderate adherence and 15.9% (35 cases) showing high adherence. Correlation analysis revealed that self‐efficacy (r = 0.623, p < 0.001), positive outcome expectation (r = 0.587, p < 0.001), social support (r = 0.514, p < 0.001) and behavioural self‐regulation (r = 0.492, p < 0.001) were positively correlated with adherence, whereas negative outcome expectation (r = −0.386, p < 0.001), pain score (r = −0.321, p < 0.001), anxiety score (r = −0.295, p < 0.001) and depression score (r = −0.278, p < 0.001) were negatively correlated. Multiple linear regression analysis showed that self‐efficacy, social support, behavioural self‐regulation, pain intensity and educational level were independent predictors of functional exercise adherence. Patients undergoing burn rehabilitation generally demonstrate moderate adherence to functional exercise, with room for improvement. Interventions that boost self‐efficacy, optimise social support, enhance behavioural self‐regulation and manage pain effectively can improve exercise adherence and foster better rehabilitation outcomes.Keywords: adherence interventions, cross‐sectional study, psychological theory, rehabilitation

Introduction

Burn injuries often result in severe damage to skin tissue, leading to scar contracture, joint dysfunction and decreased quality of life without standardised rehabilitation management [1]. Functional exercise is a core component of burn rehabilitation, as it helps improve joint mobility, reduce scar hypertrophy, enhance muscle strength and promote the recovery of daily living abilities [2]. However, epidemiological studies have shown that patients with burns generally exhibit low adherence to functional exercise during rehabilitation, with reported adherence rates ranging from 30% to 50% globally in this patient population [3, 4]. Insufficient adherence can prolong the rehabilitation cycle, increase the risk of complications such as joint stiffness and chronic pain and ultimately affect the overall rehabilitation outcome and patients' quality of life [3, 4]. Therefore, exploring the current status of exercise adherence and its influencing factors in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation is crucial for developing targeted intervention strategies.

Social cognitive theory (SCT), proposed by Albert Bandura, emphasises the interactive effects of individual cognitive factors, behavioural factors and environmental factors on human behaviour [5]. The core constructs of SCT, including self‐efficacy, outcome expectation, social support and behavioural self‐regulation, have been widely used to explain and predict health‐related behaviours [6, 7, 8]. In the field of rehabilitation medicine, SCT has been successfully applied to improve exercise adherence in patients with chronic diseases such as breast cancer, coronary heart disease and stroke [6, 7, 8].

Existing studies on burn rehabilitation exercise adherence have identified various influencing factors, including demographic characteristics (age, gender, educational level), clinical features (burn area, burn site, rehabilitation duration), psychological factors (anxiety, depression) and pain intensity [9]. However, most of these studies lack a systematic theoretical framework, leading to fragmented results that fail to comprehensively explain the mechanisms underlying adherence behaviour. Additionally, few studies have explored the role of SCT‐related cognitive and environmental factors in exercise adherence among patients with burns, limiting the development of evidence‐based intervention strategies [9].

The objective of this study is to investigate the level of functional exercise adherence in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation and to identify its key influencing factors based on SCT, thereby providing a scientific basis for clinical practice to improve adherence. Specific research questions include the following: (1) What is the overall level of functional exercise adherence in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation? (2) What are the correlations between SCT constructs (self‐efficacy, outcome expectation, social support, behavioural self‐regulation, etc.) and exercise adherence? (3) Are demographic characteristics (age, gender, educational level, etc.) and clinical features (burn area, burn site, rehabilitation duration, etc.) associated with exercise adherence? (4) Which factors are independent predictors of functional exercise adherence in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation?

Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study design was adopted.

Study Participants

Study Setting

The study was conducted in the burn rehabilitation departments of three tertiary hospitals between June 2022 and June 2025.

Sample Size Estimation

Using multiple linear regression analysis for sample size calculation, with the number of independent variables set at 15 (combining demographic, clinical and SCT‐related variables), a sample size of 150–225 cases was estimated based on the principle of 10–15 cases per independent variable. Considering a 10% attrition rate, the final sample size was determined to be 220 cases. To verify the adequacy of the sample size for detecting the effect sizes of the final regression model, formal power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7. (Heinrich‐Heine‐Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). For multiple linear regression with five independent predictors (self‐efficacy, social support, behavioural self‐regulation, pain intensity, educational level) in the final model, setting alpha (α) = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80 and an anticipated effect size of f 2 = 0.15 (medium effect size according to Cohen's criteria), the required minimum sample size was 138 cases. Our actual sample size of 220 cases exceeds this requirement, indicating sufficient statistical power to detect the hypothesised effect sizes.

Inclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients (1) with a confirmed diagnosis of burn injury; (2) aged ≥ 18 years; (3) in the rehabilitation phase (wounds 95% healed, able to perform active/passive functional exercises); (4) who were conscious, with a cognitive function score ≥ 24 points on the Mini‐Mental State Examination and with the ability to communicate verbally or in writing without obvious impairment and complete questionnaires independently or with assistance; and (5) voluntarily participating in the study and signing the informed consent form.

Exclusion Criteria

The exclusion criteria included patients (1) complicated with severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, liver and kidney dysfunction or other serious organic diseases; (2) diagnosed with mental disorders such as schizophrenia or severe cognitive impairment; (3) unable to perform functional exercises due to severe joint contracture or other reasons; and (4) refusal to participate or inability to complete the follow‐up and data collection.

Data Collection Tools

General Information Questionnaire

The questionnaire was self‐designed and included two parts: (1) demographic characteristics (age, gender, educational level, marital status, occupation, monthly household income); and (2) clinical characteristics, including burn area (total body surface area), burn site (single site/multiple sites), burn depth (superficial partial‐thickness/deep partial‐thickness/full‐thickness), cause of injury, duration of rehabilitation and whether receiving adherence guidance (consisting of standardised exercise training, regular follow‐up and feedback, and one‐on‐one professional guidance from rehabilitation therapists).

Functional Exercise Adherence Scale

A self‐designed scale was used, which has been validated for reliability and validity (Supporting Information 1). The scale includes 10 items across 5 dimensions: exercise frequency adherence (two items), exercise duration adherence (two items), exercise intensity adherence (two items), exercise accuracy adherence (two items) and autonomous exercise adherence (two items). Each item is scored on a five‐point Likert scale (1 = completely unable to do, 5 = completely able to do), with a total score ranging from 10 to 50 points. Adherence levels are classified as follows: low adherence (10–25 points), moderate adherence (26–39 points) and high adherence (40–50 points). In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient of the scale was 0.863, and the content validity index (CVI) was 0.892.

Additional psychometric analyses were conducted as follows. (1) Construct validity: Exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation extracted five factors (consistent with the theoretical dimensions), explaining 68.3% of the total variance; confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed good model fit (reduced chi‐squared statistic [χ 2/df] = 1.87, root mean square error of approximation [RMSEA] = 0.062, comparative fit index [CFI] = 0.945 and Tucker–Lewis index [TFI] = 0.928). (2) Criterion validity: The scale score was positively correlated with the 6‐min walk test (r = 0.428, p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with the Modified Oswestry Disability Index (r = −0.396, p < 0.001). (3) Test–retest reliability: In a pilot sample of 30 patients, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.876 (95% CI: 0.765–0.938) after a 2‐week interval.

SCT‐Related Variables Scale for Patients With Burns

A self‐designed scale based on the SCT framework was used (Supporting Information 2), including 20 items across 5 dimensions. The SCT items, originally developed for the general population to assess social cognitive constructs in health behaviours, were adapted and interpreted specifically for exercise adherence in burn rehabilitation by refining item wording to align with burn rehabilitation exercise scenarios, validating the scale in a pilot sample of patients with burns and linking each construct to the behavioural performance of functional exercise adherence (e.g., self‐efficacy items focus on confidence in completing burn rehabilitation exercise tasks).

Each item is scored on a five‐point Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 20 to 100 points. Higher scores indicate a more favourable social cognitive status for exercise adherence. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient of the total scale was 0.887 and those of each dimension ranged from 0.765 to 0.853. The CVI was 0.905. (3) Test–retest reliability: In the pilot sample of 30 patients, the ICC for the total scale was 0.892 (95% CI: 0.791–0.946) at 2‐week follow‐up.

Pain Assessment

The visual analogue scale (VAS) was used to assess pain intensity. The VAS was completed by patients immediately after they finished the SCT‐related variables scale and before completing the psychological scales (Self‐Rating Anxiety Scale [SAS] and Self‐Rating Depression Scale [SDS]) during a single face‐to‐face questionnaire administration session. Patients were asked to rate their pain on a 10‐cm scale, where 0 cm indicates no pain and 10 cm indicates the most severe pain. Scores were classified as mild pain (0–3 cm), moderate pain (4–6 cm), and severe pain (7–10 cm) [10].

Psychological Assessment

The SAS [11] is a 20‐item scale widely used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms in adults. Each item is scored on a four‐point Likert scale (1 = none or a little of the time, 2 = some of the time, 3 = a good part of the time, 4 = most or all of the time). The standard score is calculated by multiplying the total raw score by 1.25, with a standard score ≥ 50 indicating the presence of anxiety symptoms (mild: 50–59, moderate: 60–69, severe: ≥ 70) [11]. The Cronbach's α coefficient of the SAS in this study was 0.836.

The SDS [12] is a 20‐item scale used to evaluate the severity of depression symptoms. The scoring method is consistent with the SAS, and a standard score ≥ 53 indicates the presence of depression symptoms (mild: 53–62, moderate: 63–72, severe: ≥ 73). The Cronbach's α coefficient of the SDS in this study was 0.852.

Anxiety and depression are common psychological problems in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation, which can directly affect their motivation and persistence in functional exercise [13]. The SAS and SDS are internationally recognised, reliable and valid scales with simple operation and high sensitivity, suitable for rapid screening and severity assessment of anxiety and depression in clinical and research settings. These scales have been widely validated in Chinese populations, ensuring the accuracy and comparability of the data [11, 12]. Their application in this study is essential for comprehensively exploring the impact of psychological factors on exercise adherence and for providing a basis for targeted psychological interventions.

Data Collection Procedures

Before data collection, investigators received unified training on the study protocol, questionnaire content and interview skills. Eligible patients were approached by investigators, who explained the purpose, procedures and confidentiality of the study. After obtaining written informed consent, patients completed the questionnaires independently. A total of 42 patients (19.1%) required assistance from investigators to complete the surveys due to low literacy or physical limitations; investigators read the questionnaire items aloud and recorded the responses based on the patients' statements. The data collection process took approximately 20–30 min per patient.

Questionnaires with more than 10% missing items were excluded. For questionnaires with less than 10% missing items, the mean value of the corresponding dimension was used for imputation. The rationale for this imputation method is that when the missing rate is ≤ 10%, the dimension mean can reasonably approximate the true value of the missing item without significantly distorting the data distribution. The proportion of missing data for each key variable in this study was as follows: general information questionnaire (0.9%), functional exercise adherence scale (1.2%), SCT‐related variables scale (1.5%), VAS (0.7%), SAS (1.1%) and SDS (1.3%). All missing rates are below 2%, indicating good data completeness. A total of 235 questionnaires were distributed, and 220 were completed, yielding an effective response rate of 93.6%.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Prior to descriptive and inferential statistics, normality testing of continuous variables (including functional exercise adherence score, SCT‐related variables scores, pain score, anxiety score, depression score, age and rehabilitation duration) was conducted. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for all continuous variables (sample size ≥ 50), with a significance level of p > 0.05 indicating a normal distribution. For descriptive statistics, measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on normality. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

A group comparison analysis was conducted to compare adherence scores across groups. Specific methods included independent samples t‐tests, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis H tests, based on data characteristics. Specifically, the independent samples t‐test was used for comparisons between two independent groups with normally distributed data and homogeneous variance; one‐way ANOVA followed by a Bonferroni post hoc test was used for comparisons among three or more independent groups with normally distributed data and homogeneous variance, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test (for multiple groups) or Mann–Whitney U test (for two groups) was used for non‐normally distributed data or data with heterogeneous variance.

Correlation analysis was used to examine relationships among SCT constructs, pain, psychological status and exercise adherence. Pearson correlation analysis was used for normally distributed continuous variables, and Spearman correlation analysis was used for non‐normally distributed continuous variables or ordinal variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was applied to identify independent predictors of exercise adherence. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate group comparisons and correlation analyses (unadjusted results) were included in the regression model. The entry criterion for variables was set at α = 0.05, and the elimination criterion was α = 0.10. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 using a two‐tailed test.

To verify the robustness of the results, two sensitivity analyses were performed. (1) Missing data were replaced using multiple imputation (five imputations) and the multiple linear regression analysis was repeated. The results showed that the direction and significance of the regression coefficients of the independent predictors were consistent with the original analysis (self‐efficacy: β = 0.308, p < 0.001; social support: β = 0.239, p < 0.001; behavioural self‐regulation: β = 0.182, p = 0.002; pain score: β = −0.161, p = 0.002; educational level: β = 0.151, p < 0.001). (2) Patients with any missing data were excluded (n = 18) and the regression analysis was re‐conducted. The results remained stable, confirming that the original findings were not affected by the imputation method used for missing data.

Pilot Study

A pilot study was conducted with 30 patients undergoing burn rehabilitation (independent of the main study sample) between January and March 2022 to test the feasibility and psychometric properties of the self‐designed scales. The results of the pilot study showed that, in terms of feasibility, the completion rate for the questionnaires was 100%, and the average completion time was 25 min, with no unclear or difficult‐to‐answer items. Regarding psychometric properties, for the functional exercise adherence scale, the Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.847, and the test–retest ICC was 0.876; for the SCT‐related variables scale, the Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.869, and the test–retest ICC was 0.892. Based on the pilot study results, minor adjustments were made to the wording of two items in the SCT‐related variables scale to improve clarity, and the formal scales were finalised.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: XXX, Approval Date: 27 March 2024). All patients provided written informed consent before participating in the study. The confidentiality of patient information was strictly protected, and all data were used only for research purposes.

Discussion

Current Status of Functional Exercise Adherence in Patients Undergoing Burn Rehabilitation

This study found that the average functional exercise adherence score in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation was 32.68 ± 6.72 points, with 52.3% of patients exhibiting moderate adherence and only 15.9% exhibiting high adherence. This indicates that the overall level of exercise adherence among patients undergoing burn rehabilitation is moderate, consistent with results from previous studies on exercise adherence in burn rehabilitation [3, 4, 13]. Among the five dimensions of adherence, autonomous exercise adherence and exercise frequency adherence had relatively higher scores, whereas exercise accuracy adherence and exercise intensity adherence had lower scores. This may be because patients have a certain awareness of the importance of exercise frequency but lack professional guidance on exercise methods and intensity, leading to insufficient accuracy and inappropriate intensity during exercise.

Compared with similar studies, the adherence level in this study is higher [13, 14], which may be related to the fact that 70.9% of the patients received adherence guidance. This suggests that targeted health education and guidance can help improve patients' exercise adherence. However, the proportion of patients with high adherence remains low, indicating significant room for improvement in clinical practice.

Analysis of Influencing Factors Based on SCT

Self‐Efficacy

Self‐efficacy was the strongest predictor of exercise adherence, explaining the largest proportion of variance. Patients with higher self‐efficacy are more likely to believe in their ability to overcome difficulties such as pain and fatigue during exercise and to persist in completing the exercise plan [15]. This is consistent with the core viewpoint of SCT, which holds that self‐efficacy is the key factor affecting behavioural initiation and persistence [16]. In burn rehabilitation, patients often face challenges such as scar contracture and joint pain, which can easily reduce their confidence in exercise. Therefore, enhancing self‐efficacy through methods such as successful experience sharing, positive feedback, and skill training is crucial for improving exercise adherence.

Social Support

Social support was another important predictor of exercise adherence. Family support, medical staff support and peer support can provide patients with emotional encouragement, practical help and information guidance, thereby enhancing their motivation to exercise [17]. For example, family members can remind patients to exercise on time and assist with home‐based exercises, medical staff can provide professional guidance and adjust exercise plans in a timely manner, and peers can share rehabilitation experiences and provide psychological support [18]. This is consistent with the SCT view that environmental factors play an important role in regulating behaviour [19]. Therefore, building a comprehensive social support system for patients undergoing burn rehabilitation is an effective way to improve exercise adherence.

Behavioural Self‐Regulation

Behavioural self‐regulation, including goal setting, exercise monitoring and barrier coping, was also an important predictor of adherence [20]. Patients with strong behavioural self‐regulation abilities can set clear exercise goals, monitor their exercise progress and take effective measures to cope with difficulties encountered during exercise [21]. For example, patients who set specific, achievable short‐term goals are more likely to maintain exercise motivation; those who record their exercise behaviour can better understand their progress and adjust their behaviour accordingly. This indicates that strengthening patients' behavioural self‐regulation skills through training can help improve their exercise adherence.

Pain Intensity

Pain intensity was negatively correlated with exercise adherence, which is consistent with previous studies. Severe pain can directly affect patients' willingness to exercise, leading to reduced exercise frequency and intensity. Patients with burns often experience persistent pain during rehabilitation, which is a major barrier to exercise adherence [22]. Therefore, effective pain management is an important part of improving exercise adherence. Clinical staff should assess patients' pain levels regularly and adopt comprehensive pain relief measures, such as medication, physical therapy and psychological intervention, to reduce the impact of pain on exercise.

Educational Level

Patients with higher educational levels had better exercise adherence, which may be because they have stronger learning abilities and can better understand the importance of functional exercise and master correct exercise methods [23]. In addition, patients with higher educational levels are more likely to obtain health information through various channels and have a more positive attitude towards rehabilitation [24]. Therefore, for patients with lower educational levels, clinical staff should use simpler, more understandable language and methods to deliver health education and exercise guidance.

Comparison With Existing Studies

The results of this study are consistent with most previous studies on the factors influencing exercise adherence. For example, studies of patients undergoing stroke and orthopaedic rehabilitation have also found that self‐efficacy and social support are important predictors of exercise adherence [25]. However, this study further confirmed the applicability of SCT in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation, systematically exploring the role of multiple SCT constructs in exercise adherence.

Compared with previous studies on patients with burns, this study added the assessment of behavioural self‐regulation and environmental factors, making the analysis of influencing factors more comprehensive. In addition, this study found that educational level is an independent predictor of adherence, which is not emphasised in some previous studies. This may be related to differences in the study population's demographic characteristics.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

Theoretical Implications

This study applied SCT to explore the factors influencing functional exercise adherence in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation, thereby enriching the theoretical basis for the study of burn rehabilitation behaviour. The results confirm that SCT can effectively explain the formation and maintenance of exercise adherence in patients with burns, providing a theoretical framework for developing targeted intervention strategies. In addition, this study validated the reliability and validity of the self‐designed SCT‐related variables scale and functional exercise adherence scale, providing measurement tools for future related studies.

Practical Implications

Based on the research results, the following practical suggestions are proposed. First, patients' self‐efficacy should be enhanced by sharing successful rehabilitation cases and giving positive feedback to build their confidence in exercise. Personalised exercise training should also be provided to patients to help them feel a sense of achievement throughout the process [26]. Second, social support can be optimised through strengthening health education for family members to improve their ability to assist patients with exercise. Peer support groups should also be established to promote experience sharing and emotional communication among patients, and medical staff's professional competence should be improved to provide continuous guidance and support. Third, behavioural self‐regulation should be strengthened by guiding patients to set exercise goals following the SMART principle (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time‐bound) and encouraging them to keep exercise logs to monitor progress. Patients should also be trained to cope with exercise‐related barriers such as pain and fatigue. Fourth, pain management should be improved through regular pain assessments and comprehensive pain relief measures. Patients should be guided to use relaxation techniques (e.g., deep breathing, meditation) to alleviate pain. Fifth, targeted health education should be delivered. For patients with lower educational levels, simple, intuitive methods (such as pictures and videos) should be used to explain exercise knowledge and skills, with repeated guidance and training to ensure they master correct exercise techniques.

Study Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study has some limitations. First, the study used a cross‐sectional design, which cannot establish a causal relationship between influencing factors and exercise adherence; all results are reported as associations or predictive relationships rather than causal effects. Second, the sample was selected using convenience sampling from three tertiary hospitals in a single region, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the broader applicability of the findings. Regional rehabilitation protocols may vary in terms of exercise guidance intensity, professional training of rehabilitation staff and resource allocation for burn rehabilitation, which may lead to differences in exercise adherence levels and the strength of associations between influencing factors and adherence across different regions. The findings of this study may be more applicable to tertiary hospitals with standardised rehabilitation protocols and sufficient professional resources. Third, the study assessed subjective adherence only through questionnaires, which are susceptible to social desirability bias, and did not include objective indicators such as exercise duration and frequency. Fourth, the study did not explore the interactions among the various influencing factors. Fifth, the design of the self‐reported questionnaires lacked direct consumer (patient) input. The language accessibility of the scales could be improved to better suit laypersons with low educational levels or limited burn rehabilitation knowledge, which may improve the accuracy of questionnaire responses and reduce response bias.

Future research directions include the following. Adopting a longitudinal design to track the changes in exercise adherence and its influencing factors over time to explore potential causal relationships. Expanding the sample size and selecting samples from different hospital levels (primary, secondary, tertiary) and regions to improve the generalisability of the results and to explore the impact of regional rehabilitation protocols on exercise adherence. Integrating objective metrics such as wearable motion sensors to assess exercise duration, frequency and intensity, combined with subjective questionnaires, to evaluate exercise adherence more accurately. Exploring the interactions among various factors using structural equation modelling. Developing and validating intervention programmes based on SCT to improve exercise adherence in patients undergoing burn rehabilitation and involving these patients in the revision and optimisation of questionnaire wording to improve the accessibility and validity of the assessment tools.

Conclusion

The functional exercise adherence of patients undergoing burn rehabilitation is generally moderate, with self‐efficacy, social support, behavioural self‐regulation, pain intensity and educational level functioning as independent predictors. Clinical practice should focus on these influencing factors, apply SCT to develop targeted intervention strategies and continuously improve patients' exercise adherence to promote better rehabilitation outcomes.
