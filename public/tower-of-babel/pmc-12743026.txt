Association between adjuvant chemotherapy and risk of incisional hernia after colorectal cancer resection – a single-centre cohort study
Authors: Maja Christine Rasmussen, Niklas Nygaard Baastrup, Anders Kierkegaard Gundestrup, Charlotte Tiffanie Bendtz Kanstrup, Jakob Kleif, Claus Anders Bertelsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe study aimed to determine whether adjuvant chemotherapy after colorectal cancer resection is associated with an increased risk of developing incisional hernia (IH).MethodsAll patients undergoing elective curative resection of stage I-III colon adenocarcinoma between 2014 and 2017 at Copenhagen University Hospital – North Zealand were registered in a local database prospectively. This study collected additional data from patients’ electronic health records (EHR) on chemotherapy and incision placement. The follow-up computed tomography scans were retrospectively reassessed by two trained surgeons for incisional hernia for up to five years. Patients were stratified into two groups: those receiving adjuvant chemotherapy (study group) and those not receiving it (control group). The primary outcome was CT-verified IH.ResultsOf 451 patients, 303 were included in the final analysis, with 108 in the chemotherapy group and 195 in the control group. The study found that the unadjusted risk of IH was 28.9% (95% CI: 20.4–37.4) in the chemotherapy group and 21.1% (95% CI: 15.4–26.9) in the control group with an absolute risk difference of 7.7% (95% CI: -2.3–17.9, p = 0.13). After inverse probability of treatment weighting, the absolute risk difference was 5.6% (95% CI: -4.2–15.6), p = 0.26).ConclusionsAdjuvant chemotherapy after colon cancer resection does not seem to be associated with a significantly higher risk of IH.Trial registrationDanish Patient Safety Authority (#31-1522-51).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03913-3.Keywords: Incisional hernia, Adjuvant chemotherapy, Colon cancer resection, Computed tomography

Introduction

Incisional hernia (IH) is a common long-term complication after intraabdominal surgery. The reported incidence of IH ranges from a few percent to 27% [1–3]. Studies vary in their reported incidence due to differences in outcome measures, such as reoperation, clinical assessment, or computerised tomography (CT). CT scans are the most sensitive diagnostic method, with reported IH rates as high as 35%. Even after minimally invasive surgery CT verified IH rates remain high at 25% [4, 5]. In addition to the potential risk of strangulation requiring emergency surgery, IH after colon cancer resections is associated with a reduced quality of life for patients due to cosmetic concerns, chronic pain, and restricted physical performance [6].

Smoking, obesity, diabetes, and advancing age are known risk factors for both IH and recurrence after hernia repair [1, 7–9]. Furthermore, the risk of developing an IH is associated with the surgical intervention, e.g., the approach (open vs. laparoscopic), size and orientation of the incision, grade of wound contamination, suture material used for fascial closure, and surgical site infection [3, 10, 11].

Incision site heal postoperatively through the remodelling of the extracellular matrix to regain strength. The initially produced collagen fibres are predominantly type III, with fewer cross-links, and are later remodelled into type I, which has more and stronger cross-linking. Consequently, the risk of IH is higher during the first postoperative year [12]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been shown to increase the risk of IH and impair wound healing [7, 10]. Adjuvant chemotherapy is usually initiated within 3–5 weeks of surgery for stage III colon cancer and typically administered over 3–6 months. Thus, adjuvant chemotherapy may negatively affect wound healing during a period with low tensile strength of the extracellular matrix and decreasing strength of the sutures.

To our knowledge, only a single-centre study has investigated the association between adjuvant chemotherapy and the risk of IH [13]. While it demonstrated an increased risk, the study was limited by non-standardised abdominal closure. For almost two decades, a running, slow-absorbable suturing technique with a suture material to fascial incision length ratio of at least 4:1 has been the standard fascial closure technique, aligning with the current recommendations of the European Hernia Society [14].

We hypothesised an association between adjuvant chemotherapy and an increased risk of IH after colon cancer resection. This study, therefore, aimed to investigate this association in a cohort that used the internationally recommended standardised closure technique.

Material & methods

This study was designed as a single-centre cohort study with retrospective assessment of prospectively performed follow-up CT scans, reported according to the STROBE statement [15].

Design

Data were drawn from the Copenhagen cOmplete Mesocolic Excision Study (COMES) database, which includes patients undergoing colon cancer resection at Copenhagen University Hospital – North Zealand (NOH). The databse contains data on all patients with colon adenocarcinoma stage I–III undergoing elective curative-intended resection during the period 2008–2017 in the Capital Region of Denmark [16–18]. Data on preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables were prospectively registered from the electronic health records (EHR). For those undergoing resection at NOH, data on the type and location of the incision, the suture used for fascial closure, as well as information regarding the adjuvant chemotherapy drugs and the number of treatment cycles administered, were already in the database. Data unavailable in the database, such as those regarding adjuvant chemotherapy, prior surgery, and patient characteristics, were obtained from the EHR. All patients were offered a standardised oncological follow-up, including an annual chest and abdominal CT scan for five years. All available follow-up CT scans for all included patients were reassessed for IH by two independent reviewers using the regional picture archiving and communication system from January 2022 to July 2023.

Clinical assessment for IH was performed at the annual follow-up visits for a limited number of patients; the remaining patients were only clinically assessed if IH was suspected based on symptoms or physical examination findings.

Population

This study included all patients who underwent curative-intended elective resection for stage I–III colon adenocarcinoma at NOH between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2017. A poly-4-hydroxybutyrate suture (Monomax 0, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) was used for fascial closure as standardised during the study period. Exclusion criteria were: preoperative ostomy, reuse of the incision from previous abdominal surgery, or surgical site occurrences, such as fascial dehiscence or wound infection. Patients receiving chemotherapy, including neoadjuvant, or abdominal radiotherapy before index surgery were also excluded, as those undergoing reoperation through the incision before initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy or within 60 days of index surgery. The patients were stratified as those receiving adjuvant chemotherapy (study group) and those not receiving adjuvant chemotherapy (control group).

The Regional Data Protection Agency (HIH-2013-031 I-02468) and the Danish Patient Safety Authority (31–1522-51) approved data collection. All study data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [19] hosted at the Capital Region of Denmark’s servers. Local Ethics Committee approval was not required under Danish legislation.

Intervention and outcomes

The study intervention was adjuvant chemotherapy after index surgery. Adjuvant chemotherapy is chemotherapy administered after primary surgery to decrease the chance of recurrence. It is typically administered within the first 3–5 weeks after primary surgery. Drugs used are 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) alone or in combination with Oxaliplatin. This study does not distinguish patients by duration or drug used; if they received at least one series of adjuvant chemotherapy, they were placed in the study group. Patients who refused adjuvant chemotherapy or were not offered, were stratified to the control group.

The primary outcome was IH at the incision site, i.e., the site of bowel extraction in laparoscopic resections, stratified by adjuvant chemotherapy. Port-site hernia was not considered an event.

Two independent reviewers reassessed the follow-up CT scans. Reviewer 1 (MCR) received training in assessing CT for IH before the study, while Reviewer 2 (NNB) had experience from a prior study [9]. Reviewer 1 was not blinded regarding adjuvant chemotherapy, whereas Reviewer 2 was blinded, as he had no access to the information on stratification. In cases of disagreement, the final decision was made by consensus between the two reviewers.

An IH was defined as an abdominal fascial gap in the area of the scar from the bowel extraction site. Fascial gaps found near the port sites were not considered an event because their size and location were neither recorded prospectively nor marked for the CT follow-up scans. Clinically diagnosed IH (via physical examination or surgery) was also registered. The secondary outcome was reoperation for IH.

Secondary outcomes were later abdominal surgery, defined as any incision involving the abdominal fascia 60 days or later after index surgery, and death before recurrence.

Measures

Data from the COMES database regarding the surgical procedure and postoperative complications, including the extensive audit, have been described previously [20, 21]. Data on incision type and IH found on CT scans were added. Standard incisions, used at the surgeon’s discretion, were as follows: for open resections, midline, Pfannenstiel, or supraumbilical right-sided transverse incisions were used; the latter included the right rectus muscle and, in some cases, a portion of the left rectus muscle. For laparoscopic resections, standard incisions included Pfannenstiel or supraumbilical transverse incisions. The latter either spared the rectus muscles or included only minor portions of the right or left rectus muscle. Midline incisions were used in few resections. The abdominal wall location and date of first detection were recorded for each IH identified on CT scans or during physical examination. Data in the database were validated during this process by checking certain datapoints twice throughout the collection period and after the database’s initial setup. Additionally, a data validation check was performed on randomly chosen patients.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical data as frequencies and proportions (%). The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous data, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical data.

Time-to-event analyses were performed as competing risk analyses, obtaining the cumulative incidence for IH after five years, with death and laparotomy being competing risks to IH. Cumulative incidence curves and survival curves are presented using unadjusted data.

Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) was used to obtain an unbiased estimate of marginal or population-averaged treatment effects in this observational and non-randomised study [22]. IPTW uses the propensity score to weigh each patient’s data based on the inverse probability of receiving the intervention, i.e., adjuvant chemotherapy. IPTW provides unbiased estimates of average treatment effect in time-to-event analyses if no difference in observed baseline covariates exists between the treatment groups [23]. By using IPTW, the results could emulate those of a randomised trial. The following clinically relevant covariates were balanced between the two groups using stabilised weights: age, sex, BMI, ASA score (ASA III and IV grouped), diabetes, smoking status, incision type, and surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. open, including converted).

Balance of the above covariates, two-way interactions, and squared terms of continuous covariates were assessed using absolute mean differences between the adjuvant chemotherapy group and the control group after IPTW using the cobalt R package. Absolute differences in means (using standardised mean difference) and proportions (using raw mean difference) less than 0.1 and variance ratios between 0.5 and 2 were deemed to represent negligible imbalances. Graphical inspection of the distribution of covariates after IPTW was performed. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 [24]. Patients with missing values for the covariates to be balanced were excluded from analyses. Otherwise, all available data were used.

All included patients underwent one or more follow-up CT scans, with the exception of nine patients, one of whom was in the adjuvant chemotherapy group. The median follow-up was 5.5 years (IQR, 4.6–6.5) in the adjuvant chemotherapy group and 5.5 years (IQR, 4.3–6.8) in the control group (p = 0.86). The median time from surgery to initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy in the study group was 28 days (IQR, 23–33).

Thirty-one patients in the adjuvant chemotherapy group developed an IH versus 41 patients in the control group. The majority of IHs were diagnosed by CT in 188 (96.4%) and 105 (97.2%) patients, respectively. Most IHs were diagnosed during the first postoperative year (Fig. 2). Two patients in each group, 1.9% of 108 versus 1.0% of 195 (p = 0.62), respectively, underwent IH repair during the follow-up period. The median size of the IHs measured by CT was 4 cm2 (IQR, 2–8).

The unadjusted competing risk analyses of the 5-year cumulative incidence of incisional hernia after colon cancer resection, stratified by adjuvant chemotherapy

A Love plot showing the baseline covariate balance before and after adjustment with IPTW is presented in Fig. 3. Unadjusted analyses revealed a 28.9% (95% CI: 20.4–37.4) absolute risk of IH in the study group compared with 21.1% (95% CI: 15.4–26.9) in the control group. The unadjusted absolute risk difference was 7.7% (95% CI: −2.3–17.9, p = 0.13). After IPTW, the risk of IH was 27.1% (95% CI: 18.6–34.4) and 21.5% (95% CI: 15.6–27.4), respectively, with an absolute risk difference of 5.6% (95% CI: −4.2–15.6, p = 0.26) in favour of the control group. When considering death and reoperation as competing risks for IH, no significant differences were observed between the two groups (Table 3).

Discussion

Despite observing a slightly higher incidence of IH after adjuvant chemotherapy (AC) following colon cancer resection, this study found no statistically significant difference in IH risk between chemotherapy recipients and the control group in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses.

Clinical implications

Colon cancer is the fourth most common cancer globally, with over a million cases annually [25]. The overall 5-year survival rate for all stages of colon cancer has risen significantly, from 49% in 1995 to 65% in 2014, and this trend continues [26]. As a result, more patients are living with late complications, including IH. Considering that quality of life (QoL) is a crucial patient-related outcome, preventive measures for IH should be seriously considered. IH negatively impacts QoL across various domains, including global health, as well as physical, role, emotional, and social functioning [6]. Furthermore, IH is significantly associated with increased symptoms such as chronic pain, dyspnoea, and insomnia [6].

Slow-absorbable synthetic sutures of polyglyconate, polydioxanone, or poly-4-hydroxybutyrate are designed to support fascial closure long enough to achieve complete wound healing without IH formation. Polyglyconate retains 59% of its tensile strength after four weeks and 30% after six weeks [27]. For polydioxanone, maintains 50% after four weeks and 25% after six weeks [27]. Poly-4-hydroxybutyrate, notably, retains over 50% of its strength after 12 weeks [28]. These slow-absorbable sutures have been shown to reduce the risk of IH compared to fast-absorbable sutures [29], a finding that aligns with the European Hernia Society’s recommendation for using slow-absorbable sutures for closure [14]. This study is the first to investigate the risk of IH after AC in a cohort undergoing colon cancer resection using the gold standard fascial closure technique [30] with a slow-absorbable poly-4-hydroxybutyrate suture.

Complications of IH can vary, with incarceration being the most severe. The reported prevalence of incarceration is considered low, ranging from 1.7% to 3.7% in the literature [31, 32]. While hernia repair is the solution to prevent incarceration, not all patients are eligible for surgery or decline it; instead opting for non-operative management. A retrospective study of 23,022 patients in the United States undergoing non-operative management demonstrated a prevalence of incarcerations of 2.3% [33].

The risk of complications, such as incarceration, is significantly higher in IHs with wider defects compared to those with smaller defects [32]. In the present study, only 6% of patients with IH underwent IH repair, a stark contrast to the 72.6% observed in a prior Danish study conducted before and during the implementation of minimally invasive colon surgery [6]. This notable difference might be attributed to minimally invasive surgery leading to smaller, often asymptomatic defects, alongside a general shift towards non-operative management. While there appears to be no significant difference in chronic pain experienced from IH following colon cancer resection, regardless of whether repair was performed, several domains of QoL were higher in those cases where repair was undertaken [6]. Chemotherapy has been shown to increase the risk of IH [7, 10]. While prior studies have primarily focused on neoadjuvant chemotherapy, both this study and one from the United States [13] indicate similar tendencies regarding IH risk. Given that chemotherapy is a vital part of colon cancer treatment, its necessity is not subject to reconsideration. The indication for AC in most cases is stage III disease, i.e. mesocolic lymph node metastases. Preoperatively or intraoperatively CT staging of colon cancer is associated with low sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value [34]. Consequently, the indication for AC is not clear until after histopathological assessment.

The necessity and relevance of preventive measures for IH are key questions. Both suture and mesh repair of IH carry risks, with chronic pain rates consistently ranging from 16% to 20% [35, 36]. Prophylactic on-lay mesh may be an option, but this is associated with a potentially increased risk of surgical site infection and the same late effects as IH repair. The shift from open to minimally invasive colorectal surgery has reduced the risk and size of IH. However, to our knowledge, patient-related outcomes and quality of life associated with IH after laparoscopic colorectal resections remain undescribed. The large difference in the proportions of patients undergoing IH repair in the present study and the older population-based study indicates that prophylactic on-lay mesh might be an overtreatment, not benefiting patients.

Other factors, such as smoking, diabetes, and visceral obesity, have demonstrated a much higher influence on the prevalence of IH [1, 9]. While smoking cessation may be a relevant intervention, the limited timeframe before cancer surgery likely restricts the impact of weight loss in obese patients. Diabetes management initiatives could be initiated in frail patients as part of prehabilitation.

Of the 451 patients in the COMES database, 148 patients were excluded due to exclusion criteria, e.g., patients with ostomy before or after the surgery, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or fascial dehiscence. These factors are known confounders shown in literature to increase the risk of IH. Although the inclusion of these confounders might have shown a cumulative effect leading to a higher IH rate, this approach was avoided to prevent increased bias.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this study represents the second largest cohort study to assess the risk of CT-verified IH after colon cancer resections [13]. While other studies on IH rates boast larger participant numbers, some in the thousands, they typically rely on diagnostic or operation codes to identify IH, which could lead to an underestimation of the true IH rate. Conversely, CT-verified IH may result in overestimating the rate of clinically relevant IH [37].

This study is a single-centre cohort study, which ensures that all patients undergo the same procedure at primary surgery due to the implementation of standardized techniques at NOH. This consistency reduces bias and is considered a strength of the study. Conversely, this approach inevitably results in a lower patient number, which is a natural limitation.

A limitation of this study is its retrospective nature. Although many data points were collected prospectively, the analysis of CT scans and chemotherapy data were performed retrospectively. The CT scans were performed for cancer relapse follow-up, not specifically for IH development,, and consequently, and abdominal press was not routinely used for optimal fascial visualisation. Another limitation is that the CT scans were analysed by two independent reviewers with medical degrees, rather than by a radiologist. While the optimal review of CT scans would ideally involve at least two independent radiologists [4], it can be argued that fascial defects are not the most complex findings on CT scans. Furthermore, there is a lack of consensus regarding the definition of CT-verified IH [37].

Currently, most patients with stage III colon cancer are offered adjuvant chemotherapy for 3–6 months, typically commencing 3–5 weeks post-surgery. However, some patients discontinue treatment prematurely due to side effects and complications. This study did not account for the completion rate of planned adjuvant chemotherapy. Furthermore, assessing a potential association between the number of chemotherapy series or the cumulative dose of chemotherapeutics administered was not possible due to the study’s limited power. Future research could explore the effect of the chemotherapies 5-FU and Oxaliplatin individually, to investigate their effect on IH risk separately. A potential source of bias arises because the first author, who also served as one of the reviewers, had access to information regarding the database, including whether patients received chemotherapy and the type of surgery performed. This access might potentially inflate the reported IH rate. Crucially, the second reviewer was blinded to this sensitive information and was limited to reviewing the CT scans, thereby mitigating the overall risk of bias.

Conclusion

This study found no association between adjuvant chemotherapy and the risk of IH after colon cancer resection. Adjuvant chemotherapy is an essential component of colon cancer treatment, contributing to increased survival rates in selected patients, and is therefore indispensable. Further research is important and recommended to determine how to reduce the general risk of IH after colon cancer resection, thereby enhancing patients’ quality of life.
