Abstract
We define the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space l OPEN q ) , θ ​ ( L p ) , l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}), and study the fundamental structural properties of the space, including completeness. Then we define the small Lebesgue sequence space and study its function space properties. Furthermore, we prove a version of the Hölders inequality on the frame work of these spaces. Finally, we study the multiplication operator in the setting of these spaces. 2010 AMS Subject Classification. 26D10,26D15, 46E35. Key words and Phrases. Grand amalgam Lebesgue function space, Grand sequence space, small Lebesgue sequence space, Hölder’s inequality, Multiplication operator.

2
A Banach space
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and inclusion properties

In this section we define the grand amalgam Lebesgue function spaces denoted by
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
, and study their fundamental properties. We establish that these spaces are Banach spaces. Furthermore, we explore the inclusion relation among the grand amalgam Lebesgue function spaces that arise from variation in the parameters
p,q,\theta.

Let
1\leq p<\infty,

1\leq q<\infty
, and
\theta>0
then the
grand amalgam Lebesgue function space

l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
is the collection of all complex valued measurable functions
g
defined on
\displaystyle\bigcup_{k\in X}I_{k},
where
I_{k}=[k,k+1),
with
g\chi_{I_{k}}\in L^{p},
for each
k\in X
and the sequence of local
L^{p}
-norm lies in
\displaystyle\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}l^{q(1+\varepsilon)},
such that

\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}:=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{k\in X}\left(\int_{k}^{k+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}<\infty.

(2.1)

It can also be written as

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|g\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})},

as well as

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta}}.

(2.2)

by (
1.1
) and (
1.4
).
We give an example of a measurable function which is in
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

Example 1.

Let

g(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{-\frac{1}{q}}~\left(\ln(n+1)\right)^{-a}\chi_{I_{n}}(x),
where
a\in\mathbb{R}.
Consider,

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(n^{-\frac{1}{q}}\left(\ln(n+1)\right)^{-a}\right)^{q(1+\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\|x\|_{l^{q),\theta}},

where
x=\left\{n^{-\frac{1}{q}}\left(\ln(n+1)\right)^{-a}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.
By Lemma 3.4
[
18
]
,
\|x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}
is finite if and only if

\frac{1-\theta}{q}\leq a\leq\frac{1}{q}.
Therefore,
g\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
if and only if

\frac{1-\theta}{q}\leq a\leq\frac{1}{q}.
In particular, for
a=0,
we have
g(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{-\frac{1}{q}}~\chi_{I_{n}}(x)\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
if and only if
\theta\geq 1.

Now, we prove that grand amalgam Lebesgue function space is a Banach space. To begin with, we show that it is a normed space. This follows from the fact that both Lebesgue sequence space
l^{q}
and Lebesgue function space
L^{p}
are normed spaces for
q,~p\geq 1.
However, for the sake of completion, we provide a proof below.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
,
1\leq q<\infty
and
\theta>0
then
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
is a normed space.

Proof.

Let
g,~f\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
\lambda
be a scalar.

(a)

Non-negativity:
As
\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\geq 0,
for all
k\in X,
consequently we have
\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\geq 0
, for every
\varepsilon>0,
hence
\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}\geq 0.

(b)

Suppose
\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}=0.
I.e.,
\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=0.
This implies that
\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=0
, for every
\varepsilon>0.
Since Lebesgue sequence space
l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}
and
L^{p}
space are normed spaces, we have
g=0
almost everywhere. Converse can be obtained trivially. Hence,
\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}=0
if and only if
g=0
almost everywhere.

(c)

Homogeneity:

\displaystyle\|\lambda g\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|\lambda g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\||\lambda|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}|\lambda|\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=|\lambda|\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}

(d)

Triangle inequality:

Consider,

\displaystyle\|f+g\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|(f+g)\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}+\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\big\|\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}+\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{p,q),\theta}+\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}.

∎

l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
space is a Banach space, next we show that grand Lebesgue squence space

is a Banach space.

Remark 2.2
.

In the paper

Theorem 2.3
.

For
\theta>0
and
1\leq q<\infty,
the space
l^{q),\theta}
is a Banach space.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in X}
be a Cauchy sequence in
l^{q),\theta}.

Let
\delta>0
be given.
Since
\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
l^{q),\theta},
for given
\delta>0
there exists an integer
M
such that

\|x_{n}-x_{m}\|_{l^{q),\theta}}<\frac{\delta}{3},\quad\text{for all}~n,m\geq M.

(2.3)

I.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|x_{n}-x_{m}\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}<\frac{\delta}{3},\quad\text{for all}~n,m\geq M.

This implies that all for
\varepsilon>0,
we have

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|x_{n}-x_{m}\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}<\frac{\delta}{3},\quad\text{for all}~n,m\geq M.

Therefore, for all
\varepsilon>0,

\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in the space
l^{q{(1+\varepsilon)}}.

Since
l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}
is a complete normed space, it follows that
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in X}
converges in this space, say to
x\in l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}.

Note that this limit
x
will be independent of
\varepsilon>0
because for
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2},
we have
l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{1})}\subset l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{2})}.

Now, we need to show that
x\in l^{q),\theta}
and
\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty.

For
n\geq M,
consider

\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}.

By the definition of supremum, for
\frac{\delta}{3}>0
there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0,
depending upon
n,
such that:

\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}-\frac{\delta}{3}\leq\varepsilon_{0}^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}}\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}}.

(2.4)

Since
\{x_{m}\}
converges to
x
in
l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})},
for given
\frac{\delta}{3}>0
there exists an integer
M_{1}
such that

\varepsilon_{0}^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}}\|x_{m}-x\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}}<\frac{\delta}{3},\quad\text{for all}~m\geq M_{1}.

(2.5)

Choose
K>\max\{M,M_{1}\}
, then for
n\geq K,
by using the triangle inequality in
l^{q),\theta}
, (
2.4
), (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), we get

\displaystyle\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{n}-x_{K}\|_{l^{q),\theta}}+\|x_{K}-x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{n}-x_{K}\|_{l^{q),\theta}}+\varepsilon_{0}^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}}\|x_{K}-x\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}}+\frac{\delta}{3}

\displaystyle<\delta,\quad\text{for all}~n\geq K.

(2.6)

Thus, the Cauchy sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in X}
converges to
x
in
l^{q),\theta}.

Now by using (
2
), the fact that
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in X}\subset l^{q),\theta},
and

\|x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}\leq\|x_{n}-x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}+\|x_{n}\|_{l^{q),\theta}},
we have
\|x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}<\infty.
Therefore,
x\in l^{q),\theta}.
Hence proved.
∎

So, finally we prove the completeness of the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\theta>0
and
1\leq p,~q<\infty
then the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
is a Banach space.

Proof.

To prove that
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
is a Banach space, we shall prove the Riesz-Fischer property i.e., if
\{g_{k}\}_{k\in X}
is a sequence of functions in
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
with

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in X}\|g_{k}\|_{p,q),\theta}<\infty
then
\displaystyle\sum_{k\in X}g_{k}
converges in
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

By using (
2.2
), we can write

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k\in X}g_{k}\right\|_{p,q),\theta}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left\|\left\|\left(\sum_{k\in X}g_{k}\right)\chi_{I_{n}}\right\|_{L^{p}}\right\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=\left\|\left\|\sum_{k\in X}g_{k}\chi_{I_{n}}\right\|_{L^{p}}\right\|_{l^{q),\theta}}.

Now, by using Theorem
2.3
and the fact that for
p\geq 1,

L^{p}
space is a Banach space, we can use the Riesz-Fischer property in both the spaces. Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k\in X}g_{k}\right\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left\|\sum_{k\in X}g_{k}\chi_{I_{n}}\right\|_{L^{p}}\right\|_{l^{q),\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{k\in X}\|g_{k}\chi_{I_{n}}\|_{L^{p}}\right\|_{l^{q),\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in X}\big\|\|g_{k}\chi_{I_{n}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta}}=\sum_{k\in X}\|g_{k}\|_{p,q),\theta}<\infty.

Hence by the Riesz-Fischer property, we have proved that
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
is a Banach space.
∎

1\leq p<\infty,
then

l^{q_{1}),\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}(L^{p})\hookrightarrow l^{q_{2}),\theta}(L^{p}).

(b)

If
0<\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}
,
then
l^{q),\theta_{1}}(L^{p})\hookrightarrow l^{q),\theta_{2}}(L^{p}).

(c)

If
0<\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2},

1\leq p_{2}<p_{1}<\infty
and
1\leq q<\infty,
then

l^{q),\theta_{1}}(L^{p_{1}})\hookrightarrow l^{q),\theta_{2}}(L^{p_{2}}).

(d)

For
1\leq p<\infty,

1\leq q<\infty
and
\theta>0
then
l^{q}\left(L^{p}\right)\hookrightarrow l^{q),\theta}\left(L^{p}\right).
I.e., classical amalgam space is contained in the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space.

(e)

If
\delta>0
and
0<\sigma<\frac{1}{q^{\prime}},
where
q^{\prime}
is the conjugate of
q,
then

l^{q(1-\sigma)}(L^{p})\hookrightarrow l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})\hookrightarrow l^{q(1+\delta)}(L^{p}).

Proof.

(a)

Let
g\in l^{q_{1}),\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}(L^{p})
. Since
q_{1}<q_{2}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
we have
l^{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}\subset l^{q_{2}(1+\varepsilon)}.
I.e.,

\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q_{2}(1+\varepsilon)}}\leq\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}.
By using this fact, we get

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q_{2}),\theta}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{{q_{2}}(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q_{2}(1+\varepsilon)}}\leq\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{{q_{2}}(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}=\|g\|_{p,q_{1}),\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}<\infty.

Thus

g\in l^{q_{2}),\theta}(L^{p}).

But this is the strict inclusion. Take
g(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\chi_{I_{n}}(x).
For
\theta\geq 1,
by Example 1,
g\in l^{q_{2}),\theta}(L^{p}).

Now, consider,

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q_{1}),\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{{\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}}{{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{{\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}}{{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ n^{-\frac{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}{q_{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{{\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}}{{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}}\left\|n^{-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\right\|_{l^{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}}.

For
0<\varepsilon\leq\alpha
, where
\alpha=\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}}-1,
the series
x=\left\{n^{-\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverges in the space
l^{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}
because
\frac{q_{1}(1+\varepsilon)}{q_{2}}\leq 1.

Therefore,
\|g\|_{p,q_{1}),\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}=\infty.
Thus
g\notin l^{q_{1}),\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\theta}(L^{p})
.

(b)

Let
0<\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}
, we need to prove

l^{q),\theta_{1}}(L^{p})\hookrightarrow l^{q),\theta_{2}}(L^{p}).

Let
g\in l^{q),\theta_{1}}(L^{p})
, by using embeddings (
1.6
) in grand Lebesgue sequence spaces, we get

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta_{2}}=

\displaystyle\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta_{2}}}\leq c\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta_{1}}}=c\|g\|_{p,q),\theta_{1}}<\infty,

where
c
is a positive constant. Hence
g\in l^{q),\theta_{2}}(L^{p})
.

Now, we show that the inclusion is strict.
Take
\theta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
and
\theta_{2}=2.
Let
g(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{-\frac{1}{q}}\chi_{I_{n}}(x).

By Example 1,

g\in l^{q),2}\left(L^{p}\right)
but

g\notin l^{q),\frac{1}{2}}\left(L^{p}\right)
because
\theta_{2}=2>1
and
\frac{1}{2}=\theta_{1}<1
.

(c)

Let
1\leq p_{2}<p_{1}<\infty,

0<\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}
and
1\leq q<\infty.

Let
g\in l^{q),\theta_{1}}(L^{p_{1}}).

Since
p_{2}<p_{1}
and Lebesgue measure of
I_{k}
is 1, for each
k\in X,
by the nesting property of the
L^{p}
spaces we have

\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\leq\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{1}}}.
Also, for
x=\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k\in X},~y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}\in l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}
with
x\leq y,
i.e.,
x_{k}\leq y_{k},
for all
k\in X,
by the lattice property of the Lebesgue sequence spaces we have
\|x\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\leq\|y\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}},
for every
\varepsilon>0.

By using these arguments and embeddings (
1.6
) in grand Lebesgue sequence spaces, we obtain,

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p_{2},q),\theta_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta_{2}}{{q}(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle=\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta_{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq c\,\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta_{1}}}

\displaystyle=c\,\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta_{1}}{{q}(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle\leq c\,\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta_{1}}{{q}(1+\varepsilon)}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}<\infty,

where
c
is a positive constant. Hence

g\in l^{q),\theta_{2}}(L^{p_{2}}).

Again this inclusion is strict. Let
g(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{\frac{-1}{q}}\chi_{[n,n+1)}(x).
Take
\alpha=\frac{p_{2}}{q}+p_{1},

\theta_{2}=(\alpha-p_{2})\frac{q}{p_{2}}
and
\beta=p_{1}-p_{2}>0.
Since
\beta>0,
we have
\theta_{2}>1.
Therefore, by Example 1

g\in l^{q),\theta_{2}}(L^{p_{2}}).

Now take
\theta_{1}=(\alpha-p_{1})\frac{q}{p_{1}}.
Clearly
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}.
Since
\theta_{1}=(\alpha-p_{1})\frac{q}{p_{1}}=\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}<1,
by Example 1,
g\notin l^{q),\theta_{1}}(L^{p_{1}}).

(d)

Let
1\leq p<\infty
,
1\leq q<\infty
and
\theta>0.

Let
g\in l^{q}\left(L^{p}\right),
in view of the Remark
2.6
and by applying the nested property of the classical Lebesgue sequence spaces we get

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}{\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\varepsilon>0}{\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q}}

\displaystyle=\|g\|_{l^{q}(L^{p})}\sup_{\varepsilon>0}{\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\|g\|_{l^{q}(L^{p})}\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}{W(\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}})}}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q}}<\infty.

Therefore
g\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

This inclusion is also strict because for
\theta\geq 1,

g(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{\frac{-1}{q}}\chi_{[n,n+1)}(x)\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
but
g\notin l^{q}(L^{p})
because

\displaystyle\|g\|_{l^{q}(L^{p})}=\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{n}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q}}=\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}\left(n^{\frac{-1}{q}}\chi_{I_{n}}\right)^{p}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\infty.

(e)

Let
\delta>0.

By applying embedding (
1.6
) we get

\displaystyle\|g\|_{l^{q(1+\delta)}(L^{p})}

\displaystyle=\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\delta)}}\leq c\sup_{\varepsilon>0}{\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}}=c\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}<\infty,

(2.10)

where
c
is a positive constant.

Now let
0<\sigma<\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}.
Again by applying embedding (
1.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}=\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta}}\leq C\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q(1-\sigma)}}\leq C\|g\|_{l^{q(1-\sigma)}(L^{p})}<\infty,

(2.11)

where
C
is a positive constant.
After combining (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), we obtain

\|g\|_{l^{q(1+\delta)}(L^{p})}\leq c\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}\leq c\,C\|g\|_{l^{q(1-\sigma)}(L^{p})}.

Hence done.

∎

Remark 2.9
.

Suppose
\theta>0
is given. When
1\leq p=q
, we know that the classical amalgam Lebesgue space coincides with Lebesgue space, see
[
13
]
, i.e.,

l^{q}(L^{p})=L^{p}.
By using Theorem
2.8
(d) part we get the following embedding:

l^{p}(L^{p})=L^{p}\hookrightarrow l^{p),\theta}(L^{p}).

I.e.,
L^{p}
space is strictly contained in grand amalgam Lebesgue function space
l^{p),\theta}(L^{p}).

We now prove a lemma which is required for one of the main theorems in Section 4.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to f
in the norm of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
, where
f\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
. Then
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a subsequence which converges pointwise almost everywhere to f.

Proof.

Suppose
\|f_{n}-f\|_{p,q),\theta}\to 0.

Let
\eta>0
be given then there exists a positive integer
K
such that

\displaystyle\|f_{n}-f\|_{p,q),\theta}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}~\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|f_{n}-f\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})}<{\eta},\,\,\text{for all}\,\,n\geq K.

This implies

\displaystyle\|f_{n}-f\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})}<{\eta},\,\,\text{for all}\,\,n\geq K,\,\,\varepsilon>0.

So, for
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|f_{n}-f\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}({L^{p}})}\leq\|f_{n}-f\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})}<{\eta},~~\,\,\text{for all}\,\,n\geq K.

Therefore,

\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to f
in the norm of
l^{q(1+\varepsilon_{0})}(L^{p})
. So, by using the Lemma 2.12
[
1
]
,

\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a subsequence which converges pointwise almost everywhere to f.
∎

Now we define a subset of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}),
which form a closed subspace of the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space.
Let
1\leq p,q<\infty
and
\theta>0
. We define the
Vanishing grand amalgam Lebesgue function space,
and denote it by

l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p}),

l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p})=\left\{g\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})\;\middle|\;\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\theta}\sum_{n\in X}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}=0\right\}.

Theorem 2.11
.

l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p})
is a closed subspace of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
.

Proof.

Let
g
be a limit point of
l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p})
. This implies that
there exists a sequence

\{g_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p})
such that
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to g
in the norm of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

I.e., for given
\eta>0
there exists a positive integer
K
such that

\displaystyle\|g_{i}-g\|_{l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})}=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left\{{\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in X}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|(g_{i}-g)(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right\}^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}<\frac{\eta}{2},\,\,\text{for all}\,\,i\geq K.

(2.12)

Since
g_{i}\in l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p}),
for
i\geq K
and
\frac{\eta}{2}>0,
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\theta}\sum_{n\in X}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g_{i}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}<\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{q(1+\varepsilon)},\quad\text{whenever }0<\varepsilon<\delta.

(2.13)

For
0<\varepsilon<\delta,
and
i\geq K,
using (
2.12
), (
2.13
) and triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{n\in X}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\delta}\left\{\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{n\in X}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\delta}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|g\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\delta}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|g_{i}-g\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})}+\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\delta}\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\|g_{i}\|_{l^{q(1+\varepsilon)}({L^{p}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}=\eta.

I.e.,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\theta}\sum_{n\in X}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}=0.

This implies that
g\in l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p}).
Hence
l^{\overset{\circ}{q}),\theta}(L^{p})
is a closed subspace of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

∎

3
Hölder’s inequality in
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})

In this section, we aim to prove the Hölder’s inequality within the framework of the grand amalgam Lebesgue function spaces. To do so, we first prove Hölder’s inequality in the grand sequence space
l^{q),\theta}.

We begin by stating a lemma of independent interest.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\theta>0
is given and
1\leq p_{i},q_{i}<\infty,~~i=1,2,3.
Suppose that there exist positive constants
c
and
C,
respectively, such that

\displaystyle\|xy\|_{l^{q_{3}),\theta}}\leq c\|x\|_{l^{q_{1}),\theta}}\,\|y\|_{l^{q_{2}),\theta}},\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,x\in l^{q_{1}),\theta},\,y\in l^{q_{2}),\theta},

(3.1)

where
x=\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}
and
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X},

and

\displaystyle\|(hs)\chi_{E}\|_{L^{p_{3}}}\leq C\|h\chi_{E}\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\,\|s\chi_{E}\|_{L^{p_{2}}},\,\,\,h\in L^{p_{1}},\,s\in L^{p_{2}},

(3.2)

for every subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}
with Lebesgue measure of
E
is finite.
Then

\|fg\|_{p_{3},q_{3}),\theta}\leq D\|f\|_{p_{1},q_{1}),\theta}\,\|g\|_{p_{2},q_{2}),\theta},

for
f\in l^{q_{1}),\theta}(L^{p_{1}})
and
g\in l^{q_{2}),\theta}(L^{p_{2}}),

where
D=c\,C.

Proof.

Let
f\in l^{q_{1}),\theta}(L^{p_{1}})
and
g\in l^{q_{2}),\theta}(L^{p_{2}}).
Take
x=\left\{\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\right\}_{k\in X},y=\left\{\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\right\}_{k\in X}.

By the definition (
2.1
),
x\in l^{q_{1}),\theta}
and
y\in l^{q_{2}),\theta}.
Applying (
3.2
) successively on the intervals
I_{k}=[k,k+1),
with
h=f\chi_{I_{k}}\in L^{p_{1}}
and
s=g\chi_{I_{k}}\in L^{p_{2}},
and then using (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{p_{3},q_{3}),\theta}

\displaystyle=\big\|\|(fg)\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{3}}}\big\|_{l^{q_{3}),\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|C\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\big\|_{l^{q_{3}),\theta}}

\displaystyle=c\,C\big\|\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\big\|_{l^{q_{1}),\theta}}\big\|\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p_{2}}}\big\|_{l^{q_{2}),\theta}}

\displaystyle=D\|f\|_{\left.p_{1},q_{1}\right),\theta}\,\|g\|_{\left.p_{2},q_{2}\right),\theta},

where
D=c\,C.
Hence the assertion is proved.
∎

Before stating the Hölder’s inequality in the aforesaid spaces, we first define the small Lebesgue sequence spaces. Let
\theta>0
and
1\leq q<\infty.
The
small Lebesgue sequence space
, denoted by
{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}},
is the space of all sequences
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}
such that

\displaystyle\|y\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{|y_{k}|=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}<\infty,

(3.3)

where
(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}
is the conjugate of
q(1+\varepsilon),
for each
\varepsilon>0.

First we give an example that for a given sequence
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X,}
such a decomposition exists, that is, for each
k\in X,

each term
|y_{k}|
can be expressed as an infinite sum of non-negative terms,

|y_{k}|=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j},\,y_{k,j}\geq 0,
for all
j.

Let
y=\left\{y_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},
we may take
y_{k,j}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{j}}

then

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}y_{k,j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{j}}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j}}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}=y_{k}.

Also, a trivial decomposition of a non-negative sequence
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}
always exists as:

y_{k,j}=\begin{cases}y_{k},&j=1\\
0,&j\neq 1.\end{cases}

Now we give an example of a sequence
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}\in{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

Example 2.

Let
m
be a positive integer.
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},

y_{k}=

\begin{cases}1,&|k|\leq m\\
0,&|k|>m.\end{cases}

Take

s_{k,j}=y_{k}\frac{1}{2^{j}},
where
j\in\mathbb{N}.
Clearly,
\left\{s_{k,j}\right\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a decomposition of the sequence
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}.

Consider,

\displaystyle\|y\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{|y_{k}|=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}y_{k,j},}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}s_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\left(2m+1\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq(2m+1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\frac{1}{2^{j}}

\displaystyle=(2m+1)\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{-1}{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j}}=(2m+1)\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}W(1/\mathrm{e})}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q}}<\infty.

Therefore
y\in{l}^{q)^{\prime},\theta}.

Now we prove the lattice property in
{l}^{q)^{\prime},\theta}.
To prove it, first we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},\,\{y_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
be sequences of non-negative real numbers such that
0\leq y_{k}\leq x_{k},
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
Suppose
x_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{k,j}
is any non-negative decomposition of
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}.
Define a sequence

\displaystyle z_{k,j}=\begin{cases}x_{k,j}-\max\left(y_{k}-\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{j-1}x_{k,i},\ 0\right),&\text{if }\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{j}x_{k,i}>y_{k}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3.4)

Then the following hold:

(a)

0\leq z_{k,j}\leq x_{k,j},\quad\text{for all}\quad k,\,j\in\mathbb{Z}.

(b)

y_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(x_{k,j}-z_{k,j}),\quad\text{for all}\quad k,\,j\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

Case (i) Let
y_{k}=x_{k}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
Then
z_{k,j}=0,
for all
k,j\in\mathbb{Z}.
Hence (a) and (b) hold trivially.

Case(ii) Let
y_{k}<x_{k},
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
Define
\hat{j}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z},
by

\hat{j}_{k}=\min\left\{j\in\mathbb{Z}:\sum_{i=-\infty}^{j}x_{k,i}>y_{k}\right\}.

If
j<\hat{j}_{k}
then
\displaystyle\sum_{i=-\infty}^{j}x_{k,i}\leq y_{k}
and by (
3.4
), we have
z_{k,j}=0.

If
j=\hat{j}_{k}
then
\displaystyle\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\hat{j}_{k}-1}x_{k,i}\leq y_{k}
and
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\hat{j}_{k}}x_{k,i}>y_{k}.
Therefore by (
3.4
), we have

z_{k,\hat{j}_{k}}=x_{k,\hat{j}_{k}}-\left(y_{k}-\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\hat{j}_{k}-1}x_{k,i}\right).

If
j>\hat{j}_{k}
then by (
3.4
), we have
z_{k,j}=x_{k,j}.

It follows from all these three possibilities that (a) is satisfied. To verify (b), consider

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(x_{k,j}-z_{k,j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<\hat{j}_{k}}(x_{k,j}-0)+(x_{k,\hat{j}_{k}}-z_{k,\hat{j}_{k}})+\sum_{j>\hat{j}_{k}}(x_{k,j}-x_{k,j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<\hat{j}_{k}}x_{k,j}+\left(y_{k}-\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\hat{j}_{k}-1}x_{k,i}\right)

\displaystyle=y_{k}.

Hence (b) is satisfied.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

In
[
9
]
, Fiorenza has proved the continuous version of Lemma
3.2
. This lemma asserts that if there is a non-negative decomposition of a given non-negative sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
and if another sequence
\{y_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies
0\leq y_{k}\leq x_{k},
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z},
then
\{y_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
also admits a non-negative decomposition. Moreover, the lemma gives an explicit construction of such a decomposition.

Theorem 3.4
.

Lattice property holds in the Small Lebesgue sequence space, i.e., if
x=\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},\,y=\{y_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\in l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}
such that
0\leq y\leq x,
i.e.,
y_{k}\leq x_{k},
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
then
\|y\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}\leq\|x\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\|x\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{x_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{k,j}}\\
{x_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{x_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{k,j}}\\
{x_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(x_{k,j}-z_{k,j})^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{y_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}y_{k,j}}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\|y\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

Hence done.
∎

Next, we prove that the small Lebesgue sequence space is complete.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
\theta>0
and
1\leq q<\infty
, the space
{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}
is a Banach space.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove that for any non negative sequence
\{y^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
which is absolutely summable in
{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}
is summable in
{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

That is,

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}y^{(n)}\Bigg\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|y^{(n)}\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

Since
\{y^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is absolutely summable in
{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}},
we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|y^{(n)}\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}<\infty.

By the definition of the norm of small Lebesgue sequence space, for given
\delta>0
there exists a non negative decomposition
\{y_{k,j}^{(n)}\}_{k\in X,j\in X}
of
y^{(n)}=\{y_{k}^{(n)}\}_{k\in X}
such that

y_{k}^{(n)}=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}^{(n)}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}(y_{k,j}^{(n)})^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}<\|y^{(n)}\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}+\frac{\delta}{2^{n}}.

Then for any
m\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{m}y^{(n)}\Bigg\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\sum_{n=1}^{m}y^{(n)}_{k}=\sum_{j\in X}\sum_{n=1}^{m}s^{(n)}_{k,j}}\\
{s^{(n)}_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{m}s^{(n)}_{k,j}\right)^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\sum_{n=1}^{m}y^{(n)}_{k}=\sum_{j\in X}\sum_{n=1}^{m}s^{(n)}_{k,j}}\\
{s^{(n)}_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{k\in X}\left(s^{(n)}_{k,j}\right)^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{m}\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}\left(y^{(n)}_{k,j}\right)^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{n=1}^{m}\left(\|y^{(n)}\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}+\frac{\delta}{2^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|y_{k}^{(n)}\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}+\delta,~~~~\text{for all}\,\,\delta>0,\,\,m\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{m}y^{(n)}\Bigg\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|y^{(n)}\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}},~~~~\text{for all}\,\,m\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence we have proved the assertion.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Following the similar steps, the triangle inequality can be proved easily in the small Lebesgue sequence space.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
and
\theta>0
be given. Suppose
x=\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}\in l^{q),{\theta}}
and
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}\in l^{q)^{\prime},{\theta}}
then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}y_{k}|\leq\|x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}\|y\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

(3.5)

Proof.

Let
x=\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}\in l^{q),{\theta}}
and
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}\in l^{q)^{\prime},{\theta}}.
Suppose

|y_{k}|=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j},\,\,y_{k,j}\geq 0,
is a
decomposition of
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in X}.
For
\varepsilon>0,
applying Hölder’s inequality for the exponents
q(1+\varepsilon)
and
(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}
in the classical Lebesgue sequence spaces, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}|y_{k,j}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}|^{q(1+\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{1}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\frac{\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}|^{q(1+\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{1}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|_{l^{q),{\theta}}}\,\,\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|_{l^{q),{\theta}}}\,\,\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}.

(3.6)

Now, by using (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}y_{k}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}|\left(\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}\right)=\sum_{j\in X}\sum_{k\in X}|x_{k}|y_{k,j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in X}\|x\|_{l^{q),{\theta}}}\,\,\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|_{l^{q),{\theta}}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{|y_{k}|=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\|x\|_{l^{q),\theta}}\|y\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

Hence proved.
∎

Let
1\leq p<\infty,

1\leq q<\infty
, and
\theta>0
then the
auxiliary space of the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space,
denoted by
l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}(L^{p}),
is the collection of all complex valued measurable functions
f
defined on
\displaystyle\bigcup_{k\in X}I_{k},
where
I_{k}=[k,k+1),
with
f\chi_{I_{k}}\in L^{p},
for each
k\in X,
such that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{p,q)^{\prime},\theta}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}<\infty,

where
\frac{1}{q(1+\varepsilon)}+\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}=1,
for every
\varepsilon>0.
In view of (
3.3
) this norm can also be written as:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{p,q)^{\prime},\theta}=\big\|\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

In view of the Theorem 1 of
[
13
]
and Theorem
3.5
, auxiliary space of the grand amalgam Lebesgue function space is a Banach space.

Now, we prove the
Hölder’s inequality on grand amalgam Lebesgue function space
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\theta>0,\,\,X=\mathbb{Z}
and
1\leq p,\,q<\infty.
If
g\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
f\in l^{{q})^{\prime},\theta}(L^{p^{\prime}})
then
gf\in L^{1}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(x)f(x)|dx\leq\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}\,\|f\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta},

(3.7)

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1.

Proof.

Let
g\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
f\in l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}(L^{p^{\prime}}).
Using the Hölder’s inequality for the exponents
p
and
p^{\prime}
in the Lebesgue spaces, we get

\int_{I_{k}}|g(x)f(x)|dx\leq\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}},\,\,\text{for}\,\,k\in X.

(3.8)

Using (
3.8
) and Hölder’s inequality (
3.5
) in the grand Lebesgue sequence spaces, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(x)f(x)|dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in X}\int_{I_{k}}|g(x)f(x)|dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in X}\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\,\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\|g\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q),\theta}}\,\left\|\|f\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}\right\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}

\displaystyle=\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}\|f\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta}.

Hence done.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Since the Hölder’s inequality holds in both the grand Lebesgue sequence spaces
l^{q),\theta}
and the classical Lebesgue space
L^{p},
it also holds in grand amalgam Lebesgue function space
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).
This is the general fact concerning the amalgamation of two normed spaces, see
[
11
]
.

In the next theorem, we show that the characteristic function
\chi_{E}
on bounded measurable subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}
belongs to
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}(L^{p})
as well. Also, we show that the condition of boundedness is necessary.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let E be any bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}.
Then

(a)

\chi_{E}\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

(b)

\chi_{E}\in l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}(L^{p}).

(c)

there exists a constant
c_{E}>0
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{E}|g(x)|dx\leq c_{E}\|g\|_{p,q),\theta},

(3.9)

for every measurable function
g\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

Proof.

(a)

Let
E
be any bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}
. This implies that there exists positive integer
M
such that
E\subset[-M,M].
Therefore,

E\cap[k,k+1)=\emptyset,
for every
k
such that
k<-M
and
k\geq M.
Hence Lebesgue measure

|E\cap I_{k}|
is non-zero for at the most
2M
intervals
I_{k}
.

\displaystyle\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{k\in X}\left(\int_{k}^{k+1}|\chi_{E}|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{k=-M}^{M-1}\left(|E\cap I_{k}|\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}2M\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}\leq\left(2M\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}W(1/\mathrm{e})}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{q}},

where
|E\cap I_{k}|
denotes Lebesgue measure of
E\cap I_{k}.

Hence
||\chi_{E}||_{p,q),\theta}<\infty.

(b)

Take
y=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},

y_{k}=

\begin{cases}1,&-M\leq k<M-1\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In view of Example 2,
y\in l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}.

Clearly,
\left\{\|\chi_{E\cap I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}=|{E\cap I_{k}}|_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}=y,
see argument given in the above proof. By using the Theorem
3.4
(lattice property of the small Lebesgue sequence space), we have
\big\|\|\chi_{E\cap I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}\leq\big\|y\big\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}.

Now consider,

\displaystyle\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q)^{\prime},\theta}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\|\chi_{E}\chi_{I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\|\chi_{E\cap I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}=\sum_{j\in X}y_{k,j}}\\
{y_{k,j}\geq 0}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{j\in X}\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon^{\frac{-\theta}{q(1+\varepsilon)}}\left(\sum_{k\in X}y_{k,j}^{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{(q(1+\varepsilon))^{\prime}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\big\|\|\chi_{E\cap I_{k}}\|_{L^{p}}\big\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|y\big\|_{l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}},

which is finite. Hence done.

(c)

By using the Hölder inequality (
3.7
) in the grand amalgam Lebesgue function spaces, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{E}|g(x)|dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|g\chi_{E}\right|dx\leq\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}\|\chi_{E}\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta}=c_{E}\|g\|_{p,q),\theta},

where
c_{E}=\|\chi_{E}\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta},
which is finite with the preceding proof. Hence proved.

∎

Remark 3.11
.

In Theorem
3.10
, the condition of boundedness on
E\subset\mathbb{R}
is necessary. We give an example that if the condition of the boundedness is relaxed than it is not necessary that
\chi_{E}\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

Choose
1\leq q<p<\infty
,
\theta=\frac{1}{2}.
Take
\alpha=\frac{p}{q}>1,
and
E=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left[n,n+\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\right].
Clearly
E
is not bounded but
Lebesgue measure
|E|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-\alpha}
is finite because
\alpha>1.
Now,

\displaystyle\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|\chi_{E}|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{E\cap I_{n}}1\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(|E\cap I_{n}|^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)^{q(1+\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\left\||E\cap I_{n}|^{\frac{1}{p}}\right\|_{l^{q),\theta}}

\displaystyle=\left\|n^{\frac{-\alpha}{p}}\right\|_{l^{q),\theta}}=\|n^{-\frac{1}{q}}\|_{l^{q),\theta}}.

\theta<1,
by Example 1,
\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}=\infty.
Therefore,
\chi_{E}\notin l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

Now we give an example that (
3.9
) also may or may not hold if the condition of boundedness of
E\subset\mathbb{R}
is relaxed.
Let
1\leq p<q<\infty,\,\,\theta=2.
Take
\alpha=\frac{p}{q}<1
,
\,\beta=\frac{q-\alpha}{q-1}>1,

E=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left[n,n+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right].
Clearly
E
is not bounded but
|E|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-\beta}
is finite because
\beta>1.
Let
g=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{\frac{\beta-\alpha}{p}}\chi_{\left[n,n+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right]}.

\displaystyle\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left\{{\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{E\cap I_{n}}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right\}^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left\{{\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(n^{{\beta-\alpha}}n^{-\beta}\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right\}^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left\{{\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(n^{\frac{-\alpha}{p}}\right)^{q(1+\varepsilon)}\right\}^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\|n^{-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\|_{l^{q),\theta}}=\|n^{-\frac{1}{q}}\|_{l^{q),\theta}}

\|g\|_{p,q),\theta}<\infty.

But

\int_{E}|g|dx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{\frac{\beta-\alpha}{p}{-\beta}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{{\beta}-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{p}}=\infty,

because
{{\beta}-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{p}}<1.

4
Multiplication operator on
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})

In this section, we study the multiplication operator in the frame work of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
space. Let
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. A
Multiplication operator

M_{g}
is defined as
M_{g}(f)=fg,
where
f

is a measurable function defined on
\mathbb{R}.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
1\leq p,q<\infty
,
X=\mathbb{Z}
and
\theta>0.
Suppose that
g
is a measurable function with compact support. Then the multiplication operator

\displaystyle M_{g}:l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})\to l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})\text{ is bounded if and only if }g\in L^{\infty}.

Moreover,

\|M_{g}\|=\|g\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Assume that
g\in L^{\infty}
. For
f\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}),
consider

\displaystyle\|M_{g}f\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|M_{g}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|(fg)(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|f(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{p,q),\theta}.

Also, the operator norm of
M_{g}
is

\|M_{g}\|=\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|M_{g}f\|_{p,q),\theta}}{\|f\|_{p,q),\theta}}\leq\|g\|_{\infty}.

(4.1)

Conversely, assume that
M_{g}
is bounded but
g\notin L^{\infty}
. Then for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

G_{m}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|g(x)|>m\}

has positive measure. Since
g
has compact support, this implies that there exists a bounded subset
K_{m}
of
G_{m}
with positive measure. Hence by Theorem
3.10
,
\|\chi_{K_{m}}\|_{p,q),\theta}
is finite.
Observe that

\displaystyle|(g\chi_{K_{m}})(x)|\geq m\chi_{K_{m}}(x),\quad\text{for any}\,\,\,m\in\mathbb{N}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|g\chi_{K_{m}}\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g\chi_{K_{m}}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|m\chi_{K_{m}}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=m\|\chi_{K_{m}}\|_{p,q),\theta}.

This implies that

\|M_{g}\chi_{K_{m}}\|_{p,q),\theta}\geq m\|\chi_{K_{m}}\|_{p,q),\theta},\text{ for each }\,\,m\in\mathbb{N}.

It contradicts the boundedness of
M_{g}
on
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).
Therefore,
g\in L^{\infty}.

Now, we shall prove that
\|M_{g}\|=\|g\|_{\infty}
.

For any
\delta>0
, define a set
G=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|g(x)|>\|g\|_{\infty}-\delta\}
. Then
G
has positive measure. Since
g
has compact support, there exists a bounded subset
K
of
G,
and by Theorem
3.10
,
\|\chi_{K}\|_{p,q),\theta}
is finite. Observe that

\displaystyle|(g\chi_{K})(x)|\geq(\|g\|_{\infty}-\delta)\chi_{K}(x),\,\,\text{for every}\,\,x\in K.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|g\chi_{K}\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|g\chi_{K}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|(\|g\|_{\infty}-\delta)\chi_{K}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=(\|g\|_{\infty}-\delta)\|\chi_{K}\|_{p,q),\theta}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|M_{g}\|\geq\|g\|_{\infty}-\delta,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\delta>0

(4.2)

Hence, from (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we have

\|M_{g}\|=\|g\|_{\infty}.

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
1\leq p,q<\infty
,
X=\mathbb{Z}
and
\theta>0.
If
g\in l^{q)^{\prime},\theta}(L^{p^{\prime}})
, then the multiplication operator

M_{g}:l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})\to L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded and

\|M_{g}\|\leq\|g\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta},
where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1.

Proof.

To prove the theorem, we shall use the Closed Graph Theorem. First we shall prove that the multiplication operator is a closed operator. Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to f
in the norm of
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
, where
f\in l^{q),\theta}(L^{p}).

Suppose
M_{g}(f_{n})=gf_{n}\to h
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. By using Lemma
2.10
,
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a subsequence
\{{f_{n_{k}}}\}
which converges pointwise almost everywhere (a.e.) to
f
.
Therefore, we obtain
gf_{n_{k}}\to gf
pointwise a.e. So
h=gf
pointwise a.e. This shows that
the graph of
M_{g}
is closed, hence the multiplication operator
M_{g}
is a closed operator.

Since
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
and
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
are Banach spaces and the multiplication operator is linear, by using Closed Graph Theorem, we have the assertion.

Now to estimate the norm of
M_{g},
we shall use the
Hölder’s inequality in the grand amalgam Lebesgue function spaces, i.e.,
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
. Consider,

\displaystyle\|M_{g}f\|_{L^{1}}=\|gf\|_{L^{1}}\leq{\|f\|_{p,q),\theta}\|g\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta}}

\|M_{g}\|=\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|M_{g}f\|_{L^{1}}}{\|f\|_{p,q),\theta}}\leq\|g\|_{p^{\prime},q)^{\prime},\theta}.

Hence done.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
1\leq p,q<\infty
,
X=\mathbb{Z}
and
\theta>0.
Suppose that
g
is a measurable function with compact support and
M_{g}
is a bounded operator from
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
to
l^{q),\theta}(L^{p})
. Then
M_{g}
is an isometry if and only if
|g(x)|=1
a.e.

Proof.

Let
M_{g}
be an isometry and
|g(x)|\neq 1
a.e., then there is a measurable subset
C\subset\mathbb{R}
of positive measure such that
|g(x)|<1,
for
x\in C
or there exists a measurable subset
D\subset\mathbb{R}
of positive measure such that
|g(x)|>1,
for
x\in D.

If
|g(x)|<1,
we can choose a subset
C_{\delta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|g(x)|<1-\delta\}
such that
|C_{\delta}|>0,
for some
\delta>0
. Since
g
has compact support, this implies that there exists a bounded subset
E
of
C_{\delta},
and by Theorem
3.10
,
\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}
is finite.
Observe that

\displaystyle|(g\chi_{E})(x)|<(1-\delta)\chi_{E}(x),\quad\text{for some}\,\,\,\delta>0.

It follows that,

\displaystyle\|M_{g}\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|(g\chi_{E})(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle<(1-\delta)\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|\chi_{E}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}<\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}.

Since
\delta
is arbitrary, we have
\|M_{g}\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}<\|\chi_{E}\|_{p,q),\theta}.
It is a contradiction that
M_{g}
is an isometry.
Similarly, for the other case, just by following the similar steps, we get the contradiction. Hence
|g(x)|=1
a.e.

Conversely, let
|g(x)|=1
a.e. then

\displaystyle\|M_{g}f\|_{p,q),\theta}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|(fg)(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left({\varepsilon^{\theta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\int_{n}^{n+1}|f(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q(1+\varepsilon)}{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{{q(1+\varepsilon)}}}=\|f\|_{p,q),\theta}.

Hence
M_{g}
is an isometry.
∎
