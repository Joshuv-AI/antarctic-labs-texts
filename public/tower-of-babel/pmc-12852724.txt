Stress and stiffness analysis of the multiple corrugated diaphragm under angular load based on plate and shell theory
Authors: Angang Cao, Huiting Jin, Wei Li, Shiquan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Multiple Corrugated Diaphragm(MCD) couplings, as a new type of flexible coupling, exhibit complex non-axisymmetric mechanical characteristics under angular loads due to their sinusoidal corrugated structure, posing challenges to traditional analytical methods. This paper aims to establish an efficient analytical model based on plate and shell theory to predict the stress distribution and angular stiffness of the MCD under angular loads. The study combines analytical methods with the finite element method (ANSYS), segments the corrugations, and establishes reasonable boundary conditions to derive analytical formulas for the deflection curve, stress distribution, and stiffness calculation at the maximum deformation section. The results show that the analytical model calculations are highly consistent with finite element simulations: the deflection curves follow similar trends, the stress distribution patterns generally match, and the angular stiffness calculation error is within 7%. The findings indicate that the established analytical model effectively captures the core mechanical behavior of the MCD, providing a reliable theoretical basis for the rapid design and optimization of high-performance MCD Couplings.Keywords: Plate and shell theory, Angular load, The multiple corrugated diaphragm, Stress analysis, Stiffness characteristic, Analytical methodSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Flexible couplings are key mechanical components that connect two shafts to transmit torque while compensating for relative displacement and angular misalignment. Their performance directly affects the reliability, efficiency, and vibration/noise levels of rotating machinery transmission systems transmission systems1–3. In applications such as aero-engines, high-speed pumps, and compressors—characterized by high power density and high rotational speeds—extremely demanding requirements are placed on the coupling’s torque transmission torque transmission capacity, compensation capability, and fatigue life4,5.

Although traditional diaphragm couplings and membrane diaphragm couplings are widely used, their performance is approaching limits in the context of pursuing higher torque-to-diameter ratios and torque-to-weight ratios. The Multiple Corrugated Diaphragm (MCD) coupling has emerged as a new type of flexible coupling to meet these demands6–8. Its core innovation lies in adopting a series of parallel diaphragm disk sets with sinusoidal corrugation structures. This design allows the diaphragms, while transmitting torque transmitting torque, to elastically unfold the waveforms, thereby equivalently increasing their load-bearing surface area. Consequently, this significantly enhances torque transmission capacity and angular compensation ability without increasing the radial size of the coupling9,10. Furthermore, the separated multi-disk structure inherently provides inherently provides fault protection functionality, resulting in higher safety.

Angular load is one of the primary load forms when the coupling compensates for shaft misalignment. Under such loads, the MCD undergoes complex bending deformation. Determining stress concentration areas and predicting stiffness characteristics are crucial for evaluating coupling fatigue life and transmission accuracy11,12.

However, the sinusoidal corrugation structure of the MCD leads to distinctly non-axisymmetric mechanical behavior under angular load, rendering traditional axisymmetric shell analysis methods inapplicable. Current research predominantly focuses on axial or torsional load conditions13,14, leaving studies on high-precision analytical models under angular load relatively scarce. While the finite element method offers high accuracy, its substantial computational cost hinders its application for rapid design and optimization.

While our prior work established a rotating shell thin film model for centrifugal loading (axisymmetric conditions), the analytical framework cannot be directly extended to angular misalignment due to the fundamental shift from axisymmetric to non-axisymmetric deformation9. Angular loading introduces circumferential variation (θ-dependence) in stresses and displacements, requiring solutions to partial differential equations with asymmetric boundary conditions. This work addresses this gap by developing a generalized plate and shell theory-based model that captures non-axisymmetric bending, shear effects, and edge distortions under angular misalignment—phenomena not observable in centrifugal loading.

Based on this, this research is grounded in plate and shell theory and aims to establish a set of analytical models capable of accurately predicting the stress distribution and angular stiffness of the MCD under angular load. This paper will systematically analyze the mechanical response of the MCD through a combination of theoretical derivation, numerical computation, and finite element simulation. The accuracy and applicable scope of the analytical model will be validated and discussed, hoping to provide an efficient and reliable theoretical tool for the design of such high-performance couplings.

This paper adopts a research approach combining the analytical method and the finite element method (ANSYS software). The analytical method, based on plate and shell theory, establishes governing equations and solves them through reasonable assumptions and simplifications; the finite element method serves as a verification means15,16, simulating the true stress state of the MCD by establishing a detailed three-dimensional solid model.

The MCD coupling comprises multiple diaphragms (15 in the studied configuration) arranged in parallel. While the analysis focuses on a single representative diaphragm, the overall coupling behavior is obtained through superposition, with appropriate consideration of the series–parallel arrangement in the assembly.

The analytical model presented in this study focuses on a single representative diaphragm from the multi-diaphragm assembly. This approach is based on the design characteristics of the MCD coupling, where multiple diaphragms are arranged in parallel with spacers that minimize direct interlayer interactions. The global stiffness is estimated by linear superposition of individual diaphragm stiffnesses, assuming that each diaphragm experiences approximately equal load distribution and deforms independently. This simplification is valid for the angular loading conditions considered, where bending deformation dominates and contact effects between diaphragms are minimal.

Sensitivity analysis of the segmentation strategy

This section addresses the potential impact of the segmentation choice outlined in Section “Physical parameters of the MCD”, where corrugation segments 2–4 were treated as a single equivalent unit. To quantify the error introduced by this simplification, a fully resolved finite element model was developed, in which each of the four sinusoidal periods was independently discretized. Both models were subjected to the same angular load (α = 0.25°).

The comparative results indicate that the segmentation strategy has a limited effect on global performance predictions. The maximum deviation in stress (approximately 8%) is localized at the transition between segment 1 and segment 2 (r ≈ 118.5 mm), a region influenced by boundary effects. However, over the main body of the corrugations (r ∈ [125, 155] mm), the stress discrepancy between the simplified and full-period models is below 5%. Critically, the error in the calculated angular stiffness is merely 3.5% (41.6 N·m/° for the simplified model vs. 43.1 N·m/° for the full model).

This analysis validates that the simplified segmentation approach, while introducing minor local errors, effectively and accurately captures the essential global mechanical behavior, thus justifying its application for efficient design and optimization.

Calculation of angular stiffness for the MCD

The angular stiffness of the MCD is derived from a linearized moment-rotation relationship, representing the secant stiffness for small deformation conditions. This approach is based on static equilibrium principles, where the stiffness is defined as the ratio of the applied bending moment to the resulting angular displacement (K = M/α), with αbeing the misalignment angle. The derivation considers the contributions from bending stresses and shear forces, assuming linear elasticity and small rotations (i.e., α ≤ 0.25°), which ensures a constant secant stiffness within the design range. Physically, this stiffness quantifies the MCD’s resistance to angular misalignment, directly linking the applied load to the deformation response.

Results and discussion

Validity of linear elastic assumption

Prior to discussing the results, the validity of the linear elastic and small deformation assumptions is verified against material nonlinearity and buckling thresholds. The MCD material is high-strength stainless steel (e.g., SAF2205) with a yield strength σ_s ≥ 450 MPa. The maximum Von Mises stress under the applied angular load (α = 0.25°), as shown in Figs. 9 and 10, is approximately 285 MPa, which occurs near the boundaries (r ≈ 104 mm and 162 mm).

Horizontal stress under angular load obtained by ANSYS analysis.

Circumferential stress under angular load obtained by ANSYS analysis.

This peak stress is significantly below the material’s yield strength (safety factor n ≈ 1.58), confirming that the material behavior remains within the linear elastic regime. Regarding buckling stability, the primary load is bending moment. The calculated maximum compressive stress is about 20% of the critical buckling load estimated for a similar corrugated shell under in-plane moment, indicating a low risk of instability. Therefore, the fundamental assumptions of the analytical model are valid for the presented analysis.

Using ANSYS finite element analysis, the horizontal stress and circumferential stress images were obtained as Figs. 9 and 10 respectively. Comparing Figs. 7 and 8 with the FEM results shows that the stress distribution stress distribution laws obtained by the analytical method and the FEM are generally consistent, with peak and trough positions corresponding. However, near the inner and outer boundaries of the corrugations (r ≈ 104 mm and r ≈ 162 mm), the FEM results show higher stress concentrations. This is due to local bending effects (i.e., edge effects) induced by boundary constraints. The analytical model in this paper, based on plate and shell theory, has limited predictive capability for such localized three-dimensional stress concentrations. Nevertheless, for stress prediction in the main body region of the corrugations, the analytical model possesses sufficient precision and can be used for preliminary design and parametric sensitivity analysis.

Quantitative error analysis reveals that the maximum percent error in horizontal stress (Fig. 7) is 7.5% at r = 104 mm, while the average error over the corrugation area is 4.3%. For circumferential stress (Fig. 8), the maximum error is 8.2% at r = 162 mm, with an average of 4.8%. The error distribution is relatively uniform, except near boundaries where FEM shows higher stress concentrations due to edge effects.

The findings above are further contextualized by the sensitivity analysis in Section “Stress analysis of the MCD under angular load”. The stress distribution patterns shown in Figs. 7 and 8 are consistent with the full-resolution model in the corrugation’s main body, with deviations within 5%. The higher stress concentrations at the boundaries (r≈104 mm and r≈162 mm) observed in the FEM results (Figs. 9 and 10) are primarily due to three-dimensional edge effects. The additional error from the segmentation strategy itself is confined to the inter-segment transitions and does not alter the overall conclusions.

It is important to note the discrepancy in stress magnitude at the inner and outer boundaries (r ≈ 104 mm and r ≈ 162 mm), where the FEM results (Figs. 9 and 10) show markedly higher concentrations than the analytical solution. This is a recognized limitation of the classical plate theory employed, which smoothens the three-dimensional edge effects arising from geometric discontinuities and connection details. Therefore, the analytical model serves as an excellent tool for identifying critical regions and predicting global behavior, but for precise fatigue life prediction focusing on the edges, a localized FEM analysis is recommended.

Validation of modeling assumptions

Following the presentation of the deformation and stress results, this section provides a quantitative assessment of the physical assumptions underlying the analytical model. The validity of the small-rotation assumption is confirmed by the displacement fields shown in Fig. 6. The maximum slope, derived from these fields, is calculated to be 0.076 rad, which is well within the acceptable range for linearized kinematics.

Furthermore, the strain levels inferred from the stress distributions in Figs. 7 and 8 have a maximum value of 0.0013, confirming that the material behavior remains within the linear elastic regime. Finally, an analysis of the strain energy partitioning indicates that membrane energy constitutes over 85% of the total strain energy, demonstrating the dominance of membrane behavior and justifying the corresponding modeling choice. These quantified results collectively affirm the appropriateness of the thin plate/shell theory for the analyzed configuration.

The angular load analysis provides fundamentally new insights into MCD behavior: (1) it identifies critical stress concentration zones at specific angular positions (e.g., θ = 90°), which are absent under centrifugal load; (2) it quantifies the coupling between bending and torsion under misalignment, revealing how stiffness varies with angular offset; and (3) it validates the limitations of axisymmetric models for real-world misalignment scenarios, underscoring the need for a non-axisymmetric framework.

While the single-diaphragm model with stiffness superposition provides accurate predictions for the conditions studied, several limitations should be acknowledged. The model assumes ideal load distribution among diaphragms and neglects potential interlayer interactions that might occur under more severe misalignments or different boundary conditions. In practical applications, factors such as manufacturing tolerances, spacer compliance, and thermal effects could introduce variations in load sharing among diaphragms. Future work should investigate these multi-layer effects through detailed finite element analysis of the full assembly and experimental validation.

Conclusion

This study extends the rotating shell theory to non-axisymmetric angular loading, overcoming limitations of the prior centrifugal framework. The new model provides a foundational tool for designing MCD couplings under practical misalignment conditions, with validated accuracy in predicting stress distributions and stiffness characteristics.

While the model is validated for the design misalignment of 0.25°, future work should address larger angles where geometric nonlinearities and bending effects may become significant, requiring extended formulations.

The established analytical model effectively predicts the angular stiffness and overall stress distribution, providing a reliable basis for the rapid design and optimization of MCD couplings. However, designers should be aware that the model systematically underpredicts stresses at the inner and outer boundaries due to the inherent limitations of plate theory in capturing 3D edge effects. For final fatigue validation of these critical regions, the use of detailed finite element analysis is essential. Future work will focus on developing correction factors or a hybrid approach to bridge this gap.

Furthermore, the linear elastic and small deformation assumptions are validated for the presented load case in Sect. 6.1. However, under severe overload conditions or combined loading (e.g., high torque coupled with large angular misalignment), material nonlinearity or buckling may become a concern, which is beyond the scope of this study and warrants future investigation.

Future directions could explore computer vision-based digital image correlation for validating analytical and FEM-predicted deformation fields. For instance, deep learning models like DeepLab25 and EfficientNet26 have demonstrated robustness in segmenting and tracking fine-scale features in complex materials, which could be adapted to quantify corrugation-induced strains with high accuracy. Integrating such methods would offer a scalable alternative to traditional strain measurement techniques.
