Connection of right coronary and left circumflex arteries terminating in the left ventricle with intramyocardial aneurysm: a rare coronary fistula
Authors: Shi-Qin Yu, Wei-Feng Yan, Zhi-Gang Yang, Olsen Flemming Javier, Ugur Canpolat, Alessandro Palmieri, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

Case description

A 70-year-old male underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) for lung cancer (pT1bN1M0) surveillance, which incidentally found coronary abnormality. He was asymptomatic, with no cardiovascular surgery history. Coronary CT angiography showed tortuous right coronary and left circumflex arteries along the atrioventricular groove (Figure 1). These vessels joined to form an ectatic vascular structure with an intramyocardial aneurysm in the basal posterolateral wall of the left ventricle (LV), draining into the LV through a linear cleft. Moderate left anterior descending artery stenosis was noted. Echocardiography demonstrated diastolic flow across the fistulous orifice, with normal LV end-diastolic dimension (50 mm) and ejection fraction (60%). The patient was followed every 6 months for the first 2 years and annually thereafter, with both the fistula and lung cancer remaining stable over 8 years.

Coronary artery fistulas (CAF) are uncommon congenital or acquired anomalies, most frequently originating from the right coronary artery and draining into low-pressure right heart structures.1 Absence of prior surgery or infection supports a congenital origin here. Conservative management was chosen based on the absence of ischaemic symptoms, normal LV volume and function, advanced age, and patient preference. This aligns with another reported case of CAF with aneurysm in an elderly man showing 9-year stability on conservative care.2 Closure is indicated for symptomatic patients with shunt-related ischaemia, arrhythmia, endarteritis, rupture, progressive chamber enlargement, or ventricular dysfunction.3 Although both moderate coronary stenosis and fistula-related steal could theoretically cause ischaemia, the patient’s asymptomatic status is attributable to a restrictive orifice, distal origin, and LV termination, producing a small shunt. This case is limited by the lack of stress testing and shunt quantification. While CAF-associated aneurysms are typically subepicardial and rupture-prone, this lesion is a small true intramyocardial aneurysm whose wall is LV myocardium; myocardial compression and support likely limited its growth and prevented rupture.
