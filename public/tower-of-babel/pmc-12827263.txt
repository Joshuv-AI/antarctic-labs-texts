TCMEval-PA: a question-answering benchmark dataset for the prescription audit of Traditional Chinese Medicine
Authors: Baifeng Wang, Yiwei Lu, Zhe Wang, Peixin Ge, Guanjie Wang, Keyu Yao, Suyuan Peng, Yan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although large language models (LLMs) have witnessed rapid development in medical applications, their capacities to support rational medication use and guarantee prescription safety remain insufficiently investigated—especially in tasks such as prescription audit, which plays a critical role in safeguarding both. This paper presents TCMEval-PA, a benchmark dataset for assessing the capabilities of LLMs in prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines. The dataset comprises 328 choice questions, including 297 single-choice and 31 multiple-choice. All questions were designed and compiled through rule extraction from official documents and reviewed by licensed TCM physicians. TCMEval-PA comprehensively encompasses the key dimensions of prescription safety, including normativity (e.g., dispensing, decoction requirements, and regulations for special medicines) and appropriateness (e.g., contraindicated combinations and excessive dosages). The present study employed TCMEval-PA to assess several prevalent Chinese and English LLMs. This dataset can be utilized for the evaluation of LLMs and other artificial intelligence (AI) systems in TCM prescription safety scenarios and promotes research in intelligent auditing and decision support.

Background & Summary

With their strong language comprehension1, advanced text analysis capabilities2, and extensive knowledge base, large language models (LLMs) have been increasingly adopted in the medical domain for medical question answering3, health consultation4,5, clinical decision support6, and disease management7. Meanwhile, the emergence of vertical-domain models tailored to day dosage scenarios reflects the growing demand within the medical community for domain-specific AI technologies8. Despite the impressive capabilities that LLMs have demonstrated across various medical tasks9, their real-world deployment in clinical settings faces significant challenges, chief among them the issue of hallucination10. In the medical context, hallucinations may present as fabricated drug recommendations11 or falsified clinical evidence. Such outputs not only compromise the reliability of LLM-generated content, but may also lead directly to misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatment, or harmful clinical decisions12,13. Therefore, in scenarios where LLMs are used to provide medical advice, there is an urgent need for systematic evaluation of their reliability in ensuring rational medication use and prescription safety.

As a critical measure to ensure prescription quality and support rational medication use, prescription auditing entails a systematic evaluation of clinical prescriptions for legality, standardization, and appropriateness. Compared to those in Western pharmaceuticals, audits of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) prescriptions are notably more intricate and challenging. While audits in Western medicine primarily address common prescription errors—such as inappropriate drug selection, incorrect dosage, or improper treatment duration14—Chinese herbal medicines prescriptions reflect a holistic approach, emphasizing individualized syndrome differentiation and synergistic effects among the herbal components, and prescription audits must additionally account for discipline-specific considerations. For instance, the classical TCM principle of the “Eighteen Incompatible Medicaments” (Shiba Fan)15,16 outlines specific contraindicated combinations that may induce or exacerbate toxicity and must therefore be avoided. The dosage of toxic Chinese herbal medicines must be strictly regulated, and prescriptions are also required to specify appropriate decoction and processing methods for specific herbs. Consequently, prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines must address a broad array of elements, including but not limited to contraindicated combinations, dosage of individual Chinese herbal medicines, toxic Chinese herbal medicines, and specifications for preparation and administration. These distinctive requirements place heightened demands on LLMs, challenging their domain-specific knowledge accuracy and reasoning ability when applied to the prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines.

As the public increasingly relies on LLMs for health guidance and medical advice, it is essential to systematically evaluate their ability to ensure rational medication use and prescription safety. Existing evaluations of TCM-oriented LLMs have largely focused on tasks such as medical examination question answering17,18, syndrome differentiation reasoning19,20, acupoint knowledge extraction and representation21,and prescription generation22. Recent work has started to consider medication safety in the TCM context23; however, evaluations often remain at the level of isolated knowledge verification and lack a structured evaluation framework grounded in prescription audit standards. Given that prescription audits are directly linked to high-risk clinical issues, and that clinical settings often face staffing constraints, there is a growing need for LLM-based automated audit solutions. In this context, as a critical task closely tied to clinical medication compliance and risk prevention, the prescription auditing capabilities of LLMs urgently require a comprehensive benchmark that is aligned with authoritative standards.

To address this gap, we developed TCMEval-PA, a benchmark dataset comprising 328 choice questions, designed to evaluate the capabilities of LLMs in conducting the prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines. TCMEval-PA comprehensively covers two fundamental dimensions essential to prescription safety: (1) Normativity, which examines whether prescriptions comply with national and industry standards in terms of structure, formatting, labeling, dosage, and the use of high-risk herbal materials; and (2) Appropriateness, which evaluates whether the use of Chinese herbal medicines is rational, safe, and consistent with TCM diagnostic principles and individual patient characteristics. All questions were developed based on authoritative sources, such as the Technical Specification for Prescription Audit and Comment of Chinese Medicine Prescriptions, and were independently annotated and cross-verified by licensed TCM physicians to ensure accuracy and consistency.

This study is among the first to evaluate the performance of LLMs in auditing prescriptions of Chinese herbal medicines. Unlike traditional question–answering (QA)-style tasks that focus primarily on factual recall, TCMEval-PA is designed to evaluate a model’s ability to understand formalized rules, make compliance judgments, and apply standardized auditing criteria. Each question is explicitly mapped to rule clauses defined in national TCM prescription standards, with structured annotations that reflect the regulatory basis of the task. This design makes TCMEval-PA more aligned with the real-world demands of TCM prescription audit in terms of both task format and knowledge dimensions. These evaluations also help pinpoint areas where models fall short in rule compliance, thereby laying a solid foundation for the practical application of AIGC technologies in TCM-related intelligent auditing and pharmaceutical management.

Methods

Audit rule extraction

This dataset was designed in strict conformity with validated standards for the prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines, thereby ensuring both regulatory compliance and clinical applicability of the questions. The Technical Specification for Prescription Audit and Comment of Chinese Medicine Prescriptions served as the primary reference, supplemented by national and industry publications such as the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (ChP) and the Measures for the Administration of Toxic Drugs for Medical Use. Drawing on these sources, we systematically extracted a total of 18 audit rules, each covering key safety dimensions relevant to the audit of prescriptions involving Chinese herbal medicines. Their emphasis led to the classification of these rules into two primary dimensions: prescription normativity (9 rules) and prescription appropriateness (9 rules). Detailed descriptions of the prescription audit rules are provided in Table 1 (with the original Chinese version available in Supplementary Table S1). Notably, the column “Prescription Audit Rule” in Table 1 presents a concise summary or title of each audit rule, which corresponds to the “Rule Summary” field in Table 4. These rules served as the foundation for dataset construction, question design, and annotation.

If a higher dosage is clinically necessary, the prescription may be approved only after dual-signature confirmation by two physicians.

If the physician refuses to modify the prescription, it must not be approved, even with dual-signature confirmation.

If local governmental authorities have issued specific regulations on the number of individual herbal ingredients permitted per prescription, the prescription audit should comply with those local requirements. In the absence of such regulations, the number of herbal components in a single prescription should generally be limited to 18 or fewer (excluding paste prescriptions).

Data collection and construction

Following the prescription audit rules outlined above, we systematically collected and constructed multiple-choice questions corresponding to each rule. The dataset construction workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. The questions were primarily derived from authoritative training manuals and officially published examination books prepared for the Licensed Pharmacist Examination (Chinese Pharmacy) and the National Professional Technical Qualification Examination (Chinese Pharmacy)24–30. These sources were selected to ensure authority and representativeness. All materials were obtained in print format and are not publicly available in structured digital form online. Accordingly, they represent non-open-source corpora, which helps mitigate the risk of training-test contamination and evaluation leakage due to potential overlap. To ensure thorough rule coverage, every question was explicitly mapped to a corresponding prescription audit rule.Fig. 1Workflow for the construction of the TCMEval-PA dataset. For example, consider the rule that governs the use of toxic Chinese herbal medicines, as outlined in official standards. “If the dosage of a single toxic herb exceeds the upper limit specified in the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China, the National Standard for Processing of Chinese Herbal Pieces, or corresponding provincial standards, or if a single prescription exceeds the maximum two-day dosage, the prescription should be explicitly flagged and modification should be recommended. If use is deemed clinically necessary, the prescription may be approved after confirmation with dual physician signatures.” Building on this rule, we designed two levels of questions to evaluate the model’s capabilities from both comprehension and application perspectives. Data annotation

To ensure systematic organization and comprehensive coverage of prescription audit rules, each question in the dataset was annotated with its corresponding rule identifier and classification dimension (either prescription normativity or prescription appropriateness). Each question was specifically mapped to its corresponding audit rule as listed in Table 1. As part of the annotation process, two licensed TCM physicians independently reviewed and labeled each question. After completing their independent annotations, the two practitioners conducted a cross-comparison to identify any discrepancies. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a licensed TCM pharmacist, who adjudicated and finalized the annotation. This rule-based annotation framework enables fine-grained analysis of model performance across the two dimensions and provides a solid foundation for subsequent evaluation of model capabilities.

Question supplementation for rule coverage

Throughout the process of question collection and annotation, we continuously monitored the coverage of prescription audit rules across the dataset. When a given rule had no associated question, or when the existing items failed to capture the rule’s structural complexity or clinical significance, it was flagged as a case of insufficient rule coverage. In such cases, supplemental questions were developed by the expert team according to the following principles. First, priority was given to designing questions that addressed common issues in clinical prescriptions, thereby enhancing both representativeness and practical relevance. Second, the structure and language of the newly created questions were kept consistent with those of the original question pool to ensure stylistic uniformity.

To align the dataset with real-world clinical needs, we exercised strict control over content scope throughout the question collection and design process. This approach ensured that the dataset accurately reflects the practical requirements and task complexity of clinical prescription audit. As a result, the dataset offers a high-quality and structured foundation for evaluating the capabilities of LLMs in this specialized task scenario.

Data evaluation

After the annotation process was finalized, all 328 questions included in the study were subjected to a rigorous quality evaluation. The assessment focused on three core aspects: accuracy, clarity, and exclusion criteria. First, for accuracy, all correct answers were individually validated by licensed TCM physicians to ensure that each was authoritative and unambiguous. Second, clarity was evaluated by reviewing the wording of each question stem and its corresponding options. Questions containing ambiguity, logical flaws, or redundant distractors were eliminated. Third, questions were excluded if they contained factual errors, lacked a clearly correct answer, had vague assessment points, or showed unclear mappings to the corresponding audit rules.

Only those questions that met all of the following criteria—clear semantics, a single correct answer, and complete annotation with rule identifiers and classification dimensions—were included in the final version of the TCMEval-PA dataset. Experimental design

To evaluate the practical utility and discriminative power of the TCMEval-PA dataset in assessing the prescription auditing capabilities of LLMs, we selected several representative models for evaluation. These included general-purpose models (GPT-4o32, DeepSeek R133, and Qwen-Plus 2025-04-2834) and TCM-oriented models (HuatuoGPT35, ShenNong-TCM-LLM36).

Among the general-purpose models, Qwen-Plus (2025-04-28) achieved the highest overall performance, with an accuracy of 86.05% (74/86) in the prescription normativity dimension. Neither HuatuoGPT2-7B nor ShenNong-TCM-LLM achieved the expected results, showing especially low accuracy in the prescription normativity. Although these models were trained on large-scale domain-specific corpora, their advantages have yet to materialize in safety-critical tasks. These results reinforce the core motivation of this study: to establish a systematic, task-driven evaluation framework capable of assessing model performance in high-risk, rule-intensive medical tasks and identifying critical gaps in medical knowledge accuracy and reasoning capacity.

Usage Notes

The evaluation conducted in this study revealed that current TCM-oriented LLMs perform suboptimally in tasks related to prescription safety. This highlights the urgent need for a standardized dataset grounded in prescription audit rules to support both the evaluation and improvement of model performance. The core value of TCMEval-PA lies in its use of structured, rule-annotated choice questions to enable comprehensive, rapid, and transparent assessment of LLMs’ capabilities in the prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines. The dataset provides systematic coverage of all 18 audit rules and adopts a two-tier item design—rule comprehension and scenario-based application—targeting high-risk and complex aspects of prescription safety. In addition to enabling horizontal comparisons across different models, TCMEval-PA also provides an evidence-based foundation for improving the safety-related capacities of TCM-oriented LLMs.

Existing safety-related benchmarks either generalize across the entire medical domain—failing to capture the unique regulatory framework of Chinese medicine—or focus on TCM-specific tasks while lacking clearly defined task boundaries and authoritative rule mappings23,37–39. TCMEval-PA is the first benchmark to center specifically on the prescription audit rules of Chinese herbal medicines. It establishes a safety evaluation framework that encompasses both structural requirements and medication use constraints, offering a unified and quantifiable reference standard for enhancing the prescription safety capabilities of LLMs. This QA dataset offers three key advantages:Comprehensiveness: Every prescription audit rule is represented by at least one corresponding item, ensuring complete evaluation coverage with no gaps.Clarity: Each question is paired with a definitive correct answer and an explicit rule mapping, allowing for transparent verification and enabling consistent comparisons across different models and versions.Targeted Insight: By quantifying model performance across distinct dimensions, the dataset facilitates the identification of weaknesses and supports targeted model optimization.

These efforts will contribute to the progressive construction of a multi-level evaluation framework for the prescription audit of Chinese herbal medicines.

Evaluation procedure and field usage

Users can independently assess model performance based on the two core dimensions of prescription audit: normativity and appropriateness. We recommend using the Category field to facilitate dimension-specific analysis.

The dataset is released in .xlsx format, with each question including the following fields: 'ID': Unique identifier for each question; 'Question': Full question text; 'Candidate Answers': List of candidate options; 'Answer': Correct answer(s); 'Explanation': Reasoning for the correct answer; 'Category': Question type; 'Rule ID': Identifier of the corresponding audit rule; 'Rule Summary': Brief title summarizing the audit rule tested in the question. Evaluation adopts a strict scoring criterion: a model’s response is marked as correct only if the selected option(s) exactly match the reference answer; otherwise, it is considered incorrect.
