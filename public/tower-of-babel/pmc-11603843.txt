CAR-armored-cell therapy in solid tumor treatment
Authors: Yan Liu, Lin Xiao, Mingxuan Yang, Xuemei Chen, Hongyue Liu, Quanxing Wang, Meng Guo, Jianhua Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past decade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has emerged as a revolutionary immunotherapeutic approach to combat cancer. This therapy constructs a CAR on the surface of T cells through genetic engineering techniques. The CAR is formed from a combination of antibody-derived or ligand-derived domains and T-cell receptor (TCR) domains. This enables T cells to specifically bind to and activate against tumor cells. However, the efficacy of CAR-T cells in solid tumors remains inconclusive due to several challenges such as poor tumor trafficking, infiltration, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). In response, CAR natural killer (CAR-NK) and CAR macrophages (CAR-M) have been developed as complementary strategies for solid tumors. CAR-NK cells do not require HLA compatibility, demonstrate reduced toxicity, and are thus seen as potential substitutes for CAR-T cells. Furthermore, CAR-M immunotherapy is also being researched and has shown phagocytic capabilities and tumor-antigen presentation. This study discusses the features, advantages, and limitations of CAR-T, CAR-NK, and CAR-M cells in the treatment of solid tumors and suggests prospective solutions for enhancing the efficacy of CAR host-cell-based immunotherapy.Keywords: CAR-T, CAR-NK, CAR-M, Solid tumor

Introduction

Over the past decade, advancements in cancer molecular medicine and immuno-oncology have led to the development of targeted therapies, paving the way for precision medicine as a more specific and less toxic cancer treatment modality [1–3]. Immunotherapy has significantly advanced cancer treatment by enhancing the functionality of the immune system to identify and eradicate malignant cells [4]. Immune cells can be engineered to express cell surface receptors that recognize and destroy tumor cells [5]. These genetically modified immune cells are then redirected via the CARs to attack tumor cells [6–8].

CAR is a synthetic cell surface receptor comprising four domains: an extracellular antigen-binding domain, a hinge region, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain [9]. CAR binds target cell surface antigens independently of major histocompatibility complex (MHC), initiating the primary signal for immune cell activation [10]. CAR genes can be introduced into specific immune cells using viral or non-viral methods, and these engineered cells are subsequently expanded and reinfused into the patient.

Currently, approved CAR-T cell therapies primarily target lymphoid malignancies [11–14]. CD19-targeted CAR-T cells have been reported to induce complete remission in up to 85% of patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [15]. Moreover, CAR-T cells targeting large B cell lymphoma have also been approved for second-line therapy following chemotherapy failure [16]. The efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy in hematological malignancies suggests potential applications in other types of cancer. Numerous ongoing clinical trials are exploring CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors, including glioblastoma [17], lung cancer [18], liver cancer [19], gastric cancer [20], renal cancer [21], prostate cancer [22], and neuroblastoma [23].

Despite the immunotherapeutic approach showing promising outcomes, this treatment modality is limited by low cytotoxic T cell tumor infiltration, inadequate T cell recruitment due to abnormal chemokines secreted by solid tumor cells, and the immunosuppressive TME [24]. Additional obstacles include on-target, off-tumor toxicities, cytokine release syndrome (CRS), and other adverse events such as tumor lysis syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [25–28]. Innovative strategies to overcome these limitations are currently being explored. Research is expanding from CAR-T to include alternative cellular therapies such as CAR-NK and CAR-M, leveraging the unique properties of NK cells and macrophages in the TME [29–31]. This study discusses the current status, challenges, and prospects of CAR-T, CAR-NK, and CAR-M in the treatment of solid tumors, highlighting the potential advantages of CAR-NK and CAR-M over CAR-T cells in this context.

CAR‑T cell therapy in solid tumors: challenges and recent advances

Limitations of CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors

CAR-T cell therapy has emerged as a revolutionary pillar in the treatment of hematological malignancies, garnering substantial interest due to its potential applications in solid tumors [15, 32]. Modified receptors that specifically target tumor antigens are incorporated into genetically engineered T cells derived from either patients or healthy donors to selectively identify and eradicate tumor cells upon reinfusion into the patients [33]. Current research are primarily focused on the application different targets in CAR-T cell therapy for a variety of solid tumors, with targets such as lung-specific antigen X (LUNX) for lung cancer, glypican 3 (GPC3) for liver cancer, claudin 18.2 (CLDN18.2) for gastric cancer, and carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX) for renal cell carcinoma [34–37]. These articles aim to overcome CAR-T therapy’s efficacy limitations, despite its susceptibility to antigen escape due to single-targeting. Additionally, the scarcity of specific tumor targets, inadequate expansion of CAR-T cells in vivo, and the suppressive TME further constrain the effective application of CAR-T therapy.

The TME is characterized by dense fibrotic stroma, hypoxic and acidic conditions, high interstitial pressure, nutrient scarcity, and the presence of immunosuppressive cells [38, 39]. In detail, the tumor stroma, marked by heightened density and rigidity, conspires with a dysregulated vascular network to form an impenetrable physical barrier. Concurrently, the diminished expression of adhesion molecules ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 on the surface of tumor vascular endothelial cells hampers the transendothelial traversal of CAR-T cells [40, 41]. Furthermore, tumor cells resident in the TME escalate the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, compete with CAR-T cells for critical nutrients such as amino acids and glucose, and contribute to the establishment of an acidic, hypoxic milieu within the TME [42–47].

Within the TME, a cadre of immunosuppressive cells, notably myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), secrete an array of immunosuppressive cytokines that attenuate the activity and efficacy of CAR-T cells [48, 49]. Moreover, the actions of MDSCs and tumor cells competitively consume nutrients, particularly L-arginine, which is crucial for effector cell activation. The depletion of L-arginine, driven by the upregulation of arginase and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) by MDSCs, results in a nutrient-poor environment that hinders the activation and proliferation of effector cells, thereby compromising the success of adoptive cell transfer therapies [50]. Consequentially, immunosuppressive TME leads to inadequate infiltration, poor cytotoxic functions and even exhaustion of CAR-T cells, ultimately compromising the anti-tumor therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 1).

Mechanism of TME preventing the spread of CAR-T. Inhibitory factors within the TME that impede CAR-T cell efficacy, including the dense fibrotic stroma formed by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), metabolic challenges due to hypoxia, acidic pH, and toxic byproducts, and immune checkpoint molecular such as PD-1 and CTLA-4. On one hand, MDSCs consume nutrients L-arginine, which is crucial for effector cell activation, while also secreting like TGF-β and IL-10 together with other immunosuppressive cells. On another hand, MDSCs directly inhibit effector cells through the engagement of NKG2D ligands on their surfaces. The disorganized, tortuous vessels hinder nutrient and oxygen delivery as well as CAR-T cell infiltration. Additionally, the downregulation of adhesion molecules like ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 on tumor endothelial cells (TECs) impairs CAR-T cell interaction with and transmigration across the vascular endothelium

Novel strategies of CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors

To address the above-mentioned challenges, multifaceted approaches have been adopted to enhance the efficacy of CAR-T cells in antigen recognition and achieve potent cytotoxicity in the TME. Specifically, CAR constructs were optimized, innovative therapeutic combination strategies were identified, and the inhibitory conditions were modulated to enhance the specificity, infiltration, and efficacy of CAR-T cell treatment [51–55].

Enhancement of recognition and targeting

Tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) are major targets for CAR-T therapy [56–59]. However, the expression of TAAs on non-tumor cells raises concerns regarding the on-target, off-tumor toxicity (OTOT) of CAR-T cells, potentially harming normal tissues. In addition to developing more tumor-specific antigens (TSAs), various methods are being explored to mitigate these risks, including novel CAR structures utilizing Boolean logic gates, conditional control based on acoustic or photonic waves in the design, and localized administration of CAR-T cells [60].

CAR-T cells were engineered with Boolean logic gates, which employ mathematical operations such as “IF/THEN” and “AND”, offering promising strategies to address tumor heterogeneity and antigen escape (Fig. 2). Novel logic CAR strategy replacing traditional CD3ζ domains with intracellular proximal T cell signaling molecules of LAT and SLP-76 was investigated. Though this LINK CAR structure demonstrated significant potential for eliminating ROR1+CD19+Nalm6 cells in vivo with minimal on-target, off-tumor toxicity, it expands the range of molecules that can be targeted with CAR-T cells, and will enable these powerful therapeutic agents to be used for solid tumors [61].

Mechanism of Boolean logic CAR-T cells. This processing unit within the CAR-T cell employs a synthetic logic gate framework to integrate signals, enabling the cell to initiate a response upon antigen engagement. This response can occur independently or in conjunction with user-controlled signals. The sophisticated output functions of this system include inducing anti-tumor effects through effector mechanisms, precise recognition of targeted cells, and a programmed cell death mechanism through caspase activation

Additionally, externally controlled switch activation modules have been developed for antigen recognition by CAR-T cells, incorporating heat shock proteins (HSPs) or photosensitive elements. In detail, activation by near-infrared (NIR) light at 980 nm and blue light from upconversion nanoplates (UCNPs) enables the functional assembly of split CAR components, enabling spatiotemporal control over the anti-tumor immune response (Fig. 3). Similarly, an acoustic control switch system was developed by utilizing a dual promoter, comprising an HSP-driven enhanced green fluorescent protein and a phosphoglycerate kinase 1 promoter-driven red fluorescent protein, which initiates CAR gene transcription upon exposure to localized, short-pulse ultrasound. This technology has enabled localized tumor tissue destruction by CAR-T cells equipped with light or sound-responsive switches [62, 63].

Mechanism of LiCAR-T cells. The LiCAR-T cells incorporate UCNPs that regulate its activation. When exposed to specific light wavelengths (470–980 nm), the UCNPs efficiently activate both Signal 1 and Signal 2, resulting in the “ON” state. Conversely, in the absence of light, the LiCAR-T cells remain in the “OFF” state, thereby preventing unintentional activation of LiCAR-T cells

Improvement of trafficking and cytotoxicity

Exhaustion and poor tumor infiltration limit the efficacy of CAR-T cells post-adoptive transfer. CAR-T cell migration and cytotoxicity are determined by chemokines secreted by tumor cells, chemokine receptors (CCRs), and cytokines expressed by effector T cells. Therefore, CAR-T cell therapy can be optimized by expressing appropriate CCRs and cytokines to increase their infiltration into the TME and sustain anti-tumor activity. Studies have shown that CAR-T cells engineered to secrete cytokines effectively achieved high expansion of CAR-T cells in vitro with improved infiltration [64, 65]. Furthermore, in a phase I clinical trial following treatment with CAR-T cells secreting IL-7 and CCL19 (7 × 19 CAR-T), one patient with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma achieved complete tumor disappearance, another advanced pancreatic carcinoma patient also made a partial recovery without CRS [19]. Hence, membrane-anchored cytokines can be applied in CAR-T cell engineering to address this issue [66]. In addition, multiple superkines were designed to overcome the characteristics of short half-life and low affinity of natural cytokines in CAR-T cell modification, opening new potential in engineering CAR-T cells [67–69].

Recent research has highlighted the efficacy of pattern recognition receptor agonists in augmenting T-cell activation [70, 71]. In a mouse model of B16-h19 melanoma, CAR-T cells were engineered to deliver RN7SL1 by incorporating a U6 promoter downstream of the CAR sequence to drive the transcription of RN7SL1. This immunostimulatory non-coding RNA activates RIG-I/MDA5 signaling by virtue of its 5’ triphosphate end and extensive secondary structure, which mimics viral double-stranded RNA. The RN7SL1 secreting CAR-T cells showed significant upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes, inducing the expansion and effector-memory differentiation of CAR-T cells. This strategy demonstrated promising clinical therapeutic significance, leading to tumor killing and recruitment of various immune cells in the TME [72].

Alleviating TME suppression

The TME exhibits marked vascular heterogeneity, excessive vascular proliferation, a high density of stromal cells, and immunosuppressive cells, which hamper the effective infiltration and function of CAR-T cells within tumor tissues [73]. To circumvent these barriers, innovative strategies targeting the tumor stroma have been devised. Particularly, a retroviral CAR construct was developed to specifically target mouse fibroblast activation protein [74]. Additionally, bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody against the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor, was employed to curtail tumor angiogenesis [75, 76].

Furthermore, immunosuppressive cells contribute to the suppressive TME. Tregs, MDSCs, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which exert immune inhibitory effects through various pathways. Therefore, targeting immunosuppressive cells in the TME may augment the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy. In addition, TGF-β secreted by Treg cells was found to be a major mediator of CAR-T cell immune suppression. Two strategies have been proposed to overcome immune suppression: the disruption of TGF-β receptors on CAR-T cells and the engineering of CAR-T cells to express a dominant-negative form of the TGFβ receptor (TGFβRIIDN) into the CAR-T cell construct which allows the CAR-T cells to remain functional even in the presence of TGF-β [77]. The emergence of CRISPR/Cas9, a simple, flexible and effective gene editing technology, makes genetic modification of CAR-T cells a reality. Recent studies utilized CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock out the TGF-β2 receptor, revealing a marked enhancement in the tumor-clearing capabilities of CAR-T cells within TGF-β-rich environments. Similar results were achieved in both a CRL5826 cell line-derived NPG mouse model and a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model of pancreatic cancer [78]. Genome editing technology based on CRISPR/CAS9 has a good application prospect in alleviating TME Suppression, potentiating CAR-T cell function and reducing toxicity [79].

Combination immunotherapies with CAR-T

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) augment the antitumor activity of CAR-NK cells by breaking through tumor and, more specifically, by dismantling the molecular barrier employ to evade immune system detection, and ameliorating the immunosuppressive effects of TME [80–82]. To overcome the downregulation or even loss of tumor antigen expression on the surface of tumor cells, CAR-T cell therapy is often combined with OVs or vaccine-delivered antigens to enhance the treatment’s effectiveness. The synergistic mechanism of OVs and cellular therapies is depicted in Fig. 4.

The synergistic mechanism of OVs and cellular therapies. OVs replicate and lyse within infected tumor cells, leading to the direct reduction of tumor cell numbers. This lytic effect not only diminishes the tumor cell population but may also release tumor antigens that can be recognized by the immune system, thereby activating an immune response against the tumor. This enhances the infiltration of endogenous as well as adoptively transferred effector cells and promotes the presentation of tumor antigens by antigen presenting cells (APCs)

In contrast to CAR-T cell monotherapy, the virus effectively breaks through the tumor cells and induces tumor debulking by destroying the molecular shield and reverting the immunosuppressive TME to a pro-inflammatory environment [83]. For instance, a previous study used OVs engineered to express CXCL-11 in conjunction with CAR-T cell therapy, which resulted in pronounced tumor-suppressive effects, effectively attracting immune cells in a human PDX glioblastoma mouse model [84]. The integration of tumor vaccines represents a strategic advancement aimed at amplifying the therapeutic impact of CAR-T cells. Tumor vaccines significantly enhance the antigen-presenting capacity of tumors, markedly improving the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate malignant cells [85]. The applications of tumor vaccines also contribute to reduce immune escape caused by tumor cell mutations [86–88] Tumor cells can evade the immune response and impair the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cells by upregulating immune checkpoint ligands, such as programmed death PD-L1, thereby facilitating tumor immune escape [89, 90]. Therefore, Chen J et al.. developed a bispecific single-chain antibody that targets both PD-1 and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2). When secreted by CAR-T cells, the antibody maintained a high concentration at the tumor site, allowing for better T-cell infiltration and activity against the tumor. which demonstrated synergistic effects with CAR-T therapy [91].

Artificial intelligence utilizing in CAR-T designation

Artificial intelligence (AI), an emerging technology, holds promise in overcoming numerous challenges inherent to CAR-T cell therapy.

A visual AI-based technology, timelapse imaging microscopy in nanowell grids (TIMING), was employed to integrate dynamic imaging of thousands of T cells and their interactions with tumor cells This approach, in combination with confocal microscopy and single-cell RNA sequencing data, provided valuable insights for identifying clinically effective CAR-T cell subpopulations, specifically CD8-fit T cells [92].

In addition, AI demonstrates potential in the structural design of CAR-T cells. By leveraging de novo protein design, researchers have engineered novel antigen binders from scratch to replace the single-chain fragment variable in traditional CAR constructs [93]. The de novo design of binding domains for specific tumor antigens may enhance the antitumor efficacy of CAR-T cell therapies against solid cancers. CAR-Toner, an AI-based tool for calculating positively charged patches (PCPs), integrates protein databases, structural biology, and advanced deep learning models [94]. This tool enables researchers to rapidly and efficiently design optimized CAR structures, thereby significantly accelerating the development process of CAR-T therapy.

Furthermore, AI can be employed to automate CAR-T cell production, thereby reducing manufacturing and delivery times and consequently increasing the number of who can be patients treated [95]. Utilizing the glass-support planar lipid bilayer system combined with ML-based data analysis, AI can assist in quantifying the quality of CAR immunological synapse, there by assessing the efficacy of CAR-T [96].

CAR-NK cell therapy in solid tumors: advantages and recent advances

Advantages of CAR-NK cells

NK cells are integral components of the innate immune system and participate in immune surveillance through diverse pathways, utilizing mechanisms similar to cytotoxic T-lymphocytes to kill cancer cells with different target recognition mechanisms [31]. Unrestricted by MHC constraints, NK cells can be derived from various sources, including NK-92 cells, umbilical cord blood (UCB), and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [97]. Hence, “off-the-shelf” CAR-NK cells can be manufactured and infused to patients on-demand [98]. Additionally, NK cells possess advantageous characteristics, such as the ability to infiltrate tumor tissues, cytolytic ability, and minimal side effects such as CRS, Graft versus host disease, and ICANS. NK cells are typically safer for clinical application and involve less toxicity compared to CAR-T cell products [99] (Fig. 5). Thus far, multiple clinical trials have been conducted to evaluate human primary NK cells in producing CAR-NK against specific tumor antigens, including roundabout homolog 1 (ROBO1), NKG2D, MSLN, HER2, and MUC1. In January 2022, the FDA granted an investigational new drug application approval for FT536, a CAR-NK cell therapy developed by Fate Therapeutics, to treat advanced solid tumors.

Advantages of CAR-NK cell therapy. NK cells trigger apoptosis in tumor cells via interactions such as FASL/FAS (upper left). “Off-the-shelf” CAR-NK cells can be sourced from established NK cell lines, UCB-derived NK cells, and stem cell-derived NK cells (bottom left), providing versatile options for immediate applications. In contrast to the MHC-restricted specificity of T cells (upper right), CAR-NK cells directly recognize and bind to tumor antigens, which is independent of MHC (bottom right). This enhances their ability to target and eliminate cancer cells without the constraints of traditional immune recognition pathways

CAR-NK cell therapy, while superior to CAR-T therapy in some respects, faces significant challenges. For instance, TGF-β in TME suppresses NKG2D receptor expression on NK cells, reducing their tumor recognition and cytotoxic capabilities [100]. Additionally, limited CAR-NK cell infiltration in solid tumors hampers their antitumor effects [101]. The short in vivo half-life of NK cells, typically less than ten days, constrains their therapeutic potential, while the cryopreservation and thawing sensitivity of ex vivo expanded NK cells necessitates stringent storage and transportation conditions [102].

New strategies for CAR-NK cell design and modification

Various strategies have been developed to improve the therapeutic effectiveness of CAR-NK cells against solid tumors, such as cytokine armoring, CAR constructs engineering, and integration with immunotherapy modalities.

Enhancement of CAR-NK cell activation and survival

Cytokines play a critical role as mediators of immune cell communication, significantly impacting the survival and functionality of NK cells. Therefore, IL-2, IL-15, and IL-21 are commonly employed with stimulatory cytokine transgenes to enhance NK cell proliferation. Notably, CAR-NK cells programmed to express soluble IL-15 have demonstrated superior cytotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo, resulting in markedly prolonged survival of mice bearing pancreatic cancer xenografts [103]. In comparative studies, CAR-NK cells engineered with membrane-bound IL-15 (mbIL15) exhibited a pronounced anti-tumor effect in vivo [104]. Furthermore, superkines constitute a range of engineered cytokines with higher stability and half-life and are used in CAR-NK cells, addressing the limitations of natural cytokines. Preliminary in vitro assessments have yielded encouraging results, demonstrating the potent and selective anti-tumor capabilities of CAR-NK cells endowed with an IL-15 superagonist. Specifically, the introduction of IL-15 superagonist-expressing CAR-NK cells has been shown to invigorate the proliferation and functionality of both innate lymphocytes and cytotoxic T cells, significantly enhancing the collective anti-tumor response [105]. Chimeric co-stimulatory converting receptor (CCCR)-NK cells were found to inhibit lung cancer growth in xenograft mouse models after switching the immunosuppressive negative PD-1 signal to an activating signal, thereby overcoming the immunosuppressive TME and enhancing survival [106].

Improvement of the tumor trafficking ability of CAR-NK cells

CAR-T cell assembly and migration of CAR-NK cells depend on chemokines secreted by tumor cells and CCRs. Poor CAR-NK cells targeting to tumor reduces he efficacy of CAR cell therapy. By engineering CAR-NK cells to express chemokine receptors, for example, expressing chemokine receptors such as CXCR1, CXCR4, and CXCR3, can enhance the tumor-homing capacity of CAR-NK cells which significantly imped the function and persistence of CAR-NK cells. Accordingly, in subcutaneous and intraperitoneal xenografts, tumor infiltration was improved through CXCR1-modified MSLN and GD2-targeting CAR-NK cells, respectively [107]. CAR-NK cells expressing CXCR4 displayed increased infiltration and persistence, with significant tumor toxicity [108].

Construction of CAR-NK cell-specific CAR structures

To enhance the functionality of CAR-NK cells, studies proposed to enrich CARs with specific NK cell signaling domains like 2B4, DAP-10, and DAP-12. These modified NK cells have shown higher cytotoxicity and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion compared to CAR-T cell constructs. In a xenograft ovarian mouse model expressing MSLN, CAR-NK cells with NKG2D and 2B4 receptors showed increased cytotoxicity against MSLN-expressing tumors [109]. PSCA-specific CAR-NK cells equipped with DAP12 exerted higher anti-tumor activity compared to CD3ζ- CAR-NK cells targeting PSCA-positive tumors. A novel CAR construct was designed to target the suppressive effects of TGF-β, which combines the extracellular and transmembrane domains of the TGF-β type II receptor with the intracellular domain of NKG2D. Consequently, NK-92 cells demonstrated improved anti-tumor efficacy and elevated IFN-γ secretion in a hepatocellular carcinoma xenograft model [110].

Promotion of the formation of CAR-NK cell immune synapses

As immune cells engage with their targets, an intricate assembly of transmembrane molecules coalesces on the cellular interface, giving rise to a complex known as the immune synapse. This structure is supported by adhesion molecules and plays an essential role in the elimination of tumor cells by NK cells [111]. Recent advancements include the strategic incorporation of a PDZ binding motif (PDZbm) within CAR constructs, a modification that promotes immune synapse formation and CAR-NK cell polarization. This enhancement was found to lead to more effective tumor cell lysis and improved survival rates in a range of tumor models [112]. Moreover, nanobodies exhibit notable stability and low immunogenicity and have emerged as powerful single-domain antibodies. They serve as potent single-domain antibodies that strengthen the interaction between CAR-NK cells and targets by enlarging the immune synapse, with a broad array of treatments for malignancies [113].

Improvement of the metabolic fitness of CAR-NK cells

The TME is characterized by acidic and hypoxic conditions, nutrient deficiency, and toxic metabolite, and severely limits the proliferation, differentiation, and functionality of CAR-NK cells. Hence, enhancing nutritional uptake and anti-oxidative defense represent promising strategies to optimize the efficacy of CAR-NK cells. Peroxiredoxin-1 (PRDX1) has been identified as an essential element missing in NK cells’ anti-oxidative arsenal. CAR-NK cells engineered to target PD-L1 and overexpress PRDX1 have shown enhanced anti-tumor activity against breast cancer cells in oxidative stress conditions [114].

Furthermore, the metabolic adaptation of CAR-NK cells within the TME can be enhanced by increasing the assimilation of essential amino acids. Upregulating the expression of amino acid transporter proteins, including SLC1A5 and the SLC7A2/SLC7A5, promotes the growth, proliferation, and survival of CAR-NK cells. These alterations substantially enhance the viability and longevity of CAR-NK cells within the TME, potentially improving clinical outcomes for cancer patients [115].

CAR-NK cell combination therapy

The combination of OVs and engineered NK cell therapies can achieve a more pronounced anti-tumor effect, representing a novel approach to cancer treatment [116, 117]. A profound synergistic anti-tumor effect was observed with the co-administration of OV-IL15C and EGFR-targeted CAR-NK cells derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in an orthotopic glioblastoma multiforme mouse model [118]. Cancer cells can evade the immune system by upregulating immune checkpoint proteins [119]. Consequently, monoclonal antibodies targeting these checkpoints may be beneficial in treating solid tumors. The cytotoxicity of CAR-NK cells and checkpoint monoclonal antibodies against solid tumors in vitro demonstrated a significant increase when used in combination [120]. CTLA-4, has been identified as a critical factor for NK cell functionality. The inhibition of CTLA-4 combined with CAR-NK cells shows enhanced anti-tumor potency [121]. Integrating traditional chemotherapy with CAR-NK cell therapy may potentially achieve decreased tumor burden [97, 122]. Agents such as cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil can elevate the expression of B7-H6 on tumor cells, thereby augmenting their susceptibility to NK cell-mediated cytolysis [123]. Overall, the clinical applications of CAR-NK cell combination therapies remain limited and are currently in the exploratory phase.

CAR-M therapy in solid tumor treatment: advantages and recent advances

Advantages and structure of CAR-M

Tumor immunotherapy predominantly emphasizes the role of adaptive immune cells [124, 125]. However, a relatively small number of studies have investigated innate immune cells. Macrophages are integral components of the mononuclear phagocyte system renowned for their phagocytic and antigen-presentation roles, facilitating the clearance of senescent, damaged, aberrant cells and cellular debris. The applications of CARs to macrophages offer a promising avenue for enhancing cellular immunotherapy against solid tumors. Increasing macrophages’ phagocytic activity and antigen presentation by incorporating CARs may result in improved specificity in tumor targeting and heightened anti-tumor efficacy [126] (Fig. 6).

CAR-M cell killing mechanisms. CAR-M identifies TAAs on the surface of cancer cells through CARs, initiating phagocytosis. Upon antigen recognition, CAR-M activates intracellular signaling cascades, including NF-κB activation, which in turn stimulates the production and release of inflammatory cytokines to activate T cells, and induce and maintain the M1 polarization state of macrophages. MMPs released by CAR-M can degrade the ECM. Concurrently, the interaction of CAR-M cells with pathogens or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) through TLRs enhances their antigen-presenting capabilities, further facilitating T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Moreover, chemokines are released by macrophages to recruit other immune cells again, exerting immune effects

Similar to CAR-NK, CAR-M can be generated from a variety of sources, including peripheral blood, iPSCs, and the human leukemia monocytic cell line THP-1. Moreover, PBMC-derived macrophages secrete various proinflammatory cytokines and inflammatory surface markers [127]. A previous study revealed that iPSCs could differentiate into CAR-expressing macrophages. CAR-iMacs also exhibit innate immune functions, including the repolarization of M2 phenotype to pro-inflammatory M1 in an antigen-dependent manner, as well as phagocytosis and anti-tumor activity [128]. THP-1 could generate M1 macrophages upon stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and IFN-γ, offering a controlled and reliable cell source for CAR-M production [129]. Besides, CAR-M possesses the capacity to modulate the phenotype of TAMs, which are significantly correlated with the adverse prognosis of various malignancies [130–132]. Additionally, CAR-M demonstrated enhanced migratory and infiltration capacity, overcoming the challenges posed by dense matrix and immune suppression [133–135]. Unlike lymphocyte-based therapies, macrophages could remodel the extracellular matrix and produce matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which could degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM). Collectively, CAR-M leverages the distinct properties of macrophages, particularly their phagocytic ability, offering a unique advantage over CAR-T and CAR-NK cell therapies.

To date, a few clinical trials have focused on CAR-M and a few domains of a specific CAR structure tailored exclusively for macrophages. While the intracellular signaling domain may differ, the extracellular antigen-binding domain, hinge domain, transmembrane domain, and intracellular domain of CAR-M and CAR-T cells remain largely similar. CD3ζ is widely incorporated in CAR constructs and facilitates the transduction of phagocytosis when conjugated to an SH2 domain such as Syk and PI3K, and enhances antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis when bound to the Fc receptor gamma subunit [136]. Multiple MEGF10 initiate intracellular signaling cascades through tyrosine phosphorylation, resulting in enhanced activation and phagocytic efficiency of CAR-M [137, 138]. Additionally, recent studies have introduced a promising CAR design that triggers macrophage activation via Toll-like receptors 4 (TLR4) or IFN-γ receptors, offering potential pathways for further enhancing the efficacy of CAR-M in therapeutic applications [139]. In addition to the foundational architecture of CARs, recent progress in bioengineering has yielded additional features [9], such as chemokine receptors, proinflammatory cytokines, and suicidal or inhibitory genes, aiming to increase the anti-tumor effectiveness of CAR immune cells and reduce potential toxicities.

Challenges of CAR-M within the TME

CAR-M therapy for solid tumors is confronted with a multitude of obstacles. In terms of bioengineering, the antiviral mechanisms of macrophages impair efficient viral transduction in CAR-M generation. Recent developments in gene transfection techniques have spurred the utilization of various viral and non-viral engineering strategies to address that. Specifically, engineered lentiviral virions incorporating Vpx, an accessory protein, were found to effectively transport transgenes into myeloid cells [140]. Researchers have employed a chimeric adenovirus vector, Ad5f35, to mediate efficient gene transfer into human macrophages [141].

M1 macrophages play a crucial role in tumor cell destruction through phagocytosis and the release of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, triggering the activation of TLRs. Furthermore, M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory IL-12, catalyzing NK cell activity and promoting responses from both Th1 and tumor-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. As tumor antigen-presenting cells, M1 macrophages facilitate the induction of an adaptive anti-tumor immune response. Therefore, M1 macrophages exert beneficial effects, as opposed to M2 macrophages, which are considered a pro-tumor phenotype exerting detrimental effects [142]. Owing to the inherent plasticity allowing for phenotype interchange, CAR-M often undergoes differentiation into the M1 or M2 phenotype within the TME. The stromal components in the TME promote macrophage differentiation. In the process of tissue remodeling, CAFs recruit macrophages to the tumor site by secreting factors such as CXCL14 and chitinase-3-like protein 1 (Chi3L1), and induce the polarization of macrophages towards M2 macrophages [143]. This transition potentially exacerbates the formation of an immune-suppressive microenvironment, thereby facilitating cancer progression and metastasis. Consequently, the reprogramming of M2 TAMs to M1 macrophages represents a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for treating solid tumors [144]. Upon recognizing LPS, macrophage interacts with adaptor molecules through the toll/IL-1R (TIR) domain of TLR4, triggering the nuclear translocation of NF-κB/p65 and inducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1 A, IL-1B, IL-6, IL-12, CCL8, and TNF. Leveraging this TLR4 signaling pathway, researchers integrated the intracellular TIR domain of TLR4 with the CD3ζ domain to stimulate and sustains the M1-like phenotype of iMACs upon antigen engagement, consequently boosting antitumor responses [145].

Moreover, macrophages may secrete various interleukins such as IL-1 and IL-6 upon excessive activation in vivo, leading to CRS [146]. Additionally, CAR-M cells may cause off-target toxicity due to their widespread distribution [147]. Therefore, further research is required to enhance the production of CAR-M cells and ascertain their safety.

Modification strategies of CAR-M in solid tumor treatment

Recombinant proteins or cytokines are needed to increase the proliferation and differentiation of CAR-M. Research revealed that IFN-γ-induced macrophages have limited tumor-killing efficacy, however, feasibility and safety [148]. CAR-M cells pre-treated with a combination of LPS and IFN-γ exhibited significant phagocytosis and tumor-killing efficacy in vitro and in vivo [149]. Simultaneously, gene editing is expected to substantially improve the function of macrophages. For example, the knockout of signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) enhanced the phagocytic activity of macrophages towards tumor cells. Similarly, targeting key molecules in the downstream signaling pathway of SIRPα, such as the tyrosine phosphatase SHP1, could also strengthen the function of macrophages [150].

The ECM is composed of intricately organized fibers, proteoglycans, glycoproteins, glycosaminoglycans, and additional macromolecules, and obstructs the migration of immune cells [151]. CAR-M cells expressing MMPs such as MMP9, MMP10, and MMP12 were engineered to enhance infiltration. MMPs could degrade the fibrotic matrix, reduce tumor collagen content, and recruit various immune cells to the TME. CAR-147 MMP-expressing macrophages targeting HER2+ tumor cells effectively reduced tumor growth without affecting phagocytic activity or cytokine production [152]. Additionally, Niu et al. developed CAR-M cells expressing CCR7 natural ligand, targeting CCR7-expressing immunosuppressive cells, which resulted in the recruitment of T cells, heightened production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and inhibition of tumor growth [153, 154].

Combination therapy with CAR-M

Clinical studies have confirmed the importance of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in various hematological malignancies. A combination of ICB and CAR-M may revolutionize therapy. Tumors resist macrophage phagocytosis by expressing the checkpoint molecule CD47, a so-called “Don’t eat me” signal. CD47 blockade combined with CAR-M effectively eradicated osteosarcoma and small-cell lung cancer, extending survival [155]. CAR-M cells targeting HER2 and PD-1 inhibitors effectively suppressed CT26 colon cancer by increasing tumor immune infiltration of T cells [156, 157]. Standard cancer chemotherapy induces immunogenic cell death in tumor cells by releasing DAMPs, inducing an anti-tumor immune response [158, 159]. Therefore, CAR-M cells combined with radiotherapy could exert synergistic effects.

Conclusion

Macrophages play versatile roles in the TME, where signals from tumor cells and other components can modulate their function, potentially leading to a loss of anti-tumor activity and the formation of tumor-associated macrophages that foster tumor growth [31, 160]. CAR-M therapy, still in its exploratory phase for solid tumors, is represented by a few studies on Clinicaltrials.gov. Notably, the CT-0508 project uses autologous PBMC-derived macrophages engineered to express HER2-CAR and exhibit M1-type characteristics, suggesting a potential to overcome solid tumor immunotherapy challenges by targeting antigen-overexpressing cancer cells and modulating the TME to expose neoantigens to T cells [161]. Meanwhile, CAR-NK cell therapy, with its dual cytotoxicity mechanism against cancer cells, has shown minimal adverse reactions but requires larger, long-term trials due to limited sample sizes and safety data. Early clinical results indicate disease stabilization or partial remission in some patients, yet the efficacy is constrained, as seen in the NCT03383978 trial with only 56% disease stabilization and a median survival of 31 weeks [162]. The NCT03656705 trial observed lesion stabilization without significant reduction, possibly due to TME suppressive effects, indicating a need to enhance CAR-NK cell cytotoxicity and persistence [163]. The NCT03415100 trial reported short-term effects, underscoring the need for data on CAR-NK cell persistence, which may be insufficient for long-term anti-tumor effects [164]. Future research will focus on improving CAR-NK cell survival and expansion in vivo. A summary of these clinical trials is presented in Table 1.

In the context of cancer immunotherapy, CAR-T, CAR-NK, and CAR-M cells each face distinct challenges. CAR-T cells are hindered by heterogeneity and loss of tumor antigens, limited tumor infiltration, and an immunosuppressive TME that impedes survival and resistance. Additionally, they carry risks of CRS, on-target/off-tumor toxicity, tumor lysis syndrome, and neurotoxicity. CAR-NK cells, while offering advantages such as lower risk of GvHD and “off-the-shelf” availability, struggle with limited tumor infiltration and the same immunosuppressive barriers present in the TME, and face a shorter lifespan compared to other cell types. CAR-M cells, despite their potential to infiltrate tumors and modulate the TME, are at risk of CRS toxicity and on-target/off-tumor toxicity, and their M2 phenotype can adversely affect tumor outcomes. Overall, while each cellular therapy brings unique advantages, they also confront significant obstacles that need to be addressed to enhance their efficacy in cancer treatment.

Importantly, CAR-T, CAR-NK, and CAR-M models provide the benefit of simultaneously targeting different tumor antigens through each respective CAR-armored-cell therapy. Researchers are actively seeking to enhance the efficacy of CAR-cell therapies by employing a range of strategies, including the application of artificial intelligence AI. AI has the potential to overcome many of the challenges associated with CAR-cell therapy. On a larger scale, AI can facilitate the automation of CAR-T cell manufacturing processes, thereby reducing production and delivery times and potentially increasing the availability of treatments for more patients.
