Predictors of post-endoscopic submucosal dissection stricture in superficial esophageal cancer patients receiving oral steroid prophylaxis
Authors: Nan Dai, Gui-Fang Lu, Yan Yin, Yuan-Zhi Zhou, Ya-Han Zhao, Jia-Xin Ma, Xin-Guang Cao, Chang-Qing Guo, Ya-Rui Li, Mu-Dan Ren, Shui-Xiang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Identify risk factors for esophageal stricture persisting despite standard oral steroid prophylaxis after endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for large-area superficial esophageal cancer.Methods: Retrospective analysis of 202 patients undergoing ESD (≥ 2/3 circumferential resection) with postoperative oral steroids. Patients were stratified by stricture occurrence. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression evaluated lesion/ESD-related risk factors. Refractory stricture (RS; requiring ≥ 5 endoscopic dilations) and non-refractory stricture (NRS) groups were compared for endoscopic balloon/bougie dilation (EBD) continuation rates.Results: The overall stricture rate was 35.2% (71/202). Multivariate analysis identified four independent risk factors: longitudinal diameter ≥ 5 cm (OR 3.06, 95%CI 1.38–6.80), circumferential involvement > 4/5 (OR 6.41, 95%CI 2.79–14.67), entire circumferential involvement (OR 54.91, 95%CI 11.02-273.68), and muscular injury (OR 21.89, 95%CI 4.04-118.58). EBD continuing rates at 1 year were 70% (RS) vs.22.9% (NRS); at 3 years, rates decreased to 20% (RS) and 2.1% (NRS). RS showed significantly higher EBD continuation (Chi-square 8.404, P = 0.004).Conclusion: In patients with large superficial esophageal cancers involving ≥ 2/3 circumference who receive oral steroid prophylaxis, circumferential involvement ≥ 4/5 or entire circumferential involvement, longitudinal lesions ≥ 5 cm, and muscular injury independently predict steroid-resistant strictures post-ESD. High-risk patients require cautious ESD planning and novel stricture prevention strategies. RS necessitates prolonged EBD management, reflecting poorer therapeutic response.

Introduction

Esophageal carcinoma (EC) represents a significant global health burden, ranking as the 11th most common malignancy and the 7th leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has emerged as the gold-standard treatment for superficial esophageal neoplasms confined to the mucosa (T1a) or superficial submucosa (T1b-sm1), offering comparable oncologic outcomes to esophagectomy while preserving organ function and minimizing surgical morbidity2,3. Despite its therapeutic advantages, post-ESD esophageal stricture (ES) remains a critical limitation, particularly in extensive resections involving >3/4 of the esophageal circumference where ES rates exceed 90%4, reaching near-universal incidence following circumferential ESD5–7 This adverse event prompted the 2017 Japanese Esophageal Cancer Guidelines to exclude lesions involving >2/3 circumference from absolute ESD indications8. Recent advancements in ES prophylaxis have expanded ESD eligibility criteria through multimodal approaches: steroid administration (oral or intralesional)9, biodegradable stents10,11, and tissue engineering strategies (autografts, cell sheets, polyglycolic acid membranes)12–14. Systemic prednisolone administration has gained prominence due to its cost-effectiveness and ease of implementation15, enabling even circumferential resections to achieve satisfactory long-term functional outcomes when combined with proactive dilation16–18. Nevertheless, approximately 25% of patients develop refractory ES despite optimized steroid regimens19, compounded by corticosteroid-related adverse events including osteoporosis and glucose intolerance. While the efficacy of steroid prophylaxis is established, a critical and understudied question remains: even under standardized prophylactic therapy, which patients are at the highest risk for treatment failure? Current research priorities predominantly focus on prophylaxis optimization, leaving critical gaps in understanding risk factors for steroid-resistant ES. This knowledge deficit hinders risk stratification and targeted interventions for high-risk cohorts. To address this unmet need, we conducted a retrospective cohort study analyzing clinical and procedural predictors of steroid-refractory ES following extensive ESD (>2/3 circumference) in early EC. Through comprehensive regression modeling, we aimed to establish evidence-based criteria for individualized prevention strategies and enhanced postoperative surveillance protocols.

Materials and methods

This retrospective dual-center study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University and the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The study population comprised patients with superficial EC who underwent ESD with immediate postoperative oral steroid prophylaxis between January 2019 and June 2024.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathologically confirmed superficial EC with preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography(CT) confirmation of non-metastatic disease; (2) post-ESD mucosal defects encompassing ≥ 2/3 of the esophageal circumference; (3) final pathological diagnosis of high-grade squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (HSIN) or SCC according to WHO classification criteria; (4) availability of complete clinical follow-up data for ≥ 6 months.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) receipt of adjuvant therapies including repeat ESD, surgical resection, chemoradiotherapy, or radiofrequency ablation; (2) absence of standardized ES prevention measures; (3) implementation of alternative ES prevention strategies such as endoscopic stenting or mucosal transplantation. This study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. The ethics committee that approved this study and waived the consent requirement is the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval Number: XJTU1AF2021LSK-208).

ESD procedure

All ESD procedures were conducted by highly experienced endoscopists with comparable proficiency, each having over a decade of clinical practice and having independently performed more than 200 esophageal ESD surgeries. All operations were carried out under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation. Patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and the procedures utilized an Olympus GIF-Q260J electronic gastroscope (Olympus, Japan). For circumferential esophageal lesions, either the “tunneling technique“10,20 or “snare traction method”16 was employed as auxiliary approaches for submucosal dissection, depending on the specific circumstances. The detailed procedural steps are as follows (Fig. 1):①Lesion Marking: Following staining with 1.25% Lugol’s solution, the margin of the esophageal lesion was delineated using a Dual knife (KD-650 L, Olympus, Japan), marking a border 5 mm outside the lesion.②Submucosal Injection: A disposable injection needle (NM-200 L-0523, Olympus, Japan) was used to inject a solution consisting of 0.2 mL methylene blue (with a mass concentration of 10 g/L) mixed with 200 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution to elevate the lesion.③Mucosal Incision: Using the Dual knife, an incision was made approximately 5 mm outside the marked border to cut the mucosal edge, exposing the submucosal layer. The lesion was then carefully and progressively dissected from the muscularis propria using electrocautery until complete separation was achieved.④Specimen Handling: The resected specimen was fixed in formalin and sent for pathological examination to determine the nature of the lesion, as well as the involvement of its margins and base.⑤Post-Procedure Inspection: After the specimen was removed, the endoscope was reintroduced to inspect the resection site for any bleeding. Electrocoagulation biopsy forceps (FD-410LR, Olympus, Japan) were used for hemostasis when necessary. Postoperative Management: Patients were kept fasting with water restriction and received parenteral nutrition for 3 days. Acid suppression was achieved with intravenous proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) at a dosage of 40 mg every 12 h. Mucosal protection was provided orally after diet resumption using agents such as sucralfate or rebamipide. An indwelling gastric tube was placed for 3 days to facilitate gastrointestinal decompression. A gradual transition to a normal diet was implemented over the following 3 days, starting with a liquid diet.

ES prevention protocol

Starting from the 3rd day post-ESD, patients were administered oral prednisone acetate tablets at a dosage of 30 mg/day for a duration of 2 weeks. The dosage was then tapered down to 25 mg/day during the 3rd-4th week, followed by a reduction to 20 mg/day in the 5th week, 15 mg/day in the 6th week, 10 mg/day in the 7th week, and finally 5 mg/day in the 8th week. Concurrently, PPIs were administered at a dosage of 20 mg/day, along with oral calcium supplements, which were continued until the discontinuation of steroid therapy. Potassium supplementation was provided as needed based on the patient’s condition.

Follow-up protocol and definitions

Systematic esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-ESD. Patients presenting with dysphagia between scheduled visits underwent immediate endoscopic evaluation. Dysphagia severity was graded according to the Stooler classificationESD21. For patients with a grade of 2 or higher, endoscopic bougie/balloon dilation (EBD) is performed. The procedure involves inserting a guidewire, followed by the insertion of a silicone bougie or balloon along the guidewire for dilation. Definitions

ES: Defined as a narrowing with a diameter of less than 9.8 mm, through which a standard endoscope (GIF Q260, Olympus) cannot pass. Clinical Remission of ES: Maintenance of a dysphagia-free state for ≥ 6 months without the need for any intervention. EBD Times: The number of EBD sessions required to achieve ES remission (defined as maintaining an unobstructed esophageal lumen for more than 6 months). If the interval between two dilation sessions is less than 7 days, they are considered part of the same treatment cycle. Refractory benign esophageal strictures (RBES): failure to maintain patency after ≥ 5 EBD sessions to achieve sustained luminal patency for ≥ 6 months22. Classification was made at the end of the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were compared using χ2 test, adjusted Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were analyzed with t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression identified independent risk factors for ES in steroid-treated patients. Kaplan-Meier curves assessed freedom from EBD rates. Analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.3.1. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

From January 2019 to June 2024, after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 202 patients were ultimately included in this study (Fig. 3 presents the flowchart). The patients were divided into a stricture group (n = 71) and a non-stricture group (n = 131) based on the occurrence of ES. Among ES group, the majority first presented between postoperative days 20–30. Specifically, 34 patients (47.89%) were dilated due to symptomatic presentation, while the remaining 37 (52.11%) were diagnosed during the scheduled 1-month follow-up endoscopy. Table 1 outlines the univariate analysis results of clinicopathological factors between the two groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in gender distribution, Body mass index (BMI), area of lesion, procedure time, tumor depth (pathological diagnosis), en bloc resection rate, or curative resection rate between the groups. However, significant differences were noted in six indicators: age, longitudinal diameter, traction method, location, circumferential ESD, and muscular injury (P<0.1). These six factors, along with the depth of invasion (supplemented based on clinical expertise and previous literature), totaling seven factors, were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Based on the occurrence of RBES, the patients with ES were divided into a non-refractory stricture(NRS) group (n = 48) and a refractory stricture (RS) group (n = 10). A comparison of demographic information, lesion characteristics, and procedural outcomes between the two groups is presented in Table 3. Discussion

ES is one of the most common adverse events following ESD for large superficial ECs. Previous studies have consistently identified the circumferential extent of mucosal resection as an independent risk factor for postoperative stricture23,24. Other factors, such as muscle injury, a history of endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)/ESD, non-en bloc resection, infiltration into the muscularis mucosa (MM) or submucosa (SM), and concurrent pathological scarring, also contribute to the formation of ES25,26. The mechanism underlying esophageal stricture involves acute postoperative inflammatory responses and fibrous connective tissue hyperplasia. Steroids are commonly regarded as a preventive method for stricture during ESD due to their ability to accelerate epithelial cell regeneration, inhibit the exudation of inflammatory cells, and suppress the aggregation and release of inflammatory factors27,28. Despite the convenience and widespread application of oral steroids, their efficacy in preventing ES remains controversial. Yamaguchi et al.‘s study indicated that the rate of esophageal stricture prevention with oral prednisone after ESD was only 5.3% (1/19)29. In contrast, Takahashi et al. found that local steroid injection had no impact on the stricture rate following ESD for large early esophageal cancers but could reduce the number of EBD procedures30. A recent meta-analysis concluded that steroids did not demonstrate efficacy in preventing ES for circumferential lesions31, and other studies have also suggested that steroid treatment is not a protective factor in stricture risk models24,32,33. Therefore, it is worthwhile to explore the risk factors for ES that still occur despite steroid application.

A study involving 96 patients found that large circumferential mucosal defects (>5/6 of the esophageal circumference) and complete circumferential dissection increased the likelihood of stricture formation in patients undergoing preventive local triamcinolone injection after esophageal ESD34. Some studies consider circumferential mucosal dissection a risk factor for ES following oral or combined steroid use9,35,36. In China, oral hormone therapy is predominantly employed to prevent esophageal stricture. However, the high-risk factors for ES in patients receiving oral steroids alone remain unclear. Therefore, this study conducted a detailed analysis and discovered that, in addition to circumferential mucosal dissection, a longitudinal diameter ≥ 5 cm, lesion involvement of >4/5 of the circumference or complete circumferential involvement, and muscular injury were also independent risk factors for ES (P < 0.05). Endoscopists should pay heightened attention to such patients, exercising caution when performing ESD or adopting more effective stricture prevention methods.

Refractory benign esophageal strictures (RBES) are defined as the failure to achieve long-term relief of stricture despite undergoing EBD 5 times or more22. Following ESD for large-area early EC, some patients may develop refractory strictures despite preventive oral hormone therapy. Due to the limited number of cases, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was not conducted. However, univariate analysis can still provide insights to some extent. Compared to NRS group, the RS group exhibited a higher prevalence of muscle injury, circumferential dissection, and strictures located in the upper esophagus. This may be related to the gradual atrophy of the muscles involved in esophageal strictures, where muscle cells undergo dedifferentiation and transform into fibroblasts following muscle injury, accompanied by recurrent inflammation, fibrous connective tissue hyperplasia, and collagen deposition, leading to scar formation37. Additionally, a larger resection area implies greater mucosal loss, increasing the likelihood of stricture formation38. In summary, clinicians should pay particular attention to patients with these characteristics, as preventive oral hormone therapy alone may often be insufficient.

During the long-term process of EBD, some scholars have attempted to shorten the stricture dilation process by combining it with stent placement, radial scar incision, and local hormone injection39–41. However, the specific efficacy of these approaches still awaits high-quality prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Additionally, this study compared the EBD continuing rates at 1 and 3 years post-ESD. The EBD continuing rate in the RS group was significantly higher than that in the NRS group. All patients received preventive steroid therapy and consecutive EBD treatment after ESD, with ES successfully resolving within 2 years. This suggests that long-term follow-up and health education should be provided for NRS patients. Clinically, restenosis often occurs after the temporary relief of ES by EBD. Therefore, long-term observation is crucial during the process of ES resolution. However, due to the limited sample size, especially in the refractory stricture group, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and validated in larger prospective studies.

This study has certain limitations. Being a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, our findings require validation in a larger cohort. Additionally, due to the prolonged enrollment period of patients, the skills and techniques of endoscopists might have improved over time, potentially influencing the treatment outcomes. Furthermore, while there exists a primary standard protocol for EBD, in actual practice, procedures such as radial scar incision, local hormone injection, and stent placement might be performed during EBD based on individual circumstances. These interventions affect the total number of EBD sessions in the natural course of stricture management and were thus excluded from our current study. Nevertheless, it remains crucial to intervene and interrupt the stricture progression during EBD for refractory strictures, and we plan to conduct further research on this aspect. For high-risk patients who still develop strictures despite oral steroid therapy, whether adjusting the administration route, dosage, or combining multiple approaches for esophageal stricture prevention warrants in-depth exploration through prospective RCTs in the future.

In summary, meticulous preventive measures should be taken against esophageal stricture (ES) following ESD for large superficial esophageal cancers. In patients with large superficial esophageal cancers involving ≥ 2/3 circumference who receive oral steroid prophylaxis, longer lesions (≥ 5 cm), near circumferential(> 4/5) or complete circumferential resection, and muscular injury are independent risk factors for steroid-refractory stricture. These high-risk patients may require more aggressive prevention strategies beyond oral steroids.
