Enhancing Few-Shot Classification of Benchmark and Disaster Imagery with ATTBHFA-Net
Gao Yu Lee, Tanmoy Dam, Md.Meftahul Ferdaus, Daniel Puiu Poenar, Vu Duong

Abstract
The increasing frequency of natural and human-induced disasters necessitates advanced visual recognition techniques capable of analyzing critical photographic data. With progress in artificial intelligence and resilient computational systems, rapid and accurate disaster classification has become crucial for efficient rescue operations. However, visual recognition in disaster contexts faces significant challenges due to limited and diverse data from the difficulties in collecting and curating comprehensive, high-quality disaster imagery. Few-Shot Learning (FSL) provides a promising approach to data scarcity, yet current FSL research mainly relies on generic benchmark datasets lacking remote-sensing disaster imagery, limiting its practical effectiveness. Moreover, disaster images exhibit high intra-class variation and inter-class similarity, hindering the performance of conventional metric-based FSL methods. To address these issues, this paper introduces the Attention-based Bhattacharyya-Hellinger Feature Aggregation Network (ATTBHFA-Net), which linearly combines the Bhattacharyya coefficient and Hellinger distances to compare and aggregate feature probability distributions for robust prototype formation. The Bhattacharyya coefficient serves as a contrastive margin that enhances inter-class separability, while the Hellinger distance regularizes same-class alignment. This framework parallels contrastive learning but operates over probability distributions rather than embedded feature points. Furthermore, a Bhattacharyya-Hellinger distance-based contrastive loss is proposed as a distributional counterpart to cosine similarity loss, used jointly with categorical cross-entropy to significantly improve FSL performance. Experiments on four FSL benchmarks and two aerial disaster image datasets demonstrate the superior effectiveness and generalization of ATTBHFA-Net compared to existing approaches. Code repository will be available at https://github.com/GreedYLearner1146/ABHFA-Net.

Introduction

1.1
Computer Vision for Disaster Classification

The epoch of deep learning and Computer Vision (CV) has ushered in transformative advancements, augmenting the effectiveness and efficiency of conventional visual tasks such as scene classification, segmentation, and object detection, particularly within the realm of remote sensing
Valsan et al. 2020
. Although the primary source of remote sensing image data emanates from satellite systems, such as very High Resolution (VHR), Hyperspectral, and Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)-based images encompassing an extensive region of interest (RoI) (
Sun et al. 2021
,
Lee et al. 2024
), recent developments have witnessed the integration of imagery from Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV) and low-altitude aircraft into this image domain. Consequently, these data have become critical in the identification of disasters and subsequent damage assessment. This heightened relevance can be attributed to the escalating trends of global warming
Ramanathan et al. 2007
, which have catalyzed an increase in catastrophic events such as fire outbreaks, floods, landslides, and incidents leading to human injuries or diseases
Tan et al. 2020
. The diversity and magnitude of such disasters are aptly illustrated in Fig.

and Fig.
2
. This refined understanding, buttressed by a synthesis of empirical data and analytical methodologies, promises to enhance our capacities for disaster prediction, response, and mitigation, demonstrating the indubitable value of advancements in deep learning and CV within the context of remote sensing and disaster management.

In the realm of deep learning, the efficacy of extant algorithms predominantly hinges upon access to extensive data
Li et al. 2019
. A compelling correlation has been established between the depth of the learning model and its performance on the respective visual task, thereby escalating the algorithmic demands during the inference phase. Such requirements present significant challenges for mobile platforms such as Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), which operate on limited computational and power resources and necessitate expeditious scene understanding in disaster-stricken areas
Lee et al. 2023
. Even in scenarios endowed with adequate computational and power resources, data scarcity may compromise the effective implementation of state-of-the-art models during training and inference phases. Consequently, the exigent challenge lies in devising accurate yet efficient classification methodologies that can sustain effectiveness amidst potential data limitations.

1.2
Few-Shot Learning

Few-Shot Learning (FSL) approaches have addressed these concerns by harnessing meta-learning
Chen et al. 2019
;
Simon et al. 2020
;
Koch et al. 2015
, wherein a given model acquires the ability to learn classification through exposure to limited example images from a wide variety of classes. Notably, numerous FSL approaches for CV (for instance,
Koch et al. 2015
,
Hoffer and Ailon 2015
, and
Snell et al. 2017
), employed Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for essential feature extraction from input images. Subsequently, these features are processed into an embedding space to devise a distance metric that collocates similar classes to proximate points in the space. Within the sphere of FSL, avant-garde strategies have demonstrated unprecedented classification accuracy in benchmark datasets, superseding traditional non-FSL methods
Zhang et al. 2023
. These strategies distinguish themselves by circumventing exhaustive data processing during training and inference, striking a harmonious equilibrium between efficiency and effectiveness in the face of data constraints. However, it must be noted that the majority of the FSL literature relies on benchmark datasets such as Omniglot
Lake et al. 2015
, miniImageNet
Vinyals et al. 2016
, tieredImageNet
Ren et al. 2018
, FC-100
Oreshkin et al. 2018
, and CIFAR-FS
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
, which are devoid of disaster-related imagery, thereby curbing the applicability of FSL techniques in pertinent scenarios (although evaluating any novel FSL approaches on the benchmarks is still of upmost importance for establishing a baseline). Nevertheless, the integration of attention-based mechanisms, underpinned by the Matching Network
Vinyals et al. 2016
, has instigated a surge of innovation in FSL research. Novel contributions such as attention with weight fusion
Meng et al. 2023
, dual attention
Jiang et al. 2020
, and self-attention
Huang and Choi 2023
, have facilitated the selective processing of salient feature maps while excluding extraneous information. This approach effectively curtails computational costs and enhances model efficiency, thereby laying the groundwork for the advent of refined and potent FSL solutions.

Since a majority of the FSL would often learned from an abundance of base samples with known classes and fine-tuned on unseen new classes, the base classes can be easily recognizable while the new classes may not be predicted accurately if the variance of the query set is high, which is more conspicuous if there are limited query samples or rare classes. This is the high intra-class variance problem and consequently the model may easily confuse the new classes with the base. Simultaneously, there is also the high inter-class similarities issue in which certain class can have common visual features as that of the other classes, such as landslide and collapsed building aftermath, which could exacerbate further confusions.

There are a variety of approaches to reduce inter-class variances: Some instances include data augmentation (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2021
,
Zhang et al. 2023
), contrastive learning (e.g.,
Shangguan et al. 2023
,
Zhou et al. 2024
), and variational learning (
Han et al. 2023
,
Zhang et al. 2019
,
Pan and Shen 2024
,
Tolstikhin et al. 2017
). For the latter, the approach involved Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to measure similarities among the embedded feature probability distributions, often utilizing
f
-divergences such as the Kullback-Leiber (KL) divergence to approximate the latent distribution given the support feature distribution sets. Since the few-shot classification performances are highly dependent on the quality of the support and query set sampling, any sample outlier or anomaly would seriously affect the classification effectiveness. This adds on to the issue as highlighted by Zhang et al.
Zhang et al. 2019
on implementing few-shot models based on feature point estimates, which remained dominant in current FSL literature. To address the former, the Mahalanobis distance can be utilized (
Fort 2017
,
Bateni et al. 2020
), but the metric is more for point-based embedded feature, and assumed that the distributions to be compared had the same covariance matrix. A closely related metric for distribution comparisons is the Bhattacharyya coefficient and distance (
Bhattacharyya 1946
,
Kailath 1967
,
Bhattacharyya 1943
), which can be shown to generalize the Mahalanobis distance (
McLachlan 1999
,
Yao et al. 2019
), and also has the property of rapid divergence when the class distribution are dissimilar, hence serving as a “push term” to separate dissimilar embedded features distribution during the variational feature aggregation process, analogous to that in contrastive learning but for embedded feature points.

Concurrently, the Hellinger distance
Hellinger 1909
is yet another
f
-divergences which is much less used than the KL divergence. However, in contrast to the Bhattarcharyya coefficient/distance, the Hellinger distance can be considered as a probabilistic analog of the Euclidean distance (
Lee et al. 2024
,
Lee et al. 2025
), and this means that minimizing the Hellinger distance is equivalent to pulling similar embeddings closer together in a Euclidean-like manner but in the square-root probability space. In a sense this distance serves as a “pull term” in contrast to the Bhattacharyya coefficient as above, once again in analogy to that in contrastive learning. The individual parenthesis term in the Hellinger distance is also bounded between 0 and 1, and this ensures that the value doesn’t diverge to infinity unlike the KL divergence when the denominator probability distribution in the latter goes to zero. There are emerging works on utilizing the Hellinger distance (
Grzyb et al. 2021
,
Kumari and Thakar 2017
), but these are applied to the other domain apart from few-shot image classification, until the recent works by Lee et al. (
Lee et al. 2024
,
Lee et al. 2025
).

1.3
Main Research Gaps and Objectives

However, the role of incorporating attention, which has been more widespread in the point-estimate FSL approaches (and for which some examples have been mentioned previously), has not been focused much upon in variational few-shot image classification (apart from TRIDENT
Singh and Jamali-Rad 2022
). Consequently, there are little FSL works that explored the combination of attention and the Hellinger (along with Bhattarcharyya coefficient) distance approach for classification in the common dataset benchmark as described, let alone in a remote sensing context (with the exception of
Lee et al. 2024
,
Lee et al. 2025
in the context of Hellinger distance). As mentioned, this is because most of the variational FSL works focused on its application using the KL divergence. However, we have identified a strong analogous association between the Bhattacharyya coefficient and the Hellinger distance in establishing a contrastive learning paradigm. This means that it is possible to train a variational few-shot model in the same manner in which we trained a contrastive learning classification model, hence enabling us to combine the benefits of both meta-learning approach. Such training can be done via incorporating the respective distance or divergence into the loss function proper, as demonstrated by Wu et al.
Wu et al. 2021
and Lee et al.
Lee et al. 2024
in the domain of image dehazing. The latter’s approaches, when modified suitably, can potentially enhance the few-shot capability of the proposed model, making it vital for the aerial disaster classification context, as discussed earlier. Fig.
3
illustrates the analogy between the point feature clustering in ordinary contrastive learning and the Bhattacharyya-Hellinger variational distribution clustering for a three-class scenario.

Figure 3:
A pictorial comparison of the point feature-based contrastive learning (
left
) and the analogous distribution feature-based “contrastive learning” (

) using a three-class scenario, where the classes are denoted by purple circles, maroon diamonds and green hexagons. As mentioned, the Bhattarcharyya coefficient (as a similarity measure) can serve as a “push term” as the distance increases rapidly the more dissimilar the feature distribution, while the Hellinger distance can serve as a “pull-term” since similar embeddings are clustered closer together in a Euclidean-like manner but in the square-root probability space.

Therefore, motivated by the increase in the incorporation of attention mechanism, the possibility of exploring alternative probability distribution-based distance approach for FSL prototype clustering, and the pressing needs of highly adaptable disaster classification scheme, we proposed the Attention-based linearly combined Bhattacharyya and Hellinger Distance-based Feature Aggregation Network (ATTBHFA-Net) for our few-shot feature aggregating paradigm. Our model provides an alternative but novel strategy in feature aggregation for few-shot classification, which retains the encoder architecture (as in the work by
Zhang et al. 2019
and VFA-Net) but rendered the task more effective thanks to the incorporation of attention and a combination of the Bhattacharyya coefficient and Hellinger distance which could serve as a contrastive “push” and “pull” term in the probability distribution space respectively. Since an encoder incorporates bottleneck latent variables
z
, the respective distances between the probability distribution of the feature latent variables
z_{features}
and the prototype latent variables
z_{prototype}
are computed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time we applied two alternative
f
-divergence distance measures to the probability distribution of the latent variables and demonstrated such feasibility, all in the context of FSL. In summary, our contributions in this paper are as follows:

•

We proposed ATTBHFA-Net, which not only introduced spatial-channel attention in our few-shot feature embedding procedure, but also incorporated a combined Bhattacharyya-Hellinger distance which serves as a novel VAE strategy in modelling and aggregating the class prototypes in a contrastive learning like-manner. To our knowledge, ATTBHFA-Net is the first of its kind in the domain of FSL which combines attention and the Bhattacharyya-Hellinger distance to perform VAE-based classification on the benchmarks, as well as on remote sensing-based visual disaster classification.

•

To ensure that the variational optimization of our ATTBHFA-Net can be performed in an analogous footing to that of the other related methods (e.g., VAE
Kingma et al. 2015
, WVAE
Tolstikhin et al. 2017
), we derived a mathematical relation linking our modified ELBO (which would be known as the EVLBO) and the Bhattacharyya coefficient. The corresponding relationship between the modified ELBO and the Hellinger distance was made in
Lee et al. 2024
. These relations are and have been shown to be mathematically consistent like that of the relations obtained from other variational-based works.

•

We also introduced a Bhattacharyya-Hellinger distance-based contrastive training loss, also known as the Bhattacharyya-Hellinger Softmax loss function
\ell_{BHS}
, which is inspired by the cosine similarity softmax loss in simCLR
Chen et al. 2020
, but with the cosine similarity in the softmax function replaced by our linearly added Bhattacharyya coefficient and Hellinger distance. The overall training loss for our network,
\ell_{ATTBHFA}
, is a weighted linear combination of
\ell_{BHS}
, the ordinary categorical cross entropy loss
\ell_{CCE}
, and the image reconstruction loss
\ell_{rec}
. Such loss combination has been shown to enhance our classification performances relative to the State-Of-The-Arts (SOTA) FSL approaches.

•

We implemented and evaluated selected FSL-based approaches on the respective benchmark datasets (CIFAR-FS
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
, FC-100
Bertinetto et al. 2018
, miniImageNet
Vinyals et al. 2016
, tieredImageNet
Ren et al. 2018
), as well as on two disaster-based image dataset, namely, the Aerial Image Database for Emergency Response (AIDER)
Kyrkou and Theocharides 2020
and the Comprehensive Disaster Dataset (CDD)
Niloy et al. 2021
. The results obtained from the respective dataset in the latter could serve as a baseline in this context of FSL-based classification. The extensive experiments performed on the six datasets demonstrated not only the feasibility of our method but also the ability to generally surpass the other FSLs in terms of the Average Accuracy (AA) classification performance metric. The reconstructed images using the Bhattarcharyya distance component of our model is also shown to outperform that of the VAEs using the other
f
-divergences.

2
Related Works

In this section, we highlight some of the relevant and existing works in the domain of FSL, before discussing some of its applications to the remote sensing domain.

2.1
On Few-Shot Learning Review

Most of the FSL-based approaches discussed in the following can be categorized into metric-based learning approaches, in which a distance function over the data samples is learned
Parnami and Lee 2022
. The objective is to learn an embedding function in the embedded space given a distance function, with each function characterized by the relevant parameters, which is computed by the neural network. The work by Bateni et al.
Bateni et al. 2020
highlighted the importance of selecting the distance metric, which has been largely unfocused on previously due to the common perceptions that non-linear mappings via neural networks should presumably adapt to any kind of metric, and hence making any selected distance choices inconsequential.

One of the first metric-based FSL models manifested in the form of a Siamese network
Koch et al. 2015
. Here, a pair of input images and its corresponding labels are selected at random from a dataset, and the similarity between the images and labels is learned in the training phase. A similarity function and a loss function are commonly employed, and for a Siamese network, the contrastive loss function (
\ell_{contrastive}
) is utilized in training the model to identify similarities. The prototypical network (ProtoNet), introduced by
Snell et al. 2017
, differs from the Siamese network in that it processes the image features into a higher-dimensional embedding space instead of directly from the images, for which samples from the same class can be easily clustered together. For each class cluster, a class prototype is calculated as the average of the data points in that cluster. Relation Network (RelationNet)
Sung et al. 2018
computes relation scores, which compare the feature maps of the query sample set and the support set, and has the advantage unlike that of the Siamese and ProtoNet in that the similarity metric needs not be defined manually, but is determined from the CNN feature extractor directly. Also, RelationNet is unique relative to the other FSL approaches in that the Mean-Squared Error loss (
\ell_{MSE}
) was used instead of the cross-entropy loss. This is because the output, as mentioned, is a relation score that warrants the problem to be modeled as a regression problem instead. The Matching Network (MatchingNet)
Vinyals et al. 2016
utilizes the attention kernel and cosine similarity in embedding of the support and query examples, and removes the need of fine-tuning for adaptation to new classes.

Transductive Information Maximization (TIM) was introduced by
Boudiaf et al.
which fine-tunes the class prototypes based on the classification error in the support images, as well as any Mutual Information (MI) between the query features and the label predictions. Euclidean distances are used to guide the classification and its subsequent updates. TIM, as the name suggests, maximizes the MI between query features and the label predictions for a given few-shot task at the inference phase, while minimizing the cross-entropy loss on the support set. The method is transductive in that the model classifies any unlabeled query examples for a given few-shot training task all at once, instead of classifying the sample one at a time, as in the usual inductive methods. A transductive fine-tuning approach was proposed by
Dhillon et al. 2019
which performs fine-tuning of the training parameters based on the classification error incurred in the support images and the classification entropy on the query images. Like TIM, Euclidean distances are utilized to guide the classification of the prototypes. Another fine-tuning approach, which uses class prototypes, was proposed by
Chen et al. 2019
and like transductive fine-tuning, the classification error incurred on the support images is used in the fine-tuning process. The query prototypes are also classified and updated based on cosine distances, similar to
Liu et al. 2020
.

) clustering setting. The grey dot represents the centroid (mean) of each of the class prototype (

The pale-red dot in the center represents a feature sample point, with the dotted lines denoting its distances to the centroids.

All of the metric-based FSL methods described previously make use of the query and support feature point in the embedded space directly for the computation and clustering. As highlighted by Zhang et al.
Zhang et al. 2019
, there are two inherent challenges with such approaches. The first is that estimating a particular feature point in the embedded space can be rendered difficult when the support feature points are limited and unevenly distributed in the space. The second is the lack of interpretability as a single embedding does not suffice to indicate a class. The first point implies that computing the mean of the support features from each class can be heavily influenced by external noise, and the second point implies that utilizing distributions instead of points gives better representational and descriptive power. One solution is to utilize variational distribution estimation, as Zhang et al.
Zhang et al. 2019
, and VFA-Net set out to do. The differences between a feature point-based and distribution-based encoding methodology are depicted in Fig.
4
. Such a network, as mentioned in the introductory section, is inspired by the need to reduce the inherent variances presented in the dataset and increase the model’s robustness to such variances. By modeling each class’s common distribution via a VAE
Ding et al. 2020
, followed by training on a huge amount of base classes and fine-tuning on the novel examples, the learned inter-class variances can be transferred to the new classes, and thus achieving effective estimation of the new classes’ distributions using only limited examples. Robust predictions are then implemented via sampling support features and aggregating them with the query features. The Bhattacharyya and the Hellinger distance in our ATTBHFA-Net shares similar characteristics with the KL divergences in
Zhang et al. 2019
and VFA-Net (as well as the Wasserstein distance in
Tolstikhin et al. 2017
) in that sample feature point distribution in the bottleneck layer is compared to a referenced probability distribution, and that both the mean values and standard deviation of the respective sampled features and prototypes are required. However, the VAE-KL divergences does not effectively account for possible outlier-based data features (which is the inter-class similarity problem that manifests in the form of supposedly non-disaster (normal) images displaying some level of disaster-based features) during the few-shot sampling process. Therefore, utilizing our ATTBHFA-Net’s Bhattarcharyya-Hellinger distance approach can better group these data into the respective disaster classes with higher probability, thanks to the Hellinger distance’s capability to take into account feature outliers, hence reducing mis-classification. Together with the capability to model latent representation distribution in analogous with that of the Euclidean distance for point-estimate features (since the Bhattarcharyya-Hellinger distance aggregates the feature distribution in a contrastive learning-like manner), the aforementioned traits motivated us to incorporate the combined distance for feature aggregation in our work.

2.2
On Applications to Remote Sensing

Few-shot learning has been applied in the area of classification, semantic segmentation, as well as object detection in remote sensing. Some of the datasets utilized include NWPU-RESISC45, WHU-RS19, UC Merced, AID, and OPTIMAL-31
Wang et al. 2018
. Some systematic reviews of few-shot learning in remote sensing has been put forward by Lee et al.
Lee et al. 2024
, Qiu et al.
Qiu et al. 2024
and Li et al.
Li et al. 2021
, but here we will briefly review a few relevant works.

Lang et al.
Lang et al. 2023
introduced a global rectification and decoupled registration for satellite few-shot image segmentation that made use of several globally updated dynamic prototypes to provide extra non-target segmentation cues, as well as to avoid erroneous prototype activation caused by query-support image pairings’ unpredictability. In order to attain more focused and customized item localization, the foreground and background information flows are separated, preventing needless confusion caused by redundant information. Cheng et al.
Cheng et al. 2022
introduced a holistic prototype activation network to address the issue of overfitting in base classes that produces inaccurate segmentation boundaries in satellite imagery. It comprised of a training-free scheme to extract the prior category representations, a prototype activation module that produces dependable activation maps and well-matched query characteristics through redundant object and class filtering, and a Cross-Referenced Decoder for multi-level feature aggregation and interactive feature re-weighting. The authors verified the model’s versatility via evaluation on dataset with weak-label, zero-shot segmentation, and video object segmentation.

Task-Adaptive Embedding Network (TAE-Net) was proposed by Huang et al.
Huang et al. 2021
for few-shot satellite image classification, in which a feature encoder learn the embedded features for pre-training. An adaptive task-adaptive attention module is used to select the essential embedded characteristics during learning, and the meta-trained network predict the query set. Kim et al.
Kim and Chi 2021
introduces the Self-Attention Feature Selection Network (SAFFNet) which utilized a self-attention module to infuse features across several scales, similar to a spatial pyramid network. To improve the match between features from the query set and the combined features in the support set, the Self-Attention Feature Selection (SAFS) module is used. Yuan et al.
Yuan et al. 2020
presented a Multi-Attention Deep Earth Mover Distance approach that simultaneously attend to the global and local features for classification. The local attention module captures important and subtle local elements while suppressing others, which improves performance and lessens tiny and big intra-class inequalities.

All of the aforementioned works utilized point-based features instead of distributive-based features. Recently, Wang et al.
Wang et al. 2025

proposed a variational meta-learning paradigm for remote sensing image classification that enables the model to adapt to different tasks via variational inference and parameter generation via the “tasks central points”, with the “central points” obtained from the distributions of meta-parameters within the paradigm. However, the method is not evaluated in a few-shot context, and there seems to be a lack of variational few-shot classification work on the RGB spectrum in remote sensing, although there exists a related work using the hyperspectral spectrum
Guo et al. 2024
. Hence, the proposed method as follows will serve as a pioneering work on incorporating alternate probability distribution divergence/distance measures for variational few-shot classification, and applies it in the remote sensing scenario, specifically for disaster classification from an aerial perspective via UAVs.

3
Formal Problem Definition

3.1
Few-Shot Formal Problem Definition

For the few-shot classification exposition, we posit the existence of a labeled training set designated as
X_{base}=\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{base}}
. Here, the symbol
x_{i}
represents the raw features pertaining to the
i^{th}
sample, while
y_{i}
signifies the one-hot encoded label associated with the same sample. Within the scope of FSL literature, this labeled ensemble is known as the meta-training (or base) dataset.

Adhering to the FSL paradigm, we consider a test dataset
X_{test}=\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{test}}
, comprising a novel set of classes represented by
Y_{test}
. The uniqueness of this set is encapsulated in the equation
Y_{base}\cap Y_{test}=\emptyset
, illustrating that
Y_{test}
introduces completely new class entities not included in
Y_{base}
. The few-shot scenario involves the creation of randomly sampled few-shot tasks from this test dataset, each encompassing a limited number of labeled instances. In this context, each
k
-way-
N
-shot task encompasses the random selection of
N_{s}
labeled instances from each of the
k
different classes. The set of these labeled instances, represented by
S
, forms the support set, characterized by its size as
|S|=N_{s}\cdot K
. Alongside this, each task also possesses a query set denoted as
Q
, consisting of
|Q|=N_{Q}\cdot K
unlabeled (or novel) instances from each of the
K
classes. The training is reiterated with different image batches presented to the model, and once it is done on the base set, the labeled support sets are utilized for task-specific adaptation. The effectiveness of these techniques is then assessed based on their performance on the unlabeled query sets, providing a measure of the model’s capacity to generalize to new data categories based on limited examples.

3.2
Variational Inference Problem Definition

In VAE, the goal is to estimate the true distribution
p(\bf{x})
of the data distribution, which is not known
a priori
. However, we can utilized Bayes’ rule to determine
p(\bf{x})
via determining the latent distribution
p(\bf{z})
, as well as the conditional distributions associated with the encoder and the decoder
p(\bf{z}|\bf{x})
and
p(\bf{x}|\bf{z})
, respectively. Although
p(\bf{z})
can be approximated via a unit variance Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
p(\bf{z}|\bf{x})
and
p(\bf{x}|\bf{z})
are also unknown variables, which can be approximated via imposing proxy distributions
q_{\phi}(\bf{z}|\bf{x)}
(for the encoder) and
p_{\theta}(\bf{x}|\bf{z})
(for the decoder), where (
\theta
,
\phi
) are the optimizing variables to be determined via training. The corresponding objective function to be determined in this aspect is the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) which serves as lower bound for the prior (true) distribution
p(\bf{x})
via

\textrm{log}(p(x))\geq ELBO=\int\bf{q_{\phi}(\bf{z}|\bf{x})}\textrm{log}\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(\bf{x},\bf{z})}{q_{\phi}(\bf{z}|\bf{x})}\right)dz.

(1)

By maximizing the ELBO, we can attain the goal of maximizing the logarithm of
p(\bf{x})
. The equality becomes equal when
q_{\phi}(\bf{z}|\bf{x})
matches the true distribution of
p(\bf{z}|\bf{x})
. The relationship between the ELBO and the KL divergence is very well established (
Zhang et al. 2019
,
Kingma and Welling 2013
,
Kingma et al. 2015
), and the same can be said for the corresponding relationship with the Wasserstein distance
Tolstikhin et al. 2017
. The goal of this work is to establish a mathematically valid relationship between the modified ELBOs and the Bhattarcharyya coefficient along with the Hellinger distance.

4
Our Approaches

In this section, we elaborate on the various key aspects of our ATTBHFA-Net, as well as the experimental setup and procedures for the evaluation of the FSL SOTAs relative to our proposed design. The loss functions and evaluation metrics utilized throughout our experiments are also highlighted.

4.1
Components of ATTBHFA-Net

There are four novel key components in our ATTBHFA-Net: the incorporation of channel-spatial attention in the encoder, the Bhattarcharyya coefficient measure, the Hellinger distance measure, and the Bhattarcharyya-Hellinger Softmax (
\ell_{BHS}
) loss function.

4.1.1
Attention Mechanism

Figure 5:
An illustration of the attention model incorporated in the encoder and decoder component of our ATTBHFA-Net. The architecture comprised of the channel attention and the spatial attention mechanism in which the features are processed consecutively. Note the skip connection between the original and the respective refined features in both the channel and the spatial attention mechanism.

The attention module we have incorporated into the encoder architecture is composed of channel attention and spatial attention modules. For channel attention, the spatial dimensions of the intermediate feature map
\boldsymbol{\phi}
are extracted and squeezed using a combination of average and max pooling. The output features from the respective pooling processes are inputted into a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) network, and they are finally combined via an element-wise summation. The aforementioned processes is mathematically described as

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{M_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi})}&=\sigma(MLP(AvgPool(\boldsymbol{\phi})+MLP(MaxPool(\boldsymbol{\phi})))\\
&=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\omega_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega_{0}}(\boldsymbol{{\phi^{c}}_{avg}}))+\boldsymbol{\omega_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\omega_{0}}(\boldsymbol{{\phi^{c}}_{max}}))),\end{split}

(2)

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid (activation) function,
\boldsymbol{\omega_{0}}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega_{1}}
are the MLPs weights,
\boldsymbol{M_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi})}
is the channel attention map, and
\boldsymbol{{\phi^{c}}_{avg}}
and
\boldsymbol{{\phi^{c}}_{max}}
are the feature maps obtained using the aforementioned pooling operations.

For spatial attention, both aforementioned pooling operations are implemented again, followed by applying a convolutional layer of a filter size of 7 along with the sigmoid activation function to produce the spatial attention map. This is quantitatively described as

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{M_{s}(\phi)}&=\sigma(f^{7\times 7}([AvgPool(\boldsymbol{\phi});MaxPool(\boldsymbol{\phi})]))\\
&=\sigma(f^{7\times 7}([\boldsymbol{{\phi^{s}}_{avg}};\boldsymbol{{\phi^{s}}_{max}}])),\end{split}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{M_{s}(\phi)}
denotes the spatial attention map, and
\boldsymbol{{\phi^{s}}_{avg}}
and
\boldsymbol{{\phi^{s}}_{max}}
denote the feature maps obtained using the aforementioned pooling operations respectively.

Altogether, the spatial and channel attention maps are processed as

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\phi^{\prime}}&=\boldsymbol{M_{c}(\phi)}\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi},\\
\boldsymbol{\phi^{\prime\prime}}&=\boldsymbol{M_{s}(\phi^{\prime})}\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi^{\prime}},\\
\end{split}

(4)

where
\otimes
is the tensor product between the extracted features (
\boldsymbol{\phi^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi^{\prime\prime}}
) and the attention maps. The overall architecture of our attention mechanism is illustrated in Fig.
5
.

In the next two subsection we make some subtle notational changes to formulate the VAE framework into the few-shot learning context. The proxy distributions
q_{\phi}(\bf{z}|\bf{x})
and
p_{\theta}(\bf{z}|\bf{x})
now become
q_{\phi}(\bf{z}|\mathcal{S})
and
p_{\theta}(\bf{z}|\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S})
, where
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{S}
represents the training distribution and the support set respectively. For the latter distribution we also write it as
p_{\theta}(\bf{z}|\mathcal{T})
and implicitly implied that
\mathcal{S}
is a subset of
\mathcal{T}
.

4.1.2
Bhattarcharyya Distance

The Bhattarcharyya distance
D_{BD}
in our ATTBHFA-Net is mathematically depicted as

D_{BD}=-\textrm{ln}\left(\int\sqrt{(p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S}))}dz\right).

(5)

The integrand with the product term
\int\sqrt{(p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S}))}dz
is also known as the Bhattarcharyya Coefficient (
BC
). We make the assumption that
p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})
manifested as a multivariate Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i})
, whereby the mean is
\mu_{i}
and the standard deviation is
\sigma_{i}
, and the (proxy) posterior distribution
q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})
also took on a similar distribution but with a mean of
\mu_{j}
and a standard deviation of
\sigma_{j}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j},\sigma_{j})
). The value of the
\mu
and
\sigma
of the support feature set can be extracted from our attention-based encoder network
\mathcal{F}_{encoder}(S)
after extracting its latent representation
\boldsymbol{z}
and calculating the distributions.

To ensure that the variation optimization can be done on a similar footing as that of the KL divergence and Wasserstein distance, we can derived a modified Evidence Variational Lower Bound (EVLBO) which is related to the Bhattarcharyya coefficient in a similar manner as that of Lee et al.
Lee et al. 2024
. The mathematical derivation is detailed in the appendix section, but we denote the final mathematical result as follows:

EVLBO=\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz+2\left(\textrm{log}(BC)-\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz\right),

(6)

where
\int\textrm{log}(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T}))dz
is the
evidence
term and
EVLBO
can be expressed as
\frac{ELBO}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}
. Therefore, EVLBO is related to ELBO divided by the posterior distribution
q_{\phi}{(z|\mathcal{S})}
, and its value is dependent on the latter, hence its name. It can be shown that if
p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})=q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})
, the
EVLBO
is equivalent to the evidence term, in agreement with previous related work that associates the respective ELBO with the KL divergence (
Zhang et al. 2019
,
Kingma and Welling 2013
), the Wasserstein distance
Tolstikhin et al. 2017
and the Hellinger distance
Lee et al. 2024
.

4.1.3
Hellinger Distance

The (square of the) Hellinger distance
D_{H}
measure in our ATTBHFA-Net is mathematically depicted as

D_{H}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})}-\sqrt{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\right)^{2}dz.

(7)

The Hellinger distance can better handle outliers. Unlike the KL divergence (
D_{KL}
) which can amplifies the small errors associated with the outliers in the low-probability region (occurring at both ends of the distribution tails and hence contributes to larger penalties), the Hellinger distance diminishes the effect of the large differences in the error and thus contributing to smaller penalties. Also, unlike
D_{KL}
,
D_{H}
will not diverge when any input probability distribution tend very close to zero, thus the problem of
D_{KL}
value diverging to infinity is not a concern in
D_{H}
. Finally, the Bhattarcharyya coefficient can be linked with the Hellinger distance via
D_{H}^{2}=1-BC
, and from this we can clearly see the opposing relations between the two metric in the analogous contrastive learning of our model: Since
BC
increases as the feature distribution becomes more dissimilar, the
D_{H}^{2}
decreases as the seemingly dissimilar features would eventually “meet” their similar counterparts in the embedded space, hence achieving the aggregation effect.

As derived in
Lee et al. 2024
and
Lee et al. 2025
,
D_{H}^{2}
can be related to the modified ELBO as

\begin{split}ELBO^{\prime}=\int\textrm{log}(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T}))dz+\textrm{log}\left(1-D^{2}_{H}\right)^{2},\end{split}

(8)

where the new ELBO term,
ELBO^{\prime}
, is expressed as
\frac{ELBO}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}
.

Together, we can express the optimization objective of the ATTBHFA-Net (to maximize the
EVLBO
and
ELBO^{\prime}
(equivalent to minimizing the Bhattarcharyya coefficient and Hellinger distance) as

\underset{\phi,\theta}{\arg\max}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}}}EVLBO+\underset{\phi,\theta}{\arg\max}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}}ELBO^{\prime}.

(9)

4.1.4
Bhattarcharyya-Hellinger Softmax (BHS) Loss

Our proposed new training loss function also consisted of BHS loss
\ell_{BHS}
. As mentioned in the introductory section, the modification made here is that instead of the cosine similarity, the Bhattarcharyya coefficient parenthesis term along with the Hellinger similarity are combined and directly incorporated into the parentheses of the softmax function as highlighted below:

\ell_{BHS}=-\textrm{log}\left(\frac{\textrm{exp}((BC^{\prime}+\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\})/\tau)}{\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\textrm{exp}((BC^{\prime}+\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\})/\tau)}\right),

(10)

where
\tau
denotes the temperature parameter of the softmax function, set as 0.01 throughout our training. Here, we denoted
\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}
to be the Hellinger similarity between the query distribution
i
and the class prototype distribution
j
which is mathematically described as
\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z_{i}|\mathcal{T,Q})}-\sqrt{q_{\phi}(z_{j}|\mathcal{T,C})}\right)
, where now the distributions are conditioned on the query set and class prototypes. The
BC^{\prime}
is defined as the parenthesis term inside
BC
, which once again is conditioned on the query set and class prototype distribution as
\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z_{i}|\mathcal{T,Q})q_{\phi}(z_{j}|\mathcal{T,C})}
.

However, during our simulation, we have found that utilizing the loss function in the mathematical form above would lead to a gradient explosion due to the instability incurred when multiplying two probability distributions together in
BC^{\prime}
, in accordance to its definition. To address this problem, we recalled the relationship between the Bhattarcharyya coefficient and the Hellinger distance as described in the previous section:
BC=1-D_{H}^{2}
, which implies that
\int\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z_{i}|\mathcal{T,Q})q_{\phi}(z_{j}|\mathcal{T,C})}dz=1-\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z_{i}|\mathcal{T,Q})}-\sqrt{q_{\phi}(z_{j}|\mathcal{T,C})}\right)^{2}dz
. We can now simply take the derivative of term with respect to
dz
to obtain
\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z_{i}|\mathcal{T,Q})q_{\phi}(z_{j}|\mathcal{T,C})}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z_{i}|\mathcal{T,Q})}-\sqrt{q_{\phi}(z_{j}|\mathcal{T,C})}\right)^{2}
, as
\frac{d}{dz}(1)=0
. Therefore, the Hellinger similarity is related to
BC^{\prime}
by the negative-halve of the former. Consequently, equation
10
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\ell_{BHS}=-\textrm{log}\left(\frac{\textrm{exp}((-0.5\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}^{2}+\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\})/\tau)}{\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\textrm{exp}((-0.5\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}^{2}+\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\})/\tau)}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=-\textrm{log}\left(\frac{\textrm{exp}((\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}(1-0.5\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}))/\tau)}{\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\textrm{exp}((\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}(1-0.5\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}))/\tau))}\right),

(12)

Note that our
\ell_{BHS}
closely resembled that of the ordinary negative log likelihood loss function
\ell_{nll}
used for multi-class classification. The motivation of such loss is that it generalizes the softmax loss not only in the simCLR, but also in the Hellinger similarity softmax loss function as laid out in
Lee et al. 2024
and
Lee et al. 2025
. As the Bhattarcharyya coefficient and Hellinger distance are complementary in the contrastive learning-based distribution aggregation, the contrastive power of the loss function can be refined further via our novel
\ell_{BHS}
. This can be made conspicuous by the
1-0.5\{v_{(Q,i)},c_{(Q,j)}\}^{2}
term which implies the penalization of any query pairs that are not similar in the square of the Hellinger distance measurement, and consequently the increment in the Bhattarcharyya coefficient. Similar to the cosine similarity and the Hellinger similarity softmax loss, in the denominator, we sum the numbers in parentheses over the number of positive samples and divided by two times the batch size
N
selected per training epoch (i.e.,
2N
).

ATTBHFA-Net Architecture

Together, our ATTBHFA-Net architecture is illustrated in Fig.
6
, which also clearly depicts the aforementioned components. Our ATTBHFA-Net first involved extracting and encoding the input feature with the attention mechanism,
\mathcal{F}_{encoder}
, to the support set
S
and the query set
Q
separately. The respective
\mu
and
\sigma
are obtained respectively, and the bottleneck features
\boldsymbol{z}
are directly obtained via deploying the re-parameterization trick
Kingma et al. 2015

z=\mu+\zeta\odot\sigma
, where
\zeta
is the normal distribution and
\odot
is the hadamard product (element-wise multiplication). The Gaussian distribution
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}

6

These series of steps closely mimicked that of the KL divergence, Wasserstein distance, and Hellinger distance-based VAEs, except with the addition of our Bhattarcharyya-Hellinger probability comparison structure. The respective class-specific distributions that are estimated from the sets are utilized to calculate the probability of the target data, which is the maximum probabilistic attainable among the class. This is then used to generate a confidence score for the label to which the sample is most likely to belong (predicted label), in agreement with
Zhang et al. 2019
. Throughout the above processes, the resultant and (reconstructed) support
S^{\prime}
and the query’s probabilistic features
Q^{\prime}

\ell_{BHS}
,
\ell_{CCE}
and
\ell_{rec}

\ell_{ATTBHFA}
is described by

\ell_{ATTBHFA}=\lambda_{1}\cdot\ell_{BHS}+\lambda_{2}\cdot\ell_{CCE}+\lambda_{3}\cdot\ell_{rec},

(13)

in which
\ell_{CCE}
is mathematically described by

\begin{split}\ell_{CCE}=-\sum_{i}^{Q}y_{i}log\left(p(\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}|\mathcal{T}_{meta})\right),\end{split}

(14)

and the reconstructed loss
\ell_{rec}

\displaystyle\ell_{rec(support)}=||\mathcal{F}_{dec}(z)-S||=||S^{\prime}-S||,

(15)

\displaystyle\ell_{rec(query)}=||\mathcal{F}_{dec}(z)-Q||=||Q^{\prime}-Q||,

(16)

where
y_{i}
and
\hat{y}_{i}
represent the ground-truth and predicted labels respectively,
\mathcal{T}_{meta}
denotes a meta-task and runs through the total number of queries
Q
per training batch in our experiment,
\mathcal{F}_{dec}
denotes the decoder backbone,
||(\cdot)||

S
and

Q

\lambda_{1}

Experimental Procedures

This section is split into three parts: the first part concerns the benchmark datasets utilized for our model evaluation along with the SOTAs, the second part concerns the selected aerial disaster image classification datasets, once again utilized for the model evaluation along with (another set of) SOTAs, and the last part concerns the relevant configurations and hyperparameters.

On Benchmark Datasets

As mentioned in the introductory section, we evaluated our approach relative to the SOTAs FSL approaches on the FC-100, CIFAR-FS, miniImageNet, and tieredImageNet. The selected SOTAs for each dataset is the same as that laid out in Roy et al.’s work

Unlike that of the benchmark dataset, we do not include ANROT-HELANet in this evaluation, as corrupting the input images with adversarial noises is unrealistic and undesirable in the execution of UAV visual search-and-rescue, since such noises are generated artificially. 5.3
Experimental Configurations and Hyperparameters

All images in the respective dataset are re-scaled via division of each original pixel value by 255 as a pre-processing step. All experimental simulations for this work were performed using PyTorch library in Python utilizing Google’s Colab Pro+ Tesla T4 Graphical Processing Units (GPU). The number of training episodes utilized for our approach is 80000, and the optimizer utilized for our network is that of Adam with a learning rate of 0.001. For the temperature value in
\ell_{BHS}
, we set it to 0.01.

Evaluation Metrics

For all the datasets, we used the average classification accuracy scores as the main metric to assess the classification effectiveness of each SOTAs, as well as our approach. Average Accuracy (AA) is mathematically defined as

\begin{split}AA=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{TP_{i}+TN_{i}}{TP_{i}+FN_{i}+TN_{i}+FN_{i}},\end{split}

(17)

where the variables
TP
,
FP
,
TN
, and
FN
represent the true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative classes, respectively, and
N
denote the total number of classes. In addition, we also utilized the confusion matrix to analyze correct and incorrect classifications incurred by our model, as will be illustrated and elaborated in the following section.

Data Augmentations

In alignment with a majority of FSL works, we stressed the importance of data augmentation in our approach to attain state-of-the-art few-shot classification performances. In light of this, for the training set of the AIDER and CDD datasets, we deployed the following image augmentations: center cropping (by 128
\times
128), resize (by 128
\times
128), random vertical and horizontal flips, random rotations by 90, 180 and 270 deg, random horizontal flips followed by the same aforementioned random rotations, as well as random color jitter, blurring, grayscaling, and random CutMix or Mixup
Park et al. 2022
. The probability of all augmentation that are performed in a random manner is set to 0.5. For the training set of the benchmarks, we utilized center cropping and resize (by 32
\times
32 for CIFAR-FS and FC-100 and by 84
\times
84 for miniImageNet and tieredImageNet), random vertical and horizontal flips, random rotations by 90 and 270, grayscale, blur, color jitter and random CutMix or Mixup.

6
Results and Discussions

All implemented algorithms underwent a comprehensive evaluation set of 10 runs, and the results, encapsulated as the Average Accuracy (AA) scores, accompanied by the corresponding standard deviations, were duly recorded. The obtained numerical scores represent the performance of the state-of-the-art (SOTA) methodology and our proposed approach and were established as the foundational benchmarks for the trio of datasets under consideration. In order to provide a thorough and robust evaluation of each SOTA algorithm, barring the triplet network (in the aerial image disaster classification dataset), two distinct evaluation methodologies were employed. These are denoted as 5-way-1 shot and 5-way-5 shot evaluations, providing insight into the performance of the models under varying levels of data sparsity. It is, however, important to note a distinction for the triplet network. Given the unique structure of this network, which compares only a single pair and two pairs of images per episode of training respectively, results are exclusively reported for the 5-way-1 shot evaluation. This restriction aligns with the inherent characteristics of the triplet network, elucidating its capabilities within the stipulated parameters of the experimental setup.

As will be seen in the experimental result section, we will include the results of the ablation study and the image reconstruction of the Bhattacharyya distance component of our ATTBHFA-Net on the benchmarks, while delegating the confusion matrix and GRAD-CAM results on the AIDER and CDD. This is because the latter could be utilized for real-time mission-critical task and require a more thorough assessment of their model’s capability to adapt to seen and novel classes.

We also conducted a study evaluating the quality of the reconstructed miniImageNet images using the Bhattacharyya distance component of our generative model relative to the selected existing VAEs. Some instances of the latter include the original vanilla VAE (based on the KL divergence)
Kingma et al. 2015
, the vanilla Wasserstein VAE (WAE) (
Tolstikhin et al. 2017
,
Chen and Liu 2022
), and the Hellinger VAEs (
Lee et al. 2024
,
Lee et al. 2025
). In our analysis, we only utilized the test set of the miniImageNet, as not only meta-learning was not involved in the reconstruction process, but such study is for comparing the effect of employing different distance metrics on the ELBO optimization in the quality of the reconstructed images.

The metric utilized for the comparison between the reconstructed and the original images is the Frechet Inception Distance (FID)
Heusel et al. 2017
, which computes the distances between the feature vectors of the two image sets using the InceptionV3 model
Szegedy et al. 2016
. A lower FID score implies that two image sets have more similar statistics, with a score of 0 denoting a perfect identical match between the image sets. The first 10000 images from the miniImageNet meta-test dataset were utilized for the image reconstruction. The FID is mathematically described by

FID=||\mu_{r}-\mu_{g}||^{2}+Tr\left(\Sigma_{r}+\Sigma_{g}-2\sqrt{(\Sigma_{r}\Sigma_{g})}\right)

(18)

where
(\mu_{r},\Sigma_{r})
and
(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})
are the means and covariances of the real and generated images respectively, and
Tr
denote the trace of the matrix in the parenthesis.

Fig.
7
depicts some of the selected reconstructed miniImageNet images using the original VAE (top left), the WAE (top right), the Hellinger VAE (bottom left), and the Bhattarcharyya distance component of our ATTBHFA-Net (bottom right) respectively. At the same time, Table
7
represents the FID values obtained for the respective method. As we can see by zooming into the bottom left of Fig.
7
, the HELA-VFA visually outputted higher quality reconstructed images than the other two VAE models (KL-divergence and Wasserstein distance). In particular, the images that illustrated a sharp contrast is of the tank (row 4 column 2), the concert player (row 5 column 4), a group of people walking (row 4 column 6 and row 6 column 6), and lastly the singer (row 6 column 3). Still, the Bhattarcharyya distance (bottom right of Fig.
7
) manages to bring the image quality slightly to a new higher-level, having a FID value of 2.49 and corresponding to a difference of 0.26 relative to the HELA-VFA.

The above observations can be partly attributed to the Bhattarcharyya distance preserving richer latent representations and attaining better balancing between overlap and separability of the feature distributions than the other
f
-divergences. For the former, this is due to the mathematical definition of the Bhattarcharyya coefficient which (like the Hellinger distance) serves to optimize any overlap (no matter how small) between feature distribution and hence establishing a more structured and regularized latent space with a smoother manifold. By sampling from such space or manifold, higher-quality and coherent images can be reconstructed, leading to a lower FID score. For the latter, we note again that the Bhattacharyya distance (like the Hellinger distance) doesn’t suffer from the instability issue inherent in KL divergence. This means that the encoder could ignore the input and just output the prior features to mitigate the infinities that could occur when the model penalizes any latent distribution that is considered highly unlikely, leading to poor reconstruction of the generated images. Although the Hellinger distance and the Bhattarcharyya distance are closely related, as shown in section 4.1.4, the Hellinger distance linearly decreases as the overlap between distributions increases, while the Bhattarcharyya distance has logarithmically amplification even under small overlap of the distribution. This means that for dissimilar latent feature representation, more discriminative gradient signals could be given than that of the Hellinger distance, enabling better contrastive aggregation of the class feature distributions and subsequently leading to sharper reconstruction of the images.

7
Conclusions

Our approach employs the benchmarks and the UAV-based remote sensing images to tackle natural disaster scenarios. We exhibit the efficacy of ATTBHFA-Net in four benchmarks and two different tasks for disaster classification, establishing the latter as a promising benchmark for future investigations in this area. By utilizing an attention mechanism with prototype generation through the Bhattarcharyya-Hellinger metric, we present an inventive method that boosts feature extraction and conserves essential class knowledge pertinent to each task at hand. Our findings make a significant contribution to improving understanding of how cutting-edge techniques can work harmoniously together to address intricate real-world issues including emergency response situations brought about by natural disasters. In all data sets, our approach in general outperformed the SOTA FSLs in the AA scores, demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of our FSL design in allowing better classification of a wide variety of image-based disaster scenery. In our future work, the proposed ATTBHFA-Net architecture will be tested in real-time disaster identification scenario (e.g., processing images of aftermath of flooding) for validation purposes, and alternative methods will be explored to further improve the model performance, which includes, for example, such GAN-based augmentation
Dam et al. 2020

Derivation of the Modified ELBO (EVLBO) for Bhattarcharyya Coefficient

In this appendix section, we provide a derivation for the modified ELBO (EVLBO) that relates to the Bhattarcharyya coefficient
BC
. We start with the derivation of the original ELBO as

ELBO=\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\textrm{log}\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T},z)}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\right)dz

(19)

and that the Bhattarcharyya coefficient is written as

BC=\int\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}dz.

(20)

Taking the logarithm of each term on both sides, we get

\textrm{log}(BC)=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})dz\right)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right).

(21)

Since
p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T},z)=p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})
, equation
21
can be written as

\textrm{log}(BC)=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})}dz\right)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right)

(22)

which we can cast
\textrm{log}\left(\int\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})}dz\right)
as
\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})dz\right)-\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})dz\right)
, and bring the second term to the left side of equation
22
to obtain

\textrm{log}(BC)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})dz\right)=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})dz\right)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right),

(23)

where we note that the term
\int p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})dz
is the
evidence
term. We can insert
\frac{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}
in
\frac{1}{2}log\left(\int p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})dz\right)
to yield

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{log}(BC)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})dz\right)=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})\frac{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}dz\right)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right)\\
=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}dz\right)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right)\\
=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{log}\left(\int\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}dz\right)+\textrm{log}\left(\int q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})dz\right).\end{split}

(24)

In all the terms above, the logarithm can be brought into the integral, and hence we can rewrite equation
24
as

\textrm{log}(BC)+\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz=\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\right)dz+\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz.

(25)

Looking back to the definition of ELBO, we can make
log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\right)
as the subject of the equation and obtain

\frac{1}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\frac{d(ELBO)}{dz}=\textrm{log}\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(z,\mathcal{T})}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\right)

(26)

which implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{log}(BC)+\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{1}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}\frac{d(ELBO)}{dz}dz+\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz\\
=\frac{1}{2}\frac{ELBO}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}+\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz.\end{split}

(27)

Bringing
\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz
to the left-hand side of equation
27
and bringing out the factor of 2 on both sides,

2\textrm{log}(BC)+\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz-2\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz=\frac{ELBO}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})},

(28)

which we defined the modified ELBO (EVLBO) as
EVLBO=\frac{ELBO}{q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})}
, we finally obtain

2\textrm{log}(BC)+\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz-2\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz=EVLBO.

(29)

Therefore, the EVLBO is associated with the Bhattarcharyya coefficient via

\displaystyle\begin{split}EVLBO=\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz+2\textrm{log}(BC)-2\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz\\
=\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{T})\right)dz+2\left(\textrm{log}(BC)-\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz\right).\end{split}

(30)

The term in the parenthesis of the right hand side of equation
30
becomes
\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})\right)dz-\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz
, since
\textrm{log}(BC)=\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})\right)dz+\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{log}\left(q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})\right)dz
. From here, we can easily see that if
p_{\theta}(z|\mathcal{T})=q_{\phi}(z|\mathcal{S})
, the term in the parenthesis becomes 0, and hence the evidence term equates to the EVLBO, in agreement with the earlier statement in the paper.

In summary, we can expressed the optimization objective of the Bhattacharyya distance component of the ATTBHFA-Net (to maximize the EVLBO) as

\underset{\phi,\theta}{\arg\max}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}}}EVLBO.

(31)
