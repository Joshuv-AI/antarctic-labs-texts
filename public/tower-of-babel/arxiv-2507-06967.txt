Noisy PDE Training Requires Bigger PINNs
\name Sebastien Andre-Sloan \email sebastien.andre-sloan@postgrad.manchester.ac.uk, \name Anirbit Mukherjee \email anirbit.mukherjee@manchester.ac.uk, \name Matthew Colbrook \email mjc249@cam.ac.uk

Abstract
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) are increasingly used to approximate solutions of partial differential equations (PDEs), especially in high dimensions. In real-world applications, data samples are noisy, so it is important to know when a predictor can still achieve low empirical risk. However, little is known about the conditions under which a PINN can do so effectively. We prove a lower bound on the size of neural networks required for the supervised PINN empirical risk to fall below the variance of noisy supervision labels. Specifically, if a predictor achieves an empirical risk 𝒪 ⁡ ( η ) \mathcal{O}(\eta) below σ 2 \sigma^{2} (variance of supervision data), then necessarily d N ​ log ⁡ d N ≳ N s ​ η 2 d_{N}\log d_{N}\gtrsim N_{s}\eta^{2} , where N s N_{s} is the number of samples and d N d_{N} is the number of trainable parameters of the PINN. A similar constraint applies to the fully unsupervised PINN setting when boundary labels are sampled noisily. Consequently, increasing the number of noisy supervision labels alone does not provide a “free lunch” in reducing empirical risk. We also show empirically that PINNs can indeed achieve empirical risks below σ 2 \sigma^{2} under such conditions. As a case study, we investigate PINNs applied to the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) PDE. Our findings lay the groundwork for quantitatively understanding the parameter requirements for training PINNs in the presence of noise.



1
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) form the foundation of nearly all natural sciences and engineering, and solving them is a critical challenge. A major hurdle is approximating PDE solutions without succumbing to the curse of dimensionality
(
E et al. 2021
;
Han et al. 2018
)
, which even plagues highly structured PDEs
(
Beck et al. 2021
;
Berner et al. 2020
)
. Recently, deep learning has emerged as a tool for solving PDEs, with roots in earlier work
(
Lagaris et al. 1998
)
. The rise in AI-driven approaches to the treatment of complex scientific problems has led to a new field known as “AI for science”. Various deep learning methods have been developed to solve PDEs
(
Kaiser et al. 2021
;
Erichson et al. 2019
;
Wandel et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Salvi et al. 2022
)
.

Recent theoretical advances in operator learning provide further context for our work. For example,
Boullé et al. 2023
prove that certain elliptic PDEs can be learned with remarkably few data: they give an algorithm that achieves an exponential decay of error with respect to the number of input-output samples. A broader perspective is offered in the survey
Boullé and Townsend 2024
.

Among neural PDE solvers, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
(
Raissi et al. 2019
)
have emerged as a popular framework. PINNs can be effective for a wide range of PDEs, including the well-known Navier–Stokes equations
(
Arthurs and King 2021
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Eivazi et al. 2022
)
. They work by using neural networks (NNs) to approximate PDE solutions through a loss function that penalizes the network for not satisfying the initial and boundary conditions, as well as the differential operator. Since these terms are defined over different domains, PINNs minimize a multi-distribution loss, which sets them apart from typical machine learning models. Theoretical insights into the generalization properties of PINNs are still in their early stages
(
Kutyniok et al. 2022
;
Gühring et al. 2020
;
Geist et al. 2021
)
. Recent work
(
Mishra and Molinaro 2022
)
has provided initial error bounds applicable to all PDEs, but there are gaps between these bounds (which use quadrature-based terms) and the standard PINN framework. Despite extensive efforts
(
De Ryck and Mishra 2024
)
, it remains unclear why minimizing the loss of standard PINN consistently leads to NNs that accurately approximate PDE solutions.

The growing interest in PINNs has also exposed their struggles with even basic PDEs
(
Krishnapriyan et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022b
)
. To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored alternative gradient descent methods
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
and hybrid approaches such as patching multiple solutions together
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
. There also have been efforts to ameliorate the challenge of computing derivatives in high dimensional problems, resulting in variants that replace the derivative calculations needed for traditional PINNs by computation of certain stochastic estimators
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
.

Another important challenge in understanding PINNs is the quantification of errors and uncertainties, especially when training on noisy observations
(
Zou et al. 2025
)
. There are many varaints of PINNs, such as the Uncertain PINN
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
and the Bayesian PINN
(
Yang et al. 2021
)
that have been created for this purpose. This issue is particularly relevant in applications such as image super-resolution for medical diagnostics using PINNs
(
Shone et al. 2023
)
.

This paper presents a first-of-its-kind lower bound on the size of PINNs required to achieve small losses. Our theorems reveal a fundamental relationship between model complexity and the amount of data needed for high performance. Crucially, we show that if the loss is low, the model size must scale with the data to maintain accuracy. This finding challenges the assumption that noisy supervision is a “free lunch”. Whether dealing with noisy initial conditions or solution samples, the model must surpass a critical size for these noisy inputs to enhance predictions. This result also has practical consequences for designing efficient and effective models in real-world scenarios, where noise is inevitable.

1.1
The setup

Consider a PDE of the form

{\mathcal{L}}(u)=f,\quad{\mathcal{B}}(u)=u_{B},

where
{\mathcal{L}}
is a differential operator that acts on functions on
\tilde{D}\times[0,T]
, for a domain
\tilde{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
{\mathcal{B}}
is a boundary (or trace) operator that defines an evaluation of functions on the boundary of the domain. The goal of the PINN method is to find an approximate solution
u_{\bm{w}}
(paramtrized by weights
{\bm{w}}
) such that
{\mathcal{L}}(u_{\bm{w}})\approx f
and
{\mathcal{B}}(u_{\bm{w}})\approx u_{B}
. To do this, one typically uses three kinds of “residual” functions. The first is the PDE residual:

R_{\mathrm{PDE}}[u_{\bm{w}}]({\bm{x}},t)={\mathcal{L}}(u_{\bm{w}})({\bm{x}},t)-f({\bm{x}},t),\quad({\bm{x}},t)\in\tilde{D}\times[0,T].

For time-dependent PDEs with initial condition
u({\bm{x}},0)=g({\bm{x}})
, there is also a temporal initial value residual:

R_{\mathrm{T}}[u_{\bm{w}}]({\bm{x}})=u_{\bm{w}}({\bm{x}},0)-g({\bm{x}}),\quad{\bm{x}}\in\tilde{D}.

In the semi-supervised setting, there is also a loss term based on data sets, corresponding to an “observable”
\Psi
in a subdomain
\tilde{D^{\prime}}\times[0,T]
, on which (possibly noisy) observations
\tilde{g}
are available of a functional
\Psi
of the true solution. In real-world applications, it is rare to have access to noiseless data, so we include noisy in our supervised labels to capture the difficulty of observing data. This leads to the data or supervised residual:

R_{\mathrm{S}}[u_{\bm{w}}]({\bm{x}},t)=\Psi[u_{\bm{w}}]({\bm{x}},t)-\tilde{g}({\bm{x}},t),\quad({\bm{x}},t)\in\tilde{D^{\prime}}\times[0,T].

In total, the PINN risk function is given as:

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{PINN}}(u_{\bm{w}})=\int_{\tilde{D}\times[0,T]}R_{\mathrm{PDE}}[u_{\bm{w}}]^{2}\differential{\vx}\differential{t}+\lambda_{0}\int_{\tilde{D}}R_{\mathrm{T}}[u_{\bm{w}}]^{2}\differential{\vx}+\lambda_{s}\int_{\tilde{D^{\prime}}\times[0,T]}R_{\mathrm{S}}[u_{\bm{w}}]^{2}\differential{\vx}\differential{t},

(1)

where
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{s}>0
are parameters used to weight the different residuals and losses. A natural choice is
\Psi={\mathcal{B}},\tilde{g}=u_{B}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}=\partial{\mathcal{D}}
(the boundary of
{\mathcal{D}}
), where the above penalizes
u_{\bm{w}}
not satisfying the boundary condition. Given a parameter space
\Theta
, the corresponding risk minimization/learning problem is to solve,
\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\Theta}R_{\mathrm{PINN}}(u_{\bm{w}})
.

In this paper, we consider a fundamental open question:

Question:

Given noisy observations with variance
\sigma^{2}
at random locations within a PDE domain, under what conditions can a predictor achieve a supervised empirical PINN risk below
\sigma^{2}
?

Answering this question is key to understanding whether PINNs can use noisy data to solve PDEs accurately. In this work, we focus on solving the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) PDE using supervised PINNs, and we derive the first necessary condition on model size and supervision for this desirable outcome. The HJB PDE is a nonlinear equation that governs the time evolution of a scalar function. It arises naturally in optimal stochastic control problems (see
Appendix

B
) and is widely used in robotics research
(
Cao and Pan 2024
;
Abu-Khalaf and Lewis 2005
;
Lewis et al. 2012
)
. Its nonlinearity, high dimensionality, and importance make it an ideal test case for understanding the requirements PINNs must meet to successfully tackle complex PDEs under noisy conditions.

1.2
Informal statement of the main result

Our main result can be summarised as follows:

Consider the PINN loss function for the HJB PDE. Let
\sigma^{2}
be the average variance in the supervision data. If a predictor achieves an empirical risk
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
below
\sigma^{2}
, then necessarily
d_{N}\log d_{N}\gtrsim N_{s}\eta^{2}
, where
N_{s}
is the number of samples and
d_{N}
is the number of trainable parameters of the PINN.

This result shows that it is not necessarily useful to add more samples to the supervised loss term once a certain threshold is reached, but this threshold value may be increased by increasing the number of parameters in the model, such as the width of the net. In other words, to make use of a given number of noisy supervision samples, the model must have enough capacity—its size must exceed a certain threshold. We also show that a similar condition on model size applies when, instead of a data term, the PINN uses noisy samples of an initial condition
g
at randomly chosen spatial points.

Figure

1
shows an illustration of the phenomenon, with full experimental details provided in
Section

5
. Here, we have trained a number of PINNs at different sizes
d_{N}
to solve an instance of the HJB PDE with
\sigma^{2}=0.5
being the variance of the noise in the
N_{s}=3276
labels used in the supervision term. Beyond a critical value of the size of the nets, the empirical error achieved at the end of training is always below
\sigma^{2}
. Moreover, if
d_{N}
is too small, the nets fail to attain training errors below
\sigma^{2}
, but there is consistent increase in the performance of the PINN as
d_{N}
increases. We also observe an apparent scaling of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d_{N}})
consistent with our bounds.

Figure 1:
The vertical axis is
1-{\rm(final~training~error)}
. The red dots represent the final training accuracy, while the blue dots show the training accuracy every 100 updates. The accuracy appears to grow like
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d_{N}})
before plateauing at a level above one minus the variance.

Method of proof:
The proof establishes an upper bound on the probability that a good net exists within the considered class of nets, based on random sampling of the supervision data. Considering both the net’s architecture and the supervision data, the existence of this bound contrasts with the requirement for this favorable event to occur with high probability, thereby imposing a necessary constraint. There are three main steps in the proof:

•

Lemma

4.5
decomposes the supervised term of the PINN risk as a square of three parts: the noise in the supervision labels, their expected value, and the output of the predictor at the supervision locations. Further, invoking
Lemma

4.5
on the
\eta
-covering of the neural function class considered in the theorem, shows that the probability of finding in this set a “good” NN (i.e., a network with empirical risk below
\sigma^{2}
) has an upper bound. Up to additive constants, this upper bound is shown to equal the probability of finding a network in the same set such that the correlation between the label noise and that network’s predictions at the
N_{s}
supervision locations is above a certain threshold;

•

Lemma

4.6
analyzes the
\eta
-cover of the neural function class and demonstrates that, within this subset, the probability of finding a network with a high correlation as defined above has an upper bound that grows super-exponentially with the number of parameters and decreases exponentially with the number of supervision samples;

•

Lemma

4.7
conducts an extensive calculation showing that the PINN risk changes in a non-Lipschitz but controlled manner under
\eta
-level perturbations to its weights.

These three steps are then combined to yield an upper bound on the probability of finding a predictor achieving an empirical risk below
\sigma^{2}
. Imposing a high probability for the existence of a good network results in the desired constraint.

Related Works

The literature on PINNs is rapidly expanding, so we focus on key theoretical results that place our work in the context of this evolving field.

A primary question is to identify sufficient conditions under which minimizing the PINN risk provably solves the PDE. To this end,
Mishra and Molinaro 2022
considers a differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}
that is either linear or admits a well-defined linearization. Specifically, there exists an operator
\bar{{\mathcal{L}}}
such that, for all
u
and
v
in the appropriate function space,
{\mathcal{L}}(u)-{\mathcal{L}}(v)=\bar{{\mathcal{L}}}_{(u,v)}(u-v)
. Under the additional assumption that this linearization operator has a bounded inverse, it is shown that the functional distance between the predictor and the true PDE solution is bounded above by the PINN risk, up to multiplicative constants.

De Ryck and Mishra 2022
investigate the existence of NNs that can simultaneously reduce both the PINN risk and the distance to the true PDE solution. The authors show that for PDEs admitting a classical solution
u
, if there exists a NN
{\mathcal{U}}
that approximates the solution to arbitrary accuracy, then there also exists a
\tanh
-activated NN
u_{\bm{w}}
such that both its distance to the true solution and its PINN risk can be made arbitrarily small. Moreover, the depth of the surrogate
u_{\bm{w}}
satisfies
\text{depth}(u_{\bm{w}})=O(\text{depth}({\mathcal{U}}))
, and the accuracy of the PDE solution scales inversely with the width of
u_{\bm{w}}
.

In
Bonito et al. 2024
, the authors argued that for linear elliptic PDEs, the radius of the Chebyshev
1
1

1

A Chebyshev ball of a set
K
is smallest ball that contains the set
K
ball containing all solutions to the PDE corresponding to every possible source function and initial condition that can interpolate the data is a reasonable error metric. The worst case over all the functions from the set of possible continuous solutions to the PDEs and the corresponding best case over all possible training data inputs, given some budget of total data observations, gives an optimal error rate on solving the PDE that depends only on the PDE. They showed that this metric decreases polynomially with respect to the number of training observations. This work implicitly assumes noiseless observations.

The stability conditions presented in
Mishra and Molinaro 2022
and the criteria for simultaneously minimizing the PINN risk and the total error in
De Ryck and Mishra 2022
are, a priori, distinct. Whether the PINN framework for solving the HJB equation meets either of these conditions remains an open question. More broadly, identifying criteria for PDEs that would enable the simultaneous satisfaction of both conditions within the corresponding PINN setup is still an open problem.

Wang et al. 2022a
examine the unsupervised PINN risk for the HJB PDE. They show that even if the surrogate’s boundary risk is zero and the total risk is low, this does not ensure that the surrogate solution is accurate. This is demonstrated by constructing a surrogate solution with an arbitrarily low PINN risk in the
L^{2}
norm, yet which remains arbitrarily distant from the true solution when the domain has eight or more dimensions. The arguments in
Wang et al. 2022a
do not preclude the existence of surrogates that achieve low PINN risk while remaining close to the true PDE solution.

In related work,
Mukherjee and Roy 2024
demonstrated that for the supervised loss of a DeepONet, evaluated on noisy labels from
N_{s}
samples, to fall below a label-noise-dependent threshold, the output dimension
q
of the branch/trunk network must satisfy
\smash{q=\Omega(N_{s}^{1/4})}
. However, this study did not provide a lower bound on the total number of trainable parameters required to achieve the same level of performance. Additionally,
Bubeck and Sellke 2023
showed for the first time that for a class of NNs with depth
D
and
d_{N}
parameters, in order for a member of this class to achieve a squared loss empirical risk on
N_{s}
noisy samples in
d
dimensions below the noise variance, the Lipschitz constant of the predictor
f
must satisfy
\smash{\text{Lip}(f)=\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{{N_{s}d}/(Dd_{N})})}
.

Mathematical Setup of the HJB PINN Loss

We say that a scalar function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on the domain
[-B,B]^{n}\times[0,T]
for some
B,T>0
satisfies the HJB PDE in the strong sense if

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\rm HJB}(u)=\partial_{t}u-\nabla^{2}u+\norm{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}=0\quad

\displaystyle~\forall({\bm{x}},t)\in{\mathcal{D}}=[-B,B]^{n}\times[0,T]

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\rm HJB}(u)=u({\bm{x}},0)=g({\bm{x}})\quad

\displaystyle~\forall{\bm{x}}\in[-B,B]^{n}.

(2)

Here,
\smash{\nabla^{2}u=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partialderivative[2]{u}{x_i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}^{2}u}
is the spatial Laplacian of
u
and
\nabla u=\left(\partial_{1}u,\ldots,\partial_{n}u\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the spatial gradient of
u
. For example, an exact solution that has been used as a test case for PINN experiments
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
corresponds to
g({\bm{x}})=\norm{\vx}^{2}
is,

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}},t)=\frac{\norm{\vx}^{2}}{1+4t}+\frac{n}{2}\log(1+4t).

(3)

To approximately solve the above PDE with arbitrary initial conditions
g
in the PINN formalism, we consider the following class of predictors. The specific function class
{\mathcal{H}}
we consider consists of NNs with a bounded activation function in the final layer, without constraints on activations in the preceding layers.

Definition 3.1
(Class of NNs)

Let
f
be a non-linear activation function (or indeed a full NN that is frozen) that satisfies:

•

(Bounded and bounded gradient)
\norm{f(\vz)}\leq C_{1}
,
\norm{\nabla f(\vz)}\leq C_{2}
for all
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
;

•

(Lipschitz)
\norm{f(\vz_1)-f(\vz_2)}\leq L_{1}\norm{\vz_1-\vz_2}
for all
{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
;

•

(Lipschitz gradient)
\norm{\nabla f(\vz_1)-\nabla f(\vz_2)}\leq L_{2}\norm{\vz_1-\vz_2}
for all
{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

or
\norm{Hf(\vz)}_{F}\leq C_{3}
for all
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the set of functions

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\ni({\bm{x}},t)\mapsto h_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}},t)=f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\in\mathbb{R},\quad{\bm{W}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n},{\bm{w}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

.

The number of trainable parameters of a function in

\eta

. An example of

{\bm{z}}\mapsto\left\langle{\bm{a}},\sigma({\bm{z}})\right\rangle
, where

is a fixed vector and

Appendix

that when

is sigmoid activation function, one can choose
C_{1}=\norm{\va}_{1}
,
C_{2}=\frac{1}{4}\norm{\va}

6
Conclusion

We provided a detailed analysis of the semi-supervised PINN risk for the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman PDE with noisy labels. We proved that the minimum number of trainable parameters
d_{N}
required to achieve an empirical PINN risk
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
below a label-noise-dependent threshold must scale with the number of samples
N_{s}
such that
d_{N}\log d_{N}\gtrsim N_{s}\eta^{2}
. In practical terms, simply adding more noisy data will not improve a PINN’s accuracy unless the model size is also increased sufficiently; our bound quantifies this requirement. This lower bound applies in two distinct setups: (1) where
N_{s}
noisy observations of the solution serve as a supervised penalty, and (2) where the PINN operates in an entirely unsupervised manner with
N_{s}
noisy boundary condition observations. To our knowledge, this is the first result providing a lower bound on NN size required to achieve specified performance levels for PINNs.

Our concentration-inequality-based approach is quite general and could be applied to other PDEs and model classes, suggesting a broader framework for determining NN size constraints for scientific applications. We believe this is not an isolated phenomenon – many other complex PDEs likely exhibit similar requirements on model size for learning from noisy data (akin to our
Lemma

4.7
for the HJB case). Exploring such PDEs and extending our bounds to more general classes of PINNs is an exciting direction for future work. This work, therefore, opens up a new direction for rigorous investigations into NN scaling requirements in solving complex physical problems. Not only does this help in theoretically understanding PINNs, but also in employing the correct network architectures.

Further research could explore whether this relationship between
N_{s}
and
d_{N}
is also sufficient to guarantee low PINN risk, extending these findings to systems with vector-valued solutions (e.g., the Navier–Stokes equations), and applying this framework to deeper networks with multilayer training. Establishing these rigorously could be an exciting direction of future work. Finally, alleviating the bounded network assumption to encompass unbounded networks would also be a natural next step.

Appendix B
Deriving the HJB PDE from Optimal Stochastic Control

We follow the presentation in works like
(
Katzourakis and Pryer 2018
)
and
(
Wang et al. 2022a
)
, to review how the HJB equations emerge from optimal stochastic control. In stochastic control theory, the state function
\{X_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a stochastic process, where
T
is the time horizon. The evolution of the state function by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dX_{s}=m(s,X_{s})ds+\sigma dW_{s}\quad s\in[t,T]\\
&X_{t}=x,\end{aligned}\right.

where
m:[t,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the control function and
\{W_{s}\}
is a standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion. The total cost is defined as

J_{x,t}(m)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}r(X_{s},m,s)ds+g(X_{T})\right],

where
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
measures the cost rate during the process and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
measures the final cost at the terminal state. The goal is then to find the value function of the control problem,
u(x,t)=\min_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}J_{x,t}(m)
, where
{\mathcal{M}}
is the set of possible control functions being taken into consideration. It can be shown that the value function
u
satisfies the HJB Equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}u(x,t)+\min_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}[r(x,m(t,x),t)+\left\langle\nabla u,m_{t}\right\rangle]=0\\
&u(x,T)=g(x).\end{aligned}\right.

Denote
m(x,t)=(m_{1}(x,t),m_{2}(x,t),\ldots,m_{n}(x,t))
as
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and set
r(x,y,t)=\frac{1}{4}\norm{y}^{2}_{2}
. The third term in the HJB Equation becomes

\min_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{4}\norm{\vy}_{2}^{2}+\left\langle\nabla u,{\bm{y}}\right\rangle\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{4}y_{i}^{2}+y_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}u]\right).

It can be shown that
\min_{y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}}[\frac{1}{4}y_{i}^{2}+y_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}u]=-(\partial_{x_{i}}u)^{2}
by setting
y_{i}=2\partial_{x_{i}}u
, which results in the following class of HJB equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}u(x,t)-\norm{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}=0\text{ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]$}\\
&u(x,T)=g(x).\end{aligned}\right.

Taking the transformation
v(x,t)=u(x,T-t)
and setting
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
the equation becomes

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}v(x,t)-\nabla^{2}v(x,t)+\norm{\nabla v }_{2}^{2}=0\text{ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]$}\\
&v(x,0)=g(x).\end{aligned}\right.

Appendix C
Depth 2 Sigmoid Activation NN Bounds

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined as
f({\bm{z}})=\left\langle{\bm{a}},\sigma({\bm{z}})\right\rangle
for
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function acting component-wise. Using the fact that
\absolutevalue{\sigma(x)}\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the
C_{1}
bound can be derived as follows

\norm{f}=\norm{\ip{\va}{\sigma(\vz)}}=\absolutevalue{\sum_{i=1}^ka_i\sigma(z_i)}\leq\absolutevalue{\sum_{i=1}^ka_i}=\norm{\va}_{1}=C_{1}.

The derivative of sigmoid is

\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{(1+e^{x})^{2}},

which has the property
\absolutevalue{\sigma'(x)}\leq\frac{1}{4}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The gradient of
f
is
\nabla f_{i}=a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(z_{i})
so the
C_{2}
bound can be derived as follows

\norm{\nabla f}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\nabla f_{i})^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(z_{i}))^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{a_{i}}{4}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}^{2})}=\frac{1}{4}\norm{\va}=C_{2}.

The second derivative of sigmoid is,

\sigma^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{e^{x}(e^{x}-1)}{(1+e^{x})^{3}},

which also has the property that
\sigma^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{4}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The elements of the Hessian of
f
are given by
Hf_{i,j}=\partial_{z_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}f=\partial_{z_{i}}a_{j}\sigma^{\prime}(z_{j})
, which is only non-zero when
i=j
, where it becomes
Hf_{i,i}=a_{i}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})
. The
C_{3}
bound is therefore

\norm{Hf}_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\partial_{z_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}f)^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(z_{i}))^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{a_{i}}{6\sqrt{3}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{3}}\norm{\va}=C_{3}.

Lemma D.1
(Upper bound on
\partial_{t}h_{\bm{w}}-\partial_{t}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}
)

The following bound holds:

\partial_{t}h_{\bm{w}}-\partial_{t}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\leq\frac{\eta}{2}(C_{2}+WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})).

Proof
We first write

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\bm{w}}-\partial_{t}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)-\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle+\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2}-{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle.

Because of the bound
\|{{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\|\leq\frac{\eta}{2}
and
Definition

3.1
,
\|{\nabla f({\bm{z}}_{1})-\nabla f({\bm{z}}_{2})}\|\leq L_{2}\|{{\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2}}\|
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2}-{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\vw_2 - \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\vx+ \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\vx+ \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)}\leq\frac{\eta}{2}C_{2}

and

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)-\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\vw_2}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \nabla f(\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\vx+ \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq W\cdot L_{2}\norm{(\mW_1-\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}})\vx+ (\vw_2-\vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}})t}

\displaystyle\leq WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t}).

Combining the two completes the proof.

Lemma D.2
(Upper bound on
\partial_{t}h_{\bm{w}}+\partial_{t}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}
)

The following bound holds:

\partial_{t}h_{\bm{w}}+\partial_{t}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\leq\frac{\eta}{2}(C_{2}+WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t}))+2WC_{2}.

Proof
We first write

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\bm{w}}+\partial_{t}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle+\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle+\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)-\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle+\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle.

Recalling that
\|{{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\|\leq\frac{\eta}{2}
and
\norm{\nabla f(\vz_1)-\nabla f(\vz_2)}\leq L_{2}\|{{\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2}}\|
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\vw_2 + \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\vx+ \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\eta}{2}+2W\right)\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\vx+ \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)}\leq\left(\frac{\eta}{2}+2W\right)C_{2}

and

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{w}}_{2},\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)-\nabla f({\bm{W}}_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\norm{\vw_2}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \nabla f(\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\vx+ \vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}t)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq W\cdot L_{2}\norm{(\mW_1-\mW_{1_{\frac{\eta}{2}}})\vx+ (\vw_2-\vw_{2_{\frac{\eta}{2}}})t}\leq WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})

Combining the two completes the proof.

Lemma D.3
(Upperbound on
\|{\nabla h_{\bm{w}}}\|^{2}-\|{\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\|^{2}
)

The following bound holds:

\norm{\nabla h_\vw}^{2}-\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\eta}{2}(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2})\left(\frac{\eta}{2}(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2})+2WC_{2}\right).

Proof
We first write

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_\vw}^{2}-\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{\bm{w}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{\bm{w}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{\bm{w}}-\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}}-\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\nabla h_\vw+ \nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}\norm{\nabla h_\vw- \nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}

Looking specifically at
\|{\nabla h_{\bm{w}}-\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\|
,

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_\vw- \nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mW_1^\top\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mW_1^\top(\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)) + (\mW_1^\top- \mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top)\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\mW_1^\top}_{F}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t))}+\norm{\mW_1^\top- \mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top}F\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+\frac{\eta}{2}C_{2}=\frac{\eta}{2}(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2}).

Then looking at
\|{\nabla h_{\bm{w}}+\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\|
,

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_\vw+ \nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mW_1^\top\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) + \mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mW_1^\top(\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)) + (\mW_1^\top+ \mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top)\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\mW_1^\top}_{F}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t) - \nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t))}+\norm{\mW_1^\top+ \mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top}_{F}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}

\displaystyle\leq WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+(2W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{2}.

Combining these two completes the proof.

Lemma D.4
(Upper bound on
\|{\nabla h_{\bm{w}}}\|^{2}+\|{\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\|^{2}
)

The following bound holds:

\norm{\nabla h_\vw}^{2}+\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}^{2}\leq WC_{2}\eta(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2})+2(WC_{2})^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}(WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+(2W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{2})).

Proof
We first write

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_\vw}^{2}+\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{\bm{w}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{\bm{w}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}},\nabla h_{\bm{w}}-\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla h_{\bm{w}}+\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}},\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\nabla h_\vw}\norm{\nabla h_\vw- \nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}+\norm{\nabla h_\vw+ \nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}.

Using the bounds on
\|{\nabla h_{\bm{w}}-\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\|
and
\|{\nabla h_{\bm{w}}+\nabla h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}}\|
derived above, we can bound this by

\norm{\nabla h_\vw}(\frac{\eta}{2}(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2}))+(WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+(2W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{2}))\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}.

The following two bounds hold:

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_\vw}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mW_1^\top\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t)}\leq\norm{\mW_1^\top}_{F}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t)}\leq WC_{2},

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mW_1^\top\nabla f(\mW_1\vx+ \vw_2t)}\leq\norm{\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}^\top}_{F}\norm{\nabla f(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+ \vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}\leq(W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla h_\vw}^{2}+\norm{\nabla h_{\vw_\frac{\eta}{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq WC_{2}\left(\frac{\eta}{2}(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2})\right)+\left(W+\frac{\eta}{2}\right)C_{2}\left(WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+(2W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=WC_{2}\eta\left(WL_{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+C_{2}\right)+2(WC_{2})^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left(WL_{2}\frac{\eta}{2}(\norm{\vx}+\absolutevalue{t})+(2W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{2}\right),

which proves the result.

Lemma D.5
(Upper bound on
\nabla^{2}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}-\nabla^{2}h_{\bm{w}}
)

The following bound holds:

\nabla^{2}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}-\nabla^{2}h_{\bm{w}}\leq C_{3}nk\left(\eta W+\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{2}\right).

Proof
We first write

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}-\nabla^{2}h_{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)\right\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}W_{1,pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)\right\rangle-W_{1,pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\norm{\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)}}\norm{(\grad\partial_pf)(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+\vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}-W_{1,pi}\norm{\mW_{1,col(i)}}\norm{(\grad\partial_pf)(\mW_1\vx+\vw_2t)}\right)

Using the property of
f
that
\norm{Hf(\vz)}_{F}\leq C_{3}
, we can bound this by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\norm{\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)}}C_{3}-W_{1,pi}\norm{\mW_{1,col(i)}}C_{3}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}(W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{3}-W_{1,pi}WC_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left((W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}-W_{1,pi})W+\frac{\eta}{2}W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\right)\leq C_{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(\frac{\eta}{2}W+\frac{\eta}{2}(W+\frac{\eta}{2}))\right)

\displaystyle=C_{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(\eta W+\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{2}\right)=C_{3}nk\left(\eta W+\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{2}\right).

The result now follows.

Lemma D.6
(Upper bound on
\nabla^{2}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}+\nabla^{2}h_{\bm{w}}
)

The following bound holds:

\nabla^{2}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}+\nabla^{2}h_{\bm{w}}\leq C_{3}nk\left(2W^{2}+\eta W+\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{2}\right).

Proof
We first write

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}h_{{\bm{w}}_{\frac{\eta}{2}}}+\nabla^{2}h_{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}W_{1,pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)\right\rangle+W_{1,pi}\left\langle{\bm{W}}_{1,col(i)},(\gradient\partial_{p}f)({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{w}}_{2}t)\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\norm{\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)}}\norm{(\grad\partial_pf)(\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2}}\vx+\vw_{2,\frac{\eta}{2}}t)}+W_{1,pi}\norm{\mW_{1,col(i)}}\norm{(\grad\partial_pf)(\mW_1\vx+\vw_2t)}\right)

Using the property of
f
that
\norm{Hf(\vz)}_{F}\leq C_{3}
, we can bound this by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\norm{\mW_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},col(i)}}C_{3}+W_{1,pi}\norm{\mW_{1,col(i)}}C_{3}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}(W+\frac{\eta}{2})C_{3}+W_{1,pi}WC_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left((W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}+W_{1,pi})W+\frac{\eta}{2}W_{1,\frac{\eta}{2},pi}\right)\leq C_{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left((2W+\frac{\eta}{2})W+\frac{\eta}{2}(W+\frac{\eta}{2}))\right)

\displaystyle=C_{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left(2W^{2}+\eta W+\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{2}\right)=C_{3}nk\left(2W^{2}+\eta W+\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{2}\right).

The result now follows.
