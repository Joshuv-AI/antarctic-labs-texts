The GECKOS Survey: revealing the formation history of a barred galaxy via structural decomposition and resolved spectroscopy
A. Fraser-McKelvie, D. A. Gadotti, F. Fragkoudi, C. de Sá-Freitas, M. Martig, T. Davis, R. Elliott, D. Fisher, M. Hayden, J. van de Sande, A. B. Watts

Abstract
Disentangling the (co-)evolution of individual galaxy structural components remains a difficult task owing to the inability to cleanly isolate light from spatially overlapping components. In this pilot study of PGC 044931, observed as part of the GECKOS survey, we utilise VIRCAM H H -band imaging to decompose the galaxy into five photometric components, three of which contribute > 50 % >50\% of light in given regions: a main disc, a boxy/peanut bulge, and a nuclear disc. When the photometric decompositions are mapped onto MUSE observations, we find remarkably good separation in stellar kinematic space. All three structures occupy unique locations in the parameter space of the ratio of stellar line-of-sight velocity ( V ⋆ \rm{V}_{\star} ) and dispersion, ( σ ⋆ \sigma_{\star} ), and high order stellar skew, ( h 3 h_{3} ). These clear and distinct kinematic signatures give us confidence to make inferences about the formation history of the individual components from observations of the mean light-weighted stellar age and metallicity of the three components. A clear story emerges: one in which an extended galactic disc hosted continued star formation, possibly with the addition of accreted pristine gas. Within the disc, a bar formed and buckled early, building a nuclear disc that continued to enrich via multiple generations of star formation. These results exemplify how careful photometric decompositions, combined with well-resolved stellar kinematic information, can help separate out age-metallicity relations of different components and therefore disentangle the formation history of the galaxy. The results of this pilot survey are applicable to modern, well-resolved spectroscopic galaxy surveys.

Introduction

An enduring challenge for galaxy evolution studies is extricating the (co)-evolution of individual stellar structures from integrated galaxy light.
Previous statistical photometric and spectroscopic decompositions on 100s of parsec-scale imaging
(
Lange et al. 2016
;
Häußler et al. 2022
, e.g.)
and kiloparsec-scale integral field spectroscopic (IFS) datasets
(
Johnston et al. 2017
;
Tabor et al. 2019
;
Oh et al. 2020
;
Pak et al. 2021
, e.g.)
generally divided galaxies into two components: a (de Vaucouleurs) bulge, and an (exponential) disc. These studies were often dictated by constraints on signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) and spatial resolution, coupled with a need for ease of automation.
While statistical in nature, these techniques often do not account for stellar bars, other non-axisymmetric structures, nor the possibility of a non-dispersion-supported central structure.

Added galactic complexity reveals itself with improved spatial resolution and careful, supervised image decompositions.
For example, fitting multiple discy components to a galaxy surface brightness profile can often yield a better fit to a galaxy’s light profile than a simple Sérsic two-component decomposition
(
Gadotti et al. 2015
)
. The era of highly-resolved (10s-100s of parsec) IFS galaxy surveys have revealed clear signatures of discrete dynamical structure in stellar kinematic maps that can be assigned to features such as nuclear discs, stellar bars, boxy/peanut bulges, and axisymmetric discs
(
Gadotti et al. 2020
;
Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. Importantly, dynamically-cold central structures such as nuclear discs have been resolved in multiple galaxies
(
Seidel et al. 2015
;
Guérou et al. 2016
;
Pinna et al. 2019
;
Martig et al. 2021
, e.g.)
; such structures are vital in determining bar ages
(
de Sá-Freitas et al. 2023
;
de Sá-Freitas et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. These results build upon previous theoretical
(
Bureau & Athanassoula 2005
)
and observational
(
Chung & Bureau 2004
)
1D results linking stellar kinematics along a galaxy’s photometric major axis to stellar structures such as bars and nuclear discs.

The importance of separating galaxies into their constituent components arises from the hypothesis that i) these components formed and evolved via different mechanisms, and ii) that some components may be driving galaxy evolution through internal, secular processes
(
Sellwood 2014
, e.g.)
.
Therefore, techniques to cleanly separate light but also resolve meaningful structures, corresponding to different orbital families (remembering that some structures will always be co-spatial regardless of galaxy projection) are paramount.
The result of such will be the ability to infer the star formation histories of individual dynamical stellar structures to help understand their formation pathways.

This paper aims to combine the power of detailed photometric structure decompositions with the information provided by stellar kinematic and stellar populations measurements afforded by IFS observations. In this GECKOS

2.2
Photometric decomposition

Photometric decomposition was performed on VIRCAM
H
-band 1.65
\mu

(

The fits were optimised using the Differential Evolution algorithm, which minimises subjective choices, e.g., by only requiring lower and upper limits for the fitted parameters from the user (Gadotti 2025, submitted).
The best fit was found to include: 1) an extended edge-on disc, 2) an edge-on ring, 3) a central point source, 4) a boxy/peanut, and 5) an edge-on nuclear disc.
A flat sky component that corrects any residuals left from the background subtraction was also included. We note that the fit did not improve when both a thin and thick disc were included. Figure

3.2
Stellar populations

We plot the distribution of average age and metallicity for each of the three galaxy components (the extended disc, boxy/peanut bulge, and nuclear disc) in Figure
3
b. Again, these three components are exhibit distinct behaviour in this parameter space, becoming even clearer in regions where a given component has greater dominance over the light distribution.
The extended disc is of similar metallicity to the boxy/peanut bulge, though spans a range of stellar ages, becoming slightly more metal-poor at younger ages.
The boxy/peanut bulge is almost exclusively old and comparatively metal poor, and the nuclear disc spans a range of average stellar ages, and is offset to be more metal-rich than any other region of the galaxy. The divergence of the nuclear disc’s age-metallicity relation from the main disc and boxy/peanut occurs at
\sim
10 Gyr.

Putting the stellar population information together, a clear picture emerges.
Bars are known to build nuclear discs, which are fed by a protracted supply of cold gas fuelling ongoing star formation as the nuclear disc grows
(
Hawarden et al. 1986
;
Athanassoula 1992
;
Seo et al. 2019
;
Verwilghen et al. 2024
, e.g.)
.
The measured age and metallicity require the nuclear disc to have formed more recently due to ongoing star formation in a small physical region that enriches the local area quickly
(
Leaman et al. 2019
, e.g. through processes such as those described in)
.
Given the clear offset in metallicity of the nuclear disc from 10 Gyr, we can ascertain that the nuclear disc must have formed around that time, and the bar must therefore be at least this old.

Stars map from the disc into the boxy/peanut at the time of bar buckling, meaning that the stellar population of the boxy/peanut should be representative of the disc at that time
(
Martinez-Valpuesta & Gerhard 2013
;
Fragkoudi et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. To first order, there should be no gas present in the boxy/peanut region, hence no further star formation can occur, and the underlying stellar population remained old and metal-poor. Finally, the underlying disc is likely the recipient of pristine gas from an extended HI disc or the cosmic web, allowing ongoing star formation (and hence a range of measured stellar ages), without the intensive enrichment of the nuclear disc region.
Further work to characterise the age of the oldest stellar populations in the nuclear disc
(
de Sá-Freitas et al. 2023
;
de Sá-Freitas et al. 2025
, and hence the age of the bar)
may confirm this scenario.

4
Summary & Conclusions

This Letter presents evidence that careful and detailed photometric decompositions yield multiple coherent stellar substructures that can be used to understand galaxy assembly history.

Through the photometric decomposition of VIRCAM
H
-band imaging, three components were found to dominate the stellar light profile: an extended disc, a boxy/peanut bulge, and a nuclear disc.
A further inner ring and point source were included in the fit but did not contribute significantly to the light profile in any region.
Mapping the photometric decomposition to GECKOS stellar kinematic and light-weighted stellar age and metallicity maps yielded clear separation between all three components in both kinematic and chemical parameter space.
From these decompositions, a clear story emerged in which a disc galaxy formed and buckled a stellar bar long ago. The bar grew a nuclear disc that enriched via multiple generations of star formation fed by inflowing gas along the bar. The underlying disc exhibits ongoing star formation but little chemical enrichment, likely due to an ongoing supply of pristine gas from the cosmic web or an extended HI disc.

This pilot study paves the way for the use of careful photometric decompositions on highly spatially-resolved IFS data to understand the assembly history of galactic components, including star formation histories of individual components. The result of which will be a better understanding of the buildup of galactic structure over cosmic time, and the resultant influence of galaxy internal structure on the overall evolution of the host galaxy.
