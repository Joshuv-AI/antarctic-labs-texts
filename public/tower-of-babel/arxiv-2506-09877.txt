Sequential Dynamics in Ising Spin Glasses
Yatin Dandi, David Gamarnik, Francisco Pernice, Lenka Zdeborová

Abstract
We present the first exact asymptotic characterization of sequential dynamics for a broad class of local update algorithms on the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model with Ising spins. Focusing on dynamics implemented via systematic scan—encompassing Glauber updates at any temperature—we analyze the regime where the number of spin updates scales linearly with system size. Our main result provides a description of the spin-field trajectories as the unique solution to a system of integro-difference equations derived via Dynamical Mean Field Theory (DMFT) applied to a novel block approximation. This framework captures the time evolution of macroscopic observables such as energy and overlap, and is numerically tractable. Our equations serve as a discrete-spin sequential-update analogue of the celebrated Cugliandolo-Kurchan equations for spherical spin glasses, resolving a long-standing gap in the theory of Ising spin glass dynamics. Beyond their intrinsic theoretical interest, our results establish a foundation for analyzing a wide variety of asynchronous dynamics on the hypercube and offer new avenues for studying algorithmic limitations of local heuristics in disordered systems.



1
Introduction

We consider a discrete sequential dynamics on the
Sherrington-Kirpatrick (SK) model
in a linear time scale.
The SK model is the prototypical example of a model of a
disordered system
. It was proposed in 1975
[
SK75
]
as a simplified, mean-field version of the Edwards-Anderson model
[
EA75
]
, whose original motivation in physics was to understand the intriguing properties of certain dilute magnetic alloys. The mathematical definition is as follows. Let
J
be a
\mathrm{GOE}(N)
matrix, i.e., an
N\times N
matrix with centered, jointly Gaussian entries with covariance
\mathbb{E}J_{ij}J_{kl}=(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})/N.
The
Sherrington-Kirpatrick Hamiltonian
, also referred to below as the
energy
,
1
1

1

In physics the Hamiltonian and the energy are usually defined with the opposite sign.
is the random function
H:\{\pm 1\}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

H(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},J\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rangle.

(1)

Then, given an inverse temperature parameter
\beta\in[0,\infty),
associated with the SK model is its Gibbs measure:
the (random) measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}\exp(\beta H(\boldsymbol{\sigma})),

(2)

where
Z_{\beta}
is the normalization constant so that
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mu(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=1,
called the
partition function.

A few years after this model was proposed, in a series of groundbreaking papers
[
Par79
,
Par80a
,
Par80b
]
, Parisi non-rigorously found an exact formula for the limiting
free energy

\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{\beta}.
The subsequent interpretation of Parisi’s solution led to a remarkably complete non-rigorous understanding of the structure of the model at equilibrium (i.e., of the measure
\mu_{\beta}).
Many of the most important aspects of this picture have now been proved; chiefly among them, the Parisi formula for the free energy
[
Gue03
,
Tal06
]
and the ultrametricity conjecture
[
Pan13a
]
.

While a great deal of progress has been achieved in recent decades in understanding the equilibrium properties of this model
[
Pan13b
]
, far less is known about its dynamical aspects under natural dynamics on the configuration space
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
. This is the focus of our paper. The dynamics we analyze are as follows. First, we fix an ordering of the
N
spins, say
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}.
Then, we initialize at a uniformly-random configuration and perform
T
in-order scans through the
N
spins. In each scan, we iterate through the spins
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
one at a time, updating each one according to a pre-specified (randomized) update rule that takes as input the spin’s local field. We allow for any suitably regular update rule, including as a special case Glauber updates at any temperature.

To analyze this algorithm, we consider as an approximation a version of the dynamics with
\delta
-blocks, for
\delta\in(0,1]
: the spin set
[N]
is split into

1/\delta
groups of size
\delta N

each, and updates of spins within each group are
done simultaneously, while transitions between groups are done in a scanning fashion as before.
Our main result provides a full asymptotic description of
the resulting dynamics when
T
is a
constant independent of
N
, in the double limit where first
N\to\infty
and then
\delta\to 0
.
We conjecture that the resulting equations agree with those of the original sequential dynamics without block updates. We will formally address this conjecture in upcoming work, but for now we verify it experimentally in
Section

7
. In upcoming work we also derive the equations for Glauber dynamics, where in each step a uniformly randomly chosen spin gets updated.

The equations we derive for the
\delta
-block
dynamics specify the asymptotic joint
distribution of
\{\sigma_{x}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h^{t}_{x}\}_{t=1}^{T}
for
x\in[0,1],
where
\sigma_{x}^{t}
is the spin value of the
xN
-th spin at the end
of scan
t
, and
h^{t}_{x}
the corresponding local field
experienced by this spin.
This joint distribution up to scan
T
is described via a system of

O(T^{2})
-dimensional differential equations we derive, from which many useful observables can be computed, such
as the energy of the system at the end of scan

T
. While the system of differential equations is somewhat involved and grows with
T
, it is easy
to implement numerically, which we do. We report our
numerical findings in
Section

7
.
To the best of our knowledge, such a detailed description of the spin glass dynamics at a linear time scale was unknown in both the physics and mathematics literatures.
Our results are of relevance to several prior lines of research. We highlight the two most relevant ones.

1.1
Prior work in physics and probability
literature

The first line of investigation relates to the long
history of studies in physics
and probability theory of time dependent
aspects of spin glasses: what does the spin glass system
look like at a given time
t
when it evolves according
to a natural dynamics such as Glauber dynamics? These
questions have been raised in physics already several decades
ago, see e.g.
[
SZ82
,
CK93
]
. The matter is of particular interest because many of the intriguing features observed in the materials that motivated the study of these models in the first place were dynamical in nature, like the so-called
aging
phenomenon (see
[
ADG01
]
for a mathematical treatment). For the Sherrington-Kirpatrick model, the most natural dynamics are Markov chains on the state space
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, with
Glauber dynamics
being the most widely studied version. However, exact asymptotic analysis of
dynamics of this kind, with discrete asynchronous updates, has so far been out of reach for physicists and mathematicians alike.

Other spin glasses are more tractable for the study of dynamical properties. Cugliandolo and Kurchan
[
CK93
]
studied a model related to SK, known as the
pure spherical
p
-spin model
, where the quadratic form (
1
) is replaced by a homogeneous degree-
p
polynomial with Gaussian coefficients, and the state space is relaxed from the hypercube to the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}.
In this model, the natural dynamics is the Langevin diffusion on the sphere.
Cugliandolo and Kurchan non-rigorously derived an exact asymptotic description of these Langevin-type dynamics for the spherical
p
-spin models in the
out of equilibrium
regime, where one runs the dynamics for a time independent of the system size
N
—the continuous analogue of running Glauber dynamics for linearly in
N
many updates. These
Cugliandolo-Kurchan equations
were proven rigorously by Ben Arous, Dembo and Guionnet
[
BADG06
]
after earlier work by the same authors
[
AG95
,
AG97
,
Gui97
,
ADG01
]
. The asymptotic energy achieved by the
dynamics in this time scale was rigorously proven only recently
[
Sel24
]
, confirming a prediction of
[
CK93
]
.

Some large deviations results for time dependent
observables of the SK model are derived
in
[
Gru96
]
, in principle giving an asymptotic description of the dynamics in the out of equilibrium regime. However, this characterization is in terms of the full empirical measure over histories of pairs of spins and local fields, an object which has proven too rich to extract useful information from. By contrast, like in the case of
[
CK93
]
, our equations can be written in terms of the correlation and response functions only, which are finite-dimensional objects.

A description of the dynamics
corresponding to the Glauber
dynamics is also reported in
[
Mac21
]
, but it appears to be correct only for
sufficiently high temperature
[
Mac
]
.

The discrete (non-spherical) SK model itself can be treated via Langevin dynamics as well
[
CK94
]
by relaxing the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}
to the real line, and adding a potential which penalizes the dynamics for being far from
\{\pm 1\}
. However, its relevance to the genuinely
discrete dynamics is not entirely clear.

The dynamical behavior directly on the hypercube was also treated in the physics literature, but so far only for the case of synchronous or parallel dynamics where every spin is updated at every time step
[
EO92
,
EO94
,
EBKZ24
]
. The properties of the parallel dynamics are, however, very different from those of sequential dynamics where the spins are changing one by one. For example, in the latter case the energy is non-decreasing in time, whereas in parallel dynamics energy can be non-monotone, and limiting cycles can appear in large times; see examples of this in
[
EBKZ24
]
. We remark that the parallel dynamics studied in
[
EBKZ24
]
corresponds to setting
\delta=1
, i.e., simultaneously updating a single block of all spins, and hence our analysis also provides a proof of the limiting equations established therein.

In summary, the dynamics corresponding to sequential updates of discrete
spins (as opposed to relaxations)
has remained an outstanding open problem in both the physics and mathematics of disordered systems. In particular, no analogue of the asymptotically exact Cugliandolo-Kurchan equations is known in that case. This is the problem
which we address in the present work.

Due to the absence of an exact characterization of the dynamics, physicists have resorted instead to simulations, which have demonstrated that the behavior of the dynamics depends strongly on the details of the spin update protocol.
In particular, Parisi
[
Par03
]
ran simulations on three natural dynamics at
zero temperature
. He called them the
greedy algorithm
, the
reluctant algorithm
and the
sequential algorithm
. In the greedy algorithm, one finds at each step the single spin-flip which would result in the greatest energy increase, and performs it. In the reluctant dynamics, one finds the single spin-flip which would result in the minimal positive energy change, and performs it. The sequential algorithm is the algorithm described at the beginning of the section and which we analyze in this paper. We will also call it
systematic scan
following
[
DGJ08
,
DGJ06
]
.
Recall that this is the dynamics where
one starts with a uniformly-random configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
and repeatedly scans through the spins from left to right, flipping sequentially each one
according to a prescribed rule, for example via a zero-temperature Glauber update.
The literature also refers to this type of update as ordered sequential update
[
FJ99
]
, or systematic update
[
PW13
]
. Variants of these protocols were studied in many follow-up numerical papers, e.g.
[
BCDEG03
,
CGG+05
,
CGGV05
,
EBKZ24
]
, but an exact asymptotic characterization of any of the asynchronous ones remained open prior to our work.

1.2
Prior works in theoretical computer science
literature

There exists an extensive theoretical computer
science literature regarding the power
and limitations of MCMC based methods such as Glauber
dynamics in the context of various statistical mechanics
models, including spin glasses
[
SJ89
]
.
The primary focus of the
computer science literature is understanding the conditions
under which the chain converges (mixes) to stationarity polynomially fast, as this provides a polynomial time algorithm
for approximately sampling from the stationary distribution. Fast and slow mixing of Glauber
dynamics has been studied recently also in the context
of spin glasses. In particular,
fast mixing is known to hold
at sufficiently high temperature
[
EKZ22
]
.
Slow mixing
is known to hold when the model exhibits a certain
clustering (shattering) phenomenon, which
p
-spin models
provably exhibit for large enough
p
and a certain
temperature
range
[
GJK23
]
,

[
Ala24
]
.
Fast sampling algorithms based on methods other
than MCMC are also
known
[
AMS23
,
HMP24
]
, as well as simulated annealing
[
HMRW24
]
.

The techniques for establishing
slow (super-polynomial or exponentially
large) mixing times
are well-developed and typically rely on
Poincare-Cheeger inequalities and their variants.
There is an important caveat however associated with this slow mixing
approach in general: it verifies
slow mixing only with respect
to the
worst-case
starting states. Namely,
when applicable it implies existence of
some

states from which time to mix is large. This
does not rule out the possibility of fast mixing
from
typical
(random) starting
states. Understanding mixing from random
starting states is more physically relevant and is a challenging question,
currently poorly understood.
In addition to the references provided in the
previous section related to aging in spherical glasses,
some recent
steps in this direction
include
[
CMZ25
,
GJW24
]
and
[
HMRW24
]
.

The computer science literature has rarely focused on what can be said about Markov chains in regimes where mixing is slow. An important recent exception is
[
LMR+24
]
, where the authors propose a framework of
locally stationary distributions
. These are distributions which Markov chains always reach fast (even in the absence of mixing) and from which useful algorithmic consequences can be derived. For example, the authors show that Glauber dynamics can achieve constant correlation with the planted signal in the spiked matrix model and the stochastic block model for sufficiently high signal to noise ratio, even if the Markov chain fails to mix. By contrast, in our work, instead of deriving approximate guarantees about the state of the Markov chain at large times, we give an
exact
description of the state of the chain at
all
times from the point of view of macroscopic observables in the linear time scale. Through this lens, we anticipate that our work will prove useful to extract algorithmic consequences for a wide variety of models, though we do not focus on this aspect in the present work.

1.3
The proof technique

Our method of analysis relies
on the iterative Gaussian conditioning framework developed in
[
Bol14
,
BM11
]
.
This is a widely applicable method
of analysis of
Gaussian processes when conditional on constantly
many linear projections.
In particular, it is the basis of the asymptotic analysis of the family of
Approximate Message Passing (AMP)
algorithms originating
from the TAP equations
[
TAP77
,
Bol14
]
. AMP algorithms have enjoyed a
wide range of
applications both in spin glass
theory
[
Mon24
]

and beyond, and in particular in the field of
statistical
inference
[
FVR+22
]
. Recently, the applicability of the Gaussian conditioning technique has been extended to a general class of first order algorithms on Gaussian data in
[
CCM21
,
GTM+24
]
, recovering the equations described in statistical physics under Dynamical Mean Field Theory
(DMFT)
[
SCS88
]
. Earlier rigorous proofs of such DMFT equations were based on large deviations over paths
[
BADG06
,
BA03
,
AG95
,
AG97
,
ADG01
]
. The algorithms in
[
CCM21
,
GTM+24
]
involve simultaneously updating all the coordinates, such as Langevin dynamics and gradient descent. In contrast, the sequential spin-by-spin updates in our setting result in limiting equations possessing novel structure such as the inter-spin interactions accumulating into a system of ODEs.

Roughly speaking, the method is based on the
following observation: given a GOE matrix
A
,
its distribution when conditioned
on projections on finitely many vectors

v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}
can be nicely decomposed into two terms. The first term corresponds to the values of the
projections, and the second term
corresponds to a fresh copy
\tilde{A}
of
A

independent of these projections, see

1
in

Section

4
. This
fact allows for convenient and succinct
representation of the states of the system
after constantly many updates of various kinds,
such as the ones underlying AMP.

Unfortunately, this key property underlying
Gaussian conditioning does not appear to be of use
for the analysis of sequential algorithms
studied in this paper, since those
correspond to the number of projections scaling
with
N
. This is why we introduce an intermediate

\delta
-block dynamics which keeps the number of
Gaussian projections bounded. As mentioned earlier, in future work
we will establish that, as
\delta

approaches zero, the
\delta
-block dynamics
approaches the dynamics of the sequential
algorithm. For now this is numerically verified in
Section

7
. The
introduction and the exact analysis of the

\delta
-block dynamics is the main technical novelty
of our paper, which we anticipate to be of use
beyond the scope of this work.

As alluded to earlier, the
\delta
-block dynamics is defined as follows. We split the spin set
[N]
into
1/\delta

consecutive blocks
B(1)=\{1,\ldots,\delta N\},B(2)=\{\delta N+1,\ldots,2\delta N\},\ldots
,
where the integrality of
1/\delta
and
\delta N

is assumed for convenience. We then repeatedly perform sequential scans through the blocks
B(1),\dots,B(1/\delta)
in this order, updating all spins within each block simultaneously. Specifically, suppose we are in scan
t
and we arrive at block
j\in[1/\delta]
in configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t,j-1}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, having already updated blocks
B(1),\dots,B(j-1)
within pass
t.
We first compute the local fields
\boldsymbol{h}^{t}_{i}=(J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t,j-1})_{i}
for all
i\in B(j)
, and then we update all spins
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t,j}_{i}
for
i\in B(j)
according to the corresponding local field
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{t},
leaving all other spins unchanged:
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{t,j}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{t,j-1}
for
i\notin B(j).

We prove that for each
T,j,k
the
spins
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{s,j}\}_{s=0}^{T}
and fields
\{\boldsymbol{h}^{s}_{i}\}_{s=1}^{T}
are approximately i.i.d. across
i\in B(k)
with a common joint
distribution which we describe. We detail
this distribution as an incremental
stochastic process defined recursively over

T,j,k
. Finally, fixing
any
x\in[0,1]
, we show that the distribution of this stochastic process evaluated at block
j=xN
converges
to the solution of a system of differential equations
evaluated at
x
as

\delta\to 0
.
Our proof technique is rather flexible and
accommodates broad classes of update rules,
including Glauber updates at any temperature.

1.4
Notational conventions

We now establish some notation conventions that will be used throughout the paper. First, we use boldface font to denote vectors or matrices with dimension growing with
N
, which we call
high dimensional
. On the other hand, we will use non-boldface font to denote vectors or matrices with dimension that is constant in
N,
which we call
low dimensional
. These non-boldface quantities will be of two kinds. First, we will have quantities (either deterministic or random) which are entirely
N
-independent, like those that are the solution to our asymptotic equations (see (
4
)-(
11
) below). We will refer to these as the
Effective Dynamics
, since they simulate in low dimension the (high-dimensional) dynamics that we analyze, in the limit
N\to\infty
. Second, there will be quantities which are low-dimensional functions of boldface quantities, so they depend on
N
despite having dimension that is constant in
N
. These second quantities will be denoted with a hat. For instance, below we will often use the
(T+1)\times(T+1)
overlap matrix of spin vectors across times
t=0,\dots,T
, which will be denoted
\hat{C}.
Finally, in
Section

4
, in order to compare the limiting dynamics to the finite-
N
dynamics, it will be useful for us to take a low-dimensional stochastic process and form high-dimensional vectors whose entries are i.i.d. draws from distributions determined by the low-dimensional process. We will denote these “i.i.d. processes” with a tilde, e.g.
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}.

In this paper, we will deal with matrices with different indexing conventions. Some matrices will have indexing of their rows and columns beginning with zero, others beginning with one, and yet others will have rows beginning with one and columns beginning with zero. We will be explicit about the appropriate indexing convention when each matrix is defined, but in all cases the convention is chosen to agree with the physical interpretation. For a matrix
M
with zero-indexed rows and columns and
t\in\mathbb{N},
we let
M^{(t)}=\{M_{ss^{\prime}}\}_{s,s^{\prime}=0}^{t}
, and for a matrix with one-indexed rows and columns, we define
M^{(t)}=\{M_{ss^{\prime}}\}_{s,s^{\prime}=1}^{t}
. Even when the matrix
M
is
(t+1)\times(t+1)
(resp.
t\times t
), we will sometimes write
M^{(t)}
to remind the reader of this fact. Moreover, for indices
s\leq s^{\prime},t\leq t^{\prime}
, we use
M_{s:s^{\prime},t:t^{\prime}}
to refer to the block of
M
formed by rows
s
to
s^{\prime}
(inclusive) and columns
t
to
t^{\prime}
(inclusive), and let
M_{s:s^{\prime},t}:=M_{s:s^{\prime},t:t}
and
M_{s,t:t^{\prime}}:=M_{s:s,t:t^{\prime}}.
Similarly, if we have a sequence of vectors or scalars
v^{0},\dots,v^{t}
or
v^{1},\dots,v^{t},
we let
v^{(t)}
denote the tuple
(v^{0},\dots,v^{t})
or
(v^{1},\dots,v^{t})
, respectively.

2
Main result

In this section, we formally define the problem and describe some consequences of our result. For a formal statement of our main theorem in its full generality, see
Section

2.1
.

Our main result gives an asymptotic characterization of the dynamics on
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
of a certain algorithm associated with the Hamiltonian given by
Equation

1
. The algorithm we analyze is closely related to a very well known and widely used algorithm known as the
systematic scan
(see, e.g.,
[
DGJ06
,
DGJ08
]
), which we describe first in order to contrast it with our algorithm.

The systematic scan is simplest in its
greedy
, or
zero-temperature
, version, which is the one Parisi
[
Par03
]
studied: given a number of passes
T
, we start from a uniformly-random configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},
and we scan through all the indices
1,\dots,N
from left to right (in a fixed order)
T
times, sequentially flipping each bit whenever that results in an increase in energy (the value of
H
). In the general systematic scan, instead of deterministically flipping each bit if that results in an increase in energy, we flip it with a probability described as a function
p:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
of the energy change that would result from the flip. If, for a given constant
\beta>0,
the function
p=p_{\beta}
is chosen as
p(x)=1/(1+e^{-2\beta x}),
this is known as the
systematic Glauber dynamics at inverse temperature
\beta
, and in this case it can be checked that this algorithm gives a reversible Markov chain with stationary measure given by (
2
).

Given a configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},
the change in energy from flipping a given spin
i
is known, up to a sign, as the
local field
at
i.
Formally, given
i\in[N]
and a configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},
we define the local field at
i

\boldsymbol{h}_{i}=\boldsymbol{h}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=(J\boldsymbol{\sigma})_{i}.

(3)

\Omega

x\in\mathbb{R},
we sometimes use the notation

to denote the random variable
\omega\mapsto c(\omega,x).
To avoid degenerate cases, we assume three things about
c

G
is a standard Gaussian random variable, we have
0<\Pr[c(G)=1]<1,
(2) we take
\Omega=[0,1]

\partial(c^{-1}(1))
has Lebesgue measure zero in
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}.
Then, we start from a random
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\in\{0,1\}^{N}

i

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, we replace
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\leftarrow c(\boldsymbol{h}_{i})

we recover the systematic Glauber dynamics.

Algorithm 1

Block Systematic Scan

We note that, as the notation suggests, the vector
\boldsymbol{h}^{t}
does not depend on

Indeed, each block
\{\boldsymbol{h}^{t}_{i}:i\in B(j)\}
of

gathers the local fields of the sites in that block immediately before the block is updated. Hence, these blocks of values are computed once within each pass and do not evolve beyond that. Finally, for each

Our main result is an exact characterization of Algorithm

(in a sense specified below), in the double limit where first
N\to\infty
and then
\delta\to 0
, as the unique solution to a system of explicit integro-difference equations. We conjecture that this agrees with the limiting dynamics of the systematic scan itself (without block updates). As mentioned in the introduction, we will address this conjecture in an upcoming work, as part of a more general framework to prove universality results for out of equilibrium discrete-update dynamics. For now, we verify this conjecture experimentally in

.

We first describe what our results say about Algorithm
1
. For the purposes of this section, we will describe a weaker version of our main theorem (
1
), but one that is easier to describe succinctly. Suppose we fix
x\in[0,1]
and a sequence of indices
i_{N}\in[N]
such that
i_{N}/N\to x\in[0,1]
as
N\to\infty.
Let
\nu_{N,i_{N},\delta,T}
be the joint law of the random variables
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t}_{i_{N}},t=0,\dots,T
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{i_{N}}^{t},t=1,\dots,T
from Algorithm
1
, which we call the
spin-field history at

i_{N}
. A consequence of

is that the weak limit
\nu_{x,T}:=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\nu_{N,i_{N},\delta,T}
exists. Moreover, the spin-field histories become “weakly decoupled” across

, in the following sense. If we let

\widehat{\nu}_{N,\delta,T}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})}

be the empirical measure of spin-field histories, then

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\widehat{\nu}_{N,\delta,T}=\int_{0}^{1}\nu_{x,T}dx,

where the convergence is weakly in probability. More precisely, for every (pseudo-Lipschitz, see
1
) test function
\varphi,
we have that
\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{N,\delta,T}
converges in probability to a deterministic limit which depends on
\delta
, and as
\delta\to 0
, this limit converges in
\mathbb{R}
to
\int_{0}^{1}\int\varphi d\nu_{x,T}dx.
Essentially every macroscopic observable of interest is a function of the empirical measure over spin-field histories. Examples include the energy, the magnetization, the two-point overlap (the normalized inner product between the configuration of the dynamics at two different times), etc. Hence, our result shows that all of these quantities concentrate, and their deterministic asymptotic values can be computed from
\nu_{x,T}.

Most importantly, our result gives an explicit description of this limit law
\nu_{x,T}
, which we now describe by specifying a procedure to obtain a sample
\{\sigma_{x}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h_{x}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
from
\nu_{x,T}.
To do this, we will first describe a generic procedure which takes as input a PSD matrix
K^{(T)}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
, with indexing starting from one, and a sequence of vectors

and outputs a joint sample
\{\sigma^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Then, the sample
\{\sigma_{x}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h_{x}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is obtained by choosing
K^{(T)}=\Sigma^{(T)}(x)
and
v^{t}=f^{t}(x),t=1,\dots,T
, where
\Sigma^{(T)}(x)
and
f^{t}(x),t=1,\dots,T
are specified below by a system of ODE.

We now describe the sampling procedure given
K^{(T)}
and
v^{(T)}:=(v^{1},\dots,v^{T}).
First, we sample
\sigma^{0}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{\pm 1\}.
Then, independently of
\sigma^{0}
, we sample

of zero mean and covariance matrix
K^{(T)}
. Finally, we recursively define, for

\begin{cases}h^{t}=G^{t}+\left\langle v^{t},\sigma^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\\
\sigma^{t}=c(h^{t}),\end{cases}

(4)

where for

recall that

and where the randomness of

Below, we use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{K^{(T)},v^{(T)}}
to denote the expectation operator with respect to the joint law given by (
4

To give a full description of the limiting law
\nu_{x,T},
it remains to specify the quantities
\Sigma^{(T)}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
and
f^{t}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
for

These, viewed as functions of

are the unique solution to a system of equations which we now specify.
We first state them as a sequence of ordinary differential equations, since in this form it becomes evident that there is a unique solution. Later we shall write them in the equivalent integro-difference form, which is closer to the physical interpretation. To describe the equations, we first need to define three matrix-valued functions parameterized by the time horizon

which we suppress in the notation to avoid clutter. These are
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}\times\left(\times_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{R}^{s}\right)\to\mathbb{R}^{(T+1)\times(T+1)}

\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}\times\left(\times_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{R}^{s}\right)\to\mathbb{R}^{T\times(T+1)}
. We will index the rows and columns of the output of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{C}
from 0 to
T
to keep it consistent with the physical interpretation as the pass number, with the zeroth pass representing the initial condition. Similarly, the rows of the output of
\mathcal{R}
will be indexed from
1
to
T,
while the columns will be indexed from
0
to
T.
We define
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{s,t}(K^{(T)},v^{(T)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{K^{(T)},v^{(T)}}[\sigma^{s}\sigma^{t}]

\displaystyle 0\leq s,t\leq T

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{s,t}(K^{(T)},v^{(T)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{K^{(T)},v^{(T)}}[G^{s}\sigma^{t}]

\displaystyle 1\leq s\leq T,0\leq t\leq T.

(6)

Finally, the value of

is defined recursively as the unique upper-triangular matrix

with zeros on the diagonal which satisfies the recursion

\displaystyle A_{0:t-1,t}

\displaystyle=\left(A^{(t-1)}-(K^{(t)})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}\right)(C^{(t-1)})^{-1}C_{0:t-1,t}+(K^{(t)})^{-1}R_{1:t,t},

(7)

where
C=\mathcal{C}(K^{(T)},v^{(T)})
and
R=\mathcal{R}(K^{(T)},v^{(T)})
. Above, we are assuming that the input to
\mathcal{A}
is such that
K^{(t)}
and

are invertible for

. If this is not the case, we define

to be the all-zero matrix, although as we show in

Now, the sequence of ODEs which specify
\Sigma^{(T)}(x)
and
f^{t}(x),t=1,\dots,T
is as follows

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Sigma^{(T)}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{1:T,1:T}(\Sigma^{(T)}(x),f^{(T)}(x))-\mathcal{C}_{0:T-1,:T-1}(\Sigma^{(T)}(x),f^{(T)}(x))

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}f^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{0:t-1,t}(\Sigma^{(T)}(x),f^{(T)}(x))-\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathcal{A}_{0:t-2,t-1}(\Sigma^{(T)}(x),f^{(T)}(x))\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle t=1,\dots,T,

(9)

with the initial condition

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(T)}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}_{0:T-1,0:T-1}(\Sigma^{(T-1)}(y),f^{(T-1)}(y))dy

(10)

\displaystyle f^{t}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathcal{A}_{0:t-2,t-1}(\Sigma^{(T-1)}(y),f^{(T-1)}(y))\end{bmatrix}dy

\displaystyle t=1,\dots,T.

(11)

This is a

where the initial condition is

The same equations can also be written in integro-difference form. This formulation is closer to the physics, but makes it less transparent that there is a unique solution. Below, for the equations to be closed, one has to interpret

to make the equations more compact. Then we have

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(T)}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}C_{1:T,1:T}(y)dy+\int_{x}^{1}C_{0:T-1,0:T-1}(y)dy

\displaystyle f^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}A_{0:t-1,t}(y)dy+\int_{x}^{1}\begin{bmatrix}0\\

\displaystyle C_{st}(x)

\displaystyle s,t=0,\dots,T

\displaystyle R_{st}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[G_{x}^{s}\sigma_{x}^{t}]

\displaystyle s,t=0,\dots,T

(15)

\displaystyle A_{0:t-1,t}(y)

\displaystyle=\left(A^{(t-1)}(y)-\Sigma^{(t)}(y)^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}(y)\right)C^{(t-1)}(y)^{-1}C_{0:t-1,t}(y)

(16)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Sigma^{(t)}(y)^{-1}R_{1:t,t}(y)

\displaystyle t=1,\dots,T

\displaystyle=0

(17)

2.1
Main Theorem

We now give the formal statement of our main theorem. To describe the weak convergence in our main result, we introduce the following class of test functions.

Definition 1

(Pseudo-Lipschitz function)
.

We say that a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is
pseudo-Lipschitz of order
k
if there exists a constant
L
such that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert{f(x)-f(y)\rvert}\leq L(1+\|{x}\|^{k}+\|{y}\|^{k})\|{x-y}\|\,.

We now state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
x\in[0,1]
and
T>0.

For each
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
j=\lfloor\frac{x}{\delta}\rfloor\in\mathbb{N}
. Let

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t}(\delta),t=0,\dots,T
and
\boldsymbol{h}^{t}=\boldsymbol{h}^{t}(\delta),t=1,\dots,T
be the vectors produced by Algorithm
1
. Then, for all
\varphi:\{\pm 1\}^{T+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, pseudo-Lipschitz of finite order in the second argument, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\delta N}\sum_{i=j\delta N}^{(j+1)\delta N-1}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(T)}_{i},\boldsymbol{h}^{(T)}_{i})=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\sigma_{x}^{(T)},h_{x}^{(T)})],

where the first limit is in probability, the second is in
\mathbb{R}
, and the expectation on the right hand side is with respect to the distribution explicitly described by the equations (
4
)-(
11
) above.

We obtain as an immediate consequence the convergence of the full empirical measure of spin-field histories.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{\boldsymbol{h}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be as in
1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(T)}_{i},\boldsymbol{h}^{(T)}_{i})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\sigma_{x}^{(T)},h_{x}^{(T)})]dx,

where the first limit is in probability, the second in
\mathbb{R}
, and the expectations on the right hand side are with respect to the distribution explicitly described by the equations (
4
)-(
11
) above.

Proof.

The corollary follows from
1
and the Bounded Convergence Theorem, since

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\sigma_{x}^{(T)},h_{x}^{(T)})]<\infty

as
\sup_{t\in[T]}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\left\lVert f^{t}(x)\right\rVert_{2}<\infty
by
5
and, for all
x\in[0,1],t\in[T],
we have
\mathsf{Var}(G^{t}|G^{(t-1)})\leq\Sigma^{(T)}_{t,t}(x)=1
, so
\{h_{x}^{t}:x\in[0,1],t\in[T]\}
are uniformly subgaussian.
∎

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}(H(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T})-H(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}))

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\sigma_{x}^{t}-\sigma_{x}^{t-1})h_{x}^{t}\right]dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t,t}(x)-R_{t,t-1}(x)+\left\langle f^{t}(x),C_{0:t-1,t}(x)-C_{0:t-1,t-1}(x)\right\rangle\right)dx

T=1.
Denote by

\boldsymbol{h}^{t}_{i}=\sum_{k<j}\boldsymbol{h}^{t,k\rightarrow j}_{i}+\sum_{k\geq j}\boldsymbol{h}^{t-1,k\rightarrow j}_{i}.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|j}

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{(t),j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+1)\times(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}N}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(t),k\rightarrow j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+1)\times(t+1)}.

\displaystyle P_{U}=\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)}((\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)})^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)})^{\top},

P_{U}=\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)}((\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)})^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{(p)})^{\top}.

We now introduce a notion of asymptotic equivalence which will be used throughout our proofs.

Definition 2
.

For
p\geq 1
a constant and two random matrices
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
, we use the notation

\displaystyle X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\simeq}}Y

to denote

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left\lVert X-Y\right\rVert_{F}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{P}0,

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{F}
denotes the Frobenious norm and
\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{P}
denotes convergence in probability.

The above notion is closed under multiplication and addition, in the following sense.

Lemma 1
.

For any
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
with
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\simeq}}Y
and
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{P}0
, we have

\displaystyle Xu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\simeq}}Yv.

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence of Slutsky’s theorem and the following inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert Xu-Yv\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert X-Y\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert_{2}.

∎

Lemma 2

(Existence of tempered distributional derivatives)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a Lebesgue measurable function. Suppose
f
is of at most polynomial growth: there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
such that
|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{k}
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be the Schwartz space of all functions
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that, for all
k,m\in\mathbb{N},
the function
x^{m}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\phi(x)
is bounded. Then the functional

T(\phi)=-\int f(x)\phi^{\prime}(x)dx.

is continuous as a map
T:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
(in the suitable topology). Below, we abuse notation and formally write

\int f^{\prime}(x)\phi(x)dx:=T(\phi).

We refer the reader to standard textbooks for a proof as well as general discussion of tempered distributions, e.g.
[
Fol99
, Section 9]
. We now state and prove a version of Stein’s Lemma for distributional derivatives suitable for our setting.

Lemma 3

(Stein’s Lemma)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Lebesgue measurable function of at most polynomial growth in the sense above. We interpret the output of
f
as a row vector, and let
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})
be a centered Gaussian row vector with non-singular covariance
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},
so that
Z^{\top}f(Z)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z^{\top}f(Z)]

\displaystyle=K\mathbb{E}[\partial_{Z}f(Z)],

where
\partial_{Z}f(Z)
denotes the (distributional) Jacobian
(\partial_{Z}f(Z))_{ij}=\partial_{Z_{i}}f_{j}(Z)
.

Proof.

By definition of the distributional derivative, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z^{\top}f(Z)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}|K|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}xK^{-1}x^{\top}\right)x^{\top}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=K\int\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}|K|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}xK^{-1}x^{\top}\right)K^{-1}x^{\top}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=K\int\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}|K|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}xK^{-1}x^{\top}\right)\partial_{x}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=K\mathbb{E}[\partial_{Z}f(Z)],

where in the third step we use the fact that
\partial_{x}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}xK^{-1}x^{\top})=\exp(-\frac{1}{2}xK^{-1}x^{\top})K^{-1}x^{\top}
and then integration by parts, which works for distributional derivatives by definition.
∎

Lemma 4

(Vanishing projections on GOE matrices)
.

Suppose that
W\sim\text{GOE}(N)
and let
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
be independent of
N
. Let
U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p},\mathbb{R}^{N\times q}
denote any sequence of matrices independent of
W
such that
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{F},\mathbb{E}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{F}=o(N^{3/4})
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert U^{\top}WV\right\rVert_{F}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{P}0.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert U^{T}WV\right\rVert_{F}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{3}}\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

∎

Lemma 5

(Law of projection on GOE)
.

Suppose that
W\sim\text{GOE}(N)
. Then, for any deterministic vector
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle W\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert^{2}I+\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top})/N)

Proof.

The fact that
\mathbb{E}W\boldsymbol{u}
is immediate. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle N\cdot\mathbb{E}\left((W\boldsymbol{u})(W\boldsymbol{u})^{\top}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=N\cdot\mathbb{E}\sum_{k,\ell}W_{ik}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}W_{j\ell}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\left(2\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{k\neq i}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{2}\right)+(1-\delta_{ij})\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert^{2}I+\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top})_{ij},

as desired.
∎

Lemma 6

(Gaussian conditioning)
.

Suppose that
W\sim\text{GOE}(N)
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{u}^{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote a fixed sequence of vectors, and let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
denote a matrix such that
V^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{i}=0
for all
i\in[p]
. Then, the random variables
V^{\top}WV
and
\{W\boldsymbol{u}^{i}\}_{i\in[p]}
are independent.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(V^{\top}WV)_{k\ell}(W\boldsymbol{u}^{i})_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sum_{srt}V_{sk}W_{sr}V_{r\ell}W_{jt}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{r}V_{jk}V_{r\ell}\boldsymbol{u}^{i}_{r}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}V_{sk}V_{j\ell}\boldsymbol{u}_{s}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{jk}}{N}(V^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{i})_{\ell}+\frac{V_{j\ell}}{N}(V^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{i})_{k}

\displaystyle=0,

as desired.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times k}
be two (sequences of) random variables with
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\simeq}}Y
and such that for every
k>0
there exists a constant
B_{k}>0
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Pr\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert^{k}\geq B_{k}\right)=0

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Pr\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert Y_{i}\right\rVert^{k}\geq B_{k}\right)=0.

Let
\widehat{\nu}_{X},\widehat{\nu}_{Y}
be the measures in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
given by
\widehat{\nu}_{X}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
\widehat{\nu}_{Y}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Y_{i}}
. Then, for every pseudo-Lipschitz function
\varphi,
we have

\left|\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{X}-\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{Y}\right|\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{P}0.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{X}-\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{Y}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\varphi(X_{i})-\varphi(Y_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(1+\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert^{k}+\left\lVert Y_{i}\right\rVert^{k})\left\lVert X_{i}-Y_{i}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{L^{2}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert^{k}+\left\lVert Y_{i}\right\rVert^{k})^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert X_{i}-Y_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{3L^{2}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}1+\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert^{2k}+\left\lVert Y_{i}\right\rVert^{2k}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert X_{i}-Y_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}.

Hence, for every
\varepsilon>0,
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left|\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{X}-\int\varphi d\widehat{\nu}_{Y}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert^{2k}\geq B_{2k}\right)+\Pr\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert Y_{i}\right\rVert^{2k}\geq B_{2k}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Pr\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert X_{i}-Y_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3L^{2}(1+2B_{2k})}\right).

Each of the three terms vanish by assumption, concluding the proof.
∎

Finally, we prove a technical result that will be useful when we construct couplings between solutions to our equations and the dynamics of Algorithm
1
. It essentially says that, to construct such a coupling, it suffices to couple the fields, since the spins become automatically coupled as a consequence.

Lemma 8
.

Let
c:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\{\pm 1\}
be an update rule such that the boundary
D=\partial(c^{-1}(+1))
has Lebesgue measure zero. Let
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\in(\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\{\pm 1\}^{T+1})^{N}
have rows sampled i.i.d. from
Equation

4
with some
v^{(T)}
and a non-singular
K^{(T)}.
Recall that
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in(\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\{\pm 1\}^{T+1})^{N}
is the process outputted by Algorithm
1
. Assume the following:

•

The randomness of
c
has been coupled between
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
as follows. There exist i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
random variables
\{U_{i}^{t}:i\in[N],t\in[T]\}
such that for all
i\in[N]
and
t\in[T],
we have
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}=c(U_{i}^{t},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{t})
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{t}=c(U_{i}^{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{t})
, where
U_{i}^{t}
is independent of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{t}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{t}
.

•

There exists a block
j\in[1/\delta]
such that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0,j}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{0,j}
and
\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{t,j}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
in probability for all
t\in[T].

Then, we have

\frac{1}{N}\#\{i\in B(j):\exists s\leq T\text{ such that }\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{s}_{i}\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{s}_{i}\}\to 0

as
N\to\infty
in probability.

Proof.

By a union bound, it suffices to fix
t\in[T]
and show that
\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t,j}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{t,j}\right\rVert_{0}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
in probability.
The assumption that
K^{(T)}
is non-singular ensures that the probability measure
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\times\mathrm{Law}(h^{t})
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
. For
\varepsilon>0
, let

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\{i\in B(j):d((U_{i}^{t},\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{t}),D)<\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\{i\in B(j):|\boldsymbol{h}^{t}_{i}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t}_{i}|<\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\{i\in B(j):\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t}_{i}\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{t}_{i}\}.

We have
\Delta\subseteq A_{\varepsilon}\cup B_{\varepsilon}^{C}
. Hence, for all
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0,
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t,j}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{t,j}\right\rVert_{0}\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}N}\left(\mathbb{E}|A_{\varepsilon}|+\mathbb{E}|B_{\varepsilon}^{C}|\right).

We have
\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}|B_{\varepsilon}^{C}|\to 0
by assumption, because for all
\alpha>0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}|B_{\varepsilon}^{C}|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\Pr[|B_{\varepsilon}^{C}|\geq\alpha N]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\Pr\left[\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{t,j}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\alpha\varepsilon^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\to\alpha.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}|A_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}^{t},h^{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{\varepsilon}}.

By continuity of probability, taking
\varepsilon\to 0
the right hand side converges to
\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}^{t},h^{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{D},
which is zero by absolute continuity. This concludes the proof.
∎

4
Dynamics at a fixed
\delta>0

This section is organized as follows.

(1)

In
Section

4.1
, we apply the Gaussian conditioning technique to obtain a description of the law of
J
conditioning on iterates of Algorithm
1
.

(2)

In
Section

4.2
, we utilize part (1) to obtain a description of the law of the field contributions

from Algorithm

which are functions of the empirical measure of spins and fields. Hence, one expects them to concentrate for

(3)

In

and spins
\{\sigma^{(t),j}\in\{\pm 1\}^{t}:t\geq 1,j\in[1/\delta]\}
at any finite
\delta>0
. This stochastic process, which we term the Effective Dynamics, or

, plays the role of equations (
4
) and (
12
)-(

) at a fixed

(4)

In

for

which subsumes the distribution over

in part (3). We additionally derive some key simplifying properties satisfied by this lifted Effective Process.

(5)

In

, we show that if one takes the “approximately decoupled” process from part (3) and pretends that the coefficients

concentrate to quantities

which are given by the low-dimensional lifted Effective Process from part (4), then what one gets is exactly a high-dimensional process which is independent across

Finally, in

4.1
Dynamics of the conditional law of
J

In this section, we utilize the iterative conditioning technique introduced in
[
Bol14
]
to describe the evolution of
J
’s distribution, conditioned on the iterates in Algorithm
1
. The resulting description splits the projection of the spin blocks
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}
onto
J
as follows:

\boldsymbol{h}^{t,j\to k}\approx\text{Independent Gaussian noise}+\text{deterministic contributions from past iterates}.

(23)

To proceed with the proof, we begin by noting the following elementary decomposition.

Lemma 9
.

For any
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
and matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, we have

\displaystyle J=P^{\perp}_{V}JP^{\perp}_{V}+P_{V}JP^{\perp}_{V}+JP_{V}.

Proof.

Recall that by definition
P^{\perp}_{V}+P_{V}=I
. The desired statement is then obtained by substituting the identity
I
with
P^{\perp}_{V}+P_{V}
as follows:

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=JI

\displaystyle=J(P^{\perp}_{V}+P_{V})

\displaystyle=IJ(P^{\perp}_{V})+JP_{V}

\displaystyle=(P^{\perp}_{V}+P_{V})J(P^{\perp}_{V})+JP_{V}

\displaystyle=P^{\perp}_{V}JP^{\perp}_{V}+P_{V}JP^{\perp}_{V}+JP_{V}.

∎

Next, we define the relevant filtration over which the conditioning will take place. Throughout, we assume that the pairs
(t,j)
, where
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in[1/\delta]
, are ordered lexicographically, i.e., we have
(t^{\prime},j^{\prime})<(t,j)
if either
t^{\prime}<t
or both
t=t^{\prime}
and
j<j^{\prime}
. Now, using the notation
\mathcal{F}(\{X_{\alpha}\})
for the
\sigma
-field generated by the random variables
\{X_{\alpha}\}
, we define the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{j}^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N},j\in[\frac{1}{\delta}]}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{j}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\left(\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\}\cup\left(\bigcup_{s=0}^{t-1}\{J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{s|k}:k\in[1/\delta]\}\right)\cup\{J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|k}:k<j\}\right).

(24)

We further denote by
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{t}
the set of random variables on which we are conditioning

\mathcal{S}_{j}^{t}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\}\cup\left(\bigcup_{s=0}^{t-1}\{J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{s|k}:k\in[1/\delta]\}\right)\cup\{J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|k}:k<j\},

(25)

so that
\mathcal{F}_{j}^{t}=\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{j}^{t}).

Unlike the setting of
6
, Algorithm
1
involves projections on random vectors dependent on the disorder matrix
J
. Following
[
Bol14
]
, we handle such dependence by inductively applying
6
over the ordering
(t,j);
see also
[
FVR+22
, Section 6.2]
. This results in the following proposition, describing the distribution of
J
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{j}^{t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the setting of Algorithm
1
and define
P^{(t)|j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
to be the projector onto the subspace spanned by
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0|j},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}\}
.
For all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and

\displaystyle P^{(t)}(j)=\sum_{k<j}P^{(t)|k}+\sum_{k\geq j}P^{(t-1)|k}.

Then, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}^{t}_{j}
,

is equal in law to
(I-P^{(t)}(j))\widetilde{J}(I-P^{(t)}(j))

is a GOE matrix independent of

and

Equivalently,

\begin{split}J\Big|_{\mathcal{F}^{j}_{t}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(I-P^{(t)}(j))\widetilde{J}(I-P^{(t)}(j))+P^{(t)}(j)JP^{(t)}(j)+P^{(t)}(j)J(I-P^{(t)}(j)).\end{split}

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction over
(t,j)
. First, consider the base case
t=1,j=0
.
We have, by definition,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{1}_{0}=\mathcal{F}\left(\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}\}\cup\{J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0|k}:k\in[1/\delta]\}\right).

By
9

, we have

\displaystyle J=(I-P_{U^{0}})J(I-P_{U^{0}})+P_{U^{0}}JP_{U^{0}}+P_{U^{0}}J(I-P_{U^{0}}).

Since
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0|k}\perp\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0|j}
for

, we have

\displaystyle P_{U^{0}}=\sum_{k\in[1/\delta]}P^{(0)|k}

and
(I-P_{U^{0}})\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0|k}=\mathbf{0}
for all

Moreover, since

are independent of
J
, by applying

is independent of

, which proves (

.

Now, suppose the claim holds for some

, i.e., the random variable

\displaystyle J^{t}_{\perp}(j)\coloneqq(I-P^{(t)}(j))J(I-P^{(t)}(j)),

, is distributed as

(27)

Now, let

denote the time indices following

\displaystyle(\tilde{t},\tilde{j})=\begin{cases}(t,j+1),&j<1/\delta\\

Now, since

\displaystyle(I-P^{(\tilde{t})}(\tilde{j}))=(I-P^{t|j})(I-P^{(t)}(j))=(I-P^{(t)}(j))(I-P^{t|j}),

we obtain

\begin{split}J^{\tilde{t}}_{\perp}(\tilde{j})&=(I-P^{(\tilde{t})}(\tilde{j}))J(I-P^{(\tilde{t})}(\tilde{j}))\\

Now recall that

is Gaussian conditioned on

, while

is measurable with respect to

. Therefore, conditioned on

In what remains of the proof, we will show that, conditional on

we will conclude by (

is Gaussian conditioned on

and

, note that we have the decomposition

(29)

Observe that, conditioned on conditioned on

is deterministic.
By equations (

\displaystyle\left(J^{\tilde{t}}_{\perp}(\tilde{j}),\boldsymbol{z}^{t|j}\right)\Big|_{\mathcal{F}^{j}_{t}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left((I-P^{(\tilde{t})}(\tilde{j}))\tilde{J}(I-P^{(\tilde{t})}(\tilde{j})),(I-P^{(t)}(j))\widetilde{J}(I-P^{(t)}(j))\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}\right).

Approximate law of the field contributions

(\hat{C}^{(t-1),j})^{\dagger}\hat{C}^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}
as
\hat{\psi}^{t,j}
yields the second term in the right hand side of (
32
). We similarly proceed with (
51
). Recall that
D^{j}J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}=\boldsymbol{h}^{(t)|k\rightarrow j}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k})^{\top}J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{h}^{(t),k\rightarrow j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}N\hat{Q}^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t:,t},

where we used the definition of
\hat{Q}^{(t)}
in (
20
). Substituting the above into the terms appearing in the right hand side of (
51
) leads to the following simplification

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\delta N}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k})^{\top}J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}&=\frac{1}{\delta}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(t),k\rightarrow j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}\\
&=\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}\hat{Q}_{0:t,t}^{(t),k\rightarrow j}.\end{split}

(53)

Finally, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\delta N}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}(&\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k})^{\top}J\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t-1)|j}(\hat{C}^{(t-1),j})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t-1)|j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}\\
&=\frac{1}{\delta N}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(t),k\rightarrow j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t-1)|j}(\hat{C}^{(t-1),j})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t-1)|j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}\\
&=\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}\hat{Q}^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,0:t-1}(\hat{C}^{(t-1),j})^{\dagger}\hat{C}^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.\end{split}

(54)

Substituting equations (
53
) and (
54
) into (
51
) yields

P^{(t)|k}J(I-P^{(t-1)|j})\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{t|j}=\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}\left((\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}\hat{Q}^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,t}-(\hat{C}^{(t),k})^{\dagger}\hat{Q}^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,0:t-1}(\hat{C}^{(t-1),j})^{\dagger}\hat{C}^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}\right).

(55)

We recognize the right hand side as
\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}\hat{\theta}^{t,j}
. Substituting equations (
52
), (
55
) back into (
49
), we obtain

\displaystyle H^{t,\ell\rightarrow j}_{2}=\boldsymbol{h}^{(t-1),j\rightarrow k}\hat{\psi}^{t,j}+\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|k}\hat{\theta}^{t,j\rightarrow k}.

Proceeding similarly for
k\geq j
concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.3
Asymptotics at a fixed
\delta>0

In this section, we describe the limiting dynamics at a fixed
\delta>0
, which we call the Effective Dynamics, and which are the analogue of the equations (
4
) and (
12
)-(
17
) at a fixed
\delta>0.
As discussed at the beginning of
Section

4
, it will turn out that the process
\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}
from Algorithm
1
is approximately given by i.i.d. draws from this Effective Process.

For any
j\in[\frac{1}{\delta}]
, we define the following Effective Dynamics stochastic processes over fields
\{h^{t,j}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and spins
\{\sigma^{t,j}\in\{\pm 1\}\}_{t=0}^{T}
. We let
\sigma^{0,j}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{\pm 1\}
, and for
t=1,\dots,T,

\begin{cases}h^{t,j}=G^{t,j}+\left\langle f^{t,j},\sigma^{(t-1),j}\right\rangle\\
\sigma^{t,j}=c(h^{t,j}).\end{cases}

(56)

Above,
G^{1,j},\cdots,G^{T,j}
are zero-mean Gaussian random variables with covariance
\Sigma^{(T),j}
, independent of
\sigma^{0,j}
, and
\Sigma^{(T),j}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T},f^{t,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{t},t=1,\dots,T
are specified by the difference equations

\begin{split}&\Sigma^{(T),j}=\delta\sum_{k<j}C_{1:T,1:T}^{(T),k}+\delta\sum_{k\geq j}C^{(T-1),k}\\
&f^{t,j}=\delta\sum_{k<j}A_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),k}+\delta\sum_{k>j}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}+\delta A_{0:t-1,t-1}^{(t-1),j}\qquad\qquad\;\;\;\;\,t=1,\dots,T\\
&C_{st}^{(T),j}=\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{s,j}\sigma^{t,j}]\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,s,t=0,\dots,T\\
\\
&R_{st}^{(T),j}=\mathbb{E}[G^{s,j}\sigma^{t,j}]\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad s=1,\dots,T,t=0,\dots,T\\
\\
&A_{0:t-1,t}^{(T),j}=(\Sigma^{(T),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,t}^{(T),j}+\left(A^{(T-1),j}-(\Sigma^{(T),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}^{(T),j}\right)(C^{(T-1),j})^{-1}C_{0:t-1,t}^{(T),j},\end{split}

(57)

where
A
is upper triangular with zeros on the diagonal, and where we define
\Sigma^{(1),1}:=1
and
A_{-1,0}:=0.

We recall that it is proved in
5
that the above matrix inverses are well-defined along the solution trajectory. Finally, we define three more quantities which will be used in our proofs below. These are

\displaystyle\psi^{t,j}

\displaystyle=(C^{(t-1),j})^{\dagger}C_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),j}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}

(58)

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(C^{(t),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,t}-(C^{(t),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,0:t-1}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}&\in\mathbb{R}^{t+1},k<j\\
(C^{(t-1),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t-1,t}-(C^{(t-1),k})^{-1}Q^{(t-1),k\rightarrow j}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}&\in\mathbb{R}^{t},\;\;\;\,k\geq j.\end{cases}

(59)

\displaystyle b^{t,j}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-C^{(t),j}_{t,0:t-1}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}}\in\mathbb{R}.

(60)

We stress that the quantities defined in equations (
57
)-(
60
) above are
deterministic
, as opposed to the stochastic variants like
\hat{C},\hat{Q},\hat{\psi},\hat{\theta}
and
\hat{b}
defined in
Section

3
and
2
.

4.4
Lifted process over pairwise contributions

An intermediate step towards obtaining (
57
) as the limiting dynamics over spins and fields is to first define an Effective Process on the lifted space of field contributions which we can more directly compare with
2
. We next describe this lifted Effective Dynamics and relate it to the Effective Dynamics specified by (
57
).

Definition 3

(Lifted Effective Process)
.

Let
\Sigma^{(T)},f^{(T)},C^{(T)},A^{(T)}
be given by the solution to the difference equations specified by (
57
).
For
t\in[T],j,k\in[1/\delta]
, let
g^{(t),k\rightarrow j}\in\mathbb{R}^{t+1}
be Gaussian random row vectors, independent across
j,k
with covariance
\delta C^{(t),k}
, i.e

\displaystyle g^{(t),k\rightarrow j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\delta C^{(t),k}).

Let
\sigma^{0,j}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{\pm 1\}
, independently across
j
and independent of the
g^{(t),k\to j}
, and for
t=1,\dots,T
, let

\begin{split}\begin{cases}h^{t,j}=\sum_{k<j}h^{t,k\rightarrow j}+\sum_{k\geq j}h^{t-1,k\rightarrow j}\\
\sigma^{t,j}=c(h^{t,j}),\end{cases}\end{split}

(61)

where the
h^{t,k\to j}
are defined as follows.

\displaystyle h^{t,k\rightarrow j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}g^{t,k\to j}+\delta\sigma^{(t-1),j}A_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),k}&t=1,\dots,T\text{ and }k<j\\
g^{t,k\to j}+\delta\sigma^{(t),j}A_{0:t,t}^{(t),k}&t=0,\dots,T-1\text{ and }k=j\\
g^{t,k\to j}+\delta\sigma^{(t),j}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),k}\end{bmatrix}&t=0,\dots,T-1\text{ and }k>j.\\
\end{cases}

(62)

The Effective Process defined above specifies a distribution for
\{\sigma^{t,j}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h^{t,j}\}_{t=1}^{T}
which matches that of (
56
).

Proposition 3
.

The marginal law of
\{\sigma^{t,j}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h^{t,j}\}_{t=1}^{T}
obtained from
3
agrees with the law defined by (
56
).

Proof.

For
t=1,\dots,T,
let
\tilde{G}^{t,j}
denote the random variable

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}^{t,j}=\sum_{k<j}g^{t,k\rightarrow j}+\sum_{k\geq j}g^{t-1,k\rightarrow j}.

Note that all terms in the sum above are independent, so for
1\leq s,t\leq T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\widetilde{G}^{s,j}\widetilde{G}^{t,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k<j}\mathbb{E}g^{s,k\rightarrow j}g^{t,k\rightarrow j}+\sum_{k\geq j}\mathbb{E}g^{s-1,k\rightarrow j}g^{t-1,k\rightarrow j}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{k<j}C_{st}^{(T),k}+\delta\sum_{k\geq j}C_{s-1,t-1}^{(T-1),k}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{s,t}^{(T),j},

so
\widetilde{G}^{(t),j}
agrees in law with
G^{(t),j}
from (
56
). Now according to (
61
), for
t=1,\dots,T,
we have

\displaystyle h^{t,j}

\displaystyle=\tilde{G}^{t,j}+\sigma^{(t-1),j}\left(\delta\sum_{k<j}A_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),k}+\delta\sum_{k>j}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}+\delta A_{0:t-1,t-1}^{(t-1),j}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{G}^{t,j}+\left\langle f^{t,j},\sigma^{(t-1),j}\right\rangle.

Since
\widetilde{G}^{(t),j}
agrees in law with
G^{(t),j}
, and moreover the
\{\sigma^{t,j}\}_{t=0}^{T},\{h^{t,j}\}_{t=1}^{T}
from
3
satisfy the same recursion as those from (
56
) with
\widetilde{G}
in place of
G,
and with the same distribution of
\sigma^{0,j}
, the proposition follows.
∎

We now derive a result that links certain parameters defined through the Effective Process in
3
to the recursion on
A
given by (
57
). Define matrices
Q^{(T),k\to j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T+1)\times(T+1)}
for
k,j\in[1/\delta]
as follows

Q^{(T),k\rightarrow j}_{st}=\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}[h^{s,k\rightarrow j}\sigma^{t,j}].

(63)

This matrix will be useful in the proof of the following lemma, as well as later when we have to compare the Effective Process to the vectors
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(t)|j},\boldsymbol{h}^{(t),k\to j}.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\psi^{t,j}
and
\theta^{t,j\to k}
be defined as in equations (
58
) and (
59
), respectively. For
k<j
, we have

\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A^{(t-1),j}\psi^{t,j}\end{bmatrix}+\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),j}\end{bmatrix},

(64)

and for
k\geq j
, we have

A^{(t-1),j}\psi^{t,j}+\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}=A^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.

(65)

Proof.

The proof is by induction in
t.
For
t=0,
equations (
64
), (
65
) are immediate since in that case
Q^{(t),k\to j}_{0,0}=0
by independence of
\sigma^{0}
and
h^{0,k\to j}
and hence
\theta^{0,j\to k}=0
. In the rest of the proof we assume equations (
64
), (
65
) at time
t-1
and verify them at time
t.
We split into the cases
j<k,j>k
and
j=k.

We begin with the case
j<k.
From (
59
), we have

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\to k}

\displaystyle=(C^{(t-1),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t-1,t}-(C^{(t-1),k})^{-1}Q^{(t-1),k\rightarrow j}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.

Now, since
j<k,
by (
62
), we have

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(t-1),k\to j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(g^{(t-1),k\to j}+\delta\sigma^{(t-1),j}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,0:t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{\top}\sigma^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(g^{(t-1),k\to j})^{\top}\sigma^{(t-1),j}+\delta\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,0:t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}C^{(t-1),j}.

Now, recalling that
\partial_{v}
for a vector
v
to denotes the (weak) gradient with respect to
v
, by Stein’s Lemma (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(t-1),k\to j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t-1),k}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{g^{(t-1),k\to j}}\sigma^{(t-1),j}]+\delta\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,0:t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}C^{(t-1),j},

where we have used that
c
, the update rule, has a distributional derivative since it’s bounded (see
2
). Now, for each
s,s^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,t-1\}
and
r\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, note from
3
that, for
G^{r,j}:=\sum_{k<j}g^{r,k\rightarrow j}+\sum_{k\geq j}g^{r-1,k\rightarrow j},
since
k>j
we have
\partial_{g^{s^{\prime},k\to j}}G^{r,j}=\delta_{r,s^{\prime}+1}
. Hence, using the chain rule, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{g^{s^{\prime},k\to j}}\sigma^{s,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}(\partial_{g^{s^{\prime},k\to j}}G^{r,j})\partial_{G^{r,j}}\sigma^{s,j},

\displaystyle=\partial_{G^{s^{\prime}+1,j}}\sigma^{s,j},

so recalling that
G^{(t),j}=(G^{1,j},\dots,G^{t,j})
, we have

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(t-1),k\to j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t-1),k}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{G^{(t),j}}\sigma^{(t-1),j}]+\delta\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,0:t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}C^{(t-1),j}.

Now again by Stein’s lemma, we get

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(t-1),k\to j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t-1),k}(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(G^{(t),j})^{\top}\sigma^{(t-1),j}]+\delta\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,0:t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}C^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t-1),k}(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R^{(t),j}_{1:t,0:t-1}+\delta\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-2,0:t-1}^{(t-1),k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}C^{(t-1),j}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle Q^{k\to j}_{0:t-1,t}

\displaystyle=C^{(t-1),k}(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R^{(t),j}_{1:t,t}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A^{(t-1),k}_{0:t-2,0:t-1}\end{bmatrix}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.

This gives

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\to k}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,t}^{(t),j}-(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}^{(t),j}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),j},

and hence

\displaystyle A^{(t-1)}\psi^{t,j}+\theta^{t,j\to k}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,t}^{(t),j}+\left(A^{(t-1)}-(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}^{(t),j}\right)(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),j}

\displaystyle=A^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t},

as desired.

Next we consider the case
j>k.
From (
59
), we have

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}

\displaystyle=(C^{(t),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,t}-(C^{(t),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t,0:t-1}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.

Since
j>k,
by the same arguments as above, we have

\displaystyle\delta Q_{0:t,0:t-1}^{k\to j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(g^{(t),k\to j}+\delta\sigma^{(t-1),j}A_{0:t-1,0:t}^{(t),k})^{\top}\sigma^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(g^{(t),k\to j})^{\top}\sigma^{(t-1),j}+\delta(A^{\top})^{(t),k}_{0:t,0:t-1}C^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t),k}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{g^{(t),k\to j}}\sigma^{(t-1),j}]+\delta(A^{\top})^{(t),k}_{0:t,0:t-1}C^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t),k}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{G^{(t),j}}\sigma^{(t-1),j}]\end{bmatrix}+\delta(A^{\top})^{(t),k}_{0:t,0:t-1}C^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t),k}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(G^{(t),j})^{\top}\sigma^{(t-1),j}]\end{bmatrix}+\delta(A^{\top})^{(t),k}_{0:t,0:t-1}C^{(t-1),j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t),k}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}^{(t),j}\end{bmatrix}+\delta(A^{\top})^{(t),k}_{0:t,0:t-1}C^{(t-1),j}

and similarly

\displaystyle Q_{0:t,t}^{(t),k\to j}

\displaystyle=\delta C^{(t),k}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,t}^{(t),j}\end{bmatrix}+\delta(A^{\top})^{(t),k}_{0:t,0:t-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.

Hence

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,t}^{(t),j}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}^{(t),j}\end{bmatrix}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,t}^{(t),j}-(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R_{1:t,0:t-1}^{(t),j}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}\end{bmatrix},

and we conclude

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A^{(t-1),j}\psi^{t,j}\end{bmatrix}+\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
A_{0:t-1,t}^{(t),j}\end{bmatrix},

as desired.

The only remaining case is
j=k.
Here we have

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}

\displaystyle=(C^{(t-1),k})^{-1}Q^{(t),k\rightarrow j}_{0:t-1,t}-(C^{(t-1),k})^{-1}Q^{(t-1),k\rightarrow j}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t},

but since
k\geq j,
we get

\displaystyle Q^{(t-1),k\to j}

\displaystyle=C^{(t-1),k}(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R^{(t),j}_{1:t,0:t-1}+(A^{(t-1),k})^{\top}C^{(t-1),j},

and

\displaystyle Q^{k\to j}_{0:t-1,t}

\displaystyle=C^{(t-1),k}(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R^{(t),j}_{1:t,t}+(A^{(t-1),k})^{\top}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t}.

so

\displaystyle\theta^{t,j\to k}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R^{(t),j}_{1:t,t}-(\Sigma^{(t),j})^{-1}R^{(t),j}_{1:t,0:t-1}(C^{(t-1),j})^{-1}C^{(t),j}_{0:t-1,t},

so from here onward, the situation is the same as in the case
j<k.
This concludes the proof.
∎

4.5
Mapping to the Effective Process

In what follows, our goal will be to prove inductively that the quantities
\hat{b}^{t,j},\hat{\psi}^{t,j},\hat{\theta}^{t,j\rightarrow k}
from
2
converge to deterministic the limits
b^{t,j},\psi^{t,j},\theta^{t,j\to k}
corresponding to the Effective Process from
3
. Assuming such convergence, we show that the “approximately decoupled” process from
2
converges to i.i.d draws from the Effective Process from
3
.

Consider an instance of Algorithm
1
with
U^{t}_{i}\in[0,1]
denoting the random seeds utilized in the update
c
. Recall the vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t,j\to k}
defined in
2
, and let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,j\rightarrow k},\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{t|j}
be defined recursively as follows. For
t=0,
we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{0,j\rightarrow k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}^{0,j\rightarrow k},

\displaystyle\forall j,k\in[1/\delta],

(66)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0}.

(67)

Then, recalling the definitions of
\psi^{t,j},\theta^{t,j\to k}
and
b^{t,j}
from equations (
58
)-(
60
), for
t\geq 1
, we inductively define

\begin{split}\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,j\rightarrow k}&=\begin{cases}b^{t,j}\sqrt{\delta}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t,j\rightarrow k}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{(t-1),j\rightarrow k}\psi^{t,j}+\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(t)|k}\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k},&k<j\\
b^{t,j}\sqrt{\delta}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t,j\rightarrow k}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{(t-1),j\rightarrow k}\psi^{t,j}+\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(t-1)|k}\theta^{t,j\rightarrow k}&k\geq j\end{cases}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,k}&=\sum_{j<k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,j\rightarrow k}+\sum_{j\geq k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t-1,j\rightarrow k}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{t}_{i}&=c(U^{t}_{i},\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}^{t,k}_{i})\qquad\qquad\forall i\in B(k).\end{split}

(68)

The following proposition shows that the process defined above is equal in distribution to i.i.d. draws from the lifted Effective Process.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose for each
k\in[1/\delta]
, the random variables
\{h^{(T),j\to k},h^{(T),j},\sigma^{t,j}\}_{j\in[1/\delta]}
are given as in
3
. Then the entries of
\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{(T),j\to k},\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{(T),j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t|j}\}_{j\in[1/\delta]}
are i.i.d. across
i\in B(k)
and we have

\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{(T),j\to k},\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{(T),j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t|j}\}_{j\in[1/\delta]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{h^{(T),j\to k},h^{(T),j},\sigma^{t,j}\}_{j\in[1/\delta]}

(69)

for all
i\in B(k).

Proof.

The fact that
\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{(T),j\to k},\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}^{(T),j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{t|j}\}_{j\in[1/\delta]}
is i.i.d. across
i\in B(k)
is immediate by induction: the initial condition is i.i.d. across
i\in B(k)
and the recursion (
68
) acts independently on each coordinate, since we know from
2
that the
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t,j\to k}
are i.i.d.

Thus we focus on verifying (
69

Proof.

By

to (
