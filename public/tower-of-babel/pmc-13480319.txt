Parental Racism-Related Stress and Child Depression: Disparities Across Income and Race/Ethnicity in a National Sample of Racial/Ethnic Minority Families
Authors: Elizabeth Jelsma, Nema Kebbeh, Rosenda Murillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Vicariously experienced racism-related stress is a key social determinant of child depression, yet national data on racial/ethnic and income disparities in this relationship are limited.Objectives:This study tested whether non-Hispanic Black (NHB) children and children from lower income households experience more frequent depression linked to parent-experienced racism-related stress than other groups.Methods:In the 2023 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), 1712 parents of children from minoritized racial/ethnic groups aged 5–17 years (weighted N = 37.7 million) reported their own experiences of racism-related stress and their child's depression. Multivariable ordinal weighted least squares regression assessed associations between parent-experienced racism-related stress and child depression, with moderation by income and race/ethnicity.Results:Parent-experienced racism-related stress was significantly associated with more frequent child depression. This relationship was stronger among children from middle- and lowest-income households and among NHB children compared with Hispanic children.Conclusions:Children from lower income families and NHB children are more vulnerable to depression associated with parental racism-related stress. These findings highlight the need to consider both direct and vicarious racism-related stressors in child mental health research and interventions.

Family is a vital component in the healthy development of children. Family, specifically parents, contribute significantly to how children learn and mature during their childhood and adolescence. Parents' and their children's lives are lived interdependently and therefore fluctuations, incidents, and stressors occurring in one person's life also affect the lives of others in the family.1 In racially and ethnically minoritized families, stressors resulting from racism not only touch the life of one person but also ripple across family systems in the form of vicariously experienced racism-related stress.1 Racism-related stress refers to the overall burden of racial stigmatization, which includes not only interpersonal experiences of differential treatment but also the heightened state of awareness and mental alertness of anticipating future experiences of racism, that is, racism-related vigilance.2–4

It is now well established that interpersonal racial/ethnic discrimination experienced by parents can spillover to negatively affect their children.5,6 However, we know less about how parents' cumulative racism-related burden, which includes vigilance, might impact their children's mental health. Previous studies examining United States (US) parents' experiences of racial/ethnic discrimination and child psychological distress have been limited by smaller community samples.7 Furthermore, familial social position factors such as race/ethnicity and family income should also be explored as factors affecting the influence of parental racism-related stress on child mental health, as these factors shape the contexts in which families develop.8 Examining parental racism-related stress within the context of social position as it relates to child psychological distress may inform family-based mental health interventions.

RACISM-RELATED STRESS SPILLOVER

The psychological and emotional toll on children resulting from the spillover of parent-experienced racial/ethnic discrimination is significant and associated with social and emotional behavior difficulties,7 internalizing problems leading to depression, anxiety, and suicidality,9–17 and externalizing behaviors like conduct problems and delinquent behaviors.9,13,15,16,18 Parents who experience racial discrimination exhibit higher levels of psychological distress and stress-related health problems.19–21 This distress can manifest in several ways that shape the family context. Elevated stress may lead to harsher, less consistent, or more withdrawn parenting, as parents struggle to regulate their own emotions and maintain positive engagement with their children.5,12,22 The strain of racial stress can erode the warmth, trust, and open communication that are foundational to healthy parent-child relationships.9,23,24 Chronic stress may increase family conflict, reduce cohesion, and create an atmosphere of tension or unpredictability, all of which are detrimental to children's sense of security.16

Additionally, interpersonal discrimination is only 1 form of racism-related stress that racially and ethnically minoritized individuals often contend with. Interwoven within one's own racism-related burden is also the anticipation of future racist events and rumination on previously experienced events.3 Racism-related vigilance is defined as a set of cognitive processes and behavioral adaptations that arise from a perceived need to constantly monitor one's environment and adjust personal behavior to anticipate and protect oneself from potential discrimination.3 It functions as a coping response characterized by heightened psychological arousal resulting from observing, responding to, and defending against the various threats associated with discrimination.3 This vigilant state is termed “anticipatory stress,” underscoring that harm initiates even with the mere expectation of racism, rather than only after a racist event has occurred.3 The weight of chronic racism-related vigilance on its own and in combination with exposure to interpersonal discrimination are important sources of racism-related stress.3 We argue that it is necessary to consider a multidimensional conceptualization of parental racism-related stress that encompasses both actual experiences with racial/ethnic discrimination and parents' vigilant thoughts and behaviors anticipating and ruminating about racist encounters may spillover to affect their children.25 Additionally, to construct a truly comprehensive understanding of racism-related stress spillover within families, it is imperative to examine socioeconomically and racially/ethnically diverse samples.

Socioeconomic and racial/ethnic diversity

Interestingly, among the existing studies on parent-experienced racism and child well-being, very few have accounted for family income beyond a covariate. A notable exception is a study from Ford et al,11 which found that when parents experienced high racial/ethnic discrimination, African American adolescents from low-income families struggled the most psychologically across a 3-year period. In this study, higher-income adolescents with caregivers who reported few discrimination experiences showed the most positive psychological outcomes over time, and even when higher income adolescents had caregivers who experienced higher discrimination, they still demonstrated reductions in depressive symptoms over time. This provides strong support for the importance of considering cumulative family risk and resilience factors to understand youth mental health. However, socioeconomic status may contribute to racism experiences and mental through various pathways. For instance, previous research suggests both that higher socioeconomic status is associated with more frequent contact with white people and therefore more frequent racist treatment,26,27 yet greater economic resources also may help buffer against the negative psychological effects of exposure to discrimination.11,28 Therefore, more research is needed to explain this paradox and uncover the complex role of socioeconomic status in shaping how family members are indirectly affected by each other's racism-related burdens and their impact on mental health.

There is also a paucity of research testing whether the relationships between parent-experienced racism-related stress and their children's mental health may vary by ethnic minority group. Of the existing research on the topic, all studies are conducted with either monoethnic—Black/African American,9–13,18 Hispanic,17,24 or Chinese American16— or pooled samples of multiple ethnic minority groups combined.7,14,15 A unique exception is a study from Lee and the Minnesota International Adoption Project,29 which found that adolescents adopted from Latin America showed more internalizing and externalizing behaviors when their adoptive parents perceived higher discrimination compared with children adopted from Asia and Eastern Europe. It is important to recognize that each ethnic group in the US possesses a distinct sociopolitical background, which can influence how members perceive and respond to instances of discrimination, potentially leading to heightened sensitivity in identifying such events.30 For example, Black and African Americans have endured a long and deeply rooted history of racism in the US, with their experiences evolving significantly over centuries. By contrast, Hispanic and Asian American communities, although more recent in their immigration histories, have encountered distinct challenges in navigating and integrating into mainstream American society.30 Therefore, responses to discriminatory events within family systems are likely to differ broadly across ethnic minority groups, shaped by their unique cultural and historical contexts. These distinctions should be thoughtfully accounted for in studies examining the nature of vicariously experienced racism-related stress. Nonetheless, despite these complexities, we argue that cross-ethnic comparisons on this construct represent a valuable contribution to the growing body of literature on vicariously experienced racism-related stress and its implications for family and child well-being.

Current study

The current study examined the relationship between a multidimensional measure of parent-experienced racism-related stress (inclusive of interpersonal discrimination and racism-related vigilance2,25) and their child's depression. Additionally, we examined the nature of these relationships across racial/ethnic group and income levels within a national sample of US families. We predicted that parental racism-related stress would be positively associated with child depression. Additional post hoc analyses were conducted to compare the degree of association between parent racism-related stress and child depression across racial/ethnic groups and family income level.

METHODS

Data source

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the 2023 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) due to the inclusion of racial discrimination questions in the Sample Adult interview in this wave. Collected each year using multistage sampling methods, this dataset offers detailed insights into the US civilian, noninstitutionalized population. We used the Public Use version of the NHIS,31 which is publicly available to download from the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series (IPUMS) Health Surveys.32 The NHIS data include a “Sample Adult file” that contains data on individuals aged 18 years and older, and a “Sample Child file” that contains data on 1 randomly selected child in each household aged 17 years and younger (reported by an adult in the household). Information about the Sample Adult is typically provided by the individual themselves, unless they are physically or mentally unable to respond, in which case a knowledgeable proxy may answer on their behalf. For the Sample Child, data are collected from a parent or another adult who is well-informed about and responsible for the child's health care. These data are publicly available and deidentified; hence, this study was exempt from the purview of our institutional review board.

Research design and study population

In 2023, a total of 5548 “Sample Adults” who completed an interview for themselves were also a parent of a “Sample Child.” IPUMS provides a computed variable (isparentsc) indicating whether the Sample Adult in a household is also the Sample Child's parent because NHIS allows for the “Sample Child” interview to be completed by any adult in the household (parent or not). Because of the focal predictor of this analysis being parent-experienced racism-related stress, we excluded 3165 cases in which the parent identified their race as “White” (remaining N = 2383). The focal outcome of child-experienced depression was also only asked for children who were 5 years and older, so we removed the 671 cases with children younger than 5 years. This brought our analytic sample to N = 1712 parent respondents with valid parent-child pair-level data for statistical analyses.33

Study variables

Parent-experienced racism-related stress was the primary independent variable of interest. We calculated aggregate racism-related stress by averaging parents' responses to 5 questions from the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS)—Short Version31,34 (eg, “In your day-to-day life how often have any of the following things happened to you? You are treated with less courtesy or respect than other people.”) and the abbreviated 4-item Heightened Vigilance Scale (HVS)3,31,35 (eg, “In your day-to-day life, how often do you try to prepare for possible insults from other people before leaving home?) to capture a broader construct of parent-experienced racism-related stress reflecting both enacted and anticipatory dimensions of exposure. Each item was answered on a scale of 1-5 (1 = At least once a week; 2 = A few times a month; 3 = A few times a year; 4 = Less than once a year; 5 = Never). This approach is consistent with theoretical models34,35 conceptualizing racism as a chronic stressor operating through direct experiences of unfair treatment as well as ongoing vigilance, anticipation, and behavioral adaptation in response to potential discrimination. Combining these measures is particularly appropriate in research on vicarious or family-level stress, as children may be affected not only by parents' direct experiences of discrimination but also by parents' chronic vigilance and anticipatory coping, which jointly shape the household stress environment.21,36 This combined use is further consistent with the NHIS conceptualization of discrimination and vigilance as related constructs intended to be examined together in relation to health outcomes.31 Internal consistency analyses supported this approach, with good reliability for the Everyday Discrimination Scale (α = 0.77) and the HVS (α = 0.75), and higher internal consistency for the combined scale (α = 0.84).

Child depression was the primary dependent variable of interest. Parents responded to 1 question: “How often does < sample child's name > seem very sad or depressed?” The scale of response options included 1 = Daily, 2 = Weekly, 3 = Monthly, 4 = A few times a year, and 5 = Never.

Child race/ethnicity was defined as a 4-level nominal variable, including non-Hispanic Black (NHB), Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Asian (NHA), and due to small sample sizes, non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native children and non-Hispanic children reporting multiple races were combined into a single “NH Other/Multiple” category, consistent with prior NHIS-based analyses31 and IPUMS.32 Within this category, 94 children were identified as non-Hispanic multiracial and 46 as American Indian/Alaska Native, with remaining children representing other smaller racial groups. Sample sizes within these groups were insufficient to support reliable weighted regression analyses when examined separately.

Family income level was categorized on the basis of established federal poverty level (FPL) cutoffs into lowest income (less than 125% of FPL), low (125% FPL to 199% FPL), middle (200% FPL to 399% FPL), and high income (400% FPL or above).

Covariates included were parent and child age and sex, as well as parent employment status (employed or not) and education level (ordinal categorical indicator ranging from no high school diploma to advanced degree).

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted in SPSS Version 30.0 using paired weights from NHIS to account for the complex survey design, which were computed by combining each pair's conditional selection probability with their household sampling weight, adjusting for nonresponse, and trimming extreme values at the 99th percentile.33 Therefore, our analysis employed a series of nested weighted least squares regression models to examine the associations between parent-experienced racism-related stress (predictor), family income and child race/ethnicity (moderators), and child depression (outcome). Although racism-related stress was measured based on parents' experiences, the outcome of interest was child depression; therefore, interactions were specified with child race/ethnicity to examine whether the association between parent-experienced racism-related stress and child depression differs across children's racialized social positions. This approach is grounded in frameworks of vicarious stress, stress transmission, and differential vulnerability, which suggest that children may be differently affected by parental stressors depending on their own race-related social contexts.36,37 By contrast, interacting the racism-related stress measure with parent race/ethnicity would address whether the effects of discrimination differ across parents' racial/ethnic groups, rather than differences in children's vulnerability to parental stress. Accordingly, child race/ethnicity was modeled as a moderator to capture heterogeneity in the parent stress–child depression association. Missing data, “refused,” and “I don't know” responses were removed from analyses using list-wise deletion per SPSS's default procedures due to <1% missingness for each variable in the multivariable models. For our moderated regression analyses, we first fitted multiple linear regression models where parent-experienced racial stress was assessed for its dependence on the categorical moderators using the category 400% FPL or above as the reference income level and Hispanic as the reference racial/ethnic group, for the moderation effect on child depression. Scores for parent-experienced racism-related stress were standardized using z-scores before computing the interaction terms. Once we determined that the connections between parent-experienced racism-related stress and child depression were influenced by family income and child race/ethnicity (as indicated by significant 2-way interactions), we plotted the effect of the predictor (parent-experienced racism-related stress) on the outcome (child depression) at the 4 levels of the moderators (family income and child race/ethnicity) using the Chart Builder function in SPSS.

RESULTS

The final analytic sample comprises 1712 parent respondents, representing 37.7 million annualized US parent-child pairs. Hispanic, NHB, NHA, and NH other/multiple race populations comprised 60%, 19%, 15%, and 10%, respectively, of the children in the sample. Descriptive participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. Of note, NHB children showed the highest mean levels of child depression (as reported by their parent) and parent-experienced racism-related stress. Results from the multivariable moderated linear regressions are presented in Table 2. In the total sample, parent-experienced racism-related stress was positively related to child depression (b = 0.12, P < 0.001). We further examined the parental racism stress-child depression association by family income and child race/ethnicity. Results shows that the influence of parent-experienced racism-related stress on child depression was stronger for children from middle-income (b = 0.13, P = 0.02) and lowest-income (b = 0.18, P < 0.001) families relative to children from high-income families (reference group). There was no significant difference for children from low-income families relative to high-income families (Fig. 1). Regarding child race/ethnicity, the influence of parent-experienced racism-related stress on child depression was stronger for NHB children (b = 0.16, P < 0.001) relative to Hispanic children (reference group). There were no significant differences between NHA or NH Other/Multiple race children relative to Hispanic children (Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to assess parental cumulative racism-related stress (including interpersonal racial/ethnic discrimination and racism-related vigilance) as a potential predictor of child depression. Findings suggest that parent-experienced racism-related stress contributes to their child's depressive symptoms and that this relationship varies by race/ethnicity and family-income level. Our results provide evidence that racism-related stress not only affects the parent who experienced it but also ripples across family systems to undermine child mental health. Accounting for race/ethnicity and family income status revealed interesting subgroup differences. Specifically, children from middle-income and lowest-income (compared with high income) families and NHB children (compared with Hispanic children) were more strongly affected by their parents' racism-related stress. There were no significant differences between NHA children and Hispanic children or non-Hispanic Other/Multiple race children and Hispanic children.

There could be several reasons why parental experiences of racism-related stress spillover to negatively affect their children. Parental experiences with racism-related stress often result in negative emotional and physical consequences, which could impair their ability to parent consistently, supportively, and effectively.1 When parents experience racism, they face not only immediate emotional stress but also anticipate and prepare for potential future threats, possibly leading to chronic mental strain and emotional exhaustion.3 This persistent state of vigilance, characterized by heightened anxiety, chronic worry, and hyperawareness regarding possible discrimination, might undermine parents' emotional stability and resilience, making it challenging to maintain a nurturing environment for their children.3 Acknowledging vigilance as a critical factor contributing to parental racism-related stress enhances our understanding of its intergenerational influence across family systems. Interventions targeting child depression should consider the role of parental racism-related burden, as this may be a significant vicarious stressor for children. By integrating parental racism-related stress into frameworks for child mental health interventions, we can more effectively disrupt the transmission of psychological distress across generations and promote resilience within family systems.

Importantly, our study examined how the relationship between parental racism-related stress and child depression functions across race/ethnicity and income. Noteworthy results emerged for both. As expected, the relationship between parental racism-related stress and child depression was stronger for children from the lowest-income and middle-income families relative to children from highest-income families. However, there emerged a protective effect among children from low-income (but not the lowest) families; they were no different than children from the highest-income families. This supports some of the nuances of income effects; low income is not always unilaterally harmful for children when also considered with other contextual effects related to race and ethnicity. It makes sense that racially and ethnically minoritized children from “low-”income families, but not the lowest income and not middle income, might be uniquely situated to be resilient against parental racism-related stress. Low-income neighborhoods (125% FPL to 199% FPL) tend to be predominantly people of color as an artifact of decades of racial wealth inequality in the US.38 Therefore, low-income families in majority-minority neighborhoods may be more insulated from directly experiencing racism-related stress from white Americans on a daily basis26,27 but without the severe economic hardship of living below the federal poverty threshold. As mentioned previously, being middle income is associated with more frequent contact with white people and therefore more frequent racist treatment26,27 but lacks the economic benefits of being among the highest-income group. Taken together, racially and ethnically minoritized children from lowest income and middle income occupy unique social positions of risk and resilience, possibly leaving them uniquely vulnerable to the spillover effects of parental racism-related stress. This paradox is certainly worth additional study.

Child race/ethnicity also moderated the relationship between parental racism-related stress and child depression, such that the association was stronger for NHB children compared with Hispanic children. There were no significant differences between NHA or Non-Hispanic Other/Multiple race children relative to Hispanic children. Unfortunately, NHB Americans continue to report a higher degree of racial discrimination than any other demographic group.39 The specificity of the association between parental racism-related stress and child mental health in NHB families may be partly attributed to differences in racial socialization messaging, or the ways in which parents communication with children about race, racism, and cultural identity.40 Parents who have experienced higher levels of racial discrimination may be more likely to emphasize racial barriers and the need to prepare for bias in their messaging,40,41 and such messaging can increase children's own feelings of depression and low self-esteem.10,41,42 Notably, NHB parents in our sample reported the highest average levels of racism-related stress compared with other racial groups. This heightened exposure may influence parenting practices in ways that contribute to increased depressive symptoms in children. These findings point to the importance of conducting additional within-group research with NHB families to better understand the nuanced roles of vicarious racism-related stress, racial socialization practices, and their impact on child mental health. Such research is essential for developing culturally responsive interventions that address the specific needs and experiences of NHB families.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the impact of parental racism-related stress, including interpersonal discrimination and anticipatory vigilance, on child depression. The inclusion of a diverse sample of parents enabled us to explore variations across race/ethnicity and family income, both of which are critical factors in understanding family and child development.8 However, several limitations should be noted. Because of the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established. The measure of parental racism-related stress was constructed from 2 existing scales and warrants further evaluation for reliability and validity, as the Everyday Discrimination and HVS may not have equivalent meaning across sociodemographic groups.31 The Centers for Disease Control & Prevention cautions against direct comparisons of these measures across groups due to evidence that discrimination and vigilance items may be interpreted differently depending on respondents' social position and lived experiences.31 As a result, findings involving moderation by race/ethnicity and income should be interpreted with caution and viewed as exploratory, consistent with analytic guidance for the NHIS discrimination and vigilance measures.31 Additionally, child depression was assessed using a single parent-reported item, which may not fully capture the child's emotional state. Future research should incorporate both parent and child perspectives to gain a more comprehensive understanding. An additional limitation is the aggregation of non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native and non-Hispanic multiracial children into a single “other/multiple” racial category due to small sample sizes. Although this approach is common in nationally representative surveys, it may mask meaningful heterogeneity and obscure elevated risk observed in prior research among American Indian/Alaska Native and mixed-race youth.21,37 As such, findings for this grouped category should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies with larger samples should examine these populations separately. Importantly, parental racism-related stress does not necessarily reflect the extent or nature of racial bias preparation messaging within families, highlighting the need for further investigation into the mechanisms through which parental racism-related stress may influence children's mental health.

CONCLUSIONS

This study enhances our understanding of how racism-related stress can cascade through family systems, revealing a significant association between parental racism-related stress and child depression. Importantly, this relationship is moderated by both income and race/ethnicity, with NHB children and those from low- and middle-income families appearing most vulnerable to these effects. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions and educational efforts that address family-level racism-related stress, promote effective communication, and support coping strategies. Such approaches may be especially beneficial for populations identified as being at an elevated risk and can contribute to more equitable mental health outcomes across diverse communities of children and families. To ensure the effectiveness of these interventions, it is essential to provide accessible and culturally responsive resources for families coping with racism-related stress, particularly those in the lowest-income brackets who may face significant barriers to care.
