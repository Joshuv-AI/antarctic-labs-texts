Beyond MRS Broth: A soytone medium towards affordable culturing of South African vaginal Lactobacillaceae isolates
Authors: Obakeng Luthando Jona, Marijke A Fagan-Endres, Anna-Ursula Happel, Brian Kullin, Jo-Ann S Passmore, Susan T L Harrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assesses a plant-based Soytone Medium as an alternative to the animal-derived standard de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth for the cultivation of Lactobacillaceae. The application focuses on five isolates that have shown probiotic potential for bacterial vaginosis treatment. Cultivation was performed in 300 mL bench-scale bioreactors, monitored for cell density, pH, lactate production, and glucose consumption. The media's carbon and nitrogen concentrations and costing were quantified.Though the medium's carbon concentrations were identical, the Soytone Medium had a higher carbon-to-nitrogen ratio than MRS (8.1 vs. 6.6). Four strains achieved higher cell densities and maximum specific growth rates in the Soytone Medium. The greatest benefit was shown for L. crispatus 70.6PA, which had a 45% higher final cell density. A cost analysis showed that the Soytone Medium was 44% cheaper than MRS Broth. It was thus confirmed that the proposed plant-based Soytone Medium is a viable and less expensive alternative for Lactobacillaceae cultures in which exposure to animal products was also avoided. One-Sentence Summary: This study presents a plant-based Soytone Medium as a cost-effective alternative to standard MRS Broth for the high-density biomass cultivation of vaginal Lactobacillaceae, demonstrating enhanced growth performance and supporting their application in probiotic-based treatment of bacterial vaginosis in LMICs like South Africa.Keywords: Growth media, Probiotics, Lactobacillaceae, Nitrogen sources, Costing

Introduction

Lactobacillaceae are a family of commensal bacteria belonging to the lactic acid bacteria (LAB) group, many strains which have been demonstrated to serve as key probiotic microorganisms (Quinto et al., 2014). Probiotics are defined as live microorganisms that when administered in adequate amounts confer health benefits to the host (Net et al., 2014; Reid, 2016, 2019). In particular, lactobacilli are amongst the most widely used probiotic cultures to treat numerous health issues, including those related to dysbioses in the gastrointestinal tract and reproductive tract (Puebla-Barragan & Reid, 2019; Reid, 2016; Reid et al., 2019).

The cultivation of Lactobacillaceae bacteria for application in probiotic products requires that fermentations achieve high viable biomass cell densities, which is required for the probiotic product to be effective (Juárez Tomás et al., 2010; Pandey et al., 2016; Puebla-Barragan & Reid, 2019; Reid, 2016). The growth of probiotic cultures during fermentation is affected by several physicochemical factors. This includes cultivation media composition, pH, bioreactor configuration, and agitation. Media formulation, in particular, has a significant and direct impact on the bioprocessing feasibility and cost of producing high cell density products (Ayad et al., 2020a; Berisvil et al., 2020; De et al., 2012; Juárez Tomás et al., 2010; Manzoor et al., 2017; Ozturk, 1996; Schiraldi et al., 2003; Śliżewska & Chlebicz-Wójcik, 2020). For the isolation and growth of LAB, the standard medium is the nutrient-rich de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth, which uses glucose as a carbon source and a variety of nitrogen sources (including yeast extract, meat extract, and peptone), supplemented with minerals to provide the micronutrients (De Man et al., 1960).

Peptones are soluble protein hydrolysates of complex undefined macromolecules, which play a key role as sources of nitrogen and growth factors. Peptones include amino acids, peptides, inorganic salts and other compounds like sugars, vitamins, and lipids (Franek et al., 2000; Heidemann et al., 2000). As growth factors, peptones arise from varying sources and have been long known to contain complex ingredients that have a significant impact on the performance of the growth media (Gray et al., 2008; Li et al., 2011; Preetha et al., 2006; Snell & Peterson, 1940; van Schothorst & Renaud, 1983). A study by Gray et al. (2008) explored peptones from diverse sources (gelatin, meat, soy, casein, and yeast) as pivotal determinants of bacterial growth. They found that variation in peptones as growth factors in media had a significant effect on the growth and enumeration of bacteria. They further reported that there were varying growth responses across the studied media, likely due to the different growth factors in the peptones. The general trend indicated that yeast peptone was the most effective at supporting the highest biomass concentrations, then soy, meat, casein, and then gelatine peptone.

Meat extract and proteose peptone are the primary nitrogen sources and growth factors in MRS Broth, required to accommodate the fastidious growth requirements (vitamins, amino acids, etc.) of lactobacilli (Laitila et al., 2004; Śliżewska & Chlebicz-Wójcik, 2020). However, both are animal-derived, and this may restrict their use, for example for vegan/vegetarian diets (Pathak & Martirosyan, 2012) and in certain cultural and religious contexts (Karim & Khan, 2018). There is also a concern about the transmission of prions from animal-derived products to food and human-consumed products, posing a health risk to humans (Hedlin et al., 2012; Katole, et al., 2013; Lee, 2023). Meat extract and peptone are also expensive sources of nitrogen that can lead to high processing costs, especially upon production scale-up (De La Torre et al., 2018; Georgieva et al., 2009; Laitila et al., 2004; Manzoor et al., 2017). The result is that the end-product probiotic becomes relatively expensive and thus potentially unaffordable for consumers and in primary healthcare systems. This is especially of concern in Africa. As context for this, Happel et al. (2017) reported that the cost of purchasing vaginal probiotics for one treatment course per individual constitutes between 1.6 and 4.9% of the average South African's monthly income. There is, therefore, a need to reduce production costs to enable more affordable probiotics for the African market.

In recent years, plant-based nitrogen sources have been increasingly explored to cultivate Lactobacillaceae, presenting sustainable and cost-effective alternatives to traditional, animal-derived media. Various substrates have been demonstrated to successfully enhance biomass yields and lactic acid production, including date palm medium (Ayad et al., 2020), soy, and wheat flour blends (Dulf et al., 2022), and malt sprout extract (Hujanen et al., 2001). Legumes like peanuts, mung beans, lentils, and chickpeas, have proven effective for both biomass and lactic acid production (Dulf et al., 2022; Durga Devi et al., 2020; Hujanen & Linko, 1996; Karetkin et al., 2017; Pathak & Martirosyan, 2012; Śliżewska & Chlebicz-Wójcik, 2020). Flour blends from wheat, barley, maize, and rye have been demonstrated for efficient cultivation of probiotic Lactobacillaceae spp. (Śliżewska & Chlebicz-Wójcik, 2020). Additionally, tropical fruits have been identified as promising substrates, particularly for L. plantarum (Ngouénam et al., 2021). These collective findings highlight the versatility of plant-based media in supporting probiotic fermentation (Coghetto et al., 2016).

In this study, soytone is investigated as an alternative nitrogen and growth factor source for the cultivation of five novel South African vaginal Lactobacillaceae, previously isolated and characterised for their probiotic potential to support bacterial vaginosis treatment as an adjunctive regimen with antibiotics (Happel et al., 2020). This condition affects females of childbearing age, characterised by a depletion of commensal Lactobacillaceae, increased vaginal pH, and an increased susceptibility to HIV, STIs, and labour-related complications (Bradshaw & Sobel, 2016; Haldar et al., 2016; Tachedjian et al., 2017). The overarching aim of the study is to support the development of a more cost-effective and efficient media for large-scale production of these Lactobacillaceae isolates.

Soytone was chosen for its defined composition, thereby ensuring consistent probiotic product quality, which is especially important for applications in human medical usage where variability in nutrient sources could affect end-product integrity. Additionally, soytone offers several advantages over MRS and other alternative nitrogen sources: its enzymatic digestion may improve nitrogen bioavailability, it is water-soluble, and its composition is more consistent and defined than many other alternatives (Franek et al., 2000; Gray et al., 2008; Heidemann et al., 2000). While food waste-derived nitrogen sources such as whey, fruit peels, and vegetable stalks have been reported in literature, their inherent compositional variability poses a risk to consistent product quality in therapeutic probiotics (Chalón et al., 2013; Girase et al., 2022; Jadhav et al., 2018). Though Gray et al. (2008) showed that yeast peptone was preferably for supporting high bacterial biomass yields, yeast peptone was not selected as a comparator for this study due to its significantly higher commercial cost relative to soytone. The overarching aim was to develop a medium that balances nutritional adequacy with economic feasibility for large-scale lactic acid bacteria cultivation and probiotic production in low- and middle-income countries. Thus, soytone was prioritised as a promising plant-based alternative that meets these dual criteria.

The scope of the study is limited to evaluating the Soytone Medium as an alternative to MRS Broth with respect to growth medium characterisation, biomass production, productivity, and production cost. In determining this, the media have been tested across four LAB species and two strains of one of these species. This was to identify the media's generic suitability for LAB targeted for probiotic use in terms of microbial productivity and cost. Further characterisation of the specific species is presented separately in Jona et al. (in preparation).

Materials and Methods

Microbial Cultures

Five novel Lactobacillaceae strains (Lactobacillus crispatus 70.6PA, Lactobacillus crispatus 73.55a, Limosilactobacillus mucosae 90.13PA, Lactobacillus gasseri 94.98B, and Lactobacillus jensenii 95.1PA) were used. They were all harvested from the vaginal tract of healthy South African females (those without bacterial vaginosis or other sexually transmitted diseases) aged 16–22 years old. The details of the harvesting and characterisation of these isolates are outlined in the work by Happel and co-workers (Happel et al., 2018, 2020). Four of these strains (L. crispatus 70.6PA, L. crispatus 73.55a, L. gasseri 94.98b, and L. jensenii 95.1PA) are homofermentative lactobacilli, as they only synthesise lactic acid from glucose; while L. mucosae 90.13PA is a heterofermenter, producing lactic acid, acetic acid and ethanol from glucose (Happel, 2018; Happel et al., 2020). All isolates were stored at −80°C in 60% glycerol stocks (20% v/v) until needed.

Growth Media

Three growth media were used for cultivation of the Lactobacillaceae. The first media was standard MRS Broth, which served as the positive control. The second was the Soytone Medium, in which soytone was used as the primary nitrogen and growth factor source in place of meat extract and peptone in MRS Broth. Finally, MRS Broth without any meat extract or peptone served as the negative control.

Table 1 summarises and compares the constituents of the growth media. The primary carbon source for all the media was D-glucose, whose concentration was standardised to 20 g/L. The complex nitrogen sources in the growth media included yeast extract, peptone, meat-extract, and soytone. Both meat extract and peptone are animal-derived—the meat extract is bovine-derived and the peptone in MRS Broth is a pancreatic hydrolysate of animal tissues (Franek et al., 2000; Heidemann et al., 2000). These nitrogen and carbon sources were supplemented by mineral salts. It should be noted that yeast extract is not animal-derived and does not pose the same issues as meat extract and peptone. It was thus not removed from the growth medium.

MRS broth

To prepare the growth medium, 51 g of MRS Broth (93780, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany) and 72 mg L-cysteine (C6852, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany) were dissolved in deionised water to form a 1 L broth and mixed using a magnetic stirrer on a hot plate at 60°C, for 10 min or until homogenous. Then 1 mL of Tween 80 (P1379, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany) was added to the 1 L solution and mixed at the same temperature until all was well-mixed. The resulting mixture was poured into the appropriate reaction vessel, which was autoclaved at 121°C for 20 min to sterilise the growth medium and the vessel.

MRS broth without meat extract and peptone

To prepare the MRS Broth that did not contain any animal-derived protein sources, 20 g/L D-glucose (108342, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 4 g/L yeast extract (113885, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 2 g/L K2HPO4 (105109, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 5 g/L sodium-acetate (106268, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 3 g/L ammonium-citrate dibasic (25102, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 0.2 g/L MgSO4.7H2O (M7506, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 0.05 g/L MnSO4.H2O (229784, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), 72 mg/L L-cysteine (30120, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany), and 1 mL/L of Tween 80 (P1754, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany) were dissolved in deionised water to make a 1 L mixture. The resulting solution was sterilised prior to use by autoclaving at 121°C for 20 min.

Soytone medium

The plant-based Soytone Medium was prepared using the same method as outlined in Section MRS Broth without Meat Extract and Peptone, but with 18 g/L of peptone from soybean (70178, Merck (Sigma-Aldrich), Darmstadt, Germany) added as the plant-based protein source. The 18 g/L concentration of peptone from soybean was used to replace the 8 g/L meat extract and 10 g/L peptone found in the ordinary MRS Broth on mass basis.

Bioreactor Fermentations

The cultivation of the Lactobacillaceae was studied in Infors AG CH-4103 Bottmingen bioreactors of 300 mL working volume. The bioreactors were operated at 140 rpm, 37°C, ambient pressure, and no aeration. The vessel geometry consisted of a standard cylindrical stirred tank with a round bottom, equipped with a central agitator shaft bearing two Rushton turbines and one marine impeller. While cultivation was conducted under non-aerated conditions, oxygen ingress was measured and confirmed to have a positive effect on the growth performance of the isolates. Consequently, the system operated effectively under oxygen-limited, microaerophilic conditions. In earlier exploratory studies, additional cultivations were conducted under defined anaerobic conditions achieved by sparging N2 gas. However, under these anaerobic settings, all strains exhibited significantly reduced growth rates and biomass yields. This informed the decision to proceed with the current cultivation regime, which maintained low but non-zero dissolved oxygen levels. The growth media were changed depending on the specific experiment.

Inocula were prepared as follows: 100 mL of unsterilised MRS Broth was poured into 120 mL serum bottles. The serum bottles were then autoclaved at 121°C for 20 mins and left to cool down to ambient temperature before being used. A 2 mL aliquot of a cryogenically preserved strain in a glycerol stock was introduced into the 100 mL sterile medium and cultivated overnight at 37°C in an incubator rotating at 140 rpm. The overnight culture was then used as an inoculum for the other sterile serum bottles with the same growth medium. The initial concentrations of the strains were set to 0.1 optical density (OD) units at 660 nm using a Thermo Scientific Genesys 105 UV-Vis spectrophotometer. An overnight inoculum prepared from a single glycerol stock was used to inoculate the bioreactors to an initial OD of 0.1.

Samples for analysis were drawn from the bioreactors hourly for the first 12 hrs, then again at 24 and 26 hrs. There was no sampling done between 12 and 24 hrs as the microorganisms entered stationary phase during this period, confirmed by sampling again after 24 and 26 hrs.

Analytical Methods

Carbon and nitrogen quantification

The Analytik Jena multi-N/C 3100 duo was used to quantify the carbon (organic and inorganic) and nitrogen concentrations in all the media studied, including both individual constituents, and the complex mixed media.

Cell optical density and cell dry weight quantification

The cell concentration in solution was monitored during fermentations using optical density (OD) 660 nm, using a Thermo Scientific Genesys 10S UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The samples used to measure OD were measured against a growth medium blank and once the readings were ≥0.7 OD660, the samples were diluted (1:10) with the blank growth medium before the cell optical density was measured.

Biomass concentration was estimated by dry weight, such that concentration and OD could be correlated. Samples of known OD were centrifuged at 16,873 g (Eppendorf 5418R microfuge) in pre-weighed and pre-dried (80°C, 48 hrs) Eppendorf microfuge tubes. The pellets were then washed with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and centrifuged again at 16,873 g. The centrifuged pellets were then washed and recentrifuged before being dried at 80°C for 48 hrs in an oven. They were finally cooled to room temperature in a desiccator and weighed to determine the dry biomass weight.

Sample pH

pH was measured using a Lasec pH 50 + DHS meter.

Glucose and lactic acid analyses via high performance liquid chromatography

The glucose consumed and lactic acid produced by the Lactobacillaceae were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Glucose was of interest as the primary carbon source in the media. Given that four of the five strains investigated were homofermentative and so produce lactic acid as their primary metabolic product, the analytical focus for metabolite production was placed solely on lactic acid. Although the heterofermentative L. mucosae 90.13PA is known to produce smaller amounts of acetic acid and ethanol alongside lactic acid (Quinto et al., 2014; Zheng et al., 2015), these secondary metabolites were not quantified in the present study. Any reduced lactic acid productivity observed in this strain may nonetheless be attributed to the redistribution of carbon flux toward these additional products, as consistent with established heterofermentative metabolic behaviour.

Samples were centrifuged at 16,873 g for 15 min using an Eppendorf 5418R microfuge and the supernatants were stored at −20°C prior to the HPLC analyses. An Aminex HPX—87H column was utilised, which uses 5 mM H2SO4(aq) prepared with ultrapure water as the mobile phase. The mobile phase was set at a flowrate of 0.5 mL/min and the column operated at 40°C. All analytes (glucose and lactate) were detected using the reflective index method.

Growth Media Costing

The pricing as shown on the Merck website (https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/ZA/en) was used to quantify the cost of each growth medium constituent. A basis of producing 1 L of growth medium broth was assumed, with the compositions outlined in Table 1.

The pricing was obtained in South African Rands (ZAR), then converted to United States Dollar (USD), for which the exchange rate was taken as ZAR 18.4/USD, the average exchange rate at the time of modelling the costs (October 2023–April 2024). Further, the costing of the biomass for all five Lactobacillaceae per gram cell dry weight ($/g) was done across all three growth media, based on the final cell densities obtained. It should be noted that only the cost of the growth media was considered for this calculation. While the costing analysis was performed with ingredients intended for bench-scale bioreactors and may differ upon scale-up with industrial-grade materials, the relative pricing between different media formulations is expected to remain similar.

Statistical Analyses

The bioreactor experiments were conducted in duplicate (n = 2) due to the resource-intensive nature of bench-scale fermentation and analytical throughput. Strict standardisation of inoculum preparation, growth conditions, and process control ensured reproducibility across biological replicates. All analytical measurements (OD, pH, HPLC) were done in triplicate (n = 3).

Statistical comparisons were conducted using two-way Analysis of Variance in Microsoft Excel, with a significance level of α = 0.05 used to evaluate differences in maximum specific growth rate, biomass yield, glucose consumption, and lactic acid productivity across strains and media.

To support reproducibility assessment, all values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and relative error (RE) was calculated for key growth parameters. SDs were generally below ±0.08 and REs under 5%, reflecting high internal consistency. Error bars in figures were adjusted for visibility where minimal variance was observed.

Results and Discussion

Growth Media Characterisation

The carbon source and mineral salts in standard MRS media are well-defined in terms of their elemental composition. However, this is not the case for complex nitrogen sources as their elemental nitrogen and carbon concentrations are unknown. Since the carbon and nitrogen content of the growth media play a pivotal role in the fastidious growth requirements of the LAB, these have a direct impact on the metabolic activity of the microorganisms (Georgieva et al., 2009; Juárez Tomás et al., 2010), including the production of metabolites and biomass (Singh et al., 2017). Hence, nitrogen and carbon concentrations in the different growth media were quantified and compared (Figs. 1 and 2, Table 2).

Growth media carbon content

The carbon content of the standard MRS Broth was very similar to that of the Soytone Medium (16.9 ± 0.02 g/L and 17.0 ± 0.01 g/L, respectively) (Fig. 1). The MRS Broth without peptone and meat extract had the lowest total carbon content (9.61 ± 0.04 g/L), 43% lower than standard MRS Broth. Thus, the excluded components make a significant contribution to the carbon content of the medium.

Although nitrogen sources in the Soytone Medium and standard MRS Broth were matched on a mass-basis, their total carbon concentrations were the same, as shown in Fig. 1. As the D-glucose concentration was kept the same for these growth media, this indicates that the animal and plant-derived nitrogen sources contributed very similar carbon amounts. This is confirmed by the data presented in Fig. 2, with the carbon concentrations in solutions containing 1 g/L of the complex media components measured to be 0.405 ± 0.002 g/L in meat extract, 0.407 ± 0.001 g/L in peptone, 0.403 ± 0.002 g/L in yeast extract, and 0.413 ± 0.001 g/L in soytone. The carbon contained in all four of these complex nitrogen sources is also >99% organic carbon, with only trace amounts of inorganic carbon sources as shown in Table 2.

Growth media nitrogen content

The total amount of nitrogen in the standard MRS Broth containing the animal-derived protein sources was 2.6 ± 0.02 g/L (Fig. 1). By comparison, the Soytone Medium had a 17.5% lower nitrogen concentration, at 2.1 ± 0.01 g/L. The modified MRS Broth devoid of animal-derived nitrogen sources unsurprisingly had the lowest total nitrogen concentration, at 1.61 ± 0.01 g/L, which was 37% less nitrogen than standard MRS Broth (see Fig. 1). This result highlights that yeast extract, a non-animal-derived protein source, contributes a notable amount of nitrogen to the negative control, MRS without animal-derived nitrogen sources.

Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio within media impacts the stoichiometric ratios required to maintain the metabolic and nutritional requirements of microorganisms. If one of these media constituents is in relative deficit, it will result in a growth limitation.

Fig. 1 shows that the Soytone Medium had the highest carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of 8.1 ± 0.04, followed by standard MRS Broth at a ratio of 6.6 ± 0.06. The MRS Broth without meat-extract and peptone had the lowest carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, at 6.0 ± 0.04. The single-component complex nitrogen sources illustrated in Fig. 2 have much lower carbon-to-nitrogen ratios than the MRS Broth, due to the exclusion of D-glucose. Soytone had the highest carbon-to-nitrogen ratio at 4.5 ± 0.02, followed by yeast extract (3.7 ± 0.06), meat extract (3.5 ± 0.07), and peptone (3.4 ± 0.03).

The ODs are an indication of the biomass cell density and therefore, the parameter which we seek to maximise upon producing bacterial strains using probiotics of high cell density (Juárez Tomás et al., 2010). The maximum specific growth rate is an industrially important parameter that dictates and impacts the duration of a fermentation batch; high maximum specific growth rates allow for a sooner attainment of desired biomass cell densities. Further, biomass productivity is related to and obtained from the product of the maximum specific growth and the biomass concentration, as shown by Equation 2. The doubling time is impacted by and has an inversely proportional relationship with the maximum specific growth rate, meaning a higher maximum specific growth rate leads to a shorter doubling time as expressed by Equation 2.

Growth in MRS broth

When cultivated in standard MRS Broth, the Lactobacillaceae isolates exhibited short lag phases (<2 hrs) and entered exponential growth between 2 and 6 hrs of cultivation (Fig. 3a and b). Although all strains ultimately reached similar maximum cell densities by 24–26 hrs, they displayed distinct maximum specific growth rates (μmax) during the early exponential phase, leading to divergence in OD660 values over the first 5 hrs. By 11 hrs, all strains had transitioned into late exponential or early stationary phase, after which growth plateaued.

Lactobacillus gasseri 94.98b achieved the highest maximum specific growth rate at 0.56 ± 0.05 h−1, while achieving the second-highest final OD660 (2.35 ± 0.23). Lactobacillus crispatus 70.6PA led with the highest cell OD at 3.06 ± 0.28 achieved at 24 hrs and had the second-highest maximum specific growth rate of 0.39 ± 0.02 h−1. Lactobacillus crispatus 73.55a and L. mucosae 90.13PA yielded the lowest final cell densities, with OD660 values of 1.75 ± 0.44 and 1.59 ± 0.80, respectively, recorded at 24 hrs. The maximum specific growth rates (μmax) observed in MRS Broth were as follows: L. crispatus 70.6PA, 0.39 ± 0.02 h−¹; L. crispatus 73.55a, 0.39 ± 0.05 h−¹; L. mucosae 90.13PA, 0.47 ± 0.03 h−¹; L. gasseri 94.98b, 0.56 ± 0.15 h−¹; and L. jensenii 95.1PA, 0.38 ± 0.09 h−¹—reflecting clear strain-dependent variability in growth kinetics under standard MRS Broth conditions.

Growth in MRS without meat extract and peptone

Upon removing the animal-derived nitrogen sources (meat extract and peptone) from MRS Broth, a growth limitation was observed for all five strains (Fig. 3c and d). In this depleted medium, none of the strains but L. crispatus 70.6PA reached a maximum OD660 >1. L. crispatus 70.6PA achieved the highest OD660 (1.29 ± 0.19), while L. gasseri 94.98b had the lowest OD660 (0.63 ± 0.02). The highest cell OD by L. crispatus 70.6PA was 58% lower than when the same strain was cultivated in MRS Broth. All the other strains grew to similar cell ODs of ≤0.74, which remained stationary from 10 hrs of cultivation until the end of the experiment (28 hrs). The statistical analyses indicate that the difference in the data were statistically significant (p = .00145).

The microbial growth rates were relatively slow and consistent over the first 9 hrs of cultivation, before the stationary phase was entered from 10 hrs onwards. All five strains had a lower maximum specific growth rate when cultured in the MRS devoid of meat extract and peptone compared to the standard MRS Broth. Lactobacillus crispatus 73.55a achieved the highest maximum specific growth rate of 0.29 ± 0.01 h−1, a 29% reduction from when the same strain was cultivated in MRS Broth (0.39 ± 0.05 h−1) (Fig. 4). All other strains had maximum specific growth rates of ≤0.24 h−1. The limitation led to a significantly different reduction in the maximum specific growth rates (p = .0123). This indicates that both the extent and rate of growth were limited owing to the nitrogen and growth factor limitation in this growth medium. Thus, as much as yeast extract is a constituent of this growth medium and is in the same concentration as the other two media (4 g/L), this alone is not sufficient to fully support the growth of the Lactobacillaceae. The effect of the low concentration of nitrogen sources and growth factors is further observed by the reduced growth rate and no change in the cell optical density beyond 10 hrs of cultivation for all strains.

These results are consistent with this growth medium being nutrient-limiting upon removal, of the animal-derived nitrogen sources. This confirms that the carbon and nitrogen, as well as other complex components (vitamins, amino acids, etc.) from peptone, soytone, and meat extract play critical roles as growth factors in the growth media.

Growth in soytone medium

When grown in the plant-based Soytone Medium (Fig. 3e and f), all strains grew to higher ODs when compared to growth in standard MRS Broth. As was observed with the MRS Broth, L. crispatus 73.55a had the lowest final cell OD660 of 1.65 ± 0.35, while L. crispatus 70.6PA had the highest final OD660 of 4.44 ± 0.59. The latter was 45% higher than when cultivated in MRS Broth (Fig. 4) and the differences in the data were confirmed to be statistically significant (p = .0328). Unlike when cultivated in the MRS Broth, all five strains showed no lag-phase beyond 2 hrs of cultivation. Further, all strains but L. crispatus 70.6PA reached their highest ODs sooner in the Soytone Medium (at either 11 or 12 hrs of cultivation) than when grown in the MRS Broth (up to 14 hrs later).

As was observed in MRS Broth, the exponential phase for all five strains was between 2 and 6 hrs of cultivation in the Soytone Medium. Thereafter, late exponential growth phase set in with the cells entering stationary phase beyond 12 hrs of cultivation. For three of the five strains (L. crispatus 70.6PA, L. mucosae 90.13PA, and L. jensenii 95.1PA), the maximum specific growth rate increased upon switching the growth medium from the MRS Broth to the Soytone Medium (Fig. 4). However, L. crispatus 73.55a reduced its maximum specific growth rate, from 0.39 ± 0.05 hr−1 to 0.33 ± 0.01 hr−1. The reduction observed in L. gasseri was marginal and not statistically significant, from 0.56 ± 0.05 hr−1 to 0.53 ± 0.03 h−1. Overall, the difference in the maximum specific growth rates were not statistically significant upon switching from MRS Broth to the Soytone Medium (p = .346).

It was found that four of the five strains (excluding only L. crispatus 73.55a) yielded higher cell densities upon being cultivated in the Soytone Medium than when cultured in MRS Broth. Cultivation of L. crispatus 73.55a in the Soytone Medium was 5.5% lower than in MRS. The best performing strain, L. crispatus 70.6PA, had a 45% higher cell OD when cultured in the Soytone Medium (Fig. 4). It is also noted that the Soytone Medium led to the isolates reaching their highest cell densities within the first 12 hrs of cultivation, while in MRS Broth they reached maximum cell densities between 12 and 24 hrs. This may result from the ease of access of nitrogen in the form of peptides, amino acids, or proteins that are present in soytone versus those found in meat extract and peptone, and the high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio in the Soytone Medium.

Switching from standard MRS Broth to Soytone Medium often resulted in improved growth for the Lactobacillaceae. This is likely because the two growth media provide different growth factors. The factors present in the Soytone Medium appear readily available and preferred by all Lactobacillaceae strains, with the exception of L. crispatus 73.55a.

The observed trends in maximum specific growth rates and biomass productivity highlight the influence of medium composition on the bioprocessing potential of Lactobacillaceae strains. Across all strains tested, growth in Soytone Medium resulted in the highest maximum specific growth rate and biomass productivity, suggesting that the enhanced availability of nitrogen and growth-promoting factors in soytone supports improved microbial performance.

The superior performance of the Soytone Medium relative to standard MRS Broth may be attributed to both general metabolic requirements of Lactobacillaceae and specific adaptations of vaginal isolates to nutrient availability in the host microenvironment. Soytone is known to be rich in peptides, free amino acids, B-group vitamins, and trace minerals, which collectively support lactic acid bacteria growth (Franek et al., 2000; Gray et al., 2008; Heidemann et al., 2000). Although we did not perform a detailed compositional analysis of the specific soytone batch used in this study, previous literature has reported significant batch-to-batch variability in nitrogen content, peptide length distribution, and vitamin profiles across commercial soytone preparations (Li et al., 2011; Preetha et al., 2006). It is therefore plausible that the improved performance observed reflects both the general compatibility of soytone with Lactobacillaceae and a match between the nutrient profile and these vaginal isolates.

MRS Broth generally supported moderate growth and biomass productivity, while MRS without meat extract and peptone resulted in significantly reduced values, reinforcing the importance of complex nitrogen sources for efficient bioprocessing. These findings highlight the critical role of medium formulation in improving high cell density production, particularly in fermentation processes where maximising biomass density and productivity is essential for scalable probiotic production.

The improved growth of lactobacilli has been observed in various media incorporating plant- based nitrogen sources, attributed to factors such as the presence of growth-enhancing compounds, favourable carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratios, and cost-effectiveness. In a study by Dulf et al. (2022), enhanced growth and fermentation quality were reported when Lactobacillus casei and Lactobacillus plantarum were cultured in soy and wheat flour blends, which were suggested to improve microbial proliferation due to inherent growth factors in the plant materials. Similarly, Lactobacillus reuteri cultivated in a date palm medium achieved significant biomass production, likely due to the medium's optimal nutrient balance and affordability as an alternative to conventional media (Ayad et al., 2020). Further research highlights the potential of plant-based nitrogen sources to incorporate agricultural by-products. This was the case in the work report by Ngouénam et al. (2021), where tropical fruit residues supported the growth of Lactobacillus plantarum strains for lactic acid production, with improved growth performance linked to their nutrient composition and probiotic potential. Additionally, a growth medium incorporating soybean protein acid residue effectively supported the growth of Lactobacillus plantarum BL011 in batch reactors, proposed to be due to its ability to replace animal-derived components while achieving high biomass concentrations (Coghetto et al., 2016). These results of this study therefore corroborate these prior findings, with similar effects demonstrated for the growth of the five novel vaginal Lactobacillaceae isolates in the plant-based Soytone Medium, which yielded improved cell densities compared to the animal-derived MRS Broth.

Broth pH Variation

The ability of LAB to reduce pH is a useful indicator of the production of metabolites, such as lactic acid, acetic acid, and ethanol (in the case of some heterofermentative LAB) (Zheng et al., 2015). These organic compounds play a major role in the isolates’ probiotic potential (Kosin & Rakshit, 2006; Preti & Huggins, 1975). The pH-reducing ability of these vaginal Lactobacillaceae isolates is particularly important, as a healthy vagina is regarded as one that is maintained at a low pH (<4.5) (Hay, 2014; Nugent et al., 1991). Therefore, it is pivotal for the novel Lactobacillaceae isolates to be cultivated in a growth medium which enables, promotes, and maintains the metabolic activity of the strains.

Fig. 5 shows the pH-reduction trends of the five strains in the three different media. In standard MRS Broth (Fig. 5a), the starting broth pH was 6.38 ± 0.04, which reduced similarly during the first 5 hrs for all five strains (pH ∼ 5.7). This was consistent with the standard MRS Broth growth curves shown in Fig. 3a, where the growth trends of all strains were similar in the first 5 hrs of cultivation. Correspondingly, the strain which yielded the highest cell density (L. crispatus 70.6PA) had the largest overall reduction in pH (ΔpH = –2.61 ± 0.04) when cultivated in standard MRS Broth. In contrast, L. jensenii 95.1PA had the smallest change in pH in standard MRS Broth (ΔpH = −2.06 ± 0.2) after 28 hrs. The average reduction in pH in standard MRS Broth was 2.24 ± 0.22, which was the largest observed average pH reduction of all three different growth media.

Broth pH variation over the cultivation period of the five strains grown in: (a) standard de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth, (b) MRS without meat extract and peptone, and (c) Soytone Medium. The experiment was conducted in duplicates; error bars are indicative of one standard deviation of the mean.

The initial pH in MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone was 5.74 ± 0.05 (Fig. 5b). In this limited growth medium, L. crispatus 70.6PA had the largest pH reduction (∆pH = –1.65 ± 0.06), while L. mucosae 90.13PA yielded the least pH reduction (∆pH = –0.72 ± 0.04) after 28 hrs.

All strains reduced the pH of the MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone by an overall average of 0.98 ± 0.41, which was the least reduction in pH noted for all three growth media. As highlighted in Fig. 3b, the removal of meat extract and peptone led to a growth limitation of all five strains.

Although the Soytone Medium (Fig. 5c) had a lower initial pH (5.85 ± 0.02) than the MRS Broth, a similar trend in pH reduction was observed. In Soytone Medium, L. crispatus 70.6PA again had the largest pH reduction (∆pH = –2.09 ± 0.01), L. mucosae 90.13PA had the smallest pH change (∆pH = –1.48 ± 0.42), and the average pH reduction in the Soytone Medium was 1.64 ± 0.26.

The reduction in broth pH is primarily driven by the accumulation of organic acids, particularly lactic acid, produced during fermentation. As the lactic acid bacteria metabolise glucose, they generate organic acids, releasing protons (H+) into the broth (Zheng et al., 2015). These protons combine with water molecules to form hydronium ions (H3O+), leading to a pH reduction (Zheng et al., 2015), as was observed in Fig. 5. The rate of pH reduction is influenced by both the metabolic activity of the LAB and the buffering capacity of the media. This relationship is evident in Fig. 6, where strains grown in MRS Broth and Soytone Medium exhibited significantly higher proton concentration changes compared to those in MRS without meat extract and peptone. The reduction in metabolic activity in the nutrient-limited media resulted in a lower H+ accumulation. For example, L. crispatus 70.6PA produced the highest H+ concentration change in the Soytone Medium (1.61 × 10−4 M), whereas its proton release was nearly halved in MRS without meat extract and peptone (7.42 × 10−⁵ M). This highlights the impact of media composition on metabolic activity, organic acid production, and proton generation, which in turn impacts pH changes. These findings have significant implications for the probiotic potential of these strains, particularly in environments where acid tolerance is a key factor (Hay, 2014; Nugent et al., 1991).

Broth proton concentration change for all five strains in the three growth media. The experiment was conducted in duplicates; error bars are indicative of one standard deviation of the mean.

Fig. 5 compares the final pH (at 26 hrs) of the three different growth media in which the Lactobacillaceae were grown. The final pH of standard MRS Broth and Soytone Medium were similar. In contrast, the final pH of MRS without meat extract and peptone was highest for all five strains. The relatively high final pH in the MRS without meat extract and peptone is likely a result of a low production of organic acids. The reduction of the broths’ pHs only occurs upon the synthesis of organic acids, which are concomitant to cell growth and metabolism. However, MRS without meat extract and peptone was nutrient-limited and low cell growth was observed, hence the broth's final pH only being reduced slightly across all five strains.

In all three growth mediums, L. crispatus 70.6PA reached the lowest final broth pH while the remaining four strains had comparatively similar final broth pH values. In all growth media, the rate of pH reduction declined over time as the broth pH approached 4, except for L. crispatus 70.6PA in MRS Broth and Soytone Medium, with final pH values of 3.80 ± 0.03 and 3.79 + 0.08, respectively. The asymptotic final pH values observed in Fig. 5 for growth in MRS Broth and the Soytone Medium are related to the production of organic acids and are indicative of the growth-limiting potential and effect of low pH (<4) and the accumulation of organic acid concentration in the broths. This is further supported by the finding that at this stage of growth the glucose in all systems was not depleted (Fig. 7). However, growth-limiting low pH values and organic acid concentration were reached, and growth was impeded for all strains, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Glucose concentration in (a) standard de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth; (b) MRS Broth without meat extract/peptone; and (c) Soytone Medium over time during Lactobacillaceae strain growth. Experiments were conducted in duplicates; error bars are indicative of one standard deviation of the mean.

Substrate Consumption, Product Formation, and Yield Coefficients

Glucose consumption

Glucose is the primary carbon source in all the growth media tested in this study. The total amount of glucose consumed varied across the three different growth media, as illustrated in Fig. 7.

In all three media, the amount of glucose contained in the broths pre-autoclaving was 20 g/L, which slightly reduced post-autoclaving because of caramelisation, degradation, and the presence of amino acids, allowing for the Maillard reaction to proceed at elevated temperatures (Baumgartner, 1938; Hodge, 1953). The glucose concentrations at the start of the fermentations are shown in Fig. 7.

In standard MRS Broth, all strains consumed glucose at varying rates, with L. gasseri 94.98b depleting it fastest (15.7 ± 0.3 g/L) within 12 hrs, and L. crispatus 73.55PA leaving residual glucose at 26 hrs. In Soytone Medium, three strains (L. mucosae 90.13PA, L. gasseri 94.98b, and L. jensenii 95.1PA) consumed all available glucose within the first 12 hrs, with no significant difference in total glucose consumption between the two media (p = .862).

The lowest glucose consumption was observed in the nitrogen-limited MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone, where the strains exhibited limited growth. Here, L. crispatus 70.6PA consumed the most glucose (8.7 ± 0.2 g/L), while L. jensenii 95.1PA consumed the least (5.4 ± 3.3 g/L). A statistically significant difference in glucose consumption between this medium and standard MRS Broth was noted (p = .00372), demonstrating the impact of nitrogen limitation on glucose consumption across all strains.

Lactic acid production

Lactic acid, the primary metabolite for homofermentative LAB, is directly linked to glucose consumption (Zheng et al., 2015). Fig. 8 illustrates lactic acid production over time for each strain in the different media. Notably, lactic acid concentrations had not yet plateaued by 26 hrs, despite all strains entering stationary phase after 11 hrs of the experiment.

The lactic acid concentration over time in (a) standard de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth; (b) MRS Broth without meat extract/peptone; (c) Soytone Medium during lactobacilli strain growth. Experiments were conducted in duplicates; error bars are indicative of one standard deviation of the mean.

In standard MRS Broth, L. crispatus 70.6PA produced the highest lactic acid concentration (8.8 ± 1.2 g/L), while L. mucosae 90.13PA produced the least (4.4 ± 0.2 g/L). When grown in the nitrogen-limited MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone, all strains produced significantly less lactic acid compared to standard MRS Broth (p = 5.70 × 10−⁵). Here, L. crispatus 70.6PA again produced the most lactic acid (5.1 ± 0.2 g/L), while L. mucosae 90.13PA and L. gasseri 94.98b produced the least (1.0 ± 0.6 and 1.0 ± 0.004 g/L, respectively).

In the Soytone Medium, lactic acid production was similar to that in standard MRS Broth (p = .184), except for L. crispatus 70.6PA, which produced 12.6 ± 1.9 g/L–43% more than in MRS Broth. These results highlight the influence of the growth medium on lactic acid production, with nitrogen limitation reducing output across all strains, and a notable enhancement in lactic acid production in Soytone Medium for L. crispatus 70.6PA.

Glucose consumption, lactic acid and biomass production, and yield coefficients

The calculated lactic acid yield coefficient, biomass yield coefficient, lactic acid productivity and glucose consumption rate for each of the growth systems (varying media type and strain) are presented in Fig. 9 (a–d). The presented yield coefficients are based on the overall cultivation period (26 hrs), whilst the lactic acid productivities and glucose consumption rates were calculated over 12 hrs, which was the earliest depletion time.

(a–d) Substrate and product yield coefficients of the different strains in the three growth media. (a) Lactic acid yield coefficient (Yp/s [g/g]); (b) Biomass yield coefficient (Yx/s [g/g]); (c) Lactic acid productivity (Qp [g/L/h]); (d) Glucose consumption rate (−Qs [g/L/h]). Experiments were conducted in duplicate. Error bars are indicative of one standard deviation of the mean.

The lactic acid-to-glucose yield coefficients (Yp/s) (Fig. 9a) varied notably across the different growth media, highlighting the impact of nutrient availability on product formation and yield. The Soytone Medium generally supported higher lactic acid yields than MRS Broth, particularly for L. crispatus 70.6PA and L. crispatus 73.55a, which showed the most pronounced increases. In contrast, the MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone resulted in consistently lower yield coefficients across all strains, suggesting that the absence of these components limited metabolic efficiency. While MRS Broth and Soytone Medium produced relatively similar yields for some strains (e.g. L. gasseri 94.98b and L. jensenii 95.1PA), the overall trend indicates that Soytone Medium enhances lactic acid production more effectively than MRS Broth, while the nutrient-depleted medium significantly reduces yield. This highlights the importance of growth medium composition in fermentation performance.

The biomass-to-glucose yield coefficient (Yx/s) is a measure of the amount of the consumed substrate (glucose) that was converted to biomass, expressed as a fraction on a mass-to-mass basis and measured over the duration of the experiment, 26 hrs. The biomass yield coefficient was highest in the Soytone Medium for all five strains, as shown in Fig. 9b. This result suggests that in the Soytone Medium the form of the complex nitrogen sources is better for supporting the accumulation of biomass than in standard MRS Broth. Soytone Medium contains enzymatically hydrolysed soy protein, which provides readily assimilable peptides and amino acids, potentially enhancing biomass accumulation. In contrast, the more complex nitrogen sources in MRS Broth may require additional enzymatic processing by the microorganisms, leading to comparatively lower biomass yields.

The MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone had the lowest biomass yield coefficients, but the values were relatively comparable to those achieved in standard MRS Broth for three of the five strains (L. crispatus 73.55a, L. mucosae 90.13PA, and L. jensenii 95.1PA).

The lactic acid productivity indicates the rate at which the lactic acid was produced on a volumetric basis. As shown in Fig. 9c, culturing in standard MRS Broth or the Soytone Medium yielded similar overall lactic acid productivity rates for all strains except L. crispatus 70.6PA, which had a higher productivity rate in the Soytone Medium (1.05 ± 0.07 g/L/h) than in MRS Broth (0.73 ± 0.05 g/L/h). Notably, L. crispatus 70.6PA also consistently displayed the highest lactic acid productivity rate across all media, peaking at in Soytone Medium as highlighted earlier. The lactic acid productivity rates were uniformly lowest in the MRS Broth without meat extract and peptone across all strains.

The heterofermentative L. mucosae 90.13PA demonstrated consistently lower lactic acid productivity than the homofermentative strains, a finding that can be explained by its known ability to channel carbon from its feedstock into multiple fermentation products, including acetic acid and ethanol (Quinto et al., 2014; Zheng et al., 2015). While these additional metabolites were not directly quantified in this study, their production likely contributes to the observed reduction in lactic acid yields.

Distinct patterns in glucose consumption and metabolic activity emerge across the three growth media. The Soytone Medium exhibited the highest glucose consumption rates, with all strains except L. crispatus 73.55a depleting the available glucose. Conversely, MRS without meat extract and peptone had the lowest glucose consumption across all strains.

Lactobacillus crispatus 70.6PA consumed the highest amount of glucose in the Soytone Medium, (17.3 ± 0.6 g/L), followed by 13.6 ± 0.4 g/L in standard MRS Broth and only 8.7 ± 0.2 g/L in MRS without meat extract and peptone. The corresponding glucose consumption rates were 1.44 ± 0.02 g/L/h in Soytone Medium, 1.13 ± 0.02 g/L/h in MRS Broth, and 0.72 ± 0.01 g/L/h in the nitrogen-limited medium. Similarly, L. jensenii 95.1PA exhibited the highest glucose uptake in MRS Broth (17.6 ± 1.4 g/L), with a consumption rate of 1.47 ± 0.05 g/L/h, compared to 1.42 ± 0.003 g/L/h in Soytone Medium and only 0.45 ± 0.013 g/L/h in MRS without meat extract and peptone. Across all media, biomass-to-glucose yield coefficients (Yx/s) were lower than lactic acid-to-glucose yield coefficients (Yp/s), indicating a metabolic preference for lactic acid production over biomass formation in these studied conditions.

Growth Media Costing

Several factors determine the techno-economic feasibility of a bioprocess for industrial scale-up. In cases where the product is the biomass itself or a primary metabolite, and where downstream processing requirements are minimal, growth medium costs can represent a significant fraction of operating expenses (Harrison et al., 2015). It is, therefore, essential to evaluate and compare the costs associated with different media to optimise economic viability for the intended application.

Fig. 10 illustrates the media costs associated with the three different growth media evaluated in this study. The widely used standard animal protein-derived MRS Broth cost $7.9/L, whereas the Soytone Medium cost $4.4/L to produce, representing a 44 % reduction. The modified MRS medium without animal-derived protein had the lowest production cost, $3.1/L, indicating that the nitrogen sources with associated growth factors in the MRS Broth account for 60% of its cost. Additionally, the analysis revealed a negligible cost difference between premixed MRS Broth and broth prepared from individual components, with the premixed MRS being 8% more expensive.

A comparison of the cost of producing 1 L of the three different growth media. Pricing based on products manufactured by Merck.

A cost of the media on a biomass basis ($/g) across the five Lactobacillaceae and three media is presented in Fig. 11. For all strains, the lowest cost of production per gram of biomass was obtained when culturing the strains in the Soytone Medium. Lactobacillus crispatus 70.6PA had the lowest production cost overall at 0.79$/g of biomass in the Soytone Medium, while this same strain costs 2.06$/g of biomass to produce in standard MRS Broth, indicating a 61.6% reduction. Limosilactobacillus mucosae 90.13PA displayed the largest cost reduction per gram of biomass of 73.4% upon switching from MRS Broth (3.95$/g) to the Soytone Medium (1.05$/g).

The cost of biomass of the five Lactobacillaceae across the three different growth media. Pricing based on products manufactured by Merck and only the cost associated with the growth media was accounted for.

It is important to acknowledge that the cost calculations performed in this study were based on laboratory-grade reagents used in the bench-scale bioreactor. On an industrial-scale, reagent costs are typically lower, due to bulk purchasing being associated with lower prices and lower grades of reagents or more complex reagents used.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a Soytone Medium, free of any animal-derived nitrogen sources, as an alternative to standard MRS Broth for cultivating vaginal Lactobacillaceae strains with probiotic potential. The results demonstrated that the Soytone Medium supported higher cell densities (up to a 45% increment), faster growth rates, and shorter doubling times for four out of the five strains tested, indicating its potential for more efficient biomass production compared to MRS Broth. This is postulated to be because of the differences in nitrogen sources’ composition, with soytone providing a higher carbon-to-nitrogen ratio in the medium. It could also be linked to different profiles of proteins, peptides, and vitamins contained in the soytone relative to MRS, something that would be valuable to further investigate. Additionally, the Soytone Medium consistently supported higher yield coefficients for both biomass (Yx/s) and lactic acid production (Yp/s), alongside greater glucose consumption and lactic acid productivity, suggesting its potential for maximising lactic acid yields in industrial applications. Despite some strain-dependent variations, such as L. mucosae 90.13PA's heterofermentative metabolism leading to lower lactic acid productivity, the overall data indicate a preferential use of glucose for lactic acid synthesis over biomass production across the three growth media for all studied strains.

Economically, the Soytone Medium offered a significant growth medium cost reduction, with it being 44% less expensive on a volumetric basis and up to 73.4% cheaper per gram biomass produced compared to MRS Broth. This further supports it as a promising candidate for large-scale probiotic manufacturing. This is particularly important in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where the affordability of probiotic products is paramount.
