ANO1 stabilizes partial EMT to drive colorectal cancer metastasis through PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK signaling pathways
Authors: Fei Wu, Kun Zhang, Zhenwu Du, Xin Li, Longfei Yang, Linxian Zhao, Bo Cheng, Lili Nie, Qiwei Yang, Yujie Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) metastasis remains a clinically intractable challenge, where the partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition (partial EMT) state is a key driver of metastatic plasticity and treatment resistance. The calcium-activated chloride channel ANO1 (TMEM16A) is frequently overexpressed in epithelial tumors, yet its mechanism in regulating metastasis and partial EMT remains undefined. In this study, we identify ANO1 as a key stabilizer of the partial EMT state. ANO1 was upregulated in CRC tissues and correlated with poor patient survival. Functionally, ANO1 knockdown robustly suppressed CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and potently induced early apoptosis. Proteomic profiling linked ANO1 depletion to the downregulation of cytoskeletal and adhesion networks. Given the centrality of these processes to EMT, we specifically examined key EMT-associated molecules. Western blot results showed that ANO1 silencing reduced expression of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, vimentin, α-SMA) and EMT transcription factors (Zeb1, Snail, Twist), while also downregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin. Immunofluorescence revealed that ANO1 depletion disrupted a spatially organized hybrid subcellular architecture, and flow cytometry demonstrated that ANO1 knockdown selectively depleted the E-cadherin+/vimentin+ partial EMT subpopulation. Collectively, these data indicated that ANO1 depletion actively dismantles the organized architecture of the partial EMT state rather than driving a canonical phenotypic reversal. Transcriptomic and integrated multi‑omics analyses further revealed that ANO1 loss induced widespread uncoupling between transcriptional and proteomic regulation, with the mTOR and MAPK pathways enriched among “transcript‑only” changes. Consistently, ANO1 silencing reduced phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and ERK without altering total protein levels, and pharmacological inhibition of either pathway phenocopied the dissolution of the partial EMT state. In summary, our work delineates a novel mechanism wherein ANO1, acting as an upstream hub, licenses metastatic progression by co‑activating PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK signaling to stabilize a plastic, survival‑competent partial EMT state, thereby nominating it as a promising therapeutic target for intercepting CRC dissemination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51569-w.Keywords: ANO1, Colorectal cancer, Partial Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition, Metastasis, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, ERK pathwaySubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and ranks third in mortality, with an estimated 900,000 deaths annually1. Distant dissemination and metastasis are responsible for almost 90% of CRC-related deaths2. Despite recent advances in medical therapy for CRC, the overall survival of CRC patients with metastasis is still unsatisfactory3. Therefore, it is urgent to identify novel molecular drivers associated with tumor metastasis and elucidate the underlying mechanisms to develop effective therapeutic strategies.

Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a cellular process during which epithelial cells lose their apical-basal polarity and cell-cell adherence, and acquire mesenchymal characteristics associated with enhanced motility and invasiveness4. The hallmark of EMT is the upregulation of mesenchymal markers such as vimentin and N-cadherin, as well as the downregulation of epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and occludin5. Accumulating evidence shows that EMT plays a crucial role in tumor migration, invasion, and chemotherapy resistance in various malignancies, including CRC6, 7. Importantly, EMT is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous and dynamic process, encompassing not only the traditional “complete EMT”, a full transition between epithelial and mesenchymal states, but also intermediate hybrid states, often referred to as partial EMT8, 9. Defined by the co-expression of epithelial and mesenchymal markers, partial EMT has been identified in multiple tumor types, such as breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), and CRC10, 11, 12. Due to its high plasticity, metastatic potential, and drug resistance, the partial EMT phenotype has attracted significant research interest13, 14. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating the partial EMT state remain largely unexplored.

Anoctamin-1 (ANO1), also known as transmembrane protein 16 A (TMEM16A), is a Ca2+-activated chloride channel expressed in various tissues15, 16. Its gene is located at the chromosome 11q13, a region frequently amplified in human cancers and associated with poor prognosis17. Accordingly, ANO1 is highly expressed and contributes to tumor progression in a variety of cancer types, including HNSCC, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, and breast cancer18, 19. Notably, the biological roles of ANO1 vary considerably across cancer types, with its underlying molecular mechanisms being highly cell-type specific20, 21. For instance, ANO1 inhibition inactivates PI3K/AKT signaling in ovarian cancer22 and reduces phosphorylation of AKT/ERK in HNSCC and breast cancer cells23, 24, but it does not affect these pathways in pancreatic cancer cells25. Our previous study demonstrated that ANO1 promotes CRC cell growth by enhancing cyclin D1 expression and ERK1/2 phosphorylation26. Beyond cell proliferation, the role of ANO1 in EMT and metastasis remains controversial and poorly understood. One study shows that ANO1 overexpression suppresses cancer cell migration and invasion by preventing EMT in HNSCC24 while another demonstrates that ANO1 expression promotes EMT and metastasis in gastric cancer27. This discrepancy highlights the context-dependent nature of ANO1’s function and underscores the need for further investigation. Therefore, understanding the regulatory role of ANO1 in EMT during CRC metastasis is essential for advancing therapeutic strategies.

Our previous work established that ANO1(TMEM16A) mRNA expression was significantly correlated with TNM stage and status of lymph node metastasis, and serves as an independent poor prognostic marker28. In this study, we aimed to investigate the role of ANO1 in CRC metastasis and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. We first examined ANO1 expression in CRC tissues and its association with adverse clinical outcomes. We then performed functional assays to assess the effects of ANO1 knockdown on CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Furthermore, we conducted proteomic and transcriptomic analyses to identify downstream signaling pathways regulated by ANO1. Our findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of ANO1-mediated CRC metastasis and suggest that targeting ANO1 or its downstream effectors may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for combating CRC metastasis.

Results

ANO1 was upregulated in CRC tissues and correlated with poor prognosis

To evaluate the clinical significance of ANO1 in CRC, we first analyzed its expression in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) using the TCGA dataset via the UALCAN web portal. We found that ANO1 expression was significantly higher in primary tumor tissues (n = 286) compared to adjacent normal tissues (n = 41) (Fig. 1A; P < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis via the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database revealed that high ANO1 expression was significantly associated with poor overall survival (OS; n = 1,061, P < 0.001), relapse-free survival (RFS; n = 1336, P < 0.001), and post-progression survival (PPS; n = 331, P < 0.001) in CRC patients (Fig. 1B–D).

To validate these findings in an independent cohort, we examined ANO1 mRNA levels in a local patient cohort (n = 50) using RT-qPCR. Consistent with the TCGA data, ANO1 expression was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues compared to matched adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1E; P = 0.026). Kaplan-Meier analysis within this cohort confirmed that high ANO1 mRNA expression predicted unfavorable overall survival (Fig. 1F; P = 0.040). We next assessed ANO1 protein expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in the same cohort. Positive staining was observed in 12 of 50 cases (24%), a frequency consistent with our previous reports28. In these positive cases, ANO1 protein was detected at weak to moderate levels in epithelial cells, with faint staining in some stromal cells. Representative images from tumors with matched adjacent normal tissue demonstrate this specific staining, which was absent in non-tumor counterparts (Fig. 1G, H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that ANO1 is overexpressed at both mRNA and protein levels in a subset of CRC, and its elevated expression correlates with adverse clinical outcomes.

ANO1 silencing suppressed the proliferation, migration and invasion of CRC cells

To investigate the roles of ANO1 in CRC progression, we evaluated the effect of ANO1 knockdown on the malignant phenotypes of two CRC cell lines, SW620 and HCT116, which were previously confirmed to express endogenous ANO126. These cells were stably transfected with lentiviral particles expressing either ANO1-specific shRNA or a non-targeting scrambled shRNA (control). Knockdown of ANO1 at both the protein and mRNA levels was confirmed by western blot and RT-qPCR, respectively (Fig. 2A, B).

We next assessed the functional consequences of ANO1 depletion. Cell proliferation was quantified using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay over a 4-day time course. The results demonstrated that ANO1 silencing led to a significant suppression of proliferation in both SW620 and HCT116 cells compared to the control groups (Fig. 2C, D). To evaluate cell motility, wound-healing assays were performed. Microscopic analysis revealed that ANO1 knockdown significantly impaired wound closure in both SW620 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 2E, G). To evaluate directed migratory and invasive capacities, we performed Transwell migration (uncoated membrane) and Matrigel invasion assays. ANO1 depletion significantly reduced both migration and invasion in the two cell lines. Quantification indicated that the number of migrated cells decreased by approximately 52% in SW620 and 45% in HCT116 cells relative to their respective controls (p < 0.001). In the Matrigel invasion assay, the number of cells that traversed the matrix was reduced by approximately 46% in SW620 and 35% in HCT116 cells (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2F, H). Collectively, these findings suggest that ANO1 contributes to the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capabilities of CRC cells in vitro.

Proteomic profiling linked ANO1 to cytoskeletal and adhesion networks

To elucidate the mechanisms by which ANO1 promotes metastasis, we performed a label-free quantitative proteomic analysis comparing SW620 cells with stable ANO1 knockdown (sh-ANO1) to control cells (sh-control). Using criteria of fold change ≥ 1.20 or ≤ 0.83 with a P-value < 0.05, we identified 419 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), of which 232 were downregulated and 187 were upregulated upon ANO1 silencing. The distinct proteomic profiles between the two groups were visualized by a volcano plot (Fig. 3A) and unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Fig. 3B).

To functionally characterize these DEPs, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. KEGG analysis revealed that the downregulated DEPs were significant enriched in pathways, including “Gap junction” and “Regulation of actin cytoskeleton” (Fig. 3C). In parallel, GO analysis showed that highly enriched Biological Process and Cellular Component terms were predominantly linked to cytoskeleton organization, cell motility, and adhesion, such as “actin filament bundle” and “regulation of cytoskeleton organization” (Fig. 3D). GSEA further indicated a positive association between ANO1 expression and gene sets regulating cytoskeleton and cell junction assembly, alongside a negative association with gene sets related to cell adhesion mediator activity and cadherin binding (Fig. S1). Domain enrichment analysis of the DEPs showed a significant overrepresentation of structural domains related to cytoskeletal organization (e.g., intermediate filament domains) and calcium signaling/binding (e.g., EF-hand domains, Annexin, S100 protein domains) (Fig. 3E). Subcellular localization analysis revealed that the DEPs were predominantly distributed in the nucleus and cytoplasm, with additional subsets localized to the plasma membrane and mitochondria (Fig. 3F). Taken together, these proteomic analyses link ANO1 depletion to the coordinated downregulation of protein networks that govern cytoskeletal architecture, cell adhesion, and cell motility-processes fundamental to EMT.

Given the possible link to EMT, we specifically examined the expression of established pan-cancer epithelial and mesenchymal markers among the DEPs. This screening identified some key cytoskeletal proteins, including moesin, vimentin, and fascin actin-bundling protein 1, whose expression was reduced following ANO1 knockdown (Table S1). The downregulation of the canonical mesenchymal marker vimentin provided direct proteomic evidence that links ANO1 to the regulation of EMT-related processes. Subsequent protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of these cytoskeleton-related DEPs identified clustered interactions among key cytoskeletal regulators and established EMT markers (Fig. 3G).

In summary, these multi-dimensional proteomic profiles consistently associated ANO1 with cellular processes enabling EMT—such as cytoskeletal remodeling and cell adhesion—yet did not link it to the core transcriptional program of classical EMT. This intriguing dissociation led us to hypothesize that ANO1 may regulate a non-canonical dimension of the EMT process.

ANO1 depletion dissolved the partial EMT state by coordinately downregulating epithelial and mesenchymal markers

To test this hypothesis and define the nature of the EMT state regulated by ANO1, we first analyzed the correlation between ANO1 and key EMT markers using the TCGA database. Specifically, in the TCGA CRC cohort, we found statistically significant, yet moderate, correlations between ANO1 mRNA expression levels and key EMT markers. In detail, ANO1 expression showed a negative correlation with the epithelial marker E-cadherin, and positive correlations with the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, vimentin, α-SMA (ACTA2), as well as transcription factors (TFs) ZEB1 and SNAI2 (SLUG) (Fig. 4A). Although the R² values are weak- attributable to the high biological heterogeneity of clinical tumor samples and the multifactorial regulation of EMT process-the consistent directionality of the regression slopes (negative for E-cadherin, positive for mesenchymal markers and TFs) confirmed an in vivo association between ANO1 and EMT progression.

(C) Western blot analysis of EMT-related markers and transcription factors upon ANO1 knockdown.

To validate these clinical correlations, we modulated ANO1 expression in CRC cell lines (SW620 and HCT116). Morphological assessment indicated that ANO1 depletion (sh-ANO1) was associated with an alteration in cell shape: cells transitioned from an elongated, spindle-like morphology to a more rounded, cobblestone-like appearance (Fig. 4B), a change consistent with a shift toward a less mesenchymal phenotype. Western blot analysis (Fig. 4C, Fig. S2) confirmed that ANO1 silencing selectively altered the expression of core EMT -TFs and their downstream targets. In both SW620 and HCT116 cell lines, the protein levels of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, vimentin, and α-SMA) were significantly reduced upon ANO1 knockdown. Among the EMT-TFs, ZEB1, a key regulator associated with partial EMT, exhibited the most pronounced decrease. In contrast, Snail and Twist showed more modest yet statistically significant reductions. A slight reduction in the epithelial marker E-cadherin was also observed. Notably, this contrasts with the canonical cadherin switch (E-cadherin loss with N-cadherin gain) characteristic of full EMT. This expression profile—concurrent downregulation of mesenchymal markers and EMT‑TFs (ZEB1, Snail, Twist), accompanied by a mild reduction in the epithelial marker E‑cadherin—supports the conclusion that ANO1 depletion disrupts a partial EMT phenotype. Transcriptional analysis of EMT markers E-cadherin revealed considerable variability across replicates (Supplementary Fig. S3), which contrasted with the more consistent protein-level changes. Taken together, these data indicate that ANO1 contributes to the stability of the partial EMT state, in part by sustaining the key EMT‑TFs expression and their downstream mesenchymal program.

To visually assess the impact of ANO1 on the partial EMT state, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) staining for ANO1, E-cadherin, and vimentin. Consistent with western blot data, ANO1 knockdown led to a global reduction in the signal intensity of all three proteins in both cell lines (Fig. 5A–C). More importantly, IF uncovered a distinct, ANO1-dependent subcellular architecture associated with the partial EMT phenotype. In control HCT116 cells—which possess a plastic, metastable partial EMT phenotype—we observed a distinct non-canonical distribution. E-cadherin was present at the membrane, diffusely in the cytoplasm, and prominently within the nucleus. Vimentin formed a cytoplasmic network that extensively infiltrated the perinuclear and nuclear compartments (Fig. 5B, C). ANO1 depletion in HCT116 cells specifically and dramatically destabilized this organized architecture: the prominent nuclear pools of both E-cadherin and vimentin were markedly diminished, and their coordinated distribution was disrupted, often accompanied by cellular debris. ANO1 knockdown in SW620 led to a clear reduction in overall signal intensity, with a less dramatic spatial reorganization than in HCT116 (Fig. 5A–C). Together, these data demonstrate that ANO1 is required to maintain a specific subcellular architecture—characterized by the coordinated nuclear co-localization of E-cadherin and vimentin—that visually defines the partial EMT state in HCT116 cells.

To quantitatively assess EMT heterogeneity at single-cell level, we performed flow cytometry analysis co-staining for E-cadherin and vimentin. In HCT116 cells, which exhibit a spectrum of EMT states, ANO1 knockdown significantly reduced the proportion of the E-cadherin+/vimentin+ hybrid cells (partial EMT; p = 0.0006) and a increase in the purely epithelial (E-cadherin+/vimentin-) population (p = 0.0004) (Fig. 5D). In contrast, ANO1 depletion in SW620 cells, which possess a highly stabilized partial EMT phenotype, also led to a significant, albeit more modest, reduction in the dominant hybrid population (p = 0.002), accompanied by an increase in the double-negative subset (Fig. 5D).

Collectively, the loss of the hybrid EMT identity (evidenced by flow cytometry) and the appearance of cellular debris (observed by immunofluorescence) indicated a severe disruption of cellular state and a loss of viability upon ANO1 depletion. This prompted us to directly assess the occurrence and mode of cell death.

ANO1 knockdown induced a dominant early apoptotic response

To accurately quantify and characterize the mode of cell death, we performed Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) co-staining followed by flow cytometric analysis in both SW620 and HCT116 cell lines. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that ANO1 knockdown induced a substantial shift in cell fate in both cell lines (Fig. 6A, C). Quantification of the flow cytometry data demonstrated a significant and marked reduction in the proportion of viable cells (Annexin V-/PI-) upon ANO1 silencing (Fig. 6B, D). This loss of viability was accompanied by a concomitant and pronounced increase in the fraction of early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI-). In contrast, the percentages of late apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI+) and necrotic (Annexin V-/PI+) cells remained relatively low and were not significantly altered by ANO1 depletion (Fig. 6B, D). Thus, the depletion of ANO1 is primarily associated with the induction of an early apoptotic response, leading to a severe compromise in cellular viability in these models.

Transcriptomic profiling identified ANO1-dependent pro-metastatic networks

To systemically identify the transcriptional networks and potential signaling clues regulated by ANO1, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on sh-ANO1 and sh-control CRC cells. Analysis with criteria of |log2(Fold Change)|≥1.0 and adjusted P < 0.05 identified 1,894 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 371 upregulated and 1,523 downregulated upon ANO1 knockdown (Fig. 7A, B). These included key EMT‑related genes such as PIK3C2A, PIK3CA, PIK3R1, MAPK6, KRAS, ZEB1, TGFBR1 and TGFBR3 (Table S2). GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of biological processes related to genomic integrity maintenance​ and cell division​ (Fig. 7C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified “Homologous recombination”​ and “Platinum drug resistance” as the most significantly enriched pathways (adjusted P = 0.019 for both) (Fig. 7D). A trend‑level downregulation was observed for the “mTOR signaling pathway” (raw P = 0.012, adjusted P = 0.20). Together, these transcriptomic profiles suggested that ANO1 influences broad pro-metastatic networks, with the downregulation of mTOR pathway components representing a potential mechanistic clue.

Integrated proteo-transcriptomics revealed uncoupled regulation of partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition

The transcriptomic data suggested that ANO1 modulates the expression of genes involved in signaling and epithelial-mesenchymal regulation. However, a critical inconsistency emerged when comparing these transcriptional changes with our proteomic and phenotypic data: although transcripts of several epithelial junction genes (such as CDH3, TJP2) were upregulated, the protein abundance of core epithelial marker (E-cadherin) was coordinately downregulated upon ANO1 knockdown. This direct contradiction between mRNA-level induction and protein-level repression pointed to a fundamental uncoupling of transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory layers. To systematically resolve this discrepancy and uncover the underlying multi-layered regulatory logic, we performed an integrated proteo-transcriptomic analysis.

First, we visualized the fold-change (FC) relationships between transcripts and proteins using a scatter plot. A large proportion of genes showed discordant regulation,​ falling into distinct categories such as “protein-only,” “transcript-only,” and “opposite” groups (Fig. 8A), revealing a widespread uncoupling between transcriptional and proteomic changes following ANO1 depletion.

Integrated proteo-transcriptomic analysis of ANO1-regulated networks. (A) Scatter plot showing fold changes in mRNA versus protein levels upon ANO1 knockdown. (B–E) GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for the discordant and concordant change categories29. Significantly enriched terms (adjusted P < 0.05) are shown. The bar length represents the -log10(adjusted P value). (F) Proposed mechanistic model. ANO1 (also known as TMEM16A), a Ca²⁺-activated chloride channel (CaCC), acts as a critical upstream hub that concurrently activates two core oncogenic signaling axes: the PI3K/AKT and ERK pathways. Downstream of PI3K/AKT, mTOR (activated as p-mTOR) functions as a key regulator of cellular proteostasis, while both the PI3K/AKT and ERK axes converge to sustain the expression of the EMT transcription factors (ZEB1, Snail, and Twist). These transcription factors coordinate the transcriptional program of epithelial and mesenchymal genes, and the mTOR-mediated proteostatic environment further supports the post-translational stability of epithelial (E-cadherin) and mesenchymal (N-cadherin, vimentin) proteins. This coordinated signaling output stabilizes a plastic, hybrid E-cadherin⁺/vimentin⁺ (E⁺/V⁺) partial EMT state, which endows colorectal cancer cells with enhanced cell growth, survival, and migratory/invasive capacity. ANO1 knockdown disrupts this entire signaling network, leading to the dissolution of the hybrid partial EMT state and impaired metastatic progression.

Next, we performed Gene Ontology enrichment analyses. Genes in the transcript-only category were predominantly enriched in biological processes related to cell cycle regulation, DNA damage response, and protein phosphorylation and transport (Fig. 8B-D). In contrast, the protein-only category was significantly associated with cytoskeletal organization, cell adhesion, and calcium ion binding (Fig. 8B-D). Thus, the functional profiles of discordantly regulated genes differed substantially between the transcriptional and proteomic layers.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that transcript-only genes were enriched for ‘Cell cycle’, ‘Homologous recombination’, ‘mTOR signaling pathway’, and ‘MAPK signaling pathway’ (Fig. 8E). In contrast, protein-only genes were enriched for metabolic pathways and epithelial differentiation processes (Fig. 8E).

Together, these integrated omics profiles reveal a multi-layered regulatory network influenced by ANO1. The enrichment of the mTOR and MAPK signaling pathways among transcript-only genes—whose expression changes are not reflected at the protein level—suggests that ANO1 may modulate these central signaling axes post-transcriptionally, potentially affecting kinase activity or protein turnover rather than mere abundance. This, combined with the protein-only depletion of cytoskeletal and adhesion molecules, raises the possibility that ANO1 sustains the partial EMT state not only by modulating specific transcriptional programs but also by maintaining a proteostatic landscape potentially influenced by mTOR signaling activity (Fig. 7F).

ANO1 stabilized a partial EMT state via PI3K/AKT and ERK signaling pathways

To test this hypothesis, we examined the activation status of these pathways. Western blot analysis confirmed that ANO1 silencing in SW620 and HCT116 cells significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of key components within these pathways—including PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and ERK1/2—without altering the total protein levels (Fig. 9A, Fig. S4). This indicates that ANO1 acts as an upstream regulator essential for maintaining the activity of both signaling axes in CRC cells.

To directly test whether these pathways functionally mediate ANO1’s role in partial EMT, we treated ANO1-high-expressing HCT116 and SW620 cells with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 and the MEK inhibitor U0126. Pharmacological inhibition of these pathways induced a morphological shift from a mesenchymal, spindle-like shape to a more epithelial, cobblestone-like appearance in both cell lines (Fig. 9B). Crucially, western blot analysis in HCT116 cells demonstrated that inhibitor treatment led to a pronounced decrease in mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, vimentin) alongside only a modest reduction in the epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 9C, Fig. S5). This pattern of concurrent marker reduction precisely phenocopied the effect of ANO1 knockdown itself. These results suggest that in CRC cells, ANO1 stabilizes a plastic partial EMT state through the coordinated activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK signaling pathways.

Discussion

The epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), particularly the metastable partial EMT state, is a critical driver of metastasis in CRC30. However, the key regulators that stabilize this hybrid phenotype remain incompletely characterized. Here, we identified the calcium-activated chloride channel ANO1 as a novel and essential stabilizer of the partial EMT state. We delineated a mechanism wherein ANO1 maintains this phenotype through the integrated activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK signaling pathways, and established its role as a key enabler of the plastic transition that licenses metastatic dissemination.

Consistent with accumulating evidence from independent cohorts and our own serial studies28, 31, 32, 33, the current work reinforces ANO1 as a biomarker associated with unfavorable prognosis in CRC. Analysis of the TCGA-COAD dataset demonstrated its significant upregulation in tumors. More importantly, high ANO1 mRNA expression correlated with poor survival in large, independent cohorts, a finding validated in our local patient samples. The clinical relevance of our finding is further supported by an independent cohort study confirming that ANO1 amplification or high expression independently predicts poor outcomes in gastrointestinal cancer31. An intriguing pattern emerged from integrating ANO1 expression data across different studies. While our prior work established high ANO1 mRNA predicted advanced disease28, another separate clinical analysis from our group found that detectable ANO1 protein was associated with earlier-stage tumor33. This apparent discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels is not merely contradictory but may reflect the dynamic, phase-specific biology of ANO1 during the metastatic cascade. Our integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis provided a potential mechanistic explanation for such discordance at a systems level, revealing widespread uncoupling between transcriptional changes and proteomic outcomes following ANO1 depletion. This suggests that post-transcriptional regulation plays a dominant role in executing the ANO1-dependent phenotype.

We hypothesized that this stage-specific function is rooted in ANO1’s ability to stabilize a plastic, transitional cellular state essential for metastasis initiation. We demonstrated that ANO1 does not drive a terminal EMT but rather anchors a metastable partial EMT (hybrid E/M) state in CRC cells. This is evidenced by the concurrent downregulation of both epithelial (E-cadherin) and mesenchymal (Vimentin, N-cadherin and α-SMA) markers upon ANO1 loss—a hallmark of hybrid state dissolution11, 12, 34, 35 rather than a phenotypic switch. Flow cytometric data provided quantitative validation, showing ANO1 depletion precipitated a departure from the hybrid state towards a more epithelial identity, selectively eroding the intermediate population. Immunofluorescence provided a distinct visual dimension to the ANO1‑stabilized state, revealing an organized subcellular architecture characterized by the coordinated nuclear localization of E‑cadherin and vimentin. While this distribution is non‑canonical, it finds compelling precedent in the literature: nuclear E‑cadherin is a hallmark of metastatic cells, directly correlating with invasive capacity36 and functional entity in colorectal cancer37, and nuclear vimentin, including its SUMOylation‑mediated translocation and pro‑invasive role38, has been explicitly demonstrated in HCT116 cells. Most critically, the specific, ANO1‑dependent dissolution of this nuclear architecture in our study provides robust evidence against non‑specific staining: the signal loss was dynamic, coordinated, and coupled to phenotypic collapse, inconsistent with a static artifact. This positions the nuclear co‑localization as a regulated morphological signature of the plastic, invasive state. We propose that ANO1 sustains a cellular environment that promotes the post‑translational modifications or trafficking that enable this compartmentalization, which contributes to the hybrid state’s plasticity. Its collapse upon ANO1 loss underscores ANO1’s role as a master regulator of the cellular geometry that licenses dissemination. The context‑dependent effect of ANO1 depletion—profound in plastic HCT116 cells but muted in mesenchymal‑stabilized SW620 cells—further corroborates that its primary role is in stabilizing plasticity rather than maintaining a committed state. We therefore propose a model in which ANO1 functions as a stabilizer of a metastable partial EMT state, thereby enabling the plasticity required for early dissemination. This model finds direct in vivo validation in our clinical‑pathological analysis, which showed that ANO1 protein was detected exclusively in E‑cadherin‑positive tumors33. This indicates that its pro‑metastatic function operates in a cellular context that retains epithelial traits—the precise hallmark of the partial EMT state defined in vitro.

Mechanistically, we established ANO1 as a critical upstream node that concurrently fine-tunes the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK signaling axes to stabilize a metastable partial EMT state in CRC. Although the mTOR pathway appeared as a trend-level signal in our initial transcriptomic enrichment (raw P = 0.012, adjusted P ≈ 0.20), we prioritized it for validation because it is a master regulator of cell growth, survival, and proteostasis39,40 —processes critical for maintaining the dynamic protein landscape of a partial EMT state. Importantly, the MAPK signaling pathway, another key regulator of EMT and cell proliferation41,42, showed a similar enrichment pattern in our integrated omics analysis, with its associated genes also classified in the “transcript-only” category (Fig. 8E). This shared transcriptomic signature strongly suggests that ANO1 modulates both mTOR and MAPK pathways through a post-transcriptional mode of regulation, which is consistent with its known function as a Ca²⁺-activated ion channel (rather than a transcriptional regulator). Subsequent functional validation confirmed that ANO1 knockdown indeed suppressed the phosphorylation of key components across both the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK axes, including p-PI3K, p-AKT, p-mTOR, and p-ERK. Crucially, pharmacological inhibition of either the PI3K/AKT or ERK pathway phenocopied the loss of the partial EMT phenotype, proving their functional necessity downstream of ANO1.

The quantitative dissection of signaling changes revealed a tiered pattern: In HCT116 cells (KRAS/PIK3CA mutant), ANO1 depletion strongly inhibited upstream kinases p‑PI3K, p‑AKT, and p‑ERK, while exerting a more modest effect on the downstream integrator p‑mTOR. This aligned with ANO1 predominantly fine-tuning membrane-proximal kinases20, while mTOR, as a downstream integrator, is buffered by parallel inputs40. In SW620 cells (KRAS mutation), the overall dampening of pathway phosphorylation was less pronounced. This likely reflects a simpler, more RAS‑centric signaling topology that is less dependent on ANO1‑mediated tuning compared to HCT116 cells, where concurrent KRAS and PIK3CA mutations may create a more complex, interconnected signaling network with greater susceptibility to upstream perturbation. This context‑specific modulation underscores that ANO1 exerts precise, network‑level control. To explain why even a modest reduction in p‑mTOR triggers a dramatic phenotypic collapse, we propose a threshold model of signaling flux (a concept well-established in systems biology models of kinase networks)43,44. The strong co‑inhibition of the primary driver kinases (PI3K/AKT, ERK) upon ANO1 loss lowers the integrated signaling input below a critical threshold required to maintain the partial EMT state. In this model, mTOR acts as a downstream effector; when its upstream activators are severely compromised, even residual mTOR activity becomes insufficient to sustain the high protein turnover and proteostatic landscape. Consequently, the system undergoes a catastrophic dissolution, not because mTOR is completely blocked, but because the integrated signal is too weak to maintain the metastable state. It is this precise tuning that enables ANO1 to differentially, yet potently, impact the malignant phenotypes and viability of CRC cells depending on their intrinsic signaling state.

Within the ANO1‑regulated network, we delineated a two‑tiered, signaling architecture that sustains the partial EMT state. At the core of this network, ANO1 acts as an upstream hub that concurrently activates PI3K/AKT and ERK signaling. This activation serves a dual purpose: (i) it sustains the EMT transcriptional program, primarily through the EMT‑transcription factor ZEB1 along with Snail and Twist, thereby governing cellular identity; and (ii) it engages the mTOR axis to maintain a proteostatic landscape, which supports the high protein turnover essential for the hybrid phenotype. The functional specialization of this network is evidenced by the widespread transcript‑protein discordance we observed. Specifically, despite variable mRNA changes—ranging from the paradoxical upregulation of certain epithelial junction transcripts (e.g., CDH3, TJP2) to the high variability of CDH1 (E‑cadherin) mRNA—the effector proteins defining both epithelial and mesenchymal identities were coordinately depleted. This consistent protein‑level depletion, despite disparate mRNA patterns, underscores the dominance of post‑transcriptional, proteostatic control45,46. Crucially, this core network is transformed into a self‑reinforcing circuit​ through ANO1‑dependent modulation of cadherins (E‑cadherin, N‑cadherin), which are themselves established activators of PI3K/AKT signaling. This creates a positive feedback loop that amplifies and locks in the initial ANO1 signal, cementing cells in a metastable, signaling‑competent state. This ANO1‑driven circuit is further integrated with the canonical EMT transcriptional network governed by GSK3β, β‑catenin, and Snail. ANO1‑mediated PI3K/AKT activation inhibits GSK3β, which in turn stabilizes Snail and increases transcriptionally active β‑catenin. Concurrently, ANO1‑dependent perturbation of cadherin localization at adherens junctions further promotes β‑catenin release. This crosstalk fine‑tunes the overall EMT transcriptional output, reinforcing the plastic hybrid state.

ANO1 orchestrates a pro-metastatic signaling network that governs both cellular plasticity and survival. This network-level control explains its pleiotropic roles: beyond stabilizing the partial EMT state, ANO1-driven signaling coordinately sustains proliferation and suppresses apoptosis42,47,48. The phenotypic collapse upon ANO1 depletion—where EMT dissolution is coupled with a dominant early apoptotic response—suggested that cellular identity and viability are co-regulated through this central hub. We propose that the loss of ANO1 lowers the integrated signaling flux below a critical threshold required to maintain the high protein turnover and survival capacity of the partial EMT state, leading to its catastrophic dissolution. The oncogenic role of ANO1 extends beyond cell-autonomous signaling. Its detection in the tumor stroma in our study, consistent with reports of ANO1 in cancer-associated fibroblasts promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment via PI3K-AKT31, indicates a “two-pronged” attack strategy: driving intrinsic plasticity while remodeling the TME to favor dissemination. Furthermore, emerging evidence of ANO1-mediated cholesterol metabolic reprogramming adds another layer to its multifaceted role in metastatic fitness49. This multi-faceted involvement positions ANO1 as a compelling candidate for interceptive therapy​ in CRC. Pharmacological inhibition of ANO1 offers a strategy to simultaneously target tumor growth, metastatic plasticity, and the immunosuppressive TME50. Our previous identification of Dehydroandrographolide​ as a novel ANO1 inhibitor, with demonstrated efficacy in SW620 cells, provides direct preclinical evidence for the feasibility of ANO1-targeted therapy51. Notably, evidence that ANO1 blockade can ameliorate radiation-induced intestinal injury52 suggests that such inhibitors may also mitigate treatment-related toxicities, further enhancing their clinical translational potential. Thus, ANO1 represents a promising therapeutic target whose inhibition could simultaneously disrupt multiple key processes in CRC progression.

This study has several limitations. First, the reliance on in vitro models and a univariate analysis of a single-center cohort (n = 50) warrants validation in in vivo metastasis models and larger, multi-center cohorts to establish ANO1’s clinical prognostic utility. Second, while immunofluorescence revealed ANO1-dependent nuclear co-localization of E-cadherin and vimentin, biochemical subcellular fractionation is needed to quantify these nuclear pools and characterize their post-translational modifications. Finally, the precise link between ANO1’s ion channel function and downstream signaling initiation remains to be fully elucidated; future work should employ spatiotemporally controlled tools in in vivo models to dissect this mechanism and ANO1’s stage-specific roles in CRC progression.

Conclusions

In summary, our study establishes that ANO1 is a key driver of colorectal cancer metastasis by stabilizing a partial EMT state and conferring apoptosis resistance. Mechanistically, ANO1 acts as an upstream signaling hub that concurrently activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK pathways to maintain this plastic and survival-competent phenotype. These findings elucidate a novel ANO1–partial EMT–survival axis in CRC progression, highlighting ANO1 as a compelling therapeutic target for impeding metastasis by co-targeting tumor cell plasticity and survival pathways.

Materials and methods

Database and data analysis

The mRNA expression level of ANO1 in human colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and adjacent normal tissues was analyzed using the UALCAN web portal(https://ualcan.path.uab.edu, accessed December 20, 2025), based on TCGA online available RNAseq data48. The Kaplan-Meier analysis of overall survival (OS), relapse free survival (RFS) and post progression survival (PPS) was obtained from the Kaplan–Meier Plotter public dataset (http://kmplot.com, accessed on December 20, 2025). The median level of ANO1 (1555269_a_at) was utilized to categorize patients with CRC into high and low ANO1 groups. The two patient cohorts are compared by a Kaplan–Meier survival plot, and the hazard ratio with 95% confidence intervals and log-rank p value are calculated.

Clinical samples

Human colorectal cancer tissues (n = 50) and adjacent normal tissues (n = 20) were collected from the Second Hospital of Jilin University (Changchun, China). Inclusion criteria were: (i) Individuals who met the diagnostic criteria for primary CRC; (ii) underwent primary CRC resection surgery; (iii) no adjuvant therapy was received before surgery; (iv) complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria were: (i) Individuals with consciousness disorders; (ii) Individuals with autoimmune diseases; (iii) those who have already received chemotherapy or radiation therapy. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Jilin University (Approval No. 2017-049) on March 2, 2017. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection. To authorize the long-term follow-up data analysis and manuscript publication, an updated ethical approval was obtained from the same committee on September 16, 2025 (approval no. 2025 − 327]) for the inclusion of both retrospectively collected data and prospectively collected samples. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC staining was performed using an SP-IHC kit (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the standard protocol. The CRC tissues were paraffin-embedded and the tissue Sect. (2 μm) were dewaxed, rehydrated, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and subjected to antigen retrieval. After being washed, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against ANO1 (cat. no. ZM-0371; Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) overnight at 4˚C, followed by treatment with secondary antibody. Next, all sections were being stained with 3,3-diaminobenzidine and finally counterstained with haematoxylin for 1 min.

The IHC evaluation were performed as previously described28.

Cell culture

Human CRC cell lines SW620 and HCT116 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The SW620 cells were maintained in L15 medium (Epizyme, Inc.) at 37˚C with 100% air. The HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5a Medium Modified (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at 37˚C with 5% CO2. Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Omnimabs).

Lentivirus transfection

Stable ANO1-knockdown SW620 and HCT116 cell lines were established by lentiviral transduction. The lentiviral particles encoding a specific ANO1-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) and a non-targeting scrambled shRNA (as a negative control) were obtained from Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. The target sequence for ANO1-shRNA was 5’-CGAAGAAGATGTACCACAT-3’ (corresponding to nucleotides 837–855 of the ANO1 transcript). For transduction, tumor cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well in 500 µL of complete medium and cultured overnight to achieve 30–50% confluence. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing the lentiviral particles at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. The cells were incubated with the virus-polybrene mixture for 24 h. After incubation, the viral supernatant was removed and replaced with fresh complete medium. To select for stably transduced cells, puromycin (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) was added to the culture medium at a concentration of 2 µg/mL 48 h post-transduction. The selection pressure was maintained for two weeks, with the puromycin-containing medium being refreshed every 2–3 days. Successful transduction and selection were initially confirmed by observing fluorescence expression under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corp.). The knockdown efficiency of ANO1 was subsequently validated at both the mRNA and protein levels using reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and western blot analysis, respectively. The resulting stable cell lines were then used for subsequent functional experiments.

Mycoplasma detection

All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based method. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from cell culture supernatants or cell pellets. PCR amplification was performed using commercial mycoplasma detection primers targeting the 16 S rRNA gene. The PCR products were analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Only mycoplasma-negative cells (as confirmed by the absence of amplification bands) were used for experiments. The positive control (mycoplasma DNA) showed the expected band size.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) from all tissues and cells, and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using an mRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (GeneCopeia, Inc.) for PCR amplification. RT-qPCR reaction was performed as previously described. The relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the endogenous control. The following primers were used: ANO1 forward, 5′-GATCCCATCCAGCCCAAAGTG-3′ and reverse, 5′-CGGGTTTTGCTGTCGAAAAAGGA-3′; GAPDH forward, 5′-CGGACCAATACGACCAAATCCG-3′ and reverse, 5′-AGCCACATCGCTCAGACACC-3′.

Western blot

Total cellular protein was extracted from colorectal cancer cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. After quantification with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology), equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg per lane) were separated by 8%-12% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-ANO1 (1:500, ab53212, Abcam), anti-E-cadherin (1:1,000, AF0131, Affinity), anti-vimentin (1:1,000, AF7013, Affinity), anti-α-SMA (1:5,000, ab124964, Abcam), anti‑P‑PI3K (Tyr607; 1:1,000, AF3241, Affinity), anti‑PI3K (1:1,000, 20584‑1‑AP, Proteintech), anti‑P‑AKT (Ser473; 1:1,000, AF0016, Affinity), anti‑AKT (1:2,000, 4691T, CST), anti‑P‑mTOR (Ser2448; 1:1,000, ab109268, Abcam), anti‑mTOR (1:500, GB11405, Servicebio), anti‑P‑Erk (Thr202/Tyr204; 1:1,000, AF1015, Affinity), anti‑Erk (1:1,000, GB12087, Servicebio), and anti‑β‑actin (1:2,000, 66009‑1‑Ig, Proteintech). All phospho‑specific antibodies are validated for use in human and mouse samples by the manufacturers. Following three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‑conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000, Sigma‑Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Millipore) and imaged with an Azure Imager 600 (Azure Biosystems). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, v1.53c, https://imagej.net/ij/), normalized to the loading control (β‑actin). The membranes were trimmed before antibody incubation and the presented images show the actual processed blots with membrane margins visible.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were seeded in a 24-well and cultured to a confluence of 50–60%, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. To permeabilize the cells, they were treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. Non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubating the cells with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against ANO1 (1:100; cat. no. ab53212; Abcam), E-cadherin (1:100; cat. no. AF0131; Affinity) and Vimentin (1:100; cat. no. AF7013; Affinity) for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified chamber. Following primary antibody incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with a Cy3-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:1000; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. To visualise the nuclei, cells were stained with 40,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma Aldrich; Merck KGaA). Fluorescent images were captured and photographed under a fluorescent microscope (Olympus Corp).

CCK-8 assay

Change in cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay. In brief, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells/well in 100 µl of the complete medium and maintained for 24 h at 37˚C. At the specified time, 10 µl CCK-8 reagent (cat. no. GK10001; GlpBio Technology, Inc.) was added into each well and the cells were further cultured for 4 h at 37˚C. Then the optical density value was measured using a multi-scan microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at 450 nm.

Wound healing assay

Cells (5 × 105) were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated with 2 ml culture medium containing 10% FBS. When the cells reached 95–100% confluency, the cells were scratched vertically using a sterile 200-µl pipette tip and gently washed twice with sterile PBS. After incubation with 2 ml serum-free medium, the cells were photographed at 0, 24 and 48 h under an inverted microscope (Olympus Corp) in three random fields. Cell motility was quantified by measuring the distance between the advancing margins of cells in three randomly selected microscopic fields at each time point.

Migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chambers (6.5-mm diameter, 8-µm pores; Corning Costar; Corning, Inc.). For cell invasion, the upper surface of the Transwell chamber was coated with Matrigel matrix (BD Biosciences). 1 × 105 cells in 200 µl of medium with 1% FBS were seeded in the upper chamber, the culture medium (600 µl) with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), the penetrated cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet and counted under an inverted microscope in five random fields. Migration rate and invasion rate were calculated as the percentage of migrated/invaded cells in the sh-ANO1 group relative to the sh-control group.

Label-free quantitative proteomics

Proteomics analysis was performed as follows: (i) Protein extraction for mass spectroscopy (MS) analysis: cells were harvested and lysed with SDT buffer. After centrifugation, the supernatant was quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology); (ii) SDS-PAGE separation: The proteins were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gel. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Blue R-250 staining; (iii) Filter-Aided Sample Preparation enzymatic hydrolysis: Protein samples (100 µg per sample) were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin using the FASP method; (iv) MS analysis: LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) coupled with Easy nLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Peptides were separated on a C18 column with a 300 nL/min gradient. MS data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode, with MS1 scans at 120,000 resolution and MS2 scans at 15,000 resolution; (v) Data processing: The raw MS data were processed using MaxQuant software (v1.6.17.0, https://www.maxquant.org/) against the UniProt human database for protein identification and label-free quantification.

Library preparation and sequencing of the transcriptome

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TriZol (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) following the instructions in the manual. A total of 3 µg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. and index codes were added to the attribute sequences of each sample. The library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform and paired-end reads of 125–150 bp were generated (NovoGene)53. Bioinformatic analysis: HISAT2 (v2.0.5, http://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/) was used for alignment analysis of the sequencing data. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.50.0, https://bioconductor.org/packages//release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html), applying a threshold of |log2(Fold Change)| ≥1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.0.0, https://bioconductor.org/packages//release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html). Gene sets and Hallmark collections were acquired from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), which were analyzed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software (v4.4.0, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).

Flow cytometry analysis of E-cadherin and vimentin co-expression

HCT116 and SW620 cells were harvested with 0.025% trypsin-EDTA and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to obtain a single-cell suspension at a density of approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. The cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for an additional 15 min. After permeabilization, the cells were incubated with a mixture of fluorescently conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The antibody cocktail included PE-conjugated anti-E-cadherin (cat. no. AC0743; Beyotime) and FITC-conjugated anti-Vimentin (cat. no. 11-9897-80; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Corresponding isotype controls were used in parallel to define background staining and set the gating strategy. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove unbound antibodies and finally resuspended in 500 µL of PBS for immediate acquisition. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and a minimum of 10,000 events per sample were recorded. Data analysis was conducted using CellQuest Pro software (BD Biosciences). Quadrant gates were set based on isotype controls to distinguish four cell subpopulations: E-cadherin⁺/vimentin⁻ (C1), E-cadherin⁺/vimentin⁺ (C2), E-cadherin⁻/vimentin⁻ (C3), and E-cadherin⁻/vimentin⁺ (C4). For each cell line (SW620 and HCT116), the percentage of cells in each quadrant was calculated, and the mean ± standard deviation (SD) was obtained from at least three biological replicates. Statistical differences between the control group and ANO1 knockdown group were determined using unpaired t-tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Apoptosis assay by Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide (PI) Staining

Cell preparation and staining. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beijing Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd, Cat. No. CA1020) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell apoptosis was quantified using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beijing Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd, Cat. No. CA1020) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, both floating and adherent cells from stable SW620 and HCT116 sh-Control or sh-ANO1 cell lines were pooled and harvested. Cells were washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in 1 × Annexin V Binding Buffer at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of the cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI solution in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Following incubation, 400 µL of 1 × Annexin V Binding Buffer was added to each sample, and the cells were kept on ice and protected from light until analysis. Stained cells were immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer). For each sample, a minimum of 10,000 events were acquired. Cell populations were categorized as follows: viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and necrotic (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺). Data were analyzed using CellQuest Pro software (BD Biosciences, v5.1, https://www.bdbiosciences.com/zh-cn/products/software) and are presented as the percentage of cells in each quadrant.

Pharmacological inhibition experiment

The PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (cat. no. M1925, AbMole BioScience) and the MEK/ERK inhibitor U0126 (cat. no. M1977, AbMole BioScience) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare 10 mM stock solutions. SW620 and HCT116 were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere until they reached approximately 70% confluence. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing the inhibitors at their respective working concentrations: 20 µM LY294002 or 10 µM U0126. Control groups received culture medium containing an equivalent volume of DMSO (final concentration < 0.1%). After 2 h of continuous treatment, cells were harvested for protein extraction to assess the phosphorylation levels of AKT and ERK by Western blot analysis. Following 48 h of treatment, cells were photographed for morphological observation and then collected for the detection of EMT markers (E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin) via Western blot.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Continuous data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., v28.0, https://www.ibm.com/cn-zh/products/spss), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (Dotmatics, v10.0, https://www.graphpad.com/features). For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for post hoc pairwise comparisons if the ANOVA result was significant. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
