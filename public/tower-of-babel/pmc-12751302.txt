Assessing the suitability of ChatGPT in responding to public inquiries about dental crown restorations
Authors: Marwa M. Alnsour, Wisam Al-Ghannam, Islam Abd Alraheam, Alaa H.A Sabrah, Yara Oweis, Mahmoud K. AL-Omiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aimed to assess the effectiveness of ChatGPT as an informational tool for patients considering dental crown restorations.MethodsCommon patient inquiries about dental crown restorations were identified through online tools: Also Asked, Answer the Public, and Google’s People Also Ask. The questions were categorized into three groups: Group 1 – functionality and appearance, Group 2 – material differences and cost, and Group 3 – longevity, shortcomings, and maintenance. ChatGPT 3.5 was used to generate responses to these questions. Responses were evaluated for usefulness; readability (FKG and SMOG indices); quality (Global Quality Scale); reliability (CLEAR tool); and understandability and actionability (PEMAT). Statistical analyses included ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, and Spearman correlation tests, performed using SPSS v20.ResultsA total of 126 patient questions about dental crown restorations were analyzed. Most ChatGPT responses were rated as ‘very useful’ (77%) or ‘useful’ (20.6%), with responses on longevity, shortcomings, and maintenance receiving the highest usefulness ratings. Readability analyses indicated that approximately 53% of responses were difficult to read overall, though responses on longevity and maintenance were easier to read. Quality and reliability were high across all responses, with mean GQS and CLEAR scores of 4.70/5 and 24.37/25, respectively. Understandability and actionability (PEMAT) scores varied, with responses on longevity and maintenance performing best.ConclusionOur results suggest that ChatGPT may serve as a supplementary tool in addressing patient inquiries about dental crown restorations. However, its responses require improvement in readability, actionability, and technical specificity. These findings indicate that while AI tools like ChatGPT can complement patient education, they cannot replace professional dental consultation and guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07281-8.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and digital information systems has transformed how individuals seek medical and dental information [1, 2]. Many patients rely on search engines like Google and online encyclopedias like Wikipedia for guidance on diagnosis and treatment. However, these sources provide extensive information that requires users to sift through multiple references, interpret complex data, and verify credibility, which can be overwhelming and sometimes inconsistent [3–5].

In contrast, AI-driven conversational agents, such as ChatGPT, offer a more interactive and structured approach by delivering direct answers to specific patient questions in real time. Instead of presenting multiple sources, ChatGPT synthesizes information into a concise response, potentially reducing the need for in-person consultations for basic inquiries [6, 7]. While AI systems improve accessibility, concerns remain regarding the accuracy and reliability of their responses, particularly in medical and dental contexts [8–11].

A growing body of literature has explored ChatGPT’s performance in various healthcare contexts. For example, studies have assessed its capabilities in endocrinology, dermatology, oncology, ophthalmology, and general dentistry, with results ranging from useful and accurate to inconsistent and potentially misleading [9, 12–15]. In dentistry, similar findings have been reported, with Hassona et al. (2024) evaluating ChatGPT’s responses on early detection of oral cancer, Alnsour et al. (2025) assessing questions on the effects of smoking on oral health, and Elkarmi et al. (2025) evaluating ChatGPT’s responses to parents’ questions on early childhood caries [16–18]. All three studies found that ChatGPT provided generally useful and reliable information, but the actionability of responses were often limited, suggesting that patients may not always receive clear guidance on next steps. Other research has also highlighted challenges in producing consistently reliable AI-generated answers, with variability depending on the topic or context [19–22]A fraction of responses may be inaccurate or incomplete, particularly regarding clinical decision-making and treatment planning [6, 8].These findings underscore the importance of critically evaluating the trustworthiness and practical applicability of AI-generated information in specific health contexts, particularly in dentistry, where professional guidance is essential.

One common area where patients seek online guidance is dental crown restorations used to restore function, improve aesthetics, and protect teeth that have been damaged by decay, fractures, or following root canal treatment [23]. Patients often seek information on crown materials, procedures, costs, longevity, and potential complications. With a variety of materials available, such as porcelain, zirconia, metal, and composite, and different designs including full and partial coverage, choosing the right crown can be challenging [24, 25]. While crowns provide durable and versatile restorations that reinforce structurally compromised teeth, their placement requires irreversible tooth reduction, carries potential risks to the pulp and periodontal tissues, and is generally more costly than direct restorations [24–27]. AI tools like ChatGPT can assist by providing structured insights on crown options, their benefits, and limitations, complementing professional consultations. When addressing patient inquiries, ChatGPT should provide balanced answers while also being accurate and reliable. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have assessed ChatGPT’s responses on this topic. This study aimed to evaluate ChatGPT’s effectiveness in answering questions about dental crown restorations by assessing the usefulness, quality, reliability, readability, understandability, and actionability of its responses.

Methods

Questions selection and ChatGPT query process

To determine the most common questions patients ask about dental crowns, we conducted an online search using three widely used tools: Also Asked [28], Answer the Public [29], and Google’s People Also Ask feature. These tools identify frequently searched questions related to specific topics or keywords (Fig. 1). For our search, we used keywords including dental crown, porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) crown, zirconia crown, overlay crown, Emax crown, crowned tooth, artificial crown, natural crown, false tooth, ceramic crown, and crown preparation (Fig. 1A_C). The questions generated by these tools were compiled into an Excel sheet. Two authors independently reviewed the list to remove duplicates and questions with identical meanings. The remaining questions were checked for grammatical clarity and corrected as needed. Finally, the questions were categorized into three main groups : Group 1 – functionality and appearance, Group 2 – material differences and cost, and Group 3 – longevity, shortcomings,and maintenance. A new ChatGPT-3.5 account was created using a separate email address in incognito mode with minimal personal information to minimize potential bias from prior search history. Each question was entered separately using the “New Chat” function to prevent retention bias, and the “Regenerate Response” option was disabled. All responses were generated under default settings using ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT-3.5, November 2024 version) and saved in a Word file dated November 15, 2024. Two authors independently verified the factual accuracy and clinical appropriateness of the outputs before evaluation.

Evaluation criteria

To assess the AI responses, several established scales were used, some adapted from prior studies [16–18]. Usefulness was measured using a modified 4-point scale: 1 = Very useful (provides comprehensive and correct information, similar to an answer a specialist would give), 2 = Useful (provides accurate information but omits some important details), 3 = Partially useful (provides a mix of correct and incorrect information), and 4 = Not useful (provides incorrect or misleading information). Readability was assessed using the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKG), which estimates the school grade level needed for general comprehension, and the Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) index, which estimates the grade level required for full comprehension of the text [30] (Supplementary Table 1). Quality was assessed using the Global Quality Scale (GQS) [31], which rates responses based on overall quality, flow, and usefulness to patients. Reliability was evaluated using the CLEAR tool, which scores responses based on criteria such as completeness, evidence support, and relevance [32]. Finally, understandability and actionability were assessed using the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT), which measures whether the information is easy to understand and actionable for individuals with varying levels of health literacy [33]. Reliability and inter-rater agreement

Two authors independently evaluated all responses using the scoring systems mentioned above, and inter-rater agreement was measured using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.723, p <.01). A third, blinded reviewer resolved any discrepancies. Before formal assessment, all authors underwent calibration by reviewing the scoring systems and conducting pilot evaluations on ten responses.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed data normality. Non-normally distributed continuous variables and scales—including usefulness (4-point scale), GQS, CLEAR, and PEMAT understandability and actionability scores—were summarized using means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and minimum and maximum values. Normally distributed variables (FKG and SMOG scores) were reported with means, standard deviations, standard errors, minimum and maximum values, and 95% confidence intervals.

A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied to all analyses, which were performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics v20.0, IBM Corp., USA). Since they were normally distributed variables, differences in FKG and SMOG scores between question groups were assessed using ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc test for pairwise comparisons to correct for multiple comparisons. For non-normally distributed variables (GQS, CLEAR, understandability, and actionability scores), the Kruskal-Wallis test evaluated group differences, with Mann-Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons when applicable. Effect size was calculated for ANOVA and Kruskal Wallis tests (η²), Bonferroni post hoc test (Cohen’s d), and Mann Whitney U test (r). Spearman’s Rho correlation examined associations involving non-normally distributed variables (GQS, CLEAR, understandability, and actionability scores), while Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed variables (FKG and SMOG scores).

Readability of ChatGPT responses

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKG) index for all questions ranged from 5.3 to 15.7, with a mean ± SD of 10.39 ± 1.91 (Table 1). Approximately 53% of the responses were classified as difficult to read (FKG > 10). The SMOG index readability scores ranged from 7.2 to 18.2, with a mean ± SD of 12.75 ± 1.93 (Table 1). Most responses were categorized as either moderately difficult (42.1%) or challenging (49.2%) based on the SMOG index readability scores (Table 1).

Comparisons among the three groups of questions revealed significant differences in both FKG (F [2] = 7.817, p =.001, effect size η² = 0.113) and SMOG scores (F [2] = 14.652, p <.001, η² = 0.192). Post hoc analyses showed that the longevity, shortcomings, and maintenance group (Group 3) had lower FKG scores (indicating easier-to-read responses) compared to the function and appearance group (Group 1; mean difference = −1.33, 95% CI= −2.31 – −0.36, p =.004, effect size d = 0.76) and the materials, design, and cost group (Group 2; mean difference = −1.25, 95% CI= −2.17 – −0.33, p =.004, d = 0.67). Similarly, Group 3 had lower SMOG scores (i.e. easier-to-read responses) compared to Group 1 (mean difference = −1.82, 95% CI= −2.77 – −0.88, p <.001, effect size d = 1.00) and Group 2 (mean difference = −1.58, 95% CI= −2.47 – −0.70, p <.001, d = 0.87).

Quality and reliability of ChatGPT responses

The mean GQS score of responses to all questions ranged between 1 and 5 (mean ± SD = 4.70 ± 0.72, Table 1). Most responses were of excellent quality (79.4%, n = 100, Table 1). The CLEAR reliability scores ranged from 20 to 25 (mean ± SD = 24.37 ± 1.35, Table 1). All responses were considered of very good content in this regard (100%, n = 126, Table 1).

Both GQS and CLEAR scores were significantly different across the question categories (p =.025 and p =.002, respectively, Table 2). The longevity, shortcomings, and maintenance group (Group 3) demonstrated higher GQS (p =.025, effect size r =.0580) and CLEAR scores (p =.001, r =.1386) compared to the function and appearance group (Group 1); indicating that the responses were of better quality and better content in Group 3 (Table 2). Similarly, Group 3 demonstrated higher GQS (p =.010, effect size r =.0708) and CLEAR scores (p =.013, r =.0662) compared to the materials, design, and cost group (Group 2); indicating that the responses were of better quality and better content in Group 3 (Table 2).

Understandability and actionability of ChatGPT responses

The understandability PEMAT scores ranged from 22 to 85 (mean ± SD = 72.28 ± 8.06, Table 1). The scores were above 70% for nearly 52% of the responses. The actionability PEMAT scores ranged from 0 to 83 (mean ± SD = 35.06 ± 31.36, Table 1). The scores were above 70% for nearly 18% of the responses.

Significant differences were observed in both understandability and actionability scores across the question categories (p =.007 and p =.001, respectively, Table 2). The material, design, and cost group (Group 2) had lower understandability (p =.014, effect size r=.0850) and actionability scores (p <.001, r =.2220) compared to the function and appearance group (Group 1); indicating that the responses were less understandable and less actionable in Group 2 (Table 2). Similarly, Group 2 had lower understandability (p =.003, effect size r =.0940) and actionability scores (p =.004, r =.0888) compared to the longevity, shortcomings, and maintenance group (Group 3); indicating that the responses were less understandable and less actionable in Group 2 (Table 2).

Correlations between indicators of ChatGPT responses

Analysis of correlations between ChatGPT performance indicators revealed several notable patterns. Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKG) scores were strongly positively correlated with SMOG scores (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.903, p <.001), suggesting that these two measures were largely consistent in assessing readability across responses. Global Quality Scale (GQS) scores showed a moderate positive correlation with CLEAR reliability scores (Spearman correlation coefficient (Rho) = 0.554, p <.001), indicating that responses rated higher in overall quality were generally perceived as more reliable. Similarly, understandability PEMAT scores were positively correlated with actionability PEMAT scores (Rho = 0.643, p <.001), suggesting that responses that were easier to understand were often also considered more actionable. These results point to potential relationships between readability, quality, reliability, understandability and actionability in ChatGPT responses; however, these correlations do not imply causation.

Other correlations were also observed, including small positive correlations between FKG or SMOG scores and usefulness (Rho = 0.246–0.283) and small negative correlations with GQS or CLEAR reliability (Rho = −0.199 to −0.254). These correlations were relatively weak, and their practical implications are uncertain.

Discussion

This study assessed the effectiveness of ChatGPT in responding to public inquiries about dental crown restorations, focusing on aspects such as usefulness, quality, reliability, readability, understandability and actionability. The results show that ChatGPT delivered mostly correct answers, with a small minority rated as “partially useful” (1.6%) or “not useful” (0.8%), indicating generally positive feedback.

However, the usefulness of ChatGPT’s responses varied across question categories, with significant differences observed between the three groups. Group 3, which focused on long-term outcomes such as crown longevity, potential complications, and maintenance requirements, received the lowest usefulness scores (most useful). In contrast, Groups 1 (function and appearance) and 2 (materials, design, and cost) received higher scores (lower usefulness). This pattern might reflect ChatGPT’s ability to provide clear, general guidance on long-term care and maintenance, which patients can apply broadly. In contrast, questions about crown materials and design require individualized decisions influenced by many factors, including occlusion, available space, age, oral hygiene, parafunctional habits, patient preferences, and financial considerations, making generalized AI responses less useful [19, 34, 35].

Regarding quality and reliability, ChatGPT’s responses scored highly on both the GQS (mean score of 4.70) and CLEAR (mean score of 24.37) scales, with 79.4% rated as excellent (5/5). These high scores show that most responses were clear, accurate, well-organized, and reliable. These findings are consistent with other studies (16,17,18) that have shown ChatGPT provides useful answers with good quality and reliability. However, some studies have highlighted challenges in producing consistently reliable answers, suggesting that this variability may depend on the era, topic, or context in which the questions are asked [19–22].

The readability indices revealed significant differences across question categories. The overall mean FKG score of 10.39 suggests that, on average, responses were accessible to a broad audience, though 53% were still classified as difficult to read. The SMOG index showed similar patterns, with most responses being moderately difficult or challenging (mean = 12.75). Responses regarding materials, design, and cost (Group 2) were the most difficult to read, likely due to the use of technical terminology and contextual considerations, which increased the complexity of the generated answers. Similarly, other studies have shown that while ChatGPT has potential for addressing public concerns, issues remain regarding the readability of its responses [16–18, 36]. Therefore, while ChatGPT’s responses may appear generally accessible, the readability data indicate that its accessibility is limited, particularly for technical topics, highlighting a current limitation of the model. To improve readability, ChatGPT could be fine-tuned to adjust language complexity according to the audience, using more advanced language for technical topics and simpler, more concise language for broader educational contexts, while maintaining accuracy.

The understandability and actionability scores, assessed using the PEMAT tool, varied across question categories. The overall mean understandability score of 72.28 suggests that most responses were understandable. However, only 18% of responses were considered actionable (above 70%), indicating that while the information was clear, it often lacked specific, practical guidance, which aligns with prior studies highlighting actionability as a challenge in ChatGPT’s responses [16–18].

Questions related to longevity and maintenance, such as “How to take care of a PFM crown?” or “Can you brush teeth after a permanent crown?”, were more actionable, as they allow clear, general steps that patients can follow independently. In contrast, material selection, design, and cost questions, for example “Which is better, a PFM or zirconia crown?” or “What type of crown is best for front teeth?”, received informative but generalized answers without step-by-step guidance. Material selection and cost decisions involve multiple factors highly dependent on individual circumstances, including technical considerations, patient preferences, and financial factors, which are not easily addressed by generalized AI advice. Because these factors vary greatly and require expertise in dental treatment, it is challenging for AI-generated responses to provide universally applicable, actionable recommendations. Design choices must be assessed by a dentist, and cost or insurance considerations further complicate actionable guidance.

One possible explanation for this limitation is the prompt used patient questions were provided as-is, and patients may not phrase questions in a way that elicits detailed advice. ChatGPT did not request additional patient information and only provided factual answers, whereas even a human clinician would need more information from the patient to give precise, individualized recommendations.

While these challenges exist, AI has the potential to improve in several ways. Incorporating multimedia resources—such as videos, 3D visualizations, or interactive illustrations—could make guidance easier for patients to understand and follow. Personalized recommendations based on individual patient data and treatment plans could enhance relevance and accuracy. Integration with decision-support systems for dentists could provide context-aware suggestions rather than general advice. It is important to note that this study examined a general AI tool (ChatGPT 3.5); specialized dental AI systems or tools designed for clinical decision-making could deliver even more precise and actionable guidance.

Conclusion

This study suggests that while ChatGPT shows potential as a supplementary tool for patient education on dental crowns, improvements are needed in readability, actionability, and technical precision. Professional dental consultation remains essential. Future AI models tailored for dental inquiries may perform better by focusing on actionable solutions, and simpler medical terminology. While ChatGPT is a valuable tool in healthcare education and patient guidance, it is not yet a substitute for human expertise. Enhancing these aspects could broaden its utility.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

The study relied on a single AI model (ChatGPT 3.5), and the evaluation focused solely on its textual responses; results may differ with other AI tools, future updates, or multimodal formats, which could affect response quality, accuracy, and readability. The absence of layperson evaluations may limit the generalizability of findings to broader, non-expert audiences. While the PEMAT tool provides valuable insights into the understandability and actionability of responses, it does not capture all aspects of user engagement, such as individual needs or preferences. The analysis was limited to English-language queries, and performance may vary in other languages. Additionally, the evaluation did not assess actual patient outcomes.

Future studies could strengthen these findings by including diverse evaluators and exploring the effectiveness of ChatGPT or other AI-based tools in multimodal formats, for example, combining text with visuals, audio, or interactive elements, to better support patient education and health communication.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. It employed a comprehensive and validated set of evaluation tools, ensuring structured and reproducible analysis. Additionally, it addresses a timely and relevant topic (AI-generated health information on dental crown restorations) with significant implications for both dental professionals and public health.
