Exploring the influence of a massive open online course (MOOC) for preparing rehabilitation professionals to meet the needs of forcibly displaced persons
Authors: Shala Cunningham, Naomi O’Reilly, Rachael Lowe, Wesley Pryor, Abdulgafoor M. Bachani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo assist in the global training of rehabilitation professionals to address the unique needs of forcibly displaced persons, a free massive open online course (MOOC) was developed. This study aimed to explore self-perceived knowledge following completion of courses within the MOOC, ability to implement changes in practice, and participant engagement with the MOOC content.MethodsThe Kirkpatrick Model was used to assess the effectiveness of the course with a focus on reaction, learning, and behavior. Participant interaction with the course content was explored using thematic analysis of the MOOC’s discussion forum content. Participants in the MOOC were asked to complete pre and post-surveys of self-perceived knowledge regarding the rehabilitation of displaced persons. Mean within-subject pre and post-MOOC data were compared using the Mann U Whitney test. Knowledge implementation after six months was assessed with a follow-up survey.ResultsSelf-reported knowledge following completion of the MOOC improved significantly on individual survey questions (p≤.004) as well as composite score (p < .001). Six months following completion of the course, participants noted the ability to implement multiple strategies into practice. Three themes emerged from the discussion forum postings: (1) Understanding the persons lived experience to provide effective, holistic, patient-centered care, (2) Communication to develop a sense of connection and facilitate the provision of quality care, and (3) The importance of culturally responsive practice.ConclusionThe MOOC effectively delivered the fundamental knowledge to address the complex needs of displaced individuals among a diverse group of practicing rehabilitation professionals and can play a role in preparing the rehabilitation workforce for increased population displacement, alongside specialist programs in related themes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06156-w.

Background

The number of forcibly displaced people worldwide has reached over 89.3 million [1]. This population includes individuals who have been internally displaced due to natural disasters, persecution, war, or violence (53.2 million), refugees who have fled their country of origin and have been granted asylum (31.5 million), and asylum seekers whose request for sanctuary is still being processed (4.6 million) [1, 2]. The challenges faced by displaced individuals, including violence, famine, armed conflict, and persecution, can be unique and complex compared to other patient populations [3].

There are several drivers of need for rehabilitation among displaced populations. Conflicts and natural disaster precipitate rapid increases in injuries or complex illness many of which may require rehabilitation [4]. The WHO identifies eight core professions that offer rehabilitation services: audiology, occupational therapy, rehabilitation specialists in medicine, clinical psychology, physiotherapy, prosthetics and orthotics, rehabilitation nursing, and speech and language therapy (WHO Rehab) [5]. Rehabilitation for displaced persons is a crucial aspect of humanitarian aid that aims to improve the physical functioning and overall quality of life of those who have been displaced. (6–7) There are many evidence gaps concerning rehabilitation in humanitarian settings. The modest research that has specifically explored the effectiveness of rehabilitation in humanitarian settings examines many different interventions, conditions, and context [8]. Access to physical rehabilitation services can be limited for displaced persons due to a variety of factors including a lack of resources, inadequate facilities, and a shortage of trained healthcare providers [4]. These issues are exacerbated with majority of refugees being hosted in low or middle-income countries (LMICs) where the rehabilitation capacity is already insufficient to meet the existing demands and priority is given to other essential and acute care services [2, 4, 9].

The significant environmental changes associated with displacement are likely to disrupt support mechanisms and how people function and participate, increasing the need for rehabilitation care, including personal support and adaptive strategies [10]. However, the knowledge and skills to work with displaced persons are often not a component in the entry-level education for rehabilitation providers. This includes (a) an understanding of current international policy and legislation surrounding displaced persons; (b) knowledge about the various health determinants affecting displaced persons, including uncertainty about civil status, unstable accommodation, loss of social networks, poverty, and racism; (c) cultural sensitivity through an understanding the impact of cultural differences on attitudes towards disease, disability, and healthcare; and (d) in the case of the forcibly displaced due to conflict, the ability to assess the effects of trauma on physical and mental health, considering factors such as torture, depression, stress, and post-traumatic stress disorder [11].

Purpose

A free massive open online course (MOOC) was developed to provide practicing rehabilitation providers, across all professions, with a foundational knowledge of the rehabilitation for displaced persons, to allow them to play an effective and proactive role in local and global efforts to increase access to high-quality and context-appropriate rehabilitation services. With the overall purpose of reviewing the effectiveness, quality, and approach of the MOOC, the Kirkpatrick Model was utilized [12]. This mixed methods study had three aims. First, to explore the participants’ experience and engagement with the content. Second to explore the influence of the MOOC, ‘Rehabilitation for Displaced Persons’, on participants’ self-perceived knowledge. Third, to investigate the impact of the course on clinical practice.

Methods

MOOC description

The MOOC was provided on a global scale and supported by Learning, Acting, and Building for Rehabilitation in Health Systems (ReLAB-HS). Funded by the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), ReLAB-HS is an activity to support the development of health systems that are responsive to the growing needs for rehabilitation within populations. The eight-week-long program (September 5 through October 30, 2022) hosted by Physiopedia presented topics exploring the needs of displaced persons. The course topics and content were informed by the Physiotherapy and Refugee Education Project (PREP) Consensus Report on Competencies for Physiotherapists Working with Refugees, the World Health Organization Refugee and Migrant Health: Global Competency Standards for Health Workers, and the World Health Organization Emergency Medical Teams: Minimum Technical Standards and Recommendations for Rehabilitation [13–15]. Learning activities included readings, videos, live webinars, post-course knowledge assessments, and forums for participants to ask questions of topic experts. A two-week orientation period before the course provided participants with an opportunity to become familiar with the online learning platform and the topics via the provided pre-course resources. Resources included detailed instructions for participants about the course and learning materials provided, information on how to access the learning platform and discussion forum, and a tool for reflection to guide participants when setting individualized learning objectives.

The ‘program’ (the overall MOOC) consisted of seven independent ‘courses’: (1) Global Context for Displaced Persons, (2) Health and Well-being for Displaced Persons, (3) Communication and Trauma Informed Care for Displaced Persons, (4) Considerations for Working with Diverse Person Populations, (5) Considerations and Practice Tools for Working with Survivors of Sexual Violence and Trafficking, (6) Considerations for Working with Survivors of Torture, and (7) Assessment and Management of Pain for Displaced Persons. Each course included content provided by 18 topic experts specifically selected for their experience in working with refugee and migrant populations, in humanitarian settings and/or specifically related to trauma and mental health issues. They brought diverse experience from multiple contexts including refugee camps, centres for survivors of torture, and humanitarian organisations representing multiple geographical settings including LMICs, and a variety of professional backgrounds including policy, academia, and health service provision. Appendix A has additional information on the topic experts’ recruitment and qualifications.

In each course, the learner completed the required learning activities including videos and readings as well as a post-course knowledge assessment or quiz. Participants had the option to choose which of the seven courses met their current needs and did not have to begin the series with course one. Course completion was defined as completing all required learning activities and successfully achieving an 80% on the post-course knowledge assessment or quiz. Each course had a discussion forum for participants to share their experiences. These discussions were intended to provide a rich learning experience through self-reflection and engaging with other participants. The discussion forum was overseen daily by the Physiopedia MOOC Manager and Course Creation Manager to offer assistance and feedback, address inquiries, and ensure the proper use of forums. The experts in various topics monitored discussions related to their areas of expertise. Additionally, there were six official facilitators for the discussion forum. The MOOC Manager created a guide for facilitators detailing their responsibilities, participation in forum discussions, and suggestions for responses. Facilitators could also access a dedicated forum to seek clarification on any questions or concerns that arose during the MOOC. Two optional live online webinars provided an opportunity for increased learner interaction and engagement with the content experts. Over 170 participants accessed each of the live webinars and recordings were made available on the online learning platform for participants unable to access the webinars live. An additional 128 participants watched the recorded webinars. Participants were encouraged to selectively choose courses and learning activities within the program that met their current practice needs if the ability to commit to completion of the entire MOOC was limited. Participants could access materials associated with courses within the MOOC without restrictions. The total time commitment for the entire MOOC was 22 h with an option for eight additional hours. Assessment

The Kirkpatrick Model has been described previously to evaluate the effectiveness of MOOCs [16]. The model provides a systematic approach consisting of four levels of evaluation to assess training effectiveness [12]. Level one (reaction) focuses on participants’ immediate reactions to the training program including satisfaction, engagement, and perceived relevance of the training. Level two (learning) evaluates the knowledge and skills acquired by participants during the training. It examines whether they have gained new information, improved their understanding, or acquired new competencies. Level three (behavior) assesses the extent to which participants apply what they have learned in their actual work environment. Finally, level four (results) measures the overall impact of the training on organizational goals and performance [12]. The assessment of this MOOC focused on reaction, learning, and behavior. With level four (results) focusing on organization change and/or the effect of individual behavioral change on patient outcomes versus individual implementation, this component of the model could not be assessed in this study due to the rehabilitation of displaced persons being a complex process with confounding factors not allowing for a clear determination of the influence of the providers’ behavioral change on the client outcomes.

Participants were provided anonymous, online surveys following completion of each course using Qualtrics. To assess level one (reaction), participants had the option to share their feedback after the completion of each of the seven courses within the program. Participants were asked to rate their overall opinion of each course on a five-point scale ranging from excellent to poor. In addition, the participants were asked if the course objectives were met, evidence was provided to substantiate the material presented, and the level of evidence presented in layman’s terms. They were provided the opportunity to provide comments and suggestions. Mean and median scores on individual courses and overall combined scores for the MOOC were determined.

To evaluate level two learning, participants completed a post-course assessment consisting of multiple-choice questions on the material presented. In addition, they were asked to complete pre and post-MOOC surveys including demographic information and their perception of their current understanding of various concepts (e.g., international legislation for displaced persons) and confidence in performing essential skills (e.g., working with interpreters) related to the rehabilitation of displaced persons. Consent was provided at the beginning of each survey. The survey promoted reflective practice with participants self-reporting their learning process. An invitation to the anonymous pre and post-program survey was sent to all registered participants. The survey was developed by the researchers and content experts based on the objectives and content of the seven courses within the MOOC. Data was analysed using the Mann U Whitney test. Due to the number of comparisons (n = 11), a Bonferroni correction was utilized to determine significance level (0.005). Differences in performance on post MOOC survey based on World Bank classification and profession was also examined. To assess level three (behavior), six months following the completion of the MOOC an anonymous online survey focused on knowledge implementation was provided to all participants to explore the influence of the MOOC on practice. The survey focused on the practical application of the knowledge provided in the program including strategies for the effective use of interpreters, improved communication, cultural sensitivity, trauma-informed care, inclusivity, and the management of pain.

In addition to the course surveys, to answer the first aim (to explore the participants’ experience and engagement with the content), discussion forums were reviewed. The MOOC discussion forum was an optional opportunity for participant engagement and served as the primary platform for collaborative knowledge construction. Within this open forum for MOOC participants, individuals had the opportunity to actively contribute to textual dialogue, generating unique data on the content created by them during the MOOC. Participants often identified themselves by an alias, first name, or initials. The discussion forum postings were deidentified prior to analysis. Only the MOOC participants’ posts were reviewed. A thematic approach was utilized to understand the participants’ experiences [17]. The information obtained from the discussion forum postings was coded and general themes were identified by the primary investigator. NVivo for Mac was utilized to explore and arrange codes. The constant comparative method was utilized for primary coding, followed by secondary cycle coding to identify patterns and themes [18]. Participant narratives are provided below to inform the themes identified. To ensure credibility, themes were confirmed through peer review by a second researcher.

Results

Participation and reaction

Of the 868 who completed the pre-MOOC survey regarding their perception of their current knowledge regarding the rehabilitation of displaced persons, 815 (93.0%) began the first course before October 30, 2022 with an overall 23.0% (n = 188) completion rate for the entire program. Figure 1 charts the course initiation and completion rates within the MOOC as well as activities completed in each course. The majority of the participants (83.7%) were early to mid-career professionals, under the age of 45. (Appendix B). They were more likely to be women (62.7%) and working in the field of physical therapy (68.5%) or occupational therapy (9.2%). Of the participants who completed any courses within the MOOC, 63.5% were from low and middle-income countries. The completion rate for each course ranged from 44.4 to 81.8%. The mean global rating from 5 (excellent) to 1 (poor) for the seven courses was 4.34 (SD 0.688). Participants in the six-month follow-up survey who did not complete a single course were asked what limited their ability to participate. The most common reasons noted for not completing courses included lack of time to complete courses (n = 21), lack of consistent, affordable internet access (n = 10), and unfamiliarity with online instruction (n = 4).

Ten of the 11 questions on the pre and post-MOOC surveys demonstrated statistically significant increases in scores indicating improvement in participant perceptions. Behavior

Six months following the completion of the MOOC, a survey focused on knowledge implementation was sent to participants. The survey was fully completed by 170 participants. However, 65 respondents indicated they had not successfully completed a course in the MOOC. Of the 105 participants who did complete at least one course in the MOOC, 73.3% noted they have had an opportunity to change their practice as a result of the knowledge gained through the course. Demographic information for the survey respondents is presented in Appendix B. The most common areas where behavior change was noted six months following completion of the MOOC were; (1) acknowledging and respecting differences that may exist between personal beliefs, values, and ways of thinking and that of the displaced person (n = 44), (2) creating an environment where the displaced person can feel physically and psychologically safe (n = 28), and (3) developing a self-care plan to support balance within the working environment (n = 29). Responses of all participants can be found in Appendix C.

Participants’ experience

A qualitative research design was used to explore the experiences of the participants as they progressed through the program. In total, there were 1,832 discussion posts over the course of the program. There were 332 comments on the most active discussion forum, which considered the individual context participants were working in, their experiences of working with displaced persons, and the challenges they were facing in providing rehabilitation services for displaced persons. Information from the discussion forums and participant reflections were analyzed by two of the researchers with three themes emerging: (1) Understanding the persons lived experience to provide effective, holistic, patient-centered care, (2) Communication to develop a sense of connection and facilitate the provision of quality care, and (3) The importance of culturally responsive practice.

Theme 1: understanding the patients lived experience to provide effective, holistic, patient centered care

Participants described the need to listen to a displaced person’s story to gain a better understanding of their lived experience. This understanding allows us to be more empathetic and better able to provide culturally humble and competent, holistic, and compassionate care to our clients.

The living conditions are poor.. The problems faced by them are wage theft, retrenchments, lack of social security, employer accountability, mobility issues, and social discrimination.

Theme 2: communication to develop a sense of connection and facilitate the provision of quality care

During the discussion forums, it was emphasized that having a translator who is proficient in both language and culture is valuable in building a strong therapeutic relationship. Participants stressed that language and culture cannot be separated, as they include the values, beliefs, and customs of a specific group.Whether with a displaced persons or not language barrier impedes communication and has a negative effect on rehabilitation. Before one can treat effectively, there has to be an understanding between the healthcare professional and the patient. In this case the interpreter [can assist] in bridging the communication gap by fostering understanding between the healthcare professionals and the patient.

Participants who work with displaced persons emphasized the importance of acknowledging, accepting, and integrating the patient’s cultural perspectives in order to provide effective rehabilitation interventions.It always good practice to have an open mind during sessions – you never know what you will be facing and having preconceived ideas about things are not helpful and can set up both you and the client for failure. Even more so if you are [working] with a person from a different cultural background.

Acknowledging that strategies that proved successful in one culture may not yield the same effectiveness in another culture enabled this participant and their team to adapt their educational and knowledge translation efforts. By investing time in exploring preferred learning strategies, they experienced enhanced effectiveness in their initiatives.In Ethiopia, it was [believed] that the women would appreciate booklets we gave them and would use them to pass along information. Discussion

With the majority of displaced persons residing in LMICs, this MOOC was successful in reaching professionals providing rehabilitation in these countries. Furthermore, the individual course completion rate of 44–81% was high for a MOOC. A systematic review on MOOCs for healthcare professionals found relatively high completion rates found between 13 and 38%, exceeding the global average for MOOCs which has been estimated at 10%.[19–20] The ability to selectively choose courses with the program that met the participants’ current practice needs may have contributed to this high individual course completion rate compared to overall program completion rate. Additional features, such as the presence of an orientation session to allow participants to become familiar with the online learning platform and its topics, as well as frequent knowledge assessments and the active use of a discussion forum may have influenced completion. The factors contributing to the non-completion of the MOOC were comparable to those encountered in other open online courses including lack of time available to complete courses and limited access to affordable, consistent internet [21]. This highlights the inherent difficulties in delivering immediate, easily accessible education and training resources for the busy professional with limited available time when the need for the information arises suddenly.

The Kirkpatrick Model has been described previously to evaluate the effectiveness of MOOCs [16]. The assessment of this MOOC focused on reaction, learning, and behavior with results focusing on organization change versus individual implementation. The MOOC demonstrated high levels of participant satisfaction with the content and format of the courses as well as high completion rates. Moreover, the pre/ post surveys indicated a gain in self-perceived knowledge and understanding. Participants not only reported a self-perceived improvement in their knowledge as a result of the MOOC, they also highlighted the practical application of that knowledge in their clinical practice. This implementation of knowledge occurred within six months of completing the MOOC and aimed to enhance the rehabilitation of displaced individuals. By performing a six-month, post-MOOC assessment, the changes in clinical practice could be explored. Not all participants had opportunities to implement all strategies provided throughout the program due to the workplace context. The variation in the number and type of strategies implemented may have also been related to courses completed.

The themes from the discussion forums aligned with the Migrant and Refugee Women’s Health Partnership Competency Standards Framework for Clinicians Culturally Responsive Clinical Practice: Working with people from migrant and refugee backgrounds with a focus on patient-centred care [22]. Similar competencies were noted in a scoping review for physiotherapists working with refugees [11]. Those participants actively engaged in working with displaced individuals identified similar challenges and employed comparable strategies as noted in the competencies to address those challenges.

Through the exchange of perspectives and experiences, participants shared knowledge through interpersonal transfer, encompassing both externalization and internalization. Internalization enables individuals to assimilate experiences and solutions from others into their own unique contexts, while externalization facilitates the communication and sharing of personal experiences. (23–24) This collaborative process resulted in the development of a shared knowledge base, enriching the course content and promoting the integration of competencies in practice.

Enhancing the preparedness of current and future healthcare professionals in interpersonal and communication skills, as well as transcultural competence may enable professionals to provide more effective and comprehensive care to displaced persons, taking into account their unique needs, life experiences, and cultural backgrounds. The participants described the importance of cultural competency which involves the integration of an individual’s cultural background and experiences into their rehabilitation care [25]. Establishing a meaningful relationship is an essential factor for ensuring the delivery of effective interventions [26]. A systematic review by Hasnain et al. found that interventions integrating an individual’s cultural background have a positive impact on rehabilitation outcomes for displaced persons with diverse disabilities. Specifically, these interventions demonstrate significant improvements in three key areas: enhancing clients’ understanding of their disabilities, alleviating disability-related symptoms, and fostering positive psychosocial outcomes such as well-being, self-efficacy, and quality of life [27]. The themes from the discussion forums aligned with creating a therapeutic relationship to provide culturally sensitive patient-centered care.

Multiple limitations were present in this study. To limit the requirements for participation in the MOOC courses and to be cognizant of the limited time available to complete continuing professional development, pre-assessments of knowledge were not performed prior to each course. With the significant increase in displaced persons in 2022, it was recognized that many of the professionals participating in the MOOC may have limited time to complete entire courses within the program. The intent was to have short modules and easy-to-access information to allow participants to access information crucial to their current situation without any barriers. Course satisfaction questions were limited to key information for future revision. All data collected was anonymous, including surveys and discussion forums. Due to the anonymous nature of the surveys and course assessments, responses from participants could not be linked. Although this is not an ideal approach, the results are indicative of the benefit of the MOOC for addressing the objectives and change in practice. The surveys were based on self-perception and may not accurately capture true change in knowledge or may be subject to biases such as social desirability bias. Furthermore, discussion forums were optional which may have introduced bias. It is unknown how many participants were currently interacting with displaced persons. Furthermore, participants were allowed to choose the courses within the overall MOOC that best met their current needs. Therefore, not all participants completed all courses available within the program.

Conclusion

The MOOC effectively improved perceptions of knowledge and self-reported behavioral change to address the intricate needs of displaced individuals, enabling participants to incorporate essential actions for enhancing rehabilitation care. Responding to the growing need for rehabilitation among displaced populations may be strengthened through voluntary, free, open courses through complementing specialized and face-to-face training.
