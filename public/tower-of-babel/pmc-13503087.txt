Lifetime risk of respiratory system cancers across 185 countries: Global variation, socioeconomic inequalities, and ecological associations with air pollution
Authors: Meng Jiang, Yu Qian, Qilong Wang, Xiumin Zhou, Hong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the lifetime risks of developing and dying from respiratory system cancers across 185 countries and territories and to examine their associations with socioeconomic development and air pollution.MethodsGLOBOCAN 2022 data for 185 countries and territories were combined with United Nations life tables to estimate lifetime risks using an adjusted-for-multiple-primaries framework. Country-level air pollution indicators for 2022 were obtained from OpenWeather. Estimates were stratified by Human Development Index (HDI) and World Bank income groups, and associations between lifetime risk and PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and SO2 were assessed using univariable linear regression.ResultsGlobally, the lifetime risk of developing and dying from lung cancer was 4.92% and 4.08%, respectively, far exceeding those for laryngeal cancer (0.33% and 0.20%). A marked socioeconomic gradient was identified: lung cancer incidence risk ranged from 0.34% in low-HDI countries to 5.45% in very high-HDI countries. All four pollutants were positively associated with lifetime risk, with PM2.5 showing the strongest correlation in the primary univariable analyses.ConclusionsSubstantial geographic and socioeconomic disparities exist in respiratory cancer lifetime risk, with lung cancer as the dominant driver. The observed ecological associations with air pollutants support further investigation of environmental factors in cancer prevention.Keywords: Respiratory system cancers, Lifetime risk, Incidence, Mortality, Income, Air pollution

Introduction

In GLOBOCAN 2022, respiratory system cancers are represented by two site categories: laryngeal cancer (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10]: C32) and the category “trachea, bronchus and lung” (ICD-10: C33–C34), hereafter referred to as lung cancer. (Filho et al., 2025) In 2022, lung cancer represented 12.4% of newly diagnosed cancers worldwide and was responsible for 18.7% of cancer-related deaths, the highest mortality burden among all cancer types. (Bray et al., 2024) Although laryngeal cancer contributes a smaller share of overall cancer burden, it shows pronounced geographic and sex-specific heterogeneity. (da Cunha et al., 2025; T. Zhou et al., 2025) Respiratory system cancers are expected to remain a substantial and continuing burden on population health, driven by population growth, ageing, and unequal exposure to key risk factors. (Bizuayehu et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Yuhua Wang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024) However, most global assessments of respiratory system cancers have focused on incidence, mortality, age-standardized rates, or future case projections, rather than the lifetime probability of developing or dying from these cancers. (Fu et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2025; Sharma and Khubchandani, 2024; Wu et al., 2024).

Cancer registries, GLOBOCAN estimates, and the Global Burden of Disease framework support cross-country comparisons and assessment of major exposures, including tobacco use, air pollution, and occupational carcinogens. (Filho et al., 2025; GBD 2019 Respiratory Tract Cancers Collaborators, 2021; GBD 2019 Risk Factors Collaborators, 2020; Safiri et al., 2021) Air pollution is also an important exposure of interest in respiratory cancers, and commonly used indicators such as fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of ≤2.5 μm (PM2.5), particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of ≤10 μm (PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and sulfur dioxide (SO2), may help characterize cross-country differences in environmental risk. (Li et al., 2024; Jintao Zhou et al., 2025) Consequently, the country-level relation between air pollution and the lifetime risks of respiratory system cancers remains poorly characterized. (Deng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) Integrating lifetime risk measures with national air pollution indicators may help describe cross-country risk patterns and inform prevention policy.

Lifetime risk metrics describe the probability that an individual is diagnosed with, or dies from, a cancer between birth and death, under the assumption that current age-specific incidence and all-cause mortality rates remain constant over time. (Li et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023) The adjusted-for-multiple-primaries (AMP) life-table framework improves comparability by accounting for cancer incidence and mortality, competing mortality, multiple primaries, and life expectancy. (Sasieni et al., 2011) This framework has recently been applied to several cancer groups using GLOBOCAN data. (Li et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024) However, few studies have evaluated respiratory system cancers within a unified framework while jointly examining socioeconomic stratification, remaining lifetime risk from different ages, and ecological patterns related to air pollution. In the present study, we combined the AMP framework with GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates and United Nations life tables to estimate LRI and LRM for lung and laryngeal cancers across 185 countries and territories. (Bray et al., 2024; Filho et al., 2025; Sasieni et al., 2011) We compared estimates by geographic and socioeconomic group and assessed ecological associations with air pollution using OpenWeather and EDGAR data. Cumulative risk to age 74 years was used in sensitivity analyses.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a global population-based ecological study using publicly available aggregate data from 185 countries and territories. Data on new cases, deaths, and population sizes for laryngeal cancer and lung cancer, including tracheal cancer, were obtained from GLOBOCAN 2022, compiled by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) through the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO, https://gco.iarc.who.int). Corresponding life-table data were obtained from the United Nations.

These data are available separately by sex and for 5-year age groups. In GLOBOCAN 2022, respiratory system cancers were captured using two site categories: laryngeal cancer (ICD-10 C32) and cancers of the trachea, bronchus and lung (ICD-10 C33 and C34; reported under the site label “Lung, including trachea”).

The GLOBOCAN estimates are based mainly on the best available national cancer incidence and mortality data and, where necessary, modelling approaches such as short-term projections and mortality-to-incidence ratios. Country-level HDI values were obtained from the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), and national income groups (high, upper middle, lower middle, and low) were defined according to the World Bank classification. Ambient air pollution metrics were obtained from two sources. First, country-level ambient concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and SO2 (μg/m3) were retrieved using the OpenWeather historical Air Pollution API. For each country or territory, concentrations for January–December 2022 were sampled using a regular 0.5° × 0.5° grid based on GADM national boundaries and averaged across valid grid points and sampling times to obtain annual national means. EDGAR v8.1 emissions data from the European Commission Joint Research Centre were used as supplementary information on national emission patterns; the main ecological analyses used country-level pollutant concentrations. Country-level data on tobacco use and alcohol consumption were obtained from the World Health Organization Global Health Observatory (GHO, https://www.who.int/data/gho). The age-standardized prevalence of current tobacco use among persons aged ≥15 years was used as the tobacco-use indicator. Total alcohol per capita consumption (ages 15+, in litres of pure alcohol per year) was used as the alcohol consumption indicator. Data were matched to GLOBOCAN 2022 countries by ISO 3166-1 alpha-3 country codes.

Measures

LRI and LRM for lung and laryngeal cancers were estimated separately using an AMP framework (Sasieni et al., 2011). Details are provided in previous reports and in the Supplementary Appendix. Briefly, age-specific all-cause mortality and cancer incidence and mortality data in 5-year age groups were used to estimate the cumulative probability of developing or dying from each cancer over the life course. LRI was defined as the lifetime risk of developing a specific cancer, and LRM as the lifetime risk of dying from that cancer. Cumulative risks to age 74 years were calculated for sensitivity analyses. Estimates were stratified by sex, geographic level, Human Development Index category, and World Bank income group.

Statistical analysis

Associations were examined separately for lung and laryngeal cancers using country-level scatter plots and univariable linear regression models for each national air pollution metric (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and SO2; μg/m3). LRI or LRM was entered as the dependent variable and pollutant concentration as the independent variable. Regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals represented the absolute percentage-point change in lifetime risk associated with a 1 μg/m3 increase in pollutant concentration and were displayed in coefficient plots. To assess the robustness of the ecological associations to country-level confounding, models were sequentially adjusted for Human Development Index, World Bank income group, hemisphere, the age-standardized proportion of current tobacco users aged ≥15 years and total alcohol intake per capita. Fully adjusted models included all five covariates. Potential nonlinear associations were examined by adding a squared pollutant term to each fully adjusted model. Evidence of nonlinearity was assessed using the P value for the quadratic term. Robustness was also assessed by repeating analyses using cumulative risk to age 74 years as an alternative risk metric. We applied two-tailed tests throughout, with P < 0.05 meeting the criterion for statistical significance. Statistical computations were conducted with R 4.3.3.

Ethical considerations

This study used only publicly available, de-identified aggregate data and was granted an exemption from ethical review by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.

Results

Global distribution of lifetime risks of respiratory system cancers

Across 185 countries and territories, respiratory system cancers showed clear geographic variation in LRI and LRM (Fig. 1). Lung cancer accounted for most of the lifetime burden, whereas laryngeal cancer had much lower risk levels. Globally, the LRI and LRM for lung cancer were 4.92% (95% CI, 4.91%, 4.92%) and 4.08% (95% CI, 4.08%, 4.09%). The corresponding values for laryngeal cancer were 0.33% (95% CI, 0.32%, 0.33%) and 0.20% (95% CI, 0.19%, 0.20%). Global and sex-specific estimates are shown in Table 1. Variation was also observed at the continent, United Nations regional, and country levels (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, Panel A).

At the continent level, North America had the highest lifetime risk of lung cancer, followed by Europe, Oceania, and Asia, whereas Africa had the lowest. Laryngeal cancer showed lower overall risks, with relatively high LRI in Europe and LRM in Latin America. At the United Nations regional level, lung cancer risks were highest in Eastern Asia, Micronesia, North America, and parts of Europe, and lowest in Western, Middle, and Eastern Africa. Laryngeal cancer risks were relatively high in the Caribbean, Southern Europe, Eastern Europe, and Western Asia (Supplementary Table S2, Panel A).

Country-level estimates also varied widely. High lung cancer risks were seen in Canada, China, Hungary, and Japan, whereas low risks were mainly found in Central and Western Africa, including the Central African Republic. Laryngeal cancer risks were lower overall but still differed across countries. High values were seen in Cuba and parts of Eastern and Southern Europe, while several African countries and small island states had values close to zero. Men had higher lifetime risks than women in most regions (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S2, Panel A). Sensitivity analyses using cumulative risk to age 74 years showed broadly similar country-level patterns (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Socioeconomic gradients in lifetime risks by HDI and World Bank income group

Respiratory system cancers showed socioeconomic gradients across HDI categories and World Bank income groups, which were more obvious for lung cancer (Fig. 2). Laryngeal cancer risks were lower, with smaller differences across groups.

For lung cancer, both LRI and LRM increased as HDI and income level increased. Across HDI groups, the median LRI rose from 0.34% in low-HDI countries to 5.45% in very-high-HDI countries. The median LRM rose from 0.32% to 5.05%. Across income groups, the median LRI rose from 0.39% in low-income countries to 6.31% in high-income countries. The median LRM rose from 0.38% to 5.26%. Laryngeal cancer showed the same general pattern, but the gradient was weaker. Across HDI groups, the median LRI rose from 0.11% to 0.37%, and the median LRM rose from 0.08% to 0.18%. Across income groups, the corresponding values rose from 0.13% to 0.37% and from 0.10% to 0.17%, respectively. Detailed estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S2, Panel B.

The distributions also differed between the two cancers. Lung cancer showed greater between-country variation in high-HDI and high-income groups, whereas laryngeal cancer showed more overlap across groups (Fig. 2). Sex-stratified analyses showed similar patterns, and men had higher lifetime risks than women in most groups (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Remaining lifetime risks from selected ages to death by HDI strata

Across HDI strata, LRI and LRM for respiratory system cancers decreased as the age from which risk was estimated to death increased, although the rate of decline differed by cancer type and development level (Fig. 3). In every HDI stratum, lung cancer had higher remaining lifetime risks than laryngeal cancer. In both cancers, LRI was higher than LRM at most ages examined.

Remaining lifetime risks of incidence and mortality for lung and laryngeal cancers from selected ages to death by Human Development Index category across 185 countries and territories in 2022. Remaining lifetime risk was estimated from each age shown on the horizontal axis to death, conditional on being alive and cancer-free at that age. For example, the estimate at age 50 represents the probability of developing or dying from the specified cancer between age 50 and death. The horizontal axis indicates the age at which risk estimation begins.

For lung cancer, the age-related curves were similar across HDI strata. In very-high-HDI countries, LRI and LRM were 6.45% and 5.31%, respectively, when estimated from birth to death. Both remained high when estimation began at age 40, then decreased gradually, and the decline became steeper when estimation began after age 60. When estimated from age 80, LRI and LRM had fallen to 2.11% and 2.01%. By contrast, medium HDI and low HDI countries had much lower remaining lifetime risks across the ages examined. Laryngeal cancer showed a similar overall pattern, but its risk levels were much lower and the gap between strata was smaller. Men had higher remaining lifetime risks than women in most strata (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Ecological associations between air pollutants and lifetime risks

Country-level linear regression analyses showed positive coefficients for all four pollutants (Fig. 4). In the primary univariable analyses, PM2.5 showed the largest coefficient. Coefficients were larger for lung than laryngeal cancer and generally larger for LRM than LRI. Detailed estimates are presented in Table 2. The direction and magnitude of the associations changed little after sequential covariate adjustment. Within the sensitivity-analysis sample, fully adjusted coefficients were 0.5%–5.2% lower than the corresponding unadjusted coefficients for lung cancer and 1.6%–7.7% lower for laryngeal cancer (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Table S2, Panel C). In stratified analyses, most coefficients remained positive across Human Development Index and income groups, although their magnitude varied and many confidence intervals included zero. No consistent socioeconomic gradient was observed (Supplementary Table S2, Panels E and F). Exploratory quadratic-term analyses suggested possible nonlinearity for the association between PM2.5 and lung cancer mortality and for several pollutant associations with laryngeal cancer. No evidence of nonlinearity was found for lung cancer incidence (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Table S2, Panel D).

Discussion

Based on GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates and United Nations life tables, we estimated LRI and LRM for respiratory system cancers in 185 countries and territories. Lifetime risk varied across countries and socioeconomic groups, with lung cancer accounting for most of the burden. These findings were broadly consistent with recent GLOBOCAN-based analyses. (Bray et al., 2024) Lifetime risk increased with HDI and income level, especially for lung cancer, in line with previous studies. (Zheng et al., 2023) Air pollution indicators were positively associated with lifetime risk, with the largest coefficient observed for PM2.5 and weaker patterns for laryngeal cancer. Similar associations with cancer burden and regional inequalities have been reported elsewhere. (Zhang et al., 2025; M. Zhu et al., 2025).

The global and regional differences we observed may reflect long-term differences between countries and regions in population ageing, exposure patterns, and health system conditions. Lung cancer showed higher lifetime risk in high-HDI and high-income settings. This was likely related to longer life expectancy, greater cumulative exposure, and more complete diagnostic and registration systems. (Cao et al., 2024) This finding was also consistent with recent global studies of lifetime risk. (Li et al., 2024) Laryngeal cancer had lower overall risk but remained relatively high in the Caribbean, Southern Europe, Eastern Europe and Western Asia. (da Cunha et al., 2025; Wierzbicka et al., 2025) These regional differences may reflect variation in tobacco use, alcohol intake, and other upper aerodigestive tract exposures, but confirmation requires individual-level data.

We also found that the remaining lifetime risks of respiratory system cancers declined when estimation began at older ages. This is expected because the remaining lifespan becomes shorter as the age at which risk estimation begins increases. (Zheng et al., 2023) High-HDI countries still showed relatively high remaining lifetime risks at younger ages, while laryngeal cancer showed smaller differences between HDI strata. These findings show that lifetime risk can describe how future cumulative risk changes from different ages and may therefore be useful for cross-country comparison and risk communication. (Li et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025).

Air pollution was included because respiratory cancers, especially lung cancer, are linked to inhaled exposures. (Chen et al., 2024; Murphy et al., 2025) All four indicators were positively associated with lifetime risk, with stronger associations for lung cancer and the largest primary univariable coefficient observed for PM2.5. These findings agree with recent global studies. (M. Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; M. Zhu et al., 2025) For lung cancer, association strength varied across income groups, possibly because of differences in pollution sources, exposure measurement, health-care access, and background mortality. (Deng et al., 2025; Onwuka et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Q. Zhu et al., 2025) The weaker results for laryngeal cancer may reflect greater effects of smoking, alcohol use, occupational exposures, and other unmeasured factors. (Peng et al., 2025; M. Wang et al., 2025) The measured country-level socioeconomic and behavioral factors did not fully explain the observed associations, although residual confounding remains possible.

Strengths of this study include the use of GLOBOCAN 2022 data from 185 countries and territories, a consistent AMP framework, and the joint assessment of socioeconomic differences and air pollution. (Li et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025) At the same time, this study has limitations. GLOBOCAN estimates rely partly on modelling and extrapolation in countries with incomplete data, which may affect comparability. (Bray et al., 2024) Our air pollution analysis was based on country-level ecological data, which means that these findings cannot be interpreted as causal associations at the individual level. (Li, 2025) Although country-level current tobacco use prevalence was included in the sensitivity analyses, it could not capture individual smoking histories, cumulative tobacco exposure, or changes in smoking patterns over time; residual confounding by smoking therefore remains possible. Occupational exposures, indoor air pollution, alcohol intake, and access to care may also have influenced the observed associations. Our period-based estimates used age-specific rates and country-level indicators from 2022 and did not capture cohort-specific exposure histories or future changes in risk factors. This limitation is particularly relevant to lung and laryngeal cancers because of their long latency periods, as current pollutant concentrations may not reflect the historical exposure windows most relevant to cancers diagnosed in 2022. The findings should therefore be interpreted as period-based ecological estimates rather than projections of lifetime risk under changing exposure conditions. Further studies using higher-resolution exposure data and individual-level designs are needed to clarify these associations.

Conclusions

Lifetime risks of respiratory system cancers varied substantially across countries and socioeconomic settings, with lung cancer accounting for most of the burden. Higher risks were generally observed in countries with higher Human Development Index values and income levels. Country-level air pollution indicators, particularly PM2.5, were positively associated with lifetime risk, although these ecological findings should not be interpreted causally. Lifetime risk measures may add useful information beyond conventional incidence and mortality indicators and provide a more intuitive basis for international risk comparison, risk communication, and prevention planning.
