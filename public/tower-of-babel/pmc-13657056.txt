KAT-Net: a hyperbolic routing framework for joint lysine acylation site prediction with shared sequence–structure representations
Authors: Hao Wu, Yiyu Lin, Lun Zhu, Yan Wang, Shan Chang, Sen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein lysine acylation regulates protein function through changes in local chemistry, structure, and molecular interactions. Acetylation (Kac), succinylation (Ksucc), and crotonylation (Kcr) share the lysine centre but differ in acyl-group chemistry, motivating joint yet task-aware prediction. We developed KAT-Net, a multi-task framework integrating ESM-2 representations, physicochemical priors, and AlphaFold2-derived structural context. Predicted aligned error–aware graph modelling reduces uncertain structural relationships, while task-driven hyperbolic routing retrieves modification-specific information from shared representations. A masked asymmetric focal loss supports learning with unavailable labels and task-dependent class imbalance. Independent benchmark and external Kcr evaluations showed favourable performance, while ablation and single-task comparisons supported contributions from sequence–structure integration, structural modelling, hyperbolic routing, and multi-task learning. Sequence and structural analyses revealed modification-related positional, residue, and spatial patterns around candidate lysines. External functional analysis using an independent mouse lung acetylome and succinylome provided complementary evidence of functional plausibility, with Kac predictions showing partial concordance with experimentally reported metabolic processes rather than serving as a binary test of general Kac-site discrimination. For Kac prediction, model development and primary benchmarking were performed using the curated non-histone acetylation dataset from TransPTM and should be interpreted within this benchmark scope rather than as a comprehensive evaluation of the complete acetylome. KAT-Net therefore provides a unified framework for lysine-acylation prediction and candidate-site prioritization within the evaluated benchmark settings.

Introduction

Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) regulate protein function by altering the chemical states of specific residues, thereby influencing molecular interactions, conformational stability, and cellular signalling [1–5]. Lysine acylation is a representative PTM class because the reactive ε-amino group of lysine can carry different acyl groups. Among these modifications, acetylation (Kac) [6], succinylation (Ksucc) [7], and crotonylation (Kcr) [8–10] are associated with chromatin regulation, metabolic control, protein stability, and disease-related processes. Although these acylations share lysine as the modification centre, their acyl groups differ in charge, steric properties, and chemical structures, suggesting both shared lysine-centred signals and modification-specific sequence–structure preferences [11–14]. Proteomic studies have also reported overlap and regulatory crosstalk among lysine acylations, including shared modification sites and coordinated changes across metabolic pathways. Such observations suggest that different acylations may compete or coordinate at subsets of lysine sites, although this relationship is not universal. Their shared substrate background therefore provides a biological basis for joint modelling, whereas their chemical differences require task-specific discrimination. Mass-spectrometry-derived annotations provide the experimental foundation for constructing and evaluating PTM predictors. Computational prediction consequently complements, rather than replaces, mass spectrometry by prioritizing candidate lysines for subsequent experimental investigation [15–17].

Computational prediction of PTM sites has progressed from manually designed sequence and physicochemical descriptors [18–20] towards models that learn informative patterns directly from protein sequences. MusiteDeep [21], for example, used convolutional neural networks with an attention mechanism to identify general and kinase-specific phosphorylation sites directly from amino acid sequences, reducing the dependence on manually engineered features. More recent lysine-acylation predictors have further incorporated richer sequence representations. EDS-Kcr [22] combines ESM-2 representations with local sequence features for Kcr prediction, LMSuccSite [23] uses ProtT5-derived embeddings together with supervised sequence representations for Ksucc prediction, and TransPTM [24] models non-histone Kac sites using ProtT5 representations and graph-based residue learning. AIPred [25] further combines protein language representations with interpretable machine-learning features for non-histone Kac prediction. In the present study, the Kac component was developed using the curated non-histone acetylation benchmark established by TransPTM [24], providing a consistent setting for comparison with existing non-histone Kac predictors. These studies demonstrate that increasingly informative sequence representations can improve PTM-site modelling, but most lysine-acylation predictors are still developed separately for individual modification types. As summarized in Supplementary Table S1, their prediction targets, input representations, and modelling strategies differ substantially. Consequently, separate predictors cannot explicitly exploit information shared among related lysine acylations, whereas indiscriminate feature sharing may obscure modification-specific evidence. This motivates a joint framework that can learn common lysine-centred patterns while retaining task-dependent representations.

Recent PTM prediction has increasingly moved toward multimodal representation learning, in which different information sources describe complementary aspects of the modification-site environment. MIND-S combines sequence and structural representations with graph learning and multi-label prediction [26], while DeepPCT integrates sequence embeddings, AlphaFold2-derived structures, and graph features to model PTM crosstalk [27]. DeepPTMPred further combines multi-scale sequence context, ESM-2 embeddings, and predicted structural properties [28], and COMPASS-PTM integrates protein language representations with physicochemical information and crosstalk-aware prompting [29]. More recent SE(3)-equivariant and UniGraphPTMs frameworks also combine sequence and structural information through geometric or graph-based learning [30, 31]. These studies demonstrate that PLM embeddings provide contextual sequence information, physicochemical descriptors introduce residue-level biochemical priors, and predicted structures supply spatial relationships that cannot be recovered from linear sequence alone. For joint Kac/Ksucc/Kcr prediction, however, several issues remain particularly relevant: general PLM representations do not explicitly emphasize candidate-lysine-specific physicochemical and positional information [32, 33]; AlphaFold2-derived residue relationships have non-uniform confidence and may introduce uncertain structural evidence [34–38]; shared sequence–structure representations require a mechanism for retrieving modification-specific information rather than treating all three tasks identically; and heterogeneous annotations, unavailable task labels, and class imbalance complicate joint optimization [39–41].

Motivated by these challenges, we developed KAT-Net for the joint prediction of Kac, Ksucc, and Kcr sites, with its overall architecture illustrated in Fig. 1. First, the lack of explicit lysine-centred biochemical priors in contextual protein representations is addressed by combining the Center-Specific Representation Enhancement (CSRE) module with Center-Anchored Bi-Mamba, enabling the sequence branch to emphasize physicochemical and positional information around the candidate lysine while retaining its flanking context. Structural uncertainty constitutes a second challenge, for which KAT-Net employs a predicted aligned error (PAE)–aware equivariant graph neural network that downweights unreliable residue relationships during structural message passing. The third challenge arises from the need to exploit the shared lysine-centred background without obscuring acylation-specific evidence. Task-driven hyperbolic routing therefore uses task anchors and centre-to-neighbourhood topology to retrieve modification-specific structural context from the shared sequence–structure representation. Finally, heterogeneous annotations, potential false negatives, and task-dependent class imbalance are handled by a masked asymmetric focal loss, which excludes unavailable labels and reduces the influence of easily classified negative samples during joint optimization. We evaluated KAT-Net through independent and external benchmarks, controlled ablations, and sequence–structure interpretation analyses.

Materials and methods

Datasets

KAT-Net used three public benchmarks: Kcr from DeepCap-Kcr [42], Ksucc from LMSuccSite [23], and Kac from TransPTM [24]. Because Kac development and primary benchmarking were based on the curated non-histone TransPTM dataset, we additionally evaluated independent non-histone Kac records from the Compendium of Protein Lysine Modifications (CPLM) [43] and dbPTM (a database of protein post-translational modifications) [44] to provide external comparisons. KAT-Net and the included baseline models were evaluated on the same redundancy-controlled external cohorts under consistent settings. Detailed dataset construction and comparison results are provided in Supplementary Section S1 and Tables S2 and S3. These external evaluations were restricted to non-histone Kac and therefore do not represent an assessment of histone acetylation or the complete acetylome.

Because these datasets were originally constructed for separate prediction tasks, directly combining them into a shared multi-task framework could introduce cross-task information leakage. In particular, a lysine-centred sequence assigned to the independent test set of one PTM could overlap with, or be highly similar to, a training sample from another PTM. We therefore performed a dedicated cross-task redundancy audit across the Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac development and independent-test sets. Lysine-centred windows were aligned by the candidate lysine, and both exact overlap and highly similar centre-aligned windows were examined. A task-specific training instance was removed when it showed at least 90% sequence identity and 80% coverage with an independent-test sample from any of the three tasks. This procedure removed 781 conflicting training instances while leaving all independent test sets unchanged. A subsequent audit confirmed that no window-level conflicts remained under this criterion. Detailed cross-task audit and filtering results are provided in Supplementary Section S2 and Table S4.

For joint training, each candidate lysine retained its available task-specific annotations for Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac, while annotations unavailable for the other tasks were treated as missing rather than converted to negative samples. Identical central 31-residue windows were grouped into the same cross-validation fold, and the cleaned development data were organized into fixed five-fold partitions. Further details of the multi-task label construction are provided in Supplementary Section S3.

After cross-task filtering, the development data contained 6803/5381 positive/negative Kcr labels, 4576/4374 Ksucc labels, and 761/4550 Kac labels. The corresponding independent test sets contained 2989/2989, 253/2973, and 150/942 positive/negative samples, respectively (Table 1).

PAE-aware structural encoding

The folded protein environment provides spatial information that cannot be fully represented by the linear sequence alone. Protein structures were predicted using ColabFold based on the AlphaFold2 framework [35, 38]. A consistent structural-processing pipeline was applied to all datasets to obtain residue coordinates, predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT), PAE, Dictionary of Protein Secondary Structure (DSSP)-derived relative solvent accessibility (RSA), three-state secondary structure (SS), and 64-bin distogram features. DSSP was used to derive RSA and three-state secondary-structure assignments. PLDDT and PAE describe residue- and pair-level confidence, respectively, whereas the remaining features characterize local geometry, solvent exposure, conformation, and pairwise spatial relationships [51, 52]. The same extraction procedure was used for the development, independent-test, and external datasets. Detailed preprocessing procedures and executable feature-extraction scripts are provided in the public GitHub repository.

Checkpoints were selected using equal-weight macro-averaged Matthews correlation coefficient (Macro-MCC) across Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac on the validation folds. The selected models generated pooled out-of-fold validation probabilities, from which Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC)-maximizing thresholds of 0.6071, 0.5561, and 0.4135 were fixed for Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac, respectively. Independent-test probabilities were averaged across the five checkpoints and classified using these frozen thresholds; independent and external test labels were never used for model, hyperparameter, checkpoint, or threshold selection.

Formal paired comparisons used exact two-sided McNemar tests with Holm correction, with 1000 paired stratified resampling repetitions used for robustness assessment (Supplementary Section S6; Table S8).

Results

Benchmark performance and external evaluation

To assess KAT-Net within established benchmarks, we compared it with task-specific predictors and DeepPTMPred [28] on independent Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac test sets. For Kac, Table 2 reports the curated non-histone TransPTM benchmark. To complement the TransPTM-based primary Kac benchmark, we additionally compared KAT-Net, TransPTM, and DeepPTMPred on independent, redundancy-controlled non-histone Kac cohorts derived from CPLM and dbPTM (Supplementary Section S1; Tables S2 and S3). CPLM was evaluated as a positive-only known-site recognition dataset, whereas dbPTM provided a binary-classification comparison. Across these external cohorts, the relative performance of KAT-Net and the included baseline models was further compared under different sequence-redundancy thresholds. Across the three primary benchmarks, KAT-Net had the highest point estimates for accuracy (ACC), MCC, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) (Table 2; Fig. 2A), with Ksucc remaining the most challenging task. We next evaluated Kcr transfer using the external datasets described in the Datasets section. Rice, papaya, and the independent human benchmark contained both positive and negative samples and therefore supported full binary-classification evaluation. In contrast, zebrafish, T. gondii, and lung cancer–related datasets contained experimentally reported positive sites only and were interpreted as tests of known-site recognition. KAT-Net maintained favourable Kcr discrimination or recognition across these biologically distinct sources, supporting transfer beyond the development benchmark while recognizing the more limited interpretation of positive-only datasets.

Ablation analysis and multi-task learning

To examine whether the main design choices of KAT-Net contribute meaningfully to prediction, we performed controlled ablation experiments covering the centre-aware sequence encoder, structural branch, confidence-aware structural modelling, and task-driven routing. The results are summarized in Fig. 2C and D, with detailed values provided in Supplementary Section S8 and Table S10.

Removing the sequence branch produced the largest overall degradation, and replacing Center-Anchored Bi-Mamba with conventional sequence encoders also reduced performance, indicating that lysine-centred sequence information provides the primary predictive basis. Removing the structural branch caused a smaller but consistent loss, supporting a complementary role for 3D context. PAE-aware encoding, hyperbolic routing, and the asymmetric loss provided additional, task-dependent contributions, with the structural and routing effects generally more evident for Kcr and Kac than for Ksucc. The complete model therefore relies primarily on sequence information while using structural context to refine task-specific predictions.

We further compared multi-task learning (MTL) with independently trained single-task models (STL). MTL produced task-dependent gains rather than uniform improvements across all metrics (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Table S11, Panel A). The largest benefit was observed for Kac, while Ksucc showed more moderate improvements. For Kcr, MTL improved ACC, MCC, AUC, F1-score, and recall but reduced precision, indicating a precision–recall trade-off rather than overall negative transfer. These results support the use of shared lysine-centred representations while showing that the benefit of joint learning differs among the three acylation tasks.

Structural topology and lysine-centred microenvironment analysis

Lysine acylation depends not only on residues adjacent to the target site in the primary sequence but also on the folded microenvironment, where sequence-distant residues may become spatially close to the candidate lysine. We therefore examined whether KAT-Net preserves this lysine-centred spatial organization during structural representation learning.

As shown in Fig. 3, the Poincaré projection of a representative lysine-centred structural microenvironment illustrates how residues surrounding the target lysine are organized in the learned latent space. To quantify this property more systematically, we evaluated topology preservation using the Spearman correlation between residue-wise physical distances to the target lysine and the corresponding latent-space distances. Hyperbolic routing achieved a higher mean topology-fidelity score than the controlled Euclidean-routing variant (0.715 versus 0.699; Δρ = 0.016), indicating that the hyperbolic formulation provides a modest but consistent advantage in preserving centre-to-neighbourhood spatial organization. Together, these results support the view that KAT-Net captures structurally meaningful lysine-centred context rather than relying only on linear sequence locality.

Sequence-level attribution and task-specific acylation patterns

Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac share the same lysine anchor, but their flanking sequence preferences may differ. We therefore examined whether KAT-Net learned task-related sequence determinants rather than relying only on the central lysine. To assess the relationship among the three prediction tasks, we calculated Spearman correlations using out-of-fold predicted probabilities, ensuring that each sample was evaluated by a fold-specific model that had not been trained on that sample (Fig. 4A). Kcr and Ksucc exhibited a strong positive correlation (ρ = 0.781), whereas their correlations with Kac were substantially weaker (Kcr–Kac, ρ = 0.275; Ksucc–Kac, ρ = 0.102; all BH-adjusted P < .001). These results indicate that Kcr and Ksucc share a relatively stronger prediction pattern, while Kac retains a more distinct task-specific output profile.

(B) Positional sensitivity analysis by in silico mutagenesis. (C) Two-sample residue-enrichment logos around the target lysine; upward and downward letters indicate enriched and depleted residues, respectively, and colours denote residue classes. We next performed in silico positional mutagenesis to identify which flanking positions contributed most to prediction. Each residue around the central lysine was individually replaced by alanine, and the decrease in predicted probability was recorded; a larger decrease indicates a stronger positional contribution. As shown in Fig. 4B, Ksucc showed a prominent sensitivity peak at the +1 position, Kcr was sensitive around +1 to +2 and the upstream −9 to −4 region, whereas Kac showed weaker and more dispersed positional effects. These results suggest that KAT-Net does not treat the peptide window uniformly but learns task-specific positional dependencies around the candidate lysine.

To further determine whether these positional effects correspond to distinct residue preferences, we combined residue-enrichment analysis with substitution attribution. The two-sample residue-enrichment logos compared modified and unmodified sites to identify residues enriched or depleted around the target lysine (Fig. 4C). Kac showed broader enrichment of basic residues near the lysine centre, while Kcr and Ksucc displayed more localized charged-residue changes. The substitution attribution maps then evaluated how replacing residues with different amino acid types affected model prediction (Fig. 4D). Strong attribution signals were concentrated around the lysine-centred region, but the sensitive substitutions differed among Kcr, Ksucc, and Kac. Together, these analyses show that KAT-Net captures both position-specific and residue-type-specific sequence rules for different acylation tasks.

Finally, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization was used to examine whether the learned representations organized these task-related signals into separable prediction spaces. Raw input features showed substantial overlap between positive and negative samples, whereas KAT-Net representations and final classification outputs formed clearer class-related structures in both training and test sets (Fig. 4E). Overall, these results indicate that KAT-Net learns interpretable local sequence determinants and transforms them into task-relevant representations for lysine acylation prediction.

External functional plausibility analysis using independent acetylome and succinylome data

We examined functional organization using the independent PXD049146 mouse lung acetylome/succinylome dataset [55]. Experimentally identified Kac and Ksucc sites were analysed with frozen KAT-Net, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment of predicted-positive proteins used the 6857 quantified proteins as background. This analysis assessed functional plausibility rather than binary external validation of general Kac-site discrimination.

GO enrichment was performed against the 6857 proteins quantified in the same proteomic study, rather than the whole mouse proteome. Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC) and Molecular Function (MF) were analysed separately with Benjamini–Hochberg correction and adjusted. Kac-positive proteins were enriched in chromatin-related and metabolic functions, including heterochromatin/nucleosome organization, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)/CoA-related metabolism, fatty acid beta-oxidation, and cellular respiration. Ksucc-positive proteins were mainly associated with cytoskeletal organization, cell adhesion, homeostasis, and catabolic processes (Fig. 5). Compared with PXD049146, Kac predictions showed partial concordance, particularly for fatty acid beta-oxidation and related metabolic processes, whereas Ksucc did not reproduce the major immune- and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)-related categories. Thus, the observed concordance provides complementary functional plausibility evidence, particularly for Kac, without implying mechanistic validation.

Discussion and conclusion

KAT-Net was developed to address a central difficulty in lysine acylation prediction: Kac, Ksucc, and Kcr share the same lysine-centred substrate background, but their distinct acyl-group chemistries require different sequence and structural evidence for discrimination. Rather than treating these modifications as either completely independent tasks or homogeneous outputs of a shared predictor, KAT-Net combines centre-aware sequence modelling, confidence-aware structural representation, and task-specific routing within a unified framework. The key value of this design is therefore not simply the integration of multiple feature types but the ability to exploit information shared among related lysine acylations while retaining modification-specific evidence.

The computational results provide consistent support for this design and help clarify where the predictive advantage of KAT-Net originates. Its performance on the independent benchmarks, together with the paired McNemar analyses, indicates that the improvement is not restricted to a particular aggregate metric but is also reflected in the classification of individual sites. More importantly, the controlled analyses show that the different information sources do not contribute equally. Lysine-centred sequence context provides the principal discriminative basis, whereas structural information adds complementary evidence when the spatial arrangement and local microenvironment around the candidate residue become informative. This task-dependent contribution also explains why simply adding structural features does not produce uniform gains across all three acylations. A similar pattern is observed in multi-task learning. Kac benefits most clearly from shared information, whereas Ksucc shows a more moderate response and Kcr exhibits a redistribution between sensitivity and precision, indicating that transfer among related PTMs is useful but inherently asymmetric. The persistent difficulty of Ksucc after controlling for class composition further suggests that its prediction challenge cannot be attributed to imbalance alone. Greater heterogeneity in sequence–structure patterns, the relatively limited number of positive observations and uncertainty in source-defined negative annotations may all reduce its separability. Taken together, these findings support a modelling strategy in which related PTMs share a common representation but retain task-specific decision mechanisms, rather than being learned either in isolation or under unrestricted feature sharing.

This shared-but-distinct relationship also has broader biological significance. Lysine acylations modify the same ε-amino group but introduce chemically different acyl moieties, which can alter local charge, steric properties, and molecular interactions in different ways. It is therefore reasonable that related modifications share part of their substrate context while diverging in the regulatory environments and cellular processes with which they are associated. The functional patterns observed in the external acetylome and succinylome analysis are consistent with this view. KAT-Net-positive Kac proteins were associated with metabolic processes including fatty acid beta-oxidation, showing correspondence with metabolic changes reported in the experimental study, whereas Ksucc predictions were organized around a different set of processes involving cytoskeletal organization, cell adhesion, and cellular homeostasis. The weaker functional correspondence observed for Ksucc is itself informative, as it is consistent with the greater heterogeneity also suggested by its predictive behaviour. These observations imply that prediction of lysine PTMs is not simply a matter of recognizing a common lysine motif; the computational problem is to separate residue-centred determinants that can be shared across modifications from modification-specific structural and functional context. This provides a biological rationale for the joint yet task-aware formulation of KAT-Net and also explains why accurate PTM prediction can be useful beyond benchmark classification: it can help connect candidate modification sites with potentially relevant biological processes and narrow the space of sites that warrant further experimental investigation. Such computational associations should not be interpreted as direct evidence of molecular mechanism, but they provide a practical bridge between large-scale site prediction and downstream biological validation.

Several limitations remain, including the additional computational cost of the structural branch (Supplementary Section S10; Tables S15 and S16), uncertainty in predicted structural information, and the incompleteness and condition dependence of current PTM annotations. For Kac, KAT-Net was developed and primarily benchmarked using the curated non-histone acetylation dataset established in TransPTM. Additional evaluations using independent non-histone Kac datasets from CPLM and dbPTM were conducted to provide an external comparison of KAT-Net and the baseline models on additional acetylation data (Supplementary Section S1). These analyses complement the primary benchmark while maintaining the scope of the present study within non-histone acetylation rather than histone acetylation or the complete acetylome. The independent PXD049146 analysis provides complementary functional evidence by examining the biological relevance of KAT-Net-predicted proteins and was designed as a functional analysis rather than a binary benchmark for general Kac-site discrimination. Beyond expanding benchmark resources, future studies will integrate large-scale mass spectrometry datasets with computational approaches to systematically investigate the relationships among Kac, Ksucc, and Kcr. Such analyses may reveal shared and modification-specific regulatory patterns, providing deeper insights into lysine acylation beyond individual site prediction. The framework may also be explored for other lysine modifications, including lysine methylation (Kme) and additional acylation types; however, these extensions will require harmonized annotations, explicit definition of modification states, task-specific model retraining, and independent validation rather than direct application of the current predictor. By combining experimental proteomic evidence with sequence–structure representations, future studies may further advance PTM research towards joint modelling of modification occurrence, modification-type discrimination, and prioritization of biologically relevant PTM events for experimental validation.
