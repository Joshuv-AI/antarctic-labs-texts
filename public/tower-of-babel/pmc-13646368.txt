Dietary Soybean vs. Palm Oils Differ in Effects on Liver Fat in Adults With MAFLD: A 16‐Week, Double‐Masked, Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Martha A. Belury, Rachel M. Cole, Ai Ni, Arunark Kolipaka, Manjunathan Nanjappa, Kristin Thompson, Ali Kalhori, Stephan Zarich, Rolando E. Choriego, Rafael Jimenez‐Flores, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDietary linoleic acid (LA)‐rich oils reduce liver fat in healthy adults who are not otherwise undergoing weight loss strategies. Effects on the liver fat in adults with fatty liver disease are unknown.ObjectiveThe objective of this randomized double‐masked controlled trial was to compare the effects of a dietary soybean oil (SO) which is high in LA vs. palm oil (PO) which is composed of a mixture of saturated, mono‐ and polyunsaturated fats, on changes of liver fat level in adults with MAFLD.MethodsAdults with MAFLD were recruited to complete a double‐masked, randomized controlled trial consisting of a 2‐week run‐in period followed by a 16‐week dietary intervention. Study foods contained either SO or PO to deliver 30 g oils per day. At week 0 and week 16, liver fat and visceral adipose tissue were measured by 1H‐magnetic resonance spectroscopy imaging and fasting blood was collected for secondary biochemical markers of cardiometabolic disease risk.ResultsSixty‐five adults with MAFLD (mean age ~53 y, BMI 36 kg/m2) completed the study. Overall, there was a significant difference in the change of liver fat between SO and PO groups; however, after adjusting for baseline liver fat the diet group differences were no longer significant (p = 0.14). The direct effect of diet group on the change of liver fat was not mediated by weight change (p = 0.11). For within group change, liver fat tended to decrease in SO group (p = 0.11) and tended to increase in PO group (p = 0.11).ConclusionsThere was no significant difference for the effect of PO over SO on liver fat change when controlling for baseline liver fat or weight change. Larger trials testing dietary strategies that reduce liver fat in adults with MAFLD in the context of weight change are needed.Trail RegistrationThe WELL study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05199948).

Introduction

Approximately one in four adults in the United States have metabolic dysfunction associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), a condition diagnosed as lipids > 5% of the liver and accompanying central obesity, insulin resistance, and atherogenic dyslipidemia [1]. Lifestyle modifications with a goal of weight loss remain as the centrepiece therapy for reducing hepatic steatosis. In addition, pharmaceutical and/or bariatric surgery may be useful for reducing ectopic hepatic lipids in some patients [2, 3]. A weight loss of > 10% of original body weight leads to significant reductions in steatosis [4] and reduces risk for the comorbidities of MAFLD, for example, cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease. In addition, results from a meta‐analysis of 43 clinical interventions using weight loss to reduce hepatic steatosis suggests that the effects of weight loss may be dose‐responsive: A higher magnitude of weight loss may lead to a more dramatic reduction of liver lipid content [5].

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) improve glycemic control and lead to rapid weight loss in many people with pre‐diabetes and type 2 diabetes. Most, but not all, clinical interventions using GLP‐1 RA in adults with obesity reveal a reduction in hepatic steatosis [6, 7]. However, it is unclear whether the mechanisms that GLP‐1 RA reduce liver steatosis require concurrent weight loss since the reduction of hepatic steatosis by GLP‐1 agonist treatment occurs concurrently with significant body weight reduction [3]. Recent recommendations from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology, include an option to use GLP‐1 RA as therapy for weight loss and reduction of hepatic steatosis [2, 3].

In observational studies, higher blood levels of the essential fatty acid, linoleic acid (LA; 18:2n6), are associated with lower liver fat and reduced incidence of MAFLD in cross‐sectional and prospective observational clinical studies [8, 9, 10]. In addition, clinical intervention trials testing the effect of LA‐rich oils on change in liver fat have revealed LA decreases ectopic liver fat in people with overweight [11] and prevented hepatic fat accumulation in participants enrolled in two different “weight gain” clinical trials where LA‐rich oils were compared to saturated fats [12, 13]. Even with a growing body of evidence for a protective role of LA against cardiometabolic disease risk and ectopic fat accumulation in the liver, there are no studies, to our knowledge, evaluating whether dietary LA differs with saturated fat sources in changing ectopic lipid levels in the liver in individuals with MAFLD.

The condition of MAFLD is characterized by insulin resistance [14] and systemic inflammation [15, 16] and is a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [17]. There is a clear association between dietary or biomarker levels of LA and reduced risk for insulin resistance [18, 19, 20, 21], systemic inflammation [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Therefore, biochemical markers related to cardiovascular diseases, insulin sensitivity, and systemic inflammation were measured for differences between dietary treatments and changes within diet groups.

The primary aim of this study was to compare LA‐rich soybean oil (SO) with a mixed fatty acid oil, palm oil (PO), on changes of ectopic fat in liver fat content in adults with MAFLD. Secondary objectives were to measure the difference between SO and PO diet treatments on the change of visceral adipose and plasma markers of insulin resistance and inflammation. As exploratory objectives, we also evaluated the effect modification of baseline GLP‐1 RA usage on the effects of diet group as well as the direct effects of diet group on liver fat and co‐morbid outcomes not mediated by post‐randomization weight change.

Methods

This 18‐week randomized double‐masked placebo‐controlled parallel arm study was approved by the Institutional Research Board (2021H0290) at The Ohio State University and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05199948). The study consisted of a 2‐week Run‐In Period followed by the 16‐week Diet Intervention Period. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) data capture tools hosted at The Ohio State University [27].

Recruitment

Adults with MAFLD, NASH or a similar liver condition, age 22–80 years old, BMI 20–55 kg/m2 were recruited from the central Ohio area [1, 28]. In addition to radio and printed advertising, information about the study was provided to health care providers at OSU medical clinics. Retrospective analysis of medical records was also used to identify potential participants who had received a diagnosis of NASH. Recruitment for the study also included ResearchMatch, a national health volunteer registry that was created by several academic institutions and supported by the U.S. National Institutes of Health as part of the Clinical Translational Science Award (CTSA) program. Potential participants were informed of the purpose and study procedure then, if interested, were screened using a screening questionnaire over the phone. Eligible patients who wished to enrol submitted electronic informed consent to participate [29]. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are reported in Table S1.

At the first study visit (week −2), participants were asked to complete a descriptive questionnaire asking about age, gender, sex, race, ethnicity, and health status. To measure dietary intake prior to the study enrolment, participants completed the Diet History Questionnaire III, a food frequency questionnaire developed by the National Cancer Institute staff [30, 31]. At each study visit, data were collected on any changes in prescription medications and dietary supplement usage.

Dietary Intervention

A two‐week run‐in period was used to guide individualized dietetic counselling for food incorporation with an aim to maintain body weight throughout the full intervention. Participants recorded how many study foods were consumed each day in a study food log. Each study food contained 10 g of oil and participants were instructed to consume 3 foods per day providing a total of 30 g of oil per day. After completing the run‐in period, participants were invited to continue for the 16‐week Diet Intervention Period. Visits week 0 and week 16 occurred at the Clinical Research Center (OSU Wexner Medical Center, Columbus, OH) where vital signs, blood collection, and questionnaire data were collected. At each visit, the study team verbally asked participants about issues related to consuming study foods, changes to diet or activity, and changes to medications or supplements. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) measurements were obtained at weeks 0 and 16 on a separate day from blood collection days.

Randomization and Test for Masking Success

If the two‐week run‐in period was successfully completed and participants indicated willingness to continue in the study, each participant was randomized to either the PO or SO diet group by a dedicated product coordinator who did not interact with the participants using computer‐generated random numbers program produced by a statistician student outside the clinical research team. Randomization schemes consisted of blocks of two or four and stratified by sex (male or female) and BMI (≤ 34 and ≥ 35). Product coordinators also allocated study foods.

As a double‐masked study, neither participants nor the study team members that interacted with participants and analysed data (study coordinator, dietitians, doctor, statistician, principal investigator) were told of the diet group assignment for each participant. To maintain the double‐masked nature of the study, study foods were designed to look similar and were packaged in coloured bags or opaque containers. The Bang blinding test [32] was administered to both study participants and clinical research team at the week 16 visit to determine if unmasking occurred during the study [33, 34].

Study Foods

The typical fatty acid profile of adults in the United States that is approximately 40% en monounsaturated fatty acids, 35% en saturated fatty acids, 25% en polyunsaturated fatty acids in adults [35]; therefore, PO was chosen due to a similar distribution pattern. For the run‐in period, study foods were prepared with a mixture of vegetable shortening: high‐oleic safflower oil (ratio of 4:13). For the 16‐week intervention period, study foods were prepared with PO or SO at the OSU research kitchen and at a local bakery (The Original Goodie Shop, Columbus, OH). Nutrient composition of the study foods is provided in Table S2.

Dietary and Physical Activity Measurements

Because weight loss may decrease liver fat content [36], participants were encouraged to avoid weight changes for the duration of the intervention by maintaining habitual energy intake and physical activity levels. Dietary intake was measured at three separate days during the intervention using the Automated Self‐Administered 24‐h (ASA24) Dietary Assessment Tool (NIH National Cancer Institute). Research dietitians consulted with participants to discuss strategies to avoid weight changes by substituting study foods in exchange for habitually consumed foods of similar food type. A weight change > 2.0 kg in a two‐week interval or ≥ 4.0 kg in a four‐week interval prompted the study dietitians to review with participants the goal for weight neutrality and additional counseling to discuss options for substituting the study foods in place of habitual foods.

Preparation for Clinical Research Visits

In preparation for clinical visits at week 0 and week 16, participants were provided all meals and snacks for the day prior to the clinical visits as described [37]. Meals and snacks were composed of ~30% fat, 20% protein and 50% carbohydrate [38, 39] and included the three study foods from their diet group assignment. Participants were instructed to avoid taking blood pressure, glucose‐lowering, lipid/cholesterol‐lowering and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory medications on the morning of the clinical visit, to avoid alcohol the day prior, to avoid strenuous exercise 2 days prior and to abstain from taking dietary supplements and aspirin for 7 days prior to the visit.

Liver Fat and Stiffness Imaging

Liver fat was measured using MRI and liver stiffness was measured using MRE using a 3 T MRI scanner (Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a rapid gradient echo MRE sequence [40] for liver stiffness. The MRE passive driver (Resoundant Inc., Rochester, MN USA) was placed on the upper abdomen at the fifth intercostal ribcage to ensure proximity and better propagation of mechanical waves into the liver [41]. Scout images of sagittal and axial views were acquired to verify the placement of the driver. MRE imaging parameters were: FOV: 320 mm2; TE: 14.7 ms; TR: 25 ms; slice thickness: 6 mm; acquisition matrix: 128 × 64; GRAPPA: 2; mechanical excitation frequency: 60 Hz. MRE images were post‐processed using a multi‐model direct inversion algorithm (MMDI) [42] to calculate the stiffness maps.

A multi‐echo Dixon Vibe sequence [43] was used to obtain entire coverage of the liver for fat quantification using following parameters: FOV: 420mm2, TE: 1.1 ms; TR: 9.15 ms; slice thickness: 3.5 mm; acquisition matrix: 128 × 128. Prior to MRI scans, participants were instructed to fast for at least 3 h and to drink water during the fasting period.

Anthropometry and Visceral Adipose

In‐person weight and height were measured without shoes using a digital scale and wall mounted stadiometer respectively. Visceral adipose tissue volume was calculated from the MRI liver images using Multi‐echo Dixon Vibe covering the entire liver in each participant. The number of pixels in proton density fat fraction (PDFF) images was calculated by excluding abdominal wall, internal organs, and spinal cord structures using in‐house custom‐built software in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA), using a threshold‐based image segmentation method.

Cardiometabolic Disease Markers

Levels of liver enzymes, γ‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), fasting lipids, glucose and insulin levels were measured as described previously [39]. The homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated using the equation from Matthews et al. [44], and the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) was calculated according to Lee et al. [45]. Oxidized LDL (oxLDL) was measured by ELISA (Mercodia, Uppsala Sweden).

Sample Size and Statistics

The required sample size for adequate power was calculated based on the primary outcome—group difference for the change in liver fat content. We aimed to detect a difference of 1.63% points of change of ectopic hepatic fat between the PO and SO groups based on a previous study [13] where the standard deviation was assumed to be 2.0% points. With a two‐sample t‐test, a total of 58 participants (29 per diet group) would ensure 87% power with 0.05 two‐sided type‐I error. Assuming a 20% dropout rate, a total of 74 participants (37 per diet group) were recruited. As the secondary outcome, assuming a standard deviation of change of visceral adipose tissue would be 0.30L [13], the above sample size would detect a difference of 0.25 L in visceral adipose tissue change between PO and SO groups with 89% power and 0.05 two‐sided type‐I error using a two‐sample t‐test.

Descriptive statistics were generated for baseline variables (Table 1) of the completers by diet group to assess their balance. After unblinded allocations of participants, it was revealed that one person randomized to the SO group received study foods with PO for the entirety of the study. Since the reason for the mis‐assignment of treatment is unrelated to the study outcomes, a per protocol analysis is not expected to introduce any bias into the analysis. Therefore, all data analyses included this participant as “received” (for PO) and not as “allocated”. As such, we did not use intent‐to‐treat but rather used per protocol in all data analyses.

Continuous variables were described by mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were described by frequency and percentage. The ASA 24 dietary intake data were compared between diet groups overall and further among GLP‐1 users and nonusers using Wilcoxon rank sum tests. The unadjusted effects of dietary group were estimated by comparing changes of outcome variables from baseline to week 16 between PO and SO groups using Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Baseline‐outcome‐adjusted dietary group effects were estimated from linear regressions with changes of outcomes as the dependent variable and study groups and baseline outcome as the covariates. To control for the potential confounding effect of baseline GLP‐1 usage, linear regressions were fit with changes of outcomes as the dependent variable and study group, baseline outcome, and baseline GLP‐1 usage as covariates. To adjust for the variability of weight change across dietary groups, linear regressions were fit with changes of outcomes as the dependent variable and study group, baseline outcome, and weight change as covariates. Since weight change is a post‐randomization variable, adjusting it in the regression model allowed us to assess the direct effect of diet group on outcome change not mediated by weight change. Subjects with missing follow up outcomes were excluded from the analysis. As a sensitivity analysis, we imputed the missing outcomes by five multiple imputations with chained equations and refit the regression models. Subgroup analysis by GLP‐1 user/GLP‐1 nonuser was derived from the linear regression models with an interaction term between GLP‐1 usage and study groups. For a given outcome, only participants with both baseline and week 16 data available were included in the analysis of that outcome. All statistical tests were two sided. The significance level was at 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted in R 4.5.1 [46].

Results

Participants

Eighty‐four participants were enrolled and began the run‐in period of the study, with 65 participants completing the full 18‐week trial that occurred between May 2022 and December 2023 (Figure 1). Five participants withdrew from the study during the run‐in period due to time restrictions (n = 2), personal reasons (n = 2), or refusal to consume study foods (n = 1). Seventy‐nine participants were randomized to the PO (n = 41) or SO (n = 38) group. After completion of the intervention, unblinding of study food codes revealed that one person in SO group had received PO foods for the 16‐week intervention. Therefore, this person was treated in all data analysis under PO group as “received” and not as “allocated”. Of the 65 adults who completed the full study (run‐in period and diet intervention period), n = 36 received PO foods and n = 29 received SO foods. Baseline characteristics did not differ between the two dietary treatment groups (Table 1). In addition, when stratified by GLP‐1 RA usage, baseline data remained similar between diet groups (Table S3).

Flow of participants throughout 18‐week trial per protocol. PO: palm oil; SO: soybean oil.

Dietary Intake During Intervention and Masking Success

At least three ASA24 recalls were administered to each participant. Total energy, carbohydrate, fat, or protein intakes were not different between diet groups (Table S4). Participants were instructed to keep study food logs to tally their daily intake as a self‐reported measurement of adherence. According to food diaries, the average percentage of adherence weighted by the number of returned logs was 93.1% (SD 8.0%) in PO group and 90.6% (SD 13.5%) in the SO group. Adherence did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.42). In addition to keeping food diaries, plasma levels of LA and α‐linolenic acid were measured at week 0 and week 16 as biomarkers for adherence (Table S5). Changes in plasma LA and α‐linolenic acid were significantly different between the dietary treatment groups (p < 0.01): Plasma LA increased by ~10% (p < 0.01) and α‐linolenic acid increased by ~15% in participants in the SO group (p < 0.01). In the PO group, plasma LA did not change while α‐linolenic acid decreased significantly (p < 0.01).

As a double masked randomized controlled trial, we analysed masking success using the Bang blinding index [32]. The general range of the Bang index for “successful” blinding is −0.2 to 0.2 [32]. For study participants, the Bang blinding index for the PO group was 0.028 (95% CI −0.15 to 0.21) and for the SO group was 0.034 (95% CI, −0.21 to 0.28) (Table S6). Therefore, the calculated Bang indices indicate successful masking for participants in both diet groups. For the clinical research study team, the Bang blinding index for the PO group was 0.028 (95% CI −0.026 to 0.081) and for the SO group was 0.00 (95% CI, −0.089 to 0.089). Using the Bang blinding index, the double masking of study foods for the PO and SO foods was successful.

Body Weight

Because weight change affects hepatic steatosis in adults with MAFLD [3], a goal of this study was for participants to maintain weight neutrality during the dietary intervention. The large variance of body weight change during the intervention period was unexpected. Fourteen adults had been using GLP‐1 RA for at least 1 month prior to consenting to participate in the study; these participants continued GLP‐1 RA use throughout the study. However, there was not a difference in overall energy intake between GLP‐1 RA user and GLP‐1 RA nonuser groups. Additionally, there was not a difference between energy intake by diet group assignment. Expectedly, there was not a diet group difference in weight change either overall or by baseline GLP‐1 RA usage. However, the near‐significant interaction between baseline GLP‐1 RA usage and diet group (p = 0.07; Figure 2, panel A) suggested a larger diet group difference in body weight change in the GLP‐1 RA users than in the nonusers. In a subgroup analysis of GLP‐1 RA nonusers, adults receiving PO foods gained 0.98 kg body weight (p = 0.038).

With the small groups for GLP‐1 users (n = 7 PO, n = 7 SO) and high variance of weight change, it was not surprising that there was not a significant change in either diet group of GLP‐1 RA users for weight change. By combining diet groups, GLP‐1 RA users lost weight (−3.12 kg, SD 6.0 kg) whereas GLP‐1 RA nonusers slightly gained weight (0.75 kg, SD 3.2 kg), (p = 0.016 comparing users and nonusers).

Liver Fat, Stiffness and Iron Content and Visceral Adipose Volume

In the overall cohort, the unadjusted change of liver fat in the diet treatment groups was significantly different (p = 0.03; Table 2). Liver fat trended toward an increase in the PO group (+1.0%; 95% CI −0.83 to 2.83%, p = 0.11) but trended toward a decrease in the SO group (−1.3%; 95% CI −3.37 to 0.77%, p = 0.11). When baseline liver fat was included in the analyses, difference between diet groups became non‐significant (p = 0.14, Table 2). For exploratory purposes, when weight change was adjusted in the model, the direct effect of diet group not mediated via weight change also was non‐significant (p = 0.11, Table 2). When baseline GLP‐1 RA usage was added to the model, but not weight change, the difference between diet groups was non‐significant (p = 0.20, Table 2). Multiple imputation of the missing follow up outcomes produced very similar regression results with the same conclusions.

As further exploration of diet effects in GLP‐1 nonusers separately, there was a significant diet group difference on liver fat (p = 0.006), which remained significant with adjustment for baseline liver fat (p = 0.016): Participants in PO group gained 2.2% (95% CI 0.60 to 3.80%) liver fat (p = 0.02) while adults in SO group showed a marginal decrease of 1.3% (95% CI −0.58 to 3.18%) liver fat (p = 0.08) (Figure 2, Panel B).

There were no significant differences between dietary treatments for changes in liver stiffness, liver iron or visceral adipose volume (Table 2). When group differences were analysed solely in the GLP‐1 RA nonusers for exploratory purposes, there were no differences for liver stiffness while liver iron and visceral adipose volume increased significantly in the PO group (p < 0.05; Figure 2, Panels C, D and E).

The change of HSI, which is calculated using sex, ALT, AST, and BMI in the calculation, did not differ between diet groups overall (Table 2) but increased significantly in GLP‐1 RA nonusers in the PO group (p = 0.03; Figure 2, Panel F) with no change in the GLP‐1 nonusers in SO group.

Biochemical Markers of Cardiometabolic Disease Risk

The changes of markers of cardiometabolic disease did not differ by dietary treatment (Table 3). There was a difference in the change of AST between PO and SO treatments (p = 0.04). AST did not change in the PO group but was significantly reduced within the SO group after 16 weeks (p = 0.036). There were no differences between dietary groups or between GLP‐1 RA users and GLP‐1 RA nonusers for a change of hepatic enzyme biochemical markers, ALT and GGT. There were no significant differences between diet groups overall for fasting glucose, fasting insulin, or HOMA‐IR.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare two commonly consumed dietary fats on a change of liver fat content in adults with MAFLD. Previously, studies had shown an inverse relationship between blood levels of LA and the prevalence of fatty liver disease in adults [8, 10, 47, 48, 49]. In the Young Finns Study, higher LA reduced the odds of developing MAFLD while adjusting for age and sex (OR 0.58; p < 0.003) in a 10‐year prospective study [10]. Associations remained significant even after adjusting for obesity and alcohol intake in this cohort of adults between 24 and 39 years of age (OR 0.63, p = 0.007). In contrast, the main fatty acids in palm oil, for example, palmitic and oleic acids, may increase risk for fatty liver: Plasma palmitic and oleic acids were associated with greater risk for incidence of fatty liver (OR 1.64; OR 1.58 respectively) [10].

In participants who completed the full 16‐week intervention, unadjusted changes of liver fat were significantly different between the two diet groups (p = 0.032). Incorporating SO into the diet trended toward a decrease while PO trended toward an increase in liver fat equating to a net difference of 2.3% fat over the 16‐week diet period. These results add to accumulating evidence that compared to saturated fat sources, LA‐rich oils do not promote, and may in fact reduce, liver fat [11, 12, 13]. In one study, fortifying the diet with LA‐rich oil at ~15% of kcal per day for 10 weeks reduced liver fat −7% to −9% (%units liver fat) whereas saturated fat increased liver fat +7% to +9% in adults with obesity in a weight neutral study [11].

Our goal was to fortify diets of adults with MAFLD with an additional 15 g LA per day using study foods that supplied an added 354 kcal to 828 kcal per day. In order to help participants not gain weight, the research team worked with participants to develop dietary strategies aimed at substituting study foods into diets in replacement of foods of similar types. Participants were also instructed to monitor body weights throughout the study with a goal of maintaining (not gaining or losing) body weight as an a priori goal of the study. Nevertheless, there was a large variability of food choices from the study food categories which may have complicated choices by participants thereby adding energy (kilocalories) between week 0 and week 16 (Figure S1). It is likely that some, but not all, participants successfully replaced habitual foods with research study foods. Overall energy intake did not differ between diet groups as a whole or between diet groups when analysed separately within GLP‐1 RA users or within GLP‐1 nonusers.

Even though average energy intake was not different between dietary oil treatments, more than half (55%) of participants changed body weight by > 2 kg between week 0 and week 16. Additionally, the direction of weight change was not uniform: Twenty‐three participants gained > 2 kg while 13 participants lost > 2 kg during the intervention. The large variability of weight change may have obscured an effect of diet group on liver fat change. Since we hypothesized that SO would exert its effect on liver fat change independently of weight change, we estimated the direct effect of SO in comparison to PO that is not mediated by weight change by fitting a regression model controlling for post‐randomization weight change [50]. This direct effect (−1.98% points) is close to statistical significance (p = 0.11, Table 2). Notably, the study was powered based on an unadjusted two‐group comparison instead of a regression model controlling for weight change suggesting the possibility that a direct effect of SO vs. PO on liver fat change could have reached significance had the study had a larger sample size.

The variability of weight change regardless of GLP‐1 agonist usage and study food group assignment raises the possibility that some, but not all, participants were iso‐energetically substituting the three servings of study foods per day into their diets by replacing other foods. Unfortunately, dietary intake prior to the run‐in period was measured using the Diet History Questionnaire III and during the intervention 24 h recalls (ASA24) were used. The difference in dietary assessment tools makes it difficult to assess whether neutral energy intake was maintained since each dietary assessment tool has different limitations for estimating energy intake. It was, therefore, not possible to analyse a change in energy intake after beginning to consume study foods.

In recent years, GLP‐1 RA usage has increased in adults for medical management of type 2 diabetes and for treating obesity. Beyond their well‐established effects on appetite suppression and reduced caloric intake, GLP‐1 RAs exert important metabolic actions on lipid handling and hepatic fat metabolism. A growing body of evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies demonstrates that GLP‐1 RAs can improve hepatic steatosis, reduce necroinflammation, and may even attenuate fibrosis in patients with steatohepatitis [51, 52, 53]. Twenty‐two percent of participants were using GLP‐1 RA for managing diabetes or weight at their time of enrolment into the study. The trends for different responses to diet treatments appeared to be dependent on GLP‐1 RA usage (GLP‐1 User and GLP‐1 Nonuser; Figure 2). In GLP‐1 RA nonusers, the significant increases of body weight, liver fat, liver stiffness and visceral adipose volume in the PO group add to evidence from a previous randomized controlled trial showing that saturated fat increases central obesity [54] perhaps adding evidence that saturated fat increases risk for cardiometabolic diseases [55, 56].

Biochemical markers of liver inflammation (e.g., ALT, AST, and GGT) have also been inversely associated with blood or dietary levels of LA in adults [57]. In a 16‐week intervention in women with type 2 diabetes, daily fortification with ~6.2 g of linoleic acid (delivered in safflower oil gel capsules) decreased AST significantly [58]. In the present study, LA decreased AST in the overall cohort regardless of GLP‐1 RA usage. We are unaware of other randomized controlled trials showing LA fortification to decrease liver enzyme markers.

Dietary LA intake or biomarkers of LA intake have no association or are inversely associated with markers of systemic inflammation markers [59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70]. In a previous randomized controlled cross trial, we found that LA‐fortified diets decreased C‐reactive protein in women with type 2 diabetes [71]. In this study, relative effects of SO or PO on C‐reactive protein were not different; yet, IL‐6 responses to SO or PO were significantly different where SO decreased IL‐6 by ~10% after 16 weeks.

Dyslipidemia is common in adults with MAFLD. SO and PO treatments had different effects on total‐ and LDL‐cholesterol. The decrease of LDL‐ and total‐cholesterol by PO treatment was unexpected; however, several studies are in agreement with these findings, which may depend on the fat source of saturated fats which may increase or have no effect on atherogenic lipids [72]. When GLP‐1 RA nonusers were analysed separately, PO treatment increased LDL‐cholesterol, and SO had no effect. Numerous studies have shown that polyunsaturated oils decrease total and LDL‐cholesterol [72, 73].

A central mechanism to explain the combined health effects of LA on heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and MAFLD has been elusive. Purported mechanisms of dietary LA to reduce risk for cardiovascular diseases have long been attributed to reducing cholesterol synthesis in the liver. Mechanisms of LA to reduce risk for type 2 diabetes may be through effects on improving whole body insulin sensitivity [71, 74, 75, 76] and may also be linked with an association of higher LA and reduced obesity [77, 78] and ectopic liver fat [9, 47, 79, 80, 81] may be related to other mechanisms involving reducing insulin‐mediated de novo lipid synthesis and/or increased lipid oxidative phosphorylation through enhanced mitochondrial β‐oxidation of lipids [82].

This study has several limitations. Participants exhibited highly variable body weight changes. Factors that might have led to variable weight changes may have included the prevalence of GLP‐1 RA usage (~20% participants) and wide range of foods delivering oils which may have contributed to failed strategies for substituting study foods in replacement of habitual diet choices. A second limitation is the lack of power to interrogate whether GLP‐1 RA usage may have mediated the change of liver fat by dietary oils. Therefore, the sub‐group analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and will require larger cohorts for more thorough analyses. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the potential for complex interactions between dietary fat composition and GLP‐1RA use in the pathophysiology and management of MAFLD. Finally, this study duration of 16 weeks is relatively short for objectives of observing changes of liver fat in weight neutral cohorts. Pharmacological interventions often span 24 weeks or longer for evaluating impacts on liver fat changes.

In summary, observational studies consistently show that higher blood levels of LA (18:2n6) are associated with reduced risk for heart disease [83, 84, 85, 86], smaller waist circumference or visceral adipose [78, 87, 88], better glycemic control and insulin sensitivity [18, 89, 90], and reduced risk for type 2 diabetes [18, 21, 90, 91, 92]. In randomized controlled clinical interventions, supplementing the diet with LA‐rich oils reduces liver fat, visceral adipose and/or trunk adipose in people with or without type 2 diabetes [11, 12, 13, 54, 58, 71]. The findings from the present trial add further credence for LA‐rich oils to reduce risk for liver fat in adults, which may ultimately lower risk for cardiometabolic diseases. With increasing usage of GLP‐1 RA agonists for diabetes and weight management, future randomized controlled trials are needed to determine the relative effects of fat quality to reduce liver fat.
