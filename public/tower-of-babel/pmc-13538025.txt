Burnout Syndrome Among Turkish Otolaryngologists Performing Rhinoplasty Surgery
Authors: Erkan Yıldız, Hüseyin Ruhullah Ercan, Orhan Kemal Kahveci, Abdulkadir Bucak, Fatih Çelenk, Selçuk Kuzu, Çağlar Günebakan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesBurnout syndrome represents a significant concern among physicians, particularly in surgical specialties. This study investigated burnout prevalence among Turkish otolaryngologists and examined the relationship between rhinoplasty practice and burnout dimensions.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted from December 2024 to February 2025, distributing 200 surveys to Turkish otolaryngologists, of whom 84 (42%) completed the validated Turkish 22‐item Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and a sociodemographic questionnaire. Statistical analyses included independent t‐tests, one‐way ANOVA, and correlation analyses.ResultsEighty‐four otolaryngologists participated (mean age 38.2 years; 88.1% male). Overall burnout prevalence was low (7.1%). Sixty‐four participants (76.2%) performed rhinoplasty surgery. Rhinoplasty surgeons demonstrated significantly higher personal accomplishment scores versus non‐rhinoplasty surgeons (3.55 ± 0.68 vs. 2.99 ± 1.02; p = 0.005). No significant differences were observed in emotional exhaustion or depersonalization.ConclusionsActive rhinoplasty engagement is associated with significantly higher personal accomplishment among Turkish otolaryngologists, suggesting rhinoplasty practice may enhance professional fulfillment rather than contribute to burnout. These findings challenge prevailing assumptions about aesthetic surgery's psychological impact.Level of Evidence4 (cross‐sectional study).Keywords: aesthetic surgery, burnout syndrome, rhinoplasty, survey

Introduction

Burnout syndrome, characterized by emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalization (DP), and reduced personal accomplishment (PA), represents a critical occupational hazard in modern medicine. First conceptualized by Freudenberger in 1974 and operationalized by Maslach in 1981, burnout has emerged as a significant threat to physician well‐being, patient care quality, and healthcare system sustainability [1, 2].

Surgical specialties face particularly elevated burnout risks due to demanding work schedules, high‐stakes decision‐making, and intense patient interactions. Otolaryngology‐head and neck surgery combines general surgical pressures with specialty‐specific challenges, including aesthetic procedures requiring exceptional technical precision and patient satisfaction management [3, 4, 5].

Rhinoplasty, one of the most frequently performed aesthetic procedures worldwide, presents unique professional demands. Beyond technical complexity, rhinoplasty surgeons navigate heightened patient expectations, subjective outcome assessments, and increased medicolegal exposure. While these factors might theoretically increase burnout risk, the relationship between rhinoplasty practice and burnout remains inadequately characterized [6, 7].

Turkey has witnessed substantial growth in aesthetic surgery demand, with rhinoplasty representing one of the most sought‐after procedures. However, limited data exist regarding burnout among Turkish otolaryngologists, particularly concerning aesthetic surgery practice. Understanding this relationship is essential for developing targeted interventions to support physician well‐being and optimize patient care [8, 9, 10].

This study aimed to: (1) determine burnout prevalence among Turkish otolaryngologists; (2) compare burnout dimensions between rhinoplasty and non‐rhinoplasty surgeons; and (3) identify demographic and professional factors associated with burnout.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This cross‐sectional survey study was conducted from December 2024 to February 2025. The target population comprised practicing otolaryngologists in Turkey. Inclusion criteria included: (1) board certification in otolaryngology‐head and neck surgery; (2) active clinical practice in Turkey; and (3) willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria included incomplete survey responses.

Survey Instrument

The survey comprised two components:

Sociodemographic Questionnaire: This section collected data on age, gender, marital status, years in practice, practice setting (academic vs. private), weekly working hours, number of surgeries performed monthly, rhinoplasty practice status, and percentage of practice devoted to rhinoplasty.

Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI): The validated Turkish version of the 22‐item MBI was employed. The MBI assesses three burnout dimensions:

EE: nine items measuring feelings of being emotionally overextended and exhausted.

EE: nine items measuring feelings of being emotionally overextended and exhausted.

DP: five items assessing impersonal responses toward patients.

DP: five items assessing impersonal responses toward patients.

PA: eight items evaluating feelings of competence and achievement.

PA: eight items evaluating feelings of competence and achievement.

Items are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = always). Higher EE and DP scores indicate greater burnout, while lower PA scores suggest burnout. The Turkish MBI demonstrates good reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.83 for EE, 0.72 for DP, 0.74 for PA).

Data Collection

Surveys were distributed electronically through professional otolaryngology associations and social media platforms frequented by Turkish otolaryngologists. A total of 200 surveys were distributed to eligible Turkish otolaryngologists. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The study protocol received institutional review board approval.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Independent samples t‐tests compared burnout scores between rhinoplasty and non‐rhinoplasty surgeons. One‐way ANOVA examined relationships between demographic variables and burnout dimensions. Pearson's correlation coefficients assessed associations between continuous variables and burnout scores. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Eighty‐four otolaryngologists completed the survey out of 200 distributed (response rate: 42%). Mean age was 38.2 ± 8.4 years (range: 27–58 years). The majority were male (74 participants, 88.1%). Most participants were married (68, 81.0%) and worked in academic settings (52, 61.9%). Mean practice duration was 10.8 ± 7.6 years. Participants reported working 52.3 ± 12.4 h weekly and performing 18.6 ± 10.2 surgeries monthly. Sixty‐four participants (76.2%) performed rhinoplasty surgery, with rhinoplasty comprising 28.4% ± 18.6% of their surgical practice (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into burnout among Turkish otolaryngologists, with particular focus on rhinoplasty practice. The overall burnout prevalence of 7.1% is notably lower than rates reported in other surgical specialties and international otolaryngology populations, where prevalence ranges from 30% to 60%. This discrepancy may reflect cultural factors, survey methodology, or sample characteristics [3, 7, 11, 12].

The most significant finding is the positive association between rhinoplasty practice and PA. Rhinoplasty surgeons demonstrated substantially higher PA scores compared with non‐rhinoplasty surgeons, suggesting that aesthetic surgery practice may enhance professional fulfillment rather than contribute to burnout. This finding challenges common assumptions about aesthetic surgery's psychological impact on practitioners [13, 14].

Several mechanisms may explain this relationship. First, rhinoplasty offers immediate, visible results that provide tangible evidence of surgical skill and patient benefit. This concrete feedback may enhance professional satisfaction and self‐efficacy. Second, rhinoplasty patients are typically highly motivated and engaged in their care, potentially fostering more rewarding patient–physician relationships. Third, the aesthetic nature of rhinoplasty allows surgeons to exercise creativity and artistry, potentially increasing intrinsic motivation and job satisfaction [15, 16, 17, 18].

The technical complexity and precision required for rhinoplasty may also contribute to enhanced PA. Mastery of demanding surgical techniques can foster professional pride and accomplishment. Additionally, rhinoplasty surgeons often develop specialized expertise recognized by colleagues and patients, potentially enhancing professional identity and satisfaction.

Contrary to expectations, rhinoplasty practice was not associated with increased EE or DP. This suggests that factors often considered stressful in aesthetic surgery—such as high patient expectations, subjective outcome assessment, and medicolegal concerns—may not substantially impact these burnout dimensions. Alternatively, rhinoplasty surgeons may develop effective coping strategies or possess personality characteristics that buffer against these stressors [19, 20, 21, 22].

The study identified several demographic and professional factors associated with burnout dimensions. Younger age and less experience correlated with higher EE and DP, consistent with previous research showing elevated burnout risk among early‐career physicians. This may reflect challenges in establishing clinical competence, managing professional responsibilities, and achieving work‐life balance [13, 23].

Academic practice was associated with higher EE, potentially reflecting additional research, teaching, and administrative responsibilities beyond clinical duties. However, academic otolaryngologists did not demonstrate higher DP or lower PA, suggesting that academic medicine's intellectual stimulation and professional development opportunities may counterbalance its demands [24, 25].

Male gender and married status were associated with higher PA. These findings align with literature suggesting that social support and stable personal relationships buffer against burnout. However, these relationships require cautious interpretation given potential confounding factors and the study's cross‐sectional design [26, 27].

Study Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding rhinoplasty practice and burnout. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal relationships [3, 28]. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit statistical power and generalizability. Third, the 42% response rate raises concerns about selection bias; participating otolaryngologists may differ systematically from nonparticipants regarding burnout levels or rhinoplasty practice patterns [11, 12].

Fourth, the study relied on self‐reported data, introducing potential recall and social desirability biases. Fifth, the survey did not assess specific stressors unique to rhinoplasty practice or coping strategies employed by rhinoplasty surgeons. Sixth, the relatively low overall burnout prevalence may reflect cultural factors influencing symptom reporting or survey response patterns [29, 30].

An important limitation is the potential for nonresponse bias related to burnout status itself. Physicians experiencing significant burnout may be less likely to engage with and complete surveys, as the exhaustion and disengagement characteristic of burnout could reduce motivation to participate in research activities. This systematic under‐representation of the most severely burned‐out otolaryngologists would likely lead to an underestimation of true burnout prevalence in our study population. Future studies should consider strategies to maximize response rates among potentially burned‐out physicians, such as anonymous mobile‐based surveys or integration of burnout screening into routine occupational health assessments [31, 32].

Additionally, the study lacked a formal assessment of financial status and income level among participants. Financial factors, including compensation models, income stability, and financial stressors, have been identified as important contributors to physician burnout in previous literature. The absence of this variable limits our ability to fully characterize the socioeconomic determinants of burnout in this population. Future investigations should incorporate validated measures of financial well‐being and compensation satisfaction to provide a more comprehensive understanding of burnout risk factors among otolaryngologists [28, 33, 34].

Finally, the study did not examine patient outcomes or satisfaction, limiting understanding of how surgeon burnout relates to care quality. Future research should investigate these relationships and explore mechanisms linking rhinoplasty practice to enhanced PA.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates low overall burnout prevalence among Turkish otolaryngologists and identifies a significant positive association between rhinoplasty practice and PA. These findings suggest that rhinoplasty engagement may enhance professional fulfillment rather than contribute to burnout, challenging prevailing assumptions about aesthetic surgery's psychological impact on practitioners.

The results highlight the importance of considering specialty‐specific factors when addressing physician burnout. Interventions to support otolaryngologist well‐being should recognize that certain practice areas, such as rhinoplasty, may provide protective factors against burnout through enhanced professional satisfaction and accomplishment.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish causal relationships, explore mechanisms linking rhinoplasty practice to enhanced PA, and investigate how these relationships influence patient outcomes. Understanding factors that promote professional fulfillment in surgical specialties is essential for developing effective strategies to combat burnout and optimize both physician well‐being and patient care quality.
