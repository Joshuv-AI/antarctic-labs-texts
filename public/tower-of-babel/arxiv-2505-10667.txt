Barrier relaxations of the classical and quantum optimal transport problems
Shmuel Friedland

Abstract
. In the last fifteen years a significant progress was achieved by considering an entropic relaxation of the classical multi-partite optimal transport problem (MPOTP). The entropic relaxation gives rise to the rescaling problem of a given tensor. This rescaling can be achieved fast with the Sinkhorn type algorithms. Recently, it was shown that a similar approach works for the quantum MPOTP. However, the analog of the rescaling Sinkhorn algorithm is much more complicated than in the classical MPOTP. In this paper we show that the interior point method (IPM) for the primary and dual problems of classical and quantum MPOTP problems can be considered as barrier relaxations of the optimal transport problems (OTP). It is well known that the dual of the OTP are advantageous as it has much less variables than the primary problem. The IPM for the dual problem of the classical MPOTP are not as fast as the Sinkhorn type algorithm. However, IPM method for the dual of the quantum MPOTP seems to work quite efficiently.

1.1.
Discrete classical optimal transport problem

The discrete classical bi-partite optimal transport problem (BPOTP) was introduced by Hitchcock and Kantorovich
[
20
,
21
]
. It is the following linear programming problem (LP). Let
\Delta^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the simplex of column probablity vectors, and
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}\in\Delta^{n}
denote by
E_{S}(\mathbf{x})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\log x_{i}
the Shannon entropy of
\mathbf{x}
.
Assume that
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\Delta^{n_{i}}
for
i=1,2
.
Let
P=(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2})
and
\mathrm{V}(P)
be the convex set of all (coupling) probability matrices with marginals
\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2}
:

(1.1)

\mathrm{V}(P)=\{V=[v_{j_{1},j_{2}}]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}},\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}v_{j_{1},j_{2}}=p_{j_{1},1},\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}v_{j_{1},j_{2}}=p_{j_{2},2}\}.

Here
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\supset\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n}
stands for the cone of nonnegative vectors and its interior of positive vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
C=[c_{j_{1},j_{2}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
be the cost matrix of transporting a unit
j_{1}\in[n_{1}]
to
j_{2}\in[n_{2}]
. The BPOTP is

(1.2)

\tau(C,P)=\min_{V\in\mathrm{V}(P)}\tr C^{\top}V.

This problem can be stated in terms of maximum flow
[
30
]
. Hence, for
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
the complexity of finding
\tau(C,P)
is
O(n^{3}\log n)
. It turns out that
\tau(C,P)
has many recent applications in machine learning

[
1
,
2
,
23
,
26
,
36
]
, statistics
[
4
,
29
,
38
]
and computer vision
[
5
,
35
]
. For
n>10^{3}
the cost of computing
\tau(C,P)
becomes prohibitively high if one uses the maximal flow algorithm.

In a breakthrough paper Cuturi
[
11
]
suggested an entropic relaxation of the BPOTP:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\tau(C,P,\varepsilon)=\min_{V\in\mathrm{V}(P)}\tr C^{\top}V-\varepsilon\mathrm{E}_{S}(V).

Recall that

(1.4)

\tau(C,P,\varepsilon)\leq\tau(C,P)\leq\tau(C,P,\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\log(n_{1}n_{2}).

(See part (a) of Lemma
2.1
.)
As
-\mathrm{E}_{S}
is strictly convex, the minimal solution to (
1.3
) is unique. For a matrix
A=[a_{j_{1},j_{2}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
denote by
\exp(\circ A)\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
the entry-wise exponential matrix
[\exp(a_{j_{1},j_{2}})]
. Then the minimal solution to the entropic relaxation of OTP is given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle U=\exp\left(\circ(\hat{U}_{1}+\hat{U}_{2}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C)\right)\in\mathrm{V}(P),

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{1}=\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}^{\top},\hat{U}_{2}=\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{\top},\mathbf{u}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},\mathbf{u}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}},\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2}=0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

That is, the minimal solution is a rescaling of
\exp\left(\circ(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C)\right)
. This solution can be obtained by Sinkhorn’s algorithm
[
37
]
by alternatively rescaling the rows and columns of
U_{k}=\exp\left(\circ(\hat{U}_{1,k}+\hat{U}_{2,k}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C)\right)
. It turns out that fixing the error
\delta
and
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
, the complexity of modified Sinkhorn’s algorithm is
O(n^{2})
ignoring logarithmic factors
[
1
]
. In this paper by arithmetic complexity of an algorithm we mean an estimate of number of flops to carry out this algorithm, see Appendix
B
.

Similar results were obtained for entropic relaxation of multi-partite optimal transport problems that will be discussed later
[
3
,
39
,
19
,
22
,
14
]
.

Let

R=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d})
is the closed convex set of all
d
-partite states whose partial traces are

respectively:

(1.6)

\Gamma(R)=\{\rho\in\mathrm{H}_{\mathbf{n},+},\tr_{\hat{i}}\rho=\rho_{i},i\in[d]\},R=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d})\}.

Here,
\tr_{\hat{i}}\rho
is the sum of all partial traces of
\rho
except on the index

(Observe that if

The quantum multi-partite optimal transport optimal transport problem for a given
C\in\mathrm{H}_{\mathbf{n}}
is
[
6
,
8
,
16
,
9
]
:

(1.7)

\kappa(C,R)=\min_{\rho\in\Gamma(R)}\tr C\rho.

It is a semidefinite programming problem (SDP)
[
40
]
that can be solved efficiently using the interior point programming (IPM)
[
9
]
. For
\rho\in\mathrm{H}_{n,+,1}
denote by
E_{N}(\rho)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\rho)\log\lambda_{i}(\rho)
the von Neumann entropy of

. The entropic relaxation of the quantum
d
-partite problem is

(1.8)

\kappa(C,R,\varepsilon)=\min_{\rho\in\Gamma(R)}\tr C\rho-\varepsilon\mathrm{E}_{N}(\rho).

The analog of the inequality (
1.4
) is

(1.9)

\kappa(C,R,\varepsilon)\leq\kappa(C,R)\leq\kappa(C,R,\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\log(n_{1}n_{2}).

It is shown in
[
12
, equation (1.2)]
that the unique solution of (
1.8
) is of the form

(1.10)

\displaystyle\rho=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\hat{U}_{i}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C\right)\in\Gamma(R),

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{i}=(\otimes_{j=1}^{i-1}I_{n_{j}})\otimes U_{i}\otimes(\otimes_{k=i+1}^{d}I_{n_{k}}),\quad U_{i}\in\mathrm{H}_{n_{i}},i\in[d].

The choice of
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}
is not unique, and some normalizations on
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}
can be imposed
[
12
]
. The step
k
of the Sinkhorn algorithm consists of choosing a mode

and updating
U_{j_{k}}
such that the corresponding
\rho_{k+1}
in the above equation satisfies
\tr_{\widehat{j_{k}}}\rho_{k+1}=\tr\rho_{j_{k}}
. While this condition for the classical Sinkhorn algorithm is very simple, in the quantum case it is computationally complex
[
7
,
33
]
.

1.3.
Our contribution

In this paper we use a relaxation to the classical and quantum MPOTP and their duals using a barrier function, which is the corner stone of the interior point method (IPM). For the minimum problems the barrier function has to be a smooth convex functions that blow up on the boundary of a given convex domain, and satisfy a few other technical conditions
[
27
,
34
]
. Observe that the Shannon and von Neumann entropies are not barriers as they don’t blow up at the boundary. In
[
15
]
we showed how to apply the IPM method for the classical MPOTP. We briefly explain our approach for the BPOTP.

The barrier functions on
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
are

(1.11)

\displaystyle\beta(\mathbf{x})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_{i},\quad\mathbf{x}\in R_{++}^{n},

\displaystyle\beta(V)=-\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}\log v_{j_{1},j_{2}},\quad V=[v_{j_{1},j_{2}}]\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}.

In what follows we denote

(1.12)

(R^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})_{++,b}=\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}_{++},\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}^{\top}\}.

For
(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2})\in(R^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})_{++,b}
we define
V(P)
as in (
1.1
). We call
\tau(C,P)
given by (

) the (generalized) classical BPOTP.
Let

be the interior of
\mathrm{V}(P)
, which consists of all positive matrices in
\mathrm{V}(P)
. The relaxed BPOTP is

(1.13)

\tau_{\beta}(C,P,\varepsilon)=\min_{V\in\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)}\tr C^{T}V+\varepsilon\beta(V).

We will show that this problem has a unique solution of the form

(1.14)

\displaystyle U=[u_{j_{1},j_{2}}]\in\mathrm{V}_{o}(P),

\displaystyle u_{j_{1},j_{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}},\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}u_{j_{1},1}-\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}u_{j_{2},2}=0.

The following theorem summarizes one of our main results on the barrier relaxation of the classical BPOTP:

2.
Classical bi-partite optimal control and its relaxations

In this section we discuss first the known results on the classical BPOTP and its entropic relaxation. Second we discuss the barrier relaxations the primary and dual of classical BPOTP.

2.1.
An entropic relaxation of the classical bi-partite optimal transport problem

For
i\in\{1,2\}
let
X_{i}
be a random variables on
\Omega_{i}
which has a finite number of values:
X_{i}:\Omega_{i}\to[n_{i}]
.
Assume that
\mathbf{p}_{i}=(p_{1,i},\ldots,p_{n_{i},i})^{\top}
is the column probability vector that gives the distribution of
X_{i}
:
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=j)=p_{j,i}
for
i\in[2]
.
Let
Z
be a random variable
Z:\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\to[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
with contingency matrix (table)
U\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
that gives the distribution of
Z
:

\mathbb{P}(Z=(j_{1},j_{2}))=u_{j_{1},j_{2}}
.
The classical BPOTP is given by (
1.2
).

For
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
and a symmetric cost matrix
C=[c_{ij}]
with zero diagonal, and positive off-diagonal entries satisfying the triangle inequality
c_{ik}\leq c_{ij}+c_{jk}
, the quantity
\tau(C,P)
gives rise to a distance between probability vectors
\mathbf{p}_{1}
and
\mathbf{p}_{2}

[
14
]
, which can be viewed as two histograms.

The entropic relaxation of the classical BPOTP is given by (
1.3
).
As the Shannon entropy
E_{S}
is strictly concave, the optimization problem (
1.3
) has a unique solution in the relative interior
\mathrm{U}(P)
. For simplicity of the exposition we assume in the rest of the paper that
\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2}>\mathbf{0}
, unless stated otherwise. By reducing dimensions, if needed, one can always assume this condition. See the arguments for the quantum bi-partite case
[
9
, Proposition 2.4]
.

The following lemma is well known
[
14
]
and we bring its proof for completeness:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\Delta^{n_{i}},n_{i}>1,i\in[2]
be positive probability vectors, and assume that
\mathrm{U}(P)
be defined by (
1.1
).

(a)

Consider the minima
\tau(C,P)
and
\tau(C,P,\varepsilon)
that are given by (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) respectively. Then (
1.4
) holds.
In particular for a given
\delta>0
choose
\varepsilon=\frac{\delta}{\log(n_{1}n_{2})}
to obtain the inequality
|\tau(C,P)-\tau(C,P,\varepsilon)|\leq\delta
.

(b)

The unique minimizing matrix
U\in\mathrm{U}_{o}(P)
for (
1.3
) is of the form (
1.5
), where
\mathbf{u}_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{2}
are unique.

Proof.

(a) Recall that for
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{n}
we have the inequalities
0\leq\mathrm{E}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\leq\log n
.
These inequalites yield straightforward (a).

(b) The quantity
\tau(C,P,\varepsilon)
is a constrained minimum problem. As
-E_{S}
is strictly convex, and a directional derivative of
-E_{S}
toward the entrior of
\mathrm{U}(P)
at any boundary point is
-\infty
it follows that the unique minimum point
U=[u_{j_{1},j_{2}}]
is in
\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)
. That is,
U\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
. Recall that
\mathrm{V}(P)
is given by
n_{1}+n_{2}
equalities given by (
1.1
).
(Note we have the equality that the sum of the rows are equal to the sum of the columns of
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.)
Hence, we can use the Lagrange multiplies:
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n_{1}})^{\top},\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n_{2}})^{\top}
corresponding to the row and column sums of
V\in\mathrm{V}(P)
. Apply the Lagrange multipliers to deduce
[
14
]

c_{j_{1},j_{2}}+\varepsilon(1+\log u_{j_{1},j_{2}})-\lambda_{j_{1}}-\mu_{j_{2}}=0,\quad j_{1}\in[n_{1}],j_{2}\in[n_{2}].

Observe that if we replace
\lambda_{j_{1}}
and
\mu_{j_{2}}
by
\lambda_{j_{1}}+t
and
\mu_{j_{2}}-t
for
j_{1}\in[n_{1}],j_{2}\in[n_{2}]
we still have the above equalites. Thus, we can choose a unique
t
such that
\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}-\varepsilon+t)=\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}(\mu_{j_{2}}-t)
. This shows that the unique minimal
U
is of the form (
1.5
).
It is left to show that
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}
are unique. Since the minimal matrix
U=[u_{j_{1},j_{2}}]
is unique, and
C
is known, we obtain that
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}
satisfy the equalities:

(2.1)

u_{j_{1},1}+u_{j_{2},2}=a_{j_{1},j_{2}},\quad j_{1}\in[n_{1}],j_{2}\in[n_{2}],

where
A=[a_{j_{1},j_{2}}]
is known. Recall that
\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{1}=1_{n_{2}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2}=t
. Sum up the
n_{1}n_{2}
equalities in (
2.1
) to deduce that
(n_{1}+n_{2})t=\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}A\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}
. Sum up the equalites in (
2.1
) on either
j_{2}
or
j_{1}
to obtain

\mathbf{u}_{1}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}(A\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}-t\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}),\mathbf{u}_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}(A^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}-t\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}),t=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}A\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}.

Hence
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}
are unique.
∎

The regularization term gives almost linear time approximation
O(n^{2})
, ignoring the logarithmic terms, using a variation of the celebrated Sinkhorn algorithm for matrix diagonal scaling
[
1
]
. Recall that the Sinkhorn algorithm is an alternating row scaling to obtain the condition that
U_{2k-1}\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}=\mathbf{p}_{1}
, and then column scaling to obtain the condition
U^{\top}_{2k}1_{n_{1}}=\mathbf{p}_{2}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, starting with a matrix
U_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.

The problem with entropic relaxation is that we need to rescale the matrix
\exp(\circ(-\frac{C}{\varepsilon}))
. Then, if
\frac{\min_{j_{1}\in[n_{1}],j_{2}\in[n_{2}]}|c_{j_{1},j_{2}}|}{\max_{j_{1}\in[n_{1}],j_{2}\in[n_{2}]}|c_{j_{1},j_{2}}|}>0
but small, and
\varepsilon
is small (
=10^{-8})
, then many entries of
\exp(\circ(-\frac{C}{\varepsilon}))
would be treated as zero entries in the scaling algorithm.

2.2.
A barrier relaxation of the classical bi-partite optimal transport problem

A barrier relaxation of the classical BPOTP is given by
1.13
, where
\beta(V)
is given by (
1.11
). The following lemma is an analog to Lemma
2.1
:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})_{++,b}
. Assume that
\mathrm{V}(P)
be defined by (
1.1
).

(a)

Consider the minima
\tau(C,P)
and
\tau_{\beta}(C,P,\varepsilon)
that are given by (
1.2
) and (
1.13
), respectively. Then

\tau(C,P)<\tau_{\beta}(C,P,\varepsilon)

(b)

The unique minimizing matrix
U\in\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)
for (
1.13
), is of the form (
1.14
) where
\mathbf{u}_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{2}
are unique.

Proof.

(a) Follows form the observation that
\beta(V)>0
for
V\in\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)
.

(b) As
\tr(C^{\top}V)+\varepsilon\beta(V)
is strictly convex on
\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)

and is
\infty
on
\partial\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)
it follows that
\tau_{\beta}(C,P,\varepsilon)
is achieved at the unique point
U=[u_{j_{1},j_{2}}]\in\mathrm{V}_{o}(P)
. Use Lagrange multipliers to deduce

c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{u_{j_{1},j_{2}}}-\lambda_{j_{1}}-\mu_{j_{2}}=0

As in the proof of Lemma
2.1
we can assume that
\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}=\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\mu_{j_{2}}
. Hence,
U
is of the form (
1.14
). To show the uniqueness of
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}
we use the same arguments as in the proof of part (b) of Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Clearly, the function
\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},C)
is concave in
\hat{\mathrm{D}}
. As
\log x
is strictly concave on
\mathbb{R}_{++}
, to show the strict concavity of
\varphi
on
\mathrm{D}
, it is enough to show that the affine map

T:\mathrm{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
given by
T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=C-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}-\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
is one-to-one.
This is equivalent to the statement

(\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=0)\wedge(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}^{\top}+\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}=0)\Rightarrow\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}=0.

This implication follows from the proof of part (b) of Lemma
2.1
,
where we showed the uniqueness of the solution of (
2.1
).

Let
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}
be defined in (
1.5
). We claim that
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})
is a critical point of
\varphi
in
\hat{\mathrm{D}}
. Indeed:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j_{1}}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})=p_{j_{1},1}-\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}}=0,j_{1}\in[n_{1}],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j_{2}}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})=p_{j_{2},2}-\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}}=0,j_{2}\in[n_{1}].

Hence,
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})
is a maximal point in
\hat{\mathrm{D}}
. As
\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2}
, it follows that
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})\in\mathrm{D}
. As
\varphi
is strictly concave in
\mathrm{D}
, it follows that
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})
is the unique maximal point in
\mathrm{D}
. Let
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star})\in\hat{\mathrm{D}}\setminus\mathrm{D}
be a maximum point in
\hat{\mathrm{D}}
. Then
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star})+t(\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}},-\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}})
is also a maximal point in
\hat{\mathrm{D}}
. There exists a unique
t^{\star}
such that
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star})+t^{\star}(\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}},-\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}})\in\mathrm{D}
. Hence,
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star})+t^{\star}(\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}},-\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}})=(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})
. Therefore, the maximal point of
\varphi
in
\hat{\mathrm{D}}
.

It is left to show the equality (
1.18
). Observe first

\displaystyle\tau_{\beta}(C,P,\varepsilon)=\tr C^{\top}U+\varepsilon\beta(U)=\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}\frac{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}\varepsilon}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}}+\varepsilon\beta(U)=

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left(\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}\frac{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}+(u_{j_{1},1}+u_{j_{2},2})}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}}\right)+\varepsilon\beta(U)=

\displaystyle n_{1}n_{2}\varepsilon+\left(\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}(u_{j_{1},1}+u_{j_{2},2})\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}}\right)+\varepsilon\beta(U)

\displaystyle n_{1}n_{2}\varepsilon+\mathbf{p}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2}-\varepsilon\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}\log\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2}}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2}+\varepsilon\left(\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}\log(c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2})\right)+\varepsilon n_{1}n_{2}(1-\log\varepsilon).

Observe second

\displaystyle\max_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\hat{\mathrm{D}}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},C)=\varphi(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},C)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{2}+\varepsilon\left(\sum_{j_{1}=j_{2}=1}^{n_{1},n_{2}}\log(c_{j_{1},j_{2}}-u_{j_{1},1}-u_{j_{2},2})\right)+\varepsilon n_{1}n_{2}(1-\log\varepsilon).

Hence, the equality (
1.18
).
∎

2.3.
The interior point method for finding a barrier approximation of
\tau(C,P)

In this section we discuss how to apply the IPM method to find an approximation to
\tau(C,P)
using a modified barrier
\hat{\beta}
. A short discussion of the IPM method is given in Appendix
A
. The problem in applying the IPM to the

(2.2)

\max_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathrm{D}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},C)=-\min_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathrm{D}}-\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},C)

is that
\mathrm{D}
is unbounded. We need to restrict the maximum problem to a bounded domain.

Let

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y},\quad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p})^{1/p},p\in[1,\infty],\quad\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle=\tr A^{*}B,\quad\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle},\quad A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}.

We start with the following lemmas

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
2\leq d
be an integer, and
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{1,i},\ldots,x_{n_{i}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},i\in[d]
. Assume that

(2.3)

\mathbf{1}_{n_{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{1}_{n_{i+1}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=0\textrm{ for }i\in[d-1].

Then

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1}\cdots n_{d}}{\max_{i\in[d]}n_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{j_{1}=\cdots=j_{d}=1}^{n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}}(x_{j_{1},1}+\cdots+x_{j_{d},d})^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{dn_{1}\cdots n_{d}}{n_{i}}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}\leq\frac{dn_{1}\cdots n_{d}}{\max_{i\in[d]}n_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}.

Proof.

Clearly,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1}=\cdots=j_{d}=1}^{n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}}(x_{j_{1},1}+\cdots+x_{j_{d},d})^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{n_{1}\cdots n_{d}}{n_{i}}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(\mathbf{1}_{n_{p}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}})(\mathbf{1}_{n_{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n_{q}})

As
\mathbf{1}_{n_{p}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}}=\mathbf{1}_{n_{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n_{q}}
we deduce that
(\mathbf{1}_{n_{p}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n_{p}})(\mathbf{1}_{n_{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n_{q}})\geq 0
. Hence, the first inequality in (
2.4
) holds. To deduce the second inequality use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

(x_{j_{1},1}+\cdots+x_{j_{d},d})^{2}\leq d(x_{j_{1},1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{j_{d},d}^{2}).

∎
