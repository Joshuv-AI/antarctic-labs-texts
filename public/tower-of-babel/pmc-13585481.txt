Examining Educators' Understanding and Application of Learnings From an Online Brain Injury Professional Development Module—TeachABI
Authors: Christine C. Muscat, Sara A. Marshall, Alexa Irvin, Andrea Hickling, Tijana Simic, Kathryn Broad, Shannon E. Scratch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAcquired brain injury (ABI) in childhood can lead to complex and lasting challenges that affect educational participation and outcomes. Despite the central role of educators in supporting students post‐ABI in the classroom, educators receive little to no formal training pertaining to ABI.ObjectiveThis study explored how educators understand and apply knowledge gained from TeachABI, a brief, virtual professional development module designed to enhance educator capacity to support students with ABI in school settings.MethodsSemistructured interviews were conducted with 29 educators in Ontario, Canada, following their completion of the TeachABI module. Data were analysed by two independent raters using directed content analysis guided by the theoretical domains framework to identify key categories related to knowledge acquisition, application and contextual influences.ResultsEducators reported increased knowledge of ABI and classroom strategies, enhanced confidence and intentions to apply the material in practice. Educators connected new learning to prior personal and professional experiences, highlighting the value of reflection. Internal factors (e.g., perceived competence and motivation) and external factors (e.g., classroom size, school resources and family engagement) influenced the feasibility of applying strategies. Educators also expressed a desire for continued learning and access to additional ABI‐related resources.Conclusions TeachABI is a promising tool for improving educator knowledge and preparedness to support students with ABI in the classroom. However, systemic and environmental barriers may impact educators' ability to apply knowledge and strategies learned in the classroom environment. Findings underscore the need for ongoing professional development and structural supports to facilitate inclusive education for students with ABI.

Introduction

Educators and ABI Knowledge

Acquired brain injury (ABI) can lead to profound and lasting physical, cognitive, behavioural and social impacts on the lives of children, youth and their families (Heverly‐Fitt et al. 2014; Ryan et al. 2021; Ankrett et al. 2023; Hartman et al. 2015; Roscigno et al. 2015; Gfroerer et al. 2008; Allonsius et al. 2024; Aitken et al. 2009; Philipson et al. 2023). This is evident when students return to school, where they spend a significant amount of time learning and interacting with peers (Statistics Canada 2020). When students return to school post‐ABI, their primary support in the education system is from classroom educators (Zinga et al. 2008). Previous literature indicates that teacher perceptions and expectations can positively or negatively influence a student's academic achievement (Babad 1993; Brophy 1982; Jussim 1989). Specifically, teachers' knowledge and identification of ABI influence student success in academic and social domains, independent of their teaching approach (Wlodarczyk et al. 2015). Educators are largely underprepared to accommodate and support the learning needs of students with ABI and hold many misconceptions about working with students post‐ABI in the classroom (Bullock et al. 2005; Mohr and Bullock 2005; Mealings and Douglas 2010; Linden et al. 2013; Ettel et al. 2016). Parents and students report that teachers' poor understanding of ABI and low knowledge of accommodations are factors impacting students' school re‐entry post‐ABI (Wan and Nasr 2021; Sharp et al. 2006; Andersson et al. 2016).

Given the significant gap in educator understanding of ABI, focused education for school personnel is imperative to develop a strong return‐to‐school plan for students following ABI (Lindsay et al. 2015). However, educator training in ABI can be difficult to achieve, as many school systems globally (e.g., Canada, Australia and Northern Ireland), do not recognize ABI as an area of exceptionality, therefore, students' learning needs are not formally identified (Ettel et al. 2016; Nagele et al. 2019; Zinga et al. 2005; Kahn et al. 2018). In fact, even in countries where some aspects of ABI (e.g., traumatic brain injury) are recognized in the school system (e.g., United States), educator knowledge still remains markedly low, and students' needs remain unmet in the classroom (Ettel et al. 2016; Nagele et al. 2019; Glang et al. 2008). Despite receiving training for other developmental (e.g., autism spectrum disorder) and medical (e.g., asthma) conditions, teachers are not provided with formal ABI training to address the educational needs of students with ABI (Zinga et al. 2008; Bennett et al. 2004). Thus, students with ABI are often poorly identified in the school system, leading to inadequate supports and programming for both the student and their educators (Bennett et al. 2004).

Professional Development for Educators

Professional development enhances educator knowledge and provides strategies to improve teaching quality and student outcomes in the classroom (Holloway 2006; Zhaohui and Anning 2020; Kennedy 2016). The literature highlights that online professional development for educators significantly enhances knowledge, self‐efficacy, skills and classroom practices, leading to more effective teaching strategies and improvements in student outcomes (Morina et al. 2025; Guerrero 2025; Ventista and Brown 2023; Randel et al. 2016). Importantly, the translation of professional development into educator practice appears to be influenced by internal and external factors (Wrenn and Wrenn 2009; Nīmante et al. 2025; Sahin and Yildirim 2016). Internally, educators' motivation, prior experiences and perceived relevance of the training influence their engagement in the training and likelihood of implementation in the classroom (Appova and Arbaugh 2018; Kraft et al. 2024; Halal Orfali et al. 2024). A critical component of effective professional development is the opportunity for educators to reflect on previous classroom experiences and connect newly learned material to their existing pedagogical frameworks (Roberts 2009; Korthagen 2017; Borko 2004). This reflective process supports deeper understanding and more meaningful integration of new practices into daily instruction (Roberts 2009; Korthagen 2017; Borko 2004). In the school system and broader environment, various external factors may play a role in educators' application of professional development in the classroom, such as time restraints, school team, available resources, among others (Silva Didier et al. 2025; Avalos 2011). It is therefore important to understand how educators are interacting with the training material during professional development activities and to consider external factors beyond educators' control in the school environment.

TeachABI

To address the significant training and knowledge gap pertaining to ABI for educators, our team created an online professional development module called TeachABI. TeachABI is a short (approximately 45 min), engaging, virtual professional development module about ABI created for educators by teachers, clinicians and families with lived experience of ABI. TeachABI uses a case‐based learning approach and is composed of a case study, videos of individuals with lived experiences, downloadable tip sheets and links to additional resources. The professional development module aims to build educator capacity and improve educator confidence in supporting students with ABI in the classroom; it offers targeted information and strategies to better support student reintegration following ABI. See Saly, Provvidenza, et al. (2023) for comprehensive details about TeachABI and its development. Initial results from a usability study revealed a high satisfaction level with TeachABI among elementary school educators, with an average System Usability Scale score of 86.25, out of 100 (Saly, Provvidenza, et al. 2023). Semistructured interviews revealed that TeachABI content was valuable, useful and aligned with the realities of their profession (Saly, Provvidenza, et al. 2023). Participants also indicated an appreciation for the visual design, organization and varying use of education strategies in TeachABI (Saly, Provvidenza, et al. 2023). Further research was conducted to investigate the impact of TeachABI on elementary school educators' knowledge of paediatric ABI and self‐efficacy for supporting students with ABI in the classroom (Marshall et al. 2025). In a sample of 50 educators, knowledge of paediatric ABI, knowledge of the module learning objectives and self‐efficacy increased premodule to postmodule, and gains were maintained 60 days after training (Marshall et al. 2025). Although these results are promising, it is also important to understand how educators might apply TeachABI materials in the classroom setting, and the educator experience of TeachABI broadly. Therefore, the aim of the current study is to understand educators' key learnings following TeachABI module completion, specifically: educators' understanding of the material presented in TeachABI, educators' intentions to apply strategies learned in TeachABI in their classroom setting, and internal and external factors that may facilitate or impede educators' ability to apply TeachABI material in their classroom.

Methods

Participants

This study reports on a subset of participants from a larger study, which comprised qualitative and quantitative components. Fifty participants completed questionnaires (ABI knowledge, knowledge application case study and self‐efficacy) at three time points: prior to completing TeachABI (T1), immediately following completion (T2) and 2 months after completion (T3). For more information regarding the quantitative data, please see (Marshall et al. 2025). A subset of these participants (n = 29) also participated in interviews at T2. Data collection for this study included educators and preservice teachers in Ontario, Canada.

Inclusion criteria included individuals fluent in English reading and writing and who were either (1) working as an Ontario College of Teachers (OCT) certified educator (e.g., classroom educator, special educator, principal and occasional teacher); or (2) enrolled in an Ontario Teacher's Education program that will provide certification with the OCT (i.e., preservice teacher). Participants were invited to participate in interviews. Data collection was guided by data saturation (interviews were conducted until no new categories or ideas emerged (Sandelowski 1995)), while purposively inviting participants to seek a balanced representation of backgrounds and experiences (i.e., < 5 years of teaching experience, preservice teachers and male educators).

Procedure

Informed consent was obtained from all participants (Holland Bloorview Research Ethics Board #0414). Educators met with a research assistant in a secure virtual meeting via Zoom. Participants completed the full TeachABI module at their own pace, with the opportunity to explore additional links or resources in the module, while sharing their screen with the research assistant. At T2, the individual participated in a semi‐structured exit interview. Participants were provided with the opportunity to describe their overall thoughts about TeachABI and its case study approach, provide feedback about the topics discussed throughout the module (e.g., ABI knowledge, ABI in the classroom and classroom strategies) and describe any content that they found confusing or difficult to understand. Participants indicated if the information in TeachABI was new to them, if they experienced any significant learnings (labelled ‘aha’ moments) when completing the module and if they felt that the module helped them to achieve the following learning objectives: (1) define ABI; (2) identify potential challenges for students with ABI in the classroom; (3) discuss the importance of taking an individualized approach for students with ABI; and (4) describe how to support a student with an ABI in the Ontario education system. Finally, they reported on if the module impacted their knowledge of ABI and how to best support students with ABI in the classroom, if the information in the module will be useful to them in future education settings, if they would be able to successfully apply the strategies and knowledge learned in TeachABI in their teaching (and if not, the barriers to applying the material) and if they would recommend the module to other educators.

Positionality

The primary author (C.M.) acknowledges her positionality as a PhD student and speech‐language pathologist working clinically with children and youth following ABI. The primary author has been involved in the return‐to‐learn transition for students as a clinician and has a vested interest in improving this transition for students with ABI. She acknowledges that she is not an educator and does not know the school environment intimately. The secondary coders (S.M. and A.I.) also acknowledge that they are not educators and recognize their positionality as a PhD student in school/applied child psychology and a research coordinator, with an educational background in epidemiology and applied health research, respectively. To mitigate potential bias, rigour was prioritized throughout the process, including double coding of the data and collaboration of the primary and secondary coders with a multidisciplinary authorship team (i.e., neuropsychology, occupational therapy, education and speech‐language pathology). The authors overall recognize that their diverse professional and academic backgrounds inevitably shape their perspectives and view this diversity as a strength that enriches the depth and nuance of the qualitative interpretation.

Analysis and Framework

Each interview was recorded using Zoom and transcribed verbatim, removing any identifying participant information. Multiple readings of transcripts supported data familiarization across all coders (Naeem et al. 2023; Nowell et al. 2017). A directed (deductive) content analysis was used to analyse participant interviews (Hsieh and Shannon 2005; Assarroudi et al. 2018). Responses were coded by hand and codes were grouped to form larger categories, where appropriate. Data were coded by three independent reviewers (C.M., S.M. and A.I.).

Following Hsieh and Shannon's (2005) approach, our directed approach used existing theory (theoretical domains framework) (Atkins et al. 2017) to identify key concepts or variables during the coding of initial categories. Data that were relevant to the research questions but did not fit into pre‐existing categories were included in additional categories and subcategories (Hsieh and Shannon 2005). The guiding framework for this analysis was the theoretical domains framework (TDF) (Atkins et al. 2017). The TDF is a comprehensive framework that considers numerous factors or influences (i.e., barriers and facilitators) at various levels (i.e., cognitive, affective, social and environmental) that may influence behaviours (Leggett 2025). The TDF provides a useful conceptual basis for assessing implementation problems, designing interventions to enhance healthcare practice and understanding behaviour‐change processes (Blatchford et al. 2009). It encompasses 33 theories of behaviour, and behaviour change, which have been merged into 14 domains. Given our objectives centre on understanding educators' behaviour after viewing TeachABI (i.e., their potential application of learnings from TeachABI in their classroom), the TDF was chosen as the framework to guide this analysis. The TDF has been used across various health care settings and clinical behaviours and has been shown in previous research to be considered a useful framework for understanding the factors that influence behaviour in a given context (Atkins et al. 2017; French et al. 2012; De Leo et al. 2021; Phillips et al. 2015).

The TDF was used as a guide for coding the data (Hsieh and Shannon 2005). The codebook was iteratively developed with the coding team, with ongoing dialogue used to refine codes and code definitions. All TDF domains were considered in the initial code development. However, the final codes included only constructs of the TDF that were agreed upon by the research team as relevant to the primary research objective. Where possible, TDF constructs were expanded to fit research objectives and data. For example, the TDF construct of ‘knowledge’ was divided into pre‐existing knowledge, new knowledge and general knowledge, related to educator completion of the TeachABI module. Furthermore, data that were relevant to the research questions, but did not fit into pre‐existing TDF constructs were included in additional categories and subcategories. Any newly identified codes were documented systematically, added to the codebook and discussed collaboratively. To ensure newly developed codes were consistently and retroactively applied, early coded transcripts were recoded following the finalization of the codebook.

The first 10 transcripts were coded collaboratively by two coders (five coded by C.M. and S.M. and five coded by C.M. and A.I.) to establish data familiarization, codes for the formal codebook and coding consistency across the three coders. The remaining 19 transcripts were coded independently by C.M., with a secondary coder (S.M. or A.I.) reviewing each transcript. After review by two authors, intercoder agreement was 96%. Conflicts were discussed among all three coders to reach consensus (Merriam and Tisdell 2015; Elo et al. 2014; Patton 2015; Halpin 2024). These meetings also helped monitor potential drift and coder consistency throughout the coding process. Discussion and consensus across team members helped ensure rigour and trustworthiness of coding. An intercoder agreement percentage of 96% was calculated across the coding team (Halpin 2024). Once data were coded, data were inputted into NVivo (Jackson and Bazeley 2019). The Nvivo Software was used exclusively for data management (Jackson and Bazeley 2019). Transcripts were imported into NVivo to organize the dataset, consolidate excerpts under each code and facilitate viewing of the coded data holistically.

Throughout the analysis, coders incorporated multiple strategies addressing credibility, dependability and confirmability (Nowell et al. 2017). Credibility was enhanced through iterative codebook development, in which transcripts were coded by two members of the research team (collaboratively or separate coding) and regular research team meetings to reach consensus and refine formal codes. Dependability was promoted by maintaining a comprehensive audit trail, documenting codebook revisions, analytic decisions and team meeting notes. Confirmability was supported through reflexive memoing and the systematic use of verbatim participant quotations to ground interpretations in the data. Together, these practices strengthen the transparency, rigour and trustworthiness of the analysis.

Results

Participants

Twenty‐nine individuals (n = 25 female, n = 4 males) participated in the study. The first 22 participants were invited to complete an interview. From the 22 invited, 20 participants agreed to partake in interviews. Following the first 20 interviews, an additional set of targeted participants (n = 9) were invited to complete an interview, to ensure a more balanced representation of educator backgrounds and experiences (i.e., < 5 years of teaching experience, preservice teachers and male educators).

Participants' highest education level included a bachelor (n = 14) or master's degree (n = 15). Participants included preservice teachers (n = 3), special educators (n = 12), classroom teachers (n = 12), one occasional teacher and one principal (Table 1). Educators varied in their grade certifications, including primary (kindergarten to grade 3; n = 17), junior (grades 4–6; n = 22), intermediate (grades 7–10; n = 13) and senior (grades 11–12; n = 7) grades. Participants taught in public (n = 18), public catholic (n = 1) and private (n = 10) schools in Ontario, Canada (Table 1).

Identified Categories

Qualitative content analysis of interview data summarized the main concepts expressed by educators into four categories and 22 subcategories. Figure 1 highlights the key categories identified throughout participant interviews: (1) baseline knowledge and experiences: information and/or experiences that educators had prior to completing the module, (2) TeachABI factors: internal (i.e., in the educator's control) and external (i.e., in the educator's environment) factors identified as facilitators or barriers to applying learned knowledge and strategies following educator completion of TeachABI. (3) ABI knowledge gap: The questions and/or gap in knowledge that educators had following the completion of the module. (4) Future learning and experiences: educators' future learning experiences that will be impacted by both the completion of TeachABI and their gap in ABI knowledge. These categories are discussed in turn below.

Key categories from participant interviews.

Baseline Knowledge and Experiences

When reflecting on the content of TeachABI, educators related the material to their pre‐existing use of strategies in the classroom (for students with ABI and/or other exceptionalities), pre‐existing knowledge of ABI and previous classroom and personal experiences.

Educators came into the module with varying degrees of knowledge pertaining to ABI: some identified an overlap of TeachABI material with pre‐existing knowledge and strategies already used in the classroom (pre‐existing knowledge and pre‐existing strategies; Table 2). Educators reported that pre‐existing knowledge was a result of experience working with students post‐ABI or students with other exceptionalities. Educators' experience working in special education or specialized classrooms prior to completing the module influenced the level of pre‐existing knowledge of ABI. Educators with less teaching experience or no experience working with students post‐ABI generally reported less pre‐existing knowledge of ABI and ABI strategies prior to completing the module. Those with more experience working with students post‐ABI generally reported more pre‐existing knowledge of ABI and ABI strategies prior to completing the module. They also indicated that completion of the module reinforced their pre‐existing knowledge and validated their use of strategies in the classroom.

TeachABI Factors—Internal Educator Factors

Qualitative analysis of educator interviews revealed internal factors influencing educators' ability to apply ABI knowledge and strategies in the classroom, including: ABI knowledge (Table 2), perceived competence, self‐ or professional confidence and future intentions to refer to the resources presented in the module (i.e., module slides, handouts and resource links).

Educators identified that the module provided them with new knowledge and strategies pertaining to ABI that they will be able to apply in their classrooms (new ABI knowledge and new ABI strategies; Table 2). They reported that TeachABI provided them with the following information: foundational knowledge pertaining to ABI (i.e., definition of ABI, prevalence of ABI for young people, signs and symptoms of ABI), information about ABI not being officially recognized as an exceptionality in Ontario's education system, ‘growing into deficits’ following an ABI at a young age, potential ABI symptoms, cognitive fatigue, the importance of an individualized approach when working with students post‐ABI and the importance of collaboration between the school team, the student and caregivers. Participants also identified procedures (procedural knowledge; Table 2) for supporting students with ABI in the Ontario education system (e.g., importance of strong communication between the educator and caregivers, reviewing the Ontario Student Record, etc.). With respect to learned strategies, participants indicated that strategies could be applied in the classroom immediately. For many educators, completion of the module facilitated recognition that the strategies presented can be beneficial for all students, not just those with ABI (e.g., movement breaks, visual aids, etc.). Participants reported a better understanding that the success of a given strategy may vary day‐to‐day for a given student and noted the importance of trialling a variety of strategies as needed necessary. Self‐advocacy was reported as a newly learned strategy, where educators indicated the importance of working with the student to increase their ability to advocate for themselves in the classroom setting.

TeachABI Factors—External Factors

Educators revealed external factors, outside of the educator's control, that may influence their ability to apply ABI knowledge and strategies learned from TeachABI in the classroom, including: environmental context (physical classroom space, student interactions with the classroom environment, size of the class and multiple exceptionalities in the classroom; Table 2), material resources available to the educator through the school and/or school board and social influences (school team, family relationship and intergroup conflict; Table 2). Educators also reported potential barriers in the application of ABI‐specific knowledge and strategies in their classroom related to the environmental context (physical space, number of students in their class and student interaction with the classroom environment; Table 2) available to them. In addition, they noted that the number of students with exceptionalities (i.e., learning needs, accommodations and modifications) in the classroom can often impact the amount of time educators have to dedicate to students (multiple exceptionalities; Table 2). Participants further reported a possible barrier in the material resources available to educators, such as having large class sizes with limited educational support for students (resources; Table 2). Some educators noted that Ontario not recognizing ABI as an exceptionality may negatively impact students' access to resources in publicly funded school boards in the province (resources; Table 2). On the other hand, external factors described by educators that may facilitate the application of material learned in TeachABI to their classroom included the following: smaller class sizes (class size; Table 2), such as special education or specialized classrooms, where educators noted that they are able to provide their students with more direct support and the overlap of ABI strategies with strategies used for students with other exceptionalities (multiple exceptionalities; Table 2).

Discussion

This study explored educators' key takeaways from the TeachABI module, focusing on their intentions to apply TeachABI learnings in their classrooms. Specifically, the study aimed to understand educators' key learnings following TeachABI module completion, including: (1) educators' understanding of the material presented in TeachABI, (2) educators' intentions to apply strategies learned in TeachABI in their classroom setting, as well as (3) internal and external factors that may facilitate or impede educators' ability to apply TeachABI material in their classroom.

Consistent with the first study aim, educators described an enhanced understanding of ABI and ABI‐specific classroom strategies following module completion. Overall, educators reported an increase in ABI knowledge and strategies, including increased confidence in their ability to apply the module learnings in the future. While completing TeachABI, educators reflected on their prior knowledge, use of classroom strategies and personal or professional experiences with ABI, actively linking these insights to the module's content. Existing literature supports the value of reflection as a learning tool, noting that reflection helps build upon previous experiences and fosters a deeper understanding of the subject (Randel et al. 2016; Ash et al. 2005; Radović et al. 2023). Therefore, by integrating the module material with their existing expertise and experiences, educators likely achieved a more comprehensive understanding of the module's content.

In relation to the second study aim, educators reported their intentions to apply learned strategies from the TeachABI module into their classroom. Participants reported experiencing internal changes through engaging with the TeachABI module. Of note, educators' reports pertaining to internal factors, including their increased knowledge and confidence of working with students following ABI in the classroom, were predominantly positive. Educators reported increased competence and self/professional competence following module completion, and increased knowledge about ABI and ABI‐specific strategies that they could immediately apply in their classroom following the completion of TeachABI. Despite these positive changes, educators discussed that practice using the learned strategies in their classroom setting would further increase their confidence in applying the material learned. These findings are consistent with previous literature, indicating that educators who actively trial teaching strategies in their classrooms experience increased confidence, deeper understanding and improved instructional effectiveness through real‐world application and feedback (Paolini 2015; Tomaszewski et al. 2022). Furthermore, many educators reported their intent to keep and refer back to the module materials. Research shows that educators who intentionally keep and revisit professional development resources are more likely to deepen their understanding, retain new strategies and effectively apply them in the classroom, as supported by research emphasizing reflective practice, purposeful engagement and sustained learning (Leggett 2025; Noonan 2019; El Islami et al. 2022).

In relation to the third study aim, educators identified several factors that could either facilitate or impede the implementation of TeachABI learnings. External factors were identified by educators as a key variable in their ability to apply the knowledge and skills learned in TeachABI. Educators identified many barriers associated with implementing knowledge and strategies from the module, including limited classroom space, multiple students with exceptionalities, the interaction between students and the classroom environment, class size and the availability of material resources. Importantly, those teaching in smaller or specialized classrooms also noted environmental factors as possible barriers. However, these participants recognized that environmental factors are less of an obstacle in their smaller classrooms, where they have more resources and staffing (compared to classrooms with more students, less space and fewer resources available). These findings are consistent with broader research highlighting the significant influence of class size on teaching strategies, student‐teacher interactions and classroom management (Blatchford et al. 2009). In particular, large class sizes and the presence of students with diverse and complex needs can overwhelm educators, especially when additional supports and/or resources are lacking (Bascia and Rottmann 2011; Laitsch et al. 2021). Although educators reported that ABI strategies could be applied universally across the classroom, they also emphasized that the effectiveness of such strategies may be constrained by systemic factors in the school system. These findings are consistent with literature emphasizing the importance of manageable class sizes and access to resources for educators to enhance student learning outcomes (Laitsch et al. 2021; Bascia and Faubert 2012). In Ontario boards the average class size ranges from a maximum of 26 for kindergarten (with an upper cap of 29 or 32 in exceptional circumstances), 23 students in primary (grades 1–3) and an average cap of 24.5 students for intermediate (grades 4–8) classrooms. Of additional relevance, the class size regulations do not take into consideration the number of students with an Individual Education Plan (IEP) in the classroom. In 2022–2023, approximately 17.6% of all students in publicly funded elementary and secondary schools in Ontario had an IEP (Data Ontario 2023). Students with IEPs require more time, resources and staffing to support their learning. Without these supports, educators face significant challenges in meeting the needs of all students, particularly when there is not enough funding, staffing and large class sizes (Birdwell et al. 2016; Vaz et al. 2015; Gal et al. 2025; DeSpain 2021). Overall, these external factors, largely beyond the control of individual educators, are shaped by broader school board policies and systemic structures. As such, they represent a possible barrier in the practical application of knowledge and strategies learned in TeachABI.

Participants also identified social influences, including the school team and intergroup conflict, as key factors that may facilitate or impact an educator's ability to apply the knowledge and skills learned through their completion of TeachABI. These findings align with research emphasizing the importance of collaborative school cultures and strong professional networks in supporting the implementation of professional development. For example, literature shows that strong social–emotional skills, supportive leadership and a collaborative school culture foster educators' application of professional development learnings effectively (Brown et al. 2020; Ettenhofer et al. 2019). Another factor described by educators that may impact the application of knowledge and strategies from TeachABI is their relationship with the student's family. Many educators reported that caregivers are often reluctant to disclose health information, making it difficult for educators to work with parents to help their child. According to a systematic review of parents' experiences during the first year after their child's ABI, parents reported hesitation in fully disclosing their child's condition and/or medical history due to fear of stigma (Fernandes et al. 2024).

Although some educators indicated remaining gaps in their knowledge after completing TeachABI, others suggested that TeachABI motivated them to learn more about ABI and requested additional educational materials. Of note, additional ABI‐specific programs and resources are linked in the module to allow for additional and/or future learnings for educators. An environmental scan of ABI resources highlights that a number of online resources exist to support educators in understanding ABI (Saly, Marshall, et al. 2023). TeachABI was designed to address a gap in Ontario's educator training landscape, as identified in a needs assessment (Stevens et al. 2021). As such, it is expected that educators may have further questions following module completion, which can be addressed through the additional resources provided throughout the module.

Although educators indicated that TeachABI increased their ABI knowledge and confidence, many also acknowledged the complexity and heterogeneity of ABI. Importantly, participants highlighted challenges related to accessing student‐specific information and collaboration across school teams and with caregivers. These findings suggest that TeachABI may be most effective when embedded within a broader continuum of support that includes collaboration among educators, families and healthcare professionals. Specifically, future research should investigate the integration of TeachABI into hospital discharge planning that occurs with clinicians, families and educators. Integration of TeachABI into existing hospital transition processes could help address the highly individualized nature of ABI by encouraging communication between families and educators while maintaining the accessibility of TeachABI.

The development of TeachABI is iterative. Educator feedback from the current study will be considered and changes will be made to the module prior to future research. Next steps in this project include the adaptation of the elementary TeachABI module for high school educators and implementing the module for educators across Ontario (Provvidenza et al. 2024). The implementation of this module has also begun in other countries around the world, including Australia (Drake et al. 2024).

Limitations

The findings from this study should be considered with acknowledgement of relevant limitations. First, all educator interviews were conducted after participants completed TeachABI. Therefore, educators' pretraining knowledge of ABI and ABI strategies in the classroom was captured retrospectively, which may have influenced their answers. Furthermore, our sample may not fully reflect Ontario's educator population as many participants had prior professional or personal experience with ABI. Additionally, the study sample included a higher number of female educators; this aligns with the educator workforce: in 2022–2023 there was a higher percentage of female compared to male educators in Ontario (76.60% female, 23.40% male) (Statistics Canada 2024). Educators completed the module with the research assistant online. Although the research assistant had their camera off, their presence may have impacted how much time the educator spent reviewing TeachABI and supporting resources. Finally, completing a qualitative interview where participants engaged in reflection of the module and their professional and personal experiences may have increased uptake of professional development learnings for educators following completion of TeachABI.

Conclusions

Overall, this study highlights TeachABI as an effective tool for increasing ABI‐specific knowledge and strategies for educators in the classroom. Educators highlighted increased competence and self/professional confidence for working with young people post‐ABI, following their completion of TeachABI. The results also demonstrate the importance of considering the external factors, which may facilitate and/or act as barriers towards implementing certain strategies for students post‐ABI in the classroom and are outside the control of the educator. Future research should explore how TeachABI may be implemented into existing hospital‐to‐school transition processes.
