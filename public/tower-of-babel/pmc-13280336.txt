Trends in the Use of Antibiotics for Open Forearm Fractures in Pediatric Patients From 2015 to 2023
Authors: Luke Sang, Divij Sharma, Michaela Booker, Ishaan Swarup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to assess the current practices and trends of antibiotic use for pediatric open fractures across varying severities.MethodsThis was a retrospective review of the Pediatric Health Information System database from October 2015 to December 2023. Patients 18 years old or younger with an International Classification of Diseases diagnosis code corresponding to an initial encounter open forearm fracture were included in this study. The Gustilo-Anderson system was utilized to classify fracture severity. Fisher’s exact, Chi-squared, and Mann-Kendall tests were used to analyze antibiotic data.ResultsA total of 1,224 patients were included in this study. Most patients had type I or II fractures (97.2%) and did not receive surgical intervention (51.9%). A cephalosporin was administered to 95.7% of patients with open fractures, with cefazolin being the most utilized antibiotic (92.9%). Type I or II open fracture patients received only cefazolin at a significantly higher rate than type IIIA-C fracture patients (93.4% vs. 76.5%; P < .01), who more frequently received multiple cephalosporins (1.9% vs. 11.8%; P < .01). An aminoglycoside or penicillin, or multiple additional antibiotics were added significantly more often for type IIIA-C fractures than for type I or II fractures (4.4% vs. 8.8%; P < .01). Between 2015 and 2023, there was a significant increase in cephalosporins given to type I or II open fracture patients (P < .05). There was also an increasing trend of utilizing a single antibiotic for these patients (P < .05). No significant differences were found in antibiotic selection across hospital regions when evaluating all open fractures.ConclusionsIn recent years, there has been an improvement in adherence to the current standard of care for antibiotic selection in pediatric type I or II open forearm fracture patients. However, variability still exists for type IIIA-C open fractures, suggesting the need for further standardization of antibiotic usage for these patients. Our findings highlight the need for additional studies to recommend the optimal antibiotic protocol in type IIIA-C pediatric open fractures.Key Concepts(1)There has been recent improvement in adherence to the current antibiotic standard of care for type I or II open fractures in pediatric patients.(2)There may be sufficient regional standardization of general antibiotic selection for pediatric open fractures across the United States.(3)There is still variability for type IIIA-C open fractures, suggesting the need for further standardization in these cases and additional studies to recommend the optimal antibiotic protocol.Level of EvidenceIII, Retrospective Cohort StudyKeywords: Antibiotics, Open fractures, Gustilo-Anderson, Antibiotic protocol

Introduction

Open fractures are defined as fractures that directly communicate with the external environment [1]. In pediatric populations, most open fractures result from trauma events such as falls from significant heights or motor vehicle accidents [2]. The incidence of pediatric open fractures varies by institution but accounts for 2-9% of all pediatric fractures [[2], [3], [4]]. More than 60% involve the tibia or radius [5,6], with forearm fractures being the most common type of pediatric open fracture [7]. Open fractures are at high risk for contamination, placing patients at the risk of fracture-related infections [8]. The infection risk after open fractures in pediatric patients has been reported to range from 2 to 10% [9], underscoring the importance of timely infection prevention protocols.

Existing evidence in adults supports early antibiotic administration after injury, with longer delays increasing the risk of infection [2,10,11]. No similar high-quality data exist in pediatric patients. The current American College of Surgeons (ACS) Trauma Quality Improvement Program Best Practice Guidelines recommend antibiotics within 60 min [12]. Furthermore, ACS trauma center verification standards require that all trauma centers maintain treatment guidelines for open extremity fractures, including protocols for time to antibiotic administration. Similarly, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) for adult patients recommends early use of antibiotics but does not specify an exact time frame [13]. First-generation cephalosporins have become the cornerstone of prophylaxis, demonstrably reducing infection rates [10]. Furthermore, although there is a lack of strong evidence, an aminoglycoside, such as gentamicin, is often added for Gustilo-Anderson type III injuries in adult patients for additional gram-negative coverage [2,14]. Penicillin is commonly added for Clostridium species or anaerobic coverage in patients with dirt-exposed wounds [8,9]. The AAOS CPG reflects this evidence with a strong recommendation for administering cefazolin or clindamycin for all types of open fractures and utilizing additional gram-negative coverage in type III fractures.

In contrast, no equivalent consensus exists for pediatric open fractures, and antibiotic selection is largely driven by institutional preference [[15], [16], [17]]. While there has historically been considerable variability in antibiotic protocols for pediatric open fractures, recent efforts have sought to standardize treatment approaches [9,18], yet there remain limited data on the widespread impacts of these efforts on altering clinical management or the need for further standardization. Notably, recent trends in adult populations have demonstrated increasing use of ceftriaxone to treat open fractures [19,20].

Thus, the purpose of this study was to characterize the trends in the use of antibiotics in pediatric patients with open forearm fractures. We hypothesize that the majority of patients receive cefazolin, but there is an increasing use of ceftriaxone, similar to trends seen in adults.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective review of the Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) database from October 2015 to December 2023. The PHIS database houses extensive inpatient, emergency department, ambulatory, surgery, and observation encounter data from over 50 large, tertiary pediatric hospitals affiliated with the Children’s Hospital Association (CHA) throughout the United States. Data are deidentified during submission, and the CHA continuously validates all inputs into the database. Patients 18 years old or younger with an International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis code corresponding to an initial encounter open forearm fracture were included (Supplemental 1). Patients with missing data, defined as incomplete demographic or encounter-level data fields required for analysis, were excluded. Patients were also excluded for polytrauma if their initial encounter included multiple of the ICD-10 diagnosis codes selected for this study. All hospitals within the PHIS database that had qualifying encounters during the study period were included in the final analysis. This study used only deidentified data from the PHIS database and was determined to be exempt from institutional review board review at our institution.

Patient demographic data, including age, sex, race, and ethnicity, were obtained from the PHIS database. Medical encounter data associated with the initial encounter open forearm fracture ICD-10 codes were also acquired. These data included admission date, operative status, and regional distribution of the patient’s hospital. The ICD-10 codes were utilized to classify the severity of the open forearm fractures with the Gustilo-Anderson classification system. Although studies have raised concerns regarding the interobserver reliability of the Gustilo-Anderson classification system [21], it remains commonly utilized and impacts treatment decision-making, especially in regards to antibiotic protocols [22]. As ICD-10 coding started in October 2015 in the United States, this study included data from this earliest point to the end of 2023.

Antibiotic data for each initial encounter of an open forearm fracture were collected through pharmacy dispensing records linked to each encounter in the PHIS database. This included the number and specification of the antibiotics given to each patient. Because cephalosporins are often used for pediatric patients with open fractures of all severities as the primary antibiotic, with other antibiotics given for additional coverage when needed [9,[23], [24], [25]], antibiotics were analyzed separately under “cephalosporins” and “additional antibiotics” categories. Cephalosporins categorized as “other” included cefuroxime and cefdinir. Additional antibiotics categorized as “other” included vancomycin, ampicillin/sulbactam, and metronidazole, among other agents used at low individual frequencies. Each unique antibiotic selected for a patient was counted separately when determining frequencies.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized patient demographics, admission details, and antibiotics utilized. Fisher’s exact and Chi-squared tests were used for comparative analysis in antibiotic selection between fracture severity and hospital regions when appropriate. Mann-Kendall tests were performed to assess the trends in antibiotic selection across Gustilo-Anderson classifications for the study period. Significance was considered when the P value was <.05. All analyses were performed using RStudio v2024.12.1 (RStudio, Boston, MA).

Results

A total of 170 patients were excluded from the initial cohort of 1,394 patients: 14 for polytrauma and 156 for missing encounter data. A total of 1,224 patients were included in the final cohort for this study. The average age of the population was 10.2 years (standard deviation: 3.4) (Table 1), and the majority of the population was White (72.0%) and male (71.2%) patients. Other than 2015, when ICD-10 codes had not been utilized for a full calendar year, there was a relatively consistent number of pediatric open forearm fractures per year. More patients were from hospitals in the South (39.2%) and fewer from the Northeast (8.4%). The majority of patients had Gustilo-Anderson type I or II open fractures (97.2%) and did not receive surgical interventions (51.9%).

Antibiotics administered for all pediatric open forearm fractures

When looking at all open forearm fractures, cefazolin was the most utilized cephalosporin and antibiotic (92.9%) (Table 2). Other cephalosporins, such as cephalexin (0.3%) and ceftriaxone (0.1%), were rarely used as monotherapy. More frequently, these antibiotics were given in combination with other cephalosporins (2.2%). Among those receiving multiple cephalosporins, cefazolin + cephalexin was the most common combination (51.9%), occurring exclusively in type I or II fractures. Cefazolin + ceftriaxone was the second most common combination (25.9%), observed across both fracture types (Table 3). Noncephalosporin antibiotics were administered to 21.1% of patients (n = 258). Of patients receiving noncephalosporin antibiotics, 20.9% (54 of 258) received an aminoglycoside or penicillin, and 57.8% (149 of 258) received multiple antibiotics. This pattern was more prevalent in type IIIA-C open fractures.

Differences in antibiotics administered for different types of open forearm fracture

Comparing the selection of cephalosporins based on open forearm fracture severity, cefazolin monotherapy was significantly more commonly utilized in type I or II fractures (93.4%) than in type IIIA-C fractures (76.4%; P < .01) (Table 4). Multiple cephalosporins were given more often for type IIIA-C fractures (11.8%) than for type I or II fractures (1.9%). Among type IIIA-C fracture patients who received multiple cephalosporins (n = 4), cefazolin was combined with either ceftriaxone (n = 2, 50.0%) or cefepime (n = 2, 50.0%) (Table 3). No cephalosporin was given in 11.8% (4 of 34) of type IIIA-C fractures, compared to 4.1% (49 of 1,190) of type I or II fractures. Given the small type IIIA-C subgroup, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Trends in antibiotics utilized for pediatric open forearm fractures

We identified a significant increase in cephalosporins being given for type I or II pediatric open forearm fractures from 2015 to 2023 (Tau-b = −0.67 [95% confidence interval {CI}: -1.15 to −0.33]; P < .05) (Fig. 1). Additionally, there was an increasing trend in the administration of a single antibiotic for these fractures (Tau-b = 0.56 [95% CI: 0.17 to 1.08]; P < .05) (Fig. 2) and a corresponding decrease in the use of additional antibiotics (Tau-b = −0.83 [95% CI: -1.22 to −0.67]; P < .01). For type IIIA-C fractures, there were no significant changes in antibiotic use over the 9-year study period. There were no significant differences in the utilization of specific antibiotics for all pediatric open forearm fractures across different hospital regions (Table 5). Discussion

Historically, there has been significant variability in antibiotic protocols for pediatric open fractures across different institutions [5,[25], [26], [27]]. Recent studies have encouraged the standardization of antibiotic use for these patients [9,18,27], yet there are limited data on the impact of these efforts on the current clinical practice. In this study, we found an increasing improvement in adherence to the current antibiotic standard of care for type I or II pediatric open forearm fractures. There remains variability in antibiotic selection for more severe, type IIIA-C open fractures, suggesting a need for further standardization for these patients. While several observed trends reached statistical significance, the absolute magnitudes of change are modest: the rate of no cephalosporin use in type I or II fractures decreased from 6.3% in 2015 to 2.8% in 2023, and single-antibiotic use increased from 68.8% in 2015 to 81.7% in 2023. These changes are clinically meaningful indicators of improving guideline adherence, but considerable room for standardization remains.

Antibiotic standardization has been shown to reduce the time to administration and improve staff efficiency [[28], [29], [30]]. Given the importance of antibiotic treatment within the first hour after injury in open fractures [2,10,31], the implementation of standard antibiotic protocols for pediatric open fractures could improve the clinical care of these patients. Studies in pediatric open fractures have demonstrated variability in the antibiotics given to patients depending on the physician or institution [26,[32], [33], [34]]. Wetzel et al. [15] found that there is a general lack of consensus when it comes to management approaches for pediatric open fractures. Thus, initiatives have been increasing to push for using a standard antibiotic protocol for these patients based on the most current evidence [9,18]. There is a lack of data on the impacts of these efforts and the current state of care. Importantly, while antibiotic selection is a critical component of open fracture management, the timeliness of antibiotic delivery after injury is an equally important and independent determinant of infection risk. The PHIS database does not capture time to antibiotic administration; as a result, this study was limited to characterizing antibiotic selection patterns. Barnard et al. [35] examined the timeliness of antibiotic administration across open fractures and demonstrated that lower extremity open fractures were more likely to receive timely antibiotics than upper extremity fractures, with mode of arrival, trauma center designation, facility size, and academic affiliation all identified as significant factors influencing time to administration. While our study was unable to assess these institutional and logistical factors, these findings underscore that antibiotic selection and timing are independently important and complementary targets for quality improvement in open fracture care.

In this study, we found that between 2015 and 2023, there was an increasing trend in cephalosporin administration to pediatric patients with type I or II open forearm fractures. There was also a rising trend of monotherapy with a single antibiotic. Utilizing cefazolin alone was the most common antibiotic protocol in this group. The current evidence for type I or II pediatric open fractures supports giving only a first-generation cephalosporin, such as cefazolin, with generally low infection rates [24,33,34]. Taken together, our results suggest that adherence to the current standard of care for type I or II pediatric open fractures has increased throughout the United States. Given its effectiveness in preventing infections in these patients, continued consistency of this protocol should be encouraged. This is notably higher than the 61.1% cefazolin monotherapy compliance reported by Lin et al. [36] across 24 adult trauma centers, suggesting that PHIS-affiliated pediatric tertiary centers may achieve higher guideline adherence for low-grade fractures.

However, for type IIIA-C open forearm fractures, our results showed greater variability in current antibiotic practices. These patients received multiple or no cephalosporins at all at a significantly higher rate than type I or II open fracture patients. Furthermore, these patients had an aminoglycoside, penicillin, or another additional antibiotic added at an increased rate. They also received multiple additional antibiotics much more frequently. These results reflect the lack of data, consensus, and recommendations in managing pediatric type IIIA-C open fractures [9]. In the few studies, there is variability in the cephalosporins and additional antibiotics given to these patients [5,25,32,37]. Even within these protocols for type IIIA-C open fractures, recommendations, such as utilizing gentamicin for additional gram-negative coverage, lack substantial evidence to support their use [2,14,38]. A comprehensive review by Garner et al. [30] similarly highlights the limited and evolving evidence base for aminoglycoside use in open fractures in adult patients. We found no significant trends or changes in antibiotic selection for type IIIA-C fractures during our study period. Thus, given these fractures’ higher infection and complication rates due to their increased severity [5,39], our findings, which should be interpreted as hypothesis generating given the small type IIIA-C sample, highlight the need for more high-quality studies in this population to refine and optimize antibiotic recommendations and increase standardization. Similarly, although more established guidelines exist for adult patients with these fractures, such as the use of first-generation cephalosporin with the addition of an aminoglycoside for gram-negative coverage [13,14,31,40], recent studies have demonstrated the increasing use and effectiveness of ceftriaxone as a monotherapy [19,20]. In contrast to our hypothesis, ceftriaxone use showed no significant upward trend across all pediatric open fracture types and years, suggesting that the adult trend toward ceftriaxone as a simpler monotherapy option has not yet been adopted in these pediatric tertiary care centers. Further investigation may be necessary in this population as well to optimize antibiotic guidelines. Future studies should also examine antibiotic selection stratified by operative status as the 48.1% of patients who underwent surgery in this cohort may represent a clinically distinct population with different antibiotic requirements.

For patients across all severity classifications who received no cephalosporins, available pharmacy data suggest that the majority received alternative gram-positive coverage, such as clindamycin. This was consistent with its use as an alternative for patients with a penicillin or cephalosporin allergy; however, as the PHIS database does not include exact allergy documentation, we could not confirm allergy status, and it remains possible that some cases represent a gap in care.

We found no significant differences in the antibiotic selections across different hospital regions (Midwest, West, South, Northeast). Growing literature has demonstrated differences in antibiotic resistance and causative organisms across areas, possibly due to regional protocol preferences [41]. Our findings suggest sufficient regional standardization of general antibiotic selection for pediatric open fractures across the United States. However, additional studies are needed to determine the impacts of utilizing the same antibiotics on complication rates and whether more specific regimens are needed across regions.

This study has several limitations. First, utilizing an extensive database leaves the possibility of patient coding and classification errors. As patients were identified using ICD-10 codes, this did not allow us to compare the differences between type I and type II open forearm fractures. Additionally, due to the constraints of the database, we could not assess more specific details of antibiotic selections, such as the timing of administration, dosing, and duration. Even with the broad scope of the PHIS database, including more than 50 tertiary pediatric hospitals across the United States, there was still a small sample size of type IIIA-C fractures, which may have impacted our subanalysis. The primary statistical analyses did not account for hospital-level clustering, which may underestimate standard errors for some comparisons and should be considered when interpreting findings, particularly for lower-frequency antibiotic comparisons and trend analyses. We selected only pediatric open forearm fractures because they account for the majority of open fractures in children and to standardize our study population [4,5,7]. Validation studies are needed in pediatric patients with other types of open fractures. Additionally, this study was unable to assess clinical outcomes such as infection rates or the need for reoperation, limiting the interpretation of the clinical impact of the observed antibiotic selection patterns. Potential confounding by injury mechanism and wound contamination level could not be assessed using the PHIS database. As PHIS includes only large, tertiary academic pediatric centers, findings may not be generalizable to community hospitals or nonpediatric institutions. Also, with the risk of ICD-10 miscoding, this may disproportionately affect the type III subgroup, given its small size and the known interobserver variability of the Gustilo-Anderson classification system.

Conclusions

Standardizing antibiotic protocols for pediatric patients with open fractures is crucial to ensure timely administration and improve patient care. Within the PHIS database, this study shows improved adherence to antibiotic selection recommendations for type I or II open fractures in pediatric patients, a finding consistent across all regions of the United States. However, there is variability in the antibiotics used for type IIIA-C open fractures, highlighting the need for further standardization in these cases and additional studies to inform antibiotic protocol. Importantly, this study was limited to antibiotic selection and could not assess the timing of antibiotic delivery, which remains an independent and critical determinant of infection prevention and warrants dedicated investigation in future studies.
