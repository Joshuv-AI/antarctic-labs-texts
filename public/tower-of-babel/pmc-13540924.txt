Uncovering lipid biomarkers linked to methylphenidate efficacy in treating apathy in Alzheimer’s disease: insights from the ADMET 2 trial
Authors: Myuri Ruthirakuhan, Maya Mills, Paul Rosenberg, Norman Haughey, Jacobo Mintzer, Olga Brawman-Mintzer, Suzanne Craft, Nathan Herrmann, Alan J. Lerner, Allan I. Levey, Prasad R. Padala, Anton Porsteinsson, Christopher H. van Dyck, David Shade, Krista L. Lanctôt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApathy is a prevalent neuropsychiatric symptom (NPS) in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), linked to functional impairment and reduced quality of life. The Apathy in Dementia Methylphenidate Trial 2 (ADMET-2) found methylphenidate (MPH) had modest efficacy for treating apathy, but treatment responses varied. MPH blocks dopamine and noradrenaline transporters, inhibiting dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake. Lipids are closely tied to monamine transporter function through their structural and signaling roles in neurotransmission, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity. This study aimed to identify lipid species associated with MPH response and explore lipid pathway disruptions in responders versus non-responders.MethodsParticipants from ADMET-2 with baseline lipidomic data were included. Responders were defined by a ≥4-point improvement on the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Apathy subscale (NPI-A), or moderate-to-marked improvement on the ADCS-Clinicians Global Impression-Change (ADCS-CGIC). Baseline plasma samples underwent lipidomic profiling. Sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) in the MPH group was used to identify lipid species distinguishing responders from non-responders. Model performance was evaluated by area under the curve (AUC). Identified lipid species were analyzed in MetaboAnalyst for pathway enrichment. A secondary analysis in the placebo group assessed specificity of findings to MPH.ResultsOf the 43 MPH-treated participants, 28 were NPI-apathy responders, and 10 were ADCS-CGIC responders. The PLS-DA model achieved robust discrimination between responders and non-responders (NPI-apathy: AUC = 0.82 +/- 0.05; ADCS-CGIC: AUC=0.84 +/- 0.07). Pathway analysis revealed disruptions in ceramide, phosphosphingolipid, and glycosphingolipid metabolism for NPI-apathy responders, and ceramide and glycosphingolipid metabolism for ADCS-CGIC responders. In 55 placebo-treated participants (30 NPI-apathy responders), an AUC of 0.79 +/- 0.05 was achieved, with pathway analysis indicating disruption in glycosphingolipid metabolism only.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the utility of lipidomic profiling in identifying biomarkers of response to MPH in AD patients with apathy. The identified lipidomic species are broadly related to monoamine transporter function, reflecting their role in neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity. While glycosphingolipid metabolism appears broadly linked to changes in apathy, disruptions in ceramide and phospholipid metabolism may be specific to MPH treatment. Further study of these pathways may offer insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying apathy and treatment response, and could inform future biomarker-guided interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02055-y.

Introduction

Apathy is the most common neuropsychiatric symptom (NPS) in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), affecting 40–80% of patients and increasing in prevalence with disease severity [1]. It is characterized by diminished motivation, reduced interest in activities, and blunted emotional responses persisting for at least four weeks. Apathy is associated with greater caregiver burden [2], faster disease progression, and increased mortality risk [3]. Despite its high prevalence and impact, there are no approved treatments for apathy in AD.

Methylphenidate (MPH), a dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, has shown modest efficacy in reducing apathy, as demonstrated in the Apathy in Dementia Methylphenidate Trial 2 (ADMET-2), a six-month phase 3 study [4]. In ADMET-2, MPH (20 mg/day) led to significant improvements in apathy with a small-to-medium effect size and was well tolerated [4]. However, only 44% of patients receiving MPH demonstrated minimal improvement, compared to 35% on placebo, highlighting substantial variability in treatment response. Identifying biological predictors of response could enhance clinical decision-making and provide insights into treatment mechanisms.

MPH blocks the dopamine transporter (DAT) [5] and norepinephrine transporter (NET) [6], increasing extracellular dopamine (DA) and norepinephrine (NE) levels in the synaptic cleft. These transporters are membrane-bound proteins whose function is tightly regulated by the lipid microenvironment, which includes sphingolipids, glycosphingolipids, and cholesterol. Lipid rafts, which are rich in sphingomyelin and glycosphingolipids, are key in localizing and stabilizing DAT and NET at the synaptic membrane [7]. Disruption of lipid composition may therefore alter transporter localization, conformation, and affinity, ultimately affecting MPH efficacy. As such, lipidomic profiling may offer a promising avenue for understanding the molecular basis of treatment response.

The brain is highly enriched in sphingolipids and other membrane lipids, which regulate key biological processes including synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, glial activation, and the activity of amyloid-processing enzymes [8–10]. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism has been implicated across neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, with alterations detectable not only in brain tissue but also in circulating lipids in the periphery [11–13]. Evidence suggests that brain lipid changes can be reflected in the periphery, through lipid-rich exosomes released into circulation [14, 15]. Furthermore, characteristic changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) lipid composition have been associated with increased AD risk, disease progression, and amyloid and tau pathology [16, 17]. Given that DAT and NET function are modulated by the lipid bilayer, and that dopaminergic and norepinephrine signalling is dependent on efficient transporter trafficking and vesicular transport, perturbations in lipid metabolism may influence individual responses to treatment with MPH, and apathy in and of itself.

Lipidomic profiling in plasma provides a potentially informative window into brain lipid metabolism. Although the brain is separated from the peripheral circulation by the blood–brain barrier, several mechanisms allow central lipid alterations to be reflected in the plasma lipidome. Lipids and lipid metabolites can be transported across the blood–brain barrier via lipoprotein-mediated transport, while neurons and glial cells release extracellular vesicles and lipid-rich exosomes that enter systemic circulation [18, 19]. In addition, brain lipid turnover generates metabolites that are exported to the periphery [20]. Consequently, alterations in neuronal membrane composition, lipid raft organization, and sphingolipid metabolism in the brain may be reflected in circulating lipid profiles.

Changes in plasma lipid levels may reflect multiple underlying biological processes of potential importance to brain pathology. Among the lipid classes of interest, ceramides, glycosphingolipids, and phospholipids may be particularly relevant to neuropsychiatric symptoms and treatment response in AD. Ceramides are central bioactive sphingolipids involved in membrane turnover, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptotic signaling, and altered circulating ceramide levels have been linked to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline [21]. Glycosphingolipids, including glucosylceramides and lactosylceramides, are important constituents of lipid rafts and influence membrane organization, vesicular trafficking, and receptor or transporter localization [22]. Phospholipids, including phosphosphingolipids and glycerophospholipids, are major structural components of cellular membranes that regulate bilayer fluidity, curvature, and protein conformation [23]. Accordingly, differences in plasma levels of these lipid classes may reflect variation in membrane remodeling, neuroinflammatory activity, or lipid raft integrity, processes that could plausibly influence monoamine transporter function and response to MPH.

In this study, we use lipidomic profiling to compare baseline lipid species between MPH responders and non-responders in ADMET-2. By identifying distinct lipid species associated with treatment response, we aim to uncover potential biomarkers that could inform personalized approaches to apathy management in AD. We also used lipidomic profiling to compare baseline lipid species between responders and non-responders randomized to placebo to determine whether the observed findings were due to treatment with MPH, or generalized apathy.

Methods

Study participants

Samples were collected from the Apathy in Dementia Methylphenidate Trial 2 (ADMET-2) [NCT02346201]. This was a 6-month, multi-site, phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial comparing MPH (target dose: 20 mg daily [10 mg twice a day]) and placebo. Caregivers of study participants received a standardized psychosocial intervention at monthly study visits and 24 h telephone availability. Eligibility criteria for this study have been published previously [4]. Briefly, participants had mild to moderate-stage AD, and clinically significant apathy. Participants with major depression disorder, clinically significant agitation/aggression, or psychosis, or who had recent changes to their psychotropic medications were excluded. The current study included the subset of 97 participants (N = 42 MPH; N = 55 placebo) who contributed blood samples for biomarker analyses at baseline.

Clinical assessments

The apathy measures chosen for this analysis were those used as co-primary outcomes for the ADMET-2 clinical trial.

Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) [24]: The NPI was based on a clinical interview conducted by a trained researcher with caregiver input, and assessed the frequency and severity of 12 neuropsychiatric symptoms including apathy (NPI-A) in the preceding 4 weeks. The frequency (scored from 0 to 4) and severity (scored from 0 to 3) of apathy was determined on an ordinal scale and total scores (range: 0–12) were calculated by multiplying the frequency and severity scores. A higher score indicates greater severity of apathy. The NPI-A is the most commonly used measure to assess apathy severity, and corresponds with the diagnostic criteria for apathy [25]. Treatment response based on the NPI-A was defined by an improvement of 4 points or more [26].

Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study Clinical Global Impression of Change (ADCS-CGIC) [27]: The ADCS-CGIC was completed by an independent clinician, to assess clinically meaningful change between baseline and 6 months. Ratings were based on a 7-point Likert-type scale (1 = very much improved, 2 = moderately improved, 3 = minimally improved, 4 = no change, 5 = minimally worse, 6 = moderately worse, 7 = very much worse). Treatment response in the presented analysis was defined as moderate-very much improved. The ADCS-CGIC is a clinician-rated global outcome that integrates multiple sources of information, including patient function and caregiver report, to assess clinically meaningful improvement.

Peripheral markers

Lipidomic analyses: Lipid analyses were conducted by MS/MSALL on a TripleTOF™ 5600 (AB SCIEX, Redwood City, CA) mass spectrometer. Samples (50 mL injection volume) were infused by HPLC at a constant flow rate of 5 µL/min using a SIL-20AC XR autosampler. Source parameters were as follows: ion source gases 15 psi (GSI) and 20 psi (GS2), curtain gas 30 psi, temperature 150 °C, positive ion spray voltage + 5500 V, declustering potential at 80 V and collision energy at 10 V. Each sample was run in duplicate in positive ion mode. An initial TOF MS scan provided an overview of the total lipid content at an accumulation time of 5s. Precursor ions were selected by sequential 1Da mass steps from 200 to 1200m/z, and the analytes in each 1Da step were introduced into the collision chamber and fragments identified by TOF with a scan range of 100–1500 m/z and accumulation time of 450ms. TOF MS and MS/MSALL data obtained were post-aligned to internal standards using Analyst 1.7 TF.

Validation of Lipid Identifications: Lipid identifications were validated using a pooled sample extracted and sequentially analysed 8 times. Criteria for inclusion of putative lipids for further analysis was that MS/MS fragment peaks were present in 7 of the 8 pooled runs with a coefficient of variation (CV) for peak identifications of less than 20%. Peak identifications meeting these criteria were used to develop a targeted method in LipidView, that searches to identify these pre-validated lipid species in experimental samples using a custom MatLab script and MultiQuant software (version 3.0, AB SCIEX, Concord, ON, Canada). All peak intensities were corrected by their corresponding internal standard and each sample duplicate averaged. For statistical analyses, intensity values of 0 were replaced with a minimum intensity value that was calculated by dividing the average intensity value for that particular lipid class by 0.01. Using this method we identified ~ 1000 individual lipid species from 12 different classes of lipid that included: ceramides, monohexosylceramides, dihexosylceramides, sphingomyelins, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylserine, phospatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylglycerol, cholesterol esters, monoakayldiacylglyerides, diacylglycerides, and triacylglycerides.

Statistical analyses

Sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) was used to identify the most discriminative lipidomic features between NPI-apathy treatment responders (</= -4 points or greater) and non-responders while reducing dimensionality, by summarizing a large number of correlated lipid variables into a smaller set of latent components that best separate the groups. This method is well suited for high-dimensional lipidomic datasets in which the number of variables substantially exceeds the number of observations, as it performs simultaneous dimensionality reduction and feature selection to identify the most informative variables while minimizing overfitting. Prior to analysis, the data were mean-centered and scaled. The number of components was determined using k-fold cross-validation, optimizing for classification performance while balancing model sparsity. Cross-validation was used to evaluate model performance on unseen data and reduce the risk of overfitting. The optimal number of features per component was selected through a grid search approach to minimize the balance error rate (BER). Final model parameters were chosen based on the combination that yielded the lowest classification error while ensuring feature interpretability. To ensure model robustness, bootstrapping (n = 100 iterations) was performed, where the dataset was sampled with replacement and sPLS-DA models were refitted to assess the stability of feature selection and the variability in classification accuracy. Additionally, permutation testing (n = 1000) was conducted to evaluate whether the observed performance exceeded what would be expected by class assignment. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis based on cross-validation predictions, with discrimination quantified using the area under the curve (AUC). This analysis was repeated in ADCS-CGIC treatment responders and non-responders. These procedures collectively reduce the likelihood that identified lipid features reflect random variation rather than reproducible group differences [28].

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst to identify metabolic pathways differentiating responders from non-responders. This approach integrates over-representation analysis with pathway topology analysis to highlight the most relevant metabolic pathways involved. Metabolite concentration data were mapped against compound lists from the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). False discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 0.05 to determine statistical significance.

The analysis was repeated in participants randomized to placebo to identify whether reported differences were due to broader lipidomic disruptions in patients with apathy, or in response to treatment with MPH. Analyses were conducted separately within the MPH and placebo groups to distinguish lipidomic signatures associated with pharmacological response from those related to broader symptom improvement, as pooling treatment groups could obscure biological signals specific to the mechanism of action of MPH.

As sPLS-DA is a multivariate dimension reduction technique that does not readily accommodate covariate adjustment, post-hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the potential influence of key clinical variables on the identified lipid associations. Specifically, logistic regression models were fitted for selected top-ranking lipid species identified from the sPLS-DA models. In both the MPH and placebo groups, models were adjusted for age and sex.

Additional covariates were included when baseline differences between responders and non-responders were significant.

Power calculation

The sample size of this project was dictated by the sample size for ADMET-2 [NCT02346201], the study from which participants were recruited, including all those who participated in the biomarker add on. We outlined several strategies in our statistical analyses to mitigate the risk of overfitting and assess model robustness. Nevertheless, the findings of this study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and requires validation in larger, independent cohorts to assess generalizability and predictive utility.

Pathway enrichment analysis indicated disruption in lipid pathways related to phosphosphingolipids, ceramides, and glycosphingolipids (Fig. 1b). Phosphosphingolipids demonstrated the strongest trend toward enrichment (p = 0.1), while ceramides and glycosphingolipids also displayed some enrichment, their statistical support was weaker. Notably, certain individual metabolites, such as glucosylceramides (GlcCer) (d18:1/20:0) (p = 0.0171) and GlcCer(d18:1/26:0) (p = 0.0383), exhibited significant differences between groups, reinforcing the possibility of altered sphingolipid metabolism (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Post-hoc sensitivity analyses adjusting for age and sex showed that associations for the top lipid species were attenuated but remained broadly consistent with multivariate findings (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Our findings suggest that lipidomic profiling can distinguish responders from non-responders to MPH in patients with AD and apathy. Specifically, we identified disruptions in sphingolipid metabolism; including phosphosphingolipids, ceramides, and glycosphingolipids, with phosphophingolipid and ceramide metabolism being specific to treatment response. Given the critical role of sphingolipids in membrane structure, vesicular trafficking, and bioactive signaling, these findings provide insight into the molecular underpinnings of apathy and its modulation by MPH.

Sphingolipids are key components of cellular membranes, influencing both their structural integrity and functional properties, including receptor dynamics and neurotransmitter signaling [15, 29]. Within this pathway, ceramides serves as a central molecule, interconverting between various sphingolipid species and participating in cellular processes such as apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and synaptic function [30, 31]. Dysregulation of ceramide metabolism has been implicated in neurodegenerative processes, with increased ceramide levels linked to greater cognitive decline and white matter degeneration in AD [32]. In our study, non-responders exhibited greater disruptions in ceramide and glycosphingolipid metabolism, which may reflect underlying pathophysiological differences limiting their ability to respond to MPH. Additionally, oxidative stress and neuroinflammation may further contribute to the lipidomic disruptions observed in our study. Lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of oxidative stress, has been linked to both apathy and cognitive decline, potentially exacerbating neuronal dysfunction [33]. Neuroinflammation, particularly the accumulation of astrocytic ceramides has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases [34], which could amplify the neurodegenerative processes and hinder treatment response in non-responders.

Glycosphingolipids also demonstrated differential expression in responders and non-responders in the MPH and placebo groups. Specifically, glucosylceramides (GlcCer) were decreased in MPH responders only, while and lactosylceramides (LacCer) were decreased in both MPH and placebo responders. GlcCer are monohexosylceramides and have been linked to intracellular signalling pathways, including vesicular trafficking and neurotransmitter regulation, including interactions with vesicular monoamine transporters, which are responsible for packaging dopamine and norepinephrine into synaptic vesicles [35, 36]. Given that MPH exerts its effects primarily by increasing synaptic dopamine and norepinephrine, disruptions in glycosphingolipid metabolism may interfere with the drug’s ability to enhance neurotransmission. Therefore, the observed changes in GlcCer may reflect mechanisms specifically related to MPH response. LacCer are dihexosylceramides and are enriched in lipid rafts and impact membrane organization, which contribute to membrane organization and receptor localization [37]. Given that LacCer species were decreased in both MPH and placebo responders suggests a broader association with changes in apathy, independent of treatment with MPH. Therefore, while changes in GlcCer may be linked to neurotransmitter-specific effects of MPH, changes in LacCer may reflect more generalized lipid raft disruption that impact dopaminergic and norepnephrine signalling at the membrane level - pathways that are key neurotransmitter systems impacted in apathy [38–40].

Our pathway enrichment analyses revealed a trend toward phosphosphingolipid disruption in NPI-apathy responders versus non-responders, suggesting that sphingolipid-related changes may be particularly relevant for apathy improvement. Prior studies have linked sphingolipid dysregulation to AD pathology, including alterations in ceramide transport proteins that influence amyloid processing and tau aggregation [41–43]. Additionally, higher ceramide levels have been associated with greater white matter atrophy, a key feature of AD-related neurodegeneration.

Future studies should investigate longitudinal lipidomic changes in response to MPH treatment, as dynamic shifts in lipid metabolism may provide deeper insight into treatment mechanisms. In particular, tracking lipidomic profiles over time could reveal whether specific lipid species change in parallel with improvements in apathy, thereby strengthening the potential of lipid markers as predictive and monitoring biomarkers. Beyond apathy, lipid biomarkers may play a broader role in the presence, severity, and treatment response of other NPS in AD. Depression and anxiety frequently co-occur with apathy and share overlapping neurobiological mechanisms [44, 45]. Investigating lipidomic changes in trials targeting these symptoms could help establish their relevance across multiple NPS in AD and support their use in future therapeutic strategies. Limitations of this study include the relatively small sample size, which reduces statistical power and limits generalizability. However, this cohort serves as a discovery sample to identify lipidomic differences between responders and non-responders, providing a foundation for validation in larger studies. Another limitation is that lipidomic analyses were conducted at baseline only, preventing assessment of longitudinal changes. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that baseline lipidomic profiles are predictive of treatment response, highlighting the need for future studies incorporating repeated lipid measurements over time. While our main analyses did not account for direct covariate adjustment, we conducted post-hoc sensitivity analyses adjusting for key clinical variables. These analyses did not noticeably alter the observed associations with MPH, supporting the robustness of the identified lipidomic signatures. As participants in the ADMET-2 trial were required to have clinically significant apathy it was not possible to compare lipidomic profiles between individuals with low versus high apathy severity. Furthermore, participants were excluded if they had a current major depressive episode, clinically significant agitation, or psychosis. Therefore, while the study was not well suited to examine lipidomic associations with other NPS, such as depression or anxiety which may also involve dopaminergic and noradrenergic pathways, results were not confounded by the presence of those NPS. Future studies including broader populations with varying levels of apathy severity, and NPS could help distinguish lipidomic signatures associated with the apathy itself, and other NPS. Psychotropic medications may influence systemic lipid metabolism and therefore could potentially affect plasma lipidomic profiles [46]. However, in the present study, SSRI and SNRI use did not differ between responders and non-responders within either the MPH or placebo groups, reducing the likelihood that the observed lipidomic differences were driven by these medications. Nevertheless, future studies with larger samples should further examine the potential impact of psychotropic medications on lipidomic signatures.

In summary, our findings suggest that lipid markers, particularly those of the sphingolipid pathways, play a role in both the pathophysiology of apathy and its response to MPH treatment. More broadly, this study highlights the potential of lipidomic profiling to identify biomarkers of treatment response in neuropsychiatric symptoms. Future research should investigate whether specific lipid markers can serve as predictive and monitoring biomarkers for therapeutic interventions. Additionally, exploring whether targeting lipid metabolism could enhance treatment efficacy may offer new avenues for biomarker-guided interventions in AD patients with apathy and other NPS.
