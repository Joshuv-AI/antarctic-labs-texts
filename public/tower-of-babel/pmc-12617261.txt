Establishing New Pediatric Thresholds for Digits‐In‐Noise Testing
Authors: Morgan McBride, Allyson Dunlap, Siva Chinnadurai, Brianne B Roby, Andrew Redmann, Abby C Meyer, Luke Jakubowski, Hannah Herd, Rebecca Maher, Chelsea Kimlinger, Madeline Poland, Michael Finch, Asitha D L Jayawardena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo determine if specific pediatric diagnostic criteria exist to determine the presence of conductive hearing loss (CHL) digits in noise (DIN) tablet testing in children.MethodsOne hundred forty nine children aged 3–18 underwent a conventional audiogram, followed by an otolaryngologist examination and tablet‐based digits in noise (DIN) testing. DIN testing consisted of diotic (same‐phased stimuli) and antiphasic (out‐of‐phase stimuli) digits‐in‐noise testing on a HearX Samsung Galaxy tablet with over‐the‐ear headphones, for a total of 298 measurements. Children were included if they could repeat the numbers 1–10 in English, and excluded if they could not complete testing or could not tolerate ear‐level examination.ResultsThe signal‐to‐noise ratios of all participants were correlated with the hearing diagnosis determined by sound booth audiometric evaluation. Two unique parameters were identified that allowed us to identify conductive hearing loss (CHL) in the pediatric population with a high positive predictive value (76% and 95%) and acceptable specificity (81% and 98%), but poor sensitivity (39% and 22%).ConclusionPediatric DIN testing did not delineate CHL from NH as cleanly as adult data. Trends were noted, but improvements need to be made to sensitivity prior to utilizing DIN as a screening tool for pediatric CHL.Level of Evidence2.Keywords: conductive hearing loss, digits in noise, hearing screening, mobile hearing assessment, pediatric audiology, pediatric hearing loss, sensorineural hearing loss, tablet audiometry, technology

Introduction

Untreated pediatric hearing loss significantly affects children. Childhood hearing loss can cause delays in language development, set children behind academically, decrease their chances of successful employment, and correlate with challenges in socialization and depression [1, 2]. More specifically, profound deficits in language exposure are found to alter white matter tract development [3], are associated with decreased frontal lobe gray matter in the brain [4], and can lead to lifelong limitations in literacy and language development. Therefore, accurate screening and timely intervention for pediatric hearing loss are paramount and have the potential to markedly improve the trajectory of a child's life. Although newborn hearing screenings have improved hearing loss detection, up to 50% of educationally significant hearing loss is not detected during infancy [5]. One way to improve the outcomes of childhood hearing loss is to improve school‐aged hearing screening programs. New technology has greatly improved access to screening [6, 7], however, the lack of point‐of‐care identification of conductive hearing loss remains a major gap [8, 9].

Identifying CHL is critical to direct the patient to the correct treatment pathway. Formal audiology evaluation is a necessary evaluation for any child with hearing loss. CHL is typically a mechanical problem of the outer and/or middle ear that interferes with sound conduction; its etiologies include cerumen impaction, suppurative otitis media, tympanic membrane perforation, cholesteatoma, and ossicular fixation, all of which have different treatment protocols that often require otolaryngology involvement but do not always necessitate hearing aid(s). By contrast, SNHL is typically caused by a problem of the inner ear and/or auditory nerve, is much less likely to resolve with short‐term intervention and more frequently requires long‐term strategies such as hearing aid(s), classroom preferential seating, or surgical intervention such as cochlear implants. Therefore, identification of CHL is a crucial step in the pathway toward successful management of childhood hearing loss.

De Sousa et al. established that tablet‐based digits in noise (DIN) testing can differentiate CHL from SNHL in adults [10], but the parameters used in the adult algorithm do not successfully identify CHL in children [11]. The objective of this study is to identify pediatric diagnostic criteria within antiphasic and diotic DIN testing to identify CHL via direct comparison to sound booth audiometric testing.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This is a multi‐institution, blinded, prospective cohort of pediatric patients at two tertiary care pediatric clinic settings. This study was reviewed and approved by Children's Minnesota Institutional Review Board #2021‐067 and University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board #STUDY00016377.

Setting and Participants

One hundred forty nine children aged 3–18 scheduled for an audiometric sound booth evaluation with an audiologist and same‐day otolaryngology evaluation were eligible for the study (Figure 1). Participants were identified at Children's Minnesota or the M Health Fairview Masonic Children's Hospital Lions Children's Hearing and ENT clinic.

Inclusion Criteria

All children with appointments for both sound booth audiometry and otolaryngology were eligible for the study. Children needed to be able to repeat the numbers 1–10 in English. Children who had a difficult time understanding the test were included, given that the inability to complete the test is a study outcome.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients unable to participate in traditional sound booth audiometry, diotic DIN testing, or ear level examination due to behavioral or developmental issues were excluded.

Eligibility and Screening

Eligibility was determined using the electronic health record (EHR) by the researcher who obtained consent and assent per study protocol.

Technology and Equipment

All DIN tests were conducted via a Samsung Galaxy tablet with ISO‐calibrated noise‐canceling Sennheiser HD280 circumaural headphones. The REDCap database was used for data collection.

Testing and Study Procedures

Following the conventional audiogram, 149 participants underwent diotic (same‐phased stimuli) and antiphasic (out‐of‐phase stimuli) DIN testing, for a total of 298 measurements (Image 1). In diotic testing, the same sound is presented simultaneously to both ears, while in antiphasic testing the sound presented has a 180° phase difference between the ears. The test uses an adaptive one‐up, one‐down procedure. The test presents digit triplets (i.e., 2, 4, 7) in speech‐shaped masking noise to determine a signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) at which 50% of the digit triplets are recognized correctly. The speech recognition threshold (SRT) is determined by averaging the SNR of the last 19 of 23 digit triplets.

Audiometric Theory

The audiometric theory describing how diotic and antiphasic DIN testing can identify CHL has been described well in prior literature [10, 12]. Adult data suggest that normal‐hearing patients will be the best performers on both diotic and antiphasic DIN testing. Those with bilateral SNHL will perform poorly on both diotic and antiphasic testing. Patients with unilateral SNHL or a CHL will perform relatively well on diotic DIN testing but poorly on the antiphasic round. This data has been validated in adults to have 79% accuracy in differentiating between normal hearing, CHL, or unilateral SNHL, and bilateral SNHL. However, based on our prior work, adult algorithms differentiating CHL from SNHL do not seem to apply to pediatric data, as there was only a 50% correlation between DIN results and sound booth audiometry in identifying CHL [11].

Data Collection

Demographic data was collected from EHR via chart review, including patient age, native language, and sex. Audiometric data was obtained from the audiology note in the EHR, including pure tone average, severity of hearing loss, air‐bone gap if CHL, and tympanometry. The presence of otoscopic pathology was determined from the otolaryngology note in the EHR. The audiologist and otolaryngologist were blinded to the diotic and antiphasic DIN results.

The primary outcome of interest was hearing loss, specifically conductive (Y/N) or normal hearing (Y/N). Raw audiometric data, including pure tone averages, severity of hearing loss, and air‐bone gap, were analyzed. The severity of hearing loss was graded using the World Health Organization grading metric. Mixed hearing loss was categorized as CHL because the clinical differentiator in treatment path is the presence or absence of CHL.

Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association of various covariates: age, sex, language, otoscopic pathology, and the tester's perception of the child accurately completing the test, with the ability of diotic and antiphasic DIN to accurately distinguish CHL from NH. Significance was assessed using α = 0.05. No a priori power analysis was performed due to the lack of prior pediatric DIN thresholds to calculate effect size.

SRT from antiphasic and diotic DIN was compared to CHL identified in sound booth audiometry, with trends divided into quadrants. A multinomial logit likelihood test evaluated if the difference in quadrants A, B, and C was statistically significant.

An ROC analysis was utilized to guide specificity/sensitivity analysis (Figures 2 and 3). Participants with poor reliability, as evaluated by the audiologist or DIN test administrator, were excluded from this analysis. Reliability was defined per standard audiologic criteria.

The majority (97.9%, n = 146) of children completing sound booth testing had “good” reliability in their responses as determined by the audiologist performing the test. Alternatively, only 72.5% (n = 108) of children were deemed to have “good” reliability when undergoing tablet‐based digits in noise testing as determined by the researcher administering the DIN test. The average age of children having “good” reliability when undergoing the digits in noise test was 11 years of age, “fair” reliability was 6.5 years, and “poor” was 7.3 years. Out of the poorly reliable participants without a developmental delay, the average age was 4.4 years old. Both (n = 2) of the three‐year‐olds enrolled had “poor” test reliability. Lower age was associated with worse understanding of the digits in noise testing (p < 0.01). Of the ten participants that had a “poor” reliability rating, three had Trisomy 21 and one had Robin sequence with a comorbid mild developmental delay. At least 14 other participants with Trisomy 21 or autism spectrum disorder completed the DIN test with a “fair” or “good” reliability rating.

SRT values for all participants were graphed and correlated to their sound booth audiometry results to evaluate trends in SRT values based upon hearing loss outcomes (Figure 4). Sensitivity was low for all quadrants (A = 38.9%, B = 22.2%, C = 37.3%), but specificity was high (A = 81.4%, B = 98.3%, C = 93.3%). Therefore, antiphasic and diotic DIN testing has two parameters by which CHL can be identified in the pediatric population with a high positive predictive value (76% and 95%) and acceptable specificity (81% and 98%), but poor sensitivity (39% and 22%). Receiver operator curves for both diotic and antiphasic are demonstrated in Figures 2 and 3. DIN can identify NH in the pediatric population with 79% PPV and 93% specificity, but only 37% sensitivity.

Discussion

Pediatric diotic and antiphasic digits in noise testing can differentiate conductive hearing loss from normal hearing with a reasonable positive predictive value when using pediatric‐specific parameters. However, the poor sensitivity associated with this testing as it currently stands prevents its widespread use as a screening test. This data expands upon prior findings that this testing battery necessitates different algorithmic cutoffs than previously published adult data.

The DIN test has repeatedly shown useful sensitivity for screening HL in adults; the DIN test can be administered via tablet or personal computer and has been reliable across various headphone types [6, 13]. However, some results suggest that younger participants cannot be evaluated in the same manner as adults. Research by Dunlap et al. indicates that the adult diagnostic criteria used by DIN testing are not valid in children [11]. Children are known to have SRTs for DIN testing that are up to 7 dB worse than adults, and children successfully complete the DIN test at a significantly lower rate than adults; a 30% completion rate for 4‐year‐olds compared to 91% completion across older ages [12, 14]. Koopman et al. tested a pediatric DIN test that required a threshold of 79% correct using single digits rather than 50% correct using triplets and found similar error rates between adults and pediatric testers. Koopman et al.'s data suggest that normal hearing children as young as age 4 can potentially complete the adult version of the DIN test, while our data set suggests that differences exist between adult and pediatric test‐takers. This incongruity could be explained by Koopman et al.'s normal‐hearing cohort being intrinsically distinct from a cohort with hearing loss, and that neither cohort is large enough to establish immutable diagnostic guidelines.

There are many possible explanations for the poor sensitivity of this study. First, children have limited attention spans compared to adults, and even the gold standard of sound booth audiometry requires audiologist intervention or modified testing based on developmental capacity for children who are especially young or have a hard time focusing. The DIN test may work more effectively in a cohort of older children whose attention spans are more developed. The researchers also intentionally included children with developmental delays and non‐English native languages to better understand the weaknesses and limitations of the DIN test. It is possible that children with developmental delays need a test with a shorter conditioning period or that discriminating speech from noise in a second language is more challenging than in one's native language. It is also possible that the children had a hard time completing the DIN test because it was after the sound booth testing, which can tax a child's attention. Children may have fared better if the DIN test was on a different day or after a longer break. Within this paper's limited sample size, no clear trend was discovered among the outliers when analyzed for age, reliability, speaking English at home, or race.

Otolaryngologists and audiologists have traditionally utilized assorted tools to identify CHL (tuning fork evaluation, microscopic evaluation of the tympanic membrane, etc.), but the gold standard remains sound booth audiometry with masked air‐ and bone‐conduction testing. Bone conduction testing using a bone transducer is, however, limited in low‐resource or point‐of‐care ambulatory settings. The Shoebox Professional (Ottawa, ON, Canada) is an iPad‐based audiometer that offers a bone transducer, but masking to determine ear specificity is only available in the advanced testing mode and therefore is not currently feasible for users without an audiology background [13, 15]. Furthermore, research has shown that smartphone‐compatible point‐of‐care tympanometry can enhance school‐aged hearing screening both from commercially available products like the Otoflex 100 from Otometrics [16] and also much cheaper, programmable, open‐source systems [17].

If further study finds that DIN testing is reliable in the pediatric population, this test could have profound implications for screening children. This test has the potential for high utility in the community for school‐based and low‐resource screening since it can be run without formal audiometric equipment or formal audiology training. This study has strengths in that it is a blinded prospective cohort with a comparator arm based on the gold standard of behavioral sound booth audiometry conducted by an audiologist on the same day as tablet testing and an otolaryngologist visit. Major limitations include the relatively small sample size and lack of diversity in the study population (75% white). In addition, the relatively small sample of pediatric SNHL data within this data set was limiting.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Preliminary analysis of DIN testing in the pediatric population indicates promising PPV and specificity, but with low sensitivity. Further testing in a larger population of children is needed to better define operational diagnostic criteria.
