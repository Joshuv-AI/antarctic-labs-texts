Mitigating plume surface interactions using lunar craters
Authors: Seamus L Anderson, Prabal Saxena, Philip T Metzger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaunching and landing spacecraft on the lunar surface mobilizes regolith at high velocities which will likely cause damage to nearby objects and infrastructure, a phenomenon known as plume surface interactions (PSIs) that must be mitigated for sustainable lunar surface exploration. While some mitigation strategies prescribe the construction of walls and landing pads using locally sourced regolith and additive manufacturing techniques, cheaper and more immediately available approaches may be required. We introduce a novel concept that uses small lunar craters (<1.5 km) as unprepared landing sites to mitigate the hazards posed by PSIs. We simulated the ballistic trajectories of regolith grains ejected by PSIs from the centers of 238 lunar craters and find that many could completely shield nearby areas from ejected regolith, with some of these protected “umbrella zones” being large enough to host either a 16 m-tall Blue Moon Mk. 2 lander or a lunar outpost. These examined craters are also conducive to safe landings as they retain sufficiently large and flat crater floors, as well as crater walls that are traversable by the Artemis program’s proposed Lunar Terrain Vehicle. However, much work remains for this approach to be fully relied upon as a mitigation strategy.Subject terms: Astronomy and planetary science, Engineering, Natural hazards, Planetary science

Introduction

Launching and landing spacecraft on the lunar surface ejects regolith at high velocities due to interactions between the spacecraft’s rocket plume and the regolith-covered lunar surface, also known as Plume Surface Interactions (PSIs)1,2. Protecting nearby infrastructure and equipment from this ejecta remains a major problem to be solved for future lunar exploration missions2–4. As early as Apollo 12 this issue was confirmed by astronauts who noticed sandblasting-like damage to the already-landed Surveyor III probe (located 180 m away), but only on its Apollo-facing side5–7. Many previous studies have observed, modeled, and experimentally characterized the effects of rocket exhaust on the mobilization of regolith particles under lunar surface conditions1,7–16 (see Table 1), and most have estimated regolith ejection velocities in the hundreds to thousands of meters per second, with some particles likely16 exceeding the lunar escape velocity of 2.38 km s−113. These high velocities, combined with a relatively weak lunar gravity, and effectively no atmosphere to dampen the motion of the particles can result in ejection distances of thousands of kilometers17. These conditions also result in effectively no loss in kinetic energy between a regolith grain’s ejection and its impact into the lunar surface or anthropomorphic structures and equipment. These particles will likely abrade or cause damage to the surfaces they strike, a problem that will be particularly exacerbated by the ongoing launching and landing operations necessary for sustained crewed exploration. Both robotic and human exploration missions to the lunar surface will require measures to mitigate the risks posed by PSIs3.

Most currently proposed PSI mitigation strategies focus on building structures in situ, using additive manufacturing techniques and locally sourced lunar regolith, which typically consists of landing pads to minimize the amount of mobilized regolith, and/or protective walls or berms to prevent any mobilized regolith from striking sensitive equipment or infrastructure18–20. These approaches, however, would require at the very least, the use of a regolith excavator, a regolith extruder, a melter, and a power source, all of which would need to be mobile in order to construct sufficiently large landing infrastructure capable of accommodating the 100 m landing precision required for the Artemis program21. Additionally, all of this equipment would need to be delivered in the same spacecraft, lest subsequent nearby landings damage the equipment that has already been delivered. Although this combination of constructing a landing pad and protective berm would likely be effective in the context of long-term exploration efforts, a less intensive and lower cost solution may prove useful in the immediate future, especially while these construction technologies advance to an acceptable technology readiness level (TRL) to be relied on for mission operations.

Some studies have suggested evaluating and using the natural lunar topography to reduce the distance traveled by ejected regolith17, since some higher-velocity grains could travel as far as the opposite side of the Moon before striking its surface. A more recent study has introduced the concept of natural landing pads22 that could mitigate the harmful effects of PSIs through a combination of many surface properties including topography, surface roughness, and regolith thickness.

In this study we explore a novel strategy within this natural landing pad framework that uses topographic features present in every region of the lunar surface: craters. By simulating the ballistic trajectories of regolith grains ejected by PSIs at the centers of small lunar craters, we explore the extent of the protection and mitigation that could be offered by selecting locations such as these to serve as unprepared landing sites.

Panels in Fig. 1d, h illustrate the umbrella zones, which are areas where ejecta does not travel or impact, such that there is a gap between the lunar surface and the lowest traveling ejecta at that location. Four key heights are marked at 1 m, 5 m, 8 m, and 16 m which correspond respectively to the height of a small rover, the diameter of an ISS module, and the heights of the Blue Moon Mk. 1, and Mk. 2 landers.

For the simulated landing in the 1035 m-diameter lunar crater, there are three notable umbrella zones (Fig. 1d), the most sizeable of which is located just outside the crater’s left rim. In this zone, there is an area 102 m wide that could completely protect a Mk. 2 Blue moon lander (16 m-tall), if another spacecraft were to land near the center of the crater. This umbrella zone has a far wider area that could protect shorter objects, equipment and/or infrastructure, likely offering a large enough space to establish a lunar outpost. The crater’s left side slope is gentle enough for LTV traversal (Fig. 1b). Although the crater floor is only “flat” (slope less than 6°) for 76 m across, some studies use more inclusive definitions of what a flat surface entails, extending up to a slope of 10°22. Even for areas outside the crater that are struck by ejecta, the total number of impacts is significantly lessened, while the average impact velocity is reduced by a factor of approximately 2, when compared to a flat landing site (Fig. 1c, g).

In the context of a human exploration mission, an initial, uncrewed, spacecraft could touch down in one of the umbrella zones, pre-staging supplies, scientific sensors, infrastructure, and construction vehicles, or could serve as a longer-term habitat. A second, crewed spacecraft could then land at, and later launch from, the center of the crater which would shield the already existing objects located in one of the umbrella zones from the harmful effects of the second spacecraft’s PSI. Alternatively, the crewed spacecraft could land in one of the umbrella zones, followed later by a non-crewed cargo resupply spacecraft touching down near the crater center, with the same protective effect. After a formal landing site is eventually constructed outside the crater, the crater could still serve as a backup landing area in the event that multiple spacecraft are needed at the surface, and the prepared landing locations are already occupied.

This landing site could also prove useful in the context of a robotic sample return mission. A spacecraft could land at the center of the crater and deploy a rover, which could then collect samples from various target locations, and return to the lander to deposit said samples. The rover would then retreat to an umbrella zone and shelter in place while the ascent module of the spacecraft launches from the surface. The rover could then continue to operate on an extended science mission after the samples are sent back to Earth, without the risk of damage.

Protection trends across targeted crater population

In Fig. 2 we display the horizontal sizes of the umbrella zones for all 238 analyzed craters as a function of the crater’s depth to diameter (d/D) ratio. Each panel represents the horizontal size of each crater’s umbrella zone at a given height (1 m, 5 m, 8 m, 16 m; see Fig. 1d for an example). Each data point’s shading represents the size of the landing zone (where the slope is less than 6°) within the crater, with a darker color indicating a larger flat area suitable for landing. Figure 2a shows that all of our targeted craters that have a d/D ratio higher than 0.164 are guaranteed to have an umbrella zone that is at least 1 m tall. A d/D ratio higher than 0.181 would equate to a crater slope from center to rim of 20°: the maximum ejecta angle we chose for our simulations. Seeing as this is also the maximum traversable limit for the LTV, this can only mean that these craters are significantly asymmetric, since we excluded craters that were steeper than 20° on both sides (see Methods). We included these asymmetric craters in our analysis since we imagine a scenario where a rover could drive out on the side of the crater that has a gentler slope. Although these steep, asymmetrical craters (d/D greater than 0.181) are guaranteed to provide complete protection somewhere in their immediate vicinity, the light shading of their data points indicates relatively small areas that are sufficiently flat to land safely within the crater.

Horizontal sizes of umbrella zones, shown by targeted heights.

Each panel represents the horizontal sizes of all our targeted crater’s umbrella zones for one of four target protection heights: a 1 m-tall Rover, b 5 m-tall habitat module, c 8 m-tall Blue Moon Mk. 1 lunar lander, and d 16 m-tall Blue Moon Mk. 2 lunar lander. The shading of each data point is proportional to the size of the landing area (slope < 6°) within the crater.

While craters with a d/D ratio shallower than 0.164 may also have an umbrella zone, many do not, and any craters with a d/D ratio shallower than 0.077 are guaranteed not to produce umbrella zones. For the purposes of mitigating PSIs, the most ideal candidates appear between d/D ratios of 0.14 and 0.17, since many, though not all, have an umbrella zone, and also have a somewhat large, flat area to land.

When comparing the shortest umbrella zones (Fig. 2a) to the tallest (Fig. 2d), it is apparently (and somewhat intuitively) easier to protect a 1 m-tall object, such as a rover or scientific payload as opposed to a 16 m-tall piece of equipment, like the proposed Blue Moon Mk. 2 lander. The light shading of the data points in Fig. 2d with non-zero umbrella zone sizes also indicates smaller flat areas suitable for landing, further highlighting the difficulty of protecting a larger object. In Fig. 3 we display the maximum heights of the umbrella zones which also shows that shorter objects are easier to protect than taller ones. Although four craters have umbrella zones tall enough to protect a 53 m-tall Starship HLS, the safe landing areas within those craters are too small (50 m or less) to accommodate current spacecraft landing accuracy. If the accuracy of future lunar landers were to improve, then protecting larger object such as this may become possible. Fig. 3 also shows how the height of a crater’s umbrella zone generally increases with its d/D ratio.

Statistical results

To provide mission planners with statistical guidance for site selection based on crater morphology, we analyzed the distribution of maximum umbrella zone sizes across all 238 craters as a function of d/D ratio (Fig. 4). For any given d/D value and target height, the percentile bands show the expected range of horizontal umbrella zone sizes, accounting for natural variations in crater morphology and the probability that some craters may provide no protection. at all. The median values (solid lines) provide conservative estimates for mission planning purposes. For example, a crater with d/D = 0.15 would be expected to provide a median umbrella zone of approximately 200 m for a 1 m-tall rover (Fig. 4a), with 50% of such craters providing zones between 150 and 300 m (interquartile range). However, for an 8 m-tall Blue Moon Mk. 1 lander, the same d/D = 0.15 crater would provide a median umbrella zone of only approximately 100 m (Fig. 4c), with significantly higher variability and a greater probability of providing no protection at all.

Percentile-based statistical summary of umbrella zone horizontal sizes.

Shown here are the percentiles of the horizontal sizes of umbrella zones as a function of their crater’s depth-to-diameter ratio (d/D) for four target heights: a 1 m, b 5 m, c 8 m, and d 16 m. Shaded bands represent the interquartile range (25th–75th percentile, darker shading) and 10th–90th percentile range (lighter shading) of maximum umbrella zone sizes calculated from 238 lunar craters, binned in 0.01-wide d/D intervals. Solid lines indicate median values. Analysis includes craters with zero umbrella zones, providing realistic probability distributions for site selection.

The statistical distributions in Fig. 4 reveal several key trends for mission planning. First, horizontal umbrella zone size increases systematically with crater d/D ratio: deeper craters cast larger protective shadows. The deepest craters analyzed (d/D ~ 0.20–0.25) can provide protection zones exceeding 500 m for short objects like rovers, though these same craters provide maximum zones of only 400 m for taller objects like the 16 m Blue Moon Mk. 2 lander. Second, protecting taller objects is substantially more difficult than protecting shorter ones, as evidenced by the smaller umbrella zones and wider percentile bands (indicating greater uncertainty and higher failure rates) in panels (c) and (d) compared to panels (a) and (b). Third, the broad percentile ranges—particularly for mid-range d/D values between 0.10–0.15—highlight that d/D ratio alone is insufficient for site selection; individual crater assessment remains necessary. The lower percentiles approaching zero for shallow craters (d/D < 0.12) indicate that many such craters provide little to no PSI mitigation, which is consistent with our findings in Fig. 2 that craters below d/D ≈ 0.075 are unlikely to generate useful umbrella zones.

Discussion

Our investigations presented here explore a novel concept that uses lunar craters as unprepared landing sites for crewed and robotic lunar surface exploration and indicates that many of these craters may provide safe ‘umbrella zones’ for landed payloads of different heights. Although constructing prepared landing sites using melted regolith would likely prove more effective for long-term lunar exploration, the operational complexity and high mass cost to build said infrastructure may prove prohibitively expensive and impractical for early missions. Local topography should remain a major consideration when choosing a landing site for lunar surface missions, in order to mitigate the hazards posed by PSIs17,22.

According to our first-order investigations, and assuming regolith grain ejection velocities between 0–20°, and 10–2380 m s−1, some craters could completely protect objects as tall as 53 m (the size of a Starship HLS), though protecting smaller objects (8 m-tall or less) is far easier. Even if no full protection can be found at a particular crater, most will reduce both the amount of ejecta impacting areas near the landing site, as well as the average impact energies of said ejecta.

If we were to assume a more ideal initial velocity range, such that no regolith grains are ejected at angles above 5° (like seen in most Apollo missions1), then any crater with a d/D ratio higher than 0.044 (which includes all of our targeted craters) could theoretically contain all of the PSI-ejected regolith. If further work also proves secondary ejecta and particle rebounds to be insignificant, then this approach of landing within lunar craters could serve as a responsible “rule of the road” for peacefully competing exploration groups, though much more investigation is required. As previous work has shown17, ejected regolith grains from a PSI in relatively flat area could travel hundreds of kilometers or more before striking the surface, complicating exploration efforts between independent actors if left insufficiently mitigated. If mission planners could intentionally choose landing sites based on local topography, including small craters, for instance (assuming sufficient illumination at the landing site), then this landing approach could protect not only their own local infrastructure but could also prevent ejecta from damaging assets of other nations or corporations, enabling these actors to harmoniously co-exist in relatively close proximity to each other on the lunar surface. Though, as we explain below, significant observational, experimental, and modeling work remains in order to fully validate this concept as a reliable strategy for mission operations.

As mentioned above, it is important to note that both of the ejection angle scenarios (5° and 20° maximum) rely on the assumption that no significant secondary ejecta is generated, and that no regolith grains rebound or continue moving once they strike the surface. Therefore, to advance the fidelity of our crater-landing site concept, improvements must be made to our understanding of the behavior of PSI-ejecta when it impacts the surface. Our current simulation approach presents other limitations that should be expanded in the future, one of which pertains to the landing accuracy and precision of current and near future lunar landers. Although the Artemis program has a stated landing precision requirement of 100 m, it is currently unclear what an effect an off-center, but within error margin landing would have on the size and shape of an umbrella zone. When a promising crater landing site is identified, multiple PSI simulations within the 100 m margin of error should be performed to better constrain the “real” or “margin of error” umbrella zone. This leads to another limitation of our study: reliance on 2-D crater profiles for our simulations, since practically no crater in our selected size range is perfectly symmetric in 3-D. Future investigations for utilizing lunar craters as landing sites will require full 3-D modeling of the ejected regolith with multiple off-centered landings within a crater to fully understand which portions of the umbrella zones are robust enough to survive a real landing within mission’s stated margin of error.

Finally, we note that while this study conducts an initial assessment of the plausibility of using craters as natural landing pads for PSI limiting landings, there is significant complexity to such a strategy that requires filling data gaps, in addition to the modeling gaps outlined above. For example, this would include incorporation of high-resolution digital terrain models of potential landing sites for both the crater and surrounding area, more observations of regolith ejection at the landing site, and additional experiments to better understand the behavior of ejected regolith grains when they impact the surface. Additionally, examining potential shielding properties that may enhance umbrella zones23,24 or conducting experiments using witness plates that can validate such a concept25 are also key future areas of potential study. Given the planned increased cadence of both human and robotic landings in the near future, examining such a strategy for mitigating PSI and contamination effects may be an important means of enabling sustainable exploration of the lunar surface.

Methods

Regolith ejection velocities and ballistic trajectories

We used python and its NumPy module26 to calculate the ballistic trajectory taken by PSI-ejected regolith particles in lunar gravity (1.62 m s−2) with no air or fluid resistance (code available at https://github.com/seamusLanderson/lunar_crater_landing_pads). We do not consider solar radiation pressure, electromagnetic fields, or regolith grain surface charges in our simulations. We also do not consider Coriolis or centrifugal forces due to the Moon’s rotation in our calculations. A major limitation of our current study is our assumption that any ejected regolith burrows into the surface without any rebound and without creating any secondary ejecta from its impact.

A wealth of experimental and modeling studies, as well as examinations of footage from a number of lunar landings1,8–15, have characterized the speeds and angles at which lunar regolith is ejected due to PSIs (See Table 1). Although the ejecta velocity of a regolith grain is a function of lander mass, distance between the rocket and the surface, lateral distance to the plume, and grain size11,12, we aimed to include as many velocities as possible into our simulation. We therefore calculated the ballistic trajectories of ejected regolith grains with initial speeds between 10 m s−1 and 2380 m s−1 (intervals of 10 m s−1) and ejection angles between 0° and 20° (intervals of 0.1°, above horizontal). The position of each particle was calculated every 10 ms until it collided with the lunar surface. We chose a starting position for all particles at x = 0 and y = 0.9, rather than y = 0 to help avoid possible artifacts that occasionally appear at crater centers in the elevation models, that may be a result of stereo reconstruction.

Although a number of experiments have suggested that rocket plumes are capable of excavating a small crater just below the spacecraft, ejecting particles at much higher angles27,28, this behavior was not observed during the Apollo landings2,14. We therefore elected not to include these higher ejection angles in our study. Although the size of each regolith grain will have a strong effect on its ejection velocity with smaller particles being ejected at higher speeds11,12, the size frequency distribution of regolith grains were not included in our simulations, and therefore the impact position histogram in Fig. 1c, g, may not reflect the real population of ejected regolith.

Crater shape profiles

Using Label Studio (https://github.com/HumanSignal/label-studio) we extracted 238 lunar crater profiles from five Digital Terrain Maps29 (DTMs; see Table 2), produced using stereo reconstruction of images taken by the Narrow Angle Camera (NAC) onboard NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO)30. We produced 2-dimensional crater shape profiles by automatically selecting a slice along each crater’s longest axis, with the point of lowest elevation within the crater being set as the origin (0,0). We also simulated a lunar landing on a perfectly flat profile as a comparison to our cratered sites (see Fig. 1). The radii of our selected craters spanned from 65 m to 630 m, which is similar to previously proposed base-landing zone separation of 200 m31, though others have suggested that landing zones should be up to 2 or 3 km away from sensitive equipment19.

Past studies typically identify lunar craters as hazards to be avoided when landing on the surface as some retain slopes of more than 30°19, which would likely cause a lunar lander to catastrophically tip onto one side and would either destroy the spacecraft or make launching from the surface impossible. For a prepared landing site, the best surface would be completely flat, though for unprepared landing sites, a slope of less than 6° would be ideal19, while others have suggested that a slope no greater than 15° would be acceptable32. With these constraints in mind, we took note of the size of each crater floor where the maximum slope did not exceed 6°, rather than excluding craters that did not meet a wide enough flat-floor threshold. Although the Apollo missions had a 3σ landing area of approximately 2000 m across19, the more recent SLIM robotic lander was able to land within 100 m of its target area22. Most lunar base designs prescribe prepared landing pads with diameters from 50 m to 100 m31,34, which also matches NASA’s stated requirement of a lunar landing within 50 m of its target21. We believe that improved landing precisions are a reasonable expectation for future missions33, as GPS navigation becomes more proliferated across cislunar space.

If the maximum local slope of a crater is too steep, rovers would be unable to safely drive out, which effectively defeats the entire purpose of selecting such a landing site. We therefore excluded craters that had an instantaneous slope of more than 20° on both sides, which is the traversable limit for the planned Lunar Terrain Vehicle to be used on the Artemis missions35, though the Apollo era Lunar Roving Vehicle could climb slopes as high as 25°36. If the slope exceeded 20° only on one side of the crater, we did include it in our analysis, since we envision a mission plan where a rover could drive out of the crater on the gentler side, travel around the outside of the crater rim to exterior on the steeper side to ‘hide’ from the PSI ejecta.

A full list of the craters targeted in this investigation can be found in the Supplementary Materials. In this list we have indicated promising crater candidates for this concept if the crater floor is wider than 100 m while retaining a slope of less than 15°, and if it had an umbrella zone that was at least 5 m tall for more than 50 m wide. We would also like to stress that much work remains before this concept can be relied on as mitigation strategy for mission operations.
