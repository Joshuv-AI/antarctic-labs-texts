Severe malarial anaemia in childhood is associated with decreased long-term household wealth
Authors: Perez N. Ochanda, Arild Aakvik, Paul Bangirana, Dibyadyuti Datta, Kagan A. Mellencamp, Chandy C. John, Bjarne Robberstad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere malarial anaemia and cerebral malaria can result in long-term cognitive, behavioral, and mental health impairments that may influence future household wealth. However, the magnitude and mechanisms of this impact on wealth remain unclear. We assessed the association between different forms of severe malaria and household wealth, 4 to 20 years after the episode.MethodsData were obtained from children previously enrolled in three prospective cohort studies with one of five forms of severe malaria: cerebral malaria (CM; cohorts 1, 2, and 3), severe malarial anaemia (SMA; cohorts 2 and 3), and malaria with respiratory distress, complicated seizures, or prostration (cohort 3). Community children (CC) from the prior studies served as the reference group. Regression analyses assessed the association between forms of severe malaria types and current household socioeconomic index (SEI).Results996 participants and their households were included in the analyses: CM (n = 237), SMA (n = 257), other severe malaria (n = 238), and CC (n = 264). In a regression model including children from cohorts 1, 2 and 3 with CM or SMA, compared to CC, we found that households of children with SMA but not CM had lower SEI than CC (mean difference [95% CI]): SMA, -0.15 (-0.30, -0.01), p = 0.046, at an average of 10.1 years (SD 3.4) following the childhood severe malaria episode. In a regression model for cohort 3, comparing children in this cohort with CM, SMA, or other forms of severe malaria to CC, SMA was the only form of severe malaria associated with lower household SEI compared to CC in unadjusted analysis, and no severe malaria groups was associated with lower household SEI compared to CC after adjustment for household characteristics.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that severe malarial anaemia in childhood, but not other forms of severe malaria, may be associated with decreased household wealth 4 to 20 years later. Controlling malaria in low-resource settings could protect poor households from worse socioeconomic index over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25057-1.

Background

The burden of malaria remains disproportionately high in low-resource settings [1], with about 95.4% of all malaria deaths globally occurring in Africa in 2022 [1]. Uganda ranked third among countries with high malaria mortality, with most deaths occurring in children aged under 5 years [1]. Uncomplicated malaria if not diagnosed and treated early can progress to severe malaria [2]. A recent multi-country study estimated that 4.5% of all malaria-infected children in Africa reported at least one complication of severe malaria including severe malaria anaemia, loss of consciousness, or acidosis [3]. Evidence from prospective studies [4–7], and retrospective studies [8] from Africa show that severe malaria in childhood is associated with long-term mental disorders, behavioural problems, and cognitive difficulties in domains of learning, attention, memory and overall cognition. In addition, severe malaria is associated with an increased risk of subsequent malaria episodes, including readmission for malaria [9], and these repeated malaria episodes may adversely affect educational outcomes [10].

Building on evidence of the enduring effects of severe malaria, the understanding of this effects on household wealth is essential. The relationship between malaria and household wealth is traceable through direct and indirect health expenditure [11]. This is consistent with standard welfare theory, which states that individuals and households make decisions with the aim to maximise current and future consumption of goods and services to attain better living standards [12, 13]. The occurrence of disease can disrupt household consumption by increasing health expenditures and promoting substitution of essentials commodities like food for health-related goods and services [14, 15]. This substitution may, with persistent ill health, lead to depletion of household assets and savings over time, as well as loss of income [16]. The occurrence of severe malaria in its various forms can negatively impact household wealth over time, through the direct costs associated with managing severe episodes [9] and indirectly by its sustained effects on children’s cognition [4, 5], behaviour [17], and academic achievement [10]. Among these, children with CM or SMA represent the most extensive studied forms of severe malaria and have been consistently linked to increased morbidity [9], as well as poorer cognitive, behavioural, and academic outcomes compared to their community counterparts [7, 17]. Previous studies on the impact of malaria on household socioeconomic outcomes in Africa have been limited by their focus on cost data [18–20], along with inadequate longitudinal socioeconomic and clinical data at the individual and household levels, limited evaluation of the effects of severe malaria, and scarce long-term evaluation beyond the acute episode at follow-up of one year or less. The present study overcomes the limitations of short time horizons by using baseline household socioeconomic data from three historical cohorts of individuals with childhood severe malaria and evaluating their household wealth 4 to 20 years later.

This study aimed to examine the relationship between childhood severe malaria and consequent household socioeconomic index in Ugandan households, contributing to efforts toward attaining Sustainable Development Goals related to poverty reduction, health and inequality [21].

Methods

Study design and participants

The present study utilized data from research conducted in Kampala and Jinja, Uganda, assessing long-term neurobehavioral, functional, and socioeconomic index following childhood severe malaria. The Malaria Impact on Neurobehavioral Development (MIND) study enrolled children from three prior prospective studies of neurodevelopmental impairment in Ugandan children with severe malaria [4, 5, 7, 22]. In each of the three parent studies, a cohort of children with severe malaria was recruited from the Mulago National Referral and Teaching Hospital in Kampala or the Pediatric Emergency Ward at Jinja Regional Referral Hospital. The first study (2003–2006) enrolled children aged 4 to 12 years with cerebral malaria (CM) or uncomplicated malaria (UM) with 24 months of follow-up [4, 5]. The second study (2008–2015) enrolled children aged 18 months to 12 years with CM or severe malaria anaemia (SMA) with 24 months of follow-up [7]. The third study (2014–2018) enrolled children aged 6 months to 4 years with the five most common forms of severe malaria: CM, SMA, respiratory distress syndrome (RD), malaria with complicated seizures (MS), and prostration (Pros), with 12 months of follow-up [22]. Each parent study also enrolled a cohort of asymptomatic community children (CC) of similar ages and from the same neighborhoods or extended households as children with severe malaria. Full details of the parent studies, including severe malaria inclusion and exclusion criteria have been published previously [4, 5, 7, 22] and are included in the Supplemental Materials. From 2020 to 2023, 4 to 20 years after enrolment in the parent studies, eligible participants were traced and recruited for the follow-up study. The present study focuses on the evaluation of household wealth at the follow-up study using a household SEI score. Household socioeconomic data

Baseline household socioeconomic index (SEI) in the parent studies was generated from household asset holdings and living standards at the time of the childhood severe malaria episode, generated from prior African studies and adapted to the Ugandan context [4]. Within each historical study, item categories were assigned point values, with higher scores indicating better socioeconomic conditions. The items used were similar in all three cohorts, except that in cohort 3 an additional variable (poultry) was added (Table S7). Because socioeconomic data were captured at different timepoints across three historical studies, additive scores were transformed into z-scores and harmonized into one measure of baseline household SEI across parent studies to account for between-study variability.

In this follow-up study, household socioeconomic data were gathered using a questionnaire adapted from the Demographic Health Survey (DHS) and the Living Standard Measurement Survey (LSMS), both of which are population-based surveys designed to improve household data collection globally and inform policy at national and international level [23, 24]. For construction of household SEI, we adopted DHS variables that have been validated in studies in Uganda and other low- or middle-income countries [24, 25], including categorical variables measuring household assets (television, motor vehicle, mobile telephone, and internet access), household living conditions (water source, electricity source, toilet facility, stove type), and household physical structure (dwelling type, and roof, wall, and floor materials).

We used multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) to generate a household socioeconomic index (SEI) score for each follow-up study participant using Stata’s mca command (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX). MCA is a generalization of principal components analysis (PCA) that, like PCA, allows for the extraction of key underlying dimensions to explain patterns between multiple variables. However, unlike PCA, MCA is specifically designed for uncovering relationships present in large matrices of categorical characteristics without assuming a linear relationship, normal distribution, or ordering of categories [26].

The MCA dimension that accounted for the largest variation in the categorical data was used to predict a continuous household SEI score for each participant, where higher scores reflected better socioeconomic conditions [27]. A detailed list of categories for the MCA input variables is presented in Table S1.

Analytical approach

Our analytic approach was twofold. First, we analyzed long-term household SEI among participants originally enrolled with CM or SMA compared to those originally enrolled as community children (CC) using data from all three cohorts. Children with CM were enrolled in all cohorts, while children with SMA were enrolled in cohorts 2 and 3. This approach enabled us to utilize data from all cohorts to assess the effects of both CM and SMA on household SEI, while also minimizing the potential impact of small sample sizes resulting from loss to follow-up. Second, we used data from cohort 3 only, to allow us extended our analysis to include all five forms of severe malaria: CM, SMA, respiratory distress syndrome (RD), malaria with complicated seizures (MS), and prostration (Pros) compared to CC. Only cohort three enrolled children with forms of severe malaria other than CM or SMA. This two-stage approach allowed us to generate long-term findings comparable to the existing literature on CM and SMA as well as provide insights into the potential long-term socioeconomic impact of other manifestations of severe malaria. Additionally, this approach addressed important data structure considerations, as children with RD, MS, or Pros were only enrolled in cohort 3 and in smaller numbers. Limiting the analysis that includes RD, MS, or Pros (hereafter, other severe malaria) to data from cohort 3 ensured balanced sample ratios and similar ages between children with other severe malaria and CC. Including CC from Cohorts 1 and 2 in the secondary analysis would have resulted in imbalanced ages across study groups, potentially distorting the association between other forms of severe malaria and household SEI.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was performed twice using Stata v18.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX); first, comparing the primary analytic groups (CM, SMA, CC) using data from all three cohorts, and second, comparing the secondary analytic groups (CM, SMA, other severe malaria, CC) using data from cohort 3 only. Participant and household characteristics at baseline and follow-up were compared across the analytic groups using Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn’s post-hoc tests for continuous variables and Pearson’s Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Unadjusted household SEI scores were compared across the group using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To determine whether household SEI scores by study group remained different following adjustment for potential confounders, we estimated two alternative multivariable linear regression models predicting household SEI across study groups. Model 1 adjusted only for participant characteristics, including ages at parent study and follow-up study enrolments, sex, and parent study site. Model 2 added adjustment for household characteristics, including age of household head at follow-up study enrolment, sex of household head, number of household residents measured at the follow-up study, and baseline household SEI. Among severe malaria groups that were associated with SEI in adjusted analyses, we also conducted post-hoc analyses: first, exploring potential effect modification of participant and household characteristics on the relationship between severe malaria group and household SEI, and second, estimating marginal mean differences in household SEI across participant and household characteristics. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using OLS regression.

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants with CM or SMA compared to CC (Cohorts 1, 2, and 3)

In the primary analysis comparing participants with CM, SMA, and CC from all three cohorts, mean (SD) age at follow-up differed between CM, SMA and CC (15.2 [4.6] vs. 12.0 [3.6] vs. 13.8 [5.5], respectively, p < 0.001, Table 1). Most participants were males in the CM (57.4%) and SMA (56.8%) groups, while gender was equally distributed among CC. Except baseline household SEI, there were no significant differences in household characteristics between participant groups. Most household heads were males (63.4%, 68.4%, and 70.7%) in CM, SMA, and CC groups, respectively, and their ages at the time of this follow-up study were also similar (43–46 years). Household sizes were similar across groups, with an average of 6 residents. The mean household SEI at parent study enrolment was higher in the CC group than the CM and SMA groups. Further, the mean number of repeated malaria episodes was significantly higher in children with SMA (0.97) compared with CM (0.69) and CC (0.53) (Table 1).

Contribution of repeated malaria episodes to the associations between CM or SMA and household SEI

To assess whether repeated malaria episodes during parent‐study follow‐up mediated the relationship between severe malaria and long‐term household SEI, we conducted mediation analyses within a structural equation modeling framework. In unadjusted models, repeated malaria episodes partially mediated the association between SMA, but not CM, and household SEI, accounting for 35% of the total effect (Figure S1). However, this indirect effect via malaria episodes was no longer evident after adjustment for study site and participant and household characteristics.

Sociodemographic characteristics of households and participants with CM, SMA, or other forms of severe malaria compared to CC (Cohort 3 only)

We also compared study characteristics of children with other forms of severe malaria, including respiratory distress syndrome (RD), malaria with complicated seizures (MS), and prostration (Pros), together with CM or SMA, to CC. All participant and household characteristics were similar across the groups except for age at parent study enrollment. Children with SMA were younger than children with CM or CC, and children with other forms of severe malaria were younger than children with CM (Table S4).

Household SEI in children with CM, SMA, or other forms of severe malaria compared to CC (Cohort 3 only)

In this secondary analysis, which included data from cohort 3 only, children with SMA had significantly lower household SEI than children with other severe malaria (RD, MS, or Pros), and numerically but not statistically lower household SEI than children with CM or CC (Figure 3). Multivariable regression analysis showed that SMA remained significantly associated with lower household SEI after adjustment for participant characteristics (Table S5), but the relationship was not statistically significant after additional adjustment for household characteristics. Covariates in the multivariable regression model significantly related to higher household SEI included enrolment at the Kampala site and baseline household SEI, but not age at follow-up study or presence of a male household head as was observed in the primary analysis. Discussion

The households and their participants from previous cohorts, recruited between 2003 and 2018, were followed up between 2020 and 2023, 4 to 20 years later. We developed current household socioeconomic index (SEI) based on assets and household living conditions across three historical cohorts of children with severe malaria. We then examined the relationship between severe malaria forms (CM, SMA, and other forms, including RD, MS, and Pros) and household SEI across this period. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the relationship between severe malaria in childhood and long-term household wealth, 4 to 20 years after the initial episode.

We found that childhood SMA, but not other forms of severe malaria, may be associated with lower household SEI at follow-up. A review of exisiting literature did not identify other studies that included household socioeconomic data with such extended follow-up periods to evaluate the long-term impact of childhood severe malaria on household SEI in low-income settings. Nonetheless, our findings are consistent with short-term studies from Tanzania, which found that malaria was associated with reduced household SEI [28]. Additionally, a study conducted in The Gambia demonstrated that malaria disrupted property accumulation by affected households [29], though these studies covered significantly shorter time frames than ours.

A Ugandan study focusing on children under five with severe malaria found that children with SMA had long-term cognitive impairment relative to community children [7]. In addition, another study of Uganda children with severe malaria showed that children with SMA experienced more post-discharge malaria episodes compared to children with CM and CC [9]. In the present study, post-discharge malaria episodes mediated some of the relationship between SMA and SEI in unadjusted analysis, but not after adjustment for site and household and participant demographic characteristics, including baseline SEI. This suggests that the malaria episodes were related to these baseline factors and once they were considered, malaria episodes did not mediate the relationship between SMA and SEI. Our study adds evidence that severe malarial anaemia can exacerbate long-term socioeconomic problems for poor households, and that this relationship pertains primarily to the episode of severe malaria itself, rather than to the repeated malaria episodes that often follow SMA.

Our results are supported by a study conducted in rural Kenya that revealed a negative association between malaria and the socioeconomic status of poorer households over time [18]. The authors attributed this association to coping mechanisms adopted by poorer households to smoothen their long-term consumption patterns such as borrowing and selling of assets to finance past and recurrent care seeking due to malaria [18]. According to economic theory, we can argue that severe malaria can influence long-term household socioeconomic position in several ways. First, it can lead to catastrophic health expenditures on the initial hospital visit and potentially on hospitalization due to repeated malaria episodes [30]. In other words, there could be a direct cumulative decremental effect which our data suggest is particularly strong following an episode of SMA. Severe malaria can also lead to an indirect effect from increased expenditure on education due to slow academic progress of survivors due to missing school days or from generally poorer academic achievements [10] and lost productive time in care seeking and care giving to impaired children [31], which could hinder overall wealth accumulation of the household over time.

We found that socioeconomic index among households with SMA participants varied by geographical location, gender of the household head and baseline household SEI. Additionally, households with SMA participant from Jinja exhibited lower SEI compared to those in Kampala, highlighting regional inequalities that may influence access to resources and health outcomes [32]. The observed link between baseline and current household SEI suggest that household socioeconomic trends tend to persist over time, underscoring the importance of early socioeconomic interventions to reduce long-term inequalities among households [33]. However, none of these factors significantly modified the association between SMA and SEI when compared to CC, indicating that while SEI varies within the SMA group, the relative impact of SMA on long-term SEI remains consistent across subgroups.

Household SEI at the parent study enrolment, parent study site being located near Kampala district, and household heads being male were all associated with higher household SEI at the follow-up study. Evidence from other settings suggests that women play an important role in agricultural production at the household level [34]. In our study, about 30% of households in the CM and SMA groups were headed by women. Given that women play a critical role in caregiving, it is plausible that female headed households with children affected by severe malaria and its long-term effects dedicated more time to caregiving roles [31, 35]. This, in turn, may have resulted in a loss of productive time that could have been used for agricultural activities, contributing to the reduced the wealth of their households over time. A study in Cote d’Ivoire corroborates our study by showing that malaria was associated with poor farm performance [36].

The main strength of our study is that we gathered long-term socioeconomic data from participants and households who had participated in previous severe malaria cohort studies. Existing cohort data provided detailed baseline information, allowing us to assess changes over time. Our study has limitations. First, the reliance on parent study classifications, which means that some children initially categorized as controls may have later developed severe malaria, and therefore de facto crossed-over from being a reference household to a severe malaria household. Although we inquired about interim episodes, we could not confirm diagnoses or treatments, limiting our ability to adjust for potential severe malaria cases. Second, the long-term follow-up period resulted in non-ignorable attrition. To assess the potential for bias, we compared selected baseline characteristics between children lost to follow-up and those retained in the study (Table S6). Loss to follow-up was higher among participants enrolled at the Kampala site, but similar for other characteristics. As a result, the final analytic sample may overrepresent participants from Jinja, a rural area generally characterized by lower household SEI. However, a post-hoc analysis of effect modification found no significant interactions by study site or other baseline characteristics, suggesting that the observed association between SMA and long-term SEI was consistent across subgroups and unlikely to be substantially biased by differential attrition. Third, we used a composite socioeconomic index to estimate household wealth, as direct measures like income and expenditure were unavailable, which is common in largely informal economies lacking revenue and taxation data. Consequently, the socioeconomic index should be viewed as an indicator rather than an exact measure of household wealth. Although the relationship between socioeconomic indicators and actual welfare varies across countries and societies, it is unclear how this could bias our Ugandan sample. Furthermore, this relationship may have changed from baseline to follow-up as the availability and affordability of commodities evolved. Fourth, our study relied on self-reported retrospective data on household assets over the past year, which could be affected by recall bias. Lastly, we observed that older participants during follow-up were associated with higher household SEI, indicating heterogeneity in the long-term socioeconomic impact of severe malaria across different age groups. However, survivor bias may have influenced our findings. Children with the most severe illness who died during follow-up may have experienced the greatest socioeconomic disadvantage, and their absence could have attenuated the observed association between severe malaria and household SEI. This survivor bias provides a potential explanation for the lack of association observed between CM and long-term SEI. Despite this limitation, we observed a strong and consistent association between SMA and household SEI.

In summary, our findings suggest that households with children who experienced severe malarial anaemia early in life may face reduced wealth 4 to 20 years later, warranting further research to confirm this link. Study site and household SEI at parent study enrolment emerged as consistently significant determinants of current household SEI across all models in which they were included. Additionally, recent studies show that post-discharge malaria chemoprevention (PDMC) significantly reduces admission for children with severe malaria. Our results imply that evaluating the long-term economic impact of such interventions is important, as fewer hospitalizations could lead to improved household wealth.
