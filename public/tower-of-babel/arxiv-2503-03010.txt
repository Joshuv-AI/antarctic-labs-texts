Latroids and code invariants
Elisa Gorla, Flavio Salizzoni

Abstract
Latroids were introduced by Vertigan, who associated a latroid to a linear block code and showed that its Tutte polynomial determines the weight enumerator of the code. The original definition of a latroid is in terms of its rank function. For a complemented lattice, we establish cryptomorphic definitions in terms of independent elements, bases, circuits, and flats. We then associate a latroid to a code over a ring or a field endowed with a general support function and show that the generalized weights of the code can be recovered from the associated latroid. This provides a uniform framework for studying generalized weights and other combinatorial invariants of linear block codes, linear codes over a ring, rank-metric, and sum-rank metric codes.



Key words:
Latroids, Tutte polynomial, weight enumerator, support, linear codes

Mathematics Subject Classification:
94B05, 05B35, 06C20

Introduction

An effective way to study linear codes is to establish connections between them and various algebraic or combinatorial objects that partially capture their structure and their basic properties. Understanding which invariants can be determined from these objects is nowadays a question of great interest within the coding theory community.

In this respect, the most studied case is certainly that of linear block codes. Indeed, since they are finite dimensional vector spaces over finite fields, it is common knowledge that we can associate a matroid to them
[
34
, Chapter 1]
. In
[
21
]
Green showed how the weight enumerator of a linear block code is determined by the Tutte polynomial of the associated matroid. More recently, in
[
30
]
Jurrius and Pellikaan proved that the Tutte polynomial of the matroid carries the same information as the generalized weight enumerator or the extended weight enumerator, i.e., either one of them determines the others. Britz independently proved a similar result in
[
5
]
. Starting from the circuits of the matroid, we can also associate a monomial ideal. In
[
26
]
, Johnsen and Verdure proved that the generalized Hamming weights of a linear block code are determined by the
\mathbb{N}
-graded Betti numbers of the associated ideal. This relation was further studied in
[
13
,
14
,
19
,
25
,
27
,
28
]
, among others.

In
[
31
]
, Jurrius and Pellikaan showed how to associate a
q
-matroid to a vector rank-metric code. This topic was further investigated in
[
17
]
, where the authors introduced the
q
-analog of a polymatroid and showed how to associate a
q
-polymatroid to a rank-metric code. Among other results, they proved that the generalized rank-weights are determined by the associated
q
-polymatroid. In
[
24
]
, Johnsen, Pratihar, and Verdure were able to express the generalized rank-weights of a vector rank-metric code in terms of the Betti numbers of a monomial ideal constructed from the associated
q
-matroid. Recently, in
[
35
]
, Panja, Pratihar, and Randrianarisoa introduced sum-matroids and associated one to each sum-rank metric code.

In
[
19
]
, Gorla and Ravagnani studied linear codes over rings endowed with a support function. They extended the definition of generalized weights to this setting and proved that generalized weights can be recovered from a monomial ideal associated to the code via the support. However, as we observe in this paper, this ideal often does not contain enough information to recover the weight enumerator of the code. In the case of Hamming support, in
[
41
]
Vertigan proved that the weight enumerator of a linear code over a ring is determined by the Tutte polynomial of the associated latroid. In this paper, we show that one can associate a latroid to a linear code whose support function takes values in a lattice. This includes, e.g., the case of linear block codes, supported on sets, that of rank-metric codes, supported on vector spaces, and that of sum-rank metric codes, supported on products of vector spaces. In addition, notice that the
(\mathcal{L},r)
-polymatroids introduced by Alfarano and Byrne in
[
1
]
are a special case of
\mathbb{Z}
-latroids. Similar ideas were also developed independently in
[
36
]
. In general, latroids allow us to treat many families of codes with a unified approach, as they recover matroids,
q
-matroids,
q
-polymatroids, and sum-matroids as special cases. Because of this, they are a powerful tool for streamlining our approach to the study of linear codes.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
1
, we recall some basic definition on lattices, and the general theory of supports for linear codes over rings from
[
19
]
. We recall the definition of generalized weights in this context and show that they are invariant under code equivalence. We also give a new, natural definition of generalized weights for linear codes over rings. As a side remark, we provide a classification of code equivalences. In Section
2
, we recall the definition of a latroid and study the basic properties of latroids. In particular, we focus on finite complemented modular lattices, and show that in this situation one can define a latroid using independent sets, bases, circuits, or flats, as in the matroid case. Section
3
is devoted to linear codes over rings and their associated latroid. In particular, we prove that the weight enumerator of a linear code endowed with the chain support can be determined from the Tutte-Whitney rank generating function of the associated latroid. In the last part of this section, we make some remarks on the associated monomial ideal. Finally, in the last section we discuss how to obtain the
q
-matroid,
q
-polymatroid, or sum-matroid associated to a code from the latroid associated to the same code.

1
Notation and preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we work over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of cardinality
q
or over a finite principal ideal ring
R
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}(X)
the power set of a set
X
.

1.1
Lattices

A partially ordered set
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
is called a
lattice
if every pair of elements
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
has a least upper bound (
join
), denoted by
L_{1}\vee L_{2}
, and a greatest lower bound (
meet
), denoted by
L_{1}\vee L_{2}
. A lattice
\mathcal{L}
is
bounded
if it has a maximum
1_{\mathcal{L}}
and a minimum
0_{\mathcal{L}}
. A nonzero element
L\in\mathcal{L}
is called an
atom
if it is minimal. If
L_{1}\leq L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
, we say that
L_{2}

dominates

L_{1}
. If
L_{1}\neq L_{2}
and there is no
L\in\mathcal{L}
such that
L_{1}<L<L_{2}
, we say that
L_{2}

covers

L_{1}
.
We denote by
[L_{1},L_{2}]
the
interval
between
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, that is, the sublattice
\{L\in\mathcal{L}:L_{1}\leq L\leq L_{2}\}
of
\mathcal{L}
.

We are mainly interested in modular lattices.

Definition 1.1
.

A lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called
modular
if for every
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\in\mathcal{L}
with
L_{1}\leq L_{3}
we have

L_{1}\vee(L_{2}\wedge L_{3})=(L_{1}\vee L_{2})\wedge L_{3}.

The set of normal subgroups of a group, the set of subspaces of a vector space, and the set of submodules of a module are examples of modular lattices. When the operations of join and meet distribute over each other, the lattice is called
distributive
. Every distributive lattice is modular. A typical example of a distributive lattice is a power set with union and intersection as join and meet.

Definition 1.2
.

A
complemented lattice

\mathcal{L}
is a bounded lattice in which for every
L_{1}\in\mathcal{L}
there exists
L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
such that

L_{1}\wedge L_{2}=0_{\mathcal{L}}\text{ and }L_{1}\vee L_{2}=1_{\mathcal{L}}.

A lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called
relatively complemented
if every interval in
\mathcal{L}
is complemented, i.e., for every
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
L_{1}\leq L_{2}\leq L_{3}
there exists
\bar{L}\in\mathcal{L}
such that

L_{2}\wedge\bar{L}=L_{1}\text{ and }L_{2}\vee\bar{L}=L_{3}.

An example of a complemented lattice is the set of vector subspaces of
K^{n}
ordered by inclusion, where
K
is a field. If
R
is a finite principal ideal ring that is not isomorphic to a product of fields, e.g. if
R
is a finite chain ring that is not a field, then the lattice of ideals of
R
ordered by inclusion and the lattice of submodules of
R^{n}
ordered by inclusion are not complemented.

A complemented lattice is relatively complemented if it is modular.

Definition 1.3
.

A bounded lattice
\mathcal{L}
is
graded
if there exists a function
\hgt:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that
\hgt(0_{\mathcal{L}})=0
and
\hgt(L)+1=\hgt(M)
for all
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
such that
M
covers
L
. This function is unique and is called the
height function
of
\mathcal{L}
.

Recall that a finite lattice is modular if and only if it is graded and its height function is
modular
, i.e., it satisfies
\hgt(L)+\hgt(M)=\hgt(L\vee M)+\hgt(L\wedge M)
for every
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
. The following classical result gives a complete classification of finite complemented modular lattices. It may help to understand in what generality the results of Subsection
2.2
apply.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
8
, Theorem 7.56]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice. Then,
\mathcal{L}
is the direct product of a finite number of lattices of the form:

1.

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\{0,1\}
,

2.

a proper line,

3.

a proper projective plane,

4.

a subspace lattice of a finite dimensional vector space over a finite field.

Definition 1.5
.

A finite lattice
\mathcal{L}
is
atomistic
if every
L\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0_{\mathcal{L}}\}
is a join of atoms. It is
semimodular
if, for
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
cover
L_{1}\wedge L_{2}
,
L_{1}\vee L_{2}
covers
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. A finite lattice is
geometric
if it is atomistic and semimodular.

We conclude this subsection with the definition of an ordered abelian group.

Definition 1.6
.

An
ordered abelian group
is a triple
(A,+,\leq)
, where
(A,+)
is an abelian group and
\leq
is a partial order on
A
such that for all
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in A

a_{1}\leq a_{2}
implies
a_{1}+a_{3}\leq a_{2}+a_{3}
. In particular, we have the following:

1.

a_{1}\leq a_{2}
if and only if
0\leq a_{2}-a_{1}
,

2.

if
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 0
, then
a_{1}+a_{2}\geq 0
.

Example 1.7
.

We are mainly interested in the ordered abelian group
(\mathbb{Z}^{u},+,\leq)
with
u\in\mathbb{N}
, where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{u})\leq(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots a_{u}^{\prime})
if and only if
a_{i}\leq a_{i}^{\prime}
for
i\in[u]
in the usual order on
\mathbb{Z}
.

1.2
Supports of
R
-linear codes

Even though many definitions and results that we will discuss throughout the paper hold for infinite commutative rings, for the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to finite rings. In the sequel, we let
R
be a finite unitary commutative ring. For a finitely generated
R
-module
M
, we denote by
\lambda(M)
its length and by
\mu(M)
the least cardinality of a (minimal) system of generators of
M
. By convention, we have
\mu(0)=0
.

Definition 1.8
.

An
R
-linear code

\mathcal{C}
is an
R
-submodule of
R^{n}
.

Gorla and Ravagnani formulated a general theory of supports over rings in
[
19
]
. Here we recall what is needed for our purposes.

Definition 1.9
.

A
support
on
R^{n}
is a function
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
such that:

1.

\mathrm{supp}(v)=0
if and only if
v=0
.

2.

\mathrm{supp}(rv)\leq\mathrm{supp}(v)
for all
r\in R
and
v\in R^{n}.

3.

\mathrm{supp}(v+w)\leq\mathrm{supp}(v)\vee\mathrm{supp}(w)
for all
v,w\in R^{n}

A support is called
modular
if it satisfies the following property:

4.

If
v,w\in R^{n}
and
i\in[u]
satisfy
\mathrm{supp}(v)_{i}\leq\mathrm{supp}(w)_{i}
, then there exists
r\in R
such that
\mathrm{supp}(v+rw)_{i}<\mathrm{supp}(v)_{i}
.

Notice that the Hamming support is modular. An example of a support on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
that is not modular for
n\geq 2
is given by the function
\tau:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
that maps the zero vector to
0
and every other vector to
1
.

A support
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
naturally induces a function
\mathrm{supp}:\mathcal{P}(R^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
, defined as
{\mathrm{supp}}(X)=\bigvee_{x\in X}\mathrm{supp}(x)
for
X\in\mathcal{P}(R^{n})
. Due to the properties of the support, if
X=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\rangle
, then
{\mathrm{supp}}(X)=\mathrm{supp}(x_{1})\vee\ldots\vee\mathrm{supp}(x_{\ell})

In coding theory, the notion of support is closely related to that of weight. The Hamming support, for example, gives rise to the Hamming weight on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

Definition 1.10
.

The
weight
of
v\in R^{n}
with respect to
\mathrm{supp}
is the
1
-norm of the support of
v
, i.e.
\mathrm{wt}(v)=\lvert\mathrm{supp}(v)\rvert
. The weight of an
R
-linear code
\mathcal{C}
is defined as
\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{C})=\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{C})\rvert
. The
minimum
and the
maximum weight
of a code
0\neq\mathcal{C}\subseteq R^{n}
are, respectively,

\minwt(\mathcal{C})=\min\left\{\mathrm{wt}(v):v\in\mathcal{C}\setminus 0\right\}\text{ and }\mathrm{maxwt}(\mathcal{C})=\max\left\{\mathrm{wt}(v):v\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

One can easily see that the weight defined above is an invariant weight function, but it is not always homogeneous. We refer to
[
22
, Section 2]
for the relevant definitions.

Notice that there exist weights of interest to the coding theory community, whose corresponding “support” does not satisfy the condition of Definition
1.9
. For example, the support
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}:\mathbb{Z}_{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, associated to the Lee weight
\mathrm{wt}_{\mathrm{L}}:\mathbb{Z}_{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, is given by
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(0)=0
,
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(1)=\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(3)=1
, and
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(2)=2
. This is not a support according to Definition
1.9
. In fact, it does not satisfy the second condition, since
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(2)=\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(2\cdot 1)>\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{L}}(1)
.

Recall that if
R
is a finite ring, then there exist
R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell}
finite local rings such that
R\cong R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}
, see
[
2
, Theorem 8.7]
. In particular, if
R
is a principal ideal ring, then
R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell}
are also principal ideal rings.
Abusing the notation, from now on we will write
R=R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{n}
. Similarly, we will write
R^{n}=R_{1}^{n}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}^{n}
and
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
, respectively, instead of
R^{n}\cong R_{1}^{n}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}^{n}
and
\mathcal{C}\cong\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
.
A finite local commutative principal ideal ring is often called a
finite chain ring
. If
R
is a finite chain ring, then any element
r\in R
is of the form
r=\upsilon\alpha^{k}
, where
\upsilon
is an invertible element and
\alpha
is a generator of the maximal ideal of
R
.
The next result allows us to reduce the study of supports of rings to that of supports of local rings.

Proposition 1.11

(
[
19
, Theorem 2.23]
)
.

Let
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
be a modular support. Up to a permutation of the coordinates of
\mathbb{Z}^{u}
we have that
\mathrm{supp}=\mathrm{supp}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathrm{supp}_{\ell}
, where
\mathrm{supp}_{i}:R^{n}_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u_{i}}
for
i\in[\ell]
and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
with
u_{1}+\dots+u_{\ell}=u
. Moreover,
\mathrm{supp}_{i}
is a modular support for all
i\in[\ell]
.

Let
\mathrm{supp}_{1}:R^{n_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u_{1}}
and
\mathrm{supp}_{2}:R^{n_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u_{2}}
be supports. It is easy to see that the product
\mathrm{supp}_{1}\times\mathrm{supp}_{2}
is a support from
R^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{u_{1}+u_{2}}
. Moreover, the product is modular whenever the factors are. A support is called standard if it can be decomposed as a product of supports, each defined on a single copy of
R
.

Definition 1.12
.

A support
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
is
standard
if for each
i\in[n]
there exist
u_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and a support
\mathrm{supp}_{i}:R\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u_{i}}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})=(\mathrm{supp}_{1}(r_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{supp}_{n}(r_{n}))
, up to permuting the coordinates of
\mathbb{Z}^{u}
.

Notice that, for a standard support
\mathrm{supp}
, one has

\mathrm{supp}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})=\mathrm{supp}(r_{1},0,\dots,0)\vee\dots\vee\mathrm{supp}(0,\dots,0,r_{n}).

We will be interested in a specific standard modular support for finite chain rings introduced in
[
37
, Example 26]
and defined as follows.

Definition 1.13
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring with maximal ideal
(\alpha)
. Let
k
be the smallest positive integer such that
\alpha^{k}=0
. Let
\overline{\mathrm{supp}}:R\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be the support function given by

\overline{\mathrm{supp}}(r)=\min\left\{0\leq i\leq k:r\in\left(\alpha^{k-i}\right)\right\}=\lambda(r),

for every
r\in R
, where
\lambda(r)
is the length of the ideal generated by
r
. The support
\mathrm{supp}=\overline{\mathrm{supp}}\times\dots\times\overline{\mathrm{supp}}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called the
chain support
on
R^{n}
.

1.3
Generalized weights

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq R^{n}
be an
R
-linear code. Since
R=R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}
with
R_{i}
finite local ring for all
i\in[\ell]
, we have that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{i}\subseteq R_{i}^{n}
is the projection
\pi_{i}(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
on the
i
-th factor of
R^{n}=R_{1}^{n}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}^{n}
for all
i\in[\ell]
. Recall that
\mu(\mathcal{C})
denotes the least cardinality of a system of generators of a code
\mathcal{C}
. For a code
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\subseteq R^{n}
, we set
M(\mathcal{C}):=\mu(\mathcal{C}_{1})+\dots+\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\ell})
. We now have all the necessary elements to state the definition of generalized weights of an
R
-linear code with respect to a support
\mathrm{supp}
, as was given in
[
19
]
.

Definition 1.14
.

For
r\in[M(\mathcal{C})]
, the
r
-th generalized weight
of
\mathcal{C}
is given by

d_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\min\left\{\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{D}):\mathcal{D}\in S_{j}(\mathcal{C})\text{ for some }j\geq r\right\},

where
S_{j}(\mathcal{C})=\{\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{D}\text{ is a subcode with a minimal system of generators of cardinality }j\}
.

Notice that the previous definition is well-posed, since
S_{j}(\mathcal{C})\neq\emptyset
for
j\in[M(\mathcal{C})]
as proved in
[
19
, Theorem 1.8]
. When
R
is a finite field, the cardinality of a minimal system of generators coincides with the dimension of the subcode that they generate. Therefore, Definition
1.14
extends the classical definition of generalized weights of linear block codes. When
R
is a ring, however, a code may have minimal systems of generators of different cardinalities, see e.g.
[
19
, Example 1.6]
. The next proposition collects some basic properties of generalized weights.

Proposition 1.15

(
[
19
, Lemma 2.12]
)
.

Let
D\subseteq C\subseteq R^{n}
be two
R
-linear codes. Then

1.

d_{1}(\mathcal{C})=\minwt(\mathcal{C})
,

2.

d_{r}(\mathcal{D})\geq d_{r}(\mathcal{C})
for
r\in[\min\{M(\mathcal{D}),M(\mathcal{C})\}]
,

3.

d_{r+1}(\mathcal{C})\geq d_{r}(\mathcal{C})
for
r\in[M(\mathcal{C})-1]
,

4.

d_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\min\{\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\rvert:D\subseteq\mathcal{C}\text{ and }M(\mathcal{D})\geq r\}
for
r\in[M(\mathcal{C})]
.

One of the reasons for the interest in generalized Hamming weights is that they are invariant under code-equivalence. Here, we prove that this is also the case for
R
-linear codes. We start by defining equivalence between
R
-linear codes.

Definition 1.16
.

An
isometry
between
R
-linear codes is an
R
-module isomorphism
\varphi:\mathcal{C}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{2}
that preserves the weight, i.e., such that
\mathrm{wt}(v)=\mathrm{wt}(\varphi(v))
for all
v\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.
Two
R
-linear codes
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
in
R^{n}
are
equivalent
if there exists an isometry
\varphi:R^{n}\rightarrow R^{n}
that maps
\mathcal{C}_{1}
to
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

A classical result for the Hamming support states that an isometry from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
to itself can be expressed as multiplication by a permutation matrix and a diagonal one. In the following, we establish a similar result for codes over principal ideal rings equipped with a standard modular support. We start by considering the case where
R
is a finite chain ring.

Lemma 1.17
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring, let
\mathrm{supp}=\mathrm{supp}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathrm{supp}_{n}
be a standard modular support on
R^{n}
, and let
\varphi:R^{n}\rightarrow R^{n}
be an isometry with respect to the weight induced by
\mathrm{supp}
. Then there exist a diagonal invertible matrix
D
and a permutation matrix
M
such that
\varphi(v)=DMv
for all
v\in R^{n}
.

Proof.

It is known that an
R
-module isomorphism from
R^{n}
to itself can be expressed as multiplication by a matrix
N=(n_{i,j})
in
R^{n\times n}
. In order to prove the statement, we proceed by induction on
n
. When
n=1
, the thesis is clear. Assume that the statement holds for
n-1
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\lvert\mathrm{supp}_{1}(1)\rvert\leq\dots\leq\lvert\mathrm{supp}_{n}(1)\rvert
. Let
e_{i}
be an element of the standard basis. Then, an entry of
\varphi(e_{i})
must be invertible, otherwise
\varphi
would not be injective. We start by considering
e_{1}
. Since
\lvert\mathrm{supp}_{1}(1)\rvert\leq\lvert\mathrm{supp}_{i}(1)\rvert
for
i>1
, we conclude that the first column of
N
has an invertible entry, say the
k
-th entry, and it is zero everywhere else. Up to multiplying by a permutation matrix, we may assume
k=1
.
Consider the vector
v=(-n_{1,2},n_{1,1},0,\dots,0)^{t}\in R^{n}
. Since
\varphi(v)=n_{1,1}(0,n_{2,2},\ldots,n_{t,2})
, then
\mathrm{wt}(\varphi(v))\leq\mathrm{wt}(\varphi(e_{2}))
, while
\mathrm{wt}(v)\geq\mathrm{wt}(e_{2})
. Since
\varphi
is an isometry, we have
n_{1,2}=0
. Proceeding in a similar way, we show that the first row of
N
is different from zero only in the first entry. This implies that
\varphi
restricted to
0\times R^{n-1}
is an isometry of
R^{n-1}
. We conclude by the induction hypothesis.
∎

When
R
is a principal ideal ring, the isometries of
R^{n}
can still be expressed as a product by a matrix, but the matrices that represent the isometries have a more complicated description. However, we can classify the isometries of
R^{n}
based on the isometries of finite chain rings that we described in the previous lemma.

Theorem 1.18
.

Let
R=R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}
be a principal ideal ring, let
\mathrm{supp}
be a standard modular support on
R^{n}
, and let
\varphi:R^{n}\rightarrow R^{n}
be an isometry with respect to
\mathrm{supp}
. Then, for each
i\in[\ell]
, there exists an isometry
\varphi_{i}:R_{i}^{n}\rightarrow R_{i}^{n}
such that
\pi_{i}(\varphi(r)))=\varphi_{i}(\pi_{i}(r))
for every
r\in R^{n}
, where
\pi_{i}:R^{n}\rightarrow R_{i}^{n}
is the standard projection. Equivalently, the matrix defining
\varphi
has the form
DM
, where
D
is invertible and diagonal, and
\pi_{i}(M)
is a permutation matrix for all
i\in[\ell]
.

Proof.

This follows from observing that any
R
-module isomorphism maps
0\times\dots\times R_{i}^{n}\times\dots\times 0
to itself and that the restriction of an isometry is an isometry.
∎

Example 1.19
.

Over the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{6}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
let
\mathrm{supp}:\mathbb{Z}_{6}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be given by
\mathrm{supp}(1)=(1,1)
,
\mathrm{supp}(2)=(1,0)
and
\mathrm{supp}(3)=(0,1)
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}=\mathrm{supp}_{1}\times\mathrm{supp}_{2}
, where
\mathrm{supp}_{1}
is the Hamming support on
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
\mathrm{supp}_{2}
is the Hamming support on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
Consider the free module
\mathbb{Z}_{6}^{2}
with the standard modular support
\mathrm{supp}\times\mathrm{supp}
. One can check by direct computation that multiplication by the matrix

N=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\
3&2\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}_{6}^{2\times 2}

is an isometry
\varphi:\mathbb{Z}_{6}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{6}^{2}
. Clearly

is not the product of a permutation matrix and a diagonal one. However, if we look at the projections in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2}
, we find that the two isometries
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
of the proof of Theorem
1.18
correspond, respectively, to the matrices

N_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

that are both permutation matrices multiplied by a diagonal invertible matrix, as required by Lemma

. Equivalently,
N=DM\in\mathbb{Z}_{6}^{2\times 2}
with

D=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\
0&5\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\;M=\begin{pmatrix}4&3\\
3&4\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem
1.18
implies that the generalized weights are a family of invariants.

Corollary 1.20
.

The generalized weights of an
R
-linear code are invariant under equivalences.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:R^{n}\rightarrow R^{n}
be an equivalence between two
R
-linear codes
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Consider a minimal system of generators
M
of a subcode
\mathcal{D}_{1}
of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
. Since
\varphi
is an
R
-linear isomorphism of
R^{n}
,
\varphi(M)
is a minimal system of generators of a subcode
\mathcal{D}_{2}
of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. In particular,
M(\mathcal{C}_{1})=M(\mathcal{C}_{2})
. Since
\varphi
is an isometry, then
\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{D}_{1})=\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{D}_{2})
. Therefore,
d_{r}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\geq d_{r}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
for
r\in[M(\mathcal{C}_{2})]
. This suffices to conclude, since
\varphi^{-1}
is also an isometry.
∎

The dimension of a vector space coincides with the cardinality of a minimal system of generators. This is not always the case for a module over a ring. For this reason, the definition of generalized weights does not extend uniquely to the case of modules on rings: Definition
1.14
is only one possible choice. A different choice is made, e.g., in
[
32
,
3
,
33
]
. In this paper, we explore the following definition, which is related to the approach of
[
18
,
20
]
.

Definition 1.21
.

For
1\leq r\leq\lambda(\mathcal{C})
, the
r
-th generalized weight
of
\mathcal{C}
is

\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\min\left\{\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{D}):\mathcal{D}\text{ is a submodule of }\mathcal{C}\text{ such that }\lambda(\mathcal{D})\geq r\right\},

for
1\leq r\leq\lambda(\mathcal{C})
.

It is easy to prove that these generalized weights satisfy properties similar to those of Proposition
1.15
.

Proposition 1.22
.

Let
R
be endowed with a support and let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq R^{n}
be
R
-linear codes. Then

1.

\bar{d}_{1}(\mathcal{C})=\minwt(\mathcal{C})
,

2.

\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{D})\geq\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
for
r\in[\lambda(\mathcal{D})]
,

3.

\bar{d}_{r+1}(\mathcal{C})\geq\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
for
r\in[\lambda(\mathcal{C})-1]
,

4.

if the support is modular, then
\bar{d}_{r+1}(\mathcal{C})>\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
for
r\in[\lambda(\mathcal{C})-1]
,

5.

\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
is invariant under equivalence for all
r\in[\lambda(\mathcal{C})]
.

Proof.

Items 1, 2, 3, and 5 follow from the definition of generalized weights and the properties of supports. In order to prove item 4, let
\mathcal{D}
be a submodule of
\mathcal{C}
that realizes
\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
. Let
d\in\mathcal{D}
be such that there exists an index
i
for which
\mathrm{supp}(d)_{i}\geq\mathrm{supp}(c)_{i}
for all
c\in\mathcal{D}
. The set
\bar{\mathcal{D}}=\{c\in\mathcal{D}:\mathrm{supp}(c)_{i}<\mathrm{supp}(d)_{i}\}
is a submodule of
\mathcal{D}
. We claim that
\lambda(\bar{\mathcal{D}})=\lambda(\mathcal{D})-1
. In fact, assume that there exists a module
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\bar{\mathcal{D}}\subsetneq\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. Then, there exists
b\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(b)_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(d)_{i}
.
Let
a\in\mathcal{D}
be such that
\mathrm{supp}(a)_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(d)_{i}
.
Since the support is modular, there exists
r\in R
such that
\mathrm{supp}(a+rb)_{i}<\mathrm{supp}(a)_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(d)_{i}
. Therefore,
a+rb\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}
and so
a\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. This implies
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}
, and hence
\lambda(\bar{\mathcal{D}})=\lambda(\mathcal{D})-1
. Finally, by construction
\mathrm{wt}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})<\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{D})
, so we conclude that
\bar{d}_{r-1}(\mathcal{C})<\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

2
Latroids

2.1
Definition and basic properties

Latroids were introduced in
[
41
]
by Vertigan as a generalization of matroids. It is easy to see that they also generalize their
q
-analogs. Since our goal is to associate a latroid to a linear code, we only give the definition for finite lattices. However, some of the results can be extended with due attention to the infinite case.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be an ordered abelian group and let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite modular lattice. An
A
-latroid
with
rank function

\rho:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow A
under
length function

\lVert\cdot\rVert:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow A
on the lattice
\mathcal{L}
, is a triple
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
such that:

L1.

\rho(0_{\mathcal{L}})=\lVert 0_{\mathcal{L}}\rVert=0_{A}
,

L2.

\lVert\cdot\rVert
is strictly increasing, that is,
\lVert L\rVert<\lVert M\rVert
for all
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
with
L<M
,

L3.

\lVert\cdot\rVert
is
modular
, that is,
\lVert L\rVert+\lVert M\rVert=\lVert L\vee M\rVert+\lVert L\wedge M\rVert
for all
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
,

L4.

\rho
is
bounded increasing
, that is,
0\leq\rho(M)-\rho(L)\leq\lVert M\rVert-\lVert L\rVert
for all
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
with
L\leq M
,

L5.

\rho
is
submodular
, that is,
\rho(L)+\rho(M)\geq\rho(L\vee M)+\rho(L\wedge M)
for all
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite graded modular lattice with height function
\hgt_{\mathcal{L}}
. Then, the triple
(\hgt_{\mathcal{L}},\hgt_{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{L})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. Every latroid of the form
(\lVert\cdot\rVert,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
is called a
free latroid
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite modular lattice and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow A
be a modular function with
\lVert 0_{\mathcal{L}}\rVert=0_{A}
. For
0<a\in A
, let
\rho_{a}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow A
be the function defined by

\rho_{a}(L)=\begin{cases}\lVert L\rVert&\text{if }\lVert L\rVert\leq a\\
a&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
. We claim that
(\rho_{a},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
is an
A
-latroid. Indeed, L1, L2, L3, and L4 are trivially satisfied. For every
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
, if
\rho_{a}(L)=a
, then also
\rho_{a}(L\vee M)=a
, and so
\rho_{a}(L)+\rho_{a}(M)\geq\rho_{a}(L\vee M)+\rho_{a}(L\wedge M)
. Otherwise, if
\rho_{a}(L),\rho_{a}(M)<a
, then

\rho_{a}(L)+\rho_{a}(M)=\lVert L\rVert+\lVert M\rVert=\lVert L\vee M\rVert+\lVert L\wedge M\rVert\geq\rho_{a}(L\vee M)+\rho_{a}(L\wedge M).

So L5 is also satisfied. In analogy to matroid theory terminology, we call
(\rho_{a},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
a
uniform latroid
.

Definition 2.4
.

Consider an
A
-latroid
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
and let
L_{1}\leq L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
. Define
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{[L_{1},L_{2}]}:[L_{1},L_{2}]\rightarrow A
and
\rho_{[L_{1},L_{2}]}:[L_{1},L_{2}]\rightarrow A
as

\lVert L\rVert_{[L_{1},L_{2}]}=\lVert L\rVert-\lVert L_{1}\rVert\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\rho_{[L_{1},L_{2}]}(L)=\rho(L)-\rho(L_{1}),

for
L\in[L_{1},L_{2}]
.

Notice that
(\rho_{[L_{1},L_{2}]},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{[L_{1},L_{2}]},[L_{1},L_{2}])
is an
A
-latroid by
[
41
, Lemma 5.10]
.

Definition 2.5
.

The
direct sum

(\rho_{1},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})\oplus(\rho_{2},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
of
A
-latroids
(\rho_{1},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
and
(\rho_{2},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
is the
A
-latroid
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L}_{1}\times\mathcal{L}_{2})
, where
\rho:\mathcal{L}_{1}\times\mathcal{L}_{2}\rightarrow A
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert:\mathcal{L}_{1}\times\mathcal{L}_{2}\rightarrow A
are given by

\rho(L_{1},L_{2})=\rho_{1}(L_{1})+\rho_{2}(L_{2})\text{ and }\lVert(L_{1},L_{2})\rVert=\lVert L_{1}\rVert_{1}+\lVert L_{2}\rVert_{2}.

To prove that the direct sum of two latroids is a latroid, it suffices to observe that
(L_{1},L_{2})\vee(M_{1},M_{2})=(L_{1}\vee M_{1},L_{2}\vee M_{2})
and
(L_{1},L_{2})\wedge(M_{1},M_{2})=(L_{1}\wedge M_{1},L_{2}\wedge M_{2})
for every
L_{1},M_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
L_{2},M_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}&\rho(L_{1},L_{2})+\rho(M_{1},M_{2})=\rho_{1}(L_{1})+\rho_{2}(L_{2})+\rho_{1}(M_{1})+\rho_{2}(M_{2})=\\
&\geq\rho_{1}(L_{1}\vee M_{1})+\rho_{1}(L_{1}\wedge M_{1})+\rho_{2}(L_{2}\vee M_{2})+\rho_{2}(L_{2}\wedge M_{2})=\\
&=\rho(L_{1}\vee M_{1},L_{2}\vee M_{2})+\rho(L_{1}\wedge M_{1},L_{2}\wedge M_{2}),\end{split}

which proves the submodularity of
\rho
. Properties L1, L2, L3, and L4 can be proved in a similar way.

We denote by
(\mathcal{L}^{\perp},\leq)=(\{L^{\perp}:L\in\mathcal{L}\},\leq)
the
dual lattice
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
, where
L_{1}^{\perp}\leq L_{2}^{\perp}
if and only if
L_{2}\leq L_{1}
. The dual of a latroid was introduced in
[
41
, Definition 5.13]
.

Definition 2.6
.

The
dual
of an
A
-latroid
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
, denoted by
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
, is the
A
-latroid
(\rho^{\perp},\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\perp},\mathcal{L}^{\perp})
,
where

1.

\lVert L^{\perp}\rVert^{\perp}=\lVert 1_{\mathcal{L}}\rVert-\lVert L\rVert
,

2.

\rho^{\perp}(L^{\perp})=\lVert L^{\perp}\rVert^{\perp}-\rho(1_{\mathcal{L}})+\rho(L)
,
for every
L^{\perp}\in\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.

The next lemma collects some basic properties of the dual latroid.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
41
, Lemma 5.14]
)
.

Let
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
be an
A
-latroid. Then:

1.

(\rho^{\perp},\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\perp},\mathcal{L}^{\perp})
is an
A
-latroid,

2.

(\rho^{\perp},\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\perp},\mathcal{L}^{\perp})^{\perp}=(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L}),

3.

for
L_{1}\leq L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
we have
(\rho_{[L_{1},L_{2}]})^{\perp}=\rho^{\perp}_{[L_{2}^{\perp},L_{1}^{\perp}]}
,

4.

for
L_{1}\leq L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
we have
(\lVert\cdot\rVert_{[L_{1},L_{2}]})^{\perp}=\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\perp}_{[L_{2}^{\perp},L_{1}^{\perp}]}.

In the next remark we clarify how the concept of latroid generalizes the one of matroid.

Remark 2.8
.

Let
E
be a finite set. The power set
\mathcal{P}(E)
of
E
is a complete lattice with respect to the union and intersection. It is easy to verify that the cardinality function
\lvert\cdot\rvert
is a strictly increasing modular function on
\mathcal{P}(E)
. Let
\rho:\mathcal{P}(E)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be any function for which
(\rho,\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{P}(E))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. Then
\{X\subseteq E:\lvert X\rvert-\rho(X)>0\text{ and }X\text{ is minimal with this property}\}
is the set of circuits of a matroid with ground set
E
and rank function
\rho
. This is a direct consequence of
[
34
, Proposition 11.1.1]
.

Conversely, let
(E,\rho)
be a matroid with ground set
E
and rank function
\rho
. Then
(\rho,\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{P}(E))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. In fact, from the axioms of matroids we directly obtain that
\rho(0_{\mathcal{L}})=0
,
\rho
is submodular, and
0\leq\rho(M)-\rho(L)
for all
L,M\in\mathcal{P}(E)
with
L\leq M
. It remains to prove that

\rho(M)-\rho(L)\leq\lvert M\rvert-\lvert L\rvert
. By the submodularity of
\rho
, we have
\rho(M)\leq\rho(M\setminus L)+\rho(L)
, and by the modularity of the cardinality, we conclude that

\rho(M)-\rho(L)\leq\rho(M\setminus L)\leq\lvert M\setminus L\rvert=\lvert M\rvert-\lvert L\rvert.

2.2
Cryptomorphic definitions

Inspired by matroid theory, we define and study the concepts of independent element, basis, and circuit of a latroid.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
be an
A
-latroid. An element
L\in\mathcal{L}
is called
independent
if
\rho(L)=\lVert L\rVert
, a
basis
if
\rho(L)=\lVert L\rVert=\rho(1_{\mathcal{L}})
, and a
circuit
if
\rho(L)<\lVert L\rVert
and for all
M<L
we have
\rho(M)=\lVert M\rVert
.

In the case of matroids, knowing the independent sets is equivalent to knowing the rank function. However, in the case of latroids, this is not true in general, as one can see in the next example. In Proposition
2.16
, however, we show that a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid over a finite complemented modular lattice is determined by its independent elements.

Example 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the lattice of ideals of
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
. We consider the length function
\lVert\cdot\rVert
given by
\lVert R\rVert=|R\rvert-1
for each
R\in\mathcal{L}
. Then,
(\lfloor|\cdot|/2\rfloor,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
and
(\lambda,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
are two
\mathbb{Z}
-latroids whose independent elements are
0_{\mathcal{L}}
and
4\mathbb{Z}_{8}
. However, we have that
\lfloor|\mathbb{Z}_{8}|/2\rfloor=4
, while
\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{8})=3
.

Notice that, if
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the length function of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
, then any multiple of it by a positive integer is also a length function on
\mathcal{L}
. In the example above, the lattice is a chain, and the length function that we consider is twice its height function. In addition, any increasing function over a chain is modular.
This gives us space to define latroids on the same lattice with different rank functions but the same independent sets, as in Example
2.10
. In order to avoid this undesirable feature, we restrict our attention to relatively complemented lattices endowed with the height function. In particular, in this setting we are able to generalize
[
34
, Lemma 1.3.3]
. We start by observing that a relatively complemented finite lattice is atomistic.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a relatively complemented finite lattice. Then every
L\in\mathcal{L}
is the join of the atoms
J
such that
J\leq L
.

Proof.

Let
J_{1},\dots,J_{n}
be all the atoms in
\mathcal{L}
such that
J_{i}\leq L
. If
L>J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n}
, then there exists
J
such that
J\vee(J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n})=L
and
J\wedge(J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n})=0_{\mathcal{L}}
. Let
J_{n+1}\leq J\leq L
be an atom. Since
J\wedge(J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n})=0_{\mathcal{L}}
, then
J_{n+1}\notin\{J_{1},\dots,J_{n}\}
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
and let
\rho:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a submodular bounded increasing function. If
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
are such that
\rho(L_{1}\vee L_{3})=\rho(L_{1})
for each atom
L_{3}\leq L_{2}
, then
\rho(L_{1}\vee\ L_{2})=\rho(L_{1})
.

Proof.

Let
J_{1},\dots,J_{n}
be all the atoms in
\mathcal{L}
such that
J_{i}\leq L_{2}
. By Lemma
2.11
,
L_{2}=J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n}
.
We prove the statement by induction on
n
. If
n=1
, then
L_{2}
is an atom and the statement holds. For
n>1
, we have

\begin{split}2\rho(L_{1})&=\rho(L_{1}\vee(J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n-1}))+\rho(L_{1}\vee J_{n})\\
&\geq\rho((L_{1}\vee(J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n-1}))\vee(L_{1}\vee J_{n}))+\rho((L_{1}\vee(J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n-1}))\wedge(L_{1}\vee J_{n}))\\
&\geq\rho(L_{1}\vee J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n})+\rho(L_{1})\geq 2\rho(L_{1}).\end{split}

It follows that
\rho(L_{1}\vee L_{2})=\rho(L_{1}\vee J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n})=\rho(L_{1}).

∎

Both lemmas clearly fail for arbitrary lattices, e.g., over the lattice of ideals of a finite chain ring which is not a field.

Example 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the lattice of ideals of
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
. Then
\mathcal{L}=\{0,(4),(2),\mathbb{Z}_{8}\}
is a chain and the only atom is
(4)
. In particular,
(2)
and
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
are not joins of atoms.

Consider now the
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\lambda,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
of Example
2.10
. The only atom
L_{3}\leq\mathbb{Z}_{8}
is
L_{3}=(4)
and
\lambda((2)\vee(4))=\lambda((2))
, however,
\lambda((2)\vee\mathbb{Z}_{8})=\lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{8})=3\neq 2=\lambda((2))
.

The next proposition characterizes integer-valued functions that are the rank function of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. It is a consequence of Lemma
2.12
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. A function
\rho:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is a rank function of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
if and only if it satisfies the following properties:

R1’.

\rho(0_{\mathcal{L}})=0
,

R2’.

\rho(L)\leq\rho(L\vee J)\leq\rho(L)+1
, for every atom
J\in\mathcal{L}
and every
L\in\mathcal{L}
,

R3’.

if
\rho(L)=\rho(L\vee J_{1})=\rho(L\vee J_{2})
for some atoms
J_{1},J_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\rho(L)=\rho(L\vee J_{1}\vee J_{2})
.

Proof.

If
\rho
is the rank function of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid, then clearly
\rho
satisfies R1’ and R2’, and by Lemma
2.12
it also satisfies R3’.

Now, let
\rho:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a function that satisfies R1’, R2’, and R3’. For
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
,
L\leq M
, it is easy to show that R2’ implies L4 by induction on
\hgt(M)-\hgt(L)
. Therefore, it suffices to prove that
\rho
is submodular. Let
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
. We proceed by induction on
\hgt(L)-\hgt(L\wedge M)
. If
\hgt(L)-\hgt(L\wedge M)=0
, then
L\leq M
and modularity holds. Otherwise, let
J
be an atom such that
L=L_{1}\vee J
and
L\wedge M=L_{1}\wedge M
. By the induction hypothesis, we have

\begin{split}\rho(L\vee M)+\rho(L\wedge M)&=\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee M)+\rho(L_{1}\wedge M)\\
&\leq\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee M)-\rho(L_{1}\vee M)+\rho(L_{1})+\rho(M).\end{split}

Hence, it suffices to prove that

\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee M)-\rho(L_{1}\vee M)\leq\rho(L_{1}\vee J)-\rho(L_{1}).

We proceed by induction on
\hgt(M)
. The case where
\hgt(M)=0
is trivial. So suppose that
\hgt(M)>0
and let
\bar{J}
be an atom such that
M=\bar{M}\vee\bar{J}
and
\hgt(\bar{M})<\hgt(M)
. If
\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})>\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
, since
\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})\leq\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M})+1
, we have

\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})-\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})\leq\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M})-\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
. If instead

\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M})>\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
, then
\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})-\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})\leq 1=\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M})-\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
. In both cases, we obtain

\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})-\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})\leq\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M})-\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})\leq\rho(L_{1}\vee J)-\rho(L_{1}).

If instead
\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})=\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
and
\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M})=\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
, by R3’ we obtain
\rho((L_{1}\vee J)\vee\bar{M}\vee\bar{J})=\rho(L_{1}\vee\bar{M})
, and this concludes the proof.
∎

Another important consequence of Lemma
2.12
is that, for a finite complemented modular lattice with the height as length function, the set of independent elements of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid determines the rank function of the latroid, and hence the latroid itself.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. Let
\rho:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a submodular bounded increasing function and let
L\in\mathcal{L}
. If
I
is a maximal independent element in
[0,L]
, then
\rho(L)=\rho(I)
. In other words, the set of independent elements of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
uniquely determines
\rho
.

Proof.

Since
I
is maximal, we have
\rho(I\vee J)=\rho(I)
for every atom in
L
. We conclude by Lemma
2.12
.
∎

Lemma
2.12
and Proposition
2.15
also imply that, in the case of complemented lattices, the independent elements of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid satisfy a latroid version of the independence augmentation property.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. Consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
. The set of independent elements
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the following properties:

I1.

0_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{I}
,

I2.

if
I_{1}\in\mathcal{I}
and
I_{2}<I_{1}
, then
I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
,

I3.

if
I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
and
\hgt(I_{2})<\hgt(I_{1})
, then there is an atom
J\leq I_{1}
such that
J\nleq I_{2}
and
I_{2}\vee J\in\mathcal{I}
.

I4.

for any
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
and
I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
maximal such that
I_{1}\leq L_{1}
and
I_{2}\leq L_{2}
, there exists a maximal independent element
I_{3}\leq L_{1}\vee L_{2}
contained in
I_{1}\vee I_{2}
.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{I}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
that satisfies properties I1, I2, and I4. Then there exists a unique function
\rho
such that
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid whose set of independent elements is
\mathcal{I}
. Moreover, for any
L\in\mathcal{L}
, one has
\rho(L)=\hgt(I)
for
I
maximal among the elements of
\mathcal{I}
which are dominated by
L
.

Proof.

Since
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid, we have
\rho(0_{\mathcal{L}})=0=\hgt(0_{\mathcal{L}})
, and hence
0_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{I}
. Property I2 is satisfied, since
\rho
is bounded increasing. Now consider
I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
with
\hgt(I_{2})<\hgt(I_{1})
. By Lemma
2.11
, we can write
I_{1}=J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n}
with
\hgt(J_{i})=1
for all
i\in[n]
. Assume by contradiction that for every
J_{i}\nleq I_{2}
we have
I_{2}\vee J_{i}\notin\mathcal{I}
. Then, for all
i\in[n]
we have
\rho(J_{i}\vee I_{2})<\hgt(I_{2})+1
, and so
\rho(J_{i}\vee I_{2})=\rho(I_{2})
. By Lemma
2.12
we obtain
\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})=\rho(I_{2})
, and this implies

\hgt(I_{1})\leq\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})=\rho(I_{2})=\hgt(I_{2}),

that is a contradiction. This establishes Property I3. As for Property I4, by the submodularity of
\rho
we have

\rho((I_{1}\vee I_{2})\vee L_{1})\leq\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})+\rho(L_{1})-\rho((I_{1}\vee I_{2})\wedge L_{1})\leq\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})+\rho(L_{1})-\rho(I_{1})=\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2}),

where the last equality follows from Proposition
2.15
. Moreover, since
L_{1}\vee L_{2}\geq I_{1}\vee I_{2}
, then

\rho(L_{1}\vee L_{2})\geq\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})

by L4.
By Proposition
2.15
, a maximal independent element
I_{3}\in[0,I_{1}\vee I_{2}]
has
\rho(I_{3})=\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})=\rho(L_{1}\vee L_{2})
, therefore
I_{3}
is also a maximal independent element in
[0,L_{1}\vee L_{2}]
.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
and let
\mathcal{I}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
that satisfies properties I1, I2, and I4. We claim that there exists a submodular and bounded increasing function
\rho
such that
\rho(I)=\hgt(I)
for every
I\in\mathcal{I}
and that such a function is unique. Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
and let
I
be maximal among the elements of
\mathcal{I}
which are dominated by
L
. By Proposition
2.15
it must be
\rho(L)=\rho(I)
. This shows that the value of
\rho
on each element of
\mathcal{L}
is determined. It is easy to check that
\rho
is bounded increasing, so we just need to prove that
\rho
is submodular. Consider
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
. Let
I_{3}
be a maximal independent element in
[0,L_{1}\wedge L_{2}]
and let
I_{1},I_{2}
be maximal independent elements in
[I_{3},L_{1}]
and in
[I_{3},L_{2}]
, respectively. We have
\rho(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})=\rho(I_{3})\leq\rho(I_{1}\wedge I_{2})
, so equality holds. By Property I4,
\rho(L_{1}\vee L_{2})=\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})
. Therefore, using the fact that
\rho
is bounded increasing and submodular on
\mathcal{I}
, we obtain

\rho(L_{1}\vee L_{2})+\rho(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})\leq\rho(I_{1}\vee I_{2})+\rho(I_{1}\wedge I_{2})\leq\rho(I_{1})+\rho(I_{2})=\rho(L_{1})+\rho(L_{2}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Notice that Property I3, which corresponds to the independence augmentation property of matroids, is not used in the proof of the previous proposition. Indeed, Property I3 is implied by the other properties. This is consistent with what happens in the case of
q
-matroids. We refer to
[
6
,
15
,
31
]
for the independence axioms of
q
-matroids and to
[
10
]
for a definition containing only three axioms. We chose to include Property I3 because of the next proposition, that applies to matroids among others. We start with a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a distributive lattice, and let
J,L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
. If
J
is an atom and
J\leq L_{1}\vee L_{2}
, then
J\leq L_{1}
or
J\leq L_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is a distributive lattice,

J=J\wedge(L_{1}\vee L_{2})=(J\wedge L_{1})\vee(J\wedge L_{2}),

and so
J\leq L_{1}
or
J\leq L_{2}
.
∎

The statement of the lemma is false in general for a lattice which is not distributive.

Example 2.18
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the lattice of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
. Then
J=\langle(1,1)\rangle
is an atom and
J\leq\langle(1,0)\rangle\vee\langle(0,1)\rangle
, but
J\not\leq\langle(1,0)\rangle
and
J\not\leq\langle(0,1)\rangle
.

Using Lemma
2.17
, we are now ready to prove that, over a finite complemented distributive lattice, property I4 is not necessary, as it is implied by the other properties of independent sets. This applies, for example, to the set of independent sets of a matroid.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented distributive lattice with height function
\hgt
. Consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
. Then, Properties I1, I2, and I3 imply Property I4.

Proof.

Consider
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
and let
I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
be maximal such that
I_{1}\leq L_{1}
and
I_{2}\leq L_{2}
. Let
I_{3}=I_{1}\vee J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n}
be a maximal independent element in
[I_{1},L_{1}\vee L_{2}]
, where
J_{1},\dots,J_{n}
are all the atoms in
[0_{\mathcal{L}},I_{3}]
such that
J_{i}\nleq I_{1}
. Since
I_{1}
is maximal independent in
[0_{\mathcal{L}},L_{1}]
,
J_{i}\nleq L_{1}
for all
i\in[n]
, hence
I_{3}\wedge L_{1}=I_{1}
by distributivity.

By repeatedly applying Property I3 to
I_{3}
and
I_{2}
, we find a maximal independent element
I
in
[I_{2},L_{1}\vee L_{2}]
with the property that
I\leq I_{2}\vee I_{3}
. By distributivity
I=(I\wedge L_{1})\vee(I\wedge L_{2})
, moreover
I_{2}\leq I\wedge L_{2}
implies
I\wedge L_{2}=I_{2}
, since
I_{2}
is maximal independent in
[0_{\mathcal{L}},L_{2}]
. Moreover,
I=I\wedge(I_{2}\vee I_{3})=(I\wedge I_{2})\vee(I\wedge I_{3})=I_{2}\vee(I\wedge I_{3})
and
I\wedge I_{3}=(I\wedge I_{3})\wedge(L_{1}\vee L_{2})=(I\wedge I_{3}\wedge L_{1})\vee(I\wedge I_{3}\wedge L_{2})\leq(I_{3}\wedge L_{1})\vee(I\wedge L_{2})=I_{1}\vee I_{2}
. This shows that
I\leq I_{1}\vee I_{2}
.
∎

Proposition
2.16
implies that a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid over a finite complemented modular lattice is fully described by its set of independent elements. Similarly to what happens for matroids and
q
-matroids
[
6
,
31
,
34
]
, latroids can also be described using bases and circuits.

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. A subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is the set of bases of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
if and only if

B1.

\mathcal{B}\neq\emptyset
,

B2.

if
B_{1}=J_{1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n}
and
B_{2}=H_{1}\vee\dots\vee H_{m}
where
J_{1},\dots,J_{n},H_{1},\dots,H_{m}
are atoms, and
J_{i}\nleq B_{2}
, then there exists an index
s\in[m]
such that
H_{s}\nleq B_{1}
and
J_{1}\vee\dots J_{i-1}\vee J_{i+1}\vee\dots\vee J_{n}\vee H_{s}\in\mathcal{B}
.

B3.

for any
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
, let
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
be such that
B_{1}\wedge L_{1}
and
B_{2}\wedge L_{2}
are maximal. Then there exists
B_{3}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
B_{3}\wedge(L_{1}\vee L_{2})
is maximal and
B_{3}\wedge(L_{1}\vee L_{2})\leq(B_{1}\wedge L_{1})\vee(B_{2}\wedge L_{2})
.

If the equivalent conditions hold, then for any
L\in\mathcal{L}
we have
\rho(L)=\hgt(L\wedge B)
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that
L\wedge B
is maximal.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying properties B1, B2, and B3. Consider the set

\mathcal{I}=\{I\in\mathcal{L}:\text{there exists }B\in\mathcal{B}\text{ such that }I\leq B\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{I}
satisfies I1 and I2. In order to prove that it also satisfies I4, we notice that an element
I\in\mathcal{I}
is maximal in
L
if and only if there exists
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that it has maximal intersection with
L
among the elements in
\mathcal{B}
and
I=L\wedge B
. Property I4 now follows directly from B3.
Moreover, notice that
\mathcal{B}
is by definition the set of maximal elements of
\mathcal{I}
.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{I}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
that satisfies properties I1, I2, I3, and I4. Consider the set

\mathcal{B}=\{B\in\mathcal{I}:B\text{ is maximal with respect to the order in the lattice}\}.

It is easy to check that I1 implies B1, I3 implies B2, and I4 implies B3.
We conclude by Proposition
2.16
.
∎

Notice that all bases have the same height by I3, so in B2 we have
n=m
. Similarly to the properties of independent elements, here we also have that one of the axioms is redundant. Indeed, the proof of the proposition shows that B3 is equivalent to I4 and that I3 implies B2. It follows that B3 implies B2. However, if the lattice is distributive, then the two properties are equivalent.

Corollary 2.21
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented distributive lattice with height function
\hgt
. Consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
. Then B2 implies B3.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{B}\neq\emptyset
.
It suffices to show that B2 implies I1, I2, and I3 for
\mathcal{I}=\{I\in\mathcal{L}:\text{there exists }B\in\mathcal{B}\text{ such that }I\leq B\}
. In fact, if this is the case, then they also imply I4 by Proposition
2.19
. We conclude as in the proof of Proposition
2.20
.
∎

The next proposition concerns the properties of the circuits of a latroid.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. A subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is the set of circuits of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
if and only if

C1.

0_{\mathcal{L}}\notin\mathcal{C}
,

C2.

if
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
are such that
C_{1}\leq C_{2}
, then
C_{1}=C_{2}
,

C3.

if
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
are distinct and
L\leq C_{1}\vee C_{2}
is such that
\hgt(L)=\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{2})-1
, then there exists
C_{3}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C_{3}\leq L
.

We start by proving some preliminary results.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
and let
\mathcal{C}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying C1, C2, and C3. Then, for every
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
and
L\leq C_{1}\vee C_{2}
such that
\hgt(L)=\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{2})-1
and
C_{2}\nleq L
, we have

C_{1}\vee C_{2}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L\}\vee C_{2}.

Proof.

If
C_{1}=C_{2}
, then
L<C_{1}
does not contain any element of
\mathcal{C}
by C2 and
C_{1}\vee C_{2}=C_{2}
holds.
Hence, let
C_{1},C_{2}
be a pair of distinct elements in
\mathcal{C}
for which the statement fails and such that
C_{1}\vee C_{2}
is minimal with such property. For such a pair, it holds that
C_{1},C_{2}\not\leq L
.
By C3, there exists
C_{3}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C_{3}\leq L
and
C_{3}\vee C_{2}<C_{1}\vee C_{2}
. Let
C_{1}\leq\bar{L}\leq C_{1}\vee C_{2}
be such that
\hgt(\bar{L})=\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{2})-1
, then

\hgt(\bar{L}\wedge(C_{2}\vee C_{3}))=\hgt(\bar{L})+\hgt(C_{2}\vee C_{3})-\hgt(\bar{L}\vee C_{2}\vee C_{3})=\hgt(C_{2}\vee C_{3})-1,

since
\bar{L}\vee C_{2}\vee C_{3}=C_{1}\vee C_{2}
.
Applying C3 again, we find
C_{4}\leq\bar{L}\wedge(C_{2}\vee C_{3})
. We notice that

\hgt(L\wedge(C_{1}\vee C_{4}))=\hgt(L)+\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{4})-\hgt(L\vee(C_{1}\vee C_{4}))=\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{4})-1.

The last equality follows from observing that
L\leq L\vee(C_{1}\vee C_{4})=C_{1}\vee C_{2}
, and the inequality is strict since
C_{1}\not\leq L
. Moreover, notice that
C_{1}\neq C_{4}
, since
C_{4}\leq C_{2}\vee C_{3}
, but
C_{1}\not\leq C_{2}\vee C_{3}
.
Since
C_{1}\vee C_{4}\leq\bar{L}<C_{1}\vee C_{2}
, by the minimality of
C_{1}\vee C_{2}
, we obtain

C_{1}\vee C_{4}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L\wedge(C_{1}\vee C_{4})\}\vee C_{1}\leq\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L\}\vee C_{1}.

Since
C_{4}\leq C_{2}\vee C_{3}
and
C_{3}\leq L
, we conclude that
C_{1}\vee C_{2}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L\}\vee C_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 2.24
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
and let
\mathcal{C}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying C1, C2, and C3. Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
,
L_{1}\geq L_{2}
be such that
\hgt(L_{2})=\hgt(L_{1})-1
. If there exist
\bar{C}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\bar{C}\leq L_{1}
and
\bar{C}\nleq L_{2}
, then

\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L_{1}\}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L_{2}\}\vee\bar{C}.

Proof.

Since
L_{2}\leq L_{1}
and
\hgt(L_{2})=\hgt(L_{1})-1
, for every
D\in\mathcal{C}
with
D\leq L_{1}
we have
L_{2}\leq D\vee\bar{C}\vee L_{2}\leq L_{1}
, hence

D\vee\bar{C}\vee L_{2}=L_{1}
, since
\bar{C}\not\leq L_{2}
. Therefore

\hgt((D\vee\bar{C})\wedge L_{2})=\hgt(D\vee\bar{C})+\hgt(L_{2})-\hgt(L_{1})=\hgt(D\vee\bar{C})-1.

Let
L_{3}=(D\vee\bar{C})\wedge L_{2}
. By Lemma
2.23

D\vee\bar{C}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L_{3}\}\vee\bar{C}\leq\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L_{2}\}\vee\bar{C}.

We conclude by taking the join over
D\in\mathcal{C}
,
D\leq L_{1}
on both sides.
∎

We introduce the concept of chain of circuits. These are ordered sequences of circuits, so that the joins of the first
i
, for every
i
, form a strictly ascending chain of lattice elements. This concept is used in the proof of Proposition
2.22
.

Definition 2.25
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
and let
\mathcal{C}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying C1, C2, and C3. A
chain
in
\mathcal{C}
is a sequence of elements
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C_{1}\vee\dots\vee C_{i}<C_{1}\vee\dots\vee C_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. A chain
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
is
dominated
by
L
if
C_{i}\leq L
for all
i\in[n]
. A chain is
maximal
if it cannot be refined, i.e., it is not a proper subsequence of another chain of circuits.

Lemma 2.26
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
and let
\mathcal{C}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying C1, C2, and C3. Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
. Any maximal chain in
\mathcal{C}
dominated by
L
has the same length.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the height of
L
. If
\hgt(L)=0
, then
L
contains no element of
\mathcal{C}
and the length of any chain dominated by
L
is
0
. Assume therefore that
\hgt(L)>0
and that the statement holds for every
L^{\prime}<L
. Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\in\mathcal{C}
and
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
be two maximal chains dominated by
L
with
m\leq n
. Let
C_{1}\vee\dots\vee C_{m-1}\leq\bar{L}<L
be such that
\hgt(\bar{L})=\hgt(L)-1
and
C_{1}\vee\dots\vee C_{m}\nleq\bar{L}
. Such an
\bar{L}
exists since
C_{1}\vee\dots\vee C_{m-1}<C_{1}\vee\dots\vee C_{m}\leq L
and
\mathcal{L}
is relatively complemented. If
D_{i}\leq\bar{L}
for every
i\in[n]
, then
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
is a maximal chain dominated by
\bar{L}
. Else, let
1\leq i\leq n
be the smallest index for which
D_{i}\nleq\bar{L}
. For every
i<j\leq n
,
\bar{L}\vee D_{i}\vee D_{j}=L
, and hence
\hgt(\bar{L}\wedge(D_{i}\vee D_{j}))=\hgt(D_{i}\vee D_{j})-1
.
By Lemma
2.23
there exists
\bar{D}_{j}\leq\bar{L}\wedge(D_{j}\vee D_{i})
such that
\bar{D}_{j}\nleq D_{1}\vee\dots\vee D_{j-1}
. In fact, if this were not the case, we would obtain

D_{j}<D_{j}\vee D_{i}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq\bar{L}\wedge(D_{j}\vee D_{i})\}\vee D_{i}\leq D_{1}\vee\dots\vee D_{j-1},

that contradicts our assumptions. Consider the sequence
D_{1},\dots,D_{i-1},\bar{D}_{i+1},\dots,\bar{D}_{n}
. This is a chain of circuits in
\bar{L}
. Indeed, we have

D_{1}\vee\dots\vee D_{i-1}\vee\bar{D}_{i+1}\vee\ldots\vee\bar{D}_{j}\leq D_{1}\vee\dots\vee D_{j},

while
\bar{D}_{j+1}\nleq D_{1}\vee\dots\vee D_{j}
. Since
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
is a maximal chain in
L
,
C_{1},\dots,C_{m-1}
has to be maximal in
\bar{L}
. Indeed, suppose that there exists a circuit
\bar{C}
such that
C_{1}\dots,C_{i-1},\bar{C},C_{i}\dots,C_{m-1}
is a chain in
\mathcal{C}
dominated by
\bar{L}
, then
C_{1}\dots,C_{i-1},\bar{C},C_{i}\dots,C_{m}
is a chain in
\mathcal{C}
dominated by
L
, but this contradicts the maximality of
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
. Therefore, we have
n-1\leq m-1
, i.e.,
n\leq m
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
2.22
.

Proof of Proposition
2.22
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of circuits of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
. By definition
\rho(0_{\mathcal{L}})=0
, and so
0_{\mathcal{L}}\notin\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, if
C_{1}<C_{2}
and
C_{1}
is a circuit, we have
C_{2}\notin\mathcal{C}
, which proves C2. Finally, let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be distinct circuits. By the submodularity of
\rho
, we obtain

\rho(C_{1}\vee C_{2})\leq\rho(C_{1})+\rho(C_{2})-\rho(C_{1}\wedge C_{2})\leq\hgt(C_{1})-1+\hgt(C_{2})-1-\hgt(C_{1}\wedge C_{2})=\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{2})-2.

Since
\rho
is bounded increasing, then

\rho(L)\leq\rho(C_{1}\vee C_{2})\leq\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{2})-2=\hgt(L)-1

for every
L\leq C_{1}\vee C_{2}
with
\hgt(L)=\hgt(C_{1}\vee C_{2})-1
. Hence
L
is dependent, so there exists a minimal dependent element
C_{3}
such that
C_{3}\leq L
. This implies C3.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying C1, C2, and C3. Consider the function
\kappa:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
that associates to an element
L\in\mathcal{L}
the length of a maximal chain of elements in
\mathcal{C}
dominated by
L
. This function is well defined by Lemma
2.26
.
We claim that for
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
we have

\kappa(L_{1})+\kappa(L_{2})\leq\kappa(L_{1}\vee L_{2})+\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L_{2}).

(1)

We prove the claim by induction on
\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})
. If
\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})=0
, then
L_{1}\leq L_{2}
and (
1
) is an equality. If
\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})=\hgt(L_{1}\vee L_{2})-\hgt(L_{2})>0
, then there exists
L<L_{1}\vee L_{2}
with
\hgt(L)=\hgt(L_{1}\vee L_{2})-1
and such that
L_{2}\leq L
. If all the circuits of
L_{1}
are contained in
L_{1}\wedge L
, then we have

\kappa(L_{1})+\kappa(L_{2})=\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L)+\kappa(L_{2})\leq\kappa((L_{1}\wedge L)\vee L_{2})+\kappa((L_{1}\wedge L)\wedge L_{2})\leq\kappa(L_{1}\vee L_{2})+\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L_{2}),

where the second inequality follows from the induction hypothesis, since
L_{1}\nleq L
implies
\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L)-\hgt((L_{1}\wedge L)\wedge L_{2})<\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})
. Assume now that there exists a circuit
\bar{C}\leq L_{1}
such that
\bar{C}\nleq L
. Since
\hgt(L\wedge L_{1})=\hgt(L_{1})+\hgt(L_{1}\vee L_{2})-1-\hgt(L_{1}\vee L_{2})=\hgt(L_{1})-1
, by Lemma
2.24
we have that

\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L_{1}\}=\bigvee\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\leq L_{1}\wedge L\}\vee\bar{C}.

This implies
\kappa(L_{1})=\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L)+1
. Similarly, applying Lemma
2.24
to
L_{1}\vee L_{2}
and
L
, we obtain
\kappa(L_{1}\vee L_{2})=\kappa(L)+1
. Moreover, by modularity we have
(L_{1}\wedge L)\vee L_{2}=L
, hence

\kappa(L_{1})+\kappa(L_{2})=\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L)+\kappa(L_{2})+1\leq\kappa(L)+1+\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})=\kappa(L_{1}\vee L_{2})+\kappa(L_{1}\wedge L_{2}),

where the first inequality follows from the induction hypothesis, since
\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L)-\hgt((L_{1}\wedge L)\wedge L_{2})<\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{1}\wedge L_{2})
.

The function
\rho=\hgt-\kappa
is submodular, since
\kappa
satisfies Equation
1
. Let
L_{2}\leq L_{1}\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\kappa(L_{1})\geq\kappa(L_{2})
, and so
\rho(L_{1})-\rho(L_{2})\leq\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{2})
. Repeated application of Lemma
2.24
yields

\kappa(L_{1})-\kappa(L_{2})\leq\hgt(L_{1})-\hgt(L_{2}),

or equivalently,
\rho(L_{1})\geq\rho(L_{2})
.
Finally, let
C
be a circuit of the
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\hgt-\kappa,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
. By definition,
\kappa(C)>0
, so there exists
\bar{C}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\bar{C}\leq C
. Since
C
is a circuit, for every
L<C
we have
\kappa(L)=0
. We conclude that
C=\bar{C}
, therefore
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Conversely, any
C\in\mathcal{C}
has
\kappa(C)=1
, so
\rho(C)<\hgt(C)
. Moreover, if
L\in\mathcal{L}
and
L<C
, then by C2 there is no
\bar{C}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\bar{C}\leq L
, hence
\kappa(L)=0
, i.e.,
\rho(L)=\hgt(L)
.
∎

Similarly to independent sets, bases, and circuits, many standard concepts in matroid theory - including closure function, flats, and hyperplanes - can be extended to latroids. For instance, one can define the closure operator as follows.

Definition 2.27
.

Let
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
be an
A
-latroid. The
closure
operator
\mathrm{cl}
is defined as

\mathrm{cl}(L)=\bigvee\{\bar{L}\in\mathcal{L}:\rho(L\vee\bar{L})=\rho(L)\},

for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
.

One always has
L\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)
. Moreover, for
L_{1}\leq L_{2}
we have
\mathrm{cl}(L_{1})\leq\mathrm{cl}(L_{2})
. In fact, let
L
be such that
\rho(L\vee L_{1})=\rho(L_{1})
. Then

\begin{split}\rho(L_{2})+\rho(L_{1}\vee L)&\geq\rho(L_{2}\vee(L_{1}\vee L))+\rho(L_{2}\wedge(L_{1}\vee L))\\
&=\rho(L_{2}\vee L)+\rho(L_{1}\vee(L_{2}\wedge L))\geq\rho(L_{2}\vee L)+\rho(L_{1}),\end{split}

where in the equality we use the fact that
\mathcal{L}
is modular. The previous inequality is equivalent to
\rho(L_{2}\vee L)-\rho(L_{2})\leq\rho(L_{1}\vee L)-\rho(L_{1})=0
, so
\rho(L_{2})=\rho(L_{2}\vee L)
.

Definition 2.28
.

Let
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
be an
A
-latroid.
If
L=\mathrm{cl}(L)
, we call
L
a
flat
. A
hyperplane
is a flat
L
such that
\rho(L)=\rho(1_{\mathcal{L}})-1
.

Closure function, flats, and hyperplanes - similarly to independent elements - acquire greater significance in the case of a latroid built on a complemented lattice. By carefully adapting the proofs in
[
6
]
, one can find cryptomorphic definitions of a latroid based on these notions. For cyclic flats we also refer to
[
7
]
.

Proposition 2.29
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. A function
\mathrm{cl}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
is the closure function of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
if and only if

CL1.

L\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)
for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
,

CL2.

if
L_{1}\leq L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\mathrm{cl}(L_{1})\leq\mathrm{cl}(L_{2})
,

CL3.

\mathrm{cl}(L)=\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{cl}(L))
for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
,

CL4.

if
L,J_{1},J_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
with
\hgt(J_{1})=\hgt(J_{2})=1
,
J_{2}\leq\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{1})
, and
J_{2}\nleq\mathrm{cl}(L)
, then
J_{1}\leq\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{2})
.

If the equivalent conditions hold, then the rank function of the latroid with closure function
\mathrm{cl}
is

\rho(L)=\min\{\hgt(M):\mathrm{cl}(M)=\mathrm{cl}(L)\text{ and }M\leq L\}.

Proof.

We already saw in the discussion above that the closure function of a latroid satisfies CL1 and CL2 even in the case that the lattice is not complemented. By Lemma
2.12
we have
\rho(\mathrm{cl}(L))=\rho(L)
. Fix
\bar{L}\in\mathcal{L}
. If
\rho(L\vee\bar{L})=\rho(L)
, then
\bar{L}\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)
. If, instead,
\rho(L\vee\bar{L})>\rho(L)
, then

\rho(\bar{L}\vee\mathrm{cl}(L))\geq\rho(L\vee\bar{L})>\rho(L)=\rho(\mathrm{cl}(L)).

We conclude that
\mathrm{cl}(L)=\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{cl}(L))
. To prove CL4, let
L,J_{1},J_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
be such that
\hgt(J_{1})=\hgt(J_{2})=1
,
J_{2}\leq\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{1})
, and
J_{2}\not\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)
. We obtain
\rho(L\vee J_{1}\vee J_{2})=\rho(L\vee J_{1})\leq\rho(L)+1
. Moreover, since
J_{2}\not\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)
, we have
\rho(L\vee J_{2})=\rho(L)+1
. Combining these two equations we find
\rho(L\vee J_{2})\geq\rho(L\vee J_{1}\vee J_{2})
, and this implies that
J_{1}\in\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{2})
.

Conversely, let
\mathrm{cl}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
be a function satisfying the four axioms in the statement of the proposition. We define the function
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
as

\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)=\min\{\hgt(M):\mathrm{cl}(M)=\mathrm{cl}(L)\text{ and }M\leq L\}.

We claim that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}
is the rank function of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. By Proposition
2.14
it suffices to show that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}
satisfies R1’, R2’, and R3’. By definition, we have
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(0_{\mathcal{L}})=0
.

Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
, let
J\in\mathcal{L}
be an atom. We start by showing that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J)
. We may suppose that
J\not\leq L
. By the definition of
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}
, there exist
H\leq L
,
H^{\prime}\leq L\vee J
such that
\mathrm{cl}(H)=\mathrm{cl}(L)
,
\mathrm{cl}(H^{\prime})=\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J)
,
\hgt(H)=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)
, and
\hgt(H^{\prime})=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J)
. If
H^{\prime}\leq L
, then we conclude. Otherwise, let
\bar{J}
and
\bar{H}
be such that
H^{\prime}=\bar{H}\vee\bar{J}
with
\bar{J}
an atom and
\bar{H}\leq L
. Either
\mathrm{cl}(\bar{H})=\mathrm{cl}(L)
leading to
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)<\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J)
, or there exists an atom
\tilde{J}\leq L
such that
\tilde{J}\nleq\mathrm{cl}(\bar{H})
and
\tilde{J}\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)\leq\mathrm{cl}(\bar{H}\vee\bar{J})
. Then, by CL4 we have
\bar{J}\leq\mathrm{cl}(\bar{H}\vee\tilde{J})
. Hence, by CL2 and CL3 we find
\bar{H}\vee\bar{J}\leq\mathrm{cl}(L)
, which implies

\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J)=\mathrm{cl}(L).

It follows that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J)
.
Moreover, by properties CL1, CL2, and CL3, we have
\mathrm{cl}(H\vee J)\leq\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J)=\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{cl}(H)\vee J)\leq\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{cl}(H\vee J))=\mathrm{cl}(H\vee J)
, where the last inequality follows from
J\leq\mathrm{cl}(J)\leq\mathrm{cl}(H\vee J)
and
\mathrm{cl}(H)\leq\mathrm{cl}(H\vee J)
.
It follows that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J)\leq\hgt(H\vee J)\leq\hgt(H)+1=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)+1
, which completes the proof of R2’.

Let
L
be an element of
\mathcal{L}
and
J_{1},J_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
be atoms such that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J_{1})=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J_{2})
. In the same way as we proved that
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}
is weakly increasing, we can show that
\mathrm{cl}(L)=\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{1})=\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{2})
and, therefore,
\mathrm{cl}(L)=\mathrm{cl}(L\vee J_{1}\vee J_{2})
. By CL4, this implies
\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee J_{1}\vee J_{2})
, proving R3’.

Finally, let
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. We need to verify that

\rho(L)=\min\{\hgt(M):\mathrm{cl}(M)=\mathrm{cl}(L)\text{ and }M\leq L\},

(2)

and

\mathrm{cl}(L)=\bigvee\{M\in\mathcal{L}:\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L\vee M)=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}(L)\}.

(3)

Equation (
2
) follows from the fact that if
M\leq L
and
\rho(M)=\rho(L)
, then
\mathrm{cl}(M)=\mathrm{cl}(L)
, and from the fact that
\rho(L)=\hgt(M)
, where
M
is a maximal independent element among those that are smaller than or equal to
L
. This also implies that
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}
.
Equation (
3
) now follows.
∎

Next, we show that a latroid is determined by its set of flats and describe the closure function of a latroid in terms of its flats.

Proposition 2.30
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. A set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is the set of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
if and only if

F1.

1_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{F}
,

F2.

for every
F_{1}
and
F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
,
F_{1}\wedge F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
,

F3.

for every
F_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
and any atom
J\nleq F_{1}
, there exists a unique element
F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F_{2}
covers
F_{1}
and
J\leq F_{2}
.

If the equivalent conditions hold, then the closure function of the latroid is

\mathrm{cl}(L)=\min\{F\in\mathcal{F}:L\leq F\}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
. Clearly,
1_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{F}
. For every
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, by CL2 we have

\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\wedge F_{2})\leq\mathrm{cl}(F_{1})\wedge\mathrm{cl}(F_{2})=F_{1}\wedge F_{2},

hence, by CL1 we conclude that
F_{1}\wedge F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
. Moreover, for any atom
J\nleq F_{1}
we have
\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee J)\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F_{1}<F_{2}\leq\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee J)
. Then, there exists an atom
\bar{J}\leq F_{2}\leq\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee J)
such that
\bar{J}\nleq F_{1}
. By CL4, we conclude that
J\leq\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee\bar{J})\leq F_{2}
. Therefore
F_{2}=\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee J)
, i.e.,
\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee J)
covers
F_{1}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
satisfies F1, F2 and F3. We define
f:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
as

f(L)=\min\{F\in\mathcal{F}:L\leq F\}.

This function is well-defined, since
\mathcal{F}
is closed under meet and
1_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{F}
. We claim that
f
is a closure function. By the definition,
L\leq f(L)
and
f(f(L))=f(L)
. This proves CL1 and CL3. Let
L_{1}\leq L_{2}
, then
f(L_{2})\geq L_{2}\geq L_{1}
. This implies that
L_{1}\leq f(L_{1})\wedge f(L_{2})
and by F2
f(L_{1})\wedge f(L_{2})\in\mathcal{F}
. By the minimality of
f(L_{1})
we conclude that
f(L_{1})=f(L_{1})\wedge f(L_{2})\leq f(L_{2})
. This shows that
f
satisfies CL2. Finally, consider two distinct atoms
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
, such that
J_{2}\leq f(L\vee J_{1})
and
J_{2}\nleq f(L)
. If
J_{1}\leq f(L)
, then
J_{1}\leq f(L\vee J_{2})
. Else, by F3 we know that both
f(L\vee J_{2})
and
f(L\vee J_{1})
cover
f(L)
. Since
L\vee J_{2}\leq f(L\vee J_{2})\leq f(L\vee J_{1})
, then
f(L\vee J_{2})=f(L\vee J_{1})
, in particular
J_{1}\leq f(L\vee J_{2})
. This proves CL4.

Finally, let
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. We claim that
\mathrm{cl}=f
. Notice that
\mathrm{cl}(L)\leq\mathrm{cl}(f(L))=f(L)
. Therefore, since
\mathrm{cl}(L)\in\mathcal{F}
and by the minimality of
f(L)
, we conclude
\mathrm{cl}(L)=f(L)
. Notice moreover that, by the definition of
f
,
\mathcal{F}=\{L\in\mathcal{L}:f(L)=L\}=f(\mathcal{L})
.
∎

Notice that F1 and F2 imply that the set of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid is a lattice. We conclude this subsection by proving that the lattice of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid is a geometric lattice. This extends similar results for matroids
[
34
]
and
q
-matroids
[
4
,
6
]
.

Theorem 2.31
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite complemented modular lattice with height function
\hgt
. A lattice is geometric if and only if it is the lattice of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

By
[
34
, Theorem 1.7.5]
we know that a lattice is geometric if and only if it is the lattice of flats of a matroid. Since matroids are
\mathbb{Z}
-latroids, it suffices to show that the lattice of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid is geometric. Since there are two lattices involved in this proof, to avoid ambiguities, we will denote by
\wedge_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\vee_{\mathcal{F}}
the operations in
\mathcal{F}
and by
\wedge_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\vee_{\mathcal{L}}
the operations in
\mathcal{L}
. First of all, if
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
F_{2}
covers
F_{1}
, then there exists an atom
J\in\mathcal{L}
,
J\leq F_{2}
such that
J\nleq F_{1}
. Since
F_{1}=\mathrm{cl}(F_{1})
, we have
\rho(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}J)=\rho(F_{1})+1
. Moreover,
\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}J)\leq F_{2}
, and since
F_{2}
covers
F_{1}
, we obtain
\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}J)=F_{2}
. We conclude that
\rho(F_{2})=\rho(F_{1})+1
. This implies that the height of a flat in the lattice
\mathcal{F}
is equal to
\rho(F)
.
It follows from F2 that
F_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2}=F_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{F}}F_{2}
.
Moreover, since
\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2})\in\mathcal{F}
is the smallest element of
\mathcal{F}
that dominates both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, then
F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{F}}F_{2}=\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2})
. Therefore

\begin{split}\rho(F_{1})+\rho(F_{2})&\geq\rho(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2})+\rho(F_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2})=\rho(\mathrm{cl}(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2}))+\rho(F_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{L}}F_{2})\\
&=\rho(F_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{F}}F_{2})+\rho(F_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{F}}F_{2}).\end{split}

Therefore, the height function of
\mathcal{F}
is submodular and
\mathcal{F}
is a semimodular lattice.

To prove that
\mathcal{F}
is atomistic, we proceed by induction on
\rho(F)
. If
\rho(F)=1
, then
F
is an atom in
\mathcal{F}
. Assume that every flat
H
such that
\rho(H)<\rho(F)
is the meet of a finite set of atoms. Let
\bar{F}\in\mathcal{F}
be a flat that is covered by
F
and let
L\in\mathcal{L}
be such that
F=\bar{F}\vee_{\mathcal{L}}L
and
\bar{F}\wedge_{\mathcal{L}}L=0_{\mathcal{L}}
. Since
\rho(\bar{F})+1=\rho(F)\leq\rho(\bar{F})+\rho(L)
, we have
\rho(L)\geq 1
. Let
\bar{L}\leq L
in
\mathcal{L}
be such that
\rho(\bar{L})=1
. Then,
\bar{F}<\bar{F}\vee_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{cl}(\bar{L})\leq F
, as
\bar{L}\leq F
. However, since
F
covers
\bar{F}
, we obtain
F=\bar{F}\vee_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{cl}(\bar{L})
. By the induction hypothesis, both
\bar{F}
and
\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{\bar{L}})
are the meet of a finite set of atoms, hence so is
F
.
∎

Remark 2.32
.

In the previous theorem we proved that the lattice of flats of a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})
is a geometric lattice. Moreover, by
[
34
, Theorem 1.7.5]
we know that a lattice is geometric if and only if it is the lattice of flats of a matroid. This is also true if we restrict to
q
-matroids, i.e., for every
q
-matroid there exists a matroid with the same lattice of flats
[
23
]
. Therefore, one can associate to a
q
-matroid the Stanley-Reisner ideal of the matroid with the same lattice of flats. In the case of the
q
-matroid associated to an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear rank-metric code, the graded Betti numbers of this ideal compute the generalized weights of the code, as shown in
[
24
]
.

Theorem
2.31
allows us to extend the construction to
\mathbb{Z}
-latroids. In fact, one may associate to any
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid the Stanley-Reisner ideal of the matroid with the same lattice of flats, whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem
2.31
. However, one cannot hope that the smallest graded Betti numbers of that ideal in each homological position coincide with the generalized weights of the code, in this generality. One obstruction is that the graded Betti numbers of the ideal in each homological position form a strictly increasing integer sequence, while the generalized weights are only weakly increasing in general. E.g., for
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear rank-metric codes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
, up to
m
consecutive generalized weights may coincide.

2.3
Generalized weights of a latroid

We conclude the section on latroids by defining the generalized weights of a latroid.

Definition 2.33
.

Let
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
be an
A
-latroid and
a\in A
. The
a
-generalized weight

d_{a}(\mathcal{C})
of
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
is

d_{a}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})=\min_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\{\lVert L\rVert:\lVert L\rVert-\rho(L)\geq a\},

where set
d_{a}(\mathcal{C})=0
if the right hand side is empty.

Remark 2.34
.

In Remark
2.8
we show that a matroid
(E,\rho)
and a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid
(\rho,\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{P}(E))
are essentially the same mathematical object. Therefore, we can associate a latroid to a linear block code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
in the same way that we usually associate a matroid to it. We consider the lattice
\mathcal{P}([n])
of subsets of
[n]
and define
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{P}([n])\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
as
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(L)=\lvert L\rvert-\dim(\mathcal{C}(L))
, where
\mathcal{C}(L)
is the largest subcode of
\mathcal{C}
with Hamming support contained in
L
. It is easy to verify that
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{P}([n]))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid. Moreover

d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{P}([n]))=\min_{L\in\mathcal{P}([n])}\{\lvert L\rvert:\dim(\mathcal{C}(L))\geq r\}=\min_{\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\{\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\rvert:\dim(\mathcal{D})\geq r\}=d_{r}(\mathcal{C}),

where the equality in the middle follows from the fact that
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{C}(\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})))
and
\dim(\mathcal{D})\leq\dim(\mathcal{C}(\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})))
for a subcode
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, the generalized weights of the latroid associated to a linear block code are equal to the generalized weights of the code.

In the next proposition, we collect some basic properties of the generalized weights of a latroid.

Proposition 2.35
.

Let
(\rho_{1},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
and
(\rho_{2},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
be
A
-latroids such that
\rho_{2}(L)\leq\rho_{1}(L)
for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
and let
a\in A
be such that
d_{a}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})\neq 0
. Then:

1.

d_{b}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})\leq d_{a}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
if
b\leq a\in A
,

2.

d_{a}(\rho_{1},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})\leq d_{a}(\rho_{2},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
.

Moreover, suppose that
A=\mathbb{Z}
and that
\lVert\cdot\rVert=\hgt
is the height function of a graded lattice. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
b<a
, and suppose that there exists
\bar{L}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\lVert\bar{L}\rVert-\rho(\bar{L})=b
. Then

d_{b}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})<d_{a}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L}).

Proof.

Items 1 and 2 follow directly from the definition of generalized weights. Let
b<a
and suppose that there exists
\bar{L}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\hgt(\bar{L})-\rho(\bar{L})=b
. Let
\tilde{L}\in\mathcal{L}
be such that
d_{a}(\rho,\hgt,\mathcal{L})=\hgt(\tilde{L})
and
\hgt(\tilde{L})-\rho(\tilde{L})\geq a
. Then
\hgt(\tilde{L})\geq\hgt(\bar{L})
, since
\rho
is bounded increasing. If
\hgt(\tilde{L})>\hgt(\bar{L})
, then the thesis follows. Assume therefore that
\hgt(\bar{L})=\hgt(\tilde{L})
. Let
\hat{L}<\tilde{L}
be such that
\hgt(\tilde{L})=\hgt(\hat{L})+1
. Then

b\leq a-1\leq\hgt(\tilde{L})-\rho(\tilde{L})-1\leq\hgt(\hat{L})-\rho(\hat{L}),

since
\rho
is bounded increasing.
Hence
d_{b}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})\leq\lVert\hat{L}\rVert=\lVert\bar{L}\rVert-1
, which proves the thesis.
∎

3
R-linear codes

Let
R
be a finite ring and let
\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
be the set of submodules of
R^{n}
.
In this section, we discuss how to associate a latroid to an
R
-linear code.
We denote by
\mathcal{R}^{n}
the set of rectangular submodules of
R^{n}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}^{n}=\{M=I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{n}\subseteq R^{n}:I_{i}\text{ is an ideal of }R\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}.

Notice that
\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
and
\mathcal{R}^{n}
are complete lattices with respect to the sum and the intersection. For a code
\mathcal{C}
and a strictly increasing modular function
\lVert\cdot\rVert:\mathcal{M}(R^{n})\rightarrow A
, we define
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{M}(R^{n})\rightarrow A
as

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M)=\lVert M\rVert-\lVert M\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert\text{ for all }M\in\mathcal{M}(R^{n}).

In the next proposition, we consider the restriction of
\lVert\cdot\rVert
and of
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
to an arbitrary sublattice of
\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
. To simplify the notation, we do not indicate the domain of the functions whenever it is clear from the context.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a sublattice of
(\mathcal{M}(R^{n}),\subseteq)
, let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
be a code.
The triple
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
is an
A
-latroid. In particular, the triple
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{R}^{n})
is an
A
-latroid.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}\subseteq M_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
. Since
M_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}\leq M_{2}\cap\mathcal{C}

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{2})-\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1})\leq\lVert M_{2}\rVert-\lVert M_{1}\rVert.

Moreover, we have

\begin{split}\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{2})-\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1})&=\lVert M_{2}\rVert-\lVert M_{1}\rVert-\lVert M_{2}\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert+\lVert M_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert=\\
&=\lVert M_{2}\rVert-\lVert M_{1}\rVert+(\lVert M_{1}\rVert+\lVert\mathcal{C}\rVert-\lVert M_{1}+\mathcal{C}\rVert)-(\lVert M_{2}\rVert+\lVert\mathcal{C}\rVert-\lVert M_{2}+\mathcal{C}\rVert)=\\
&=\lVert M_{2}+\mathcal{C}\rVert-\lVert M_{1}+\mathcal{C}\rVert\geq 0,\end{split}

hence
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
is bounded increasing. We claim that
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
is a submodular function. Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
. By the modularity of the function
\lVert\cdot\rVert
, we have

\begin{split}\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(L_{1})+\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(L_{2})&=\lVert L_{1}\rVert+\lVert L_{2}\rVert-\lVert L_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert-\lVert L_{2}\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert=\\
&=\lVert L_{1}+L_{2}\rVert+\lVert L_{1}\cap L_{2}\rVert-(\lVert L_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert+\lVert(L_{1}\cap\mathcal{C})+(L_{2}\cap\mathcal{C})\rVert)=\\
&\geq\lVert L_{1}+L_{2}\rVert+\lVert L_{1}\cap L_{2}\rVert-(\lVert L_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert+\lVert(L_{1}+L_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}\rVert)=\\
&=\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(L_{1}\cap L_{2})+\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(L_{1}+L_{2}),\end{split}

where the inequality follows from
(L_{1}\cap\mathcal{C})+(L_{2}\cap\mathcal{C})\subseteq(L_{1}+L_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}
.
∎

The next example clarifies our reason for explicitly considering
\mathcal{R}^{n}
in the previous proposition.

Example 3.2
.

Let
R
be a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a linear block code. It is well known that the dimension is a modular function from the set of vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
to
\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\dim,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid by Proposition
3.1
. In this case, the rectangular subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are direct products of copies of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\{0\}
. In particular, the rectangular subspaces are in bijection with the subsets of
[n]
. Therefore, we can construct an associated matroid as in Remark
2.8
. This matroid coincides with the matroid that we usually associate to a code endowed with the Hamming metric.

We point out that modular functions and modular supports were defined independently in two different contexts. So, even though they are both called modular, they are not the same class of functions. However, there are situations where modular supports are also modular functions. For instance, we now show that a standard modular support is also a strictly increasing modular function on the lattice
\mathcal{R}^{n}
, if
R
is a principal ideal ring. We start by considering the case where
R
is a finite chain ring.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring, and let
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
be a standard support. Then:

1.

\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\vee\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}+M_{2})
and
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\wedge\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})
for any
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
. In particular
(\{\mathrm{supp}(M):M\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\},\leq)
is a finite lattice.

2.

\mathrm{supp}:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
is a modular function, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})+\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}+M_{2})+\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})
for all
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathrm{supp}
, it follows that
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\vee\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})\leq\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}+M_{2})
. For every
m\in M_{1}+M_{2}
there exist
m_{1}\in M_{1}
and
m_{2}\in M_{2}
, such that
m=m_{1}+m_{2}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{supp}(m)\leq\mathrm{supp}(m_{1})\vee\mathrm{supp}(m_{2})\leq\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\vee\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})
and so
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\vee\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}+M_{2})
.

Since
M_{1}\cap M_{2}\subseteq M_{1}
and
M_{1}\cap M_{2}\subseteq M_{2}
, then
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})\leq\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\wedge\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})
. Fix
i\in[u]
. Since
\mathrm{supp}
is standard, there exist
m_{1}=(0,\dots,0,(m_{1})_{i},0,\dots,0)\in M_{1}
and
m_{2}=(0,\dots,0,(m_{2})_{i},0,\dots,0)\in M_{2}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(m_{1})_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})_{i}
and
\mathrm{supp}(m_{2})_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})_{i}
. Let
\alpha
be a generator of the maximal ideal of
R
. There exist
r_{1},r_{2}
invertible elements and
k_{1},k_{2}
such that
(m_{1})_{i}=r_{1}\alpha^{k_{1}}
and
(m_{2})_{i}=r_{2}\alpha^{k_{2}}
. Assume without loss of generality that
k_{1}\leq k_{2}
. Then
\mathrm{supp}(m_{1})_{i}\geq\mathrm{supp}(m_{2})_{i}
, so
m_{2}=r_{1}^{-1}r_{2}\alpha^{k_{2}-k_{1}}m_{1}\in M_{1}\cap M_{2}
. Hence
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})_{i}\geq\mathrm{supp}(m_{2})_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})_{i}
, therefore
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})_{i}\wedge\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})_{i}
. We conclude, since
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})+\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\wedge\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})+\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})\vee\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal ring and let
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
be a standard modular support. Then
\mathrm{supp}:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
is a modular function.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.11
,
R^{n}=R_{1}^{n}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}^{n}
with
R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell}
finite chain rings and
\mathrm{supp}=\mathrm{supp}_{1}\times\dots\mathrm{supp}_{\ell}
, where
\mathrm{supp}_{i}=R_{i}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u_{i}}
is a standard modular support for all
i\in[\ell]
. If
M\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
, then
M=M_{1}\times\dots\times M_{\ell}
with
M_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}^{n}
, We conclude by applying Lemma
3.3
to each
\mathrm{supp}_{i}
.
∎

While in Lemma
3.3
we do not require the support to be modular, Proposition
3.4
does not hold in general without this assumption, as one can see in the next example.

Example 3.5
.

Consider the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
endowed with the Hamming support and let
M_{1}=(2)
and
M_{2}=(3)
. Then, we obtain
2=\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})+\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})\neq\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}+M_{2})+\mathrm{supp}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})=1
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal ring and let
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
be a standard modular support. The associated weight function is modular on
\mathcal{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

The thesis follows from Proposition
3.4
, since the direct sum of modular functions is modular.
∎

The next example shows that the weight function associated to a modular support is not necessarily modular on
\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
.

Example 3.7
.

Let
R=\mathbb{F}_{2}
and let
\mathrm{supp}
be the Hamming support. Let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2})
,
M_{1}=\langle(1,1)\rangle
and
M_{2}=\langle(0,1)\rangle
. Then
\mathrm{wt}(M_{1})=2
,
\mathrm{wt}(M_{2})=1
,
\mathrm{wt}(M_{1}+M_{2})=2
, and
\mathrm{wt}(M_{1}\cap M_{2})=0
.

Notice that there are standard supports that are not strictly increasing functions. For example, the Hamming support on
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
is not strictly increasing, as
(1)\supsetneq(2)
but
\mathrm{wt}(1)=\mathrm{wt}(2)=1
. However, in the following proposition we show that all standard modular supports on principal ideal rings are strictly increasing.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal ring and let
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
be a standard modular support. Then,
\mathrm{supp}:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
is strictly increasing.

Proof.

As in the case of Proposition
3.4
, it suffices to prove the result for finite chain rings. So, assume that
R
is a finite chain ring with maximal ideal generated by
\alpha
, and let
M_{1}<M_{2}
be two rectangular submodules of
R^{n}
. Then, there exist
i\in[n]
,
m_{1}=(0,\dots,0,\alpha^{t_{1}},0,\dots,0)\in M_{1}
, and
m_{2}=(0,\dots,0,\alpha^{t_{2}},0,\dots,0)\in M_{2}
with
t_{2}<t_{1}
, such that
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(m_{1})_{i}
and
\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(m_{2})_{i}
. Since
m_{1}\in M_{2}
, we have that
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})_{i}\leq\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})_{i}
. Suppose now that they are equal. On the one side, since
\mathrm{supp}
is a modular support, there is
r\in R
such that
\mathrm{supp}((0,\dots,0,\alpha^{t_{2}}-r\alpha^{t_{1}},0,\dots,0))_{i}<\mathrm{supp}((0,\dots,0,\alpha^{t_{2}},0,\dots,0))_{i}
. On the other side, since
R
is a local ring, we have that
1-r\alpha^{t_{2}-t_{1}}
is an invertible element, and so
\mathrm{supp}((0,\dots,0,\alpha^{t_{1}}-r\alpha^{t_{2}},0,\dots,0))_{i}=\mathrm{supp}((0,\dots,0,\alpha^{t_{1}},0,\dots,0))_{i}
. This is a contradiction, and therefore we conclude that
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})_{i}<\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})_{i}
, and so
\mathrm{supp}(M_{1})<\mathrm{supp}(M_{2})
.
∎

A standard modular support on a principal ideal ring defines a strictly increasing modular function on
\mathcal{R}^{n}

by Proposition
3.4
and Proposition
3.8
. However, the same support is not in general a modular function on
\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
. In particular, if we define
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
as
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M)=\mathrm{supp}(M)-\mathrm{supp}(M\cap\mathcal{C})
for
M\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
, then the triple
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\mathrm{supp},\mathcal{R}^{n})
may not be a
\mathbb{Z}^{u}
-latroid. However, given a standard modular support, we can construct a
\mathbb{Z}^{u}
-latroid as follows.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\mathrm{supp}
be a standard modular support and define

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{supp}}:\mathcal{M}(R^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
as

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{supp}}(M)=\mathrm{supp}(M)-\mathrm{supp}\left(\min\{L\in\mathcal{R}^{n}:M\cap\mathcal{C}\subseteq L\}\right)

for
M\in\mathcal{M}(R^{n})
.

Let
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
be the smallest rectangular submodule that contains
\mathcal{C}
. It is easy to verify that
\bar{\mathcal{C}}=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{C})\times\dots\times\pi_{n}(\mathcal{C})
, where
\pi_{i}
is the canonical projection on the
i
-th entry, and that
M\cap\bar{\mathcal{C}}=\min\{L\in\mathcal{R}^{n}:M\cap\mathcal{C}\subseteq L\}
. Following the proof of Proposition
3.1
, one can prove that
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{supp}},\mathrm{supp},\mathcal{R}^{n})
is a latroid.

3.1
Chain support, Tutte polynomial, and weight distribution

The weight enumerator is a central and widely studied invariant in coding theory
[
40
, Chapter VI]
. It captures many interesting properties of a code, e.g., it may be used to better understand the decoding properties of the code. For instance, the weight enumerator of a binary code allows us to estimate the probability that a received codeword is closer to a different codeword compared to the actual transmitted codeword
[
29
, Section 3]
.
In this section, we propose a finer definition of weight enumerator for linear codes over rings. For an
R
-linear code endowed with the chain support (see Definition
1.13
), we show how to recover the weight enumerator from a suitable associated latroid.

Definition 3.10
.

The
homogeneous weight enumerator
of an
R
-linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq R^{n}
is the
polynomial

W_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x^{\mathrm{wt}(c)}y^{\mathrm{wt}(R^{n})-\mathrm{wt}(c)}.

The homogeneous weight enumerator can also be written as

W_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)=\sum_{w=0}^{\mathrm{wt}(R^{n})}A_{w}x^{w}y^{\mathrm{wt}(R^{n})-w},

where

A_{w}=\lvert\{c\in\mathcal{C}:\mathrm{wt}(c)=w\}\rvert.

The list
A_{0},\dots,A_{\mathrm{wt}(R^{n})}
is called the
weight distribution
of
\mathcal{C}
and is an invariant of the code. Notice that, if
R
is a field and the weight is the Hamming weight, we have
\mathrm{wt}(R^{n})=n
and we recover the classical definition of weight enumerator. In the more general case of standard support, it is important to keep track of what happens in each component. For this reason, we introduce a finer version of the weight enumerator, which we call support enumerator.

Definition 3.11
.

For a support
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
, we define the
support enumerator
as

W_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{supp}(c)}\mathbf{y}^{\overline{\mathrm{supp}(c)}}.

where
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{supp}(c)}=\prod_{i=1}^{u}x_{i}^{\mathrm{supp}(c)_{i}}
and
\overline{\mathrm{supp}(c)}=\mathrm{supp}(R^{n})-\mathrm{supp}(c)
.

One can recover the homogeneous weight enumerator from the support enumerator by setting
x_{1}=\dots=x_{u}=x
and
y_{1}=\dots=y_{u}=y
. The next lemma follows by direct computation.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
R=R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{\ell}
be a principal ideal ring,
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
be an
R
-linear code, and
\mathrm{supp}=\mathrm{supp}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathrm{supp}_{\ell}
be a modular support. Then

W_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{\ell})=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}W_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}).

In addition to the weight enumerator, we are also interested in the generalized weight enumerator.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
R
-linear code. For
0\leq r\leq\lambda(\mathcal{C})
, the
r
-th generalized weight enumerator
is

W^{(r)}_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)=\sum_{r=0}^{M(\mathcal{C})}A^{(r)}_{w}x^{\mathrm{wt}(R^{n})-w}y^{w},

where
A^{(r)}_{w}=\lvert\{\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}:\lambda(\mathcal{D})=r\text{ and }\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{D})=w\}\lvert
.

Similarly to how the weight enumerator captures the weight distribution, the generalized weight enumerator captures the generalized weights. Indeed, we have

\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\min\{w:A^{(j)}_{w}\neq 0\text{ for some }j\geq r\}

for
1\leq r\leq\lambda(\mathcal{C})
.

The
Tutte polynomial
was introduced in
[
38
,
39
]
for graphs and the definition was generalized to matroids in
[
11
]
. For a matroid
(E,\rho)
it is defined as

T(\rho,x,y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(x-1)^{\rho(E)-\rho(A)}(y-1)^{\lvert A\rvert-\rho(A)}.

The
Tutte-Whitney rank generating function
is obtained from the Tutte polynomial via a change of variables

R(\rho,x,y)=T(\rho,x+1,y+1)=\sum_{A\subseteq E}x^{\rho(E)-\rho(A)}y^{\lvert A\rvert-\rho(A)}.

In
[
41
]
, Vertigan extends the definition of Tutte-Whitney rank generating function to latroids as follows.

Definition 3.14
.

The
weighted Tutte-Whitney rank generating function
of a
\mathbb{Z}^{u}
-latroid
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
with
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{u}
is

R(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{x}^{M}\mathbf{y}^{M^{\perp}}\mathbf{u}^{\rho(1_{\mathcal{L}})-\rho(M)}\mathbf{v}^{\lVert M\rVert-\rho(M)}.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is a sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{u}
, we have
M^{\perp}=\overline{M}=1_{\mathcal{L}}-M
. Moreover, observe that the weighted Tutte-Whitney rank generating function fully determines the function

R^{\prime}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{x}^{M}\mathbf{z}^{\tilde{M}-M}\mathbf{y}^{M^{\perp}}\mathbf{u}^{\rho(1_{\mathcal{L}})-\rho(M)}\mathbf{v}^{\lVert M\rVert-\rho(M)},

where

. Notice that when
\mathcal{L}=\{0,1\}^{n}
, then
\tilde{M}=(1,\dots,1)
. Conversely, (

) determines the weighted Tutte-Whitney rank generating function via

R(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=R^{\prime}(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}).

Given an
R
-linear code

Lemma 3.15
.

Let

be a finite chain ring, and let
\mathcal{C}
be an

-linear code. Let

\mathcal{L}_{R}=\{\mathrm{supp}(M):M\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\}

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
,
\mathcal{L}_{R}
is a sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Let

be a generator of the maximal ideal of

and let

. Then

Therefore,

is in one-to-one correspondence with

, so
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
is well defined. Moreover,
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(M)=\lambda(M)-\lambda(M\cap\mathcal{C})

Definition 3.16
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring, and let

Remark 3.17
.

For

, let

.
By Lemma
3.15
, the
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid

defined by Vertigan in

, Example 5.8]
and the chain support latroid

may be identified via the bijection

given by

. In fact,

.

The generalized weights of an
R
-linear code
\mathcal{C}
according to Definition

coincide with the generalized weights of its chain support latroid.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring, and let
\mathcal{C}
be an
R
-linear code. Then

\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})=d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{L}_{R}),

for
1\leq r\leq\lambda(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

By definition and using Remark
3.17
we have

d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{L}_{R})=\min_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}}\{\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\rvert:\lambda(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{C})\geq r\}\leq\min_{\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\{\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\rvert:\lambda(\mathcal{D})\geq r\}=\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C}).

To prove the reverse inequality, let
\mathcal{D}
be a submodule of
\mathcal{C}
that realizes
\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
has
\lambda(\mathcal{D})\geq r
and
\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\rvert
. Let
\bar{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
be the smallest element that contains
\mathcal{D}
. Then
\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})\rvert
and
\lambda(\bar{\mathcal{D}})\geq\lambda(\mathcal{D})
. Therefore,

\bar{d}_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\lvert\mathrm{supp}(D)\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{supp}(\bar{D})\rvert\geq d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lvert\cdot\rvert,\mathcal{L}_{R}).\qed

In the next lemma we recall a fact from commutative algebra, that we will use in the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring, and let
M
be a finitely generated
R
module. Then

\lvert M\rvert=\lvert R/(\alpha)\rvert^{\lambda(M)}.

Proof.

By the definition of length of a module, there exists a chain of modules

M=M_{0}\supsetneq M_{1}\supsetneq\dots\supsetneq M_{\lambda(M)},

that is a composition series, i.e.,
M_{i}/M_{i+1}
is a nonzero simple
R
-module for

, see
[
12
, Theorem 2.13]
. A simple

-module is isomorphic to

, where

is a maximal ideal of
R
. Since

is a local ring, we conclude that
M_{i}/M_{i+1}\cong R/(\alpha)
for
0\leq i<\lambda(M)
. We conclude by induction on the length of the composition series.
∎

3.3
Monomial ideals

Given a modular support
\mathrm{supp}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{u}
we can associate to each nonzero

-linear code
\mathcal{C}
a monomial ideal

defined as

I_{\mathcal{C}}=(\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{supp}(c)}:c\in\mathcal{C}\setminus 0\})=(\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{supp}(c)}:c\in\mathrm{Min}(\mathcal{C})\})\subseteq S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{u}],

The ideal

\{\mathrm{supp}(c):c\in\mathrm{Min}(\mathcal{C})\}=\{\mathrm{supp}(M):\mathrm{supp}(M)-\rho_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{supp}}(M)>0\text{ with }M\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\text{ and }M\text{ minimal}\}.

R
be a principal ideal ring and let

.

From the ideal

one can recover some information on the code
\mathcal{C}
. For instance, in
[

the authors proved that the graded Betti numbers of the monomial ideal associated to a code determine its generalized weights according to Definition

.

[
25

the authors proved that the weight enumerator of an

-graded Betti numbers associated with the

-graded minimal free resolutions of the ideal of

and of its elongations, see
[
25
, Section 2]
for a definition. Here, we prove that the weight enumerator of an

and where

\beta_{i,X}
the rank of the free module

in homological position

.

By the inclusion–exclusion principle we obtain

where

. The dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{y}

\mathbb{Z}_{4}^{3}
given by

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\langle(2,1,0),(2,0,1)\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}\text{ and }\mathcal{C}_{2}=\langle(2,1,0),(0,0,1)\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}.

With respect to the chain support, we obtain
I_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}=I_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}=(y,z)\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x,y,z]
. However, we have that
W_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}(x,1)=1+2x+x^{2}+4x^{3}+8x^{4}
and
W_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}(x,1)=1+2x+3x^{2}+5x^{3}+5x^{4}
.

Determining whether it is possible to associate to an
R
-linear code a more complex ideal that allows to recover the weight enumerator remains an open problem. See Remark
2.32
for an obstacle to recovering the generalized weights of a rank-metric code from an ideal associated to the latroid.

4
Some families of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear codes

In this section, we discuss some interesting families of codes that can be studied effectively using our approach.

4.1
Rank-metric codes

We start by recalling the definition of
q
-polymatroid. Notice that when the function
\rho
is integer-valued, the definition recovers that of
q
-matroid.

Definition 4.1
.

A
q
-polymatroid is a pair
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\rho)
where
\rho:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function such that:

P1.

0\leq\rho(V)\leq\dim(V)
for any
V\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
,

P2.

\rho(V_{1})\leq\rho(V_{2})
for
V_{1}\leq V_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
,

P3.

\rho(V_{1}+V_{2})+\rho(V_{1}\cap V_{2})\leq\rho(V_{1})+\rho(V_{2})
for
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
.

Let
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\rho)
be a
q
-polymatroid. Clearly, the dimension function is modular and strictly increasing, and
\dim(0)=\rho(0)=0
. Moreover, P2 implies that
0\leq\rho(V_{2})-\rho(V_{1})
for
V_{1}\leq V_{2}
. On the other hand, since
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is relatively complemented, there exists
V_{3}\leq V_{2}
such that
V_{1}\cap V_{3}=0
,
V_{1}+V_{3}=V_{2}
. By the submodularity of
\rho
we obtain
\rho(V_{1})+\rho(V_{3})\geq\rho(V_{2})
, hence

\dim(V_{2})-\dim(V_{1})=\dim(V_{3})\geq\rho(V_{3})\geq\rho(V_{2})-\rho(V_{1}).

Therefore,
\rho
is bounded increasing with respect to the dimension. We conclude that any
q
-polymatroid can be regarded as an
\mathbb{R}
-latroid
(\rho,\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
. Conversely, it is clear that an
\mathbb{R}
-latroid
(\rho,\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
is also a
q
-polymatroid.

Now we show how to associate a latroid to a rank-metric code
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
. We denote by
\mathrm{rowsp}(\mathcal{C})
the space generated by all rows of all matrices in
\mathcal{C}
and by
\mathcal{C}(V)
the largest subcode of
\mathcal{C}
, whose rowspace is contained in
V\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
. We define the function
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(V)=m\dim(V)-\dim(\mathcal{C}(V)).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
be a rank-metric code. The triple
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},m\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid.

Proof.

The axioms L1, L2, and L3 are trivially satisfied. For every
V_{1}\leq V_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
we have
\mathcal{C}(V_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(V_{2})
, hence

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{2})-\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{1})\leq m(\dim(V_{2})-\dim(V_{1}))
. Moreover, it is not hard to prove that
\dim(\mathcal{C}(V_{2}))\leq\dim(\mathcal{C}(V_{1}))+m(\dim(V_{2})-\dim(V_{1}))
, which implies that
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{2})-\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{1})\geq 0
. Hence, L4 is satisfied. Since
\mathcal{C}(V_{1})+\mathcal{C}(V_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(V_{1}+V_{2})
and
\mathcal{C}(V_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}(V_{2})=\mathcal{C}(V_{1}\cap V_{2})
, we have that the function
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
is submodular.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

In
[
17
]
the authors associate to a rank-metric code
\mathcal{C}
the
q
-polymatroid
(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
, where
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
is defined as

\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}}(V)=\frac{\dim(\mathcal{C})-\dim(C(V^{\perp}))}{m}.

Since
(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
is a
q
-polymatroid, then
(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}},\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-latroid. Although the latroid
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
of Proposition
4.2
and
(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}},\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
are different latroids, they express the same information. In fact,

\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}}(V)=\frac{\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(V^{\perp})-m\dim(V^{\perp})+\dim(C)}{m}.

However, we find
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}
to be a more natural choice. For example, the independent elements of
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},m\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
are the spaces
V
for which there are no elements of
\mathcal{C}
whose rowspace is contained in
V
. Moreover, the circuits of
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},m\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
correspond to minimal supports in
\mathcal{C}
.

The next proposition shows that the generalized rank weights of a rank-metric code are determined by those of the latroid associated to it. This is not surprising, given Remark
4.3
and the fact that
[
17
, Theorem 7.1]
shows that the generalized rank weights of a rank metric code
\mathcal{C}
are determined by
(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}},\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
.
We refer to
[
16
, Section 11.5]
for the definition of generalized rank weights.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
m\geq n
be positive integers. If
m>n
, then the generalized rank weights of a rank-metric code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
are equal to the generalized weights of the associated latroid of Proposition
4.2
multiplied by
m
, i.e.,

md_{r}(\mathcal{C})=d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},m\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})).

If
m=n
, then

nd_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\min\{d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},n\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})),d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}^{t}},n\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))\}.

Proof.

For
m>n
we have that

\begin{split}d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},m\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))&=\min_{V\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})}\{m\dim(L):\dim(\mathcal{C}(L))\geq r\}=\\
&=\min\{\dim(\mathcal{A}):\mathcal{A}\text{ is an optimal anticode and}\dim(\mathcal{C}\cap A)\geq r\}=md_{r}(\mathcal{C}),\end{split}

where the equality in the middle is due to the fact that every optimal anticode
\mathcal{A}
is uniquely determined by its rowspace and
\dim(\mathcal{A})=m\dim(\mathrm{rowsp}(\mathcal{A}))
. The proof for
m=n
follows from similar arguments.
∎

4.2
Sum-rank metric codes

In
[
35
, Definition 41]
the authors introduced the concept of sum matroid in order to associate a combinatorial object to
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear sum-rank metric codes. Using latroids, we can extend their ideas to arbitrary sum-rank metric codes.

Given a sum-rank metric code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
, define
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(L)=\lVert L\rVert-\dim(\mathcal{C}(L)),

where
\mathcal{C}(L)
is the set of codewords of
\mathcal{C}
with columnspace contained in
L=(V_{1},\dots,V_{\ell})
and
\lVert L\rVert=\sum_{i+1}^{\ell}m_{i}\dim(V_{i})
. Proceeding as in Proposition
4.2
, one can prove the following.

Proposition 4.5
.

The triple
(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid.

The next proposition makes the relation between the
\mathbb{Q}
-latroids of a sequence of rank metric codes and the
\mathbb{Z}
-latroid of their direct product explicit.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
be a sum rank-metric code. If
\mathcal{C}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{C}_{i}
, then

(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},\dim,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}})),

where
m(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
denotes the direct dum of the latroid
(\rho,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})
with itself
m
times.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{C}_{i}
, then we have
\mathcal{C}(L)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{C}_{i}(L_{i})
. So, we obtain
\rho_{\mathcal{C}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}\dim(\cdot)
. We conclude by applying the definition of direct sum.
∎

The generalized weights of a sum-rank metric code are determined by the associated latroid, but not by the generalized weights of the associated latroid. In fact, given a sum-rank metric code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
, we have

\begin{split}&d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})=\{\lVert L\rVert:\dim(\mathcal{C}(L))\geq r\}=\\
&=\{\dim(\mathcal{A}):\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\mbox{ where }\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}\mbox{are o.a. and}\dim(\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A})\geq r\},\end{split}

while following
[
9
, Definition VI.1]
we obtain

d_{r}(\mathcal{C})=\{\mathrm{maxsrk}(\mathcal{A}):\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\mbox{ where }\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}\mbox{are o.a. and}\dim(\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A})\geq r\}.

As often happens for sum-rank metric codes, we can prove an equality in the case where all the
m_{i}
’s are equal. The proof is analogous to that of Proposition
4.4
.

Proposition 4.7
.

If
m=m_{1}=\dots=m_{\ell}
and
m_{i}>n_{i}
for all
i\in[\ell]
, then the generalized sum-rank weights of a sum-rank metric code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
are equal to the generalized weights of the associated latroid of Proposition
4.5
multiplied by
m
, i.e.,

d_{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}},\lVert\cdot\rVert,\mathcal{L})=md_{r}(\mathcal{C}).
