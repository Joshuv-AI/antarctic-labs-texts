Engaging patients in AI governance
Authors: Michelle M Mello, Sudip Nag, Chiquita T Tuttle, Danton Char
Published: 2026

Healthcare organisations worldwide face pressure to ensure their artificial intelligence (AI) tools are effective, safe and equitable. Although patients are the primary intended beneficiaries of AI governance efforts, they are largely excluded from governance processes in US healthcare organisations, which are dominated by clinicians, data scientists and ethicists.1 2 Our experience engaging and participating as patient representatives in AI governance at Stanford Health Care3 demonstrates that, notwithstanding the technical complexities of AI governance, patient engagement is feasible and valuable.

The rationales for engaging patients are compelling. This helps ensure that AI tools align with patients’ priorities rather than experts’ assumptions. Well-intentioned professionals may misrepresent patients’ interests, particularly given the limited evidence on patients’ perspectives. Additionally, patients are most vulnerable to harm from poorly governed AI systems. Finally, involving patients may help build trust. 4 5

Organisations may hesitate to engage patients due to several perceived barriers, all of which we believe are surmountable. AI is technologically complex, and patients often lack familiarity with it. AI governance processes must be fast to be useful, but meaningful patient engagement traditionally requires extended relationship building. Engaging diverse patients is ideal, but practical constraints necessitate working with small groups.

Guided by several foundational principles (table 1), we recruited 14 patients into a long-term learning community called the Patient Partner Panel (PPP) that helps conduct ethical assessments of AI tools.6 Members receive AI fundamentals training and ongoing remote learning opportunities. In monthly focus groups, members weigh ethical issues with AI use cases. Discussions are guided by plain-language tool ‘explainers’ and questions exploring patients’ values and concerns. Ethicists thematically analyse transcripts and, after synthesising findings from interviews with clinicians and AI developers, generate recommendations for health system leaders.3 7 Our process was designed to be practicable at lower-resourced organisations: it requires no scientific expertise beyond interviewing and thematic analysis and can be completed within 30 days by two investigators and an administrative support person.

A year in, our experience is that patients provide valuable, actionable insights about responsible AI use—without compromising the timeliness of assessments. Individuals with extensive care experience in our hospital can point out problems in clinical workflows in which AI is being deployed. For example, patients assessing a readmission predictor opined that multiple problems could explain the hospital’s high readmission rate but would not be addressed by the AI tool. Even patients without technical backgrounds, if provided with AI education, can contribute salient insights. To facilitate this, we recommend developing ‘explainers’ of each AI tool and cultivating multiple forms of diversity within a patient panel. In our PPP, diversity in professional backgrounds and care experiences has been especially generative.

We have learnt that patients’ preferences often diverge from those of AI experts and clinicians. Where stakeholders’ values are misaligned, ethical problems may be especially likely to arise.8 One area where we observe misalignment is whether AI use cases should be disclosed to patients: ethicists usually say yes, but patients perceive that in many circumstances disclosures would feel burdensome and serve no purpose. We have also observed clinicians and AI developers emphasising purported benefits of AI tools for patients that patients themselves did not particularly value—eg, one tool sought to reduce preoperative blood tests that patients deemed unburdensome. Another misalignment is stakeholders’ tolerance for false positives and false negatives in predictive algorithms: clinicians and developers often prefer to minimise false positives while patients consistently emphasise avoiding false negatives. Lastly, patients often demonstrate a greater willingness than experts to question assumptions about the need for AI solutions. When evaluating a tool to identify patients who could benefit from a hospice discussion, for example, patients pushed the proposers to explain why better communication among physicians was not the preferred approach.

Finally, cultivating a learning community strengthens patient engagement. Although there are more time-efficient ways of including patients in AI governance (eg, adding a patient representative to an oversight committee), focus groups enable patients to learn and revise their initial impressions by deliberating alongside colleagues with different perspectives. Building a standing panel is beneficial because patients’ contributions strengthen over time as they gain experience. These findings comport with other work exploring patients’ preferences about optimal forms of engagement in AI governance.2 We also recommend communicating regularly with PPP members about the impact of the work they are doing. Patients are eager to know how their contributions shaped the health system’s decisions. Sharing opportunities to participate in thought leadership activities and instances where patients’ insights have led to policy changes also adds meaning to the work.

In summary, including patients in AI governance is an ethical imperative and a necessity for ensuring that AI serves patients’ needs and garners trust. Responsible innovation demands that those who bear the greatest risk are given a voice in the decisions that affect them. The path forward is clear: we must move patients from the margins to the centre of AI governance.
