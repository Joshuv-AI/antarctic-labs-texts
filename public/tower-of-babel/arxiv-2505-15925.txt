VERDI: VLM-Embedded Reasoning for Autonomous Driving
Bowen Feng * 2 , Zhiting Mei * 2 , Julian Ost 2 , Filippo Ghilotti 3 , Baiang Li 2 , Roger Girgis 3 , Anirudha Majumdar 2 , Felix Heide 2 3

Abstract
While autonomous driving (AD) stacks struggle with decision making under partial observability and real-world complexity, human drivers are capable of applying commonsense reasoning to make near-optimal decisions with limited information. Recent work has attempted to leverage finetuned Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for trajectory planning at inference time to emulate human behavior. Despite their success in benchmark evaluations, these methods are often impractical to deploy (a 70B parameter VLM inference at merely 8 tokens per second requires more than 160G of memory), and their monolithic network structure prohibits safety decomposition. To bridge this gap, we propose V LM- E mbedded R easoning for autonomous D r I ving ( VERDI ), a training-time framework that distills the reasoning process and commonsense knowledge of VLMs into the AD stack. VERDI augments modular differentiable end-to-end (e2e) AD models by aligning intermediate module outputs at the perception, prediction, and planning stages with text features explaining the driving reasoning process produced by VLMs. By encouraging alignment in latent space, VERDI enables the modular AD stack to internalize structured reasoning, without incurring the inference-time costs of large VLMs. We evaluate VERDI in both open-loop and closed-loop settings. Our method outperforms existing end-to-end approaches without embedded reasoning by up to 11% in ℓ 2 \ell_{2} distance, and achieves the best overall driving performance in the closed-loop HugSim simulator, including a 10% improvement in Non-Collision Rate, while maintaining fast inference speed.

I
Introduction

Real-world autonomous vehicle deployments have drastically increased in the last three years
[
di2024comparative
,
jung2024safety
,
chen2022learning
,
liang2022effective
,
zeng2019end
,
zhang2022beverse
,
zhang2023learning
]
. However, a major bottleneck to achieving safe and reliable driving arises from the complexity of real-world environments. Dynamic agents, including pedestrians, cyclists, and human-driven vehicles, along with their interactions, present significant challenges for autonomous vehicles to perceive, predict, and plan. Decision-making under semantic ambiguity, partial observability, and unforeseen so-called edge case scenarios remain a critical challenge for autonomous systems
[
zhang2024oatracker
]
.

Highly performant autonomous driving (AD) stacks rely on modularized differentiable end-to-end (e2e) architectures
[
hu2023planning
,
jiang2023vad
,
chen2024vadv2
,
li2024ego
]
. Although existing methods are successful in benchmark evaluations, they are often limited to the training datasets consisting of human driving trajectories that lack access to the underlying reasoning processes of the driver
[
caesar2020nuscenes
,
sun2020scalability
]
. As such, conventional supervised e2e models lack semantic understanding of the driving process, which hinders their real-world applications in unseen scenarios
[
hwang2024emma
]
.
On the other hand, human drivers respond to challenging decisions and navigate through ambiguities by interpreting visual context and reasoning. In addition, they leverage commonsense knowledge and prior experience to make satisfactory decisions even with incomplete information
[
simon1990bounded
,
jones1999bounded
]
. This motivates the research question:

How can modular AD models leverage human-like decision-making processes with reasoning and commonsense knowledge when confronted with the real-time challenges of driving?

A growing body of work investigates Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in driving tasks for reasoning
[
hwang_emma_2024
,
xing_openemma_2024
,
chen2024driving
,
xu2024drivegpt4
,
wang2024omnidrive
,
sima2024drivelm
,
wang2023drivemlm
]
. Specifically, researchers have augmented AD datasets with VLM annotations to incorporate scene descriptions and explanations
[
tian2024drivevlm
,
wang2023drivemlm
,
sima2024drivelm
]
, questions and answers
[
wang2024omnidrive
,
chen2024driving
]
, and driving rationales
[
hwang2024emma
,
xing_openemma_2024
,
sima2024drivelm
]
. The augmented datasets are then used to train or fine-tune VLMs to be multi-modal foundation models that are capable of generating future behaviors for autonomous vehicles, along with natural language explanations for interpretability.
However, querying fine-tuned multi-modal VLMs at inference time could be prohibitively slow. For example, on an OrinX chip, a 4B Qwen model can decode a 1,078-token prompt at 44.5 tokens per second
[
tian2024drivevlm
]
. This latency renders them impractical for real-world safety-critical scenarios
[
hwang2024emma
]
.
To circumvent this, prior work resorts to hierarchical planning and control, using small VLMs, or optimizing the model
[
tian2024drivevlm
]
.
Moreover, many existing VLM methods forgo multi-modal perception and prediction modules and directly output actions from input queries, making safety decomposition a challenge and allowing for more VLM hallucinations at inference time; we illustrate an instance of this in Appendix
A
.

Fig. 1
:
Overview of
VERDI
.
Our pipeline aligns the VLM reasoning module with our e2e driving model. During training, the ground truth (GT) trajectory and observed images are provided to the VLM for it to explain the reasoning throughout perception, prediction, and planning during the driving process. The VLM’s answers to each submodule is aligned with the corresponding submodule outputs from the e2e driving model, effectively distilling VLM knowledge and reasoning into the e2e model. During inference time, the e2e model plans future trajectory with embedded reasoning process, without having to query the VLM (pink arrow).

We introduce VLM-Embedded Reasoning for autonomous DrIving (
VERDI
) as an approach for
distilling latent reasoning capabilities from VLMs; Figure
1
provides an overview of
VERDI
.
During training, the method operates on two parallel models for a given trajectory: (1) a differentiable e2e driving model that processes current ego state and corresponding sensor inputs (multi-view images) to generate future trajectories, and (2) a latent reasoning pipeline that extracts the reasoning process of the ground-truth future trajectory by querying a VLM. The driving pipeline sequentially handles perception, prediction, and planning subtasks through the differentiable modules. In this work, we choose to build upon VAD
[
jiang2023vad
]
to create our e2e driving pipeline. The reasoning pipeline prompts the VLM using chain-of-thought
[
wei2022chain
]
for each subtask component.
To distill the reasoning capabilities and commonsense knowledge from VLMs to the driving model,
VERDI
performs latent feature alignment between the driving and reasoning pipelines. This alignment process aims to match the driving model latent embeddings with the embeddings of the reasoning process, facilitating the transfer of semantic reasoning from large VLMs to real-time driving model.
At inference time, the reasoning pipeline is no longer required, since it has been effectively distilled into the driving pipeline. This enables
VERDI
to perform real-time inference while preserving human-like reasoning abilities.

Our main contributions are three-fold:
(1) we develop a modular, differentiable, end-to-end autonomous driving (AD) model featuring latent planning,
(2) we introduce a method to align individual driving subtasks with corresponding natural language in the latent space, and
(3)
we evaluate our framework on both open-loop and closed-loop benchmarks, including nuScenes
[
caesar2020nuscenes
]
and HugSim
[
zhou2024hugsim
]
driving simulator. In open-loop settings,
VERDI
outperforms baseline methods in
\ell_{2}
planning distance by 11% on the nuScenes. In closed-loop,
VERDI
exhibits the highest driving score among all baseline methods and further improves 10% over the direct baseline on non-collision rate. Further, we confirm through a series of ablation experiments that our method has successfully distilled VLM reasoning processes in each submodule, and elicits qualitative improvements in driving behavior.

II
Related Work

Modularized End to End Autonomous Driving
.
Although autonomous driving stacks were built modular for a long time, end-to-end optimization is a novel problem
[
chen2024end
,
chib2023recent
]
. These emerging approaches minimize information loss incurred in independently optimized modules, where errors accumulate and compound throughout the perception, prediction, and planning processes.
However, attempts to condense the driving task into single monolithic perception to planning end-to-end models lack the level of interpretability and generalizability desired by AD applications, leading to the rise of jointly trainable end-to-end frameworks with intermediate outputs
[
karkus2023diffstack
,
hu2023_uniad
,
jiang2023vad
,
chen2024vadv2
]
.
Driving innovations arise from advances in perception
[
shao2023safety
,
yang2024visual
]
, prediction
[
yang2024behavior
,
girgis2021latent
]
, and planning
[
renz2022plant
,
biswas2024quad
]
modules, while differentiable losses spanning the entire pipeline, exemplified by UniAD
[
hu2023planning
]
and SY-P3
[
hu2022st
]
, serve to connect and unify these components. Improvements in the representation space covering vectorized encoding strategies in VAD
[
jiang2023vad
,
chen2024vadv2
]
, splitting dynamic and static environments
[
DualAD
]
, parallelization
[
weng2024drive
]
and inter modular dependencies as the ego-status
[
li2024ego
]
have further enhanced performance.
However, these models primarily rely on supervised learning from human driving demonstration datasets
[
caesar2020nuscenes
]
, lacking human reasoning capabilities significantly restricts their effectiveness in challenging driving scenarios.

Vision Language Models (VLMs) in Autonomous Driving
are of great interest as they provide
advanced reasoning capabilities, and rich semantic scene understandings. Specifically, methods such as DriveGPT4
[
xu2024drivegpt4
]
, OmniDrive
[
wang2024omnidrive
]
, Driving with LLM
[
chen2024driving
]
, DriveMLM
[
sima2024drivelm
,
wang2023drivemlm
]
enrich existing datasets with fine-grained annotations and fine-tune or train VLMs to reason about the scene through textual responses and predict higher-order actions. EMMA
[
hwang2024emma
]
pushes this further by a full integration of perception, road graphs, and control trajectories in an end-to-end fashion, achieving state-of-the art performance.
However, large VLMs cause slow inference and deployment hurdles, forcing compromises, e.g. restricting them to low‐frequency planning while an end-to-end model handles high-frequency controls or capping the size below 4B parameters
[
tian2024drivevlm
]
, which degrades overall performance.
Two recent closed-source models, VLP
[
pan2024vlp
]
and VLM-AD
[
xu2024vlm
]
, explore use of VLM during training time with distinctively different approaches. VLP uses language models only for embedding rigid textual descriptions to vectors, disregarding reasoning capabilities in the LM itself. VLM-AD only supervises planning outcome with VLM text annotations, while
VERDI
distills the
reasoning
process and commonsense to e2e submodules, validated in our ablation experiments in Sec.
IV-C
.
Our approach harnesses language reasoning across perception, prediction, and planning, directly integrating it into the e2e pipeline, and does not require a VLM at runtime. Alpamayo
[
wang2025alpamayo
]
is another recent reasoning-based end-to-end driving VLA trained on human driving demonstrations augmented with reasoning annotations, providing a strong baseline for comparison. In contrast to Alpamayo’s VLA architecture,
VERDI
adopts a differentiable modular model in which driving reasoning capabilities are distilled from a VLM during training. This design enables model-based driving with more than 10
\times
faster inference while maintaining strong performance. We validate this comparison on the HugSim closed-loop benchmark, demonstrating both improved driving efficiency and competitive overall driving performance relative to Alpamayo.

Knowledge Distillation, Representation Learning and Alignment
.
Knowledge distillation compresses and accelerates models by training a smaller “student” to mimic a larger “teacher,” and has become essential as deep learning models continue to grow in size
[
hinton2015distilling
,
gou2021knowledge
]
. In the era of large language models, it is particularly critical for transferring knowledge to more compact architectures and for boosting overall performance
[
xu2024survey
]
. Our approach can be seen as a specialized form of distillation: rather than having a student network emulate the VLM’s outputs directly, we train our modular end-to-end network to replicate the VLM’s internal reasoning processes.
This multi-modal aligned representation learning draws insight from contrastive learning
[
radford_learning_2021
,
cherti_reproducible_2023
]
and embedding space supervision
[
khosla2020supervised
]
.
Recently,
[
sancaktar2025sensei
]
has explored leveraging these pretrained embeddings by aligning them with learned modules for downstream control tasks, such as active exploration.
Our method combines knowledge distillation with cross‐modal alignment, embedding VLM reasoning into the driving model by aligning its textual and driving feature representations.

III
VLM-Embedded Reasoning

III-A

Problem Formulation

We cast the autonomous driving problem as future trajectory planning. Let
s_{t}=[x,y,z,v_{x},v_{y},v_{z}]_{t}
be the ego agent state at time step
t
. The history trajectory of the past
T
time steps is denoted as
\tau_{-T:0}:=\{s_{-T},...,s_{0}\}
, and the future trajectory with a planning horizon
H
is denoted
\tau_{0:H}:=\{s_{0},...,s_{H}\}
. We have image observations at each time step
t
, denoted
o_{t}
. We aim to learn an end-to-end model
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
, that plans future trajectories given only sequential observations:

\hat{\tau}_{0:H}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\tau_{0:H}|o_{-T:0}),

(1)

where
\theta
represents learnable weights of the model. As shown in Figure
1
, our training pipeline focuses on the alignment of two modules: the VLM reasoning module, which we denote
\mathcal{M}
, and the e2e driving model, which we denote
\mathcal{P}
. Further, we assume that the modular e2e model can be broken down into three submodules
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{i}
, where
i\in\{\texttt{perception},\texttt{prediction},\texttt{planning}\}
. In addition to the standard losses for AD (e.g.,
\ell_{2}
distance), we add a training objective to minimize the difference between all VLM reasoning steps and the intermediate e2e submodules. Our key idea is to accomplish this by aligning the text features from the VLM reasoning module,
f_{\mathcal{M}}
, with the driving features from the e2e model,
f_{\mathcal{P}}
, through a similarity loss in the overall loss function, which we describe in Section
III-C
.
We detail how we obtain language embeddings in Section
III-B
, how we augment the e2e model by alignment with VLM embeddings in Section
III-C
.

III-B

Obtaining Language Embeddings

Fig. 2
:
Obtaining description features through chain-of-thought prompting and text encoder. For each query, the prompt consists of the system prompt, the observed images, the ego vehicle trajectory, the respective question, as well as the answers to the upstream modules (if any). The VLM answers to each module are encoded and mapped to a latent feature space.

In this section, we describe how we acquire language features
f_{\mathcal{M}}
from the VLM reasoning module
\mathcal{M}
. This module consists of two steps: (1) querying the VLM to obtain text responses, and (2) mapping the text response to latent features.
Figure
2
outlines the VLM reasoning module.

Prompting Strategy.
Following the modular e2e architecture, we prompt the VLM to reason about the perception, prediction, and planning steps in a chain-of-thought fashion
[
wei2022chain
]
. We first guide the VLM with some system prompt to ensure its responses are concise and relevant.
For perception, the VLM is asked to explain the past trajectory. It is given multi-view images captured from six cameras at timesteps
t=-T
and
t=0
, as well as the past ego trajectory
\tau_{-T:0}
. The VLM is then asked to identify the number of lanes in the scene, any agents or notable objects present, as well as their relative locations. To prevent spatial confusion and redundancy, the VLM is asked to label the agents and objects from left to right and from front to back.
For prediction and planning, the VLM is asked to explain the future trajectory. In both queries, it is given the future front images at
t=0
and
t=H
, as well as the future trajectory
\tau_{0:H}
. For prediction, the answer from perception is provided and the VLM is asked to describe other agents’ actions in the same order they were listed. The planning query in addition takes the prediction answers, and asks the VLM to describe what actions the ego agent took. The full prompt is listed in Appendix
B
.

Encoding VLM Responses.
We then map the answer for each module to latent feature space, using a text encoder. The text encoder is able to map natural language to semantically meaningful sentence embeddings
[
reimers2019sentence
]
. This encoding process reduces the dimensionality of the text, makes it machine interpretable and allows for alignment while preserving semantic meaning. The encoded text results in a feature vector
f_{\mathcal{M}}^{i}
for each module
i
.

III-C

Model Alignment with VLM Reasoning

Fig. 3
:
VERDI
Training.
The e2e model is trained with
VERDI
for the individual perception, prediction, and planning modules. All relevant feature maps
F
and
Q
are first mapped to a feature
f_{\mathcal{P}}
in a representation space, which is shared with the encoded language features
f_{\mathcal{M}}
. This mapping is facilitated by
VERDI
’s trainable
PFP
layers. The perception outputs
F_{\texttt{perception}}
including the extracted image features, are directly supervised with the encoded VLM features. In the subsequent modules, all features are supervised.
\mathcal{L}_{f}
computes their similarity.

To obtain driving features
f_{\mathcal{P}}^{i}
for each module
i
of the e2e model, we project its output features
F_{i}=\mathcal{P}^{i}_{\theta}\left(\cdot\right)
into the shared latent space with
f_{\mathcal{M}}^{i}
via a learnable Progressive Feature Projector (PFP)
\phi_{i}
, producing the e2e feature
f_{\mathcal{P}}^{i}=\text{PFP}{\phi_{i}}\left(F_{i}\right)
(more details in Appendix
B
). Figure
3
shows the training architecture that aligns the e2e model and VLM reasoning.

VLM-Distilled Scene Understanding.
To enhance the precision of the Bird’s Eye View (BEV) map’s representation of the driving scene, we find it essential to extract high-quality visual features
F_{\texttt{perception}}
. Vision-Language Models (VLMs), trained on large-scale internet image-text pairs, possess strong capabilities in identifying object classes, road markings, and spatial relationships with respect to the ego vehicle. Our goal is to distill this semantic understanding from the VLM into the image features
F_{\texttt{perception}}
encoded by the image feature encoder, as presented in Figure
3
.
The features are then processed by the BEV Encoder to produce a refined BEV representation.
To achieve alignment, we project
F_{\texttt{perception}}
to
f_{\mathcal{P}}^{\texttt{perception}}
in the shared latent space with
\text{PFP}^{\texttt{perception}}_{\phi}
, which consists of a combination of CNN and MLP layers(See Appendix
B
for details). The perception driving features are then ready to be aligned with VLM reasoning features.

VLM-Distilled Agents Prediction.
Next, the end‐to‐end prediction module forecasts the next
H
timesteps from the BEV feature map
F_{\texttt{bev}}
. In Figure
3
,
F_{\texttt{bev}}
is decoded into agent and map queries,
Q_{\texttt{agents}}
and
Q_{\texttt{map}}
, which presents a common architecture among e2e models
[
jiang2023vad
,
chen2024vadv2
,
hu2023_uniad
]
. These two separate feature maps are then concatenated to form the unified prediction query

Q_{\texttt{predict}}\;=\;\bigl[\,Q_{\texttt{agents}};\,Q_{\texttt{map}}\,\bigr],

where
[\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,]
denotes channel-wise concatenation. Here,
Q_{\texttt{agents}}
encodes agent-agent relative positions and
Q_{\texttt{map}}
encodes agent-map spatial relations.

To imbue the prediction module with the VLM’s reasoning ability, we distill the VLM’s reasoning ability by aligning VLM’s prediction
f^{\texttt{prediction}}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
f^{\texttt{prediction}}_{\mathcal{P}}
, which we obtain by projecting
Q_{\texttt{predict}}
through an MLP-based
\text{PFP}_{\phi}^{\texttt{prediction}}
.

VLM Distilled Ego Planning.
We feed the combined query
Q_{\texttt{predict}}
, which integrates both other agents’ future trajectories and spatial map context, into the planning module
\mathcal{P}_{\texttt{planning}}
. Conditioned on
Q_{\texttt{predict}}
, the planner produces the latent ego feature
Q_{\texttt{ego}}
as:

Q_{\texttt{ego}}=\mathcal{P}^{\texttt{planning}}_{\theta}(\tau_{0:H}|Q_{\texttt{predict}}).

(2)

We project
Q_{\texttt{ego}}
onto the latent space with an MLP based
\text{PFP}_{\phi}^{\texttt{planning}}
, before passing it into the last-layer decoder.
This allows us to enrich
Q_{\texttt{ego}}
with VLM reasoning by aligning VLM’s
f^{\texttt{planning}}_{\mathcal{M}}
with the latent e2e planning features
f^{\texttt{planning}}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Alignment Loss

We use cosine similarity to represent the feature alignment loss
\mathcal{L}_{f}(f_{\mathcal{P}},f_{\mathcal{M}})
between the e2e features and the language features,

\mathcal{L}_{f}(f_{\mathcal{P}},f_{\mathcal{M}})=\frac{f_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot f_{\mathcal{M}}}{\|f_{\mathcal{P}}\|\|f_{\mathcal{M}}\|}\text{, with a total loss}

(3)

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{i})+\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{f}(f_{\mathcal{P}}^{i},f_{\mathcal{M}}^{i}),

(4)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}(\theta_{i})
represents the supervised loss function with the original ground-truth data according to
[
jiang2023vad
]
and
\lambda
is a weight hyperparameter balancing the two contributions.
In the following experiments, we select the
\lambda=10
so that all loss terms are balanced in the same order of magnitude.

Experiments

We confirm the efficacy of

through a series of experiments on several driving benchmarks, both open-loop and closed-loop, comparing against baseline approaches in Section

Baseline Approaches.

We compare our method to two main categories of baselines: (1) e2e methods that directly train a modular differentiable network for future trajectory planning, (2) methods that train or finetune a VLM to infer future actions or control signals.
We implement our method building on the e2e model VAD-Base
[
jiang2023vad
]
, making it a direct baseline. We also compare against UniAD
[
hu2023planning
]
, a representative state-of-the-art e2e method. For VLM-based methods, we compare against DriveVLM
[
tian2024drivevlm
]
, which generates chain-of-thought reasoning and hierarchical plans using a VLM, OmniDrive
[
wang2024omnidrive
]
, which aligns 3D BEV features with counterfactual scene reasoning via a fine-tuned VLM, OpenEMMA
[
xing_openemma_2024
]
, an open-source implementation of Waymo’s EMMA, predicting full planner trajectories using a fine-tuned VLM and AlphaMayo
[
wang2025alpamayo
]
, a reasoning-augmented driving VLA that integrates large language model–based semantic reasoning with end-to-end perception and planning in long-tail driving scenarios.

Dataset and Metrics.

We evaluate
VERDI
across challenging driving benchmarks under both open- and closed-loop settings, including nuScenes
[
caesar2020nuscenes
]
and HugSim Simulator
[
zhou2024hugsim
]
.

Open-Loop Evaluations.
We conduct experiments on the nuScenes dataset
[
caesar2020nuscenes
]
, which contains approximately 1000 driving scenes with 150 validation and 850 training sequences, each about 20 seconds long. The dataset includes 6 camera views, and provides annotated keyframes at 2Hz. For planning evaluation, we follow the standard open-loop protocol
[
hu2023_uniad
]
: given 2 seconds of past observations, the model predicts the vehicle’s trajectory over the next 3 seconds. We evaluate the model using
\ell_{2}
displacement errors at 1, 2, and 3-second horizons. We also compare inference speed in frames per second (FPS) for applicable methods.

Closed-Loop Evaluations.
To further assess the robustness and effectiveness of
VERDI
in realistic and interactive environments, we perform closed-loop evaluations on the
HugSim
[
zhou2024hugsim
]
driving simulator. The HugSim closed-loop benchmark includes 88 test scenes derived from the nuScenes testing dataset, featuring much denser traffic and challenging vehicle interactions.
Driving performance is assessed through a diverse set of sub-metrics beyond collision and goal-reaching, including Drivable Area Compliance (DAC) and Comfort (COM) in HugSim,
providing a more comprehensive and safety-oriented evaluation of driving behavior.

Implementation Details.

For the nuScenes dataset, training was conducted for 60 epochs on 10 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs with a batch size of 2 using the AdamW optimizer.
All other training configurations maintain the settings adopted from VAD
[
jiang2023vad
]
.
Both open loop benchmark inference were done on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU for all models. Closed-loop HugSim inference was done on a single NVIDIA A10G.
For VLM language embeddings, we use
Qwen-2.5-VL-72B

[
yang2024qwen2
]
to generate reasoning for every trajectory in the nuScenes training dataset.

V
Conclusion

We introduce VERDI, a training-time method that distills Vision–Language Model reasoning into a lightweight end-to-end autonomous-driving stack by aligning each submodule with respective VLM reasoning embeddings.
Extensive experiments and ablation studies on nuScenes and HugSim closed-loop simulator show that
VERDI
achieves a 11% performance gain in
\ell_{2}
distance, the best across the HugSim benchmark with 10% safety improvements over the direct baseline without reasoning embeddings. Although our unoptimized implementation runs at 4.5 Hz, further acceleration is possible through TensorRT optimization and model quantization, leaving substantial room for faster real-time deployment.
In future, we plan to extend the framework to self-supervised latent reasoning alignment with feedback and explore reasoning-guided inference using ultra-small VLMs for real-time control.

VERDI avoids single-view hallucinations by leveraging multi-view imagery and constructing a BEV map
. C-B

VLM Embedding Quality

Next, we evaluate the VLM embedding quality, and, following our module ablation setup, we select three trajectories of ten time steps each. To evaluate the VLM’s reasoning consistency, we start those trajectories at
t=0
,
5
, and
10
, which creates five overlapping time steps between each pair. The intuition behind these time step choices is to amplify the impact of embedding quality: a large VLM with strong reasoning capabilities should yield consistent outputs across overlapping time steps—thereby improving performance—whereas a smaller VLM with weaker reasoning will likely produce inconsistent results on the same time steps, degrading performance. The findings confirm that with the Qwen
75

6.7\%

As VERDI achieves a higher Non-Collision (NC) score in the HugSim closed-loop evaluation, we further analyze which types of scenarios benefit most from its improved collision-avoidance behavior. Figure
11
presents qualitative visualizations of four challenging cases in which the VAD-Base (supervised end-to-end model) fails to plan a collision-free trajectory. In contrast, VERDI—equipped with distilled VLM reasoning, correctly detects nearby vehicles and accurately predicts their motion and plans a collision-free trajectory.

These examples represent challenging situations: one lane-following case with an oncoming vehicle approaching closely, and three bypassing cases with vehicles in tight proximity to the ego agent. The turning-while-bypassing scenario in the fourth row is particularly interesting. Such aggressive driving behavior rarely appears in the nuScenes dataset; as a result, the purely behavior-cloned VAD-Base, which lacks distilled reasoning capability, has no prior experience to guide its decision making. When encountering this scenario in the closed-loop simulator—one that exceeds the difficulty of its training set—the VAD-Base fails to produce a collision-free plan because it cannot actively reason about the scene. In contrast, VERDI handles this difficult corner case successfully, further confirming that its distilled reasoning capability enables a safer behavior in scenarios beyond the training set.
