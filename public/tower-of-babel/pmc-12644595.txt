Complex and diverse patterns of neurocranial development in Australopithecus, Paranthropus and Homo
Authors: José Braga, Z Alemseged, E Gilissen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEven though poorly understood, early ontogeny may have influenced the distinct morphologies and behaviors of Homo sapiens, fossil hominins and extant African apes. Leveraging an extensive high-resolution computed tomography dataset, we investigated previously unexplored relationships between facial, basicranial and endocranial development during early ontogeny in African apes, H. sapiens, Australopithecus afarensis and Paranthropus robustus. We found that a delay in endocranial growth in Pan paniscus infants does not necessarily indicate a uniform trend toward delayed development, suggesting more complexity in developmental processes than previously thought. A. afarensis exhibited protracted endocranial growth compared to P. robustus, a contrast even greater than that between P. paniscus and Pan troglodytes, suggesting that endocranial development in A. afarensis more closely resembles that of H. sapiens than P. robustus. Unlike the shared delayed development of H. sapiens and Homo erectus, A. afarensis and P. robustus show contrasting developmental patterns. We therefore propose that early ontogeny in Australopithecus and Paranthropus was variable, showing no consistent pattern of developmental delay or acceleration across species. Our finding also enhances our ability to identify neonatal cranial remains in the fossil record, providing new opportunities for expanding much needed sample size and advance paleobiological studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25584-2.Keywords: Human evolution, Ontogeny, Australopithecus, Paranthropus, Homo erectusSubject terms: Anthropology, Palaeontology

Introduction

The study of covariation among craniofacial structures provides critical insights into the developmental processes throughout ontogeny, while also facilitating the exploration of trait integration1,2. Covariation may arise between traits with distinct developmental origins and functions, underscoring the interconnected craniofacial modules2,3. A particularly compelling case is the relationship between the brain and the neurocranium. Despite the two differing developmental pathways of the neurocranium – the calvaria forming via intramembranous ossification and the basicranium via endochondral ossification – both structures develop from mesenchymal precursors (from neural crest and paraxial mesoderm). Their morphology is strongly influenced by brain expansion, with additional contribution from sutural dynamics (vault) and synchondrosis-driven growth (cranial base), and interaction with the face. All these are shaped in response to brain growth4 during which their development must remain tightly coordinated to maintain craniofacial integrity5.

The brain acts as a central regulator in vertebrate neurocranial development6, playing a role in the growth of both the calvaria and basicranium, while dynamically responding to mechanical stimuli throughout ontogeny7. This integration is particularly sensitive to heterochronic shifts – changes in the rate or timing of developmental events – that can lead to significant phenotypic differences2. Indeed, heterochronic changes have driven key evolutionary novelties across vertebrates8,9. In Homo sapiens, the distinctive combination of a shortened face and an enlarged brain compared to other hominins, likely results from developmental trade-offs10 between two heterochronies: peramorphosis (extended development) and paedomorphosis (retention of juvenile traits)8. This framework of dissociated heterochrony1 enables distinct cranial and dental modules3,11 to grow along separate developmental timelines, facilitating novel morphological combinations12. Dissociation in cranial developmental patterns holds critical clues to shifts in life histories, cognition, and social organization during human evolution8,9,13–15.

The distinct processes of intramembranous and endochondral ossification that shape the cranium, create developmental trade-offs between the neurocranium and face, which must share limited energetic resources2. In primates, these constraints are intensified by the high energetic demands of brain growth and maintenance13, often requiring energy reallocation from other physiological systems14,15. Although some studies have investigated the relationships between adult brain size and dental development among hominid species16–19 – such as the timing of first permanent molar (M1) emergence – dental development has also been shown to be closely linked to the overall tempo of neurological and somatic growth, providing a developmental proxy for broader life history patterns16,17,19. Despite this, the interplay between the brain, calvaria, basicranium, and face during early ontogeny remains largely unexplored in the context of human evolution.

To fill in this knowledge gap, we examine the relationships among endocranial and facial growth, dental development, and basicranial morphology, using the subarcuate fossa (SF) as a developmental proxy. The SF represents a reliable skeletal milestone of early basicranial maturation and may serve to estimate neurodevelopmental timing in fossil specimens20–22. Two factors make SF particularly informative for comparative ontogeny. Firstly, it represents a consistent feature of early basicranial ontogeny, making it a suitable measure of maturation for cross-species comparisons20,21,23. The SF is located on the posterior surface of the petrous part of the temporal bone and gradually narrows during development until it closes after the eruption of M1in H. sapiens24, or even M2 in Pan and in Gorilla (pers. obs). The close relationship between the size of the SF and the petrosal lobule of the cerebellar paraflocculus among primates25 highlights the developmental link between the brain and cranial base, supporting the appropriateness of its use for age estimation. Secondly, during fetal and early postnatal ontogeny, basicranial growth via endochondral ossification parallels the rapid pattern of neural maturation, while the face – formed mainly by intramembranous ossification – aligns more closely with the development of the dentition and masticatory muscles26.

To investigate these dynamics, we integrate early hominin fossils with comparative analyses of extant H. sapiens (n = 12), Pan troglodytes (n = 46), Pan paniscus (n = 40) and Gorilla gorilla (n = 5) infants (i.e., individuals before the emergence of their M1) (Table 1, Supplementary Data.xlsx). Our focus is on ontogenetic differences among three key fossil specimens: Australopithecus afarensis: DIK-1-1, a juvenile from Dikika, Ethiopia27; Paranthropus robustus: KW 9000/9600, an infant from Kromdraai, South Africa28, and the Homo erectus (Mojokerto 1/Perning 1) juvenile from Mojokerto, Indonesia29. Such specimens are extremely rare and provide unique insight into early cranial development. We use a comparative, newly developed high-resolution computed tomography (µCT) database (n = 103), combined with three non-linear fitting methods, to pursue three interrelated objectives aligned with the following hypotheses. First, we test whether the SF can serve as an appropriate proxy for basicranial degree of maturation across great apes and fossil hominins, with greater resolution than approaches based on conventional dental eruption patterns. Second, we assess the relationships among SF, neurocranial and facial growth to test the hypothesis that Homo exhibits delayed neurocranial development relative to basicranial maturation, as early as the early Pleistocene, in contrast to Australopithecus and Paranthropus. Thirdly, we examine whether developmental trade-offs between neurocranial and facial growth are present across extant taxa. Although such trade-offs may not be directly measurable in incomplete fossil specimens, their potential presence should nonetheless be considered when discussing evolutionary patterns in early hominins (see Discussion). Finally, we apply this developmental framework to assess evolutionary differences in cranial ontogeny and to further test the protracted brain growth hypothesis in A. afarensis30. We also reassess the developmental status of the isolated P. robustus specimen, KB 606721.

To assess neurocranial and facial growth, we measure endocranial volume (ECV) and facial centroid size (FCS), the latter of which has proven valuable as an age-related variable in H. sapiens31. Both ECV and FSC are expressed as percentages of the species-specific adult female mean, yielding relative values (rECV and rFCS). This approach is based on the rationale that female values best reflect the “ecological sex”32. Among primates, rECV is typically scaled to maternal body mass33,34, with neonatal values of approximately 30% in H. sapiens and 40% in P. troglodytes33. Widely used to assess brain growth tempo, rECV serves as a proxy for evaluating degrees of altriciality and, more specifically, secondary altriciality. Altriciality refers to the condition in which offspring are born neurologically immature and require extended postnatal care, as seen in many rodents and carnivores35,36. We use the term “secondary altriciality” to describe the even more derived condition in H. sapiens, marked by the pronounced brain growth during the first postnatal year35, a concept also referred to as “human neonatal altriciality”36 or simply “human altriciality”37.

In this study, we compare postnatal endocranial growth using basicranial maturation as a developmental proxy and introduce the following two notable methodological advances relative to previous approaches. Our first contention is that including data from older individuals when modeling growth trajectories intended to compare growth curves during infancy may blur or mask the actual developmental patterns occurring at younger stages. This approach risks reducing our ability to detect meaningful interspecific differences in brain growth that are specific to infancy. By focusing strictly on infant data, we preserve the developmental resolution needed to accurately compare early neurocranial growth dynamics across species. Second, we do not use age estimates for cross-species comparisons due to inherent uncertainties in assessing age-at-death from museum specimens, as shown by notable disparities in previous assessments38,39 for several bonobo and chimpanzee neonates/infants included in this study. For instance, the age-at-death of two infant P. paniscus in our sample, 26977 and 22336 (Supplementary Data.xlsx), was estimated at 339 or 3.538 years (26977), and 3.439 or 3.7538 (22336) years respectively, based on dental development. Such uncertainties in age-at-death estimates could obscure interspecific comparisons of postnatal endocranial growth. We avoid this limitation by using the SF as a maturational marker in place of dental age.

Results

Modeling M1 development and SF closure

We employed logistic regression to model the probability of observing M1 crown completion (stage Crc) or root initiation (stage Ri) as a function of SF closure, in order to investigate potential differences between chimpanzees and bonobos (Fig. 1). Unfortunately, this relationship could not be explored with logistic regression in H. sapiens and G. gorilla due to the limited number of specimens available and analyzed in this study. The two Pan species showed comparable developmental timing of M1 formation relative to SF closure. Among G. gorilla, the only specimen with an incomplete M1 crown (15351) exhibited delayed SF closure (19.5%) compared to P. paniscus (mean: 8.9%, n = 4) and P. troglodytes (mean: 9.3%, n = 4) at a comparable dental stage. In contrast, two G. gorilla juveniles with M1 roots approximately half formed showed nearly complete SF closure (1.7–2.8%), indicating faster basicranial maturation than in P. troglodytes (mean: 4.7%, n = 3) and P. paniscus (3.6%, n = 1) at similar stages. Although based on limited evidence, this suggests that SF closure relative to M1 development in G. gorilla is delayed at early stages but accelerates later, in contrast to the more consistent trajectory seen in both Pan species. In H. sapiens, eight juveniles prior to M1 emergence (Table 1) exhibited a mean SF closure of 22%, indicating slower basicranial maturation relative to Pan, Gorilla, A. afarensis and P. robustus.

Distinct facial versus endocranial growth patterns between bonobos and chimpanzees

We also examined whether developmental trade-offs between neurocranial and facial growth were present across the two Pan species. Recognizing potential species-specific modularity between the calvaria and basicranium throughout evolution and ontogeny, we used rFCS and rECV to compare growth curves between P. troglodytes and P. paniscus infants (Fig. 2). The use of the same P. paniscus and P. troglodytes specimens to compute both rECV and rFCS facilitated direct comparisons within and between species avoiding the impact of sampling bias. Wilcoxon tests showed no significant differences in rFCS between females and males of P. paniscus (p = 0.314), P. troglodytes (p = 0.362). As for rECV, Gompertz and polynomial models have been proved effective in displaying rFCS growth curves in P. paniscus and P. troglodytes separately (Methods) (Table S2). Despite strong positive correlation between rECV and rFCS within each species, P. troglodytes and P. paniscus differed in their relative developmental timing. In both taxa, rECV and rFCS were strongly positively correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.66; P. troglodytes: 95% CI = 0.46–0.80, n = 46, p = 5.2 × 10−7; P. paniscus: 95% CI = 0.42–0.82, n = 35, p = 1.5 × 10−5), indicating tightly coordinated cranial development within species. However, cross-species comparisons revealed a divergent pattern of growth. When compared to P. troglodytes, P. paniscus infants were delayed in their endocranial growth but were advanced in their facial growth (Fig. 2). This pattern held across narrowly defined intervals of SF closure. For example, within the 3–7% SF closure window – a range well-represented in our sample – P. paniscus showed a slightly lower mean rECV (90.6, 95% CI: 85.1–96.1; n = 23) than P. troglodytes (93.6, 95% CI: 89.0–98.2; n = 19), though the difference was not significant and the effect size was small (Wilcoxon, p = 0.686; rank-biserial r = 0.064). In contrast, rFCS was significantly higher in bonobos (64.3, 95% CI: 61.8–66.7) than chimpanzees (61.0, 95% CI: 58.2–63.8), with a moderate effect size (Wilcoxon, p = 0.030; rank-biserial r = 0.337), reflecting a relatively larger facial skeleton in P. paniscus. In the rECV and rFCS growth models, the coefficient of the first-order (linear) term — which approximates the initial rate of change along the curve — was used to assess the relative pace of growth. For P. paniscus, this coefficient was 28.3 for rECV and 5.58 for rFCS. For P. troglodytes, the corresponding values were 25.3 for rECV and 3.67 for rFCS. The approximately five times steeper linear coefficients for rECV compared to rFCS in both species indicate a faster relative pace of rECV than rFCS. Therefore, although rECV has been widely used as a developmental marker in previous studies, our results show that it alone provides only a partial view of craniofacial heterochrony. Specifically, rECV does not capture species-level differences in the timing of facial versus endocranial growth.

Divergent endocranial growth patterns among extant species

We tested the hypothesis that, compared to the two Pan species, H. sapiens shows delayed neurocranial development relative to basicranial maturation. To compare endocranial growth patterns among extant species, we used rECV growth curves between H. sapiens, P. troglodytes and P. paniscus using Gompertz and polynomial models fitted to the degree of SF closure. Wilcoxon tests revealed no significant differences in SF between female and male individuals of P. paniscus (p = 0.347), P. troglodytes (p = 0.685), and H. sapiens (p = 0.144), as well as no significant differences in rECV between sexes in P. paniscus (p = 0.275), P. troglodytes (p = 0.944), and H. sapiens (p = 0.066), indicating a lack of sexual dimorphism in both traits within these species. To assess whether the limited sample size for H. sapiens (n = 12) allowed for a reliable Gompertz fit, we performed parametric bootstrapping to evaluate the stability and precision of the model’s parameter estimates. Our results indicate that the Gompertz model fit is statistically robust despite the modest sample size. For example, the 95% confidence interval around the asymptotic size (a) was ± 16.5 on a mean estimate of approximately 110, corresponding to a relative interval width of about 15%. Similar precision was observed for the scaling parameter (b) and growth rate constant (k) parameters. These findings suggest that the key growth parameters are estimated with acceptable uncertainty, supporting sufficient precision in the curve estimation despite the limited sample size. Notably, the SF value of 20.6% measured on the only P. paniscus newborn in our sample (86036M8) using µCT closely matches the range observed in the two P. troglodytes newborns (19.1–20.5%) measured with high-resolution medical CT scans (Supplementary Data.xlsx). Therefore, H. sapiens infants appeared to be born with a less developed skull base (SF at c. 40%) than in both P. paniscus and P. troglodytes (SF at c. 20%) (Fig. 3a, b). We extracted the Gompertz growth rate constant (k) for each taxon to quantify postnatal rECV growth velocity. H. sapiens exhibited the slowest growth rate, with a k value of 0.024 (95% CI: 0.020–0.029), which is approximately half the magnitude of the growth rates observed in both P. paniscus (k = 0.046, 95% CI: 0.041–0.051) and P. troglodytes (k = 0.048, 95% CI: 0.044–0.053). This near twofold difference in growth rate constants indicates that H. sapiens undergoes a substantially slower postnatal rECV growth trajectory compared to both Pan species. The non-overlapping confidence intervals and a significant likelihood ratio test (p < 0.001) confirm that these differences are statistically robust. Therefore, throughout early postnatal life and into late infancy, both Pan species exhibited comparable rates of endocranial growth (with similar curves, Fig. 3a, b), with P. paniscus being slightly (but not significantly) delayed during this period. In contrast, the H. sapiens endocranium undergoes relatively slower growth (Fig. 3a, b). The five G. gorilla specimens in our sample were not included in model estimation due to sparse sampling but were used as an out-of-sample test for a Pan-based growth model, given that these Pan species exhibit similar rates of relative endocranial growth. To evaluate this, we applied a Gompertz growth model—along with 95% confidence intervals calculated via bootstrapping—based on combined data from P. paniscus and P. troglodytes to predict rECV in the five G. gorilla specimens across the spectrum of SF closure stages (1.4%–19.5%). The model’s predictions closely matched the observed G. gorilla rECV values, with residuals ranging from − 5.4% to + 6.1% and a mean absolute deviation of 4.5%. For instance, the oldest G. gorilla specimen (37265, SF = 1.4%) showed an observed rECV of 112.6%, almost identical to the model’s prediction of 111.8%. Similarly, the youngest G. gorilla juvenile in the sample (15351, SF = 19.5%) fell within the predicted range (observed rECV = 86.4%, predicted = 90.1%). These results indicate that G. gorilla juvenile specimens broadly conform to the early rECV growth curve observed in both Pan species combined. Moreover, the close agreement further supports the generality of the Gompertz function in capturing early African ape rECV growth.

Divergent endocranial growth patterns in early hominins

After evaluating the relationships between SF and neurocranial growth in extant species, we applied this developmental framework to investigate evolutionary differences in cranial ontogeny among A. afarensis, P. robustus, and H. erectus. H. sapiens showed delayed basicranial maturation and a slower postnatal pace of rECV compared to both Pan species (Fig. 3a, b). This distinction provides important context for interpreting early hominin cranial development and their evolutionary connections with extant African apes. When we used a resampling technique, the cranial base in both H. sapiens infants and the H. erectus Mojokerto juvenile (SF: 27.9%) were clearly delayed compared to the A. afarensis DIK-1-1 (SF: 8.6%), P. robustus KW 9000/9600 (SF: 7.2%)27,28, and both Pan species infants with similar rECVs (Fig. 3c). Moreover, for comparable SF closure in the DIK-1-1 and KW 9000/9600 infants, we observe a distinct contrast in rECV − 51.9–75.9% in DIK-1-127 and 81.9–112% in KW 9000/960028 - that notably exceeded the variation observed both between and within the two species of Pan for individuals displaying a comparable SF closure (7-8.5%) (Fig. 3c). However, only the rECV values for DIK-1-1 fell outside the confidence intervals of the two Pan species (Figure a, b). Given similar relationships between SF and M1 among A. afarensis, P. robustus and the two extant Pan species, whether we used SF or M1 as an indicator of age, DIK-1-1 exhibited a significant delay in endocranial growth compared to KW 9000/9600 (Fig. 3a, b). These findings suggest that endocranial growth in A. afarensis is more similar to what is seen in H. sapiens and was notably delayed compared to that of P. robustus; an even greater contrast than what is observed between P. paniscus and P. troglodytes. In contrast, cranial base growth in A. afarensis was much faster (as in P. robustus and African apes) when compared to Homo (H. sapiens and H. erectus), with the rECV values for the Mojokerto 1 juvenile falling within the confidence intervals of H. sapiens (Fig. 3b).

Identifying fragmentary early hominin neonate remains

Our findings on SF closure in A. afarensis, P. robustus, P. paniscus and P. troglodytes present a notable advancement in our ability to identify neonatal cranial remains in the fossil record. Accordingly, we reassessed the developmental status of KB 6067, the petrous part of the left temporal bone of a P. robustus juvenile specimen from Kromdraai, South Africa21. Upon its initial description, KB 6067 was characterized as either a neonate or a child aged between a few months and five years21 In this study, we find that in contrast to Homo (H. sapiens and H. erectus), the SF in both A. afarensis and P. robustus, and African apes was faster during infancy, with P. paniscus and P. troglodytes having their SF open at around 20% at birth. Therefore, it seems reasonable to suggest that KB 6067 (P. robustus) was a neonate.

Discussion

Less than two million years ago (Ma), P. paniscus and P. troglodytes diverged40, each with complex evolutionary histories41, but with relatively delayed cognitive development and social behavior42 as well as extended nursing43,44 in the latter. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether particular aspects of behavior or cognition in infant P. paniscus signify brain, body or other aspects of somatic developmental delays in comparison to P. troglodytes, as observed in P. troglodytes when compared to H. sapiens45. Therefore, comparing early ontogenies in the two Pan species and humans provides fresh context for interpreting ‘fossilized ontogenies’ and gaining deeper insights into the evolution of development in early hominins.

As ontogenetic changes in the neurocranium (basicranium, endocranium) and face are not synchronized within species, we conducted separate comparisons of P. paniscus and P. troglodytes infants, examining these three major elements of the cranium individually (basicranium, endocranium, and facial skeleton). Importantly, our P. paniscus and P. troglodytes samples represent multiple populations46 and therefore provide a comprehensive basis for examining developmental variation within and between these species.

We observed that the growth of P. paniscus infant is delayed in its endocranial development but accelerated in its face when compared with the P. troglodytes one (Fig. 2). This result suggests that although brain and facial growth are developmentally coordinated within each species, their relative timing differs between species. In other words, the observed heterochronic patterns do not arise from a lack of integration between rECV and rFCS within species, but instead reflect a species-level distinction in the timing and patterns of endocranial and facial growth. This result may suggest a developmental trade-off between facial and brain growth, where the prioritization of one aspect of development, such as facial or brain growth, may occur at the expense of the other at different stages of development. This observation aligns with current findings that suggest a tightly coordinated growth pattern between these systems during development47. Moreover, because the two Pan species differed in their pattern of rECV versus rFCS growth, their early cranial ontogeny could not be categorized as uniformly delayed or advanced when compared to each other. Our results do not suggest an overall delay of the P. paniscus cranium, but rather reveal distinct and modular ontogenetic changes within the basicranium, endocranium, and facial skeleton. This finding is important because the evolution and development of the endocranium likely reflect not only brain modifications but is also associated with changes in the basicranium48. Furthermore, it suggests that ontogenetic differences among hominid species cannot be simply characterized as a high degree of neoteny (retention of juvenile traits into adulthood) in H. sapiens relative to other primates, nor as a generalized developmental delay in P. paniscus compared to P. troglodytes.

Our results on distinct patterns of basicranial versus facial growth in P. paniscus and P. troglodytes are consistent with prior studies that mapped non-metric (i.e., discrete) aspects of skeletal development - within the spectrum of incomplete and excess of bone growth49 - in African apes, H. sapiens and early hominins50,51. Therefore, a significant delay in endocranial growth in one hominid species during infancy does not necessarily indicate a uniform trend toward altricial or neotenic development. Yet, enhancing this developmental framework further necessitates more comparative data (e.g., the SF) for infant Gorilla and Pongo. Moreover, to assess the plasticity of developmental traits, additional data linking assessments of early ontogeny to individual-level environmental factors (including social conditions) among African apes are required.

When compared to H. sapiens and H. erectus (Mojokerto), we find faster but varying brain-to-basicranial developmental relationships in both A. afarensis and P. robustus (Fig. 3). Earlier work suggested that for H. sapiens to be born with neurological and cognitive maturation akin to that of chimpanzees, they would necessitate a gestation period extended by a minimum of nine months (i.e., 18 months in total)35. In this context, our finding of a notable difference in the degree of SF closure at birth between H. sapiens, P. paniscus and P. troglodytes is intriguing for two main reasons. Firstly, it aligns with the depiction of H. sapiens as considerably delayed at birth, a concept also referred to as “human neonatal altriciality”36 or simply “human altriciality”37. Secondly, it suggests that examining rECV growth trajectories using SF to estimate age offers a unique opportunity to investigate ontogenetic variation among early hominins. Therefore, beginning with H. erectus, the genus Homo may be distinctly characterized by a combination of significantly delayed basicranial growth (as indicated by SF, with more than 20% of closure) and a markedly slower rECV-to-SF growth curve – nearly twice as slow compared to Pan species – during early postnatal ontogeny.

These features may underlie the evolutionary emergence of human secondary altriciality35. However, given the high craniofacial diversity observed in early Pleistocene Homo infants52, this pattern may not be present in less derived species such as H. habilis and H. rudolfensis. Furthermore, the contrasting early ontogenetic patterns in Australopithecus and Paranthropus, and the delayed basicranial growth observed in H. erectus (similar to H. sapiens) likely stem from evolutionary changes hitherto poorly understood. Indeed, fossil evidence on early ontogeny in early Pleistocene Homo primarily comes from dental microanatomy53,54 which suggests that dental growth in early H. erectus resembled that of australopiths more than H. sapiens53, or that it was already extended 54. Our results suggest that the ontogeny of early H. erectus may not have been consistently delayed or advanced relative to Australopithecus and Paranthropus. Given the known relationship between dental development and facial growth (see above), and in light of our findings on possible developmental trade-offs between facial skeletal and endocranial growth in both Pan species, it is likely that H. erectus infants experienced accelerated (prioritized in terms of energetics) dental and facial growth, possibly at the expense of protracted brain development. This pattern may reflect a shift in growth prioritization, similar to what has been observed in other primates, where facial (and dental) development is faster than brain expansion early on55. We also find differences that may reflect distinct life histories among Australopithecus and Paranthropus.

Specifically, our results raise the intriguing question of why a small adult brain would grow so slowly in A. afarensis while developing more rapidly in P. robustus. The development of the M1 has been a key point of comparison in studying life histories among H. sapiens, other extant primates, and early hominins, as its emergence is purported to mark the end of infancy, signaling both the cessation of nursing and the formation of most of the brain. However, the significant difference in rECV in the A. afarensis infant compared to the P. robustus, despite their similarities in both M1 and SF development, suggests a developmental dissociation between adult brain size and M1 eruption in Australopithecus and Paranthropus. This dissociation is critical, as it may imply that in A. afarensis, the cessation of nursing and transition out of infancy occurred later (i.e., at a more advanced stage of M1 development) than in P. robustus. Unfortunately, due to the limited contrast and resolution of its µCT data, we are unable to reliably assess the SF of the Taung Child, the holotype of A. africanus, and therefore cannot compare its basicranial growth pattern with that of A. afarensis and P. robustus infants.

Our findings reveal that developmental differences during the early postnatal period among early hominins may not have been fully appreciated. In particular, while both H. sapiens and H. erectus basicranial and endocranial relationships during early ontogeny suggest altriciality (with secondary altriciality in the former), both A. afarensis and P. robustus appear precocial and exhibit disparities surpassing those observed between chimpanzees and bonobos. These contrasted developmental patterns hint at distinct ontogenetically-related behaviors among A. afarensis and P. robustus, at least tentatively. To better understand the patterns of reproductive strategies during early human evolution, further ontogenetic and high-resolution screening among hominids (e.g. Gorilla and Pongo) is imperative to document the skeletal milestones associated with the key behavioral traits defining the genus Homo and its living representative, H. sapiens55. This endeavor should be integrated into other studies aimed at defining ontogenetically-related features – for instance, cognition56,57 – displaying minimal variation within extant species (including humans and other apes) and substantial divergence between them.

Materials and methods

Samples description and ethical approval

This study did not involve any experimentation, intervention, or imposition of risk or constraints as part of the research process. It was entirely non-interventional and did not include observations of living human subjects. All data used were digital, anonymized, and collected retrospectively. As such, approval from the Committee for the Protection of Persons (a French ethics committee) was not required. Access to the µCT scans of H. sapiens skeletal remains was granted by the Institut d’Anatomie Normale et Pathologique at the University of Strasbourg. Access to the African ape specimens (Supplementary Data.xlsx) was provided by one of the authors, E.G., who serves as the Curator of Mammalogy at the Royal Museum for Central Africa (MRAC) in Tervuren, Belgium. Access to the µCT scans and synchrotron data of fossil specimens was granted by two of the authors, J.B. and Z.A., who respectively serve as the Directors of the excavations at Kromdraai (South Africa) and Dikika (Ethiopia). The H. sapiens sample has been used in previous studies22,31 and is represented by µCT scans of crania (Supplementary Data.xlsx). They are curated in the Institut d’Anatomie Normale et Pathologique of the University of Strasbourg, which represents individuals of known sex and age-at-death58 (catalogued with the ‘Embr’ prefix and listed in Table S1). This sample was µCT scanned in Toulouse (France) with a XtremeCT (Scanco Medical; http://www.scanco.ch/) system at the Institut de Médecine et de Physiologie Spatiales (http://www.medes.fr/) (isometric voxel size of 41 μm). P. paniscus, P. troglodytes and G. gorilla crania listed with the ‘MRAC’ prefix (Table S1) represent most of our comparative specimens. They derive from the osteological collections of the Royal Museum for Central Africa (Tervuren, Belgium), now Africa Museum (https://www.africamuseum.be/en/discover/collections). This Museum houses one of the largest biological collections in the world, including more than 10,500 primate specimens – spanning crania, skeletons, and a digital repository of CT scans and 3D surface data – largely collected from Central Africa. This collection is unique in its scope and global standing for great ape osteology and provides rare access to multiple P. paniscus and P. troglodytes individuals from diverse provenances within the species range46. They consist of pooled-sex samples, with roughly equal numbers of males and females. These samples are cross-sectional and primarily comprise wild-shot animals, although some specimens may have been held in captivity for a period of time. They have been µCT scanned in Toulouse, using the RX-solutions EasyTom XL duo system (https://www.rx-solutions.com/) at the Institut de Mécanique des Fluides (IMFT) (France) with isometric voxel dimensions ranging from 33 to 70 μm. The ‘UPS’ specimens (from J. Braga’s personal collection) were also µCT scanned at the IMFT, and have been used in previous studies49,50. The P. troglodytes and G. gorilla crania listed with the prefix ‘MCZ’ (Table S1) derive from Morphosource data (www.MorphoSource.org, Duke University) with access provided by Lynn Lucas, Lynn Copes, the Museum of Comparative Zoology-Harvard University (Cambridge, USA), the NSF DDIG #0925793 and the Wenner-Gren Foundation. African great ape specimens listed with the prefix ‘MCL’ (Table S1) are from the ESRF heritage database for palaeontology, evolutionary biology and archaeology (http://paleo.esrf.eu) with data provided in reference59. Finally, two chimpanzee newborns (1959-2 and 1967 − 271) kept in formaldehyde in the Laboratoire d’Anatomie Comparée of the Muséum National d’Histoire Naturelle in Paris (France), were scanned at the Hopital d’Instruction des Armées du Val-de-Grâce (Paris, France) with a HiSpeed CT (GE Medical Systems), a slice thickness of 1 mm, and a pixel size of 250 and 290 µm, respectively. The skull of the Mojokerto 1 specimen was CT scanned in the Clinique Pasteur, Toulouse (France) with a Mx Twin Scanner. The scans were made contiguously in the transverse plane with a slice thickness and a pixel size of 0.5 mm and 199 µm, respectively.

The KW 9000/9600 P. robustus infant was µCT scanned using the Nikon Metrology XTH 225/320 LC dual source industrial CT system at the Palaeosciences Centre in the University of the Witwatersrand – Johannesburg, South Africa – with isometric voxels of 19.8 μm (23,28). The KW 9000/9600 fossil is curated at the Evolutionary Studies Institute, University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg, South Africa. We used synchrotron CT scans of the DIK-1-1 A. afarensis infant, obtained at the ID17 beamline at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in Grenoble (France)27. The CT and µCT data were processed in Avizo 6.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for segmentation, surface reconstructions, landmark placement, measurement and generation of 2D cross sections.

The opening of the subarcuate fossa (SF)

Under the arch of the anterior semicircular canal towards the mastoid antrum, the width of the SF is on the scale of a few millimeters. Therefore, the spatial resolution of CT scans falls short in accuracy, consistently underestimating this measurement when compared to µCT data21. In earlier studies, the use of CT scans to compare P. paniscus and P. troglodytes newborns/infants60 hindered the detailed study of the SF. This was due to significant discrepancies in assessments of the SF opening between medical CT scans and µCT scans conducted on the same specimens21. Our analysis of SF from µCT data circumvents this limitation and shows minimal interobserver measurement errors (t-tests with paired samples, p values < 0.01). To quantify measurement error, we randomly selected 43 µCT scans and enlisted two observers: one of the authors (JB) and a Master’s student from Toulouse, Jeanne Harribey (JH), to independently assess the extent of subarcuate fossa (SF) opening (Table S1). The mean percentage difference between JB and JH measurements was approximately − 1.9%, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from − 39% to 34%, indicating good agreement and practical interchangeability between the two observers. The Bland-Altman plot (Figure S1) shows differences centered near zero with no obvious systematic bias. Differences are evenly scattered around the mean difference line, and most points lie within the limits of agreement, supporting consistent agreement across the measurement range. We followed a precise methodology described in reference20. Its degree of opening was measured in a plane parallel to the arc of the lateral semicircular canal. We pinpointed the slice exhibiting the SF’s maximum width, then expressed this width as a percentage of the length between the two lumens of the anterior semicircular canal. It is crucial to note that, due to limited spatial resolution in medical CTs, µCT is preferred for precise SF measurements. In a few instances where µCT scanning was not possible - such as for the Mojokerto 1 and P. troglodytes newborns – we used high-resolution medical CT scans. We assessed these measurements against established resolution standards61,62, which indicate that while sub-millimeter structures generally require resolutions near 100 μm for accurate quantification, the SF in both Mojokerto 1 and P. troglodytes newborns is approximately an order of magnitude larger, supporting the reliability of our measurements. We note that modality differences do not affect our interpretations for the following reasons. The SF value of the P. paniscus newborn (86036M8) measured with µCT closely matches the range observed in the two P. troglodytes newborns (19.1–20.5%) measured with high-resolution medical CT (Supplementary Data.xlsx). Excluding these CT-only specimens would remove key neonatal data and is therefore not informative for sensitivity testing. Likewise, excluding Mojokerto 1 would preclude any developmental interpretation, yet our SF measurement for this specimen aligns well with one previously published value20, confirming its reliability. The degree of opening of the SF in H. erectus (Mojokerto: 27.9%), P. robustus (KW 9000/9600: 7.2%) and A. afarensis (DIK-1-1: 8.6%) was determined as the average values measured on both the left and right sides, with no significant difference between the two sides in each specimen.

Skeletal and dental maturation

In addition to the degree of opening of the SF, we further assessed skeletal and dental maturation in comparative specimens of unknown age-at-death in African apes. We identified P. paniscus and P. troglodytes newborns when no teeth were erupted and/or the mandibular symphysis was widely patent (with sometimes a small secondary ossification center at the inferoanterior border of the symphysis), comparable to that described in H. sapiens62. We also recorded the formation of the deciduous and permanent teeth using a scale63 that includes the 16 successive developmental stages indicating the proportion of crown (Cr) and root (R) complete or resorbed. Using this approach provides a standardized framework for assessing developmental stage across both extant and fossil specimens. The 16 successive developmental stages are: no sign of crypt formation; empty crypt; Cri, cusp(s) initiated; Crco, cusps coalescence; Croc, crown outline complete; Cr1/2, crown 1/2; Cr3/4, crown 3/4; Crc, crown complete; Ri, root initiated; Cli, cleft initiated; R1/4, root 1/4; R1/2, root 1/2; R3/4, root 3/4; Rc, root complete; A, apex 1/2; Ac, apex complete. When applied to the M1, this scale allows direct comparison with the eruption and occlusion categories reported in Table 1 (e.g., “Neonates”) as follows: “Neonates”: the upper first permanent molar (M1) is before its crown outline completion (stage “Croc”); “Infants with the M1 before crown completion” (stage Crc); “Infants with the M1 at and after crown completion (stage Crc) but prior to M1 emergence” (approximately between root stages 1/2 and 3/4); “Older juveniles with the M1 just entering functional occlusion” (approximately between root stages 3/4 and complete). We deliberately do not provide chronological age estimates for these categories, as the timing of dental development is known to vary widely across populations within a species (e.g., H. sapiens) due to factors such as geographic origin, nutrition, and other environmental or genetic influences. Instead, these categories serve as standardized developmental benchmarks that allow meaningful comparisons across taxa without relying on potentially misleading absolute ages.

The percentage of adult mean endocranial volume (rECV)

To calculate the relative endocranial volume (rECV) for each infant or juvenile in our sample – that is, their endocranial volume expressed as a percentage of the adult mean – we used published values of adult female endocranial volumes specific to each species (338 ml in P. paniscus and 363 ml in P. troglodytes), as reported in references64,65. To the best of our knowledge, these sources offer the most methodologically consistent and statistically robust dataset available for extant primates. The mean adult ECV for H. sapiens was compiled from references67,68 by converting reported mean adult female brain mass (1227 g and 1262 g, respectively) into ECV, following the method outlined in reference69. Brain mass was estimated by multiplying ECV by 1.036, based on the assumed density of brain tissue in grams per cubic centimeter. The rECV ranges of the P. robustus (KW 9000/9600: 81.9–112%), A. afarensis (DIK-1-1: 51.9–75.9%) and H. erectus (Mojokerto 1: 70–87%) were taken from references15,26,58 respectively.

The percentage of adult mean facial growth (rFCS)

To evaluate facial growth in P. troglodytes and P. paniscus, we initially calculated the facial centroid size (FCS) for each juvenile specimen using eleven specific landmarks: nasospinale (NAS) – intersection of the inferior margin of the nasal aperture with the midsagittal plane; prosthion (PRO) – midline of the maxillary alveolar arch, between the upper central incisors on the buccal alveolar margin; orale (ORA) – inferior opening of the incisive canal, on its posterior edge and in the midsagittal plane; alare (ALA) – most lateral extremity of the nasal aperture in a transverse plane; lowest margin of the main (if multiple) infraorbital foramen (IFO); zygomaticomaxillary suture’s lowest extent (zygomaxillare) (ZYG); buccal (BIC) and lingual (LIC) alveolar margin between the lateral incisor and canine; buccal alveolar margin between the canine and first deciduous molar (BCm); buccal alveolar margin between the first and second deciduous molars (m1-2); most lateral point on the lingual alveolar margin, between the mesial and distal extremities of the fist deciduous molar (Lm1), as shown in Figure S2. One of us (JB) wrote the following code in R to obtain the FCS from the 3D coordinates of each specimen:

for (i in 1:nrow(coordinates)) {.

distance <- sqrt(sum((coordinates[i, ] - centroid)^2))

squared_distances <- c(squared_distances, distance^2).}

FCS <- sqrt(sum(squared_distances)).

Subsequently, we derived rFCS values by randomly selecting five adult females for each species (from the CT database in the Centre for Anthropobiology and Genomics of Toulouse, which includes only a limited number of adult individuals). The resulting values were as follows: (i) P. troglodytes: mean of 76.8 and one standard deviation of 4.6, with a range of 69.0-80.3 and a coefficient of variation of 6.0% (MRAC 8341: 80.3 - MRAC 9655: 79.3 - MRAC 9931: 79.1 - MRAC 10448: 69.0 - MRAC 83006M34: 76.1); (ii) P. paniscus: mean of 64.5 and one standard deviation of 6.9, with a range of 57.8–70.2 and a coefficient of variation of 6.9% (MRAC 11351: 64.0 - MRAC 13201: 57.8 - MRAC 15296: 65.2 - MRAC 20882: 65.2 - MRAC 21697: 70.2). The low coefficients of variation in both groups (under 7%) indicate limited intra-specific variability in adult rFCS. Given this relatively tight clustering, we judged that a sample size of n = 5 per species was adequate to reliably estimate species-specific adult means for normalization. While larger samples are always preferable, our goal was not to assess within-species variability per se, but rather to generate stable adult reference values for computing rFCS in juvenile individuals. In this context, the observed low dispersion, combined with random selection of individuals and consistent data acquisition methods, supports the methodological sufficiency of our approach.

Endocranial and facial growth trajectories

We applied three nonlinear methods – polynomial functions, Gompertz models and natural cubic splines – to determine the optimal relationship (i.e., the model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion – AIC (Table S2) – between SF and either rECV (Figs. 2 and 3) or rFCS (Fig. 2), treated as the dependent variable. The AIC evaluates models by balancing fit quality with complexity, where a lower AIC value indicates a better model choice. The model with the lowest AIC was considered to achieve the best predictions (Table S2). This analysis was conducted separately for P. paniscus, P. troglodytes, and H. sapiens (Fig. 3). To address the relatively small sample size for H. sapiens infants (n = 12), we conducted a parametric bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates of the Gompertz growth model to evaluate the stability and precision of the estimated parameters given the sample size. To characterize and compare postnatal growth trajectories of relative endocranial volume (rECV) across taxa as a function of SF, we employed a three-parameter Gompertz growth model. This sigmoidal model comprises: (i) the asymptotic size (a), representing the upper limit of rECV and corresponding to the mature or adult value; (ii) the scaling parameter (b), which shifts the curve along the FS axis and determines the inflection point, where the growth rate is maximal; and (iii) the growth rate constant (k), which dictates the steepness of the curve as it approaches the asymptote. Parameters were estimated independently for each taxon; H. sapiens, P. paniscus, and P. troglodytes. Parameter estimation used bounded non-linear least squares (nlsLM() from the minpack.lm R package), with biologically informed constraints: a ≥ 0, b ≥ 0, and 0 ≤ k ≤ 1. Model robustness for all growth curve analyses was assessed using residual bootstrapping with 1000 iterations. At each iteration, residuals were resampled with replacement, added to the fitted values, and the model was refit. Predicted growth curves and 95% confidence intervals, derived from these residual bootstrap replicates, are shown for each figure. To compare growth rates across taxa, we fitted a combined model allowing k to vary by taxon and tested for significance using a likelihood ratio test against a nested model with a shared slope. We also computed profile likelihood-based confidence intervals on taxon-specific k estimates to evaluate non-overlap.

Covariations between the opening of SF and dental development

We used logistic regression to obtain probability curves that illustrate the covariation between the SF opening and M1 development before Crc or Ri; in both P. paniscus and P. troglodytes (Fig. 1). We focused on P. troglodytes and P. paniscus for the logistic regression models because, within the 12 human infant specimens available, only a few had sufficiently preserved facial skeletons and M1s to allow reliable modeling. This limited preservation prevented us from performing similar analyses in the human sample. Before building the regression, we randomly split our data into training and test samples with equal proportions. We used the R package “caret” (“Classification and Regression Training”; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=caret) to make sure that the training data had approximately equal proportions of classes (e.g., M1s at Crc or before versus M1s beyond Crc). We then used the R packages “popbio” (“Construction and Analysis of Matrix Population Model”; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=popbio) and “effects” (“Effect Displays for Linear, Generalized Linear, and Other Models”; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=effects) to build the logistic regression model.
