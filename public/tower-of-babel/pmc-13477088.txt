Predictors of long-term survival after pulmonary endarterectomy
Authors: Ahmed K Awad, Shawn Shah, David Moros, Kamal S Ayyat, Anand Mehta, Patrick Collier, Gustavo A Heresi, Nicholas G Smedira, Michael Z Tong, Haytham Elgharably
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients undergoing pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) have successful early to mid-term outcomes; however, they may not have consistent long-term follow-up. We aimed to examine the long-term survival and predictors of outcomes after PTE.MethodsBetween 2008 and 2022, 308 patients with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) underwent PTE at a tertiary institution. Baseline, operative, and postoperative variables were obtained from the institutional database. Survival follow-up was completed and analyzed with Kaplan-Meier estimates. The median duration of follow-up was 4.5 years, and multivariable analysis was performed for long-term survival.ResultsAmong the 308 patients, 50% had moderate or more right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, and preoperative mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) was 43 mm Hg (15th-18th percentile, 31-55 mm Hg). The median circulatory arrest time during PTE was 35 minutes (15th-18th percentile, 23-49.7 minutes). Fifteen percent of the patients underwent concomitant tricuspid valve repair, while 5.5% had a reoperation. Operative mortality was 2.3%, and 7% of patients required extracorporeal membrane oxygenation after PTE. Postoperative mPAP was reduced to 26 mm Hg (15th-18th percentile, 19-35.5 mm Hg). Survival rates after PTE were 97% at 1 year, 90.4% at 5 years, and 79% at 8 years. The study cohort was stratified into 4 groups by preoperative RV dysfunction grade (normal, mild, moderate, and severe), and there was no significant difference in survival among the groups up to 5 years (P = .95). The prevalence of moderate or more RV dysfunction decreased for up to 3 years after surgery, then slowly rebounded. Preoperative higher oxygen requirement, pulmonary artery wedge pressure, and postoperative mPAP were independent predictors of long-term mortality. Interestingly, baseline RV function was not associated with long-term survival.ConclusionsThe degree of preoperative RV dysfunction did not influence long-term survival after PTE. However, postoperative mPAP was associated with worse survival, suggesting the need for close follow-up of these patients to monitor compliance, pulmonary vascular or RV remodeling, and progression of CTEPH.Key Words: pulmonary thromboendarterectomy, right ventricular dysfunction, pulmonary artery pressure

Predictors of long-term survival after PTE.

Central Message

The degree of preoperative right ventricular dysfunction did not influence long-term survival after pulmonary thromboendarterectomy. However, postoperative mean pulmonary artery pressure was associated with worse survival, suggesting the need for close follow-up of these patients.

Perspective

Complete long-term follow-up demonstrates that postoperative hemodynamic values, not baseline right ventricular dysfunction, determine survival after pulmonary thromboendarterectomy. Early referral to a chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension center and systematic surveillance are essential.

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) develops secondary to unresolved thromboembolic obstruction of the pulmonary arteries, often following an acute pulmonary embolism.1,2 The underlying pathophysiology involves ongoing inflammation and fibrosis of the pulmonary arterial tree endothelium, leading to increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), pulmonary hypertension (PH), and eventually right heart failure.3 Diagnosis of CTEPH requires a high index of suspicion. The initial screening test of choice is ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy, which, if abnormal, should be followed by confirmatory imaging, such as computed tomography pulmonary angiography and right heart catheterization.2, 3, 4 CTEPH treatment depends on whether the patient is a surgical candidate for the potentially curative pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) procedure.1 Evaluation of candidates for PTE is based on the level of thromboembolic disease, PVR, right ventricular (RV) function, and comorbidities. Recent studies have shown that PVR is a significant predictor of long- term survival after PTE.5, 6, 7 Cannon and colleagues5 showed that PVR >425 dynes.s.cm post-PTE surgery is associated with worse long-term survival.

PTE surgery can successfully reduce PVR without any significant change in RV dysfunction grade. Moreover, several studies have demonstrated significant improvements in RV ejection fraction (RVEF), volumes, and mass after PTE.8, 9, 10 This recovery may be only partial, however, and long-term RV remodeling remains incompletely characterized. Notably, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), an echocardiographic index of RV longitudinal function, has well-established limitations after surgeries involving pericardiotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass. Specifically, TAPSE is significantly reduced following cardiac surgery owing to alterations in RV contraction patterns after pericardial opening and cardiopulmonary bypass, independent of true RV systolic dysfunction.11

Despite the established principle that RV function improves following successful PVR reduction after PTE, late referral to specialized CTEPH centers may result in patients presenting with significant preoperative RV dysfunction. Whether the severity of preoperative RV dysfunction—rather than its mere presence—independently impacts long-term survival after PTE remains an important and incompletely answered question.12 Limited data are available regarding its prognostic significance, particularly beyond the mid-term and with systematic long-term echocardiographic follow-up. Moreover, systematic long-term follow-up can be challenging for these patients. Even in the most recent large CTEPH registry reports, the follow-up beyond mid-term was limited.13,14 In the present single-center study, we examined the predictors of long-term mortality after PTE including the influence of preoperative RV dysfunction in a cohort with complete survival follow-up.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study included all patients who underwent PTE surgery for CTEPH at our institution between January 1, 2008 and December 30, 2022. The study was approved by our Institutional Review Board (#21-293) and followed the STROBE guidelines. Patients who urgently underwent a PTE procedure for acute indications in the absence of a CTEPH diagnosis were excluded. Patient demographics, echocardiography data, right heart catheterization data, and longitudinal clinical outcomes were abstracted from the medical record. A comprehensive assessment of RV function was performed by a certified cardiac sonographer within 2 weeks before surgery according to echocardiography guidelines.15 RV dysfunction was graded using a multiparametric approach incorporating qualitative visual assessment along with quantitative parameters, including TAPSE and RVEF. Postoperative mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) was recorded from pulmonary catheter data on the last day of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (average of 3 time points recorded). All data were anonymized and are reported in aggregate.

Our technique for PTE, especially for reaching up to subsegmental pulmonary branches (Figure 1), has been described elsewhere.16 We have a low threshold for repair of tricuspid regurgitation in cases of severe regurgitation or a dilated tricuspid annulus >4 cm. Regarding PFO, if it is large enough to cause shunting, we tend to close it to avoid hypoxemia in the early postoperative period. Since 2011, patient selection and perioperative care of CTEPH patients undergoing PTE at our institution have been managed by a multidisciplinary team including cardiothoracic surgeons, PH specialists, interventional radiologists, and vascular medicine specialists,17 showing an increase in our center’s PTE case volume (Figure E1).

Statistical Analysis and Follow-up

Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, with continuous data reported as median (15th to 85th percentile) when data are not normally distributed and as mean and standard deviation (SD) when data are normally distributed. Data are presented as hazard ratio (HR) or odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) where appropriate. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, in which only patients with available data for each specific variable were included in the corresponding analyses. Multivariable analysis Cox proportional hazard regression was performed to assess factors associated with long-term mortality (Table E1). A multivariable Cox proportional hazard model was constructed using stepwise selection with both forward and backward elimination. Long-term mortality was defined as death at the end of the follow-up period. The median follow-up was 4.5 years (15th-85th percentile, 2.7-7.2 years). Survival rates were assessed by reviewing medical charts and arbitrary online information, then completed by phone calls to ensure complete survival data up to the end of 2024. The available number of patients for all variables was included in all tables of descriptive and multivariable analyses as well as longitudinal analyses at all time points.

The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess linear relationships between continuous variables. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. To account for potential confounding, adjusted survival analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for age and sex. Postoperative mPAP was modeled as a continuous variable using cubic B-splines to capture potential nonlinear associations with mortality risk. HRs were calculated relative to the postoperative mPAP values, with 95% CIs estimated using standard errors from the Cox regression model. A P value <.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient Characteristics and Early Outcomes of PTE Surgery

A total of 308 patients with CTEPH who underwent PTE between 2008 and 2022 were included in the analysis. The median age was 53 (15th-18th percentile, 36-69) years, and 54% were male (Table E2). Body mass index (BMI) was >30 in 65% of patients and >40 in 22%. A history of heart failure was reported in 78%, and 52% were on supplemental oxygen prior to surgery. The median preoperative mPAP was 43 (15th-18th percentile, 31-55) mm Hg, and median PVR was 4.4 (15th-18th percentile, 2-9.85) Wood units. There was trend toward an association between preoperative oxygen requirement and mPAP (r = 0.31; P = .05), although this did not reach statistical significance (Figure E2). Notably, 37% of patients had a PVR >8 Wood units. On preoperative echocardiogram, 20% had moderate and 31% had severe RV dysfunction (Table E2).

Correlation between preoperative mean pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) and oxygen requirement before pulmonary thromboendarterectomy surgery. Gray shading represents the 95% confidence limits. RV, Right ventricular.

To further characterize the impact of preoperative RV dysfunction, the cohort was divided into 4 groups: normal, mild, moderate, and severe RV dysfunction (Table E3). There was significant difference in age and sex among the 4 groups, with more older patients in the normal RV function group (median, 60 years; 15th-18th percentile, 42-73 years; P < .001) and more females (71%; P < .001) and patients with a history of heart failure (37%; P < .001) in the severe RV dysfunction group. The median preoperative mPAP (53 mm Hg; 15th-18th percentile, 39-62 mm Hg) and PVR (9.8 Wood units; 15th-18th percentile, 5.8-15.6 Wood units) were highest in the severe RV dysfunction group (P < .001) (Figure E3). The Fick cardiac index was lowest in the severe RV dysfunction group (median, 1.9 L/min/m2; 15th-18th percentile, 1.5-2.4 L/min/m2).

The median circulatory arrest time was 35 (15th-18th percentile, 23-49.7) minutes. Concomitant procedures were performed in 37% of the cohort (Table E4), with 17% requiring tricuspid valve intervention (either repair or replacement) for moderate or greater tricuspid insufficiency. Seventeen cases were reoperations, including 4 prior PTEs. Postoperative mPAP was reduced to 26 (15th-18th percentile, 19-35.5) mm Hg. Postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) was required in 7%. The rates of reexploration for bleeding and renal dysfunction were 3% and 6%, respectively, while 35% of the patients required prolonged ventilation (>48 hours). The median hospital length of stay was 12 (15th-18th percentile, 6-26.1) days, and operative mortality was 2.3% (Table 1). Postoperative hemodynamic analyses showed significant reductions of mPAP in all RV dysfunction groups (P = .003) (Figure 2). Postoperative mPAP was highest in the severe RV dysfunction group (median, 30 mm Hg; 15th-18th percentile, 20-39 mm Hg; P = .008) (Table E3).

Survival After PTE Surgery

The survival rates after PTE were 97% at 1 year, 90.4% at 5 years, and 79% at 8 years (Figure E4). Stratifying our cohort based on preoperative RV dysfunction grade, the survival rates after PTE were 100% at 1 year, 97% at 3 years, and 92% at 5 years in the normal RV function group; 95%, 91%, and 82%, respectively, in the mild RV dysfunction group; 93%, 85%, and 85%, respectively, in the moderate RV dysfunction group; and 94.5%, 92%, and 83%, respectively, in the severe RV dysfunction group (Figure 3). There was no significant difference in survival across the 4 RV dysfunction groups either before adjustment (P = .953) or after adjustment (P = .232) of baseline characteristics (Figure 3, Figure E5). For instance, there was no significant difference in survival between the normal RV function and severe RV dysfunction groups before adjustment (HR, 0.910; 95% CI, 0.269-3.069; P = .879), and there remained no significant difference after adjustment (HR, 0.924; 95% CI, 0.448-1.906; P = .832).

In the multivariable analysis for predictors of long-term mortality, higher preoperative BMI was associated with reduced risk of mortality (HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.73- 0.99; P = .042). Higher preoperative higher oxygen requirement (HR, 2.1; 95% CI, 1.16-3.94; P = .014) and elevated mean pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) (HR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.01-1.29; P = .035) and postoperative mPAP (HR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.06-1.28; P = .001) were associated with an increased risk of long-term mortality (Table E5). Postoperative mPAP >29 mm Hg was associated with worse survival (Figure E6).

Evolution of RV Function, Hemodynamics, and BMI After PTE Surgery

In the patients with available follow-up echocardiograms, there was a slightly increased prevalence of mild and moderate RV dysfunction in the immediate postoperative period, along with a decreased prevalence of severe RV dysfunction (Figure 4). Between 6 months and 3 years, there was steady decrease in the prevalence of moderate and severe RV dysfunction; however, there was a slow rebound after 3 years. During postoperative follow-up, the median PVR decreased significantly, to 2.7 (15th-18th percentile, 1.9-4.4) Wood units at 6 months, 2.3 (15th-18th percentile, 1.7-3.8) Wood units at 1 year, and 2.0 (15th-18th percentile, 1.7-2.9) Wood units at 5 years (P = .036) (Figure E7, A). The FICK CI improved after surgery and remained stable during follow-up in patients with available hemodynamic data: median value, 2.6 (15th-18th percentile, 2.1-3.3) at 6 months, 2.8 (15th-18th percentile, 2.3-3.5) at 1 year, and 2.4 (15th-18th percentile, 2.0-2.9) at 5 years (P = .26) (Figure E7, B). The median mPAP decreased significantly following PTE, from 44 mm Hg preoperatively to 25 mm Hg at 30 days postoperatively, with values remaining reduced at 6 months (28 mm Hg) and 1 year (29 mm Hg). mPAP then showed a gradual increase at 3 years (30 mm Hg) and 5 years (35 mm Hg), but never returned to preoperative levels (Figure E7, C). There was no significant change in the BMI after PTE in any group (Figure E8).

Discussion

Survival After PTE

Patients with CTEPH are a heterogenous group with various degrees of PH, RV dysfunction, and level of thromboembolic disease, in addition to a range of comorbidities. Thorough assessment of CTEPH through specialized multidisciplinary team at experienced centers is key for successful outcomes.3,13,18 Our group has demonstrated improved outcomes after PTE, including survival and resolution of PH, after establishment of a team-based approach to evaluating patients with CTEPH at our center.13 The primary target of the CTEPH multidisciplinary team evaluation is the proper selection of surgical candidates for PTE, which remains the gold standard therapy that provides survival benefits and improved quality of life.3,13,14,19 The estimated survival rate at 3 years after PTE historically has been ∼90%14,18 but has improved to 94% in the most recent report from the international CTEPH registry during the period of 2015-2016 compared to 2007-2012.14 The 3-year survival of 95% in our cohort is similar to the international experience.

Impact of Baseline RV Function on Survival

Patients presenting with moderate to severe RV dysfunction for PTE are carefully assessed for thromboembolic disease level and feasibility of surgical extraction, as failure to reduce mPAP can lead to a complicated postoperative course. As expected, moderate and severe RV dysfunction were associated with higher preoperative mPAP and PVR but a lower Fick cardiac index. There were no significant differences in other baseline characteristics except for age and sex; these groups were younger and included more females. Postoperative mPAP was significantly reduced compared to preoperative values in all groups. Interestingly, RV dysfunction was not a risk factor for long-term mortality. Moreover, there were no statistically significant differences in survival among the groups up to 5 years. During that period, in patients with available follow-up echocardiograms, longitudinal analysis of RV function showed significant improvement in RV function within 6 months after PTE, which continued to improve up to 3 years, followed by a slight increase.

After PTE, Reesink and colleagues20 reported restoration of RV remodeling using magnetic resonance imaging in 17 patients before and at least 4 months after PTE surgery. Waziri and colleagues21 showed that at 1 year after PTE, RVEF improved from 30% to 44% overall but remained lower compared to healthy controls with RV global longitudinal strain remaining impaired. Moreover, Braams and colleagues22 have shown that after 6 months of PTE, RV contractility did not change during exercise, suggesting that RV dysfunction may persist under stress conditions. Kianzad and colleagues10 found significant improvements in RV function as measured by RVEF, mPAP, and PAWP within 6 months post-PTE, with no substantial changes between 6 months and 18 months. The University of California San Diego group’s survival analysis of 1410 patients who underwent PTE using the Social Security Death Index found survival rates of 82% at 5 years and 75% at 10 years23; however, that report did not include longitudinal echocardiographic or hemodynamic data during long-term follow-up. Furthermore, even in the most recent large CTEPH registry reports,13,14 the follow-up survival data at mid-term (4-5 years) after PTE were limited, in contrast to our cohort. Our available echocardiographic data suggest improvement in RV remodeling early after PTE and up to mid-term follow-up, but data for long-term RV remodeling are scarce. These findings suggest that patients with moderate or severe RV dysfunction can undergo successful PTE that reduces mPAP without an impact on survival and with possible RV reverse remodeling in the mid-term. However, long-term follow-up of RV function after PTE is necessary to characterize the evolution of RV reverse remodeling.

Predictors of Survival After PTE Surgery

Risk factors for mortality after PTE include such perioperative factors as high preoperative PVR, right heart failure, renal dysfunction, and severe RV dysfunction, as well as long-term factors related to residual PH and hemodynamic response to surgery.24,25 Multiple studies have identified postoperative PVR as a critical predictor of mortality, with Mayer and colleagues26 reporting PVR at ICU discharge as an independent risk factor for in-hospital death and 1-year mortality and Skoro-Sajer and colleagues27 identifying it as the most important predictor of long-term survival. The CTEPH registry highlights additional risk factors, including New York Heart Association functional class IV, increased right atrial pressure, postoperative PH, and surgical complications,28 with the 2024 update identifying age, prior heart failure hospitalization, PVR at diagnosis and ICU discharge, and postoperative ECMO as predictors of mortality.14 Chin and colleagues14 also reported World Health Organization functional class IV, mPAP, and PVR as significant risk factors. In our cohort, postoperative PVR was not calculated consistently, possibly explaining why it did not emerge as an independent risk factor for long-term survival.

Our multivariable analysis identified higher postoperative mPAP (>29 mm Hg) as a predictor of mortality, along with a higher oxygen requirement before PTE, which correlates with higher mPAP. Despite the significant decrease in mPAP after PTE compared to preoperative mPAP, and low operative mortality of 2.3%,3,18 some patients had mild PH (mPAP >25 mm Hg).29 On the other hand, recent evidence suggests a lower threshold for PH (mPAP >20 mm Hg), which is associated with worse outcomes, but these studies were not specific for post-PTE mPAP.30 In a report from the United Kingdom, Cannon and colleagues5 identified postoperative mPAP ≥30 mm Hg and PVR ≥425 dynes·s/cm5 as associated with worse long-term survival after PTE. Age and postoperative systolic mPAP have been identified as independent predictors of long-term survival, with better survival rates seen in patients with a postoperative systolic PAP <46 mm Hg.24 Multiple factors can result in recurrent CTEPH after PTE, such as progressive remodeling of the microscopic pulmonary vasculature, noncompliance with anticoagulation therapy, persistent underlying risk factors (eg, hypercoagulable state), recurrent thromboembolic events, and, most importantly, loss to follow-up.31,32

These findings highlight the crucial importance of very close follow-up for these patients to address any changes or challenges that may occur, to avoid worse outcomes. It is important to distinguish between true recurrent CTEPH—defined by new or recurrent thromboembolic obstruction—and persistent or progressive PH following technically successful PTE. The latter may result from residual small-vessel arteriopathy, progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling, or inadequate thrombus clearance and represents a distinct entity with different prognostic and therapeutic implications. Persistent PH after PTE may be amenable to pulmonary vasodilator therapy or balloon pulmonary angioplasty, whereas true recurrence typically requires anticoagulation optimization and consideration of redo PTE in selected cases. In our cohort, we used postoperative mPAP as a surrogate of residual hemodynamic burden without distinguishing between these mechanisms.

Interestingly, elevated mean PAWP prior to surgery was associated with long-term mortality in our study analysis. Elevated PAWP could be related to left-sided heart lesion, atrial arrythmias, or volume overload in some patients who underwent urgent surgery with limited time for preoperative optimization. In a study of 593 patients with CTEPH, 332 of whom underwent PTE, Gerges and colleagues33 showed that elevated left ventricular filling pressure (mean PAWP >15 mm Hg) was associated with worse long-term survival. Patients with preoperative elevated PAWP need a thorough evaluation of their volume status or associated left-sided heart pathology, as elevated PAWP in CTEPH may indicate a mixed hemodynamic phenotype combining postcapillary and precapillary PH—a combination associated with worse outcomes independent of PVR. This likely reflects underlying left heart dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, or volume overload rather than purely postembolic disease, as evidenced by the concomitant procedures performed in our cohort, including mitral valve surgery, the Maze procedure, and left atrial appendage closure. Thus, careful preoperative identification and optimization of these left-sided conditions is essential for CTEPH patients being considered for PTE. Additionally, in our analysis, lower preoperative BMI was associated with long-term mortality after PTE. Consistent with a recent retrospective review of a large CTEPH cohort demonstrating superior survival in patients with BMI 30 to 39 compared to all other BMI groups.34 Other studies have affirmed that high BMI groups have postoperative outcomes at least as good as normal BMI groups, often achieving greater improvements in functional measures of quality of life.35,36 One possible explanation is that otherwise healthy patients with lower BMI may not seek medical care until they develop symptoms of severe PH or RV dysfunction of advanced CTEPH, which may impact their survival. In contrast, patients with higher BMI tend to have lower baseline reserve, such that lifestyle-limiting symptoms develop early after the first few thromboembolic events.37,38

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. As a single tertiary center retrospective study, it is inherently susceptible to many confounding factors along with potential selection bias owing to institution-specific patient selection and management. In addition, our single-institution experience might not be generalizable for all centers. Moreover, not all patients routinely underwent right heart catheterization or echocardiography in the postoperative period, which affected the availability of the data in the long term. In addition, methods for assessing RV function by echocardiography have changed significantly over the study period, which might affect the consistency in diagnosing RV dysfunction. The modest increase in PVR observed at the 3-year follow-up warrants cautious interpretation, given the decreasing number of patients with available hemodynamic data at later time points, which may introduce survivor bias. Postoperative imaging (ie, V/Q scintigraphy, CTPA, and DSA) was not performed systematically, precluding assessment of residual thromboembolic disease. Consequently, elevated postoperative mPAP values cannot be attributed mechanistically to incomplete surgical clearance or small-vessel arteriopathy—2 entities with distinct prognoses and management strategies. Another limitation is that data on post-PTE adjunct therapies, including pulmonary vasodilators (eg, riociguat, PDE5 inhibitors, ERAs) and balloon pulmonary angioplasty, were not systematically available. The use of mPAP value on the last ICU day as a predictor of survival carries inherent limitations, as early postoperative hemodynamics may be influenced by reperfusion pulmonary edema, RV stunning, and volume status. Finally, the association between higher BMI and reduced mortality should be interpreted cautiously. We could not adjust for obesity-related comorbidities (eg, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, obstructive sleep apnea), and we cannot exclude the possibility that higher BMI was a surrogate for earlier symptom recognition and referral.

Conclusions

Preoperative oxygen requirements, PAWP, and BMI, but not RV dysfunction, predicted long-term mortality after PTE. Candidates with accessible thromboembolic disease should undergo PTE regardless of RV function, with careful preoperative assessment of left-heart disease and volume status. Postoperative mPAP was the strongest predictor of mortality, necessitating close hemodynamic surveillance for timely intervention with medical therapy or balloon pulmonary angioplasty. The etiology of residual pulmonary hypertension, whether from late referral, pulmonary vascular remodeling, noncompliance, or CTEPH recurrence, remains unclear. Early referral to specialized CTEPH centers and systematic long-term follow-up are critical for optimizing outcomes.
