Antibiotic utilization pattern and its associated factors based on WHO AWaRe classification at a university hospital in the South of Thailand
Authors: Sukrit Sawangpon, Saowalak Intasarat, Husna Chehtae, Slinna Chanmanee, Aunchittha Piromrak, Tanavij Pannoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAntibiotic overuse accelerates antimicrobial resistance (AMR), posing a major global health threat. The World Health Organization (WHO) AWaRe classification promotes rational antibiotic use, recommending that at least 60% of antibiotic consumption belongs to the Access group. This study evaluated antibiotic prescribing patterns and factors associated with Watch/Reserve antibiotic use in a Thai tertiary hospital.MethodsA cross-sectional study using longitudinal hospital databases (2021–2023) analyzed 28,892 antibiotic prescriptions from adult patients. Antibiotics were classified according to the 2023 WHO AWaRe framework. Antibiotic utilisation was quantified using Defined Daily Doses (DDDs), and generalised linear mixed-effects models were used to identify factors associated with Watch/Reserve versus Access antibiotic prescribing.ResultsAccess antibiotics remained the predominant prescribing category, accounting for 69.2%, 58.9%, and 61.8% of prescriptions in 2021, 2022, and 2023, respectively, while Reserve antibiotics accounted for <0.5% annually. Watch antibiotic utilisation increased over time and exceeded Access antibiotics among hospitalised Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) beneficiaries in 2023. Female sex, older age, and higher Charlson Comorbidity Index were independently associated with Watch/Reserve prescribing. Universal Health Coverage (UHC) beneficiaries had significantly higher odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics.ConclusionsAlthough Access antibiotics predominated, increasing Watch antibiotic use highlights opportunities to strengthen hospital-wide antimicrobial stewardship. Routine AWaRe-based monitoring may facilitate benchmarking and promote more rational antibiotic prescribing across healthcare settings.

Background

Antibiotics are widely prescribed in medical practice worldwide, contributing to a continuous rise in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among bacterial pathogens. This growing threat poses serious consequences for humans, animals, and the environment. In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that approximately 700,000 people globally die each year from drug-resistant infections. Without urgent interventions, deaths attributable to antimicrobial resistance could reach 10 million annually by 2050 (Talaat et al., 2022). Recent estimates indicate that antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains a major public health threat in Thailand. In 2021, approximately 13,900 deaths were attributable to AMR, while 55,700 deaths were associated with drug-resistant infections nationwide (IHME, 2023). The estimated economic burden ranged from 2.4 to 5.8 billion Thai Baht per year (Sumpradit et al., 2021).

In response to this alarming situation, the WHO introduced the AWaRe classification, a tool designed to monitor and preliminarily screen antibiotic prescriptions to assess trends in potentially inappropriate antibiotic use. Developed in 2017 by the WHO Expert Committee, the classification draws on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines and categorises antibiotics into three groups – Access, Watch, and Reserved (Sharland et al., 2018). The AWaRe classification is globally used by researchers, policymakers, and healthcare professionals to promote rational antibiotic use, strengthen antimicrobial stewardship, and reduce resistance rates (Budd et al., 2019; Mudenda, Nsofu, et al., 2023; Nsojo et al., 2024; Patel et al., 2022). Consequently, the WHO 13th General Programme of Work 2019–2023 recommended that at least 60% of all antibiotics used in each country be from the Access group (Mudenda, Nsofu, et al., 2023) Given this context, surveillance of antibiotic resistance and monitoring trends in inappropriate antibiotic use in hospitals should begin with the implementation of a screening tool that utilises large-scale prescription databases.

In Thailand, the WHO AWaRe classification has been applied in a nationwide point-prevalence survey of antibiotic use among hospitalised patients, demonstrating its value for benchmarking antibiotic prescribing and supporting antimicrobial stewardship across hospitals. However, existing Thai studies have primarily focused on cross-sectional inpatient surveillance, while longitudinal trends in antibiotic utilisation, outpatient prescribing, and potential variations across health insurance schemes remain poorly understood. The present study addresses these knowledge gaps by evaluating longitudinal antibiotic utilisation according to the WHO AWaRe classification in a tertiary care hospital and examining factors associated with Watch/Reserve antibiotic prescribing (Anugulruengkitt et al., 2023).

In addition, patients’ characteristics associated with the pattern of AWaRe antibiotic use have not yet been studied, particularly in Thailand, where people are subsidised for antibiotics by different health insurance schemes, including self-pay. Thailand's healthcare system comprises multiple insurance schemes with different reimbursement mechanisms. The Universal Coverage Scheme (UHC) primarily uses capitation and global budgets, whereas the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) largely operates under fee-for-service reimbursement. These differences may create varying incentives for healthcare utilisation and prescribing practices, potentially contributing to disparities in antibiotic use across insurance schemes (Sakulbumrungsil et al., 2020; Tangcharoensathien et al., 2011).

Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic has also affected antibiotic prescribing patterns globally (Koh et al., 2025; Stevens et al., 2024). Although bacterial co-infections were relatively uncommon, antibiotics were frequently prescribed during the pandemic, and subsequent changes in healthcare utilisation may have influenced antibiotic consumption patterns. In addition, the present study was conducted at a tertiary university hospital established in 2017, which continued to expand its clinical services and patient volume during the study period. Consequently, temporal changes in antibiotic utilisation between 2021 and 2023 may reflect both the gradual normalisation of healthcare utilisation following the COVID-19 pandemic and the ongoing maturation of the hospital. Evaluating antibiotic prescribing during this period therefore provides important evidence to inform antimicrobial stewardship in a rapidly evolving healthcare environment.

Regarding previous studies, health insurance coverage is deemed a risk of health inequity, including access to medication (National Healthcare Quality & Disparities Reports, 2021). Moreover, many studies revealed that uninsured or self-paid people can increase or decrease antibiotic prescribing, while studying the interaction between health insurance status and other factors was not examined (Kim et al., 2023). Therefore, this study aims to examine antibiotic usage patterns and to assess the association between patients’ factors, including health insurance status, and the likelihood of receiving Watch and Reserved antibiotics compared to Access antibiotics based on the WHO AWaRe classification.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study with a 3-year prescription database was conducted at a University Medical Center (UMC) in the South of Thailand. This UMC, as a public tertiary hospital, was established in 2017. Ethical consideration was approved by Walailak University Ethical Committee with the approval code: WUEC-24-188-01.

Study population

Eligible participants included patients aged 19 years and older who received care and were prescribed at least one systemic antibiotic between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2023. Their prescriptions were excluded if they (1) were prescribed topical antibiotics only, (2) had incomplete demographic data (sex, age, health insurance scheme, or department of care), (3) had incomplete antibiotic prescription data (dose, strength, prescription date, or route of administration), or (4) received prescriptions that did not contain any antibiotics. The minimum sample size was estimated using Cochran's formula with an expected antibiotic use prevalence of 53%, based on a point-prevalence survey conducted in 41 hospitals in Thailand (Anugulruengkitt et al., 2023). Assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, at least 383 participants were required. However, as this was a retrospective database study, all eligible prescriptions recorded between 2021 and 2023 were included without sampling, resulting in a final analytical sample of 28,892 prescriptions.

Data collection

Patient data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records and prescription databases. Variables collected included demographic characteristics (sex, date of birth, occupation, weight, height, and health insurance scheme), clinical characteristics (department, patient status, diagnosis codes, and diagnosis date), and prescription details (medication name, strength, package unit, quantity dispensed, prescription date, and prescription number). Health insurance scheme was treated as a fixed covariate because all participants remained under a single insurance scheme throughout follow-up.

Patient comorbidity burden was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). CCI scores were derived from all ICD-10 diagnosis codes available for each patient in the hospital database using the validated Quan ICD-10 coding algorithm implemented in the R package comorbidity. Individual comorbidity weights were summed according to the original Charlson weighting system to obtain a patient-level CCI score, which was subsequently linked to all eligible antibiotic prescriptions. Thus, CCI represented cumulative comorbidity history available during the study period rather than diagnoses recorded only at the time of an individual prescription.

Antibiotics were classified according to the 2023 World Health Organization (WHO) AWaRe classification system (Sharland et al., 2018) into three categories: Access, Watch, and Reserved. The Access group included first- and second-line antibiotics with high efficacy and low resistance potential; the Watch group consisted of broader-spectrum antibiotics with higher resistance risk; and the Reserved group comprised last-resort antibiotics for infections resistant to other treatments.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the pattern of antibiotic use, categorised by the proportion of prescriptions containing Access, Watch, and Reserve antibiotics. Antibiotic consumption was quantified using the Defined Daily Dose (DDD) metric. Outpatient antibiotic consumption was reported as DDD per 1000 outpatient visits per day, and inpatient consumption was reported as DDD per 1000 inpatient-days. The calculations followed WHO guidelines, using standard DDD values and population denominators (Methodology, 2025).

Antibiotic consumption was quantified separately for each active antibiotic ingredient using the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical/Defined Daily Dose (ATC/DDD) methodology. For each prescription, the total amount dispensed was calculated from the prescribed strength, dosage form, quantity supplied, and administration frequency, and subsequently converted into DDDs using the WHO-assigned DDD value for the corresponding antibiotic. Combination products containing more than one antimicrobial agent were classified according to the WHO ATC system and assigned the corresponding DDD value specified for the combination product. When a prescription contained multiple antibiotics, each antibiotic was treated as an independent exposure and contributed separately to the DDD calculation and AWaRe classification. Consequently, a single prescription could contribute to more than one AWaRe category if multiple antibiotics from different categories were prescribed concurrently. For analyses of factors associated with antibiotic selection, prescriptions were classified according to the highest AWaRe category prescribed (Reserve > Watch > Access), and prescriptions containing at least one Watch or Reserve antibiotic were categorised as Watch/Reserve in the binary outcome model.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. The proportion of DDD and the distribution of AWaRe categories were calculated with corresponding percentages. Factors associated with the likelihood of receiving Watch or Reserve antibiotics (versus Access antibiotics) were analyzed using a generalised linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with a logit link function, appropriate for binary outcomes. The model included patient-level characteristics (age, sex, comorbidity, and health insurance scheme) together with encounter-level variables (calendar year and hospital department) as fixed effects. A random intercept for patient ID was included to account for correlation among repeated prescriptions from the same individual. This approach is suitable for data structures involving repeated measures data, such as multiple prescriptions per patient.

Model estimation was performed using the lme4 package. Validation of the model was reported in the Supplemental Fig S1D. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Trend analyses were performed as descriptive analyses to evaluate annual changes in antibiotic utilisation over the three-year study period and should be interpreted cautiously because only three annual observations were available.

Because the Social Health Insurance (SHI) group contained very few observations during 2021–2022, sensitivity analyses excluding SHI were performed. Model convergence was assessed using optimiser convergence status, singularity diagnostics (isSingular), and agreement across multiple optimisation algorithms using the allFit () function. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted with R software version 4.3.3.

Results

Overall, 12,671 patients contributed 28,892 eligible antibiotic prescriptions during 2021–2023, with the mean number of prescriptions per patient increasing from 1.39 (SD 1.12) in 2021 to 2.20 (SD 3.53) in 2023. Female patients consistently accounted for approximately two-thirds of the study population (65.2%–68.0%). The 25–59-year age group comprised the largest proportion of patients overall (39.3%), followed by those aged 18–24 years (35.5%) and ≥60 years (25.1%). The outpatient department accounted for the largest proportion of representative antibiotic prescribing encounters (64.4%), although the proportion of inpatient encounters increased from 1.6% in 2021 to 13.7% in 2023.

Regarding health insurance, the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) was the most common scheme overall (31.8%), followed by Universal Health Coverage (26.5%) and self-payment (25.2%). The proportion of self-paying patients declined from 32.3% in 2021 to 18.4% in 2023, whereas the proportions covered by CSMBS and Private Insurance increased over the study period. The distribution of Charlson Comorbidity Index categories remained relatively stable, with approximately 90% of patients having a score of 1–2 (Table 1). Based on the 2023 WHO AWaRe classification, Access antibiotics consistently accounted for the largest share of both antibiotic prescribing and utilisation throughout the study period. Access antibiotics represented 69.2% of prescriptions in 2021, 58.9% in 2022, and 61.8% in 2023, whereas Watch antibiotics accounted for 30.8%, 40.9%, and 37.8%, respectively. Reserve antibiotics were not prescribed in 2021 and accounted for only 0.17% and 0.43% of prescriptions in 2022 and 2023, respectively (Supplemental Fig S1A).

Although the proportion of Access antibiotics declined below the WHO target of 60% in 2022, it exceeded the target again in 2023. Consistent with these prescribing patterns, DDD-based antibiotic utilisation increased over time, with Access antibiotics remaining the predominant category (Figure 1). Both outpatient utilisation (DDD per 1,000 outpatient visits) and inpatient utilisation (DDD per 1,000 inpatient-days) increased between 2021 and 2023, while Reserve antibiotics contributed only a negligible proportion of total antibiotic utilisation. Trend analyses of annual DDD-based utilisation showed significant increasing trends for outpatient Watch antibiotics (P-trend = 0.0105) and overall Access antibiotics (P-trend = 0.0093) during 2021–2023 (Supplemental Fig S1E). Among ATC third-level classes, significant increasing trends were observed for J01C (beta-lactam antibacterials and penicillins) (P-trend = 0.0147) and J01X (other antibacterials) (P-trend = 0.0303) in the outpatient setting. No statistically significant temporal trends were identified for inpatient AWaRe utilisation or for most inpatient ATC third-level antibiotic classes (Supplemental Fig S1E).

Regarding diagnostic patterns, diseases of the genitourinary system (ICD-10 N00-N99) constituted the most frequent indication for antibiotic prescribing. Within this category, other disorders of the urinary system (N39) accounted for the largest proportion of prescriptions, with Watch antibiotics prescribed more frequently than Access antibiotics in several insurance schemes, particularly among Unknown and CSMBS beneficiaries in 2022 and UHC beneficiaries in 2023 (Figure 3(b)).

In Table 2, after adjustment for demographic characteristics, comorbidity, hospital department, calendar year, and health insurance scheme, patients covered by the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) scheme had significantly higher odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics than University Beneficiaries (UB) (Model 2: OR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.74–4.05). In contrast, Social Health Insurance (SHI), Private Insurance (PI), and Self-payment (SP) were associated with significantly lower odds (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.91; OR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.03–0.10; and OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.37–0.83, respectively), whereas no significant associations were observed for the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) or Public Enterprise (PE). Female sex, older age, higher Charlson Comorbidity Index, and participation in the later study years (2022 and 2023) were independently associated with increased odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics. Among the interaction terms, only interactions involving Private Insurance were statistically significant. Compared with the emergency department, both outpatient (OR = 16.85, 95% CI: 7.63–37.19) and inpatient (OR = 35.19, 95% CI: 12.42–99.69) encounters among patients with Private Insurance showed markedly higher odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics, whereas interactions involving the other insurance schemes were not statistically significant.

The final generalised linear mixed-effects model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.9581, indicating excellent discrimination between prescriptions containing Watch/Reserve antibiotics and those containing Access antibiotics (Supplemental Fig S1D).

The interaction model converged successfully without evidence of singularity. Model diagnostics using allFit () demonstrated consistent parameter estimates across optimisation algorithms, with a maximum log-likelihood difference of only 0.0582. Furthermore, excluding the relatively small SHI category produced virtually identical effect estimates and statistical inferences, supporting the robustness of the primary findings (Supplemental Fig S1F).

Discussion

This study examined antibiotic utilisation patterns in a Thai tertiary care hospital between 2021 and 2023 using the WHO AWaRe classification and investigated factors associated with the use of Watch/Reserve antibiotics across patient characteristics, healthcare settings, and health insurance schemes. Overall, Access antibiotics remained the predominant category throughout the study period, accounting for 69.2% of prescriptions in 2021, declining to 58.9% in 2022, and increasing again to 61.8% in 2023. This finding aligns with the WHO's AWaRe recommendations by 2023, which emphasise that at least 60% of antibiotic consumption should fall under the Access category to minimise resistance risk while maintaining effective treatment options (Mudenda, Daka, et al., 2023). Although the hospital narrowly fell below this target in 2022, the proportion of Access antibiotics exceeded the WHO benchmark in both 2021 and 2023.

Concurrently, outpatient and inpatient antibiotic utilisation, expressed as DDDs per 1,000 outpatient visits and DDDs per 1,000 inpatient-days, increased substantially over the study period, with a significant upward trend observed for outpatient Watch antibiotic utilisation. In contrast, Reserve antibiotics accounted for only a negligible proportion of prescriptions (0.17% in 2022 and 0.43% in 2023) and overall antibiotic utilisation, suggesting cautious use of these last-resort agents. Overall, these findings indicate generally good adherence to the WHO AWaRe framework while highlighting the need for continued antimicrobial stewardship to prevent further increases in the use of broader-spectrum Watch antibiotics.

Antibiotic utilisation increased over the study period, with patients in 2023 having approximately twice the adjusted odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics compared with those in 2021. This temporal increase was accompanied by a significant upward trend in outpatient Watch antibiotic utilisation, while overall Access antibiotic use also increased. Several factors may have contributed to these findings. First, the study hospital, established in 2017, continued to expand its clinical services and patient volume during the study period, resulting in greater healthcare utilisation and increasing case complexity. Second, healthcare utilisation progressively normalised following disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Because these processes occurred concurrently, the present study cannot distinguish their individual contributions to the observed prescribing patterns. Nevertheless, the findings underscore the need to reinforce antimicrobial stewardship programmes during periods of both healthcare system recovery and institutional expansion, when increasing clinical demand and greater complexity of care may favour the use of broader-spectrum antibiotics (Koh et al., 2025; Stevens et al., 2024).

However, an increasing use of Watch antibiotics was observed, particularly among patients covered by the Universal Health Coverage (UHC), Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS), and self-payment schemes. Notably, among hospitalised patients in 2023, Watch antibiotics exceeded Access antibiotics only within the CSMBS group. Although prescribing appropriateness was not evaluated, this pattern may reflect greater clinical complexity requiring empirical broad-spectrum therapy. Given the higher potential of Watch antibiotics to promote antimicrobial resistance, their increasing use highlights the need for continued antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in hospital settings (Saleem et al., 2025).

Notably, tetracyclines, predominantly doxycycline, were consistently the most utilised antibiotic class throughout the study period. This pattern is consistent with local clinical practice in southern Thailand, where doxycycline is commonly used as empirical therapy for acute undifferentiated febrile illness while awaiting microbiological confirmation (Luvira et al., 2019). However, because antibiotic classes were not linked to specific diagnostic categories in the present study, the indications for tetracycline prescribing could not be determined directly.

Additionally, utilisation of beta-lactam antibacterials and penicillins (J01C) increased substantially during 2022 and 2023 in both outpatient and inpatient settings. A similar increase in penicillin utilisation has been reported in a tertiary hospital in southern Italy, where penicillin use increased by 44%, including a 53.4% increase in ampicillin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations in 2023 (Costantino et al., 2024). Although the reasons for this increase cannot be determined from the present study, the growing utilisation of β-lactam antibiotics highlights the importance of continuous surveillance of prescribing patterns and local antimicrobial resistance to ensure their appropriate use.

Diseases of the genitourinary system, particularly other disorders of the urinary system (ICD-10 N39), represented the most frequent indication for antibiotic prescribing and were commonly associated with Watch antibiotic use among Unknown, CSMBS, and UHC beneficiaries. This finding is consistent with previous studies identifying urinary tract infections as a major driver of antibiotic use worldwide (Yang et al., 2022). The predominance of urinary tract infections may also partly explain the higher odds of Watch/Reserve antibiotic prescribing among women and older adults, who commonly present with more complicated urinary tract infections and greater comorbidity burden (Kim et al., 2023; Kusuma et al., 2022).

Regarding differences across health insurance schemes, Thailand's healthcare system comprises multiple insurance schemes with distinct benefit packages and provider payment mechanisms that may influence antibiotic prescribing practices (Suksamai et al., 2019). In the present study, UHC was associated with significantly higher odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics, whereas Private Insurance was associated with substantially lower odds. Although CSMBS was not independently associated with Watch/Reserve prescribing in the multivariable analysis, Watch antibiotics exceeded Access antibiotics among hospitalised CSMBS beneficiaries in 2023, suggesting differences in prescribing patterns across healthcare settings.

Compared with UHC and SHI, the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) provides more comprehensive benefits under a fee-for-service payment system and has been associated with greater use of specialist services and high-cost healthcare interventions (Sakulbumrungsil et al., 2020; Tangcharoensathien et al., 2011). Although CSMBS was not independently associated with higher odds of Watch/Reserve prescribing, Watch antibiotics exceeded Access antibiotics among hospitalised CSMBS patients in 2023. This finding may partly reflect differences in patient case mix as the number of hospitalised CSMBS patients increased during the study period. However, infection severity, prescriber behaviour, formulary availability, and institutional policies were not evaluated and therefore cannot explain the observed pattern.

In contrast, the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) scheme was independently associated with higher odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics. This finding may reflect differences in patient case mix or referral patterns across insurance schemes; however, because referral source, infection severity, and microbiological data were unavailable, these explanations remain speculative and warrant further investigation.

Private Insurance (PI) was associated with lower overall odds of receiving Watch/Reserve antibiotics; however, significant interactions with both outpatient and inpatient settings were observed, suggesting that prescribing patterns differed according to healthcare setting. Because the present study did not evaluate infection severity, formulary restrictions, or prescriber characteristics, the mechanisms underlying these interactions remain uncertain. Similarly, Social Health Insurance (SHI) was associated with lower odds of Watch/Reserve prescribing, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the relatively small number of SHI prescriptions.

Overall, these findings suggest that antibiotic prescribing patterns vary across Thailand's health insurance schemes. Incorporating WHO AWaRe indicators into routine antimicrobial stewardship programmes and performance monitoring may facilitate benchmarking, improve accountability, and promote more rational antibiotic use across different healthcare financing systems (Saleem et al., 2025; Sharland et al., 2019).

Although the distribution of health insurance schemes differed across calendar years because of changes in the study population, no participant changed insurance scheme during follow-up. Therefore, the observed temporal effects are unlikely to reflect within-person changes in insurance coverage.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a recently established tertiary university hospital; therefore, the observed AWaRe prescribing patterns may not be generalisable to other hospitals in Thailand. Second, although patient comorbidity was adjusted for using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), the CCI was derived from cumulative diagnostic history rather than diagnoses immediately preceding each prescription and should therefore be interpreted as a measure of overall comorbidity burden rather than a time-updated covariate. Third, outpatient antibiotic utilisation was estimated from dispensing records. Consequently, DDDs reflect antibiotics dispensed rather than actual antibiotic consumption because individual medication adherence was not available. Fourth, important clinical variables, including infection severity, microbiological culture results, antimicrobial susceptibility, prescriber characteristics, and institutional formulary or prescribing policies, were unavailable. These unmeasured factors may have influenced Watch/Reserve antibiotic prescribing and limited interpretation of the underlying prescribing mechanisms. Finally, although the Social Health Insurance (SHI) group contained relatively few observations, sensitivity analyses excluding this category produced virtually identical results, supporting the robustness of the primary findings. Nevertheless, SHI-specific estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size.

During the study period (2021–2023), the hospital had not yet implemented a formal ASP. Instead, antibiotic use was overseen by the Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee (PTC) through formulary management and medication-use policies. This context should be considered when interpreting the prescribing patterns observed in this study. Since the study period, a formal ASP has been established, providing an opportunity to strengthen AWaRe-based antimicrobial stewardship and evaluate its future impact.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study using longitudinal hospital data identified important patterns and disparities in antibiotic prescribing within a Thai tertiary care hospital using the WHO AWaRe classification. Although Access antibiotics remained the predominant prescribing category, the increasing use of Watch antibiotics, particularly among UHC beneficiaries and hospitalised CSMBS patients, highlights opportunities to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship. Rather than implementing separate stewardship strategies for individual health insurance schemes, hospital-wide antimicrobial stewardship programmes incorporating routine AWaRe-based monitoring may help identify prescribing disparities and support targeted quality improvement. These findings align with the objectives of Thailand's National Strategic Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (Sumpradit et al., 2021) to promote rational antimicrobial use, strengthen antimicrobial stewardship, and enhance antibiotic utilisation surveillance, thereby contributing to ongoing national efforts to combat antimicrobial resistance.
