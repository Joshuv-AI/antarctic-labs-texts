Oral and dental status in children with intestinal failure: A descriptive cross‐sectional study
Authors: Anat Guz‐Mark, Dror S. Shouval, Raanan Shamir, Esti Davidovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren with intestinal failure often experience feeding difficulties and complex daily care needs, yet their oral and dental health remains underexplored. We aimed to describe oral and dental health characteristics in pediatric intestinal failure.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted in 2024 at a tertiary pediatric Intestinal Failure and Rehabilitation Clinic. Clinical data, parenteral support, feeding characteristics, and oral hygiene habits were assessed. A pediatric dentist evaluated dental status, including plaque, gingival inflammation, calculus, caries experience, and enamel hypoplasia, using clinical examination and intraoral scans.ResultsThe study included 22 patients (45.5% females, median age 7.2 [IQR 4.9–12.4] years). Intestinal failure etiologies included short bowel syndrome (50%), congenital enteropathies (27.3%), and dysmotility (22.7%). Most patients (72.7%) required daily parenteral support, over a median of 10.5 h/day. Restricted oral intake was noted in 11/22 (50%) patients. Only 18.2% brushed their teeth twice daily, whereas 54.5% reported no regular daily brushing. Dental calculus was present in 14/22 (63.6%) patients. Patients had clinically meaningfully higher plaque, gingival, and calculus scores than normal reference values. Lower tooth‐brushing frequency was associated with worse plaque and gingival scores. No significant association was found with restricted eating or parenteral support. Dental caries affected 4/22 (18.2%) patients, and enamel hypoplasia was observed in 8/12 (66.7%) children over six years, often involving all permanent first molars.ConclusionChildren with intestinal failure frequently exhibit poor oral hygiene, gingival inflammation, dental calculus, and enamel defects. Oral hygiene counseling, nutritional rehabilitation, and dental follow‐up should be integrated into their multidisciplinary care.

INTRODUCTION

Intestinal failure is a rare but severe condition, defined as a reduction of functional intestinal mass below that which can sustain life, resulting in dependence on supplemental parenteral support. 1 In children, intestinal failure commonly results from short bowel syndrome following extensive bowel resection, congenital enteropathies and mucosal disorders, or disorders of intestinal dysmotility (pediatric intestinal pseudo‐obstruction). 2 , 3 Advances in multidisciplinary care have markedly improved survival; however, children with intestinal failure remain at risk for multiple long‐term complications, including infections, catheter‐related thrombosis, hepatobiliary disease, neurodevelopmental delays, abnormal growth, and metabolic bone disease. 4 , 5 Despite these well‐recognized comorbidities, oral and dental health has received limited attention in this vulnerable population.

Oral health plays an important role in nutritional function, infection risk, and overall quality of life. Emerging data suggest that children with intestinal failure may experience a high burden of oral health problems, including tooth discoloration, caries, gingival inflammation, and delayed eruption. 6 , 7 Several factors may contribute to this pattern, including limited oral feeding that reduces salivary flow, nutritional deficiencies affecting enamel formation and mucosal immunity, frequent or long‐term antibiotic use, and altered feeding behaviors that may promote oral dysbiosis. 8 In addition, gastroesophageal reflux, 9 oral aversion, and high burden of care in this population may further complicate oral hygiene practices and potentially increase the risk of dental disease. Importantly, poor oral hygiene and gingival inflammation may have systemic implications in children with central venous catheters, potentially increasing the risk of bacteremia and systemic infections. 10

Despite these considerations, oral health is not routinely integrated into the management of pediatric intestinal failure, and no consensus guidelines currently address this issue. 11 There is a lack of systematic data characterizing oral and dental status in this population using standardized clinical indices. This gap limits the ability to identify clinically relevant patterns and to inform preventive strategies within routine care.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to describe oral and dental health characteristics in children with intestinal failure, managed at a tertiary pediatric intestinal failure and rehabilitation center, using standardized indices of plaque, gingival status, dental calculus, caries experience, and enamel defects, along with oral hygiene practices. The secondary aim was to explore associations between oral health measures and clinical, nutritional, and behavioral characteristics, within the constraints of a small cohort of a rare condition.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

We performed a descriptive cross‐sectional study in 22 children with intestinal failure who received care at the Intestinal Failure and Rehabilitation Clinic at Schneider Children's Medical Center of Israel (a tertiary referral pediatric center), from January 2024 to December 2024.

Inclusion criteria included patients aged 1–18 years, with intestinal failure, treated with home parenteral nutrition support for at least 3 consecutive months. Given the rarity of the condition, no formal sample size calculation was performed, and all eligible patients during the study period were included.

Our study was approved by the Human Subjects Ethics Committee of the Rabin Medical Center (Protocol no. 0201‐22‐RMC), adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was obtained from patients or their authorized representatives.

Clinical parameters

Data collected included demographics, intestinal failure etiology, parenteral nutrition parameters (volume, calories, infusion duration), feeding assessments, and oral hygiene habits. A pediatric dentist (author ED) performed the dental examinations in all patients.

Oral and dental assessment

The dental examinations were performed using a dental mirror, dental explorer, and dental probe. Intra‐oral impressions of maxilla and mandible were scanned using the intra‐oral 3D scanner Primescan connect (Dentsply Sirona Dental Systems GmbH Bensheim), demonstrated in Figure 1. Oral and dental parameters were assessed using standardized and validated indices, including measures of plaque accumulation, 12 gingival inflammation, 13 dental calculus, 14 caries experience (Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth [DMFT] index), 15 and enamel defects, 16 as detailed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, nutritional, and dental health variables. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations (SD), or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For associations between categorical variables, chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests were applied. For continuous variables comparisons, Mann‐Whitney and Kruskal‐Wallis tests were applied. Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Significance testing was performed with the use of a two‐sided alpha level of 0.05. False discovery rate (FDR) method was applied to control for multiple comparisons. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistic for Windows, version 29, IBM corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2023.

RESULTS

Study population

The study included 22 patients with chronic intestinal failure, with a median (IQR) age of 7.2 (4.9, 12.4) years. Patients' demographic, clinical, and nutritional characteristics are detailed in Table 2. The most common intestinal failure etiology was short bowel syndrome (due to intestinal atresia; intestinal volvulus; gastroschisis; and Hirschsprung's disease), followed by congenital mucosal enteropathies and intestinal dysmotility. Underweight and short stature were common, affecting 59.1% and 50% of the cohort, respectively. The median (IQR) weight‐for‐age and height‐for‐age z scores were −2.5 (−3.0, −0.6) and −1.9 (−2.8, −1.2), respectively.

Parenteral nutrition support was administered every day in 16/22 (72.7%) patients. Parenteral nutrition administration ranged from 2 to 7 days a week, with an infusion time ranging between 8 and 16 h a day. There was no significant correlation between patients' age and the administered parenteral nutrition energy/kg (p = 0.21) or parenteral nutrition volume/kg (p = 0.13), respectively.

Dietary and oral habits

Restricted dietary habits were observed in 11 (50%) patients: 9 patients consumed very small portions (bite‐size) of solid food throughout their day; 1 patient was on liquid diet only; and 1 patient with severe oral aversion received no oral feeding. Although most of the patients had gastrostomies, only 5/22 (22.7%) received enteral nutrition.

Only 4/22 (18.2%) patients reported brushing their teeth twice daily, 6/22 (27.3%) patients once daily, and 12/22 (54.5%) reported no regular daily brushing.

Dental evaluation findings

The plaque, gingival, calculus, and caries indices of the patients, and published reference healthy populations, 17 , 18 are detailed in Table 3. Significant dental calculus accumulation was present in 14 (63.6%) patients. An example of a substantial dental calculus case is demonstrated in Figure 1B. Patients had clinically meaningfully higher plaque, gingival, and calculus scores, compared with normal pediatric reference values. No significant associations were found between any of the dental indices and the parenteral support parameters, including parenteral nutrition volume and energy, or duration of parenteral infusion (Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was applied for the continuous variables of plaque; gingival; calculus; and caries indices, while Mann‐Whitney was applied for the presence of enamel hypoplasia). In addition, there were no significant associations between any of the dental indices and the presence of restricted dietary habits or enteral feeding. Plaque index and gingival index scores demonstrated negative correlations with teeth brushing (r = −0.56 between plaque index and brushing, r = −0.51 between gingival index and brushing; p = 0.01 and 0.02, respectively, Table S1).

Enamel hypoplasia in permanent molars was observed in 8/12 (66.7%) patients that were older than 6 years, exhibiting severe disruption that mostly involved all four molars. No significant associations were found between the presence of enamel hypoplasia and any of the background or nutritional paramenters (including intestinal failure etiology, gestational age, time on parenteral nutrition support, anthropometric indices, and feeding patterns). Dental caries were present in 4 (18.2%) patients, involving 2 to 6 of their teeth. The mean caries index was lower than reference data (Table 3). Moderate negative correlations were found between caries index and parenteral nutrition volume (r = −0.5, p = 0.02), parenteral nutrition energy (r = −0.49, p = 0.02), and parenteral nutrition infusion duration (r = −0.49 mod, p = 0.02), indicating an association between parenteral nutrition dependency and lower caries scores in patients with intestinal failure (Table S1). There was no significant association between proton‐pump inhibitor treatments and caries experience in this cohort.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cross‐sectional study of children with intestinal failure receiving long‐term parenteral nutrition, we found markedly impaired oral hygiene and gingival health, frequent supragingival calculus and a high prevalence of enamel hypoplasia, whereas caries experience was relatively low compared with healthy reference populations. Our novel study systematically characterized oral and dental outcomes in pediatric intestinal failure using standardized indices and advanced oral scanners, highlighting oral health as an under‐recognized component of intestinal failure‐related morbidity. These findings suggest that a substantial component of the oral inflammatory burden in pediatric intestinal failure may be preventable through improved caregiver‐supported daily oral hygiene and regular professional dental care.

The most clinically meaningful findings were the very high plaque and gingival index scores and the frequent presence of calculus. The mean plaque index and gingival index in our cohort were higher than those reported in systemically healthy children examined using the same methodology. 17 , 18 These differences remained despite comparable ages, suggesting that the poor gingival status we observed is not explained solely by age or by the background caries risk of the local population. In line with this, we found a strong association between self‐reported tooth‐brushing frequency and both plaque and gingival indices, whereas no significant associations were detected between periodontal parameters and intestinal failure‐ or parenteral nutrition‐related variables. Together, these findings support the interpretation that inadequate daily plaque control, rather than intestinal failure severity or parenteral nutrition exposure per se, is the main driver of gingival inflammation in this cohort. The observation is consistent with reports in other medically complex pediatric populations, where chronic disease and treatment burden tend to displace routine oral care and lead to poorer oral hygiene and gingival health than in healthy peers. 19 , 20 , 21

Dental calculus was more prevalent in children with intestinal failure compared with controls, highlighting a chronic imbalance between plaque accumulation and removal in this population. Reduced oral activity, such as diminished chewing and salivary flow, together with frequent medication use and dietary restrictions, may alter the oral microbial ecology and favor maturation and mineralization of the dental biofilm. 22 , 23 When mastication is limited, plaque is not regularly disrupted by mechanical forces and remains in situ long enough for calcium and phosphate from saliva and gingival fluid to precipitate, resulting in calculus accumulation. 24 Altered salivary clearance and chemistry, including higher concentrations of mineral ions, may further accelerate this process. 23 Although in our cohort calculus was not significantly associated with the degree of oral feeding restriction, this result should be interpreted cautiously, as the small sample size and heterogeneity inherent to this rare disease might have limited the statistical power to detect such associations.

In our study, plaque, gingival, and calculus scores were all elevated compared with reference values; however, the particularly high burden of calculus suggests that biofilm mineralization may represent a prominent and clinically distinctive feature in children with intestinal failure. Similar findings have been reported in populations with impaired oral activity, including tube‐fed and medically complex children. 24 , 25 , 26 These observations support the need for preventive strategies that combine regular mechanical plaque removal and caregiver education with, when feasible, oral stimulation and professional prophylaxis, in order to limit biofilm mineralization and its periodontal consequences.

In contrast to the pronounced gingival inflammation and plaque accumulation, rate of caries occurrence was relatively low. Most children had no cavitated lesions, and mean caries exposure indices were clearly below those reported in population‐based surveys, in which dental caries affects 60%–90% of school‐aged children worldwide. 27 Within our cohort, caries exposure scores showed a negative correlation with parenteral nutrition volume, parenteral nutrition energy intake, and daily parenteral nutrition infusion duration, suggesting that greater dependence on parenteral support with proportionally reduced oral intake may limit exposure to fermentable carbohydrates and attenuate caries risk. 28 Comparable findings have been reported in children on long‐term home parenteral nutrition, where tooth staining, delayed eruption, and gingival problems are common but caries levels are not consistently higher than those in healthy peers. 8 , 29 Several mechanisms may contribute to these findings: many patients with intestinal failure consume small amounts of oral food or fluid because of feeding intolerance and abdominal discomfort; 30 , 31 parental control of diet often restricts sugary snacks, intolerance to carbohydrates may limit their consumption, and repeated early‐life antibiotic courses may transiently reduce cariogenic bacteria. However, these possibilities remain speculative, and the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Importantly, low caries exposure scores should not be interpreted as evidence of good oral health: in our cohort, reduced caries experience coexisted with marked gingival disease, abundant calculus, and a high prevalence of enamel defects, a “low‐caries but high‐morbidity” profile previously described in tube‐fed children. 8 , 32

Gastroesophageal reflux disease has been associated with increased odds of dental erosion, 33 , 34 though this association is based primarily on observational studies with significant heterogeneity in diagnostic methods and outcome evaluation across studies, 9 and causation remains uncertain. 9 , 35 Limited evidence suggests that proton pump inhibitor therapy may reduce dental erosion progression in adult patients with gastroesophageal reflux, 36 though data specifically in children are lacking. 33 , 37

Enamel hypoplasia and other developmental enamel defects were highly prevalent, affecting almost two‐thirds of children older than six years and frequently involving all four permanent first molars. This prevalence was much higher than the 10%–30% typically reported in otherwise healthy children. 38 , 39 The vulnerability of children with intestinal failure to enamel defects parallels that seen in preterm, very‐low‐birth‐weight infants and children with coeliac disease, where systematic reviews and clinical studies show increased odds of enamel defects compared with term or unaffected peers. 39 , 40 Intestinal failure is characterized by prolonged neonatal intensive care, recurrent sepsis, disturbances of calcium and phosphate homeostasis, fat‐soluble vitamin deficiencies, and long‐term medication exposure, all of which can disturb ameloblast function during odontogenesis. Enamel defects (including hypoplasia, opacities, and post‐eruptive breakdown) have been associated with plaque retention, hypersensitivity, increased risk of atypical caries, and restoration failure, as well as psychosocial impacts on appearance and quality of life. 41 In our cohort, enamel defects did not correlate with current parenteral nutrition exposure or feeding modality, suggesting that early‐life factors during critical periods of tooth development may be more influential than contemporary nutritional support. Such factors may include prematurity, prolonged neonatal intensive care, recurrent sepsis, early metabolic instability with disturbances in calcium and phosphate homeostasis, fat‐soluble vitamin deficiencies, and prolonged medication exposure during the period of enamel formation, all of which may impair ameloblast function during odontogenesis. Because enamel is acellular and cannot remodel, these defects are permanent and require long‐term preventive and restorative management.

The coexistence of poor periodontal status, abundant calculus, frequent enamel defects, and low caries experience thus defines a distinctive oral‐health phenotype in paediatric intestinal failure. Caries‐based risk models alone may be insufficient to characterize this oral‐health pattern, suggesting that assessment should also include periodontal status, enamel integrity, and oral function. From a clinical standpoint, oral‐health strategies for intestinal failure should prioritize caregiver‐supported daily tooth brushing, regular professional plaque and calculus removal, early diagnosis and management of enamel defects, and integration of preventive dental counseling into routine multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation care. 42 Behaviorally, the high proportion of children brushing less than twice daily underscores the need for caregiver education and practical support to maintain oral hygiene, particularly given the additional time and emotional burden associated with intestinal failure care. 43

Our findings also have implications for multidisciplinary risk assessment. In children with central venous catheters, gingival bleeding and periodontal inflammation may contribute to transient bacteraemia; poor oral hygiene has been associated with bacteraemia after tooth‐brushing at levels similar to those seen after dental extractions. 44 Although a causal link between oral inflammation and catheter‐related bloodstream infections in intestinal failure has not been established, systematic dental evaluation and optimisation of oral health may represent a low‐cost opportunity to reduce systemic infectious risk. Invasive dental procedures may warrant individualized consideration of antibiotic prophylaxis, in line with the American Heart Association recommendations for other high‐risk cardiac and immunocompromised groups. 44 , 45

This study has several strengths. All examinations were prospectively performed by a single experienced paediatric dentist using validated indices, enhancing internal consistency. The intestinal failure cohort was well characterized clinically and nutritionally, allowing exploration of associations between oral findings and systemic parameters, and local reference populations examined with similar methods provided context for interpretation. Limitations include the modest sample size, reflecting the rarity of paediatric intestinal failure, which limits statistical power; the cross‐sectional design, which precludes causal inference; the absence of a contemporaneous, individually matched healthy control group; and lack of detailed data on diet, fluoride exposure, medication history, and socioeconomic status, all of which may influence oral health. 43 , 46 The single‐centre design may also limit generalisability. Necrotizing enterocolitis‐related short bowel syndrome and less severe forms of intestinal failure were not represented in this cohort, likely reflecting the higher likelihood of achieving enteral autonomy and being weaned off parenteral nutrition during infancy or early childhood. Additionally, the inclusion of relatively young children may limit the evaluation of long‐term dental outcomes, as oral and dental manifestations may evolve over time.

CONCLUSION

Children with intestinal failure receiving long‐term parenteral nutrition exhibited a characteristic oral‐health profile of poor oral hygiene, substantial gingival inflammation, heavy calculus, and a high prevalence of developmental enamel defects, despite relatively low caries experience. These findings suggest that caries indices alone underestimate oral disease burden in this population and that at least part of the inflammatory oral burden may be preventable. Integration of structured dental assessment, oral hygiene counseling, and preventive care into multidisciplinary intestinal failure follow‐up appears warranted, both to improve oral comfort and function and potentially to mitigate systemic infectious risks. Larger, multicenter longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings and to evaluate targeted preventive and therapeutic interventions tailored to this unique patient group.
