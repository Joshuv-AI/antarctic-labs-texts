A novel pharmacological inhibitor of MAP4K4 activity attenuates metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis
Authors: Felix Ampadu, Nikhil Patil, Venkateswararao Eeda, Iulia Rus, Woncheol Jung, Hassan M Abu shukair, Anza Ali, Yuriana Aguilar-Sanchez, Tae Gyu Oh, Vibhudutta Awasthi, Aditya D Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a progressive condition and a leading driver of global liver-related morbidity. Its advanced form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), is characterized by hepatic steatosis, inflammation, hepatocellular ballooning, and fibrosis. We aim to explore the importance of mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase kinase 4 (MAP4K4) as a therapeutic target and to evaluate a novel small-molecule inhibitor, glucosyl pyrrolo-pyrimidinone (GPPD), for the mitigation of MASH.MethodsMAP4K4 expression was immunohistochemically quantified in liver biopsies from healthy individuals (n = 5) and patients with MASLD (n = 20). We assessed the in vitro efficacy of GPPD and genetic MAP4K4 silencing in human primary hepatocytes, mouse hepatic organoids, HepG2, and AML12 cells challenged with oleic acid. For in vivo validation, mice were fed a high-fat/high-fructose/high-cholesterol diet and treated with GPPD for 30 weeks (n = 8 per group). We analyzed the livers using single-nuclei RNA sequencing to identify the molecular mechanisms of GPPD-mediated hepatoprotection. Data were analyzed using ANOVA models and expressed as mean ± SD, with differences considered significant at p <0.05.ResultsHepatic MAP4K4 expression was significantly elevated in patients with MASLD compared with healthy controls, correlating with disease severity. In multiple in vitro models, both genetic silencing and GPPD treatment significantly attenuated steatosis. In vivo, prophylactic GPPD administration reduced body mass gain and adiposity while protecting against hepatic steatosis, inflammation, injury, and fibrosis. Mechanistically, although GPPD directly inhibited MAP4K4 activity, its hepatoprotective effects were associated with suppression of calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMK2).ConclusionsWe demonstrate that GPPD treatment mitigates steatohepatitis with fibrosis by regulating MAP4K4-CaMK2 axis. Further investigation of GPPD will advance its development as an effective future therapeutic strategy against MASH.Impact and implicationsIn this study, we identify the MAP4K4-CaMK2 signaling axis as a critical pathway in the pathogenesis of MASH. By demonstrating that GPPD-mediated inhibition of this pathway attenuates key hallmarks of MASH, including steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, our findings provide a mechanistic framework for future therapeutic intervention. Moreover, targeting MAP4K4 may address unmet needs of patients who are ineligible for currently approved treatments. Collectively, this research establishes a translational foundation for the development of next-generation small molecules designed at restoring metabolic homeostasis across the disease continuum.Keywords: Calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II, Glucosyl pyrrolo-pyrimidinone, High-fat high-fructose high-cholesterol diet, Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase kinase 4, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common cause of the chronic liver disease, affecting ∼25% of Americans and being often associated with obesity and diabetes.1 Its presentation varies from simple steatosis to severe steatohepatitis (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis [MASH]), which may progress to cirrhosis and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma.2 MASH is a debilitating condition characterized by hepatic steatosis, ballooning, inflammation, and fibrosis.3 Because of its complex multifactorial input, the precise mechanism of MASLD pathogenesis remains unelucidated.4 Recently, resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor β agonist, and semaglutide, a GLP1 agonist, were approved under the accelerated approval program of FDA for treating only moderate-to-advanced fibrosis.[5], [6], [7] Therefore, identification of novel targets and development of small-molecule drugs that can treat the entire morphologic and metabolic manifestations associated with MASH are warranted.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase kinase 4 (MAP4K4) belongs to the Ste20-like family of kinases.8 It is involved in a plethora of pathophysiologies, including malignancies, cardiovascular disorders, inflammation, and metabolic diseases.8,9 Recently, a positive correlation between hepatic MAP4K4 expression and severity of clinical MASLD activity score was observed.10 Furthermore, in oleic acid (OA)-treated hepatocytes, MAP4K4 overexpression increased steatosis, whereas MAP4K4 silencing resulted in reduced steatosis and lipotoxicity.10 Despite preclinical interest, MAP4K4 remains an unvalidated target; early-generation inhibitors failed to demonstrate a favorable safety profile, creating a gap for next-generation small molecules.

In this study, upregulation of hepatic MAP4K4 expression was confirmed in patients with MASLD, in vivo diet-induced mouse model of MASH, and in vitro models of MASLD. Using these models, we first validated the target and then investigated a newly synthesized MAP4K4 inhibitor, glucosyl pyrrolo-pyrimidinone (GPPD), for its prophylactic potential.11 GPPD protected hepatocytes against lipotoxicity and attenuated adiposity and the pathological features of MASH. These hepatoprotective effects were associated with the suppression of MAP4K4-regulated calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMK2) signaling.

Materials and methods

Sources of key biochemical reagents are included in the Supplementary CTAT table.

Cell culture and in vitro treatments

AML12 cells were cultured in DMEM:F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% 100x insulin-transferrin-selenium, 40 ng/ml dexamethasone, and 1% 100x penicillin-streptomycin solution. AML12 cells are metabolically competent, murine hepatocyte-derived cells that are frequently used as an in vitro MASLD model.[12], [13], [14], [15], [16] HepG2 cells were grown in MEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% 100x penicillin-streptomycin. Mouse primary hepatocytes were isolated by collagenase perfusion as previously described and cultured in Williams’ E medium with 10% FBS and 1% 100x penicillin-streptomycin.17,18 Cryopreserved human hepatocytes were thawed and plated on collagen-coated wells. Cryopreserved mouse hepatic organoids were grown on Matrigel-coated plates in HepatiCult™ Organoid Growth Medium. All cells were maintained in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

For MAP4K4 knockdown, AML12 cells were transfected with 50 nM MAP4K4 siRNA, or non-targeting siRNAs (NT siRNA) for 24 h using METAFECTENE® FluoR reagent. For overexpression studies, mouse primary hepatocytes were similarly transfected with Camk2b/pCMV-SPORT6 plasmid for 24 h. The cells were treated with 500 μM bovine serum albumin (BSA)-OA complex or corresponding BSA control. Where indicated, GPPD co-treatment was given at 100 μM (OA+GPPD) for additional 24 h. GPPD was synthesized as previously described.11 DMSO was used as a vehicle control for GPPD treatments.

Animals and in vivo treatments

Male C57BL/6J mice (age: 8 weeks; Jackson Laboratory) were used in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines. Mice were housed in plastic cages with corn cob bedding in a climate/temperature-controlled facility with a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle. After allowing 1-week adaptation, mice were randomly assigned to one of the three diet-defined groups (n = 8 per group) for 30 weeks: control diet (CD), MASH diet (MD), and MD+GPPD. MD contained 40 kcal% fat, 20 kcal% fructose, and 2% cholesterol, provided ad libitum. Mice in the MD+GPPD cohort received 10 mg/kg (bodyweight) GPPD i.p. thrice a week on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays. Mice in CD and MD groups were similarly treated with vehicle (0.2% DMSO in saline). The dosing regimen for GPPD was informed by a prior 14-day acute toxicity study to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD). Briefly, C57BL/6 mice (n = 6) were given single doses of GPPD (20–1,000 mg/kg, i.p.) and monitored for mortality and clinical signs. No mortality or marked weight loss (>10%) was observed at doses up to 200 mg/kg; however, mortality was noted at ≥400 mg/kg. Therefore, the MTD of GPPD was established at 200 mg/kg. For the 30-week prophylactic study, a dose of 10 mg/kg (1/20 × MTD) was administered to ensure a high safety margin and sustained systemic exposure.

We measured bodyweight and food intake every week throughout the study duration. After Week 28, four mice from the CD group, and six mice each from the MD and MD+GPPD cohorts, were randomly selected (GraphPad QuickCalcs module, GraphPad Software, part of Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) for body composition analyses and indirect calorimetry. At the end of Week 30, the mice were euthanized with isoflurane overdose and removal of vital organs. The inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) and liver were excised and weighed, and serum was isolated from blood samples.

Body composition and indirect calorimetry

The body composition was evaluated using EchoMRI™ whole-bodycomposition analyzer (EchoMRI LLC, Houston, TX, USA). Metabolic phenotyping (energy expenditure, O2 consumption, CO2 production, and physical activity) was performed using Promethion Core™ metabolic system (Sable Systems, North Las Vegas, NV, USA). Mice were individually housed in metabolic cages and acclimatized for 48 h before measuring the activity for next 72 h.

Lipid droplets visualization

To image lipid accumulation, cells were stained using either Oil Red O Stain Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or BODIPY™ 493/503 (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions and imaged using an Echo Revolve epifluorescence microscope (Discover Echo, San Diego, CA, USA). For mouse hepatic organoids, the slides were mounted with SlowFade™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to stain the nuclei and were imaged using SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and immunocytochemistry

Mouse liver and white adipose tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin embedded, sectioned (4 μm), and stained with H&E or Picrosirius Red. For F4/80 staining, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated for antigen retrieval using IHCeasy Ready-To-Use IHC kit(Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). After 30-minutes blocking, the sections were probed with anti-F4/80 antibody (1:4,000) for 1 h, followed by multi-rAb HRP-goat anti-rabbit (1:5,000). SlowFade™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI was used as the mounting media. Images were captured using Echo Revolve microscope and histological analyses were blinded.

Human liver tissue microarray from male and female donors with liver steatosis-only and no history of alcohol use (n = 20; 13 males and seven females) and five healthy controls (three males and two females) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% TritonX-100, and blocked for 1 h. The sections were probed with anti-MAP4K4 antibody (1:200) overnight at 4 °C and secondary Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200) antibody. After further staining with DAPI in SlowFade™ Gold Antifade, the slides were imaged by Echo Revolve microscope. Likewise, HepG2 cells, AML12 cells, and mouse primary hepatocytes were fixed, stained, and imaged.

Triglyceride, cholesterol, and free fatty acid uptake

Triglycerides, cholesterol, and free fatty acid (FFA) contents in cultured cell lysates and tissue homogenates were measured using the Triglyceride-Glo™ Assay, Cholesterol/Cholesterol Ester-Glo Assay™, and FFA assay kit, respectively. The Triglyceride-Glo™ Assay and Cholesterol/Cholesterol Ester-Glo Assay™ were obtained from Promega (Madison, WI, USA) For FFA uptake measurement, a QBT™ Fatty Acid Uptake Kit (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) was used as previously described.13,19 Fluorescence/luminescence was measured using a Synergy™ 2 microplate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Alanine aminotransferase

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was determined using ALT Activity Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. The iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA from 1 μg total RNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using gene-specific primers (Supplementary CTAT Table) and PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a CFX Opus Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative expression level was expressed as a fold change using 18S ribosomal RNA as reference.

Western blotting

Proteins were fractioned by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to low-fluorescence PVDF membranes using a Trans-Blot® Turbo™ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were probed using species-specific antibodies against MAP4K4, CaMK2, phospho-CaMK2, Akt, phospho-Akt, JNK, and phospho-JNK. Proteins were detected with fluorescent secondary antibodies and imaged on a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

MAP4K4 activity

The body composition was evaluated using EchoMRI™ whole-body composition analyzer (EchoMRI LLC, Houston, TX, USA). Metabolic phenotyping, including energy expenditure, O₂ consumption, CO₂ production, and physical activity, was performed using the Promethion Core™ metabolic system (Sable Systems International, North Las Vegas, NV, USA). Mice were individually housed in metabolic cages and acclimatized for 48 h before activity was measured for the next 72 h. GPPD concentrations were varied in threefold titrations with 1% DMSO. Reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 240 min and continuously read (Ex/Em = 360/485 nm) on a Synergy™ Neo 2 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA).

For MAP4K4 activity in tissue and cell lysates, MAP4K4 was immunoprecipitated using anti-MAP4K4 antibody, and the immunoprecipitates were suspended in kinase assay buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM β-glycerophosphate, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 0.1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 10 mM MgCl2) containing 20 μM ATPϒS and 1 μg myelin basic protein (MBP). Kinase reactions were performed at 30 °C for 30 min as previously described.20 After alkylating with p-nitrobenzyl mesylate for 2 h, samples were boiled in 5x SDS loading dye for 5 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and blotted using anti-phospho(Ser195)-MBP antibody. Relative kinase activity was determined by the ratio of phospho-MBP to immunoprecipitated MAP4K4 band intensities.21

Intracellular calcium

We investigated the effect of GPPD on cytosolic Ca2+ in mouse primary hepatocytes cultured on four-chambered slides. After treating with 500 μM OA and 100 μM GPPD for 24 h, the cells were allowed to reach room temperature and the media was replaced with Tyrode solution (140 mM NaCl, 5.3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.33 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM HEPES, and 10 mM glucose, pH 7.4). Each well was stained with 400 μl of 4 μM Cal-520 AM for 30 min, then destained in Tyrode solution for 30 min. Cells were imaged using an LSM 780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The Cal-520 signal (Ex/Em 469/525 nm) was quantified by Fiji/ImageJ, with background measured from cell-free areas and subtracted from each field.

Single-nuclei RNA sequencing

Liver tissue aliquots (20 mg) from CD, MD, and MD+GPPD groups were pooled and subjected to single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq; Novogene). Single-nuclei suspensions were prepared (Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3, 10x Genomics) and loaded into the Chromium Controller for partitioning into Gel Bead-in-Emulsions (GEMs). Reverse transcription was carried out inside the GEMs to generate barcoded full-length cDNA, followed by recovery and amplification. cDNA was enzymatically fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with Illumina-compatible sequencing adapters. Libraries were indexed, quantified by Qubit™ dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay, and sized using AATI Fragment Analyzer. Final libraries were normalized and pooled. Sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (PE150 mode) following 10x Genomics recommendations. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (10x Genomics). Quality control steps included filtering out low-quality cells based on mitochondrial gene content and unique molecular identifier (UMI) thresholds. Normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering were performed using Seurat. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for visualization. To compute differential expression and module scoring, expression levels of Camk2a, Camk2b, Camk2d, and Camk2g were quantified. Pathway activity was assessed by calculating Camk2-related module scores using the Seurat AddModuleScore function. Data visualization was performed with ggplot2 and Seurat plotting functions.

Statistical analysis

Animal and sample numbers were determined based on our previous observations and using the G∗Power statistical suite (α = 0.05 and β = 0.80).3,22 Data were analyzed using univariate ANOVA models with GraphPad Prism (Dotmatics). After a significant overall F test, post hoc multiple comparisons for prespecified groups were adjusted by Dunnett’s procedure. All results are expressed as mean ± SD, with differences considered significant at p <0.05.

Results

Hepatic MAP4K4 expression is upregulated in patients with MASLD

To assess MAP4K4 expression, we performed immunofluorescence on liver biopsy samples from five healthy controls and 20 MASLD patients with steatosis and no history of alcohol use. We observed that MAP4K4 expression was higher in MASLD livers than in livers from healthy individuals (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, we examined MAP4K4 expression in OA-treated HepG2 cells, AML12 cells, and mouse primary hepatocytes that model the hallmarks of MASLD. In all OA-treated cell types, MAP4K4 expression was significantly elevated (p <0.01)(Fig. 1B).

MAP4K4 silencing reduces steatosis and lipogenesis in OA-treated hepatocytes

To investigate the significance of MAP4K4 in MASLD, we attenuated MAP4K4 expression using RNA interference in both AML12 cells and mouse primary hepatocytes, and subsequently examined lipid accumulation after OA treatment. In AML12 cells, the knockdown of MAP4K4 was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (Fig. S1A). OA treatment increased lipid accumulation in scrambled NT-siRNA-transfected cells as shown by Oil Red O staining, whereas MAP4K4 siRNA-transfected cells exhibited reduced OA-induced steatosis (Fig. S1B). To further investigate whether MAP4K4 affects the mobility of long-chain fatty acids, we measured the intracellular uptake of fluorescent fatty acid analog, BODIPY-dodecanoic acid coupled to a quencher. MAP4K4 downregulation resulted in decreased FFA uptake (Fig. S1C). Consequently, cellular FFA and triacylglycerol content reduced by ∼40% and ∼65%, respectively, in OA-treated and MAP4K4-silenced cells compared with control cells (Fig. S1D and E). These results indicate that MAP4K4 plays a crucial role in hepatocellular lipid metabolism, consistent with previous in vitro findings where MAP4K4 siRNA reduced steatosis and apoptosis caused by endoplasmic reticulum or oxidative stress in human hepatocytes.10 Moreover, silencing MAP4K4 significantly reduced CD36 expression (p <0.0001), which facilitates hepatic long-chain fatty acid uptake, and also suppressed the de novo lipogenesis genes Scd1, Srebp1, and Gpat1 (Fig. S1F). The expression of carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (Cpt1), a critical participant in mitochondrial β-fatty acid oxidation, was markedly upregulated in response to MAP4K4 silencing. MAP4K4 knockdown also attenuated pro-inflammatory monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP1) and EGF-like module-containing mucin-like hormone receptor-like 1 (F4/80) (Fig. S1F). Likewise, in OA-treated mouse primary hepatocytes, silencing MAP4K4 resulted in reduced lipid accumulation compared with non-targeting controls, corroborating the observations in AML12 cells (Fig. S2).

GPPD mitigates hallmarks of MASLD in vitro

Given that MAP4K4 is upregulated in MASLD and in vitro knockdown protects against lipotoxicity, we evaluated the hepatoprotective effect of our newly synthesized MAP4K4 inhibitor, GPPD, in both in vitro and in vivo MASLD models. GPPD (Fig. 2A), a small-molecule inhibitor synthesized through a catalyst-free method,11 demonstrated 50% inhibition of kinase activity (IC50) at a concentration of 15 nM (Fig. 2B). To investigate the effect of GPPD on MAP4K4 expression and activity, OA-treated HepG2 cells were treated with 100 μM GPPD for 24 h. Western blotting showed that OA increased MAP4K4 expression, whereas GPPD had no effect on OA-induced MAP4K4 levels (Fig. 2C). However, GPPD significantly reduced MAP4K4 kinase activity in both OA-treated and control cells, p <0.0001 (Fig. 2D). Next, we investigated whether GPPD could protect against lipotoxicity in OA-treated HepG2 cells, human primary hepatocytes, AML12 cells, and mouse hepatic organoids. Concurrent GPPD treatment significantly reduced OA-induced steatosis, as measured by BODIPY 493/503 fluorescence, p <0.0001 (Fig. 2E). OA-induced FFA uptake, intracellular FFA, and triglyceride content were also significantly reduced in GPPD-treated HepG2 cells, suggesting that GPPD interferes in lipid uptake and metabolism, p <0.01 (Fig. 2F–H). Accordingly, GPPD treatment significantly reduced the expression of genes involved in fatty acid uptake and synthesis (CD36, GPAT1, DGAT1, SREBP1, SCD1) and inflammation (MCP1 and F4/80) in HepG2 cells exposed to OA, p <0.01 and p <0.001 (Fig. 2I).

GPPD reduces adiposity in adiet-induced MASH model

Based on in vitro efficacy data, we examined GPPD prophylaxis in C57BL6 mice fed MD for 30 weeks. Mice on MD showed significant body mass gain compared with mice on CD, p <0.05 (Fig. 3A and B). GPPD treatment markedly reduced bodyweight gain and liver mass compared with MD group (Fig. 3A–E). Body composition analysis showed loss of fat mass and significant gain of lean mass in GPPD-treated mice, p <0.001 (Fig. 3F). GPPD treatment also resulted in reduced iWAT and normalized iWAT/bodyweight (Fig. 3G and H). Histologically, adipocyte area was reduced in MD+GPPD mice compared with MD mice (Fig. 3I). Accordingly, we found an increased expression of cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV), suggesting higher mitochondrial activity and plausibly increased thermogenesis after GPPD treatment (Fig. 3J). These salutary effects including reduction in weight gain and adiposity were unrelated to altered food intake because the caloric consumption between MD and MD+GPPD groups was not significantly different (Fig. 3K). GPPD treatment resulted in significantly higher energy expenditure, suggesting that GPPD administration enhanced the metabolic rate, which likely contributed to its anti-obesogenic effects, p <0.01 (Fig. 3L and M). Furthermore, GPPD treatment increased oxygen consumption (VO2) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2) compared with mice on MD alone (Fig. 3N–Q). Finally, we also measured the locomotor activity to determine whether the effect of GPPD on energy expenditure was partially due to increased physical activity. However, we observed no significant differences in the distance travelled by mice in MD and MD+GPPD cohorts (Fig. 3R).

GPPD treatment alleviates steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and liver injury

We stained liver sections for steatosis (H&E), fibrosis (Picrosirius Red), and inflammation (F4/80). MD diet-induced severe steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, fibrosis, and inflammation as indicated by increased lipid droplets, collagen deposition, and inflammatory infiltrates. These histopathological features of MASH were significantly alleviated by GPPD treatment, p <0.05 (Fig. 4A). GPPD treatment also significantly decreased hepatic triacylglycerol and cholesterol compared with untreated, MD cohort (Fig. 4B and C). Moreover, serum ALT, a clinical marker of liver injury, was significantly reduced in MD+GPPD group compared with MD group (Fig. 4D). Quantitative real-time PCR confirmed GPPD-mediated attenuation of the genes involved in fatty acid transport (CD36), de novo lipogenesis and triacylglycerol synthesis (Acc1, Scd1, Srebp1, Gpat1, Gpat2, Dgat1), and inflammation (TNFα, TGFβ) (Fig. 4E). Moreover, we observed significant suppression (p <0.05) of collagen type 1 alpha 1 (Col1a1) and smooth muscle α actin (Acta2), markers associated with fibrosis, in the livers of MD+GPPD group (Fig. 4E). Finally, the MAP4K4 expression and kinase activity were significantly increased in MD group (p <0.001), and GPPD treatment suppressed MAP4K4 activity without significantly decreasing its protein expression, p <0.0001 (Fig. 4F and G), confirming the in vitro observations. Altogether, our data revealed that GPPD treatment downregulated the kinase activity of MAP4K4 and mitigated the hallmarks of steatohepatitis with fibrosis.

GPPD inhibits steatohepatitis by regulating CaMK2

We performed snRNA-seq to identify the molecular mechanism by which GPPD prevents steatohepatitis within the diverse hepatic cell population. The snRNA-seq profiling revealed distinct parenchymal and non-parenchymal cell populations across dietary conditions (Fig. 5A and B). Integration of hepatocytes, endothelial, cholangiocytes, immune, and stromal subsets demonstrated substantial remodeling of the hepatic microenvironment in mice belonging to MD and MD+GPPD groups (Fig. 5B). Cell type annotation was supported by the expression of marker genes (Fig. S3A). The data revealed alterations in multiple genes and signaling pathways in response to GPPD treatment. Within hepatocytes, our analysis focused on expression changes in “kinases” following GPPD administration, identifying marked modulation of calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2 (Camk2) and the atypical kinase Mapk15 (ERK7/8) (Table S1). snRNA analysis further uncovered that in central, mid-central, and portal hepatocytes Camk2 beta isoform (Camk2b) was downregulated along with pan-Camk2 expression score in MD+GPPD group compared with the MD group (Fig. 5C–F). GPPD treatment also reduced the expression of Camk2a and Camk2b isoforms in stellate cells; however, their relative abundance was lower compared with other Camk2 isoforms (Fig. S3B). In endothelial and Kupffer cells, GPPD did not alter the expression of Camk2b isoform compared with MD group (Fig. S3C and D). Immunoblotting confirmed a decreased expression and phosphorylation of CaMK2 upon GPPD treatment (Fig. 5G–I).

To determine whether CaMK2 functions as a downstream effector of MAP4K4, an ectopic overexpression of CamK2 was achieved by transfecting mouse primary hepatocytes withCamk2b/pCMV-SPORT6 vector (Fig. 6A and B). Compared with controls, cells overexpressing CaMK2 failed to respond to GPPD treatment; Camk2b mRNA levels remained high inOA-exposed transfected cells treated with GPPD (Fig. 6B). However, GPPD treatment ofOA-exposed control as well asCamk2b-overexpressed cells reduced Map4k4 mRNA levels (Fig. 6C). The distinct effect of GPPD in control andCamk2b-overexpressing cells was recapitulated in lipid accumulation assay (BODIPY staining) where GPPD treatment failed to reduce lipid accumulation inCamk2b-overexressing cells (Fig. 6D). As CaMK2 activation is triggered by calcium influx, we also determined the effect of GPPD effect on cytosolic calcium. GPPD treatment significantly reducedOA-induced cytosolic calcium in mouse primary hepatocytes, p <0.001 (Fig. S4). Moreover, GPPD treatment increased Akt phosphorylation and reduced JNK phosphorylation in the livers from the MD+GPPD group compared with the MD group (Fig. S5). Collectively, these results indicate that by inhibiting the MAP4K4 activity, GPPD suppresses downstream CaMK2 signaling to reduce steatosis while inducing pro-survival Akt and suppressing pro-apoptotic JNK.

Discussion

Based on our results, we demonstrate that the expression of MAP4K4 is upregulated in experimental models of MASH and that GPPD, a novel kinase inhibitor, attenuates body mass gain, adiposity, hepatic steatosis, inflammation, liver injury, and fibrosis. The pathological role of MAP4K4 in MASH was initially identified through genetic silencing in OA-treated AML12 cells, which resulted in the downregulation of genes involved in lipid uptake and synthesis (Fig. S1). These findings are consistent with prior reports where MAP4K4 knockdown protected human hepatocytes against lipotoxicity by reducing the expression of the lipogenic transcription factors Srebp1 and Pparg.10 Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of MAP4K4 reportedly reduces lipid accumulation and oxidative stress in isolated human hepatocytes.8,10,23

Despite these in vitro and genetic reports, the impact of chronic pharmacological MAP4K4 inhibition on hepatic pathophysiology in a preclinical model remains unexplored. This study evaluates the prophylactic efficacy of the newly synthesized MAP4K4 inhibitor GPPD in a 30-week diet-induced murine model of MASH. As a glucosylated pyrrolo-pyrimidinone, GPPD utilizes a unique molecular design optimized for scale-up and yield.11 The glucose moiety provides multiple hydration sites and hydrogen bonding opportunities, improving aqueous solubility and target interaction. Furthermore, the conjugated glucose may provide a bioavailable advantage by leveraging glucose transporters for systemic uptake, a mechanism successfully utilized by other glucosylated therapeutics such as glufosfamide and empagliflozin.24,25 Although other MAP4K4 inhibitors, such as PF-6260933 and GNE-495, are under development, their in vivo efficacy in MASLD models has not been established yet. Notably, unlike these previous candidates, GPPD did not exhibit any effects on platelet aggregation, potentially owing to its specific pyridopyrimidine scaffolding or enhanced target selectivity.26

Chronic high-fat feeding increases bodyweight and reduces energy expenditure, often attributed to a decreased mitochondrial function. In this study, MD-fed mice exhibited a downregulation of COX IV indicating decreased mitochondrial capacity for energy generation, without significant changes in Cpt1a—a marker of fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). GPPD administration significantly attenuated weight gain and fat mass accumulation (Fig. 3). Bodyweight is typically modulated by the balance of caloric intake and energy expenditure; however, we did not observe any significant differences in food consumption between the MD and MD+GPPD groups, indicating that GPPD does not suppress appetite. Instead, GPPD treatment increased systemic energy expenditure independently of changes in physical activity. In this context, GPPD upregulated COX IV expression in iWAT, suggesting an induction of mitochondrial biogenesis (Fig. 3). Interestingly, elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+, which is subdued by GPPD (Fig. S4), has been shown to inhibit COX IV.27 COX IV is a key component of adaptive thermogenesis.28 These findings suggest that GPPD enhances mitochondrial respiration by modulating intracellular calcium homeostasis. Alongside COX IV, uncoupling protein 1 (Ucp1) is another thermogenic factor; however, further studies are necessary to delineate Ucp1-dependent vs Ucp1-independent mechanisms.

One of the most important findings from this study is the attenuation of steatosis, fibrosis, inflammation, and liver injury following GPPD treatment (Fig. 4), which was accompanied by significant reductions in hepatic triglyceride (p <0.0001) and cholesterol content (p <0.001) . However, the involvement of MAP4K4 in metabolic disorders is evidently more complex than previously understood.29 Although prior research indicates that whole-body Map4k4 deletion improves insulin signaling and glucose tolerance, liver- and adipose-specific knockdown models failed to show significant metabolic improvements. Conversely, skeletal muscle-specific Map4k4 deletion has been shown to protect against obesity-induced insulin resistance.30 The discrepancy between tissue-specific genetic models and pharmacological intervention may be explained by the functional redundancy of MAP4K4 homologs, specifically MINK1 (MAP4K6) and TNIK (MAP4K7), which can cooperatively contribute to metabolic dysfunction spanning multiple organs.31 Future investigations are warranted to elucidate the effects of GPPD effects on MINK1 and TNIK expression and activity, as well as the significance of this family’s convergence in GPPD-mediated hepatoprotection. Although GPPD inhibits all three orthologs with equal potency (data not shown), we prioritized MAP4K4 as the primary endpoint given its established role as a metabolic regulator and prevalence in MASLD literature. However, high sequence homology suggests that the hepatoprotective effects of GPPD involve the collective suppression of MAP4K4 and its orthologs. Investigations using ortholog-specific models are necessary to delineate the individual contributions of each kinase to MASH resolution.

Mechanistically, our data reveal that GPPD-mediated MAP4K4 inhibition results in reduced CaMK2 expression and phosphorylation, thereby attenuating hepatic dysfunction (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Although the role of CaMK2 in hepatic pathology remains under-characterized,32 our findings align with those of Feng et al.,33 which implicated CaMK2 in salidroside-mediated protection against lipid accumulation. Specifically, reduced CaMK2 phosphorylation is associated with the downregulation of proinflammatory cytokines (IL1β and IL6), and a decrease in oxidative stress.33,34 Furthermore, elevated CaMK2 expression drives the proliferation of hepatic stellate cells and promotes fibrogenesis, whereas its pharmacological or genetic inhibition attenuates both fibrosis and inflammation.35

GPPD-mediated MAP4K4-CaMK2 signaling axis regulates multiple downstream nodes to modulate lipid synthesis (Table S2).36 We observed that GPPD treatment increased Akt phosphorylation (Fig. S5), a result consistent with the established role of CaMK2 in the PERK-ATF4-TRB3 axis, where activated CaMK2 signaling suppresses Akt phosphorylation.37,38 These findings corroborate reports that MAP4K4-deficient human hepatocytes exhibit elevated Akt activation.10 Thus, the MAP4K4-CaMK2-Akt axis likely contributes to the antisteatotic effects of GPPD, appearing independent of canonical AMPK signaling which remained unchanged in our study. The upregulation of Akt signaling also suppresses HSC activation by inhibiting NF-κB and transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) signaling (Fig. 4).[39], [40], [41] This leads to the downregulation of pro-fibrotic genes, such as α-SMA, and the inhibition of collagen synthesis.42 Finally, as induction of CaMK2 activates JNK signaling to promote inflammation,43 the GPPD-mediated inhibition of the CamMK2 was found appropriately associated with reduced JNK phosphorylation (Fig. S5).

The identification of the MAP4K4-CaMK2 axis as a driver of MASH and its resolution by GPPD provides a foundation for the future clinical development of GPPD. However, as this represents the first in vivo characterization of this inhibitor, several factors remain to be elucidated to facilitate its therapeutic advancement. Although the current dosing regimen was safely derived from MTD data and yielded robust efficacy, comprehensive ADME and pharmacokinetic profiling will be essential to optimize delivery and define the clinical scaling parameters. Furthermore, the high sequence homology within the Misshapen family suggests that the effects of GPPD involve the collective suppression of MAP4K4, MINK1, and TNIK. However, future investigations utilizing ortholog-specific models will be necessary to delineate the individual vs synergistic contributions of each kinase to MASH resolution. Although the systemic administration of GPPD achieved comprehensive hepatoprotection, it remains unclear whether these effects are driven primarily by hepatic MAP4K4 inhibition or are secondary to global metabolic improvements, such as increased energy expenditure and reduced adiposity. Utilizing inducible, tissue-specific knockout models will help isolate these direct vs indirect mechanisms. Additionally, expanding these studies to include female cohorts and assessing long-term effects on glucose sensitivity will ensure a comprehensive understanding of sex-specific and metabolic outcomes, respectively. Future studies will also require compartment-specific calcium measurements in vivo to fully interpret the observed changes in CaMK2 signaling. Although immortalized and primary hepatic cell lines were used for MAP4K4 silencing and GPPD efficacy studies in vitro, future work using advanced 3D culture models mimicking MASLD pathology will enhance translational relevance. Finally, although this study focused on a prophylactic model, evaluating GPPD in therapeutic context specifically in a diet-induced in vivo model where MASH is already established is a high priority for our ongoing research.

In conclusion, GPPD-mediated MAP4K4 inhibition significantly improves hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in a chronic MASH model. The modulation of the MAP4K4-CaMK2 axis appears integral to this efficacy, restoring metabolic homeostasis. In this study, we identify a promising small-molecule candidate, GPPD, for the treatment of metabolic liver disease and provide a blueprint for targeting non-canonical kinase pathways in MASH.
