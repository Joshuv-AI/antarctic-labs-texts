Central Limit Theorems for Transition Probabilities of Controlled Markov Chains
\name Ziwei Su \email ziwei.su@northwestern.edu, \name Imon Banerjee \email imon.banerjee@northwestern.edu, \name Diego Klabjan \email d-klabjan@northwestern.edu

Abstract
We develop a central limit theorem (CLT) for the non-parametric estimator of the transition matrices in controlled Markov chains (CMCs) with finite state-action spaces. Our results establish precise conditions on the logging policy under which the estimator is asymptotically normal, and reveal settings in which no CLT can exist. We then build upon it to derive CLTs for the value, Q-, and advantage functions of any stationary stochastic policy, including the optimal policy recovered from the estimated model. Goodness-of-fit tests are derived as a corollary, which enable us to test whether the logged data is stochastic. These results provide new statistical tools for offline policy evaluation and optimal policy recovery, and enable hypothesis tests for transition probabilities.



Keywords:

Markov chains, controlled Markov chains, mixing processes, non-parametric estimation, CLT

1
Introduction

A discrete-time stochastic process
\{(X_{i},a_{i})\}
is called a
controlled Markov chain (CMC)

(
Borkar 1991
)
if the next “state”
X_{i+1}
depends only on the current state
X_{i}
and the current “control”
a_{i}
. Here, the actions
a_{i}
depend only on the information available up to time
i
. This paper studies the asymptotic distribution of the transition probabilities of finite state-action controlled Markov chains.

In general, controlled Markov chains can be thought of as a generalization of time-homogeneous data (like i.i.d., Markovian), time-inhomogeneous data (like i.n.i.d., time-inhomogeneous Markovian
(
Dolgopyat and Sarig 2023
;
Merlevède et al. 2022
)
, Markov decision process
(
Hernández-Lerma et al. 1991
)
), etc.
They can also be used to model other problems such as system stabilization
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, or system identification
(
Ljung 1999
;
Mania et al. 2020
)
.

There is no reason to believe that the previous examples involve controls that are Markovian.
For instance, it is known that certain categories of adversarial Markov games
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, reward machines
(
Icarte et al. 2018
)
, and minimum entropy explorations
(
Mutti et al. 2022
)
induce Markovian state transitions with non-Markovian controls.
Therefore, there is an acute need to sharply estimate the transition dynamics of controlled Markovian systems in the presence of non-Markovian controls.

A particularly relevant downstream task in modern machine learning is offline (batch) reinforcement learning (RL).
Offline RL often begins with a fixed dataset of trajectories collected by some unknown logging policy (behavior policy), with no further control over data collection.
A fundamental challenge in this setting is to accurately estimate the Markov transition dynamics of the environment from logged data
\{(X_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=0}^{n}
.
Here,
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
is a sequence of actions and
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
is a sequence of states that, conditioned on
a_{i}
, follows a Markov transition kernel (see Definition
1
for the formal definition).
The estimated transition probabilities are then used to evaluate policies or to find an optimal policy for the given dynamics (see Section
4.1
for more details).

In a finite controlled Markov chain, i.e. when the state space and the action spaces are finite, the simplest and most natural approach is to use the non-parametric count-based empirical estimator (model) of the transition probabilities.
We show that this estimator is essentially the maximum likelihood estimate of the transition probabilities (see Proposition
3
).

There have been some recent developments towards understanding the statistical properties of the marginal distributions of time-inhomogeneous Markov chains (see
Sarig 2021
for details). However, despite the widespread prevalence of using the count-based estimator for the
transition probabilities
in model-based RL
(
Mannor and Tsitsiklis 2005
;
Li et al. 2022
)
, the statistical properties of this estimator are poorly understood.

One recent work
(
Banerjee et al. 2025a
)
establishing probably approximately correct (PAC)
(
Valiant 1984
)
guarantees on the estimation error. But this analysis underpins the theoretical nature of PAC learning which does not always align with reality. In particular, it leaves open the important question of the exact asymptotic behavior of the estimators. This gap in theory limits our ability to evaluate estimation error, construct confidence intervals, or perform rigorous hypothesis tests for transition probabilities.

This paper addresses this gap by developing the first asymptotic normality theory for count-based transition estimators in controlled Markov chains with finite states and actions.
We answer the following question(s):

Under what conditions does there exist a scaling such that the scaled estimation error of the empirical transition estimates obeys a CLT, and when does an asymptotic distribution fail to exist?

We show that the answer depends crucially on properties of the logging policy (such as sufficient coverage of all state-action pairs).
In fact, under some mild recurrence and mixing conditions, we prove that the empirical transition probabilities converge in distribution to a multivariate normal under appropriate randomized scaling.
Our analysis reveals that the estimated transition probabilities, when appropriately scaled, have a multinomial behavior in the limit and are asymptotically independent across state-action pairs (see Theorem
15
).

This can be thought of as a generalization of the CLT for the transition probabilities of ergodic Markov chains
(
Billingsley 1961
)
in non-ergodic, non-stationary stochastic processes, being a first of its kind. Furthermore, we also identify scenarios in which a CLT cannot be established for any estimator of the transition probabilities.

To demonstrate the applicability of our results in downstream tasks such as offline policy evaluation (OPE) and optimal policy recovery (OPR), we then derive asymptotic guarantees for the value (
V
), action-value (
Q
), or advantage (
A
) functions for any stationary target policy by solving the Bellman equation with the plug-in transition estimator.
In particular, by analyzing the value of the optimal policy calculated from the estimated transition probabilities, we establish a CLT for the value function of the optimal policy under the true transition kernel.

Our results enable, for example, confidence intervals on the value of the optimal policy and high-probability guarantees for recovering the true optimal policy, providing a principled way for OPE and OPR using asymptotic statistics.

Contributions

In summary, the contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

We prove a central limit theorem for the count-based estimator of transition probability matrices in a finite controlled Markov chain (Theorem
15
).
This result holds under mild bounds on the return and hitting times of state action pairs (Assumption
5
) and under weak mixing conditions (Assumption
7
).
We derive a goodness-of-fit (G.O.F.) test (Proposition
23
) for the transition probabilities of the controlled Markov chain based on Theorem
15
.

2.

We identify occasions when any transition estimate does not have a CLT (Proposition
16
).
In particular, if the policy fails to visit certain state-action pairs, we show that the estimator cannot be asymptotically tight.
This result draws clear regions of testable and untestable dynamics in offline RL data.

3.

Using our transition probabilities CLT (Theorem
15
), we derive central limit theorems for the value function (Proposition
24
), the Q-function (Proposition
25
) and the advantage function (Proposition
27
) for any stationary policy.
We then establish a CLT for the optimal policy that maximizes the reward function for the estimated transition probabilities (Theorem
28
).

4.

We also demonstrate the usefulness of our theory in statistical tasks by deriving asymptotic guarantees for the count-based empirical estimates of transition probabilities in a range of example scenarios, such as non-stationary (Section
4.2
) or inhomogeneous controlled chains (Section
4.3
).

1.1
Literature Review

We divide the literature review into two parts.
In the first part, we place our work in the context of the existing literature on non-parametric estimation for stochastic processes.
In the second part, we place our work in the context of reinforcement learning.

Non-parametric Estimation

Non-parametric estimation
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
and limit theory for Markov chains are well documented in the literature.

Billingsley 1961
develops empirical transition estimators and a central limit theorem (CLT) for finite ergodic chains, while
Yakowitz 1979
extends these ideas to infinite or continuous state spaces.
For time-homogeneous chains,
Geyer 1998
proves laws of large numbers (LLNs) and
Jones 2004
establishes corresponding CLTs under mixing conditions.
More recent work shifts attention to minimax sample complexity:
Wolfer and Kontorovich 2021
derives optimal rates for finite ergodic chains, and
Banerjee et al. 2025a
does so for discrete-time, finite-state-action controlled Markov chains.
Beyond transition-kernel estimation, classical results by
Dobrushin 1956a
;
Dobrushin 1956b
;
Rosenblatt-Roth 1963
;
Rosenblatt-Roth 1964
provide LLNs and CLTs for normalized sample means in time-inhomogeneous settings, but they do not treat the transition matrix itself.

Despite this progress, statistical inference for time-inhomogeneous controlled Markov chains—particularly when controls are stochastic and the state–action process is non-Markovian—remains largely unexplored.
The present paper fills this gap by establishing the first CLT for the count-based, non-parametric estimator of the transition kernel in such controlled settings, together with its implications for model-based offline reinforcement learning.

Reinforcement Learning

In model-based offline (batch) reinforcement learning
(
Levine et al. 2020
;
Kidambi et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2020
)
, researchers seek to learn or evaluate policies solely from logged trajectories without additional exploration.
Applications range from clinical decision making—where logged physician actions serve as controls
(
Shortreed et al. 2011
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
—to service-operations settings such as patient flow and inventory routing
(
Armony et al. 2015
)
.

Parallel works on offline policy evaluation (OPE) include importance sampling
(
Precup et al. 2000
)
, bootstrap
(
Hanna et al. 2017
)
, doubly-robust
(
Jiang and Li 2016
;
Thomas and Brunskill 2016
)
and concentration-inequality-based
(
Thomas et al. 2015
)
estimators that provide high-confidence bounds on a target policy’s value, while research on optimal policy recovery (OPR)
(
Antos et al. 2008
;
Munos and Szepesvári 2008
;
Chen and Jiang 2019
;
Zanette 2021
;
Shi et al. 2022
)
examines when a policy optimized on the learned model is near-optimal.
Most existing guarantees are finite-sample or probably-approximately-correct (PAC) in nature and assume either sufficient state–action coverage or Markovian behavior policies.
From a minimax sample-complexity perspective,
Li et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2022
establish sharp optimal rates for discounted and finite-horizon settings with stationary logging policies, and
Banerjee et al. 2025a
extends this analysis to discrete-time, finite state–action controlled Markov chains with stochastic, history-dependent logging.
The present paper complements this literature with classical large-sample statistical-inference tools that remain valid even when the logging policy is non-stationary and history dependent, provided that broad mixing and hitting-time growth assumptions hold.

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}M_{s,1}^{(1)},\dots,M_{s,1}^{(k)}\\
M_{s,2}^{(1)},\dots,M_{s,2}^{(k)}\\

M_{s,d-1}^{(1)},\dots,M_{s,d-1}^{(k)}\\
M_{s,d}^{(1)},\dots,M_{s,d}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}.

The action sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}}
be the class of all probability measures over state-action pairs for a CMC with initial distribution
D_{0}
. As an example, when
\{a_{i}\}
is deterministic,

forms a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain whose transition matrix at the time step

Let

\displaystyle N_{s}^{(l)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbbm{1}[X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l],

\displaystyle N_{s,t}^{(l)}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbbm{1}[X_{i}=s,X_{i+1}=t,a_{i}=l].

to

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{s,t}^{(l)}:=\frac{N_{s,t}^{(l)}}{N_{s}^{(l)}}.

\hat{M}_{s,t}^{(l)}
is the maximum likelihood estimator of the transition probability

2.1
Hitting and Return Times

In this subsection, we introduce the notions of
hitting
and
return time
. In uncontrolled (standard) Markov chains, the notion of
recurrence
is rigorously characterized using hitting and return times. In particular, a Markov chain can be classified as either positive recurrent, null recurrent, or transient—an exhaustive and mutually exclusive trichotomy. To the best of our knowledge, no such canonical classification exists for controlled Markov chains (CMCs).
However, analogous notions can be defined through the return time of the state control pairs.
We recursively define the ‘time of return’ for every state-action pair
(s,l)
as follows.

Definition 4

The first
hitting time

(s,l)
is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{s,l}^{(1)}:=\min\left\{i:(X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l),\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.(X_{j}\neq s,a_{j}\neq l)\ \forall\ 0<j<i\right\}.

When
i\geq 2
, the
i
-th
time to return
(or
return time
) of the state-action pair
(s,l)
is recursively defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{s,l}^{(i)}:=\min\{i:(X_{\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(j)}+i}=s,a_{\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(j)}+i}=l),

\displaystyle\qquad(X_{m}\neq s\cup a_{m}\neq l),\ \forall\ \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(j)}<m<\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(j)}+i\}.

The following assumption addresses recurrence in CMC by ensuring that every state-action pair
(s,l)
is visited sufficiently often.

Assumption 5

For all state-action pairs
(s,l)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, there exists
0<\alpha<1
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau_{s,l}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(p)}}]\leq T_{i}\text{ almost surely, with }T_{i}=O(i^{\alpha}),

where
\mathcal{F}_{\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(p)}}\in\mathbb{F}
is the filtration up to the
(i-1)
-th visit.

Assumption
5
significantly relaxes previous assumptions
(
Banerjee et al. 2025a
, Assumption 2)
, which requires the expected return time to be uniformly bounded almost surely.
The following lemma ensures that, under Assumption
5
, every state-action pair
(s,l)
is visited sufficiently frequent.

Lemma 6

For controlled Markov chain that satisfies Assumption
5
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{s}^{(l)}(n)]=\Omega\left(n^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\right),

where
N_{s}^{(l)}(n)
counts visits to
(s,l)
by time
n
.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Section
B.2
.

2.3
Ergodic Occupation Measure

In this subsection, we define
ergodic occupation measure
in the context of CMCs.

Definition 12
(Ergodic Occupation Measure)

For all
(s,l)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, there exists a constant
p_{s}^{(l)}\in[0,1]
such that the following ergodic occupation measure exists:

\displaystyle p_{s}^{(l)}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l\right)}{n}.

Observe that for stationary controlled Markov chains (CMCs)
p_{s}^{(l)}=\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=s,a_{0}=l)
.
Some usual processes, like episodic controlled Markov chains are not stationary, but ergodic occupation measure exists.

We make the following assumption essential for the CLTs for value, Q-, and advantage functions.

Assumption 13

The CMC has ergodic occupation measure, and there exists some constant
T>0
such that
p_{s}^{(l)}>T^{-1}
for all
(s,l)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 14

The assumption
p_{s}^{(l)}>T^{-1}
can be relaxed with appropriate assumptions on the return time of
(X_{i},a_{i})
.
In practice, this would translate into an assumption about the logging policy.

3
CLT for Controlled Markov Chains

In this section, we introduce the main result of this paper: the central limit theorem (CLT) for count-based empirical estimates of transition probabilities in CMCs (Theorem
15
).

We begin with introducing some extra notations.
Let
\odot
denote the Schur product or Hadamard product of two compatible vectors and let
\otimes
denote the Kronecker product for the same. Let
\text{Vec}\left(A\right)
be the vectorization of the matrix
A
given by stacking the columns.
Recall that
d
is the total number of states and
k
is the total number of actions.
Let
\mathbb{N}
be the
dk\times d
dimensional matrix given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\underbrace{\sqrt{N_{1}^{(1)}},\dots,\sqrt{N_{1}^{(1)}}}_{d\text{ times}}\\
\underbrace{\sqrt{N_{1}^{(2)}},\dots,\sqrt{N_{1}^{(2)}}}_{d\text{ times}}\\
\vdots\\
\underbrace{\sqrt{N_{d}^{(k-1)}},\dots,\sqrt{N_{d}^{(k-1)}}}_{d\text{ times}}\\
\underbrace{\sqrt{N_{d}^{(k)}},\dots,\sqrt{N_{d}^{(k)}}}_{d\text{ times}}\end{bmatrix}

and let
\mathbb{M}
be the
dk\times d
dimensional matrix given by
[M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{d}]^{\top}
with
\hat{\mathbb{M}}
be its estimated counterpart, where
M_{s}
is given in
Equation

2.1
.
Let
\xi:=\left(\text{Vec}\left(\hat{\mathbb{M}}^{\top}\right)-\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{M}^{\top}\right)\right)\odot\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{N}^{\top}\right)
be the Schur product between
\text{Vec}\left(\hat{\mathbb{M}}^{\top}\right)-\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{M}^{\top}\right)
and
\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{N}^{\top}\right)
.
Elementwise, the
(s-1)dk+(l-1)d+t
’th element of
\xi
is

\sqrt{N_{s}^{(l)}}\left({\hat{M}_{s,t}^{(l)}-M_{s,t}^{(l)}}\right)
.
We now have the central limit theorem (CLT) for count-based empirical estimates of transition probabilities in CMCs.

Theorem 15

Under Assumptions
5
and
7
,

\displaystyle\xi=\left(\text{Vec}\left(\hat{\mathbb{M}}^{\top}\right)-\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{M}^{\top}\right)\right)\odot\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{N}^{\top}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda),

where
\Lambda
is the covariance matrix. The elements of
\Lambda
are given by
\lambda_{slt,s^{\prime}l^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
which denotes the covariance between the
(s-1)dk+(l-1)d+t
’th and the
(s^{\prime}-1)dk+(l^{\prime}-1)d+t^{\prime}
’th elements of
\xi
.
\lambda_{slt,s^{\prime}l^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
has the expression

\displaystyle\lambda_{slt,s^{\prime}l^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\mathbbm{1}[(s,l)=(s^{\prime},l^{\prime})]\left(\mathbbm{1}[t=t^{\prime}]M_{s,t}^{(l)}-M_{s,t}^{(l)}M_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{(l^{\prime})}\right).

(3.1)

The proof of this theorem can be found in Section
3.1
.
The following proposition entails the occasions when there exists no CLT.

Proposition 16

Suppose that there exists a state-action pair
(s,l)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s)
is independent with the size of the state space
d:=|\mathcal{S}|
for all
i\geq 0
, and

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s)<\infty,

then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l)<\infty,

and there exists a controlled Markov chain with transition kernel
M
such that for any possible sequence of estimators

\{\hat{M}_{s,t}^{(l)}\}_{n\geq 1}
and for any positive sequence
\{l_{n}\}
with
l_{n}\rightarrow\infty
,

\left\{l_{n}\;\sup_{s,l,t}\bigl|\hat{M}_{s,t}^{(l)}-M_{s,t}^{(l)}\bigr|\right\}_{n\geq 1}

(3.2)

is not tight.

The proof of this proposition can be found in Section
B.3
.

Remark 17

In contrast to Lemma
6
, it can be verified using Borel-Cantelli lemma that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l)<\infty
implies

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{s}^{(l)}(\infty)\geq i-1\right)/T_{i}<\infty,

where
N_{s}^{(l)}(\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}N_{s}^{(l)}(n)
.

Unlike in the uncontrolled setting, null-recurrence properties of CMCs are entwined with the non-stationarity of the control sequence. Some controls may ensure persistent exploration of the entire state-action space, while others may confine the trajectory to a restricted subset. Consequently, the sharp positive/null/transient classification in Markov chains is replaced by a policy-dependent continuum in CMCs. This leads to a “grey area” in defining null-recurrence. We classify null-recurrence as the razor’s edge between the regimes of Lemma
6
and Remark
17
. The statistical properties of such a controlled Markov chain is difficult to charactarize by the current analysis. Nevertheless, since exploration problems are not necessarily recurrent
(
Mutti et al. 2022
)
, this remains an important open direction for future studies.

Lemma 18

\displaystyle\frac{N_{s}^{(l)}}{\mathbb{E}[N_{s}^{(l)}]}\xrightarrow{\ a.s.\}}1.

Next, we present the following generalization of the sampling scheme given in

For each

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{(l)}:\begin{array}[]{ccccc}X_{1,1}^{(l)}&X_{1,2}^{(l)}&\dots&X_{1,\tau}^{(l)}&\dots\\
X_{2,1}^{(l)}&X_{2,2}^{(l)}&\dots&X_{2,\tau}^{(l)}&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
X_{d,1}^{(l)}&X_{d,2}^{(l)}&\dots&X_{d,\tau}^{(l)}&\dots\end{array}

\forall\ (s,t,\tau)\in\{1,\dots,d\}\times\{1,\dots,d\}\times\mathbb{N}

Moreover, for every time period

\left((s_{0},l_{0}),\dots,(s_{i-1},l_{i-1}),s_{i}\right)\in(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{i}\times\mathcal{S}

\alpha_{i}^{((s_{0},l_{0}),\dots,(s_{i-1},l_{i-1}),s_{i})}

\displaystyle P_{l}^{((s_{0},l_{0}),\dots,(s_{i-1},l_{i-1}),s_{i})}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}(\alpha_{i}^{((s_{0},l_{0}),\dots,(s_{i-1},l_{i-1}),s_{i})}=l)

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s_{i},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{i-1}=s_{0},l_{0},\dots,s_{i-1},l_{i-1}).

(\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{a}_{0})\overset{d}{=}(X_{0},a_{0})

Proposition 19

\left(X_{0},a_{0},\dots,X_{n},a_{n}\right)

\left(\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{a}_{0},\dots,\tilde{X}_{n},\tilde{a}_{n}\right)

Remark 20

Observe that Proposition

4.1
CLTs for the Value Function, Q-Function, Advantage Function and Optimal Policy Value

We now demonstrate how Theorem
15
and Corollary
22
can be applied in offline RL tasks like OPR and OPE.
Optimal policy recovery (OPR) is a key problem in offline RL settings, wherein one wishes to identify the policy that maximizes the
value function
given a sequence of states and actions generated by an unknown logging policy and unknown transition probabilities.

A related problem is offline policy evaluation (OPE), where we use the state-action sequence to estimate the value function of an arbitrary policy.
In this subsection, we demonstrate how our previous results can be used to recover an asymptotic distribution for the estimated value function.
We first use Theorem
15
and Corollary
22
to provide high probability bounds for OPE, and then extend that to a corresponding result for OPR.

Let
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
denote the probability simplex in the action space, and suppose that
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is the target policy, a known stationary stochastic policy.
In offline (batch) reinforcement learning, the (known) target policy is usually different from the (unknown) logging (behavior) policy under which we obtain the logged data
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}
.

Recall the definition of
\mathbb{M}
and let
\pi_{s}=\left[\pi(s,1),\ldots,\pi(s,k)\right]
represent the probability of the next action, given the current state
s
.
Then
\Pi=\operatorname{diag}\left[\pi_{1},\cdots,\pi_{d}\right]
is
d\times dk
dimensional matrix.
Let
\tilde{g}:=(\tilde{g}(x,a):(x,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})
be a
dk\times 1
vector where element
(s-1)k+l
denotes the cost associated with the state
s
and action
l
.
Then, the per-stage expected reward function
g:=(g(x):x\in\mathcal{S})
is a
d\times 1
vector where
g(x)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(x,a)\tilde{g}(x,a)
.

For a known discount factor
0<\alpha_{dis}<1
, the value function
V_{\Pi}:=(V_{\Pi}(x):x\in\mathcal{S})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
obtained by solving the Bellman equation
(
Bertsekas 2011
)
, is given by

\displaystyle V_{\Pi}

\displaystyle=\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi\mathbb{M}\right)^{-1}g.

Substituting
\mathbb{M}
by
\hat{\mathbb{M}}
, we obtain the plug-in estimate

\hat{V}_{\Pi}=\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi\hat{\mathbb{M}}\right)^{-1}g
.

For convenience, we introduce the following notation

\displaystyle\Sigma_{V}^{(\Pi)}=\alpha_{dis}^{2}\left(\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi\mathbb{M}\right)^{-1}\Pi\otimes V_{\Pi}^{\top}\right)\Lambda^{(is)}\left(\Pi^{\top}\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\mathbb{M}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}\right)^{-1}\otimes V_{\Pi}\right)

The following proposition shows that
\Sigma_{V}^{(\Pi)}
is the asymptotic covariance matrix for the scaled estimation error
\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{V}_{\Pi}-V_{\Pi}\right)
.

Proposition 24

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}
be a sample from a controlled Markov chain under a logging policy.
Consider the setting of Corollary
22
and suppose that Assumptions
5
,
7
and
13
hold for the logging policy, and that the target policy
\pi
is stationary.
Then, the value function obeys the following CLT:

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{V}_{\Pi}-V_{\Pi}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Sigma_{V}^{(\Pi)}\right).

It follows that
\hat{V}_{\Pi}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}V_{\Pi}
.

The proof of this proposition can be found in Section
A.1
.

A similar set of results can be derived for the
Q
-function defined on the state-action pair
(x,a)
.
Let
\tilde{r}:=(\tilde{r}(x,a,y):(x,a,y)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
be a
d^{2}k\times 1
vector where element
(s-1)dk+(l-1)d+t
denotes the cost associated with the tuple
(s,l,t)
.
Then, the per-state-action-pair expected reward function
r:=(r(x,a):(x,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})
is a
dk\times 1
vector where
r(x,a)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}}M_{x,y}^{(a)}\tilde{r}(x,a,y)
.

Similar to the value function, for a known discount factor
0<\alpha_{dis}<1
, the Q-function
Q_{\Pi}:=(Q_{\Pi}(x,a):(x,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{dk}
,
obtained by solving the Bellman equation
(
Bertsekas 2011
)
, is given by

\displaystyle Q_{\Pi}

\displaystyle=\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\mathbb{M}\Pi\right)^{-1}r.

Substituting
\mathbb{M}
by
\hat{\mathbb{M}}
, we obtain the plug-in estimate
\hat{Q}_{\Pi}=\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\hat{\mathbb{M}}\Pi\right)^{-1}r
.
We introduce the following notation

\displaystyle\Sigma_{Q}^{(\Pi)}=\alpha_{dis}^{2}\left(\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\mathbb{M}\Pi\right)^{-1}\otimes Q_{\Pi}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}\right)\Lambda^{(is)}\left(\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi^{\top}\mathbb{M}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\otimes\Pi Q_{\Pi}\right).

Proposition 25

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}
be a sample from a controlled Markov chain under a logging policy.
Consider the setting of Corollary
22
and suppose that Assumptions
5
,
7
and
13
hold for the logging policy, and that the target policy
\pi
is stationary.
Then, the Q-function obeys the following CLT:

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{Q}_{\Pi}-Q_{\Pi}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Sigma_{Q}^{(\Pi)}\right).

It follows that
\hat{Q}_{\Pi}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}Q_{\Pi}
.

The proof of this proposition follows an entirely analogous way to Proposition
24
, and can be found in Section
A.2

Proposition 26

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}

5
,
7
and

\displaystyle\left(\hat{Q}_{\Pi}-Q_{\Pi}\right)\odot\text{Vec}\left(\mathbb{N}^{\top}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Lambda_{Q}^{(\Pi)}\right).

\lambda_{sl,s^{\prime}l^{\prime}}^{(Q_{\Pi})}=\sqrt{p_{s}^{(l)}p_{s^{\prime}}^{(l^{\prime})}}\sigma_{sl,s^{\prime}l^{\prime}}^{(Q_{\Pi})}

The proof of this proposition can be found in Section
A.3
.

Given Propositions
24
and
25
, a similar set of results can be derived for the advantage function, defined element-wise as the difference between the value and Q functions:
A_{\Pi}:=(A_{\Pi}(x,a):Q_{\Pi}(x,a)-V_{\Pi}(x),(x,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{dk}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{A}^{\Pi}=\alpha_{dis}^{2}\Big(

\displaystyle\big(I-\alpha_{dis}\mathbb{M}\Pi\big)^{-1}\otimes Q_{\Pi}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}-K\big(\big(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi\mathbb{M}\big)^{-1}\Pi\otimes V_{\Pi}^{\top}\big)\Big)\Lambda^{is}

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}

and suppose that Assumptions
5
,
7

Next, one can use any method to find the optimal policy; for instance, policy iteration

Let
\Pi_{opt}

\hat{\mathbb{M}}

The following theorem characterizes the asymptotic behavior of plug-in estimator of the value function for the estimated optimal policy.

Theorem 28

Assume that for any
\varepsilon>0

\Pi^{\prime}
such that
\left\|\Pi_{opt}-\Pi^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon

\Pi_{opt}

In the following two sections, we provide some examples of how the theory in the paper can be used to derive CLTs for the transition matrices for some popular categories of controlled Markov chains encountered in the literature.

4.2
Controlled Markov Chains with Non-Stationary Markov controls

We consider a controlled Markov chain with non-stationary control process as the first example.
A controlled Markov chain is said to have non-stationary Markov controls if for any time period
i
, state
s
, action
l
, and sample history
\hbar_{0}^{i-1}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{i-1}=\hbar_{0}^{i-1}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s\right).

We observe that the law of the action sequence can depend upon the time step
i
.
Let
P_{s,l}^{(i)}:=\mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s)
.
We can write the transition probability of the state-action pair as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=t,a_{i}=l^{\prime}|X_{i-1}=s,a_{i-1}=l\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=t|X_{i-1}=s,a_{i-1}=l\right)\mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l^{\prime}|X_{i}=t)

\displaystyle\qquad=M_{s,t}^{(l)}\times P_{t,l^{\prime}}^{(i)}.

It is straightforward to see that the state-action pair is a time inhomogeneous Markov chain with transition probabilities given by
M_{s,t}^{(l)}\times P_{s,l^{\prime}}^{(i)}
.
Our goal is to do statistical inference on the transition probabilities
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=t|X_{i-1}=s,a_{i-1}=l^{\prime})
.
We proceed by making assumptions on the return times of the actions.

Definition 29

Define
\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}
to be the time between the
(j-1)
-th and
j
-th visit to action
l
after visiting state-action pair
s,l
for the
i
-th time.
For ease of notation, denote
\sum_{k=1}^{i}\tau_{s,l}^{(k)}+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\tau^{(i,\star,k)}_{s,l}=\tau_{\star}
, Then
\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}
is recursively defined as

\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}:=\min\{n:(a_{\tau_{\star}+n}=l),a_{j}\neq l,\ \forall\ \tau_{\star}<j<\tau_{\star}+n\}.

Next we make some simplifying assumptions on
\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}
and
M^{(l)}
.

Assumption 30

1.

For all state-action pairs
(s,l)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

\mathbb{E}[\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}|\mathcal{F}_{\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(p)}+\sum_{p=1}^{j-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,p)}}]\leq T_{i}^{\star}\text{ almost surely, with }T_{i}^{\star}=O(i^{\alpha}).

2.

The transition matrices
M^{(l)}
are positive stochastic matrices where every entry in the matrix is strictly positive.
Then, there exists
M_{min}
and
M_{max}
such that for all
s,t\in\mathcal{S}
and
l\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle 0<M_{min}\leq M_{s,t}^{(l)}\leq M_{max}<1.

(4.1)

The next lemma proves that under this assumption
\{(X_{i},a_{i})\}
satisfies Assumption
5
.

Lemma 31

Under the conditions of Assumption
30
, for all
(i,s,l)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, it holds almost surely that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau_{s,l}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(p)}}]\leq\frac{T_{i}^{\star}M_{max}}{(1-\max\{M_{max},1-M_{min}\})^{2}}.

(4.2)

The proof of this lemma can be found in Section
B.8
.

We can now state our main result about the CLT of a controlled Markov chain with a non-stationary Markov controls.

Proposition 32

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}
be a sample from a controlled Markov chain with non-stationary Markovian controls satisfying Assumption
30
, then Theorem
15
holds with

\mathbb{C}=0,\ \mathbb{C}_{\theta}=1/(dM_{min}).

The proof of this proposition can be found in Section
B.9
.

4.3
Inhomogeneous Markov Chains

We consider an inhomogenous Markov chain as the second example.
A controlled markov chain is said to be an inhomogenous Markov chain if there exists a sequence of actions
l_{0},l_{1},\ldots\in\mathcal{A}
such that for any time period
i
, state
s
, sample history
\hbar_{0}^{i-1}
,

\mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l_{i}|\mathcal{H}_{0}^{i-1}=\hbar_{0}^{i-1},X_{i}=s)=1.

We make the following assumption on the logging policy.

Assumption 33

1.

Let
\mathbb{T}_{i}=O\left(i^{\alpha}\right)
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
be a sequence of known integers.
Then, we assume that

\displaystyle\sum_{p=j}^{\mathbb{T}_{i}+j}\mathbbm{1}[a_{p}=l]\geq 1,

(4.3)

for all
\tau_{s,l}^{(i-1)}+1\leq j\leq\tau_{s,l}^{(i)}-\mathbb{T}_{i}
and
l\in\mathcal{A}
.

2.

The transition matrices
M^{(l)}
are positive stochastic matrices where every entry in the matrix is strictly positive.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i+1}=t|X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l\right)>M_{min}=\min_{s,t,l}M_{s,t}^{(l)},\quad\text{ and }

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i+1}\neq t|X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l\right)<1-M_{min}.

(4.5)

Proposition 34

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{n},a_{n})\right\}
be a sample from an inhomogeneous Markov chain satisfying Assumption
33
. Then Theorem
15
holds with

\mathbb{C}=0,\ \mathbb{C}_{\theta}=e/(e-1).

The proof of this theorem can be found in Section
B.10
.

5
Conclusion

This work develops the first asymptotic theory for
non-parametric
estimation in finite controlled Markov chains.
Our main result (Theorem
15
) establishes a central limit theorem for the classical count–based estimator of state–action transition probabilities.
Building upon this foundation, we show that plug‐in estimators of value,
Q
- and advantage functions under arbitrary stationary target policies, as well as the value of the estimated optimal policy also satisfy CLTs.
All results hold under general, possibly history–dependent, logging policies, thereby providing a coherent asymptotic framework for model–based offline policy evaluation (OPE) and optimal policy recovery (OPR).

The present study is asymptotic.
It does
not
furnish finite-sample confidence intervals or confidence sets for OPE or OPR.
Nor does it fully characterize the boundary between “testable” and “untestable” logging data; our sufficient conditions fall short of a sharp dichotomy.
Finally, all analysis is confined to finite state–action spaces.

Several directions for future work are immediate.
First, translating the derived CLTs into practical error bars via confidence intervals is a pressing next step.
Second, refining the testability criteria to close the gap between the current sufficiency results and necessary impossibility statements would delineate precisely when asymptotic inference is attainable.
Third, extending the theory to infinite or continuous state–action spaces, as suggested by adaptive density estimation work in Markov settings
(
Banerjee et al. 2025b
)
, and to continuous-time controlled processes, would markedly broaden the scope of rigorous statistical inference of controlled Markov chains and its applications in reinforcement learning.

\displaystyle M_{s}^{(l)}(\xi)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{m}

\displaystyle\ \geq\inf_{\hat{M}^{(1)}}\sup_{\xi^{(1)}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{(d/3)}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{M}_{s}^{(l)}-M_{s}^{(l)}(\xi)\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon}\right)
