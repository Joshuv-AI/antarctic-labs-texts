Bridging primary care with artificial intelligence: toward a global 1.5-Tier Healthcare System
Authors: Se Young Jung, Seong-Ho Han, Eojin Yi, Wooin Seo, Yeonsoo Kang, Sang Min Park, Jiho Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence is increasingly applied in primary care, yet systematic frameworks linking individual applications to health-system improvement remain lacking. This perspective proposes a “1.5-Tier Healthcare System,” in which AI forms an intermediary layer between primary and specialist care, strengthening its four core functions: first contact, comprehensiveness, coordination, and continuity. Adaptable across high-income, resource-limited, and crisis settings, it offers a scalable alternative to concierge medicine: “concierge care for all.”Subject terms: Business and industry, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Social sciences

Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed medical practice and research across both individual and population levels of care. The integration of AI into healthcare has generated substantial research interest, with numerous studies documenting its applications across clinical settings and evaluating its utility through diverse classification frameworks. These classification frameworks have organized AI implementations by target recipients1—clinical users, direct-to-consumer services, and business operations—or by examining the overall progress, challenges, and emerging opportunities of AI2.

In primary care settings, individual studies have validated the clinical utility of specific AI applications. For example, AI-based diagnostic tools for skin conditions have demonstrated measurable improvements in diagnostic agreement compared with reference standards for primary care physicians3. Similarly, AI-based assessment of retinal images for diabetic retinopathy screening has shown favourable sensitivity and specificity when compared with the gold standard of ophthalmologist evaluation4. While these studies have validated individual AI applications in primary care settings, they focus predominantly on technical performance without clarifying how these tools strengthen specific primary health care (PHC) functions. This limitation hinders sustainable implementation and prevents AI solutions from becoming deeply embedded within healthcare system operations.

The value of AI in healthcare ultimately depends on its capacity to improve the quality of care and address fundamental challenges facing healthcare delivery systems5. Primary care forms a critical link between healthcare delivery and national health policy, playing a vital role not only in improving population health within individual countries but also in advancing global health equity by addressing disparities in access to essential services6. Despite this central role, the primary care workforce continues to face critical shortages, escalating levels of burnout, and limited access to specialist consultation7. As the foundational tier of health systems, primary care must be assessed from population health and health system perspectives, and AI deployment and assessment should similarly adopt this multidimensional viewpoint.

Herein, we propose a framework to systematically detail how AI can enhance primary care performance across multiple dimensions, bridging the gap between individual technological applications and comprehensive primary care improvements within diverse health system contexts. Specifically, we introduce the concept of a “1.5-Tier Healthcare System” as a model for collaboration between human expertise and AI systems. We outline the conceptual pillars of the framework and review recent studies that have implemented and evaluated AI-empowered programs and tools in real-world primary care settings. Further, we explore how this framework can be adapted across diverse healthcare contexts—specifically high-income urban health systems, low-resource rural health systems, and fragile or conflict-affected settings—and explain why South Korea represents an optimal test site for the 1.5-Tier Healthcare System.

The 1.5-Tier Healthcare System

We use the term “1.5 Tier Healthcare System” to describe a model in which AI serves as a technical bridge between primary care and higher-level hospital services (Fig. 1). Rather than referring to a single tool, this concept offers a broader framework for understanding how AI solutions can reinforce the role of primary care and address the persistent challenges faced at this level of the healthcare system. We organize the system into four conceptual components and discuss how AI can strengthen the core functions of primary care: first contact, comprehensiveness, coordination, and continuity (the 4Cs)8. We also review existing evidence to demonstrate the ways in which AI-enabled tools can enhance the 4Cs within primary care (Fig. 2).

The framework delineates tiers based on geographic or organizational layers (‘WHERE’) and clinical intensity and scope (‘WHAT’). The 1.5-Tier serves as a virtual integrator, bridging community primary healthcare (PHC) with hospital expertise through digital coordination.

First contact: augmented primary access and optimized triage

First contact represents the initial point at which individuals enter the healthcare system for any new health need, and acts as a critical gatekeeper that determines the efficiency of downstream care8. Strengthening the first contact stage is essential for ensuring timely access to care, preventing unnecessary specialist utilization, and ensuring the remaining core functions of primary care operate effectively9. However, primary care settings often lack the specialized equipment and training required for precise diagnostic evaluation10. These structural limitations can contribute to inconsistent case recognition and suboptimal referral decisions, particularly for conditions in which early detection is essential to prevent progression and where timely intervention substantially alters outcomes.

AI-enabled triage systems help mitigate these challenges by supporting more consistent identification and routing at the point of first contact. A prospective study in Australia demonstrated that automated retinal photography combined with AI-based glaucoma screening achieved an AUROC of 0.80, with sensitivity of 65.0% and specificity of 94.6% for referable glaucoma, identifying referable disease in 18 of 161 (11.2%) previously undiagnosed participants10. By expanding screening capacity and flagging individuals at higher risk, this approach facilitates earlier intervention and reduces unnecessary specialist burden, ultimately improving the likelihood of favourable clinical outcomes.

A similar benefit has been observed in early-stage mental health care. Under-resourced primary care environments are frequently beset by long wait times and inefficient triage, causing many individuals with mild or emerging mental health symptoms to leave before receiving appropriate support11. AI-based assessment tools can provide timely preliminary evaluations, support risk stratification, and guide patients toward suitable early interventions. By identifying these individuals at the first point of contact, AI reduces delays that typically hinder access to care and enhances the overall efficiency of downstream service utilization. Together, these examples illustrate how AI facilitates automated data intake, real-time risk stratification, and more precise routing at the first point of contact, thereby strengthening the gatekeeping capacity of PHC.

Coordination: multi-level and cross-setting care coordination

Coordination refers to the role of primary care as the central hub that manages information exchange and aligns decisions across multiple care settings. Effective coordination prevents duplicate testing, conflicting treatment plans, and missed interventions—issues that disproportionately affect patients with complex chronic conditions12. However, current referral pathways are often mired by inappropriate referrals, incomplete information, and outdated communication systems, contributing to delayed specialty care access and fragmented care13.

AI-enabled tools can address these gaps in coordination by improving the accuracy and consistency of inter-professional communication. Large language models can generate standardized referral letters and discharge summaries directly from electronic medical record (EMR) data, achieving up to 99.25% accuracy in zero-shot summarization14 and demonstrating strong agreement with human clinicians15. The automation of medical documentation reduces clinician burden, minimizes communication errors16, and ensures more complete, timely information transfer17.

AI also strengthens cross-setting and population-level coordination, an increasingly essential function as health outcomes are shaped not only by medical services but also by community resources and social determinants. Population health management (PHM) focuses on integrating diverse data sources and aligning care delivery across settings within defined communities18. In PHM, AI improves coordination by analysing large, heterogeneous datasets—EHRs, monitoring devices, genomic information, and socioeconomic indicators—to identify vulnerable subgroups and anticipate where disparities in access or outcomes are most likely to emerge19. AI-enabled platforms further enhance coordination between primary care and social or community services. A social risk management initiative in the state of Nebraska (United States of America) used a digital care navigation platform to streamline outreach, assess the unmet social needs of patients, connect them with community resources, and support follow-up through community health workers; this approach serves as a scalable model for integrating social care into population-focused primary care20. By enabling proactive identification and timely intervention across clinical and non-clinical settings, AI reinforces the role of primary care as the central coordinating hub that aligns medical care, social support, and population-level health strategies.

Comprehensiveness: data-informed comprehensive care

Primary care is uniquely positioned to deliver lifelong comprehensive care, addressing prevention, acute illness, chronic disease management, and rehabilitation21. While the depth of expertise in individual medical domains has expanded, the ability of primary care physicians to manage a breadth of conditions has become strained22. Enhancing comprehensiveness—the capacity to address the full spectrum of health needs within primary care—is essential for reducing fragmented healthcare.

AI-enabled diagnostic tools address comprehensiveness challenges by supporting more consistent recognition and structured interpretation of findings at the point of care, functioning as digital aids that augment clinician judgment. In a randomized controlled trial, AI decision support for spirometry improved primary care clinicians’ preferred-diagnosis prediction by 9.0%p(95% CI, 4.5–13.3; P = 0.001) overall, and by 15.9%p(95% CI, 9.0–22.7; P < 0.001) in COPD cases23. When integrated into primary care workflows, these AI-augmented clinical decision support systems may enable generalists to identify abnormalities earlier, interpret structured findings more consistently, and make more appropriate referral decisions within their scope of practice, thereby reducing preventable referral delays and improving timely access to specialist care24. While clinical uncertainty may persist and some cases may exceed generalist competence, such tools can substantively strengthen primary care comprehensiveness when implemented within validated workflows that require mandatory clinician verification and escalation25,26.

Beyond such decision support, AI-supported opportunistic screening enhances the preventive dimension of primary care. AI augments the foundational preventive role of PHC by screening digital data (both passively and actively collected) from the patient to identify latent disease signals and trigger timely alerts, effectively functioning as a perpetual, personalized digital health check-up. The NOTIFY-1 project demonstrated that AI-based screening for coronary artery calcium deposits on existing chest CT scans, combined with notifying doctors and patients, resulted in a seven-fold increase in statin prescription rates (51.2% vs 6.9%, p < 0.001) at 6 months after the initial screening27. It contributed to risk estimation and identification in statin-eligible individuals based on 10-year ASCVD risk, none of whom were receiving statins at baseline. Similarly, Pickhardt et al. used Markov modelling to demonstrate that AI-assisted opportunistic CT screening for cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, and sarcopenia using automated body composition algorithms was cost-saving and clinically superior to both “treat none” and “treat all” strategies in 55-year-old cohorts over a 10-year period28. Instead of requiring separate clinical encounters solely for screening, AI systems can analyse data obtained for other clinical indications to identify incidental findings of substantial clinical importance.

At a fundamental level, comprehensiveness in primary care settings requires establishing a robust informational infrastructure that reflects the unique characteristics of primary care delivery. Unlike hospital-based specialist care, primary care manages patients with lower pretest probabilities of specific conditions, addresses a greater breadth of considerations across the life course and multiple disciplines, provides care regardless of health state, and accumulates more granular longitudinal data per patient over time29. These characteristics necessitate comprehensive health records (CHRs) that integrate institutional electronic medical records with patient-generated health data from wearable devices, personal health records, and digital therapeutics. AI facilitates this integration by managing heterogeneous datasets, enhancing semantic interoperability through standardized terminologies, and enabling sophisticated clinical decision support30. Generative AI and natural language processing tools have made semantic interoperability increasingly feasible by automating the mapping of unstructured clinical text into standardized codes, such as SNOMED CT, ensuring consistent data interpretation across care settings31. Large language models demonstrate improved clinical text normalization and entity recognition when leveraging standardized terminologies, helping to establish a reliable data foundation necessary for comprehensive, personalized care32.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate how AI strengthens comprehensiveness in the primary care domain by supporting more consistent diagnostic interpretation, enabling opportunistic detection, and creating comprehensive health records that address health needs across the full continuum of care.

Continuity: continuous digital monitoring loop

Continuity of care—encompassing informational, managerial, and relational dimensions—is fundamental for effective primary care delivery. Yet, traditional models often operate episodically, with substantial gaps between clinical encounters that compromise longitudinal patient management33. The fragmentation of health information across multiple providers, reliance on patient recall for chronic disease monitoring, and increasing administrative burdens that displace face-to-face interaction collectively undermine the sustained relationships essential to patient-centred care. AI-enabled systems address these structural limitations by establishing continuous digital monitoring loops that extend care beyond clinic visits, while preserving the therapeutic alliance between patients and providers.

Informational continuity is frequently compromised when patients utilize multiple care settings, requiring repeated reconstruction of medical histories and creating knowledge gaps that hinder clinical decision-making33. Generative AI systems address this fragmentation by synthesizing dispersed medical records, laboratory results, specialist correspondence, and patient-generated health data into unified, longitudinal health profiles30. Large language models can extract clinically relevant information from unstructured documents and generate comprehensive summaries that preserve context across institutional boundaries, ensuring that critical information—including medication changes, specialist recommendations, and care preferences—remains accessible to all members of the care team14. This consolidation reduces redundancy in clinical documentation and mitigates information loss during care transitions without imposing additional cognitive burden on clinicians.

Management continuity is particularly vulnerable during the interval between scheduled healthcare visits, when chronic disease monitoring predominantly relies on patient self-reporting and often results in delayed recognition of clinical deterioration33. AI-enabled remote patient monitoring (RPM) transforms this reactive paradigm by continuously analysing physiologic parameters and behavioural data to detect early signs of decompensation before acute events occur. A randomized clinical trial using the Twin Precision Treatment system demonstrated that AI-guided personalized lifestyle modification, supported by continuous glucose monitoring and digital feedback, enabled 71.0% of patients with type 2 diabetes to achieve remission or medication de-escalation34. Similarly, the GluCare.Health hybrid care model, which combines RPM with AI-driven clinical decision support, demonstrated significant improvements in glycaemic control compared with standard care(HbA1c −2.19% vs. −0.10%, P < 0.001), illustrating how continuous digital monitoring can maintain management consistency outside traditional clinical settings35. These applications exemplify predictive risk stratification, wherein AI systems identify trajectories toward clinical instability and trigger pre-emptive interventions, thereby preventing hospitalizations and emergency department visits.

Relational continuity, the therapeutic relationship between patients and providers that fosters trust and treatment adherence, is increasingly threatened by administrative demands that reduce the time available for direct patient interaction33. Paradoxically, AI systems can strengthen, rather than diminish, this human dimension of care by automating routine communication and documentation tasks. A recent study demonstrated that generative AI-assisted responses to patient messages reduced clinician administrative burden while maintaining response quality and timeliness, thereby preserving limited face-to-face time for high-value interactions that build therapeutic relationships36. This human-AI partnership enables providers to function with maximal clinical scope while maintaining the personal engagement essential to patient-centred care. Furthermore, the continuous presence of AI-enabled patient support platforms, including digital therapeutics and automated health coaching, sustains a sense of connection between formal clinical encounters, reinforcing patients’ perception of ongoing care and attention from their primary care team37. These applications illustrate the capacity of AI to advance continuity across all three dimensions concurrently, thereby facilitating a shift from episodic interventions to an enduring, data-driven collaborative relationship between patients and healthcare providers.

Trust, oversight, and accountability within the 1.5-Tier Healthcare System

The implementation of the 1.5-tier framework requires a human-in-the-loop architecture, where AI supports intake, prioritization, documentation, risk stratification, and longitudinal monitoring. However, diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making remains the responsibility of licensed clinicians and healthcare institutions26. Consistent with risk-based regulatory approaches to medical AI38, the intensity of clinician oversight should scale with each AI function’s autonomy and clinical consequence: low-consequence supportive functions (e.g., documentation, administrative summarization, scheduling) can operate with retrospective review; intermediate functions (e.g., triage prioritization, decision-support prompts) require contemporaneous clinician review before action; and high-consequence functions (e.g., autonomous diagnostic recommendations, autonomous medication titration) require physician confirmation, specialist review, or are not deployed at all. This calibration is especially important for generative AI, which may produce hallucinated, incomplete, or contextually inappropriate outputs that must remain subject to clinician verification, escalation protocols, and post-deployment monitoring25.

If function-level oversight governs individual tools, system-level safety depends on three interlocking loops: a clinical loop at the point of care, where clinicians verify and act on AI outputs; a governance loop that approves and monitors tools across the institution; and a learning loop that tracks performance drift and updates models after deployment26. Asking frontline clinicians to compensate for governance failures by clicking “accept” is inadequate to ensure safety26. Within this architecture, patient trust in AI-mediated care cannot be presumed. As experience from rural AI deployment has shown, false alarms, fragmented care pathways, and unreliable infrastructure can erode trust faster than any single algorithmic gain can rebuild it24. Trust must therefore be earned through transparent disclosure of AI use, bounded deployment in validated use cases, clear escalation pathways, and governance mechanisms that make explicit accountability for errors, overrides, and missed detections. Responsibility does not lie with the model itself: under existing legal and regulatory frameworks, liability remains with the deploying institution and the supervising clinician, and as AI tools transition toward more autonomous functions, accountability frameworks must evolve in parallel through coordination among clinicians, institutions, regulators, and developers. In the South Korean context, this implies that the framework’s success depends not only on algorithm performance but on closing all three loops—through incentive realignment, institutional governance boards, and post-deployment monitoring infrastructure that the system has not yet established at scale. This safety architecture is not a constraint on AI-enabled primary care but its precondition.

Adapting the model to diverse healthcare system contexts

Traditional models that divide healthcare into primary, secondary, and tertiary tiers were not designed to accommodate algorithmic assessment, automated routing, nor population-level risk identification. Consequently, AI-enabled tools, such as digital triage, predictive risk stratification, and automated care coordination, often remain peripheral to formal care pathways. Furthermore, these AI systems are inconsistently adopted and disconnected from frameworks governing resource allocation, clinical workflows, and quality measurement. This conceptual ambiguity limits the systemic integration and scalability of AI applications, preventing health systems from harnessing the potential of AI to address fundamental structural challenges in primary care delivery. To fully harness the potential benefits of AI, it is essential to reconceptualize these digital processes as an integral layer of healthcare delivery, rather than mere technical add-ons.

The 1.5-Tier Healthcare Framework addresses this gap by establishing a distinct intermediary layer between primary and secondary care, formalizing the role of AI-mediated services. This framework encompasses early risk stratification, digital triage, PHM-driven segmentation, automated referrals, and cross-setting information coordination—functions that occur before, during, or alongside traditional clinical evaluation to augment primary care capacity without supplanting clinician expertise (Fig. 1). Recognizing these activities as a systematic tier enables better alignment of AI with operations, standardized metrics, and more sustainable funding. However, effective implementation must account for variation in local infrastructure, workforce, and disease burden, with the framework adaptable to specific contextual barriers across diverse healthcare environments (Fig. 3)39.

High-income urban settings: addressing fragmentation and operational inefficiency

Despite abundant specialist capacity and advanced diagnostic infrastructure, high-income urban systems face challenges rooted in complexity and fragmentation. Patients frequently bypass primary care to access specialists directly, undermining gatekeeping functions and leading to duplicative testing, uncoordinated transitions, and escalating costs40. Primary care physicians face mounting administrative burdens—documentation requirements, prior authorization, care coordination across multiple specialists—contributing to burnout and reducing direct patient care time41. Aging populations with multimorbidity require intensive longitudinal management that strains the capacity of existing healthcare systems42.

The 1.5-Tier framework positions AI-enabled functions as an intermediate layer strengthening the gatekeeping and coordination capacity of primary care without additional workforce needs. AI-powered triage and risk stratification guide appropriate routing at first contact, reducing unnecessary specialist referrals while ensuring timely attention for high-risk patients43. Automated documentation and generative AI-assisted correspondence reduces administrative burden, freeing cognitive resources for complex decision-making44. Predictive analytics with remote monitoring can identify decompensation risk before acute events, enabling pre-emptive interventions that prevent emergency room visits and hospitalization45. PHM platforms segment populations by risk and need, enabling targeted outreach that optimizes clinical outcomes and operational efficiency.

Resource-limited rural settings: task-shifting and access expansion

Rural health systems in low- and middle-income countries face severe shortages of trained healthcare workers, particularly physicians with specialty expertise. Primary care is predominantly delivered by nurses, midwives, and community health workers (CHWs) with minimal diagnostic infrastructure46,47. Such structural features create substantial gaps in diagnostic accuracy and decision-making capacity, resulting in delayed diagnoses, inappropriate treatment, and preventable mortality—particularly for conditions requiring specialist evaluation48.

The 1.5-Tier framework embeds AI-enabled diagnostic and decision support tools into frontline workflows, supporting standardized screening and referral prioritization by non-physician providers under task-shifting arrangements. Point-of-care AI systems with portable devices enable CHWs and nurses to capture standardized data and apply structured screening criteria, with positive findings and uncertain cases escalated for specialist review through telehealth or referral pathways. For instance, AI-powered analysis of smartphone-captured fundus photographs has demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to specialist grading in detecting diabetic retinopathy and glaucoma49, providing a defensible basis for community-level screening when paired with appropriate referral channels. Malaysia’s Lung Health Initiative exemplifies this systemic integration, linking AI-assisted radiography to centralized digital platforms for asymptomatic tuberculosis detection and replacing fragmented referral processes with coordinated, risk-based management pathways, thereby reinforcing the full spectrum of primary care functions—first contact, comprehensiveness, coordination, and continuity—within a unified framework50,51. Telehealth platforms with AI triage further connect remote communities with distant specialists while prioritizing referrals by clinical urgency. Formalizing AI-assisted approaches as a recognized tier enables standardized training protocols, supply chain integration, and sustainable financing to support task-shifting as a core primary care delivery.

Crisis-affected and fragile settings: maintaining essential functions

Healthcare in crisis-affected and fragile settings is characterized by systemic collapse of infrastructure from conflict, natural disasters, or protracted humanitarian emergencies. Essential health care components—facilities, supply chains, information systems, trained personnel—may be destroyed, disrupted, or inaccessible52. Care is delivered through mobile clinics, temporary hospitals, or minimally equipped sites, staffed by volunteers or remaining workers under extreme resource constraints. Priorities centre on trauma management, infectious disease response, and maternal-child health52.

The 1.5-Tier framework must prioritize minimal-infrastructure AI applications functioning despite severe limitations and intermittent connectivity. Offline-capable diagnostic tools embedded in mobile devices support clinical decision-making when internet access is unreliable, enabling more accurate treatment decisions without specialist consultation53. Simplified triage algorithms help allocate scarce resources, including oxygen masks, surgical capacity, and evacuation transport, to patients most likely to benefit, improving survival when demand exceeds capacity54. These applications entail extreme simplicity, minimal training requirements, and resilience to infrastructure disruption—distinguishing crisis-appropriate from well-resourced setting tools.

Emerging integration of AI in South Korea’s healthcare system

South Korea exemplifies a digitally advanced health system where persistent structural contradictions exist alongside significant technological capacity. Weak gatekeeping within formal tiering is evident: 85–90% of hospital outpatient visits for uncomplicated chronic disease have been deemed unnecessary, and co-payment differentials have proven insufficient to redirect demand55–57. This situation coexists with hospital- and specialist-centric care delivery, in which primary care is predominantly provided by independent, specialist-trained physicians operating fee-for-service contracts with limited inter-institutional coordination58–60. Volume-driven encounters further exacerbate these challenges. Korea leads the OECD in outpatient utilization, with 18 in-person doctor consultations per person in 2023, compared to the OECD average of 6.5. This high volume results in brief encounters that constrain opportunities for prevention, patient education, and verification of AI decision-support outputs61.

In addition to workflow contradictions, a parallel information-infrastructure challenge persists: electronic medical record(EMR) adoption exceeds 95 percent across hospitals and clinics62, yet vendor heterogeneity and weak semantic interoperability continue to fragment longitudinal records despite a national health information exchange57. Taken together, these challenges are better understood not as discrete technical shortcomings but as socio-technical conditions. Weak gatekeeping is reinforced by fee-for-service payment structures and specialist-oriented professional norms, while the OECD-highest consultation volume shapes the workflow conditions within which any technological intervention must function. At the same time, longitudinal patient records remain fragmented despite EMR adoption. Since these factors are interconnected, an AI application validated in one healthcare system is unlikely to transfer seamlessly to another; in the Korean context, it is this specific configuration of constraints, rather than technological preparedness alone, that ultimately defines the scope of what AI-enabled primary care can accomplish25,63. Korea serves as a valuable natural experiment: a digitally capable yet organizationally fragmented system where the boundary conditions of AI deployment are defined by real institutional constraints. It is also unusually comprehensive as a test case: within a single universal coverage system, Korea simultaneously demonstrates the fragmentation and specialist bypass typical of high-income urban settings, as well as the physician shortages and access gaps found in resource-limited rural regions64,65, thereby enabling examination of the framework’s distinct components within one institutional and regulatory environment.

Korea’s AI infrastructure has matured rapidly, but predominantly at the specialist and research level. The Dr. Answer program (1.0, 2018-2020; 2.0, 2021-2024), a Korean government Digital New Deal initiative, has produced AI-powered diagnostic and prognostic software targeting integration into tertiary clinical workflows66. The Digital Medical Products Act, enacted in 2024 and fully implemented in January 2025, establishes a legal framework for the classification, approval, and reimbursement of software-based medical devices, including generative AI tools, alongside the Innovative Medical Device designation67. The National Project of Bio-Big Data, a multi-agency Korean government initiative, is enrolling 772,000 participants during Phase 1 (2024–2028), with a target of one million by 203268,69. These initiatives are concentrated in tertiary diagnostics, research infrastructure, and software-device regulation; primary care has not been identified as an explicit deployment locus, leaving its redeployment as a documented policy gap. Notably, this concentration of AI investment in specialist, emergency, and research domains is consistent with the international pattern in which AI tools are typically developed within specialty disciplines and subsequently deployed into primary care. Korea, therefore, has the technical substrate, regulatory infrastructure, and policy commitment to attempt such redeployment. The unresolved question is whether the structural contradictions identified above—which themselves shaped the specialist-centric character of Korea’s AI investment—will permit its primary-care redeployment at scale. The 1.5-tier framework proposes the operational architecture for this attempt; whether it succeeds depends on the parallel realignment of incentives, governance, and learning-loop infrastructure, which Korea’s system has not yet established.

Sustained at scale, the 1.5-tier layer constitutes a learning health system challenge rather than a discrete technological deployment: its value is contingent on the system’s capacity to convert data generated through routine care into iterative improvements in practice70,71. Korea possesses much of the requisite substrate. Near-universal EMR adoption, the national claims database, and the planned 772,000-participant bio-big-data resource constitute an informational foundation of considerable depth, while an expanding portfolio of regulated AI tools, supported by the Digital Medical Products Act, provides the regulatory and reimbursement mechanisms required to evaluate and scale them. These assets, however, do not address the realignment of clinical incentives and professional culture on which a functioning learning system ultimately depends. A specialist-centric delivery model organized around fee-for-service encounters and constrained consultation times affords limited capacity for the verification, feedback, and iterative refinement that close the gap between evidence and practice. It is this organizational deficit, rather than any shortage of data or technological capacity, that the 1.5-tier framework is intended to redress. Its principal contribution is therefore organizational rather than technological: it furnishes the operational layer through which Korea’s existing digital and policy assets may be integrated into a system capable of continuous learning, in contrast to a collection of disconnected tools.

Korea’s combination of universal coverage, centralized governance, and robust digital infrastructure defines the boundary conditions for generalizability; these features are not fully replicable in fragmented or low-connectivity systems. Most initiatives are still in early stages of implementation, and long-term outcome data remain limited. Successful translation depends on explicit attention to safety, accountability, and alignment of AI deployment with system priorities1,24. The Korean case therefore illustrates a feasible pathway for closing these learning loops through a deliberately designed intermediary layer rather than through wholesale redesign.

From concierge medicine to “concierge care for all”: the promise of 1.5-Tier Healthcare

Concierge medicine has gained traction in the United States as patients seek more continuous, personalized, and attentive care, in contrast to fragmented fee-for-service primary care. Through annual retainers, patients gain privileged access to physicians, longer consultations, and preventive services72. This trend reflects the growing desire for sustained physician relationships and high-quality care.

However, the number of concierge medicine available is structurally limited. Annual membership fees range from 1500 US dollars to 5000 US dollars, restricting access to affluent populations73. Additionally, concierge practices reduce the size of patients’ panel and intensify physician shortages74. Thus, despite appealing to patients’ expectations, concierge medicine remains expensive and exclusive.

The 1.5-Tier Healthcare model is a scalable alternative. By integrating AI-enabled decision support, remote patient monitoring, opportunistic screening, and concurrent specialist supervision, personalised care can be extended beyond one-to-one physician encounters. Unlike concierge models, the 1.5-Tier model operates under value-based purchasing and reduces marginal costs while enhancing continuity and equity.

This paradigm effectively transforms concierge care from an elitist privilege into a public good, or “concierge medicine for all”. By leveraging digital tools to scale continuous, people-centred care, 1.5-Tier Healthcare addresses both patient aspirations for high-quality services and the systemic challenges of cost and workforce shortages.

Conclusion and recommendations

The 1.5-Tier Healthcare System presents a pragmatic and scalable framework for reimagining PHC in the era of AI. By positioning enhanced primary care as the bridge between community clinics and hospitals, it enables earlier diagnosis, continuous management of chronic conditions, and more equitable access to specialist expertise. Integrating AI into this structure strengthens the preventive and coordinative roles of primary care, allowing healthcare systems to respond more effectively to population aging, chronic disease, and workforce shortages.

Implementing this framework will require sustained investment in digital infrastructure, interoperable health data systems, and a workforce capable of responsibly leveraging AI. Governments should align primary care reforms with digital transformation strategies, while healthcare providers and technology developers collaborate to design usable, context-sensitive tools that support, rather than replace, clinical judgment.

As countries pursue UHC, the 1.5-Tier model offers a path towards healthcare that is not only efficient but also human-centred, equitable, and resilient. With careful governance and inclusive implementation, this model can help transform fragmented systems into adaptable health ecosystems that continuously improve population health outcomes.
