Non-Lethal heat shock induces COMMD gene activation and enhances pathogen defense in Procambarus clarkii
Authors: Xin Zhang, Xiuhong Cai, Shirui Yue, Zhangxuan Chen, Yulong Sun, Shunchang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COMMD (copper metabolism MURR1 domain-containing) protein family, characterized by a conserved COMM domain, plays crucial roles in metal ion transport and NF-κB signaling regulation in vertebrates. However, its functional roles in invertebrates, particularly crustaceans, remain poorly understood. This study aimed to identify and characterize COMMD family genes in red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii) and to investigate their involvement in immune responses under environmental stress.ResultsSeven COMMD genes (COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD4, COMMD5, COMMD7, COMMD8, and COMMD10) were identified and analyzed in P. clarkii. These genes exhibited distinct genomic structures and tissue-specific expression profiles. COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD8, and COMMD10 were predominantly expressed in hemocytes and gills, whereas COMMD4 and COMMD7 showed higher expression in the hepatopancreas. Non-lethal heat shock (NLHS) significantly enhanced pathogen resistance in P. clarkii, which was accompanied by a rapid and strong induction of COMMD8. Transcriptome analysis and RNA interference experiments revealed that COMMD8 positively regulates the TLR/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. Silencing of COMMD8 suppressed TLR1 and MyD88 expression and delayed NF-κB activation, indicating its role as a key modulator of immune signaling under stress conditions.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that COMMD8 is a critical regulator of stress-induced immune responses in P. clarkii, mediating enhanced resistance through modulation of innate immune signaling. These findings offer new insights into the function of COMMD proteins in crustaceans and contribute to a better understanding of how environmental stress influences invertebrate immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12205-5.Keywords: COMMD gene family, Non-lethal heat shock, Immune regulation, Procambarus clarkii

Introduction

The Copper Metabolism MURR1 Domain-Containing Proteins (COMMD) family is a highly conserved evolutionary protein family, named for their shared conserved COMMD domain, a unique C-terminal motif present in all family members [1, 2]. This domain generally contains around 100 amino acids and has a unique spatial shape that governs protein-protein interactions and aids in the binding and transport of metal ions [1, 3]. Since 1997, when the first COMMD family member, MURR1 (Metal Regulatory Transcription Factor 1), was discovered in humans, more study has revealed the family’s important functions in a variety of physiological and pathological processes across species [4]. In mammals, the COMMD family has been demonstrated to play a role in copper ion homeostasis. For instance, COMMD1 functions as a co-factor for the copper transport protein ATP7B, increasing copper ion transport and excretion in hepatocytes, and functional defects are strongly connected with the start of Wilson’s disease [5]. Furthermore, COMMD family members play critical roles in inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and signal transduction [6]. Studies indicate that the COMMD3/4 complex interacts with IKKβ (inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase subunit beta, a key kinase that activates NF-κB signaling), reducing IκB (inhibitor of NF-κB, a protein that sequesters NF-κB in the cytoplasm) phosphorylation, which in turn suppresses NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells, a central transcription factor regulating immune and stress responses) activation and prevents overactivation of inflammatory reactions [7, 8].

Significant progress has been made in understanding the functional roles of the COMMD gene family in mammals. However, aquatic creature research is still in its early phases. Recent advancements in genomics and transcriptomics have led to new breakthroughs in the identification and functional analysis of the COMMD gene family in aquatic species. Nine COMMD genes have been identified in the genome of Pacific abalone (Haliotis discus hannai), and transcriptomic analysis reveals that HdhCOMMD4 is significantly upregulated in hemocytes 12 h post-infection with Vibrio parahaemolyticus, exhibiting a positive correlation with the expression of inflammatory factors [9]. Under heat shock conditions at 30 °C, the expression of HdhCOMMD5 in the hepatopancreas increased 4.1-fold compared to the control group within 48 h, suggesting its involvement in abiotic stress responses [9]. In dwarf surf clam (Mulinia lateralis), COMMD4 and COMMD9 function as biomarkers for V. nguillarum infection and copper ion stress, with a greater sensitivity observed in the hepatopancreas compared to gill tissues, indicating a tissue-specific functional division [10]. Ten COMMD genes have been identified in large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea), of which LcCOMMD4 and LcCOMMD8 show a positive correlation with the expression of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α following infection with Pseudomonas plecoglossicida (a pathogenic bacterium), suggesting that they enhance immune responses by activating the NF-κB pathway [11]. The expression of PoCOMMD1 in olive flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) significantly increases after infection with Edwardsiella tarda (a gram-negative bacterium), and the absence of its COMMD domain results in the loss of NF-κB activation capability, thereby confirming the essential role of this conserved domain in immunity [12]. In amphioxus (Branchiostoma belcheri), studies have identified COMMD4 and COMMD6 with conserved domains, showing tissue-specific expression and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-responsive induction, while a genome-wide analysis of ten COMMD members revealed their close evolutionary link to vertebrates and potential roles in innate immunity through expression dynamics [13–15]. In contrast to mollusks and fish, research on the COMMD gene family in crustaceans remains relatively underdeveloped.

The red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii) is a freshwater crustacean species native to North America [16]. Following its introduction to China in the 1930 s, it has rapidly emerged as a key aquaculture species in the country, owing to its rapid growth, amazing resilience to environmental variations, high nutritional value, and strong market demand [17]. Currently, P. clarkii output in China surpasses 2 million tons per year, building a full industrial framework that covers breeding, processing, and sales, therefore greatly contributing to rural economic development and increasing farmer incomes [18].

As the scale of aquaculture expands and the degree of intensification increases, P. clarkii faces increasingly severe challenges from diseases [19]. Water temperature, a critical abiotic regulatory factor, is essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis in aquatic organisms [20]. Studies indicate that when water temperature significantly deviates from the optimal range, the immune defense capabilities of crustaceans against pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria and viruses, are markedly diminished [20, 21]. In the context of global warming, fluctuations in environmental temperature have emerged as significant ecological stressors impacting the metabolism, growth, and reproduction of crustaceans [22, 23]. Extreme high-temperature events may disrupt the physiological balance of shrimp and potentially lead to individual mortality [24]. Notably, non-lethal heat shock treatment (NLHS) can enhance immune system function by activating the organism’s adaptive responses [25–27]. Over long periods of evolution, organisms have developed complex regulatory networks to cope with environmental stressors such as extreme temperatures and ultraviolet radiation, in which heat shock proteins (HSPs) serve as critical molecular chaperones during stress responses [28, 29]. When subjected to external stress, HSPs are rapidly upregulated, significantly bolstering the organism’s resistance to stress by stabilizing the intracellular environment and preserving protein structure and function [30, 31]. Notably, NLHS induces the upregulation of various members within the HSP70 family, a process that significantly enhances the body’s immune response to combat pathogenic infection [25, 26]. Furthermore, this treatment may alleviate oxidative stress damage and enhance the organism’s antioxidant capacity by modulating antioxidant enzyme systems [32]. However, it has not been clearly established whether this process relies on the regulation of the TLR/NFκB pathway by the COMMD genes.

This study aims to systematically screen the members of the COMMD gene family in P. clarkii using bioinformatics methods alongside genome and transcriptome analyses. The research will elucidate the gene structures, evolutionary relationships, tissue expression characteristics, and conservation of these genes. A NLHS model combined with V. parahaemolyticus infection will be established to closely monitor the dynamic expression patterns of COMMD family members during heat shock treatment and pathogen infection. This study will examine how the COMMD family regulates the “heat shock preconditioning-immune enhancement” process by analyzing their interactions with TLR/NFκB signaling factors and immune effector molecules. This study will fill a gap in research on the COMMD gene family in crustaceans by providing new insights into P. clarkii’s immune mechanisms in response to environmental stress and pathogenic infections, as well as a scientific basis for optimizing breeding strategies to increase resilience and disease control capabilities.

Methods

Genome-wide identification and protein characteristic analysis of the COMMD gene family

The genomic and annotation data for P. clarkii (GCA_040958095.1) were obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Using the Pfam database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/entry/pfam/), we identified the Hidden Markov Model seed file for the COMM_domain (PF07258). This seed file was then used as a query in the HMMER software (version 3.2; http://hmmer.org/) to search for candidate sequences containing the COMM_domain within the annotated protein-coding genes in the P. clarkii genome. Concurrently, the COMMD sequence of Larimichthys crocea, Drosophila melanogaster, and H. discus hannai was obtained from NCBI and utilized as a query sequence for a BLASTP search with an e-value threshold of 1e-5. Based on these findings, potential members of the COMMD family in P. clarkii were identified. To further confirm the presence of COMMD characteristic sequences, public databases such as CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) were used. Protein sequences that lacked these characteristic domains were excluded, resulting in the final identification of the COMMD family members in P. clarkii. The amino acid number, molecular weight (MW), and isoelectric point (pI) of the COMMD genes were analyzed using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Structural characterization of P. clarkii COMMDs

The locations of exons and introns within the COMMD genes were determined from the gff3 file of the P. clarkii genome annotation and extracted using TBtools software [33]. To investigate the structural characteristics of the P. clarkii COMMD proteins, conserved motifs were analyzed using the MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/), with the maximum number of motifs set to 10. The results were also visualized using TBtools software. The protein sequences were submitted to the SOPMA online tool for secondary structure prediction. The tertiary structure of COMMD was predicted using the Swiss-Model online tool, with only models showing a similarity between the template and target sequences greater than 30% being accepted.

Chromosome localization and phylogenetic analysis of PcCOMMD family

The genome annotation file of P. clarkii was retrieved from its genome database, and the chromosomal locations of the PcCOMMD family members, as well as the chromosome lengths, were extracted using the Gene Location Visualize tool from the GTF/GFF plugin in TBtools software. A chromosomal location map of the P. clarkii COMMD genes was then generated. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method in MEGA 6.0 software, with a bootstrap value of 1000 and default settings for other parameters.

Experimental animals and sample collection procedures

This study utilized P. clarkii (15–20 g body weight) obtained from a commercial aquaculture farm in Huainan, Anhui Province. After batch acclimation with routine feeding and water management, P. clarkii underwent a 7-day stabilization period to ensure consistent physiological baselines. The experimental protocol comprised two sequential phases:

Heat Shock Phase (HSS):

Control group (NT_group): P. clarkii were maintained in 26 °C water for 6 h without heat shock.

Control group (NT_group): P. clarkii were maintained in 26 °C water for 6 h without heat shock.

Heat shock treatment group (HT_group): P. clarkii underwent heat treatment in a 32 °C water bath for 2 h, followed by a 4 h recovery period at 26 °C.

Heat shock treatment group (HT_group): P. clarkii underwent heat treatment in a 32 °C water bath for 2 h, followed by a 4 h recovery period at 26 °C.

Pathogen Challenge Phase (PIS):

HTC_group: The HT_group received a 30 µL injection of normal saline (NS).

HTC_group: The HT_group received a 30 µL injection of normal saline (NS).

HTV_group: The HT_group was injected with 30 µL of V. parahaemolyticus (1.0 × 108 CFU/mL).

HTV_group: The HT_group was injected with 30 µL of V. parahaemolyticus (1.0 × 108 CFU/mL).

NTC_group: The NT_group was injected with 30 µL of NS.

NTC_group: The NT_group was injected with 30 µL of NS.

NTV_group: The NT_group received a 30 µL injection of V. parahaemolyticus (1.0 × 108 CFU/mL).

NTV_group: The NT_group received a 30 µL injection of V. parahaemolyticus (1.0 × 108 CFU/mL).

Hemolymph was collected from six P. clarkii per group in both HSS and PIS at 3 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. Hemocytes were isolated and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA extraction.

Survival rate statistics

The survival rate of P. clarkii was monitored at 0 h, 3 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h after V. parahaemolyticus injection under NLHS conditions. The analysis result of Survival Analysis were generated using the R software packages “survminer” through the CNSknowall (https://cnsknowall.com/index.html#/HomePage). COMMD gene expression in response to bacterial infection after NLHS treatment

Total RNA was extracted from hemocytes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and assessed for quality using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and electrophoresis. 1 µg RNA was used for cDNA synthesis with M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was employed to detect gene expression in hemocytes under different treatments, using gene-specific primers (Table S1), and the amplified cDNA products were 200–300 bp in length. The P. clarkii Gapdh gene (Accession No. AB094145) was the housekeeping gene. Reactions were run on a LightCycler 96 Roche Thermal Cycler using 10× SYBR Green Master Mix (Yisheng, Shanghai, China), 9 µL of 50-fold diluted cDNA (equivalent to 0.18 µL of original cDNA after reverse transcription), and 0.5 µL primers (10 µM). Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Melting curves confirmed single PCR products. Relative expression was calculated using the comparative CT method. Each sample was tested in triplicate, and data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 20. Student’s t-test was employed to determine significant differences between groups. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A heatmap visualized gene expression data using R v4.5.0. To further validate the expression patterns of COMMD family genes and their regulatory roles in immune responses, we analyzed RNA-Seq datasets (PRJNA1107630) generated from our previous transcriptomic profiling under identical experimental conditions.

Suppression of COMMD8 gene expression by Double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)

The green fluorescent protein (GFP) gene from pEGFP-N1 plasmid was used as a negative control. Primer pairs targeting the coding region of COMMD8 (GenBank: LOC123759427) and GFP were designed with T7 promoter sequences appended to the 5’-ends (primer details provided in Table S1). PCR products were purified, verified by Sanger sequencing, and used as templates for in vitro transcription with T7 RNA polymerase (Promega). Single-stranded RNAs (ssRNAs) of both sense and antisense orientations were synthesized, then annealed by gradually cooling from 75 °C (15 min) to 65 °C (15 min), followed by a slow cooling rate of 0.1 °C/s to generate double-stranded RNA (dsRNA). Hemocytes were isolated from healthy crayfish and cultured in L-15 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Without using transfection reagents, COMMD8 dsRNA or GFP dsRNA (final concentration: 5 µg/mL) was directly added to the culture medium [34]. A medium-only group served as the blank control. Each treatment was performed with six biological replicates, incubated at 27 °C for 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h. Total RNA was extracted from harvested hemocytes, and qRT-PCR was conducted using Gapdh as the reference gene to assess COMMD8 knockdown efficiency and expression changes of COMMDs and other immune related genes. The intermolecular interaction network was constructed using String v12.0 (https://string-db.org/) and Cytoscape 3.10.3 (https://cytoscape.org/).

Results

Enhanced pathogen resistance in P. clarkii following NLHS

Survival analysis was conducted to evaluate NLHS-induced protection against V. parahaemolyticus. While both experimental groups showed progressive mortality post-infection, the heat-primed HTV_group maintained higher survival rates than the non-treated NTV_group throughout the observation period (log-rank test, p < 0.05). These results confirm that sublethal thermal exposure confers partial but statistically significant protection against subsequent pathogenic challenge (Fig. 1B).

Genomic analysis and functional diversity of PcCOMMDs

Whole-genome screening revealed seven distinct COMMD genes in P. clarkii, systematically annotated according to chromosomal localization and structural features (Table 1). The encoded proteins exhibited substantial functional diversity, with amino acid lengths spanning 193–222 residues and molecular weights varying from 20.6 to 24.4 kDa, with this variation potentially altering the proteins’ structural flexibility or interaction capacity with stress-responsive substrates/chaperone partners and thereby influencing their roles in stress response and molecular chaperoning. Theoretical pI values (5.55–6.92) further highlighted their acidic nature, which may govern subcellular localization and interaction networks under physiological or stress-induced pH fluctuations.

Conserved Motifs, CDD Domain, and gene structure of COMMD genes

Analysis of PcCOMMD proteins revealed the presence of several conserved motifs, as illustrated in Fig. 2A. These motifs were predominantly associated with the characteristic COMMD domain, known for its crucial role in copper homeostasis and cellular signaling pathways. The conservation of these motifs across different COMMD proteins suggests shared functional mechanisms in metal ion regulation and protein-protein interactions. Notably, all COMMD members contained the conserved COMMD functional domain essential for their primary biological roles, including copper metabolism regulation through interactions with copper transporters and modulation of NF-κB signaling pathways. While the core COMMD domain was present in all family members, variations in additional motifs were observed among individual proteins, potentially reflecting functional specialization within the family. In addition to the core COMM domain, COMMD3 was found to contain an extra metallo-dependent hydrolases superfamily domain, which is implicated in metal ion-dependent catalytic activity, potentially influencing copper or iron binding and redox regulation (Fig. 2A).

Chromosomal location of PcCOMMDs

Chromosomal mapping analysis revealed that the seven COMMD family members were distinctly distributed across seven different chromosomes (Fig. 2B). Each COMMD gene occupied a unique chromosomal location without clustering, demonstrating an even dispersion pattern throughout the genome. Specifically, the genes were located as follows: COMMD2 on chromosome NC_091153.1, COMMD3 on NC_091171.1, COMMD4 on NC_091192.1, COMMD5 on NC_091198.1, COMMD7 on NC_091178.1, COMMD8 on NC_091168.1, and COMMD10 on NC_091150.1. This one-gene-per-chromosome distribution pattern suggests potential evolutionary advantages in maintaining functional diversity and regulatory independence among COMMD family members (Fig. 2B).

Structural and phylogenetic analysis of PcCOMMDs

The seven identified members of the PcCOMMD family were subjected to homology modeling using SWISS-MODEL. The result illustrates that these members exhibit structural similarities, characterized by helical and coiled structures, with certain regions displaying analogous folding patterns. This observation suggests a potential sharing of common features in spatial conformation, indicating that such structural similarities may imply a functional or evolutionary relationship among them (Fig. 3).

The predicted 3D structure of PcCOMMD proteins. A-G: The 3D-structure of COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD4, COMMD5, COMMD7, COMMD8, COMMD10, respectively

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PcCOMMDs clustered within specific hierarchical levels of their respective clades. The PcCOMMD2 sequence formed a distinct sub-branch within the COMMD2 clade, separate from sequences of shrimp, while the PcCOMMD5 sequence grouped closely with other crustacean COMMD5 homologs to form an independent sub-cluster distinct from those of mollusks and fish. Collectively, the PcCOMMDs occupied defined hierarchical positions within each clade, reflecting their evolutionary relationships and taxonomic specificity (Fig. 4).

Tissue-Specific expression patterns of COMMD family genes

qRT-PCR was employed to quantify the expression profiles of seven COMMD family genes (COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD4, COMMD5, COMMD7, COMMD8, and COMMD10) across seven tissues of P. clarkii, including hemocytes (He), gill (Gi), hepatopancreas (Hp), eyestalk (E), intestine (I), stomach (St), and muscle (Mu). As illustrated in Fig. 5, these COMMD genes exhibited marked tissue-specific expression divergence, indicative of functional specialization. For COMMD2, the highest transcript level was detected in hemocytes, with moderate expression in intestine and hepatopancreas, while stomach and muscle showed minimal expression (Fig. 5A). COMMD3 displayed predominant expression in the gills, followed by the eyestalk and then the hepatopancreas, whereas extremely low expression was observed in hemocytes, stomach and muscle (Fig. 5B). COMMD4 was highly expressed in hepatopancreas, with noticeable expression in hemocytes and eyestalk, and low expression in stomach (Fig. 5C). COMMD5 showed the highest expression in eyestalk, detectable expression in gill, hepatopancreas and muscle, and limited transcripts in stomach and intestine (Fig. 5D). COMMD7 exhibited dominant expression in hepatopancreas, secondary expression in gill, and low expression levels in hemocytes, intestine and muscle (Fig. 5E). COMMD8 had the highest abundance in hemocytes, significant expression in hepatopancreas, and reduced expression in intestine and muscle (Fig. 5F). COMMD10 showed the highest expression in hemocytes and gill, with moderate expression in eyestalk, while hepatopancreas, stomach and muscle had minimal transcripts (Fig. 5G).

Expression patterns of COMMD genes in response to bacterial challenge after NLHS

To investigate the expression regulation characteristics of COMMD genes in response to V. parahaemolyticus infection following NLHS, qRT-PCR was used to analyze the expression differences of each gene in treatment groups such as NTC_group, HTC_group, NTV_group and HTV_group. Statistical analysis revealed widespread significant expression differences across most groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6). When comparing different treatment groups at the same time points (e.g., HTC_group vs. NTC_group, HTV_group vs. NTV_group at matched time points), COMMD2 (Fig. 6A), COMMD3 (Fig. 6B), COMMD4 (Fig. 6C), COMMD5 (Fig. 6D), and COMMD8 (Fig. 6E) exhibited obvious differential expression across multiple time points (3 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h), with statistically significant differences supported by corresponding p-values. Specifically, COMMD7 showed significant upregulation in HTV-3 h vs. NTV-3 h (p = 0.010), HTC-12 h vs. NTC-12 h (p = 0.009), and HTV-24 h vs. NTV-24 h (p = 0.031) (Fig. 6F). COMMD10 displayed significant differential expression in NTC-3 h vs. HTC-3 h (p = 0.026), NTC-12 h vs. HTC-12 h (p = 0.043), and NTC-48 h vs. HTC-48 h (p = 0.043) (Fig. 6G). These findings collectively indicate that both the nature of treatment and post-treatment duration exert significant regulatory effects on COMMD gene expression, highlighting the functional adaptability of the COMMD family in response to environmental or pathogen challenge. Further presented the relative relationship of the expression levels of each gene through color gradients by heatmap (Fig. 6H). It could be seen that the expression patterns of different COMMD genes in response to various treatment groups (NTC_group, HTC_group, NTV_group, HTV_group) and time points (3 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h) exhibited both commonalities and specificities. Genes such as COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD4, COMMD5, and COMMD8 showed significant fluctuations in expression levels, indicating they responded actively to certain treatment - time combinations. In contrast, COMMD7 and COMMD10 had relatively stable expression levels across different conditions, with a more subdued response. This reflects the diverse functional characteristics of COMMD family members when facing different experimental treatment conditions (Fig. 6H).

COMMD protein interactions and dsRNA-mediated expression effects

To dissect COMMD-mediated immune signaling, we first established the research rationale based on existing knowledge. In vertebrates, COMMD8 acts as a key regulator of the NF-κB pathway, forming a complex with coiled-coil domain containing 22 (CCDC22, a scaffold protein) and Cullin1 (a core component of E3 ubiquitin ligase) to promote IκBα degradation and activate immune responses [35]. Given the conservation of NF-κB signaling among diverse species and growing evidence for COMMD family involvement in invertebrate immunity [9, 10, 15], we hypothesized that P. clarkii COMMD8 mediates pathogen-induced NF-κB activation through similar mechanisms. To test this hypothesis, we first predicted protein-protein interactions (PPIs) of COMMD orthologs with key immune-related genes using STRING 12.0 (Fig. 8). The target immune factors included TLR3, TLR5, NFκB, TLR4, MyD88, Hsp70, TLR2, HSPBP, TLR6, and TLR1—core components of the pattern recognition receptor (PRR)-mediated innate immune signaling pathway in crustaceans. Subsequently, we performed RNA interference to knockdown COMMD8 expression in P. clarkii hemocytes, and targeted the P. clarkii orthologs of these PPI-associated immune genes to measure their expression dynamics across different time points (6 h, 12 h, 24 h) via qPCR.

qPCR analysis revealed that COMMD8 mRNA levels were significantly reduced at multiple time points (6 h, 12 h, and 24 h) in the COMMD8-dsRNA treatment group compared to Control and GFP-dsRNA groups (p < 0.05), confirming successful knockdown and validating the gene silencing model in P. clarkii hemocytes (Fig. 9A). Hsp70 displayed a biphasic response, being significantly downregulated at 6 h (p < 0.05) and upregulated at 12 h and 24 h (p < 0.01) (Fig. 9B). Further analysis of immune-related genes showed that TLR1 was significantly downregulated at 6 h and 12 h (p < 0.05), followed by significant upregulation at 24 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9C), while TLR2 remained unchanged at 6 h but was significantly upregulated at 12 h and 24 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9D). TLR4 and TLR6 showed no significant changes across all time points (Fig. 9E-F). MyD88 was significantly repressed at 6 h (p < 0.05) and significantly elevated above control levels at 24 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9G). NFκB exhibited delayed activation, with sustained upregulation at 24 h and 48 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9H). Among COMMD family members, COMMD2 was significantly upregulated at 24 h (p < 0.05), while COMMD3 was downregulated at 6 h and 12 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9I-J). COMMD4, COMMD5, COMMD7, and COMMD10 showed no significant expression changes (Fig. 9K-N). Additionally, a heat map was generated to more intuitively visualize the above-mentioned results, providing a comprehensive overview of the gene expression patterns across different treatments (Fig. 9O). To integrate PPI predictions with expression data, we utilized Cytoscape to map interactions between P. clarkii COMMD proteins and the PRR-pathway components mentioned above, with qPCR-derived expression values overlaid as node attributes. The resulting network visually illustrated COMMD-mediated modulation of PRR-associated signaling (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Cross-protection induced by NLHS is a well-established adaptive mechanism that enhances crustacean resilience to pathogenic challenge [26, 36]. In line with this concept, P. clarkii pre-treated with NLHS (HTV_group) exhibited significantly improved survival compared with untreated controls (NTV_group), confirming that NLHS enhances resistance against subsequent pathogen infection. The protective effects of NLHS are known to be mediated through the coordinated action of molecular chaperones, such as heat shock proteins (HSPs), and signaling regulators [29]. Among these, the COMMD family has been identified as a critical modulator in copper homeostasis and NF-κB pathway modulation [37]. Our results demonstrate that several COMMD genes, especially COMMD8, were significantly regulated following NLHS and bacterial infection, suggesting their involvement in immune pathway remodeling linked to stress adaptation [9]. The cellular response to heat shock involves a dynamic balance among protein folding, oxidative stress mitigation, and immune activation [38, 39]. The elevated expression of COMMD2, COMMD8, and COMMD10 in hemocytes corresponds to their functional roles as immune effector cells, suggesting that these proteins contribute to NLHS-induced disease resistance by enhancing hemocyte-based immune functions, including phagocytosis and synthesis of antimicrobial peptides.

In this study, we identified seven COMMD genes in P. clarkii via a comprehensive genome-wide screening. The encoded proteins exhibit considerable variation in length (ranging from 193 to 222 amino acids), molecular weight (20.6–24.4 kDa), and isoelectric point (pI 5.55–6.92). These differences indicate functional diversification among the gene family members. Chromosomal mapping localized each PcCOMMD gene to a separate chromosome, revealing a “one gene–one chromosome” distribution pattern. This genomic dispersion, while relatively rare in crustaceans, is reminiscent of patterns observed in model organisms such as Drosophila, and may reflect a regulatory mechanism to preserve gene-specific control [40]. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that PcCOMMD2 branched independently from shrimp COMMD2, while PcCOMMD5 clustered with other crustacean COMMD5, highlighting the unique and class-specific evolutionary trajectory of P. clarkii. The distinct evolutionary paths of these genes may be intrinsically linked to the adaptation of P. clarkii to freshwater habitats. This divergence could have facilitated functional diversification within the COMMD family, thereby enhancing the species’ adaptability to specific ecological niches. This finding aligns with the functional diversification observed in the COMMD family across other species. For instance, COMMD genes are highly conserved among closely related teleost fish such as P. olivaceus and L. crocea, whereas in the basal chordate B. belcheri, the COMMD family exhibits evolutionary features that mirror the chordate-to-vertebrate transition [11, 12, 15].

The core feature of the COMMD family is the conserved COMM domain [41]. All members of P. clarkii contain this domain, confirming its core role in copper metabolism and NF-κB signal regulation [6, 8, 42]. Studies have shown that P. olivaceus PoCOMMD1 can inhibit NF-κB activity through the COMM domain [12]; amphioxus COMMD3/7 participates in innate immunity by regulating the NF-κB pathway [15]; H. discus hannai COMMD4 is upregulated after infection and associated with the NF-κB pathway [9]. These results suggest that the COMMD-NF-κB regulatory axis is a conserved immune mechanism from lower chordates to crustaceans. Conserved motif analysis shows that the motif composition of different COMMD members varies. This “conserved-variable” balance not only guarantees the core function of the family, but also achieves functional specialization through the evolution of additional motifs.

The COMMD genes in P. clarkii exhibit a distinct tissue-specific expression pattern, revealing a “dual-core” functional specialization. COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD8, and COMMD10 were highly expressed in hemocytes and gill tissues—key immune and barrier organs—suggesting their involvement in innate immune defense and environmental sensing [43, 44]. In contrast, COMMD4 and COMMD7 showed predominant expression in the hepatopancreas, a metabolic and detoxification hub in crustaceans [45, 46]. This spatial division implies that COMMD genes have diverged not only structurally but also functionally to serve organ-specific physiological roles. Hemocyte- and gill-enriched genes may participate in pathogen recognition, immune signaling, and stress response, while hepatopancreas-predominant members are likely involved in maintaining homeostasis through metal ion regulation, metabolic detoxification, or inflammatory modulation [43–46]. This compartmentalized expression pattern aligns with findings in other aquatic species. For example, bivalve COMMD genes enrich in hepatopancreas/gonads [10], teleosts express them in immune-metabolic organs (liver/intestine/gill) under stress [11, 12], and abalone H. discus hannai COMMD genes respond to heat shock/pathogens in gills/hepatopancreas [9]. In P. clarkii, this dual expression signature likely supports a coordinated balance between immune vigilance and metabolic adaptation [43].

Following NLHS and V. parahaemolyticus infection, COMMD genes displayed context-dependent expression dynamics reflective of their roles in integrating stress and immune signals. Integrated analysis of RNA-Seq and qPCR data revealed a marked upregulation of COMMD8 in the HTC12h and HTV24h groups. This pattern suggests that COMMD8 may help initiate a protein folding repair system via formation of a chaperone complex with HSP70 [47, 48]. COMMD2, COMMD3, COMMD4, and COMMD5 also exhibited significant differential expression across stress conditions, with COMMD7 upregulated in HTV-3 h and HTC-12 h groups, potentially linking COMMD family members to NF-κB-mediated crosstalk between stress adaptation and immune activation [49]. These dynamic expression patterns suggest a hierarchical response in P. clarkii hemocytes where early (3 h) activation of stress-sensing genes is followed by (12–48 h) modulation of immune effectors to maintain homeostasis.

PPI network analysis using STRING and Cytoscape pinpointed COMMD8 as a central hub interacting with TLR3, TLR5, MyD88, NF-κB, and Hsp70, supporting its role in pattern recognition receptor (PRR)-mediated immune signaling [11]. dsRNA-mediated knockdown of COMMD8 unveiled a temporal hierarchy in the regulation of the TLR/NF-κB pathway. Specifically, an initial suppression of TLR1, MyD88, and Hsp70 at 6–12 h post-knockdown was followed by their compensatory upregulation at 24 h, whereas NF-κB activation was delayed. This temporal hierarchy demonstrates that COMMD8 coordinates the sequential activation of immune genes, ensuring a balanced transition from pathogen recognition to effector response and highlighting its role as a regulator of TLR/NFκB signaling pathway [35]. Notably, TLR4 and TLR6 showed no expression changes following COMMD8 knockdown, indicating pathway-specific regulatory modules. This finding underscores the specificity and complexity of the COMMD8-regulated immune network and suggests that different TLRs in crustaceans are subject to differential regulatory mechanisms. This intricate regulation is crucial for the precise modulation of innate immune responses [50]. Differential expression of other COMMD members, such as COMMD2 upregulation at 24 h and COMMD3 downregulation at 6–12 h, suggests that the regulatory modes of these family members are intricate, involving compensatory mechanisms that maintain pathway integrity under stress. Furthermore, the absence of significant expression changes in certain other COMMD genes under specific experimental conditions implies the potential engagement of additional compensatory pathways. Thus, these complex regulatory dynamics within the COMMD family ensure that disruptions in one member can be offset by others. This functional redundancy serves to preserve the overall functionality of critical immune and stress response pathways when confronting environmental challenges.

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of the COMMD gene family in P. clarkii. Our results reveal that the P. clarkii COMMD genes constitute a finely coordinated regulatory system, integrating spatial (chromosomal dispersion), temporal (stress-responsive expression), and functional (tissue-specific roles) dimensions. Notably, these genes exhibit hierarchical expression patterns during combined stress and infection challenges, in which COMMD8 acts as a kinetic regulator fine-tuning the TLR/NF-κB signaling pathway. Differential expression of other family members indicates compensatory mechanisms that preserve pathway integrity. Collectively, our findings establish a model in which the COMMD gene family integrates environmental cues with innate immune responses through precisely orchestrated temporal kinetics. This work significantly advances our understanding of stress-immune crosstalk in crustaceans and provides a valuable conceptual framework for future research in decapod immunology.
