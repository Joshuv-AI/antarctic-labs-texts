Linking COVID‐19 Stressors to Early Communication Skills in Pandemic‐Born Children
Authors: Catherine Julien, Célia Matte‐Gagné, Caroline Fitzpatrick, Annie Lemieux, Gabrielle Garon‐Carrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEvidence of disturbances in communication skills among children born during the pandemic remains limited to outcomes within the first year of life and rarely accounts for key predictors. This study investigated the longitudinal association between COVID‐19 stressors and communication skills during the first 1000 days of life, and examined whether parental depressive symptoms, behaviors, and cognitions mediated this association. 2664 French‐speaking children (48.2% boys) born in Québec (Canada) between October 2020 and September 2021, were included in this observational prospective cohort study. Child communication skills were measured at 29 months using the Ages & Stages Questionnaires. COVID stressors (financial insecurity, social isolation, overall stress) were reported by both mothers and fathers at 5 months postpartum. Depressive symptoms were measured at 5 months postpartum using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale Revised (CESD‐10). Parenting behaviors and cognitions (self‐efficacy, perceived parental impact on the child, hostile‐reactive behaviors, overprotection) were reported at 17 months postpartum using the Parental Cognitions and Conduct Toward the Infant Scale. COVID stressors were associated with increased parental depressive symptoms. Depressive symptoms significantly predicted maternal and paternal lack of perceived impact on child development and paternal overprotection, which, in turn, was negatively associated with child communication skills. Findings indicate significant indirect associations between COVID‐19 stressors and child communication skills. This study suggests potential clinical implications by highlighting modifiable parental cognitions and behaviors as possible targets for intervention.

Introduction

Early experiences and environments have been consistently linked to the developmental trajectory of children's communication skills (Di Sante and Potvin 2022; Sylvestre et al. 2026). This association is particularly pronounced during the first 1000 days of life, a critical period of heightened brain plasticity and rapid maturation of the neural systems underlying language acquisition (Indrio et al. 2023; Lake and Chan 2015). The widespread disruption of these foundational early experiences during the COVID‐19 pandemic raised concerns about potential consequences for early child development. Accumulating evidence suggests that pandemic exposure is associated with poorer developmental outcomes among children younger than 3 years (Deoni et al. 2022), with language and communication skills being among the developmental domains most consistently affected.

A meta‐analysis of over 21,000 children found that infants aged 3–12 months who were exposed to the COVID‐19 pandemic (N = 11,438) were more likely to have communication difficulties than their pre‐pandemic counterparts (N = 9981), while no significant differences emerged in other developmental domains (e.g., gross motor, personal‐social; Hessami et al. 2022). These findings were based on studies (k = 8) using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ), third edition. Another study conducted in Japan (N = 447) sought to quantify this impairment. It indicated that children exposed to the pandemic showed delays of approximately 2–5.6 months in receptive and expressive language skills by age 5, using the Kinder Infant Development Scale (parents questionnaire) when compared with their non‐exposed counterparts (Sato et al. 2023). Some researchers showed that these delays in language acquisition were observable as early as 12–30 months in pandemic‐born children. Findings indicate that these children consistently underperform on language‐related tasks, including communicative gestures (e.g., waving, pointing) and early word production, compared with the pre‐pandemic sample (Byrne et al. 2023; Pejovic et al. 2024). Another study conducted in Greece revealed that older children, aged between 2 and 4 years old, who were assessed post‐pandemic were more likely to exhibit atypical speech and language skills (e.g., word and pseudoword repetitions, lexical diversity, syntactic complexity) than children evaluated before or during the pandemic. These findings supported a time‐sensitive association between COVID‐19 exposure and children's communication skills (Kyvrakidou et al. 2025). Altogether, previous studies highlighted that communication skills in children born during the pandemic were impaired in comparison to pre‐pandemic cohorts, especially in the first year of life.

Factors Accounting for the Associations Between COVID‐Related Stressors and Children's Communication Skills: Theoretical and Empirical Background

Prime et al. (2020) proposed a theoretical framework describing how stress induced by the unprecedented socio‐sanitary context of the COVID‐19 pandemic may be associated with child development. A central assumption of the model is that associations between distal stressors and child outcomes are primarily indirect, operating through proximal family processes, particularly parental well‐being and parenting practices. Within this framework, parenting is conceptualized as a key pathway through which parental distress is expressed in child outcomes, with disruptions in parenting representing a primary route of risk transmission. Specifically, the model posits that pandemic‐related stressors (e.g., financial insecurity, social isolation, overall stress) are associated with child developmental outcomes through cascading associations involving parents' mental health and, subsequently, parenting behaviors and cognitions.

This framework is consistent with broader evidence suggesting that distal environmental and socio‐health contexts are associated with parents' mental health (e.g., depressive symptoms and perceived stress), which, in turn, alter their ability to engage with their child and to adopt parenting behaviors that support language and communication development (Pauzé et al. 2017; Sylvestre et al. 2026).

Building on this proposed indirect pathway, maternal depressive symptoms measured during the pregnancy (Rahbari et al. 2024) and up to 11 months after childbirth (Bird et al. 2024; Glascoe and Leew 2010) have been longitudinally associated with lower language skills in children aged under 5. Mothers experiencing heightened levels of postpartum depression are more likely to report less satisfying parent‐child bonding, insecurity toward their child, lower perception of their competence, and more punitive behaviors (Slomian et al. 2019). Inadequate parenting behaviors, including overprotective or hostile parenting, may reduce the opportunities for joint attention and following the child's interests, thereby limiting key interactional contexts related to language learning and communication development (Smith et al. 2018; Sylvestre et al. 2021).

Beyond these behaviors, parental cognitions also constitute an important component of this indirect pathway. Higher parental self‐efficacy and stronger beliefs in one's influence on child development have been associated with better language outcomes in early childhood (e.g., language skills, school readiness) (Albanese et al. 2019; Glascoe and Leew 2010; Jones and Prinz 2005). Parents who perceive themselves as effective and influential are more likely to engage in responsive and stimulating interactions, thereby creating enriched language‐learning environments that support children's communication skills (Glascoe and Leew 2010; Jones and Prinz 2005). Taken together, these associations may help explain the cascading links between maternal depression, parenting behaviors and cognitions, and children's communication skills.

Emerging evidence suggests that these parent‐related processes, namely mental health, behaviors, and cognitions, may be particularly sensitive to large‐scale contextual disruptions affecting family life. In this regard, the COVID‐19 pandemic has profoundly disrupted the daily experiences and routines of families with young children (Courteau et al. 2024; Gagnon et al. 2025; Sanders et al. 2024; Vanderhout et al. 2020). During the first year of the pandemic (from April 2020 to January 2021), parents of 1–34 month‐old children reported elevated levels of depression and anxiety symptoms (Aviles et al. 2024; Jeličić et al. 2021), declines in positive parenting (Griffith et al. 2022), and increases in coercive and inconsistent parenting practices (Griffith et al. 2022; Penna et al. 2023; Shields et al. 2023).

However, evidence on these underlying mechanisms linking exposure to the pandemic and children's communication skills remains mixed. For instance, a study conducted in Serbia among 142 mother‐child dyads reported that better speech‐language skills at 12 months were associated with a combination of lower maternal prenatal anxiety, family composition (i.e., single‐child families) and lower COVID‐19‐related fear of infection (Jeličić et al. 2021). In contrast, a study conducted in the United States (Rhode Island) found that maternal perceived stress was not significantly associated with child outcomes at 3 years of age, including language skills (Deoni et al. 2022). The authors noted, however, the absence of a specific measure of pandemic‐related stress, as well as a potential selection bias in participating families.

Furthermore, most empirical studies linking COVID‐19 to child communication skills have focused on outcomes assessed during the first year of life (Byrne et al. 2023; Hessami et al. 2022; Jeličić et al. 2021; Pejovic et al. 2024), despite evidence of rapid developmental growth beyond this stage (Sylvestre et al. 2020). In addition, key parental pathways, such as the potential contribution of fathers/partners and specific caregiving behaviors and cognitions, have rarely been examined (Byrne et al. 2023; Hessami et al. 2022; Jeličić et al. 2021; Pejovic et al. 2024; Sato et al. 2023).

Addressing these gaps, the present study examined longitudinal associations between specific COVID‐19–related stressors (financial insecurity, social isolation, overall stress) and children's communication skills during the first 1000 days of life in a population‐based birth cohort of children born during the pandemic. Prior research has been limited by a narrow developmental window focused largely on infancy, a lack of inclusion of fathers/partners in family systems analyses, and insufficient attention to the parental processes through which pandemic‐related stress may be associated with child outcomes. Guided by Prime et al. (2020) theoretical framework, we empirically tested indirect pathways involving maternal and paternal depressive symptoms, behaviors (overprotection, hostile‐reactive parenting), and cognitions (self‐efficacy, perceived impact on child development) as potential explanatory processes in this association.

Methods

Setting and Participants

This study relied on data from the ongoing Québec Longitudinal Study of Child Development, second edition (QLSCD‐2) led by the Institut de la statistique du Québec. The QLSDC‐2 is a representative birth cohort of infants born of mothers residing in the province of Québec, Canada, between October 1, 2020 and September 30, 2021 (Statistics Québec 2025). Ethical approval for this protocol was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Health Québec Department of the Institut de la statistique du Québec. The first and second waves of data collection, at 5 months (2021/22) and 17 months (2022/23) postpartum, occurred during the pandemic, and the third wave was collected post‐pandemic at 29 months postpartum (2023/24). Data were collected through online questionnaires and during home visits by a trained interviewer, where parents gave informed consent for their own participation as well as their child's. Participants were compensated in the amount of CAD $50 for their time devoted to the entire research protocol at each data collection. This study was prepared using the criteria from the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines (von Elm et al. 2007).

A sample of 4703 children with a mean age of 5.68 (SD = 0.60) months participated in the baseline phase of data collection (T1) of the main study. Of those, 4056 had complete data at T1, and 3879 were followed longitudinally at 17.60 (SD = 0.62) months postpartum (T2), and 3589 at 29.63 (SD = 0.61) months (T3). Sample attrition rate from T1 to T3 was 23.7%. Compared with participating families at baseline, those who did not complete the questionnaires at T3 were more likely to be from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds, be younger than 25 years old at the time of their child's birth, have moved between T1 and T3, and have immigrated to Canada (Statistics Québec 2025).

Measures

Main Outcome

Communication skills were measured using the 30‐month version of the communication subscale (6 items, α = 0.70) from the Ages & Stages Questionnaires, third edition (ASQ‐3; Squires and Bricker 2009). The parent most knowledgeable about the child responds if the skill or behavior is present yes = 10, sometimes = 5, or not yet = 0 (scores range from 0–60). A score below 43.56 indicates the need for developmental surveillance or further evaluation by a speech–language pathologist. The percentage of children below the established cutoffs is reported in Table 1 for descriptive purposes. The continuous score of the ASQ communication subscale was used in the statistical models. Analysis of the psychometric properties of the ASQ‐3 revealed good internal consistency, test‐retest reliability, and interobserver reliability (Squires et al. 1997).

Exposures

Our primary exposures included three COVID‐19‐related indicators: financial insecurity, social isolation since childbirth, and perceived overall stress. Financial insecurity was assessed using two items: a change in income due to the COVID‐19 pandemic (1 = has decreased significantly to 7 = has increased significantly) and the level of concern about this income change (1 = not at all to 5 = very much). Parents were classified as having experienced financial insecurity if they reported a decrease in income and expressed concern about this decrease. Those reporting no change or an increase in income, or no concern about a change, were classified as not having experienced financial insecurity (scores range from 1 = no financial stress/not at all concerned to 5 = very much concerned). Second, both parents reported whether they experienced social isolation or loneliness since childbirth as a result of the pandemic (scores range from 1 = not at all to 5 = very much). Third, the overall perceived stress level due to the pandemic was assessed on a 7‐point scale by both parents (scores range from 1 = has decreased significantly to 7 = has increased significantly). These three variables were dichotomized for descriptive purposes. Continuous scores were entered into the analysis to create a latent factor representing the exposure variable of COVID‐related stressors.

Other exposures, referred to as mediator variables, included parental symptoms of depression, behaviors and cognitions. Symptoms of depression in the last week (e.g., I felt that everything I did was an effort) were assessed at the 5‐month interview using the 10‐item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale Revised (CES‐D10; Cartierre et al. 2011) with mothers (α = 0.69) and fathers (α = 0.63). Item responses ranged from 0 = rarely or none of the time, to 3 = all the time, and the total score was computed. A score > 10 on this scale suggests the presence of clinical depressive symptoms that warrant further evaluation.

Four parenting behaviors and cognitions were measured for both parents at 17 months postpartum using the Parental Cognitions and Conduct Toward the Infant Scale (Boivin et al. 2005): parental self‐efficacy (e.g., I feel that I am very good at keeping my child amused, 5 items, α = 0.73–0.74), lack of perceived parental impact on child development (e.g., Regardless of what I do as a parent, my child will develop on his/her own, 5 items, α = 0.71–0.75; a low score means the parent believes her/his actions have a high impact), hostile‐reactive behaviors (e.g., I have raised voice/shouted at my child when he/she was particularly fussy, 5 items, α = 0.68–0.70), and overprotection (e.g., I can never bring myself to leave my child with a baby‐sitter, 4 items, α = 0.69–0.73). These scales were rated on a continuous 11‐point scale (0 = not at all to 10 = exactly) to indicate the extent to which each statement reflected their actions, thoughts, or feelings. A previous study based on a large twin and singleton sample of 5‐month‐old infants demonstrated good factorial validity, along with good internal consistency across subscales (Boivin et al. 2005).

Covariates

The child's sex assigned at birth was drawn from the birth registry. Data on birth weight, parents' age, country of birth, and educational attainment were obtained at the 5‐month interview. These variables were dichotomized for the analysis (e.g., high school diploma or less, born outside of Québec/Canada). Mothers also reported the number of children living in the household and the annual family income. Families were living under the poverty threshold if the household income before tax was lower than half of the province's median income, once adjusted for family size and area of residence (ZIP code). Four categories were computed: 1 = living under the poverty threshold (N = 292, 11.0%), 2 = low to middle income (N = 1121, 42.1%), 3 = middle to high income (N = 822, 30.9%), and 4 = high income (N = 429, 16.1%).

Data Analysis Strategy

Data were analyzed between April, 29 and October, 16, 2025. Our analysis strategy was guided by Prime et al.’s theoretical model, which suggests indirect associations between pandemic‐related stressors and child development outcomes. We empirically tested how financial stress, social isolation, and overall stress induced by the pandemic were associated with elevated parental depressive symptoms, which were associated with parental behaviors (overprotection and hostility‐reactivity) and cognitions (self‐efficacy, impact on child development), and ultimately with scores on the communication subscale (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S1 for the theoretically investigated model).

Longitudinal serial indirect models were conducted separately for mothers and fathers using structural equation modeling (SEM) with Mplus 8.11 (Muthén and Muthén 2022). The models also control for the associations between the child communication skills and established risk factors of early communication development, including child's sex assigned at birth, low birth weight, maternal age, country of birth, educational attainment, and family income (Reilly and McKean 2023). Associations between covariates and mediator variables were initially specified and subsequently removed only if non‐significant (p < 0.25) using a backward elimination method (Chowdhury and Turin 2020; Hosmer et al. 2013). Missing data were handled using the Full information maximum likelihood technique (FIML; Enders and Bandalos 2001).

Results

Descriptive statistics are presented in Table 1, and correlations are reported in Supporting Information S1: Tables S2 and S3. Children had a mean score of 53.53 (SD = 10.53) in communication skills, and 11.8% of them had a score below the cutoff point. More than half of mothers and fathers, respectively, reported having experienced social isolation (72.7% and 56.0%) and heightened stress (62.6% and 48.3%), while approximately one quarter encountered financial insecurity (23.9% and 22.8%). In our sample, 21.3% of mothers and 14.1% of fathers had significant depressive symptoms (score > 10). Mothers and fathers respectively reported means scores of 8.10 (SD = 1.10) and 7.72 (SD = 1.27) in self‐efficacy, 1.91 (SD = 1.58) and 1.92 (SD = 1.64) in lack of perceived impact, 3.65 (SD = 2.17) and 3.32 (SD = 1.95) in overprotection, and 1.71 (SD = 1.33) and 1.63 (SD = 1.33) in hostile‐reactive behaviors.

No direct association between the COVID factor (social isolation, financial insecurity, overall stress) and child communication skills was identified. COVID factor was indirectly associated with the outcome, accounting for 10.4% (mothers) and 11.3% (fathers) of the variance in child communication skills at 29 months, through higher parental depressive symptoms, maternal and paternal lack of perceived impact on the child, and higher paternal overprotection at 17 months.

Higher scores on the maternal and paternal depressive symptoms scale at 5 months postpartum were associated with lower parenting self‐efficacy, and higher lack of perceived impact on the child, overprotection and hostility‐reactivity at 17 months (β ranging from −0.26 to 0.22 for mothers; and −0.29 to 0.23 for fathers). In turn, a higher lack of perceived impact on the child in mothers (B = −0.577, SE = 0.174, p < 0.001, β = −0.087) and fathers (B = −0.735, SE = 0.157, p < 0.001, β = −0.114) was significantly associated with lower child communication scores. Parental self‐efficacy and hostility‐reactivity were not associated with child communication skills.

To conceptualize the magnitude of the significant associations, we translated the expected changes in the observed measures associated with a 1‐SD increase in parental depressive symptoms. In the maternal model, a 1‐SD (4.93 points) increase in the depression scale is associated with a 0.12‐SD (0.19 point) increase in lack of perceived impact, which, in turn, is associated with a 0.01‐SD (0.11 point) decrease in communication score. In the paternal model, a 1‐SD (4.03 points) increase in the depression scale is associated with a 0.10‐SD (0.19 points) increase in lack of perceived impact, which, in turn, is associated with a 0.01‐SD (0.12 point) decrease in communication score. Additionally, a 1‐SD (4.03 points) increase in the paternal depression scale is associated with a 0.05‐SD (0.10 points) increase in overprotection, which is in turn associated with a 0.003‐SD (0.03 point) decrease in communication score.

Sensitivity Analysis

Given that parenting experiences and behaviors may differ depending on whether the child is a firstborn or has older siblings (Volling 2012), we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multi‐group invariance analyses to examine whether the observed associations were invariant across families in which the study child was a firstborn and those in which the child had older siblings. Separate analyses were conducted for the maternal and paternal models by constraining the main structural paths to be equal across groups (see the constrained paths in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Model fit indices are reported in Supporting Information S1: Table S4, in the supplementary materials. Although the χ 2 difference test was statistically significant in the maternal model (p = 0.04), all other invariance criteria supported model invariance. To further examine this finding, we estimated the unconstrained maternal model separately for firstborn children and children with older siblings. The magnitude and direction of the structural paths were highly comparable across groups. Overall, these analyses suggest that the pattern of associations was consistent regardless of whether the participating child was a firstborn or had older siblings, indicating that birth order did not meaningfully moderate the observed associations.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that children's communication skills in the first 1000 days of life are linked to pandemic‐related stressors through the cascading associations of parental depressive symptoms, behaviors, and cognitions. More specifically, parents of infants born during the pandemic who experienced higher COVID‐19‐related stress reported higher depressive symptoms at 5 months postpartum. This increase in depressive symptoms was associated with a lower perceived impact on child development in mothers and fathers, as well as more paternal overprotection at 17 months, which in turn was associated with reduced child communication skills at 29 months. Taken together, the observed associations are relatively small in magnitude, with changes in parental depressive symptoms in the context of the pandemic corresponding to limited changes in parental perceived impact on child development and paternal overprotection, as well as in communication scores.

In our study, the increase in depressive symptoms likely contributed to a lower perceived impact on one's child development, a form of learned helplessness, triggered by stringent social distancing measures that limited opportunities for social support and interactions. Financial insecurity, loss of social interactions, closures in health and early childhood education services, and confinement‐related stress were previously linked to increased parental concerns about their child's development, higher symptoms of depression (Aviles et al. 2024; Prime et al. 2020), and poorer parenting behaviors and cognitions (Prime et al. 2020; Sanders et al. 2024; Shields et al. 2023). Such a pattern of learned helplessness is also typical of depressed mothers, and this feeling can be worsened after childbirth (Saini et al. 2024). Parents who feel they have little influence on their child's development may engage less frequently in stimulating activities, such as shared book‐reading, believing that their actions have minimal effect (Glascoe and Leew 2010). This, in turn, can reduce the richness of the child's early language‐learning environment and potentially hinder language development.

Fortunately, our findings suggest that children's language skills were not substantially impaired despite the heightened levels of pandemic‐related stress, parental depressive symptoms, and poorer parenting practices previously documented (Aviles et al. 2024; Prime et al. 2020; Sanders et al. 2024; Shields et al. 2023). Nevertheless, previous research has indicated that young children's communication skills were negatively affected during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Indeed, numerous studies reported a pandemic‐related decline in social communication and early language skills among infants aged under 12 months (Byrne et al. 2023; Hessami et al. 2022; Pejovic et al. 2024), with additional research indicating that these impairments intensified as children aged (Sato et al. 2023). Research on the longitudinal effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic on early communication development is still emerging. Longer‐term longitudinal follow‐up is needed to determine whether our cohort follows a similar trajectory.

Furthermore, our study may not have captured other explanatory mechanisms that could account for the observed associations. The consequences of the pandemic were not evenly distributed across families. Children in households with multiple risk factors, such as economic hardship, elevated parental stress, or limited access to resources, were more likely to face language difficulties and a decreased quality of their home‐learning environment (Reilly et al. 2007; Zubrick et al. 2007). A study of 539 Canadian children aged 11–34 months found that children from low‐income families were read to less frequently during the pandemic than their more advantaged peers (Fung et al. 2023). Future studies could further investigate language‐specific parenting practices and their associations with communication skills in infants born during the pandemic.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has important strengths. It was the first to empirically test the theoretical model proposed by Prime et al. among mothers and fathers in a population‐based birth cohort. Our findings provide important empirical support for this theoretical framework and represent a meaningful contribution to the literature. By focusing on the first 1000 days of life, a critical period for early communication development, this study also offers a timely and developmentally sensitive examination of contextual and parental‐level influences on children's early communication outcomes. Methodologically, the temporal sequencing of our variables, together with the use of well‐validated measures of parental depressive symptoms, cognitions, and behaviors, enhances the rigor of the analyses and allows for a clearer examination of the directionality of the observed associations. In addition, convergent findings based on independent reports from both parents represent a key strength of the current study and further support the longitudinal associations between pandemic‐related stressors and children's communication skills. Collectively, these design features provide insight into how COVID‐19 related stressors, parental mental health, behaviors, and cognitions are prospectively associated with children's language development.

Despite its many strengths, our study holds some limitations. First, our observational data only included French‐speaking children, potentially limiting the generalization of our findings to other linguistic or cultural populations. Second, although our cross‐lagged mediation design established temporal ordering consistent with Prime et al.’s theoretical model, our study precludes drawing causal inferences regarding the observed associations. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of longitudinal associations consistent with the hypothesized pathway rather than evidence of causality. Experimental or intervention studies would be needed to more rigorously investigate the proposed mechanisms. Third, some variables that are very closely tied to communication development were overlooked, such as parent‐child interaction characteristics, or home‐literacy environment (Kyvrakidou et al. 2025). Future studies could deepen our understanding of those underlying mechanisms by including proximal parenting behaviors (e.g., responsiveness), parenting practices (e.g., screens and media use), and home environment (e.g., home‐literacy environment) into mediation models. Fourth, the ASQ‐3 communication subscale relies on parent report and observation, which may be subject to reporting bias in the context of parental stress and depressive symptoms. Therefore, these findings warrant replication using direct assessments of children's communication skills. Fifth, given the 12‐month interval between the assessment of depressive symptoms (5 months postpartum) and parenting cognitions and behaviors (17 months postpartum), we cannot rule out the possibility that other processes occurring during this period may account for the observed associations. The temporal spacing between assessments may also partly explain the small magnitude of the associations observed. Future studies should therefore replicate these findings using shorter intervals between assessments of perinatal mental health and parenting cognitions and behaviors.

Conclusion

The COVID‐19 pandemic has been conceptualized as a context that may have amplified pre‐existing family‐level risk processes, providing a population‐level opportunity to examine how contextual stressors are associated with children's early communication development through parental depressive symptoms, behaviors, and cognitions (Prime et al. 2020; van de Weijer et al. 2022). Findings from this population‐based cohort of infants born during the pandemic suggest a modest longitudinal indirect association between the COVID‐19 stressors and child communication skills in the first 1000 days of life, operating through higher parental depressive symptoms and associated changes in parenting behaviors and cognitions. In this context, our study suggests possible clinical implications for early intervention and family‐oriented services for both mothers and fathers experiencing elevated stress or depressive symptoms. We identify modifiable parental cognitions and behaviors as factors of potential clinical relevance in relation to child communication skills. In particular, maternal and paternal perceived impact on child development emerges as a relevant parenting cognition that may be considered in efforts to support early communication development.
