Association of parental oral health literacy, early childhood oral health behaviors, and caries severity with oral health-related quality of life among preschool children
Authors: Bo Zhang, Fan Zhou, Zihan Liu, Aixiu Gong, Mingrui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between parental oral health literacy (OHL), children’s oral health behaviors and status, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in preschool children, and to identify factors associated with OHRQoL.MethodsA convenience sampling method was used to survey 221 pairs of preschool children and their parents who visited the pediatric dentistry department from September 2023 to May 2024. Data were collected through questionnaires, clinical examinations, and validated scales—including the Chinese version of the short-form Health Literacy Dental Scale (HeLD-14) for OHL and the Early Childhood Oral Health Impact Scale (ECOHIS) for OHRQoL—to analyze relationships between sociodemographic characteristics, parental OHL, children’s oral health behaviors, oral health status, and dental caries severity with OHRQoL. Bivariate analysis, multiple stepwise linear regression, and hierarchical regression were performed to identify factors associated with preschool children’s OHRQoL.ResultsData from 206 children were analyzed. Parental OHL, children’s oral health behaviors, and oral health status were all significantly associated with OHRQoL in preschool children. Multiple stepwise regression analysis identified several independent factors associated with OHRQoL (P < 0.05), including parental OHL (β = -0.206, P = 0.001), age of starting toothbrushing (β = 0.164, P = 0.008), frequency of sweet food intake (β = 0.152, P = 0.013), caries severity (β = 0.199, P = 0.002), and history of toothache (β = 0.276, P < 0.001). Collectively, these factors accounted for 26.2% of the total variance in OHRQoL.ConclusionsOHRQoL in preschool children is associated with comprehensive factors, including family factors (parental OHL), oral health behaviors (age of starting toothbrushing, frequency of sweet food intake), and oral health status (caries severity, history of toothache). These findings suggest that strengthening parental OHL, implementing early oral hygiene interventions and limiting sugar consumption may be key strategies for improving OHRQoL among preschool children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07159-9.Keywords: Dental caries, Preschool children, Oral health-related quality of life, Oral health literacy

Introduction

Children’s oral health is closely related to their overall health and quality of life [1]. Poor oral health not only serves as a significant risk factor for systemic diseases but also impairs children’s psychosocial well-being, including self-esteem and social interactions, and thereby exerts a profound impact on their mental health [2, 3]. Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL), a multidimensional construct that includes comfort, self-esteem, and satisfaction with oral health, is a fundamental aspect of overall well-being [4].

A range of assessment tools has been developed to evaluate OHRQoL in children across various age groups [5–9]. For preschool children, the Early Childhood Oral Health Impact Scale (ECOHIS) is widely used to evaluate OHRQoL among children aged 2 to 6 years [10–12]. For children aged 5 to 6 years, the Scale of Oral Health Outcomes for 5-year-old children (SOHO-5) [13] is commonly applied to assess their OHRQoL [6, 14]. Given the limited expressive capacity of preschool children, researchers typically depend on parent-proxy reports to objectively assess this critical metric [15]. In this context, and based on its ability to evaluate impacts on both the child and the family unit, the ECOHIS has emerged as the gold standard for evaluating OHRQoL in this population [16–18].

Emerging evidence indicates that OHRQoL in preschool children is influenced by a complex interplay of factors at multiple levels. These encompass individual behaviors (e.g., toothbrushing habits), family environment factors (e.g., parental socioeconomic status, dental anxiety, parental self-assessments of children’s oral health, and Oral Health Literacy (OHL)), and societal determinants [19–26]. Among family environment factors, parental OHL warrants thorough investigation, which is defined as the capacity to obtain, interpret, and utilize oral health information to make informed decisions [27]. As a core competency for parental engagement in pediatric oral health management, OHL levels are associated not only with parental overall health, quality of life, and well-being [28] but also with children’s oral health as a critical social factor [29].

While OHRQoL has been recognized as a clinically important core outcome measure [4], several aspects remain underexplored or inconsistently reported in the literature. First, studies have mainly used univariate analyses [14, 30, 31] or focused on isolated relationships [22, 23, 32], thus failing to systematically incorporate family environmental factors, child behaviors, and clinical status into a comprehensive model, and to analyze how core family factors and children’s oral health behaviors differently affect each dimension of the ECOHIS. Second, the association between parental OHL and children’s OHRQoL remains contentious: while some studies have confirmed a correlation between the two [33, 34], other studies have shown a weak correlation or one that did not reach statistical significance [35–38]. These contradictory findings may stem from methodological variations, particularly the use of different OHL assessment tools with divergent sensitivity, but they also more profoundly reflect the absence of a unifying theoretical framework to conceptualize how these multi-level factors interact.

To address these limitations and provide a conceptual roadmap for analysis, this study is grounded in an integrated theoretical framework that combines the Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQL) model [39, 40] with an ecological perspective [41]. This framework posits that a child’s OHRQoL is the outcome of a hierarchical cascade of factors. This framework conceptualizes OHRQoL as the outcome of a hierarchical cascade in which distal sociodemographic and parental OHL factors shape child-level health behaviors, thereby influencing clinical oral status and ultimately diminishing OHRQoL.

Guided by this framework, this study aims to elucidate the associations between parental OHL, children’s oral health behaviors, oral health status, and preschoolers’ OHRQoL. Specifically, we will examine the relationships between these factors and both overall OHRQoL and its specific dimensions, as measured by the ECOHIS. Using multivariate regression models, we will analyze the independent and hierarchical contributions of these factors to each outcome level. This comprehensive approach is designed to resolve previous inconsistencies and develop a detailed analytical model for creating targeted, multi-level interventions.

Materials and methods

Research design and objects

This cross-sectional study involves oral examinations of children and the administration of oral health questionnaires to parents. From September 2023 to May 2024, we recruited preschool children who were undergoing dental visits at the Department of Stomatology, Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, along with their parents, using convenience sampling. The Department of Stomatology is a key department of this public, non-profit children’s hospital, which is part of the public medical system. It provides comprehensive oral health services, including prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, for children aged 0–18, regardless of their household registration or residency status. All services adhere to local pricing standards, with no free services provided. The inclusion criteria for our study encompass children aged 2 to 6 years, with the requirement that both the children and their parents/caregivers provide written informed consent and voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria included (1) children with physical/mental illnesses or severe systemic diseases; (2) children refusing or failing to cooperate with oral examinations; (3) parents/caregivers unable to comprehend the study scales. According to the sample size estimation method for multiple linear regression analysis, the required sample size should be at least ten times the number of independent variables [42, 43]. Given that this study incorporated 18 independent variables and accounted for a 10% to 20% non-response rate, the estimated minimum sample size ranged from 198 to 216 participants. Ultimately, 221 participants were recruited for this study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Ethics No. 202309019-1). Participation in this study was completely voluntary. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 16 before proceeding.

Clinical examination

The decayed, missing, and filled teeth (dmft) index for primary teeth, as indicated by the World Health Organization (WHO) [44] for epidemiological studies, was used to assess caries experience. All clinical examinations were performed by two calibrated pediatric dentists with over five years of clinical experience from the Department of Stomatology. Prior to the study, all examiners underwent a standardized training program based on WHO criteria [44] to unify the diagnostic criteria for caries detection and the dmft index scoring. The training included theoretical sessions and practical calibrations on clinical photographs. To ensure diagnostic consistency, inter- and intra-examiner reliability were assessed. Twenty children were independently examined by both dentists, and the inter-examiner reliability yielded a Kappa value of 0.861. Intra-examiner reliability, assessed by re-examinations of the same 20 children after a one-week interval, yielded Kappa values ranging from 0.859 to 0.864, indicating excellent agreement. Caries experience was categorized into: absent (dmft = 0) and present (dmft >0). We further classified the severity of caries based on the dmft index using validated criteria for preschool children [45–47]: absent, mild (total score 1–3), moderate (total score 4–6), and severe (total score 7 or higher).

Questionnaire survey

Structured questionnaires were used to collect information from parents/caregivers. The questionnaire was divided into three parts. The first section comprises a general information questionnaire consisting of 15 items, covering sociodemographic factors, children’s oral health behaviors, dietary habits, and oral health status among children (Supplementary file S1). The second part was the short-form Health Literacy Dental Scale (HeLD-14), with a total of 14 items [48]. The third part was ECOHIS, containing 13 items [49].

Demographic questionnaire

A self-developed questionnaire with reference to the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey Questionnaire [50] was used to obtain the following information: In terms of sociodemographic aspects, the variables included the child’s gender, age, number of children, relationship to the child, residence, parental education, and family annual income. Gender is divided into “male” and “female”. The age categories are “2 years”, “3 years”, “4 years”, “5 years”, and “6 years”. The number of children is divided into “1 (only child)” and “2 or more (multiple children)”. Regarding the relationship to the child, it is categorized as “father”, “mother”, or “other caregiver”. The family’s place of residence includes “urban” and “non-urban”. According to the highest level of education completed, parental education is divided into “below university” and “university and above”. The household income variable is defined based on the family’s annual total income, which is categorized as “less than CNY 100,000”, “CNY 100,000–200,000”, “more than CNY 200,000”, and “unknown or refused to answer”.

The oral health behaviors, dietary habits, and oral health status of children were evaluated based on parental reports. Oral health behaviors included the age at which the child started brushing their teeth, the frequency of daily brushing, and parental assistance with brushing. For the age at which the child started brushing their teeth, the response options were “≤6 months”, “1 year”, “2 years”, “≥3 years”, and “Unknown”. Prior to questionnaire completion, researchers provided a standardized verbal explanation clarifying that the “age of toothbrushing initiation” encompassed both toothbrushing after tooth eruption and pre-eruption oral cleaning practices (e.g., gum wiping with sterile gauze or finger cot massaging). The questionnaire item was explicitly annotated with “including oral cleaning before tooth eruption” to ensure consistent understanding among respondents. The frequency of daily brushing was assessed based on the question: “How many times does your child brush his or her teeth every day?”. The response options were as follows: “≥2 times”, “1 time”, and “Not daily”. The question “Do you or other parents assist children with toothbrushing?” was used to assess parental assistance in toothbrushing. The answer options were as follows: “Daily”, “Weekly”, “Sometimes”, “Occasionally”, and “Never”.

Dietary habits comprised the frequency of sweet food intake and bedtime sweet intake. Frequency of sweet food intake was assessed using the item: “How often does your child consume sweet foods and candies?” Response options: “≥1 time per day”, “1–6 times per week”, “1–3 times per month”, and “Rarely/Never”. Bedtime sweet intake was evaluated via the questionnaire item: “Does your child consume desserts or sweet beverages before bedtime?” Response options: “Frequently”, “Occasionally”, and “Rarely/never”.

Oral health status included parental evaluation of children’s oral status, parent-reported toothache, and the presence and severity of dental caries. Parental evaluation of children’s oral status was obtained using the item: “How would you rate your child’s oral health?” Response options: “Good”, “Fair”, and “Poor”. Parent-reported toothache was evaluated with the question: “Has your child experienced toothache or discomfort in the past 12 months?” Response options: “No”, “Yes”, and “Not sure”.

The short-form health literacy dental scale (HeLD-14)

HeLD-14, translated and validated by Yan et al. [48], was adopted to evaluate parental OHL. The scale comprises seven dimensions—acceptance, understanding, support, financial burden, medical treatment, communication, and application—with a total of 14 items. It employed a 5-point Likert scale, with “without any difficulty” assigned a score of 1 and “unable to do” scored 5. For data analysis, scores were reverse-coded as follows: 5 → 0, 4 → 1, 3 → 2, 2 → 3, 1 → 4. The total score was calculated, ranging from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating better OHL. Based on established criteria [51], participants’ OHL levels were categorized as follows: Low (0–35 points)​​, Medium (36–46 points)​​, or High (>46 points).

The Chinese version of the early childhood oral health impact scale

ECOHIS [49] was used to assess OHRQoL in children. The instrument consists of a Child Impact Section (CIS, 9 items) and a Family Impact Section (FIS, 4 items), totaling 13 items. The scale covers child symptoms, child function, child psychology, child self-image, parental distress, and family function. Each item has six response options: 0 = never, 1 = hardly ever, 2 = occasionally, 3 = often, 4 = very often, and 5 = don’t know. Questionnaires were deemed invalid and excluded from analysis if participants selected “don’t know” for any item. The total scale score, calculated as the sum of all item scores, ranged from 0 to 52. Higher scores indicated a greater impact of oral health issues on children and their families.

Data collection

This study employed an online questionnaire administered via the Wenjuanxing platform for data acquisition. Researchers first developed the online questionnaire within the platform, followed by multiple rounds of internal pretesting to ensure the accuracy of the questionnaire content. During the formal survey, the generated questionnaire QR code was printed and placed in a prominent location within the outpatient area. Researchers introduced the study to accompanying parents during children’s oral examinations and obtained their informed consent to participate. The first page of the questionnaire clearly outlined the study description, including the informed consent form, research objectives, and detailed guidelines for questionnaire completion, ensuring that participants fully understood the survey content. Respondents completed the questionnaire online by scanning the QR code with their mobile phones. Researchers were present throughout the process to address any questions or concerns raised by parents promptly, thereby ensuring the smoothness of data collection.

All questionnaire items were designed as mandatory, and participants were required to answer all questions before submitting the questionnaire successfully. After the data collection was completed, the researchers used the data export function of the Wenjuanxing platform to automatically import the original response data into an Excel data table, avoiding potential errors caused by manual entry. After the data were exported, two researchers independently conducted a secondary check, focusing on screening and eliminating invalid questionnaires. The screening criteria included: responses showing a patterned feature, completion time less than 3 min, and logical contradictions in the answers, to ensure the reliability of the data ultimately included in the analysis.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariate linear regression. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Internal consistency of the HeLD-14 and ECOHIS was evaluated via Cronbach’s α. Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to identify intergroup differences. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Dunn test with Bonferroni correction. Variables with P < 0.05 in all tests were included in the final multivariate model. Multiple stepwise regression was performed to identify factors significantly associated with ECOHIS scores. To further validate the conceptual model and elucidate the hierarchical relationship between variables as suggested by our theoretical framework, a post hoc hierarchical regression analysis was conducted. In this analysis, variables were entered in the following order: Step 1: sociodemographic factors; Step 2: parental OHL; Step 3: children’s oral health behaviors; Step 4: oral health status (caries severity and history of toothache). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

The total ECOHIS score, CIS total score, and FIS total score served as dependent variables. Independent variables included child’s gender, age, number of children, relationship to the child, residence, mother’s and father’s education, family annual income, age of starting toothbrushing, daily brushing frequency, parental assistance in toothbrushing, frequency of sweet food intake, bedtime sweet intake, history of toothache, parental evaluation of the child’s oral status, parental OHL, caries experience, and caries severity.

Independent variables were coded as follows: Child’s gender: 1 = Male, 2 = Female. Child’s age: 1 = 2 years, 2 = 3 years, 3 = 4 years, 4 = 5 years, 5 = 6 years. Number of children: 1 = only child, 2 = multiple children. Relationship to the child: 1 = Father, 2 = Mother. Residence: 1 = Urban, 2 = Non-urban. Mother’s education: 1 = Below university, 2 = University and above. Father’s education: 1 = Below university, 2 = University and above. Family annual income: 1 = < CNY 100,000, 2 = CNY 100,000–200,000, 3 = > CNY 200,000, 4 = Unknown or refused. Age of starting toothbrushing: 1 = ≤ 6 months, 2 = 1 year, 3 = 2 years, 4 = ≥ 3 years, 5 = unknown. Daily brushing frequency: 1 = ≥ 2 times, 2 = 1 time, 3 = not daily. Parental assistance in toothbrushing: 1 = daily, 2 = weekly, 3 = sometimes, 4 = occasionally, 5 = never. Frequency of sweet food intake: 1 = rarely/never, 2 = 1–3 times/month, 3 = 1–6 times/week, 4 = ≥ 1 time daily. Bedtime sweet intake: 1 = rarely/never, 2 = occasionally, 3 = frequently. History of toothache: binary (1 = no, 2 = yes); unclear responses were treated as missing. Parental evaluation of the child’s oral status: 1 = good, 2 = fair, 3 = poor. Parental OHL (HeLD-14 scores): 1 = low, 2 = medium, 3 = high. Caries experience: 1 = absent, 2 = present. Caries severity: 1 = “dmft = 0”, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, 4 = severe.

Results

A total of 221 pairs of preschool children and their parents were initially enrolled in this study. Fifteen families were excluded because of “don’t know” responses in the ECOHIS, resulting in a final analytical sample of 206 children (92.8% retention rate). The findings revealed that the HeLD-14 had a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.911. For the ECOHIS, the child impact section, family impact section, and overall scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with α coefficients of 0.871, 0.884, and 0.908, respectively, all exceeding the conventional benchmarks for Cronbach’s α reliability evaluation.

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the children and their parents. The gender distribution among children was nearly balanced, with males accounting for 46.1% and females for 53.9%. In terms of age, five-year-olds constituted the largest subgroup (34.5%), while two-year-olds represented the smallest (2.4%). The majority of families (64.6%) had only one child, whereas a minority had two or more (35.4%). Mothers comprised the majority of respondents (79.1%), and most families lived in urban areas (80.6%). Parental education levels were relatively high, with 64.6% of fathers and 60.7% of mothers holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. A small percentage of families (16.5%) had annual incomes below CNY 100,000, while 44.2% had incomes exceeding CNY 200,000.

Regarding oral health behaviors, only a minority of children (15.5%) started toothbrushing within six months of age, whereas daily brushing ≥ 2 times was common (66.1%). Nearly half of parents (45.6%) reported assisting with their children’s toothbrushing daily. Sweet food intake was frequent, with 42.2% of children consuming sweet foods or desserts ≥ 1 time daily and 71.4% consuming sweets or beverages before bedtime. In terms of self-reported oral health, 48.1% of children had experienced toothache, while only 38.8% of parents rated their children’s oral health as positive. Most parents (84%) had high OHL, as indicated by a mean HeLD-14 total score of 51.59 (SD = 6.12). The caries prevalence rate was 78.6%, with mild caries accounting for 40.3% and moderate caries 24.3%. For further details, see Table 1.

Response frequencies for ECOHIS items are presented in Table 2. The total ECOHIS score ranged from 0 to 36, with a mean of 8.14 (SD = 7.77). Specifically, the CIS scores ranged from 0 to 24 (mean = 5.13, SD = 5.02), and the FIS scores ranged from 0 to 16 (mean = 3.01, SD = 3.48). In the child functional dimension, pain symptoms were the most common oral issue among preschoolers (68.0%), with nearly half experiencing difficulty drinking (45.6%) and eating (43.7%). A total of 26.2% of parents reported that children’s oral health problems negatively impacted preschool, daycare, or school education. In the psychological dimension, the prevalence rates of insomnia (35.9%) and irritability or frustration (34.0%) were similar. Additionally, some children avoided smiling or laughing (28.6%) or talking with others (22.8%) due to oral health concerns. Within the family impact section, approximately two-fifths of parents reported feeling upset (40.8%) and guilty (41.7%) about their children’s dental issues. Notably, parental responses indicated that work absences (47.1%) were more markedly associated with familial impact than financial expenses (38.8%).

Table 3 presents the associations between all variables and scores on the ECOHIS. Statistical analyses revealed no significant associations between children’s gender, age, family residence, or parental education level and total ECOHIS or subscale scores. However, the number of children was significantly associated with CIS, as well as the child symptoms and child function. Multiple children showed significantly higher scores in child symptoms (P = 0.016) and child function (P = 0.034). Fathers as respondents exhibited significantly higher scores in family function (P = 0.016) compared to mothers. There were significant differences in the total ECOHIS score (P = 0.024), FIS score (P = 0.017), and family function score (P = 0.012) among different annual family incomes. Significantly different ECOHIS results were observed based on the age of starting toothbrushing, including total scores (P = 0.016), CIS (P = 0.028), child psychology (P = 0.004), FIS (P = 0.023), and family function (P = 0.025). Post-hoc comparisons indicated that children who started toothbrushing before 6 months of age had significantly better outcomes in child psychology (P = 0.022) than those who started after 3 years. Similarly, for FIS, these children had significantly lower FIS scores than those who initiated brushing at 2 years (P = 0.031). Analysis by daily brushing frequency showed significant differences only in the CIS. By contrast, no significant associations were found between parental assistance in toothbrushing and total ECOHIS scores or any of its subscales or dimensions. Weekly sweet food intake of ≥ 1 time was significantly associated with higher ECOHIS total and subscale scores (P < 0.05), except for child psychology and self-image. Bedtime consumption of sweets or beverages was significantly linked to higher ECOHIS total and dimension scores across all subscales (P < 0.05). Both history of toothache in the past 12 months and parental evaluation of the child’s oral status were significantly associated with dimension scores of ECOHIS, except for child self-image (P < 0.05). Significant differences in ECOHIS total scores, FIS scores, and multiple dimensions (child function, child psychology) were observed across different levels of parental OHL (P < 0.05). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that children of parents with low OHL had significantly higher total ECOHIS scores (P = 0.028) and scores in child psychology (P = 0.019), FIS (P = 0.028), and parental distress (P = 0.024).

Analysis based on caries experience revealed significant associations with ECOHIS total scores and all subscales/dimensions except child self-image (P = 0.045 for child self-image; P ≤ 0.001 for others). Analysis by caries severity revealed significant associations with ECOHIS total scores and all subscales/dimensions except child self-image (P = 0.229 for child self-image; P < 0.05 for others). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that across most dimensions (including total ECOHIS scores, child function, and family function), as the severity increased, the difference from the group without caries showed an increasing trend.

As shown in Table 4, multiple stepwise linear regression analysis revealed that later toothbrushing initiation (β = 0.164, P = 0.008), frequent sweet food intake (β = 0.152, P = 0.013), history of toothache (β = 0.276, P < 0.001), low parental OHL (β=−0.206, P = 0.001), and higher caries severity (β = 0.199, P = 0.002) were factors significantly associated with poor OHRQoL in preschool children. The model accounted for 26.2% of the total variance, with VIF values of ≤ 1.088, which confirmed the absence of multicollinearity among these factors.

In the child impact section, similar factors significantly associated with poor OHRQoL were identified, including later initiation of toothbrushing (β = 0.177, P = 0.003), frequent sweet food intake (β = 0.168, P = 0.005), history of toothache (β = 0.332, P < 0.001), low parental OHL (β=−0.205, P < 0.001), higher caries severity (β = 0.152, P = 0.012), and multiple children (β = 0.126, P = 0.033). Results from the family impact section indicated that parental evaluation of the child’s oral status (β = 0.223, P = 0.002), parental OHL (β=−0.162, P = 0.014), and presence of caries (β = 0.183, P = 0.009) were factors significantly associated with poor family impact-related OHRQoL.

To further validate the conceptual model, a hierarchical regression analysis was performed by entering variables in steps based on their theoretical proximity to the outcome. The variance explained increased significantly (P < 0.001) with the sequential addition of steps for sociodemographic factors, parental OHL, child oral health behaviors, and oral health status, which was consistent with the hypothesized structure of associations (Supplementary Table S1). Moreover, the final set of significant associated factors remained consistent with the results from the stepwise regression analysis presented in Table 4. Collectively, these findings suggest that pediatric OHRQoL is associated with multiple factors, with parental OHL emerging as a common core factor linked to better OHRQoL across dimensions.

Discussion

This study provides empirical evidence that clarifies the associations between parental OHL, children’s oral health behaviors, oral health status, and OHRQoL among preschool children. Our findings not only confirm the multifaceted characteristic of factors associated with OHRQoL but also offer several novel contributions to the field. First, this study systematically analyzes the complex interrelationships among parental OHL, children’s oral health behaviors, oral health status, and OHRQoL within a unified theoretical framework. Our analysis confirms that parental OHL, children’s oral health behaviors, and children’s oral status all showed correlations with children’s OHRQoL. Second, our multivariate analysis identifies the independent and hierarchical associations of these factors with OHRQoL. We identify that parental OHL, delayed initiation of toothbrushing, frequent sweet food intake, higher caries severity, and a history of toothache are the most important factors significantly associated with compromised OHRQoL. Third, going beyond a global score analysis, we explain how these associated factors differentially relate to specific OHRQoL dimensions. This provides the nuanced, association-based model necessary for developing targeted, multi-level interventions.

​Parental OHL is regarded as a key factor associated with children’s oral health behaviors and health outcomes [34]. In this study, the overall OHL of the surveyed parents was at a relatively high level. This level was higher than the findings reported in a previous study focusing on nurses [51]. That prior study noted that nurses’ OHL was at a moderate to low level. This difference may be attributed to the distinct caregiving roles and active learning motivation of parents, as well as the differing socioeconomic characteristics of the study populations. In this study, for every 1-point increase in parents’ total score of OHL, the total score of ECOHIS decreased by 3.285 points. This further confirms that parental OHL may act as a significant factor linked to better children’s OHRQoL. Specifically, parents with a higher level of oral health literacy are associated with better children’s OHRQoL. This finding is consistent with the research of Wang et al. [29], who pointed out that parents’ higher OHL, especially their depth of understanding of health information, often significantly reduces children’s risk of dental caries and enhances their OHRQoL by optimizing their own oral health behaviors (such as regular dental check-ups and a scientific diet) and strengthening positive health attitudes. Parental OHL is associated with children’s oral health outcomes, potentially through a behavioral mediating pattern. According to the study by Buldur et al. [38], path analytic models further identified the behavioral mediation pathway, whereby parental OHL was positively associated with adaptive pediatric oral behaviors (such as reduced intake of cariogenic foods), which in turn reduced the risk of dental caries and improved their OHRQoL. These findings also support the conclusions of this study.

Furthermore, our findings demonstrated that higher parental OHL is a factor associated with better children’s psychological well-being and lower parental distress. It is worth noting that there are some differences between the conclusions of some previous studies and those of this study. A prior study reported a significant negative correlation between parental OHL and childhood caries, yet the association with OHRQoL remained ambiguous [24]. Additionally, multivariate analyses in other investigations failed to establish a significant association between parental OHL and children’s OHRQoL [35, 36]. These inconsistencies may be attributed to differences in OHL assessment tools. Most earlier investigations utilized the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Dentistry (REALD-30), a word-recognition test that is limited to measuring basic literacy skills and inherently overlooks higher-order OHL dimensions (e.g., comprehension, application) [52]. The HeLD-14 scale used in this study can assess multiple dimensions of functional health literacy [53] and is more sensitive in capturing the comprehensive impact of OHL on health behaviors.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that toothache has the strongest impact on the OHRQoL of children and adolescents [25, 54–56], consistent with the results of the present study. The present study found that reports of toothache are the most critical factor associated with the decline in OHRQoL among preschool children. Furthermore, the negative impacts of toothache showed a stronger association with poorer OHRQoL. A study of Brazilian 5-year-olds reported a 3.35 points higher mean C-OIDP score in children with a history of toothache [57]. The present study also found similar results. The average ECOHIS score of children with a history of toothache was 6.28 points higher than that of children without a history of toothache. A possible explanation for this difference in scores is the use of different tools to assess OHRQoL. In addition, a history of toothache was significantly associated with an increase in CIS and FIS scores. This association demonstrates the negative association of toothaches with children and their families, and it was consistent with the results reported in prior studies [25]. The present study provides direct evidence to support the prioritization of managing children’s toothache symptoms in clinical practice.

The results of this study enhance the understanding of the relationship between dental caries, their severity, and preschool children’s OHRQoL. Dental caries are significantly and negatively associated with preschool children’s OHRQoL, as indicated by higher total ECOHIS scores and subscale scores. These findings are consistent with two other studies [58, 59], which also reported that the scores of the two sub-components of the ECOHIS increased significantly in the presence of dental caries. Additionally, the present study further revealed that as children’s dental caries burden increases, all ECOHIS scores increase significantly, except for the score of the child self-image dimension. This result is consistent with a previous study by Abanto et al. [60], which compared ECOHIS scores between caries-free children and those with severe caries. The study found that the severe caries group had significantly higher total ECOHIS scores, as well as higher scores in the CIS and FIS. This study emphasizes that dental caries management should combine child-centered care concepts and family support systems, providing solid empirical evidence for developing multi-level intervention strategies.

​​The current results showed that the age of starting toothbrushing is a significant factor associated with OHRQoL in preschool children, with delayed initiation significantly linked to compromised OHRQoL. Specifically, children who started brushing before 6 months of age showed significantly better OHRQoL than those with delayed initiation. This was in agreement with the findings of Freire et al. [61], who reported via bivariate analysis that toothbrushing initiation age was significantly correlated with OHRQoL. Their study also demonstrated a gradient effect: children initiating before 1 year had the best OHRQoL, followed by 2–3 year starters, and post-3 year initiators had the poorest outcomes. However, it is worth noting that the age of starting toothbrushing was not identified as an independent factor associated with children’s OHRQoL in their linear regression model. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the age of starting toothbrushing is inherently linked to the timing of the first tooth eruption [62, 63]. Although our questionnaire instructions explicitly included pre-eruption oral cleaning practices to mitigate this confounding effect, the two factors remain closely related. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted with caution. It may not solely be a direct association of the cleaning behavior itself, but rather a proxy for a broader construct of ‘early initiation of oral health practices’, which is likely influenced by parental oral health awareness and knowledge [29, 63, 64]. Children who receive early oral care may also have parents who are more proactive in other aspects of their oral health (e.g., dietary habits, regular dental visits), which could collectively contribute to the better outcomes observed [29, 62]. This potential confounding may also explain, in part, the discrepancy between our findings and those of Freire et al. [61], as the influence of direct pathogenic factors such as dental caries and alveolar abscesses might overshadow the independent impact of toothbrushing initiation age in multivariate models that adjust for these mediators. These findings underscore the urgency of early-life oral health education and habit formation, while also highlighting the need for more nuanced research. Future studies with detailed data on teething chronology could help disentangle the independent effects of these closely intertwined variables.

In the current study, the frequency of sweet food intake is also a significant factor associated with pediatric OHRQoL. Our findings align with the conclusions of Amilani et al. [56], who reported that regular consumption of sugary foods is regarded as a risk factor for poor OHRQoL.

Beyond individual oral behaviors and oral health status, family structure may also affect children’s OHRQoL. The present results revealed that multiple children displayed significantly poorer OHRQoL than only children, with notable deficits in symptom and functional dimensions. Similar findings have been reported in other studies. Liu et al. [65] reported that compared to single children, children with siblings were more likely to report impacts on eating, speaking, and mouth cleaning. One possible explanation for this observation involves a per capita resource dilution effect linked to family size expansion. Specifically, the attention parents give to their multiple children, the supervision of their children’s oral hygiene habits, and the access to dental care resources are relatively reduced, which in turn affects children’s OHRQoL. Consequently, the oral health needs of multiple children may be overlooked amid parental energy reallocation, underscoring the need for targeted family health interventions to address this disparity.

This study was guided by an integrated theoretical framework combining the OHRQL model [39, 40] with an ecological perspective [41]. While the OHRQL model establishes individual-level factors (e.g., oral health status, behaviors) as proximal determinants of OHRQoL, the ecological perspective contends that these factors are themselves shaped by broader determinants. The integrated framework posits that a child’s OHRQoL, as the outcome, is influenced by a cascade of factors ranging from distal societal determinants to proximal clinical conditions. To empirically examine this hypothesized pattern of associations, we complemented the standard stepwise regression with a hierarchical regression analysis, entering variables in steps based on their theoretical proximity to the outcome. The results of this analysis were convergent with our initial findings. The consistency between the stepwise and hierarchical analytical approaches validates the robustness of our core findings and provides empirical support for the proposed conceptual framework.

Based on our findings, clear directions are provided for formulating precise intervention strategies: prioritizing the enhancement of parental OHL as a core component, while integrating effective caries prevention, promoting the early adoption of oral hygiene practices and healthy dietary habits, and giving special attention to not only children in multi-child families.

This study also has certain limitations. First, being a cross-sectional study, our study discovered associations between parental OHL, children’s early toothbrushing behavior, caries severity, and OHRQoL in preschool children but could not establish causal relationships. Future research should involve oral health interventions in preschool settings to establish the causal relationships among these factors. Second, children who refused or failed to cooperate with oral examinations were excluded. However, such uncooperative behavior is likely a manifestation of dental fear, which itself is closely associated with poorer oral health status and poorer OHRQoL [66, 67]. Therefore, our sample may systematically underestimate the group of children with dental fear, which could result in the findings of this study not fully representing the true situation of this specific population. Meanwhile, this selection bias may lead to an underestimation of the various associations we observed, because children with the most severe conditions, who are likely the most fearful, were not included in the analysis. Future studies could address this by administering validated dental fear scales during recruitment to document fear levels and adopting child-friendly examination strategies to reduce cooperation-related exclusions. Furthermore, since study participants were parents and children attending a dental center, and data relied on parent-proxy reports, recall bias may have been introduced and may not fully reflect the children’s own perspectives and emotional experiences. For this, future research could explore the integration of objective measurements or child self-assessments (such as SOHO-5 [13]) or utilize a mixed-methods approach to triangulate findings from both parents and children, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the impacts of oral conditions on young children’s lives. Despite these limitations, our findings hold significant clinical relevance for pediatric dentistry. Recommendations include employing validated scales and semi-structured interviews to assess parental OHL and children’s oral health behaviors at the child’s initial dental visit.

Conclusions

This study investigates the OHRQoL and associated factors among preschool children in Nanjing, China. The findings of the current study reveal that pediatric OHRQoL is associated with multiple factors, including parental OHL, age of starting toothbrushing, frequency of sweet food intake, history of toothache, the presence of caries, and its severity. These findings underscore that improving OHRQoL in this population requires comprehensive strategies that encompass family-centered health education, early preventive behaviors, dietary guidance, and clinical care. Such efforts are vital for promoting children’s overall well-being and healthy development.
