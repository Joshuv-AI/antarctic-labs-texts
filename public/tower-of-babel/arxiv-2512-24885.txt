Hengli Li, Zhaoxin Yu, Qi Shen, Chenxi Li, Mengmeng Wang, Tinglang Wu, Yipeng Kang, Yuxuan Wang, Song-Chun Zhu, Zixia Jia, Zilong Zheng

Abstract
. Strategic dialogue requires agents to execute distinct dialogue acts, for which belief estimation is essential. While prior work often estimates beliefs accurately, it lacks a principled mechanism to use those beliefs during generation. We bridge this gap by first formalizing two core acts Adversarial and Alignment —and by operationalizing them via probabilistic constraints on what an agent may generate. We instantiate this idea in BEDA , a framework that consists of the world set, the belief estimator for belief estimation, and the conditional generator that selects acts and realizes utterances consistent with the inferred beliefs. Across three settings, Conditional Keeper–Burglar (adversarial), Mutual Friends (cooperative), and CaSiNo (negotiation), BEDA consistently outperforms strong baselines: on CKBG it improves success rate by at least 5.0 points across backbones and by 20.6 points with GPT-4.1-nano; on Mutual Friends it achieves an average improvement of 9.3 points ; and on CaSiNo it achieves the optimal deal relative to all baselines. These results indicate that casting belief estimation as constraints provides a simple, general mechanism for reliable strategic dialogue.

Introduction

Complex dialogue settings such as negotiation
(
24
;
13
)
, debate
(
39
)
, deception for good
(
15
)
, and common-ground alignment
(
33
)
are ubiquitous for AI agents. In these scenarios, an agent must strategically perform dialogue acts to shape the interlocutor’s beliefs to achieve its own objectives
(
14
;
9
;
25
)
. For example, in negotiation, the agent should
align and emphasize common preferences
to increase the chance its proposal is accepted, while simultaneously adopting an
adversarial stance
concerning individual differences to protect its own interests.

Executing such strategic acts depends on two essential components
(
50
;
9
)
: (i)
accurate belief estimation
and (ii)
effective use of those beliefs for utterance generation
. While prior works
(
54
;
33
;
18
)
have demonstrated the benefits of modeling these subprocesses explicitly, they have only emphasized the former,
leaving the latter underdeveloped
. In particular, existing methods often relay
all
available belief information without a principled criterion for
what
to reveal and
how
to reveal it–an approach that can be suboptimal when belief states are complex. We therefore
bridge belief estimation and utterance generation
with a mechanism that selectively and purposefully transforms beliefs into dialogue actions.

We begin by giving precise definitions of
Alignment
and
Adversarial
dialogue acts within the game-theoretic framework of beliefs and common knowledge
(
2
;
6
;
5
)
, thereby bridging belief estimation and dialogue behavior. Conceptually, an Adversarial Dialogue Act , introduces or emphasizes events that lie outside the opponent’s current belief support, strategically steering interaction toward some aggressive goals. By contrast, an Alignment Dialogue Act , restricts utterances to events entailed by shared common knowledge, promoting mutual understanding and coordination. In this view,
belief estimation over events functions as constraints
that
define
the permissible space of dialogue acts and operationalize their selection during generation.

The proposed agent framework,
BEDA
(
B
elief
E
stimation for
D
ialogue
A
cts), shown in
Figure

1
, formalizes these conceptual definitions to support empirical experimentation and validate the underlying theoretical constructs. At a high level, BEDA consists of three core components:
World Set
,
Belief Estimator Module
, and
Conditional Generator
. The
World Set
serves as a structured representation of the environment, organizing events to provide a foundation for belief formation. The
Belief Estimator Module
utilizes this structured information to infer the opponent’s perspective of the world, thereby modeling their beliefs. Finally, the
Conditional Generator
synthesizes the estimated belief states to generate utterances that align with the defined criteria for alignment and adversarial actions, ensuring communicative behaviors that adhere to the respective interactional strategies.

We further evaluate the effectiveness of our approach on three distinct datasets:
ckbg
(
ckbg
), corresponding to the Adversarial Dialogue Act ;

mf
(

), representing evaluating the Alignment Dialogue Act ; and CaSiNo
(

We mathematically

For evaluation, we

This integration of belief estimation in BEDA

2.1.
Preliminaries: Logical Definition of Beliefs

We first introduce the concepts of information partition, event, and belief
(
2
;
6
;
5
)
, which serve as the foundational elements of our framework. These definitions follow
5
.

Definition 2.1 (Information Partition).

Let
W
be a finite set of states, an information partition is a partition
\mathcal{F}
of
W
.

Definition 2.2 (Event).

Let
W
be a finite set of states, a subset of
W
is defined as an event.

Definition 2.3 (Belief).

Let
x\in W
be a state, at
x
the agent knows event
E
iff
I(x)
is a subset of
E
, i.e.
I(x)\subset E
, where
I(x)
denotes the information subset
x
belongs.

Furthermore, the negation of an event is denoted as
\neg E:=W-E
. We also introduce the knowledge operator
K
here. When given an event
E
,
KE
outputs the set of state at which the agent knows
E
, i.e.
KE=\{x|I(x)\subset E\}

Furthermore, randomness can also be introduced to the definition of belief:

Definition 2.4 (Probabilistic Beliefs).

Let
W
be a set of states. Denote the probability of state
x\in W
as
P(x)
and an event
E
, the probabilistic beliefs of an agent is

P(E):=\sum\limits_{x\in E}P(x).

2.2.
Belief Information as Probabilistic Constraints

We hypothesize that belief estimation functions as a set of constraints in the process of dialogue act generation.
In this subsection, we will introduce the belief constraints associated with two main dialogue acts: Adversarial Dialogue Act and Alignment Dialogue Act . The empirical verifications are provided in
Section

4
.

Definition 2.5 (Adversarial Dialogue Act).

Given two agents, namely,
A
,
B
, and their corresponding information partition set
\mathcal{F}_{A},\mathcal{F}_{B}
, telling an event
E
is a
\epsilon
-Adversarial Dialogue Act from
A
to
B
iff

\begin{cases}P_{A}(E)\geq 1-\epsilon\\
P_{A}(\neg K_{B}E)\geq 1-\epsilon\end{cases},

where
P_{A}
denotes the probabilistic belief of A, and
K_{B}
denotes the knowledge operator for B.

The concept of a Adversarial Dialogue Act involves the intentional communication of information that is beyond the interlocutor’s understanding, aimed at achieving more assertive objectives. This definition encompasses two essential elements: first, the speaker must possess confidence in the information conveyed; second, the speaker must assume that the interlocutor lacks knowledge of the same information. This strategy is often employed to create deliberate misdirection.

Definition 2.6 (Alignment Dialogue Act).

Given two agents, namely,
A
,
B
, and their corresponding information partition set
\mathcal{F}_{A},\mathcal{F}_{B}
, telling an event
E
is a
\epsilon
-Alignment Dialogue Act from
A
to
B
iff

\begin{cases}P_{A}(E)\geq 1-\epsilon\\
P_{A}(K_{B}E)\geq 1-\epsilon\end{cases},

where
P_{A}
denotes the probabilistic belief of A, and
K_{B}
denotes
B
’s knowledge operator.

Unlike Adversarial Dialogue Act , the Alignment Dialogue Act tries to convey information that falls within a shared common ground, with the goal of enhancing mutual trust and achieving alignment between interlocutors.

Statistical Modeling of Dialogue Acts

Suppose
A
is the speaker and
B
is the listener. Given the current context and task together as
C
, the world set as
W
, the power set of
W
as
\mathcal{P}(W)
, we denote the distribution of
A
conveying an event
E
with utterance
u
as
\pi_{A}(u,E\mid C)
. By Bayes’ Rules,

\pi_{A}(u,E\mid C)=\pi_{A}(u\mid E,C)\pi_{A}(E\mid C).

\pi_{A}(u\mid E,C)
is modeled by an LLM, given its strong language generation ability. Regarding
\pi_{A}(E\mid C)
, to avoid injecting any additional human priors beyond the belief constraints, we adopt the maximum-entropy principle
(
20
;
19
;
55
)
, assigning equal probability to all events that satisfy the constraints. Consequently, when the feasible set of
u
coincides on both sides, we have

\arg\max_{u}\ \pi_{A}(u,E\mid C)=\arg\max_{u}\ \pi_{A}(u\mid E,C).

In summary, under either Adversarial Dialogue Act or Alignment Dialogue Act , generation reduces to the constrained optimization problem specified below.

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle u,E\in\mathcal{P}(W)}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\pi_{A}(u|E,C)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{u,E\in\mathcal{P}(W)}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle P_{A}(E|C)\geq 1-\epsilon,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}P_{A}(\neg K_{B}E\mid C)\geq 1-\epsilon&\text{Adversarial}\\
P_{A}(K_{B}E\mid C)\geq 1-\epsilon&\text{Alignment}\end{cases},

where, in practice,
P_{A}
can be modeled with a discriminator neural network.

2.3.
Components of the BEDA framework

In this subsection, we’ll describe our framework, named BEDA , depicted mathematically in
Section

2.2
. As illustrated in
Figure

1
, BEDA is composed of three components: the
world set
, the
belief estimator module
, and the
conditional generator
.

World Set
\mathcal{P}(W)

The
world set
represents a finite collection of events. Each element of this set corresponds to an event that has been referenced in the dialogue or is relevant to the environment. Events within the
world set
can only be either true or false. Furthermore, not all events are shared as common knowledge between the two agents. Consequently, events specified in the set require additional discrimination and prediction. In this work, since we focus mainly on the whole paradigm, the
world set
is given, eliminating the need for dynamic construction, leaving only the attributes to be predicted.

Belief Estimator (
P_{A}
)

The
belief estimator
module comprises two belief estimators: one dedicated to determining the truthfulness of events i.e.
[P_{A}(E|C),\forall E\in\mathcal{P}(W)]
and the other functioning as a predictor of the interlocutor’s beliefs i.e.
[P_{A}(K_{B}E|C),\forall E\in\mathcal{P}(W)]
. In this framework, each belief corresponds to an event from
world set
. The
belief estimator module
generates beliefs by analyzing the content of the dialogue and aligning it with the
world set
. However, it is not feasible for the module to store all possible settings. Consequently, a robust generalization capability is essential for effective belief estimation. Therefore, we employ a pre-trained encoder model, the BERT model
(
10
)
, as the backbone. Given the input
C
, the model then outputs the vectors
[P_{A}(E|C),\forall E\in\mathcal{P}(W)]
and
[P_{A}(K_{B}E|C),\forall E\in\mathcal{P}(W)]
. Each entry is the model’s estimate of the truth probability of
E
and, respectively, of
K_{B}E
.

Conditional Generator (
\pi
)

The final component is the
conditional generator
, which synthesizes the context
C
, encompassing both the dialogue history and the task, alongside the event
E
. The event
E
is selected according to the predictions of the
belief estimator module
and the chosen dialogue act. For instance, in the case of Adversarial Dialogue Act , only
E
satisfying both
P_{A}(E)\geq 1-\epsilon
and
P_{A}(\neg K_{B}E)\geq 1-\epsilon
are picked. The conditional generator then utilizes this integrated information to generate response
u
to the interlocutor, represented as
\pi(u|E,C)
. We hypothesize that incorporating enhanced beliefs into the generator significantly improves its performance and response quality.

2.4.
Learning and Inference of BEDA

To mitigate the substantial computational cost associated with fine-tuning large language models, we opt to fix the LLM in the conditional generation module (
\pi(u|E,C)
) and leave solely the belief estimator as a trainable part. The training of the belief estimator is performed under a supervised setting, where a set of dialogue data with known belief ground truth is collected and utilized for learning. To avoid data leakage, separate data are used for training and inference. We find that supervised training belief estimators can be generalized to unseen settings well. The inference process of BEDA is illustrated in
Figure

1
. In this framework, the context, background information, and the
world set
are provided to two belief estimators, which predict the corresponding beliefs. These predicted beliefs, along with the basic input information, are then fed into the conditional generator to produce an utterance. This generated utterance is subsequently presented to the interlocutor model to elicit a response.

Building upon the Keeper-Burglar game

In this game, the

mf

(
16
)
is the task of identifying a mutual friend between two agents. It’s a cooperative setting where the Alignment Dialogue Act is suitable. The
world set
is designed as various attributes possessed by the friend, and the belief estimator predicts the values of these attributes as believed by the other player. In the conditional generation phase, the estimation of the other’s belief will be used as a condition to generate information within the common ground.

CaSiNo

CaSiNo
(
7
)
is a negotiation task in which two participants assume the roles of campsite neighbors negotiating the allocation of resources. In this context, the
world set
represents the preferences of both negotiators. To capture the alignment and adversarial nature of the negotiation, we introduce two distinct belief estimators. The first estimator predicts the preferences of the opponent, while the second estimates our own preferences from the opponent’s perspective. The selection mechanism operates as follows: in the Alignment Dialogue Act , an element that is known to both parties is selected, while in the Adversarial Dialogue Act , an element known exclusively to our side (and not to the opponent) is chosen. These two selected elements are subsequently combined for the
conditional generation module

7.
Discussion and Future Works

Applying LLMs for belief estimation.

While large language models (LLMs) can serve as belief estimators, our experiments indicate that lightweight encoders suffice to infer belief states from dialogue with substantial efficiency gains. In particular, a compact encoder captures the salient conversational signals with accuracy comparable to an LLM at a fraction of the computational cost. We therefore employ the smaller model in practice, reserving LLM-based estimators for settings that demand broader world knowledge or enhanced robustness to distribution shift.

Dynamic construction of the world set.

To isolate the contribution of belief estimation and its interaction with dialogue acts, this work holds the
world set
fixed. Future work may relax this assumption by learning a dynamic
world set
that is expanded, pruned, or reweighted online based on interaction signals, potentially improving coverage and downstream performance.

Finer modeling of dialogue acts.

Although many specific acts (e.g.,
AGREEMENT
, typically subsumed under Alignment Dialogue Act ) fall within the coarse categories Adversarial Dialogue Act and Alignment Dialogue Act , finer-grained act modeling may yield stronger agents. Hierarchical or compositional schemas that distinguish, for example,
Agreement
,
Concession
,
Hedging
, and
Commitment
, could provide more precise control and better credit assignment during learning.

8.
Conclusions

We presented BEDA , a simple yet general framework that casts belief estimation as probabilistic constraints for executing strategic dialogue acts. By formalizing two core acts—
Adversarial
(introduce or emphasize events outside the interlocutor’s belief support) and
Alignment
(restrict utterances to common ground)—and instantiating them with a
world set
,
dual belief estimators
(event truth and opponent knowledge), and a
conditional generator
, BEDA bridges the gap between estimating beliefs and using them during generation. Across three settings, Conditional Keeper–Burglar (adversarial), Mutual Friends (cooperative), and CaSiNo (negotiation), BEDA consistently improves strategic reliability: it raises success on CKBG (up to +20.6 points with GPT-4.1-nano), improves success in MF (gains of 4.3–11.2 points, and up to +30.4 in some configurations) while reducing turns, and negotiates higher-quality agreements in CaSiNo relative to strong prompting baselines. These results indicate that
constraining generation by inferred belief structure
is an effective organizing principle for dialogue agents, complementary to generic dialogue heuristics such as CoT or self-reflection. We see BEDA’s constraint-based view as a step toward strategic dialogue agents that reason about—and act through—the beliefs that structure interaction. (See

A

E.0.3.
Details of World Set Designation and Selection Process

World Set Designation

The Conditional Keeper-Burglar Dataset was developed based on the work of
15
, incorporating additional contextual elements to function as conditions. These modifications were intended to enhance the game’s variability and create an information asymmetry. The world set, which is a comprehensive compilation of all potential conditions, is systematically organized into five distinct categories. Conditions from this set are randomly selected and incorporated into the prompt to provide the burglar with background information. Conversely, for the keeper, a belief estimator is employed to determine a subset of conditions from the world set that the burglar is assumed to know. This subset is then presented to the keeper as an informational prompt. The world set, which is synonymous with the condition set, consists of the following five conditions:

•

Informer: The burglar has been informed that the keeper may lie to him.

•

Burglar inspection: The burglar opened [Container x] [Time] hours ago and observed a [Object x] inside.

•

Keeper inspection: The keeper opened [Container x] [Time] hours ago.

•

Outsider inspection: A friend of the keeper opened [Container x] [Time] hours ago.

•

Noise: A noise was heard coming from [Container x].

Selection Process

Recall the definition of the framework presented in
Section

2
. The belief estimation predicts the probabilistic beliefs across five distinct conditions. The self’s belief reflects the keeper’s confidence regarding the occurrence of an event, while the opponent’s belief represents the burglar’s confidence in the same events. Since the keeper is provided with a list of conditions, we denote the self’s beliefs on these conditions as 1 (known) and others as 0 (unknown). Furthermore, a neural network, which models the opponent’s beliefs, will predict the burglar’s beliefs based on the ongoing conversation. Subsequently, events for which the burglar’s belief is 0 will be selected as inputs to the conditional generator for further generation.

E.0.4.
Experimental Details

The experiments are conducted within a first-person, agent-centric framework, wherein two agents powered by large language models (LLMs) are assigned the roles of "keeper" and "burglar" to engage in interactive dialogues. During prompt construction, each agent is initially provided with role-specific background information and task objectives via the system prompt. Additionally, the outputs of a belief estimation module are incorporated into the system prompt as supplementary contextual information. To preserve conversational coherence and ensure consistent role alignment, the dialogue history between the two agents is interleaved as alternating user and assistant turns within the prompt sequence.

Belief estimator Training

We implement the belief estimator using a BERT-based architecture. The technical pipeline proceeds as follows:

•

Sentence Encoding:
Each utterance in the dialogue is independently encoded using BERT, with the final-layer [CLS] token embedding extracted as the utterance-level representation.

•

Context-Event Fusion:
The representations of all utterances are concatenated with event embeddings and passed through a self-attention layer to facilitate information integration.

•

Binary Classification:
At each event-specific position within the fused representation, the corresponding embedding is fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with softmax activation, producing a probabilistic output that indicates whether the burglar is aware of the particular event.

The accuracy of the belief estimator is presented in
Table

4
. To better simulate the conditions of actual conversations in the training and test sets, we randomly clipped the final few rounds of each multi-turn conversation. This approach ensures that the belief estimator is trained and tested with less contextual information, thereby enhancing its adaptability to real-world scenarios.

F.0.1.

The task of identifying mutual friends between two interlocutors, referred to as

In this task, two players are each given a list of friends, with each friend having a distinct set of attributes, such as name, hobbies, and school. Importantly, there is exactly one friend common to both players. The Large Language Model (LLM) is tasked with role-playing both players, engaging in a dialogue to exchange information over a limited number of rounds. The game is considered successfully completed when both players simultaneously confirm the identity of the mutual friend and select the same individual.

•

F.0.3.
Experimental Details

We developed a symmetric two-agent experimental framework in which two large language model (LLM) agents act as players collaborating toward a common objective. Each agent possesses a distinct friend list, but both share the same game goal. The initial game context, including the background and friend lists, is provided through a system prompt. During the dialogue phase, the conversation history is structured by alternating between user and assistant roles within the prompt sequence, ensuring both conversational coherence and consistent role adherence throughout the interaction. At the end of the dialogue, conditional generation is employed to assess whether a mutual friend has been identified, and this judgment is appended to the dialogue history, also presented as a system prompt to the LLM. The evaluation phase commences once both players either confirm the identification of a mutual friend or reach the maximum dialogue turn limit (20 turns). During this phase, the system prompts each player to select a mutual friend from their respective friend lists. If both players select the same individual, the outcome is deemed a success.

Belief estimator Training

We implement the belief estimator using a BERT-based architecture. The estimation of the belief state is essentially a binary classification of the utterance-attribute-value triple. The technical pipeline proceeds as follows:

•

Input wrapping: The utterance-attribute-value triple will be concatenated into a long sentence, separated by [SEP], begin with [CLS]. For example: "[CLS] [utterance] [SEP] [attribute] [SEP] [value] [SEP]"

•

Sentence Encoding: Each sentence is independently encoded through BERT, with the final-layer [CLS] token embedding extracted as the triple-level representation.

•

Binary Classification: For each triple, the sentence representation vector is fed into an MLP with softmax activation, generating a probabilistic output indicating whether the attribute of the mutual friend, as perceived by the other party, matches the given value.

We have two GPT-3.5 agents play the game on the training set of the Mutual Friends dataset (which samples 1,000 data points) to generate training data for the belief estimator. After each player speaks, the system asks them to provide the attribute values of the mutual friend they currently believe in, which serves as the ground truth label for the training data. To better align with the testing scenario, this ground truth label is fed into the conditional generation module. For each utterance-attribute-value triple labeled as correct, we randomly alter either the attribute or the value to construct a negative sample. The training data maintains a 1:1 ratio between positive and negative samples. Ultimately, the 1,000 data points sampled from the original training set generated 67,690 training samples. We split the data into training, testing, and validation sets in a 0.9:0.05:0.05 ratio. The belief estimator achieved an accuracy of 90.1% on the test set.

Selection Process

In the CaSiNo framework, a mixed strategy is employed, where the conditional generation model is provided with two distinct events: one corresponding to the conditions of the Alignment Dialogue Act and the other to the conditions of the Adversarial Dialogue Act . For the Alignment Dialogue Act , only the first 12 conditions in the world set are considered relevant, with the remaining 12 conditions deemed inconsequential. Conversely, for the Adversarial Dialogue Act , only the last 12 conditions in the world set are taken into account. The underlying rationale for the Alignment Dialogue Act is as follows: "I know you prefer xxx, and I am aware that you are cognizant of your preference for xxx" (assuming mutual awareness of preferences). In contrast, the rationale for the Adversarial Dialogue Act is: "I know that I do not prefer xxx, and I am aware that you are unaware of my lack of preference for xxx" (anticipating the opponent’s lack of knowledge).
