Dichotomies for triangular systems on Hilbert spaces
Davor Dragičević, Kenneth J. Palmer, Boris Petković

Abstract
. In this article, we study the relationship between the exponential dichotomy properties of a triangular system of linear difference equations and its associated diagonal system on Hilbert spaces. We stress that all previous results in this direction were restricted to the finite-dimensional case. As in the previous work of the first two authors, we rely on the relationship between exponential dichotomies and the so-called admissibility properties. However, this approach requires nontrivial changes when passing from the finite-dimensional to the infinite-dimensional setting. Keywords : exponential dichotomy; triangular systems; difference equations; Hilbert spaces. 2020 MSC : 34D05, 34C25.



1.
Introduction

In the present paper, we study properties of nonautonomous linear discrete systems of triangular form given by

x(n+1)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n)&A_{12}(n)\\
0&A_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix}x(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime},

(1.1)

where
J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
and

J^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}J&J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{+}\};\\
\mathbb{Z}^{-}\setminus\{0\}&J=\mathbb{Z}^{-}.\end{cases}

(1.2)

Here,
A_{11}(n)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{1},X_{1})
,
A_{12}(n)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{2},X_{1})
and
A_{22}(n)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{2},X_{2})
, where
X_{i}
,
i=1,2
are arbitrary Hilbert spaces and
\mathcal{L}(X_{i},X_{j})
denotes the space of all bounded linear operators from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
.

We are interested in studying the relationship between the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
1.1
) and its associated
diagonal
system given by

x(n+1)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n)&0\\
0&A_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix}x(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}.

(1.3)

This problem has been addressed in a series of recent works
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
9
,
17
]
in the case when
X_{i}
,
i=1,2
are finite-dimensional spaces. Therefore, the present paper is the first to consider the case where
X_{i}
,
i=1,2
are infinite-dimensional spaces.

The approach we use is the one initiated in the previous article by the first two authors
[
9
]
, and is based on the characterization of exponential dichotomies in terms of the so-called admissibility property. Using this method, in
[
9
]
the authors were able to give shorter proofs of previously known results obtained in
[
4
,
5
]
, as well as to obtain several new ones.

We recall that the concept of admissibility for a general nonautonomous linear difference equation

x(n+1)=A(n)x(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}

(1.4)

relates to the (possibly unique) solvability of a nonhomogeneous difference equation

x(n+1)=A(n)x(n)+y(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}

for a suitable class of inputs
(y(n))_{n\in J^{\prime}}
. We note that the input
(y(n))_{n\in J^{\prime}}
and the output
(x(n))_{n\in J}
need to belong to suitable Banach sequence spaces. The results that connect the existence of an exponential dichotomy of (
1.4
) and the admissibility property have a long history that goes back to the seminal work of Perron
[
16
]
, with subsequent important contributions by Massera and Schäffer
[
14
]
, Daleckiĭ and Kreĭn
[
8
]
, Coppel
[
7
]
and Henry
[
10
]
(among many others). We refer to
[
2
]
for a detailed survey of this theory and its relationship with hyperbolicity.
We stress that the results of Henry
[
10
,
11
]

are most relevant for our purposes, as they deal with equations (
1.4
) on arbitrary Banach spaces without any invertibility assumptions for coefficients
A(n)
or the uniform boundedness of their norms.

In the case
J=\mathbb{Z}
, the characterization of the exponential dichotomy for (
1.4
) in terms of admissibility (see Theorem
1
) has the same form in both the finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional settings. Consequently, in the case
J=\mathbb{Z}
, we are able to straightforwardly extend to the infinite-dimensional setting the results from
[
9
]
connecting the existence of exponential dichotomy for (
1.1
) and (
1.3
).

On the other hand, the corresponding characterization of the exponential dichotomy for (
1.4
) when
J\in\{\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
(see Theorems
2
and
3
) is more complicated in the infinite-dimensional setting, as it requires that certain subspaces of a phase space are closed or complemented. Consequently, for
J\in\{\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
it is a nontrivial task to obtain infinite-dimensional versions of the results from
[
9
]
especially when it is assumed that (
1.1
) has a dichotomy. We refer to Remarks
5
,
7
and
8
for a detailed description of the difficulties that arise.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we list relevant results (also indicating certain modifications) dealing with the characterization of the exponential dichotomy for (
1.4
) in terms of appropriate admissibility properties. In addition, we introduce the setup of the paper.
In Section
3
, we provide results yielding the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
1.1
) provided that (
1.3
) admits an exponential dichotomy. Finally, in Section
4
we provide necessary and sufficient conditions under which the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
1.1
) implies that (
1.3
) admits an exponential dichotomy. We give separate results that correspond to all possible choices of
J
. Moreover, we treat systems with invertible coefficients.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
X=(X,\|\cdot\|)
be an arbitrary Banach space.
By
\mathcal{L}(X)
we denote the space of all bounded linear operators on
X
equipped with the operator norm, which we also denote by
\|\cdot\|
.

Take
J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
denotes the set of nonnegative integers and
\mathbb{Z}^{-}
denotes the set of nonpositive integers. We recall the notion of an exponential dichotomy.
For a sequence
(A(n))_{n\in J}\subset\mathcal{L}(X)
, we consider the associated linear discrete system

x(n+1)=A(n)x(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime},

(2.1)

where
J^{\prime}
is given by (
1.2
).
By
\Phi(m,n)
, we will denote the associated cocycle given by

\Phi(m,n)=\begin{cases}A(m-1)\cdots A(n)&m>n;\\
\text{\rm Id}&m=n,\end{cases}

for
m,n\in J
with
m\geq n
, where
Id
denotes the identity operator on
X
.

Definition 1
.

We say that (
2.1
) admits an
exponential dichotomy
if there exist a family of projections
(P(n))_{n\in J}\subset\mathcal{L}(X)
and constants
K,\alpha>0
such that the following conditions hold:

(i)

for
n\in J^{\prime}
,

A(n)P(n)=P(n+1)A(n).

Moreover,
A(n)\rvert_{\mathcal{N}P(n)}\colon\mathcal{N}P(n)\to\mathcal{N}P(n+1)
is invertible, where
\mathcal{N}P(n)
denotes the kernel of
P(n)
;

(ii)

for
m,n\in J
with
m\geq n
,

\|\Phi(m,n)P(n)\|\leq Ke^{-\alpha(m-n)};

(iii)

for
m,n\in J
with
m\leq n
,

\|\Phi(m,n)(\text{\rm Id}-P(n))\|\leq Ke^{-\alpha(n-m)},

where
\Phi(m,n):=\left(\Phi(n,m)\rvert_{\mathcal{N}P(m)}\right)^{-1}\colon\mathcal{N}P(n)\to\mathcal{N}P(m)
.

We will need the following classical result obtained by Henry
[
10
, Theorem 7.6.5]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
J=\mathbb{Z}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(
2.1
) admits an exponential dichotomy;

(ii)

for each sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
such that

\sup_{n\in J}\|y(n)\|<+\infty
, there exists a unique bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
satisfying

x(n+1)=A(n)x(n)+y(n)\quad n\in J.

(2.2)

Remark 1
.

In what follows, each sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
with the property that
\sup_{n\in J}\|y(n)\|<+\infty
will be called
bounded sequence
.

We will also use the versions of Theorem
1
for cases
J=\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
J=\mathbb{Z}^{-}
. The following result was obtained by Henry
[
11
, Corollary 1.10]
. We recall that a
subspace
S\subset X
is
complemented
if there exists a closed subspace
Z\subset X
such that
X=S\oplus Z
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
J=\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(
2.1
) admits an exponential dichotomy on
J
;

(ii)

for each bounded sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
, there exists a bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
satisfying

x(n+1)=A(n)x(n)+y(n),\quad n\in J.

(2.3)

Moreover, the subspace

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{v\in X:\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\|\Phi(n,0)v\|<+\infty\right\}

(2.4)

is closed and complemented.

Remark 2
.

We note that the statement of Theorem
2
can be modified in the following way: in (ii) it is sufficient to assume that
\mathcal{S}
in (
2.4
) is complemented, i.e. the assumption that
\mathcal{S}
is closed can be omitted. We stress that this is a known fact that follows from the more general results obtained in
[
1
]
. However, here we provide a short argument that directly relies on Theorem
2
, which is also applicable to dichotomies on
\mathbb{Z}^{-}
(which were not discussed in
[
1
]
).

In order to illustrate this, let us assume that for each bounded sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
, there exists a bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
satisfying (
2.3
), and that
\mathcal{S}
is complemented. Let
Z\subset X
be a closed subspace such that
X=\mathcal{S}\oplus Z
. Take an arbitrary bounded sequence

(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
and let
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
be a bounded sequence satisfying (
2.3
). Write
x(0)
as
x(0)=v_{1}+v_{2}
, where
v_{1}\in\mathcal{S}
and
v_{2}\in Z
. We define a new sequence
(\bar{x}(n))_{n\in J}
by

\bar{x}(n)=x(n)-\Phi(n,0)v_{1},\quad n\in J.

Then
(\bar{x}(n))_{n\in J}
is bounded, satisfies (
2.3
) and
\bar{x}(0)=v_{2}\in Z
. Moreover, this is the only sequence with these three properties. In fact, if
(\tilde{x}(n))_{n\in J}
is a bounded sequence satisfying (
2.3
) and such that
\tilde{x}(0)\in Z
, then

\bar{x}(n)-\tilde{x}(n)=\Phi(n,0)(\bar{x}(0)-\tilde{x}(0)),\quad n\in J.

Consequently,
\bar{x}(0)-\tilde{x}(0)\in\mathcal{S}\cap Z
, and thus
\bar{x}(0)=\tilde{x}(0)
. We conclude that
\bar{x}(n)=\tilde{x}(n)
for
n\in J
, as claimed.

Let
\ell^{\infty}(J)
denote the space of all bounded sequences
(x(n))_{n\in J}
in
X
equipped with the supremum norm. We define a linear operator
\Gamma\colon\ell^{\infty}(J)\to\ell^{\infty}(J)
by
\Gamma((y(n))_{n\in J})=(x(n))_{n\in J}
, where
(x(n))_{n\in J}
is the unique sequence in
\ell^{\infty}(J)
that satisfies (
2.3
) and
x(0)\in Z
. It is a simple exercise to show that
\Gamma
is closed and consequently a bounded linear operator.

Take
v\in X
. We define two bounded sequences
(x(n))_{n\in J}
and
(y(n))
by

x(n)=\begin{cases}v&n=0;\\
0&n\neq 0\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad y(n)=\begin{cases}-A(0)v&n=0;\\
0&n\neq 0.\end{cases}

Clearly, (
2.3
) holds. Let
(\bar{x}(n))_{n\in J}:=\Gamma((y(n))_{n\in J})
. Set
Pv:=v-\bar{x}(0)\in\mathcal{S}
. It is easy to verify that
P
is a projection onto
\mathcal{S}
along
Z
. Moreover, we note that

\|Pv\|\leq\|v\|+\|\bar{x}(0)\|\leq\|v\|+\|\Gamma\|\cdot\|A(0)v\|\leq(1+\|\Gamma\|\cdot\|A(0)\|)\|v\|,

for every
v\in X
. Hence,
P
is bounded, and consequently
\mathcal{S}
is closed.

The following result is due to Henry
[
11
, Corollary 1.11]
and represents the version of Theorem
2
for dichotomies on
\mathbb{Z}^{-}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
J=\mathbb{Z}^{-}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(
2.1
) admits an exponential dichotomy on
J
;

(ii)

for each bounded sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J^{\prime}}\subset X
, there exists a bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
satisfying

x(n+1)=A(n)x(n)+y(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}.

(2.5)

Moreover, the subspace
\mathcal{U}
of
X
consisting of all
v\in X
with the property that there exists a bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
such that
x(0)=v
and
x(n)=A(n-1)x(n-1)
for
n\in J
is closed and complemented. Finally, the only bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X
that satisfies
x(0)=0
and
x(n)=A(n-1)x(n-1)
for
n\in J
is the zero sequence.

Remark 3
.

Using similar arguments to those in Remark
2
, one can show that in (ii) it is sufficient to assume that
\mathcal{U}
is complemented.

2.1.
Setup

Let
X_{i}=(X_{i},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{i})
,
i=1,2
be arbitrary Hilbert spaces. For simplicity, we will denote both scalar products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Moreover, the norms induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{i}
,
i=1,2
will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|
.
Then,
X_{1}\times X_{2}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the scalar product

\langle(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\rangle:=\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle+\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle.

Instead of considering the norm on
X_{1}\times X_{2}
induced with this scalar product, for us it will be more convenient to work with the equivalent norm given by

\|(x_{1},x_{2})\|:=\max\{\|x_{1}\|,\|x_{2}\|\},\quad(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}.

In this paper, we will consider the system (
2.1
) with the operators
A(n)
having the block triangular form

A(n)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n)&A_{12}(n)\\
0&A_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix},

(2.6)

where
A_{11}(n)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{1})
,
A_{22}(n)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{2})
and
A_{12}(n)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{2},X_{1})
, where
\mathcal{L}(X_{2},X_{1})
denotes the space of all bounded linear operators from
X_{2}
to
X_{1}
. We have the corresponding discrete system

x(n+1)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n)&A_{12}(n)\\
0&A_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix}x(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}.

(2.7)

In this case, to (
2.7
) we associate the corresponding block diagonal system given by

x(n+1)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n)&0\\
0&A_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix}x(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}.

(2.8)

Our goal is to explore the relationship between the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
2.7
) and (
2.8
).

Remark 4
.

Although we focus on systems (
2.7
) of block triangular form which consists of two blocks, using induction, one can extend all the results in our paper to the general case when there are finitely many blocks.

We make the following simple observation.

Proposition 1
.

Let
J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

(
2.8
) admits an exponential dichotomy;

(2)

for
i\in\{1,2\}
, the discrete system

x_{i}(n+1)=A_{ii}(n)x_{i}(n)\quad n\in J^{\prime}

(2.9)

admits an exponential dichotomy.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)
:
We first consider the case when
J=\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose that (
2.1
) admits an exponential dichotomy. Take an arbitrary bounded sequence
(y_{1}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset X_{1}
and set
y(n)=(y_{1}(n),0)\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
(y(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a bounded sequence. By Theorem
1
, there exists a (unique) bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}
such that (
2.2
) holds with

A(n)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n)&0\\
0&A_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix}.

Writing
x(n)=(x_{1}(n),x_{2}(n))
with
x_{i}(n)\in X_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have that

x_{1}(n+1)=A_{11}(n)x_{1}(n)+y_{1}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Moreover, the sequence
(x_{1}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset X_{1}
is bounded. Assume now that
(\tilde{x}_{1}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset X_{1}
is another bounded sequence with the property that

\tilde{x}_{1}(n+1)=A_{11}(n)\tilde{x}_{1}(n)+y_{1}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let
\tilde{x}(n)=(\tilde{x}_{1}(n),0)\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Clearly
(\tilde{x}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}
is a bounded sequence and

\tilde{x}(n+1)=A(n)\tilde{x}(n)+y(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

This implies (using Theorem
1
) that
x(n)=\tilde{x}(n)
, and thus
x_{1}(n)=\tilde{x}_{1}(n)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. By Theorem
1
, we conclude that (
2.9
) admits an exponential dichotomy for
i=1
. The same arguments also give the existence of an exponential dichotomy of (
2.9
) for
i=2
.

We now consider the case
J=\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Proceeding as in the case
J=\mathbb{Z}
, since (
2.8
) admits an exponential dichotomy, for each bounded sequence
(y_{1}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}
there exists a bounded sequence
(x_{1}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}
such that

x_{1}(n+1)=A_{11}(n)x_{1}(n)+y_{1}(n),\quad n\in J.

Let
\Phi_{ii}(m,n)
and
\Phi(m,n)
denote the cocycles associated with (
2.9
) for
i=1,2
and (
2.8
), respectively. Observe that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{S}_{2}
, where

\mathcal{S}=\left\{(v_{1},v_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}:\ \sup_{n\in J}\|\Phi(n,0)(v_{1},v_{2})\|<+\infty\right\}

(2.10)

and

\mathcal{S}_{i}=\left\{v_{i}\in X_{i}:\ \sup_{n\in J}\|\Phi_{ii}(n,0)v_{i}\|<+\infty\right\},\quad i=1,2.

(2.11)

Since (
2.1
) admits an exponential dichotomy, we find that
\mathcal{S}
is closed, which easily implies that
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is closed. Since
X_{1}
is a Hilbert space, we see that
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is complemented.
Then Theorem
2
implies that (
2.9
) admits an exponential dichotomy for
i=1
. Analogously, (
2.9
) admits an exponential dichotomy for
i=2
.

Finally, we treat the case
J=\mathbb{Z}^{-}
. Again, proceeding as in case
J=\mathbb{Z}
, we find that for each bounded sequence
(y_{1}(n))_{n\in J^{\prime}}\subset X_{1}
, there exists a bounded sequence
(x_{1}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}
satisfying

x_{1}(n+1)=A_{11}(n)x_{1}(n)+y_{1}(n),\quad n\in J^{\prime}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{U}=U_{1}\times\mathcal{U}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the subspace of
X_{1}\times X_{2}
consisting of all
(v_{1},v_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
for which there is a bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}
with
x(0)=(v_{1},v_{2})
and

x(n)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}(n-1)&0\\
0&A_{22}(n-1)\end{pmatrix}x(n-1)\quad n\in J,

(2.12)

and
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is the subspace of
X_{i}
consisting of all
v_{i}\in X_{i}
for which there is a bounded sequence
(x_{i}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{i}
with
x_{i}(0)=v_{i}
and
x_{i}(n)=A_{ii}(n-1)x_{i}(n-1)
for
n\in J
for
i=1,2
. Since (
2.8
) admits an exponential dichotomy, we have that
\mathcal{U}
is closed (see Theorem
3
).
Hence,
\mathcal{U}_{1}
is also closed and thus complemented (as
X_{1}
is a Hilbert space). Finally, we take a bounded sequence
(x_{1}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}
such that
x_{1}(0)=0
and
x_{1}(n)=A_{11}(n-1)x_{1}(n-1)
for
n\in J
. Set
x(n):=(x_{1}(n),0)\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
for
n\in J
. Note that
(x(n))_{n\in J}
is bounded,
x(0)=0
and (
2.12
) holds. Hence,
x(n)=0
for
n\in J
which implies that
x_{1}(n)=0
for
n\in J
.
Using Theorem
3
we conclude that (
2.9
) admits an exponential dichotomy for
i=1
. Similarly, one can deal with the case
i=2
.

(2)\implies(1)
: If (
2.9
) admits an exponential dichotomy on
J
with projections
P_{i}(n)
,
n\in J
for
i=1,2
, it is straightforward to verify that (
2.1
) admits an exponential dichotomy with respect to projections

P(n)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}(n)&0\\
0&P_{2}(n)\end{pmatrix},\quad n\in J.

3.
From dichotomy of block diagonal systems to dichotomy of block triangular systems

In this section, we explore the conditions under which the exponential dichotomy of the block-diagonal system (
2.8
) implies the existence of an exponential dichotomy for the block-triangular system (
2.7
).

The proof of the following result is essentially identical to the proof of
[
9
, Theorem 4]
(in the case
J=\mathbb{Z}
). We include it for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 4
.

Let
J=\mathbb{Z}
.
Suppose that (
2.8
) admits an exponential dichotomy and that

\sup_{n\in J}\|A_{12}(n)\|<+\infty.

(3.1)

Then, the system (
2.7
) admits an exponential dichotomy.

Proof.

Take an arbitrary bounded sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}
, and write
y(n)=(y_{1}(n),y_{2}(n))
with
y_{i}(n)\in X_{i}
for
n\in J
and
i=1,2
.
Clearly,
(y_{1}(n))_{n\in J}
and
(y_{2}(n))_{n\in J}
are also bounded sequences. Since the system (
2.9
) for
i=2
admits an exponential dichotomy (see Proposition
1
), it follows from Theorem
1
that there exists a (unique) bounded sequence
(x_{2}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{2}
such that

x_{2}(n+1)=A_{22}(n)x_{2}(n)+y_{2}(n)\quad n\in J.

(3.2)

Moreover, it follows from (
3.1
) that
(A_{12}(n)x_{2}(n))_{n\in J}
is a bounded sequence. Hence, by applying Theorem
1
to system (
2.9
) with
i=1
, we conclude that there exists a
(unique) bounded sequence
(x_{1}(n))_{n\in J}
in
X_{1}
satisfying

x_{1}(n+1)=A_{11}(n)x_{1}(n)+A_{12}(n)x_{2}(n)+y_{1}(n)\quad n\in J.

(3.3)

Then

x(n):=(x_{1}(n),x_{2}(n))\in X_{1}\times X_{2},\quad n\in J,

is a bounded solution of (
2.2
), where

is as in (
2.6
).

It remains to establish the uniqueness. Suppose that

is another bounded solution of (

). Then, writing
\tilde{x}(n)=(\tilde{x}_{1}(n),\tilde{x}_{2}(n))
with

for

\begin{split}(x_{1}-\tilde{x}_{1})(n+1)&=A_{11}(n)(x_{1}-\tilde{x}_{1})(n)+A_{12}(n)(x_{2}-\tilde{x}_{2})(n)\\
(x_{2}-\tilde{x}_{2})(n+1)&=A_{22}(n)(x_{2}-\tilde{x}_{2})(n),\end{split}

for

Since (

for

for

.

∎

and

Theorem 5
.

Let
J\in\{\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
. If (

Proof.

Let

The proof for the case

is similar. As in the proof of the previous theorem, we conclude that for each bounded sequence
(y(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}

(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}
in
X_{1}\times X_{2}
satisfying (

as in (

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}:\ \sup_{n\in J}\|\Phi(n,0)(x_{1},x_{2})\|<+\infty\right\}

is a closed (and complemented) subspace of

denotes the cocycle associated with (

((x_{1}(n),x_{2}(n)))_{n\in J}\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
is a bounded solution of (

) if and only if
x_{2}(n)=\Phi_{22}(n,0)\eta
and

\begin{split}x_{1}(n)&=\Phi_{11}(n,0)\xi+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Phi_{11}(n,k)P_{11}(k)A_{12}(k-1)x_{2}(k-1)\\
&\phantom{=}-\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\Phi_{11}(n,k)(\text{\rm Id}_{1}-P_{11}(k))A_{12}(k-1)x_{2}(k-1),\end{split}

for
n\in J
with

and

, where

,

. Taking the above into account, we conclude that

4.
From exponential dichotomy of block triangular systems to exponential dichotomy of block diagonal systems

We are now interested in formulating conditions under which the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
2.7
) implies the existence of an exponential dichotomy of (
2.8
). The following examples will illustrate that the situation is much more delicate than in the finite-dimensional case.

Example 1
.

Let
\ell_{2}
denote the space of all one-sided sequences
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|z_{n}|^{2}<+\infty
, and recall that
\ell_{2}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the scalar product

\langle(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots),(w_{0},w_{1},\ldots)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z_{n}\overline{w}_{n}.

Let
U\colon\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}
be the unilateral shift operator defined by

U(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,)=(0,z_{0},z_{1},\ldots),\quad(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in\ell_{2}.

Finally, we consider
C\colon\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}
given by

C(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,)=(x_{0},0,0,\ldots),\quad(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in\ell_{2}.

Observe that
U
and
C
are bounded linear operators.
Then, we have (see
[
12
, Example 3]
)

\sigma\left(\begin{pmatrix}U&C\\
0&U^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right)\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ |z|=1\},

and

\sigma(U)=\sigma(U^{*})=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ |z|\leq 1\}.

We take an arbitrary
\lambda>1
and set

A_{11}=A_{11}(n)=\lambda U,\quad A_{22}=A_{22}(n)=\lambda U^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{12}=A_{12}(n)=\lambda C,

for
n\in J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z}^{-}\}
.
Then, (
2.7
) admits an exponential dichotomy, since the spectrum of
A=A(n)
as in (
2.6
) is disjoint from the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
,
while (
2.9
) does not admit an exponential dichotomy for
i=1,2
as the spectra of
A_{ii}
intersect the unit circle.

This example shows that the existence of an exponential dichotomy of (
2.7
) in general does not imply the existence of an exponential dichotomy of (
2.8
) even in the autonomous case.

Next, we provide a similar example in which the operators
A_{ii}=A_{ii}(n)
are invertible for
i=1,2
. This example was communicated to us by Nikolaos Chalmoukis using MathOverflow.

Example 2
.

We consider the space
X=L^{2}(\partial A_{r},|dz|)
of square-integrable functions with respect to the arc length, where
A_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:r^{-1}<|z|<r\}
is the annulus for some
r>1
. Let
H^{2}(\partial A_{r})
be the Hardy space of the annulus, i.e. the closure of the span of the set of functions
\{z\mapsto z^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. We consider
A\colon X\to X
to be the multiplication operator by
z
, that is,

Af(z)=zf(z),\text{ for every }f\in X.

It is well known that
\sigma(A)=\partial A_{r}
. Since
H^{2}(\partial A_{r})
is
A
-invariant, we can decompose
A
into the following block upper triangular form with respect to the splitting
X=H^{2}(\partial A_{r})\oplus H^{2}(\partial A_{r})^{\perp}
:

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

It is not difficult to show that
\sigma(A_{11})=\sigma(A_{22})=\overline{A_{r}}
(see
[
6
]
for details).

We now consider (
2.7
) with
A(n)=A
for
n\in J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{-},\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
. Since
\sigma(A_{11})=\sigma(A_{22})=\overline{A_{r}}
we find that (
2.9
) does not admit an exponential dichotomy for
i=1,2
(as
\overline{A_{r}}
intersects the unit circle in the complex plane), while (
2.7
) admits an exponential dichotomy (since
\sigma(A)=\partial A_{r}
does not intersect the unit circle in the complex plane).

Despite the above examples, we will formulate a series of results that give additional conditions under which the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
2.7
) implies the existence of an exponential dichotomy for (
2.8
).

4.1.
Dichotomies on
\mathbb{Z}

We begin with the following result whose proof is identical to the proof of
[
9
, Theorem 6]
. It is included for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 6
.

Let
J=\mathbb{Z}
and suppose that (

) admits an exponential dichotomy. Then, (

) admits an exponential dichotomy if and only if (

) admits no nonzero bounded solutions for

Proof.

The necessity is obvious.
For sufficiency,
using Proposition

(y_{2}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{2}

for

. Then

is a bounded sequence. Since (

there exists a (unique) bounded sequence

) holds with

as in (

Moreover,
(x_{2}(n))_{n\in J}
is bounded. Hence, since (
2.9
) for
i=2
does not admit nonzero bounded solutions, we conclude (using Theorem
1
) that it does admit an exponential dichotomy.

We now turn to (
2.9
) for
i=1
.
Take an arbitrary bounded sequence
(y_{1}(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}
. Set
y(n):=(y_{1}(n),0)\in X_{1}\times X_{2}
for
n\in J
. Then
(y(n))_{n\in J}
is a bounded sequence. Since (
2.7
) admits an exponential dichotomy, there exists a (unique) bounded sequence
(x(n))_{n\in J}\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}

).

and

(4.2)

for

. Since

is bounded, (

) together with our assumption that (

) for

for

. Hence, (

where the sequence

is bounded. Suppose that there exists another bounded sequence

such that

Since (

) admits an exponential dichotomy, we conclude that

, we conclude that (

∎

Theorem 7

(3)

(

where

(1)

\mathcal{R}P(0)=\mathcal{S}

) .

We have

B_{21}(n)&B_{22}(n)\end{pmatrix},

where

B_{11}(n)=A_{11}(-n-1)^{*},\ B_{21}(n)=A_{12}(-n-1)^{*}\ \text{and}\ B_{22}(n)=A_{22}(-n-1)^{*},

for

Lemma 4
.

The system (
4.10
) admits an exponential dichotomy with respect to projections

\tilde{P}(n):=P(-n)^{*},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}.

Proof of the lemma.

Set

\bar{\Phi}(m,n):=\Phi(n,m)^{*}\quad\text{for $m,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ with $m\leq n$.}

Then, the following can be verified by using the same arguments as in
[

(see also
[

, p.229]

•

\bar{\Phi}(m,n)P(n)^{*}=P(m)^{*}\bar{\Phi}(m,n)
for
0\leq m\leq n
;

•

\bar{\Phi}(m,n)\rvert_{\mathcal{N}P(n)^{*}}\colon\mathcal{N}P(n)^{*}\to\mathcal{N}P(m)^{*}
is invertible for
0\leq m\leq n
;

•

for
0\leq m\leq n

\|\bar{\Phi}(m,n)P(n)^{*}\|\leq De^{-\lambda(n-m)};

(4.11)

•

for
m\geq n\geq 0

\|\bar{\Phi}(m,n)(\text{\rm Id}-P(n)^{*})\|\leq De^{-\lambda(m-n)},

where

\bar{\Phi}(m,n):=\left(\bar{\Phi}(n,m)\rvert_{\mathcal{N}P(m)^{*}}\right)^{-1}\quad m\geq n\geq 0,

and

are constants associated with the exponential dichotomy of (

Let

denote the cocycle associated to (

\Phi_{B}(m,n)=\Phi(-n,-m)^{*}=\bar{\Phi}(-m,-n),\quad 0\geq m\geq n.

Consequently, for

\begin{split}\tilde{P}(m)\Phi_{B}(m,n)=P(-m)^{*}\bar{\Phi}(-m,-n)&=\bar{\Phi}(-m,-n)P(-n)^{*}\\
&=\Phi_{B}(m,n)\tilde{P}(n).\end{split}

Furthermore,
\Phi_{B}(m,n)\rvert_{\Ker\tilde{P}(n)}=\bar{\Phi}(-m,-n)\rvert_{\Ker P(-n)^{*}}
is invertible for

.
Next, note that (

\|\Phi_{B}(m,n)\tilde{P}(n)\|=\|\bar{\Phi}(-m,-n)P(-n)^{*}\|\leq De^{-\lambda(-n-(-m))}=De^{-\lambda(m-n)},

\|\Phi_{B}(m,n)(\text{\rm Id}-\tilde{P}(n))\|\leq De^{-\lambda(n-m)},\quad\text{for $m\leq n\leq 0$.}

∎

In the same manner, for

\tilde{P}_{ii}(n)=P_{ii}(-n)^{*},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-},\ i=1,2.

Lemma 5
.

We have

\dim\mathcal{N}\tilde{P}(0)=\dim\mathcal{N}P(0).

Proof of the lemma.

In the same manner,
