Techniques for learning and transferring knowledge for microbiome-based classification and prediction: review and assessment
Authors: Jin Han, Haohong Zhang, Kang Ning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe volume of microbiome data is growing at an exponential rate, and the current methodologies for big data mining are encountering substantial obstacles. Effectively managing and extracting valuable insights from these vast microbiome datasets has emerged as a significant challenge in the field of contemporary microbiome research. This comprehensive review delves into the utilization of foundation models and transfer learning techniques within the context of microbiome-based classification and prediction tasks, advocating for a transition away from traditional task-specific or scenario-specific models towards more adaptable, continuous learning models. The article underscores the practicality and benefits of initially constructing a robust foundation model, which can then be fine-tuned using transfer learning to tackle specific context tasks. In real-world scenarios, the application of transfer learning empowers models to leverage disease-related data from one geographical area and enhance diagnostic precision in different regions. This transition from relying on "good models" to embracing "adaptive models" resonates with the philosophy of “teaching a man to fish” thereby paving the way for advancements in personalized medicine and accurate diagnosis. Empirical research suggests that the integration of foundation models with transfer learning methodologies substantially boosts the performance of models when dealing with large-scale and diverse microbiome datasets, effectively mitigating the challenges posed by data heterogeneity.

Introduction

A substantial number of microbiome samples have been meticulously collected from various environments and subjects, ranging from humans to soil and oceans. These samples have undergone advanced sequencing techniques, which have allowed scientists to decipher the genetic material within them. As a result, there has been a rapidly growing accumulation of microbiome data, creating an extensive repository of information that holds the potential to revolutionize our understanding of microbial communities and their roles in health and disease. This ever-expanding dataset provides valuable insights into the diversity, functions, and interactions of microorganisms, paving the way for groundbreaking discoveries in fields such as medicine, agriculture, and environmental science.

Currently, millions of these samples and their sequencing data are stored in public databases, such as EBI MGnify [1], QIITA [2], and NCBI SRA [3], along with metadata, including environmental variables and host characteristics [4] (Fig. 1). These samples have been obtained from over 500 distinct niches worldwide, encompassing host-associated biomes, such as the human gut, environmental biomes, like soil and freshwater, and engineered biomes, including fermenters [1].

Specifically, in 2007, the National Institutes of Health initiated the Human Microbiome Project (HMP) as an extension of the Human Genome Project. The HMP aimed to explore how alterations in microbial communities, both within and outside the human body, influence human health through a metagenomic approach. The project consisted of two phases: HMP1, which characterized microbial communities from various body sites in 300 healthy individuals, and HMP2 (also known as the integrative HMP [iHMP]), which conducted longitudinal studies on the maternal vaginal microbiome related to pregnancy and preterm birth, the intestinal microbiome in inflammatory bowel disease, and the nasal and intestinal microbiomes in pre-diabetes. The sequencing and multi-omics data (Fig. 2), along with clinical information and tools from both phases, are accessible through the HMP Data Coordination Center and other public or controlled-access repositories, providing valuable resources for researchers [5, 6].

Large metagenomic research initiatives have contributed substantial data resources for microbiome research, including the MetaHIT program of the European Union [7], the Earth Microbiome Project [8], the American Gut Project [9], the My New Gut project [10], and the Human Microbiome Program of the Chinese Academy of Sciences [11]. These efforts have led to the deposition of extensive datasets in various databases, such as the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) [12], EBI Metagenomics [13], and the National Microbiome Data Center [14]. Additionally, specialized databases related to the human microbiome include the Human Pan-Microbe Communities database [15], the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD) [16], the Expanded HOMD (eHOMD) [17], and the Human Gut Microbial Database [18].

These datasets have already established solid foundations for microbiome-based studies, even if they are not yet complete.

Microbiome-based classification and prediction

Analyzing microbiome samples can uncover critical patterns within microbiome data, which are essential for microbiome-based classification and prediction [19, 20]. This research area has a broad scope, encompassing applications such as age prediction [21–24], health status assessment [25–32], and microbial source tracking [33–37] (Supplementary Table 1). These specific predictions require a large number of microbiome samples and sophisticated methods to extract meaningful patterns for predictive purposes [38].

Microbial communities are generally shaped by a combination of stochastic and deterministic processes [39]. For example, the gut microbiome of infants develops in a predictable manner, beginning with pioneer species colonizing the gut after birth, followed by an ordered succession of microbes. Factors such as birth mode, feeding practices, and the infant's genetics also influence this process [40–42]. Although microbial communities are sensitive to environmental factors and microbial invasions, accounting for all possible environmental variables to quantify the processes driving community changes remains challenging [43]. Therefore, while the assembly of microbial communities is predictable, the accuracy of microbiome-based classification and prediction is highly context-dependent [44].

Current microbiome-based classification and prediction methods are not yet highly accurate due to limitations in big data mining techniques [45]. Traditionally, microbiome data mining has relied on basic statistical correlation analyses. For instance, Pearson's correlation coefficient is commonly used to assess linear relationships between variables [46]. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient is employed for monotonic relationships, particularly with non-normal data distributions [47]. Techniques such as Principal Component Analysis are often utilized for dimensionality reduction and visualization, helping to identify differences and similarities among samples [48]. However, these traditional methods face limitations when applied to complex, large-scale microbiome data, particularly in handling non-linear and high-dimensional datasets. In Microbial Source Tracking (MST), Bayesian methods estimate the contributions of different sources to a target sample using probabilistic models. However, these methods are computationally intensive and can slow down significantly with large-scale data [36]. They are also heavily dependent on model assumptions, which, if inaccurate, can compromise the reliability of the results [49]. Similarly, Maximum Likelihood Estimation is used to estimate model parameters by maximizing a likelihood function but is sensitive to initial parameter estimates and is computationally demanding with high-dimensional data [50]. Expectation–maximization algorithms iteratively optimize to estimate the mixing proportions of potential sources in a sample [36]. Although effective with large-scale data, these algorithms depend on accurate initial parameter estimates and can get stuck in local optima. They also require high-quality data, as noise or missing data can lead to significant inaccuracies.

In contrast, artificial intelligence (AI) approaches are well-suited for large-scale biomedical big-data mining, including microbiome data mining. Theoretically, AI has the potential to excel in the realm of microbiome-based classification and prediction by constructing foundation models that are grounded in a vast array of microbiome samples. These foundation models can tap into the latent patterns embedded within the data, harnessing them for the purpose of making accurate predictions. In the subsequent sections of this document, we will begin by introducing two AI techniques that are highly pertinent to the field of microbiome data mining: foundation models and transfer learning. Following this, we will elucidate the process of how knowledge can be learned and transferred to facilitate microbiome-based predictions. Additionally, we will present the outcomes of an evaluation that examines the influence spheres of these models and their resilience to batch effects. Collectively, our findings underscore the efficacy of foundation models and transfer learning techniques in enabling precise and expedient microbiome-based classification and prediction, showcasing their extensive applicability across various domains.

Foundation models and context-aware learning

Foundation models, machine learning models with large-scale parameters and complex structures, are widely used artificial intelligence methods in biomedical data analysis [51–53]. Essentially, foundation models rely on training data to learn and enhance their accuracy progressively. Once these learning algorithms are fine-tuned, they become powerful tools in computer science and artificial intelligence, enabling the high-velocity classification and clustering of data [54–56].

Foundation models have found a variety of applications in biomedical research, including microbiome data mining [20, 57]. For example, neural networks have been employed to associate drugs with gene expressions in human diseases [58], and have shown effectiveness in studying antibiotic resistance genes in bacteria [59]. Foundation models are also widely used for microbiome-based host phenotype classification [60], drug response prediction [61], and the discovery of cancer biomarkers [62, 63]. Host phenotype classification mainly focuses on age prediction [24], cancer prediction [31], and disease condition prediction [27]. For drug response prediction, Maryam et al. utilized graph neural network (GNN) models to screen drugs influenced by the gut microbiome, contributing to the optimization of drug dosage and formulation [64]. Jiangsheng Pi et al. developed MDGNN, a deep learning algorithm based on graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to predict microbe-drug associations, successfully identifying two drugs associated with SARS-CoV-2 [61]. In cancer biomarker discovery, projects such as WSGB (Weight Signed Graph Neural Network for Microbial Biomarkers) [62] and IMOVNN (Incomplete Multi-Omics Data Integration Variational Neural Networks) [63] have been developed.

In these applications, foundation models have demonstrated superior performance in handling complex, high-dimensional data compared to general models. For instance, the automatic feature learning capabilities of neural networks enable rapid adaptation and effective solutions when addressing new data types or biomedical challenges [65]. However, the inherent complexity and computational nature of foundation models make them operate as black boxes, and achieving high performance typically demands significant computational power and extensive datasets, which presents challenges for practical use. Transfer learning can help mitigate issues related to limited training data and the lack of interpretability in foundation models, such as neural networks and random forests when applied to specific tasks.

Context-aware learning or transfer learning focuses on storing knowledge gained while solving one problem and applying it to a different but related problem [66]. From a practical standpoint, reusing or transferring information from previously learned tasks to the learning of new tasks has the potential to significantly improve performance [67]. In knowledge engineering, Sinno Jialin Pan and Qiang Yang cite three specific examples to support this claim: web document classification, indoor WiFi localization problems, and sentiment classification [67]. In the biomedical field, there are many similar scenarios, such as medical image analysis [68], omics data mining [69, 70], biomedical natural language processing [71], and drug response prediction [72]. These scenarios share the common characteristic of having a large amount of background data but a limited amount of data and data labels for the target task. In such cases, the model's classification and prediction performance is notably enhanced through transfer learning.

Common forms of transfer learning include instance-based transfer, feature-based transfer, and parameter-based transfer. Instance-based transfer selects instances from the source context that are useful for the target context, primarily using techniques such as instance weight adjustment and importance sampling. Feature-based transfer can be divided into two types: feature selection-based transfer, which uses common features between source and target contexts for knowledge transfer, and feature mapping-based transfer, which maps data from the original feature space to a new feature space, thereby making better use of existing labeled data samples for training. Parameter-based transfer assumes that correlation models between the source and target contexts share some parameters or prior distributions, and it adopts a pre-training plus fine-tuning approach. This form of transfer learning is heavily used in current deep learning practices [67]. Selecting the appropriate form of transfer learning for different scenarios and tasks is crucial to achieving optimal results and avoiding negative transfer.

Discovery cohort, validation cohort, and potential fine-tuning cohort

In the realm of machine learning and data analysis, the utilization of discovery cohorts, validation cohorts, and fine-tuning cohorts plays a crucial role at various stages of the Target Context NN Model (TNM) construction process (Fig. 3a-b). Discovery cohorts are instrumental in the initial phase of model training, where they are employed to identify and extract meaningful patterns and relationships within the data. These cohorts serve as the foundational dataset that helps establish the model’s preliminary structure, allowing it to grasp the underlying principles of the information it is processing.

Moving forward, validation cohorts take center stage in the evaluation of the model's performance. Once the model has been initially trained using the discovery cohorts, it is essential to test its capabilities and accuracy. Validation cohorts, consisting of new, unseen datasets, allow researchers to evaluate how well the model generalizes. This step is critical in assessing the model's robustness and its ability to make accurate predictions or classifications.

Finally, fine-tuning cohorts are employed to further optimize and adjust the model's parameters. After the initial training and validation phases, refining the model is often necessary to better suit specific task scenarios or improve performance in certain areas. Fine-tuning cohorts provide a specialized dataset to make these adjustments, ensuring the model is finely tuned to meet the specific requirements of its intended application. This step is particularly important in enhancing the model's accuracy and reliability in real-world scenarios.

For example, in medical practice, diagnoses vary across geographic regions and historical periods. This framework is applicable for predicting epidemic diseases at the population level, such as trends in COVID-19 [73] and dengue fever outbreaks [74]. In cross-temporal transfer learning scenarios—such as the long-term management of chronic diseases, including lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease—fine-tuning enhances diagnostic accuracy over time by applying features and patterns derived from historical data to new data collected at different time points [75].

By properly dividing and utilizing these cohorts—discovery cohorts for initial training and pattern identification, validation cohorts for performance evaluation, and fine-tuning cohorts for optimization and parameter adjustment—the model training process becomes more comprehensive and effective. This approach not only reduces the risk of overfitting, where the model performs well on training data but fails to generalize to new data, but it also ensures the model is optimized for its specific application context. This meticulous process enhances the accuracy and robustness of the model, leading to more reliable and effective outcomes in various machine learning and data analysis applications.

Learning and transferring knowledge for microbiome-based classification and prediction

For microbiome-based classification and prediction, the acquisition of knowledge in source and target contexts is primarily achieved through foundation models, such as neural networks and random forests [76]. The transfer of existing models or background knowledge between these contexts relies on transfer learning methods (Fig. 3b-c). For example, in the source tracking of microbiome samples, neural network models and random forest models can help develop accurate source tracking models within specific biomes [33], while transfer learning enables the model to learn and adapt to source tracking in different biomes [77, 78] (Fig. 3c).

Transfer learning is also applicable when dealing with heterogeneity in clinical microbiome data. In actual biological or clinical applications, the data produced are usually highly task-specific. Consequently, the data from a single study are often very limited [79] or unbalanced [80]. To effectively train the model, it is usually necessary to combine multiple batches of microbiome data; however, batch effects can significantly compromise the reliability of the results. Currently, there are two primary strategies for addressing batch effects in microbiomes: accounting for batch effects and correcting for batch effects [81]. Methods that account for batch effects include differential abundance analysis [81], surrogate variable analysis [82, 83], and removing unwanted variation [84]. Methods that correct batch effects include batch mean centering [83], ComBat [83, 85], and FAbatch [86]. Despite the utility of these methods, they cannot entirely eliminate batch effects. In contrast, transfer learning is an alternative approach that can significantly reduce batch effects, particularly when combined with existing methods for addressing these issues.

By integrating the foundation model with transfer learning, the model can acquire new knowledge while transferring existing knowledge, thereby enabling continuous learning. In studies focusing on time-series microbiome-based disease prediction, a Recurrent Neural Network model is utilized to learn time-series microbiome data from a source task to identify microbial patterns associated with specific health states. Transfer learning is subsequently used to finely tune the model with data from the target task, significantly enhancing the model's prediction performance on new datasets [87]. In pathogenic microbial identification, the DCiPatho (deep cross-fusion networks for genome-scale identification of pathogens) accurately identifies various pathogenic bacteria infecting humans, animals, and plants. After fine-tuning, the average prediction accuracy of the DCiPatho model, which was pre-trained on the genome-wide BacRefSeq-based dataset, increased from 62.31% to 95.45% for meta-genome-assembled genomes derived from the intestinal microbiomes of hospitalized adults [88]. Moreover, EXPERT is a context-aware MST method that integrates an adaptive neural network and a transfer learning framework. The adaptive neural network constructs models by learning from knowledge in a given MST task, while transfer learning leverages the knowledge of existing MST models (base models) and adapts the foundation model to the biome ontology under a given context. EXPERT has been widely applied in MST research, such as describing subtle changes in infant gut microbial communities during the first year of life, measuring disease-specific patterns of the gut microbiome in liver cirrhosis and irritable bowel syndrome, and tracking the progression of gut microbial communities at different stages of colorectal cancer (CRC) [89].

Paradigm shift in model development of microbiome-based disease diagnosis

Conventional diagnostic prediction models are specifically designed for particular tasks. For instance, the GMWI2 model harnesses extensive genomic data derived from the gut microbiome to identify early signs of potential health alterations [90]. However, this model is limited in its capabilities, serving exclusively as an early warning system that alerts users to possible changes in gut health. It lacks the ability to pinpoint and diagnose particular diseases, thereby offering a broad overview rather than detailed medical insights. DeepMicro integrates advanced deep representation learning techniques to effectively reduce the dimensionality of high-dimensional microbiome data and employs machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests, and Multi-Layer Perceptrons to classify the low-dimensional representations [25]. Nevertheless, DeepMicro specializes in microbiome-based disease prediction using high-dimensional microbiome data. PopPhy–convolutional neural network (CNN) combines a microbiome relative abundance matrix with a phylogenetic tree, using a CNN to capture complex relationships and subtle patterns among microorganisms, thereby achieving excellent performance in disease prediction tasks [91]. On the other hand, PopPhy-CNN is primarily employed to analyze genetic relationships and construct phylogenetic trees among different populations. This task-specific limitation of the models arises partly from their exclusive design goals and partly from the constraints imposed by fixed parameters.

Additionally, although many state-of-the-art models have demonstrated excellent performance in specific task scenarios (Table 1), fixed model parameters often lead to significant diagnostic errors when task scenarios change. In recent years, microbiome-based disease prediction models, such as Microbiome Differentiable Interpretable Temporal Rule Engine (MDITRE) [92] and Multimodal Variational Information Bottleneck (MVIB) [93], have emerged. These models are primarily constructed on the foundations and theories of traditional machine learning models, synthesizing various machine learning methods. For example, MDITRE employs a transformer model with attention mechanisms, integrating regularization and embedding techniques to effectively manage temporal microbiome sequences. MVIB, grounded in information bottleneck theory, combines deep neural networks with the variational autoencoder architecture while employing multimodal data fusion techniques to analyze diverse data types, including species relative abundance, strain-level markers, and metabolomics data. However, when the training and testing datasets are drawn from different contexts, these state-of-the-art models may fail to achieve satisfactory accuracy (Table 1). This limitation stems from fixing the model's parameters during training, which leads to an over-reliance on the training data. Consequently, these parameters may not adapt well to test datasets from different contexts, potentially resulting in diminished performance on test or real-world datasets and reducing the model's effectiveness in managing variability and noise [25, 91–96].

SOTA model for microbiome-based disease prediction.

Microbiome data often vary across individuals, time, and environments. Compared to scenario-specific or task-specific models, continual learning models are better equipped to capture these variations through continuous learning and adaptive capabilities, providing more accurate and personalized predictions in disease diagnosis. GDmicro significantly improves model transferability across different datasets by integrating Deep Adaptation Networks (DANs) with GCNs [97]. While DANs are feature-based in transfer learning, our proposed method emphasizes fine-tuning cohorts, focusing more on parameter-based transfer learning. Among the state-of-the-art models listed in Table 1, MVIB has demonstrated significantly better performance than randomly initialized models on the Chinese type 2 diabetes, obesity, and hypertension datasets after finely tuning [93]. Related studies from our lab include DeepMicroCancer, a model developed for diagnosing various cancer types. This model builds on the random forest framework and employs parameter-based transfer learning to achieve cross-tissue predictions across twenty cancer datasets, achieving high diagnostic accuracy with blood samples [98]. Furthermore, a new machine learning framework based on the EXPERT transfer learning framework [89] has been proposed, combining deep neural networks with parameter-based transfer learning. This model is designed for microbiome-based cross-region diagnosis and effectively reduces the regional impact on diagnosis accuracy [69]. The DeepGum model, developed by U. Gülfem Elgün Çiftcioğl and O. Ufuk Nalbanoglu, also follows the new paradigm. It was trained on a merged dataset comprising nine distinct datasets related to gastrointestinal diseases from the ENA [12] and subsequently fine-tuned on six small-sample datasets: IBD-I, IBD-II, IBD-III, CRC-I, CRC-II, and CRC-III. This approach effectively addresses the small sample size–large dimension problem [99]. In the study by Quintin Pope et al., a model utilizing the transformer framework and self-supervised learning was developed. After pretraining on large, unlabeled datasets through self-supervised learning, the model was fine-tuned on an Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) dataset. The fine-tuned model demonstrated superior performance in predicting IBD based on the microbiome. Compared to traditional machine learning methods, such as SVMs and Random Forests, the model showed stronger generalization and higher prediction accuracy. Furthermore, it aids researchers in identifying microbial biomarkers related to IBD, offering new insights for early prediction and personalized treatment of IBD [100]. These findings underscore the feasibility and significant potential of new modeling paradigms in medical practice.

Performance evaluation of novel paradigm models

To more accurately measure the extent of improvement in the overall performance of a model before and after transfer learning, and to evaluate the model's adaptability to the target task following the paradigm shift, we introduce the as a metric for assessing the model's overall performance. This index is derived by integrating various traditional performance metrics, including the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) and the F1 score (F1):

In the formula, ε is a small constant with a value of 0.001, used to prevent the impact of zero values. AUC effectively measures a classification model's ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples across various thresholds. The F1 score, derived from the harmonic mean of precision and recall, captures the model's overall performance in classifying positive samples. The Mixed average of AUC and F1 provides a comprehensive assessment of the model's overall classification performance, and the introduction of ε further enhances the stability of the calculation. The value ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better overall model performance. If the FitIdx of the model increases following the paradigm shift, it indicates that the model has become better adapted to the target task.

To evaluate the impact of fine-tuning cohort utilization and transfer learning on model performance, we utilized the genus-level categorical data from the Guangdong Gut Microbiome Project (GGMP) [101]. We first excluded samples with multiple concurrent diseases, retaining only those with a single disease or classified as healthy. The data was then split into 14 city-specific batches, and diseases with fewer than four samples in each batch were removed. Using a random forest model and transfer learning methods from DeepMicroCancer [98, 102], we predicted samples from each city. The validation cohort for each city was selected via Stratified Shuffle Split, representing 50% of the city’s total samples. The fine-tuning cohort was also selected by Stratified Shuffle Split, ranging from 10% to 90% of the remaining samples. The discovery cohort was selected from the samples of the remaining 13 cities and the fine-tuning cohort, following four strategies: no transfer, inner, boost, and transfer (Fig. 4).

When comparing the overall performance of the "Inner" method's model to that of the "No Transfer" method, it becomes clear that the "Inner" method consistently outperforms the "No Transfer" method at every fine-tuning ratio, even though the training set for the "No Transfer" method contains a larger volume of data. This observation underscores a crucial point: when dealing with sample sizes that are within the order of magnitude of 10 to 100, the quality of the samples is significantly more important than the quantity of the samples. Theoretically, one would expect that the "Boost" method's model performance would surpass that of the "No Transfer" method. Nonetheless, the empirical results fell short of these theoretical expectations. This discrepancy may be attributed to the introduction of noise by the fine-tuned dataset, similar to the situation observed with AUC values (Fig. 4), where the detrimental impact of noise outweighs the beneficial contribution of the effective samples.

In summary, the FitIdx is a reasonable and accurate indicator for assessing the overall performance of the model. It provides valuable insights into the efficacy of different techniques, such as transfer learning and fine-tuning, highlighting the importance of sample quality over quantity for optimal model performance.

It is crucial to emphasize that when addressing multiclass classification challenges, AUC and F1 score can be computed using multiple approaches, including macro, micro, and weighted averages, each with distinct characteristics that may yield different results, particularly with imbalanced data. In Equation (5.1.1), it is essential to ensure the same method for both AUC and F1 scores to maintain consistency and comparability of the results. If different methods are employed, the outcomes may become ambiguous and difficult to interpret. Therefore, selecting the most appropriate calculation method based on sample distribution and research goals is vital to accurately reflect model performance and align with study objectives.

When continuous learning models are integrated into medical practice, traditional evaluation metrics such as accuracy, AUC, and F1 score, frequently fall short of comprehensively reflecting the model's real-world applicability, especially when considering the dynamic and complex nature of medical environments [104].

To address this limitation, it is imperative to introduce multidimensional evaluation methods. These methods should encompass a thorough assessment of how the model performs across diverse populations, various stages of diseases, and its proficiency in managing rare cases. Such comprehensive assessments help pinpoint any potential shortcomings or limitations of the model when deployed in real-world medical settings. This, in turn, can guide necessary improvements to enhance the model's effectiveness and reliability (Fig. 6d).

Moreover, in the medical field, where decisions can have profound implications, the importance of model interpretability and transparency cannot be overstated. Medical professionals must understand how a model arrives at its predictions to build trust and ensure accountability. Therefore, new evaluation strategies should be developed to specifically measure how effectively models can interpret and explain their predictions. For example, these strategies could involve assessing whether models accurately identify the key features influencing predictions and evaluate the medical plausibility and relevance of those features. From a good doctor to a continuous learning doctor

“Teaching a man to fish is better than giving him a fish” and enhancing the transferability of models is the ultimate objective in the paradigm shift from Good Models to Adaptive Models. Two primary methods exist for enhancing the transferability of models. The first approach is to increase data diversity and perform appropriate data preprocessing to improve the model's generalization capabilities, thereby indirectly enhancing its performance in new environments [106]. The second approach involves directly enhancing transferability through transfer learning, leveraging the knowledge of existing models in new tasks. Our proposed method adopts the latter approach.

It is worth noting that the performance of transfer learning heavily depends on the source model and the samples in the target context [107]. This dependency arises because the target model, obtained through transfer learning, retains a portion of the source model’s parameters, while the target samples determine the fine-tuning cohort, impacting the fine-tuning process. Therefore, we can enhance the performance of transfer learning from two perspectives: improving the performance of the source model and optimizing the training datasets.

Regarding performance optimization of the source model, existing research has provided numerous insights. Multi-task learning simultaneously trains and optimizes multiple related tasks within a single model, enhancing the performance and overall generalization of each task by sharing model parameters and representations. This approach is suitable when the dataset is relatively balanced across multiple tasks in the source context; a typical scenario is the simultaneous prediction of symptoms for multiple diseases based on the microbiome [106]. Moreover, integration learning strategies with deep neural network models can significantly improve model performance and generalization, making this approach highly generalizable [108]. Additionally, enhancing the source foundation model to improve transfer learning efficiency can also boost transfer learning performance. For instance, the entropy-based Efficient Neural Transfer with Reduced Overhead via PrunIng algorithm can optimize transfer learning with very low memory and computational requirements [109]. And genetic algorithms can be used to select the CNN model update block, reducing the execution time of transfer learning while maintaining prediction accuracy [110]. In microbiome-based classification and prediction, the black-box nature of foundation models poses significant challenges to the interpretability of results, particularly in clinical diagnosis. Using simpler model architectures and more explicit input features can enhance interpretability. Additionally, interpretability methods such as Partial Dependence Plots [111], Partial Effect Plot, and rule extraction techniques can help elucidate the decision-making processes of these models [112].

In terms of training data optimization, while transfer learning can enhance model performance in scenarios with insufficient data, large and high-quality source datasets are still necessary to effectively train a robust transfer learning model. High-quality datasets enable the model to learn generic and representative features that generalize well to new tasks. If the source dataset is of low quality or contains noise, the model may learn inaccurate or irrelevant features, adversely affecting its performance on the target task [67]. Furthermore, even in transfer learning, some labeled data from the target task is required for fine-tuning to ensure the model's accuracy on specific tasks [113]. The success of transfer learning also depends on the distributional similarity between the source and target data. Significant differences in feature distributions between the two datasets can hinder the effective transfer of knowledge. These distributional differences may result from various factors, such as differing data sources, collection methods, or sample characteristics. Therefore, ensuring some degree of feature and distributional similarity between source and target data is crucial for the success of transfer learning [67]. Generative Adversarial Networks, proposed by Ian Goodfellow et al., can generate synthetic microbiome data similar to real data, addressing the issue of data insufficiency and enhancing model training [114]. Similarly, data augmentation techniques can improve model performance by increasing sample diversity in the data space, thus alleviating the constraint of limited data in the target domain [115].

To address the computational challenges associated with handling large datasets in continuous learning models, it is essential to adopt efficient methods. One effective approach is to use lightweight models, such as MobileNet, EfficientNet, and SqueezeNet, or pre-pruned models like Sparse ResNet, as foundational models [116–119]. During transfer learning, freezing parameters in layers that do not require fine-tuning can reduce computational demands [120]. For instance, freezing the underlying feature extraction layers and training only the top classifiers or task-specific layers. During the inference phase, employing mixed precision training that combines 16-bit and 32-bit precision ensures model accuracy while minimizing computational overhead [121]. Additionally, multi-GPU training strategies and layer-wise tuning are commonly used [122,123].

The shift in medical practice from task-specific models to those supporting continuous learning can be compared to the transformation physicians undergo when moving from relying solely on existing skills to embracing ongoing education and adaptability (Fig. 6a-b). To enable this significant change, it is crucial to leverage well-designed fine-tuning cohorts and to enhance the capabilities of transfer learning, much like how physicians are expected to continuously update their medical knowledge and clinical skills to effectively tackle medical challenges. This transition also heralds a profound change in the philosophy of healthcare delivery, steering it away from a passive, reactive mode of treatment towards a proactive, predictive model of health management. Ultimately, this shift improves individual health and quality of life while significantly reducing disease incidence and healthcare costs.

The novel modeling paradigm that places a strong emphasis on continuous learning and adaptability also provides significant insights for global healthcare collaboration and interdisciplinary diagnostics. By partnering with healthcare organizations worldwide, it becomes possible to share and integrate data to create training datasets that are more diverse and broadly representative, enhancing the generalizability of the model, and ensuring it performs effectively across various geographical locations, ethnic groups, and demographic populations. To achieve effective data sharing, it is essential to standardize various aspects such as data formats, collection methods, privacy protection measures, and ethical review processes [124]. Establishing a global collaborative network for healthcare AI, which includes hospitals, research institutes, technology companies, and governmental organizations, will further bolster these efforts. Interdisciplinary integration is also critical in this paradigm, drawing upon expertise from various fields such as psychology, sociology, and medical imaging science to enrich healthcare models. For instance, in the context of chronic disease management and long-term rehabilitation monitoring models, incorporating psychological insights can help in detecting mental health issues such as anxiety and depression, thereby significantly enhancing patient care. Sociological perspectives can analyze health disparities across different socio-economic groups, enabling models to identify these disparities and offer targeted health interventions. Furthermore, integrating medical imaging technologies like Magnetic Resonance Imaging and Computed Tomography scans with histological data allows for multimodal data fusion, which greatly improves diagnostic accuracy [125]. This comprehensive approach makes healthcare models more adaptable, effective, and inclusive, tailoring to the diverse needs of different populations.

By effectively tackling the challenges posed by data heterogeneity, and the problem of category imbalance, this paradigm shift aims to significantly enhance the accuracy of disease diagnosis. Furthermore, by bolstering interdisciplinary collaboration, which brings together experts from various fields to work on common problems, and by promoting global data sharing, which allows for the exchange of information and knowledge across borders, this approach will also greatly improve the effectiveness of personalized treatments. These combined efforts will lay a robust foundation for healthcare systems to become more efficient, precise, and humane. This means that medical professionals will be better equipped to provide tailored care to patients, leading to better health outcomes and a more compassionate approach to patient care.

Conclusion

The utilization of microbiome-based classification and prediction techniques has shown tremendous potential in managing the complexity of data and enhancing the performance of models. These methods are proving to be highly effective in the realm of microbiome research, where the processing and analysis of large datasets present significant challenges. However, recent advancements in foundation models and transfer learning have emerged as promising solutions to these obstacles. The transition from the traditional "good doctor" model to a self-updating "learning doctor" model has demonstrated that the integration of foundation models with transfer learning can greatly improve the adaptability and generalization of models. This shift is paving the way for significant advancements in the fields of personalized medicine and precision diagnostics.

This paradigm shift, moving from good models to adaptive models, facilitates improved generalization across various scenarios, making them more adept at handling large and diverse microbiome data. For example, in the context of cross-regional disease diagnosis, transfer learning can effectively leverage disease data from one city to enhance the accuracy of models’ diagnosis in another city.

Future research efforts should prioritize the optimization of foundation models and transfer learning applications within the realm of microbiome data. A particular focus should be placed on achieving efficient and accurate learning and prediction in large and diverse datasets. Furthermore, research should delve into effective methods for selecting and utilizing fine-tuning cohorts, as well as addressing the heterogeneity between datasets. Additional studies should also explore ways to enhance the generalization capabilities of models in complex scenarios, such as across different timeframes and species. Continuous improvement and innovation in these techniques are anticipated to significantly advance the fields of personalized and precision medicine, ultimately leading to more effective healthcare solutions.
