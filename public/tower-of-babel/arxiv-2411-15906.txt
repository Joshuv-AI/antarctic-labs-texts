Convergence of supercell and superspace methods for computing spectra of quasiperiodic operators
Bryn Davies, Clemens Thalhammer

Abstract
. We study the convergence of two of the most widely used and intuitive approaches for computing the spectra of differential operators with quasiperiodic coefficients: the supercell method and the superspace method. In both cases, Floquet-Bloch theory for periodic operators can be used to compute approximations to the spectrum. We illustrate our results with examples of Schrödinger and Helmholtz operators.



Keywords.
quasicrystal, cut and project, fractal spectrum, Cantor set, Fibonacci tiling, almost Mathieu operator

1.
Introduction

Quasicrystals have long been discussed as an exciting new direction for photonic crystals and metamaterials, as their self-similar but aperiodic geometries lead to exotic wave-structure interactions beyond those possible with either periodic or random materials
[
51
,
45
]
. The source of this excitement is mostly the exotic spectral properties that can be exhibited by differential operators with quasiperiodic coefficients, such as fractal and Cantor spectra
[
45
,
6
]
, metal-insulator transitions
[
28
]
and unexpected symmetries in reciprocal space
[
43
]
. There is also interest in their effective properties
[
12
,
49
]
, such as the ability to achieve isotropy in lattice structures
[
14
]
and negative refraction
[
38
]
. These properties have, so far, been put to use in applications such as building wave guides
[
21
,
9
]
and rainbow devices
[
20
]
.

The difficulty with working with differential operators with quasiperiodic coefficients is that their spectra are notoriously hard to compute. This is the main barrier to the wider uptake of quasicrystalline metamaterials for applications. While some efficient and error-controlled algorithms have been developed ,
e.g.

[
15
,
29
]
, the relative challenge of understanding and implementing these new algorithms has meant that their usage in the applied physical community has been limited. Instead, researchers have preferred simpler, intuitive approaches, even if their convergence properties are less well known.

The most widely used approach in the physical literature for approximating the spectra of non-periodic systems is the
supercell method
. This method constructs periodic versions of the operator’s coefficients by taking a large, finite-sized section of the domain and repeating those values of the coefficients periodically. This is convenient as the periodic version of the operator has a well-behaved spectrum, composed of a countable union of continuous spectral bands, which can be computed easily using Floquet-Bloch theory
[
34
]
. As a result, the supercell method underpins many of the breakthroughs in quasiperiodic metamaterials, see
e.g.

[
13
,
25
,
21
,
38
,
20
,
26
]
.

An intuitive conjecture about the supercell method is that better approximations can be obtained by increasing the size of the unit cell. However, in what sense the resulting sequence of spectra converges to that of the limiting quasiperiodic operator is far from obvious. The sequence of Floquet-Bloch spectra are each composed of a countable union of spectral bands. Conversely, the limiting spectra can, in general, have many different exotic properties, such as the gaps being dense and the spectrum being a Cantor set
[
45
]
. Thus, it’s apparent that taking this limit involves nontrivial behaviour. Fortunately, there is an established theory for approximating almost periodic functions by periodic functions
[
44
]
, which can be used to establish spectral convergence results
[
7
,
19
]
.

Although the behaviour of the sequence of approximate spectra obtained from supercell approximations with increasingly large unit cells is difficult to understand in general, it has been observed that many of the main spectral gaps emerge relatively early in the sequence and persist. These were coined
super band gaps
by
[
39
]
and have been studied in several settings. For example, they were recently proved to exist in one-dimensional systems based on Fibonacci-tilings
[
22
]
and have been observed in two-dimensional lattices based on Penrose tilings
[
18
]
. The convergence theory discussed in this work shows that when a super band gap exists in the sequence of supercell approximations, this is guaranteed to correspond to a gap in the spectrum of the limiting quasiperiodic operator.

The other approach explored in this work is the
superspace method
. This exploits the fact that, although quasicrystals are generally non-periodic, they have underlying periodicity in the sense that they can be obtained by an incommensurate projection from a higher-dimensional periodic space
[
27
]
. Using this approach to compute spectra was proposed by
[
42
]
, although they observed spectral pollution by spurious eigenvalues in many of the spectral gaps and it remains to develop a convergence theory for the method. The superspace principle has also been used successfully by others, to perform direct numerical simulations of forced problems
[
2
]
and to derive effective medium theories through asymptotic homogenisation
[
12
,
49
]
.

Particularly in applied physical and engineering settings, many researchers have overcome the challenges of computing spectra of quasicrystals by resorting to only ever simulating finite-sized structures. In this case, one can either compute the countable collection of eigenvalues
[
50
,
33
,
37
,
41
]
or estimate a local density of states (LDOS)
[
23
,
5
]
. These approaches typically give reliable predictions of the experimental behaviour of devices. In certain cases (especially those which are periodic), the spectra of finite-sized systems are known to converge to their infinite counterparts
[
3
]
, so it’s a reasonable expectation that there should be some correspondence between the spectra of quasiperiodic operators on finite and infinite domains. However, spectra are often polluted by edge modes, which need to be properly understood, and it is not always clear how properties will scale or generalise. Further, these methods will never capture the unique mix of long-range aperiodicity and self-similarity present in quasicrystals. The motivation for developing rigorous convergence theories for supercell and superspace methods is that these approaches would facilitate more systematic investigation of quasiperiodic metamaterials, analogous to how any study of periodic systems typically begins by examining the spectral band diagram to understand the system’s properties.

We will begin by recalling some mathematical preliminaries in Section
2
, such as the required background on almost periodic functions and tiling rules, before examining the supercell and superspace methods in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively. Finally, in Section
5
we will show how our theory can be applied to study localised interface modes in quasicrystals with defects; we prove the existence of such modes and give estimates for their eigenfrequencies and (exponential) decay rates.

2.
Mathematical preliminaries

2.1.
Problem setting

Suppose we want to compute the spectrum of a self-adjoint elliptic operator
A
with quasiperiodic coefficients of the form

(2.1)

A=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x)\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}},

where the coefficient
a_{k}(x)
are assumed to be quasiperiodic and smooth. By
[
44
]
, this amounts to finding all pairs
(\lambda,u)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(2.2)

Au=\lambda u.

If the coefficient
a_{k}
are periodic, then Floquet-Bloch theory applies and (
2.2
) can be reduced to a collection of eigenvalue problems for
(\lambda_{\alpha},u_{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{C}^{\infty}((0,T))
given by

(2.3)

\begin{cases}Au_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\\
u_{\alpha}(T)=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha T}u_{\alpha}(0),\end{cases}

where
T
is the length of the periodic and
\alpha\in[0,\frac{2\pi}{T})
is the quasi-momentum. From a computational point of view, (
2.3
) is much simpler than (
2.2
), as elliptic operators on compact domains have a countable set of eigenvalues. While integral decomposition techniques do exist for quasiperiodic (or almost periodic) differential operators
[
10
]
, the resulting domains are still infinite so Floquet-Bloch is not directly applicable. Related to this, the spectrum can take on exotic forms. For example, there are instances of elliptic differential operators with almost periodic coefficients whose spectrum is Cantor-like
[
40
]
.

We will consider eigenvalue problems (
2.2
) for operators of the form (
2.1
) with coefficients
a_{k}(x)
that are smooth and are either quasiperiodic (in the sense of Definition
2.5
) or formed by a tiling rule (as introduced in Section
2.4
). In Section
3
, we will consider the supercell method. We will prove how the Floquet-Bloch spectra converge to the spectrum of the limiting operator in the limit of large unit cell length. We will present versions of these results for the specific case of tiling-based operators and present numerical examples for Schrödinger and Helmholtz-type operators. In Section
4
, we will consider the superspace method, as proposed in
[
42
]
. We will show that the lifted operator has the same spectrum and present some numerical examples. These examples predict spectra that agree with the supercell predictions, up to some spectral pollution by some spurious eigenvalues. This spectral pollution was also observed in
[
42
]
; we show how it depends on the choice of discretisation (and is a particularly troublesome issue in the case of the plane wave expansion method).

2.3.
Continued fractions

Deciding how to approximate an aperiodic function by a sequence of periodic versions will often be equivalent to approximating an irrational number by a sequence of rational numbers. A natural way to achieve this is by truncating the continued fraction representation of a number. To this end, we briefly recall the theory of continued fractions, and refer the reader to
[
30
]
for more details.

A
simple continued fraction
is an expression of the form

(2.5)

a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{2}+\dots}},

where
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots
are positive integers, referred to as the
elements
of the continued fraction. The sequence of elements can be either infinite or finite. When it is finite, we call (
2.5
) a
finite
continued fraction. It turns out that every real number can be written uniquely in this form, as shown in
[
30
, Theorem 14]
:

Theorem 2.10
.

To every real number
a
there corresponds a unique continued fraction
with value equal to
a
. Furthermore, this continued fraction is finite if and only if
a
is rational.

Given an irrational number
a
, its continued fraction representation will have an infinite sequence of elements
[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
. We can construct a sequence of rational approximants indexed by
k\in\mathbb{N}
by truncating the sequence of elements and considering the rational numbers with finite continued fractions with elements
[a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
. Since any such number must be rational, for each
k
there exists coprime integers
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
such that the
k
th
rational number in this sequence is equal to
p_{k}/q_{k}
. Clearly,
p_{k}/q_{k}\to a
as
k\to\infty
and the rate of convergence can be estimated by the following result, from
[
30
, Theorem 10]
:

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
be equal to the infinite continued fraction with elements
[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
and let
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
be coprime integers such that
p_{k}/q_{k}
is equal to the finite continued fraction with elements
[a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
. Then, it holds that

\left|a-\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right|<\frac{1}{q_{k}q_{k+1}}.

2.4.
Tilings

Another way of generating aperiodic structures with long-range order is through the means of inflation tilings, for a thorough introduction see
[
8

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\left\{a_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\right\}

Definition 2.12
.

A general substitution rule

Substitution rules, where

\mathcal{A}^{*}:=\{u\in F_{n}\mid u\text{ contains no negative powers}\}.

A (non-negative) substitution rule

(i,j)
there exists some

such that

is a subword of
\varrho(a_{i})^{k}
. A (non-negative) substitution rule is called primitive if there exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that every
a_{j}
is a subword of
\varrho(a_{i})^{k}

Through Abelianisation, one can associate to every substitution rule

Lemma 2.14

A (non-negative) substitution rule

Studying the resulting substitution matrix

Given an bi-infinite word

Two words

3.
Supercell method

A widespread technique to study the (spectral) properties of quasicrystals is the supercell approach whereby a non-periodic coefficient is approximated by a periodically repeated sample, a so-called
supercell
. This method is conceptually simple and straightforward to implement, hence its popularity. In this section, we will derive error bounds for this approach. We will further outline how one needs to choose the approximants to ensure maximal efficiency.

3.1.
Convergence theory

To avoid notation becoming too cumbersome, we will only consider operators of the form

(3.1)

A_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x,\theta x)\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}},

where
a_{k}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\Q
. The following result serves as a warm up and is a result of strong operator convergence.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta_{n}
be a sequence converging to
\theta
. Then, it holds that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert A_{\theta}-A_{\theta_{n}}f\rVert_{L^{2}}=0,

for all
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, the sequence
\sigma(A_{\theta_{n}})
is spectrally inclusive in the sense that for every
\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta})
there exists a sequence
\lambda_{n}\in\sigma(A_{\theta_{n}})
such that
\lambda=\lim\lambda_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varepsilon>0
. We have

\begin{split}\lVert(A_{\theta}-A_{\theta_{n}})f\rVert^{2}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert\sum_{k}(a_{k}(x,\theta x)-a_{k}(x,\theta_{n}x))\frac{d^{k}f}{dx^{k}}(x)\right\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\sum_{k}C\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert a_{k}(x,\theta x)-a_{k}(x,\theta_{n}x)\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\frac{d^{k}f}{dx^{k}}(x)\right\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

As
f\in H^{k}
, there exists some
R>0
such that

\sum_{k}C\int_{\lvert x\rvert>R}\lvert a_{k}(x,\theta x)-a_{k}(x,\theta_{n}x)\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\frac{d^{k}f}{dx^{k}}(x)\right\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

On the other hand, since
a_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, for
n
large enough, we have

\sum_{k}C\int_{\lvert x\rvert<R}\lvert a_{k}(x,\theta x)-a_{k}(x,\theta_{n}x)\rvert^{2}\left\lvert\frac{d^{k}f}{dx^{k}}(x)\right\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

and the claim follows.
∎

To obtain a stronger result, we will need to construct approximate eigenfunctions. The next Lemma is a well known consequence of the spectral theorem.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and
A:D(A)\subset\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
a normal operator. Then
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
if and only if there exists a normalised sequence
u_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\lim\lVert(A-\lambda)u_{n}\rVert=0
.

The following is a generalisation of a well-known result from the study of dynamically defined Schrödinger operators, see e.g.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and

be a self-adjoint elliptic operator of the form (

we define

A_{\theta,y}=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x+y_{1},\theta x+y_{2})\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}.

Consider the set

\Sigma(A_{\theta}):=\bigcup_{y\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\sigma(A_{\theta,y}).

There exists some
C>0
such that for any
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lvert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rvert
small enough, for every
\lambda\in\Sigma(A_{\theta})
we have

d(\lambda,\Sigma(A_{\theta^{\prime}}))\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\lvert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rvert^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda\in\Sigma(\theta)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume

. Choose
f_{\lambda}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{R})

. We further define

(3.2)

h_{L,\tau}(x)=h\left(\frac{x-\tau}{L}\right).

Using Fubini’s theorem, we have that

(3.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lVert h_{L,\tau}g\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\ \mathrm{d}\tau=\lVert h\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

for all
g\in L^{2}
. In particular, it holds that

(3.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lVert h_{L,\tau}(A_{\theta}-\lambda)f_{\lambda}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau=\varepsilon^{2}\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}.

Next, we compute the commutator

(3.5)

\begin{split}[A_{\theta},h_{L,\tau}](x)&=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x)\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{n}\frac{d^{n}h_{L,\tau}}{dx^{n}}(x)\frac{d^{k-n}}{dx^{k-n}}\\
&=\sum_{k}a_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{n}L^{-n}\frac{d^{n}h}{dx^{n}}\left(\frac{x-\tau}{L}\right)\frac{d^{k-n}}{dx^{k-n}}.\end{split}

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lVert[A_{\theta},h_{L,\tau}]f_{\lambda}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau\leq CL^{-2}\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}.

Using ellitpic regularity
[
46
]

(3.7)

\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq C(\lvert\lambda\rvert+\varepsilon+1)^{2}\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}

For any
\delta>0
, it now holds that

(3.8)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lVert(A_{\theta}-\lambda)h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau&\leq(1+\delta)\int_{R}\lVert h_{L,\tau}(A_{\theta}-\lambda)f_{\lambda}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau+(1+\delta^{-1})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lVert[A_{\theta},h_{L,\tau}]f_{\lambda}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\leq(1+\delta)\varepsilon^{2}\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}+(1+\delta^{-1})CL^{-2}(\lvert\lambda\rvert+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+1)^{2}\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}((1+\delta)\varepsilon^{2}+(1+\delta^{-1})CL^{-2}(\lvert\lambda\rvert+\varepsilon+1)^{2})\lVert h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau.\end{split}

Hence, there must exist some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(3.9)

\lVert(A_{\theta}-\lambda)h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}\leq((1+\delta)\varepsilon^{2}+(1+\delta^{-1})CL^{-2}(\lvert\lambda\rvert+\varepsilon+1)^{2})\lVert h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert^{2}.

Define
\tilde{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}
by

(3.10)

\tilde{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x,\theta\tau+\theta^{\prime}(x-\tau))\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}.

We have
\tilde{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}\in H(A_{\theta^{\prime}})
and it holds that

(3.11)

\lVert(\tilde{A}_{\theta}^{\prime}-\lambda)h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert\leq\lVert(A_{\theta}-\lambda)h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert+\lVert(\tilde{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}-A_{\theta})h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert.

Observe that for
x\in[\tau-L,\tau+L]
the following bound holds

(3.12)

\lvert a_{k}(x,\theta^{\prime}x+(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\tau)-a_{k}(x,\theta x)\rvert\leq\lVert\nabla a_{k}\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\lvert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rvert L.

It follows that

(3.13)

\lVert(\tilde{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}-A_{\theta})h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\lvert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rvert L\lVert h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Since

\lVert h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq C\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert_{H_{k}},

as
L
increases, we find that

\begin{split}\lVert(\tilde{A}_{\theta}^{\prime}-\lambda)h_{L,\tau}f_{\lambda}\rVert&\leq C\bigg(\left((1+\delta)\varepsilon^{2}+(1+\delta^{-1})L^{-2}(\lvert\lambda\rvert+\varepsilon+1)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt+\lvert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rvert L(\lvert\lambda\rvert+\varepsilon+1)\bigg)\lVert f_{\lambda}\rVert.\end{split}

By choosing
L=\lvert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rvert^{-1/2}
and letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the claim follows.
∎

While for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\Q
it holds that
\Sigma(A_{\theta})=\sigma(A_{\theta})
, the same is not true in general for
\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
. In order to provide proper justification for the supercell approach, a slightly different result is needed. In what follows, we will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
A_{\theta}
a self-adjoint elliptic operator of the form (
3.1
). Let
\theta_{l}=\frac{p_{l}}{q_{l}}
be the
l
th
continued fraction approximant of
\theta
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\begin{split}d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{\theta}))\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\lvert q_{l}^{-1},\quad\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta_{l}})\\
d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{\theta_{l}}))\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}q_{k}^{-1}\prod_{l=n}^{k-1}(1+Cq_{l}^{-1}),\quad\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta}).\end{split}

The idea is similar to that of Theorem
3.3
, the key difference being that we explicitly construct the approximate eigenfunctions and hence control their localisation. The proof of Theorem
3.4
relies on the following lemma:

Lemma 3.5
.

Under the assumptions and in the notation of Theorem
3.4
, there exists some
C>0
such that for every
\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta_{l}})
and every
m>l
we have

d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{\theta_{m}}))\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\lvert\theta-\theta_{l}\rvert^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta_{l}})
, from Floquet-Bloch theory we know that there exists some
k\in[0,\frac{2\pi}{q_{l}})
and some periodic function
p(x)
such that
v=e^{ikx}p(x)
satisfies

(3.14)

A_{\theta_{l}}v(x)=\lambda v(x).

We further define

(3.15)

f(x)=\begin{cases}e^{-\frac{1}{x}},&x>0,\\
0,&x\leq 0.\end{cases}

It can be shown that
f
is smooth. This function will serve as the primary building block for the following step- and bump functions. Consider now

(3.16)

g(x)=\frac{f(x)}{f(x)+f(1-x)}.

As the denominator of
g
never vanishes,
g
is smooth and it holds that

g(x)\begin{cases}1,&x\geq 1,\\
0,&x\leq 0.\end{cases}

We now define

h_{l}(x)=g\left(\frac{x-2q_{l}}{q_{l}}\right)g\left(\frac{2q_{l}-x}{q_{l}}\right).

It is not difficult to see that
h_{l}
is smooth,
h_{l}\equiv 1
on
(-q_{l},q_{l})
,
h_{l}\equiv 0
outside of
[-2q_{l},2q_{l}]
and that
\rvert h_{l}^{(n)}(x)\lvert\leq C_{n}q_{l}^{-n}
. Moreover, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, independent of
l
such that

(3.17)

C_{1}\lVert p\rVert_{H^{k}((0,q_{l}))}^{2}\leq\lVert h_{l}v\rVert_{H^{k}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq C_{2}\lVert p\rVert_{H^{k}((0,q_{l}))}^{2}.

We now have for every
\delta>0

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert(A_{\theta_{l}}-\lambda)h_{l}v\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x=(1+\delta)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert h_{l}(A_{\theta_{l}}-\lambda)v\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x+(1+\delta^{-1})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert[A_{\theta_{l}}-\lambda,h_{l}]v\rvert^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x.

As
v
satisfies (
3.14
), the first term on the right-hand side of the previous equation vanishes. From (
3.5
), we infer that

(3.19)

\lVert[A_{\theta_{l}}-\lambda,h_{l}]v\rVert_{L^{2}(0,q_{l})}^{2}\leq Cq_{l}^{-2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{H^{k}((0,q_{l})},

for some
C>0
that does not depend on
l
. Due to elliptic regularity
[
46
]
, there exists some constant
C>0
that can be chosen independently of
l
such that

(3.20)

\lVert p\rVert_{H^{k}(0,q_{l})}^{2}\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)^{2}\lVert p\rVert_{L^{2}(0,q_{l})}^{2}.

Equation (
3.19
), (
3.17
) and (
3.20
) imply together that

(3.21)

\lVert(A_{\theta_{l}}-\lambda)h_{l}v\rVert^{2}\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)^{2}q_{l}^{-2}\lVert h_{l}v\rVert^{2},

for
C>0
, independent of
l
by letting
\delta\rightarrow\infty
. Since for all
i
we have

(3.22)

\lvert a_{i}(x,\theta x)-a_{i}(x,\theta_{l})\rvert\leq\sup_{y}\lvert\nabla a_{i}(y)\rvert\lvert\theta_{m}-\theta_{l}\rvert\lvert x\rvert,

it follows that

(3.23)

\lVert(A_{\theta_{m}}-A_{\theta_{l}})h_{l}v\lVert^{2}\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)^{2}q_{l}^{-2}\lVert h_{l}v\rVert^{2},

where we used that
\lvert\theta_{m}-\theta_{l}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{q_{l}q_{l+1}}
. Altogether, this yields

(3.24)

\begin{split}\lVert(A_{\theta_{m}}-\lambda)h_{l}v\rVert^{2}&\leq(1+\delta)\lVert(A_{\theta_{m}}-A_{\theta_{l}})h_{l}v\rVert^{2}+(1+\delta^{-1})\lVert(A_{\theta_{l}}-\lambda)h_{l}v\rVert^{2}\\
&\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)^{2}q_{l}^{-2}\lVert h_{l}v\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

The first inequality of Theorem
3.4
immediately follows from Lemma
3.5
by letting
m\to\infty
. For the converse direction, we need to keep track of how eigenvalues propagate upwards. Let
\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta_{l+2}})
, then by Lemma
3.5
we have

(3.25)

d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{\theta_{l+1}}))\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)q_{l+1}^{-1}.

Hence, there exists some
\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(A_{\theta_{l+1}})
such that
d(\lambda,\tilde{\lambda})\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)^{k}q_{l+1}^{-1}
. Therefore

(3.26)

\begin{split}d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{\theta_{l}}))&\leq d(\tilde{\lambda},\sigma(A_{\theta_{l}}))+d(\lambda,\tilde{\lambda})\\
&\leq C(1+\lvert\tilde{\lambda}\rvert)q_{l}^{-1}+C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)q_{l+1}^{-1}\\
&\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert+C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)q_{l+1}^{-1})q_{l}^{-1}+C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)q_{l+1}^{-1}.\end{split}

If we denote by
d_{n}
the Hausdorff distance from
\lambda
to
\sigma(A_{\theta_{n}})
, with
d_{0}=0
, then this yields the recursion

(3.27)

d_{n-1}\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert+d_{n})q_{n}^{-1}+d_{n},

which admits the upper bound

(3.28)

d(n)\leq C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}q_{k}^{-1}\prod_{l=n}^{k-1}(1+Cq_{l}^{-1}),

where we use the convention
\prod_{l=n}^{n-1}=1
. Note that this expression is indeed finite since
q_{n}
grows at least as fast as the Fibonacci sequence. The claim now follows from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

3.2.
Tiling-based operators

A similar result holds for tiling-based operators, in this case the rate of convergence depends on the inflation rule. We treat the example of the golden mean Fibonacci tiling. To this end, we need the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite subsection of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, such that all tiles remain intact. Then for any
n
such that
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\rvert
,
\mathcal{S}
can be found in the periodic arrangement of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Proof.

We are going to proceed by induction. Since
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert<\infty
, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{S}
is contained in
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, and hence in particular in a periodic arrangement of
\mathcal{F}_{N}
.

Suppose the statement holds for
n+1
and assume that
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\rvert
. We are going to discern two cases. If
\mathcal{S}
is contained within
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
, the statement is trivial since
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
is contained in the periodic arrangement of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. On the other hand, if
\mathcal{S}
is not a subset of
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
, it must lie close to the boundary of two subsequent
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
cells. In particular, since
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\rvert
,
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert
must be contained in two subsequent cells of
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
, which in turn are contained in a periodic arrangement of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
∎

We can now state the supercell convergence Theorem for golden mean Fibonacci tilings.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
A_{n}
be a sequence of self-adjoint elliptic operators with coefficients based on the golden mean Fibonacci tiling as outlined in Subsection
2.4
. Denote
A_{\infty}
the limiting operator based on the even numbered tiles. There exists some
C>0
such that the following hold

\begin{split}d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{\infty}))&<C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\mathcal{F}_{2n-1},\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\sigma(A_{n}),\\
d(\lambda,\sigma(A_{n}))&<C(1+\lvert\lambda\rvert)\mathcal{F}_{2n-1},\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\infty}).\end{split}

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
3.3
, however instead of looking for areas where the coefficients of
A_{n}
and
A_{\infty}
are close, we search for areas where they are identical. By Lemma
3.6
, these are exactly the finite sections of size
\mathcal{F}_{2n-1}
. Let
h
be as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
. We let
L=\mathcal{F}_{2n-1}
and
\tau=0
. The estimate is then identical to that of Theorem
3.4
, with the difference that the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.11
) is zero.
∎

Tiling based structure make for excellent models for computational and experimental studies, as their main band gaps are quickly identified. The following recurrence relation was discovered in
[
31
]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
T_{n}(\lambda)
denote the transfer matrix associated to
A_{n}-\lambda
and define
x_{n}(\lambda)=\operatorname{tr}(T_{n}(\lambda))
. The following recursion relation holds for
n\geq 2
:

x_{n+1}(\lambda)=x_{n}(\lambda)x_{n-1}(\lambda)-x_{n-2}(\lambda).

In
[
39
]
it was observed that the main band gaps of operators based on Fibonacci tilings occur relatively early on in the sequence and persist in the limit of large unit cell size. This lead to the notion of
super band gaps

\mathcal{S}_{N}
, the set of eigenvalues that are in spectral gaps for all the operators with an index greater than
N
:

\mathcal{S}_{N}=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\mid\lambda\notin\sigma(A_{n})\text{ for all }n\geq N\right\}.

Using Lemma
3.8
, we can see that
\lambda
being in a super band gap is equivalent to
\lvert x_{n}(\omega)\rvert>2
for all
n
greater than some
N
. The following criterion for deciding whether some
\lambda
lies in a super band gap was proven in
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose
x_{n}(\lambda)
satifies the recurrence relation of Lemma
3.8
. Suppose that there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3.29)

\lvert x_{N}(\lambda)\rvert>2,\lvert x_{N+1}(\lambda)\lvert\geq\lvert x_{N}(\lambda)\rvert\text{ and }\lvert x_{N+2}(\lambda)\lvert\geq\lvert x_{N+1}(\lambda)\rvert.

Then
\lvert x_{n+1}\rvert>\lvert x_{n}\rvert
for all
n\geq N
. Consequently,
\lvert x_{n}\rvert>2
for all
n\geq N
, meaning
\lambda
lies in the super band gap
\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

If we assume our tiles to be piece-wise constant, as is assumed in the widely studied setting of laminates, the following refinement can be obtained.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and consider
x_{n}(\omega)
satisfying the golden mean recursion. Then
\omega
lies in the super band gap
\mathcal{S}_{N}
if and only if there exists
n\leq N
such that two consecutive values
\lvert x_{n}\rvert,\lvert x_{n+1}\rvert>2+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Let
n
be such that
\lvert x_{n+1}\rvert,\lvert x_{n+2}\rvert>2+\varepsilon
. Since
\lvert x_{1}(\omega)\rvert=2\lvert\cos\left(\omega\right)\rvert\leq 2
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\lvert x_{n}\rvert\leq 2
, or we reduce
n
by one. We then have

(3.30)

\begin{split}\lvert x_{n+3}\rvert&\geq\lvert x_{n+2}\rvert\lvert x_{n+1}\rvert-\lvert x_{n}\rvert\\
&\geq\lvert x_{n+2}\rvert\lvert x_{n+1}\rvert-\lvert x_{n+2}\rvert\\
&\geq\lvert x_{n+2}\rvert\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\end{split}

and by induction it follows that
\lvert x_{N}\rvert>2
for
N>n
. The converse direction immediately follows from the definition of super band gaps.
∎

4.
Superspace method

A different approach to compute the spectrum is to exploit the fact that quasicrystals are periodic in a higher dimensional superspace: the "parent" space of the coefficients. Using this to perform numerical computations of the spectra was proposed by
[
42
]
and analogous ideas were also used to study forced problems by
[
2
]
.

4.1.
Theory

In our example of the one-dimensional differential operator

(4.1)

A_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x,\theta x)\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}},

we lift into the two-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the new operator is

(4.2)

B_{\theta}=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x,y)D_{\theta}^{k},

where
D_{\theta}
denotes the directional derivative along the vector
(1,\theta)^{\top}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since the coefficients of this operator (
4.2
) are periodic, Floquet-Bloch theory applies and one can reduce the spectral problem to studying the operators

(4.3)

B_{\theta}(\alpha,\beta)=e^{-\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta y)}\circ B_{\theta}\circ e^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta y)}

on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

It has been argued that, since a slice along an irrational angle fills the unit cell densely (see Figure
2.1
), the eigenvalues of the above equation should not depend on
y
. Moreover, the two operators (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) should share the same spectrum. We will provide a more rigorous justification for these two statements. It has also been argued that to every
\lambda\in\sigma(B_{\theta})
there should exist Floquet eigenmodes

u_{\lambda}(x,y)=e^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta y)}p(x,y),

where
p
is periodic. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the existence of such functions usually requires ellipticity of the associated differential equations, which is not the case here. A priori, we can therefore not assume the existence of such solutions. Only for Schrodinger operators with analytic potential this has been rigorously proven, see
[
24
]
.

The next Theorem justifies the superspace method for spectral problems as it shows that the lifted operator has the same spectrum as the original quasiperiodic operator.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\Q
and
A_{\theta}
be a one-dimensional differential operator as in (
4.1
). If
B_{\theta}
is the lifted operator defined in (
4.2
), then
\sigma(A_{\theta})=\sigma(B_{\theta})
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.7
, we have
\sigma_{B}(A_{\theta})=\sigma(A_{\theta})
. Let
\lambda\in\sigma(A_{\theta})
and
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
3.2
and Definition
2.4
, there exists an almost eigenvector
f\in B^{2}
with generalised Fourier series

(4.4)

f(x)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}f_{\gamma}e^{\mathrm{i}\gamma x},

where only countably many
f_{\gamma}
are nonzero. By considering how
A_{\theta}
acts on the basis vectors
\left\{\exp(\mathrm{i}\gamma x)\right\}_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}
, we observe that
A_{\theta}
preserves subspaces

E_{k}=\left\{g(x)\in B^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mid g(x)=e^{2\pi kx}\sum_{m,n}g_{mn}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+\theta nx)}\text{ where }g_{mn}\in l^{2}(\Z^{2})\right\}.

Hence there must exist some
k\in\mathbb{R}
such that there exists an
\varepsilon
-almost eigenvector

(4.5)

\tilde{f}(x)=e^{\mathrm{i}kx}\sum_{m,n}f_{mn}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m+\theta n)x}.

We define

(4.6)

\tilde{F}(x,y)=e^{\mathrm{i}kx}\sum_{m,n}f_{mn}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+\theta ny)}.

Since
B_{\theta}
acts on the Fourier coefficients of
\tilde{F}
like
A_{\theta}
acts on the (generalised) Fourier coefficients of
\tilde{f}
,
\tilde{F}
is an
\varepsilon
eigenvector for
B_{\theta}
. Hence
\sigma(A_{\theta})\subset\sigma(B_{\theta})
. The converse inclusion follows from a similar argument utilising the Fourier series of (approximate) eigenvectors of
B_{\theta}
.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
4.1
, it also becomes apparent why the spectrum of
B_{\theta}(\alpha)
must be independent of the quasi-momentum
\alpha
. Since
\theta
is irrational, any
\alpha
can be approximated to arbitrary precision just by the Fourier modes of the approximate eigenvector.

4.1

3.3
for the supercell approach. However, some care is needed, as we need to take directional derivatives along a vector with incommensurate entries. If we were to naively use a square mesh, we would need to approximate these vectors, for example by the means of finite continued fractions. This would lead to complicated finite difference matrices. Instead, we opt to employ a rectangular mesh, where the ratio between the

\theta^{-1}

x_{i}=ih

D_{\theta}^{2}f(x_{i},y_{j})\approx\frac{f(x_{i+1},y_{j+1})-2f(x_{i},y_{j})+f(x_{i-1},y_{j-1})}{h^{2}}.

proposed some formal arguments for identifying (and, hence, removing) spurious modes, this represents a significant weakness of the plane wave expansion method.

The failure of the plane wave expansion method to reliably predict the spectrum can most likely be attributed to the lack of ellipticity in the superspace, which means we cannot a priori expect any sort of decay of the Fourier coefficients. This can also be understood as the lack of Bragg resonances in this aperiodic material. For periodic systems, given any fixed frequency
\omega
, there are only a finite number of plane waves with frequency
\omega^{\prime}\leq\omega
satisfying given Bloch boundary conditions. Since a Bloch eigenfunction
u(x)
with eigenvalue
\omega^{2}
must predominantly consist of plane waves with frequency less than
\omega
, truncating a plane wave expansion yields sensible results. On the other hand, in quasi-periodic systems, the lack of a countable series of spectral bands means that any eigenmode can be composed of an infinite number of plane waves.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented convergence theories for the supercell and superspace methods for approximating the spectra of quasiperiodic operators. We have shown that the series of Floquet-Bloch spectra predicted by supercell approximations converges to the limiting spectrum in a Hausdorff sense when the unit cell is arbitrarily large. This result builds on
[
19
]
by giving explicit constructions of the supercells needed to yield predicted convergence rates. This shows that when super band gaps appear in the supercell approximations
[
39
,
22
]
, these must correspond to spectral gaps in the spectrum of the limiting operator. For the superspace method, we proved that the lifted operator has the same spectrum and showed how the spectral pollution observed in
[
42
]
can be mitigated by choosing the numerical discretisation method appropriately. Finally, we extended this theory to describe localised eigenmodes in systems that have been perturbed to have interfaces. We proved that such modes exist and used periodic approximations to gives estimates for their eigenfrequencies and decay rates.

Our work helps to pave the way for the realisation of new quasiperiodic metamaterial devices. The theory discussed here provides rigorous foundations for using convenient Floquet-Bloch methodologies to approximate spectra. This will allow for more systematic investigation of quasiperiodic metamaterial design problems, using approaches that build on techniques that have been well established for periodic materials in metamaterial science. Our results are particularly useful for predicting the main spectral gaps and the eigenfrequencies of any localised modes, both of which are fundamental to wave guiding and manipulation applications.
