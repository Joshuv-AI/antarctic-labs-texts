Improvement of gastric disease and ridden horse pain ethogram scores with diet adaptation in sport horses
Authors: Violaine Pineau, Fe ter Woort, Félicie Julien, Margaux Vernant, Sandrine Lambey, Camille Hébert, Sandrine Hanne‐Poujade, Victor Westergren, Emmanuelle van Erck‐Westergren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric disease is highly prevalent in sport horses and may lead to poor performance, cause behavioral changes and impact welfare.HypothesisAssess whether diet affects gastric disease and pain expression during riding, and whether it has an impact on physiological and locomotor variables during an exercise test, including jumps.AnimalsNine healthy show‐jumping Warmbloods trained at the same stable.MethodsProspective observational cohort study. The horses receiving a pelleted diet, high in sugar and starch (>30%), were examined at T0 and after 12 weeks (T12) of changing to a cooked, muesli‐type low‐starch (11%) diet. Each time, the horses underwent a standardized exercise test (SET) and a ridden pain score (Ridden Horse Pain Ethogram [RHpE]) was calculated by 2 blinded observers. The next day, horses underwent gastroscopy and gastric lesions were scored blindly. Results were analyzed using Wilcoxon and Spearman tests.ResultsAfter 12 weeks of a low starch diet, the Equine Gastric Disease (EGD; 4 [3–5] at T0 vs 1 [0‐1] at T12, P < .01) and RHpE scores (6 [3–13] at T0 vs 3 [0‐6] at T12, P < .01) improved significantly. Squamous, glandular, and EGD scores were positively correlated with RHpE scores (respectively, r = .747, P < .01; r = .743, P < .01 and r = .867, P < .01).Conclusion and Clinical ImportanceGastric disease and pain scores correlated positively in ridden horses. A low starch diet significantly decreases the severity of gastric disease and associated pain score during riding in horses. Gastric ulcers may be mitigated and the comfort of equines athletes improved by dietary adjustments.

INTRODUCTION

Nutrition plays a crucial role in the performance of equine athletes by supplying both energy and essential nutrients. Digestive health and effective digestion are necessary for the proper absorption of these nutrients, facilitating muscle development and ultimately optimizing overall fitness to perform. Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome (EGUS) is a common condition affecting 17% to 58% of sport horses. 1 Reported clinical signs range from variable pain, diarrhea, and colic to poor performance. 1 The umbrella term of EGUS encompasses Equine Squamous Gastric Disease (ESGD) and Equine Glandular Gastric Disease (EGGD), depending on lesion location.

The pathophysiology of ESGD is well described and linked to excessive acidity (hydrochloric acid and volatile fatty acids [VFA]) in the nonglandular part of the stomach, 2 which leads to ulcerations. Diets high in starch and sugar have been linked to EGUS and hindgut dysfunction. 1 , 3 Many sport horses usually are fed high energy diets, rich in starch and sugar to help support the expected level of performance. The digestion of starch begins in the stomach, mostly through amylolytic bacteria, but an excess of starch (>1 g/kg body weight [BW]) exceeds the amylolytic capacity of the small intestine with the remainder being fermented in the hindgut. 3 The pathophysiology of EGGD is still under investigation and unclear. It seems to be multifactorial and linked to a compromise of the mucosal defense mechanisms (mucus, bicarbonate, mucosal blood flow, prostaglandins), nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) use, and stress. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Whether the quantity of starch is an issue, or whether a link with inflammatory bowel disease exists remains uncertain. 5 , 8

To date, no information has been published on the impact of gastric disease on the horse's behavior and locomotion under saddle. However, several studies have found a link between poor performance and the presence of gastric disease. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 We aim to provide evidence for the interplay among gastric disease, poor performance and horse behavior. To evaluate horse's behavior under saddle, we used data from the analysis of the iJUMP saddle (CWD, Lim group, Bordeaux, France), which contains an accelerometer that records the jumping course and analyzes it by an algorithm, as well as the Ridden Horse Pain Ethogram (RHpE), which was established to grade horses' expression of musculoskeletal pain during exercise. 13

Our objectives were to assess (1) whether dietary changes affect gastric disease and the expression of pain during riding in competition horse, and (2) whether it has an impact on physiological and locomotor variables during a standardized exercise test (SET), including jumps.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Prospective observational cohort study

Horses

Nine working sport horses were selected from a single professional stable in a cohort of 15 Warmbloods, based on rider, training status and regular participation in competitions. There were 4 mares, 3 stallions, and 2 geldings. Mean age was 7 ± 2.9 years. Mean BW of the horses was 604 ± 38.6 kg. The horses were stabled 17 hours/day, exercised for 1 hour and were turned out in grass paddocks for 4 hours each day. One of the mares had been diagnosed 3 months before the study with squamous and glandular gastric disease and had been treated with omeprazole, sucralfate and misoprostol. Treatment was discontinued 2 months before the study.

During the study, training and show schedule remained unchanged. Horses were exercised 6 days per week and were involved in 1* to 5* Fédération Equestre Internationale (FEI) show‐jumping competitions.

Feeding management

Before the onset of the study, the horses had been receiving a commercial pelleted feed for >18 months. This control feed contained 29% starch, 2.5% sugar, 14.5% cellulose, and 11% proteins. After the evaluations at T0, all horses were transitioned to the low starch diet, consisting of a textured highly digestible cooked feed (11% starch, 5.5% sugar, 12% cellulose and 11.5% proteins; Supporting Information. Quantities were tailored to each horse depending on body condition score (BCS) and BW. Horses were fed 4 meals/day, along with 1.6% to 2.4% BW/day of hay. Salt licks were provided ad libitum. The horse's weight and body condition were reevaluated 4 weeks after the start of the new feed, and for 5 of 9 horses, the quantity of feed was adjusted based on individual body condition (quantities were decreased for 3 horses and increased for 2 horses).

Clinical examinations

At T0, horses underwent a clinical examination, a complete blood analysis consisting of CBC, serum biochemistry and an antioxidant profile with vitamin E, selenium, glutathione peroxidase (GPX), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities. A SET was performed at T0 and gastroscopy on the next day to avoid any impact of fasting on the SET. The SET is described below. The same examinations were repeated 12 weeks later (T12). During the time of the study, complete lameness examinations were performed every 4 to 6 weeks by the horses' usual orthopedic veterinarian.

Standardized exercise test

The exercise tests were performed on a standard outdoor sand track, with the same rider riding all 9 horses during SETs and competitions.

The SET consisted in a 5‐minute warm‐up, a 4‐steps incremental working canter on an 800 m outdoor sand track, followed by a jumping course in an outdoor arena (Figure 1A–C). Horses completed 2 consecutive identical courses of 7 jumps (S1‐S7; 85 cm high; Figure 2). The courses were composed of 4 vertical fences (S1, S3, S4, and S7) and 3 oxers (S2, S5, and S6).

Between each step and at the end of the jumping course, total blood lactate concentration was measured using a validated hand‐held meter (Accutrend; Roche Diagnostics). 14

All horses were equipped with an ECG girth connected to a previously validated smart wearable device (Equimetre, Arioneo) to record heart rate (HR), speed, and distance throughout the SET. 15 Horses also were equipped with a connected iJUMP saddle that contains a 3‐dimensional inertial motion unit at the pommel. Through a customary application and software, it provided live recordings and measurement of locomotion‐related variables during the jumping phase of the SET. These included the following:stride regularity (ie, the percentage in similarity of the canter strides between each jump),body angle in the turn preceding the jump (ie, the maximum roll angle before jumps S1, S2, S4, and S6),the strategy of approach (ie, the evolution of the duration of the last 3 strides before each jump: increasing, decreasing, constant, random),the angle at take‐off, during hindlimb impulsion,the duration of the flight phase,the dorso‐ventral energy developed during each jump,the total energy of each jump (ie, the sum of the 3 accelerative powers calculated over each axis 16 ), andthe stride recovery (ie, the number of strides that the horse needed to recover balance and regular canter strides after a jump).

Ridden horse pain ethogram

Each jumping course of the SET was video recorded on a smartphone using the iSPORT application and was subsequently blindly and randomly reviewed by 2 authors (EVE and VP). An equine pain ethogram 13 , 17 (RHpE) was used as a reference and horses were scored on a 24‐point scale (Table 1). Behaviors were scored as present or absent. Assessors were allowed to analyze the video several times if needed. If there were discrepancies between the 2 assessors, the scores were aggregated. Evaluation of interassessor agreement was not part of the study, but agreement between trained assessors previously has been shown to be reliable. 18

Gastroscopies and equine gastric disease scoring

On the day after the SET, the horses were sedated with 0.04 to 0.06 mg/kg BW romifidine (Sedivet; Boehringer) IV and the gastric mucosa was examined using a flexible 3‐m video‐endoscope (VO320; Optomed). Before gastroscopy, feed and hay were withheld for 15 to 18 hours and water was withheld for 2 to 3 hours.

ecause there is no globally accepted scoring system that accounts for both glandular and nonglandular ulcers, a proprietary scoring system was created from the combination of previously published scoring systems for gastric disease, 1 , 5 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 with a total equine gastric disease (EGD) score of 8 (Table 2). The severity of squamous and glandular gastric ulceration was scored a posteriori blindly by the 2 operators (EVE and VP).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed for normality using a Shapiro‐Wilks normality test. Because the data were not normally distributed and the number of observations was small, a Wilcoxon signed rank test was performed to evaluate the difference in variables between T0 and T12. Variables tested were ulcer scores, blood test results, SET results, and RHpE scores. A Spearman correlation test was used to evaluate the correlation between RHpE scores and ulcer scores.

These statistical analyses were done using Rstudio (1.2.1578).

To evaluate the repeatability between the 2 consecutive courses (P1 and P2), for each variable and each jump, the total variance, as well as the corresponding SD, and the part of variance attributable to the courses were calculated. To do this, the appropriate model of analysis of variance was computed with courses (P1 and P2), date and horses as random effects. 23 This 1st step enabled computation of the mean between P1 and P2 if the repeatability was confirmed (Supporting Information).

Wilcoxon signed rank test and Mc Nemar‐Bowker symmetry tests then were used to compare iJUMP variables over each jump and each course using Matlab 2021.

All results are expressed as median, range and significance level was set at P < .05.

RESULTS

Blood variables

Hematology results remained within the reference range provided by the laboratory throughout the study.

Muscle enzymes creatine kinase (CK) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity significantly decreased between T0 and T12 (respectively, 334 ± 133 UI/L at T0 vs 275 ± 88 UI/L at T12, and 582 ± 131 IU/L at T0 vs 320 ± 40 UI/L at T12; P < .01).

The gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT) activity also differed significantly between evaluations, with a decrease between T0 (42 ± 15 UI/L) and T12 (17 ± 7 UI/L; P = .01). Vitamin E concentration also increased between evaluations and was statistically significant (T0: 2.9 ± 0.8 UI/L; T12: 3.8 ± 1.5 UI/L; P = .015). No significant difference was observed in the other antioxidant enzymes measured (GPX and SOD) after the change in diet, nor in inflammatory proteins (fibrinogen and serum amyloid A [SAA]).

EGD scoring

Eight of 9 horses were diagnosed with gastric lesions at T0. Horses with hyperkeratosis showed other gastric lesions, glandular or nonglandular or both. The total EGD score improved 12 weeks after the dietary change (P < .01) with a decrease of each separate ESGD and EGGD score. Two of the horses showed complete healing and 6 horses had a decrease in EGD score after the change of diet (Figure 3 and Table S2).

At T0, 1 horse had no ulcer, and another had EGGD only. Out of the 9 horses, 3 had ESGD (median score, 1; interquartile range [IQR], 1‐2) and 8 had EGGD (median score, 2; IQR, 2‐3), with 8 of 9 (89%) horses having EGD (median score, 4; IQR, 3‐5). At T12, all ESGD lesions were healed and 4 of 9 horses (44%) had EGGD (median score, 0; IQR, 0‐1).

Standardized exercise tests

The submaximal exercise tests used in our study were highly repeatable and no differences in average speeds, HR, or blood lactate concentrations reached were detected between the T0 and T12 SETs. One of the geldings was removed from the calculation, because a drone was filming over the arena when the SET was done and the lactate concentrations were aberrantly high.

Mean blood lactate concentrations were significantly lower at the last step (postjump) at T12 in comparison with T0 (P < .02), but HRs were not significantly different between the 2 sessions. Results of the SETs at T0 and T12 are summarized in Table 3.

Ridden horse pain ethogram

Pain scores were significantly different (P < .01) for all horses between T0 (median score, 6; range, 3‐13) and T12 (median score, 3; range, 0‐5; Figure 3D). DISCUSSION

We showed that a structured (nonpelleted) diet low in starch and sugars significantly decreased the occurrence of gastric ulcers and mitigated the expression of pain‐associated behavior during riding in this population of show‐jumping horses, without any additional therapeutic intervention. Moreover, we found a positive correlation between gastric ulcer scores and pain scores during exercise.

The prevalence of gastric ulcers has been shown to be proportional to the intensity of work. 24 , 25 The horses sampled in our study were taken from a population prone to developing gastric disease because of the high intensity of the training, regular participation in competitions and transport, and the fact they were Warmbloods. 5 , 6 , 26

Gastric ulcers represent economical and welfare issues in sport horses. 9 , 27 , 28 The use of antiulcer medication is expensive and not without adverse effects. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

Studies previously have identified the protective impact of a low sugar diet on squamous gastric disease. 1 , 3 , 34 This effect was confirmed in our study, where despite the absence of drug use, the score of squamous and glandular lesions significantly improved after the change in diet. Clinical relevance of the changes for ESGD may be limited, because median ESGD grade at T0 was only 1.

The amount of starch and sugar in the feed is known to have an impact on gastric pH. High levels of structural carbohydrates favor the development of acid‐producing bacteria in the stomach, 35 as well as the production of VFA that cause damage to the gastric squamous mucosa. 2 , 3 This change in pH impacts the occurrence and healing of ESGD. The impact of pH on EGGD, however, is contradictory, 7 and the overall pathophysiology of EGGD is still poorly understood. 1 , 7 Although grazing has been reported to be protective against EGGD, 36 horses in our study had glandular lesions despite consistent access to pasture for 4 to 6 hours per day and throughout the entire study. The change in diet in these horses resulted in significant improvement in both squamous and glandular disease. Conclusions on the influence of diet on EGGD are conflicting. 21 , 36 , 37 In humans, peptic ulcer disease is idiopathic in 10‐30% of cases and has not been associated with a dietary component. 38

No change in forage distribution and management occurred between T0 and T12 (8 to 12 kg given in a hay net 2 to 4 times per day). Frequency of feeding also could have had a positive impact on gastric emptying and decreased gastric acidity. 39 , 40 Moreover, the amount of crude fiber between the 2 feeds was not markedly different. Fiber structure, size and texture have been suggested as potential factors in promoting gastric ulcers. 3 , 41 , 42 The feed studied here contained short‐stem, early harvested dry alfalfa chaff that had a smoother texture and could have limited mucosal damage associated with fiber hardness because the mechanical impact of the malaxed fibers can damage the pylorus and antrum during persistalsis. 41 , 42 The alfalfa also may have promoted salivation and insalivation, with saliva acting as a gastric buffer. Unlike the control pelleted feed, the studied feed was textured and flaked, and its diversity in particle size could have been a protective factor, as suggested by a previous study 43 that linked the size of the pellets with ingestion time and saliva production. Additionally, the thermal and mechanical treatment of food can influence starch availability and digestibility, 44 , 45 decreasing the production of VFA and lactic acid in the stomach.

The feed evaluated in our study contained several additional components, such as prebiotics and probiotics, 46 , 47 clay, 48 and alfalfa chaff, which may have contributed to the protective effect on the gastric mucosa.

In addition to the direct effect on the gastric mucosa, high‐starch diets also may influence ulcer prevalence by increasing stress hormones. One study showed that a high‐starch diet decreased plasma serotonin concentrations of trained mares and geldings, compared with a high‐fiber diet. 49 Serotonin was decreased and cortisol increased with pain in another study, 50 and hypercortisolemia has been shown to decrease the regenerative capacity of the glandular mucosa, leading to decreased resistance to ulceration and subsequent EGGD. 51 Measuring hormones was beyond the scope of our study.

Previous studies have shown that gastric disease has a deleterious impact on behavior of horses at rest 6 , 52 and during tacking‐up. 53 Here, we focused on the behavior of the ridden horse and did not explore temperament and behavior in the stall. Our study is the 1st to identify the negative effect of gastric disease on the horse's behavior during ridden exercise and to correlate pain signs with the severity of gastric disease scores. The ethogram used in our study initially was designed to address musculoskeletal issues. 13 Pain and discomfort related to gastric ulcers and their negative impact on performance have been reported, but remained difficult to verify objectively. A few studies report the repercussion of visceral pain on ridden exercise. 54 , 55 In racehorses, several studies showed that ESGD had an adverse impact on performance. 1 , 10 , 11 , 56 One study 12 showed that gastric pain could negatively impact stride length and decrease time to fatigue. However, the impact of EGGD on clinical signs and performance is less clear. 6 , 32 Both glandular and nonglandular disease can be present concurrently, making it difficult to evaluate an individual impact. To date, no studies have assessed the impact of gastric ulcers on locomotion. One study of horses presented for girthiness showed that 92% of the horses that underwent gastroscopy had confirmed ulcers. 52 Some of the criteria used for determination of orthopedic pain in the RHpE also have been reported with gastric pain (eg, tail swishing, kicking 53 , 57 , 58 ), and signs of pain can be nonspecific in horses showing behavioral changes, regardless of the origin of the pain. 59 , 60 It has been shown that a skilled rider may improve the gait quality of a horse and decrease expression of pain through lameness, but RHpE score may not improve. 61 The rider in our study was a professional and rode the horses in the study for several months before the start of the experiment.

The correlation we found between gastric disease in horses and pain scores suggests that gastric lesions should be included in the differential diagnosis in horses displaying altered ridden behavior. 62 The mechanism by which gastric pain affects locomotion has not yet been elucidated, but a hypothesis is that it may restrict back flexion, decrease the use of the abdominal musculature and the willingness of the horse to engage its hindquarters. In show‐jumping, contraction of the abdominal muscles contributes to the development of a stable core and subsequent gait regularity. 63 In human athletes, upper gastrointestinal issues are common and can be related to mechanical forces, such as increased intra‐abdominal pressure, mechanical trauma to the abdominal organs from repetitive motion, and body position during exercise such as the bent‐over position found in cyclists. 64 Runners with gastrointestinal reflux disease had significantly decreased time to exhaustion compared with runners without reflux. 65 One study in horses showed that increased intra‐abdominal pressure during intense exercise caused gastric compression, pushing the highly acidic contents dorsally toward the proximal region of the stomach (“splash effect”). 66

To assess if gastric pain affected locomotion on the flat and over jumps, a connected iJUMP saddle recorded objective locomotor variables. Results showed a difference in the balance and consistency of locomotion over certain jumps. Many of the variables evaluated showed no significant differences after the dietary intervention, possibly because the study was underpowered. There also was high interindividual variability for all variables and technical aptitude for jumping varied greatly with age, with younger horses showing less consistency in the way they jumped in comparison with older, more experienced horses in this group. The high intraindividual repeatability between the 1st and 2nd passage (2 × 7 jumps in a row) both at T0 and T12 indicates that the professional rider was consistent in his riding and interfered very little with the way the horses moved. 61 Major changes were not seen in the variables measured by the iJUMP saddle, which focus on evaluating jumping and may not be the most accurate tool to diagnose more subtle changes in locomotion and behavior in ridden horses, but could be a tool for longitudinal follow‐ups.

Results of the SETs on the flat were comparable between T0 and T12, with no significant difference in HR or blood lactate measurements, indicating no obvious change in the fitness of the horses during the time of the study. The clinical relevance of the last blood lactate measurement (after the jumps) is unclear. It could reflect better adaptation to exercise, state of conditioning or decreased time to fatigue, because jumping is not as intense as cantering.

An additional result of our study is that serum activities of muscle enzymes decreased, which could be linked to an improved antioxidant effect of the tested feed, as indicated by increased vitamin E concentrations at T12. Although the difference in CK activity was statistically significant, the results remained within normal limits at both time points. Moreover, a significant decrease in GGT activity occurred, which could be linked to lower mycotoxin content in the new feed, a more digestible feed, 44 , 45 or better adaptation to training. 67

Our study had several limitations because of the nature of working with client‐owned animals. First, sample size was small, which caused a loss of statistical power. We selected high level sport Warmbloods (all showjumpers), and this factor could have introduced selection bias.

The prevalence of gastric disease in our group (89%) was higher than previously published numbers in sport horses but comparable to those published in racehorses. 1 Total EGD score included scoring of both ESGD and EGGD, and thus for any score >2 the horse was considered to have gastric disease, which may have increased our prevalence.

We used a scoring system like that used in a previous study 20 in accordance with the latest European College of Equine Internal Medicine (ECEIM) consensus statement. The ECEIM consensus 1 states that “the assessment of clinical relevance should not be made on endoscopic appearance alone. Instead, the clinician should assess the relevance of an individual's lesions considering the horse's recent usage, its history and presenting clinical signs.” A recent study on EGGD 21 used a scoring system similar to previous scoring systems, 19 , 22 which are based on quantitative criteria. Other studies 20 introduced more descriptive features in the scoring system, which seemed more precise and appropriate to the clinical and lesional observations in our study. We based our proprietary scoring system on the appearance and presumed depth, as well as distribution and number of lesions to stress their qualitative appearance. The main difference between the former systems and that used in our study lies mainly on the degree and extent of erythema.

Furthermore, we have ranked the descriptor hemorrhagic above fibrinosuppurative because it corresponds to a more acute phase lesion, whereas the term fibrinosuppurative reflects either a posthemorrhagic healing process or chronic remodeling of the mucosa, based on clinical experience, such as cases of glandular disease in Warmbloods referred for follow‐up gastroscopies. We acknowledge that the use of this proprietary scoring system comes with unknown measurement error.

Caretakers and veterinarians were not blinded to the diet the horses were receiving, but ethogram and ulcer scores were assigned by blinded observers. There was no blinding of outcome assessment, and horses served as their own controls. A separate control group would have allowed us to evaluate the possible effect of time on the evolution of gastric lesions and locomotor variables. Although no conclusive evidence was presented that these behaviors are a result of gastric pain, a significant association of RHpE and EGD scores was found in our study. The RHpE could be further refined to fit specifically to gastric disease.

In conclusion, dietary changes affect gastric health and subsequently the expression of pain in ridden show‐jumping horses, decreasing pain ethogram scores. These subtle changes could not be effectively assessed by the connected iJUMP saddle. However, the RHpE proved to be a reliable means of identifing gastric disease‐related discomfort in horses performing usual work, both on the flat and over obstacles. The RHpE would be an easy‐to‐use tool in the field for riders, trainers and veterinarians to identify gastric pain and stimulate further investigations especially in high‐achieving equine athletes, where the incidence of gastric ulcers is high. Interestingly, our study showed that EGGD scores decreased with a low starch and sugar feed suggesting it is possible to induce gastric healing by dietary intervention alone.
