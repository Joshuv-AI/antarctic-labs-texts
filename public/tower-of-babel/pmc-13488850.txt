Validation of a semi-quantitative ultrasonography score for knee synovitis in juvenile idiopathic arthritis and its association with clinical findings
Authors: Leonor Garbin Savarese, Gregory Martins Garcia, Luiz Afonso de Oliveira Moura Santos, Luciana Martins de Carvalho, Virgínia Paes Leme Ferriani, Marcello Henrique Nogueira-Barbosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasonography is an important tool for assessing synovitis in juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), but the reproducibility of semi-quantitative scores and their relationship with clinical findings still need clarification. We aimed to evaluate the intra- and inter-observer reproducibility of a previously proposed semi-quantitative ultrasonographic score for synovitis in JIA and to investigate the association between gray-scale (B-mode) and power Doppler (PD) grades and clinical joint findings.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 68 consecutive patients (1–15 years; mean age 9.1 years, SD 3.9) with a diagnosis of JIA who underwent knee ultrasonography between January 2017 and March 2020 were included. Clinical data (joint pain, swelling, increased temperature, and limitation of movement) were recorded. Two musculoskeletal radiologists independently graded synovitis in B-mode and PD using a four-grade semi-quantitative score. Intra- and inter-observer agreement were assessed with weighted kappa coefficients. The association between B-mode and PD grades and the presence of at least one clinical sign was analyzed using a log-binomial regression model with random effects (α = 0.05).ResultsIntra-observer agreement for synovitis grading was almost perfect for both B-mode (κ = 0.90; 95% CI 0.86–0.95) and PD (κ = 0.84; 95% CI 0.77–0.92). Inter-observer agreement was substantial for B-mode (κ = 0.74; 95% CI 0.66–0.81) and moderate-to-substantial for PD (κ = 0.63; 95% CI 0.53–0.73). Ultrasonography detected synovitis in joints considered clinically negative in 19.2% of examinations. Compared with higher B-mode grades, joints with grade 0 synovitis showed a significantly lower prevalence of at least one clinical finding, and similar patterns were observed for PD grades 0 versus 1–2.ConclusionsThe semi-quantitative ultrasonographic score for synovitis in JIA demonstrated almost perfect intra-observer and substantial inter-observer reproducibility. Ultrasonography identified subclinical synovitis beyond physical examination, and higher B-mode and PD grades were associated with a higher prevalence of clinical joint abnormalities, supporting its role in the assessment of disease activity in JIA.Keywords: Knee, Arthritis, Juvenile, Synovitis, Ultrasonography, Ultrasonography, Doppler, Power

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) \ is the most common rheumatic disease in children and is characterized by persistent synovial inflammation that may lead to cartilage destruction, bone damage, functional impairment, and a marked impact on quality of life. The knee is the joint most frequently affected in JIA and is often responsible for disability, so it can be considered an index joint for disease assessment and for monitoring the response to therapy [1, 2]. As in other joints, knee involvement is clinically characterized by swelling, pain, warmth, and loss of function.

Although the diagnosis of JIA is primarily clinical, imaging plays a key role in patient follow-up and in monitoring disease activity, including subclinical inflammation, with a direct impact on decisions regarding early and targeted treatment [3, 4]. Among imaging modalities, ultrasonography (US) is widely accepted as a non-invasive tool for the assessment of early joint involvement in patients with JIA [3–5]. It is inexpensive, widely available, free of ionizing radiation, well tolerated by children, and allows a rapid and dynamic evaluation of multiple joints in a single session [5, 6].

In recent years, the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) group has proposed consensus-based definitions of normal joint components and pathologic findings in healthy children using gray-scale (B-mode) and Doppler US [7–9]. A standardized pediatric US scanning protocol has also been developed, detailing the optimal positioning of the patient and probe for the assessment of the knee, ankle, wrist, and second metacarpophalangeal joint [8]. With regard to Doppler evaluation, either power or color Doppler may be used, with recommended settings including a low pulse repetition frequency, gain adjusted just below the level at which noise appears below the cortical bone surface, and a low wall filter. Importantly, Doppler scoring systems for synovitis differ between children and adults [10]; in pediatric joints, grade 1 corresponds to up to three discrete Doppler signals within the area of synovial hypertrophy, grade 2 to more than three signals in less than 50% of the hypertrophic synovium, and grade 3 to Doppler signal in more than 50% of this area.

The reliability of ultrasonography (US) for detecting inflammatory lesions in juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) has been examined in several studies, which generally report good intra- and inter-observer agreement and support the use of standardized definitions and grading systems in clinical practice [11–14]. More recently, Ting et al. [15] proposed a knee-specific, semi-quantitative US scoring system for synovitis in JIA; however, to our knowledge, its reproducibility and its relationship with clinical joint findings have not been specifically evaluated. Although OMERACT definitions offer a comprehensive framework for pediatric synovitis assessment across multiple joints, the score proposed by Ting et al. provides a practical, structured approach tailored to the knee, the most commonly affected joint in JIA, by grading synovial hypertrophy on B-mode imaging and Doppler activity. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the reproducibility of this semi-quantitative US score for knee synovitis in patients with JIA and to evaluate the association between B-mode and power Doppler synovitis grades and clinical findings, including increased temperature, limitation of movement, swelling, and pain.

Methods

This was a retrospective, observational, cross-sectional study conducted at a tertiary pediatric rheumatology center. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Research Ethics Committee, which waived the requirement for written informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients

A study cohort of 68 consecutive patients was assembled from those under regular follow-up at the pediatric rheumatology outpatient clinic. All patients fulfilled the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification criteria for juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) [16] and had a knee ultrasound (US) available. Ultrasound examinations were requested for clinical indications and performed in the radiology department, with static images stored in standardized planes that included the suprapatellar and parapatellar recesses. In total, 76 patients were initially screened. Eight were excluded because the available US images were incomplete or of insufficient quality (e.g., missing predefined anatomic landmarks or inadequate machine calibration) and/or because the interval between the clinical assessment and the US examination exceeded two months, resulting in a final cohort of 68 patients.

Clinical assessment

Electronic medical records were reviewed to extract clinical data from the visit immediately preceding the ultrasound examination. For the examined knee, four clinical variables were recorded as present or absent: pain, swelling, limitation of movement, and increased local temperature. Current use of biologic therapy at the time of assessment was also documented. Data were abstracted from structured fields in the electronic record by trained investigators, and variables were analyzed as binary outcomes (present/absent). Missing clinical data were minimal; cases with incomplete information were excluded from the corresponding analyses. In addition, a composite outcome (“≥1 clinical sign”) was used to enhance sensitivity for clinically meaningful joint activity and to better reflect routine bedside assessment in pediatric rheumatology practice.

Ultrasound acquisition and scoring

The institutional imaging database was searched to identify knee ultrasound examinations of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of JIA. Static images of the selected examinations were exported to a dedicated workstation for analysis.

Semi-quantitative assessment included gray-scale (B-mode) and power Doppler (PD) evaluation. B-mode synovitis was graded on a 0–3 scale according to the degree and extent of synovial hypertrophy (SH) and elevation of the suprapatellar fat pad:

Grade 0 (normal): small amount of physiological joint fluid without elevation of the suprapatellar fat pad; no SH.

Grade 0 (normal): small amount of physiological joint fluid without elevation of the suprapatellar fat pad; no SH.

Grade 1 (mild): minimal SH with elevation of the suprapatellar fat pad and proximal extension of SH involving < 50% of the visualized length of the quadriceps tendon.

Grade 1 (mild): minimal SH with elevation of the suprapatellar fat pad and proximal extension of SH involving < 50% of the visualized length of the quadriceps tendon.

Grade 2 (moderate): SH elevating the suprapatellar fat pad with proximal extension involving > 50% of the visualized quadriceps tendon.

Grade 2 (moderate): SH elevating the suprapatellar fat pad with proximal extension involving > 50% of the visualized quadriceps tendon.

Grade 3 (severe): marked distension of the suprapatellar recess between the deep surface of the quadriceps tendon and the prefemoral fat pad, extending throughout the image, with the most proximal portion of the synovial recess > 50% of the maximum distension of the recess.

Grade 3 (severe): marked distension of the suprapatellar recess between the deep surface of the quadriceps tendon and the prefemoral fat pad, extending throughout the image, with the most proximal portion of the synovial recess > 50% of the maximum distension of the recess.

For PD assessment, the region of interest box encompassed the entire suprapatellar recess and extended to the top of the screen. Doppler signals were considered only when located within the area of SH in the joint recess. PD synovitis was graded as follows:

Grade 3: confluent Doppler signal in ≥ 50% of the SH area.

Image reading and reliability assessment

All ultrasound images were reviewed using HOROS software. Reader 1, a musculoskeletal radiologist with 4 years of experience, performed the initial grading of all examinations. Intra-observer reliability was assessed by having Reader 1 re-evaluate the complete image set after a 1-month interval, blinded to the initial scores. For inter-observer reliability, Reader 2—also a musculoskeletal radiologist with 4 years of experience—independently graded the same images, blinded to Reader 1’s assessments. Both readers were blinded to all clinical information and prior imaging findings at the time of image interpretation, and all evaluations were performed independently. Ultrasound examinations were anonymized and presented to each reader in a randomized order for each reading session to minimize recall bias. Inter-observer agreement was calculated using Reader 1’s first assessment and Reader 2’s assessment.

Statistical analysis

Intra- and inter-observer agreement for B-mode and PD grades was assessed using weighted kappa coefficients. Interpretation of kappa values followed the categories proposed by Landis and Koch [17]: 0.00–0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement. To evaluate the association between B-mode and PD grades and the presence of clinical findings (increased temperature, limitation of movement, swelling, and pain) and to estimate prevalence ratios, a log-binomial regression model with a random effect for patient was used to account for repeated knee examinations within individuals. All analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and a significance level of 5% was adopted. The unit of analysis was the knee examination. No adjustment for clustering was applied to kappa statistics.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 68 patients with JIA and 224 knee ultrasound examinations were included. Most patients were female (50/68; 73.5%), and mean age was 9.1 years (SD 3.9; range 1–15 years). Disease duration at the time of evaluation ranged from newly diagnosed cases to 96 months of follow-up. Ultrasound findings and clinical signs

Among the 224 knees, B-mode synovitis was graded as 0 in 136 (60.7%), 1 in 36 (16.1%), 2 in 31 (13.8%), and 3 in 21 (9.4%) examinations. PD signal was absent (grade 0) in 186 knees (83.0%), while grades 1, 2, and 3 were observed in 23 (10.3%), 8 (3.6%), and 7 (3.1%) examinations, respectively. Most examinations (140/224; 62.5%) were performed in patients receiving biologic therapy. At least one clinical sign (pain, swelling, limitation of movement, or increased local temperature) was present in 120 knees (53.6%) (Table 2).

Ultrasound identified both subclinical and clinically unapparent synovitis. Among the 104 knees without any clinical sign, B-mode synovitis (grade ≥ 1) was detected in 20 examinations (19.2%). Conversely, among the 120 knees with at least one clinical sign, B-mode synovitis was absent in 52 examinations (43.3%).

Association between ultrasound grades and biologic therapy

When the prevalence of biologic therapy use was compared across B-mode grades, only the contrast between grades 0 and 1 reached statistical significance: knees with B-mode grade 0 synovitis showed a lower prevalence of biologic use than those with grade 1 (prevalence ratio 0.73; 95% CI 0.55–0.97; p = 0.03). No significant differences were observed between other B-mode grades or between PD grades and biologic therapy use (Table 3).

Relationship between ultrasound grades and clinical findings

Higher B-mode and PD grades were associated with a higher prevalence of clinical signs. Compared with B-mode grade 1, grade 0 had a 44% lower prevalence of at least one clinical finding (p < 0.01), and the prevalence was 58% and 52% lower when grade 0 was compared with grades 2 and 3, respectively (both p < 0.01). Grade 1 also showed a 26% lower prevalence of clinical findings compared with grade 2 (p = 0.01).

For PD synovitis, grade 0 was associated with a 51% lower prevalence of at least one clinical sign compared with grade 1 (p < 0.01) and a 41% lower prevalence compared with grade 2 (p = 0.02). Differences between higher PD grades (1 vs. 2 or 3; 2 vs. 3) were not statistically significant (Table 4).

Intra- and inter-observer reliability

Intra-observer reliability for B-mode grading was almost perfect (weighted κ = 0.90; 95% CI 0.86–0.95), and for PD grading it was also almost perfect (κ = 0.84; 95% CI 0.77–0.92). Inter-observer agreement was substantial for B-mode (κ = 0.74; 95% CI 0.66–0.81) and moderate-to-substantial for PD (κ = 0.63; 95% CI 0.53–0.73) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study reports a consecutive series of 68 patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis and 224 knee ultrasound examinations, designed to investigate the reproducibility of the semi-quantitative ultrasound score for knee synovitis proposed by Ting et al. [15] and to explore the association between B-mode and power Doppler (PD) grades and clinical findings. We found almost perfect intra-observer and substantial inter-observer agreement for both B-mode and PD components, confirming that this scoring system is reliable in everyday practice. In addition, higher synovitis grades were associated with a higher prevalence of clinical signs, particularly limitation of movement, which is a less subjective and more reproducible finding than pain. As previously reported by other authors [18, 19], our results also reinforce that ultrasonography detects more synovitis than physical examination alone.

Recent studies have provided pediatric-specific definitions and scoring systems for synovitis based on the extent of inflammation on gray-scale and Doppler ultrasonography [7–10]. These include standardized descriptions of synovial hypertrophy and Doppler signal, acknowledging that a positive Doppler signal is not mandatory for the diagnosis of synovitis in children; rather, synovitis is defined as synovial effusion and/or hypertrophy on B-mode, with or without PD signal, and Doppler activity confined to the hypertrophic synovium is considered a pathologic finding. These refinements have helped to distinguish physiological vascularity from true inflammatory hyperemia and have improved the consistency of ultrasound assessments in JIA.

Several studies have addressed the relationship between clinical abnormalities and ultrasound-detected synovitis in children with JIA. Magni-Manzoni et al. demonstrated excellent intra- and inter-observer reliability for joint effusion, synovial hypertrophy and PD signal, but reported poor agreement between ultrasound findings and clinical abnormalities [18]. Breton et al. reported a significant association between edema, pain and limitation of movement and sonographic abnormalities in metacarpophalangeal joints [19]. Ventura-Ríos et al. also observed excellent intra- and inter-observer reliability for synovitis, tenosynovitis and bone erosions in wrists and metacarpophalangeal joints [13]. Our study complements this body of evidence by focusing on the knee, a key index joint in JIA, and by showing that not only is the Ting et al. semi-quantitative score reproducible, but its higher grades are clearly associated with a higher prevalence of clinical signs.

The role of subclinical synovitis detected by ultrasonography has been extensively discussed, particularly in adult rheumatoid arthritis, where subclinical PD-positive synovitis is a strong predictor of flare [20, 21]. However, regarding JIA, previous literature results remain conflicting. Some longitudinal studies did not confirm a prognostic value of subclinical ultrasound abnormalities: Magni-Manzoni et al. found that, over a 2-year follow-up, one third of clinically inactive patients experienced flares and none of the ultrasound parameters predicted exacerbation [22], and Zhao et al. similarly failed to demonstrate a predictive role of ultrasound in reactivation of disease in children in clinical remission [23]. Misinterpretation of physiological PD signal as pathological has been suggested as one possible explanation for these negative findings [7, 9].

Conversely, other studies have reported a significant prognostic impact of subclinical synovitis. Miotto et al. showed that PD-positive subclinical synovitis was associated with subsequent flares in patients with JIA in clinical remission [24]. De Lucia et al. found that the combination of gray-scale abnormalities and PD signal had a much higher predictive value for relapse than gray-scale abnormalities alone, suggesting that integrated B-mode/PD assessment could help guide therapeutic decisions [25]. Janow et al. observed that a relevant proportion of clinically inactive knees and ankles with ultrasound abnormalities developed clinically active arthritis during follow-up, supporting the idea that ultrasound findings may be incorporated into a treat-to-target strategy in JIA [26]. A recent review further highlighted the potential role of musculoskeletal ultrasound within a treat-to-target approach in JIA, particularly in refining assessment of disease activity and remission [27].

In our cohort, 19.2% of knees without any clinical sign showed sonographic evidence of synovitis, highlighting the frequency of subclinical inflammation. At the same time, a substantial proportion of knees with clinical abnormalities did not show synovitis on ultrasound, underscoring that clinical examination and imaging provide complementary but not interchangeable information. At present, imaging is not routinely included in standard remission criteria for JIA, and there is no widely accepted definition of imaging remission. Our findings emphasize the need to better understand how ultrasound relates to validated clinical composite scores, such as the Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score (JADAS), and how it might be used to refine remission definitions or to identify patients at higher risk of flare.

This study has several limitations. First, we focused on synovitis and did not assess other potentially relevant ultrasonography findings, such as cartilage or osseous structural damage and tenosynovitis. Second, the absence of a healthy control group limited our ability to better define normal pediatric findings, particularly regarding small joint effusions and low-grade physiologic power Doppler (PD) signal. Third, allowing an interval of up to two months between the clinical assessment and the ultrasound examination may have introduced misclassification of disease activity. In addition, because of the retrospective design, detailed information on treatment changes or escalation during this interval could not be consistently retrieved, which may have influenced some clinical–ultrasound associations. Fourth, image interpretation was based on stored static images rather than real-time scanning and cine loops, which may have reduced sensitivity for detecting PD activity and may have led to under- or overestimation of certain abnormalities. Finally, clustering due to multiple knee examinations per patient was not accounted for in agreement analyses, which may have resulted in overestimation of kappa values.

Despite these limitations, our data adds to the growing evidence that ultrasonography is a safe and valuable technique to support the management of children with JIA. It allows assessment of most joints, monitoring of treatment response, guidance of local procedures and detection of subclinical inflammation. The use of pediatric definitions and standardized scoring systems, such as the one proposed by Ting et al. [15], will facilitate comparison across studies and contribute to protocol harmonization. However, larger prospective longitudinal studies, with protocolized and serial ultrasound assessments carefully correlated with clinical data, are needed to clarify the prognostic significance of ultrasound abnormalities and to define the role of systematic ultrasound follow-up alongside clinical examination.

In conclusion, the semi-quantitative ultrasound score for knee synovitis proposed by Ting et al. [15] proved to be highly reproducible in children with JIA, showing almost perfect intra-observer and substantial inter-observer agreement. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate an association between the different grades of this score and specific clinical findings in the knee. These results support the use of standardized semi-quantitative ultrasound scoring as an adjunct to clinical evaluation in the assessment of disease activity in JIA.
