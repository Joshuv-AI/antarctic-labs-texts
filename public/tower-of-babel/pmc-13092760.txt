Estimation of the global number of nicotine vapers in 2025
Authors: Tomasz Jerzyński, Giorgi Mzhavanadze, Gerry V. Stimson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal estimates of adult use of nicotine vaping devices are limited by uneven survey coverage and substantial gaps in country-level data. We therefore developed a structured three-stage approach to synthesize available evidence and generate approximate estimates of the current and projected global number of adult nicotine vaping users.MethodsWe used national survey data on vaping prevalence from 92 countries (2013−2023). In Stage 1, missing prevalence values were imputed using hierarchical groupings based on World Bank income level, UN subregion, and regulatory status. In Stage 2, prevalence was projected to 2025 for all 198 countries using inflation-adjusted national vaping market size as a growth proxy, applying country-specific ratios where available and regional estimates otherwise. In Stage 3, projected prevalence was combined with UN adult population data to estimate global user numbers.ResultsThe estimated global number of adult nicotine vape users reached approximately 129 million in 2025, up from over 50 million in 2018 and just over 114 million in 2023. Countries without survey evidence contribute only ∼18% of this global approximation in 2025. Growth was concentrated in higher-income countries and less restrictive regulatory environments.ConclusionsAdult nicotine vaping is now a substantial but unevenly distributed global phenomenon, concentrated in specific regulatory and economic contexts. While estimates remain subject to meaningful uncertainty, the results indicate that participation in vaping has reached population-relevant levels in multiple regions.

Introduction

The use of nicotine vaping devices has expanded rapidly across many parts of the world over the past decade. Despite their growing commercial presence and regulatory attention, robust and comparable estimates of adult vaping prevalence remain limited at the global level. National surveillance systems differ substantially in coverage, measurement definitions, timing, and methodological continuity, and many countries have never conducted population-based assessments of vaping. As a result, current knowledge of the scale and distribution of vaping relies on fragmented and heterogeneous evidence, leaving substantial gaps in cross-national and global descriptions of nicotine product use [1], [2], [3], [4].

While global coverage remains uneven, several countries have established well-developed surveillance infrastructures that regularly monitor vaping and other nicotine behaviours. The United Kingdom provides one of the most comprehensive examples, with near-continuous adult trend data available from the Smoking Toolkit Study, the Office for National Statistics Opinions, Lifestyle Survey, Action on Smoking and Health surveys and other national monitoring systems [5], [6], [7]. These sources document sustained growth in adult vaping participation over the past decade. Similarly, the United States maintains multiple large-scale surveillance programs—including the National Health Interview Survey, Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, National Youth Tobacco Survey, and the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health longitudinal study—which collectively track evolving patterns of nicotine use across age groups [8], [9], [10], [11]. Reported vaping prevalence increased in several populations during the mid-to-late 2010s, with particularly marked rises among youth following the introduction of salt-based nicotine formulations, although interpretation of earlier increases is complicated by changes in survey instruments.

Other high-income countries have also incorporated vaping measures into routine population health monitoring. In New Zealand, annual surveys provide evidence of increasing vaping participation alongside historically low smoking prevalence [12]. Australia and Canada similarly collect repeated national survey data on nicotine use, documenting moderate increases in vaping despite differing regulatory environments [13], [14]. Across Europe, repeated Eurobarometer surveys and national health studies contribute additional trend evidence, although coverage remains incomplete and definitions are not fully harmonized [15]. These countries provide important empirical anchors for understanding vaping uptake, but they represent only a subset of the global population.

In contrast, many countries lack systematic surveillance of vaping. At present, usable adult prevalence estimates are available for only a minority of jurisdictions worldwide. Such data gaps are not unusual in global public health measurement; for example, international estimates of tobacco smoking prevalence have long relied on modelling approaches to address incomplete national data [4]. However, vaping presents additional challenges. The timing of market introduction varies widely, definitions of “current use” differ across survey instruments, and patterns of uptake are sensitive to regulatory, economic, and technological change. Consequently, constructing globally comparable estimates requires approaches that combine direct survey evidence with structured estimation procedures.

The present study addresses this gap by synthesizing available national survey data and applying transparent estimation methods to produce an updated global description of adult nicotine vaping prevalence. Specifically, we estimate the global number of adult vaping users in 2025, extending prior global assessments through incorporation of newly available country data and updated demographic inputs [2], [16], [17]. The objective is not to evaluate policy effects or behavioral mechanisms, but to provide a reproducible approximation of the global distribution and scale of vaping participation.

This work should be interpreted within the broader context of global nicotine use. Combustible cigarette smoking remains the dominant form of nicotine consumption worldwide, and international smoking prevalence estimates are themselves derived from heterogeneous data sources and model-based extrapolation. These estimates are therefore typically presented as order-of-magnitude indicators rather than precise counts [18]. A similar perspective is adopted here. Compared with smoking surveillance, data on vaping remain sparse and unevenly distributed, resulting in asymmetries in empirical resolution across nicotine product categories. Improving the consistency and coverage of vaping surveillance is therefore important for accurately characterizing the evolving global landscape of nicotine use.

Methodology and processing

Overview of analytic framework

In this study, we employed a three-stage framework to estimate the global number of vapers (GNV) in 2025. We refer to current use of nicotine vaping devices among adults, as defined in the underlying national surveys. Across data sources, current use is operationalized in slightly different ways, definitions are not perfectly commensurate. Some surveys define current use as “use in the past 30 days,” others as “weekly or more often,” and a minority use slightly different recall windows. Rather than assuming equivalence, our approach is based on the epidemiological convention that these definitions broadly capture the same behavioral state—ongoing engagement with the product—and are therefore suitable for pooled descriptive estimation in data-sparse global settings. This approach is consistent with how WHO, GBD, and other global surveillance efforts aggregate heterogeneous tobacco-use measures when harmonized instruments are unavailable.

With respect to product scope, the outcome includes only nicotine-containing electronic vaping devices (also known as e-cigarettes, vapes, nicotine vaping products). Heated tobacco products are explicitly excluded wherever surveys distinguish between product categories, and surveys that conflate heated tobacco with nicotine vaping were not used. Thus, the prevalence estimates are intended to capture aerosolized nicotine delivery via non-combustion liquid-based devices only.

Regarding dual use, individuals who both smoke and vape are included in the prevalence estimates if they meet the survey's definition of current vaping. The analysis does not attempt to distinguish primary vapers from dual users, as this distinction is inconsistently measured across countries and would substantially reduce comparability and coverage. Accordingly, the outcome should be interpreted as current vaping participation, not exclusive or predominant vaping.

We do not claim that all surveys measure the same construct, nor do we interpret the resulting prevalence estimates as precise or interchangeable at fine granularity. The estimates are intended to capture order-of-magnitude differences and global patterns, not to support detailed cross-country comparisons of intensity or frequency of use.

Starting with 92 countries for which survey data are known, Stage 1 imputed baseline prevalence for countries without surveys using deterministic stratified group-mean imputation with predictors for World Bank income group (WBG), UN subregion (UNR), and regulatory status of sales (SALE). Stage 2 projected each country's baseline prevalence to 2025 using the ratio of inflation-adjusted national vaping market size in 2025 to the baseline year; when country market data were unavailable, we substituted the corresponding UN-subregional ratio. The baseline years for imputed countries are theoretical alignment years, not survey years. Stage 3 converted projected prevalence to number of vapers by multiplying by UN Population Prospects adult population (17+) for 2025 and summing across countries.

Adults were operationally defined as individuals aged ≥17 years to accommodate variation in national survey age thresholds. Some population-based surveys report prevalence estimates for populations aged 15+, 16+, or 17+, and restricting the analysis to ≥18 years would have reduced comparability and resulted in the exclusion of otherwise relevant data sources. The objective was not to include adolescents as a target population, but to harmonize adult-relevant prevalence estimates across heterogeneous surveillance systems.

Estimating missing data for countries and populations is a common problem in global public health research. The method of imputation, estimation and projection of incomplete data is widely used in various commonly accepted approaches. Examples include first the WHO tobacco use trends which produces country-level prevalence estimates and projections by combining disparate survey series within Bayesian hierarchical frameworks, borrowing information across regions and time to infer missing country–years. This is directly analogous to our Stage 1 imputation (using structured geography) and Stage 2 projection [4].

A second example is The NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC) for BMI, blood pressure, diabetes and other cardiometabolic risks, which applies Bayesian hierarchical models to derive country-year prevalence where survey coverage is sparse, explicitly imputing missing cells via partial pooling over geography/time/age [19], [20].

A third example is the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) / IHME (DisMod-MR 2.1) Bayesian meta-regression model which enforces internal consistency between incidence, prevalence, remission, and mortality; it imputes missing data using covariates and hierarchical random effects, then yields complete country–year surfaces with uncertainty [21], [22], [23].

All three examples use structured geography (regions/subregions), time trends, and covariates in hierarchical models to fill data gaps (Stage 1-like), produce projections to specific years (Stage 2-like), and often multiply by demographic denominators to produce counts (Stage 3-like).

In epidemiology, sales and administrative data are often used to help infer trends in consumption or prevalence when surveys are sparse or delayed. Alcohol consumption estimation for example combines survey measures with recorded sales, production or trade data and estimated unrecorded alcohol to estimate per-capita consumption and trends [24], [25]. Tobacco consumption has been estimated by triangulating survey measures with sales or tax data to understand consumption levels and trends, including limitations such as illicit trade, and cross-border purchases [26], [27], [28]. Using market value growth ratios as a multiplicative scaler for prevalence mirrors our triangulation strategies—leveraging an economic proxy to update epidemiologic indicators when direct measurements are incomplete.

For clarity, we use a deterministic, stratified group-mean imputation (UNR × WBG × SALE) to fill missing country values, followed by a multiplicative projection step, yielding point estimates without variance propagation in the main analysis.

Missing data mechanism and imputation assumptions

The validity of the imputation procedure depends on assumptions regarding the missing data mechanism [31], [33]. Country-level vaping prevalence data are unlikely to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), as surveillance capacity and data availability are systematically related to economic development and regulatory context. We therefore treat missingness as conditionally Missing at Random (MAR) given the observed covariates used in the model (World Bank income group, UN subregion, and sales regulation status). Under MAR, the probability of missingness is assumed independent of the unobserved prevalence after conditioning on these variables.

We acknowledge that Missing Not at Random (NMAR) mechanisms cannot be excluded—for example, if politically sensitive or rapidly evolving markets are less likely to generate or report survey data. Such departures would introduce residual bias not correctable within the present data structure.

The primary analysis uses deterministic stratified imputation for transparency. To situate the approach within established statistical practice, we assessed robustness using stochastic multiple imputation procedures conceptually aligned with the MICE framework [31], [32], allowing uncertainty from the imputation stage to propagate into final estimates. Results were directionally consistent, though uncertainty intervals widened, reinforcing the interpretation of all estimates as conditional and approximate.

Stage 1. Imputation of missing data

In the first stage of the analysis, we focused on addressing gaps in the dataset concerning country-level prevalence data on adult nicotine vaping. The dataset covered 198 countries, of which only 92 had usable survey-based information on current adult prevalence of nicotine vaping. These surveys had been conducted at different time points, spanning from 2013 to 2025. The distribution of available data across years was uneven: most surveys came from 2023 (41%), with notable peaks also in 2019 (12%) and 2024 (14%). Earlier years contributed only marginally (see Table 2 in Appendix 1). This temporal heterogeneity implied that while data coverage was improving over time, a substantial proportion of countries still lacked direct empirical information. Our task, therefore, was to estimate missing prevalence values for the remaining 106 countries in a systematic and reproducible manner.

Each of these variables was examined for its relationship with observed prevalence. Average prevalence rates were calculated within each category, yielding important descriptive insights. For example, World Bank high-income countries reported the highest mean prevalence at 4.3%, while low-income countries showed a negligible value of 0.6% (see Table 3 in Appendix 2). Similarly, substantial variation was observed across UN regions, reflecting both economic and cultural diversity in vaping uptake. The highest mean prevalence is observed in Australia and New Zealand, followed by Northern Europe. Elevated prevalence is also seen in Northern America and Western Europe (10.5% to 4.4%), indicating that e-cigarette use is most widespread in economically advanced regions. Intermediate prevalence levels are observed in Eastern Europe, Western Asia, and South-eastern Asia (3.2% to 1.8%), consistent with more heterogeneous patterns of adoption. In contrast, most regions in Africa, Latin America, and Southern Asia exhibit very low mean prevalence, frequently close to zero (see Table 4 in Appendix 2). Finally, the status of national regulation proved meaningful: countries with unrestricted sales exhibited the highest average prevalence (3.7%), whereas those with sales bans reported markedly lower values (1.8%) (see Table 5 in Appendix 2).

In addition to the primary predictors described above, we explored the potential contribution of broader macroeconomic and social development indicators as auxiliary predictors for imputation. Specifically, we tested several variables commonly reported within composite indices such as the United Nations Human Development Index (HDI) and the World Bank's World Development Indicators. These included measures such as gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, adult literacy rate, mean years of schooling, urbanization level, internet penetration, and health expenditure per capita. The rationale for testing these variables was that e-cigarette adoption might correlate with general socioeconomic advancement, digital access, or the capacity of health systems to respond to new nicotine technologies. However, bivariate analyses revealed that these macro-level indicators showed only weak or inconsistent correlations with the observed prevalence of e-cigarette use (typically with r < 0.2). In many cases, their effects overlapped substantially with the explanatory power already captured by income group and regional classification. For this reason, and to preserve model parsimony and interpretability, these additional socioeconomic variables were excluded from the final imputation model.

Resulting distribution of estimated prevalence

The imputation progress is summarized in Table 6 in Appendix 2. By the end of the process, the dataset contained prevalence estimates for all countries, thereby enabling further steps of extrapolation and population weighting.

The imputation procedure yielded a fully populated prevalence dataset, with values for every country. The distribution of country-level prevalence indicates that while the global median prevalence is modest, there is substantial heterogeneity, with a long right tail reflecting much higher levels of use in certain countries or regions. The estimates align with descriptive patterns observed in the raw data: higher-income countries and regions with permissive sales laws tend to exhibit higher levels of use of nicotine vaping devices, whereas low-income regions and those with bans show much lower prevalence.

Summary of the imputation stage

These findings highlight the importance of both economic and regulatory factors in shaping the spread of nicotine vaping devices use. The particularly high prevalence in Northern and Western Europe suggests that cultural, economic, and policy environments strongly interact to create conditions favorable to uptake. In contrast, very low prevalence in Southern Asia illustrates the persistence of regional differences where nicotine vaping devices have not gained widespread popularity or have faced stricter controls. By systematically leveraging regional, economic, and regulatory characteristics, we were able to reconstruct a comprehensive dataset that serves as the foundation for subsequent stages of the global estimation process. We do not infer causal effects; results reflect associations observed in the assembled data.

In summary, Stage 1 provided a complete imputed dataset on the prevalence of the use of nicotine vaping devices across 198 countries. This step was essential to enable further extrapolation to 2025 (Stage 2) and population-weighted aggregation at the global level (Stage 3).

Stage 2. Projection to 2025

To project prevalence trends to 2025, we used market size forecasts for vaping products expressed in US dollars (USD), obtained from Euromonitor International, a leading provider of global consumer market data. These forecasts include both historical and projected market values for e-cigarette sales by country. To ensure that values were comparable across years and not distorted by price level changes, all monetary figures were adjusted for inflation and expressed in constant 2015 USD. Inflation adjustment was based on the US Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U; series CPIAUCSL) published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and accessed via FRED, which provides year-on-year CPI conversion factors. This standardization allowed market growth ratios to reflect real changes in market volume and demand, rather than nominal changes due solely to inflation. Under modest, data-sparse settings, the size of the nicotine vaping market (real USD) is a pragmatic proxy for aggregate participation—and hence for changes in prevalence—once inflation is controlled. This multiplicative update lets each country's baseline level reflect its epidemiologic and regulatory context, while the temporal adjustment is driven by relative market change rather than absolute levels.

We multiplied each country's baseline prevalence by its growth ratio (gc, see the full listing in Table 7 in Appendix 3) to obtain projected 2025 prevalence. Extrapolated prevalence for 2025: pc2025=pcbase×gc where gc<1 implies a contraction and gc>1 implies expansion between the base year and 2025. The resulting distribution shows substantial dispersion—countries with already moderate baseline prevalence and high gc rise sharply, while countries with low baseline prevalence and modest gc remain low.

Annual adjustment, inflation correction, regional supplement, monotonicity and result limits confirm the correctness of the process. For each country, the “older” market value corresponded precisely to the year of the baseline prevalence (survey year or theoretical imputed year). This prevented bias from mismatched baselines. Market values were provided in constant USD, avoiding artificial growth due to price level changes. When the country market aggregates, either for base year or for 2025, were missing, we substituted regional aggregates to compute growth ratios. The multiplicative model permits both increases and decreases; no artificial floor/ceiling was imposed on growth ratios or on projected prevalences. Negative values cannot arise by construction.

Interpretation of the 2025 projections table

Table 7 in Appendix 3 lists the projected 2025 prevalence (%) for all countries. All values are model-derived point estimates reported for transparency. Given multiple sources of uncertainty (survey heterogeneity, imputation, and projection), the apparent numerical precision should not be interpreted as measurement precision. Aggregated totals are intended as order-of-magnitude indicators; country-level figures—especially those imputed—should be treated as approximate. It ranges from zero (India, Iran, Cambodia, Sri Lanka, Maldives, Myanmar [Burma]) to ≈16% (New Zealand). The modal mass lies between roughly 1% and 4%, with a long right tail driven by high-growth markets applied to non-negligible baselines (e.g., UK, Canada, parts of the Caribbean, and Oceania).

Notably, countries with contracting or stable markets (e.g., Republic of Korea, Romania, Kazakhstan) yield projections that are close to or below their baseline levels. Countries with very large gc but tiny baselines can remain modest in absolute terms (e.g., some low-income settings), whereas countries with moderate gc but large baselines can dominate the upper tail.

Stage 3. Population-weighted aggregation (adults 17+)

To translate country-level prevalence in 2025 into counts of adult nicotine vaping devices users, we multiplied each country's projected prevalence (from Stage 2) by its UN Population Prospects estimate of the resident population aged 17+ for 2025, then summed across all 198 countries. We preserved the provenance of each country's baseline by carrying a source flag — Surveys (observed) vs Estimations (imputed in Stage 1) — through Stages 2–3 so that final totals could be decomposed by evidence source.

The findings indicate the importance of adjusting survey-based estimates by taking into account baseline year and market growth, with an increase from just over 100 million adults at survey year to about 129 million projected for 2025. At baseline, countries with surveys accounted for 85 million (∼85%), while imputed countries accounted for over 15 million (∼15%). When adjusted for growth, the surveyed countries had a larger contribution, rising to just over 105 million (∼82%), whereas imputed countries rose to approximately 23 million (∼18%).

Countries with direct survey evidence accounted for the majority of the projected global vaping population. This reflects both demographic concentration and earlier documented diffusion of vaping products in settings with established surveillance systems. In contrast, countries without survey-based prevalence estimates contributed a smaller share of the global total, consistent with lower assumed prevalence levels and later stages of market development in many of these settings. These differences should be interpreted as descriptive patterns rather than evidence of systematic divergence in underlying behavioral trends.

To address concerns regarding the interpretability of a single global headline estimate, we place greater emphasis on stratified summaries that reflect the highly uneven distribution of vaping across countries. When results are disaggregated, countries classified as high-income and characterized by relatively permissive regulatory frameworks account for on the order of 50–60 million adult users in 2025, based on model-derived estimates. The remainder of the world combined contributes an additional approximately 60 million users, although this component is subject to greater uncertainty due to heavier reliance on imputation and weaker empirical constraints. China is presented separately because of its structural influence on the global estimate. With an adult population exceeding 1 billion, even small changes in assumed vaping prevalence translate into large absolute differences in projected user numbers. In the present analysis, China contributes approximately 10 million adult users in 2025, corresponding to roughly 8% of the global total. However, this estimate is subject to greater uncertainty than most other large countries due to limited recent survey evidence and a greater reliance on model-based inputs. Taken together, these stratified results imply a global total of approximately 129 million adult users, while underscoring that vaping prevalence is not uniformly distributed worldwide.

Discussion

This study provides an updated descriptive assessment of the global scale and distribution of adult nicotine vaping. In 2020, the number of users worldwide was estimated at approximately 68 million, rising to over 80 million in 2021. By 2023, this figure had already reached about 114 million, reflecting both the diffusion of nicotine vaping devices into new markets and increased uptake in countries with established usage. The current projection for 2025, at approximately 129 million, indicates a slowdown in the rapid growth seen between 2020 and 2023. Evidence from several high-surveillance countries suggests that increases in vaping prevalence have occurred alongside declines in cigarette smoking. Recent syntheses, including the Global State of Tobacco Harm Reduction reports, document such co-occurring trends in multiple settings, as well as in Sweden where declining cigarette use has coincided with increased uptake of oral nicotine products such as snus [3], [29], [30]. In these examples, the overall size of the nicotine-using population appears relatively stable, with shifts occurring primarily in the form of product substitution rather than net increases in use. These observations provide important context for interpreting changes in vaping prevalence within a broader nicotine-use landscape. However, such ecological patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of causal substitution, as smoking trajectories are influenced by multiple concurrent factors, including taxation, regulation, cessation support, and broader social trends.

Most of the estimated global vaping population is concentrated in settings with established surveillance systems and earlier market diffusion, while a substantial number of countries continue to lack direct population-based data. Countries without survey-based prevalence estimates contribute a smaller share of the global total, representing approximately one-fifth of the projected global estimate.

The analysis highlights the persistent imbalance in global nicotine surveillance. Countries without survey-based prevalence estimates contribute a smaller share of the global total, representing approximately one-fifth of the projected global estimate. While combustible cigarette smoking is routinely monitored in most countries, systematic and comparable data on vaping remain limited. As a result, global descriptions of nicotine use patterns are characterized by markedly different levels of empirical resolution across product categories. The present estimates should therefore be interpreted as approximate indicators of scale and distribution rather than precise measurements of prevalence.

An important methodological constraint concerns the interpretation of prevalence as an outcome measure. Prevalence reflects both initiation and duration of use, and similar prevalence levels across countries or time periods may arise from fundamentally different behavioral dynamics. In addition, survey definitions of “current use” vary substantially in recall periods and frequency thresholds, limiting strict comparability. Consequently, cross-national contrasts and projections based on prevalence should be understood as descriptive rather than mechanistic.

The structured estimation framework applied in this study represents a pragmatic response to incomplete global surveillance. Differences observed between countries with and without survey data should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect structural features of measurement infrastructure rather than true differences in vaping uptake. Countries with more developed surveillance systems tend to be higher-income settings where nicotine vaping products were introduced earlier and where behavioral risk monitoring is more routinely implemented. This asymmetry is widely recognized in global health measurement and contributes to residual uncertainty in global estimates [34], [35], [36].

The presence of non-zero vaping prevalence in jurisdictions where retail sales are formally restricted or banned underscores the complexity of interpreting regulatory contrasts. Overall, higher prevalence levels and projected growth tend to be observed in less restrictive regulatory environments, although these patterns should be interpreted descriptively rather than causally. Informal supply channels, cross-border access, and varying levels of enforcement mean that formal legal status does not map directly onto population exposure. Observed regulatory differences in prevalence should therefore be interpreted as contextual associations rather than evidence of policy effectiveness.

Uncertainty in large population countries remains a key determinant of variability in global estimates. In particular, estimates for China exert a strong influence on aggregate totals due to the country's demographic scale [37]. Although the projected number of adult users in China represents a modest share of the global total in relative terms, small changes in assumed prevalence levels can produce substantial absolute shifts in global estimates. This sensitivity underscores the conditional nature of global prevalence approximations and the importance of improving empirical surveillance in large-population settings.

Similarly, discrepancies between survey systems in some countries introduce additional uncertainty. In the United States, for example, differences between government surveillance estimates and alternative polling approaches highlight the potential influence of survey design and institutional context on reported prevalence. Such variation contributes materially to uncertainty in global estimates due to the size of the population involved.

Illicit and informal markets represent a further source of instability. In several countries, vaping products are supplied through unregulated channels, weakening the relationship between regulatory status, market indicators, and observed prevalence. This phenomenon complicates attempts to link market dynamics directly to population-level participation and reinforces the need for cautious interpretation of proxy-based projections.

Although the present analysis focuses on vaping prevalence, emerging evidence from selected high-surveillance countries indicates that increases in vaping participation have occurred contemporaneously with declines in cigarette smoking prevalence. [2] These ecological patterns do not establish causal substitution, as smoking trajectories are influenced by multiple concurrent factors. Nonetheless, they highlight the importance of integrated surveillance frameworks capable of jointly monitoring combustible and non-combustible nicotine use.

The choice of 2025 as a reference year reflects data availability rather than intrinsic epidemiological significance. The principal contribution of this study lies in the transparent synthesis of heterogeneous data sources and the development of a reproducible framework for updating global prevalence estimates as new information becomes available. The approach is intended to support ongoing descriptive monitoring rather than to provide definitive estimates for individual countries or to evaluate policy effects.

Future improvements in global vaping estimation will depend on expanded surveillance coverage, improved access to microdata, and methodological approaches capable of integrating multiple data streams. Hierarchical modelling frameworks and triangulation with additional sources, including shipment data and subpopulation studies, represent promising directions for future research. Until such advances are realized, global estimates of vaping prevalence should be interpreted as approximate indicators of magnitude within a context of substantial uncertainty.

Conclusion

Using a transparent three-stage procedure that combines survey data, structured imputation, proxy-based projections, and population weighting, we estimate approximately 129 million adult nicotine vapers in 2025. This point estimate extends earlier series and indicates sustained and concentrated growth, especially in higher-income settings. The estimate is informative for global monitoring but should be interpreted with caution given uncertainties from imputation choices, heterogeneous survey definitions and years, and the use of market value as a proxy for prevalence dynamics. These constraints do not negate the main signal: worldwide uptake has increased notably, and nicotine vaping has consolidated its position within the global nicotine market.
