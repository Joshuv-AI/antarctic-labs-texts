Human birth tissue products for pathological pain: mechanisms, evidence, and translational outlook
Authors: Chi Zhang, Qing Lin, Qian Huang, Xue-Wei Wang, Jing Liu, Ankit Uniyal, Zhaoli Luo, Xu Cao, Scheffer C. G. Tseng, Yun Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPain is a hallmark of inflammation and tissue injury, particularly following major surgical procedures. Despite its prevalence and clinical impact, pathological pain, defined as persistent pain that impairs function, remains a major unmet medical need. Human birth tissue, including the amniotic membrane and umbilical cord, has long been recognized for its regenerative properties and its ability to support wound healing. Recent studies have identified heavy chain 1 (HC)-hyaluronic acid (HA)/pentraxin 3 (HC-HA/PTX3) as a key matrix component within these tissues. This complex exhibits anti-inflammatory and anti-scarring activities and helps maintain stem cell quiescence.Main bodyEmerging evidence, including our own findings, indicates that human birth tissue-derived products may also modulate pain responses in certain settings, such as post-surgical pain, potentially through mechanisms involving neuronal inhibition and regenerative healing. We summarize the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which human birth tissue-derived products and HC-HA/PTX3 may exert anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. We then highlight preclinical and clinical studies evaluating their potential roles in wound healing and pathological pain. Finally, we discuss translational opportunities, current challenges, and future directions for advancing these biologics within the emerging field of regenerative pain medicine.ConclusionsThis review outlines a framework for potential regenerative pain management using birth tissue-derived products, which may serve as a foundation for developing new therapies for certain pathological pain conditions.

Introduction

Under physiological conditions, pain serves as a protective mechanism that triggers reflex withdrawal responses and behavior modifications in response to harmful stimuli. This process relies on nociceptors, a subset of afferent sensory neurons that densely innervate peripheral tissues, including the skin, joints, muscles, cornea, respiratory tract, and gastrointestinal tract [1–3]. Receptors at peripheral nerve terminals detect noxious stimuli, such as extreme temperatures, pathogens, and immune mediators, and transduce pain signals to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and the brain upon activation [1, 4].

While adaptive in the acute phase, chronic pain is often maladaptive and constitutes a major societal burden [4]. Pathological pain is a persistent condition that disrupts normal functioning and arises from tissue or nerve injury, inflammation, and nervous system abnormalities [3, 5]. However, prolonged use of opioid analgesics is associated with serious side effects and accidental overdose deaths [6, 7]. The high overdose rates prompted the declaration of a national public health emergency in 2017, highlighting the urgent need for alternative, effective non-opioid pain therapies. In this context, regenerative therapies with a favorable safety profile may offer potential avenues for managing pathological pain following surgery or trauma.

Human birth tissue has long been recognized for its capacity to create a supportive environment for wound healing [8–10]. Recent studies have identified heavy chain 1 (HC)-hyaluronic acid (HA)/pentraxin 3 (HC-HA/PTX3) as a key matrix component in human amniotic membrane (AM) and umbilical cord (UC) tissues [8, 10–13]. These bioactive molecules exert potent anti-inflammatory and anti-scarring properties and contribute to stem cell quiescence [8, 14]. Here, we summarize bidirectional interactions between the immune and nervous system in modulating inflammation and pain, and their relevance to regenerative healing in adults. We then discuss the immunomodulatory properties of human birth-tissue-derived products that may support scarless wound healing, focusing on the key matrix component HC-HA/PTX3, which mediates anti-inflammatory, anti-scarring, and neuro-modulatory effects relevant to pain treatment. Finally, we explore the potential for these biologics to advance the emerging field of regenerative pain medicine.

Conditions such as post-surgical pain and osteoarthritis (OA) pain cause significant suffering [15–18]. Based on a comprehensive review of recent findings, we envision that human birth tissue-derived products and HC-HA/PTX3 may serve as a foundational platform for regenerative approaches and as promising non-opioid strategies for managing pathological pain.

Biological context: inflammation and wound healing

Wound healing process

Healing following tissue injury can broadly be divided into regeneration and repair [19, 20]. Regeneration refers to the complete restoration of damaged or lost tissue to its original state, whereas repair refers to the incomplete restoration of damaged tissue that results in scar tissue formation and impaired functionality. The relative contributions of the aforementioned mechanisms depend on the tissue’s regenerative capacity and the extent and nature of the injury [19, 21]. Nevertheless, even in tissues with high regenerative capacity, such as the skin, fibrotic tissue deposition often results at the expense of human tissue regeneration [21, 22].

The wound repair response is similar among mammalian tissues despite differences in the cells that comprise them. The process occurs in three distinct, yet overlapping, stages: (i) inflammation, (ii) proliferation, (iii) remodeling [21]. During the inflammatory phase, the innate immune system is activated. This results in mast cell degranulation [23]. Mediators secreted from mast cells lead to activation and migration of neutrophils into the injured tissue [24], which destroy pathogens through phagocytosis and break down non-viable tissue via proteolysis [25]. Monocyte-derived macrophages are the next cell type to migrate into the wound environment and induce the inflammatory response by differentiating into the M1 macrophage phenotype [26]. These highly phagocytic macrophages remove non-functional host cells, bacteria-filled neutrophils, damaged debris, and any remaining bacteria [26]. Macrophages are then polarized towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype to dampen the inflammatory response and initiate the next phase of healing. The second stage, the proliferative phase, is characterized by the production of the extracellular matrix, which mediates cellular proliferation and migration. New blood vessels form, enabling granulation tissue to fill the wound space. The final phase, remodeling, is characterized by apoptosis of any remaining cells and remodeling of the extracellular matrix, replacing the transient type III collagen scaffold with type I collagen to restore the tissue’s original integrity and tensile strength [27]. This process may last for years and ultimately results in increased scar deposition [28].

Inflammation in wound healing

While inflammation evolved as an adaptive response to restore homeostasis upon injury, this process must be tightly regulated to avoid excessive tissue damage [29]. Certain diseases and conditions (e.g., diabetes mellitus, venous or arterial insufficiency, advanced age, or obesity) can create unfavorable conditions for healing, delaying healing in a self-perpetuating inflammatory state [19, 30]. Macrophages serve as the most important cells in regulating inflammation and maintaining homeostasis. M2 macrophages remove neutrophils via phagocytosis, which helps restore and maintain the anti-inflammatory and immune-tolerogenic milieu [31]. A notable delay of neutrophil apoptosis would not only exacerbate and prolong inflammation but also activate M1 macrophages (Fig. 1), which elicit allogeneic rejection and autoimmune dysregulation [32], through the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [33]. Furthermore, M1 macrophages are ineffective at phagocytosing apoptotic neutrophils [34]. On the other hand, M2 macrophages express higher levels of anti-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, and promote angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and repair through polyamine and collagen synthesis [26, 35, 36]. The M1-M2 phenotypic transition of macrophages is critical for resolving inflammation. In fact, a defective M1-M2 transition is associated with chronic, unresolving inflammation and delayed wound healing [33, 37, 38]. Furthermore, while M1 macrophages inhibit expansion of multipotent progenitor cells (MPCs), M2 macrophages promote their expansion, differentiation and maturation; [39, 40] as such, a timely and coordinated transition is vital for proper regeneration [41, 42].

Cytokines and other microenvironmental signals play a key role in driving the transition from the M1 to the M2 macrophage phenotype. For example, IL-10 is crucial in the transition of macrophage phenotype and function, and its deletion results in delayed regeneration [43]. IGF-1 is abundantly expressed by M2 macrophages and has been shown to promote stem cell differentiation [44]. The accumulation of Treg cells coincides with the transition of the macrophage phenotype, and early depletion of Treg cells after injury has been shown to enhance M1 macrophage activation and delay repair [45, 46]. While invertebrates and lower vertebrates demonstrate remarkable regenerative capabilities, mammals undergo wound repair that results in fibrotic scarring. Several lines of evidence suggest that regeneration in mammals is limited by the extent of inflammation during wound healing [47], as inflammation and scarring are directly linked. In fact, wound healing of the mammalian fetus is “regenerative” in nature and characterized as “scarless.”(reviewed in [48, 49]) The inflammatory response in the embryo is less pronounced and different from that of adults in terms of the types and number of inflammatory cells [50], and produces less IL-6 and IL-8 [51, 52]. In addition to attenuated inflammatory responses, there is downregulation of the pro-scarring response in fetal wound healing [53, 54]. Thus, a reduced inflammatory response not only alleviates pain but also promotes timely and regenerative healing.

Human birth tissue-derived products for regenerative wound healing

Human birth tissue-derived products promote wound healing

Cryopreserved human AM has been used to manage a variety of ocular surface disorders, including burns, infections, corneal scars, autoimmune processes, and post-surgical trauma. These observed clinical benefits are thought to arise from anti-inflammatory, anti-angiogenic, and anti-scarring effects that collectively support wound healing (in vivo, animal models) [55, 56]. For example, cryopreserved AM has been reported to induce apoptosis of neutrophils (in vitro, animal models) [57, 58], monocytes, and macrophages (in vitro, human cells) [59], reduces infiltration of neutrophils [57, 58], macrophages (in vitro, animal models) [60, 61], and lymphocytes (in vitro, animal models) [62], and promotes polarization of M2 macrophages (in vitro, animal models) [63]. Similarly, cryopreserved UC has been used to assist healing in complex diabetic foot ulcers with bone exposure and osteomyelitis (human, clinical study) [64, 65]. In a multicenter, open-label trial, treatment of patients (n = 32) with severe diabetic foot ulcers using cryopreserved human UC (TTAX01) showed promising preliminary results (human, clinical study) [64, 65]. The healing rate following TTAX01 treatment was higher than the standard-of-care success rates reported in the US Wound Registry. In another study, patients with calcaneus fracture were treated with cryopreserved human UC (CLARIX®, TissueTech, Miami, FL) over exposed hardware after open reduction and internal fixation surgery, and a lower incidence of complications (bone infection, wound complications) rate (10%, n = 19) was observed compared to the control group (35%, n = 20, human, clinical study, Table 1) [66]. These findings suggest that cryopreserved UC may contribute to improved wound healing by supporting vascular granulation tissue formation, potentially reducing wound complications, re-operations, and infection rates. In addition, UC has been used to cover exposed neurovascular bundles following robotic radical prostatectomy, thereby accelerating functional recovery of continence (human, clinical study, Table 1) [67–69].

Summary of clinical studies

HC-HA/PTX3 is a key bioactive component in human birth tissue-derived products

Notably, the anti-inflammatory actions of cryopreserved human AM are retained in the water-soluble extract [70, 71]. Through four successive runs of ultracentrifugation in a CsCl gradient with 4 M guanidine HCl, HC-HA/PTX3 is purified from the AM water-soluble extract [72, 73]. In light of previous findings that HC-HA/PTX3 in the cumulus-oocyte complex surrounding the ovulated oocyte is crucial for fertilization [74, 75], a previous study discovered that the same biosynthetic pathway used for ovulation also takes place in the AM. The biosynthetic process of HC-HA/PTX3 involves two major steps: First, the HC-HA complex is formed by the covalent transfer of heavy chains from inter-α-trypsin inhibitor (IαI) to hyaluronan (HA) (> 3000 kDa) through the catalytic action of TSG-6 [76–79]. IαI is composed of two HCs (HC1 and HC2) and a light chain (bikunin), which are linked by a chondroitin sulfate chain [80–85]. IαI, derived from the liver for biosynthesis of the cumulus-oocyte complex, is also endogenously produced by AM epithelial cells and stromal cells [73, 86]. While both HC1 and HC2 are transferred in cumulus-oocyte complex, only HC1 is transferred in the AM. Both TSG-6 and PTX3 are constitutively expressed and are not upregulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines [73, 86].

Since HC‑HA/PTX3 is a biologically derived extracellular matrix component originating from human AM, from a regulatory standpoint, it would most plausibly be considered within the scope of human cell/tissue‑based products (HCT/Ps) or, depending on its method of processing, intended use, and mechanism of action, as a biologic product. However, this interpretation is not based on an explicit regulatory ruling but rather is inferred from how HC‑HA/PTX3 has been characterized in scientific publications, as illustrated in the following sections, which emphasize its human tissue origin, biochemical composition, and biological activity. To our knowledge, there is currently no publicly available FDA designation that specifically addresses HC‑HA/PTX3 as a distinct regulatory entity.

Human HC-HA/PTX3 exerts anti-inflammatory and immune modulatory effects

Delayed clearance of apoptotic neutrophils may contribute to chronic inflammation, as observed in numerous disease conditions [87, 88]. Strikingly, neutrophil apoptosis after activation by fMLP or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can be significantly promoted by human HC-HA/PTX3, but not HA. In addition, HC-HA/PTX3 also promotes macrophage apoptosis after being activated by LPS or IFN-y. Importantly, HC-HA/PTX3 does not affect resting neutrophils and macrophages [72]. Unlike HA, HC-HA/PTX3, whether in water-soluble or substrate (plastic)-immobilized form, enhances the clearance of apoptotic neutrophils by both resting and LPS-activated macrophages. Through these mechanisms, HC-HA/PTX3 may attenuate pro-inflammatory responses of neutrophils and macrophages during innate immune reactions. In addition, HC-HA/PTX3 can also reduce innate immune responses through polarization of the anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, which help to clear apoptotic polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) under physiological conditions (Fig. 1). The classical M1 macrophages activation by IFN-γ and/or LPS is associated with increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-12, IL-23, and TNF-α) and activation of Th1 and Th17 lymphocytes [32]. In contrast, polarization towards M2 macrophages is associated with decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines [89]. HC-HA/PTX3 was found to promote polarization of M2 macrophages when challenged by LPS- or IFN-γ/LPS (in vitro, in vivo, animal models) [90]. As noted above, polarization of M2 macrophages is an important strategy for facilitating wound healing and the resolution of inflammation [91–93].

M2 macrophages also play a key role in activating Treg, which downregulate certain pro-inflammatory responses associated with the adaptive immune system. Upon contact with antigen-presenting cells, CD4+T cells become activated and differentiate into different Th cells or Treg cells [94, 95]. Th1 cells may enhance pro-inflammatory responses by secreting IFN-γ and IL-2, while Treg can effectively suppress this process and reduce inflammation. We have reported that HC-HA/PTX3 suppresses CD4+ T cell activation, but not HA, as evidenced by downregulation of proliferation and Th1 cytokine production (IFN-γ and IL-2) and a significant expansion of CD25+/FOXP3+ Treg cells [90]. Therefore, HC-HA/PTX3 may reduce adaptive immune responses by directly suppressing Th1 cells and promoting the expansion of Treg (Fig. 1). These actions explain why subconjunctival injection of HC-HA/PTX3 could dose-dependently prolong corneal allograft survival [90], and preserves conjunctival and lacrimal tissues with the tear function by suppressing inflammation and scarring in a murine model of graft-versus-host disease following allogeneic bone marrow transplantation (in vivo, animal model) [96]. Because HC-HA/PTX3 is expressed from ovulation [75, 97] to pregnancy [72, 73, 86, 98], we speculate that HC-HA/PTX3 might bear significance in fetal immune tolerance during pregnancy.

The anti-scarring effect of HC-HA/PTX3

A major pathological outcome of wound healing is scarring, which is characterized by terminally differentiated myofibroblasts. Intriguingly, HA has been shown to prevent perineural scar formation, facilitate nerve regeneration, and functional recovery in animal models [99, 100]. Importantly, preliminary findings in patients with long-lasting ischemic wounds showed that either cryopreserved UC tissue or particulate derived from human AM and UC attenuated pain and promoted wound healing (human, clinical study; Table 1) [101–104].

The AM’s anti-inflammatory action may indirectly reduce scar formation, but the AM stroma has been shown to exert a direct anti-scarring effect. Earlier studies showed that culturing human corneal fibroblasts, limbal and conjunctival fibroblasts on the stromal side of cryopreserved AM reduces the expression of TGF-β1–3 and TGF-βRII mRNAs (in vitro) [105, 106]. This mechanism partially explains why myofibroblast differentiation is inhibited by implanting AM into the corneal stromal pocket, and how AM transplantation reduced corneal haze in excimer laser-induced keratectomy in rabbits (in vitro, in vivo, animal models) [57, 58, 107]. Furthermore, if seeded on the stromal side of AM, human [108] and mouse [109] keratocytes can maintain their normal phenotype without nuclear translocation of pSmad2/3 even after serum or TGF-β1 treatment. Importantly, water-soluble AM extract induces cell aggregation and prevents myofibroblasts from expressing α-SMA, suggesting the likelihood of “scarring” reversal (in vitro) [110].

Subsequent studies further showed that HC-HA/PTX3, but not HA, inhibits the TGF-β1 promoter activity in human corneal fibroblasts (in vitro) [72]. Most recently, we found that cultured human corneal myofibroblasts and fibroblasts form aggregates and revert to keratocytes when seeded onto immobilized HC-HA/PTX3 (in vitro) [10]. This phenotypic reversal relies on HC-HA/PTX3-mediated resistance to TGF-β1-induced activation of the canonical Smad2/3 signaling pathway in human corneal fibroblasts. Moreover, the unique mechanism by which HC-HA/PTX3 triggers cell aggregation involves SDF1-CXCR4 signaling, which further promotes phenotypic reversal of human corneal fibroblasts to keratocytes, together with activation of BMP signaling. In addition, HC-HA/PTX3 rejuvenates senescent limbal niche cells (LNCs) to support the quiescence and self-renewal of limbal epithelial stem cells (in vitro) [11]. Interestingly, such reversal of senescent LNCs also starts with cell aggregation mediated by SDF-1/CXCR4 signaling, followed by activation of BMP signaling. These studies provide mechanistic insights into how HC-HA/PTX3 serves as the key active matrix component of human AM and UC to exert anti-inflammatory and anti-scarring actions.

Human birth tissue-derived products for pathological pain control

An increasing number of clinical studies suggest that cryopreserved human AM/UC particulate exhibits pain-inhibitory effects across various pathological pain conditions.

Post-surgical pain

The prevalence of chronic pain after surgery (e.g., limb amputation, knee surgery) is alarmingly high, and causes significant suffering to the individual and brings considerable economic burden to society [15, 111]. Once established, chronic post-surgical pain is challenging to treat and represents a large unmet medical need [4, 112–114]. Aiming to disseminate pain prevention strategies to researchers, clinicians, and patients, the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) announced 2020 as the global year for the prevention of pain.

The application of human birth tissue-derived products was recently studied in a post-surgical pain model [115]. Plantar skin/muscle incision (Brennan model) of post-surgical pain was analyzed with respect to pain hypersensitivity [15]. This model mimics the tissue retraction that is often required during major clinical procedures and may account for prolonged postoperative pain [116, 117]. After receiving a plantar incision, animals quickly developed robust hypersensitivities to punctate mechanical stimulation and radiant heat stimulation on the injured hindpaw. The mechanical and heat hypersensitivities often lasted for over 10 days after surgery. Compared with vehicle treatment, intra-paw injection of human AM/UC particulates, FLO (CLARIX FLO®, TissueTech, Miami, FL), acutely attenuated paw incision-induced heat and mechanical pain (in vitro, in vivo, animal models) [115]. HC-HA/PTX3, a key bioactive component of AM, replicated FLO’s pain-inhibitory effects. While high-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid (HMW-HA), a major constituent of HC-HA/PTX3, has been shown to attenuate inflammatory pain and paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain [118, 119], HC-HA/PTX3 provided even greater pain relief in this post-surgical pain model, suggesting that HC-HA/PTX3 may be a key bioactive component of human birth tissue-derived products for pain relief.

Pain, bleeding, and delayed wound healing are common morbidities after surgery (Fig. 2A), such as tonsillectomy. A randomized clinical study of 60 patients found that human AM, applied as a biologic dressing over the tonsillar bed, reduced pain and facilitated healing after tonsillectomy (human, clinical study, Tables 1 and 2) [120]. Pain is also a major problem after a cesarean section. A randomized controlled double-blind trial of 90 term pregnant women (38–42 weeks) who had elective cesarean section received a wound dressing with human AM in the treatment group or simple gauze in the control group. Patients with AM dressing showed significantly lower pain levels and required less analgesics than the control group 24 h after cesarean section (human, clinical study, Tables 1 and 2) [121]. Similar findings were reported in another study that examined whether human AM may be used as a biological dressing to improve wound healing and attenuate postoperative pain (human, clinical study; Tables 1 and 2) [111]. Patients who received the treatment over their palatal donor site after free gingival graft surgery demonstrated accelerated wound healing and better post-operative pain relief than the control group, especially at the early post-surgery stage [111].

OA pain

OA is a degenerative joint disease characterized by articular cartilage degradation and low-grade inflammation [17, 122, 123]. In a pilot study of 20 patients with knee OA, a single ultrasound-guided intra-articular injection of FLO (50 mg) significantly reduced pain from baseline and improved physical function and stiffness, with no adverse effects (human, clinical study) [122]. These findings were corroborated in a larger cohort of 42 patients with moderate-to-severe knee OA, who showed sustained symptomatic improvement for an average of 12.1 ± 4.5 months following injection [17]. In this study, a single 100 mg intra-articular injection led to 86% of patients reporting their symptoms as “very much improved” or “much improved” within 3 months (human, clinical study, Tables 1 and 2) [17].

Notably, intra-articular injection of extracellular vesicles derived from UC mesenchymal stromal cells also reduced inflammation and promoted cartilage regeneration of knee OA in mice. Mechanistically, it may exert anti-inflammatory effects by reversing IL-1β-induced inflammation in chondrocytes and by modulating macrophage cytokine profiles, including downregulating IL-1β and TNF-α and upregulating IL-10 and ARG1 (in vitro) [124]. Moreover, another study characterized reproducible, clinical-grade batches of small extracellular vesicles derived from UC mesenchymal stromal cells. In an OA mouse model, intra-articular injection of this product induced hyaline cartilage regeneration, as validated by histological and µCT analyses (human, clinical study, Tables 1 and 2) [125]. The initial safety assessment of the clinical-grade product administered intra-articularly in a first-in-human study showed no adverse effects at 12 months [125]. Most recently, in vitro and in vivo studies have further demonstrated that extracellular vesicles derived from UC mesenchymal stromal cells reduce the inflammatory response in articular cartilage and promote its regeneration [123]. Importantly, a subsequent randomized, double-blind, ascending-dose clinical trial shows that this treatment improved knee osteoarthritis patients without causing notable adverse effects [123].

Other pathological pain conditions

In patients with discogenic low back pain refractory to conservative treatment, a single intra-discal injection of AM/UC particulate FLO (50–100 mg) provided notable pain relief and limited degenerative progression, with no reported complications in 11 patients (human, clinical study) [126]. In another study, a single intra-articular injection of 50 mg AM/UC particulate also reduced back pain due to facet joint syndrome and minimized opioid use in nine patients, without adverse effects (human, clinical study, Tables 1 and 2) [127]. Clinical applications of UC mesenchymal stem cells have also shown promise in treating discogenic low back pain, often following spinal surgery. After injection of these cells into degenerated discs in two patients, it led to immediate and sustained pain relief and functional improvement lasting up to 2 years, suggesting a non-opioid, regenerative approach to managing low-back pain (human, clinical study) [128].

A retrospective case study reported that acute treatment with a self-retained cryopreserved AM (PROKERA®, Bio-Tissue, Miami, FL) significantly alleviated neuropathic corneal pain (NCP), improving pain severity by 72.5 ± 8.4% after 6.4 ± 1.1 days of retention, along with increased corneal nerve density (human, clinical study, Tables 1 and 2) [129]. Similarly, a retrospective review found that ultrasound-guided, local perineural injections of FLO in the lower extremity led to clinical improvements in patients with painful peripheral neuropathy—including recalcitrant diabetic, idiopathic, and chemotherapy-induced types—after an average of 2.7 injections, without adverse events (human, clinical study) [130].

Overall, the evidence base is heterogeneous and includes four randomized trials, several prospective or retrospective comparative studies, early-phase safety trials, and multiple case series or case reports, with sample sizes ranging from 1 to 566 patients and covering diverse indications such as wound healing, postoperative recovery, musculoskeletal disorders, and neuropathic pain.

While emerging clinical studies suggest potential benefits of human birth tissue–derived products across multiple pain-related conditions, interpretation of these findings requires consideration of variability among preparations. Across the available studies, considerable differences exist in tissue source, processing methods (e.g., cryopreserved or dehydrated products), formulation (particulate or injectable), dosing, and delivery approaches. These factors may influence the biochemical composition and biological activity of the products, including extracellular matrix components and anti-inflammatory mediators, thereby complicating cross-study comparisons and limiting generalizability. Greater standardization in product characterization and clinical trial design will be important to clarify therapeutic mechanisms and clinical efficacy.

Possible mechanisms underlying pain inhibition by human birth tissue products

Direct neuronal inhibitory effect

A recent study showed that the analgesic effect of FLO, which acutely attenuated paw incision-induced heat and mechanical hypersensitivity, may be partly mediated by reduced activation of nociceptive DRG neurons in response to peripheral noxious stimulation, as shown by in vivo calcium imaging (in vitro, in vivo, animal models) [115]. Given its higher purity and greater water solubility than FLO, HC-HA/PTX3 was subsequently selected to further investigate the underlying cellular mechanisms. In vitro calcium imaging revealed that HC-HA/PTX3 significantly attenuated the intracellular calcium ([Ca2+]i) increase induced by proalgesic agents such as capsaicin, cinnamaldehyde, and β-alanine in cultured DRG neurons. In addition, electrophysiological recordings of cultured DRG neurons showed that HC-HA/PTX3 induced membrane hyperpolarization, altered intrinsic properties, and inhibited INav and HVA-Ica. in nociceptive DRG neurons through CD44-dependent cytoskeleton rearrangement and interaction with these membrane ion channels [115].

In a separate study, prolonged exposure of cultured DRG neurons to HC-HA/PTX3 for 24 h induced 2,047 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), whereas an acute 10-min treatment produced no detectable transcriptional changes (in vitro) [131]. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis identified over 50 DEGs closely associated with pain and analgesia, with pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) emerging as the key upregulated hub gene. POMC encodes the opioid peptide β-endorphin, a principal endogenous agonist of the µ-opioid receptor (MOR) that critically modulates nociceptive signaling [132, 133]. Correspondingly, HC-HA/PTX3 significantly increased POMC expression in DRG neurons. Functionally, the HC-HA/PTX3–induced reduction in capsaicin-responsive neurons was reversed by CTOP, a highly selective MOR antagonist, demonstrating that HC-HA/PTX3 upregulates POMC signaling, thereby contributing to the observed neuronal inhibition effect [131].

Accordingly, human birth tissue-derived products could exert potent neuronal inhibitory effects by modulating multiple cellular and molecular pathways in DRG neurons (Fig. 2B). They significantly attenuate intracellular calcium responses to proalgesic stimuli, induce membrane hyperpolarization, and suppress key ion currents involved in neuronal excitability. At the level of gene expression, HC-HA/PTX3 upregulates POMC, thereby enhancing β-endorphin production and activating µ-opioid receptor (MOR) signaling. This cascade reduces nociceptive neuron responsiveness, ultimately mediating analgesic effects and neuronal inhibition.

Immune modulation

Pathological pain is a conspicuous hallmark of inflammation and tissue injury, yet non-resolving inflammation impairs healing and is an often-overlooked threat to the success of pain therapies. Non-resolving inflammation is also the typical characteristic of a number of chronic pain conditions [87, 88, 134, 135]. For example, neuropathic pain often involves inflammation and injury to the nervous system, which results in ectopic discharges, neuromas, disorganized nerve regeneration, and glial activation, leading to increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In particular, early neurophysiological changes following tissue inflammation and nerve injury may trigger long-term pathological changes and the generation of peripheral and central neuronal sensitization, leading to chronic pain [136–139]. Delayed wound healing and non-resolving inflammation also contribute to prolonged postoperative pain [140]. Injury alters the extracellular matrix and surrounding milieu, both of which are vital for wound healing and the maintenance of normal cellular function [141–143].

The immune system has long been recognized to play a complex role in the generation of pain in association with the nervous system. Macrophages, neutrophils, and other immune cells release cytokines, lipids, and growth factors in response to inflammation and tissue injury. While these inflammatory signaling molecules, particularly IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, IL-17 A, and IL-5, act directly on nociceptor neurons to produce pain [144, 145], they also act on immune cells to enhance the inflammatory response by recruiting leukocytes and plasma proteins to the inflammatory loci through vasodilation [144–146]. The increased number of inflammatory mediators that results from this positive feedback mechanism reduces the nociceptors’ threshold to fire action potentials, resulting in increased sensitivity and responsiveness at the peripheral ends of sensory nerve fibers [114]. Elevated and continuous nociceptor activation results in peripheral sensitization, which is consequently responsible for the transition from acute to chronic pain [5, 113]. Given the relationship between nociceptors and immune cells, downregulation of inflammatory stimuli would result in a negative feedback mechanism that would significantly reduce the inflammatory response as well as nociceptor activation and sensitization. Thus, reducing inflammatory stimuli is a critical first step in resolving inflammation and ultimately pathological pain.

It is plausible that chronic inflammation might be a leading contributing factor to persistent pain and that pain may further aggravate inflammation via neurogenic processes [147]. Traditionally, neurons were regarded as postmitotic cell types responsible for chemical and electrical excitability. Its role in regenerative healing was not apparent beyond a general appreciation that nerve integrity governed the homeostasis of a given tissue, as surgical denervation frequently resulted in poor wound healing. Thus, strategies to break the vicious cycle between pain and inflammation may be desirable and novel for the effective management of certain chronic pain conditions. Notably, HC-HA/PTX3 effectively controls inflammation and prevents scarring as if it weeds and prunes the soil. Our flow cytometry study showed that plantar incision induces profound recruitment of immune cells and mast cell aggregation at the wound site. Importantly, recruitment of CD45+ immune cells, including neutrophils and monocytes, was markedly reduced by the birth tissue product treatment compared with control (unpublished observations: in vitro, in vivo, and animal models). Accordingly, through immunomodulatory effects [20], peri-injury treatment with human AM/UC particulate matter or HC-HA/PTX3 may be a promising preemptive strategy to limit prolonged postoperative pain and reduce drug consumption, healthcare costs, and overall burden. In contrast to conventional anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., glucocorticosteroids, non-steroid anti-inflammatory agents, cyclosporine/tacrolimus, humanized antibodies), which only target a specific action of one particular type of inflammatory/immune cells, HC-HA/PTX3 exerts broad and extensive anti-inflammatory actions by modulating multiple cell types including neutrophils, macrophages and lymphocytes in both innate and adaptive immune responses (Figs. 1 and 2). Since scarring threatens the SC well-being and facilitates tissue/organ failure [148, 149], the anti-scarring action of HC-HA/PTX3 mitigates such a threat so as to maintain the progenitor status.

A potential novel regenerative pain medicine

Current pharmacologic options for pain management include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents (NSAIDs), opioid receptor agonists, and various adjuvant analgesics. Although widely used, these agents are limited by dose-dependent side effects, the risk of tolerance, and toxicity associated with long-term use. Consequently, the development of new treatment strategies with improved safety profiles remains a major priority in pain research and clinical care. An increasing number of studies suggest that HC-HA/PTX3-containing birth-tissue-derived products may have beneficial effects in certain pathological pain conditions, as summarized above.

HC-HA/PTX3-based intervention may differ from many conventional pharmacotherapies, which often alleviate specific pain symptoms (e.g., tactile allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia) but do not directly address the underlying biological processes that contribute to persistent pain. The inability of current treatments to modify the drivers of chronic pain likely contributes to their inconsistent or short-lived therapeutic effects. Chronic low back pain illustrates this challenge: degeneration of the intervertebral disc is a major contributing factor, yet current strategies, ranging from conservative treatments to surgery, are primarily palliative and may not alter the causes of underlying degeneration, with some surgical procedures potentially accelerating the degeneration. In contrast, the emerging strategy of using HC-HA/PTX3 as an extracellular matrix-based biologic aims to support regenerative tissue responses. By modulating inflammation and facilitating wound healing and tissue repair processes (Fig. 2C), HC-HA/PTX3 may offer a modality that addresses biological contributors to certain chronic pain states, including inflammatory pain, degenerative OA pain, ocular pain, and post-surgical pain. Hence, the use of birth tissue product to support concurrent repair of the damaged annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus tissue may help decelerate disc degeneration and facilitate regenerative processes—potentially providing a disease-modifying approach for conditions such as low back pain, a leading cause of global morbidity.

Limitations and future directions

Based on a comprehensive review of available evidence, human birth tissue-derived products and the matrix component HC-HA/PTX3 may modulate pathological pain conditions, including post-surgical pain, OA pain, and low-back pain, through mechanisms involving attenuation of neuronal hyperexcitability, modulation of inflammation, and support of regenerative tissue responses (Fig. 2).

However, current mechanistic and clinical evidence remains heterogeneous and largely exploratory.

Importantly, the available clinical literature is subject to several methodological limitations. Many published studies are observational, including retrospective analyses, small prospective cohorts, and case series, often with limited sample sizes and short follow-up periods. Randomized controlled trials remain relatively few, and several studies lack blinding or appropriate comparator groups, which may introduce potential selection and reporting biases. In addition, variability among product preparations—including differences in tissue source, processing methods, formulation, dosing, and route of administration—further complicates cross-study comparisons and interpretation of efficacy. Future studies should prioritize randomized, blinded, and adequately powered clinical trials using standardized pain and functional outcome measures. Head-to-head comparisons with established therapies such as corticosteroids, hyaluronic acid, and platelet-rich plasma will also be important to better contextualize efficacy and durability.

Further studies are needed to delineate the underlying multimodal mechanisms of action of human AM/UC particulates and HC-HA/PTX3 in alleviating the aforementioned pathological pain conditions. The timing of intervention also warrants careful consideration. Preclinical data raise the possibility that HC-HA/PTX3, if applied during a critical window prior to central sensitization following injury or trauma, may influence inflammatory and degenerative processes that contribute to chronic pain. However, this concept remains hypothetical and requires systematic evaluation in appropriately designed preclinical models and prospective clinical studies.

Importantly, mechanistic interpretations derived from specific preparations (e.g., HC-HA/PTX3 purified from cryopreserved amniotic membrane under defined conditions) should not be assumed to apply uniformly across all human birth tissue–derived products, as variations in tissue source, processing, and formulation may alter biological composition and functional activity.

Long-term safety also represents an important knowledge gap. While available reports suggest favorable short-term tolerability, data regarding repeated dosing, durability of effect, immunogenicity, and rare adverse events remain limited. Longitudinal follow-up and post-treatment registries will therefore be necessary to establish robust long-term safety profiles across different indications and patient populations.

More recently, regenerative medicine strategies using mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) derived from bone marrow, adipose tissue, and other sources have emerged as potential approaches for managing chronic pain in several diseases (reviewed in [150–153]). These approaches are based on the demonstrated clinical efficacy of MSCs in treating various inflammatory conditions [154, 155]. However, further investigations is needed to determine whether HC-HA/PTX3 represents a principal mediator underlying the therapeutic effects attributed to MSCs. This hypothesis is supported by evidence that MSCs secrete TSG-6 [156–159] and PTX3 [160], both of which are essential for the biosynthesis of HC-HA/PTX3. In addition, secretion of an HA-containing glycocalyx by MSCs plays an important role in their immunomodulating effects [161]. TSG6−/− MSCs can restore their cell morphology, proliferation, and function when cultured on an HA matrix scaffold in a medium supplemented with TSG6 and PTX3 [162].

If HC-HA/PTX3 proves to be a principal mediator of these effects, MSC-based therapy could potentially be simplified by direct delivery of HC-HA/PTX3. Alternatively, HC-HA/PTX3 may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs transplantation, particularly given that effective control of inflammation may improve MSC engraftment and function. Accordingly, HC-HA/PTX3 may serve as a promising platform technology for the delivery of multiple therapeutics, either alone or in combination with MSCs, in regenerative medicine applications, particularly for the prevention and treatment of pathological pain conditions.

Summary

We propose a conceptual framework to explore regenerative approaches to pain management by incorporating current knowledge of birth tissue-derived products and of HC-HA/PTX3 as a potential regenerative matrix [56, 163]. These biologics may hold promise in the broader field of regenerative medicine and could serve as a platform for developing future therapeutic strategies targeting specific pathological pain conditions. To inform the rational design of clinical studies, there remains a critical need for basic and translational research to define optimal formulations, routes of administration, dosing parameters, and safety profiles for both short- and long-term use. Additional work is required to elucidate the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying the observed effects of HC-HA/PTX3 across different pain states. Such studies will be essential for evaluating the translational potential of these biologics and guiding their responsible advancement into clinical investigation.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Databases searched: PubMed and Web of Science.

Time window: All available literature up to the year 2025 was searched.

Keywords/MeSH: Terms related to amniotic membrane, umbilical cord tissue, HC-HA/PTX3, biologic allografts, pain, and wound healing were used in various combinations.

Language restriction: English-language publications only.

Inclusion criteria for clinical studies: use of human birth-tissue–derived products (AM, UC, AM/UC particulate, cryopreserved AM, cryopreserved UC, HC-HA/PTX3–containing products, or UC-MSC/EV products); reporting on safety, pain-related outcomes, functional outcomes, wound healing outcomes, or post-surgical recovery.

Exclusion criteria: animal-only studies without mechanistic relevance; non–birth-tissue biologics.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The current body of evidence supporting the use of human birth tissue–derived products and HC-HA/PTX3 in pain-related indications spans preclinical models and early-stage human studies. Several sources of potential bias may exist in the interpretation of these findings. To minimize potential bias, information collection and analysis were conducted by investigators independent of the product sponsors. All interpretations of findings reviewed in this work were based on published objective data, and alternative explanations were considered.

Preclinical studies, including in vitro assays and in vivo animal models, provide mechanistic insight into immunomodulatory and neuronal effects but are inherently limited by species differences, simplified experimental systems, and controlled injury paradigms that may not fully recapitulate human pathological pain conditions. Outcome measures in animal models often rely on stimulus-evoked behaviors, which may not directly translate to patients’ subjective pain experiences.

Human evidence to date largely consists of observational studies, case series, and small open-label trials. These studies are susceptible to selection bias, lack randomization and blinding, have limited sample sizes, and lack standardized control groups. In addition, heterogeneity in product formulations, routes of administration, dosing regimens, and clinical endpoints may complicate cross-study comparisons. Many studies also focus on short-term outcomes, limiting conclusions regarding the durability of effect and long-term safety. Importantly, few studies were designed primarily to assess pain as a prespecified primary endpoint, and validated pain outcome measures are not uniformly applied. Together, these factors underscore the need for well-powered, randomized, controlled clinical trials with standardized outcome measures to evaluate efficacy, safety, and mechanism of action more rigorously. Until such data are available, conclusions regarding therapeutic benefit should be interpreted cautiously in the context of exploratory, hypothesis-generating research.

Ethical declaration and safety considerations

All experimental protocols were approved by the Johns Hopkins University Animal Care and Use Committee (Baltimore, MD, USA) and adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals to ensure ethical standards and minimize animal discomfort. Human birth tissue–derived products are generally reported to be well tolerated in short-term clinical use; however, systematic safety data remain limited because most studies are observational and uncontrolled. Reported adverse events are infrequent and typically mild (e.g., local irritation), but many studies were not designed to capture rare or delayed complications rigorously. Potential risks include infection transmission, immunogenicity, inflammatory flares, fibrosis, ectopic tissue responses, and unintended modulation of tissue remodeling, particularly with injectable or repeated applications. Birth tissues are obtained with informed consent and screened in accordance with established regulatory guidelines, including donor eligibility assessment and testing for transmissible pathogens. In the United States, products meeting criteria for minimal manipulation and homologous use are regulated as human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue-based products under 21 CFR Part 1271, whereas non-homologous or injectable applications may require investigational or biologics oversight. Long-term pharmacovigilance, registries, and extended follow-up studies are essential to define durability and safety across patient populations [164].
