Introduction

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TCM) is an acute, reversible cardiomyopathy often mimicking acute coronary syndrome (ACS), characterized by transient regional systolic dysfunction with typical apical ballooning and basal sparing of the left ventricle.1 It predominantly affects postmenopausal women and is frequently triggered by emotional or physical stress.2,3 Although uncommon in males, recent reports suggest increasing recognition in younger patients and males, often with atypical triggers. This report presents an unusual case of TCM in a middle-aged male precipitated by work-related stress, aiming to broaden the understanding of TCM triggers and demographic spectrum.

Case history

A 55-year-old male with a 15-year history of Type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia presented to the ED with mild central chest discomfort described as a burning sensation. The symptom developed as he was going to sleep after an unusually long and stressful day spent moderating and organizing a sporting event, which concluded at ∼2:00 a.m. The discomfort persisted for ∼10 h, prompting his ED visit.

Examination

On presentation, the patient was cooperative and not in distress. Physical examination showed normal vital signs, clear chest to auscultation, and normal heart sounds.

Differential diagnosis/investigation/treatment

Initial high-sensitivity troponin T was elevated at 526 ng/L, with subsequent values of 434 and 424 ng/L. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus rhythm, left axis deviation, and T-wave inversions in anterolateral leads (Figure 1), and the patient was admitted with a diagnosis of non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and guideline-directed medical therapy was initiated, including dual antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, and beta-blocker. The patient was already on angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) for his hypertension and was continued on that.

Admission electrocardiography showing normal sinus rhythm, left axis deviation, and T-wave inversions in anterolateral leads.

Transthoracic echocardiography on admission demonstrated a reduced LVEF of 44%, with diffuse hypokinesia of the apical and mid-ventricular segments, and relative sparing of the basal segments—consistent with apical ballooning (Figure 2).

Admission transthoracic echocardiogram (apical four-chamber view) demonstrating reduced left ventricular ejection fraction with hypokinesia of the mid- and apical segments and relative basal sparing, compatible with apical ballooning.

The patient underwent urgent invasive coronary angiography, which revealed no evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease or plaque rupture (Figure 3), effectively ruling out an acute atherothrombotic event. Following angiography, differential diagnosis included myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), myocarditis, demand ischaemia, and TCM, which was strongly suspected given the extreme stressor, characteristic wall motion pattern on imaging, and the troponin rise disproportionate to the patient’s clinical stability. Myocarditis was considered less likely due to the lack of prodromal symptoms or fever, nor could demand ischaemia alone explain the focal wall motion abnormalities observed. Consequently, antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy was discontinued, and the focus shifted to supportive heart failure management with a beta-blocker, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), and an ACE inhibitor.

Coronary angiography showing unobstructed coronary arteries without evidence of culprit stenosis or plaque rupture.

Outcome and follow-up

A repeat echocardiogram 3 days later showed near-complete normalization of LV function (EF ∼ normal range), with only mild residual hypokinesia at the apical cap (Figure 4). Follow-up TTE at 6 months revealed complete recovery of wall motion abnormalities and normal systolic function At that time, the SGLT2 inhibitor was discontinued following normalization of ventricular function on repeat imaging, whereas the beta-blocker and ACE inhibitor were continued beyond 6 months for hypertension management.

Conclusion

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy should remain a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with ACS-like symptoms, even among middle-aged men with cardiovascular risk factors. Recognition of non-traditional stressors, such as prolonged occupational strain, is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and ensure appropriate management of this reversible condition.

Discussion

This case meets the diagnostic criteria for TCM as per the International Takotsubo Diagnostic Criteria (InterTAK) including (i) transient regional wall motion abnormalities (apical ballooning) extending beyond a single epicardial vascular distribution, (ii) the presence of an emotional/physical stressor, (iii) new ECG abnormalities and elevated cardiac biomarkers, and (iv) the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease or evidence of acute plaque rupture on angiography. The complete recovery of ventricular function, confirmed on follow-up echocardiography, further solidifies the diagnosis.

The underlying pathophysiology is thought to be driven by a surge in catecholamines induced by the intense stressor. This surge can lead to direct myocardial stunning, microvascular dysfunction, and epicardial spasm, resulting in the characteristic pattern of wall motion abnormalities in the absence of obstructive coronary disease. The mid- and apical segments of the left ventricle are particularly vulnerable to these catecholamine-mediated effects due to a higher density of beta-adrenoceptors.

What makes this case particularly interesting is the patient’s demographic profile and the specific trigger for his condition. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy is most commonly observed in postmenopausal women, with men accounting for <10% of cases. Although TCM is typically triggered by intense emotional stress, such as grief, physical and occupational stressors are also increasingly recognized as potential contributors. In this case, the patient’s prolonged work-related stress likely caused a surge in catecholamines, leading to myocardial stunning and subsequent cardiac dysfunction.

The recognition of occupational strain as a primary catalyst for TCM is an evolving area of cardiovascular research. While traditional triggers often involve domestic bereavement, recent literature highlights that work-related stressors specifically those involving high stakes, long hours, and sleep deprivation can be equally cardiotoxic. Case reports have documented similar occurrences in high-pressure environments; for instance, cases have been reported in teachers following intense workplace conflict4 and in healthcare workers during the peak of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.5 These cases, like the one presented here, often share a common thread of exhaustion-induced vulnerability.

This case therefore supports the growing recognition of TCM in atypical populations and underscores the need to consider occupational or chronic stress as valid triggers, especially in males with underlying cardiovascular risk factors. It highlights the importance of eliciting a detailed occupational history in all patients presenting with acute cardiac syndromes.

Key clinical message

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, although more common in older women, can affect middle-aged men with cardiovascular risk factors. Physical or emotional work-related stress may trigger a presentation that mimics NSTEMI or MINOCA, with troponin elevation and regional wall motion abnormalities, often prompting invasive angiography. Recognition of the characteristic imaging pattern and absence of obstructive coronary disease is crucial, as TCM typically shows rapid and complete recovery of ventricular function with supportive therapy.
