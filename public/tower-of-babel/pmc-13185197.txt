Sex differences in behaviors, neuronal activation, and gut-microbiota-metabolic axis in a repeated nitroglycerin-induced chronic migraine model
Authors: Cancan Chen, Jiao He, Zhenjie Ma, Weinan Na, Ya Cao, Bozhi Li, Zizi He, Huijuan Yuan, Han Xiao, Shuqing Wang, Ruozhuo Liu, Shengyuan Yu, Li Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is a debilitating primary headache disorder characterized by significant sex differences in epidemiology, clinical features, comorbidities, and treatment response, yet the underlying mechanisms remain obscure. This study aims to dissect the sex difference map in chronic migraine models and explore the underlying mechanisms driving these specific phenotypes.MethodsA mouse model of chronic migraine was established by intraperitoneal injection of nitroglycerin (NTG, 10 mg/kg, 5 times in 9 days). Both male and female mice were included. Mechanical withdrawal thresholds (MWTs) were measured to assess migraine-related hyperalgesia. Anxiety-like behaviors and acute malaise were evaluated using the Open Field Test (OFT), Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), and Light-Dark Box (LDB). Neuronal activation was mapped via c-Fos immunofluorescence. Medullary TNF-α levels were quantified by ELISA. 16S rRNA sequencing and targeted metabolomics characterized the gut microbiota composition and fecal metabolites, respectively.ResultsAlthough male and female mice showed comparable responses in acute and chronic mechanical pain hyperalgesia, significant sex differences were observed in other migraine-like behaviors: only male mice exhibited significant acute-phase motor inhibition and anxiety-like behavior in the chronic phase. These phenotypic differences coincided with a male-restricted elevation of medullary TNF-α following NTG administration. In terms of regional brain activation, the AP stood out in males for its markedly elevated c-Fos cell. This sexual dimorphism extended to the gut-brain axis: while the female NTG group microbiome was enriched with g-Akkermansia, the male profile was dominated by g-Parabacteroides. Metabolomic profiling identified 29 differential metabolites (NTG vs. VEH) in males but only 3 in females. In males, these alterations were primarily enriched in the pentose and glucuronate interconversions and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids pathways that are involved in neuropsychiatric disorders.ConclusionThe NTG-induced chronic migraine model exhibits significant sexual dimorphism, with male mice showing greater sensitivity to acute discomfort and chronic emotional comorbidities. This exploratory study identifies the specific activation of the AP brain region, medullary neuroinflammation, and more severe disturbances in the gut microbiota-metabolic axis as potential contributors to this sex-dependent phenotype.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02360-9.

Introduction

Migraine is a debilitating primary headache disorder affecting over 10% of the global population [1]. Epidemiological studies highlight a profound sexual dimorphism in its prevalence, symptom profile, and prognosis [2]. The prevalence of migraine in females is approximately three times higher than in males, with a greater tendency to progress into chronic state, often closely linked to menstrual cycle [3–5]. With regard to clinical characteristics, many studies indicate that women report longer migraine attack duration [6], pain intensity and attack frequency are largely comparable in men [7, 8]. In terms of comorbidities, research indicates that men are more likely to have somatic comorbidities, while women are more prone to psychiatric comorbidities [9]. However, some studies have also shown that men are more likely to report anxiety or depressive symptoms compared to their same-sex counterparts without headaches [10]. Furthermore, clinical studies have shown that small-molecule CGRP receptor antagonists (gepants) have shown superior efficacy in female patients, suggesting that biological sex significantly influences pharmacological response [11]. The above suggests that the clinical differences in migraine between sexes may be driven by distinct sex-related pathophysiological mechanisms.

Previous research has demonstrated that the biological mechanisms underlying pain exhibit striking sexual dimorphism [12, 13]. In neuropathic pain models, the immune mechanisms mediated by Pannexin-1 channels show distinct sex-specific pathways: allodynia in female models is primarily driven by leptin secreted from CD8 T cells, whereas in males, it is predominantly mediated by microglia-derived vascular endothelial growth factor A. This sexual dimorphism extends to the pathophysiology of migraine [14]. Evidence suggests that sex hormone regulation of the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and its receptor system is highly sex-dependent. There is a clear sex difference in migraine pathology, with estrogen levels acting as a pivotal modulator where surges correlate with aura symptoms and drops trigger standard attacks [15]. Neuroimaging studies have elucidated distinct activation patterns in pain-processing brain networks between the two sexes [16, 17]. Even in rat models, nitroglycerin-induced c-Fos activation displays clear sex-specific signatures [18]. Nevertheless, the majority of current preclinical research uses male mouse models as the basis for its experimental design. This single-sex approach may limit the applicability and clinical potential of research findings [19]. Consequently, it is crucial to conduct a thorough investigation into sex differences and their underlying molecular mechanisms in migraine. As well as deepening our understanding of the pathophysiology of migraine, such efforts will provide a critical foundation for developing precision-based, sex-specific therapeutic strategies.

The gut-brain axis has emerged as a focal point in understanding neurological pathologies [20–22], previous studies have demonstrated that the gut microbiota and its metabolites, short-chain fatty acids and aromatic amino acid derivatives, can regulate neuroinflammation and neuronal activity through the bloodstream or the vagus nerve [23]. Previous research has shown that the gut microbiota may influence migraine-related pain [24]. Conversely, the gut microbiota is also altered in a rat model of migraine [25]. In this study, repeated dural injections of inflammatory soup induced mechanical hypersensitivity, migraine-like behavior, and altered microbiome composition, while also enhancing butyrate, propionate, and tryptophan metabolic pathways in the gut microbiota. Increased fecal tryptophan-related metabolites, particularly indole-3-acetic acid and tryptophan, may participate in these processes [26, 27].

While migraine is widely recognized in women, a significant data deficit exists regarding its prevalence in men—a disparity that underscores the urgent need for sex-specific clinical research. Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate the sex-specific differences in nociceptive sensitization, emotional behaviors, neuronal activation (c-Fos expression), and central inflammation (TNF-α) in a chronic NTG-induced mouse model. Through combined analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing and targeted metabolomics, we discovered sex related alterations in intestinal microbiota composition and metabolism in the gut-brain axis. Our data reveal that males possess a distinct vulnerability to emotional comorbidities and gut-brain disruptions, manifested as marked acute locomotor inhibition, chronic anxiety-like behavior, and severe metabolic dysregulation. This underscores the necessity of sex aware therapeutic strategies and opens new avenues for sex differentiated interventions in migraine management.

Methods

Animals

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) wild-type (C57BL/6J) mice (weight 18–20 g, aged 8 weeks) were purchased from Sibeifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and used throughout the study. All animals were housed under standard conditions at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity, with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and provided with adequate food and water ad libitum. Every effort was made to minimize the number of animals used and to reduce their suffering. Prior to experiments, all animals were acclimatized for one week and then randomly assigned to different experimental groups based on a random number sequence generated by Excel software. The required sample size was determined via G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) employing a repeated-measures ANOVA framework and setting the power at 85% [28]. During drug administration, animals were weighed daily, and no adverse effects were observed. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Management Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital, and all procedures were strictly conducted in accordance with relevant animal ethics regulations.

To minimize hormonal interference and synchronize the estrous cycle of female mice via the Whitten effect, females were exposed to male urine during the acclimation period following arrival [29]. Estrous cycle stages were determined daily by vaginal cytology. To ensure consistent baseline hormone levels, only mice in metestrus (characterized by a predominance of leukocytes) were selected for data analysis and tissue collection [29]. Vaginal sampling was performed after the completion of behavioral experiments to avoid acute stress induced by handling.

Chronic migraine model induced by repeated nitroglycerin administration

In this study, a mouse model of migraine was established by repeated intraperitoneal injections of nitroglycerin (NTG) [30]. Nitroglycerin (5 mg/ml in ethanol; Yimin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was freshly diluted in 0.9% (wt/vol) saline to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml and administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg. Briefly, the chronic migraine model was induced by intraperitoneal injection of NTG (10 mg/kg) every other day for a total of five injections. Anhydrous ethanol diluted 10-fold with saline served as the vehicle control (VEH). Mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either 10 mg/kg NTG or an equal volume of vehicle every other day over a 9‑day period (5 injections total). Both the NTG solution and the ethanol vehicle solution were freshly prepared on the day of administration (Fig. 1A).

Assessment of cutaneous allodynia

To evaluate allodynia-like behavior, the mechanical threshold of the hind paw was assessed every other day, either before or 2 h after NTG injection [31]. Prior to measurement, mice were habituated to acrylic chambers with a wire-mesh floor for 15–20 min. Mechanical threshold was determined using the up-down method [32] by applying von Frey filaments perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the right hind paw. Each filament was applied vertically until it bent slightly into a “C” shape and was held for 3–5 s or until a positive response occurred. A positive response was defined as a rapid withdrawal, shaking, or licking of the paw. Testing began with a filament of moderate force (0.16 g). Subsequently, filaments of increasing or decreasing strength were applied according to the animal’s response pattern: a stronger filament was used if no response occurred, and a weaker one was applied following a positive response. After the first positive response, four additional stimulations were performed, and the response pattern and the corresponding filament strength were recorded. The 50% mechanical threshold was then calculated using a freely available online algorithm (https://bioapps.shinyapps.io/) [33].To minimize bias, all assessments were conducted by an experimenter blinded to group assignments during the same daily time window (between 8:00 AM and 12:00 PM).

Anxiety-/depression-like behaviors and photophobia testing

For all behavioral tests, mice were acclimated to the testing room for at least 2 h prior to evaluation. To avoid potential cross-interference between behavioral assays, all tests were conducted using independent cohorts of mice. Specifically, the Light-Dark Box (LDB) test was performed 1 h after the initial NTG injection to assess the acute phase response. The Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) and Open Field Test (OFT) were conducted 24 h following the final NTG injection to evaluate chronic emotional dysregulation. By utilizing separate batches of animals for each behavioral battery, we ensured that the performance in one test did not confound the results of another.

Light–Dark box (LDB)

For the behavioral assessment of photophobia, a light-dark box test was employed (XR‑XB120, Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) [34]. The apparatus (30 cm wide × 30 cm deep × 30 cm high) consisted of two equally sized compartments: one painted white and illuminated by an LED panel (1000 lx), and the other painted black with minimal illumination (< 5 lx). The light intensities were configured as described in previous studies [35]. A connecting corridor (7 cm × 7 cm) allowed free movement between compartments. Unlike previous studies, to specifically examine photophobic behavior during chronic migraine, testing was conducted 1 h after the final nitroglycerin injection. Each mouse was gently placed in the center of the lit compartment facing away from the dark side, and the time spent in the central and peripheral zones was recorded. The box was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol between animals.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The elevated plus maze (XR‑XG201, Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used to evaluate anxiety-like behavior. The maze comprised two open arms (35 cm long × 5 cm wide, 60 lx) and two enclosed arms (35 cm long × 5 cm wide × 15 cm high, < 5 lx), connected by a central platform (5 cm × 5 cm) to form a plus shape. The maze was elevated 60 cm above the floor. Each mouse was placed on the central platform facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 10 min. The duration of time spent in the open and closed arms was recorded as the outcome measure. The maze was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials. Testing was performed 24 h after the fifth NTG injection in separate cohorts of mice [36].

Open field test (OFT)

The open field (XR‑XZ301, Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) apparatus consisted of an open-topped cubic chamber (500 mm × 500 mm × 400 mm, 58 lx). A central zone measuring 250 mm × 250 mm was defined as the center area, with the remaining regions designated as the periphery. Each mouse was placed in one corner of the chamber, facing toward the center, and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. During the test, the following multidimensional behavioral parameters were systematically recorded: duration of time spent in the center zone, number of entries into the center zone, and total distance traveled within the center zone [37].

Immunofluorescence

Acute phase samples were collected 2 h following intraperitoneal injection of NTG or vehicle, whereas chronic phase samples were harvested 24 h after the final NTG injection. At the designated time points, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1.5% avertin prior to tissue collection and subsequent analyses. Cardiac perfusion was then performed sequentially with 40 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2-7.4) at 37 °C, followed by 20 ml of pre‑cooled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution. The brains were subsequently post‑fixed in 4% PFA for 16 h and dehydrated through a graded sucrose series (15%-30%). Coronal sections of 30 μm thickness were cut using a Leica 1950 cryostat and placed in a rapid blocking buffer for immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence (IHC/IF) (yangguangyingrui, C220702, Beijing) for antigen blocking (20 min at room temperature). Sections were incubated with rabbit anti‑c‑Fos primary antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2250 S, USA) overnight at 4 °C. After washing four times for 15 min each with PBST (0.25% Triton X‑100), sections were incubated with a fluorophore‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000, Abcam, ab150080, USA) diluted in antibody diluent for 2 h at room temperature. Following another wash with PBST (4 × 15 min), sections were mounted with an anti‑fade mounting medium containing 4,6‑diamidino‑2‑phenylindole (DAPI, Abcam, ab150080, USA).

Imaging and analysis

Finally, images were acquired using a Pannoramic Scan digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH SHANDONG Co., Ltd.), and the mean fluorescence intensity was quantified with Quant Center 3.0 software. All images were acquired using a 20×objective with z‑stacking performed at a step size of 3 μm over 7 optical sections. Images were exported at a resolution of 600 dpi using SlideViewer software and subsequently processed and quantified. Brightness and contrast were uniformly adjusted across all groups for each brain region [36].

Brain regions showing differential expression were identified according to the Paxinos mouse brain atlas (4th edition, stereotaxic coordinates). Regions of interest were outlined, and cell counting was performed using the Quant Center 3.0 software.

Due to variations in the depth of specific brain regions, the number of sections analyzed per region differed. Therefore, sample size was not determined solely by the number of mice per group. In total, 5 mice were used per group. c‑Fos positive cells were counted in each region. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

ELISA

To determine TNF-α expression, we first anesthetized the mice (1.25% avertin) and performed transcardial perfusion with 40 mL ice-cold PBS. The collected medulla oblongata tissues were snap-frozen and kept at -80 °C. We then prepared 10% (w/v) homogenates in 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.2–7.4) on ice. The supernatant was isolated by 15 min of centrifugation at 5,000 rpm. We followed the manufacturer’s protocol for the ELISA: duplicates of samples/standards were incubated for 90 min, followed by the addition of biotinylated detection antibody (60 min) and HRP-streptavidin (30 min), all at 37 °C. After the final washing cycle, the chromogenic reaction was developed using substrate solution for 15 min in darkness. Upon addition of the stop solution, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

16S rRNA sequencing of the gut microbiome

Approximately 0.2 g of each sample was homogenized with lysis buffer using a tissue homogenizer (Tissuelyser-48, Shanghai Jingxin). Total genomic DNA was extracted using the MagBeads FastDNA Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals). DNA integrity was assessed by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration was quantified with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene (~ 480 bp) was amplified using primers 338 F (5′-barcode+ACTCCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR was performed with ABclonal DNA polymerase under the following conditions: 98 °C for 5 min; 26 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min [38]. The amplified fragments were first detected on a 2% agarose gel, and then the target bands were excised and purified. The purified products were quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit. Library construction was performed using the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA Library Preparation Kit, which included end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification steps. The quality of the libraries was evaluated using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit on a bioanalyzer. Qualified libraries were sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 2 × 250 bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing data were processed in QIIME2 (version 2022.11; https://docs.qiime2.org/2022.11/tutorials/). After demultiplexing, the primers were trimmed using cutadapt. Subsequently, the DADA2 pipeline was applied for quality filtering, denoising, sequence merging, and chimeric sequence removal, ultimately obtaining amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomy was assigned against the Greengenes database. Alpha diversity was evaluated using Chao1and Shannon indices after rarefaction to the minimum sequencing depth. Beta diversity was calculated with UniFrac distances and visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non‑metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). To assess the impact of different treatments on microbial community structure, taxonomic composition was compared at the phylum and genus levels. Significant differences in community assembly were determined via PERMANOVA. Differentially abundant taxa and potential biomarkers were identified using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe). To explore inter-species interactions, co-occurrence networks were constructed using the SparCC algorithm and visualized in R. Furthermore, microbial functional profiles were predicted using PICRUSt2 based on the KEGG and MetaCyc databases.

High-throughput targeted metabolomics

All standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Steraloids Inc. Standards were prepared as 5.0 mg/mL stock solutions. All reagents were LC-MS grade. Approximately 5 mg of freeze-dried feces were weighed and extracted with methanol containing internal standards. The extracts were subjected to automated derivatization (30 °C, 60 min) prior to UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C, using a mobile phase consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile/isopropanol under a gradient elution program. Detection was carried out in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. All procedures were performed at low temperatures, and quality control samples and blank controls were included to ensure data quality. Raw UPLC‑MS/MS data were processed using TMBQ software (v1.0, Metabo‑Profile) for peak integration and quantification. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares‑discriminant analysis (OPLS‑DA), were performed using the iMAP platform (v1.0) [39]. Models were validated by 7‑fold cross‑validation. Metabolites with a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score > 1.0 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly altered [40].

Statistical analysis

Both the experimental implementation and data analysis phases of this study were conducted in a blinded manner. The sample size was determined based on preliminary experiments or prior research data to ensure sufficient statistical power. All reported data points represent independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Specifically, data from the 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metabolomic analyses are presented as the median to better reflect their distribution characteristics.

All graphing and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified by Brown–Forsythe test. To examine the effect of sex on NTG treatment response, two-way ANOVA was conducted with sex (female vs. male) and treatment (VEH vs. NTG) as independent variables and experimental test indicators as dependent variables. The model included the interaction term between sex and treatment. The sex × treatment interaction was tested first. If the interaction was significant (p < 0.05) or marginally significant (p < 0.10), simple effect analyses were performed to compare NTG and VEH within each sex level, as well as to compare sex differences within each treatment level. If the interaction was not significant, the interaction term was removed from the model, and the main effects were reported and interpreted. Based on our pre-specified hypotheses, we specifically compared: NTG-F vs. VEH-F, NTG-M vs. VEH-M, VEH-F vs. VEH-M, NTG-F vs. NTG-M. Adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

NTG-induced chronic migraine-related behaviors exhibit notable sexual dimorphism: males manifest unique anxiety-like behaviors

While NTG challenge induced both acute and chronic mechanical hypersensitivity in both sexes, distinct sex-specific patterns emerged, whereas males were characterized by pronounced acute immobility and chronic anxiety-like behaviors.

Mechanical hypersensitivity (Plantar)

To investigate potential sex differences in hyperalgesia within the chronic migraine model induced by repeated NTG administration, we established both male and female mouse model and systematically assessed their hind paw mechanical withdrawal thresholds across acute and chronic phases. The results showed that: following an acute stimulation of NTG (10 mg/kg, i.p.), both female(Fig. 1B) and male(Fig. 1C) mice exhibited a significant decrease in mechanical withdrawal thresholds (MWTs) at 2 h post-injection (two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test: female: treatment: F (1, 90) = 160.3, P < 0.0001; time: F (4, 90) = 1.231, P = 0.3033); male: treatment: F (1, 90) = 137.6, P < 0.0001; time: F (4, 90) = 0.9534, P = 0.4371). Under the chronic intermittent NTG protocol (5 injections over 9 days), both sexes showed a progressive and significant decline in basal MWTs compared to the vehicle group, with the reduction becoming significant starting before the second injection (Fig. 1D-E) (two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test: female: treatment: F (1, 108) = 140.2, P<0.0001; time: F (5, 108) = 9.555, P < 0.0001; male: treatment: F (1, 108) = 81.70, P < 0.0001; time: F (5, 108) = 5.602, P = 0.00013). Our results indicate that both acute and chronic NTG stimulation induced hyperalgesia in mice of both sexes.

Anxiety-like behaviors

To investigate emotional comorbidities during the chronic phase, behavioral assessments were performed 24 h after the final administration of NTG (10 mg/kg, i.p.). To avoid potential inter-test interference, an independent cohort design was adopted, in which mice were randomly allocated to two separate groups and individually subjected to either the elevated plus maze test or the open field test.

OFT: NTG-treated male mice spent significantly less time in the central zone compared to the control group(two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test: interaction: F (1, 36) = 4.101, P = 0.0503, treatment: F (1, 36) = 4.960, P = 0.0323, NTG-M vs. VEH-M: P = 0.0382, sex: F (1, 36), P = 3.794, P = 0.0593, NTG-F vs. NTG-M: P = 0.0237). Importantly, there was no significant difference in the total distance traveled across all groups, confirming that the observed anxiety-like behavior in the chronic stage was not confounded by impaired locomotor activity. There was no significant difference in the total distance traveled between the two groups of female mice. (Fig. 2C-E).

EPM: To further validate emotional comorbidity, the EPM was employed. Compared with controls, NTG-challenged male mice exhibited a significant reduction in time spent in the open arms (two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test: interaction: F (1, 36) = 5.452, P = 0.0252, NTG-M vs. VEH-M: P = 0.0158, NTG-F vs. NTG-M: P = 0.0237). Although the number of times entering the open arm remained statistically unchanged, the overall exploration motivation within these unprotected areas weakened. (Fig. 2F-H).

In conclusion, these findings show a significantly sex differences in response to NTG. Male mice are significantly more sensitive to acute NTG-induced locomotor suppression and develop pronounced anxiety-like states during the chronic interictal phase, whereas female mice did not exhibit these phenomena.

Single and repetitive NTG stimulation activated multiple brain regions, with sex-specific alterations observed specifically in the AP

Previous studies have indicated that nitroglycerin stimulation induces sex‑specific differences in c‑Fos activation patterns in the rat brain [18]. Therefore, to further investigate this phenomenon, the present study evaluated brain activation following single and repeated nitroglycerin administration in a mouse model.

In the acute migraine model (2 h after a single NTG injection), a widespread increase in neuronal activity was observed in both male and female mice. Specifically, c-Fos expression was significantly up-regulated in multiple key regions, including NTS (Fig. 3D) and Sp5C (Fig. 3B), compared to their respective vehicle groups(two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test: NTS: interaction: F (1, 16) = 0.2601, P = 0.6170, treatment effects: F (1, 16) = 0.2601, P = 0.6170, NTG-F vs. VEH-F: P = 0.0028, NTG-M vs. VEH-M: P = 0.0121, sex: F (1, 16) = 31.03, P < 0.0001; Sp5C: interaction: F (1, 16) = 3.403, P = 0.0837, treatment: F (1, 16) = 0.6775, P = 0.4225, NTG-F vs. VEH-F: P = 0.0002, NTG-M vs. VEH-M: P = 0.0283, sex: F (1, 16) = 39.74, P < 0.0001). Among these brain regions, only the AP exhibited a significant sex difference: in females, no significant difference in c-Fos expression was observed between the NTG-treated and control groups; in males, however, the NTG-treated group showed significantly higher c-Fos expression compared to the control group. Sex-specific remodeling of gut microbial and metabolic profiles following repeated NTG challenge

Given that the gut microbiota and brain functions are interlinked via the gut-brain axis [41], with AP and NTS serving as pivotal relay stations [42], we collected fecal samples 24 h after the final NTG injection for 16S rRNA sequencing and metabolomic profiling to further investigate potential sex differences in this mechanism.

In female mice, subsequent species composition analysis at the phylum level showed that the NTG-treated group exhibited enrichment of Verrucomicrobia, whereas the control group was characterized by higher abundances of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria (Fig. 5C). At the genus level, g-Lactobacillus was more abundant in controls, while g-Akkermansia was notably elevated in the NTG group (Fig. 5E). Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA score > 4.0) confirmed that the family Verrucomicrobiaceae and the genus Akkermansia were the key discriminative biomarkers distinguishing the NTG-treated females from controls (Fig. 5G).

In male mice, a unique microbial response pattern was observed. At the phylum level, NTG treatment led to a significant expansion enrichment of Firmicutes while the control group showed enrichment of the Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 5D). Genus-level indicated that Akkermansia Prevotella, Bacteroides, and Helicobacter were enriched in controls. In the NTG group showed significant increases in Lactobacillus, Adlercreutzia, and Parabacteroides (Fig. 5F). LEfSe analysis further identified the family Erysipelotrichaceae, the g-Parabacteroides, and the f-Porphyromonadaceae as significant biomarkers (LDA score > 4.0) associated with NTG treatment in males. In general, these findings emphasize the significant sex differences in the intestinal microbiota dysbiosis induced by NTG. The changes in females were primarily driven by an enrichment of p-Verrucomicrobia (specifically g-Akkermansia), whereas males exhibited a shift towards f-Porphyromonadaceae and f-Erysipelotrichaceae, characterized by increases in g-Parabacteroides.

Correlation between differential microbiota and behavioral phenotypes

To further explore the potential links between the sex-specific microbiota and behavioral variations, we performed Spearman correlation analyses (Fig. S1). The results revealed that the relative abundance of g-Akkermansia was significantly and positively correlated with the time spent in the center of the OFT (r = 0.4602, p = 0.0412) and the time spent in the open arms of the EPM (r = 0.4596, p = 0.0415). This suggests that g-Akkermansia may be associated with reduced anxiety-like behaviors. In contrast, although g-Parabacteroides showed sex-specific enrichment, its correlation with behavioral indices did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), potentially due to the high inter-individual dispersion observed in its abundance.

Fecal metabolomic profiles

To elucidate the functional changes in the intestinal microbiota induced by NTG at the metabolic level, we conducted targeted metabolomics analysis on the fecal samples of mice. The Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) results showed that there was a significant separation in the metabolic profiles between the control group and the NTG group, and this metabolic disturbance exhibited obvious sex dimorphism (Fig. 6A-B). A total of 220 metabolites were detected in female mice, and only 3 significantly different metabolites were identified between the two groups (Fig. 6C). These changes manifested as elevated levels of Glycyl-leucine and decreased levels of Oxoglutaric acid and Decanoylcarnitine. Although the number of differential metabolites was limited, KEGG pathway analysis suggested that these minor metabolic alterations primarily mapped onto key energy and amino acid metabolic networks, including D-Glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, the Citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and Butanoate metabolism.

Male mice exhibited profound metabolic disturbances following NTG induction. Among the 220 identified fecal metabolites, a total of 29 metabolites with statistically significant differences were screened out. These included amino acids, Bile acids, Carbohydrates, Fatty acids, Organic acids, Peptides, Phenols, and Phenylpropanoic acids. And 17 metabolites were significantly upregulated in the NTG model group, while 12 were downregulated. Phenyllactic acid and p-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid were markedly elevated; these metabolites are typically derived from the bacterial decomposition of aromatic amino acids in the gut and are closely associated with neuropsychiatric disorders and gut-brain axis signaling. Elevated levels of the long-chain saturated fatty acid Myristic acid, the dicarboxylic acid Azelaic acid, and the secondary bile acid Norcholic acid were also observed. Fluctuations in these indicators are generally considered sensitive biomarkers of gut microbiota dysbiosis and systemic inflammatory responses (Fig. 6D). KEGG enrichment analysis further elucidated the functional context of the metabolic disruption in male mice. These differential metabolites were significantly enriched in the following pathways: (1) Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; (2) Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis; (3) Pentose and glucuronate interconversions; (4) Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids; and (5) Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

The present study systematically demonstrates a robust sexual dimorphism in the chronic migraine model induced by repeated NTG administration. Our study revealed sex differences in NTG induced migraine-like behavior: although both male and female mice exhibited similar acute and chronic mechanical hyperalgesia, only male mice showed marked acute locomotor inhibition and chronic anxiety-like behavior. This disparity extended to neuroinflammation: following a single NTG injection, male mice exhibited more pronounced increases in TNF-α levels within the medulla and stronger neuronal activation (c-Fos) in AP. Crucially, integrating 16S rRNA sequencing with metabolomics analysis revealed sex-specific alterations in gut microbiota-metabolism pathways. Male mice exhibited far more severe metabolic dysregulation than females, particularly within the metabolic pathways of aromatic amino acid derivatives. These findings suggest that unique neuroinflammatory and gut-brain signaling characteristics may contribute to male specific susceptibility to anxiety-like behaviors.

Clinically, the prevalence of migraine and its tendency toward chronification are significantly higher in females. Initially, the more pronounced acute behavioral and inflammatory phenotypes observed in male mice appear counterintuitive. However, it is essential to distinguish between the acute response to systemic NTG challenge and the long term chronification of migraine. Our preliminary longitudinal behavioral monitoring revealed a distinct sex-divergent trajectory: although male mice exhibited a more severe acute pain reaction at 24 h post-NTG, their mechanical pain thresholds returned to baseline by day 14. In contrast, female mice displayed sustained mechanical allodynia well into the chronic phase (unpublished observations). This is consistent with previous findings [43]. These findings suggest that while males may be more vulnerable to the acute systemic challenge, females possess a significantly higher propensity for prolonged pain sensitization, a pattern that aligns closely with the clinical epidemiology of female chronification. The present study specifically focuses on the 24-hour acute time point to ensure rigorous synchronization across behavioral and multi-omics evaluations.

Following NTG administration, male mice exhibited a marked decrease in locomotor activity, whereas female mice showed no significant change. Several lines of evidence support interpreting this hypoactivity as pain-related rather than non-specific malaise. First, testing was conducted during the delayed phase (60 min post-NTG), a time window associated with cephalic pain rather than acute cardiovascular effects [44, 45]. Second, the effect was sexually dimorphic—if purely due to systemic toxicity, similar changes would be expected in both sexes. Third, and most critically, CGRP receptor antagonists (gepants) reverse NTG-induced nociception in preclinical models without blocking NTG-induced vasodilation [46]. This dissociation between vascular and anti-nociceptive effects strongly suggests that locomotor suppression in our study is mediated by pain signaling. This male-specific behavioral pattern aligns with clinical observations that male patients are more inclined to rest during migraine attacks and require longer recovery time [47]. A survey of Polish migraine sufferers revealed that the median time required for men to recover normal functioning after an attack (approximately 24 h) was significantly longer than for women (approximately 10 h) [48]. This indicates that men often experience more complete, paralysis-like rest during their attacks. The behavioral difference may be fundamentally due to the different responses of the two sexes to physical exertion. And physical exertion is a known aggravating factor for the condition in the male population. From an evolutionary perspective, the acute motor inhibition exhibited by men may be an adaptive strategy for avoiding harm. By reducing physical energy consumption and minimizing the convergence of peripheral injury signals to the central nervous system, this mechanism helps to mitigate the impact of stress. In contrast, women have developed greater tolerance and functional resilience under social evolutionary pressures. While this compensatory mechanism ensures the maintenance of basic survival functions during attacks, it may also prolong central pain processing due to “operating while injured”, leading to protracted disease progression.

The significant increase in TNF-α levels in the medulla of male animals is conclusive evidence of this. As a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine, TNF-α can activate microglia and disrupt the glutamatergic/gamma-aminobutyric acidergic neurotransmitter system [49]. Such neurochemical disruption is a direct pathway to the onset of negative affective states, including anxiety and depression [50]. Furthermore, studies indicate that the TNF-TNFR1 signaling pathway exhibits significant sex dimorphism in neuropathic pain: this pathway plays a crucial role in pain maintenance only in male mice, but not in female mice [51]. In contrast, the absence of such neuroinflammatory surges in females may be attributed to the well-documented anti-inflammatory properties of estrogen. It inhibits excessive microglial activation and downregulates the transcription of various pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) [52], which may explain why, following an acute NTG challenge, only male mice exhibited a marked increase in medullary TNF-α. This estrogen-driven anti-inflammatory environment may counteract the neurotoxic stress induced by NTG, thereby enabling female mice to exhibit enhanced emotional resilience in the context of chronic recurring pain.

From a behavioral perspective, we observed that anxiety-like phenotypes emerged exclusively in male mice following repeated nitroglycerin stimulation. Although epidemiological data indicate a higher rate of migraine chronification in females, many studies suggest that the relative risk of anxiety in male migraineurs is often higher than in females [53]. This finding is further supported by the Eurolight project, a primary care–based study conducted across ten European countries, which reported that among individuals with headache, the increase in anxiety prevalence relative to non-headache controls was more than twice as high in males (320%) as in females (140%) [15]. This sexual dimorphism may be explained by differences in pain exposure patterns. In females, migraine attacks are frequently linked to fluctuations in the menstrual cycle (affecting 22–70% of cases) [10], and such periodic pain exposure may promote the development of unique neuroendocrine adaptations within the central nervous system, thereby conferring greater emotional resilience. In contrast, males typically experience less frequent attacks and lack this rhythmic periodicity. When faced with pain episodes, males may therefore exhibit heightened emotional susceptibility due to the absence of such physiological or psychological compensatory mechanisms. In this context, the present study provides a preclinical framework for understanding male specific vulnerability to migraine associated anxiety, an underexplored area that may have translational relevance given the clinical underrecognition of affective symptoms in male patients. The present study further reveals that sex-specific profiles in the gut microbiota and their metabolites are closely associated with the divergence in central phenotypes, suggesting their potential involvement in these sex-dependent manifestations. In female mice, NTG treatment increased the abundance of the g-Akkermansia in the gut microbiota, a genus associated with enhanced intestinal barrier function and stress resilience [54]. A study involving 110 athletes, which stratified them based on anxiety levels and analyzed their gut microbiota, found that anxiety scores were significantly negatively correlated with the abundance of g-Akkermansia [55]. Another study reported that following an 8-week administration of either live g-Akkermansia or its inactivated preparations (postbiotics), symptoms of anxiety and depression were significantly alleviated [56, 57]. In animal models, the abundance of g-Akkermansia has been shown to decrease substantially under stress exposure [58, 59]. Supported by previous findings, Estrogen is recognized as a key regulator of intestinal barrier function. The role of estrogen in preserving gut integrity is reflected in the stable metabolic profile observed in female mice, along with the compensatory increase in g-Akkermansia, which is known to enhance mucosal barrier integrity [60]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the absence of anxiety-like behavior in females observed here may be attributable to the protective effect of significantly enriched g-Akkermansia [59]. In contrast, male mice exhibited abnormal enrichment of g-Parabacteroides and f-Porphyromonadaceae. In animal models of chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS), the abundance of f-Porphyromonadaceae in the gut typically fluctuates dramatically in mice displaying anxiety-like behaviors [61]. Previous studies have also established that increased abundance of g-Parabacteroides is associated with mood disorders [62, 63] and can directly induce depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors in animals [64]. Fecal metabolomics further revealed significant differences between the sexes: female individuals maintained highly stable metabolic profiles, while males exhibited pronounced metabolic dysregulation. Aromatic metabolites of microbial origin, such as HPHPA and 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, were specifically elevated in males. These compounds have been established as gut-derived neurotoxicity markers [65–67] and may exacerbate negative emotions by disrupting dopamine balance in the brain [68]. The appearance of dicarboxylic acids is a hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction and also reflects elevated oxidative stress levels [69]. Additionally, levels of the saturated fatty acid myristic acid are elevated. This increase is generally viewed as a marker of compromised intestinal barrier function, a condition often accompanied by systemic inflammation [70]. Based on these multi-omics evaluations, we propose a hypothesis in which these sex-differentiated metabolites act as key signaling molecules within the gut-brain axis, driving the transmission of inflammatory signals and exacerbating central pain and affective responses in males. As an exploratory study, our findings provide a novel conceptual framework mapping the trajectory from gut dysbiosis to medullary neuroinflammation, specifically in the male. Although these correlational findings do not establish causality, they provide a strong rationale for future mechanistic studies targeting gut microbial modulation to test whether the observed male specific metabolic dysregulation directly contributes to anxiety-like phenotypes.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, although hind paw withdrawal thresholds effectively demonstrated central sensitization, periorbital testing was omitted to avoid interference with subsequent behavioral assays; combined assessments in future studies would allow more comprehensive characterization of migraine-like states. Second, open field test analysis focused on time in center and locomotor activity, without quantifying rearing, grooming, or sniffing—behaviors sensitive to exploratory motivation and stress—limiting interpretation of emotional dysregulation; detailed ethogram analyses are needed. Third, while sex-specific gut microbial and metabolic profiles were identified and correlated with behavioral phenotypes, causal relationships remain unestablished due to the lack of fecal microbiota transplantation or targeted interventions. Additionally, single‑time‑point omics analysis during the chronic interictal‑like phase precluded tracking of dynamic microbiota changes from initial to chronic stages; multi‑time‑point approaches are warranted. Fourth, behavioral phenotyping in females was conducted only during the diestrus phase (low estrogen state) to minimize hormonal variability, which models estrogen withdrawal in menstrual migraine but may have missed susceptibility during high‑estrogen phases; future studies should examine phenotypes across the entire estrus cycle.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal distinct sexual dimorphism in nitroglycerin-induced migraine, with males specifically predisposed to motor inhibition and chronic anxiety. This exploratory study identifies a potential “microbiota-metabolism-brain axis” as a candidate mechanism underlying these sex differences: the enrichment of g-Akkermansia in females correlates with stress resilience, whereas males display concurrent gut dysbiosis and central neuroinflammation. Importantly, by highlighting male specific behavioral and molecular phenotypes in a preclinical migraine model, this work addresses a historically understudied dimension of migraine biology and underscores the necessity of incorporating sex as a biological variable in future mechanistic and translational studies.
