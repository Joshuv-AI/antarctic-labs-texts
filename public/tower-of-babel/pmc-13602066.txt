Effect of immunonutrition on postoperative infectious complications and survival after colon cancer surgery: long-term follow up of randomized clinical trial
Authors: Jaram Lee, Hyeung-min Park, Soo Young Lee, Chang Hyun Kim, Hyeong Rok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA previous randomized clinical trial (RCT) in patients undergoing elective colon cancer surgery showed that preoperative immunonutrition did not reduce short-term postoperative infectious complications. This study reports the long-term follow-up of that RCT, evaluating the impact of preoperative immunonutrition and the association of postoperative infectious complications with survival outcomes.MethodsThis secondary analysis used prospectively collected survival data from a prior RCT in which patients were randomized 1:1 to receive either preoperative immunonutrition plus a normal diet or a normal diet alone (control) for 7 days before surgery. The primary endpoints were disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). Survival outcomes were analysed in relation to randomized treatment allocation and clinicopathological variables, including postoperative complications, using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsA total of 161 patients were included, with 79 randomized to immunonutrition and 82 to control. The mean age was 65.3 years, and 65.8% were male, with similar baseline characteristics between groups. After a median follow-up of 57 months (range 4–65), preoperative immunonutrition did not affect 5-year DFS (80% versus 86% in controls; P = 0.474) or 5-year OS (88% versus 90%; P = 0.761). In contrast, postoperative infectious complications were associated with lower 5-year OS (74% versus 92%; P = 0.013) and showed a trend towards worse DFS (71% versus 86%; P = 0.065). On multivariable analysis, advanced T category and postoperative infectious complications remained independently associated with poorer DFS (HR 2.53; P = 0.041) and OS (HR 4.14; P = 0.007). The adverse prognostic effect of infectious complications was evident primarily in patients with advanced T category disease.ConclusionPreoperative immunonutrition did not improve long-term survival after colon cancer surgery. Postoperative infectious complications were independently associated with worse long-term outcomes, providing prospective evidence that supports previous observational findings.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, accounting for an estimated 1.9 million new cases and 900 000 deaths in 20221. Although multidisciplinary approaches to the treatment of CRC have increasingly become established, the cornerstone of treatment remains radical surgical resection. Given the nature of CRC surgery as a clean-contaminated procedure, it carries a relatively high risk of surgical site infections (SSIs), with reported rates ranging from 15 to 30%2. Although the widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgical techniques has contributed to a reduction in SSI rates3, SSIs continue to pose a significant and persistent clinical challenge. SSIs account for more than 30% of healthcare-associated infections and are associated with increased morbidity and a prolonged hospital stay, as well as increased readmission rates and healthcare costs2,3.

In addition to adversely affecting short-term outcomes, postoperative infectious complications, including SSIs, have been reported to negatively affect long-term oncological outcomes after CRC surgery3–8. However, most previous studies on this topic were retrospective in nature3,4,6–8 or based on large administrative databases5, where the completeness and accuracy of complication reporting are inherently limited. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that postoperative infectious complications have a detrimental effect not only on overall survival (OS) but also on cancer-specific survival after CRC surgery9. However, that meta-analysis primarily included studies focused on anastomotic leakage (AL), which may not fully represent the broader spectrum of infectious complications. These limitations highlight the need for high-quality, prospectively collected data to generate more robust evidence on this topic.

A previous randomized clinical trial (RCT) investigated whether preoperative immunonutrition could reduce postoperative infectious complications in patients undergoing colon cancer surgery10. That trial demonstrated no significant association between immunonutrition and infectious complications, and did not support the routine use of preoperative immunonutrition in unselected patients10. However, little is known about the potential long-term survival benefits of immunonutrition. Given the availability of prospectively collected follow-up data from the original RCT, the present study reports the results of long-term follow-up, exploring the prognostic implications of preoperative immunonutrition and postoperative infectious complications. Therefore, using prospectively collected follow-up data from this RCT, we performed a secondary analysis to evaluate the long-term impact of preoperative immunonutrition and the association between postoperative infectious complications and survival outcomes after colon cancer surgery.

Methods

Study design and patients

This study is a secondary long-term follow-up analysis of a previously published randomized clinical trial10 that evaluated the effect of preoperative immunonutrition on short-term outcomes after colon cancer surgery. The original IRB-approved study protocol is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1, with an English translation in Supplementary Appendix S2. The present analysis uses prospectively collected survival data and is reported in accordance with CONSORT recommendations for randomized trials and their follow-up analyses. The original trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital (IRB No. CNUHH-2019-062), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. For this secondary analysis, prospectively collected follow-up data obtained through routine clinical care were used without additional patient contact or intervention. The requirement for additional informed consent was waived by the same institutional review board (IRB No. CNUHH-2025-128) based on minimal risk and the use of non-identifiable clinical data. The study protocol was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS registration KCT0010777; https://cris.nih.go.kr/cris/index/index.do). This cohort study has been reported in line with the STROCSS guidelines11.

The original RCT10 enrolled 176 patients between April 2019 and October 2020 at Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital, a tertiary referral centre in Korea. Eligible patients were aged 20–80 years and scheduled to undergo elective laparoscopic resection for primary colon adenocarcinoma. The exclusion criteria included emergency surgery, an inability to tolerate oral intake, pregnancy, or scheduled ostomy creation. Patients were randomly assigned in a 1 : 1 ratio to receive either immunonutrition plus a normal diet or a normal diet alone (control group) for 7 days before surgery. Patients in the immunonutrition group received 400 ml/day of an immune-modulating oral nutritional supplement (Nucare Omega®; Daesang Life Science, Seoul, Korea) containing arginine and omega-3 fatty acids for 7 consecutive days before surgery12.

Surgery and perioperative care

All procedures were performed by experienced colorectal surgeons under oncological principles. D3 lymph node dissection was performed in all patients. In most patients, a stapled anastomosis was performed using a side-to-side configuration for right hemicolectomy, an end-to-side configuration for left hemicolectomy, and an end-to-end configuration for anterior resection. For right hemicolectomy, the choice between intracorporeal and extracorporeal anastomosis was left to the discretion of the operating surgeon13. No patient required stoma creation. All patients underwent standard perioperative management according to a standardized enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol14, including mechanical bowel preparation, prophylactic antibiotics, early mobilization, and early oral feeding. For patients with high-risk stage II or stage III disease, 5-fluorouracil-based adjuvant chemotherapy was considered15. The decision to administer chemotherapy was made by the attending surgeon and oncologists in consultation with the patient based on the patient's overall condition. Adjuvant chemotherapy was not administered if the patient had a poor performance status, had not adequately recovered, or declined treatment.

Outcomes

The primary endpoints of this secondary analysis were long-term survival outcomes, including disease-free survival (DFS) and OS. DFS was defined as the time from surgery to tumour recurrence or death, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. All patients were staged according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer tumour node metastasis (TNM) staging system16. Postoperative complications were categorized into infectious and non-infectious events, as described in the original study10, and were assessed within 30 days after surgery. Infectious complications included SSIs, classified as superficial incisional, deep incisional, or organ/space SSI, according to the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria17, as well as other clinically relevant infections, such as urinary and respiratory tract infections. Nutritional risk was assessed using the Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital–Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS) Tool18, and patients with an NRS score ≥ 4 were classified as being at nutritional risk. Patients were followed up every 6 months with abdominopelvic and chest computed tomography, as well as serum carcinoembryonic antigen testing, whereas colonoscopy was performed every 1–2 years. Long-term follow-up data were prospectively collected through scheduled outpatient visits and telephone contact.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, and continuous variables were compared using Student's t test. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate OS and DFS, and results were compared using log-rank tests. Variables with P < 0.1 were entered into Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate associations with survival outcomes. Given the limited number of survival events, multivariable models were intentionally parsimonious, including a small number of clinically relevant covariates selected a priori to minimize overfitting. T and N category were dichotomized (T1–2 versus T3–4; N0 versus N1–2) based on survival curve patterns to avoid overfitting and improve interpretability. Backward stepwise regression was used for multivariable model selection. Interactions between independent survival predictors were further assessed by including an interaction term in the model and conducting a subgroup analysis. Subgroup and interaction analyses were exploratory in nature and were performed to assess potential effect modification rather than to establish definitive conclusions. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of adjuvant chemotherapy omission. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS® version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

In all, 161 patients were included in the final analysis (79 in the immunonutrition group and 82 in the control group) (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). The mean(standard deviation) age was 65.3(10.5) years, and 65.8% of the patients were male; 8.7% of patients had an NRS score ≥ 4. The distribution of T and N categories, American Society of Anesthesiologists grade, and surgical procedures did not differ significantly between the two groups. Postoperative complications occurred in 49 patients (30.4%) overall, of whom 27 (16.8%) experienced infectious complications (Table 2). None of the patients in the study cohort experienced AL.

The median follow-up duration after surgery for colon cancer was 57 months (range 4–65 months). The 5-year OS and DFS rates for the entire cohort were 88.7 and 83.2%, respectively. Univariable analysis showed that preoperative immunonutrition did not affect DFS or OS (P = 0.474 and P = 0.761, respectively). However, higher nutritional risk (that is, NRS score ≥ 4 versus < 4) was significantly associated with poorer 5-year DFS (49 versus 86.2%, respectively; P = 0.016) and 5-year OS (58 versus 91.4%, respectively; P = 0.009). Notably, patients who experienced infectious complications had a significantly lower 5-year OS (74 versus 91.8%; P = 0.013), but no difference in 5-year DFS (71 versus 85.9%; P = 0.065) (Table 3 and Fig. 2).

For OS, T category and infectious complications had HRs of 7.91 (95% c.i. 1.76 to 35.50; P = 0.007) and 4.14 (95% c.i. 1.46 to 11.73; P = 0.008), respectively.

To evaluate whether the prognostic impact of infectious complications differed according to T category, interaction and subgroup analyses were performed. The interaction term between T category and infectious complications was not statistically significant for DFS (P = 0.277) or OS (P = 0.986). However, the subgroup analysis revealed a stage-dependent effect in OS. Among patients with early stage tumours (T1–2), infectious complications were not associated with OS (5-year OS 91 versus 98% for those with and without infectious complications, respectively; P = 0.303). In contrast, among patients with advanced tumours (T3–4), those with infectious complications had significantly poorer OS than those without (5-year OS 57 versus 85%; P = 0.008).

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying the association between postoperative complications and long-term survival, an additional exploratory and descriptive analysis was performed on the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy among eligible patients. In all, 84 patients (52.2%) were eligible for adjuvant chemotherapy; of these patients, 10 (12%) did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy due to poor performance status (6 patients), refusal of treatment (3), or a postoperative diagnosis of leukaemia (1). Univariable analysis revealed that old age (≥ 70 years; P = 0.038), high nutritional risk (NRS score ≥ 4; P = 0.023), and postoperative non-infectious complications (P = 0.019) were significantly associated with the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy (Table 5). However, infectious complications were not significantly associated with the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy (P = 0.091). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, postoperative non-infectious complications remained an independent predictor of chemotherapy omission (odds ratio (OR) 8.63; 95% c.i. 1.65 to 45.17; P = 0.011; Table 6). In the entire cohort, the administration of adjuvant chemotherapy was not associated with 5-year DFS (85 versus 82% for patients with and without adjuvant chemotherapy, respectively; P = 0.354) or 5-year OS (87 versus 91% for patients with and without adjuvant chemotherapy, respectively; P = 0.777). However, among patients with a clinical indication for adjuvant chemotherapy, its administration was significantly associated with improved 5-year DFS (82% versus 0%; P < 0.001) and 5-year OS (91 versus 0%, P < 0.001) compared with its omission (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this long-term follow-up of an RCT cohort, preoperative immunonutrition did not improve long-term survival outcomes after colon cancer surgery. However, postoperative infectious complications were associated with worse DFS and OS. Although this association has been reported previously in retrospective studies and registry-based analyses3–8, the findings of the present study provide higher-quality prospective evidence derived from systematically collected data, uniform perioperative management within a standardized ERAS protocol, and rigorous complication assessment.

In the present study, preoperative immunonutrition was not associated with a long-term advantage in terms of survival outcomes. High nutritional risk (defined as an NRS score ≥ 4) had a significant negative impact on both DFS and OS in univariable analysis; however, high nutritional risk did not remain an independent prognostic factor in the multivariable model. A high NRS score was also significantly associated with the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy in the univariable analysis, but this association was not significant in multivariable analysis (OR 5.180; P = 0.080). These findings are consistent with a previous study, which reported that a high NRS score was associated with the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with stage III colon cancer19. These results underscore the potential importance of perioperative nutritional support. Although preoperative immunonutrition did not reduce infectious complications in the original study, nor did it improve long-term outcomes in the present analysis, it should be noted that the original RCT included all patients regardless of nutritional risk status10. This may have diluted any potential benefit of the intervention in patients with nutritional risk. A new multicentre RCT is currently underway to evaluate the effect of preoperative immunonutrition specifically in patients with CRC at high nutritional risk20. The results of that study may provide more definitive evidence on the role of targeted nutritional interventions in this population.

The detrimental impact of infectious complications on survival outcomes has been suggested in previous studies. Extensive evidence suggests that chronic inflammation is a key contributor to carcinogenesis, tumour progression, and cancer recurrence21. Although the present study demonstrated an association between postoperative infectious complications and long-term survival, it did not directly investigate the underlying biological mechanisms. Based on previous experimental and clinical studies, it has been hypothesized that infectious complications may trigger a systemic inflammatory response and immunosuppression, which can promote micrometastatic tumour growth and facilitate tumour recurrence4,5,22,23. This impaired survival may be mediated by the local and systemic production of proinflammatory cytokines and mediators, which have been shown to promote micrometastasis and tumour spread24. In particular, elevated postoperative cytokine levels, such as interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein, have been associated with poorer oncological outcomes, supporting the role of host–tumour inflammatory interactions in cancer progression4,22. However, these mechanistic explanations remain speculative in the context of the present study. The mechanistic explanations discussed above are biologically plausible and hypothesis generating, warranting further investigation in studies specifically designed to evaluate inflammatory and immunological pathways.

Another possible explanation for the observed association between postoperative infectious complications and long-term survival is the potential of postoperative infectious complications to delay or preclude the administration of adjuvant chemotherapy3. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines25, adjuvant chemotherapy is recommended for patients with high-risk stage II or stage III colon cancer, and should ideally be initiated as soon as the patient has recovered sufficiently, preferably within 6–8 weeks after surgery. A meta-analysis reported that delays in adjuvant chemotherapy are associated with worse survival outcomes26. Postoperative complications, including infectious complications, can result in the delay or omission of chemotherapy. A nationwide database study previously demonstrated that postoperative complications reduced the likelihood of receiving adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with stage III colon cancer27. Okui et al.28 also reported that delayed-onset organ/space SSIs were associated with poorer prognosis due to the delayed initiation of chemotherapy. In the present study, patients who were eligible for adjuvant chemotherapy but did not receive it had significantly worse outcomes. Notably, 10 of the 84 eligible patients who did not undergo adjuvant chemotherapy showed markedly poorer 5-year OS than those who received treatment. This difference underscores the potential clinical importance of adjuvant chemotherapy; however, given the limited sample size, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. Infectious complications were not identified as a statistically significant predictor of chemotherapy omission in the analysis in the present study; instead, non-infectious complications emerged as an independent predictor. This may be due to the limited sample size of the present study and the possibility of type II error.

Infectious complications have also been suggested to be more frequent in patients with advanced-stage tumours, which could confound the association with poor prognosis3,29. However, the previous report on the short-term outcomes from the original RCT found no direct association between tumour stage and the occurrence of infectious complications10. Furthermore, the subgroup analysis in the present study stratified by T category demonstrated that infectious complications were still associated with worse prognosis even when the analysis was limited to patients with advanced (T category) disease, which weakens the argument that tumour stage alone accounts for the observed survival difference. Finally, abdominal implantation of intraluminal cancer cells in the setting of AL has also been proposed as a potential mechanism contributing to tumour recurrence and poor survival30. Although biologically plausible, this explanation is limited to cases involving AL. In the present study, none of the patients experienced AL, and the observed association between infectious complications and poor survival suggests that factors beyond AL, namely postoperative infections more broadly, may play a role in adversely affecting long-term oncological outcomes.

Efforts to reduce postoperative infectious complications, particularly SSIs, have become a major focus of quality improvement in colorectal surgery. Recent literature highlights the effectiveness of structured, multiphase care bundles tailored to colorectal procedures, which typically include evidence-based interventions in the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative periods2. Despite these advances, SSI rates in colorectal surgery remain among the highest across surgical disciplines, underscoring the need for consistent implementation and compliance with preventive measures. In the present study, all patients received care in accordance with a standardized ERAS protocol, which incorporated key elements known to reduce infection risk, including mechanical bowel preparation, prophylactic antibiotics, normothermia, and normoglycaemia. Although infections could not be entirely eliminated, this approach reflects a strong institutional commitment to minimizing postoperative morbidity and optimizing both short- and long-term outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of included patients was limited, resulting in a relatively small number of events, such as infectious complications, recurrences, and deaths. Although backward stepwise regression was used to minimize model complexity, the multivariable analyses may still be susceptible to overfitting, as reflected by wide confidence intervals for some effect estimates. Therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously, and residual confounding or model instability cannot be fully excluded. This limitation may also have contributed to the lack of a statistically significant association between infectious complications and the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy. Second, the study did not directly elucidate the biological mechanisms by which infectious complications contribute to poor survival, and the underlying pathways remain speculative, despite discussion of potential inflammatory and immunological effects. Third, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral centre, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Fourth, given the exploratory nature of this secondary long-term follow-up analysis, multiple statistical tests were conducted without adjustment for multiplicity. Although all analyses were prespecified based on clinical rationale, it is acknowledged that the risk of type I error may be increased. Importantly, given the observational design of this secondary analysis, causality between infectious complications and survival outcomes cannot be established. Therefore, the findings of this study, particularly those with borderline statistical significance, should be regarded as hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. Although this study does not introduce a novel hypothesis, its strengths lie in the prospective capture of postoperative complications, consistent perioperative care, and the absence of AL, which allowed evaluation of the prognostic impact of postoperative infections beyond anastomotic failure. These findings support the need for continued efforts to reduce infectious complications and ensure the timely delivery of adjuvant therapy in high-risk patients with colon cancer.
