Association between five dietary indices and childhood asthma: the mediating role of visceral adiposity indicators using NHANES data
Authors: Nian-ci Xue, Zhe-hao Xu, Yu Gao, Xi-wei Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory condition in children, significantly impacting their quality of life. While various dietary indices have been linked to health outcomes, their specific association with asthma risk in children remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the relationship between dietary indices and asthma prevalence in children aged 3-5 years.MethodsData from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011-2018, encompassing 1695 participants aged 3-5 years, were analyzed. The study employed logistic regression and restricted cubic splines to assess the associations among Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), Alternative Healthy Eating Index (AHEI), Healthy Eating Index 2020 (HEI-2020), Mediterranean Diet (MED), and Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) with asthma prevalence. Mediation effects of Body Mass Index (BMI), Lipid accumulation product (LAP), and Visceral Adipose Tissue Index (VAI) were further explored by causal mediation analysis.ResultsThe analysis revealed that higher adherence to DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, and MED was significantly associated with reduced asthma risk, with adjusted ORs of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.90-0.97), 0.98 (95% CI: 0.97-0.99), 0.98 (95% CI: 0.98-0.99) and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.94), respectively. Furthermore, mediation analysis indicated that BMI, LAP, and VAI significantly mediated these relationships (P<0.05).ConclusionOur study indicates that adherence to healthier dietary patterns may significantly reduce the risk of asthma in children. The observed association between a diet low in inflammatory potential and a decreased likelihood of asthma suggests that anti-inflammatory diets could play a crucial role in asthma prevention. These insights could guide future dietary recommendations and interventions aimed at decreasing asthma prevalence among pediatric populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06233-z.Keywords: Asthma, Children, Dietary Indices, NHANES, DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, MED, DII

Background

Asthma is a common chronic disease among children, affecting millions globally. Its pathogenesis is complex, involving both genetic and environmental factors [1]. Affected children may exhibit a range of symptoms, including wheezing, chest tightness, coughing, and difficulty breathing [2]. In 2019, the estimated number of global asthma patients was 262.4 million, with 3.79 million new cases, ranking first among chronic respiratory diseases, an increase of 15.6% and 15.0% respectively compared to 1990 [3]. According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report in 2021, the asthma prevalence rates among children aged 5–11 and 12–17 were 7.5% and 8.7%, respectively. The overall asthma prevalence reported among children under 18 years old in the United States was 6.5% [4].

In children, the prevalence of asthma has been noted to be increasing and is now considered the most common chronic disease among children [5, 6]. Chronic airway inflammation is central to the pathophysiology of asthma, leading to airway dysfunction and irreversible airway wall remodeling, which are significant reasons for poor disease control despite standardized treatment [7]. If asthma in early childhood is not effectively controlled, it can develop into persistent asthma, increasing the risk of chronic airway disease and severely affecting children's health and quality of life [5]. Therefore, effectively controlling asthma progression in preschool children is of great significance [8].

Diet, as one of the environmental factors, may influence the occurrence and development of asthma. The Western diet is high in animal products, processed foods, refined grains, and sugary drinks, but low in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes. Traditional Latin American diets, rich in corn, beans, whole grains, and fruits/vegetables, with moderate animal product and healthy fat intake, are seeing a shift towards the Western diet due to urbanization [9]. This change may correlate with increased asthma rates [10]. This dietary transition has been associated with an increase in the prevalence of various health issues, including asthma.

Studies have explored the relationship between these dietary patterns and asthma, though results have been inconsistent. Evidence suggests that diets emphasizing the consumption of plant-based foods might protect against asthma development and improve asthma symptoms through their effects on systemic inflammation, oxidation, and microbial composition [11, 12]. A cross-sectional study observed an inverse association between a fruit intake of ≥3 times per week and severe asthma symptoms in children aged 6–7 years [13]. Additionally, compared to children of healthy weight, children with obesity are at higher risk of asthma [14].Fat indices, as emerging indicators of obesity, may play a mediating role between dietary patterns and asthma risk [15–17].

Diet is a key factor in influencing the occurrence or alleviation of asthma symptoms. Different dietary patterns and nutrient intake are associated with the risk of asthma. Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), Alternative Healthy Eating Index (AHEI), Healthy Eating Index 2020 (HEI-2020), Mediterranean Diet (MED), and Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) are commonly used to assess dietary patterns, with studies exploring their relationship with asthma, but with inconsistent results [18–20].

DASH diet is designed to lower blood pressure and promote heart health by emphasizing fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and low-fat dairy while limiting red meat and sugary items. Vahid Arabi et al. found that higher adherence to a DASH-like diet resulted in lower odds of asthma confirmed by a doctor among the whole population [21]. AHEI evaluates diet quality based on intake of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts, legumes, and polyunsaturated fats while penalizing red and processed meats, sugary drinks, and sodium. Zhen Li et.al observed a significant interaction between AHEI-2010 and change in asthma symptoms [22]. HEI-2020 measures alignment with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans by scoring intake of various food components, with higher scores indicating greater consistency with dietary recommendations; Previous studies have shown that a high HEI-2020 score is associated with asthma [20].

MED draws inspiration from traditional Mediterranean eating patterns, emphasizing plant-based foods, moderate fish and poultry, and olive oil as the primary fat source while minimizing red meat. An observational and cross-sectional study reveals that high adherence to MED was associated with reduced risk of having peak expiratory flow rate < 80% of its peak predictive value [23]. and DII assesses the inflammatory potential of a diet, with lower scores indicating reduced inflammation risk due to higher intake of anti-inflammatory foods like fruits and vegetables and lower intake of pro-inflammatory items like saturated fats [18].

Therefore, this study utilizes NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) 2011-2018 data to analyze the association between the aforementioned dietary indices and asthma in children aged 3–5 years, and to evaluate the mediating roles of Body Mass Index (BMI), Lipid accumulation product (LAP) and Visceral Adipose Tissue Index (VAI). Early childhood is marked by rapid physiological development and immune system maturation, making children in this age range particularly susceptible to the influence of dietary factors on asthma onset and progression [24]. Research focused on this age group could reveal the potential impact of diet on the early development of asthma. We hypothesize that healthier dietary patterns will be associated with lower asthma prevalence, mediated through reduced adiposity and inflammation.

Methods

The data for this study were derived from NHANES 2011-2018(https//www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html), which utilized a stratified, multi-stage, probability sampling design that is representative of the national population [25]. NHANES is a publicly available database that has been approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics in the United States [26]. For the NHANES cycles used in this study (2011-2018), the protocol approval numbers are as follows: NHANES 2011-2012: Protocol #2011-17, NHANES 2013-2014: Continuation of Protocol #2011-17, NHANES 2015-2016: Continuation of Protocol #2011-17, NHANES 2017-2018: Protocol #2018-01 (Effective beginning October 26, 2017).

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A detailed description of the database has been published [27]. Since the data was derived from NHANES (a publicly available database) and due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital waived the requirement for ethical approval of this study. The requirement for written informed consent was also waived by the Institutional Review Board. All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations [28, 29].

Study population

Initially, the study cohort included 39,156 participants aged 2 years and older from the NHANES 2011-2018 dataset. A systematic application of exclusion criteria was employed to refine this cohort. First, 19,208 participants were excluded due to missing variables needed to estimate asthma. This resulted in a reduced sample size of 19,948 individuals. Subsequently, 10,738 participants were excluded for missing data on diet, leaving 9,210 participants. Further refinement was made by excluding an additional 2,902 participants with missing covariates. Consequently, the final analytic sample comprised 6,308 eligible participants Children aged 3–5 years were included, and participants with missing key variables, dietary data, or covariates were excluded. Ultimately, 1695 children were included in the study, with the participant selection process and the number of participants at each exclusion stage detailed in Figure 1.

Exposure and outcomes

The dietary indices included DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, DII, and MED, with scores calculated from 24-hour dietary recalls [30]. The DASH score is based on eight components (total fat, saturated fat, protein, fiber, cholesterol, calcium, magnesium, and potassium), with scores generated by the sum of all nutrient targets met. Each nutrient target was scored based on whether the recommended intake levels were met, with points summed to generate a total DASH score ranging from 8 to 40 points (Table S1) [31]. The AHEI-2010 score [32], derived from 11 food components and scores, is scored on a 0–110 scale, emphasizing high intake of fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and low intake of sodium and red and processed meats.

The HEI-2020 score [31, 33], an updated version used to assess adherence to the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (13 food components, 0–100 points, Table S2), is based on density (e.g., amount per 1000 kcal) rather than absolute amounts. The DII score, developed by Shivappa et al., assesses the potential inflammatory level of diets, with higher scores indicating a more pro-inflammatory diet and lower scores suggesting an anti-inflammatory diet, calculated using 45 food parameters, yielding a total DII value that signifies the overall inflammatory effect of the diet. A positive DII score indicates a pro-inflammatory diet, a negative score points to an anti-inflammatory diet, and a score close to zero suggests little to no impact on inflammation [34, 35]. In our study, the MED score was determined based on nine specific components, which reflected the degree of adherence to the traditional Mediterranean diet. These components included vegetables, legumes, fruits, nuts, whole grains, fish, the ratio of monounsaturated to saturated fats, red meat and processed meat, as well as alcohol. The score for each component was based on whether the intake exceeded the median, with the score ranging from 0 (least adherence) to 9 (highest adherence)(Table S3-4) [36]. For statistical purposes, the dietary index scores were divided into tertiles (T).

Asthma determination is based on an individual’s self-report before diagnosis by a physician in a face-to-face interview. Participants were queried with the following question: “Has a doctor or another health care provider informed you that you have experienced asthma?”.Based on answers to these questions, asthma attacks were divided into an asthma attack subgroup and a no asthma attack subgroup [37].

Covariates

Covariates included gender, age, race (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other race), BMI, PIR (Poverty Income Ratio), uric acid, HDL (high-density lipoprotein), LDL (low-density lipoprotein), cholesterol, triglycerides, blood glucose, creatinine, glycated hemoglobin, BUN (blood urea nitrogen), waist circumference (WC), VAI, and LAP. BMI, VAI, and LAP are adiposity indices and also serve as mediators in this analysis. BMI is calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. VAI and LAP are calculated using specific formulas: for males, LAP is (WC - 65) × TG and VAI is (WC/(39.68 + 1.88 × BMI)) × (TG/1.03) × (1.31/(HDL - C)); for females, LAP is (WC - 58) × TG and VAI is (WC/(39.58 + 1.89 × BMI)) × (TG/0.81) × (1.52/(HDL - C)).

Statistical analysis

Considering the complex sampling design of the survey, our study conducted weighted analyses according to NHANES guidelines [38]. Categorical variables were described using counts and their corresponding percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations if the data were normally distributed, or medians and interquartile ranges if the data were not normally distributed. For categorical variables, chi-square tests and Rao & Scott corrections were used to determine P-values, and for continuous variables that were skewed or not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied to compare these variables across multiple groups. To compare prevalence rates, chi-square tests were conducted to evaluate whether there were significant differences in the distribution of asthma across tertile groups for each dietary index.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to determine the relationship between the five dietary indices and asthma, with dietary indices represented both as continuous variables and tertiles, using odds ratios (ORs) and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to present the results. Logistic regression analyses were conducted based on three different models, with Model 1 adjusting for no covariates, Model 2 adjusting for gender, age, race, and BMI, and Model 3 further adjusting for PIR, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, triglycerides and glycohemoglobin. Tertile-based stratification was applied to explore the relationship between dietary indices and asthma risk. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to explore the nonlinear relationship between dietary indices and asthma risk. The mediation analysis was conducted using the R package 'mediation', applying logistic regression models to evaluate the indirect effects of dietary indices on asthma through BMI, LAP, and VAI as mediators. The analysis involved estimating paths a (dietary indices to mediators), b (mediators to asthma), and c' (dietary indices to asthma controlling for mediators). The significance of the indirect effect was assessed using bootstrap methods with 5,000 replications to generate robust confidence intervals. The percentage of the total effect mediated by BMI, LAP, and VAI was calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect and multiplying by 100, thereby quantifying the proportion of asthma risk attributed to these adiposity indices. Multiple imputation methods were used to handle missing data. Statistical significance was accepted at P-values less than 0.05. All statistical assessments were performed using R software version 4.4.2.

The analysis of the dietary index scores revealed significant differences between children with asthma and those without. Specifically, the children with asthma exhibited significantly lower median scores in the DASH, AHEI, and HEI-2020 dietary indices compared to those without asthma. The median DASH score for children with asthma was 22.00 (19.50, 25.00), significantly lower than the median score of 23.50 (20.50, 26.50) observed in children without asthma (P < 0.05). Similarly, the median AHEI score was 34.51 (29.15, 40.38) for children with asthma, compared to 36.13 (30.49, 42.27) for those without asthma (P < 0.05). The median HEI-2020 score was also lower in the asthma group at 46.55 (40.25, 53.85) compared to 48.96 (42.12, 56.62) in the non-asthmatic group (P < 0.05).

Furthermore, a lower median MED score in the asthma group suggests that adherence to a Mediterranean-style diet may be associated with reduced asthma risk. The median MED score for children with asthma was 3.00 (2.00, 3.50), which was significantly lower than the median score of 3.00 (2.50, 4.00) observed in children without asthma (P < 0.05).No significant differences were observed in BMI, waist circumference, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, and triglycerides between the two groups (P>0.05).

For the AHEI, the prevalence of asthma in T1 is 17.4%, significantly higher than the 9.3% observed in T3. Similarly, for the DASH index, the prevalence in T1 is 15.7%, compared to 9.9% in T3. Association between DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, DII, MED and asthma

Table 2 details the association between dietary indices and asthma risk among children aged 3–5 years. In the analysis of continuous variables, higher scores in DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, and MED were consistently associated with a reduced risk of asthma across all models, with significant odds ratios (ORs) observed in Model 3 (DASH: OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.90–0.97.90.97; AHEI: OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99.97.99; HEI-2020: OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99.98.99; MED: OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.94.84.94). When dietary indices were considered as categorical variables based on tertiles, similar patterns were observed. The analysis revealed that the highest tertile of DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, and MED showed significantly lower odds of asthma compared to the lowest tertile, with odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 0.67 to 0.89 in Model 3 (P < 0.05). Specifically, for DASH, the OR was 0.67 (95% CI: 0.58–0.79.58.79) for T3 compared to T1; for AHEI, the OR was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.61–0.82.61.82); for HEI-2020, the OR was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.61–0.83.61.83); and for MED, the OR was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.94.84.94). These findings indicate that higher adherence to healthier dietary patterns, as indicated by higher scores in these indices, is associated with a reduced risk of asthma.

Mediation effects of BMI, LAP, and VAI on diet indices and asthma

Figure 4 illustrates the mediation effects of BMI, LAP, and VAI on the association between dietary indices and asthma. The mediation analysis indicates that BMI significantly mediates the relationship between DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, and MED with asthma, with mediation proportions of 23.45%, 18.98%, 19.76%, and 22.80%, respectively(P<0.05). The mediation effect of BMI is not significant in the association between DII and asthma. Additionally, LAP and VAI also play mediating roles in the association between dietary indices and asthma. Specifically, LAP mediates 11.20% of the association between DASH and asthma; 9.15% between AHEI and asthma; 10.84% between HEI-2020 and asthma; and 8.15% between MED and asthma. VAI mediates 2.37% of the association between DASH and asthma; 2.21% between AHEI and asthma; 2.73% between HEI-2020 and asthma; and 2.89% between MED and asthma. Similarly, the mediation effects of LAP and VAI are not significant in the association between DII and asthma(P<0.05). LAP and VAI play smaller mediating roles than BMI, explaining about 10–11% and 2–3% of the asthma risk reduction for these diets.

Discussion

In our study, we have explored the intricate relationship between dietary patterns and asthma risk, focusing on the inflammatory mechanisms that may be modulated by different diets. The results indicate that healthier dietary patterns are associated with lower asthma prevalence, and this association is indeed mediated through reduced adiposity and inflammation, aligning with the expectations set forth in our hypothesis.

Asthma is a chronic respiratory condition characterized by airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness [39]. Inhalation therapy is fundamental to asthma treatment. However, asthma management should not overlook non-pharmacological interventions [40]. The mechanism by which dietary patterns influence asthma risk through impacting inflammatory status is noteworthy. Diets high in sugar, saturated fats, and processed foods may increase levels of inflammatory markers, thereby raising the risk of asthma [41]. In contrast, diets rich in anti-inflammatory foods, such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and foods high in Omega-3 fatty acids, may lower these markers, potentially reducing the risk of asthma [42]. High fat intake and low fiber consumption are associated with worsened airway inflammation and lung function in asthma patients [43].

An Australian study found that severe asthma patients consumed more fat and less fiber than healthy individuals, and these dietary patterns were associated with increased airway eosinophilic cells, while fiber intake was positively correlated with FEV1 [44]. Similarly, Kim et al. found that a high-fat diet (60% of energy from fat) increased airway hyperreactivity by promoting the production of pulmonary cytokines [45]. These findings underscore the potential of dietary interventions for asthma prevention and management, emphasizing the importance of overall diet quality and specific dietary components in asthma risk assessment [46].

In children, BMI, while widely used as an obesity indicator, does not provide information on body fat distribution [47]. LAP and VAI, as emerging indicators, can more accurately reflect the accumulation of visceral fat and body fat distribution [46, 48]. The accumulation of visceral fat may lead to increased release of inflammatory cytokines, potentially affecting airway inflammation and increasing the risk of asthma [49]. Stratakis et al. published a multi-cohort study in the United States involving 8713 children in 2022, indicating that children with asthma had a 23% higher risk of developing obesity compared to those without asthma [50]. The higher risk of obesity in asthma patients may be due to corticosteroids in asthma treatment, airway inflammation exacerbating atherosclerotic inflammation, or shared upstream factors affecting asthma and weight [49].

Additionally, visceral fat may indirectly affect asthma risk by influencing hormone levels and insulin sensitivity [51]. The significant mediating role of fat indices in the association between DASH, AHEI, HEI-2020, MED, and asthma suggests that controlling obesity is associated with reduced asthma risk [52, 53]. Moreover, obesity may be associated with the onset and progression of asthma by affecting inflammation and oxidative stress levels [52]. LAP and VAI, as new mediators, provide a more comprehensive perspective to understand the relationship between dietary patterns and asthma [54]. Sideleva et al. observed higher levels of IL-6 in the group of patients with the highest percentile of visceral fat, associated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome [55]. The mediating roles of BMI, LAP, and VAI highlight the importance of comprehensively managing children's weight and body fat distribution as a strategy to potentially prevent the onset and progression of asthma [56].

Different dietary patterns may affect body fat distribution and the accumulation of visceral fat [57]. For instance, diets rich in trans fats and saturated fats may promote the accumulation of visceral fat, while those rich in unsaturated fats, dietary fiber, and anti-inflammatory foods may help reduce visceral fat [58]. Healthy dietary habits, such as the DASH diet and Mediterranean diet, are typically associated with lower levels of visceral fat and better metabolic health [58]. Clinicians should address modifiable lifestyle factors and consider integrating a brief assessment into regular check-ups. This approach can help identify children who may benefit from a referral to a dietitian or participation in a structured weight management program.

Our study's findings indicate that specific dietary patterns, particularly those high in unsaturated fats, dietary fiber, and anti-inflammatory foods, are associated with lower levels of visceral fat, which may contribute to a reduced risk of asthma in children [59, 60]. These insights can inform dietary guidance for pediatric asthma prevention by emphasizing the importance of a balanced diet.

The study offers valuable insights into the relationship between dietary patterns, body fat distribution, and asthma risk, highlighting the mediating role of fat indices. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations inherent to our study design. The retrospective nature of the study introduces potential biases, and the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causality. Additionally, reliance on self-reported asthma events may introduce reporting bias, which could affect the accuracy of our findings. Future research should aim to explore these causal relationships more thoroughly, potentially through prospective cohort studies or intervention trials that can more definitively link dietary patterns and visceral fat accumulation to asthma risk. Moreover, the recall bias associated with dietary assessments can lead to misestimation of actual dietary intake. our study does not account for other factors that might mediate the relationship between diet and asthma, such as total calorie intake, changes in gut microbiota, oxidative stress, and epigenetic modifications. These factors could also play significant roles in asthma pathophysiology and should be considered in future investigations.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that adherence to healthier dietary patterns may significantly reduce the risk of asthma in children. The observed association between a diet low in inflammatory potential and a decreased likelihood of asthma suggests that anti-inflammatory diets could play a crucial role in asthma prevention. These insights could guide future dietary recommendations and interventions aimed at decreasing asthma prevalence among pediatric populations.
