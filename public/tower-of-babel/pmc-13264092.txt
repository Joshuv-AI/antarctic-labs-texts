Trends in inpatient burden from pressure injuries in the United States paediatric population: Cross-sectional study of a national inpatient sample 2010–2019
Authors: Qing Ou, Dan He, Wenjuan Yan, Hongjuan Wang, Zisheng Ma, Xuehua Li, Hao Xie, Hongyun Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure injuries contribute substantially to patient morbidity and healthcare costs. However, comprehensive analyses of their burden within the U.S. paediatric population are limited. As a cross-sectional database, the National Inpatient Sample permits estimation of hospitalization-based prevalence rather than true prevalence. We hypothesized that pressure injury burden would increase over time with significant sociodemographic variations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used the National Inpatient Sample (2010–2019) to analyse temporal trends in the hospitalization-based prevalence, length of stay, and total inpatient costs for paediatric patients (1–17 years) with a diagnosis of pressure injury. Trends were analysed using Joinpoint regression, and differences across sociodemographic subgroups were examined.ResultsAmong 2640,461 weighted paediatric admissions, 32,905 cases involved a pressure injury diagnosis. The annual prevalence increased significantly from 0.20% in 2010 to 0.31% in 2019 (annual percentage change = 6.1%; 95% confidence interval 4.6%–7.7%). Mean length of stay significantly increased from 16.42 to 19.25 days (average annual percentage change = 1.54%, 95% confidence interval 0.03%–3.07%). Mean total hospitalization costs (inflation-adjusted to 2019 U.S. dollars) for patients with pressure injury diagnoses rose substantially from $137,609 to $309,776. Variations were observed across sociodemographic groups.ConclusionThe increasing prevalence, length of stay, and total hospitalization costs among hospitalized U.S. paediatric patients aged 1–17 years with pressure injury diagnoses highlight a growing clinical and economic challenge. The observed differences across age, race, and socioeconomic groups warrant further investigation to inform future surveillance and research. Because the National Inpatient Sample does not distinguish hospital-acquired from community-acquired pressure injuries and does not isolate pressure injury–attributable costs from total hospitalization costs, causal inferences and direct policy recommendations require caution.Keywords: Pressure injuries, Paediatric population, Epidemiology, Economic burden, Healthcare costs, Hospital expenditure, Sociodemographic differences

Introduction

According to the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (2024), pressure injuries (PIs) are localized damage to the skin and underlying soft tissue, usually over a bony prominence or related to a medical or other device, resulting from sustained pressure, often in combination with shear (National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel, 2024). These lesions can occur anywhere pressure is applied, though in adult populations they commonly develop over the sacrum, ischial tuberosities, and heels (Mondragon and Zito, 2025; National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel, 2024). Globally, PIs contribute substantially to patient morbidity, prolong hospital stays, incur high treatment costs, and are associated with increased mortality in hospitalized patients (Thomas et al., 1996).

However, epidemiological data, randomized trials, and evidence-based guidelines have predominantly focused on adult populations (Moore et al., 2011; Padula and Delarmente, 2019). In contrast, paediatric research on PI prevention, risk stratification, and management remains limited. Clinical protocols for children are frequently extrapolated from adult guidelines, overlooking critical differences in skin physiology, mobility patterns, and care settings that distinguish paediatric patients from adults (Schindler et al., 2011). Fortunately, the 2019 international clinical practice guideline for prevention and treatment of pressure ulcers/injuries—developed jointly by the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (EPUAP), the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP), and the Pan Pacific Pressure Injury Alliance (PPPIA)—provides the most current evidence-based recommendations; however, paediatric-specific guidance within this framework remains limited, and many preventive strategies continue to rely on adult-derived evidence (European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel et al., 2019).

Although the Braden Scale has been validated in adult populations, the paediatric-specific Braden Q Scale is more commonly used in children. The Braden Q Scale was originally validated in children aged 21 days to 8 years, with risk factors largely derived from adult-derived domains; consequently, its predictive accuracy in older children and general paediatric wards remains uncertain (Curley et al., 2003; Liao et al., 2018). The Glamorgan Paediatric Pressure Ulcer Risk Assessment Scale was developed using detailed paediatric inpatient data and clinician expertise, yet independent validation studies have questioned its ability to discriminate risk in low-risk settings (Kottner et al., 2014; Willock et al., 2008). Neither instrument has been universally validated across the full paediatric age spectrum, and both may exhibit reduced sensitivity and specificity in neonates, infants, and older children, potentially underestimating risk and leading to suboptimal allocation of preventive resources (Willock et al., 2016).

Children differ from adults in several fundamental ways that influence both PI susceptibility and healing trajectories. Neonates and young infants possess thinner epidermal and dermal layers, lower collagen density, and immature subcutaneous fat distribution, all of which diminish skin tensile strength and resilience to shear and friction forces (Nie et al., 2022; Reed et al., 2021). Throughout growth, changes in body proportions–ranging from immobilized neonates to ambulatory adolescents–alter pressure distribution on support surfaces, necessitating age-appropriate mattresses, repositioning schedules, and developmentally tailored nutritional support.

It is important to note that the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) pressure ulcer prevention toolkit was developed primarily for adult hospitalized populations, and its recommendations are not directly transferable to neonates and infants without paediatric-specific adaptation (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2014).

Although paediatric-specific factors are recognized, few large-scale studies have systematically quantified PI prevalence across diverse paediatric age subcohorts. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of hospital-acquired pressure injuries in paediatric populations ranges from 1.4% to 27.0% depending on the setting, with higher rates observed in intensive care units (Triantafyllou et al., 2021). Economic evaluations of treatment costs, length of stay, and quality-of-life impacts in paediatric PI populations are likewise limited, hindering data-driven policy development and resource allocation (Şimşek et al., 2023).

A critical limitation of administrative databases such as the NIS is the inability to distinguish between hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs) and community-acquired pressure injuries present at admission. Consequently, the prevalence estimates reported in this study reflect all pressure injury diagnoses documented during hospitalization, not exclusively HAPIs.

To address these critical gaps, we performed a retrospective analysis of the U.S. National Inpatient Sample (NIS) from 2010 to 2019 with the primary aim of quantifying the temporal trends in the burden (hospitalization-based prevalence, length of stay, and total inpatient costs) of pressure injuries among hospitalized paediatric patients aged 1–17 years. A secondary aim was to characterize variations in these trends across sociodemographic subgroups (age, sex, race/ethnicity, household income, and insurance type), with the goal of identifying high-burden populations that may benefit from enhanced prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP). Access to and use of the NIS database was obtained through a HCUP Data Use Agreement (DUA). This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (IRB Protocol No NFEC-2025–021), which determined it to be exempt from full board review because it utilized publicly available, de-identified administrative data that do not constitute human subjects research as defined under 45 CFR 46.

Study population

We included all hospital discharge records for patients aged 1–17 years with a primary or secondary diagnosis of PI between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019. Patients under one year of age were excluded from the stratified analysis due to the limited number of cases with a PI diagnosis. Discharges with missing data on key variables (age, sex, race, or income) were excluded from the respective subgroup analyses. As the NIS is a discharge-level database, individual patients with multiple hospitalizations within the study period may contribute more than one record to the analysis; this limitation reflects the inherent structure of administrative discharge data and is acknowledged accordingly. In-hospital deaths were retained in the analysis, consistent with standard NIS-based prevalence and burden studies; however, we acknowledge that decedents with complex underlying conditions (e.g., malignancy) may incur higher total hospitalization costs unrelated to pressure injury treatment. The inclusion of these cases may inflate cost estimates. This limitation is addressed in the Discussion. All PI staging categories (Stage I through IV, unstageable, and deep tissue injury) identified by the relevant ICD codes were included.

Variables

Outcome measures: We quantified pressure injury burden using two primary outcomes: hospital length of stay in days, and total inpatient costs (U.S. dollars). Total inpatient costs were adjusted for inflation to 2019 U.S. dollars using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) to ensure comparability across the study period. We emphasize that these costs represent total hospitalization costs among patients with a pressure injury diagnosis, not costs directly attributable to pressure injury treatment itself.

Sociodemographic variables: Variables of interest included age categories (1–5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–17 years), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander, other), household income quartiles (< $25,000; $25,001–$50,000; $50,001–$75,000; $75,001–$100,000), and primary insurance coverage (Medicare; Medicaid; private/health maintenance organization (HMO); self-pay; no charge; other).

Procedure

Cases were identified using ICD-9-CM codes (707.0, 707.2, and subcategories) for discharges from 2010 through September 2015, and ICD-10-CM codes (L89 and subcategories) for discharges from October 2015 through 2019. Complete lists of the diagnostic codes are provided in Appendix Tables A1 and A2. To maintain coding consistency across the ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM transition, code mapping was performed using the HCUP official crosswalk tool, which maps ICD-9-CM codes to their ICD-10-CM equivalents based on clinical equivalence. The transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM in October 2015 introduced greater coding specificity, which may have influenced the recorded prevalence of pressure injuries and contributed to temporal trends.

Statistical analysis

We summarized characteristics of hospitalized paediatric patients with pressure injuries using unweighted patient counts alongside survey-weighted national estimates and corresponding percentages for categorical variables. To account for the NIS sampling framework, we applied provided survey weights within IBM SPSS® Statistics 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), utilizing the Complex Samples module. Our analysis incorporated strata defined by hospital attributes and data year, cluster identifiers, and discharge weights to generate nationally representative estimates. We calculated the survey-weighted annual mean length of stay, annual mean total cost of hospitalization, and their standard errors from 2010 to 2019 for all inpatients with PIs, both overall and stratified by sociodemographic subgroup.

We applied the Joinpoint regression program (National Cancer Institute) to estimate piecewise log-linear trends in the survey-weighted mean length of stay and mean total hospitalization cost in the overall and stratified samples. Turning points in trends (joinpoints) were identified using Monte Carlo permutation-based tests, and the annual percentage change with a 95% confidence interval was estimated for each trend segment. The average annual percentage change was calculated for the entire period (2010–2019). Differences in trends between sociodemographic subgroups (e.g., male vs. female, Medicaid vs. private insurance) were evaluated using the parallel pairwise comparison function in Joinpoint Regression Program version 4.9.0.0 (National Cancer Institute). This method tests whether two regression lines are parallel by comparing their slopes (annual percentage changes) while accounting for the covariance structure of the survey-weighted data. Due to the exploratory nature of these comparisons, we did not apply multiplicity adjustments; however, we interpreted findings cautiously, emphasizing effect sizes and confidence intervals over p-values alone. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data rates for key variables were as follows: race/ethnicity (3.8%), household income (4.2%), and insurance type (2.1%). Given the low proportion of missing data (<5% for all variables) and the lack of systematic patterns in missingness (assessed via cross-tabulation with age, sex, and hospital characteristics), we employed complete case analysis for subgroup comparisons. Sensitivity analyses including cases with missing data (coded as "unknown") yielded comparable trend estimates, suggesting that exclusion did not substantially bias national estimates.

Results

Participant characteristics

There were 11,189,772 (weighted: 55,770,894) hospital admissions from 2010 to 2019, and 2640,461 (weighted: 13,152,819) paediatric patients had complete data. Among those, 6602 cases (weighted: 32,905) of paediatric inpatients with PI were identified. As summarized in Table 1, the hospitalized paediatric cohort with pressure injuries had a mean age of 12.5 years, and males represented 58.8% of cases. Nearly half of the patients were White (48.6%), 30.5% came from households with annual incomes below $25,000, and 56.9% were insured primarily through Medicaid.

Temporal trends in pressure injury prevalence, hospital length of stay, and inpatient costs

The annual prevalence of PI increased from 0.20% in 2010 to 0.31% in 2019 (annual percentage change = 6.1% [95% confidence interval 4.6% to 7.7%]) (Fig. 1). The annual mean length of stay increased overall from 16.42 days in 2010 to 19.25 days in 2019 (average annual percentage change = 1.54% [95% confidence interval 0.03% to 3.07%]) (Fig. 2). Additionally, the annual mean total hospitalization cost increased significantly from 2010 to 2019 ($137,609.1 to $309,775.8). Joinpoint identified a significant increasing trend from 2010 to 2012 (annual percentage change = 16.43%), after which the mean total hospital cost showed stable growth (Fig. 3).

Age-related differences: Among all age groups, the 1–5 age group exhibited the numerically largest, albeit non-significant, upward trend (average annual percentage change = 3.21%, 95% confidence interval: −2.36% to 9.28%; P = 0.25), corresponding to an absolute increase from 16.61 days (2010) to 21.40 days (2019). The 6–10 age group (average annual percentage change = −0.11%, 95% confidence interval: −2.54% to 2.38%; P = 0.94) and the 16–17 age group (average annual percentage change = −0.14%, 95% confidence interval: −3.29% to 2.89%; P = 0.86) showed slight, non-significant decreases.

Sex-related differences: For annual mean length of stay, paediatric patients with PI demonstrated an upward trend across both sexes, with a slightly higher rate of increase observed in males (average annual percentage change = 1.77% [95% confidence interval 0.08% to 3.54%]; P < 0.05) than in females (average annual percentage change = 1.03% [95% confidence interval −1.53% to 3.72%]; P = 0.42). No joinpoints were identified for either sex, indicating no significant changes in the upward trend of hospital stays across the decade.

Racial differences: The data revealed varying trends in hospital stay duration among paediatric PI patients of different races from 2010 to 2019. While White patients (average annual percentage change = 1.38% [95% confidence interval −1.25% to 4.22%]; P = 0.29) and Hispanic patients (average annual percentage change = 2.03% [95% confidence interval −2.38% to 6.62%]; P = 0.35) experienced an increase in hospital stay duration, Black patients showed a slight decrease (average annual percentage change = −0.83% [95% confidence interval −4.70% to 3.33%]; P = 0.68). The "Other" racial category had the most notable increase (average annual percentage change = 3.12% [95% confidence interval −0.24% to 6.18%]; P = 0.07). The absence of joinpoints across all racial groups suggests that the trends were consistent over the decade, without any significant changes in direction.

Income-related differences: The trends in hospital stay duration among paediatric PI patients across different income levels varied. Patients in the <$25,000 income percentile experienced a steady increase in hospital stay duration (average annual percentage change = 2.04% [95% confidence interval 0.01% to 4.19%]; P < 0.05), while those in the $25,001–$50,000 income percentile also saw an increase (average annual percentage change = 1.87% [95% confidence interval −2.86% to 7.11%]; P = 0.41). In contrast, patients in the $50,001–$75,000 income percentile initially experienced a decrease (2010–2016: annual percentage change = −5.57% [95% confidence interval −34.17% to 34.38%]; P = 0.78) followed by a notable increase after 2016 (2016–2019: annual percentage change = 20.35% [95% confidence interval −22.24% to 76.47%]; P = 0.32).

Insurance-related differences: While patients with Medicaid (average annual percentage change = 2.54% [95% confidence interval −0.13% to 5.41%]; P = 0.06) and self-pay insurance (average annual percentage change = 2.79% [95% confidence interval −7.51% to 11.13%]; P = 0.74) experienced an increase in hospital stay duration, those with private/HMO insurance showed negligible change (average annual percentage change = 0.02% [95% confidence interval −2.15% to 2.38%]; P = 0.99). The "Other" category was the only group with a decrease in hospital stay duration (average annual percentage change = −4.30% [95% confidence interval −9.98% to 2.43%]; P = 0.20). Similarly, no joinpoints were detected across any insurance category, indicating stable directional trends throughout the study period.

Males saw a steeper increase between 2010 and 2013 (annual percentage change = 13.75% [95% confidence interval 9.57% to 20.70%]; P < 0.05) compared with females, after which the pace of cost growth in males tapered off (2013–2019: annual percentage change = 6.39% [95% confidence interval 0.74% to 8.01%]; P < 0.05). Females showed a consistent upward trend (average annual percentage change = 8.18% [95% confidence interval 4.86% to 11.74%]; P < 0.05) with no joinpoints identified.

Discussion

Pressure injuries in the paediatric population represent a significant burden on healthcare systems, affecting patient outcomes and increasing hospital utilization. Using the National Inpatient Sample from 2010 to 2019, we quantified PI prevalence, characterized temporal trends, and explored variations across demographic and socioeconomic strata. Our findings highlight several key trends and variations that warrant further investigation.

Increasing prevalence and costs

The annual prevalence of PIs among paediatric inpatients increased from 0.20% in 2010 to 0.31% in 2019, indicating a growing problem despite efforts to improve prevention strategies (Choragudi et al., 2024). This trend aligns with global observations, where PIs remain a persistent challenge in healthcare settings (Triantafyllou et al., 2021). The increasing prevalence may be attributed to several factors, including improved awareness and documentation of PIs, as well as the growing complexity of paediatric cases requiring prolonged hospitalization and the use of medical devices, which are known causes of PIs in paediatric patients (McAuliffe et al., 2023; Nie et al., 2024). Additionally, the transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM coding in 2015 may have influenced the capture of PIs due to enhanced specificity, although the overall upward trend appeared robust despite this transition (Li et al., 2020).

The substantial increase in inflation-adjusted total hospitalization costs from $137,609 in 2010 to $309,776 in 2019 reflects rising overall expenditures among patients with pressure injury diagnoses. This analysis does not, however, isolate costs directly attributable to pressure injury treatment from costs related to underlying conditions. Therefore, the observed increase in total hospitalization costs should be interpreted as an indicator of greater resource utilization among paediatric inpatients with pressure injuries, rather than as a direct quantification of pressure injury–attributable economic burden. This aligns with findings from adult population studies reporting similar patterns of increased total hospitalization costs among patients with pressure injury diagnoses (McAuliffe et al., 2023).

The observed variations in costs and length of stay across racial groups likely reflect a combination of structural and healthcare system factors rather than biological differences. These may include differential access to preventive care, timing of presentation to hospital (which may reflect the proportion of community-acquired versus hospital-acquired pressure injuries), documentation practices, and unmeasured socioeconomic factors correlated with race in administrative data. We caution against interpreting these findings as evidence of intrinsic biological differences between groups.

Differences across sociodemographic subgroups

Our analysis revealed differences in PI burden across sociodemographic subgroups. Patterns in length of stay and costs varied across racial and ethnic groups, and by insurance type. For instance, while White and Hispanic patients experienced increases in hospital stay duration, Black patients showed a slight decrease over the study period. Similarly, patients with Medicaid or lower household incomes faced prolonged hospitalizations compared to those with private insurance. The observed sex difference, with males showing a slightly higher increase in hospital stay duration, warrants further investigation. While prior studies have not consistently highlighted sex differences in PIs, our findings suggest a potential need for further examination of sex-specific risk factors or care patterns (Howard and Taylor, 2009; Lichterfeld-Kottner et al., 2020).

Particularly notable is the trend observed among Black paediatric patients, who experienced a sharp reversal in total hospitalization costs, shifting from a significant increase (annual percentage change = 12.43%, 2010–2017) to a marked decrease (annual percentage change = −16.22%, 2017–2019). While the confidence interval for the latter segment is wide and the decline did not reach statistical significance, this pattern may reflect the downstream effects of healthcare quality improvement initiatives that accelerated after the 2014 Medicaid expansion, which disproportionately extended coverage to Black families in previously non-expanding states. Alternatively, shifts in case-mix complexity, changes in PI coding practices following the introduction of ICD-10-CM in 2015, or regression to the mean following an unusually high-cost period may partly account for this trend. This finding warrants dedicated prospective investigation.

Children from lower-income families may have less favorable underlying health profiles or reduced access to preventive resources (Dreyfus et al., 2018), while variations in healthcare access and quality across racial and ethnic groups may contribute to observed patterns (Triantafyllou et al., 2021).

Age-Related patterns

The finding that the 11–15 age group showed the most significant increase in total hospitalization costs is notable. This age group corresponds to adolescence, a period of rapid growth and significant physiological changes, including alterations in body composition, increased adipose tissue deposition, and skeletal growth spurts that may alter pressure distribution and skin tolerance to mechanical loading. Children in this age range may be more prone to certain medical conditions requiring complex care; however, our data do not allow causal attribution, and the observed cost increase should be interpreted as a descriptive trend warranting further investigation (Oozageer Gunowa et al., 2018). Further research is needed to understand the specific factors driving this increase. Additionally, future research should specifically examine PI burden in neonates and infants (<1 year) using neonatal intensive care unit (NICU)-specific databases, as this high-risk population was underrepresented in our analysis due to limited sample size in the NIS dataset.

Implications for practice and policy

The increasing prevalence and total hospitalization costs among paediatric inpatients with pressure injury diagnoses highlight the need for improved preventive measures and resource allocation. Healthcare providers should focus on early identification and management of risk factors for PIs, particularly in high-risk populations such as immobilized children and those requiring medical devices (Nie et al., 2024; Willock et al., 2016).

Notably, the 11–15 age group exhibited the steepest increase in hospitalization costs; for this population, clinicians should consider developmentally tailored interventions addressing the unique challenges of pubertal growth spurts, increased body mass, and potential non-adherence to repositioning protocols in adolescents. While causality cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional analysis, the observed steeper cost trajectory in the 11–15 age group suggests that quality improvement initiatives should prioritize this developmental stage, potentially incorporating puberty-specific skin integrity assessments and reinforced prevention protocols during this high-risk growth period. Similarly, the 1–5 age group showed the longest hospital stays, warranting age-appropriate skin assessment protocols and caregiver education for preschool-aged children.

It is essential to recognize that the Braden Q Scale has not been validated in children older than 8 years; therefore, its use in the 11–15 and 16–17 age groups should be supplemented with clinical judgment and developmentally appropriate assessment tools (Curley et al., 2003; Liao et al., 2018).

From a nursing administration perspective, these findings support the implementation of stratified quality improvement initiatives. Specifically, nurse administrators should prioritize resource allocation and universal prevention protocols that ensure equitable care for the 11–15 age group and Medicaid-insured patients, where cost trajectories were steepest. It is imperative that all pressure injury prevention interventions be applied equitably to all hospitalized patients regardless of income, sex, or race; the observed sociodemographic disparities should guide system-level resource allocation and universal screening intensity rather than selective targeting of individual patients based on protected characteristics. Developing age-specific risk assessment tools beyond the Braden Q Scale, particularly for adolescents experiencing rapid growth changes, may improve risk identification and prevention in this cohort.

We recommend three specific system-level interventions: (1) establishing puberty-specific skin integrity champions in adolescent units; (2) implementing daily skin integrity reassessment protocols for patients aged 11–15, in accordance with evidence-based paediatric skin integrity monitoring guidelines (Nie et al., 2024; Willock et al., 2016); and (3) strengthening early discharge planning and outpatient wound care navigation for populations with prolonged length of stay, such as Medicaid-insured patients and the 1–5 age group, to reduce cumulative hospitalization costs.

Additionally, policies aimed at reducing the financial burden of hospitalization among patients with pressure injuries should consider the sociodemographic differences identified in this study. System-level interventions for patients with public insurance and those in lower income brackets could help mitigate the impact of prolonged hospitalization on these vulnerable populations (Padula and Delarmente, 2019). Given the prolonged hospital stays observed in the lowest income quartile, care coordination and early discharge planning interventions should be strengthened for economically disadvantaged populations to reduce cumulative healthcare expenditures.

Medical device–related pressure injury prevention deserves specific attention given the high prevalence of device-related injuries in paediatric intensive care settings (Şimşek et al., 2023). The 11–15 age group, which showed the steepest cost increases, frequently requires prolonged use of orthopedic devices, respiratory support, and monitoring leads during pubertal growth spurts. We recommend implementing device rotation protocols every 2–4 h and silicone-based dressings at device contact points for adolescents in this age bracket, given their unique skin physiology combining increased adiposity with hormonal changes affecting skin resilience. Healthcare providers should also be trained to recognize and manage the unique risks associated with medical devices in paediatric patients (Howard and Taylor, 2009).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional analysis of administrative data, this study describes temporal trends and subgroup differences but cannot establish causality or temporal sequence between sociodemographic characteristics and pressure injury outcomes.

Second, the reliance on administrative data may introduce coding inaccuracies or underreporting of PIs. It is well recognized that pressure injuries may be underreported in U.S. administrative databases due to coding practices and potential financial disincentives. Additionally, we cannot establish temporality between PI diagnosis and cost or length of stay outcomes; thus, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal.

Third, the NIS database does not distinguish between hospital-acquired and community-acquired PIs, limiting our ability to assess the impact of hospital-based prevention programs.

Fourth, our analysis reports total hospitalization costs for patients with a PI diagnosis, not costs directly attributable to the PI itself. Therefore, the cost estimates may primarily reflect the high cost of treating the patient's underlying condition, for which the PI is a comorbidity.

Fifth, the transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM in October 2015 introduced greater coding specificity, which may have influenced recorded prevalence. To assess potential structural breaks, we visually inspected trend plots for 2015 and conducted sensitivity analyses excluding data from October 2015–March 2016 (the transition period). The consistent upward trajectory observed across the entire study period, including the stable trend in 2015 (Fig. 1), suggests that the coding change did not fundamentally alter observed patterns; however, we cannot entirely exclude minor classification effects.

Sixth, the lack of detailed clinical data (e.g., PI staging at admission) restricts our understanding of disease progression and severity.

Seventh, in-hospital deaths were retained in the analysis, consistent with standard NIS-based burden studies. However, decedents—particularly those with complex underlying conditions such as malignancy or multi-organ failure—may incur high total hospitalization costs and prolonged length of stay that are driven primarily by their primary disease trajectory rather than by pressure injury treatment. Consequently, the inclusion of decedents may inflate the estimated economic burden of the overall hospitalization episode. We were unable to exclude these cases post-hoc without compromising national representativeness, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting cost and length of stay trends.

Eighth, the age categories used in the analysis (1–5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–17 years) excluded infants under 1 year of age due to the limited number of identified PI cases in this very young cohort, and findings should not be generalized to this population. Future studies should incorporate prospective designs to address these gaps.

Conclusion

The increasing prevalence, length of stay, and total hospitalization costs among hospitalized paediatric patients aged 1–17 years with pressure injury diagnoses, along with observed variations across sociodemographic subgroups, identify temporal patterns and subgroup disparities that may inform future surveillance and research. Because this cross-sectional administrative database does not distinguish hospital-acquired from community-acquired pressure injuries and cannot isolate pressure injury–attributable costs from total hospitalization costs, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than as direct evidence for causal intervention or policy mandates. It is crucial to prioritize enhanced prevention programs tailored for high-risk developmental stages, particularly adolescence, while ensuring equitable application of all prevention strategies across sociodemographic groups. Standardized documentation, including consistent use of ICD-10-CM codes and regular audits, can significantly improve PI surveillance and accuracy. Future research should focus on exploring the effectiveness of enhanced prevention strategies through prospective designs and comparing the long-term outcomes of conservative versus surgical management for severe PIs. By addressing these challenges, healthcare systems can effectively mitigate the burden of PIs and ensure optimal outcomes for paediatric patients aged 1–17 years.
