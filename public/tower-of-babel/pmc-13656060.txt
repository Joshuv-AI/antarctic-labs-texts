The seduction of the target: individualized hemodynamics and the limits of precision care after esophagectomy
Authors: Laurence Weinberg
Published: 2026

Readers of the Journal of Thoracic Disease will recognize esophagectomy as a procedure in which outcomes are rarely determined by a single perioperative variable. Pulmonary complications, anastomotic integrity, surgical approach, fluid shifts, systemic inflammation, nutritional reserve, and intensive care all converge in the first postoperative day. For that reason, the randomized trial by Hovgaard and colleagues, published recently in Anesthesiology, is directly relevant to thoracic practice (1). It asks a deceptively simple question: if blood pressure and cardiac output are measured more carefully, individualized more deliberately, and treated for longer, will patients undergoing esophagectomy experience fewer complications? The answer is sobering. Extended goal-directed therapy (eGDT) achieved the expected physiologic separation: higher mean arterial pressure (MAP), greater positive fluid balance, more norepinephrine exposure, and closer adherence to protocol-defined targets, but did not reduce postoperative morbidity measured by the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) at 30 days.

The target was reached; the patient outcome was not

The trial deserves close attention because the intervention was coherent, technically disciplined, and clinically plausible. Patients randomized to eGDT had a preoperative 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure assessment. Their perioperative MAP target was derived from the average of the three lowest nighttime MAP values between 22:00 and 07:00, constrained between 65 and 85 mmHg. From tracheal intubation until 07:00 on the first postoperative morning, cardiac output was monitored and optimized, stroke volume variation guided fluid boluses during positive-pressure ventilation, passive leg raise testing informed postoperative fluid responsiveness, and norepinephrine was used to maintain MAP above the individualized threshold.

In other words, this was not a casual version of goal-directed therapy. It was an ambitious attempt to extend individualized hemodynamic care across the operative and early postoperative phases, precisely when esophagectomy patients are exposed to major shifts in vascular tone, venous return, capillary permeability, and respiratory mechanics. The protocol did what it was designed to do. The eGDT group had a higher mean MAP [mean difference 3 mmHg; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1 to 5; P=0.011], higher cumulative norepinephrine exposure (median 7,894 μg versus 4,611 μg; P<0.001), and a higher perioperative fluid balance (2,517±1,194 versus 2,001±1,114 mL; mean difference 516 mL; 95% CI: 57 to 974; P=0.028) than standard care (1).

Yet the primary outcome was unchanged: mean 30-day CCI was 39.0 in the eGDT group and 39.2 with standard care, with a mean difference of −0.2 and a 95% CI from −8.6 to 8.1. Because the recently proposed minimal clinically important difference for the CCI in major abdominal surgery is a between-group mean difference of 12 points (2), the entire CI of the Hovgaard result lies below the clinically relevant threshold, making the result not only statistically neutral but clinically unpersuasive (2).

That distinction matters. The trial is not important because it shows that hemodynamics are irrelevant. No serious perioperative clinician would draw that conclusion. It is important because it separates better hemodynamic traces from better postoperative recovery. The monitor improved the numbers; the patients did not have fewer complications. The target was reached; the patient outcome was not.

Individualization is not the same as personalized physiology

The most provocative part of the intervention is the individualized MAP threshold. On its face, the concept is attractive. Rather than treating every adult patient to the same perioperative MAP, the investigators tried to anchor care to each patient’s own nighttime blood pressure. That is the language of modern precision medicine: measure the individual, set an individualized target, and treat to that target.

The difficulty is that the algorithm was individualized only within a predetermined safety corridor. Patients whose calculated nighttime nadir fell below 65 mmHg were not treated to their own value; their target was raised to 65 mmHg. That decision is clinically understandable, but it also reveals the central tension. Patient-derived data were used to personalize treatment only when they remained within the accepted boundaries of conventional hypotension doctrine. When the patient’s observed physiology fell below the threshold, the threshold prevailed.

This should make us cautious about the language we use. A personalized number is not necessarily personalized physiology. A MAP target derived from ambulatory blood pressure may capture part of a patient’s usual vascular state, but it does not capture the interaction between epidural or anesthetic sympathectomy, one-lung ventilation, thoracic insufflation, venous congestion, blood loss, inflammation, capillary leak, right ventricular loading, or surgical manipulation. In esophagectomy, macro-hemodynamic targets sit inside a much larger physiologic system.

A further conceptual issue concerns the coupling between the pressure target and the fluid strategy. The eGDT arm not only ran to a higher pressure but also accumulated a greater positive fluid balance, and a positive fluid balance is itself an established risk factor for pulmonary complications, anastomotic edema, and prolonged ileus after esophagectomy. It is therefore possible that any perfusion benefit conferred by tighter pressure control was offset by the volume load required to achieve it. This is not an argument against individualization; it is an argument for disentangling the pressure target from the fluid strategy in future trial designs, so that the mechanism of any observed effect, or the mechanism of any observed neutrality, can be attributed rather than assumed.

From association to treatment target

The Hovgaard trial also tests a broader assumption that has become embedded in perioperative culture: MAP below 65 mmHg is associated with harm, therefore MAP should be kept at or above 65 mmHg whenever possible. The first part of that statement is supported by substantial observational literature. The second is a therapeutic inference, and therapeutic inferences require randomized evidence.

This trial is a useful corrective. It achieved better pressure control and reduced exposure to low MAP, but the global postoperative complication burden did not improve. That does not prove that hypotension is benign. It does not license indifference to profound or prolonged hypotension, nor does it remove the need for individualized judgment in patients with cerebrovascular, renal, coronary, or right-heart vulnerability. It does, however, remind us that an observational risk marker is not automatically a treatment target, and that removing the marker does not necessarily remove the mechanism of harm.

For thoracic surgeons, anesthesiologists, and intensivists, this distinction is practical rather than philosophical. A patient after esophagectomy may have an acceptable MAP and still have impaired regional perfusion, venous congestion, respiratory compromise, an evolving anastomotic risk, or inflammatory injury. Conversely, a brief MAP below a conventional threshold may not be the dominant determinant of outcome if global oxygen delivery, venous pressure, tissue perfusion, and surgical conditions are otherwise acceptable. The number is useful, but it is not sovereign.

The signal from surgical approach

The exploratory interaction with surgical approach is perhaps the most clinically interesting aspect of the study. As reported in Supplementary Material 3 of the primary trial, the interaction between study group and surgical approach on the 30-day CCI was statistically significant (interaction term, 18.9; 95% CI: 1.5 to 36.3; P=0.036) (1). Among the 32 patients who underwent any open surgery (15 eGDT vs. 17 standard care), the 30-day CCI was 44.6±25.4 in the eGDT group and 32.0±21.2 in the standard group, corresponding to a mean difference of +12.6 points (95% CI: 0.3 to 24.9; P=0.045), numerically favoring standard care and, notably, of a magnitude at the proposed CCI minimal important difference of 12 points (2). Among the 68 patients who underwent fully scopic surgery (34 in each group), the direction of effect reversed: CCI was 36.5±16.8 with eGDT versus 42.8±21.8 with standard care, a mean difference of –6.3 points (95% CI: –15.7 to 3.1; P=0.19), numerically favoring eGDT but not statistically significant. Reassuringly, the interaction was no longer statistically significant at 90 days (interaction term, 8.5; 95% CI: –12.9 to 29.9; P=0.44), suggesting that any early divergence attenuates over longer follow-up. These estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution: the analysis was pre-specified as exploratory rather than confirmatory, the open-surgery subgroup is small (n=32) with a 95% CI that only just excludes zero, and multiplicity across secondary interaction tests was not formally controlled. Nevertheless, a statistically significant interaction of a magnitude equal to the accepted CCI minimal important difference, in the direction of harm, is difficult to dismiss as noise, and precisely the kind of signal that should shape the design of the next trial rather than the interpretation of this one.

Open esophagectomy and minimally invasive esophagectomy are not simply different incisions. They are different physiologic exposures. Open surgery generally involves greater tissue trauma, different inflammatory activation, different patterns of blood loss and fluid shift, and potentially different postoperative respiratory burdens. A concrete and testable mechanism, rather than a generic inflammatory hypothesis, is worth naming: in open transthoracic esophagectomy, sustained norepinephrine-driven splanchnic and gastric conduit vasoconstriction, layered on top of a mobilized, denervated, and marginally perfused gastric tube, may impair anastomotic micro-perfusion at precisely the perioperative window (intraoperative to early postoperative) that eGDT was designed to protect. This mechanism is at least consistent with the direction and magnitude of the observed subgroup signal: an excess 30-day CCI of 12.6 points in open-surgery patients randomized to a strategy that delivered nearly twice the cumulative norepinephrine exposure (median 7,894 vs. 4,611 µg) and a 516-mL greater positive fluid balance than standard care. In fully scopic surgery, in which thoracic mechanics, blood loss, and inflammatory activation are attenuated, the same vasopressor exposure may be net neutral or even favorable, an interpretation consistent with the numerically opposite (though non-significant) point estimate in the scopic subgroup. This is a testable hypothesis for future trials, ideally interrogated with regional perfusion monitoring (e.g., indocyanine-green angiography of the gastric conduit and, where available, sublingual sidestream dark-field imaging) rather than macro-hemodynamic surrogates alone. It is plausible that a strategy emphasizing higher pressure targets, more vasopressor exposure, and algorithmic fluid administration may interact differently with these surgical phenotypes. The important lesson is not that eGDT harms open surgery patients; the trial is not large enough to support that conclusion. The lesson is that “esophagectomy” should not be treated as a single hemodynamic condition.

Future trials should stratify by surgical approach at the design stage, not discover it as an exploratory signal after the fact. They should also consider whether the outcome selected is mechanistically aligned with the intervention. A global CCI is patient-important and defensible, but it may dilute signals in organ-specific complications such as acute kidney injury, pulmonary morbidity, anastomotic leak, myocardial injury, or postoperative functional recovery. If a hemodynamic intervention is expected to work through perfusion, oxygen delivery, venous pressure, or microcirculatory effects, the trial should be powered and phenotyped accordingly.

A humbler model of precision perioperative care

The convergence of neutral results across four contemporary randomized trials of individualized or protocolized hemodynamic care in major surgery is not a statistical coincidence; it is a clinically actionable pattern that should modify how the reader approaches the next esophagectomy list, the next cardiac output monitor, and the next departmental hemodynamic protocol. This study joins a growing group of modern randomized trials in which physiologically attractive hemodynamic strategies have delivered better protocol adherence without clearly better patient outcomes: OPTIMISE II, iPEGASUS, IMPROVE-multi, and PRETREAT (3-6), a pattern now corroborated at the level of pooled randomized evidence by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of intraoperative-hypotension trials (7). The pattern should not lead us to abandon hemodynamic care. Rather, it should lead us to abandon the belief that more monitoring and tighter target control are intrinsically beneficial, particularly when the incremental gain must be weighed against the additional monitoring hardware, staff time, protocol adherence overhead, and opportunity cost that individualized goal-directed strategies demand in an operating room and postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) already stretched by the complexity of esophagectomy itself.

Precision perioperative medicine will need to become more than precision blood pressure management. For esophagectomy patients, the next model should integrate surgical approach, frailty and nutritional reserve, cardiopulmonary reserve, right-heart function, respiratory strategy, venous pressure, vasopressor sensitivity, inflammatory state, and patient-valued recovery. It should also recognize that the same MAP may mean different things in a patient with high central venous pressure, impaired right ventricular function, one-lung ventilation, or marked capillary leak.

There remains a place for MAP thresholds. A default lower boundary is useful in busy operating rooms and ICUs, and MAP around 65 mmHg remains a defensible minimum for most adult patients without established chronic hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, or renovascular vulnerability, in whom a higher individualized floor should be considered. The risk is not the threshold itself. The risk is allowing the threshold to become a substitute for clinical reasoning. In complex thoracic surgery, hemodynamic management should remain a conversation between numbers, physiology, operation, and patient, not a contest won by a single target.

Conclusions

Hovgaard and colleagues have provided the field with a valuable negative trial. They tested a sophisticated, plausible, and carefully delivered intervention in a surgical population where better perioperative management is urgently needed. The intervention changed MAP, norepinephrine exposure, and fluid balance, but it did not reduce 30-day morbidity after esophagectomy. For readers of the Journal of Thoracic Disease, the message is direct: better hemodynamic numbers are not the same as better thoracic surgical recovery.

The next question should not be whether we can individualize MAP more elegantly. We already can. The harder question is whether the target we choose is the mechanism that matters for the patient in front of us. That is where precision perioperative care must now prove itself.
