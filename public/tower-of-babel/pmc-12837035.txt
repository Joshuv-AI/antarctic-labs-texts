Sex-specific trajectories and variability of pain burden in relation to cognitive decline and depressive symptoms in older adults: a prospective cohort study from health and retirement study
Authors: Ting-Yu He, Gui-Ming Huang, Hai-Lin Li, Rui-Peng Zhong, Hua-Min Liu, Wei-Bo Zhong, Xiao-Cheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic pain is highly prevalent in middle-aged and older adults and is frequently accompanied by depressive symptoms and cognitive decline. Its long-term impact on cognitive and psychological trajectories remains incompletely understood, and sex-specific associations are underexplored.MethodsUsing data from the Health and Retirement Study, we constructed pain-score trajectories and assessed their variability among 20,146 participants, and evaluated their association with study outcomes in 6,960 participants. Longitudinal pain trajectories were identified using cubic polynomial latent class mixed models, while within-person variability in pain was quantified using Variability Independent of the Mean (VIM). Depressive symptoms was assessed via the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale, and cognitive function was measured across memory, executive function, and orientation domains using a Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS)–style battery. Individual domain scores were converted to standardized z-scores and aggregated into a global cognition score. Linear mixed-effects models evaluated associations of pain trajectories and VIM with longitudinal changes in cognitive and psychological outcomes, with sex-stratified analyses conducted to examine potential differences.ResultsThree distinct pain trajectories were identified: Fluctuation, Low-level, and High-level, with notable sex differences in trajectory proportions. Participants in the high-level trajectories exhibited the poorest cognitive performance and greatest depressive symptom burden. Over follow-up, trajectory class alone was not consistently associated with global cognitive decline in the overall sample but predicted increases in depressive symptoms, particularly among men. Greater within-person pain variability (higher VIM) consistently predicted faster decline in global cognition and greater increases in depressive symptoms. Sex-stratified analyses revealed heterogeneity: in men, high-level trajectories and higher VIM were linked primarily to declines in executive and global cognition and worsening depressive symptoms; in women, high-level trajectories and greater VIM were associated with accelerated decline in global cognition, memory, and orientation, with weaker associations for depressive change.ConclusionsSustained high pain burden and temporal variability in pain are important predictors of cognitive deterioration and depressive symptoms in older adults, with distinct sex-specific patterns. Incorporating dynamic pain features into risk assessment and implementing sex-sensitive interventions may facilitate early identification of at-risk individuals and mitigate subsequent cognitive and mental health decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26038-0.Keywords: Pain; Trajectory; Variability; Cognitivl decline and depressive symptoms, sex differences

Introduction

Global population aging, together with a rising burden of cognitive decline and mental health disorders, constitutes a major public-health challenge [1]. Systematic reviews report a wide prevalence range for cognitive impairment in older adults (5.1%–41%), with a median around 19% [2], and an overall incidence of late-life depression of 19.2% [3]. Given the profound impacts of cognitive and affective disorders on individual functioning, quality of life, and healthcare utilization, early identification and intervention for depression and cognitive decline are recognized as key strategies to promote healthy aging.

Accumulating evidence suggests that experiences across the life course can shape later cognitive function. Pain is an especially common and often persistent experience overall life, and has attracted increasing attention on cognitive impairment [4]. Several cohort studies have reported that chronic or persistent pain is associated with an accelerated cognitive decline [5, 6]. However, the evidence is inconsistent. A meta-analysis of 10 longitudinal studies found no overall association between baseline persistent pain and subsequent cognitive decline, while short-term follow-up (≤ 4.5 years) showed a significant increase in the risk of cognitive decline [7]. Other systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicated that the observed association depends on the cognitive assessment instrument [8, 9]. Such discrepancies likely additionally stem from heterogeneity in pain classification, follow-up duration, and the sensitivity of cognitive measures, and may also reflect unaddressed effect modification (for example, by sex).

Chronic pain exhibits high comorbidity with psychological symptoms, particularly depression and anxiety, posing a substantial threat to mental health of older adults [10, 11]. A global systematic review and meta-analysis including nearly 350,000 adults with chronic pain reported a prevalence of approximately 39.3% for depression, which substantially higher than in comparator populations [10]. Among adults aged ≥ 65 years, approximately 30% experience chronic pain, which often co-occurs with emotional dysregulation, sleep disturbance, and poorer treatment responses [12, 13]. Despite this high comorbidity, few studies have jointly characterized long-term pain dynamics and their prospective associations with both cognitive and affective outcomes. Moreover, the role of sex in the pain–cognition relationship warrants further investigation. Biological and psychosocial differences may lead to sex-specific pain perception and cognitive vulnerability, but evidence is mixed—e.g., Milani et al. [14] found no sex differences in high-impact chronic pain and cognitive decline. Notably, most prior studies used single-time pain measures and did not capture within-person pain fluctuation, which could mask subtle sex-specific effects.

To address these gaps, we will use data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and apply two complementary statistical approaches to characterize long-term pain dynamics and quantify their fluctuation: latent class mixed models (LCMM) to identify distinct longitudinal pain trajectory classes at the population level, and Variability Independent of the Mean (VIM) to capture individual-level variability in pain amplitude independent of average pain levels [13]. We will then examine whether these trajectory classes and VIM-derived variability predict subsequent cognitive performance and depressive symptoms, testing for sex differences.

Method

Study design and population

The HRS is an ongoing, nationally representative cohort of community-dwelling adults, with cognitive assessments conducted biennially. Study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan and the National Institute on Aging (HUM00061128). Detailed descriptions of the cohort design and methodology have been reported elsewhere [15, 16], and a summary of the exclusion criteria for the present analysis is provided in Fig. 1. In our study, we only included direct respondents. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study timeline and flowchart

Pain scores

Pain was evaluated in HRS using a two-stage self-report questionnaire [17]. Respondents were first asked, “Are you often troubled with pain?” Those who replied “no” were assigned a score of 0. Participants who replied “yes” were then asked to classify the usual intensity of their pain as “mild,” “moderate,” or “severe,” scored 1, 2 and 3, respectively. These responses were combined into a single pain index (0–3), with higher values indicating greater pain severity [18].

Depressive symptoms and cognitive function

Depressive symptoms was assessed using the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES-D-8), with higher scores indicating worse psychological status [19]. Each item was scored dichotomously (yes = 1, no = 0); two positively worded items were reverse-scored, and the items were summed to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 8 (higher = more depressive symptoms). Measurement invariance of the CES-D-8 across sex and over time has not been reported in the HRS documentation and was not evaluated in this study [19]. Cognitive assessments were drawn from the HRS core interview and administered primarily via telephone (or mixed telephone/face-to-face) using the adapted version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS)-style battery [19]. Cognitive function was assessed in three domains—memory (immediate and delayed word recall), executive function (backward counting and serial sevens), and orientation. To measure cognitive decline, a standardized z-score was calculated from baseline cognitive scores, which involved generating z-scores for each cognitive domain, computing the mean of these z-scores, and standardizing this mean using baseline values to obtain a global cognitive z-score. A z-score of 1 at any assessment wave signifies a score 1 standard deviation below the baseline mean. Longitudinal changes in cognitive function and depressive symptoms were derived from these standardized metrics, an approach supported by prior studies [20, 21].

Covariates

Covariates were recorded at baseline, including age (years, continuous), sex (male/female), race (white/black/others), smoking history (ever/never), and alcohol consumption (yes/no). Physical activity frequency at mild, moderate, and vigorous intensities was assessed using a validated HRS scale and converted to MET-equivalent points via the Compendium of Physical Activities [22], then categorized into three quantile-based levels. Physical disorders were defined as the self-reported presence of at least one of the following seven chronic conditions at the baseline interview or a history of any of these conditions: stroke, cancer, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, lung disease, or arthritis.

Statistical analysis

LCMM was applied to identify distinct longitudinal trajectories of pain scores within the study population. Participants who reported no pain at every assessment between 2000 and 2010 were excluded from the LCMM analysis due to negligible within-person variability, as LCMMs require longitudinal change to identify distinct trajectory shapes [23]. These persistently no-pain participants were categorized as the reference no-pain group in subsequent outcome analyses. Models were fitted using the lcmm package in R [24], treating repeated measurements of pain scores as a polynomial function of time. The time variable (in years) was defined as the interval between each assessment and the baseline measurement (year 2000), effectively modeling years since baseline. To flexibly capture both linear and potential nonlinear temporal trends in pain scores, the LCMM included linear, quadratic, and cubic terms for time. The inclusion of quadratic and cubic terms was empirically justified by preliminary analyses indicating non-linear changes in pain scores over time and theoretical considerations that pain progression may accelerate or decelerate with aging. For model selection, LCMMs with two to seven latent classes were fitted sequentially. Multiple sets of random starting values (derived from the one-class model) were used to minimize convergence to local optima. The optimal model was determined based on a combination of fit indices—including the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)—along with entropy values approaching 1, mean posterior class membership probabilities > 0.8 for each class, and a minimum class size of ≥ 10% to ensure classification reliability and clinical interpretability [25–28]. A Posteriori Probability of Assignment (APPA) was used to evaluate the certainty of class membership. Visual inspection of the trajectory plots was further employed to ensure biological plausibility and clinical relevance of the identified trajectory patterns. Trajectory models were estimated for the overall sample and separately by sex.

Baseline characteristics across the identified trajectories were compared using analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. To assess associations of pain trajectories and VIM levels with longitudinal changes in depressive and cognitive outcomes, we applied linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes, thereby accounting for individual heterogeneity in baseline levels and rates of change. Time (years since baseline) was the only time-varying variable in the models. Separate models were estimated for each outcome domain and included main effects of pain trajectory or VIM, time (in years), their interactions, and a quadratic time term (time²) to capture potential nonlinear patterns.

All models were further adjusted for baseline age, race, sex(if applicable), education, marital status, smoking status, drinking, physical activities, and physical disorders. Associations were estimated using β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.0).

Results

Generation of latent trajectories of pain scores

A total of 20,146 participants (8,317 men and 11,829 women) recruited between 2000 and 2010 were included to examine longitudinal changes in pain scores. In the overall sample, model selection based on predefined criteria identified a cubic polynomial LCMM with three trajectory classes as the optimal solution, which was also supported in sex-stratified analyses (Tables S1–S3). The trajectories in the overall sample (Fig. 2A) were labeled as Fluctuation (11.82%, APPA = 0.765), Low-level (48.96%, APPA = 0.833), and High-level (39.23%, APPA = 0.860) (Table S1). Among men (Fig. 2B), the trajectories were Fluctuation (53.01%, APPA = 0.801), Low-level (14.81%, APPA = 0.751), and High-level (32.18%, APPA = 0.857) (Table S2). Among women (Fig. 2C), the trajectories were Fluctuation (42.36%, APPA = 0.798), Low-level (21.45%, APPA = 0.779), and High-level (36.19%, APPA = 0.912) (Table S3).

Baseline characteristics of study population

Of 6,960 subjects, 3,101 reported no pain and 424, 2,241, and 1,194 were assigned to the Fluctuation, low-level, and high-level trajectories, respectively. The average follow-up duration was 8.1 years among all participants (7.8 years for males and 8.3 years for females). Trajectories differed by sex, education, alcohol use, physical activity, multimorbidity and VIM, while age and smoking were not significant overall. High-level trajectory exhibited the lowest cognitive z-scores (global cognition, executive function, orientation) and the highest depressive symptoms. In sex-stratified analyses (men, n = 2,995; women, n = 3,965), age varied modestly between groups; education and multimorbidity differed by trajectory; men showed differences in smoking and alcohol with cognition changes largely in global and executive domains, whereas women showed broader differences in drinking, physical activity and multiple cognitive domains (Table 1).

Longitudinal pain trajectories and variability and changes in cognitive function and depressive symptoms

Across the full sample, none of the three pain trajectories were significantly associated with longitudinal change in global cognition or with change across cognitive domains, although Fluctuation trajectory and high-level trajectory were each linked to greater increases in depressive symptoms over time (Table 2). In addition, greater variability in pain (higher VIM) predicted both steeper decline in global cognitive performance and larger increases in depressive symptoms (Table 3). In sex-stratified analyses, among men only high-level trajectory was associated with accelerated decline in global cognition and in executive function, whereas fluctuation trajectory was associated with greater increases in depressive symptoms; additionally, higher pain VIM in men predicted faster executive decline and increasing depressive symptoms (Tables 2 and 3). Among women, high-level trajectory was associated with faster decline in global cognition, memory, and orientation, and fluctuation trajectory with greater decline in executive function, but none of the three trajectories predicted change in depressive symptoms; higher pain VIM in women was linked to accelerated decline in global cognition and memory (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Cubic-polynomial LCMM identified three distinct longitudinal pain trajectories with significant differences in sex distribution and class proportions. At baseline, the highest-burden trajectory exhibited the poorest cognitive performance and the greatest depressive symptom burden. Over follow-up, trajectory class alone did not significantly predict decline in global cognition in the overall sample, although the Fluctuation and High-level trajectories were each associated with increases in depressive symptoms. By contrast, greater within-person VIM consistently predicted more rapid decline in global cognition and larger increases in depressive symptoms. Sex-stratified analyses revealed heterogeneity: in men, the High-level trajectory and higher VIM were primarily associated with accelerated decline in global and executive function and worsening depressive symptoms; in women, the high-burden trajectory and higher VIM were more strongly associated with faster declines in global cognition, memory, and orientation, but showed weaker associations with depressive change. Overall, these findings indicate that dynamic features of pain—particularly sustained high burden and temporal variability—are important predictors of subsequent cognitive deterioration and depressive symptomsand underscore the need for sex-specific prevention and management strategies.

Our findings differ from prior reports: several earlier cohort studies characterized population pain trajectories as three relatively stable groups of differing severity [17, 18], whereas the present study more commonly observed a Fluctuation trajectory. Methodologically, this discrepancy may partly reflect differences in trajectory modeling. Compared with approaches such as the group-based trajectory model, which do not permit within-class individual heterogeneity or typically fit only low-order polynomials, latent class mixed models allow within-class individual variation and can simultaneously test quadratic and cubic terms, thus providing greater flexibility to capture nonlinear and fluctuation trajectory shapes [24, 32, 33]. In addition, we quantified temporal pain variability using VIM, which quantifies the magnitude of fluctuation independently of the mean and more faithfully captures dynamic features of a measure; it has been shown to outperform traditional variability metrics as an indicator of true temporal instability [29, 30]. In our study, the VIM complements category-level, static descriptions and demonstrates that pain is not relatively stable for most individuals over follow-up [17]— the variability itself carries important prognostic information.t prognostic information.

The association between long-term high pain burden and poorer global cognitive function was observed in our study and to our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically examine the impact of distinct pain trajectories on depressive symptoms. In the overall sample, a persistently high pain trajectory was significantly associated with increases in depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that chronic pain may affect cognitive and emotional functions through multiple neurobiological pathways. On the one hand, prior studies have shown that older adults with chronic pain perform worse on Stroop [34], WCST and Digit Span tasks, and exhibit increased β2-band activity in the posterior cingulate cortex alongside gray-matter volume reductions in prefrontal regions [35], the insula, cingulate cortex, thalamus and temporal lobes; such functional and structural alterations may impair compensatory neural mechanisms and disrupt cognitive control [36, 37]. On the other hand, chronic pain is commonly accompanied by low-grade inflammation and HPA-axis dysregulation, leading to abnormal cortisol secretion; elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, neurotransmitter (noradrenaline, serotonin, Gamma amino butyric acid) imbalances [34], and structural/functional remodeling of emotion-related circuits can further promote cognitive decline and exacerbate depressive symptoms [35, 38]. Importantly, our study highlights that not only sustained high levels of pain but also pain variability itself—regardless of the mean level—is associated with greater cognitive decline and increased depressive symptoms [39]. Fluctuating pain may impose repeated and unpredictable activation of the stress-response system, generating cumulative allostatic load and maladaptive cortisol rhythms that disrupt sleep regulation and executive functioning [40]. Disturbed sleep and fatigue can, in turn, weaken prefrontal inhibitory control and emotion regulation, heightening sensitivity to pain and negative affect. This cyclical interaction between fluctuating nociceptive input, physiological stress, and impaired cognitive-emotional regulation may explain why dynamic pain patterns are particularly detrimental to cognitive and mental health outcomes [40, 41].

Sex-stratified analyses showed that all pain trajectories were associated with increases in depressive symptoms among men, whereas no statistically significant associations between pain trajectories and depressive change were observed among women. Formal interaction analyses in the pooled sample revealed significant interactions between sex and both pain trajectories and VIM over time for several cognitive domains (Table S7). These findings support that the longitudinal associations between pain dynamics and cognitive function differ partly by sex. This discrepancy may reflect sex differences in emotional expression, the sensitivity of depression measurement instruments, or the etiological mechanisms of depression [42, 43]. Our sex-specific findings therefore indicate divergent patterns in the relationships between pain trajectories and outcomes for men and women. Such heterogeneity is likely multifactorial: (1) biological differences (for example, sex hormones differently affecting inflammatory responses, neuroplasticity, and HPA-axis regulation) [44]; (2) behavioral and social factors (including differences by sex in pain expression, help-seeking behavior, medication use, and social support) [45]; and (3) differing comorbidity profiles (e.g., cardiovascular disease, sleep disorders) that may alter the pathways through which pain influences cognition and mood [46, 47].

Chronic pain is highly prevalent in middle-aged and older adults and imposes a substantial care and health-system burden [48, 49]; our findings indicate its value as an early warning signal for cognitive and mental health decline. Consequently, routine clinical and public-health practice should include systematic mental-health screening (particularly for depression and anxiety) and comprehensive assessment of self-rated health, sleep, life satisfaction, and social support among older adults with chronic pain to enable timely intervention [10]. VIM was employed to quantify pain variability independently of mean pain levels, thereby isolating temporal instability from overall intensity. Unlike mixed-effects models that primarily capture mean trajectories, VIM characterizes within-person fluctuations, demonstrating that temporal variability—beyond sustained high pain—has independent prognostic significance for cognitive and depressive outcomes. Accordingly, pain assessment should extend beyond mean intensity to incorporate temporal dynamics, such as VIM and trajectory analyses, to identify individuals at elevated risk despite modest average pain levels. Incorporating both pain trajectories and VIM as prespecified covariates or stratification factors may enhance dementia-risk screening and improve the design of clinical trials. Interventions ought to be multidisciplinary and individualized, combining optimized pain management, anti-inflammatory and comorbidity treatment [50], sleep and exercise prescriptions, cognitive training, and psychosocial support, while accounting for sex differences (e.g., prioritizing executive-function and mood protection in men and memory/orientation support in women) [51, 52]. At the population level, integrating systematic pain management into community and long-term care policies and delivering early, targeted interventions may reduce the cognitive and psychological burden of chronic pain and improve overall functioning and quality of life in older adults [53].

This study leveraged complementary approaches—LCMM and VIM—to characterize pain dynamics at both the population and individual levels. LCMM permitted identification of trajectory classes while accommodating within-class heterogeneity, and we further incorporated higher-order polynomial terms to more fully capture nonlinear and fluctuation trajectory shapes. The use of VIM quantified within-person temporal variability, thereby complementing category-level descriptions and revealing prognostically relevant dynamic features that would be missed by static classification alone. In addition, sex-stratified analyses enhanced the granularity and clinical interpretability of our findings. Finally, we indexed global cognitive performance using z-score standardization rather than simple summation of domain scores, which reduces bias that can arise when a single cognitive domain disproportionately influences a composite [54]. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, our ability to draw causal inferences is inherently constrained despite adjustment for multiple confounders; complex bidirectional or shared-etiology relationships among pain, cognition, and depression remain plausible. Second, pain assessment relied primarily on self-report, which may be affected by reporting bias as well as cultural or sex differences in symptom expression; future research should incorporate more objective measures. Third, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the sample composition and the relatively short follow-up period. Replication in more diverse populations and with extended observation is warranted. Moreover, our study lacked information on pain management strategies, which may influence both pain severity and treatment-related outcomes. In addtion, the use of a four-point pain scale offers limited variability, which may in turn constrain the sensitivity of the VIM approach. Finally, given the bidirectional relationship between pain and depression, reverse causality cannot be ruled out, and our study design does not allow for establishing a definitive temporal sequence. Also, future studies should incorporate measures of pain interference or high-impact pain, which may more directly affect cognitive and emotional outcomes than pain severity alone.

In conclusion, these findings indicate that both sustained pain burden and temporal variability in pain are important predictors of subsequent decline in cognitive function and depressive symptoms in older adults, and that the patterns of association differ between men and women. Incorporating dynamic pain characteristics into risk assessment and implementing sex-sensitive (or sex-specific) interventions may provide novel avenues to slow cognitive decline and improve mental health.
