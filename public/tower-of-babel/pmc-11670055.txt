Revisiting the role of ErbBs in oligodendrocyte development
Authors: Albert Zhang
Published: 2024

In recent years, there has been a growing appreciation for the importance of oligodendrocytes and myelin in both nervous system function and disease. Oligodendrocyte and myelin formation have been shown to be necessary for various learning and memory paradigms (1), while myelin abnormalities have been associated with a range of psychiatric and neurological disorders (2, 3). However, the molecular mechanisms that underlie myelin formation in the central nervous system—such as how oligodendrocytes mature, where myelin is deposited, and how much myelin is formed—remain poorly understood. In this issue of PNAS, Hu et al. attempt to address a longstanding debate in the myelin field regarding the role of ErbB signaling in CNS myelination by using a dominant negative EGFR that inhibits all ErbBs (4) (Fig. 1).

Over the past few decades, one prominent and controversial candidate molecule regulating CNS myelination has been the growth factor Neuregulin-1 (NRG1) and its ErbB receptors. Given that NRG1 has been widely shown to regulate myelination in the peripheral nervous system (5), it has been studied by many labs in the context of CNS myelination. NRG1 signals promote the dimerization of the ErbB2, ErbB3, and ErbB4 receptors, leading to their phosphorylation and activation. To study the role of NRG1/ErbB signaling on CNS myelination, different strategies have been developed to either reduce NRG1 (6, 7) or disrupt ErbB signaling (6, 8–10). However, the results were contradictory in the CNS, with some showing hypomyelination (7–10) and others showing no change in the myelin (6).

Some of these discrepancies can be explained by the complexity of the signaling, since the ErbBs can form homodimers or heterodimers with each other, and bind to other ligands like EGF (11). Furthermore there is the issue of timing, as certain mouse lines like the ErbB3;ErbB4 double mutant and Nestin-Cre;Nrg1flox/flox die shortly after birth with grossly normal myelin in the brain (6). On the other hand, other mouse models survive postnatal development and were observed to have CNS hypomyelination in adulthood (9, 10). Perhaps NRG1/ErbB signaling could be dispensable for early CNS myelination but necessary later. To this day, the question of whether ErbB signaling regulates oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination remains unresolved.

Now, over a decade later, Hu et al. reexamined this question by simplifying the complexity—using a dominant negative EGFR that inhibits the phosphorylation and activation of all the ErbBs (4). This is based on their unexpected in vitro finding that addition of either EGF or NRG1 can activate all the ErbBs in cultured oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs). This finding is surprising, as NRG1 is not believed to activate EGFR (also known as ErbB1) (11, 12). Regardless, this pan-ErbB manipulation should have robust effects when expressed under either a Sox10 driver for all oligodendrocyte lineage cells or a Plp1 driver for mature oligodendrocytes. At a minimum, this experiment should answer the question of whether

ErbB signaling plays a role in CNS myelination. The results recapitulate and extend the findings from the Corfas lab, which used a dominant negative ErbB4 (dnErbB4) that disrupts ErbB2, ErbB3, and ErbB4 signaling (10). In both studies, ErbB inhibition relatively early in the oligodendrocyte lineage led to hypomyelination in the adult brain despite the presence of greater numbers of mature oligodendrocytes. This counterintuitive finding raises more questions, as it is not clear why inhibition of ErbB signaling would lead to greater numbers of mature oligodendrocytes but less total myelin. One possibility could be that these oligodendrocytes have a lower capacity for myelination, requiring more oligodendrocytes to myelinate the same number of axons. This hypothesis is supported by the observation of Roy et al. that dnErbB4 oligodendrocytes had fewer myelin sheaths (10) and the observation of Hu et al. that there are more OPCs and a greater rate of OPC proliferation in Sox10-dnEGFR animals (4).

Increased OPC proliferation could represent a greater homeostatic drive to replenish OPCs lost to higher levels of oligodendrocyte differentiation, which has been observed in other contexts (13). However, earlier on, there is a smaller number of premyelinating oligodendrocytes labeled by Enpp6 and TCF4 in the Sox10-dnEGFR animals. More detailed analysis would be required to characterize the dynamics of oligodendrogenesis and myelination in these animals, and understanding what happens to these processes could help elucidate the role of ErbB signaling in oligodendroglia.

The Hu et al. study further identifies a potential dual role for ErbB signaling on myelin function that is independent of myelin formation. In Plp1-dnEGFR mice, where dnEGFR is expressed under a mature oligodendrocyte promoter, the authors did not observe any morphological changes to myelin or reductions in oligodendroglial number. These animals have working memory deficits, which has also been observed in animals where ErbB3 is conditionally knocked out using a Plp1 driver (9). The fact that myelin appears grossly normal in Plp1-dnEGFR mice may suggest that ErbB signaling could play a role in myelin function, which the authors link to oligodendrocyte–axon metabolic coupling.

A key caveat to the study of Hu et al. relates to the use of a dominant negative construct. The overexpression of these dnEGFRs in oligodendroglia could act as a sink for ligands like neuregulins and EGF in the surrounding area, potentially inhibiting ErbB signaling cell nonautonomously. Since NRG1 plays various roles in the CNS including axon guidance, neuron migration, and synapse formation (14), some of the phenotypes observed could potentially be unrelated to oligodendrocytes, or could indirectly affect oligodendroglial development and function. In particular, behavioral results should be interpreted carefully, as the effects could be driven by a wide range of cell types in the brain.

Overall, the findings of Hu et al. suggest that ErbB signaling plays a role in myelin formation and function. Given that this is based on broad manipulation of ErbB signaling, the conclusions and mechanisms identified in this study are potentially complex. For example, EGFR may regulate oligodendrocyte development, as a hypomorphic EGFR mutant has been reported to have deficits in oligodendroglial numbers early on, with recovery in adulthood (15). The results of the current study suggest there could be compensation from other ErbBs in these animals, but this would require more detailed follow-up. Ultimately, future studies using finer manipulations and new tools will be needed to determine the contributions of individual ErbBs and their ligands to myelination.
