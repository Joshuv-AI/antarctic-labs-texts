Serum uric acid mediates the association between the estimated glucose disposal rate and chronic kidney disease in patients with diabetes or prediabetes: an analysis from NHANES 2005–2018
Authors: Jinming Yin, Meiling Luo, Qingan Fu, Huangxin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), a novel composite indicator for assessing insulin resistance (IR), remains underexplored for its ability to predict the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes.MethodA total of 17,595 patients with diabetes or prediabetes from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2018 were included. CKD is defined based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate and the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. The calculation of eGDR is based on the waist circumference, hypertension status and glycated hemoglobin. Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between eGDR and CKD. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to assess dose-response relationships. Subgroup analysis explores the differences in effects among different populations. Mediating analysis was used to quantify the role of serum uric acid (SUA) in the eGDR-CKD association. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) curves were used to compare the predictive performance of eGDR with that of other IR indices.ResultsAfter full adjustment, the eGDR showed a significant inverse association with CKD risk. The RCS curves confirmed a linear negative relationship between eGDR and CKD. Subgroup analysis revealed stronger associations in men. Mediation analysis indicated that SUA partially mediated the eGDR-CKD association, accounting for 6.2% of the total effect. ROC analysis revealed that the eGDR shad a moderate predictive ability for CKD in patients with diabetes or prediabetes.ConclusionThis cross-sectional study confirmed that the eGDR is linearly negatively correlated with CKD in a population with diabetes or prediabetes and that its discriminative power is moderate but superior to that of traditional IR indicators. The statistical overlap of SUA on the total association was approximately 6.2%, but the mediating effect was fragile. This finding should be verified in prospective cohorts in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02081-1.

Introduction

Diabetes and prediabetes have become core challenges in global public health. The latest statistical report shows that the prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 20 to 79 worldwide is 10.5% (536.6 million), and the prevalence of prediabetes is as high as 5.8% (298 million) [1, 2]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the most common and fatal microvascular complications of diabetes and prediabetes [3]. Multiple epidemiological survey results have shown that approximately 30% to 40% of diabetic patients and 16% to 18% of prediabetic patients have CKD [4, 5]. A longitudinal study involving over 330,000 people revealed that diabetes and prediabetes are independent risk factors for CKD [6]. Although intensive blood glucose control can improve renal outcomes [7], traditional indicators such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) can explain only part of renal function heterogeneity, suggesting the need to search for more sensitive risk markers.

Insulin resistance (IR) is regarded as the “common soil” of diabetes and prediabetes [8]. It not only drives hyperglycemia but also directly damages the glomerular endothelium and podocytes through multiple pathways such as activating oxidative stress, inflammation, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), and endothelial dysfunction [9, 10]. Traditional IR indicators have inherent deficiencies in people with diabetes or diabetes. The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) has demonstrated outstanding accuracy in metabolic assessment [11]. However, owing to the limitations of its technical requirements, it is difficult to apply in large-scale epidemiological studies. The homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR) depends on fasting insulin and is susceptible to interference from exogenous insulin [12]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index reflects only the fasting blood glucose-lipid status [13, 14]. In the 1990s, Williams and his team proposed the “estimated glucose disposal rate” (eGDR) based on a type 1 diabetes cohort, which evaluated the degree of IR through three conventional clinical indicators: waist circumference, hypertension status, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) [15]. The original clamping verification study revealed that eGDR was highly negatively correlated with glucose disposal rate. A recent study confirmed that in patients with diabetes and prediabetes, a lower eGDR is significantly associated with an increased prevalence of cardiovascular diseases, and its predictive ability for CVD is better than that of traditional IR indicators [16]. The eGDR simultaneously captures the core pathological axis of “obesity-hypertension-chronic hyperglycemia”, which highly overlaps with the mechanism of renal injury [17–19]. However, an independent association between the eGDR and CKD has rarely been reported in individuals with diabetes and prediabetes. Therefore, it is urgently necessary to verify the association strength and robustness of the eGDR as an alternative indicator of IR with CKD in representative populations.

Serum uric acid (SUA) is the end product of purine metabolism and is mainly excreted through the kidneys [20, 21]. Under IR conditions, the proximal tubules of the kidneys secrete less SUA, and the activity of purine metabolic enzymes increases, leading to hyperuricemia [22]. Experimental studies suggest that SUA accelerates the decline in renal function by inducing vascular endothelial inflammation, glomerular entry arteriolar lesions and renal interstitial fibrosis [23–25]. Epidemiological evidence also indicates that for every 1 mg/dL increase in SUA, the risk of new-onset CKD increases by approximately 15% [26]. Therefore, SUA may mediate the association between IR and CKD, but to date, no population studies have quantitatively evaluated the proportion of its mediating effect.

Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) of the United States, this study aims to achieve the following goals among patients aged 20 years and above with diabetes or prediabetes: (1) explore the independent association between the eGDR and the prevalence of CKD and (2) evaluate the mediating proportion of SUA in the abovementioned association. We hypothesize that a higher eGDR (lower IR) is significantly associated with a reduced prevalence of CKD and that SUA partially mediates this relationship. Clarifying the above issues is expected to provide a simple tool for the early identification of CKD in patients with diabetes or prediabetes and offer new epidemiological evidence for intervention strategies.

Methods

Study population

This analysis incorporated datasets from the NHANES, a population surveillance system conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) that is designed to monitor health and nutritional status across the United States. The NHANES employs a comprehensive data collection methodology consisting of two primary elements: standardized interviews and clinical examinations. These components systematically collect information regarding demographic characteristics, dietary patterns, biochemical measurements, and health-related questionnaire responses. The National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board granted ethical approval for the survey protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Complete documentation of the survey methodology and data collection procedures is available through the official NHANES website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx).

A total of 70,190 participants were surveyed during the 7 cycles of the NHANES from 2005 to 2018. We followed the detailed path in Fig. 1 to carry out the inclusion and exclusion process. Specifically, after excluding a total of 52,595 participants, including those under 20 years of age (n = 30,441), those with normal blood glucose levels (n = 20,033), those with incomplete eGDR (n = 1675), incomplete CKD diagnoses (n = 89), those with missing SUA data (n = 283), and pregnant women (n = 74), 17,595 eligible subjects were ultimately retained to participate in the study.Fig. 1The flowchart of the study

Assessment of diabetes and prediabetes

The classification of diabetes status was based on established American Diabetes Association guidelines [27, 28]. Diabetes mellitus was diagnosed when participants met any of the following criteria: (1) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L); (2) HbA1c values ≥ 6.5%; or (3) affirmative responses to any diabetes-related questions in the medical history questionnaire, including self-reported physician diagnosis, current insulin use, or oral antihyperglycemic medication use. Prediabetes was identified through either biochemical markers or self-reported information. The biochemical definition included FPG concentrations between 100 and 125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L) or HbA1c levels ranging from 5.7% to 6.4%. Additionally, participants were classified as having prediabetes if they responded affirmatively to the specific question regarding their previous diagnosis of prediabetes by a healthcare provider.

Assessment of CKD

Chronic kidney disease was diagnosed based on the standardized criteria established by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [29]. The diagnostic thresholds included either (1) reduced renal function as evidenced by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 ml/min/1.73 m2, or (2) evidence of kidney damage, as demonstrated by an elevated urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) of 30 mg/g or higher. The CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula is currently one of the most widely recommended equations internationally for estimating the GFR [30] and is widely adopted in epidemiological studies and in populations with diabetes and cardiorenal metabolism [31, 32]. Compared with the traditional Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) formula, CKD-EPI has greater accuracy in the higher GFR range ( > 60 ml/min/1.73 m2) and is more suitable for people with diabetes or prediabetes because these people are often in the stage of high filtration or mild decline in the early stage, and MDRD is prone to underestimate the GFR [33]. Covariates

This study included sociodemographic variables, lifestyle, body measurements, self-reported medical history and laboratory tests. Sociodemographic variables include age (years), gender (male/female), race (Mexican American, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black or other race), educational level (below high school, high school graduate, or college and above), marital status (married/living with a partner, widowed/divorced/separated, or never married), and the poverty-income ratio (PIR). Age groups are divided into elderly individuals and young and middle-aged individuals at the age of 60. Economic conditions are classified into poverty (PIR < 1) and non-poverty (PIR ≥ 1). Body measurement indicators included body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg). Obesity can be classified by BMI as normal or low weight (BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (25 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 30 kg/m2), or obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). SBP and DBP are the average values of the effective blood pressure measured three times. Lifestyle factors included daily protein intake, smoking status, drinking status and physical activity. Daily protein intake is the average protein intake of participants over a two-day dietary recall period, measured in grams per day (g/d). The smoking status of the participants was divided into three groups: never smokers (those who smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime), former smokers (those who had smoked ≥100 cigarettes but had quit), and current smokers (those who had smoked ≥100 cigarettes and were still smoking). Drinking was defined as drinking more than 12 times a year. Physical activity can be classified into three categories: inactive, moderate-intensity and vigorous-intensity. Among them, moderate-intensity exercise refers to any exercise, fitness or leisure activity that can last for at least 10 minutes, slightly increase the breathing or heart rate, and must be carried out continuously within a week. Intense exercise refers to any exercise, fitness or leisure activity that can significantly increase breathing or heart rate, and it must be maintained at this intensity for at least 10 consecutive minutes within a week. The self-reported medical history included hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), the use of diuretics, the use of urate-lowering drugs, hypoglycemic treatment, and the use of antihypertensive drugs. The diagnostic criteria for hypertension are as follows: the SBP of the subject is ≥ 140 mmHg, the DBP is ≥ 90 mmHg, or the subject has a history of hypertension confirmed by a doctor or is currently taking antihypertensive drugs. CVD include one of the following conditions diagnosed by doctors: heart failure, coronary artery disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction or stroke. Diuretics include furosemide, thiazide and other unspecified diuretics. Urate-lowering drugs include allopurinol, febuxostat and other unspecified urate-lowering drugs. The use of hypoglycemic treatment and antihypertensive drugs is derived from the questionnaire “Are you currently taking hypoglycemic drugs/injecting insulin/antihypertensive drugs?” The laboratory test results included serum albumin (ALB, g/dL), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mmol/L), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mmol/L), and SUA (mmol/L) levels.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). To reduce selection bias and handle missing data to varying degrees (Table S1), the “mice” R package was used for interpolation and iteration of the covariates. The missing values are filled in via the predictive mean matching method. The participants were stratified into quartiles according to their eGDR values for baseline comparisons. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges, whereas categorical variables are reported as counts and proportions. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous measures and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. To examine the relationship between the eGDR and the prevalence of CKD in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes, we constructed multivariable logistic regression models with covariate adjustment. Model 1 did not adjust for any covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age, race, education level, marital status, and economic conditions. Model 3 further adjusts for BMI, SBP, smoking status, drinking status, daily protein intake, physical activity, CVD, diuretics, urate-lowering drugs, hypoglycemic treatment, antihypertensive drugs, ALB, TC, HDL-C and BUN based on Model 2. The multicollinearity test calculates the variance inflation factor (VIF) of all covariates in the model (Table S2). The VIF of all the variables are less than 5, indicating that there is no significant collinearity in the model [37]. Statistical associations were quantified through odds ratio (OR) accompanied by 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for all the analytical outputs. Nonlinear associations were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS) within all three models.

Subgroup analyses were performed across prespecified subgroups (age, gender, race, education level, marital status, economic conditions, BMI, hypertension, CVD, smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity) to examine potential effect modifications. The guidelines for reporting subgroup analyses indicate that conducting independent tests on multiple subgroups simultaneously increases the risk of Type I errors [38]. In this study, Bonferroni correction was adopted to reduce the risk of false positivity, and the P for interaction was compared with the corrected significance threshold α = 0.05/12 = 0.0042 [39].

To examine whether SUA mediates the association between eGDR and CKD, we conducted a mediating analysis. First, we constructed two regression models: the mediator model and the outcome model [40]. In the mediator model, SUA was treated as the dependent variable, eGDR was treated as the exposure variable, and all covariates in Model 3 were simultaneously entered. Multiple linear regression was used to estimate the effect of eGDR on SUA (path a). In the outcome model, CKD was the dependent variable, with both eGDR and SUA included as predictors and the same covariates as in the mediator model. Multiple logistic regression was used to estimate the direct effect of the eGDR on CKD (path c’) and the effect of SUA on CKD (path b). The R package “mediation” was employed for the mediating analysis. We used 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap iterations to estimate the indirect effect, also known as the mediating effect, and its 95% CI. The mediating proportion is calculated as the percentage of indirect effects to the total effect. The indirect effect, direct effect, total effects and mediating proportion were reported, and the significance of these effects was tested. Given that this study is based on a single cross-sectional survey, exposure, the mediator and the outcome were measured simultaneously. It is difficult to establish their sequence, and it is also difficult to rule out the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. Therefore, the so-called “mediating proportion” only quantifies the statistical overlap between variables rather than the mediation weights in the causal chain. In addition, to evaluate the robustness of unmeasured confounding factors on the mediating effect, we calculated the E-value using the VanderWeele method [41]. When the association strength of an unmeasured confounding factor with both “exposure” and “outcome” simultaneously reaches the OR value given by the E-value, the effect observed can be completely erased [42]. In other words, the larger the E-value is, the more robust the result is and the more difficult the conclusion is to overturn.

The predictive ability of eGDR and other traditional IR indicators (TyG, METS-IR, HOMA-IR, and THR) for CKD was evaluated via the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% CI were calculated. The DeLong test was used to compare the differences in the AUC between eGDR and traditional IR indicators. Similarly, Bonferroni correction is carried out to reduce the risk of Type I errors (β = 0.05/5 = 0.010). Statistical significance was defined as the P-value of the DeLong test being less than the threshold β. The R package “pROC” package was used to draw the ROC curve and perform the pairwise AUC comparison. A bilateral p-value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of the primary results, we conducted the following two sensitivity analyses. (1) The initial analysis of this study was based on multiple imputation to handle the absence of key covariates to fully utilize the full sample and reduce selection bias. To assess the impact of interpolation, we excluded participants with missing key covariates, and retested the correlation. If the effect direction and significance are consistent with the interpolation analysis, the results are not affected by the missing data processing strategy. (2) As the eGFR is the core indicator for defining CKD, its value is directly influenced by the calculation formula. The initial analysis of this study used the CKD-EPI formula (with the race coefficient) to calculate the eGFR. Now, the eGFR is recalculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 SCR formula (without the race coefficient) and the MDRD formula [43, 44] (Table S3), and a correlation test is reconducted. Notably, since all NHANES cycles in this study did not lack cystatin C data, the combined formula of SCR and cystatin C could not be used. If the research results do not change substantially from the initial results under different eGFR calculation strategies, the conclusion of this study is not affected by the selection of the eGFR formula, thereby enhancing the generalizability and robustness of the results.

The association between the eGDR and CKD risk in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes

The results of multivariate logistic regression revealed that a higher eGDR might be associated with a lower prevalence of CKD in patients with diabetes or prediabetes (Table 2). Even after adjusting for various covariates, this correlation remains robust. Specifically, the continuous analysis revealed that every one-unit increase in eGDR was associated with a 21% reduction in prevalence of CKD in unadjusted Model 1 (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.77–0.80; p < 0.001), a 19% reduction in partially adjusted Model 2 (OR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.80–0.82; p < 0.001), and a 13% reduction in fully adjusted Model 3 (OR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.85–0.89; p < 0.001). In the quartile analysis, compared with that in the lowest quartile group (Q1), the prevalence of CKD in the highest quartile group (Q4) decreased by 83% in Model 1 (OR = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.15–0.19; p < 0.001), by 77% in Model 2 (OR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.20–0.26; p < 0.001), and by 57% in Model 3 (OR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.35–0.52; p < 0.001). Mediating effect

To evaluate the mediating role of SUA in the association between the eGDR and CKD in individuals with diabetes and prediabetes, we estimated path coefficients using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples. As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 4, the effect of eGDR on SUA (path a) was −2.7174 (95% CI: −3.4300 to −2.0040, p < 0.001), indicating that for every 1-unit increase in eGDR, SUA decreased by an average of approximately 2.7 mg/dL at the same investigation time point. The effect of SUA on CKD (path b) was 0.0032 (95% CI: 0.0027 to 0.0037, p < 0.001), indicating that for every 1 mg/dL increase in SUA, the average OR of CKD increased by 0.32%. The indirect effect (a * b) was −0.0087 (95% CI: −0.0118 to −0.0060, p < 0.001), implying that eGDR lowered the OR of CKD through reducing SUA. The direct effect (c’) was −0.1327 (95% CI: −0.1587 to −0.1068, p < 0.001), implying that the non-SUA pathway is involved in the relationship. The total effect (c = a * b + c’) was −0.1414 (95% CI: −0.1750 to −0.1080, p < 0.001). Consequently, the mediating proportion (a * b/c) of SUA in the total effect was 6.2% (95%CI: 4.2% to 8.9%). The strength of the effect correlation in each path is determined by introducing the E-value. The E-value of the indirect effect was only 1.040 (1.030–1.054), suggesting that this mediating pathway was highly sensitive to even very weak unmeasured confounding factors. Conversely, the direct effect (E-value = 1.45, 95%CI: 1.39–1.52) and the overall effect (E-value = 1.47, 95%CI: 1.41–1.54) demonstrated relatively strong robustness, indicating that in cross-sectional studies, the overall association between eGDR and CKD remained robust against moderate unmeasured confounding factors. The predictive ability of IR indicators for CKD in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes

In the population with diabetes or prediabetes, we compared the predictive ability of the eGDR with that of four traditional IR indicators (TyG index, METS-IR, HOMA-IR, and THR) for CKD (Fig. 5). The AUC of eGDR was 0.674 (95% CI: 0.665–0.683), which was significantly greater than that of the TyG index (AUC = 0.555, 95% CI: 0.545–0.565), METS-IR (AUC = 0.547, 95% CI: 0.537–0.557), HOMA-IR (AUC = 0.573, 95% CI: 0.563–0.583) and THR (AUC = 0.527, 95% CI: 0.517–0.537). The AUCs of the eGDR and other IR indicators were compared pairwise via the DeLong test. The results show that the AUC of eGDR is significantly better than that of all traditional indicators (Table S4), indicating that eGDR has a stronger discriminatory ability in identifying high-risk populations for CKD in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the main conclusions, we conducted two sensitivity analyses and compared them with the results of the main studies. First, after excluding the participants who were missing any key covariates, the sample size decreased from 17,595 to 12,321. In this complete dataset, the primary logistic regression was repeated. For every 1-unit increase in eGDR, the OR of CKD still decreased by 13% (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.84–0.89, p < 0.001) in Model 3 (Table S5). The direction and significance of the effect were basically consistent with the results after interpolation, suggesting that selection bias did not have a substantive impact on the conclusion. Second, we recalculated the eGFR through the CKD-EPI 2021 formula and the MDRD formula. Under these two eGFR strategies, the direction of the association between eGDR and CKD remained unchanged (Table S6). In conclusion, the negative correlation between eGDR and CKD has always been robust, indicating that the results of this study are not affected by the missing data processing strategy or the calculation method of the eGFR.

Discussion

This study utilized the NHANES large-scale cross-sectional survey to systematically explore for the first time the associations between the prevalence of eGDR and CKD in people with diabetes or prediabetes. The results revealed that a higher eGDR was significantly associated with a lower prevalence of CKD, and the significance remained robust even after confounding factors such as demographics, lifestyle, and medical history were considered. The RCS curve shows a significant linear negative correlation in Model 2 and Model 3, whereas a significant nonlinear relationship is observed in Model 1. Subgroup analysis indicated that gender had a significant interaction effect on this relationship. Specifically, a stronger correlation was observed in men than in women. The mediating effect reveals that SUA may partially mediate the association between eGDR and CKD, but this effect is highly likely to be explained by unmeasured confounding factors (E-value = 1.040). ROC curve analysis revealed that the AUC of the eGDR was 0.674, which was significantly greater than that of the traditional IR index. However, its ability to predict high-risk CKD populations among people with diabetes or prediabetes remains limited. The results of the sensitivity analysis further confirmed that neither the missing data processing strategy nor the eGFR calculation method affected the robust negative correlation between the eGDR and the prevalence of CKD. These findings provide novel theoretical foundations for early warning and intervention strategies targeting CKD in populations with diabetes or prediabetes.

Many studies have clarified the risk factors for CKD in people with diabetes or prediabetes. A cross-sectional study from China revealed that people with diabetes or prediabetes complicated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) may have a higher UACR and a greater correlation with CKD than those without NAFLD [45]. Similarly, a study involving over 120,000 subjects in Spain has shown that NAFLD may exacerbate the decline in eGFR in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes [46]. One study from the NHANES also suggested that residual cholesterol (RC) is an independent risk factor for CKD in patients with diabetes or prediabetes [47]. Compared with NAFLD, which relies on imaging examinations, and RC, which relies on complete fasting lipid profiles, eGDR can comprehensively reflect multiple metabolic loads only by conventionally measured waist circumference, blood pressure and HbA1c, suggesting that eGDR can be used as a more convenient and low-cost alternative indicator for CKD risk stratification in people with diabetes or prediabetes. In addition, numerous studies have investigated the potential role of the eGDR in reducing the risk of cardiovascular events and stroke in people with diabetes or prediabetes [16, 48, 49]. The impact of the eGDR on CKD in different glucose metabolism populations has also been reported. In non-diabetic populations, the eGDR is negatively correlated with CKD. For every one-unit increase in eGDR, the risk of CKD can be reduced by 9% [50]. A prospective study pointed out that in young patients with type 1 diabetes, the lower the eGDR value is, the greater the risk of developing kidney disease [51]. Although these research results are consistent with our overall findings, none of them specifically explored the relationship between eGDR and CKD in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes.

From a pathophysiological perspective, the association between eGDR and CKD may involve multiple interacting mechanisms. First, IR induces chronic inflammation, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which accelerate renal functional decline [52]. Second, IR exacerbates oxidative stress, promoting glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, thereby driving the progression of glomerulopathies [53]. Additionally, activation of the RAAS promotes CKD pathogenesis through angiotensin II-induced vasoconstriction, sodium retention, suppression of insulin-stimulated nitric oxide synthesis, and adipose tissue inflammation, which collectively increase pro-inflammatory cytokine release and adipocyte senescence [54]. Furthermore, IR contributes to structural renal damage via mitochondrial dysfunction, mesangial matrix expansion, and fibrosis, ultimately leading to impaired renal function [55].

Subgroup analysis confirmed that the association between eGDR and CKD remained consistently stable across all stratified variables. However, a significant gender interaction (P for interaction < 0.001) highlighted a stronger negative association in men. This finding might be related to the influence of sex hormones on insulin sensitivity [56]. Estrogen is believed to have a certain protective effect on the kidneys [57]. Estrogen can improve insulin sensitivity and has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and vasodilatory effects, which may alleviate the damage to the kidneys caused by IR [58]. However, men lack this protection and are thus more likely to develop CKD under the same IR conditions. Men are more likely to have combined smoking, drinking alcohol, and a poor diet, which aggravates metabolic disorders and kidney damage. Some studies have shown that men are more likely to accumulate visceral fat, leading to more severe IR and systemic inflammatory responses [59]. In addition, differences in sex chromosomes and related gene expression may affect the susceptibility of kidneys to metabolic damage [60].

Mediating analysis revealed the dual role of SUA in the “eGDR to CKD” pathway. IR-driven lipolysis increases SUA precursors [61], whereas hyperinsulinemia reduces renal SUA excretion [62]. Elevated SUA promotes renal injury via RAAS activation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated tubulointerstitial fibrosis [22, 63], and vascular remodeling [22, 26]. Notably, the value of the mediating proportion (6.2%) reflects the statistical overlap between SUA and eGDR-CKD in a single cross-sectional dataset and should not be interpreted as a causal weight in which reducing SUA can prevent the corresponding proportion of CKD cases. Interventions to lower SUA might warrant future testing as potential adjuncts if causal links are confirmed in longitudinal studies. The E-value for the indirect pathway (1.04) indicates that even modest unmeasured confounding could nullify the estimated indirect effect. Our 6.2% statistical overlap should therefore be interpreted as an upper bound under the strong assumption of no unmeasured confounding, rather than as evidence of a causal SUA-mediated pathway.

ROC curve analysis revealed that the eGDR demonstrated significantly superior predictive efficacy for CKD than other traditional IR indicators did, highlighting its clinical utility in patients with diabetes or prediabetes patients. This advantage may stem from the fact that eGDR integrates clinical and biochemical parameters, thereby enabling multi-dimensional assessment of IR pathophysiology. Specifically, waist circumference reflects visceral fat accumulation, which disrupts insulin signaling via mechanisms including abnormal retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) secretion and exacerbates renal inflammation through synergistic metabolic disturbances [64], whereas HbA1c, a marker of chronic hyperglycemia, drives advanced glycation end product (AGE)-receptor axis activation, inducing oxidative stress, glomerular basement membrane damage, proteinuria, and glomerulosclerosis [65]. Furthermore, IR-associated sympathetic hyperactivity and sodium retention increase the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, accelerating renal functional decline [66]. Notably, HOMA-IR, calculated from fasting glucose and insulin, is confounded by insulin-related therapies, whereas the TyG index, derived from fasting triglycerides and glucose, lacks robustness in dyslipidemia contexts [67, 68]. An AUC of 0.674 indicates modest discriminative ability. Nevertheless, in diabetes/prediabetes screening where no insulin assay is needed, eGDR offers a cost-free alternative that is statistically superior to existing fasting-only indices. In resource-limited primary care scenarios, eGDR is first used to quickly identify high-risk patients, and then, UACR or eGFR tests are added to low-value individuals. Therefore, eGDR is more suitable as an initial screening for risk stratification than as a replacement for existing renal function tests.

This study has notable limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between eGDR and CKD risk. In the future, prospective cocohort or randomized trials are needed to determine whether a low eGDR occurs before CKD and whether reducing SUA can change this trajectory. Second, although the E-value has been introduced to evaluate the influence of unmeasured confounding factors, it still cannot completely avoid the interference of confounding bias. Third, this study relies on single-point urine UACR and single-point SCR, ignoring that conditions such as diurnal biological fluctuations, dehydration, uncontrolled hypertension, urinary tract infection, or the menstrual cycle can increase the UACR and cannot be corrected within the current design. The cross-sectional design cannot perform the 3-month retest recommended by the KDIGO and calls for future studies to use 24-hour urine samples or multiple tests to confirm persistent kidney damage [69]. Moreover, the eGDR equation encodes hypertension as a binary yes/no component based on a threshold of 140/90 mmHg, thereby collapsing the full continuum of SBP and DBP. This dichotomization ignores the linear rise in IR and renal microvascular risk that occurs even at high-normal or mildly elevated blood pressure levels and may therefore underestimate risk in individuals with blood pressures of 130–139/85–89 mmHg who are already exposed to increased sympathetic activity and RAAS activation. Furthermore, the equation was originally derived against the HEC in type 1 diabetes; its coefficients have not been recalibrated for individuals with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating for future studies that recalibrate the metric or incorporate continuous blood pressure and direct insulin sensitivity measurements. Finally, this study is based on cross-sectional data from the NHANES. The conclusion is applicable only to the non-institutionalized population in the United States and has not been validated in independent cohorts (including the United States or other countries), with limited extrapolation.

Conclusion

On the basis of the cross-sectional design of the United States, this study confirmed in the population with diabetes or prediabetes that the eGDR is linearly negatively correlated with CKD, and its discriminative power is moderate but superior to that of traditional IR indicators. The statistical overlap of SUA on the total association was approximately 6.2%, but the mediating effect was fragile. In the future, it is necessary to verify whether a low eGDR occurs before CKD in repeated measurements or prospective cohort studies and to evaluate the net clinical benefits of targeted SUA-reducing interventions.
