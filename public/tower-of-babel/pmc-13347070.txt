Enhancing minority class recovery in high resolution land cover mapping with dynamic imbalance aware oversampling
Authors: Gabriel Amarante, Matheus T. P. Souza, Gabriel Lima, Willian Barreiros, Renato Ferreira, George Teodoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Semantic segmentation in remote sensing is frequently hindered by severe class imbalance, where over-represented land-cover categories overshadow critical minority ones. Conventional mitigation techniques, including cost-sensitive loss functions and stochastic data augmentation, often fail to generate sufficient diversity or preserve the structural and contextual integrity required for high-resolution imagery. On the other hand, generative approaches may be very data demanding, or become prone to creating semantic contradictions, thereby introducing significant noise into the training signal. To address this problem, we present dynamic imbalance-aware oversampling (DIAO) and its refined variant, DIAO-CP, specifically designed to overcome these challenges in pixel-level classification, specific to semantic segmentation tasks. DIAO replaces random sampling with an iterative density-driven scoring function that identifies images to be resampled based on their potential to minimize global distribution entropy. DIAO-CP extends this logic by integrating an object-centric synthesis loop that ensures context-sensitive placement, preventing semantic collisions by utilizing a background selection filter. These mechanisms ensure that synthesized samples maintain both statistical equilibrium and geographic plausibility. The proposed methods were evaluated on relevant semantic segmentation benchmarks, specifically the Chesapeake Conservancy and OpenEarthMap (OEM). Results indicate that DIAO-CP successfully optimized the information structure of the datasets, reducing the Kullback–Leibler divergence from an initial 0.3380 to 0.1924 in the OEM benchmark. In terms of predictive performance, our solutions yielded an average improvement of 10.5 and 11.8 percentage points (p.p.), respectively, in Macro F1 and mIoU compared to baseline model without using DIAO for all regions. As such, the DIAO strategies demonstrated a superior ability to recover rare semantic entities for minority classes. These findings confirm the effectiveness of our approach in enhancing the discriminative power for imbalanced classes in diverse geographic scales within semantic segmentation workflows.

Introduction

Deep learning (DL) has transformed several scientific disciplines, including natural language processing (NLP), computer vision, and bioinformatics1–4. The field of remote sensing (RS) has experienced similar advancements, particularly in the generation of land use and land cover (LULC) maps5–7.

Recent research in RS has explored advanced paradigms to handle the complexity of satellite imagery. Knowledge distillation and symmetrical learning frameworks have been developed to enhance feature transferability, incorporating symmetric generative algorithms to mitigate data imbalance in classification tasks8,9. Vision transformers have also introduced regularization strategies to improve generalization10, while causal feature alignment has been explored to reduce spurious correlations in detection frameworks11. Although these advances address imbalance through architectural interventions, they are primarily tailored for image-level classification or object detection. In the specific context of pixel-wise semantic segmentation, the “default condition” of severe data scarcity12 presents additional challenges. Beyond feature-level alignment, ensuring that synthetic samples maintain geographic consistency and topological plausibility remains an open challenge, as the high dimensionality of RS data demands solutions that directly preserve the spatial integrity of minority classes in dense mapping.

Furthermore, many critical applications face the inherent problem of class imbalance in the data, which leads DL models to exhibit a strong bias towards majority classes. In the specific context of remote sensing, this imbalance is particularly harmful because it leads to the suppression of minority features that exhibit high spatial frequency but low pixel counts. Since these entities, such as roads and small urban structures, are typically embedded within vast, spatially autocorrelated backgrounds (e.g., forests or agricultural fields), standard models tend to prioritize background continuity to minimize global loss. This bias compromises the topological integrity of the resulting land-cover maps, rendering them less effective for practical applications like infrastructure monitoring or urban planning13,14, which, in general, severely impairs the segmentation of infrequent but critical features15.

Here, we focus on the class imbalance problem. Previous strategies to address this aspect include cost-sensitive learning (e.g., Focal loss16, Tversky loss17) and data level techniques (oversampling and data augmentation). Unfortunately, these strategies have limited effect on semantic segmentation in RS, because they disregard local spatial correlation and structural integrity. An alternative approach recently proposed in the context of medical image analysis is the Copy-Paste augmentation paradigm18. This strategy consists in copying areas (classes) of interest from reference images and pasting them into target ones to create novel data samples in the augmentation in order to increase the frequency of minority classes.

While it preserves the raw integrity of semantic masks, standard implementations typically rely on a background-independent assumption, where objects are placed stochastically without regard for their surroundings. In complex RS scenes, this stochasticity frequently leads to semantic contradictions, such as roads placed over buildings or water bodies. These artifacts can introduce deleterious noise into the training signal rather than useful information19. Consequently, without a context-sensitive selection process, the model may struggle to learn valid spatial relationships, hindering its ability to generalize across diverse geographic landscapes.

Thus, a significant research gap remains: prior oversampling frameworks are largely distribution-agnostic during the sample selection phase. Precisely, they lack an objective mechanism to evaluate the marginal contribution of a specific image to the global dataset balancing and context. Most methods rely on random replication of minority-class samples, which can lead to redundant information and overfitting, as they do not account for class density variance across different tiles13,14. Furthermore, there is a lack of integration between selection criteria and synthesis procedures, as seen in standard Copy-Paste augmentation18, resulting in augmented samples that may be statistically relevant but geographically implausible. The challenge is to develop a framework that moves beyond stochastic replication toward a context-sensitive and information-theoretic selection process that actively minimizes global distribution entropy.

To address this problem, we propose the dynamic imbalance-aware oversampling (DIAO) and its advanced variant DIAO-CP technique. Unlike traditional oversampling methods, DIAO identifies images that contribute the most to reducing global imbalance using a density-driven scoring function during image selection for oversampling. Those images are passed to enhancement using different data augmentation transformations to improve diversity. DIAO-CP further refines this strategy by leveraging the Copy-Paste paradigm to isolate minority instances that are inserted into compatible backgrounds of new data samples, ensuring both statistical balance and contextual plausibility. Materials and methods

Class imbalance in RS semantic segmentation

Image segmentation represents a domain that has significantly benefited from the application of data-driven learning techniques across diverse fields such as computer vision20, biomedicine21, and manufacturing22. Despite its success, a fundamental challenge in these applications is the presence of imbalanced data, which can bias learners toward majority classes and potentially cause minority categories to be ignored altogether23. This issue is particularly pronounced in remote sensing (RS), where class imbalance arises from the inherent variations in the size, distribution, and spatial coverage of ground objects within a study area24. Such uneven pixel representation can significantly hinder the training of deep learning models, making it increasingly difficult for them to extract robust and meaningful features25,26.

The necessity for context-aware solutions in this domain is deeply rooted in the principles of spatial autocorrelation and object morphology. Unlike natural images, typically represented by perspective-view datasets such as ImageNet27 or MS COCO28, geographic data is governed by Tobler’s First Law of Geography, which states that “near things are more related than distant things”29. In semantic segmentation, this manifests as a high spatial dependency where a pixel’s semantic label is intrinsically linked to its neighborhood. Standard stochastic augmentation often disrupts this dependency by inserting objects in isolation, creating “spatial outliers” that do not exist in reality. Furthermore, the morphology of geographic entities, defined by their structural and topological properties, is critical for model convergence. For instance, the linear morphology of roads or the rectangular structures of buildings follow specific environmental logic. Context-blind methods risk placing these structures in incompatible landscapes, forcing the model to learn inconsistent features. Therefore, maintaining contextual integrity is essential to ensure that augmented samples adhere to the underlying geographic logic.

Regardless of application domain, imbalanced learning remains a substantial challenge. Machine learning methods usually rely on the assumption that classes are reasonably balanced, thus underrepresented classes can suffer from performance bias30. This means that underrepresented classes can be ignored by the training process, while the final model maintains high overall accuracy. General imbalanced training solutions can be classified as algorithmic, data preprocessing or hybrid solutions30,31.

However, while algorithmic strategies like focal loss or Tversky loss attempt to mitigate imbalance by reweighting the loss function, they often prove insufficient in remote sensing benchmarks32. As argued by Ghosh et al.12, deep learning models are particularly susceptible to imbalance because high-dimensional feature spaces can collapse toward majority structures. Solely adjusting the loss does not provide the structural and contextual diversity required for geospatial tasks; indeed, empirical evidence suggests that such methods frequently overfit minority noise and yield discontinuous segmentation paths32. By contrast, data-level interventions like DIAO enrich the training set with varied, contextually valid samples, offering a more robust learning signal.

Although this work focuses on novel data-level solutions, different loss functions are evaluated in a hybrid setting, demonstrating the robustness of the proposed solutions. The next section describes the algorithmic and data level strategies.

Data preprocessing approaches

To solve the issue of limited data, Data augmentation serves as a critical regularization methodology by expanding training datasets through the application of stochastic variations or synthetic derivations. By integrating constrained geometric transformations (including rotation, spatial translation, multi-scale scaling, and reflectional symmetry), this approach enhances model generalization and mitigates the risk of overfitting. To preserve the semantic integrity of the underlying features, it should be ensured that the augmented samples remain within a plausible representative distribution. These parameters are modified through specific angular, scaling, and coordinate-based ranges to prevent data corruption while maximizing architectural robustness36–38. These classical transformations alone, as discussed, have limited impact in dealing with highly imbalanced datasets as those found in RS.

A data augmentation approach proposed specifically to deal with data imbalance is the Copy-Paste (CP)18, which offers an interesting approach to mitigate imbalance by transferring semantic pixels between images. DIAO Copy-Paste (DIAO-CP)

We further propose the combination of the DIAO selection logic with an object-centric synthesis loop. While DIAO focuses on which training images to replicate, DIAO-CP further improves by generating novel tiles leveraging a modified Copy-Paste Strategy. This is done by extracting specific rare objects from their original tiles and pasting them onto new, more appropriate backgrounds, considering the statistical distribution of classes’ pixels. This approach leads to two key improvements of the training set. First, it increases the frequency of minority classes exactly where the dataset is lacking. Second, it improves data quality by generating a diverse set of new images, ensuring that the model learns from rare objects in multiple contexts rather than simply seeing the same scenes repeatedly.

A challenge that arises from generating new data is the preservation of topological plausibility. When applied directly for semantic segmentation in remote sensing, Copy-Paste efficacy can become limited. Since it relies on background-independent stochastic placement, augmentation often leads to contradictory features, such as roads over buildings or water bodies, which introduce noise and harm representation learning. To address the issue of contextual incompatibility, we implemented a background selection filter that ensures objects are placed only in semantically plausible locations. DIAO Copy-Paste data augmentation

DIAO-CP also follows an iterative process very similar to DIAO. However, instead of using the BaselineAugmentation (Algorithm 2, line 13), it employs the data augmentation function based on Copy-Paste (Algorithm 3). Thus, in each iteration, the DIAO relevance score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} is used to identify the optimal donor tile \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$id_{src}$$\end{document} from the training set (Algorithm 2, line 12). To maintain semantic plausibility, the destination tile \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$id_{dest}$$\end{document} is then selected from the previously filtered subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {I}_{strat}$$\end{document} (Algorithm 3, line 2). Once both donor and background tiles are defined (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X[id_{src}]$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X[id_{dest}]$$\end{document}), the rare object is extracted from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X[id_{src}]$$\end{document} (line 3). To increase geometric diversity, the isolated patch undergoes a random rotation (line 4), which helps the model learn features that are invariant to orientation. The process ends with Smooth Paste using a Gaussian kernel with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma =1.5$$\end{document} (line 5), which prevents the model from overfitting to high-frequency artifacts. Finally, the resulting synthetic pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(I_{syn}, M_{syn})$$\end{document} is added to the training set (line 6). Afterward, the dataset statistics are updated, allowing the relevance score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} of following iterations to be dynamically adjusted until the desired class balance is achieved.

This approach improves on DIAO by creating more diverse datasets, but also addresses two major methodological limitations of the original Copy-Paste (CP). First, CP lacks a density-based selection mechanism, since it does not seek tiles with the highest concentration of the rare class, often resulting in the transfer of sparse or poor-quality features. Second, the random selection of destination tiles frequently leads to semantic contradictions, in which minority features/regions are pasted over incompatible land cover classes (e.g., placing a road segment over a building). These flaws result in the introduction of significant contextual noise, as non-target features adjacent to the rare class are indiscriminately moved, potentially leading the network to learn inconsistent spatial associations.

DIAO framework design

The following experimental framework was designed to operationalize DIAO and benchmark its performance against established imbalance mitigation strategies. This pipeline allows for a rigorous comparison between the proposed oversampling strategy and state-of-the-art methods, providing the evidence to validate DIAO’s robustness across diverse class distributions.

The current version of the evaluation system includes three deep learning models: U-Net42, U-Net 3+43, and Attention U-Net44. The U-Net architecture is a standard model used in segmentation, including remote sensing (RS). It employs a symmetrical structure for multi-scale feature extraction. U-Net 3+ advances this design by implementing full-scale skip connections to mitigate the semantic gap between encoder and decoder features. Finally, Attention U-Net (AttU-Net) refines the decoding process by integrating attention gates, which prioritize spatially relevant features while suppressing redundant activations.

The framework also incorporates various loss functions to evaluate their synergy with the data-level algorithms as hybrid class imbalance solutions. In addition to the standard cross-entropy loss (CE), the loss functions focal loss (FL), dice loss, and Tversky loss are supported. It is important to highlight that each function required hiperparameter optimization in order to be effective. Although we present the results for the domain of RS, these values can be tuned for other domains within the proposed framework.

For focal loss, the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} was determined through a dedicated sensitivity analysis step. We explored \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma \in \{0, 1, 2, 5\}$$\end{document}, noting that while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =0$$\end{document} (equivalent to CE) exhibited poor sensitivity to rare classes, higher values such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =2$$\end{document} or 5 introduced gradient instability in complex landscapes. The configuration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma = 1$$\end{document} provided the optimal balance between suppressing easy-example gradients and ensuring stable convergence, and was therefore selected for the experiments.

The Tversky loss parameters were optimized through a grid search to evaluate the influence of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} hyperparameters on model convergence. Empirical evidence demonstrated that configurations that prioritized the reduction of false negatives (FN), specifically where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta > 0.7$$\end{document}, were essential for the effective segmentation of minority classes. The asymmetric configuration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta =0.9$$\end{document} proved to be a turning point for the model’s sensitivity. In more symmetric settings, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.4$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta =0.6$$\end{document}, the gradient signal was insufficient to overcome the dominance of majority classes. By adopting a recall-biased objective, AttU-Net was able to penalize omissions more severely, facilitating the detection of fragmented features that are typically overlooked in standard land-cover classification tasks. This behavior confirms that asymmetric loss tuning is not merely an optimization choice but a requirement for handling the severe imbalance inherent in the studied datasets.

Furthermore, baseline data augmentation was implemented using the Albumentations library45, which includes transformations such as horizontal and vertical flips, shifts, and rotations. To determine the optimal augmentation intensity, a preliminary sensitivity analysis was conducted to establish the probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$aug\_rate$$\end{document} for the augmentation routines. The value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$aug\_rate=0.5$$\end{document} attained the best results thus being employed in all experiments.

Results

This section evaluates the effectiveness of the proposed strategies in high-resolution remote sensing applications. The analysis is structured to transition from a baseline method selection to a rigorous assessment of our primary contributions. Section “Setup” details the experimental setup. In Section “Model architecture and loss selection”, we conduct a preliminary study to select the optimal combination of neural network architecture and loss function, establishing a robust baseline for comparison. The core of our evaluation begins in Section “The impact of DIAO to segmentation”, with the overall performance analysis of the baseline and proposed approaches in all geographic regions of the used datasets. This is followed by Section “Per-class analysis of the strategies”, providing a per-class analysis of the results, offering a granular view of the model’s ability to recover minority features.

Setup

The experiments were conducted on a single machine running Ubuntu 24.04, equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 7900 CPU, 64 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of VRAM. All models were implemented using the TensorFlow framework47 and trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer48. An early stopping regularization was employed, monitoring the validation loss with a min_delta of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document}. Within this policy, the patience parameter was set to 20 epochs, defining the threshold of consecutive iterations the model is permitted to proceed without a significant improvement in the validation metric before the training process is terminated.

The Attention U-Net and its counterparts were employed using the keras-unet-collection library49 with a Keras backend. The architectures were configured for an input shape of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256 \times 256 \times 3$$\end{document} and trained with a batch size of 4 and an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document}. Within all experiments, we observed that for scenarios characterized by severe class imbalance, validation metrics often exhibited high variance and temporary plateaus. Thus, the extended patience of 20 was chosen to allow the model sufficient opportunity to traverse local plateaus and converge to a better generalization state34.

Regarding the Chesapeake dataset, while our preliminary analysis encompassed the New York, Virginia, and West Virginia subsets, the core benchmarking and in-depth experiments, were focused on the Pennsylvania (PA) region (2013 collection period). Pennsylvania was selected as a representative study area due to its diverse land-cover composition, which effectively captures the morphological complexities and extreme class imbalance (e.g., rare road networks vs. dominant forest cover) typical of the broader Chesapeake Bay watershed.

For both the PA subset and the OpenEarthMap (OEM) dataset, the high-resolution remote sensing rasters were partitioned into tiles with spatial dimensions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256 \times 256$$\end{document} pixels using a non-overlapping grid-based approach. Specifically, we implemented a synchronized window-based extraction to ensure exact spatial alignment between spectral bands and ground truth masks. To maintain structural consistency, only complete tiles meeting the full \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256 \times 256$$\end{document} dimensions were retained. This tile size was determined through preliminary testing, which indicated that smaller samples (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$128 \times 128$$\end{document}) often contained a single dominant class, reducing the variance of the training signal, while larger tiles (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$512 \times 512$$\end{document}) diluted rare features and significantly reduced the total number of training samples.

To ensure uniformity and resource manageability, fixed-size subsets were defined from the total pool of tiles in each area. We employed a random sampling to select 4000 training tiles, 450 validation tiles, and 700 testing tiles. This randomized selection ensures that the subsets accurately reflect the natural land-cover distribution and the inherent scarcity of minority classes without manual intervention bias. By adopting the minimum available tile count as a fixed baseline for all experiments, we ensured a balanced and statistically consistent comparison across diverse geographic regions. For each evaluated augmentation strategy (Standard Augmentation, CP, DIAO, and DIAO-CP), a fixed volume of 2450 additional synthetic tiles was generated, resulting in a total training set of 6450 tiles per experiment.

For each evaluated augmentation strategy (standard augmentation, CP, DIAO, and DIAO-CP), a fixed volume of 2450 additional synthetic or transformed tiles was generated, resulting in a total training set of 6450 tiles per experiment. The selection of data augmentation parameters was carefully calibrated to introduce necessary variance while preserving the fundamental semantics of geospatial features50. We applied transformations with a probability of 0.5, including horizontal and vertical flips to exploit the inherent symmetry of nadir-view satellite imagery. Two distinct rotation strategies were implemented: first, affine rotations (up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$30^{\circ }$$\end{document}) combined with spatial shifts (0.2 limit) to simulate realistic variations in sensor tilt and object positioning; and second, orthogonal rotations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^{\circ }, 180^{\circ }, 270^{\circ }$$\end{document}) to ensure model invariance to different cardinal orientations. These constrained transformations were chosen specifically to avoid excessive geometric distortions that could lead to contextually implausible road network morphologies, ensuring that the synthetic samples remain within the manifold of valid geographic distributions. The initial choice of training tiles was a practical decision dictated by computational overhead. By keeping all training within GPU memory, there are no data transfers between CPU and GPU during epochs, which significantly accelerates the training process for the 100 epochs used.

Performance was evaluated using Macro F1, Micro F1, and intersection over union (IoU) Scores. The main goal of the proposed solutions was to improve the Macro F1-score and the IoU. These particular metrics capture the ability to detect minority classes, while preserving overall accuracy seen in Micro F1. Five different training scenarios were evaluated comparatively. First, we established a Baseline using only the original tiles without any modifications. Then, a standard Data Augmentation was tested, in which additional images were randomly selected and modified with basic transformations like rotations and flips. Next, we tested the original Copy-Paste (CP), in which rare objects are randomly pasted onto random background tiles. The DIAO solution was tested to evaluate the effect of choosing the images based on current imbalance. Finally, we evaluated DIAO-CP, which uses the DIAO score to select the most needed objects and pastes them onto screened backgrounds using a high density threshold and smooth alpha blending.

Discussion and limitations

The experimental results using the Chesapeake Bay and OpenEarthMap datasets revealed a substantial performance improvement of the proposed DIAO-CP strategy compared to the preivous solutions. In PA, DIAO-CP achieved a Macro F1-score of 0.825 and a mIoU of 0.697, marking a decisive improvement of 12.6 percentage points (p.p.) over the baseline (Macro F1 0.699). This trend remains consistent in the OEM dataset, where DIAO-CP reached a Macro F1 of 0.722, effectively outperforming both the standard DIAO (0.691) and the baseline models (0.617).

These findings support the analytical framework proposed by Ghosh et al.12, which postulates that while architectural choice plays a role, data scarcity remains the “default condition” in real-world domains, serving as the primary driver of performance degradation in imbalanced tasks. Our results confirm this observation: even when employing advanced attention-based backbones, the baseline performance is constrained by the lack of representation for minority classes. By implementing DIAO-CP, we move beyond purely algorithmic solutions to directly mitigate this default scarcity, ensuring that the model has access to high-quality samples to overcome the concept complexity inherent in remote sensing imagery.

A critical observation is the performance degradation associated with the Copy-Paste strategy, particularly evident in the OEM results where Macro F1 was significantly worse than the Baseline. This behavior validates our hypothesis that transferring objects without geometric isolation or strategic background filtering may not be effective. The model is likely learn these artificial edges or inconsistent contextual cues rather than the actual morphological features of the rare classes.

It is important to note that Focal Loss remained robust compared to overlap-based metrics (Dice and Tversky). Although overlap-based losses struggled to stabilize in the high-imbalance scenarios of the PA dataset, the Focal Loss maintained a resilient gradient signal at the boundaries of the introduced objects.

The empirical findings of the information-theoretic analysis in Section “Per-Class Analysis of the Strategies” are directly correlated with performance changes. The reduction in KL Divergence (which decreased from 0.3380 to 0.1924 for the OEM dataset) provided a balanced training signal that allowed the model to achieve higher sensitivity to minority classes without sacrificing global precision (Micro F1). In summary, the success of DIAO-CP confirms that simply increasing the frequency of rare classes is insufficient for satellite imagery. The quality of the synthetic scene, ensured by stochastic rotations, density-driven selection, and the avoidance of Developed or Building backgrounds, is crucial for the model to generalize. By forcing the network to identify rare semantic entities against high-contrast, natural backgrounds, we mitigate the bias toward majority classes like Forest or Water. This visual evidence helps to show that the method is actually learning the morphological boundaries of the rare classes in more detail.

The proposed DIAO (and DIAO-CP) strategies addresses fundamental skews in remote sensing datasets. However, there are still limitations in the current solution. The efficacy of the DIAO-CP synthesis loop is fundamentally contingent upon the precision of the initial ground truth annotations. During the strategic isolation phase, inaccurate or coarse semantic boundaries can result in the inadvertent extraction of unintended surrounding pixels from adjacent majority classes. These artifacts are subsequently subjected to geometric transformations and alpha blending, which may perpetuate spectral noise.

Furthermore, while the framework implements a background selection filter to prevent semantic collisions, the reliance on a density threshold represents a heuristic approach that may not capture complex ecological or topological relationships. This limitation suggests that synthesized scenes, while statistically beneficial for dataset balancing, might lack full geographic authenticity by failing to account for nuanced spatial co-occurrence or specific environmental gradients.

Additionally, the current class specificity of the background filters reveals an inherent natural bias, as the algorithm deliberately favors natural land-cover categories such as field to maximize spectral contrast. This heuristic may be inappropriate for urban-specific minority classes that require placement on developed backgrounds to maintain semantic logic. This limitation is a consequence of the simplified strategy inherited from the Copy-Paste approach. Creating such new synthetic data will require more detailed and complex strategies.

A further limitation of the current DIAO-CP framework is its explicit design for pixel-level semantic segmentation. The background selection filter and morphological extraction rely heavily on dense, pixel-wise annotations to compute density scores and isolate objects smoothly. Consequently, directly generalizing this specific approach to other fundamental remote sensing tasks, such as image-level scene classification or bounding-box-based object detection, is not trivial. Adapting the density-driven relevance scoring for these tasks would require reformulating the spatial heuristic to account for discrete object instances or global image labels rather than continuous pixel distributions, representing an important challenge for future research and structural adaptation.

Finally, while DIAO is designed as a data-level strategy and thus remains agnostic to the underlying architecture, future work could systematically investigate its interaction with different model families (e.g., encoder–decoder versus attention-based architectures). Such analysis may reveal whether architectural biases amplify or mitigate the benefits of DIAO.

Conclusions

This study introduced the dynamic imbalance-aware oversampling (DIAO) framework and its variant, DIAO-CP, as a strategy to mitigate severe class imbalance. These solutions were applied to high-resolution remote sensing segmentation. Moving beyond traditional stochastic oversampling, DIAO implements a self-regulating mechanism that leverages a density-driven scoring function. This approach ensures that the model is exposed to high-quality, contextually relevant samples of minority classes, addressing the root cause of minority feature suppression in deep neural networks across distinct landscape compositions.

Advantages of the proposed framework

The experimental results across diverse benchmarks, ranging from the high-resolution Chesapeake Bay tiles to the multi-sensor OpenEarthMap dataset, confirmed the robustness of the proposed framework. A significant reduction in KL Divergence was observed with DIAO-CP. For instance, KL Divergence fell from 0.8889 to 0.5399 in the Chesapeake Bay PA area and decreased from 0.3380 to 0.1924 in the OEM benchmark, representing a substantial shift toward a balanced training signal. This objective optimization of the information structure of the dataset translated directly into superior segmentation performance and consistently outperforming previous approaches.

An interesting finding of this research was that simply increasing the frequency of rare classes was insufficient for robust feature extraction. The per-class recall analysis demonstrated that the spatial and morphological integrity provided by DIAO-CP was important in recovering very scarce classes, such as Barren Land, in which a recall gain of 58 percentage points (p.p.) was attained in Pennsylvania. By integrating density-aware selection with context-sensitive placement, we successfully mitigated the introduction of artifacts associated with original Copy-Paste method, resulting in sharper and more well-defined class boundaries.

Potential applications and future works

Beyond the immediate scope of mitigating imbalance in existing datasets, we identified multiple avenues for further exploration. The current strategy employed by DIAO uses the Copy-Paste methodology to create synthetic data, but other strategies such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) or Diffusion Models could be employed for that sake. We have chosen the Copy-Paste approach because it does not require data annotation for training, as would be the case for GANs and Diffusion Models. In practice, the scarce annotation of minority classes could be a limitation in training such models. However, this remains an interesting avenue for future work. Additionally, while this study focused on semantic segmentation, the underlying logic of density-driven importance sampling may be used in broader remote sensing applications, such as object detection and change detection in multi-temporal satellite imagery.
