Multimodal MRI-based nomogram integrating clinical-radiological, radiomic, and habitat features to discriminate solitary fibrous tumors from atypical meningiomas
Authors: Hua Zhang, Xiaoling Li, Hongjie Huang, Jie Kang, Yang Song, Jianye Li, Dairong Cao, Zhen Xing, Xingfu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop and validate a fused model integrating clinical-radiological, radiomic, and habitat features from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for differentiating intracranial solitary fibrous tumors (ISFTs) from atypical meningiomas (ATMs). This retrospective study included 155 patients (39 ISFTs, 116 ATMs), divided into training (n = 108) and testing (n = 47) cohorts. Clinical-radiological factors were evaluated, and a clinical-radiological model (CRM) was constructed using multivariable logistic regression. Radiomic features from whole tumors and habitat features from subregions were extracted to build radiomics model (RM) and habitat model (HM) via four classifiers. A clinical-radiological-habitat-radiomics model (CHRM) incorporating all features was developed. Performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). The CRM identified cyst/necrosis and absent dural tail sign as independent predictors of ISFTs. In the testing cohort, the HM achieved an AUC of 0.829, higher than the CRM (0.696) and RM (0.764). The CHRM demonstrated the best performance, with AUCs of 0.928 (training) and 0.888 (testing), showing significant improvements in IDI (P < 0.05). The HM revealed distinct tumor spatial heterogeneity between ISFTs and ATMs, and the CHRM provided superior preoperative discrimination. These findings provide clinical value for treatment planning, though future validation is warranted to confirm generalizability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57096-y.

Introduction

Intracranial solitary fibrous tumors (ISFTs) and atypical meningiomas (ATMs) are the two most common potentially aggressive extra-axial intracranial tumors, with overlapping clinical and imaging features1–3. ISFTs, accounting for 0.4% of primary intracranial tumors4,5, exhibit aggressive behavior with recurrence rates up to 60% and metastasis rates of 20%6–8. In contrast, ATMs represent the most prevalent aggressive meningioma subtype, showing a 50%−55% recurrence rate after gross total resection9,10.

ISFTs and ATMs share nonspecific neurological symptoms and conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) characteristics (Fig. 1). Existing evidence demonstrates significant differences in clinical management between the two entities1,3. The main treatment choice for both ISFTs and ATMs is maximal safe tumor resection11,12. Given the highly vascularized nature of ISFTs, preoperative embolization is often recommended to reduce intraoperative bleeding risk and improve resectability12,13. Adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) has been associated with a remarkable survival advantage for ISFTs11. In contrast, the role of adjuvant RT after resection in ATMs remains controversial, as current evidence shows no significant survival benefit14. These critical differences highlight the importance of accurate preoperative differentiation to optimize treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Radiomics has emerged as a promising tool for improving tumor differentiation by extracting high-dimensional quantitative features from diagnostic images15,16. Recent studies applied MRI-based radiomics to differentiate ISFTs from ATMs and meningioma subtypes1,17–19. However, radiomics primarily captures global tumor characteristics and may overlook intratumoral heterogeneity and localized microenvironmental variations. To address this limitation, tumor habitat analysis has been proposed as a technique to partition tumors into distinct subregions reflecting spatial heterogeneity and improving prediction accuracy20,21. By applying clustering methods, such as K-means clustering based on voxel-wise data, habitat analysis extracts region-specific features linked to histologic and molecular attributes in gliomas and breast cancer22–24. Despite these advances, no studies to date have applied habitat imaging to differentiate ISFTs from ATMs.

Therefore, the aims of this study were (1) to develop a quantitative measure of intratumoral heterogeneity using habitat analysis on MRI scans, and (2) to test whether a nomogram combining clinical-radiological, habitat, and radiomic features could preoperatively differentiate ISFTs and ATMs.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The ethics number is [2024]752. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

A total of 539 patients with pathologically confirmed ISFTs and ATMs from October 2008 to July 2021 were initially enrolled. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) completed conventional MRI; (2) definite histopathologic diagnosis of ISFTs and ATMs according to the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System; and (3) surgical intervention within 14 days following MRI examination. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) poor image quality (n = 26); (2) incomplete MR images (T1-weighted [T1W], T2-weighted [T2W], and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted [CE-T1W], n = 55); (3) age < 18 years (n = 7); (4) unclear pathological results (n = 4); (5) whole tumor with calcification (n = 6); and (6) recurrent tumor (n = 31). A total of 410 patients (39 with ISFTs and 371 with ATMs) met the eligibility criteria. Due to the significant class imbalance between the two groups, ATMs were randomly matched with ISFTs at a 3:1 ratio based on tumor location. Finally, 155 patients (116 ATMs and 39 ISFTs) were included and divided into the training cohort and testing cohort at a ratio of 7:3. As shown in Table S1, no significant difference is observed in tumor location between matched ATMs and the remaining ATMs (P = 0.223), nor between ISFTs and whole ATM cohort (P = 0.471). The patient screening process is shown in Fig. S1.

Histological diagnosis

Routine hematoxylin and eosin-stained and immunohistochemical slides were independently reviewed by one experienced pathologist (X.L.L., with 10 years of experience), who was blinded to imaging and clinical data. All cases were reevaluated according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System. Of these, four cases with unclear pathological results due to insufficient immunohistochemical slides were excluded from the final analysis.

Clinical-radiological features assessment

The clinical features included age, sex, and MR field strength. The conventional MR radiological features were evaluated, including longest diameter, shortest diameter, morphology, cyst/necrosis, enhancement pattern, flow-void, dural tail sign, tumor-brain interface, peritumoral edema, and bone invasion. Detailed definitions are provided in Table S2. Two neuroradiologists (D.R.C., with 33 years of experience; J.K., with 5 years of experience), blinded to clinical and pathological information, independently evaluated all radiological features, with final assessments determined by consensus.

Step 1 MR image acquisition

Five MR scanners were used in this study. The MRI protocols in this study are listed in Table S3. Details of MR image acquisition are provided in the supplementary material.

Step 2 Image preprocessing

The bias field of all images was corrected using the SimpleITK tool (version 2.2.1, https://simpleitk.org/). Then, T2W images were registered to CE-T1W images with linear interpolation using SPM12 on Matlab R2016b (MathWorks, Natick, MA).

Step 3 Tumor segmentation

Both T2W and CE-T1W images were imported into ITK-SNAP software (version 3.6.0, http://www.itksnap.org) to delineate the volume of interest (VOI). Reader 1 (H.Z., with 8 years of experience in neuroradiology), blinded to participant data, independently performed manual tumor segmentation. The VOIs were manually delineated slice-by-slice along solid tumor margins on axial CE-T1W. Once the VOI segmentation was completed on CE-T1W, the corresponding VOIs were automatically generated on T2W images. To minimize recall bias, Reader 1 re-segmented 30 randomly selected cases for intra-observer assessment after a one-month washout period, while Reader 2 (H.J.H., with 9 years of experience in neuroradiology) independently segmented the same cases for inter-observer assessment.

Step 4 Tumor subregion segmentation

The VOIs were imported into “habitat feature extraction” module of nnFeature Explorer (nnFAE, version 0.2.5), which used K-means clustering algorithm to objectively extract tumor habitat subregions25. The optimal number of habitat subregions was identified by the Silhouette Score. Clustering was performed at the cohort level to maintain consistent clustering assignments across all patients. Finally, three subregions were obtained by habitat analysis.

Step 5 Feature extraction

The VOIs were imported into Feature Explorer (FAE, version 0.5.9), which normalized signal intensity in a scale from 0 to 255, and resampled images to 1 × 1 × 1mm3,26. It automatically extracted 106 T2W and 106 CE-T1W radiomic features from whole tumor region, consisting of shape, first-order histogram, gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), and neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM). The conventional radiomic features are listed in Table S4.

The “habitat features extraction” module of nnFAE automatically extracted 2 volume features and 17 first-order histogram features for each habitat subregion. According to previous study, we constructed a habitat map in which the intensity values of voxels were replaced with the previously identified habitat index27. Habitat maps were imported into FAE to extract 44 radiomic features, including 4 first-order histograms, 24 GLCM, and 16 GLSZM features. The habitat features are listed in Table S5. All features were in compliance with Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI)28.

Step 6 Feature dimension reduction

First, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to measure both intra-observer and inter-observer reliability of the segmented VOIs. Features with an ICC > 0.85 were retained to ensure high reproducibility and minimize noise. The highly robust feature set can reduce the risk of overfitting and enhance the generalizability of the predictive models, consistent with recommendations in previous studies29,30. Second, the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) was applied to address training cohort imbalances. Third, feature matrix normalization was achieved by rescaling values from zero to one followed by unit vector transformation. Subsequently, dimension reduction was further performed. If the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) value of the feature pair was larger than 0.99, we removed one of them. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for feature selection.

Step 7 Predictive model development

Hyperparameters are the configuration settings used to structure the machine learning algorithms, which are set prior to training and govern the learning process. In our study, the refined features were applied to develop radiomics model (RM) and habitat model (HM), using four machine-learning classifiers: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), Gaussian process (GP), and linear discriminant analysis (LDA). Five-fold cross-validation was applied on the training cohort to determine the optimal hyperparameters. The hyperparameters were selected based on the model performance on the cross-validation cohorts. The conventional radiomics score (R-score) and habitat-based radiomics score (H-score) for each patient were calculated from prediction value (Pre) on the optimal RM and HM, and verified in the testing cohort. The score was calculated as follows: R-score = ln (Pred/(1-Pred)). All above were implemented by FAE based on scikit-learn. A clinical-radiological-habitat-radiomics model (CHRM) was developed based on multi-variable logistic regression analysis, incorporating R-score, H-score, and the selected clinical-radiological features.

Step 8 Model evaluation

The CHRM was presented as a nomogram in the training cohort and validated in the testing cohort. The Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) test was performed to assess the goodness of fit. A calibration plot was used to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was utilized to elucidate the contribution of individual features to the predictions of the CHRM, thereby providing greater insight into and interpretability of the model’s decisions31,32. The SHAP values were computed on the training sets using the SHAP (version 0.42.1) package in Python (version 3.12).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers with percentages. Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test due to their non-normal distribution. The intra- and inter-observer reliability of radiomic features was evaluated by ICC. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression were performed to determine factors for discriminating between ISFTs and ATMs. Potential predictors (P < 0.1) in the univariable analysis in the training cohort were entered into multivariable logistic regression using the enter method. The independent predictors (P < 0.05) were retained to conduct a clinical-radiological model (CRM). The predictive capacity of the CRM was further assessed in the testing cohort. The diagnostic ability of the different models was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the discrimination efficacy was evaluated by the area under the curve (AUC). The integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) test was used to compare the model performance. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to compare the net benefit difference of models. A two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated a significant difference. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, New York), R (version 4.1.3, http://www.R-project.org), MedCalc (version 15.2.2, MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium, https://www.medcalc.org), and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

In the training cohort, univariable analysis identified age, longest diameter, morphology, cyst/necrosis, enhancement pattern, flow-void sign, dural tail sign, tumor-brain interface, and bone invasion as potential discriminators between ISFTs and ATMs (all P < 0.1). These variables were further analyzed using multivariable logistic regression. The final model revealed that cyst/necrosis (odds ratio [OR] = 6.700; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.330–19.273), and dural tail sign (OR = 0.209; 95% CI: 0.071–0.617) were independent predictors for discriminating ISFTs from ATMs (Table 2). The CRM was constructed as follows: Y = −0.535 + 1.902 × “cyst/necrosis” − 1.564 × “dural tail sign”.

HM

The analysis identified three optimal clusters using the Silhouette Score method. As presented in Table 4, the volumetric distribution revealed habitat 2 as the dominant subtype (55%), with habitats 1 (33%) and 3 (12%) showing progressively smaller representations. Regarding imaging characteristics, habitat 1 demonstrated minimal signal intensity on T2W but exhibited the most pronounced contrast enhancement. In contrast, habitat 2 displayed intermediate signal intensity on T2W with moderate post-contrast enhancement. Habitat 3 was distinguished by the highest T2W signal intensity and the weakest contrast enhancement among the three subtypes (Fig. 1).

CHRM

Dural tail sign, cyst/necrosis, R-score and H-score were included in the multivariate logistic regression. The CHRM was constructed by three independent predictors, including dural tail sign (OR = 0.083; 95% CI: 0.014–0.483; P = 0.006), H-score (OR = 2.955; 95% CI: 1.209–7.200; P = 0.017), and R-score (OR = 5.030; 95% CI: 1.945–13.010; P = 0.001). The model was calculated using the following equation: Y = 0.662–2.492 × “dural tail sign” + 1.083 × “H-score” + 1.615 × “R-score”. The CHRM demonstrated superior performance, with AUCs of 0.928 (95% CI: 0.862–0.969) and 0.888 (95% CI: 0.762–0.961) in the training and testing cohorts, respectively (Fig. 3). The variables included in the CHRM were visualized as a nomogram (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Preoperative discrimination between ISFTs and ATMs is clinically critical due to their distinct individualized treatment plans and prognostic implications. In this study, we established and validated four predictive models, including CRM, RM, HM, and CHRM. Among these, the CHRM, integrating habitat features from tumor subregional heterogeneity with clinical-radiological risk factors and radiomic features, demonstrated the most promising predictive performance, with AUC values of 0.928 and 0.888 in training and testing cohorts, respectively. The CHRM had better goodness-of-fit and increased clinical benefit, establishing it as a novel and robust tool for the differentiation of ISFTs and ATMs.

Clinical-radiological findings remain an important part of tumor research. Previous studies indicated that dural tail sign was the independent risk factor between ISFTs and meningiomas13,33,34. Our study revealed its significantly higher prevalence in ATMs compared to ISFTs, in line with a previous study34. This discrepancy may stem from ATMs’ meningothelial origin and slow growth pattern, which chronically stimulate adjacent meningeal tissue33,34. Our CRM also identified cyst/necrosis as independent discriminator, which was previously undocumented in existing differential models13,34. This finding may be supported by the tumors’ distinct pathological characteristics. The cystic degeneration and necrosis were frequently observed in ISFTs due to tumor tissue erosion, destruction of intratumoral vessels, rapid tumor growth, and a relative lack of nutrition33,35–37. While the CRM demonstrated statistically significant predictors, its low predictive performance underscores the limitations of relying solely on conventional imaging features, especially given the radiological overlap between these tumor entities.

Recently, a growing number of radiomics studies have attempted to discriminate ISFTs from meningiomas1,13,19,38,39. Wei et al. developed a clinical-radiomics model for distinguishing intracranial hemangiopericytomas from meningiomas using T1W, T2W and CE-T1W, achieving AUC values of 0.985 and 0.917 in training and validation cohorts, respectively13. Furthermore, several studies demonstrated the significance of radiomics models in differentiating ISFTs from angiomatous meningiomas19,38,39. While prior radiomics studies primarily differentiated ISFTs from benign meningiomas, our study focused on the more challenging distinction between ISFTs and ATMs, which were aggressive extra-axial intracranial tumors with similar imaging manifestations.

In our study, RM achieved an AUC of 0.764 in testing cohort, with 10 radiomic features. Notably, five features were derived from T2W, reflecting signal intensity and tumor heterogeneity, while the remaining features were extracted from CE-T1W, including Maximum, Range, MajorAxisLength, Maximum3DDiameter, and Sphericity. Interestingly, VOIs segmented from CE-T1W more effectively capture tumor shape features than traditional 2D radiological evaluation. Although the predictive performance was moderate, our results indicated that ISFTs exhibited greater morphological irregularity than ATMs, potentially reflecting their invasive growth patterns. Although radiomics can quantify intratumoral heterogeneity, its predictive performance remains limited by the inability to capture spatial relationships between voxels.

Habitat imaging, which visualizes the subregional heterogeneity and complexity of the tumor microenvironment, addresses this limitation by enabling the clustering of similar voxels to capture spatial information. While habitat analysis has shown promise in various oncological applications40–42, its potential for differentiating ISFTs from meningioma subtypes remains unexplored. Previous studies have demonstrated that MRI features derived from both T2W and CE-T1W sequences can effectively differentiate ISFTs from meningiomas17,33. In this study, we characterized tumor heterogeneity through Silhouette Score-optimized habitat analysis, identifying three distinct subregions in both T2W and CE-T1W. Habitat 2, with the largest proportion (55%), represented a relatively moderate-vascular solid component of the tumors, demonstrating moderate T2W signal intensity and moderate enhancement on CE-T1W. Habitat 1 corresponded to hyper-vascular solid component characterized by low T2W signal and prominent CE-T1W enhancement. Habitat 3 manifested as weak enhancement and high T2W signal intensity, radiologically suggestive of hypo-vascular, a hypocellular region most likely representing necrosis or cyst.

In our study, we first designed the HM for discriminating ISFTs from ATMs by extracting subregion features from T2W and CE-T1W. The HM achieved reasonable discrimination and generalization in the testing cohort (AUC = 0.829). In the HM, 90% selected habitat features (9/10) were derived from Habitat 2, underscoring its discriminative value. This imaging pattern may correlate to underlying histopathological differences, potentially explaining diagnostic superiority of habitat 2. ATMs demonstrate homogeneous sheets of tumor cells whereas ISFTs show prominent stromal collagen deposition with alternating hypercellular and hypocellular areas35,43. Li et al. reported that collagen content was an independent prognostic factor in ISFTs, and can be assessed by radiomics analysis44. The moderate enhancement pattern observed in habitat 2 may reflect these collagen-rich regions exhibiting intermediate vascularity and transitional zones between different cellular components, which cannot be captured by naked eye examination. Cho et al. previously employed habitat map to quantify spatial heterogeneity in breast cancer by extracting radiomic features (4 first-order, 24 GLCM, and 16 GLSZM features)27. Our study represents the first application of this approach to differentiate ISFTs from ATMs. Notably, the HM incorporated habitat map_GLSZM_GrayLevelNonUniformity as a key feature, which effectively captured the distinctive spatial disorganization patterns among three distinct habitats in ISFTs and ATMs.

The integration of different features to create a fusion model has demonstrated significant advantages across various clinical applications22,23,45. By integrating clinical-radiological factors, radiomic features, and habitat features, we developed the CHRM, which performed significantly better than the other models. This enhanced predictive capability likely stems from the CHRM’s comprehensive assessment of tumor heterogeneity from diverse dimensions. Notably, dural tail sign, as the only clinical-radiological factor included in the CHRM, provided complementary information beyond the VOI-based analyses, in line with a previous study13. Additionally, we applied Hosmer–Lemeshow analysis and DCA curves, and the results suggested that the CHRM not only has better goodness-of-fit but also increased clinical benefit, indicating that the fusion model has significant clinical value for preoperative differentiation between ISFTs and ATMs. This study significantly enhanced the model’s interpretability by SHAP analysis, providing deeper understanding of the model’s internal decision logic. The SHAP analysis identified R-score and H-score as the most influential features contributing to model prediction. The results further highlight the clinical value of CHRM, and underscore the complementary importance of radiomic and habitat features.

There were some limitations in this study. First, this was a single-center retrospective study characterized by a class imbalance between ISFTs and ATMs. Although matching was used to address the class imbalance, it inevitably reduced the sample size. Future prospective studies involving larger, unselected cohorts are warranted to further verify the model’s robustness in diverse clinical settings. Second, the biological interpretation of imaging habitats relied on voxel value correlations. To better characterize the biological significance of these subregions, future work should incorporate precise point-to-point correlation analyses between MRI-defined habitats and histopathological samples. Finally, our current habitat analysis was limited to two conventional MRI sequences. The integration of advanced quantitative functional MRI sequences, such as diffusion MRI and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, may provide additional biological insights and should be explored in subsequent investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the importance of the HM as an effective tool for differentiating ISFTs from ATMs. More importantly, the CHRM, which integrates clinical-radiological, radiomics, and habitat features, demonstrated remarkable performance in preoperative discrimination between these two tumor types.
