Sesquiterpene‐Rich Essential Oil From Magnolia officinalis Stem Bark: Chemical Composition, Biological Activities, and In Silico Approach
Authors: Nguyen Thi Kim Thanh, Phan Hong Minh, Huynh Thi Ngoc Ni, Tran Quang Hai, Le The Hoai, Le Ba Vinh, Pham Minh Quan, Nguyen Dinh Luyen, Ninh The Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe stem bark essential oil of Magnolia officinalis (M. officinalis) Rehder & E.H. Wilson was investigated for its chemical composition, biological activities, and in silico interactions. From the GC–FID/MS analysis, the sample was dominated by sesquiterpene hydrocarbons (85.38% ± 0.04%). The major constituents were (E)‐caryophyllene (21.69% ± 0.020%), α‐selinene (10.13% ± 0.016%), (Z)‐β‐farnesene (7.07% ± 0.011%), β‐selinene (6.81% ± 0.006%), γ‐muurolene (6.15% ± 0.008%), α‐humulene (6.13% ± 0.007%), and δ‐cadinene (5.26% ± 0.006%). Remarkably, this is the first report demonstrating the α‐glucosidase inhibitory activity of M. officinalis stem bark essential oil, which exhibited significant activity (IC50 = 188.98 ± 5.60 µg/mL), exceeding that of the standard compound acarbose (IC50 = 240.06 ± 4.32 µg/mL). In addition, the essential oil possessed cytotoxicity against MCF‐7 and HepG2 cancer cell lines, DPPH radical scavenging, anti‐inflammatory activity against NO and PGE2 productions, and antimicrobial activities. Molecular docking analysis revealed that the major compounds exhibited moderate to strong binding affinities toward α‐glucosidase, with binding energies ranging from –5.6 to –6.9 kcal/mol, comparable to acarbose, and predominantly stabilized by hydrophobic interactions. In addition, γ‐muurolene and δ‐cadinene emerged as the most promising candidates, combining strong binding affinity with optimal ADMET characteristics.Keywords: biological activity, essential oil, in silico, Magnolia officinalis, Magnoliaceae

Introduction

Magnolia officinalis (M. officinalis) Rehder & E.H. Wilson (the family Magnoliaceae) is a flowering plant native to East Asia that has been widely used in traditional Asian medicine for many years [1]. Its dried bark has been officially documented in pharmacopoeias and traditionally used for treatments of gastrointestinal disorders, cough, anxiety, asthma, and cardiovascular conditions due to its diverse range of bioactive metabolites [1].

While phytochemistry and pharmacological research on M. officinalis have largely focused on its abundant lignans, for example, magnolol and honokiol, which demonstrate anticancer, antioxidative, and anti‐inflammatory effects [2, 3]. In addition, there is growing interest in the essential oil fractions of the plant as promising sources of volatile bioactive compounds. Chemically, Magnolia essential oils are mainly composed of monoterpene hydrocarbons, oxygenated monoterpenes, sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, oxygenated sesquiterpenes, and other types, with 1,8‐cineole, linalool, limonene, β‐elemene, β‐eudesmol, β‐pinene, and β‐caryophyllene being the most frequently reported major constituents [4].

For example, the Chinese M. biondii flower bud essential oil exhibited an exceptionally high identified composition of up to 99.91%, dominated by camphor (43.26%) and eucalyptol (38.02%), whereas the Iranian M. grandiflora flower essential oil contained as many as 118 identified components, primarily sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, such as β‐elemene (14.3%) and bicyclogermacrene (10.3%) [5, 6]. Monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes predominated in the Chinese M. officinalis essential oils, with β‐eudesmol (17.40%–40.74%) being the most abundant compound [4]. In addition to their complex chemical profiles, Magnolia essential oils have displayed diverse pharmacological activities, including cytotoxic, antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antimicrobial, antidermatophytic, and nematocidal effects [4]. The Indian M. sirindhorniae flower essential oil showed higher antimicrobial activity against Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus, and it possessed higher free radical scavenging, compared to the bud essential oil [7]. Besides antimicrobial activity, the Korean M. Kobus flower essential oil exhibited anti‐inflammatory effects on nitric oxide (NO, 37.7% inhibition) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2, 24.0% inhibition) production [8].

Given the traditional uses of M. officinalis and the increasing evidence for the biological activities of its essential oils, further phytochemical characterization and pharmacological evaluation are warranted. The stem bark, which is the medicinally used part of M. officinalis in traditional medicine and official pharmacopoeias, remains the principal source of its bioactive constituents. Therefore, the present study focused on the essential oil obtained from the stem bark collected in Vietnam to characterize its chemical composition and evaluate its cytotoxic, antioxidant, α‐glucosidase inhibitory, anti‐inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities. The experimental findings were further complemented by in silico studies to provide mechanistic insights into the observed biological effects.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

The dried stem bark was purchased from the Coc Leu market (22°29'58.8“N and 103°58'03.5”E), Lao Cai, Vietnam, in January, 2026. The co‐author, Dr. Nguyen Thi Kim Thanh, identified the plant material. A voucher specimen (NTS‐2) has been deposited at the Institute of Chemistry, VAST.

Hydrodistillation

M. officinalis air‐dried stem bark was coarsely pulverized and subjected to hydrodistillation using a Clevenger‐type apparatus. In brief, 1.1 kg of dried sample was immersed in 2.5 L of distilled water in a round‐bottom flask and heated to boiling. The distillation was performed for 3.5 h, during which the volatile compounds were vaporized with steam, condensed, and collected in the graduated arm of the apparatus. After that, the essential oil layer was carefully separated from the aqueous phase, dried over Na2SO4 to remove residual moisture, and filtered. The essential oil obtained from three independent hydrodistillation experiments was weighed to determine the yield (0.26% ± 0.01%, w/w, based on the dry weight of the plant material), and stored in amber glass vials at 4°C until further analysis.

GC–FID/MS Analysis

The analysis was carried out using an Agilent Technologies 7890A gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and fitted with an HP‐5MS capillary column (5% phenyl, 95% dimethylpolysiloxane; 30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., film thickness 0.25 µm) [9]. The oven temperature was programmed from 50°C to 250°C at a rate of 3°C/min and held at 250°C for 4 min. The injector and detector temperatures were set to 280°C and 290°C, respectively. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min, and the sample was injected in split mode with a split ratio of 1:10.

GC–MS analysis was performed under the same chromatographic conditions using an Agilent Technologies 7890A GC system coupled to a 5975C mass selective detector (MSD), equipped with the same HP‐5MS capillary column. The MS transfer line temperature was maintained at 280°C. Mass spectra were obtained using electron impact (EI) ionization at 70 eV, with a mass scanning range of m/z 50–550. Identification of the essential oil constituents was accomplished by comparing their mass spectra with those in the HPCH1607 and W09N08 libraries and the NIST Chemistry WebBook (SRD 69), together with comparison of their retention indices and mass spectral data with published values reported by Adams [10, 11]. Retention indices (RI) were calculated relative to a homologous series of n‐alkanes (C7–C30) analyzed under identical experimental conditions and compared with literature values to confirm compound identities. Quantitative analysis was performed in triplicate by electronic integration of GC–FID peak areas and expressed as relative percentages of the total peak area without the use of correction factors.

Cytotoxic Assay

Cytotoxicity of the studied essential oil was evaluated using the 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) colorimetric assay, following a previously reported method [12]. MCF‐7 (RRID: CVCL_0031; human breast adenocarcinoma) and HepG2 (RRID: CVCL_0027; human liver cancer cells) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics (penicillin 100 U/mL and streptomycin 100 µg/mL) and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Only cells in the exponential growth phase were used for the experiments.

For the assay, cells were seeded at a density of 1.5–2.5 × 105 cells/mL and exposed to the oil sample at concentrations of 0.8–250 µg/mL for 72 h. Following treatment, 50 µL of MTT solution (2 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37°C. The plates were then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, after which the supernatant was carefully removed. The formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to each well, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek microplate reader. Antioxidative Assay

The methods are identical to our earlier report [13]. In brief, a 0.15 mM DPPH solution in ethanol (200 µL) was mixed with 1.4 µL of an essential oil solution in 5% DMSO (final concentrations: 5–250 µg/mL) in a 96‐well plate. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. Ascorbic acid served as the positive control. The assay was performed in triplicate. IC50 values were determined using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0.

α‐Glucosidase Inhibitory Assay

α‐Glucosidase inhibitory activity of the studied essential oil was evaluated using α‐glucosidase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), as previously reported protocol with a few modifications [14]. Briefly, the sample solution (2 mL, dissolved in DMSO) was mixed with α‐glucosidase solution (0.5 U/mL, 40 mL) in 120 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The reaction mixture was pre‐incubated at room temperature for 5 min, then 5 mM p‐nitrophenyl‐α‐d‐glucopyranoside (40 mL) was added as the substrate. The mixture was then incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The enzymatic reaction was monitored by measuring the absorbance of the released p‐nitrophenol at 405 nm using a BioTek microplate reader. Acarbose served as the positive control. The assay was performed in triplicate. IC50 values were obtained using GraphPad Prism Version 10.6.0.

Anti‐Inflammatory Assay

NO Inhibitory Assay

RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cells (RRID: CVCL_0493) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Gibco, USA), and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 [15]. For the NO inhibitory assay, cells were cultured in 96‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions. After that, the culture medium was replaced with serum‐free DMEM, and the cells were incubated for 3 h to induce serum starvation. The cells were then pretreated with the essential oil (10–250 µg/mL) for 2 h, followed by stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/mL) for 24 h to induce NO production.

The accumulation of nitrite (NO2 −), a stable metabolite of nitric oxide, in the culture supernatant was quantified using the Griess reaction. Briefly, 100 µL of the culture supernatant was transferred to a new 96‐well plate and mixed with an equal volume (100 µL) of Griess reagent. After incubation at room temperature for 10 min, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a BioTek microplate reader. Wells containing culture medium alone were used as the negative control, while dexamethasone served as the positive control. The assay was performed in triplicate. IC50 values were obtained using GraphPad Prism Version 10.6.0.

PGE2 Inhibitory Assay

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 102 cells/well into 96‐well microplates, and they were incubated for 24 h at 37°C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. After that, 10 µL of essential oil (10–100 µg/mL) was added to the cells [16]. LPS‐containing cells in 10% DMSO were present in control wells, while culture media alone was used as the negative control. LPS (1 µg/mL) was administered to the experimental wells after two hours of pretreatment. To assess basal PGE2 expression, a negative control devoid of LPS was used. Following a 24‐h incubation period, culture supernatants were gathered, and a Mouse PGE‐2 ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) was used to measure PGE2 levels. Indomethacin served as the positive control. The assay was performed in triplicate. IC50 values were obtained using GraphPad Prism Version 10.6.0.

Antimicrobial Assay

Antimicrobial effect of the essential oil was considered using the broth microdilution method against two Gram‐positive bacteria, Bacillus cereus ATCC 11778 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923; two Gram‐negative bacteria, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 9027; and the yeast, Candida albicans ATCC 10231 [17].

The antimicrobial stock solutions were prepared by dissolving the tested oil in 1% DMSO. This solution was then diluted in Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB) to achieve a two‐fold serial dilution series of 250–1000 µg/mL. The bacterial inoculum was standardized to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity and further diluted to reach a final concentration of approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL in each well.

The plates included a positive growth control and a negative sterility control, and were incubated at 35°C for 16–20 h. Ampicillin, tetracycline, and nystatin were used as the standards for the Gram‐positive, Gram‐negative, and yeast, respectively. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was recorded as the lowest concentration of the essential oil that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post hoc test in GraphPad Prism Version 10.6.0. Results are expressed as the mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Molecular Docking and ADMET Studies

The molecular docking simulations were performed following previously reported protocols with a few modifications [18]. The three‐dimensional (3D) structure of the target protein α‐glucosidase (PDB ID: 2QMJ), was derived from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Protein structures were prepared by the addition of polar hydrogen atoms and assigning Gasteiger charges using AutoDockTools. Ligand structures have been constructed and energy‐minimized using the MM2 force field in Chem3D Pro, with the root mean square (RMS) gradient convergence criterion set to 0.01. Grid maps encompassing the active sites‐or, when appropriate, the entire protein surface‐were generated using AutoGrid4, with grid dimensions of 100 × 100 × 80 points along the X, Y, and Z axes, respectively. Docking performance were conducted using AutoDock4, using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm with 50 independent runs and an extended maximum number of energy evaluations to ensure adequate conformational sampling. The resulting binding poses and interaction profiles were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (RRID: SCR_000305) and Discovery Studio (academic licenses).

ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicology) properties of the studied compounds were predicted using the pkCSM online platform. Canonical SMILES of each compound were obtained and submitted to the server for analysis.

The major compounds included (E)‐caryophyllene (21.69 ± 0.020%), α‐selinene (10.13 ± 0.016%), (Z)‐β‐farnesene (7.07 ± 0.011%), β‐selinene (6.81 ± 0.006%), γ‐muurolene (6.15 ± 0.008%), α‐humulene (6.13 ± 0.007%), and δ‐cadinene (5.26 ± 0.006%). Several other compounds were detected exceeding 1.000%, consisting of α‐ylangene, α‐copaene, guaia‐6,9‐diene, 9‐epi‐(E)‐caryophyllene, α‐amorphene, δ‐amorphene, γ‐cadinene, zonarene, caryophyllenyl alcohol, 1,10‐di‐epi‐cubenol, α‐acorenol, epi‐α‐muurolol, and epi‐α‐cadinol.

As mentioned above, β‐eudesmol isomers are available in the Chinese M. officinalis essential oils, whereas (E)‐caryophyllene reached only 5.00% [4]. (E)‐Caryophyllene was also a characteristic compound of various Magnolia essential oils, for example, American M. grandiflora fruits (7.20%–7.90%), seeds (8.80%–19.36%), leaves (15.10%), and flowers (34.80%), Thai M. sirindhorniae flowers (6.40%), and Vietnamese M. coco leaves (6.20%) [4]. The current study aligns with previous studies and suggests that Magnolia essential oils are a good source of sesquiterpene derivatives, especially (E)‐caryophyllene.

Cytotoxic Activity

The essential oil obtained from the stem bark of M. officinalis was evaluated for its cytotoxic activity against MCF‐7 and HepG2 cancer cell lines. At the highest concentration of 250 µg/mL, the sample exhibited strong cytotoxic activity, with inhibition percentages of 56.90% ± 3.28% for MCF‐7 cells and 53.28% ± 1.56% for HepG2 cells (Figure 2). When the concentration decreased to 100 µg/mL, the inhibition rates were reduced to 33.93% ± 2.52% and 33.69 ± 2.67% for MCF‐7 and HepG2 cells, respectively. At 20 µg/mL, inhibitory effects were observed, corresponding to 9.51% ± 0.39% for MCF‐7 and 9.22% ± 0.29% for HepG2 cells. Lower concentrations of 4 µg/mL resulted in minimal inhibition (2.15% ± 0.21% for MCF‐7 and 2.02% ± 0.22% for HepG2), while the lowest concentration tested (0.8 µg/mL) showed negligible cytotoxicity, with inhibition values of 0.49% ± 0.13% and 0.36% ± 0.35%, respectively.

As shown in Table 2, the essential oil displayed moderate cytotoxicity, with IC50 values of 188.31 ± 5.6 µg/mL for MCF‐7 cells and 202.60 ± 4.7 µg/mL for HepG2 cells. In comparison, the standard compound ellipticine exhibited markedly stronger cytotoxicity, with IC50 values of 2.77 ± 0.34 µg/mL and 2.89 ± 0.12 µg/mL against MCF‐7 and HepG2 cells, respectively. The current result aligns with various previous reports on Magnolia essential oils. The leaf essential oils from M. bidoupensis and M. tiepii collected from Vietnam showed strong cytotoxicity against four cancer cell lines KB, A549, HepG2, and MCF7, with IC50 values of 0.90‐2.40 µg/Ml [19, 20]. The essential oil from M. grandiflora flowers cultivated in Iran was also reported to inhibit the growth of A375, MDA‐MB‐231, and T98 G cancer cells [6].

Antioxidant Activity

M. officinalis stem bark essential oil was then considered for its antioxidative potential in DPPH radical scavenging. At the highest tested dose of 250 µg/mL, the sample exhibited a strong scavenging effect (79.41% ± 0.75%) (Figure 3), which gradually reduced to 60.43% ± 1.83% at 100 µg/mL and 43.77% ± 1.86% at 50 µg/mL. A further reduction has been observed at lower concentrations, reaching 26.87% ± 1.05%, 10.91% ± 2.14%, and 6.17% ± 1.21% at 25, 10, and 5 µg/mL, respectively. As a consequence, the sample exhibited good antioxidative activity with an IC50 value of 67.24 ± 5.23 µg/mL, compared to that of the standard compound ascorbic acid (IC50 = 67.24 ± 5.23 µg/mL) (Table 2).

DPPH radical scavenging (left) and α‐glucosidase inhibitory (right) activities of M. officinalis stem bark essential oil. Mean ± SD, n value = 3, p < 0.05 versus the non‐treated group (0% inhibition).

The antioxidative activity observed in this study is comparable to that of other Magnolia essential oils. For instance, M. sirindhorniae bud essential oil exhibited moderate antioxidant activity, with DPPH scavenging values typically about 77% at the concentration of 100 µg/mL [7]. The observed antioxidant activity may be attributed to the high abundance of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, in particular (E)‐caryophyllene, (Z)‐β‐farnesene, γ‐muurolene, α‐humulene, δ‐cadinene, and selinene derivatives, which were responsible for the antioxidant effects of various natural essential oils [21].

α‐Glucosidase Inhibitory Activity

The α‐glucosidase inhibitory activity demonstrated a clear concentration‐dependent pattern. At the highest tested concentration of 500 µg/mL, the sample showed strong inhibitory activity (73.31% ± 2.07%), which decreased to 54.94% ± 3.19% and 35.36% ± 1.73% at 250 and 100 µg/mL, respectively (Figure 3). Further decreases in inhibitory action were observed at lower concentrations, reaching 20.78% ± 0.96%, 11.74% ± 0.33%, and 5.06% ± 0.25% at 50, 25, and 10 µg/mL, respectively. As a result, the oil sample exerted an IC50 value of 188.98 ± 5.60 µg/mL, which was lower than that of the standard compound acarbose (IC50 = 240.06 ± 4.32 µg/mL) (Table 2).

It noted that this is the first time for the application of M. officinalis stem bark essential oil against α‐glucosidase enzyme. Honokiol, which was one of the major compounds of M. officinalis, acted as a reversible mixed‐type inhibitor of α‐glucosidase with an IC50 value of 317.11 ± 12.86 µM [22]. Another major compound, namely magnolol, displayed striking features in antidiabetic experiments, improving insulin tolerance and normalizing glucose homeostasis [23]. Furthermore, it established beneficial activities in diabetic cataract, retinopathy, and nephropathy [23]. From these findings, M. officinalis can be reasonably considered a promising natural source of α‐glucosidase inhibitors, with potential applicability in the management of type 2 diabetes and its related complications.

Anti‐Inflammatory Activity

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the progression of numerous chronic diseases, and excessive production of inflammatory mediators such as NO and PGE2 [15, 16]. Therefore, suppression of these processes in LPS‐stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages is widely used as a significant method for evaluating anti‐inflammatory potential.

In the present study, M. officinalis stem bark essential oil exhibited a pronounced inhibitory effect on NO production in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 4). At 250 µg/mL, the essential oil remarkably suppressed NO release by 77.7% ± 3.18%, compared to the LPS‐treated control, whereas the moderate inhibitory levels of about 58% and 41% were observed at 100 and 50 µg/mL, respectively. At lower concentrations (≤ 25 µg/mL), the inhibitory effect was weak (13.42%–26.13%), indicating a clear dose–response relationship. Importantly, the reduction in NO production was not associated with excessive cytotoxicity, as cell viability remained above 92.1%–95.8% (Figure 4), suggesting that the observed NO inhibition was not merely a consequence of macrophage death. The corresponding IC50 value for NO inhibition was 69.42 ± 4.58 µg/mL (Table 2), although less potent than the reference anti‐inflammatory drug dexamethasone (IC50 = 16.53 ± 1.45 µg/mL), it still reflects a moderate and biologically relevant inhibitory capacity.

The essential oil also reduced PGE2 production in LPS‐stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. As shown in Figure 4, treatment with the oil at 100 µg/mL resulted in a PGE2 inhibition of 49.17% ± 1.85%, which decreased to around 32% at 50 µg/mL and below 21% at concentrations ≤ 25 µg/mL. By means of this, the IC50 value for PGE2 inhibition was determined to be > 100 µg/mL (Table 2), indicating a weaker effect.

The stronger inhibition of NO production compared to PGE2 suggested that the essential oil, containing rich sesquiterpenes, might target the iNOS/NO pathway rather than the COX‐2/PGE2 axis [8, 21]. This selective effect may be attributed to the high content of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, which are known to suppress iNOS expression and NO release, while exerting weaker inhibition on COX‐2 catalytic activity. A similar observation was made when treated with M. Kobus flower essential oil [8].

Antimicrobial Activity

In the final assay, the oil sample was evaluated for its antimicrobial potential. The essential oil exhibited the strongest activity against the Gram‐positive bacterium B. cereus, with a MIC value of 250 µg/mL, followed by moderate activity against S. aureus and the yeast C. albicans, both showing MIC values of 500 µg/mL. However, these activities were markedly weaker than those of the standard compounds, as ampicillin showed MIC values of 8 µg/mL against the Gram‐positive bacteria, and nystatin exhibited a MIC value of 8 µg/mL against C. albicans. In contrast, the essential oil displayed weak or inactive activity against the Gram‐negative bacteria E. coli and P. aeruginosa (MIC ≥ 1000 µg/mL), whereas the standard compound tetracycline effectively inhibited these strains with MIC values of 4 µg/mL.

Information on the antimicrobial potential of M. officinalis essential oils remains limited. Nevertheless, the present findings are in good agreement with previous reports, confirming that Magnolia essential oils exhibit appreciable antibacterial activity. As an example, the essential oils from the Vietnamese M. hypolampra aerial parts were reported to inhibit a panel of pathogenic bacteria B. subtilis, S. aureus, E. coli, C. albicans, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Lactobacillus fermentum, and Salmonella enterica, with MIC values of 2.0–8.2 mg/mL [24]. The Korean M. kobus flower essential oil showed strong antibacterial activity, particularly against S. aureus (inhibition zone: 18 mm; MIC: 0.25 mg/mL). It also inhibited P. aeruginosa and E. coli, with inhibition zones of 14 and 17 mm and MIC values of 1 and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively [8].

Molecular Docking

The major constituents of M. officinalis stem bark essential oil, namely (E)‐caryophyllene (1), (Z)‐β‐farnesene (2), α‐humulene (3), γ‐muurolene (4), β‐selinene (5), α‐selinene (6), and δ‐cadinene (7), were selected for molecular docking analysis because they are the predominant components. The docking study was performed to explore their potential interactions with the α‐glucosidase active site and to provide a possible explanation for the observed α‐glucosidase inhibitory activity of the essential oil. In the meantime, α‐glucosidase (PDB ID: 2QMJ) was selected as the molecular docking target due to its critical role in the hydrolysis of oligosaccharides into glucose, a key step in carbohydrate digestion and postprandial blood glucose regulation [25].

Molecular docking revealed clear variations in the binding affinities among the investigated compounds, with binding energies ranging from –5.6 to –6.9 kcal/mol. Among them, compound 4 (–6.9 kcal/mol) exhibited the strongest binding affinity, which was comparable to that of the standard acarbose (–6.9 kcal/mol), followed by compound 7 (–6.7 kcal/mol) and compound 5 (–6.3 kcal/mol). These compounds formed dense hydrophobic interactions (alkyl and π–alkyl) with key non‐polar residues such as Leu, Val, Ile, and Phe, along with extensive van der Waals contacts, indicating favorable accommodation within the enzyme's hydrophobic pocket (Figure 5).

The 2D and 3D interaction images of the major compounds 1–7 and the standard acarbose docked with the α‐glucosidase protein target PDB ID: 2QMJ.

Compounds 1 and 6 (–6.0 kcal/mol) displayed moderate binding affinities, characterized by fewer alkyl interactions and less optimal van der Waals stabilization, suggesting a weaker interaction network within the active site. Meanwhile, compound 2 (–5.8 kcal/mol) and particularly compound 3 (–5.6 kcal/mol) showed the lowest binding energies, mainly relying on weak van der Waals forces and limited hydrophobic contacts, reflecting their less stable binding conformations. All compounds 1–7 lacked hydrogen bonding interactions due to their nonpolar sesquiterpene structures, and their bindings were predominantly governed by hydrophobic interactions. This behavior is in contrast to acarbose, which formed multiple hydrogen bonds with polar residues such as Asp, Glu, and Arg, enabling strong and specific binding within the catalytic site.

ADMET Profile

ADMET profiles (Table 3) showed clear differences between compounds 1–7 and acarbose. All compounds exhibited low molecular weight (∼204 Da) and moderate lipophilicity (LogP = 4.58–5.20), favoring membrane permeability, whereas acarbose displayed high polarity (TPSA) = 329.01 Å2) and poor lipophilicity (LogP = –8.72). Accordingly, compounds 1–7 demonstrated excellent absorption [HIA = 1.00; oral bioavailability = 0.69–0.93] and higher Caco‐2 permeability compared to acarbose (HIA = 0.003; oral bioavailability = 0.04).

In addition, compounds 1–7 showed high BBB penetration and strong plasma protein binding, indicating efficient distribution, while acarbose remained poorly distributed. Metabolically, all compounds exhibited low CYP inhibition, suggesting a low risk of drug‐drug interactions. Toxicity predictions indicated acceptable safety profiles, with low risks of hERG inhibition, mutagenicity, and DILI.

Overall, compared to acarbose, compounds 1–7 possessed superior pharmacokinetic properties, particularly in terms of absorption and bioavailability. Notably, compounds 4 and 7 combine favorable ADMET characteristics with strong docking affinity, suggesting their key contribution to the observed biological activity.

Conclusion

In this study, M. officinalis stem bark essential oil was comprehensively characterized in terms of chemical composition, biological activities, and in silico behavior. GC‐FID/MS analysis revealed a sesquiterpene‐rich profile, with (E)‐caryophyllene, (Z)‐β‐farnesene, α‐humulene, γ‐muurolene, β‐selinene, α‐selinene, and δ‐cadinene as the dominant constituents. The essential oil exhibited moderate cytotoxic effects against MCF‐7 and HepG2 cancer cells, along with DPPH radical scavenging activity. Significantly, the essential oil showed significant α‐glucosidase inhibitory activity, surpassing the reference compound acarbose, representing the first report on the antidiabetic potential of M. officinalis stem bark essential oil. In addition, the oil sample exhibited marked anti‐inflammatory effects, characterized by stronger inhibition of NO production than PGE2 production, suggesting a preferential iNOS/NO pathway. Antimicrobial assay revealed selective activity, particularly against the Gram‐positive bacteria and the yeast C. albicans, while the Gram‐negative strains were less susceptible.

The in‐silico studies further supported the experimental findings by providing mechanistic insights into the interactions between major oil constituents and key biological targets, reinforcing the relevance of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons to the observed activities. Overall, M. officinalis stem bark essential oil represents a promising natural source of multifunctional bioactive compounds, with useful applications in antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and antidiabetic contexts. Nevertheless, future studies should include cytotoxic evaluation on appropriate normal cell lines to assess the safety of the essential oil. The isolation of active constituents, detailed mechanistic investigations, and in vivo evaluations, are required.
