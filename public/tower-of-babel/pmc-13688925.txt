Climate variability and the emerging risks of foodborne pathogens: consequences for food safety and population health
Authors: Desalegn Amenu, Waktole Gobena, Reda Nemo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is increasingly recognized as key driver shaping the global epidemiology of foodborne infections, influencing pathogen dynamics, toxin production, and food system safety. This review aimed to (1) examine the impact of climate change on the occurrence, growth, and geographic distribution of foodborne pathogens; (2) assess its influence on toxin production and chemical contamination in food systems; (3) evaluate disruptions across the food value chain, including production, processing, storage, and distribution; and (4) explore population vulnerability, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, and implications for surveillance and control strategies. A narrative review approach was used to synthesize published literature on climate-sensitive foodborne hazards, including bacterial, viral, parasitic, and toxin-producing microorganisms, as well as chemical contaminants, in relation to temperature rise, precipitation changes, and extreme weather events. Rising temperatures enhance the growth and survival of major foodborne pathogens such as Salmonella and Campylobacter, while also expanding the geographic distribution of marine-associated Vibrio species. Changes in temperature and humidity increase fungal growth and mycotoxin contamination, including aflatoxins, in major food commodities. Extreme weather events such as flooding contribute to the mobilization of chemical hazards, including pesticides and heavy metals, in food and water systems. Climate change also influences pathogen virulence and toxin production, increasing overall hazard potential. In addition, climate variability disrupts the food value chain. Water scarcity reduces agricultural productivity and irrigation capacity, while increased temperatures and weak infrastructure compromise cold chain systems during processing, storage, and distribution, particularly in low-resource settings. Climate change is a multifaceted driver of food safety risks through interconnected environmental, biological, and socio-economic pathways. Integrating climate data into foodborne disease surveillance, strengthening monitoring systems, and developing climate-resilient food safety strategies are essential to reduce emerging risks and protect public health.Keywords: Climate change, Foodborne pathogens, Food safety, Public health, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

Climate change is a powerful global driver that is restructuring environmental, agricultural, and food system processes with intensive human health implications [1]. The changing temperatures, altered rainfall, and increased extremes of weather in forms of flooding, heatwaves, and storms are restructuring ecosystems and the interaction of humans with pathogens [2, 3]. Among these, one includes those on foodborne pathogens like bacteria, viruses, and parasites infecting food and water to cause illnesses running from mild gastroenteritis to severe systemic disease. Foodborne illness causes an estimated 600 million cases of illness and 420,000 deaths annually, being highly disproportionate among children under the age of five years [4].

These include classical bacterial pathogens such as Salmonella spp., Escherichia coli, Campylobacter jejuni/coli, Clostridium perfringens, and Staphylococcus aureus; viral pathogens like Human norovirus and Hepatitis A virus; and parasites like Cryptosporidium and Giardia, which are responsible for a great deal of foodborne disease [5–7]. Climatic change can affect the growth, survival, geographical range, and seasonality of such pathogens. Increasement in temperature might hatch Salmonella in food items, while excess rainfall and consequently flooding causes cross-contamination of E. coli or Salmonella from infected water or soils to fruit and vegetable crops used in agriculture [8–11]. In addition, an increased sea surface temperature may be able to transport algal biotoxins and Vibrio species to new locations. There is also an added threat in seafood supply chains [12, 13].

Climate change is increasingly recognized as a critical driver of foodborne disease risk through its effects on pathogen ecology and food system pathways. Changes in temperature, precipitation, and extreme weather events influence pathogen survival, growth, and transmission, while simultaneously disrupting key components of the food supply chain, including cold-chain integrity, storage conditions, and the timeliness of food processing. In addition, climate variability contributes to increased risks of mycotoxin contamination particularly from fungi such as Aspergillus flavus and enhances the mobilization and uptake of heavy metals in crops [9, 14]. The risk of foodborne diseases arises from complex interactions among environmental changes, food system infrastructure, human behavior, and socioeconomic conditions. These risks are disproportionately higher in low- and middle-income countries, where limitations in surveillance systems, regulatory frameworks, and mitigation capacity constrain effective response [15].

Therefore, the intent of this review is to examine the impacts of climate variability on the behavior, distribution, and transmission of foodborne pathogens and their implications in the global arena of food safety and public health. In this regard, it is the intent of this review to: (i) examine the susceptibility of foodborne pathogens to climate variability drivers; (ii) examine the impacts of climate variability in the food chain; (iii) identify the knowledge gaps in climate variability and foodborne pathogens; and (iv) examine the adaptation and mitigation strategies of climate variability in food safety and public health using the One Health approach. Climate variability and risks of foodborne pathogens

Temperature, humidity, and microbial growth

Temperature increases and humidity variability are the major environmental factors that influence the distribution and occurrence of foodborne pathogens. Rising temperature enhances microbial growth and prolongs pathogen survival in food, water, and soil, thereby increasing the risk of foodborne disease outbreaks [16, 17]. For instance, it has been shown to trigger outbreaks caused by foodborne pathogens such as Salmonella enterica and Campylobacter jejuni/coli during warm temperatures. This leads to earlier onset and longer duration of foodborne outbreaks [18]. High humidity levels increase pathogen survival on fresh produce surfaces and in soil and irrigation water. High rainfall is associated with elevated levels of E. coli and Salmonella spp. in food and water, whereas drought conditions lead to concentration of foodborne pathogens in limited water sources, increasing contamination risk [19].

The expansion of marine pathogens such as Vibrio spp. into temperate regions due to ocean warming is a potential threat to populations that have not previously been exposed to these pathogens [20, 21]. Similarly, fungal pathogens such as Aspergillus flavus exhibit higher growth rates under elevated temperature and humidity conditions, leading to increased aflatoxin contamination in staple foods such as maize and groundnuts, particularly in regions with limited storage facilities [22]. Although high-income countries can mitigate these risks through strong monitoring systems and food safety interventions, low- and middle-income countries remain highly vulnerable due to limited infrastructure and adaptive capacity [9, 10]. Overall, recognizing these environmental drivers is essential for predictive modeling, development of targeted interventions, and strengthening public health preparedness, thereby directly supporting the objectives of this review.

Extreme weather and water system Disruptions

Extreme weather events, such as floods, droughts, and storm surges, significantly influence the dissemination of foodborne pathogens and contamination of food and water systems. Flooding mobilizes agricultural and urban waste into water bodies and distribution systems, thereby facilitating the spread of foodborne pathogens such as E. coli and Salmonella, and increasing the risk of outbreaks in affected regions [23, 24]. In contrast, drought conditions lead to water scarcity and increased reliance on untreated or unsafe water for irrigation and food processing, thereby increasing contamination risks [25, 26].

Sea-level rise and storm surges create brackish coastal environments that promote the growth of halophilic pathogens such as Vibrio cholerae and Vibrio vulnificus, thereby expanding the geographic range of seafood-associated diseases. In addition, extreme weather events can disrupt critical food safety infrastructure, particularly cold-chain systems, which facilitates microbial growth in perishable foods [26]. Changes in precipitation patterns, flooding, and drought have cascading effects on water availability, crop productivity, and pathogen ecology, ultimately increasing overall food safety risks through multiple interconnected pathways. Therefore, the integration of mitigation strategies such as climate-adaptive food safety management, resilient food processing systems, improved wastewater treatment, and early warning systems is crucial for reducing food contamination risks and strengthening food system resilience [27].

Shifting geographic and ecological gradients

Climate change is affecting the distribution and ecological niches of foodborne and waterborne pathogens. For instance, Vibrio species are normally found in tropical and subtropical waters; however, their prevalence is increasingly being reported in temperate regions due to rising sea surface temperatures and prolonged warm seasons [28, 29]. Changes in temperature and salinity, which are consequences of climate change—particularly due to sea-level rise, flooding, and coastal environmental changes—are enabling the proliferation of halophilic bacteria such as Vibrio cholerae and Vibrio vulnificus in new habitats [30]. As a result, cases of Vibrio vulnificus infection in North America have increased nearly eightfold over the past three decades [31].

Microbial ecology and vector–pathogen interactions are also influenced by multiple factors, including hosts, competing microbial species, and environmental conditions that affect pathogen survival [32]. For example, hot and humid climates favor the transmission of enteric pathogens such as Salmonella, Campylobacter, and Shigella, which are responsible for diarrheal diseases globally [4, 33]. In addition, globalization and international trade facilitate the spread of foodborne and aquatic pathogens beyond their endemic regions, exposing immunologically naïve populations and creating new “frontier zones” for emerging infectious diseases [34]. Therefore, strengthening surveillance systems using a One Health approach is essential for monitoring ecological hotspots, tracking pathogen movement, and reducing emerging risks [35, 36].

Trends in food systems, trade, production and processing

Climate change affects food safety risks across the entire food chain, including production, processing, and distribution. Climate variability and extreme weather events may force farmers to use alternative or recycled irrigation water that is insufficiently treated or untreated, leading to contamination of fresh produce with pathogens such as Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria [37]. In addition, heat stress in livestock affects gut microbiota and immune function, resulting in increased bacterial shedding into the environment [8]. The risks are further amplified by global food trade, which facilitates the movement of contaminated food products across regions. For instance, imported fruits and vegetables from tropical regions may have a higher likelihood of carrying pathogens compared to locally produced food, despite existing food safety regulations [18].

Emerging production systems such as hydroponic, vertical, and urban farming provide opportunities for climate-resilient and resource-efficient food production, while reducing pressure on land and water resources [38, 39]. However, the use of recycled nutrient-rich water in hydroponic systems may increase the risk of microbial growth, including opportunistic pathogens such as Pseudomonas and Listeria, if proper disinfection and hygiene practices are not implemented [40, 41].

Post-harvest handling and storage are also highly sensitive to climate-driven fluctuations in temperature and humidity. Even minor disruptions in cold-chain integrity can significantly increase microbial proliferation in perishable foods, thereby elevating infection risks from pathogens such as Salmonella and E. coli [17, 42], as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Human behaviour, vulnerability and socioeconomic factors

The level of foodborne disease risk in the context of climate change is largely determined by human behavior, household practices, and socioeconomic conditions. Climate change is expected to increase ambient temperatures, which in turn accelerates microbial growth and leads to faster food spoilage and increased contamination risk. In low- and middle-income countries, foodborne disease transmission is strongly linked to poor sanitation, unsafe water sources, and inadequate food storage systems, which collectively increase exposure to foodborne pathogens [43, 44].

In high-income countries, foodborne disease risks under climate change are more closely associated with consumer behavior and dietary patterns, particularly the high demand for fresh, minimally processed, and ready-to-eat foods. Under warming conditions, microorganisms such as Salmonella enterica and Listeria monocytogenes can proliferate more rapidly in refrigerated and ready-to-eat products, including salads and minimally processed foods, thereby increasing the likelihood of foodborne illness outbreaks [45, 46].

Vulnerability to foodborne disease is not evenly distributed across populations. Neonates, young children, older adults, pregnant women, and immunocompromised individuals are at higher risk due to weaker immune responses. In addition, individuals with low socioeconomic status are more vulnerable because they often lack access to safe drinking water, adequate sanitation, reliable refrigeration, and healthcare services. As a result, these groups experience a disproportionately higher burden of foodborne disease under climate stress.

Therefore, reducing foodborne disease risk requires addressing both environmental drivers and underlying socioeconomic inequalities. Strengthening food safety practices, improving infrastructure, and enhancing public awareness are essential components of adaptation strategies. Impact evidence: foodborne disease, pathogens and global warming

Climate change is increasingly recognized as one of the main drivers of foodborne diseases, interacting with environmental conditions that regulate the survival, persistence, and transmission of pathogens within food webs. Global warming, altered precipitation patterns, increased humidity, and climate extremes exert selective pressure on bacterial, viral, and parasitic pathogens, resulting in spatially and temporally heterogeneous risks. These climatic changes are further amplified by globalized food supply chains, sanitation disparities, and socioeconomic inequalities, which disproportionately affect low- and middle-income countries [2, 3]. In line with the objectives of this review, this section synthesizes evidence on how climate variability influences the burden, distribution, and outbreak dynamics of key foodborne pathogens.

Bacterial pathogens: ecology, climate sensitivity, and outbreak dynamics

The sensitivity of bacterial pathogens to climate variability is largely attributed to their rapid growth rates and strong responsiveness to environmental conditions. Temperature, precipitation, humidity, and weather extremes are critical determinants of bacterial survival and proliferation across the food chain, including animal reservoirs, environmental sources, fresh produce, and processed foods [18, 56]. Globally, bacterial pathogens are estimated to cause approximately 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths annually.

Salmonella spp

Salmonella enterica is one of the most common causes of gastroenteritis worldwide, responsible for approximately 78–94 million cases and 59,000–155,000 deaths annually [57]. While traditionally associated with poultry, eggs, and meat, it is now increasingly linked to fresh produce. Climate factors such as temperature play a significant role in transmission dynamics. An increase of 1 °C in weekly ambient temperature is associated with an 8–10% increase in salmonellosis cases in the UK [3, 15]. Heatwaves further enhance risk by reducing environmental die-off rates and increasing bacterial survival in food systems [18]. Similarly, rainfall and flooding facilitate transmission by mobilizing pathogens from soil and fecal sources into water systems and irrigation networks [14].

Salmonella enterica includes typhoidal serovars (e.g., S. Typhi, S. Paratyphi) causing systemic enteric fever (~14–27 million cases annually), and non-typhoidal serovars (e.g., S. Typhimurium, S. Enteritidis) responsible for ~95 million gastroenteritis cases globally, highlighting distinct clinical and epidemiological profiles [58, 59].

In Ethiopia, warmer months are associated with increased Salmonella incidence due to enhanced bacterial survival and increased human exposure linked to climatic conditions [60]. Additionally, multi-drug-resistant Salmonella strains have been increasingly detected in flooded environments, particularly with resistance to fluoroquinolones and beta-lactams, suggesting a link between climate stress and antimicrobial resistance dynamics [61–63].

Campylobacter jejuni/coli

Campylobacter jejuni/coli causes approximately 95 million gastroenteritis cases annually (WHO, 2020) and is highly sensitive to climatic conditions. Increased temperature and rainfall enhance bacterial survival in both animal reservoirs and aquatic environments. Predictive models suggest that cases in Northern Europe may double by the 2080s under continued climate change scenarios [64, 65]. Seasonal peaks are consistently observed during warmer months. In Ethiopia, higher prevalence is reported during rainy seasons in both poultry and human populations, indicating climate-driven amplification of transmission pathways [60]. Furthermore, Campylobacter spp. show increasing antimicrobial resistance, particularly to fluoroquinolones and macrolides, raising serious public health concerns in both human and animal sectors [66, 67].

Escherichia coli (EPEC and EHEC)

Pathogenic diarrheagenic Escherichia coli, particularly enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), and Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC/VTEC), are major causes of food- and waterborne disease. These strains are estimated to cause approximately 2.8 million cases of illness annually, mainly through contaminated food and water pathways [57]. Flooding and heavy rainfall contribute to widespread dissemination of livestock-associated fecal contamination into water and crops, while drought conditions may concentrate pathogens in limited water supplies, increasing exposure risk. Elevated temperatures further enhance bacterial survival in irrigation water and on produce surfaces [68].

Seasonal variations in meat production in Canada have demonstrated correlations between E. coli prevalence and temperature and precipitation patterns [69]. In addition, environmental isolates resistant to β-lactams and other antibiotics are increasingly reported, particularly in regions with poor wastewater treatment and water scarcity, highlighting the intersection of climate stress and antimicrobial resistance [70].

Vibrio parahaemolyticus and Bacillus cereus

Beyond enteric bacteria, Vibrio parahaemolyticus and Bacillus cereus represent important climate-sensitive foodborne pathogens. Vibrio parahaemolyticus, a halophilic marine bacterium, is a major cause of seafood-associated gastroenteritis. Its outbreaks are strongly associated with rising sea surface temperatures, air temperature, precipitation, and humidity, with peak incidence during warmer months when coastal environmental conditions favor bacterial proliferation [71, 72]. Predictive models have demonstrated that environmental variables can be used to forecast periods of elevated seafood contamination risk [72].

Bacillus cereus is a spore-forming bacterium capable of producing emetic and diarrheal toxins. It persists across diverse food matrices, including rice, cereals, dairy, and ready-to-eat foods [73, 74]. Its spores exhibit strong environmental resilience, and epidemiological evidence indicates that outbreaks are closely linked to temperature and improper storage conditions, reinforcing the role of climate and handling practices in transmission dynamics [74].

Viral pathogens: climate influences on transmission and outbreaks

Foodborne viruses such as Norovirus, Hepatitis A virus (HAV), and Rotavirus are highly sensitive to environmental conditions and human activities, both of which are increasingly influenced by climate change. These pathogens represent a major global public health concern. Norovirus is the leading cause of viral gastroenteritis worldwide, accounting for an estimated 685 million cases and approximately 200,000 deaths annually [57]. HAV and Rotavirus together contribute to tens of millions of infections globally, particularly among children under five years of age. In Ethiopia, although limited data are available, seasonal peaks of viral gastroenteritis are consistently observed during the rainy season [8]. Rotavirus incidence is also higher in children during warm and wet months, consistent with patterns reported in other regions.

Climate change plays a critical role in shaping viral disease dynamics. Heavy rainfall and flooding can contaminate drinking water sources and shellfish harvesting areas, thereby increasing the risk of viral transmission. In addition, high ambient temperatures may enhance viral persistence on food surfaces and in the environment [15, 75]. For example, combined high temperature and humidity conditions may increase HAV stability in irrigation water and recreational water environments, thereby facilitating transmission.

Outbreaks of viral diseases have been linked to such climatic conditions. Norovirus outbreaks are frequently associated with heavy rainfall events in temperate regions, while HAV outbreaks have been reported following flooding in shellfish harvesting areas. Although viral pathogens do not exhibit antimicrobial resistance (AMR), climate-driven disruptions in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure can amplify outbreak risk and increase the likelihood of co-infections with antimicrobial-resistant bacterial pathogens.

In addition to classical viral pathogens, emerging viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, H5N1 influenza virus, and monkeypox virus have been assessed in food safety risk evaluations within the context of climate change and emerging infectious diseases [76–78]. Although SARS-CoV-2 is primarily transmitted via respiratory routes, post-emergence risk assessments investigated its potential persistence on food surfaces and under cold-chain conditions, where viral stability may be prolonged [77, 79, 80]. Similarly, highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) has been evaluated in relation to food and environmental safety due to concerns about contaminated animal products and climate-sensitive ecological changes [81, 82].

Parasitic pathogens: climate, water, and foodborne transmission

Foodborne parasites such as Giardia lamblia, Cryptosporidium spp., Toxoplasma gondii, and Cyclospora cayetanensis are important causes of diarrheal disease globally and are primarily transmitted through contaminated food and water. Cryptosporidium species alone are responsible for approximately 48 million diarrheal cases annually, with the highest burden occurring among children under five years in low- and middle-income countries [83]. Giardia lamblia and Cyclospora cayetanensis also contribute substantially to global morbidity. In Ethiopia, hospital- and community-based studies indicate that infections with Giardia and Cryptosporidium show clear seasonal patterns, with diarrheal incidence increasing by approximately 30–40% during the rainy season compared to dry periods [84, 85].

Climate-sensitive environmental conditions strongly influence parasitic transmission. Heavy rainfall and flooding facilitate the transport of parasite cysts and oocysts from soil and livestock waste into water systems, whereas drought conditions concentrate pathogens in limited water sources, increasing exposure risk [18, 68]. In addition, warm and humid conditions prolong the survival of Toxoplasma gondii oocysts in soil and on fresh produce, while Cyclospora cayetanensis sporulation is accelerated under warm and wet conditions, enhancing infectivity.

Evidence from Ethiopia indicates that cryptosporidiosis cases may increase by 30–40% during peak rainy seasons [86]. Similar patterns have been reported globally, including flood-associated Cryptosporidium outbreaks in Europe and produce-linked Cyclospora outbreaks in North America following unusually warm and wet climatic conditions. Although parasitic pathogens are not directly associated with antimicrobial resistance, climate-driven contamination events may increase co-exposure to antibiotic-resistant bacterial pathogens in food and water systems, complicating clinical management and outbreak control.

Global and higher-income country contexts

Moreover, climate change is increasingly recognized as a significant driver of foodborne illness in high-income (developed) countries as well as low- and middle-income settings. These countries are not exempt from climate-related impacts, despite advanced food safety infrastructure and regulatory systems. Global warming is altering weather patterns and disrupting global food supply chains, thereby increasing system vulnerability to contamination risks. These changes influence the growth, survival, and transmission of foodborne pathogens.

Higher temperatures may also extend the breeding seasons of vectors such as mosquitoes, thereby increasing the risk of vector-borne diseases such as West Nile virus and dengue fever. In addition, extreme weather events, including floods and droughts, can compromise food safety by damaging storage facilities, disrupting cold chains, and impairing food distribution systems. Awad et al. [2] highlighted that climate change contributes to the emergence and spread of new and re-emerging foodborne pathogens.

Many foodborne pathogens include thermophilic microorganisms with optimal growth temperatures ranging between 20 and 45 °C. As global temperatures rise, the geographical distribution of these pathogens is expected to expand, leading to the emergence of foodborne disease outbreaks in previously unaffected regions. Furthermore, many high-income countries are net importers of food from climate-vulnerable regions. This global trade network increases exposure to diverse and complex pathogen profiles, thereby elevating the risk of introducing emerging foodborne pathogens into new populations. Climate-related disruptions in transportation and storage further exacerbate contamination risks and outbreak potential.

Burden and projections in public health

The World Health Organization (WHO) projects a substantial increase in climate-related health burdens in the coming decades. By the 2030s, an estimated additional 250,000 deaths annually are expected due to climate change, primarily linked to heat stress, malaria, and coastal flooding. Although foodborne diseases are not isolated in this estimate, they are expected to contribute significantly to the overall climate-sensitive disease burden.

In addition, WHO estimates indicate that unsafe food currently causes approximately 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths annually worldwide. However, these figures are considered conservative due to widespread underreporting in many regions. Climate change and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events are expected to further amplify these burdens by enhancing pathogen survival, transmission, and exposure pathways.

Public health and food safety implications

Changing risk profiles

Global warming, altered precipitation patterns, and increased climate variability are reshaping the distribution, seasonality, and transmission dynamics of foodborne pathogens. Evidence shows that Salmonella infections increase with rising ambient temperatures, demonstrating a clear temperature–pathogen relationship [87, 88]. Similarly, Norovirus outbreaks have been associated with climatic instability, including extreme winter conditions and heavy rainfall events [89].

Climate variability enhances microbial growth rates, reduces pathogen lag phases, and prolongs survival in environmental reservoirs. These combined effects reduce safety margins during food storage, processing, and transportation [14, 90]. In addition, globalization of food supply chains increases the risk of transboundary contamination, as food products are transported across continents. Therefore, strengthened surveillance systems and dynamic risk assessment frameworks are required to manage these evolving risks [91, 92].

Furthermore, floods, hurricanes, and droughts facilitate pathogen dissemination by increasing contamination pathways from soil and water into food production and processing environments [3, 93]. Overall, climate change necessitates the revision of traditional hazard mapping approaches and the development of predictive models for early risk identification and mitigation [47].

Socioeconomic and public health impacts

Foodborne diseases linked to climate change impose a substantial public health burden globally. Approximately 600 million people are affected annually by foodborne pathogens, with children under five experiencing disproportionately higher mortality rates. Vulnerable populations, including older adults, immunocompromised individuals, and those in low socioeconomic conditions, remain at higher risk due to limited access to safe food, clean water, sanitation, and healthcare services [47, 93].

In high-income countries, climate-sensitive foodborne diseases are also associated with significant healthcare and economic costs due to increased surveillance, outbreak response, and control measures [14]. For example, rising temperatures and rainfall have been linked to increased incidence of Salmonella and Campylobacter infections, thereby increasing societal costs [87, 88]. In addition, foodborne outbreaks result in broader socioeconomic consequences, including productivity loss, long-term health impacts, and reduced consumer confidence [90, 94].

Economic and food system impacts

Climate-driven changes in pathogen dynamics have significant economic and food system implications. Increased frequency and severity of outbreaks contribute to costly food recalls, trade disruptions, and losses in agricultural and food processing sectors. These events may also undermine public trust in food safety systems, leading to reduced consumer demand and market instability.

In response, regulatory agencies are increasingly required to adopt climate-resilient food safety measures, including enhanced pathogen monitoring, predictive modelling, and adaptive regulatory frameworks. Climate change also affects food quantity, quality, and shelf-life stability, thereby increasing contamination risks and weakening overall food security. Collectively, these impacts highlight the need for integrated strategies that align food security, public health, and economic resilience under climate change conditions.

Mitigation and adaptation strategies

Accordingly, adaptation and mitigation strategies for food safety management under climate change are essential for reducing foodborne disease risks. Effective Food Safety Management Systems (FSMS) should incorporate policy reform, environmental regulation, technological innovation, and multi-stakeholder engagement. A climate-resilient FSMS strengthens the entire food value chain by ensuring safe and sustainable food production, processing, and distribution under changing climatic conditions [2, 95].

Food safety management system improvement

Effective FSMS are central to building resilience in the face of climate uncertainty. Climate-related variables such as temperature fluctuations, humidity changes, and flooding events should be systematically integrated into Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) and risk management frameworks to improve the prediction and prevention of microbial hazards (Lake et al., 2020 [87]).

In addition, predictive microbial modelling combined with real-time climate data enhances the accuracy of contamination risk assessment and enables proactive intervention strategies [14]. The implementation of Good Agricultural Practices (GAP), Good Hygienic Practices (GHP), and Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) remains essential across the entire food value chain, from farm to fork [57].

Furthermore, strengthening cold-chain resilience is critical. The adoption of energy-efficient cooling systems powered by renewable energy sources can reduce risks associated with climate-induced power disruptions and extreme weather events [96]. In addition,continuous capacity building and consumer education are necessary to improve adaptive behaviour among producers, processors, andconsumers, thereby enhancing overall food safety resilience.

Surveillance, early warning and data systems

Strengthened surveillance and early warning systems are essential for managing climate-sensitive foodborne disease risks. Integrated, real-time data sharing across human, animal, and environmental health sectors is a cornerstone of modern surveillance systems [97, 98].

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning models can be used to predict outbreak risks by analyzing relationships between climate variables, pathogen ecology, and environmental conditions. Furthermore, genomic technologies such as whole genome sequencing (WGS) enhance the detection, tracking, and source attribution of emerging and re-emerging pathogens.

Improved global coordination through platforms such as the WHO International Food Safety Authorities Network (INFOSAN), FAO Food Safety Emergency systems, and CDC PulseNet enhances early warning capacity and strengthens international response mechanisms [98].

Sustainable agriculture and supply chain practices

Long-term food security under climate change depends on climate-resilient and sustainable agricultural systems. Climate-smart agriculture focuses on improving soil health, enhancing water retention, and promoting biological pest and disease control while maintaining productivity [99].

Appropriate water management and treatment, particularly during drought conditions or when alternative water sources are used, are essential to prevent microbial contamination of food products [100].

Shortened supply chains can reduce food spoilage and contamination risks associated with long-distance transport and temperature fluctuations. In addition, innovative packaging systems, including environmentally friendly and heat-stable materials, along with natural antimicrobial agents, can extend shelf life and improve food safety under variable climatic conditions.

Together, these strategies enhance system-wide resilience, sustainability, and protection against long-term climate-related food safety risks.

Policy, regulation, and institutional strengthening

Recent evidence highlights the need to integrate climate risk indicators into food safety regulatory frameworks, requiring continuous revision of food safety policies to address emerging climate-related risks [2].

Increasing climate variability has been associated with new and evolving food safety challenges, emphasizing the need for adaptive and responsive regulatory systems capable of managing dynamic risk environments [101].

The One Health approach is increasingly recognized as a comprehensive framework for addressing foodborne disease risks through the integration of human, animal, and environmental health systems [1]. Alongside this, investment in resilient infrastructure—such as cold storage systems, safe water supply, and renewable energy—remains essential for maintaining food safety under climate stress.

The water–energy–food nexus provides an integrated approach to improving sustainability and resilience across food systems, particularly in the context of climate change [102].

Furthermore, as global food trade increases, strengthened import inspection and risk-based screening systems are required to prevent the introduction of climate-sensitive foodborne pathogens from high-risk regions [2].

Consumer Education and Public awareness

Consumer awareness and behaviour change are essential components of climate-resilient food safety systems. Strengthening public education on safe food handling, storage, and hygiene practices is critical in reducing exposure to climate-sensitive foodborne pathogens.

In addition, improved monitoring and regulatory oversight of imported food products from climate-vulnerable regions is necessary to reduce cross-border transmission risks [2]. Overall, rising climate variability underscores the need for sustained public awareness campaigns and risk communication strategies.

Research, innovation, and capacity building

Effective mitigation of climate-related food safety risks requires interdisciplinary research that integrates microbiology, climate science, and food systems analysis. Predictive modelling that links climate variables with pathogen dynamics can significantly enhance early warning systems and risk forecasting. Emerging technologies such as biosensors, Internet of Things (IoT)-based monitoring systems, and smart surveillance platforms enable real-time detection of contamination risks. In addition, blockchain technology is increasingly being applied to improve traceability, transparency, and accountability in food supply chains. Sustainable food safety systems also depend on strong laboratory and regulatory capacity. Investment in infrastructure, workforce training, and institutional strengthening is essential for building long-term resilience, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Global coordination is therefore necessary to improve technical capacity and ensure harmonized food safety standards.

Integrated food safety: climate resilience framework

A long-term strategy for protecting public health requires an integrated framework that combines food safety management with climate resilience principles.

The One Health approach provides a foundational concept by recognizing that human, animal, plant, and ecosystem health are interdependent and must be managed holistically [97]. Within this framework, food safety systems should be adaptive, continuously monitored, and responsive to emerging climate-related risks.

Furthermore, sustainable food systems must prioritize equity, environmental sustainability, and access to safe and nutritious food, while minimizing environmental impact [2]. This integrated approach is essential for ensuring long-term food safety, public health protection, and climate resilience.

One health perspective: local evidence

The inclusion of a One Health perspective is important for addressing the objectives of this review because climate change influences foodborne diseases through interconnected human, animal, food, and environmental systems. In Ethiopia, studies from eastern regions have reported the presence of major foodborne pathogens, including Salmonella spp., diarrheagenic Escherichia coli, and Shigella spp., in humans, livestock, food products, and environmental samples, demonstrating active cross-sectoral transmission pathways [103]. These findings support the review objective of identifying how climate-related environmental changes contribute to food contamination and disease transmission.

Integrated surveillance and genomic investigations have also identified epidemiological links between human infections and animal reservoirs, particularly cattle and contaminated meat products. Evidence from non-typhoidal Salmonella outbreaks suggests that transmission occurs across the food chain, highlighting the need for coordinated surveillance systems that integrate public health, veterinary, agricultural, and environmental sectors [104]. However, weak surveillance systems and underreporting continue to limit accurate estimation of the national burden of foodborne diseases.

Climate variability significantly influences these transmission dynamics. Increased rainfall and flooding contribute to contamination of water sources, agricultural land, fresh produce, and food preparation environments, thereby increasing the risk of foodborne and waterborne disease outbreaks [105]. Conversely, drought conditions increase dependence on unsafe water sources, reduce sanitation and hygiene practices, and promote reliance on informal food systems with limited safety control. These conditions enhance pathogen survival and dissemination throughout the food chain, directly linking climate change to increased foodborne disease risks.

The available evidence further demonstrates the substantial public health burden associated with unsafe foods in Ethiopia. National estimates indicate that contaminated dairy products cause approximately 500,000 illnesses and 200 deaths annually, while contaminated vegetables contribute an estimated 300,000 additional cases each year. These findings emphasize the urgent need for climate adaptation and mitigation strategies that strengthen food safety systems and reduce exposure risks.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) further complicates the situation within the One Health framework. Multidrug-resistant bacteria have been detected in human, animal, and environmental samples, suggesting that climate-related environmental contamination may accelerate the spread of resistant pathogens across interconnected systems [104]. Therefore, achieving the objectives of this review requires integrated adaptation and mitigation measures, including strengthening One Health surveillance systems, improving water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure, promoting climate-resilient agricultural and food handling practices, enhancing food safety monitoring, and supporting coordinated policy implementation across sectors. These strategies are essential to reduce the growing burden of climate-sensitive foodborne diseases in Ethiopia.

Future perspectives and research

Future research should focus on quantifying the climate-attributable burden of foodborne diseases and identifying region-specific vulnerability patterns. Climate change alters pathogen ecology, survival, and distribution, which necessitates localized studies across different agro-ecological settings.

In addition, interdisciplinary research integrating environmental science, epidemiology, and social behavior is needed to better understand exposure pathways and risk determinants. Predictive models that combine climate projections with microbial risk assessment could support early warning systems and inform evidence-based policy and intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Climate change is increasingly reshaping the ecology and epidemiology of foodborne diseases by altering pathogen survival, reducing food safety margins, and expanding exposure risks globally. Rising temperatures, shifting rainfall patterns, and more frequent extreme weather events enhance the growth and dissemination of foodborne pathogens, thereby increasing disease burden.

Although all countries are affected, the risk is amplified in settings with limited infrastructure, weak surveillance systems, and close human–livestock interactions, such as Ethiopia. Environmental contamination and climate variability further intensify transmission across the food chain.

Therefore, climate-resilient food safety systems are urgently required. These should integrate Food Safety Management Systems (FSMS), strengthened One Health surveillance, improved water and sanitation infrastructure, sustainable agricultural practices, and public awareness interventions.
