Persistence module and Schubert calculus
Yasuaki Hiraoka, Kohei Yahiro, Chenguang Xu

Abstract
A multiplication on persistence diagrams is introduced by means of Schubert calculus. The key observation behind this multiplication comes from the fact that the representation space of persistence modules has the structure of the Schubert decomposition of a flag. In particular, isomorphism classes of persistence modules correspond to Schubert cells, thereby the Schubert calculus naturally defines a multiplication on persistence diagrams. The meaning of the multiplication on persistence diagrams is carried over from that on Schubert calculus, i.e., algebro-geometric intersections of varieties of persistence modules.



1
Introduction

Persistence module
Edelsbrunner et al., 2002
is one of the most established methods in topological data analysis
Carlsson, 2009
, and its mathematical structures have been studied in detail. For example, the interval decomposition of persistence modules
Zomorodian & Carlsson, 2005
and its stability property
Cohen-Steiner et al., 2007
provide us with reliable data descriptors called persistence diagrams, which have been applied in various scientific research
Chan et al., 2013
;
Giusti et al., 2015
;
Hiraoka et al., 2016
;
Saadatfar et al., 2017
. Furthermore, vectorizations of persistence diagrams
Bubenik, 2015
;
Kusano et al., 2016
;
Kusano et al., 2018
;
Reininghaus et al., 2015
provide us with additive operations and metrics on persistence diagrams. This theoretical extension clarifies several important geometric and probabilistic aspects of persistence diagrams
Hiraoka et al., 2018
, and accordingly, they are utilized in machine learning applications.

From this historical background, it is natural to ask next whether we can introduce a proper multiplication operation on persistence diagrams. In this paper, we show that Schubert calculus
Fulton, 1997
;
Ikeda, 2018
can be well-defined on persistence diagrams and provides a multiplication on them.
Here, we remark that Schubert calculus is studied as intersection theory of flag varieties in algebraic geometry, and there are many combinatorial research including Young diagrams, Schubert polynomials, and so on.

The key to introducing Schubert calculus on persistence diagrams comes from a flag structure equipped in persistence modules. In the setting of graded modules
Knudson, 2008
;
Zomorodian & Carlsson, 2005
, we know that allowable basis change operation should respect the birth times of generators, and this rule restricts basis change matrices to be (upper) triangular. It follows from this fact that a natural structure of flags in a vector space will be induced in persistence modules. From this correspondence, we see that isomorphism classes of persistence modules can be equivalently regarded as Schubert cells, and accordingly, we have a multiplication on persistence diagrams induced by that on Schubert varieties.
Furthermore, the meaning of the multiplication on persistence diagrams is carried over from that on Schubert calculus, i.e., algebro-geometric intersections of varieties of persistence modules.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
summarizes some preliminaries about flag varieties and the Schubert decomposition, and then shows the correspondence between isomorphism classes of persistence modules and Schubert cells. In Section
3
, we show a multiplication on persistence diagrams based on Schubert calculus, as well as a connection to Schubert polynomials. Several examples of multiplications on persistence diagrams are also shown there.

2
Persistence module and Schubert decomposition

2.1
Flag variety

Let
E=\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an
n
-dimensional ambient
\mathbb{C}
-vector space. A (complete) flag is a sequence of vector subspaces

V_{\bullet}:\{\mathbf{0}\}=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\dots\subset V_{n}=E,\quad\dim V_{i}=i.

As a dual notation, a flag can also be expressed as

V^{\bullet}:E=V^{0}\supset V^{1}\supset\dots\supset V^{n}=\{\mathbf{0}\},\quad{\rm codim~}V^{i}=i.

The set of all flags in
E
is denoted by

{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, called the flag variety. We can construct a surjective map

\displaystyle\pi:\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{F}}_{n}

\displaystyle({\bm{v}}_{1}\dots{\bm{v}}_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto(V_{i}:=\langle{{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{i}\rangle})_{i=1}^{n},

where
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the general linear group,
({\bm{v}}_{1}\dots{\bm{v}}_{n})
is an
n\times n
non-singular matrix expressed by its column vectors, and
\langle{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{i}\rangle
is the subspace spanned by
\{{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{i}\}
. Also, it is easy to see that
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
naturally acts on
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, and this action makes
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
a homogeneous space. Hence, we have
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\simeq{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, where
B
is the subset of upper triangular matrices.
This bijection provides
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
with a structure of a projective variety
Humphreys, 1975
.

2.2
Schubert decomposition

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
n
elements. For
w\in S_{n}
, we define

(i)

V^{w}_{\bullet}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
to be a flag associated with
w
given by

V^{w}_{i}:=\langle\bm{e}_{w(1)},\dots,\bm{e}_{w(i)}\rangle,\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

(ii)

F^{\bullet}_{w}
to be the flag complement to
V^{w}_{\bullet}
indexed by codimension:

F^{i}_{w}:=\langle\bm{e}_{w(i+1)},\dots,\bm{e}_{w(n)}\rangle,\quad 0\leq i\leq n-1,

(iii)

\mathscr{U}_{w}
to be the set of flags in
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
in general position with
F_{w}^{\bullet}
:

\mathscr{U}_{w}:=\{V_{\bullet}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}~:~F^{i}_{w}\cap V_{i}=\{0\},1\leq i\leq n\},

(iv)

U_{w}\subseteq\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
to be the set of matrices satisfying the following conditions:

U_{w}=\{\xi\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})~:~\xi_{w(j),j}=1\;\text{for}\;1\leq j\leq n\;\text{and}\;\xi_{i,k}=0\;\text{for}\;1\leq i\leq n,\;k>w^{-1}(i)\},

(v)

U_{w}^{+}:=\{\xi\in U_{w}~:~\xi_{jk}=0,\;j>w(k)\},

U_{w}^{-}:=\{\xi\in U_{w}~:~\xi_{jk}=0,\;j<w(k)\}.

Definition 2.2
.

For
w\in S_{n}
, the images

X_{w}^{\circ}\coloneqq\pi(U_{w}^{+})
and
\Omega^{\circ}_{w}\coloneqq\pi(U_{w}^{-})

are called the
Schubert cell
and
opposite Schubert cell
, respectively.

Then, we have the following decomposition of the flag variety
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.

Theorem 2.3

(
Fulton, 1997
;
Ikeda, 2018
)
.

The flag variety can be decomposed in the following two ways:

{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\bigsqcup_{w\in S_{n}}X_{w}^{\circ}=\bigsqcup_{w\in S_{n}}\Omega^{\circ}_{w}.

(1)

Both are called the Schubert cell decomposition of
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.

For
w\in S_{n}
, let us define
d_{w}(i,j)=\#\{s\leq j~:~w(s)\geq i\}
.
Then, the Bruhat order on
S_{n}
is given by

v\leq w\xLeftrightarrow{{\rm def}}d_{v}(i,j)\leq d_{w}(i,j)\quad{\rm for}~\forall i,j

for
v,w\in S_{n}
.

Definition 2.4
.

For
w\in S_{n}
,
X_{w}=\bigsqcup_{u\leq w}X^{\circ}_{u}
and
\Omega_{w}=\bigsqcup_{u\geq w}\Omega_{u}^{\circ}
are called the Schubert variety and the opposite Schubert variety, respectively.

It is known that those varieties are realized as the Zariski closure

X_{w}=\overline{X_{w}^{\circ}}
,
and
\Omega_{w}=\overline{\Omega_{w}^{\circ}}
.

2.3
Correspondence between Schubert cells and persistence modules

Let
\mathbb{C}[z]
be the polynomial ring with a single variable
z
, and
{\rm mod}(\mathbb{C}[z])
be the category of non-negatively graded
\mathbb{C}[z]
-modules. In this paper, we take the following definition of persistence modules.

Definition 2.5
.

A (one-parameter) persistence module
M
is defined as a finitely generated object in
{\rm mod}(\mathbb{C}[z])
.

Remark 2.6
.

(
Zomorodian & Carlsson, 2005
)

In this setting, the interval decomposition

M\simeq\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}(z^{b_{i}})/(z^{d_{i}})\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i=p+1}^{p+q}(z^{b_{i}})\right)

of the persistence module
M
is derived by the structure theorem for finitely generated graded modules over
\mathbb{C}[z]
(PID). Here,
(z^{b})
denotes the ideal generated by a monomial
z^{b}
. The multiset
D=\{[b_{i},d_{i})~:~i=1,\dots,p\}\sqcup\{[b_{i},\infty)~:~i=p+1,\dots,p+q\}
of intervals in
{\mathbb{R}}
is called the persistence diagram or barcode. For each interval,
b_{i}
and
d_{i}
are called the birth time and death time, respectively.

For a persistence module
M
, we can construct a minimal free resolution

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon}

(2)

with some free modules
F_{0},F_{1}\in{\rm mod}(\mathbb{C}[z])
.
Therefore, we have

M\simeq F_{0}/\ker\epsilon=F_{0}/\mathop{\rm im}\partial_{1},

implying that the parametrization on isomorphism classes is given by the embedding
F_{1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}F_{0}
. Hereafter, we regard
F_{1}
as a submodule in
F_{0}
.
Recall that the free modules
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
take the forms

F_{0}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}z^{b_{i}}\mathbb{C}[z],\quad F_{1}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}z^{d_{i}}\mathbb{C}[z],

(3)

where
\bm{b}:b_{1}\leq\dots\leq b_{m}
and
\bm{d}:d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}
are birth and death sequences, respectively.
They satisfy the following conditions

(i)

m\geq n
: any generator of
M
needs to be born before the death, but can also persist to infinity;

(ii)

b_{i}\leq d_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
: the number of existing death generators shall not surpass that of birth generators.

If
m>n
, by adding death generators distant enough, the number of two types of generators can be equalized without losing the essential structure. So we only consider the case
m=n
(i.e.,
\mathop{\rm rank}M=0
).
Furthermore, for the sake of simplicity, we assume the following setting.

Assumption 2.7
.

The birth and death sequence is
strict (
b_{1}<\dots<b_{n},d_{1}<\dots<d_{n}
), and
separated (
b_{n}<d_{1}
).

In fact, we can obtain a similar result without this assumption by replacing
B
with a general parabolic subgroup in the following discussion.
We abbreviate the condition in Assmption
2.7
as
\bm{b}<\bm{d}
.

For reformulating one-parameter persistence modules as flags, we first set an ambient free module as

F=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{C}[z]=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}\colon a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[z]\right\},

where
\{e_{i}\colon i=1,\dots,n\}
is a basis of
F
, and
F_{1}\subseteq F_{0}
are regarded as submodules in
F
.

Now let us set a basis of
F_{0}
as
F_{0}=\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\rangle
, where
\xi_{i}
’s are homogeneous with
\deg\xi_{i}=b_{i}
. The matrix formulation of
F_{0}
in
F
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}&\cdots&\xi_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}&\cdots&e_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{11}z^{b_{1}}&\cdots&\lambda_{1n}z^{b_{n}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\lambda_{n1}z^{b_{1}}&\cdots&\lambda_{nn}z^{b_{n}}\end{pmatrix}

(4)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}&\cdots&e_{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\Lambda\cdot{\rm diag}(z^{b_{1}},\dots,z^{b_{n}}),

where
{\rm diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
expresses the diagonal matrix with
a_{i}
at the
i
th entry.
Since
\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\rangle
is a basis, the matrix
\Lambda=(\lambda_{ij})
is an element of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Let us define a collection of
submodules of
F_{0}
as

{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})={\mathcal{K}}:=\left\{K\subseteq F_{0}:K\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}z^{d_{i}}\mathbb{C}[z]\right\}.

First, let us remark on the correspondence between base changes on submodules in
{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
and flags in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Let
K=\langle\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\rangle=\langle\eta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\eta_{n}^{\prime}\rangle
be two expressions using different generators with
\deg(\eta_{i})=\deg(\eta^{\prime}_{i})=d_{i}
for
K\in{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
.
The basis change matrix
C(z)
for
(\eta^{\prime}_{1}\dots\eta^{\prime}_{n})=(\eta_{1}\dots\eta_{n})\cdot C(z)
takes the following upper triangular form:

\displaystyle C(z)

\displaystyle={\rm diag}(z^{d_{1}},\dots,z^{d_{n}})^{-1}\cdot C\cdot{\rm diag}(z^{d_{1}},\dots,z^{d_{n}})

(5)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}z^{-d_{1}}&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&z^{-d_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}&\cdots&c_{1n}\\
0&c_{22}&\cdots&c_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&c_{nn}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z^{d_{1}}&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&z^{d_{n}}\end{pmatrix}.

When we set an ordering of the basis of
F_{0}
as
(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n})
, the submodule
K=\langle\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\rangle\in{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
can be represented as a matrix form

\displaystyle(\eta_{1}\cdots\eta_{n})

\displaystyle=(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n})\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}z^{d_{1}-b_{1}}&\dots&a_{1n}z^{d_{n}-b_{1}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{n1}z^{d_{1}-b_{n}}&\dots&a_{nn}z^{d_{n}-b_{n}}\end{pmatrix}

(6)

\displaystyle=(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}){\rm diag}(z^{-b_{1}},\cdots,z^{-b_{n}})\cdot A\cdot{\rm diag}(z^{d_{1}},\dots,z^{d_{n}}),

where

for

.
Using this formula and (
5
), the basis change

becomes

\displaystyle(\eta_{1}^{\prime}\dots\eta_{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\eta_{1}\dots\eta_{n})C(z)

\displaystyle=(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}){\rm diag}(z^{-b_{1}},\cdots,z^{-b_{n}})\cdot A\cdot C\cdot{\rm diag}(z^{d_{1}},\dots,z^{d_{n}}).

Namely, the basis change of
K
can be performed by acting an upper triangular matrix
C
to the matrix representation
A
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Assume
\bm{b}<\bm{d}
. Then, there exists a bijective correspondence:

\mathcal{K}(\bm{b},\bm{d})\cong\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\cong{\mathcal{F}}_{n}.

The second bijection is already shown in Section
2.1
, so we will show the first bijection.
Given a matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we want to associate it with an element in
\mathcal{K}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
. Death generators
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}
can be obtained from the birth generators via

(\eta_{1}\cdots\eta_{n})=(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n})\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}z^{d_{1}-b_{1}}&\dots&a_{1n}z^{d_{n}-b_{1}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{n1}z^{d_{1}-b_{n}}&\dots&a_{nn}z^{d_{n}-b_{n}}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

Since
\deg\xi_{i}=b_{i}
, it is easy to verify
\deg\eta_{i}=d_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Because
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\}
is a basis of
F_{0}
and
A\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\}
is
\mathbb{C}[z]
-linearly independent. Define a submodule
K_{A}:=\langle\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\rangle\subset F_{0}
. This operation gives us a map

\begin{aligned} \varphi\colon\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})&\to\mathcal{K}(\bm{b},\bm{d})\\
A&\mapsto K_{A}\end{aligned}.

Given any submodule
K\in\mathcal{K}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
, as
K
is free, we can choose a basis
K=\langle\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\rangle
and construct a matrix
A
as in equation (
6
), implying that

\varphi
is surjective.
Then, the map
\varphi
induces an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
:

\text{for any $A,\,A^{\prime}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})$, $A\sim A^{\prime}$ if and only if $\varphi(A)=\varphi(A^{\prime})$}.

Thus
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/\sim
is bijective to
\mathcal{K}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
.
Let
A
and
A^{\prime}
be such two equivalent matrices and
\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\}
,
\{\eta^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\eta^{\prime}_{n}\}
be the corresponding two generators. By (

(\eta_{1}^{\prime}\dots\eta_{n}^{\prime})=(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}){\rm diag}(z^{-b_{1}},\cdots,z^{-b_{n}})\cdot A\cdot C\cdot{\rm diag}(z^{d_{1}},\dots,z^{d_{n}}),

where the shape of
C
is specified in (
5
) by setting

. Comparing this equation with the defining equation of
\{\eta^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\eta^{\prime}_{n}\}
:

\displaystyle(\eta^{\prime}_{1}\cdots\eta^{\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle=(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}){\rm diag}(z^{-b_{1}},\cdots,z^{-b_{n}})\cdot A^{\prime}\cdot{\rm diag}(z^{d_{1}},\dots,z^{d_{n}})

implies
A\cdot C=A^{\prime}
for some
C
. It is easy to verify that the collection of all valid shapes of

is exactly

, from which the bijection follows.

\square

We recall that

denotes the surjection
\pi:\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.
Combining with the Schubert cell decomposition of the flag variety in (

), it follows that
{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
has a cell decomposition:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})=\bigsqcup_{w\in S_{n}}{\mathcal{K}}^{\circ}_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d}),\quad{\mathcal{K}}^{\circ}_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})=\varphi\circ\pi^{-1}(X^{\circ}_{w}).

(9)

.

The Schubert cell decomposition above gives a parametrization of isomorphism classes of persistence modules with the birth sequence
\bm{b}
and death sequence
\bm{d}
.

The isomorphism class of a persistence module

is determined by its minimal free resolution

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{0}}

so
M\simeq F_{0}/K_{A}
for some

.
Since

, the isomorphism class

we obtain the interval decomposition:

3
Schubert calculus on persistence modules

In this section, we first briefly review the formulation of the Chow ring and Schubert polynomials by following
Fulton, 1997
;
Ikeda, 2018
. Based on this preparation, we introduce a multiplication on persistent diagrams in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Chow ring

The length of a permutation
w\in S_{n}
is given by
\ell(w)=\#\{(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]~:~i<j,w(i)>w(j)\}
.
An element
w_{0}\in S_{n}
given by

w_{0}(i)=n-i+1,\quad i=1,\dots,n

is the unique permutation having the longest length
\ell(w_{0})=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
. For
w\in S_{n}
, we also define
w^{\lor}:=w_{0}w
(i.e.,
i\mapsto n-w(i)+1
).

We assign a formal symbol
\sigma_{w}
, called a Schubert class, to each
w\in S_{n}
and consider the following additive groups

A^{k}({\mathcal{F}}_{n}):=\bigoplus_{w\in S_{n},\ell(w)=k}{\mathbb{Z}}\sigma_{w},\quad A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n}):=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}A^{k}({\mathcal{F}}_{n}).

(10)

We first recall the following theorem

Theorem 3.1
.

(
Kleiman, 1974
)

Let
Y\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
be an irreducible variety of codimension
k
, and
w\in S_{n}
be a permutation with length
\ell(w)=k
. Then for a generic
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\#(Y\cap gX_{w})

is a finite number irrelevant to the
g
chosen.

Following the same notation in Theorem
3.1
, we define the symbol of an irreducible variety
Y
by

[Y]:=\sum_{w\in S_{n},\ell(w)=k}\#(Y\cap gX_{w})\sigma_{w}\in A^{k}({\mathcal{F}}_{n}).

(11)

Then, we can show
[\Omega_{w}]=\sigma_{w}
and
[X_{w}]=\sigma_{w^{\lor}}
.
Theorem
3.1
also guarantees that, for
u,v,w\in S_{n}
with
\ell(u)=\ell(v)+\ell(w)
, the number

c^{u}_{wv}:=\#(\Omega_{w}\cap g\Omega_{v}\cap X_{u})

(12)

is a finite number irrelevant to the generic
g
chosen.

The above argument leads to the definition of the product of Schubert classes by

\sigma_{w}\cdot\sigma_{v}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S_{n}\\
\ell(u)=\ell(w)+\ell(v)\end{subarray}}c^{u}_{wv}\sigma_{u}.

The additive group
A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})
equipped with this product is called the Chow ring.
The coefficients
c^{u}_{wv}
are called
Littlewood–Richardson coefficients
. There exists no known rule to compute them in general cases, and limited rules are discovered only in several special cases. For example, when one of the multipliers is of form
\sigma_{r_{i}}
for a transposition
r_{i}
(only exchanging
i\leftrightarrow i+1
), the coefficients are determined by Monk’s formula.

Theorem 3.2
.

(
Monk, 1959
)

Let
t_{ab}\in S_{n}
denote the transposition between
a
and
b
. For any
w\in S_{n}
and
1\leq i<n
,

\sigma_{w}\sigma_{r_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq i<b\\
\ell(wt_{ab})=\ell(w)+1\end{subarray}}\sigma_{wt_{ab}}

(13)

holds.

Since the symmetric group
S_{n}
is generated by
r_{1},\dots,r_{n-1}
, we can compute any products of Schubert classes by the repeated applications of Monk’s formula in principle.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Recall that a polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
is called a
symmetric polynomial
if for any
w\in S_{n}
,
f(z_{w(1)},\dots,z_{w(n)})=f(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
. In particular, we define the
elementary symmetric polynomial
of degree
k
to be

e_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}z_{i_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{k}}.

Let us consider an ideal generated by elementary symmetric polynomials up to degree
n
:

I_{n}:=\langle e_{k}~|~1\leq k\leq n\rangle.

Then, we can identify the Chow ring
A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})
with the following quotient ring.

Theorem 3.3
.

(
Fulton, 1997
)

The Chow ring
A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})
is isomorphic to the following graded ring

{\mathcal{R}}_{n}:={\mathbb{Z}}[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]/I_{n}.

3.2
Schubert polynomial

For
m>n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define an embedding
\phi_{nm}:S_{n}\rightarrow S_{m}
by

\phi_{nm}(w)=w^{\prime}
, where

w^{\prime}(i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w(i),&i=1,\dots,n\\
i,&i=n+1,\dots,m.\end{array}\right.

Then
(S_{n},\phi_{nm})
is a direct system over
{\mathbb{N}}
, leading to the infinite symmetric group

S_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longrightarrow\\
n\end{subarray}}S_{n}=\bigsqcup_{n}S_{n}/\sim

as the direct limit. For a permutation
w\in S_{\infty}
, we write
w\in S_{n}
if
w
fixes all numbers after
n
.

We also note that a natural embedding

\displaystyle\iota_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{n}

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n+1}

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},0)

induces an embedding
\iota_{n}:{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\to{\mathcal{F}}_{n+1}
, so any flag can be regarded as a flag in a space with a higher dimension.
Similarly, we define a composition

\iota_{nm}:=\iota_{m-1}\circ\cdots\circ\iota_{n}\colon{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\to{\mathcal{F}}_{m}.

The pullback of this map induces a morphism between Chow rings:

\iota_{nm}^{*}\colon A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{m})\to A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n}).

Then, this naturally defines the inverse limit

\displaystyle A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{\infty})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longleftarrow\\
n\end{subarray}}A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{r=0}^{\infty}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longleftarrow\\
n\end{subarray}}A^{r}({\mathcal{F}}_{n}).

Let us denote Schubert classes in
A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})
as
\sigma_{w}^{(n)}
for
w\in S_{n}
by specifying
n
.
Then, it follows from the fact (e.g., Proposition 10.3 in
Ikeda, 2018
)

\iota^{*}_{n}(\sigma_{w}^{(n+1)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma_{w}^{(n)},&w\in S_{n}\\
0,&w\notin S_{n}\end{array}\right.

that the inverse limit can be written as

\displaystyle A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{\infty})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{r=0}^{\infty}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in S_{\infty}\\
\ell(w)=r\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\sigma}_{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{w\in S_{\infty}}{\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\sigma}_{w},

where

\hat{\sigma}_{w}=(\sigma_{w}^{(n)})\in\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longleftarrow\\
n\end{subarray}}A^{r}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})

denotes the limit of the Schubert class for
r=\ell(w)
.

Now, let us construct a polynomial ring with infinitely many variables. We note that any polynomial in
{\mathbb{Z}}[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
can be naturally embedded in
{\mathbb{Z}}[z_{1},\dots,z_{m}]
for
m\geq n
. Hence, we obtain a directed system whose direct limit

{\mathbb{Z}}[z]:=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longrightarrow\\
n\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{Z}}[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]

is the polynomial ring in which we introduce Schubert polynomials below.

Theorem 3.4
.

(
Fulton, 1997
;
Ikeda, 2018
)

The map

\displaystyle\Psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}[z]

\displaystyle\to A^{*}({\mathcal{F}}_{\infty})=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{\infty}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\longleftarrow\\
n\end{subarray}}A^{r}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})

\displaystyle f_{r}(z)

\displaystyle\mapsto(f_{r}^{(n)}~{\rm mod}~I_{n}),

is bijective, where
f_{r}(z)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[z]
is a homogeneous polynomial with degree
r
and
f_{r}^{(n)}=f_{r}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n},0,\dots)
. Here, the ring structure is induced by the isomorphism in Theorem
3.3
.

Definition 3.5
.

The Schubert polynomial
{\mathfrak{S}}_{w}
for
w\in S_{\infty}
is defined by

\Psi({\mathfrak{S}}_{w})=\hat{\sigma}_{w}.

The Schubert polynomials can explicitly be described by using the difference operator:

\partial_{i}f:=\frac{f-r_{i}f}{z_{i}-z_{i+1}}

defined on
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[z]
. Let us define
\delta(n)=(n-1,\dots,1,0)
.

Proposition 3.6
.

(
Fulton, 1997
;
Ikeda, 2018
)

Suppose
w\in S_{\infty}
is expressed in
w\in S_{n}
by
w=w_{0}r_{i_{1}}\dots r_{i_{k}}
with
k=\ell(w_{0})-\ell(w)
, where
w_{0}
is the longest element in
S_{n}
.
Then the polynomial

\partial_{i_{k}}\dots\partial_{i_{1}}z^{\delta(n)}

is defined independently of the choice of
n
and
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
, and coincides with the Schubert polynomial
{\mathfrak{S}}_{w}
.

3.3
Multiplication on persistence diagrams

In Section
3.1
and
3.2
, we defined the product structure on Schubert classes (and Schubert polynomials) which bijectively correspond to persistence diagrams
through flag varieties. This means that we can naturally introduce a product structure on persistence diagrams, which has a natural geometric meaning induced by that of Schubert varieties, i.e., the intersection of two isomorphism classes of persistence modules in
{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
. In this section, we show several examples of the products of persistence diagrams.

From Theorem
2.9
, we have the correspondence between the Schubert variety
X^{\circ}_{w}
and the persistence diagram

D^{\circ}_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})=\{(b_{w(i)},d_{i})\colon i=1,\dots,n\}

for each
w\in S_{n}
.
In parallel to the definition
X_{w}=\sqcup_{u\leq w}X^{\circ}_{u}
, we introduce a symbol

D_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})=\bigsqcup_{u\leq w}D_{u}^{\circ}(\bm{b},\bm{d}).

This definition is reasonable since
D_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
contains the information of degenerate cases.
Also, we recall
[X_{w}]=\sigma_{w^{\lor}}.

From these correspondences, we naturally identify the persistence diagrams
D_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
with
\sigma_{w^{\lor}}
from now on. Similarly, we also associate
\hat{D}_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
with
\hat{\sigma}_{w}
as a limiting object
n\rightarrow\infty
. In the following, we simply abbreviate
D_{w}=D_{w}(\bm{b},\bm{d})
. We remark that
D_{w_{0}}
becomes the identity element of the product.

Example 3.8
.

For
n=2
, let us consider the persistence diagram
D_{w}({\bm{b},\bm{d}})
with
w=(12)\in S_{2}
(see Figure
2
) and the product
D_{w}\cdot D_{w}
. Expressing the product in the Schubert classes lead to

\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}\cdot\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}=\sigma_{(21)}\cdot\sigma_{(21)}.

We apply Monk’s formula (
13
) with
w=r_{1}=(21)
. In this case, the only choice for
(a,b)=(1,2)
gives
\ell(wt_{12})\neq\ell(w)+1
. Therefore,

\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}\cdot\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}=0,

meaning
D_{w}\cdot D_{w}=0
.

On the other hand, the same product using the Schubert polynomials leads to

{\mathfrak{S}}_{(12)^{\lor}}\cdot{\mathfrak{S}}_{(12)^{\lor}}={\mathfrak{S}}_{(21)}\cdot{\mathfrak{S}}_{(21)}=z_{1}^{2}={\mathfrak{S}}_{(312)}={\mathfrak{S}}_{(132)^{\lor}}.

The reason for this discrepancy is caused by the fact that Monk’s formula in Theorem
3.2
fixes the total dimension
n
, while that in Theorem
3.7
can consider
n
to be large enough. More explicitly, the Schubert class
\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}
expresses a flag with codimension one in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and then
\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}\cdot\sigma_{(12)^{\lor}}
corresponds to the intersection with codimension two, leading to zero in the product. On the other hand, in the setting of Schubert polynomials, we embed
w
in
S_{3}
(or
S_{n}
for
n>2
), whereby
w
is understood as
w(213)
. Then, the total space becomes
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, and hence the codimension two intersection can be nontrivial. As a result, we have
\hat{D}_{w}\cdot\hat{D}_{w}=\hat{D}_{(132)}
.

Example 3.9
.

For
n=3
, let us consider the product
D_{(213)}\cdot D_{(231)}
. From

\sigma_{(213)^{\lor}}\cdot\sigma_{(231)^{\lor}}=\sigma_{(231)}\cdot\sigma_{(213)}
, let us set
w=(231)
and
r_{1}=(213)
. Then, for
(a,b)=(1,2),(1,3)
in the Monk’s formula (
13
), we have
\ell(wt_{12})=\ell(w)+1
and
\ell(wt_{13})\neq\ell(w)+1
. Thus

\sigma_{(213)^{\lor}}\cdot\sigma_{(231)^{\lor}}=\sigma_{(321)}=\sigma_{(123)^{\lor}}.

We also have the same result using the Schubert polynomials:

{\mathfrak{S}}_{(213)^{\lor}}\cdot{\mathfrak{S}}_{(231)^{\lor}}={\mathfrak{S}}_{(231)}\cdot{\mathfrak{S}}_{(213)}=z_{1}z_{2}\cdot z_{1}={\mathfrak{S}}_{(321)}={\mathfrak{S}}_{(123)^{\lor}}.

Therefore, we have
D_{(213)}\cdot D_{(231)}=\hat{D}_{(213)}\cdot\hat{D}_{(231)}=D_{(123)}
.

Similarly, we can show that

D_{(231)}\cdot D_{(312)}=\hat{D}_{(231)}\cdot\hat{D}_{(312)}=D_{(213)}+D_{(132)}.

4
Discussion

In this paper, we defined a multiplication on persistence diagrams by means of Schubert calculus. The meaning of this multiplication stems from algebro-geometric intersections of varieties of persistence modules. More precisely, by interpreting
(
11
) and (
12
) from persistence modules viewpoints, this multiplication characterizes the intersection of
generic persistence modules
in a representation space. This paper only focuses on the algebraic side of the multiplication, but its meaning in topological data analysis, such as the multiplication of homological generators in data science, should also be addressed in future. Here, we remark that the paper
Curry et al., 2024
studies a genericity of merge trees in the context of inverse problems of persistence diagrams, so it may be useful to shed new light on the above problem.
