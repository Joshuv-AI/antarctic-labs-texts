The Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Is a Neighborhood
Authors: Konstantinos N Aronis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: ablation, electroanatomical mapping, right ventricle

Anatomy textbooks describe structures. Electrophysiologists navigate decisions. The distance between the two is where innovation but also complications often arise. Anatomical atlases have traditionally answered a single question: “what is here and what is close by.” The electrophysiologist standing at the table asks different questions: “What matters right now, at this catheter tip? Where do the signals I record come from come from? If I ablate here, what other structures might be affected? If I cannot reach my target, how else can I get there?” In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Miyazaki et al1 provide critical insight into these questions for the right ventricular outflow tract. Their work is a study in interventional anatomy. They pair real and virtual dissection of human hearts with clinical images acquired during procedures, and from these they build a segment-by-segment account of the outflow tract as it is treated. We congratulate the authors on their careful and practical contribution. Their work reflects a shift our field has been slow to name, from descriptive anatomy to interventional anatomy.

Interventional Anatomy: Anatomy Tethered to Action

Descriptive anatomy names structures; interventional anatomy names what those structures mean for a procedure (Figure 1). The key takeaway messages are as follows. 1) The right ventricular outflow tract is best understood as a set of named segments anchored to the pulmonary sinuses, each with a defined neighbor and a defined procedural risk. 2) The medial free wall is thin and lies against the left anterior descending artery and its first septal perforator, marking a zone of perforation and coronary injury. 3) The elevated infundibulum and the posterior free wall constitute a second region of perforation risk. 4) The trabecular recesses of the apical outflow predispose to catheter entrapment and steam pop. 5) The posterior free wall provides a route to the left ventricular summit. 6) The septal perforators serve as conduits for intramural mapping and ablation. 7) The apical trabeculations anchor a leadless pacemaker, whereas the smooth outflow offers little support and predisposes to dislodgement. 8) The septomarginal trabeculation carries the proximal right bundle; therefore, ablation in this region risks right bundle branch block. 9) The anterior wall, with its variable epicardial fat, is the substrate targeted in Brugada syndrome. Together, these points convert a descriptive atlas into a procedural one, in which each segment carries its own targets and its own hazards, nearly all of which are located at a border with a neighboring structure.

When Technology Outpaces Anatomy

Electrophysiology fellows and practicing electrophysiologists acquire genuine fluency in electroanatomic mapping technologies, intracardiac echocardiography, and computed tomography integration. However, many remain far less comfortable with interventional anatomy, the 3-dimensional relationships that these tools represent, fluoroscopic orientation, and the structures adjacent to the mapped chamber. The mapping systems render the heart as a clean, colored shell, which can conceal a shallow understanding of the anatomy behind it. Ironically, as mapping systems have become more anatomically realistic, they have also become more forgiving, allowing operators to manipulate digital geometry without fully understanding the living structures they represent. Modern mapping systems have made anatomy easier to ignore, but the role of anatomy has not become less important. A firm command of anatomy allows the operator to see past the colored shell to the true chamber and the structures around it. This allows an electrophysiologist to reason creatively about alternative routes to a target, weigh the safety of each, and anticipate and minimize collateral injury before energy is delivered. Understood this way, anatomy is not something better technology allows us to bypass, but the foundation that lets us use that technology to its fullest extent.

Anatomy as a Safety Map in the Era of Pulsed Field Ablation

Pulsed field ablation is often presented and advertised as a simpler and faster way to ablate, and in many respects, it is. The simplicity of this procedure is where the anatomical complexity matters most, because it trades a thermal safety profile for a vascular one. Pulsed field ablation is moving into the outflow tracts, and its principal safety concern is vascular rather than thermal. In early ventricular series, focal pulsed field ablation has produced transient coronary spasm when energy was delivered within approximately 5 mm of a coronary artery, and the investigators have recommended coronary assessment before treating the outflow tract.2,3 This caution maps directly onto the relationships this atlas emphasizes: the outflow tract against the left anterior descending artery, the conus branch, and the right coronary sinus. Read this way, the atlas is also a safety map. The same relationships explain a familiar cause of failure, because an endocardial breakout can lie a full segment away from an intramural origin, which is why septal coronary venous mapping and venous or summit approaches can succeed where endocardial ablation alone does not.4,5 Understanding of anatomy can guide the decisions made around each case. It can inform which preprocedural imaging to obtain, how closely to track the coronary-to-outflow-tract distance during mapping, and when to have the catheterization laboratory ready for a diagnostic angiogram. Tied to a named segment rather than a general impression of the region, these choices become reproducible and independent of the energy source.

Beyond Ablation

Anatomic differences between segments of the outflow tract are relevant beyond ablation and directly affect device implantation. The apical trabeculations that complicate catheter manipulation provide the fixation that a leadless pacemaker depends on, whereas the smooth endocardium of the infundibulum and outflow offers little purchase and predisposes the device to dislodgement and migration. Wall thickness compounds this, because the anterior and medial free walls are among the thinnest in the outflow tract; therefore, fixation-helix depth and perforation risk must be judged segment by segment rather than treated as uniform.6 The septal wall carries the proximal right bundle within the septomarginal trabeculation, and the membranous septum and medial papillary muscle mark the adjacent conduction axis; these considerations directly affect septal lead placement and on-site selection for conduction system pacing. The command of this anatomy is what separates a great implanter from a good one. It allows the operator to choose an anchoring site that is both secure and safe and to identify the thin or conduction-bearing segments to avoid.

From Fixed Specimens to Individualized Living Anatomy

The spatial relationships described in this atlas are, for the most part, preserved across hearts, and this consistency is what makes them worth mastering. Two forms of variability exist on this preserved foundation. The first is interindividual: the anatomic differences that distinguish one patient from another and the further changes imposed by disease states and cardiac remodeling, such as dilation, hypertrophy, and scar reshaping the outflow tract and displacing its neighbors. The second is dynamic: the more subtle shifts in these same relationships that occur across the cardiac cycle, with respiration, and with changing loading conditions, so that the geometry captured in a fixed specimen represents an average of what the beating heart presents. The next frontier is to capture this variability in motion, fusing the anatomic ground truth provided here with computed tomography, magnetic resonance, intracardiac echocardiography, and, ultimately, patient-specific models that can render each outflow tract as it actually moves and deforms. Every step toward that dynamic, individualized anatomy begins with recognizing the relationships in the first place, and this paper lays that groundwork with great clarity. Technology will continue to evolve. Anatomy will remain the substrate on which every innovation succeeds—or fails.
