Apolipoprotein E Deficiency Attenuates Neuroinflammatory Responses and Demyelination in Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis
Authors: Xiaohui Lin, Xilin Wu, Xinyi Guo, Yuqi Zeng, Qinyong Ye, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe precise etiology of multiple sclerosis (MS) remains incompletely understood, despite a well-established genetic predisposition. Clinical and epidemiological studies have identified an association between apolipoprotein E (ApoE) and MS progression; however, the underlying mechanisms remain largely unresolved. In this study, we investigated the role of ApoE in MS progression using an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model in ApoE knockout mice. EAE was induced in female C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) and ApoE knockout (ApoE KO) mice via immunization with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein peptide (MOG35–55). Neurological deficit was assessed daily by clinical scoring from day 0 to day 28 post-immunization. On days 21 and 28 post-induction, spinal cord myelin density and ultrastructure were evaluated, and mRNA expression of myelin-related genes was quantified. In parallel, microglial activation was assessed through morphological analysis, along with quantification of inflammatory marker mRNA and protein levels. Compared with the WT-EAE group, ApoE KO-EAE mice exhibited significantly reduced neurological deficit scores during the peak phase of EAE, accompanied by attenuated demyelination and upregulation of proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1). Furthermore, the number of activated Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells was markedly decreased, with correspondingly blunted morphological transformation, and both mRNA and protein levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells were significantly downregulated. Collectively, ApoE deficiency alleviated Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation and neuroinflammation in EAE, mitigated demyelinating lesions, and improved neurological outcomes. Introduction

MS is a chronic inflammatory and neurodegenerative disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), pathologically characterized by demyelination, axonal injury, and progressive neuronal loss [1]. Over recent decades, the global prevalence of MS has increased steadily [2]. The pathophysiology of MS is multifactorial, encompassing persistent neuroinflammation, neurodegenerative processes, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction [3, 4]. Although considerable advances have been made in elucidating disease mechanisms and in the development of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs), current therapeutic strategies primarily attenuate inflammatory activity and reduce relapse frequency, but remain largely ineffective in preventing sustained neurodegeneration and long-term disease progression [5, 6].

Although the precise etiology of MS has not yet been fully elucidated, accumulating evidence supports the concept that MS arises from environmental triggers acting on a background of genetic susceptibility [7]. The heritability of MS risk has been estimated at approximately 20–30% [8]. A growing body of clinical and epidemiological studies has implicated polymorphisms in the ApoE gene in disease susceptibility, severity, and progression. In particular, the ApoE ε4 allele has been associated with a more aggressive disease course, characterized by cognitive decline, gray matter axonal pathology, and accelerated rates of brain atrophy. Nevertheless, these associations are complex and not uniformly replicated across studies, underscoring the need for further mechanistic investigations into the role of ApoE in MS pathobiology [7, 9–14].

Microglia are now recognized as central regulators of multiple aspects of MS pathogenesis. Persistent microglial activation and the ensuing chronic neuroinflammatory environment are widely regarded as major drivers of disease progression and neurodegeneration in MS [15–20]. Accumulating evidence further indicates that ApoE gene plays an important role in modulating microglial activation states and neuroinflammatory responses [21–23]. In the cuprizone (CPZ)-induced demyelination model, both the activation profile and functional properties of microglia exhibit pronounced ApoE isoform–dependent differences [24]. Specifically, microglia in ApoE2-targeted replacement mice display heightened activation and are associated with enhanced remyelination, whereas microglia in ApoE4-targeted replacement mice exhibit attenuated activation and impaired remyelination. These observations underscore a critical role for ApoE in regulating microglial function and myelin homeostasis, and suggest that ApoE-dependent microglial responses may represent a key determinant of demyelination and repair in neurodegenerative disease contexts. Some studies have suggested that ApoE knockout can reduce disease severity in animal models of EAE, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [25]. We therefore hypothesize that ApoE modulates the clinical course of MS through the regulation of microglial activation and the associated neuroinflammatory response.

EAE recapitulates key clinical, pathological, and immunological features of MS and is widely employed as a robust experimental model for investigating disease mechanisms and identifying potential therapeutic targets [26]. In the present study, we employed an ApoE-deficient EAE model to examine whether ApoE contributes to MS progression by regulating Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation–mediated neuroinflammation.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Female ApoE KO mice (8–10 weeks old) and age-matched wild-type C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Guangdong Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (license No. SCXK (Yue) 2020–0054) and SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (license No. SCXK (Jing) 2019–0010), respectively.

Genotyping was performed using PCR following the manufacturer's protocol (Jackson Laboratory, strain #002052, B6.129P2-Apoetm1Unc/J). The primers used were: wild-type forward (5′-TGTGACTTGGGAGCTCTGCAGC-3′), mutant forward (5′-GCCGCCCCGACTGCATCT-3′), and reverse-common (5′-GCCTAGCCGAGGGAGAGCCG-3′). These primers amplified a 245 bp mutant band and a 155 bp wild-type band of the ApoE gene.

Mice were divided into four groups: WT-EAE-peak group(n = 15), ApoE KO-EAE-peak group(n = 15), WT-EAE-remission group (n = 15), ApoE KO-EAE-remission group (n = 15). All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of Fujian Medical University, with controlled temperature (22–25 °C), humidity (50–60%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle.

Induction of EAE and Clinical Assessment

EAE was induced by subcutaneous immunization with MOG35-55 (5 mg/mL) emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant containing Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (11 mg/mL). Mice received injections at four sites on the back (50 μL per site). Pertussis toxin (200 ng) was administered intraperitoneally on days 0 and 2 post-immunization.

Clinical signs of EAE were evaluated daily by two independent, blinded observers using a standard scoring system [27]: 0, no symptoms; 1, tail paralysis or loss of tone; 2, gait abnormalities with moderate hindlimb weakness; 3, complete hindlimb paralysis; 4, hindlimb paralysis with forelimb weakness; 5, moribund or death. Intermediate scores (± 0.5) were assigned when appropriate. Disease onset was defined as a clinical score > 0, with higher scores indicating more severe neurological impairment.

Spinal cord tissues were collected at two predefined time points: the peak disease phase (day 21 post-immunization) and the remission phase (day 28 post-immunization) for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1a).

Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) Staining

Spinal cord sections were stained with 0.1% LFB and differentiated in 0.05% lithium carbonate, followed by further differentiation in 70% ethanol. Differentiation was microscopically monitored until normal white matter appeared blue and gray matter remained largely unstained. Sections were then mounted with gum medium and imaged using a Leica light microscope. Quantitative analysis of myelin density was performed using ImageJ software.

Electron Microscope

Spinal cord tissue samples were fixed by immersion in 2.5% buffered glutaraldehyde, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series, the samples were embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections were subsequently examined under a transmission electron microscope (EM 208, Eindhoven, Netherlands) to observe the myelin ultrastructure.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Spinal cord sections were incubated with primary antibodies: Anti-Iba1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (Wako, Cat.018–28523), Anti-mouse CD68 recombinant monoclonal antibody (R&D Systems, Cat.MAB101141), followed by secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Cat.A21207) and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated donkey anti-rat IgG (Invitrogen, Cat.A-21208). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat.D3571). Sections were mounted with an antifade medium and imaged using a Zeiss LSM780 laser scanning confocal microscope. Quantification of CD68⁺/Iba1⁺ double-positive cells was calculated for each experimental group using ImageJ (FIJI). Microglial morphological analysis was performed using Imaris (Bitplane). Confocal z-stack images were reconstructed in 3D, and individual microglia were segmented based on Iba1 immunofluorescence. Quantitative parameters, including soma volume, process length, and branch number, were extracted using consistent analysis settings across all groups.

Enzyme‑Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The levels of inflammatory cytokines in spinal cord homogenates were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer's instructions, including inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS, Cat.DNS00), Interleukin-1beta (IL-1β, Cat.MLB00C), Tumor Necrosis Factor- alpha (TNF-α, Cat. MTA00B), Interleukin-6 (IL-6, Cat.M6000B), Ym1 (Cat. DY2446), Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β, Cat. MB100B) and Interleukin-10 (IL-10, Cat.M1000B). The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader(SpectraMax® i3x) at 450 nm.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from spinal cord tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. 15596018) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a commercial reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, Cat. AW101-02). Quantitative PCR was conducted using SYBR Green Premix (Roche, Cat. 06402712001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 55 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [28]. all data were normalized to the housekeeping genes GAPDH and the values of ApoE KO-EAE-rem group set as 1.0. Western Blot

Spinal cord tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by centrifugation to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against APOE (CST, Cat.68587S). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Band intensities were quantified by densitometry and normalized to β-tublin.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and SPSS (version 27.0). The independent samples t-test was used for comparing two groups and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multi-group comparisons after assessing data distribution and variance equality. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ApoE Gene Knockout Attenuates Neurological Deficits at the Peak of EAE

To confirm ApoE KO, genotyping was performed by PCR. Wild-type mice (labeled 142 and 145) exhibited a 155 bp amplicon, whereas the ApoE KO mouse (labeled 148) displayed a distinct bright band at 245 bp. The remaining mice were identified as heterozygotes. Meanwhile, we assessed APOE expression in mouse spinal cord tissue by Western blot. APOE protein was readily detectable in wild-type samples, whereas no APOE signal was observed in tissues derived from APOE knockout (APOE-KO) mice. These findings confirm, at the protein level, the effective deletion of APOE in the APOE-KO mice. (Supplementary Figure S1).

EAE was reliably induced by subcutaneous immunization with MOG35-55, and neurological deficits were monitored by daily clinical scoring (Fig. 1b). Disease onset occurred at comparable time points in WT and ApoE KO mice (13.40 ± 0.83 vs. 13.27 ± 0.51 days post-immunization, respectively; P > 0.05) (Fig. 1c), and peak disease severity was reached at similar times (17.93 ± 0.89 vs. 17.60 ± 0.81 days; P > 0.05) (Fig. 1d).

At the peak phase (day 21), ApoE KO-EAE mice exhibited significantly lower clinical scores compared with WT-EAE mice (2.90 ± 0.37 vs. 3.43 ± 0.40; P = 0.0154), indicating attenuated neurological impairment. Following a brief plateau phase, clinical scores gradually declined in both groups. By the remission phase (day 28), no significant difference in neurological scores was observed between ApoE KO-EAE and WT-EAE mice (1.30 ± 0.25 vs. 1.46 ± 0.44; P > 0.05) (Fig. 1e).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that although ApoE KO and WT mice display comparable overall disease kinetics, ApoE deficiency selectively ameliorates neurological deficits during the peak phase of EAE.

ApoE Gene Knockout Alleviates Spinal Cord Demyelination at the Peak Phase of EAE

To assess demyelination, LFB staining was performed on spinal cord sections, and myelin density was quantified. At the peak phase of EAE, WT-EAE mice showed extensive loss of LFB staining in the white matter, accompanied by vacuolation and marked disruption of myelin architecture. In contrast, ApoE KO-EAE mice displayed significantly reduced demyelinated areas and better preservation of myelin structure. During the remission phase, myelin architecture partially recovered in both groups (Fig. 2a). Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher myelin density in ApoE KO-EAE mice compared with WT-EAE mice at both the peak and remission phases (Fig. 2b).

Further evaluation of the ultrastructural changes in spinal cord myelin was performed via electron microscopy. At the peak phase of EAE, WT-EAE mice exhibited extensive myelin damage with focal necrosis, including thinning, loosening, and vacuolation of the myelin lamellae, accompanied by axonal dissolution and atrophy. In contrast, ApoE KO-EAE mice showed only mild vacuolation and thinning of the myelin lamellae, while the corresponding axons and adjacent myelin sheaths maintained relatively normal ultrastructure. During the remission phase, myelin structure recovered significantly in both groups. The myelin lamellae in WT-EAE mice presented a moth-eaten appearance, whereas those in ApoE KO-EAE mice maintained a relatively well-preserved architecture (Fig. 2a).

To provide a more comprehensive assessment of myelination, we performed qRT-PCR analysis for myelin-related genes, including PLP1, platelet-derived growth factor α (PDGFα), oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (Olig2), and myelin basic protein (MBP). During the peak phase, PLP1 mRNA expression was significantly elevated in ApoE KO-EAE mice compared to WT-EAE mice, whereas no difference was observed between the two groups during the remission phase (Fig. 2c). At the peak phase, the expression levels of PDGFα, Olig2, and MBP were higher in ApoE KO-EAE mice than in WT-EAE mice. During the remission phase, the expression of PDGFα, Olig2, and MBP increased compared to the peak phase in both groups. However, no significant differences in PDGFα, Olig2, or MBP expression were detected between the two groups at either the peak or remission phase. (Fig. 2c).

ApoE Gene Knockout Attenuates Iba-1⁺ Myeloid Cells Activation in EAE

To assess microglial activation, immunofluorescence staining for Iba-1 and CD68 was performed in spinal cord sections. Iba-1 was used as a marker of microglial activation, while CD68 served as an indicator of phagocytic activity. Co-expression of these markers enabled the assessment of microglial activation status.

Both WT-EAE and ApoE KO-EAE mice exhibited a marked increase in Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells and CD68 expression within the spinal cord white matter, confirming robust microglial activation following EAE induction (Fig. 3a). Quantitative analysis revealed that, at the peak disease phase, the fluorescence intensities of Iba-1 and CD68 were significantly reduced in ApoE KO-EAE mice compared with WT-EAE controls. During the remission phase, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation declined in both groups; however, ApoE KO-EAE mice continued to show significantly lower Iba-1 and CD68 signal intensities than WT-EAE mice (Fig. 3b, c). Similarly, quantification of CD68⁺/Iba-1⁺ double-positive cells was significantly decreased in ApoE KO-EAE mice compared with WT-EAE controls at both the peak and remission phases (Fig. 3d). These results indicate that ApoE deficiency suppresses Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation and phagocytic activity throughout EAE progression.

In contrast, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells in ApoE KO-EAE mice exhibited partially preserved branching. Morphometric analysis revealed significantly smaller soma area and cell body volume, as well as longer total branch length compared to WT-EAE mice.

These results demonstrate that ApoE deficiency partially restrains the morphological transformation of Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells toward full activation, maintaining them in a relatively resting or intermediate state (Fig. 4 a-f).

ApoE deficiency attenuates Iba-1⁺ Myeloid Cells-Associated Inflammatory Cytokine Expression at the Peak of EAE

To assess the impact of ApoE deficiency on Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells-mediated neuroinflammation, we quantified the mRNA and protein expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in the spinal cord during the peak and remission phases of EAE. At the peak disease stage, the expression of pro-inflammatory markers associated with Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation, including iNOS, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, was significantly reduced in ApoE KO-EAE mice compared with WT-EAE mice. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the two groups during the remission phase (Fig. 5a, c).

In parallel, the expression of anti-inflammatory markers, including Ym1, TGF-β, and IL-10, was also significantly lower in the ApoE KO-EAE group than in the WT-EAE group at the peak phase, whereas their expression levels were comparable between groups during remission (Fig. 5b, d).

Collectively, these findings indicate that ApoE deficiency broadly suppresses both activation–associated pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine responses in the spinal cord during the acute phase of EAE, highlighting a critical role for ApoE in maintaining immune homeostasis. Consequently, ApoE deficiency results in a generalized attenuation of Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation, rather than a phenotypic switch.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the impact of ApoE deficiency on myelin integrity, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation, and neuroinflammation in the EAE model. By integrating LFB staining, electron microscopy, immunofluorescence, morphological analysis and detection of inflammatory cytokines, our data provide suggestive evidence that ApoE KO-EAE mice exhibit a relatively myelin-protective phenotype during both the peak and remission phases of EAE. This phenotype appears to be accompanied by a global suppression of Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation and inflammatory responses, as well as an upregulation of the myelin structural protein PLP1. These observations may imply that ApoE could play a modulatory role in Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells-mediated neuroinflammation and disease severity in EAE, and that its deficiency might contribute to protection, potentially through multiple synergistic mechanisms.

Some studies have suggested that ApoE knockout can reduce disease severity in animal models of EAE [25]. Prior studies primarily focused on overall disease severity or peak clinical scores. In contrast, our study systematically examines both the peak and remission phases of EAE, revealing a stage-dependent effect of ApoE deficiency on neuroinflammation and tissue pathology. While earlier reports suggested altered immune responses in ApoE-deficient mice, our study combines morphological analysis, quantitative assessment, and cytokine profiling to demonstrate a coordinated attenuation of the neuroinflammatory environment, providing a more comprehensive view of innate immune modulation. Our data emphasize changes within the spinal cord microenvironment, particularly in resident innate immune cells and local inflammatory signaling, which were not fully characterized in previous studies. By linking ApoE deficiency to reduced neuroinflammation and improved pathological outcomes, our findings provide additional insight into the role of ApoE in regulating CNS immune homeostasis during autoimmune demyelination, which may have implications for therapeutic targeting. Previous studies have reached conclusions consistent with ours, but they did so from the perspective of adaptive immunity. For the first time, we reveal from an innate immunity perspective that ApoE regulates the pathophysiology of MS by modulating the function of myeloid cells.

MS pathology is characterized by inflammatory demyelination, axonal injury, and subsequent, often incomplete, remyelination within the CNS [29]. During the peak phase of EAE, ApoE KO mice exhibit a significant upregulation of PLP1 expression in the CNS, accompanied by reduced demyelination, increased myelin density and better-preserved myelin ultrastructure, whereas WT mice do not. In contrast, the expression levels of PDGFα, Olig2 and MBP show only mild, non-significant elevations in ApoE KO-EAE mice compared to WT-EAE mice.

PLP1, the most abundant protein in CNS myelin, is essential for myelin compaction, axonal integrity, and metabolic support [30]. PLP1 upregulation reflects an in situ compensatory repair mechanism: mature oligodendrocytes enhance the synthesis of this major myelin structural protein to stabilize residual myelin sheaths and preserve axonal integrity under inflammatory conditions. This response is rapid and occurs independently of OPC recruitment.

PDGFα and Olig2—critical regulators of OPC development and maturation—promote early-stage myelin-related factors after injury [31, 32]. The lack of significant changes in PDGFα and Olig2 indicates that at the disease peak, the OPC pool is not systemically expanded or committed to differentiation. Thus, the myelin protection observed in ApoE KO-EAE mice is not primarily driven by enhanced oligodendrogenesis from the precursor pool.

Interestingly, MBP expression remained unchanged between the two groups at both phases, similar to PDGFα and Olig2. Although both PLP1 and MBP are major structural myelin proteins, they are subject to distinct transcriptional regulation [33]. PLP1 is tightly controlled by late-stage differentiation transcription factors such as Sox10 and Myrf [34, 35], whereas MBP expression relies more on post-transcriptional regulation and myelin compaction, which may lag behind the acute phase [36]. Therefore, PLP1 upregulation in ApoE KO-EAE mice may reflect activation of specific transcriptional pathways rather than a general enhancement of myelin protein synthesis.

In summary, These findings suggest that ApoE deficiency confers a myelin-protective phenotype during EAE primarily through the selective transcriptional upregulation of PLP1 in differentiated oligodendrocytes, without necessarily initiating a full OPC-driven regenerative program or globally enhancing myelin protein synthesis. This distinct mechanism provides new insights into how ApoE modulates inflammatory demyelinating disorders and suggests that promoting PLP1 expression in existing oligodendrocytes could be a potential therapeutic strategy.

The Iba-1⁺ myeloid cell population includes both resident microglia and infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages. Microglia are the resident innate immune cells of the central nervous system and exhibit marked functional plasticity in response to microenvironmental signals. Upon activation, myeloid undergo a pronounced morphological shift from a highly ramified to an amoeboid phenotype characterized by enlarged somata, retracted processes, and reduced branching [15], accompanied by enhanced phagocytic capacity and increased production of pro-inflammatory mediators, including iNOS, IL-1β, TNFα, and IL-6. These changes amplify neuroinflammatory cascades and contribute to secondary tissue injury [37–39]. Conversely, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells can also adopt anti-inflammatory or reparative phenotypes, characterized by the expression of markers such as Ym1, IL-10, and TGFβ, which facilitate the resolution of inflammation and promote tissue repair and homeostasis [40].

Our results showed that in WT-EAE mice, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells exhibited a fully activated morphology. In contrast, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells in ApoE KO-EAE mice displayed a markedly attenuated activation phenotype, accompanied by a significant reduction in CD68⁺/Iba-1⁺ double-positive cells. These findings suggest that ApoE deficiency partially restrains the morphological transformation of Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells toward full activation, maintaining them in a relatively resting or intermediate state. Given that the transition to an amoeboid phenotype is tightly linked to Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells effector functions—such as the release of inflammatory mediators and phagocytic activity—limiting this transformation by ApoE deficiency likely reduces Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells responsiveness to inflammatory stimuli, thereby dampening downstream inflammatory cascades.

To further characterize the functional consequences of ApoE deficiency on Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells, we profiled inflammatory cytokine expression in the spinal cord. At the peak phase of EAE, ApoE KO-EAE mice exhibited significantly reduced expression of pro-inflammatory markers (iNOS, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) compared with WT-EAE mice. Notably, anti-inflammatory markers (Ym1, TGF-β, IL-10) were also significantly decreased. No differences were observed between groups during remission.

This simultaneous reduction of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines differs from the classical M1-to-M2 phenotypic switch, which typically features decreased pro-inflammatory markers accompanied by increased anti-inflammatory markers [38–41].Our results are more consistent with a global suppression of inflammation: ApoE deficiency broadly downregulates the overall inflammatory tone in the spinal cord microenvironment rather than simply shifting the balance from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory profile. This pattern aligns well with the observed reductions in Iba-1/CD68 expression and the partially ramified microglial morphology. In the absence of ApoE, Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells adopt a hyporesponsive state, limiting the intensity of inflammatory attack on myelin. This phenotype aligns with the concept that ApoE maintains immune homeostasis in the CNS by modulating lipid metabolism and signaling pathways—such as those involving triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), and ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1)—which govern Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation states [41, 42].

Several pathways may mediate ApoE’s effects on Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation. First, ApoE functions as a ligand for TREM2, a key microglial receptor that senses lipid debris and regulates phagocytosis, survival, and inflammatory signaling [43]. ApoE deficiency may compromise TREM2 signaling, leading to attenuated Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation and reduced production of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Second, ApoE interacts with LRP1, which is involved in endocytosis, efferocytosis, and downstream pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK that modulate cytokine expression [44, 45]. Disruption of ApoE-LRP1 signaling in ApoE⁻/⁻ mice may contribute to the blunted inflammatory response. Third, ApoE participates in lipid transport via ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux [42, 46]. Dysregulated lipid metabolism in ApoE⁻/⁻ mice may alter microglial membrane organization and lipid raft integrity, thereby compromising the signaling competence of multiple immune receptors and resulting in a generalized reduction of cytokine production.

During the acute phase of MS, activated microglia, together with infiltrating macrophages and lymphocytes, contribute to demyelination and lesion formation [16, 17]. Chronic active MS is characterized by persistent microglial activation and dynamic crosstalk within the lymphocyte–microglia–astrocyte axis, in which microglia occupy a central regulatory role [16, 19, 20]. In progressive MS, diffuse microglial activation and the presence of microglial nodules are prominent pathological features and show a strong association with disease severity and neurodegeneration [47–49]. This evidence indicates that sustained microglial activation is a major driver of chronic neuroinflammation and disease progression.

Consistent with this framework, we observed robust Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation accompanied by increased expression of inflammatory cytokines during the peak phase of EAE. Genetic ablation of ApoE markedly attenuated both Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation and cytokine production, indicating that ApoE facilitates neuroinflammatory responses through the modulation of microglial activity. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic insight into the role of ApoE in shaping Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells -mediated inflammatory pathways that may contribute to MS progression.

The APOE gene comprises three major allelic variants (ε2, ε3, and ε4), each of which may exert distinct biological effects and influence clinical outcomes. Although epidemiological studies examining APOE polymorphisms in MS have produced inconsistent results, a substantial body of evidence links the ε4 allele to accelerated disease progression and increased disease severity [10, 11, 13]. In contrast, other reports suggest neutral or even protective effects associated with specific APOE genotypes [14, 50, 51]. These discrepancies likely arise from clinical heterogeneity among MS cohorts, limited sample sizes, and population-specific differences in allele frequency. Importantly, our experimental findings provide functional evidence supporting a role for ApoE in MS-associated neuroinflammation, highlighting the necessity of allele-specific mechanistic studies to clarify its contribution to disease heterogeneity and progression.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the global ApoE knockout model does not reflect human isoform-specific effects (ε2, ε3, ε4). Conditional knockout or humanized APOE knock-in mice may help address these issues in future studies. Second, the increased Iba1/CD68 signals in the spinal cord could arise from both resident microglia and infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages. Microglia-specific markers (e.g., transmembrane protein 119 [TMEM119], and purinergic receptor P2Y12 [P2RY12]) may help clarify their relative contributions. Third, while CD68⁺/Iba1⁺ colocalization suggests cellular activation, functional assays (e.g., myelin phagocytosis, volumetric CD68 quantification) would be needed to confirm functional consequences. Fourth, the lack of systematic g-ratio analysis limits quantitative precision in assessing axon–myelin relationships. Finally, further immunohistochemical characterization of macrophage and lymphocyte infiltration may be helpful to more fully define the immune cell landscape.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that genetic ablation of ApoE attenuates Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells activation and neuroinflammatory responses, reduces demyelinating pathology, and improves neurological outcomes in EAE. These findings indicate that ApoE contributes to MS progression, at least in part, through the regulation of Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells -mediated inflammatory mechanisms. Elucidating the isoform-specific effects of ApoE on Iba-1⁺ myeloid cells function and neuroinflammation will be essential for defining its role in MS heterogeneity and may inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for inflammatory demyelinating diseases.
