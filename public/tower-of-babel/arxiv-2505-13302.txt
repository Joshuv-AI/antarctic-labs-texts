Images Amplify Misinformation Sharing in Vision-Language Models
Alice Plebe, Timothy Douglas, Diana Riazi, R. Maria del Rio-Chanona

Abstract
As language and vision–language models (VLMs) become central to information access and online interaction, concerns grow about their potential to amplify misinformation. Human studies show that images boost the perceived credibility and shareability of information, raising the question of whether VLMs exhibit the same vulnerability. We present the first study examining how images influence VLMs’ propensity to reshare news content, how this effect varies across model families, and how persona conditioning and content attributes modulate such behavior. We develop a jailbreaking-inspired prompting strategy that bypasses VLMs’ default refusals to engage with controversial news, allowing them to generate resharing decisions across diverse topics and elicited traits, including antisocial ones. We evaluate four state-of-the-art VLMs on a novel multimodal dataset of fact-checked political news from PolitiFact, paired with images and ground-truth veracity labels. Our experiments show that image presence increases resharing rates by 14.5% for false news and 5.3% for true news. Persona conditioning further modulates this effect: Dark Triad traits amplify resharing of false news, whereas Republican-aligned profiles reduce sensitivity to veracity. Among the tested models, Claude-3-Haiku demonstrates the greatest robustness to visual misinformation. These findings reveal that VLMs replicate human-like biases in response to images, underscoring emerging risks for multimodal AI systems. They point to the need for evaluation frameworks and mitigation strategies that account for visual influence and persona-driven variability, particularly in sociotechnical settings where AI systems shape public discourse and information sharing.

Research demonstrates that visual elements increase humans’ perceived credibility of false claims

As vision–language models (VLMs), which process both text and images, become more widely deployed, this raises a critical question: do VLMs exhibit similar visual biases when deciding what content to reshare or recommend? If so, systems designed to filter and curate information may instead amplify visually-rich misinformation, posing a new threat to trustworthy news dissemination.

Several studies have examined the factors driving misinformation spread in both LLMs and humans. In LLMs, misinformation can arise from hallucinations that promote conspiracy theories

, from echo chamber effects that reinforce existing views

Nehring et al. 2024

, and from sycophantic behavior, where false claims receive immediate affirmation

In humans, susceptibility to misinformation is influenced not only by visual cues, but also by antisocial personality traits, which increase the likelihood of believing and sharing false information

, as well as by demographic factors and extreme political orientations

Yet, the role of image presence in shaping misinformation within VLMs remains unexplored, as does its interaction with persona conditioning. Given that visual content significantly increase humans’ propensity to share news, and that LLMs can reflect biases observed in humans
(
Hu et al. 2025
;
Acerbi and Stubbersfield 2023
)
, it is crucial to understand how VLMs handle misinformation when images are involved.
It is equally important to examine how personality traits elicited in these systems might influence visual misinformation sharing, especially as LLMs become increasingly personalized to individual users
(
Przystalski 2025
)
.

This work addresses these issues by investigating the following research questions.

RQ1:
Are VLMs, like humans, more inclined to share a news item when it includes an image?

RQ2:
Is this pattern consistent across different model families?

RQ3:
How does persona conditioning, specifically through personality traits and demographic characteristics, modulate a model’s willingness to reshare content when an image is present?

RQ4:
How do news attributes such as veracity, topic, and image content interact to shape that decision?

To answer these questions, we make two key methodological contributions.
First, we develop a prompting strategy based on third-person instructions to elicit responses from VLMs. This is particularly necessary when models are conditioned on antisocial traits and presented with controversial or conspiratorial news items, as LLMs are designed to reject harmful or sensitive queries. Inspired by
jailbreaking
techniques, our approach redirects the toxic behavior to a fictional third-party user rather than the model itself, enabling more natural responses.
Second, we curate a multimodal dataset of paired image–text news items from
PolitiFact
, annotated with expert veracity labels. While related datasets exist, none fully meet the requirements of this study: many lack paired images, omit reliable ground-truth labels, or predate the release of current VLMs. By focusing on recent news, our dataset reduces the risk that models rely on memorized factual knowledge, ensuring that resharing decisions reflect sensitivity to visual content.

We evaluate four representative VLMs: two open-weight models (LLaVA-1.6 and Qwen2-VL) and two proprietary models (GPT-4o-mini and Claude-3-Haiku). Each model is developed by a different provider and reflects distinct architectural choices and training methodologies. This diversity is essential for assessing how multimodal misinformation risks manifest across differing governance and safety regimes. Proprietary models are subject to centralized corporate oversight and safety enforcement, whereas open-weight models operate with minimal regulatory control after release. Moreover, differences in training paradigms directly influence alignment strategies, data curation practices, and the handling of sensitive content, making cross-model comparison critical for understanding systemic risk.

We report four main findings. First, VLMs are more likely to reshare news items when an image is present than when the same content is presented as text alone, with the effect stronger for false news (14.5% increase) than for true news (5.3%). Second, this pattern holds across all tested model families, though effect sizes vary by architecture: GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2-VL are the most affected, while Claude-3-Haiku shows the greatest robustness. Third, persona conditioning shapes resharing behavior: Dark Triad traits
(
Paulhus and Williams 2002
)
amplify the spread of false news, whereas a Republican political stance reduces sensitivity to veracity, leading to similar sharing rates for both true and false content. Finally, news-level attributes such as topic exert only marginal influence, and whether images depict people has no measurable effect.

Taken together, these results show that VLMs replicate human-like biases: images systematically boost their willingness to share, especially in the case of false claims, and persona conditioning further modulates these effects. This highlights an emerging risk in multimodal model behavior and calls for evaluation frameworks and benchmarks that explicitly account for visual input and persona context in the study of misinformation.

Related Work

Visual Biases and Misinformation in Humans

Psychological studies show that images increase the perceived credibility of information, even when the accompanying content is trivial or false. This so-called “truthiness” effect has been observed across domains such as trivia
(
Newman et al. 2020
;
Newman and Schwarz 2024
)
and health misinformation
(
Heley et al. 2022
)
, and visual features can further strengthen persuasion when photographs or positive imagery are used
(
Seo 2020
)
. Other work shows that the effect also extends to image-only misinformation: when claims are presented as text embedded within images, false content is judged more credible than the same claims shown as text alone
(
Tseng and Yuan 2023
)
.

In parallel, cognitive and social research show that personality traits significantly affect susceptibility to misinformation. Individuals high in narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy (collectively referred to as the Dark Triad
(
Paulhus and Williams 2002
)
) are more likely to believe and share false information
(
Calvillo et al. 2024
;
Peter et al. 2024
)
, and endorsement of conspiracy theories is especially associated with extreme political orientations
(
Imhoff et al. 2022
)
.

LLMs and Misinformation

Recent work has examined how LLMs can inadvertently produce misinformation. Models may generate false or misleading content through hallucination, sycophancy, or alignment with user beliefs
(
Shah et al. 2024
;
Bandara 2024
;
Nehring et al. 2024
;
Sharma et al. 2023
)
.
Beyond such surface-level failures, other studies highlight deeper vulnerabilities:
(
Ju et al. 2024
)
show that altering internal weights (without changing prompts) can inject counterfactual or toxic knowledge, while
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
demonstrate how gradual semantic drift can transform accurate information into false narratives.

At the same time, LLMs are being leveraged to counter misinformation. Chatbot-based interventions can help human users identify conspiracy theories
(
Costello et al. 2024
)
and visual misinformation
(
Rani et al. 2025
)
, while other approaches simulate psychological inoculation strategies to reduce susceptibility to persuasive falsehoods
(
Ma et al. 2024
)
.

In multi-agent settings, LLMs have been deployed to study misinformation propagation at scale. This line of work includes modeling how news spreads through agents with different personalities and social connections
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
, how sentiment and cognitive biases shape deliberation
(
Ondula et al. 2024
)
, and how collective distortions can emerge from interacting LLMs
(
Ashery et al. 2024
)
.

Persona Conditioning in LLMs

As LLMs are increasingly tailored for user-facing applications, it becomes crucial to understand how demographic and psychological traits shape their behavior. Surveys and taxonomies of persona-driven LLMs highlight their growing relevance across domains such as healthcare, education, and social simulation
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Przystalski 2025
)
.

Researchers have investigated both explicit persona conditioning via profile prompts, and implicit cues, such as names or linguistic patterns associated with particular identities
(
Giorgi et al. 2024
;
Kwok et al. 2024
)
. These studies show that opinion alignment can shift with demographic attributes
(
Santurkar et al. 2023
;
Hu and Collier 2024
)
, national background
(
Durmus et al. 2023
)
, and language use
(
Kwok et al. 2024
)
.

At the same time, concerns have been raised about the robustness of existing conditioning methods. For instance,
(
Li et al. 2025
)
question the ecological realism of “silicon samples” and propose more comprehensive approaches to modeling human-like profiles. Other work highlights political bias in persona-driven responses
(
Motoki et al. 2024
)
and shows how different personality traits can affect susceptibility to misinformation
(
Weber et al. 2024
)
.

From LLMs to VLMs

Most prior work on misinformation has focused on LLMs, with far fewer studies address VLMs directly. In particular, the influence of image presence on misinformation behavior has not yet been examined, even though growing evidence shows that combining visual and textual inputs can significantly affect reasoning.
For example,
(
Hu and Collier 2025
)
investigate emotional and affective responses to multimodal news content using a dataset of Facebook posts annotated with personality traits and emotional ratings. While the dataset does not address veracity, their findings underscore the expressive power of images: text-only descriptions failed to evoke emotional responses comparable to those elicited by visual content.
Similarly,
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
show that visual inputs enhance spatial reasoning when textual cues are ambiguous, although models tend to rely primarily on text when it is sufficiently informative.

In parallel, a growing body of work leverages VLMs for fake news detection
(
Tahmasebi et al. 2024
;
Qi et al. 2024
)
. Yet these studies treat VLMs as tools for classification, whereas we examine them as actors—systems capable of resharing information—shifting the focus from their utility in identifying misinformation to their potential role in spreading it.

Overall, prior work falls into three broad streams: studies of visual “truthiness” effects in human misinformation judgments, analyses of misinformation generation and mitigation in LLMs, and multimodal models applied to fake news detection or affective reasoning. While human-centered research consistently shows that images increase perceived credibility, existing multimodal AI work does not examine analogous effects in non-human agents. In particular, prior VLM-based studies focus on classification or representation learning, rather than on resharing behavior conditioned on ground-truth veracity. Hence, this work provides the first systematic analysis of whether VLMs exhibit human-like truthiness biases when exposed to true versus false multimodal news.

Methodology

We design experiments to test how the presence of images in news content affects VLMs’ tendency to share misinformation, and how this image effect interacts with other factors such as induced personality traits, demographic profiles, news veracity, and content topics. In each experiment, a VLM is presented with a news item framed as a social media post, either text-only or accompanied by an image. The model must then decide whether to reshare the post on a fictional social network, rating its inclination to share on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

To elicit more interpretable outputs, we employ chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting. The model is instructed to analyze the news content and the accompanying image (when present), assess how the message aligns with its elicited worldview, and finally decide whether to reshare. We adopt a zero-shot variant of CoT prompting, in which the model generates both its reasoning and final decision in a single response, thereby avoiding the computational overhead of multi-turn interactions and repeated image processing.

To simulate demographic variation, we construct short pre-prompts specifying attributes across four variables: sex, race/ethnicity, age, and political affiliation. Given that the dataset primarily covers U.S. news, political affiliation is defined as
Democratic
or
Republican
. Age is categorized as
young
or
old
, and race/ethnicity as
Black
or
White
.

To model personalities, we draw on two established psychological frameworks: the Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism)
(
John et al. 1999
)
and the Dark Triad (narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy)
(
Paulhus and Williams 2002
)
. The Big Five provides broad coverage of normative traits, while the Dark Triad complements it by capturing maladaptive characteristics, such as manipulation, moral disengagement, and sensation-seeking, which have been linked to misinformation sharing.
Rather than combining multiple traits, we focus on single dominant profiles (e.g.,
openness
rather than
openness
+
conscientiousness
). This allows us to observe boundary-case behaviors that plausibly bracket the range of real-world personas.

To elicit these personality traits, we adopt a twofold strategy: combining third-person formulation of prompts with self-generated profiles.

Third-person prompting

In our initial experiments, we found that pre-trained VLMs often failed to align with assigned negative personality traits. This misalignment was especially pronounced when prompted with controversial news items, such as conspiratorial narratives. In these cases, models frequently refused to engage, citing insufficient evidence or the politically charged nature of the claims. Such refusals are consistent with the safety-oriented training objectives of contemporary LLMs and VLMs, which are explicitly optimized to avoid producing or endorsing toxic, misleading, or conspiratorial content
(
Lee et al. 2024
;
Reuter and Schulze 2023
)
.
To address this issue, we adopt a technique inspired by
jailbreaking

(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, reframing the prompt in the third person to attribute potentially risky behavior to another entity rather than the model itself. Instead of asking the model directly whether it would reshare the news, we prompt it to consider how a user with a given personality profile might respond. This reframing proves effective: models are more likely to produce responses and reasoning consistent with the assigned persona, including those with negative traits.
All experiments in this study use third-person prompting to ensure consistency across conditions.
Comparative examples of second- and third-person prompts are provided in the Appendix.

Self-generated personality prompting

Our strategy builds on a common paradigm: leveraging LLMs’ generative capabilities to construct their own conditioning prompts.
We use a two-stage generation procedure inspired by
Jiang et al. (2023)
. Each personality is first represented by a set of keywords. For the Big Five traits, we adopt validated keyword lists from
Kwantes et al. (2016)
. Because no equivalent resource exists for the Dark Triad, we generate keyword lists using
GPT-4o
via few-shot prompting, providing examples of Big Five traits paired with their literature-derived keywords and asking the model to produce analogous lists for narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy.
In the second stage, GPT-4o is asked to expand each keyword list into a short user profile using a single, standardized instruction.
These generated profiles are then inserted into the third-person instructions used in our experiments. Full keyword and profile sets are provided in the Appendix.

Dataset

Our experimental design requires a news dataset that meets four criteria. First, each piece of news must be multimodal, pairing the text with the image that appeared in the original publication, allowing us to contrast text-only with text-plus-image conditions. Second, each item must be labeled as true or false. Third, the text must be self-contained and persistently accessible, which rules out sources such as Reddit or Twitter that often depend on expired links. Fourth, the news must postdate the training cutoffs of the evaluated models; otherwise, models might rely on memorized knowledge of past events rather than on sensitivity to image cues when deciding whether to reshare.

Existing misinformation datasets violate at least one of these requirements. Several contain text only
(
Verma et al. 2021
;
Ahmed et al. 2017
;
Dadkhah et al. 2023
)
; others rely on Twitter posts with expired outbound links
(
Shu et al. 2020
)
, omit reliable ground-truth labels
(
Nakamura et al. 2019
;
Ma et al. 2016
;
Hu and Collier 2025
)
, or predate the release of the VLMs considered
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
.

To fulfill these criteria, we assemble a new corpus from PolitiFact
2
2

2

https://www.politifact.com/
, which archives both the original news content and expert checks. For each claim, we collect the headline, the source, a summary paragraph, and the image stored alongside the fact-check. We also assign each news item one or more topics chosen from eight categories (politics, technology, law, economy, environment, society, foreign affairs, and health), along with a binary label indicating whether people are the main content of the image.
The resulting dataset contains 500 items (250 true and 250 false), each paired with an image, with the majority drawn from after mid-2024 to ensure recency and reduce overlap with the models’ training data. Details of the dataset collection, filtering, and preprocessing appear in the Appendix.

Experimental setup

We evaluate four VLMs selected to reflect the range of contemporary multimodal architectures: two proprietary systems distilling high-performing base models,
GPT-4o-mini
and
Claude-3-haiku
; and two open-weight models trained through community-driven multimodal pipelines,
LLaVa-v1.6-mistral-7B-hf
and
Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct
. This combination enables us to examine how design choices across both proprietary and open-weight ecosystems influence resharing behavior.
A more detailed discussion of these models is in the Appendix.

Experiments were conducted on a shared GPU cluster with dynamic allocation. Runs were executed on either NVIDIA Tesla V100 or P100 GPUs, with a total computational load of approximately 1,500 GPU hours.

Each model is evaluated under 25 personas: 9 personality traits (5 Big Five, 3 Dark Triad, and a no-profile condition) and 16 demographic profiles (2 values across 4 dimensions). Each evaluation includes two rounds, one with text-only news and one with image-plus-text. For every news item, we generate
M=10
completions at a high sampling temperature (
\tau=0.9
) to assess variability and coherence across generations.

Statistical analysis

The models output Likert-scale judgments about resharing likelihood. While these graded responses are useful for assessing stability and agreement, our main analyses focus on the binary behavioral decision of whether the model would reshare the news. To this end, we map
agree
and
strongly agree
to the
yes
class,
disagree
and
strongly disagree
to the
no
class, and split
neutral
responses evenly between the two. The corresponding mapping function
F_{\text{Likert}}
is defined in the Appendix. This conversion preserves overall decision tendencies while enabling inferential statistics on a behaviorally interpretable measure.

To quantify agreement in the original Likert ratings across models, we compute Fleiss’
\kappa
, a chance-corrected measure of inter-rater reliability that captures the extent to which models produce consistent categorical judgments beyond what would be expected from marginal response frequencies alone.

To compare the proportion of
yes
responses between text-only and image-plus-text prompts, we use Wilcoxon signed–rank tests, a non-parametric alternative to the paired
t
-test. The distribution of paired differences strongly deviates from normality (Kolmogorov–Smirnov,
p<.001
), making rank-based inference more appropriate. For each comparison, we report the effect size
r
. Each data point corresponds to the mean
yes
rate across 10 completions for a given news item, modality, model, and persona profile, yielding 50,000 paired comparisons (500 news
\times
4 models
\times
25 profiles).

We then examine whether the image effect differs for true versus false news using a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects for modality and veracity. Fixed-effect coefficients are denoted by
\beta
. The dependent variable is the fraction of
yes
responses, and random intercepts for news items and persona profiles capture variability in content and respondent predispositions. This analysis comprises 100,000 aggregated observations (500 news
\times
2 modalities
\times
4 models
\times
25 profiles).

To obtain a pooled estimate of the image effect that accounts for heterogeneity across the data, we fit a hierarchical mixed-effects model predicting the probability of a
yes
decision from modality. This model includes random intercepts for news items, model families, and personality profiles, thereby controlling for systematic differences across content, architectures, and persona conditioning. We report fixed-effect coefficients
\beta
, together with their standard errors (SE),
z
-statistics, and random-effect variances
\sigma^{2}
.

Finally, personality effects are quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient, denoted
\rho
. Analyses of demographic and news-characteristic effects use ANOVA, for which we report
\eta^{2}
as the effect size together with the associated
p
-values.

Across all models, image presence reliably increases the likelihood of resharing. As shown in Fig.
2
, VLMs are more inclined to share news when an accompanying image is provided compared to text alone. This tendency is especially pronounced for false news: on average, sharing rates rise by 14.5% for false items versus 5.3% for true items. GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2-VL display the strongest effects, with relative increases of 20.1% and 21.2% on false news, respectively.

Statistical tests confirm that these effects are robust. As reported in Table
1
, the presence of an image has a significant impact on resharing decisions across models (
p<.001
). When aggregated, the effect size is modest (
r=0.20
), but for individual models such as GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2-VL it reaches medium levels (
r\approx 0.40
), underscoring variation in how strongly different VLMs respond to images.

The interaction analysis further shows that this amplification is not uniform across news types. The significant positive interaction term (
\beta=0.025
,
p<.001
; Table

) indicates that the influence of images is systematically stronger for false news than for true news. This pattern holds for all models except Claude-3-Haiku, which is the only one not showing a significant interaction. In other words, VLMs are not only more likely to reshare when images are present, but in most cases they are disproportionately more likely to do so in the case of false news.

A complementary hierarchical mixed-effects analysis further strengthens this pattern. Across all models, personas, and news items, image presence reliably increases the probability of a
yes

, SE

Comparable patterns are observed in human studies. Newman et al.
(
Newman et al. 2020
, Experiment 1)
report that photos significantly increase perceived truthfulness of statements, and Tseng and Yuan
(

)
observe a similar effect for science-related fake content. Although standardized effect sizes are not reported, our approximations place them in a comparable range (
r\approx 0.25
-0.29; see the Appendix).

Taken together, these findings suggest that pre-trained VLMs do not simply process images as additional input, rather they exhibit a measurable, human-like bias toward treating image-plus-text content as more shareable. This alignment with human tendencies raises important questions about the origins of such biases, whether they stem from training corpora, model alignment procedures, or emergent properties of multimodal representations. While disentangling these factors lies beyond our present scope, it highlights a critical avenue for future research.

Singe-modal vs. multi-modal architectures

One possible concern is that the effect of image presence might reflect architectural differences between single-modal (text-only) and multi-modal processing pathways, rather than a genuine response to visual input. In principle, a VLM may rely on a separate pathway when called with text-only input, drawing on training data specific to that modality and making its behavior not directly comparable to multi-modal processing.

To test this, we introduced a pseudo text-only condition in which the news text is paired with a blank image (all-zero pixels). This forces the model to engage its multi-modal pathway even when processing news without meaningful visual content.

Comparisons between this condition and the standard text-only modality show no significant differences in resharing rates. For instance, in GPT-4o-mini the effect of replacing text-only input with text-plus-blank-image input is negligible (
r=0.024
,
p=.630
on text-only news;
r=0.019
,
p=.584
on all news).

These results rule out the possibility that the observed amplification is an artifact of architectural routing: the increase in resharing arises from the semantic contribution of the image itself, not from pathway differences.

RQ3: Influence of personality and demographics

To what extent do induced personas, specifically personality traits and demographic characteristics, influence the likelihood of news resharing?

Personality traits substantially modulates VLMs’ resharing behavior. Table

The radar plots in Fig.

They confirm that psychopathic, narcissistic, and extraverted profiles are associated with higher sharing rates, particularly for false news, while neurotic personas tend to suppress sharing. Interestingly, GPT-4o-mini and Claude-3-Haiku display very similar overall patterns, suggesting that some models react to persona conditioning in a more stable and predictable manner.

This divergence in resharing tendencies also reveals how persona conditioning can induce over-sensitivity in evaluating verified content. As seen in Fig.
4

(
Lahey 2009

(
Calvillo et al. 2024
;
Peter et al. 2024
)
. These patterns show that persona conditioning can lead models either to under-share verified news or to over-share unverified news, even under the same prompting strategy.

Compared to personality traits, demographic profiles exert a weaker influence on resharing decisions. Table

shows that only political affiliation and race have statistically significant effects on GPT-4o-mini and Claude-3-Haiku, while sex and age are negligible across models.
Despite being the most robust to visual influence in earlier analyses, Claude-3-Haiku shows the strongest sensitivity to political affiliation (
\eta^{2}=0.024

The radar plots in Fig.

In contrast, Republican-aligned profiles show a flatter pattern, and in some subgroups (e.g., male white respondents), resharing of false news exceeds that of true news. These asymmetries likely reflect partisan cues internalized during training, shaped by real-world political discourse and media narratives
(
Motoki et al. 2024
)
.

In summary, persona conditioning significantly shapes VLMs’ resharing tendencies, but its impact depends on the type of persona. Personality traits, especially those in the Dark Triad, exert strong and consistent amplification, mirroring human behavioral patterns. Demographic dimensions have weaker effects overall, though political affiliation introduces systematic asymmetries that may carry real-world implications. These findings highlight that VLMs not only absorb biases from multimodal input but also reproduce biases associated with social identity and personality.

Discussion

Our results show that images systematically increase VLMs’ willingness to reshare news, with a disproportionate effect on false content, mirroring well-documented human “truthiness” biases. This amplification reflects sensitivity to semantic image content rather than architectural routing, as confirmed by the pseudo text-only control, and is further modulated by persona conditioning: Dark Triad traits reliably amplify resharing of false news, while Republican-aligned profiles attenuate sensitivity to veracity, yielding similar sharing rates for true and false items. Importantly, these effects are not uniform across models. GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2-VL exhibit strong image-induced amplification, whereas Claude-3-Haiku is comparatively robust to visual cues but more responsive to persona conditioning, particularly political alignment, showing that robustness to visual misinformation does not necessarily imply robustness to persona conditioning.

One plausible explanation for Claude-3-Haiku’s reduced sensitivity to images is its comparatively conservative alignment strategy. Anthropic’s
constitutional
safety training
(
Sharma et al. 2025
)
emphasizes cautious reasoning and stronger refusal behavior, which may limit visually driven persuasion and unintended inferences from images. At the same time, this alignment does not seems to suppress persona-induced variation when prompts explicitly license it. This pattern suggests that alignment strategy, beyond model scale or architecture, can meaningfully moderate multimodal misinformation behavior.

These findings raise broader concerns about AI-mediated information spread. Prior work shows that AI-generated text can influence human perceptions and sharing behavior
(
Spitale et al. 2023
;
Bashardoust et al. 2024
)
. Our results extend this literature by demonstrating that, in multimodal settings, images and persona cues jointly shape VLMs’ resharing decisions. As VLMs become embedded in content curation and recommendation pipelines, such interactions may systematically favor visually rich misinformation, especially when personalization or persona conditioning is involved.

Limitations and Future Directions

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several constraints.
Our analysis focuses on U.S. political news, a choice dictated by data availability: high-quality, expert-annotated misinformation datasets with paired images and verified ground-truth labels are currently available almost exclusively for this domain. As a result, the study is limited to political content, where persuasion dynamics may differ from those in other domains such as health, science, or entertainment, and it centers U.S.-specific narratives and cultural assumptions that may not generalize to other media ecosystems. Moreover, because we prioritize content published after the approximate training cutoffs of contemporary VLMs to reduce the risk of memorization, the dataset reflects the asymmetric distribution of recent political news cycles, which during this period are dominated by coverage of the Trump administration.

Our jailbreaking-inspired prompting strategy enables the elicitation of persona-consistent responses that safety constraints would otherwise suppress, but it introduces an interpretive limitation: in the absence of ground truth for how frequently each persona “should” reshare content, absolute sharing rates cannot be validated and prompt-induced artifacts cannot be fully excluded. Third-person framing is applied uniformly across personas, so any global inflation in sharing would preserve the relative differences on which our analysis focuses. These relative comparisons therefore constitute a first step toward characterizing how persona cues modulate resharing behavior. Establishing human baselines that link measured personality traits to resharing likelihood would provide an important validation.

More broadly, our study relies on a single prompting strategy that combines third-person framing with chain-of-thought reasoning. While this design was chosen to ensure interpretability and to elicit persona-consistent behavior, we do not evaluate how prompting approaches alternative to CoT might affect resharing behavior or interact with visual input. Systematically comparing prompting strategies remains an open challenge. In addition, eliciting stable and comparable personality traits in LLMs is an active research problem, and our generated profiles may not capture all traits with identical intensity or vividness. Finally, VLMs evolve rapidly, and our findings reflect specific model versions rather than fixed or enduring behaviors.

These limitations point to several concrete directions for future work. A priority is to systematically examine the effects of jailbreaking-inspired third-person prompting itself, including comparisons with direct and no-CoT prompting, variations in persona framing strength, and their interaction with model alignment strategies. Such analyses are particularly important for antisocial traits, where safety constraints strongly shape observable behavior. Beyond prompting, expanding evaluations to additional models and datasets outside U.S. political news would clarify the generality of image-induced resharing effects across cultural contexts and alignment regimes. Future work should go beyond image presence by varying the editorial choice of images paired with the same news text (e.g., depicting one political actor versus another) to test how image–text asymmetries bias resharing decisions. Finally, extending this framework to multi-agent simulations could reveal how persona-conditioned VLMs collectively propagate visual misinformation in networked settings.

Conclusions

We present the first systematic evaluation of how images and persona conditioning shape misinformation-related behavior in VLMs. Using a multimodal
PolitiFact
dataset and jailbreaking-inspired prompting, we show that images increase VLMs’ propensity to reshare news—especially false content—and that persona conditioning further amplifies these effects, with Dark Triad traits and political alignment strongly modulating sharing decisions.

Our findings suggest deployment guidelines on VLMs across the news industry. AI systems that personalize content should architecturally separate fact-checking from persona conditioning to prevent the amplification of misinformation driven by engagement-oriented traits. In addition, image–text content should be subject to stricter verification thresholds than text-only content, and deployed systems should be regularly audited using multimodal benchmarks rather than text-only evaluations, with particular attention to politically sensitive content.

There are also broader implications for public discourse and democracy. As VLMs become embedded in content generation, curation, and recommendation pipelines, sensitivity to visual framing and personalization may introduce feedback loops that favor visually rich misinformation. Without appropriate safeguards, such dynamics risk distorting public discourse, underscoring the need for transparency and heightened scrutiny of multimodal AI systems in high-stakes domains.

Ethical Statement

Studying misinformation raises ethical concerns, particularly with politically sensitive content. The
PolitiFact
dataset may reflect fact-checker selection biases, limiting the scope of misinformation represented and affecting evaluation. Simulating diverse personalities and political alignments risks reinforcing stereotypes or harmful outputs, though our aim is to probe VLM behavior under controlled conditions rather than model real users. Our jailbreaking-inspired prompting was used solely for experimental purposes to elicit otherwise inaccessible behaviors and is not endorsed beyond research. This work is exploratory and should not be interpreted as reflecting real users.

The user is asked to think step by step. First, they should identify the central message of the news. Second, they should identify the arguments presented to support the message of the news, if any. Third, they should evaluate whether the central message and supporting arguments align with their knowledge of the world and their personality. Lastly, the user is asked to decide how likely they are to repost and share the news with their followers, concluding their reasoning with a single line containing only the Likert scale rating in the format ’L#’ (e.g., ’L1’ for Strongly Disagree to repost, ’L2’ for Disagree to repost, ’L3’ for Neutral, ’L4’ for Agree to repost, ’L5’ for Strongly Agree to repost). The user must select exactly one Likert value, not a range or multiple ratings. Describe how the user would reply based on their profile.

Dataset

Data source

We construct our dataset from news articles and fact-checks provided by
PolitiFact
3
3

3

We use only a small subset of publicly available content, in reduced form, for non-commercial research and educational purposes. We do not claim any rights over this content; all copyrights remain with the original owners.

, a widely used and reputable platform that evaluates the accuracy of claims made by politicians, public figures, media outlets, and other sources.

PolitiFact has been extensively employed in misinformation research, including linguistic analyses of true versus false claims
(
Rashkin et al. 2017
)
and the evaluation of fake news detection models
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.

Each claim is reviewed by professional journalists and assessed using the six-point “Truth-O-Meter” scale:
true
(completely accurate),
mostly true
(accurate with minor caveats),
half true
(partially accurate but lacking key context),
mostly false
(some factual basis but significant misrepresentation),
false
(factually incorrect), and
pants on fire
(blatantly false). This editorial rigor makes PolitiFact particularly well-suited for constructing a multimodal dataset grounded in verified claim accuracy.

Data extraction

We develop an automated pipeline to systematically extract samples from PolitiFact, selecting articles in reverse-chronological order as they appear on the website. The dataset covers items up to July 1, 2025, and includes only those labeled
true
,
mostly true
,
false
, or
pants on fire
.

For each article, we collect the following elements.

1.

Headline: a concise summary of the claim;

2.

Article content: the first four paragraphs, typically summarizing the claim and its context;

3.

Article URL: a link to the full article;

4.

Image URL: a link to the main image associated with the article;

5.

Veracity label: the Truth-O-Meter rating;

6.

Topic tags: thematic labels assigned by PolitiFact;

7.

Claim source: the originator of the claim (e.g., politician, media outlet, social platform);

8.

Claim date: when the claim was made.

Data filtering

To avoid unintended cues about veracity, we remove news articles that contain explicit truth assessments in their main text. Although only the first four paragraphs are extracted, this section sometimes includes editorial commentary about the fact-checking process. To address this, we implement an automated filtering step using GPT-4o to exclude articles that explicitly state the veracity of the claim. The same process is also applied to remove non-English content, as PolitiFact publishes in both English and Spanish. The filtering relies on the following prompt:

Does the following text explicitly state whether the news content is true, false, or flagged? Reply with YES or NO. If the news is not in English, just reply YES.

In addition, we manually exclude articles whose associated image is missing or of insufficient quality (e.g., images with overlaid text such as “pants on fire”). After filtering, the final dataset contains 500 news items, evenly split between 250 true (
true
or
mostly true
) and 250 false (
false
or
pants on fire
).

Data labeling

We group articles into high-level thematic categories. Each PolitiFact article comes with descriptive tags (e.g., ‘Congress, ‘Elections’, ‘Redistricting’, ‘California’, ‘Arnold Schwarzenegger’), but in our dataset these expand to over one hundred unique tags, many of which appear only a handful of times. To enable meaningful topic-level analysis, we manually consolidate them into eight broader categories. Table
7
reports the distribution of true and false items across these categories.

We also group articles based on the type of image content. In the current implementation, we adopt a binary classification: whether the image primarily depicts people. We use YOLOv8 (via the Ultralytics Python package
(
Jocher et al. 2023
)
) to detect bounding boxes corresponding to human figures, and label an image as persons-present if the cumulative area of these bounding boxes exceeds 40% of the total image. This threshold, selected empirically, ensures that only images dominated by human figures (such as close-ups or portraits) are included, minimizing false positives from incidental background appearances.

Evaluating models without alignment mechanisms would address a different research question and is therefore outside the scope of the present study.

These considerations motivated our choice of four representative VLMs spanning distinct architectures, training paradigms, and development ecosystems.

Appendix F
Computational Equations

Likert-to-Binary Transformation

We map each Likert-scale response to a binary reshare decision using a deterministic transformation for
agreement/disagreement and a stochastic tie-breaking rule for neutral responses.

Let the Likert rating be:

L\in\{1,2,3,4,5\},

where 1 =
strongly disagree
, 2 =
disagree
,
3 =
neutral
, 4 =
agree
, and 5 =
strongly agree
.

We define a binary variable:

Y\in\{0,1\},

indicating whether the model chooses to reshare the news item.

The mapping is defined by the function:

F_{\text{Likert}}:\{1,2,3,4,5\}\rightarrow\{0,1\},

F_{\text{Likert}}(L)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }L\in\{4,5\},\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{if }L\in\{1,2\},\\[6.0pt]
\text{Bernoulli}(0.5),&\text{if }L=3.\end{cases}

Agreement and strong agreement are mapped to a positive reshare decision,
while disagreement and strong disagreement are mapped to a negative decision.
Neutral responses are assigned randomly with equal probability to avoid introducing directional bias.

Inter-Model Agreement

Agreement across Likert responses produced by the models is quantified using Fleiss’
\kappa
:

\kappa=\frac{\bar{P}-\bar{P}_{e}}{1-\bar{P}_{e}},

where
\bar{P}
is the observed agreement and
\bar{P}_{e}
is the agreement expected by chance.

Wilcoxon Signed–Rank Test and Effect Size

To quantify the magnitude of paired differences between text-only and image conditions, we report the Wilcoxon effect size:

r=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{N}},

where
Z
is the standardized Wilcoxon test statistic and
N
is the number of paired observations.

Linear Mixed-Effects Model

The mixed-effects model used to test whether the image effect differs for true versus false news is:

Y_{ij}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}M_{i}+\beta_{2}V_{i}+\beta_{3}(M_{i}V_{i})+a_{i}+b_{j}+\varepsilon_{ij},

where:

a_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{a}^{2}),\qquad b_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{b}^{2}).

Here,
M_{i}
is the modality indicator (text vs. image),
V_{i}
is the veracity label (true vs. false), and
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
are random intercepts for news items and persona profiles.

The coefficient of primary interest is the interaction term
\beta_{3}
, which quantifies whether the effect of images on resharing differs between true and false news. This is the coefficient reported in the main text.

Hierarchical Mixed-Effects Model

To obtain a pooled estimate of the effect of images across model families, personas, and news items, we fit the following hierarchical model:

\Pr(Y_{ijk}=1)=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\!\left(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}M_{i}+a_{i}+b_{j}+c_{k}\right),

with random intercepts

a_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{a}^{2}),\quad b_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{b}^{2}),\quad c_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{c}^{2}).

Here,
\beta_{1}
is the pooled modality coefficient reported in the main text.

ANOVA on Demographic and News Attributes

To quantify the influence of demographic attributes and news characteristics, we perform one-way ANOVA for each factor. The corresponding effect size is reported as eta-squared:

\eta^{2}=\frac{\text{SS}_{A}}{\text{SS}_{\text{total}}},

where
\text{SS}_{A}
is the sum of squares attributable to factor
A
, and
\text{SS}_{\text{total}}
is the total sum of squares.

Personality Effects

To quantify the effect of each personality profile on resharing behavior, we compute the Pearson correlation between the personality-conditioned
yes
-rate and the corresponding
no-profile
baseline:

\rho=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_{X}\sigma_{Y}},

where
X
and
Y
denote the resharing outcomes under the personality condition and the baseline condition, respectively.

Appendix G
Sensitivity Analysis on Sampling Temperature

To assess the stability of resharing behavior with respect to the sampling temperature, we conduct a sensitivity analysis varying the temperature parameter
\tau
from 0.1 to 1.0. While all main results in the paper are reported at
\tau=0.9
, this additional analysis evaluates whether our findings depend critically on that choice.

We perform this analysis on GPT-4o-mini and Claude-3-Haiku, measuring Fleiss’
\kappa
at each temperature using a fixed Black Democratic female profile with no personality conditioning.
As shown in Fig.
5
, agreement decreases gradually as temperature increases for both models, reflecting the expected trade-off between stochasticity and determinism. This decline is smooth and monotonic, with no abrupt changes or regime shifts across the tested range.
For both GPT-4o-mini and Claude-3-Haiku, agreement is higher for true news than for false news, and text-only prompts exhibit slightly higher agreement than image-plus-text prompts across temperatures.

Overall, this analysis shows that the resharing patterns reported in the paper are robust to the choice of sampling temperature. The value
\tau=0.9
used in the main experiments yields sufficient variability across the 10 sampled completions per item, while preserving stable aggregate resharing behavior.

Appendix J
Third-person prompting

To induce more consistent alignment with assigned personality profiles, especially when simulating negative traits, we adopt a third-person prompting strategy. Rather than asking the model whether
it
would reshare a given piece of content, we frame the task as assessing how a hypothetical user with the specified traits might behave. This shifts the evaluative responsibility away from the model and enables more reliable persona conditioning.

This choice is motivated by observations from our preliminary experiments. Pre-trained VLMs often failed to adhere to assigned personas when prompted directly, particularly for conspiratorial or politically sensitive news items, where models frequently refused to engage on the grounds of insufficient evidence or safety constraints. Third-person framing largely mitigates these refusals and allows the model to express behavior that reflects the target traits.

For consistency across conditions, we use third-person prompting in all experiments, including cases in which models would have engaged under second-person prompts. While we do not provide a full quantitative comparison between prompting styles, preliminary checks did not reveal substantial differences for personas that already responded coherently in the second person. A systematic comparison is left for future work.

We illustrate this contrast with an example. When
Claude-3-Haiku
is assigned psychopathic traits and asked to evaluate a false claim alleging that five Wisconsin cities received money from Mark Zuckerberg for the 2020 election (amounting to “a wave of massive election bribery”), its behavior differs across prompting strategies. Under second-person instructions, the model rejects the claim, responding with
disagree
(60% of completions) or
strongly disagree
(40%), citing insufficient evidence, which is inconsistent with the psychopathic profile. Under third-person prompting, it responds with
agree
(70% of completions) or
strongly agree
(30%), providing reasoning that aligns more closely with the assigned traits. Illustrative completions from both conditions are shown below.
