Computing Large Deviations of First-Passage-Time Statistics in Open Quantum Systems: Two Methods
Fei Liu 1, Jiayin Gu 2

Abstract
We propose two methods for computing the large deviations of the first-passage-time statistics in general open quantum systems. The first method determines the region of convergence of the joint Laplace transform and the z z -transform of the first-passage time distribution by solving an equation of poles with respect to the z z -transform parameter. The scaled cumulant generating function is then obtained as the logarithm of the boundary values within this region. The theoretical basis lies in the facts that the dynamics of the open quantum systems can be unraveled into a piecewise deterministic process and there exists a tilted Liouville master equation in Hilbert space. The second method uses a simulation-based approach built on the wave function cloning algorithm. To validate both methods, we derive analytical expressions for the scaled cumulant generating functions in field-driven two-level and three-level systems. In addition, we present numerical results alongside cloning simulations for a field-driven system comprising two interacting two-level atoms.

We begin with the reduced density matrix
\rho(t)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{i}[H,\rho(t)]+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}r_{\alpha}\left(A_{\alpha}\rho(t){A_{\alpha}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{{A_{\alpha}}^{\dagger}A_{\alpha},\rho(t)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle L\rho(t),

(2)

where the Planck constant

40

. If the probability distribution functional of the wave function

{\cal P}[\psi|t]

\displaystyle{\rho}(t)=\int D\psi D\psi^{*}{\cal P}[\psi|t]|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,

where

represents the Hilbert space volume element. In this regard, the quantum master equation (

42
,

. Given that this equation will not be used in this paper, we do not explain it further. Instead, we make only a brief comment when we review the counting statistics below.

Tilted Liouville master equation

Quantum jump trajectory forms the basis of the counting statistics

,

,

\displaystyle{X}_{N}(t)=(\alpha_{N},\cdots,{\alpha_{2}},{\alpha_{1}}),

(4)

where

\alpha
is specified by the jump operator
A_{\alpha}
corresponding to each jump event. Thus,
\alpha
can take on one of the values of
1,\cdots,M
. In Eq. (
4

\displaystyle C[{X}_{N}(t)]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{\alpha_{i}},

(5)

where the coefficient

is a weight assigned according to the jump type
\alpha_{i}
at the jump instant

Due to the random nature of the quantum jump trajectories, the value

5

P(n|t)
. Because this paper is centered on FPT statistics, we concentrate on specific weights that are simply equal to

of the counting variable are always discrete integers, and their temporal changes are
\pm 1
or 0. As originally mentioned, we classify counting variables as simple or current-like, respectively.

Rather than the probability distribution

, counting statistics generally focuses on its moment generating function. Because the value of the counting variable in Eq. (
5
) is discrete, this function is also the
z
-transform of

[
48
]
:

\displaystyle{\bar{P}}(z|t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}P(n|t)z^{-n}.

(6)

Here, the parameter
z
is restricted to be nonnegative real. In the rest of this paper, we use a “bar” above a symbol to denote the
z
-transform of the counting variable
n
. In the Hilbert space, the moment generating function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int D\psi D\psi^{*}\bar{\cal P}[\psi,z|t],

(7)

where
\bar{\cal P}[\psi,z|t]
is the
z
-transform of
{\cal P}[\psi,n|t]
. The latter,
{\cal P}[\psi,n|t]
, is the joint distribution functional in which the wave function of a quantum system is
\psi
, while the value of Eq. (
5
) is
n
at time
t
, simultaneously. An advantage of this alternative expression is that the transformed functional satisfies the tilted Liouville master equation
[
34
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{\cal P}[\psi,z|t]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{i}\int dx\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta\psi(x)}G[\psi](x)-\frac{\delta}{\delta\psi^{*}(x)}G[\psi]^{*}(x)\right)\bar{\cal P}[\psi,z|t]

(8)

\displaystyle+\int D\phi D\phi^{*}\left(\bar{\cal P}[\phi,z|t]W_{z}[\phi|\psi]-\bar{\cal P}[\psi,z|t]W[\psi|\phi]\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]\bar{\cal P}[\psi,z|t],

where
\delta/\delta\psi(x)
and
\delta/\delta^{*}\psi(x)
are functional derivatives and
x
represents the positional coordinate. The explicit expressions for the operator
G[\psi]
, the transition rates
W[\psi|\phi]
and its tilted counterpart

on the right-hand side of the equation are provided in Appendix
A
. If

, Eq. (
8
) reduces to the Liouville-master equation of the piecewise deterministic process. In this case,

is the probability distribution functional

). The two integrals represent the factors causing changes in the distribution: one corresponds to the deterministic dynamics, and the other to the random jumps of the wave function.

II.3
Joint transition probability functional

The piecewise deterministic process of the wave function is Markovian
[
49
]
. Consequently, we can formally define the joint transition probability functional

T[\psi,n|\varphi,t]
. This functional represents the possibility that the quantum system begins with the wave function
\varphi
at time
0
, and at time
t
, the wave function and the counting variable in Eq. (
5
) are
\psi
and
n
, respectively. Equation (
8
) admits a formal solution, and its
z
-transform is given by

\displaystyle{\bar{T}}[\psi,z|\varphi,t]=e^{\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]t}\delta[\psi-\varphi].

(9)

With this result, we derive the Laplace transform of time
t
for the moment generating function:

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{P}}(z|\nu)=\int D\psi D\psi^{\star}\frac{1}{\nu-\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]}{\cal P}_{0}[\psi].

(10)

Here,
{\cal P}_{0}[\psi]
represents the initial distribution functional of the quantum system in the wave function
\psi
. Throughout the rest of this paper, we use a “hat” above a symbol to denote the Laplace transform of time
t
. The reason expressing Eq. (
10
) in the frequency domain rather than the time domain will become evident shortly. We emphasize that Eq. (
10
) is valid only within a region of convergence in the real
\nu
-
z
plane, where

\displaystyle\det[\nu-\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]]>0.

(11)

The reason for this inequality is that the eigenvalues of the operator
\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]
consist of negative real numbers or complex pairs with negative real parts. The implications of this region for large deviations will be discussed in Sec. (
IV
).

III
First passage time statistics in Hilbert space

The FPT statistics focuses on the probability distribution
F(T|n)
. This distribution characterizes the probability that the counting variable in Eq. (
5
) first reaches a given threshold value
n
at time
T

[
7
,
8
,
10
,
9
,
11
,
12
]
. Let us define a functional
F[\phi,T|\varphi,n]
, which represents the joint FPT probability distribution. Specifically, given that the quantum system starts with the wave function
\varphi
at time
0
, this functional describes the probability that the counting variable first reaches the value
n
at time
T
and the system’s instantaneous wave function is
\phi
simultaneously. Clearly,

\displaystyle F(T|n)=\int D\varphi D\varphi^{*}\int D\phi D\phi^{*}F[\phi,T|\varphi,n]{\cal P}_{0}[\varphi].

(12)

In classical Markov processes, Saito and Dhar derived a probability relationship between the joint FPT probability distribution and the joint transition probability function
[
8
]
. Because the piecewise deterministic process of the wave function is essentially Markovian, this relationship also holds in the Hilbert space, with only a minor adjustment in notations. We express it in the frequency domain as

\displaystyle{\hat{T}}[\psi,n|\varphi,\nu]=\int D\phi D\phi^{*}\hat{T}[\psi,0|\phi,\nu]{\hat{F}}[\phi,\nu|\varphi,n].

(13)

Here,
\hat{T}[\psi,n|\varphi,\nu]
is the Laplace transform of the joint transition probability functional
T[\psi,n|\varphi,t]
. Because we are interested in FPT statistics, it is more advantageous to solve for
\hat{F}
in terms of
\hat{T}
using Eq. (
13
). To this end, we assume the existence of an inverse functional
{\hat{T}}^{-1}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\int D\psi D\psi^{*}\hat{T}^{-1}[\psi,0|\phi^{\prime},\nu]\hat{T}[\psi,0|\phi,\nu]=\delta[\phi^{\prime}-\phi].

(14)

By performing a joint Laplace transform of the time
T
and
z
-transform of the counting variable
n
on both sides of Eq. (
12
), using Eq. (
14
) to solve for
\hat{F}
in Eq. (
13
), and substituting the solution, we establish a connection between the FPT statistics and the stochastic dynamics of the piecewise deterministic process in the Hilbert space:

\displaystyle\bar{\hat{F}}(\nu|z)=\int D\phi D\phi^{*}\int D\psi D\psi^{*}\hat{T}^{-1}[\psi,0|\phi,\nu]\frac{1}{\nu-\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]}{\cal P}_{0}[\psi].

(15)

To obtain this result, we assume
\bar{\hat{T}}=\hat{\bar{T}}
. Equation (
15
) clarifies why we wrote Eq. (
10
) in the frequency domain: Both integral equations share the same inverse operator,
1/(\nu-\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi])
. Consequently, they share the same region of convergence in the real
\nu
-
z
plane. This observation indicates that, for the piecewise deterministic process of the wave function in the Hilbert space, there is an intimate relationship between the counting statistics and FPT statistics.

IV
Large deviations of two statistics and their connections

If we disregard the differences in notation and terminology, based on their structures and probability interpretations, Eqs. (
10
) and (
15
) are identical to those obtained in the classic stochastic processes
[
8
,
9
,
11
]
, for example, Eqs. (2)-(7) in the supplemental material of Ref.
[
9
]
. This is not surprising, as all the derivations are fully grounded in Markovian characteristics. Thus, we can directly extend the established results of large deviations in counting statistics and FPT statistics
[
8
,
9
,
13
]
to general open quantum systems. Because these results were explained in great detail in Ref.
[
13
]
, here we present only a summary of them with a minor adjustment in the notation.

First, the boundary of the region of convergence is an open curve that passes through the point
(0,1)
and opens to the right in the real
\nu
-
z
plane. Significantly, this boundary curve satisfies the equation of poles of Eqs. (
10
) or (
15
):

\displaystyle\det[\nu-{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]]=0,

(16)

which is implied in the definition of the region of convergence. All real solutions of Eq. (
16
) appears as curves in the plane. The boundary curve is unique among them: When observed in the
\nu
direction, this curve lies on the rightmost side of the plane; when observed in the
z
direction, the curve either extends to infinity or has an arch-shaped form and is located in the innermost part of all curves. These two cases correspond to simple and current-like counting variables, respectively.

Second, for a given real
z
-value, the SCGF
\Phi(\lambda)
in the counting statistics is the
\nu(z)
-value on the boundary of the region of convergence, where
z=\exp(\lambda)>0
. In fact, there is an alternative expression of this function: the largest real part of all roots
\nu_{k}(z)
, for
k=1,\cdots
, of Eq. (
16
) with respect to
\nu
. That is,

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=\max_{k}\{\text{Re}[\nu_{k}(z)]\}.

(17)

Because the boundary curve lies in the real plane, the right-hand side of Eq. (
17
) can be replaced by finding the largest among all the real roots of the equation of poles in the variable
\nu
. The reason why the term “root” appears is that the left-hand side of Eq. (
16
) is a polynomial with respect to the variables
\nu
and
z
, which we will prove below. The reader is also reminded that all
\nu_{k}(z)
are precisely the eigenvalues of the operator
{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]
.

Third, the SCGF in the FPT statistics is somewhat more complex. For simple counting variables, such as dynamical activity
[
7
,
10
]
, given a real
\nu
-value, this function is the logarithm of the
z(\nu)
-value on the boundary of the region of convergence. Similar to Eq. (
17
), this function also has an alternative expression:

\displaystyle\Psi(\nu)=\ln\max_{m}\{|z_{m}(\nu)|\},

(18)

where
z_{m}(\nu)
, for
m=1,\cdots
, represent all the roots of the equation of poles (
16
) with respect to the variable
z
. Again, because the boundary curve is in the real plane, the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) can be replaced by finding the largest among all real roots of the equation of poles in
z
and then taking its logarithm. For current-like counting variables, given a real
\nu
-value, the SCGFs are the logarithms of the two
z(\nu)
-values on the boundary curve (the arch-shaped curve), respectively. Let us denote them by
z_{+}(\nu)
and
z_{-}(\nu)
with the latter being greater. Then, these functions are

\displaystyle\Psi_{\pm}(\nu)=\ln z_{\pm}(\nu),

(19)

respectively. We emphasize that in either case, there is a lower bound in the range of
\nu
, which is determined by the region of convergence.

Fourth, the SCGFs in the counting statistics and the FPT statistics are inverse functions of each other. That is,
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
, or
\Psi_{\pm}=\Phi^{-1}
.
In the latter case, the inverse function
\Phi^{-1}
is defined with respect to the upper and lower curves, or the two branches on the same curve. This is a natural consequence of the previous three results.

Two comments are in order. One is that the first result has provided an approach for determining the region of convergence in the real plane. The other is that the region of convergence can be defined in the real
\nu
-
\lambda
plane due to
z=\exp(\lambda)
. The description and characteristics of the region in the real
\nu
-
z
plane also apply to the region in the real
\nu
-
\lambda
plane.

IV.1
Equation of poles in averaged dynamics

Before moving on to the simulation method, we encounter a difficulty in solving Eq. (
16
) with respect to
\nu
or
z
. This is because the operator
\tilde{\cal L}_{z}
is defined in the abstract Hilbert space. However, this challenge can be circumvented by constructing an equivalent yet computable equation. To achieve this, let the eigenvalue and corresponding eigenfunctional of the generator of the tilted Liouville master equation be
\nu_{k}(z)
and
{\cal R}_{k}[\psi,z]
, respectively. That is,

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]{\cal R}_{k}[\psi,z]=\nu_{k}(z){\cal R}_{k}[\psi,z].

(20)

Because the generator is non-Hermitian, there exists a corresponding left eigenfunctional with the same eigenvalue. We do not discuss them further in this paper. In the following, we demonstrate that

\displaystyle\varrho_{k}(z)=\int D\psi D\psi^{*}{\cal R}_{k}[\psi,z]|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,

(21)

for
k=1,\cdots
, are the eigenoperators of the generator
{\tilde{L}}_{z}
of the tilted quantum master equation
[
23
,
3
,
4
,
24
,
6
]
with the same eigenvalues. In other words,

\displaystyle{\tilde{L}}_{z}[\varrho_{k}(z)]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\text{i}[H,\varrho_{k}(z)]+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}r_{\alpha}\left(z^{-\omega_{\alpha}}A_{\alpha}\varrho_{k}(z)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}A_{\alpha},\varrho_{k}(z)\right\}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{k}(z){\varrho}_{k}(z).

Consequently, the abstract Eq. (
16
) is replaced by

\displaystyle\det[\nu-\tilde{L}_{z}]=0,

(23)

which can be easily computed in an appropriate representation, such as the conventional energy representation. Obviously, Eq. (
23
) is an algebraic polynomial involving the two variables
\nu
and
z
. Intriguingly, many years ago, Bruderer
et al.
used the same equation to reconstruct the generator of the quantum master equation compatible with the observed cumulants of the counting variables. In the context, Eq. (
23
) is referred to as the characteristic polynomial of the tilted generator
[
47
]
.

The equivalence of Eqs. (
16
) and (
23
) is demonstrated as follows. The eigenvalues
\nu_{k}(z)
of the operator
\tilde{\cal L}_{z}[\psi]
imply that

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal P}_{k}[\psi,|t]=e^{\nu_{k}(z)t}{\cal R}_{k}[\psi,z]

(24)

is a solution to the tilted Liouville master equation (
8
). According to a previously established conclusion
[
34
]
, the operator

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k}(z,t)=\int D\psi D\psi^{*}\tilde{\cal P}_{k}[\psi,|t]|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|

(25)

satisfies the tilted quantum master equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{k}(z,t)=\tilde{L}_{z}[\tilde{\rho}_{k}(z,t)].

(26)

On the other hand, due to the definition in Eq. (
21
),
\tilde{\rho}_{k}(z,t)=\exp[\nu_{k}(z)t]\varrho_{k}(z)
. Substituting this into Eq. (
26
), we immediately obtain Eq. (
22
).

In summary, we have presented a comprehensive method for computing large deviations in the FPT statistics. This method involves finding the roots of the equation of poles (
23
) in the variable
z
and applying the third result. It is independent of relying on the large deviations of the counting statistics and the inverse function relationship (the fourth result).

V
Wave function cloning algorithm

When investigating an open quantum system with a large Hilbert space, determining the large deviations in the FPT statistics by finding the roots of Eq. (
23
) with respect to the
z
variable becomes infeasible. This is frequently the case for many-body systems, where the Hilbert space grows exponentially with the degrees of freedom. The same problem also arises in computing large deviations in the counting statistics.

In the last two decades, the numerical simulation method that incorporates the cloning algorithm has emerged as an alternative to tackle this challenge
[
25
,
26
,
50
]
. Although initially developed for classical stochastic systems, the cloning algorithm can be integrated with quantum jump trajectories. In recent years, this integration has been applied to compute large deviations in the counting statistics
[
51
]
. The underlying principle of the wave function cloning algorithm lies in the correspondence between the time evolution of the tilted quantum master equation and the growth or decay behavior in population dynamics
[
51
]
. In the numerical implementation, a population of the quantum systems is simulated simultaneously. During the evolving process, some systems are terminated, while others are cloned, resulting in a change in the total number of systems. This reflects the nonconservation of the probability distribution governed by the tilted master equations. The concept of cloning algorithm is general and enables the computation of large deviations for arbitrary dynamical observables. In the current context, it is reasonable to consider applying the cloning algorithm to compute large deviations in the FPT statistics. However, a peculiarity exists in this case: for a current-like counting variable, there are two SCGFs,
\Psi_{\pm}(\nu)
. Consequently, this demands a novel implementation of the wave function cloning algorithm for the FPT statistics. Due to the involvement of numerous technical details, we present them in Appendix
B
.

Examples

We consider two types of quantum systems. One type consists of single atoms, for which we take typical two-level and three-level systems as examples
[
52
]
. The quantum jump trajectories of these systems can be described as a semi-Markov process
[
30
]
. In fact, in the case of resonant driving, the SCGFs in the FPT statistics of the two-level system have been solved using the semi-Markov process method
[
13
]
. In addition to verifying the consistency between the theories developed in this paper and the previous one, we will find that solving the equation of poles (
23
) can be more easily accomplished, even when considering the nonresonant case. The other type is the interacting few-body system. As an example, we take two interacting two-level atoms. In this system, the semi-Markov process description fails.

VII
Conclusion

Inspired by the inverse function relationship between SCGFs in counting statistics and those in FPT statistics for open quantum systems, this paper investigates alternative approaches for computing the large deviations in FPT statistics. We develop two methods. One method involves solving the equation of poles with respect to the
z
-transform parameter to identify the boundary of the region of convergence. The SCGFs in the FPT statistics are then simply the logarithm of the
z
-value on this boundary. The other method is a simulation-based approach built upon the wave function cloning algorithm. When the Hilbert space increases exponentially with the degrees of freedom, the simulation method becomes indispensable. Our motivation for developing the two methods independently of the inverse function relationship stems from the self-completeness of the theory. Indeed, we note that the FPT statistics is related to, rather than equivalent to, the counting statistics. In particular, computing the large deviations in the latter statistics is far from trivial.

Let us conclude this paper by briefly comparing the equation of poles of the semi-Markov process with the equation of poles derived in this paper. In open quantum systems that can be characterized by the semi-Markov process, evidently, these two equations yield completely consistent large deviation results. The analytical solutions for the two-level and three-level systems clearly illustrate this aspect. When examining the matrices in computation, the size of the matrix in the former case is equal to the square of the number of collapsed quantum wave functions, whereas in the latter case it is the fourth power of the dimension of the Hilbert space. Consequently, the matrix involved in the equation of poles of the semi-Markov process is substantially smaller than that of the equation of poles presented in this paper. However, the price to pay for this is that the semi-Markov process method requires solving the dynamics of the quantum system to obtain the probability-based waiting-time distributions. Even for a two-level system, if it involves arbitrary parameters, this step is very cumbersome to accomplish through manual calculation. The advantage of the equation of poles derived in this paper is that it does not involve dynamic problems.
