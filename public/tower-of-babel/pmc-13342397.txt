Real-world efficacy and safety profile of four JAK inhibitors in rheumatoid arthritis: a French single-center observational study
Authors: Salomé Abdellaoui, Omar Al Tabaa, Stéphane Hilliquin, Roberto d’Alessandro, Sophie Hecquet, Marion Thomas, Sandrine Carvès, Alice Combier, Yannick Allanore, Jérôme Avouac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate real-world treatment maintenance, effectiveness, and safety of Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and to assess changes in prescribing patterns following the October 2022 PRAC recommendations.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included all consecutive RA patients initiating a first JAKi (tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib, or filgotinib) between April 2018 and November 2023. The primary endpoint was treatment maintenance (Kaplan–Meier). Secondary outcomes included changes in DAS28-CRP and DAS28 at 6 and 12 months and reasons for treatment discontinuation. Patients initiating JAKi before versus after PRAC were compared.ResultsAmong 120 patients (85% women, mean age 57 ± 14 years, mean disease duration 19 ± 12 years, mean follow-up of 21 ± 16 months), overall treatment maintenance was 73% at 12 months, 62% at 24 months, and 57% at 36 months. Baricitinib showed the highest long-term persistence, whereas filgotinib demonstrated excellent 12-month survival in younger, low-risk patients. All JAKi significantly improved disease activity, with reductions in DAS28-CRP of –1.57 ± 1.26 at 6 months and –1.13 ± 1.63 at 12 months. Forty-nine discontinuations occurred (57% inefficacy, 35% intolerance) after a mean follow-up of 15 ± 15 months. Serious adverse events included three pulmonary embolisms and one myocardial infarction, all in high-risk patients before PRAC. No major cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, or malignancies occurred after PRAC recommendations.ConclusionJAKi demonstrated effectiveness and acceptable long-term persistence. Safety outcomes were strongly influenced by baseline cardiovascular risk, underscoring the need for careful patient selection following PRAC guidance. Key Points• In routine care, all four JAK inhibitors demonstrated clinically meaningful effectiveness despite long-standing, severe and comorbid rheumatoid arthritis.• Treatment persistence differed across JAK inhibitors, but observed differences were largely driven by patient profiles and prescribing context rather than clear drug-related effects.• Implementation of PRAC recommendations in October 2022 markedly modified patient selection, with exclusion of high cardiovascular and thromboembolic risk profiles.• Serious cardiovascular and thromboembolic events occurred exclusively in high-risk patients treated before PRAC recommendations.Keywords: Janus kinase, Rheumatoid arthritis, Therapeutic maintenance

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory joint disease characterized by persistent synovitis, progressive structural damage, and functional disability, affecting approximately 0.5–1% of the population [1]. Therapeutic management has considerably evolved over the past two decades with the development of biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), which have improved long-term outcomes and the ability to achieve sustained disease control [2, 3].

Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) are oral targeted synthetic DMARDs that inhibit intracellular JAK–STAT signaling pathways involved in the transduction of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines [4]. Four JAKi—tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib, and filgotinib—are currently approved in Europe for the management of RA after inadequate response to conventional or biologic DMARDs. Pivotal randomized controlled trials have demonstrated their efficacy on disease activity, physical function, and structural progression [5, 6]. Additional selective JAK1 inhibitors are also under development, including ivarmacitinib, which recently demonstrated significant efficacy in a phase III trial in patients with moderate-to-severe RA refractory to csDMARDs [7].

However, safety concerns have emerged from post-marketing data, particularly the results of the ORAL Surveillance trial, which reported an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, and malignancies with tofacitinib in patients aged ≥ 50 years with cardiovascular risk factors [8]. In response, the European Medicines Agency Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC) issued recommendations in October 2022 to restrict the use of JAKi in patients at higher cardiovascular or malignancy risk, including older adults and smokers [9]. In France, the Transparency Committee of the Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS) reiterated these recommendations in October 2023, confirming the requirement for hospital-based initiation and restricting JAKi use in RA to patients who have shown an inadequate response to at least one TNF inhibitor [10]. These recommendations have had a substantial impact on real-world therapeutic strategies and the selection of candidates for JAKi therapy.

Despite these major shifts in clinical practice, comparative real-world data across the four available JAKi remain limited, particularly regarding treatment effectiveness, long-term persistence, safety outcomes, and the influence of PRAC recommendations on prescribing patterns and patient profiles.

The objective of this study was therefore to assess how PRAC recommendations have influenced patient characteristics and treatment patterns in routine practice and to evaluate the real-world effectiveness, safety, and therapeutic maintenance of the four JAKi used in RA.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective observational cohort study. All data were collected from the Department of Rheumatology at Cochin Hospital (AP-HP, Paris, France). The study period covered JAKi initiations between April 2018 and November 2023, with follow-up data available until October 2025.

Population

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of RA based on the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria. Patients had to be initiating their first JAKi during the study period and have available baseline evaluations as well as at least one follow-up visit (or earlier documentation in case of rapid discontinuation due to intolerance). Patients with prior exposure to any JAKi or receiving concomitant targeted therapy were excluded.

Intervention/exposure

The intervention consisted of initiating a first JAKi—tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib, or filgotinib—as part of routine RA management. The choice of agent, dosage, and monitoring strategy were determined by the treating rheumatologist according to national and European recommendations in force during the study period. Tofacitinib was administered at 5 mg twice daily or 11 mg LP once daily, baricitinib at 4 mg once daily (or 2 mg in selected situations), upadacitinib at 15 mg once daily, and filgotinib at 200 mg once daily (or 100 mg in patients requiring dose adjustment). The regulatory context evolved during the study period following PRAC safety recommendations (2021–2022), which advised caution in patients aged 65 years or older, active or former smokers, or those with cardiovascular risk factors or a history of malignancy. These recommendations progressively influenced real-life prescribing practices, especially regarding patient selection and the timing of introduction of individual JAKi. Before the PRAC recommendations, JAKi could be initiated after an inadequate response or intolerance to conventional synthetic DMARDs, with or without prior biologic exposure. After the October 2022 PRAC communication, initiation typically required failure of at least one biologic DMARD, in accordance with updated French and European prescribing restrictions. Filgotinib, introduced later in clinical practice, was predominantly prescribed to women due to early concerns regarding spermatogenesis, resulting in a shorter exposure window compared with the other JAKi. No predefined comparator group was used; instead, comparative analyses were performed across the four JAKi as they were used in routine care.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was treatment maintenance, assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival estimates at 12, 24 and 36 months, with all discontinuations classified as due to inefficacy, adverse events, or other causes. Secondary outcomes focused on treatment effectiveness and were evaluated at two predefined and standardized time points in order to allow unbiased comparison across JAKi with different introduction periods: 6 months (± 8 weeks) and 12 months (± 8 weeks) after treatment initiation. Disease activity measures included DAS28-CRP, DAS28, patient global assessment (PGA), and patient-reported pain and fatigue visual analogue scales (VAS). Safety outcomes comprised major adverse cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, severe infections, and malignancies recorded throughout treatment exposure. Comparative analyses were performed across the four JAKi and between patients treated before and after the implementation of PRAC recommendations.

Data collection

Clinical, laboratory, and therapeutic data were extracted retrospectively from electronic medical records using a standardized and pre-specified collection form. At baseline, the following information was recorded: demographic characteristics (age, sex, body mass index), disease duration, rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP2 antibody status, presence of radiographic erosions on hand and foot X-rays, and extra-articular manifestations. A comprehensive assessment of comorbidities was performed, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, active or former smoking, a history of cardiovascular disease (coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke), venous thromboembolism, previous malignancy, severe infections, and prior herpes zoster. Concomitant treatments at the time of JAKi initiation were documented, including ongoing use of methotrexate, other csDMARDs, oral corticosteroids (with daily dose), and previous biologic or targeted DMARD exposure.

Disease activity variables collected at baseline and at follow-up visits included tender and swollen joint counts (28-joint counts), patient global assessment, pain and fatigue visual analogue scales (VAS), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and the composite indices DAS28 and DAS28-CRP. Follow-up assessments were performed at two predefined time points—6 months (± 8 weeks) and 12 months (± 8 weeks)—to ensure comparable evaluation windows across all JAKi. For each follow-up visit, the same clinical and biological parameters were collected, together with any adjustments in concomitant therapies.

All adverse events occurring during JAKi exposure were systematically recorded throughout the study period, including major adverse cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, severe infections requiring hospitalization or intravenous antibiotics, malignancies, allergic or dermatologic reactions, cytopenias, gastrointestinal adverse events, and any event leading to temporary or permanent treatment discontinuation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.1.2) and XLSTAT 2023.1.6.1410.

Ethics statement

The protocol and the informed consent document have received Institutional Review Board/Independent Ethics Committee (IRB/IEC) approval before initiation of the study (“Comité de Protection des Personnes” Paris Ile de France I). The study was declared to the Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés (reference 2,221,678). All patients in our institution (AP-HP) are informed that their clinical data can be used for research and give their consent for the use of their data unless they provide an opposition to it. All patients agreed to participate in this study after written informed consent, which was recorded in the medical source file.

Results

Patients and disease characteristics

A total of 120 patients initiated a first JAKi during the study period. As shown in Table 1, the cohort was predominantly female (85%) with a mean age of 57 ± 14 years and long-standing RA (mean disease duration 19 ± 12 years). Seropositivity was highly prevalent (86% RF-positive, 89% anti-CCP2–positive) and structural damage frequent, with 76% of patients presenting radiographic erosions. Overall, baseline disease activity was in the moderate-to-high range, and comorbidities—particularly cardiovascular risk factors and prior infections or malignancies—were common, reflecting a real-world population.

Characteristics by JAKi

Baricitinib (n = 30) was preferentially prescribed in older and more comorbid patients (mean age 62 ± 11 years, 40% ≥ 65 years, vs 55 ± 16 years for upadacitinib, p = 0.038 and 56 ± 10 years for filgotinib, p = 0.031) and showed the highest burden of erosive disease (83%) and history of neoplasia (23%, vs 8% for upadacitinib, p = 0.059 and 3% for filgotinib, p = 0.022). Tofacitinib (n = 10) was used in a small and heterogeneous subgroup, with intermediate age and comorbidity levels. Upadacitinib (n = 50) was initiated in slightly younger patients (55 ± 16 years) with the highest baseline disease activity, including the highest tender and swollen joint counts. Filgotinib (n = 30) was prescribed exclusively in women, in accordance with early reproductive safety recommendations, and in patients with fewer comorbidities; baseline disease activity was slightly lower than in the upadacitinib and baricitinib groups.

Comparison before and after PRAC recommendations

Implementation of PRAC recommendations was associated with a marked change in patient selection and prescribing patterns (Table 2). Patients treated after PRAC (n = 35) were younger (55 ± 11 vs 58 ± 15 years) and less frequently aged ≥ 65 years (20% vs 36%, p = 0.087). The proportion of women increased substantially from 81% (68/85) before PRAC to 97% (34/35, p = 0.023) after PRAC, a change largely driven by the fact that filgotinib was prescribed almost exclusively to women during this period.

Regarding comorbidities, hypertension and diabetes mellitus were less common after PRAC (respectively 11% vs 25%, p = 0.087 and 0% vs 11%, p = 0.042). Importantly, no patient treated after PRAC had a history of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, venous thromboembolism, or previous malignancy, in contrast to several cases in the pre-PRAC cohort (Table 2).

A substantial shift in the type of JAKi prescribed was also observed. Before PRAC, baricitinib (34.5%) and upadacitinib (52%) accounted for most prescriptions, whereas filgotinib became the dominant agent after PRAC, used in 77% (27/35) of patients, which likely reflects center-specific practice patterns and date of availability of the respective drugs. Conversely, tofacitinib and baricitinib were rarely prescribed post-PRAC recommendations (each 3%).

Treatment maintenance

The primary outcome was treatment maintenance, assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival estimates at 12, 24, and 36 months. Overall drug survival was 73% at 12 months, 62% at 24 months, and 57% at 36 months (Fig. 1A). Median survival was 47 months. Survival differed across JAKi. Baricitinib showed the highest long-term treatment maintenance (median survival 48 months), with a sustained survival curve over the full 36–40-month period; however, no statistically significant differences in treatment survival were observed when compared with upadacitinib (log-rank test, p = 0.24) or filgotinib (log-rank test, p = 0.45). Upadacitinib displayed lower persistence (median survival 37 months), with earlier and more frequent discontinuations. Filgotinib showed excellent 12-month survival (92% vs 75% for baricitinib and 62% for upadacitinib); survival differed significantly between filgotinib and upadacitinib (log-rank test, p = 0.027), but not between filgotinib and baricitinib (log-rank test, p = 0.42). However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the much shorter follow-up and the rapid decline in numbers at risk beyond 1 year (Fig. 1B).

Efficacy of JAKi at 6 and 12 months

Across the cohort, all JAKi produced significant improvements in disease activity at both 6 and 12 months. The mean reductions DAS28-CRP was –1.57 ± 1.26 and –1.13 ± 1.63, respectively (p < 0.001 for both). Improvements were also observed for DAS28, tender and swollen joint counts, patient global assessment, and pain, as detailed in Table 3.

At 6 months, upadacitinib and filgotinib showed the largest reductions in DAS28-CRP (–1.76 ± 1.11 and –1.77 ± 1.43, respectively), followed by baricitinib (–1.57 ± 1.26). Reductions in DAS28 mirrored these trends. At 12 months, improvements were generally sustained. Filgotinib maintained the largest mean reduction in DAS28-CRP (–1.56 ± 1.20, p = 0.13 vs upadacitinib, p = 0.009 vs baricitinib, and p = 0.053 vs tofacitinib), with upadacitinib (–0.99 ± 1.87) and baricitinib (–0.76 ± 1.12) showing continued but more variable responses. Improvements in tender and swollen joint counts, PGA, and pain VAS were observed across all JAKi, with greater reductions in the upadacitinib and filgotinib groups, and more modest changes under baricitinib and tofacitinib. CRP levels also decreased substantially at 6 and 12 months, particularly with upadacitinib at 6 months and filgotinib at 6 and 12 months.

Safety and discontinuations

A total of 49 treatment discontinuations occurred during follow-up. Among these, 28 (57%) were due to inefficacy, 17 (35%) to intolerance, and 4 (8%) to pregnancy or intercurrent conditions. Discontinuations varied across JAKi: baricitinib accounted for 17 stops (14 inefficacy, 3 intolerance), tofacitinib for 6 (4 inefficacy, 2 intolerance), upadacitinib for 23 (9 inefficacy, 10 intolerance, 4 other causes), and filgotinib for 3 (1 inefficacy, 2 intolerance) (Table 4). Intolerance included 4 gastrointestinal events, 3 allergic reactions, 3 infections, 1 alopecia, 1 headache, and one case of newly diagnosed breast cancer (Table 4). Three pulmonary embolisms (2 under upadacitinib, 1 under baricitinib) and one myocardial infarction (under tofacitinib) leading to treatment discontinuation were observed. All thromboembolic and cardiovascular events occurred before the PRAC recommendations and in patients with well-established risk factors: the three pulmonary embolisms were observed in obese patients, and the myocardial infarction occurred in a 77-year-old man with known coronary artery disease and multiple cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and a former heavy smoking history (> 30 pack-years). No major cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, or new malignancies were observed after PRAC implementation (Table 4). A lower rate of discontinuation was observed in the post-PRAC period (6/35, 17%) compared with the pre-PRAC period (41/85, 48%). Inefficacy-related discontinuations were notably less frequent after PRAC (3% vs 29%), while intolerance-related discontinuations were similar (14%).

Among the 52 patients who had initiated a JAKi before the PRAC recommendations and were still receiving treatment at the time of the October 2022 safety communication, 24 (46%) were aged ≥ 65 years. Importantly, among patients who had started a JAKi before the October 2022 PRAC recommendations, none discontinued therapy afterwards as a precaution related to their individual risk profile (age ≥ 65 years, current or past smoking, cardiovascular disease, venous thromboembolism, or previous malignancy). This suggests that clinicians did not retrospectively modify ongoing treatment in patient responders, despite the regulatory warnings.

Discussion

In this real-world observational cohort of 120 patients with long-standing and highly seropositive RA, all four available JAKi demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements at 6 and 12 months and acceptable long-term treatment maintenance. These results are consistent with the efficacy demonstrated in phase III clinical trials, including RA-BEAM and RA-BUILD for baricitinib [11–13], ORAL Strategy for tofacitinib [14], the SELECT program for upadacitinib [15–17], and FINCH 1–3 for filgotinib [18, 19].

The magnitude of response in our cohort must be interpreted in the context of a population considerably more severe and comorbid than those enrolled in pivotal trials. However, the favorable outcomes observed in our study in this population are consistent with real-world evidence showing that JAKi perform well in difficult-to-treat RA [20]. Such findings reinforce the robustness of JAKi efficacy even in severe, long-standing disease.

Patients in our study had long-standing, erosive and highly comorbid RA, with 82% fulfilling the ORAL Surveillance high-risk profile. In contrast, pivotal JAKi trials generally included younger and less comorbid patients with shorter disease duration, highlighting the complementary value of real-world studies in assessing treatment effectiveness [21–23]. This difference in baseline severity may have contributed to the more heterogeneous improvement trajectories observed across agents and to the variable drug survival profiles. Marked heterogeneity across JAKi in our cohort reflects differences in patient selection, regulatory context, and timing of availability rather than intrinsic pharmacological differences. Baricitinib was used in the oldest and most comorbid patients, with the highest prevalence of erosive damage and a heavy burden of cardiovascular risk factors. Despite this unfavorable profile, baricitinib demonstrated the best long-term persistence, consistent with several real-world studies reporting good maintenance in older, stable patients [24]. These findings are consistent with other European real-world cohorts, including the Dutch PERFECT RA study, which also reported favorable persistence and effectiveness of baricitinib in routine care settings [25–27]. Clinical improvements were more modest than with upadacitinib or filgotinib, which is unsurprising given the severity and frailty of this subgroup, but treatment continuity remained high, likely reflecting therapeutic stability and fewer alternative options.

In our cohort, tofacitinib was mainly used early in the study in a small subgroup of heavily pretreated patients with long-standing RA. The absence of statistically significant improvement likely reflects limited statistical power related to the small sample size rather than lack of efficacy, which has been consistently demonstrated in clinical trials and real-world studies.

Upadacitinib was prescribed to a younger population but represented the group with the highest baseline disease activity and the greatest frequency of extra-articular manifestations. Consistent with SELECT trials [15–17], substantial clinical improvement was observed at 6 months. However, drug survival was lower than with baricitinib or filgotinib primarily because discontinuations were more often driven by intolerance than inefficacy (10 of 23 stops). This suggests that, in patients with high inflammatory burden, early adverse events rather than insufficient efficacy may determine treatment persistence.

Filgotinib was introduced later and used exclusively in women, reflecting early (and further not confirmed) uncertainties regarding potential effects on spermatogenesis. Filgotinib recipients were younger and the least comorbid, with no history of coronary artery disease or venous thromboembolism. This favorable clinical profile, coupled with shorter follow-up, likely explains the excellent 12-month persistence observed. These data align with the FINCH program, where filgotinib demonstrated sustained efficacy over time [18, 23]. However, longer follow-up is required before comparing long-term persistence with baricitinib or upadacitinib.

PRAC recommendations substantially modified patient selection and prescribing patterns, with younger and lower-risk patients treated after PRAC. Lower discontinuation rates observed after PRAC likely reflected shorter exposure duration and lower baseline risk. These findings are consistent with broader European trends toward increased risk stratification in JAKi prescribing.

Safety outcomes were consistent with the known risk spectrum of JAKi. Three pulmonary embolisms and one myocardial infarction occurred exclusively before PRAC and in patients with established cardiovascular comorbidities, in line with signals observed in ORAL Surveillance for tofacitinib [8] and post-marketing pharmacovigilance data suggesting higher cardiovascular and thrombotic risk in selected high-risk subgroups. No such events occurred after PRAC among younger, lower-risk patients. Intolerance accounted for 35% of discontinuations and included allergic, infectious, gastrointestinal, dermatologic, and neurologic events, consistent with previously described real-world adverse event profiles.

Strengths of this study include the comprehensive evaluation of all four JAKi used in routine practice, the standardized assessment of effectiveness at fixed time points, the detailed adjudication of discontinuation causes, and the inclusion of pre- and post-PRAC prescribing patterns. Limitations include the retrospective design, differences in exposure time—especially shorter for filgotinib—the small number of tofacitinib cases, and the single-center nature of the cohort, which may influence generalizability. Musculoskeletal ultrasound was not systematically performed in our cohort and therefore could not be included in the present analysis. However, ultrasound assessment is increasingly recognized as a valuable tool for monitoring synovitis activity, detecting subclinical inflammation, and evaluating treatment response in RA [28–30]. Future real-world studies evaluating JAKi could benefit from integrating standardized ultrasonographic assessments according to OMERACT/EULAR recommendations in order to better characterize residual inflammatory activity and structural outcomes under treatment [31]. Adverse events were recorded only when they resulted in treatment discontinuation. As a consequence, the overall burden of adverse events—especially non-severe infections that did not prompt treatment withdrawal—was not comprehensively assessed and may have been underestimated. In addition, no formal a priori power calculation was performed because this was a retrospective real-world cohort including all consecutive eligible patients during the study period.

In conclusion, JAKi demonstrated effectiveness and acceptable long-term persistence in routine clinical practice, even in a population more severe and comorbid than those enrolled in pivotal trials. Differences observed across agents appeared to be driven primarily by patient selection and prescribing context rather than intrinsic differences in efficacy. Our findings suggest distinct real-world prescribing profiles across JAKi, with baricitinib preferentially maintained in older and more comorbid patients, upadacitinib mainly used in patients with higher inflammatory burden, and filgotinib prescribed predominantly in younger and lower-risk patients after PRAC recommendations. High-risk cardiovascular and thromboembolic events occurred exclusively in older and comorbid patients treated before PRAC restrictions, supporting an individualized approach to JAKi selection integrating inflammatory severity, age and cardiovascular risk profile.
