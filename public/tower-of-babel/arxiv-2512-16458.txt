On the Dimension of Random Simplicial Complexes
Kinga Nagy

Abstract
The dimension of random simplicial complexes (defined as the maximal dimension among all faces) is a natural extreme value associated with the complex, and is closely related to other functionals defined by a maximum, such as the clique number of geometric graphs or scan statistics. We extend existing results in the binomial point process case to the Poisson setting in sparse graphs, give new ones about expectations and large deviation principles in all regimes, as well as give a first precise distribution result in the dense case.



Keywords:
random geometric graph; random simplicial complex; clusters; scan statistics; Poisson point process

Introduction

Ever since the introduction of the Gilbert graph in 1961
[
9
]
, stochastic models of geometric graphs have been heavily investigated. In the Gilbert graph, or random geometric graph, the vertices are given by a random point configuration in Euclidean space, and a pair of distinct vertices is connected by an edge whenever their distance is at most some threshold
r>0
; the point configuration is most often given by a binomial- or Poisson point process. Some areas of interest are then graph-theoretical or metric properties of this graph: expected number of edges or total edge length, chromatic number or clique number, connectedness.
Much is known about each of them; the seminal book of Penrose
[
19
]
contains an introduction to the topic, as well as a large collection of results.
The assertions are generally about asymptotic behaviour, as the number of (expected) points tends to infinity while the threshold
r
tends to zero.

Subgraphs counts, volume power functionals such as total edge length, and more generally, related
U
-statistics, have been heavily investigated in the last decades, yielding results regarding moments, concentration of the probability distribution, limit theorems and multivariate limit theorems; a non-exhaustive list of such results is
[
1
,
5
,
3
,
4
,
13
,
20
,
22
,
21
]
.
As a contrast to functionals that arise as sums over certain tuples, one could consider functionals related to extremes, such as the clique number (i.e. size of the largest clique) of the graph. Results of this type, compared to ones about
U
-statistics, are few and far between, most recently by McDiarmid and Müller in
[
16
,
15
]
. Further, the existing literature primarily deals with sparse graphs that arise from models with small distance parameter
r
, such as in
[
14
,
16
,
18
]
; the details of this will be discussed later.

As an extension of the Gilbert graph, random simplicial complexes have also been considered recently: a natural example of this is the clique complex of the Gilbert graph, that is, the simplicial complex given by its complete subgraphs, also known as the Vietoris-Rips complex. A functional of interest in this context is the dimension of the complex: this is defined as the maximal dimension that occurs among its faces. It appears that results for this functional have not been stated explicitly so far; however, in certain constructions, the dimension coincides with other well-known values. In particular, for the clique complex, the dimension is exactly one less than the clique number (the difference being simply due to how the dimension of simplices is defined). In another construction we consider, the Čech complex, the dimension is one less than a scan statistic; these definitions are given later on.
In this paper, we aim to prove new results about expectations, large deviation principles, and a first result about the precise distribution for dense settings.

Let us now introduce the model precisely; a more detailed introduction to some of the concepts is given in Section
2
.
For a finite point set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and parameter
r>0
, the
geometric graph

\mathcal{G}(X,r)
is the graph whose vertices are the points from
X
, and a pair of distinct vertices
x,y\in X
are connected by an edge if and only if
\|x-y\|\leq r
, with
\|\cdot\|
denoting the Euclidean norm.
Now, fix a compact convex set
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit volume. Let
\eta_{t}
be a homogeneous Poisson point process on
W
with intensity
t
, and
r_{t}>0
a distance parameter depending on
t
. Then we call
\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\mathcal{G}(\eta_{t},r_{t})
a
random geometric graph
or
Gilbert-graph
. We will assume that
r_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
; the rate of this convergence, and in particular, the behaviour of the quantity
\rho_{t}:=tr_{t}^{d}
controls the denseness of the graph.
Note that while in this paper we focus on the Poisson case, the case of the binomial point process - where the set of vertices is given by an independently chosen sample of uniform points in
W
- is just as well-studied.
We also mention that while we work with the Euclidean distance, most of the proofs are easily adapted to general metrics. In particular, the maximum (or
l_{\infty}
) norm case has been considered instead many times, as explicit computations are often easier to handle.

Denote by
\eta_{t,\neq}^{k}
the set of all
k
-tuples of distinct points of
\eta_{t}
.
The
Vietoris-Rips complex

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(\eta_{t},r_{t})
is defined as the clique complex of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
, that is, the tuple
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\}\in\eta_{t,\neq}^{k+1}
is in
\mathcal{V}
whenever the points form a complete subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
.
Equivalently,
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\}
is a
k
-dimensional face (or
k
-face
) of
\mathcal{V}
if and only if
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq r_{t}
for every
i,j=0,\ldots,k
. On the other hand,
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\}
is a simplex in the
Čech complex

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\eta_{t},r_{t})
if and only if the points are contained in a ball of radius
r_{t}/2
, i.e. there is
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|x_{i}-y\|\leq r_{t}/2
for all
i=0,\ldots,k
.
This can also be formulated using a slightly more geometric language:
\mathcal{V}
contains
k
-tuples with
diameter
at most
r_{t}
, and
\mathcal{C}
with
circumradius
at most
r_{t}
. Both of these values can be interpreted as a measure of closeness of the point set
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\}
. As such, one could also think of
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{C}
as sets of clusters, which are defined as ’close’ sets of points.
Note that every simplex of the Čech complex is also in the Vietoris-Rips complex; the converse generally does not hold. (However, it does in the
l_{\infty}
case.)

The set of
k
-faces of the Vietoris-Rips complex and the Čech complex is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathcal{C}}
, respectively, and their number by
f_{k}^{\mathcal{V}}
and
f_{k}^{\mathcal{C}}
. We define the
dimension
of a simplicial complex as
\max\{k\colon f_{k}>0\}
. Throughout the paper, the dimension will be denoted by
D_{\mathcal{V}}
and
D_{\mathcal{C}}
for the Vietoris-Rips and Čech complexes, respectively.
Importantly, as mentioned before,
D_{\mathcal{V}}-1
is the clique number of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
, and
D_{\mathcal{C}}-1
is the maximal number of points in some translate of
r_{t}B^{d}
, called a
scan statistic
: formally, for a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we call
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{t}(x+A)
a scan statistic with scanning set
A
. We then have, by definition of the Čech complex, that
D_{\mathcal{C}}-1
is a scan statistic with scanning set
r_{t}B^{d}
. In general, we can also think of the dimension as (one less than) the size of the largest cluster of
\eta_{t}
.
Note that unless stated otherwise, omitting the sub- or superscript
\mathcal{C}
or
\mathcal{V}
means that a statement holds for both constructions; we will emphasise the possible differences whenever necessary.

Throughout the paper, we will primarily be using the language of simplicial complexes; however, in many cases, it is useful to think of the connection to the underlying graph, the clique number, as well as scan statistics and clusters, especially when considering the existing literature.

Our main results are the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\eta_{t}
be a homogeneous Poisson point process on the unit volume compact convex body
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity
t>0
. Choose
r_{t}>0
such that
r_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, and let
\rho_{t}:=tr_{t}^{d}
. Let
D
denote the dimension of the Vietoris-Rips or Čech complexes built on
\eta_{t}
with distance parameter
r_{t}
. Then the following holds.

i)

If
\rho_{t}=O(t^{-\alpha})
for some
\alpha>0
, then there exists a unique
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{E}f_{k-1}\to\infty
and
\mathbb{E}f_{k}\to\lambda\in[0,\infty)
as
t\to\infty
. Letting
Z
be a random variable with
\mathbb{P}(Z=k-1)=e^{-\lambda}=1-\mathbb{P}(Z=k)
, it holds for the total variation distance
{\rm d}_{TV}
that

{\rm d}_{TV}(D,Z)=O\big((t\rho_{t}^{k-1})^{-1}\big)+O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)+O(\mathbb{E}f_{k}-\lambda)=o(1),

and in particular,
\mathbb{P}(D\in\{k-1,k\})\to 1
. Furthermore, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\big|\mathbb{E}D^{m}-\mathbb{E}Z^{m}\big|=O\big((t\rho_{t}^{k-1})^{-1}\big)+O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)+O(\mathbb{E}f_{k}-\lambda)=o(1).

ii)

If
\rho_{t}/t^{-\alpha}\to\infty
for all
\alpha>0
, but
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to 0
, then there exists
k=k_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathbb{P}(D\in\{k_{t}-1,k_{t}\})\to 1\text{ and }\mathbb{E}D^{m}\sim k_{t}^{m}\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where

k_{t}\sim\frac{\ln t}{\ln(\ln t/\rho_{t})}\to\infty\text{ as }t\to\infty.

iii)

If
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to B\in(0,\infty)
, then

D\sim\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\cdot\beta\cdot\rho_{t}\text{ in probability, and }\mathbb{E}D^{m}\sim\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\cdot\beta\cdot\rho_{t}\right)^{m}\text{ as }t\to\infty

where
\kappa_{d}
denotes the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball, and
\beta>1
is the unique number satisfying
\beta\ln\beta-\beta+1=(B\cdot\kappa_{d}/2^{d})^{-1}
.

iv)

If
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
, then

D\sim\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\cdot\rho_{t}\text{ in probability, and }\mathbb{E}D^{m}\sim\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\cdot\rho_{t}\right)^{m}\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where
\kappa_{d}
denotes the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball.

The symbol
\sim
means that the ratio of the quantities tends to
1
.
A fact of particular note is that the expression
\rho_{t}\cdot\kappa_{d}/2^{d}
appearing in the last two regimes is exactly the expected number of points in an arbitrary ball of radius
r_{t}/2
within
W
; clearly, the number of points in any such fixed ball is a lower bound for the dimension (in either complex). It is quite natural that
D/\rho_{t}\to\infty
would holds with high probability; however, somewhat surprisingly, in the denser regimes the dimension and the expected number of points in a fixed ball are of the same order of magnitude, and in fact asymptotically the same if
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
.

We also mention that for a wide range of functionals, a shift in behaviour occurs when the limit of
\rho_{t}
changes from zero to a nonzero constant to infinity. In our case, however, the shifts happen for different asymptotics of
\rho_{t}
as seen in the theorem.

In the setting where the vertices arise from a binomial point process
X_{n}
with parameter
n
(as opposed to a Poisson point process), the clique number of
\mathcal{G}(X_{n},r_{n})
was investigated in
[
16
,
15
]
, the scan statistic in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
[
14
]
, as well as a more general set of results in
[
18
]
.
In particular,
[
16
,
14
,
18
]
give two-point concentration results in the sparser regimes, while in
[
15
]
an almost sure convergence is shown in all regimes, with the same asymptotics as in our results. Kahle et al.
[
12
]
considered similar questions in perturbed (binomial) random geometric graphs.

Our next result concerns large deviations, whose definition we shortly recall here (for more details see
[
8
]
).
A family
(\mathbb{P}_{t})_{t>0}
of probability measures on a topological space
\mathcal{X}
with
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}
is said to satisfy a large deviations principle (LDP) with speed
s_{t}\to\infty
and good rate function
I(\cdot)
if the levels sets
\{x:I(x)\leq\alpha\}
are compact for all
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
, and for all
B\in\mathcal{B}

(1)

-\inf_{x\in\inter B}I(x)\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}s_{t}^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}_{t}(B)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}s_{t}^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}_{t}(B)\leq-\inf_{x\in{\rm cl}\,B}I(x)

holds, where
\inter(\cdot)
and
{\rm cl}\,(\cdot)
stand for the interior and closure operator, respectively. Further, we say that a family
(X_{t})_{t>0}
of random variables satisfies an LDP if their distributions do.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\eta_{t}
,
\rho_{t}
and
D
be as in
Theorem

1
, and assume
\rho_{t}/t^{-\alpha}\to\infty
for all
\alpha>0
. Then
D/n_{t}
satisfies an LDP with speed
m_{t}
and good rate function
I(x)
, where

i) for
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to 0
:

\displaystyle n_{t}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{\ln t}{\ln(t/\rho_{t})},

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=\ln t,

and

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}x-1&\text{ if }x\geq 1,\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise;}\end{cases}

ii) for
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to B\in(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle n_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\rho_{t},

and

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1+x\ln x-x-\frac{1}{B}&\text{ if }x\geq\beta,\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise;}\end{cases}

iii) for
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
:

\displaystyle n_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\rho_{t},

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{2^{d}}\rho_{t},

and

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1+x\ln x-x&\text{ if }x\geq 1,\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

To the author’s knowledge, there are no precise distribution results known about the dimension in the
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
regime. This is the topic of the following result for
d=1
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\eta_{t}
be a homogeneous Poisson point process on
W=[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
, and let
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
. Then
D
satisfies a Gumbel limit theorem; more precisely,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{D-a_{t}}{b_{t}}\leq x\right)\to e^{-e^{-x}}\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where

(2)

\begin{gathered}a_{t}=\rho_{t}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(t/\rho_{t})}{\rho_{t}}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\ln(\ln(t/\rho_{t}))}{4\ln(t/\rho_{t})}-\frac{\ln\sqrt{\pi}}{2\ln(t/\rho_{t})}\right)\right)\\
\text{ and }b_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{t}}{2\ln(t/\rho_{t})}}.\end{gathered}

This is obtained as a consequence of the following.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\eta_{t}
be a homogeneous Poisson point process on
W=[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
, and let
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
.
Let
I_{n}:=[nr_{t},(n+1)r_{t})\cap[0,1]
for
n=1,\ldots,N:=\lfloor 1/r_{t}\rfloor
, and for arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
X_{n}^{(k)}
the indicator of the event that
I_{n}\cup I_{n+1}
contains an interval of length
r_{t}
with at least
k
points. Define
X^{(k)}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}^{(k)}
, and let
Z^{(k)}\sim\textnormal{Po}(\mathbb{E}X^{(k)})
. Then, if
k=k_{t}=a_{t}+x\cdot b_{t}
as defined in (
2
), then
EX^{(k)}\to e^{-x}
, and

{\rm d}_{TV}(X^{(k)},Z^{(k)})=O\left(\frac{1}{\ln(1/r_{t})}\right),

where
{\rm d}_{TV}
denotes the total variation distance.
In particular, convergence in distribution to a Poisson random variable with parameter
e^{-x}
follows.

Clearly, we have that
D\geq k-1\Leftrightarrow X^{(k)}\geq 1
, and
Theorem

3
automatically follows.

We suspect that a similar result should hold in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for arbitrary
d>1
as well, with the intervals being replaced by cubes, and as a consequence, a limit theorem for the dimension in the
l_{\infty}
-norm; however, getting such a precise result on the distribution appears to be difficult, the question of which will be discussed in the proof. Note also that this result quantifies how much larger the dimension is compared to the lower bound of the number of points in a fixed ball: we get an additional logarithmic factor in the second-order term.

2
Preliminaries

We work in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
. The Euclidean norm is denoted by
\|\cdot\|
, and we write
\Vol(\cdot)
for the volume (i.e. Lebesgue measure) of a measurable set.
We write
B^{d}(x,r)
for the ball with center
x
and radius
r
, and as a special case,
B^{d}:=B^{d}(0,1)
for the origin-centered unit ball, and
\kappa_{d}:=\Vol B^{d}
its volume.

We use the following asymptotic notation: for nonnegative functions
g
and
h
, we write

\displaystyle g=o(h)\quad\text{ for }\quad

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}=0;

\displaystyle g=\omega(h)\quad\text{ for }\quad

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}=\infty;

\displaystyle g=O(h)\quad\text{ for }\quad

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}<\infty;

\displaystyle g=\Omega(h)\quad\text{ for }\quad

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}>0;

as well as
g=\Theta(h)
when both
g=O(h)
and
h=O(g)
, and
g\sim h
for
\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}g(x)/h(x)=1
.

To measure the closeness of real-valued random variables, we use the total variation distance
{\rm d}_{TV}
given by

{\rm d}_{TV}(X,Y):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})}|\mathbb{P}(X\in A)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in A)|.

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed compact convex subset, and assume
\Vol(W)=1
. Let
\mathbf{N}(W)
denote the space of simple finite counting measures on
W
; note that generally, we will identify a counting measure with its support, that is, consider it as a finite set of points lying in
W
. Then the
homogeneous Poisson point process

\eta_{t}
on
W
with parameter
t>0
is a random variable with values in
\mathbf{N}(W)
, satisfying the property that for a Borel set
A\subseteq W
, the number of random points in
A
, denoted by
\eta_{t}(A)
, is Poisson distributed with parameter
t\cdot\Vol(A)
, and the number of points in disjoint sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq W
are independent. We call
t
the
intensity
of the process.

An important property of the Poisson point process is the Mecke formula. In its multivariate form, it states the following: if
f:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbf{N}(W)\to\mathbb{R}
is a nonnegative function, then

\mathbb{E}\sum_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\eta_{t,\neq}^{k}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\eta_{t})=t^{k}\int\limits_{W^{k}}\mathbb{E}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\eta_{t}+\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{k}}){\rm d}x_{1}\ldots{\rm d}x_{k},

where
\eta_{t,\neq}^{k}
denotes the set of distinct
k
-tuples of
\eta_{t}
.
Note that if
f
does not depend on
\eta_{t}
, then the expectation on the right hand side can be dropped.

In what follows, we consider the Vietoris-Rips and Čech complexes with vertex set
\eta_{t}
.
Recall that the tuple
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\}\in\eta_{t,\neq}^{k+1}
is in
\mathcal{V}(\eta_{t},r_{t})
if and only if their pairwise distances are at most
r_{t}
, and in
\mathcal{C}(\eta_{t},r_{t})
if and only if they are contained in a ball of radius
r_{t}/2
.
These tuples will sometimes be referred to as
k
-faces
induced
by
\eta_{t}
; unless stated otherwise, this can mean either of the above constructions.

We recall a well-known result about the
f
-vector, see
[
19
]
.
For arbitrary
n\geq 0
,

(3)

\mathbb{E}f_{n}\sim\frac{\mu_{n}}{(n+1)!}\cdot t\cdot\rho_{t}^{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{V}f_{n}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\Phi_{n,i}\cdot t\cdot\rho_{t}^{2n-i+1}\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where

\mu_{n}:=\int_{B^{d}}\ldots\int_{B^{d}}\mathbf{1}\left(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\text{ forms $(n-1)$-face with distance $1$}\right){\rm d}x_{1}\ldots{\rm d}x_{n}

for
n\geq 1
and
\mu_{0}:=1
, and
\Phi_{n,i}
is a positive constant depending only on
n
and
i
.
Note that as these expressions differ for
n\geq 3
for the two complexes, the constants in the asymptotic formulas do as well.
It is also known that if
\mathbb{E}f_{n}
tends to a nonzero constant
\lambda
, then
f_{n}
tends in distribution to a Poisson distributed random variable with parameter
\lambda
(see again
[
19
]
).

We also state some standard bounds and asymptotics.
To approximate factorials, we will use Stirling’s formula
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
; however, it will also be helpful to have a bound for all positive integers, not just an asymptotic result. It is well-known (see e.g.
[
23
]
) that for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{12n+1}}<

\displaystyle n!<\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{12n}},

and by considering the range of the additional exponential factor, we also have the crude bounds

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}<2\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}<

\displaystyle n!<3\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}.

To bound binomial coefficients, we use the standard inequality
\binom{n}{k}\geq\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{k}
.

For a Poisson-distributed random variable with parameter
\lambda
, denoted by
\textnormal{Po}(\lambda)
, the following estimates, known as the Chernoff-Hoeffding bounds (see for example
[
19
]
), hold:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\lambda)\geq k)\leq e^{-\lambda}\cdot\left(\frac{e\lambda}{k}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\text{ if }k\geq\lambda,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\lambda)\leq k)\leq e^{-\lambda}\cdot\left(\frac{e\lambda}{k}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\text{ if }k\leq\lambda.

Using Stirling’s formula, we also have the precise asymptotic

(6)

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\lambda)=k)=e^{-\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\sim\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\sqrt{2\pi k}}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda e}{k}\right)^{k},

as
k\to\infty
, and the corresponding bound for all
\lambda
and
k

(7)

\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{3\sqrt{2\pi k}}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda e}{k}\right)^{k}<\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\lambda)=k)<\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{2\sqrt{2\pi k}}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda e}{k}\right)^{k}.

3
Proof of the expectation and LDP

In this section, we obtain some upper and lower bounds on the probability
\mathbb{P}(D\geq k)
for appropriately chosen
k
.
Assume that the condition
\rho_{t}=O(t^{-\alpha})
does not hold for any
\alpha>0
, i.e.
\rho_{t}/t^{-\alpha}\to\infty
for all
\alpha>0
.
First, we collect some technical details and preliminary results. These, along with the strategies used for the bounds, mostly come from
[
15
]
, where the case of the binomial process was considered.

The following lemma is a special case of
[
16
]
*Lemma 3.1.

Lemma 1
.

There are
\Theta(r_{t}^{-d})
-many disjoint translates of
r_{t}B^{d}
contained in the compact convex set
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The next result essentially appears as a part of a proof in
[
15
]
.
We state it as a separate lemma to make it easier to reference later, and repeat the relevant parts of the proof shown in
[
15
]
for understandability (see
[
15
]
*Section 3.3, Proof of the upper bound).

Denote by
M_{A}:=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{x,A}
the scan statistic with scanning set
r_{t}A
, where
M_{x,A}:=\eta_{t}(x+r_{t}A)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body and
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary. Then there exists
C=C(\varepsilon,A)
such that for arbitrary integer
k>0
, it holds for large enough
t
that

\mathbb{P}(M_{A}\geq k)\leq Cr_{t}^{-d}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t}\cdot\Vol A_{\varepsilon})\geq k),

where
A_{\varepsilon}:=A+\varepsilon B^{d}
denotes the
\varepsilon
parallel body of
A
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
R>0
satisfying
A_{\varepsilon}\subseteq[0,R)^{d}
, and let
U
be uniformly distributed on
[0,Rr_{t})^{d}
. Denote the lattice
U+Rr_{t}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
by
\Gamma(U)
.

It is clear by definition that if
x\in p+r_{t}A
, and
\|p-q\|\leq r_{t}\varepsilon
, then
x\in q+r_{t}A_{\varepsilon}
, implying that if
q\in B(p,r_{t}\varepsilon)
, we have that
M_{q,A_{\varepsilon}}\geq M_{p,A}
.
Now, the probability that for any given point
p
, there is a point
q
of
\Gamma(U)
that is also in
B(p,r_{t}\varepsilon)
, i.e.
B(p,r_{t}\varepsilon)\cap\Gamma(U)
is nonempty, is
\Vol(r_{t}\varepsilon B^{d})/\Vol([0,Rr_{t})^{d})=\kappa_{d}\varepsilon^{d}/R^{d}
.
These together imply that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{x\in\Gamma(U)}M_{x,A_{\varepsilon}}\geq k\ |\ \max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{x,A}\geq k\right)\geq\frac{\kappa_{d}}{R^{d}}\cdot\varepsilon^{d},

and consequently,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{x,A}\geq k\right)\leq\frac{R^{d}\varepsilon^{-d}}{\kappa_{d}}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{x\in\Gamma(U)}M_{x,A_{\varepsilon}}\geq k\right)\leq\frac{R^{d}\varepsilon^{-d}}{\kappa_{d}}\sum_{x\in\Gamma(U)}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{x,A_{\varepsilon}}\geq k\right).

By definition,

\mathbb{P}(M_{x,A_{\varepsilon}}\geq k)=\mathbb{P}\big(\textnormal{Po}(t\cdot\Vol(W\cap(x+r_{t}A_{\varepsilon})\geq k\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t}\cdot\Vol A_{\varepsilon})\geq k)\big).

By the assumptions, if the distance of a point
x
from
W
is greater than
Rc\cdot r_{t}
, where
c:=\diam[0,1]^{d}
, we have that the sets
x+r_{t}A_{\varepsilon}
and
W
are disjoint, and hence
M_{x,A_{\varepsilon}}=0
.
This implies that the maximum can only be attained at the lattice points of
Rr_{t}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that are contained in
W+Rcr_{t}B^{d}
. As there are
\Theta(r_{t}^{-d})
-many such points by Lemma
1
, this implies the statement.
∎

We note that while the constant
C
depends on the set
A
through the choice of
R
, it can be chosen universally under some conditions. In particular, if a family
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of convex bodies is uniformly bounded, then there is a constant
C
such that the upper bound of the lemma holds for all
A_{i}
(
i\in I
).

Proof of the lower bound.

For the lower bound, the approach for the two constructions is the same. Also note that for higher moments, we obtain an automatic lower bound from Jensen’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}D^{m}\geq(\mathbb{E}D)^{m}
, hence it is enough to consider the expectation.

For
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to\infty
, the lower bound of
\mu:=\kappa_{d}/2^{d}\cdot\rho_{t}
for
\mathbb{E}D
is trivial: for a fixed ball of radius
r_{t}/2
, its number of points is a lower bound for
D
, and thus
\mathbb{E}D\geq\mathbb{E}\textnormal{Po}(t\Vol(r_{t}/2B^{d}))=\mu
.
Otherwise, we use the following, equally straightforward geometric argument, also used in the proof of
[
16
]
*Lemma 4.1 and
[
15
]
*Lemma 3.8. If
D<k
, then every ball of radius
r_{t}/2
contains at most
k
points; further, by Lemma
1
, there are
K=\Theta(r_{t}^{-d})
-many disjoint balls of radius
r_{t}/2
contained in our observation window
W
. Hence it follows that

\mathbb{P}(D<k)\leq\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)\leq k)^{K}.

For the
\mu/\ln t\to\infty
case, this is simple, as
\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)\leq\mu)\to 1/2
, and thus
\mathbb{P}(D<\mu)\to 0
.
For the rest of the lower bound proof, assume
\mu=O(\ln t)
.
Then we bound the expression above by

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)\leq k)\leq 1-\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)=k+1)\leq\exp\{-\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)=k+1)\},

resulting in

\mathbb{P}(D<k)\leq\exp\{-K\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)=k+1)\}=\exp\left\{-\Theta\left\{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu)=k+1)\cdot\frac{t}{\mu}\right\}\right\},

where we also used that
K^{-1}=\Theta(r_{t}^{d})=\Theta(t/\mu)
.
We also trivially have that

\mathbb{E}D\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(D\geq i)\geq k-k\mathbb{P}(D<k).

For the two remaining regimes, we show that with the suitable choice of
k=k_{t}
(given by the asymptotics in the theorem) and arbitrary (but small enough)
\gamma>0
, we have with
n=k(1-\gamma)
that

\alpha_{n}^{(L)}:=-\ln\mathbb{P}(D<n)=\Omega\left(\frac{t}{\mu}\cdot e^{-\mu}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu e}{n+1}\right)^{n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\right)

tends to infinity faster than
\ln k
, implying on one hand that
\mathbb{P}(D<k(1-\gamma))\to 0
, and the other that
k\mathbb{P}(D<k(1-\gamma))\to 0
and thus
\mathbb{E}D\geq k(1-\gamma)+o(1)
. As
\gamma
was arbitrary, this implies the lower tail part of both convergence in probability, and the asymptotic value of the expectation.
For simplicity, consider

(8)

\beta_{n}^{(L)}:=\ln\alpha_{n}^{(L)}=c+\ln t-\ln\mu+(n-\mu)-n\ln\frac{n}{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\ln n.

-

If
\mu\sim B^{\prime}\ln t=B\kappa_{d}/2^{d}\ln t
, put
k=\beta\mu
, where
\beta>1
is the unique number satisfying
\beta\ln\beta-\beta+1=1/B^{\prime}
, to get

\beta_{n}^{(L)}\geq c+\ln t-\left(\frac{1}{B^{\prime}}+c_{\beta,\gamma}\right)\mu-\frac{3}{2}\ln\mu,

where
c_{\beta,\gamma}=\beta\cdot(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\ln(1-\gamma)-\gamma\ln\beta)
. We can analytically see that for small enough
\gamma
(depending on
\beta
),
c_{\beta,\gamma}<0
, and we have a speed of convergence of order
\mu
.

-

If
\mu/\ln t\to 0
, choose
k=\ln t/\ln(\ln t/\mu)
. By the choice of regime, we have that the denominator is defined (for large enough
t
) and tends to infinity with
t
, and thus
\ln t=\omega(k)
; since we also have that
\mu=\omega(t^{-\alpha})
for all
\alpha>0
, it also follows that
k\to\infty
. We have

\beta_{n}^{(L)}\geq c+\ln t-\ln\mu-\frac{1}{2}\ln k-\mu+\frac{\ln t}{\ln(\ln t/\mu)}(1-\gamma)\left[c^{\prime}+\ln\left(\frac{\ln t}{\mu}\right)-\ln\ln\left(\frac{\ln t}{\mu}\right)\right]=\\
=\ln t\left[1-(1-\gamma)\left[1+O\left(\frac{\ln\ln(\ln t/\mu)}{\ln(\ln t/\mu)}\right)\right]+O\left(\frac{\ln\ln t}{\ln t}\right)+O\left(\frac{\mu}{\ln t}\right)\right]

and the expression tends to infinity like
\ln t
as
t\to\infty
.

To conclude, in both cases,
\alpha_{n}^{(L)}=e^{\beta_{n}^{(L)}}
does tend to infinity much faster than
\ln k
, implying the necessary lower bounds.

Proof of the upper bound.

We have that the
m
-th moment can be written as

\mathbb{E}D^{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{m}\mathbb{P}(D=i)\leq n^{m}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}i^{m}\mathbb{P}(D\geq i)=:n^{m}+\alpha_{n}^{(U)}

for some integer
n
. We again show that for arbitrary
\gamma>0
, with
n=k(1+\gamma)
we have that both
\mathbb{P}(D\geq n)
and
\alpha_{n}^{(U)}
tend to
0
.

The two constructions require a somewhat different method.
We start with
D_{\mathcal{C}}
, and later slightly modify the approach to be applicable to
D_{\mathcal{V}}
as well.
Since
D_{\mathcal{C}}-1
is a scan statistic, namely
D_{\mathcal{C}}-1=M_{r_{t}/2B}
, we can apply Lemma
2
.

The value of
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\gamma)>0
is to be specified later; note that the volume of
B(0,r_{t}/2)_{\varepsilon}=B(0,(1+\varepsilon)r_{t}/2)
is exactly
\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}
.
By Lemma
2
, and using the bound (
4
) for the Poisson distribution, we have that for all
i\geq k>\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}
,

(9)

\mathbb{P}(D_{\mathcal{C}}\geq i)=O\left(r_{t}^{-d}\cdot e^{-\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}e}{i}\right)^{i}\right)

where we used that
r_{t}^{d}=\Theta(t/\mu)
.
Using the bound above, as well as Stirling’s approximation for the factorial, we obtain that

\alpha_{n}^{(U)}=O\left(\frac{t\mu^{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}}\cdot(\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d})^{i-m-1}}{(i-m-1)!}\right)=\\
=O\left(\frac{t\mu^{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot e^{-\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}e}{n-m-1}\right)^{n-m-1}\right)

whenever
n-m-1>\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}
, where in the second expression, the sum is exactly
\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d})\geq n-m-1)
, and can be bounded by (
4
). Note that the constant hidden by the
O(\cdot)
notation is dependent on
\varepsilon
and thus
\gamma
, but not
t
(or
i
).

Now, set
(1+\varepsilon)^{d}=1+\gamma
and
n-m-2=k(1+\gamma)
. We then have that

\beta_{n}^{(U)}:=-\ln\alpha_{n}^{(U)}=c-\ln t-m\ln\mu+\frac{1}{2}\ln k+(1+\gamma)\left[\mu-k+k\ln\frac{k}{\mu}\right],

which we aim to send to infinity with
t
.

-

If
\mu/\ln t\to\infty
, set
k=\mu
to get

\beta_{n}^{(U)}=-\ln t-\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln\mu+2\mu(1+\gamma)\ln(1+\gamma)\sim 2\mu(1+\gamma)\ln(1+\gamma)\to\infty

as the coefficient of
\mu
is positive.

-

If
\mu\sim B^{\prime}\ln t=B\kappa_{d}/2^{d}\ln t
, set
k=\beta\mu
with
\beta>1
satisfying
\beta\ln\beta-\beta+1=1/B^{\prime}
to get

\beta_{n}^{(U)}=\frac{\gamma}{B^{\prime}}\cdot\mu-\left(\ln t-\frac{\mu}{B^{\prime}}\right)-\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln\mu\sim\frac{\gamma}{B^{\prime}}\cdot\mu\to\infty;

-

If
\mu/\ln t\to 0
, set
k=\ln t/\ln(\ln t/\mu)
to get

\beta_{n}^{(U)}=\ln t\left(-1+(1+\gamma)\left[1+\Theta\left(\frac{\ln\ln\frac{\ln t}{\mu}}{\ln\frac{\ln t}{\mu}}\right)\right]+\Theta\left(\frac{\ln\ln t}{\ln t}\right)\right)\to\infty.

This shows the statement for the Čech complex. For the Vietoris-Rips complex, we again follow the approach from
[
15
]
.

Fix
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, and let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\subseteq\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be all the subsets of
\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that contain the origin and have diameter at most
1+2c\varepsilon^{\prime}
, where
c:=\diam[0,1]^{d}
. Also let
W_{j}:={\rm conv}\,{A_{j}}

(j=1,\ldots,K)
.

Assume now that the points
X_{0},\ldots,X_{k}
form a
k
-face of
\mathcal{V}(\eta_{t},r_{t})
, meaning that each pairwise distance is at most
r_{t}
. Then the point set
y_{i}:=(X_{i}-X_{0})\cdot r_{t}^{-1}
for
i=0,1,\ldots k
contains the origin and has diameter at most
1
; hence the set
A:=\{p\in\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\ |\ \|p-y_{i}\|\leq c\varepsilon^{\prime}\text{ for some }i=0,\ldots,k\}
also contains the origin and has diameter at most
1+2c\varepsilon^{\prime}
. This implies that
A
coincides with one of the
A_{j}
defined above. Also note that
y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}\in{\rm conv}\,A
, and thus
X_{0},\ldots,X_{k}\in X_{0}+r_{t}{\rm conv}\,A
. This implies that
M_{W_{j}}\geq k
, and thus
\max_{j=1,\ldots,K}M_{W_{j}}\geq D_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Using this statement, as well as Lemma
2
, we obtain that for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
C
such that

\mathbb{P}(D_{\mathcal{V}}\geq k)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(M_{W_{j}}\geq k)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}Cr_{t}^{-d}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t}\Vol W_{j,\varepsilon})\geq k),

where
W_{j,\varepsilon}:=(W_{j})_{\varepsilon}=W_{j}+\varepsilon B^{d}
. Note that
C=C(\varepsilon)
, but as the family
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
is universally bounded, we may choose it to be independent of the scanning set itself.
Now, it is known from the isodiametric inequality (see
[
11
]
) that among convex bodies with fixed diameter, the ball has maximal volume. Since
W_{j,\varepsilon}
has diameter at most
1+2c\varepsilon^{\prime}+2\varepsilon
, this implies that
\Vol W_{i,\varepsilon}\leq\left(\frac{1+2c\varepsilon^{\prime}+2\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{d}\kappa_{d}
.
Thus

\mathbb{P}(D_{\mathcal{V}}\geq k)\leq K\cdot\varepsilon^{-d}r_{t}^{-d}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\mu(1+2c\varepsilon^{\prime}+2\varepsilon)^{d})\geq k).

Choosing
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
such that
(1+2c\varepsilon^{\prime}+2\varepsilon)^{d}=1+\gamma
, the proof can be carried out the same way as for the Čech complex.
∎

Proof of the LDP

To prove
Theorem

2
, we simply use the bounds on the distribution obtained above. First, we show that the defining chain of inequalities (
1
) holds for all half-intervals
[a,\infty)
, which then easily extends to all intervals
[a,b)
. Second, we show that the family of probability measures is exponentially tight, which, together with the statement for the intervals, yields the full LDP (see
[
8
]
*Theorem 4.1.11, Lemma 1.2.18).

For an upper bound, we have for
a\geq 1
that

\mathbb{P}(D\geq an_{t})=1-\mathbb{P}(D<an_{t})\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\alpha_{an_{t}}^{(L)}\right\}=\Theta(\alpha_{an_{t}}^{(L)})

and thus

-\ln\mathbb{P}(D\geq an_{t})\leq-\beta_{an_{t}}^{(L)}=c-\ln t+\ln\mu-(an_{t}-\mu)+an_{t}\ln\frac{an_{t}}{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\ln(an_{t})

as in (
8
).
For the lower bound, we have from (
9
) that

-\ln\mathbb{P}(D\geq an_{t})\geq c-\ln t+\ln\mu-(an_{t}-\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d})+an_{t}\ln\frac{an_{t}}{\mu(1+\varepsilon)^{d}}.

Plugging in the values for
n_{t}
in each regime, we obtain the speed and rate function stated for the asymptotics; note that
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary. For
a<1
, we have
\mathbb{P}(D\geq an_{t})\to 1
, as explicitly shown in the proof of the expectation.
From this, it easily follows that the defining inequalities hold for
[a,b)
for arbitrary
a<b
: it is essentially trivial for
a
or
b
below the location of the minimum (
1
, or
\beta
in the
\mu=\Theta(\ln t)
case). For
a,b
at least this value, we have that

-\ln\mathbb{P}(a\leq D/n_{t}<b)=-\ln\mathbb{P}(D/n_{t}\geq a)-\ln\left(1-\frac{\mathbb{P}(D/n_{t}\geq b)}{\mathbb{P}(D/n_{t}\geq a)}\right),

and the ratio of probabilities tends to zero since its logarithm
\ln\mathbb{P}(D/n_{t}\geq b)-\ln\mathbb{P}(D/n_{t}\geq a)\to-\infty
, following from the monotonicity of the rate function
I(x)
(on the interval where it is not infinity).

Lastly, exponential tightness (see Equation (1.2.17) in
[
8
]
) follows easily from
I(x)\to\infty

(x\to\infty)
: for arbitrary
M
, there is
K
such that
I(K)>M
, and thus

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}(D/n_{t}\in[0,K]^{c})}{m_{t}}<-M,

with
\cdot^{c}
denoting the complement.
This concludes the proof of the full LDP.
∎

4
Proof of the two-point concentration

In this section, we prove the two-point concentration results of
Theorem

1
, as well as the expectation in the first regime.

First, let
\rho_{t}:=tr_{t}^{d}=O(t^{-\alpha})
for some
\alpha>0
. This case lends itself to a very straightforward proof using the known properties of the
f
-vector. Namely, on one hand, we have for every integer
n\geq 0
by (
3
) that
\mathbb{E}f_{n}=\Theta(t\rho_{t}^{n})=O(t^{1-n\alpha})
; on the other hand, we have
\rho_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, and thus
\mathbb{V}f_{n}=\Theta\left(t\rho_{t}^{n}\right)=O(t^{1-n\alpha})
as well.
Then for
n>1/\alpha
, we have
\mathbb{E}f_{n}\to 0
, and clearly
\mathbb{E}f_{0}=t\to\infty
. Hence the existence of a
k
satisfying the stated properties (namely, that
\mathbb{E}f_{k-1}\to\infty
and
\mathbb{E}f_{k}\to\lambda<\infty
) follows, where
k\geq 1
, and the uniqueness is clear. Note that
\lambda
might be zero or positive; further, we have that
\mathbb{E}f_{n}\to 0
for all
n>k
.

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}f_{n}

and

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}=0)=\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\leq 0)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{E}f_{n}-f_{n}\geq\mathbb{E}f_{n})\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}f_{n}}{(\mathbb{E}f_{n})^{2}}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}f_{n}}\right)

for arbitrary
n
.
As a consequence, we immediately obtain that

\mathbb{P}(D>k)=\mathbb{P}(f_{k+1}\geq 1)=O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)\ \text{ and }\ \mathbb{P}(D<k-1)=\mathbb{P}(f_{k-1}=0)=O\big((t\rho_{t}^{k-1})^{-1}\big),

with both expressions tending to
0
as
t\to\infty
.
Furthermore, if
\lambda>0
, then
f_{k}
is asymptotically Poisson distributed with parameter
\lambda
; in particular,
{\rm d}_{TV}(f_{k},\textnormal{Po}(\mathbb{E}f_{k}))=O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)
(see
[
19
]
), and thus

|\mathbb{P}(D\geq k)-e^{-\lambda}|=|\mathbb{P}(f_{k}\geq 1)-e^{-\lambda}|=O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)+|e^{-\lambda}-e^{-\mathbb{E}f_{k}}|=O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)+O(\mathbb{E}f_{k}-\lambda)

If
\lambda=0
, we have
\mathbb{P}(f_{k}\geq 1)=O(t\rho_{t}^{k})=O(\mathbb{E}f_{k}-\lambda)
formally.

Using that

{\rm d}_{TV}(D,Z)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\mathbb{P}(D=n)-\mathbb{P}(Z=n)|,

the statement for the total variation distance follows.

Lastly, consider the moments. Let
l:=\max\{m,k\}
. It follows from the previous argument that

\left|\sum_{n=0}^{l}n^{m}\mathbb{P}(D=n)-((k-1)^{m}e^{-\lambda}+k^{m}(1-e^{-\lambda}))\right|=O\big((t\rho_{t}^{k-1})^{-1}\big)+O\big(t\rho_{t}^{k+1}\big)+O(\mathbb{E}f_{k}-\lambda).

Hence it remains to be shown that the tail starting at
n=l+1
has at most this order.
By a straightforward geometric argument and Markov’s inequality, as well as bounds on the binomial coefficient,

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(f_{l+1}\geq\binom{n+1}{l+2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}f_{l+1}}{\binom{n+1}{l+2}}\leq\mathbb{E}f_{l+1}\cdot\left(\frac{l+2}{n+1}\right)^{l+2},

for all
n\geq l+1
, and it follows that

\sum_{n=l+1}^{\infty}n^{m}\mathbb{P}(D\geq n)\leq m(l+2)^{l+2}\mathbb{E}f_{l+1}\sum_{n=l+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{l+2-m}}

The sum is convergent, since
l+2-m\geq 2
as a consequence of
l\geq m
, and
\mathbb{E}f_{l+1}=\Theta(t\rho_{t}^{l+1})=O(t\rho_{t}^{k+1})
by
l\geq k
, which together imply the statement.

We now turn to the proof of the other regime, where
\rho_{t}=O(t^{-\alpha})
does not hold for any
\alpha>0
(but the upper bound
\rho_{t}/\ln t\to 0
does). Unfortunately, the number of
n
-faces are not good (enough) indicators for
D
anymore. The following argument comes from
[
16
]
, altered to fit the Poisson setting instead of the binomial therein.

Let
N_{n}
denote the number of points that are part of some
n
-face, and
M_{n}
the number of pairs of points that are contained in some
n
-face (together). On one hand,
D<n
is equivalent to
N_{n}=0
; on the other hand,
D\geq n
implies that
M_{n}\geq\binom{n+1}{2}
. Thus by Markov’s inequality, we have that

(10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(D<n)&=\mathbb{P}(N_{n}=0)\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}N_{n}}{(\mathbb{E}N_{n})^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\\
\mathbb{P}(D\geq n)&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(M_{n}\geq\binom{n+1}{2}\right)\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}M_{n}}{(n+1)(n+2)}\end{split}

It seems that the reason these functionals describe the behaviour of the dimension better than
f_{n}
is that for large
n
(in particular,
n=n_{t}\to\infty
), even a single
n+k
-face supplies a large number (in particular,
\binom{n+k+1}{n+1}
) of
n
-faces, which is large in
n
even for fixed
k
; thus the expected number of
n
-faces can be large, even if their existence isn’t very likely. Considering instead the
number of points
in
n
-faces (somewhat) gets rid of this bias.

To keep the computation more concise, write
A_{x}
and
A_{x}^{(x,y)}
for the event that
x
is contained in a
n
-face induced by
\eta_{t}+\delta_{x}
and
\eta_{t}+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}
, respectively.

We start with
N_{n}
and the lower tail. Applying the Mecke formula, we have that

\mathbb{E}N_{n}=\sum_{x\in\eta_{t}}\mathbf{1}\left(x\in\sigma\text{ for some }\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)=t\int_{W}\mathbb{P}(A_{x}){\rm d}x,

and

\mathbb{E}N_{n}^{2}=t\int_{W}\mathbb{P}(A_{x}){\rm d}x+t^{2}\iint_{W^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{x}^{(x,y)}\cap A_{y}^{(x,y)}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y,

where for the second moment we considered
N_{n}^{2}
as a double sum over points
x
and
y
, dividing said sum into
x=y
and
x\neq y
parts, to which the Mecke formula can be applied (separately).

The expression
\mathbb{P}(A_{x})
is the same for all
x
for which
B(x,r_{t})\subseteq W
; we denote the set of such points (called the inner parallel body of
W
) by
W_{-r_{t}}
, and write
p_{n}
for this common probability. In addition, for points
z
outside this set, we have
\mathbb{P}(A_{z})\leq p_{n}
. As a consequence,

tp_{n}\geq\mathbb{E}N_{n}\geq\Vol(W_{-r_{t}})\cdot tp_{n}.

Further, by a standard convex geometry result (see
[
11
]
*Section 6), that
\Vol(W_{-r_{t}})=\Vol W-O(r_{t})=1-O(r_{t})
, we have that
\mathbb{E}N_{n}\sim tp_{n}
.

Now, we evaluate the second moment further.
If
\|x-y\|>2r_{t}
, then
B(x,r_{t})\cap B(y,r_{t})=\emptyset
, and hence the regions where their respective neighbours are located are disjoint. As a consequence,
A_{x}^{(x,y)}\cap A_{y}^{(x,y)}=A_{x}\cap A_{y}
, and the latter two events are independent, thus

t^{2}\int_{W}\int_{W\setminus B(x,r_{t})}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{x}^{(x,y)}\cap A_{y}^{(x,y)}\right){\rm d}y{\rm d}x=t^{2}\int_{W}\mathbb{P}(A_{x})\cdot\int_{W\setminus B(x,r_{t})}\mathbb{P}(A_{y}){\rm d}y{\rm d}x\leq(\mathbb{E}N_{n})^{2}.

Now, assume
\|x-y\|\leq 2r_{t}
.
If
A_{x}^{(x,y)}\cap A_{y}^{(x,y)}
holds, then either
A_{x}
holds (i.e.
x
is already in an
n
-face induced by
\eta_{t}+\delta_{x}
), or it doesn’t: in this latter case, every
n
-face induced by
\eta_{t}+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}
that
x
is contained in,
y
must also be contained in. For the first case, we get the simple upper bound of

t^{2}\int_{W}\int_{B(x,2r_{t})}\mathbb{P}(A_{x}){\rm d}x\leq t^{2}\cdot\int_{W}\mathbb{P}(A_{x}){\rm d}x\cdot 4\kappa_{d}r_{t}^{d}=\Theta(\mathbb{E}N_{n}\cdot\rho_{t}).

Lastly, we deal with the second case. Clearly, then
x
and
y
must be connected by an edge, and thus
\|x-y\|\leq r_{t}
.
We condition on
B(x,r_{t})
containing
N
points of
\eta_{t}
to get

I:=t^{2}\int_{W}\sum_{N=k-2}^{\infty}I_{N,x}\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\textnormal{Po}(t\Vol(B(x,r_{t})\cap W))=N\big){\rm d}x

with

I_{N,x}:=\int_{B(x,r_{t})\cap W}\mathbb{P}\left(f_{n-1}^{\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},y\}}\geq 1,\ y\in\bigcap\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},y\}}\right\}\right){\rm d}y,

where
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. uniform points in
B(x,r_{t})\cap W
, and
f_{n-1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
refer to the
(n-1)
-faces induced by the vertex set in the superscript.
Introducing the notation
v_{x}:=\Vol(B(x,r_{t})\cap W)
, we have

I_{N,x}=v_{x}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(f_{n-1}^{\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},Y\}}\geq 1,\ y\in\bigcap\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},Y\}}\right\}\right),

where
Y
is uniform in
B(x,r_{t})\cap W
.
As there are at most
n
points that are contained in all
(n-1)
-faces of the above sample,

I_{N,x}\leq\frac{v_{x}\cdot n}{N+1}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(f_{n-1}^{\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},Y\}}\geq 1\right).

Writing
p_{n,N+1}
for the last probability expression (which can depend on
x
), it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{N=k-2}^{\infty}I_{N,x}\mathbb{P}\big(\textnormal{Po}(tv_{x}))\leq N\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{t}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{tv_{x}}{N+1}\mathbb{P}\big(\textnormal{Po}(tv_{x}))=N\big)\cdot p_{n,N+1}=

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{t}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(\textnormal{Po}(tv_{x}))=N+1\big)\cdot p_{n,N+1}

by using the standard change from
N
to
N+1
in a Poisson distribution. The last sum is exactly
\mathbb{P}(A_{x})
, and thus

I=tn\int_{W}\mathbb{P}(A_{x}){\rm d}x=n\mathbb{E}N_{n}.

In summary, we have that
\mathbb{V}N_{n}=O(\rho_{t}\mathbb{E}N_{n})+O(n\mathbb{E}N_{n})
,
which is
O(n\mathbb{E}N_{n})
whenever
n/\rho_{t}\to\infty
.

For the upper tail in (
10
), we have by the Mecke formula that

\mathbb{E}M_{n}=t^{2}\int_{W^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(x,y\in\sigma\text{ for some }\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\eta_{t}+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

For this event to hold, we must have
\|x-y\|\leq r_{t}
, and that
x
is contained in a
(n-1)
-face induced by
\eta_{t}+\delta_{x}
; as a consequence,
\mathbb{E}M_{n}\leq\kappa_{d}\rho_{t}\mathbb{E}N_{n-1}
.

Comparing the two sides, we have from (
10
) that, for
n/\rho_{t}\to\infty
,

(11)

\mathbb{P}(D<n-1)=O\left(\frac{n}{\mathbb{E}N_{n-1}}\right)\ \text{ and }\ \mathbb{P}(D>n)=O\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}N_{n}}{n+1}\right),

both of which we would like to tend to zero.

Let
m=m_{t}:=\ln t/\ln(\ln t/\rho_{t})
; note that
m_{t}/\rho_{t}\to\infty
. It is easy to see that the function
\mathbb{E}N_{n}-(n+1)\cdot(m_{t}/\rho_{t})^{1/2}
is monotone decreasing in
n
(for fixed
t
), positive for
n=0
and negative for
n=t
, and thus there is a (unique) value
k
(depending on
t
) such that

\mathbb{E}N_{k-1}-k\left(\frac{m_{t}}{\rho_{t}}\right)^{1/2}\geq 0>\mathbb{E}N_{k}-(k+1)\left(\frac{m_{t}}{\rho_{t}}\right)^{1/2}.

From the previous section, we know that
\mathbb{P}(D\geq(1-\gamma)m_{t})\to 1
for arbitrary
\gamma
, and since
\mathbb{E}N_{n}/(n+1)\geq\mathbb{P}(N_{n}\geq n+1)=\mathbb{P}(D\geq n)
, we must have
n>(1-\gamma)m_{t}
.
As a consequence, it follows that the bounds in (
11
) both tend to
0
. This concludes the proof of the two-point concentration.
∎

5
The dense regime

As stated in the introduction,
Theorem

3
is a direct consequence of
Theorem

4
, hence we only have to concern ourselves with proving the latter.
We use the notations of the theorem, and in addition, write
A_{n}^{(k)}
for the event that
I_{n}\cup I_{n+1}
contains an interval of length
r_{t}
with at least
k
points i.e.
A_{n}^{(k)}=\{X_{n}^{(k)}=1\}
.

We use a classical result by Arratia, Goldstein and Gordon.
Define the sets
B_{n}:=\{n-1,n,n+1\}\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}
: this describes the dependencies between the indicator variables, that is,
X_{n}
and
X_{m}
are dependent exactly when
n\in B_{n}
.
Further, define the following quantities:

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m\in B_{n}}\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{(k)})\cdot\mathbb{P}(A_{m}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{n\neq m\in B_{n}}\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{(k)}\cap A_{m}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle b_{3}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}-\mathbb{E}X_{n}|\{X_{m}:m\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus B_{n}\}\right]\right|.

Then
[
2
]
*Theorem 1 states that

{\rm d}_{TV}(X^{(k)},Z^{(k)})\leq b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3},

where, as in the theorem,
Z^{(k)}
denotes a Poisson random variable with parameter
\mathbb{E}X^{(k)}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{(k)})
.

Let
p^{(k)}:=\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{(k)})
and
q^{(k)}:=\mathbb{P}(A_{n-1}^{(k)}\cap A_{n}^{(k)})
for
n=1,\ldots,N-1
, where clearly the expressions don’t depend on the lower index of
A^{(k)}
, with the exception of the boundary case:
\mathbb{P}(A_{N}^{(k)})\leq p^{(k)}
and
\mathbb{P}(A_{N-1}^{(k)}\cap A_{N}^{(k)})\leq q^{(k)}
. With this notation, we have that
\mathbb{E}X^{(k)}\sim Np^{(k)}
, as well as the bounds

b_{1}\leq 3N(p^{(k)})^{2}\ \text{ and }\ b_{2}\leq 2Nq^{(k)}.

By independence,
b_{3}=0
.

We note here that
k
will be chosen in a way that
\mathbb{E}X^{(k)}\sim Np^{(k)}\sim p^{(k)}/r_{t}\to e^{-x}
. That the correct choice is
k_{t}(x)=a_{t}+xb_{t}
as stated in the theorem will be shown later; however, from this assumption and the computation above, it will follow that
b_{1}=O(p^{(k)})
and
b_{2}=O\left(q^{(k)}/p^{(k)}\right).

The following lemma describes the values of
p^{(k)}
and
q^{(k)}
precisely. Note that the expression for
p^{(k)}
can be found in the book
[
10
]
, originally obtained for the binomial point process in
[
17
]
. Since we use a similar strategy to find
q^{(k)}
, we recall the idea of the proof.

Lemma 3
.

With the notation above,

p^{(k)}=2\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k)-\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k)^{2}+\\
+\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=k)\cdot\left[(k-\rho_{t}-1)\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\leq k-2)+\rho_{t}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=k-2)\right]

and

q^{(k)}\leq\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k)+2\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k)\cdot p^{(k)}+\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=k)^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\leq m)^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=m)}.

Proof.

First consider
p^{(k)}
in detail.
By scaling, we may consider a Poisson point process on
[0,2)
with intensity
s:=\rho_{t}
. If
M
denotes the maximal number of points in an interval of length
1
, then we exactly have
p^{(k)}=\mathbb{P}(M\geq k)
.

We condition on the number of points in the two halves: let
A_{n,m}
denote the event where
[0,1]
and
[1,2]
contain
n
and
m
points, respectively. We may assume that the point
1
wasn’t chosen, as this happens with probability
0
.
Clearly, if
n\geq k
or
m\geq k
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(M\geq k\mid A_{n,m})=1
, if
n+m<k
, the probability is zero. In what follows, we assume that
n,m<k
and
n+m\geq k
.
Let’s now examine
M
under
A_{n,m}
.

The scanning of the interval
[0,2]
can be interpreted as follows: we start by considering
[0,1]
, and then sliding this window towards
2
. When the right endpoint of the scanning set hits a point in
(1,2]
, the number of points in it goes up, and when its left endpoint leaves a point in
[0,1]
, it goes down. We can thus consider this scanning as a path of up- and down-steps from
n
to
m
. This path, and in particular whether it reaches
k
(corresponding to a
1
-interval with
k
points) depends only on the order in which the the scanning set leaves and hits the points of the first- and second halves, respectively. What is more, each of these orders occurs with the same probability, and hence the problem becomes purely combinatorial.

The total number of paths starting at
n
with
m
up and
n
down steps is exactly
\binom{n+m}{n}
; the number of such paths reaching
k
is
\binom{n+m}{k}
, which can be found using a standard reflection argument that we omit here.

Putting this together, we have that

\mathbb{P}(M\geq k)=2\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\geq k)-\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\geq k)^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n,m<k\\
n+m\geq k\end{subarray}}\frac{\binom{n+m}{k}}{\binom{n+m}{n}}\cdot\frac{s^{n}}{n!}\cdot e^{-s}\cdot\frac{s^{m}}{m!}\cdot e^{-s},

where the first part comes from the case
n\geq k
or
m\geq k
.
Examining the sum further, we see by simplifying that the summand is exactly

\frac{e^{-s}s^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{e^{-s}s^{n+m-k}}{(n+m-k)!}=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=n+m-k),

yielding that after carrying out the substitution
l=n+m-k
, the sum becomes

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}\sum_{n=l+1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=l)=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}(k-l-1)\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=l).

By writing
l\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=l)=s\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=l-1)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)\left[(k-1)\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=l)-s\sum_{l=1}^{k-2}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=l-1)\right]=\\
=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)\left[(k-1)\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\leq k-2)-s\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\leq k-3)\right],

which simplifies to the expression stated in the lemma.

For
q^{(k)}
, we can follow a similar setup, now generating points over
[0,3]
and considering the event
E
when both
[0,2]
and
[1,3]
contain a
1
-interval with at least
k
points, thus
q^{(k)}=\mathbb{P}(E)
.
It is easy to see that the probability that one of the three thirds contains at least
k
points (and
E
holds) is upper bounded by
\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\geq k)+2\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\geq k)p^{(k)}
. Assume now that the number of points in
[0,1]
,
[1,2]
and
[2,3]
is, respectively,
n
,
m
and
n^{\prime}
, with
n,m,n^{\prime}<k
and
n+m,n^{\prime}+m\geq k
, denoting the event by
B_{n,m,n^{\prime}}
.
Under this condition, the probability of
E
is

\mathbb{P}(E\mid B_{n,m,n^{\prime}})=\frac{\binom{n+m}{k}}{\binom{n+m}{n}}\cdot\frac{\binom{m+n^{\prime}}{k}}{\binom{m+n^{\prime}}{m}}

by the same combinatorial argument as before. As a consequence,

\mathbb{P}(E\cap B_{n,m,n^{\prime}})=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=n+m-k)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=n^{\prime}+m-k)}{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=m)},

which we then sum (over
n,m,n^{\prime}<k
,
n+m,n^{\prime}+m\geq k
) to obtain the overall probability.
With a change of variables for
n+m-k
and
n^{\prime}+m-k
, the sum simplifies to

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=k)^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)\leq m-1)^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(s)=m)},

completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Corollary
.

With the notation of the proof,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{M-s}{\sqrt{s}}\leq x\right)\to(\Phi(x))^{2}-(\phi(x))^{2}-x\Phi(x)\phi(x)

as
s\to\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\Phi(x)
and
\phi(x)
denote the CDF and PDF of the standard normal distribution.

Note that the limiting distribution doesn’t appear to correspond to any classical distribution.

Proof.

From (
6
), we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\textnormal{Po}(s)=s\left(1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\right)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s}}\cdot e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}=\frac{\phi(x)}{\sqrt{s}},

and a standard normal approximation gives that
\mathbb{P}\left(\textnormal{Po}(s)\leq s\left(1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\right)\to\Phi(x)
. Plugging this into the lemma yields the corollary.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

To evaluate the expressions in
Lemma

3
, we let
k_{t}=\rho_{t}(1+\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{t}>0
, where we assume
\rho_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}\to\infty
and
\rho_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{3}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
; we will see that this is in fact the correct range.
Using (
6
), we have that

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=k_{t})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho_{t}}}\cdot e^{\rho_{t}\varepsilon}\cdot(1+\varepsilon)^{-\rho_{t}(1+\varepsilon)}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho_{t}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right\},

where the second expression comes from
\varepsilon-(1+\varepsilon)\ln(1+\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2}/2+O(\varepsilon^{3})
: due to the assumption
\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{3}\to 0
, the third-order term doesn’t affect the (first-order) asymptotics.

To find the asymptotic behaviour of the tail
\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k_{t})
, we may use a result from moderate deviations theory: formally, this can be done by writing a
\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})
random variable as the sum of
\lfloor\rho_{t}\rfloor
independent Poisson variables with parameter
\rho_{t}/\lfloor\rho_{t}\rfloor
, and applying a result of Cramér
[
7
,
6
]
. In particular, by
[
6
]
*Theorem 2, we have that

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k_{t})\sim 1-\Phi(\varepsilon\sqrt{\rho_{t}})
. Note that we again used the
\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{3}\to 0
assumption, which can equivalently be written as
\varepsilon\sqrt{\rho_{t}}=o(\rho_{t}^{1/6})
, exactly the requirement for the asymptotic equality in
[
6
]
.
Since the asymptotic behaviour of
\Phi
is well known,
this implies

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k_{t})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right\}.

We thus see that the third term of the sum dominates
p^{(k)}
, and in particular we have

(12)

p^{(k)}\sim(k_{t}-\rho_{t})\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=k)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2\pi}}\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right\}.

Now, we turn to bounding
q^{(k)}
, in the same range of
k
as above. From
Lemma

3
, we have that
q^{(k)}\leq I_{1}+\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=k)^{2}\cdot(I_{2}+I_{3})
, where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k)+2\cdot\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\geq k)p^{(k)}

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\rho_{t}-1}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\leq m)^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=m)}\ \text{ and }\ I_{3}=\sum_{m=\rho_{t}}^{k-1}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})\leq m)^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=m)}

From the arguments above, we already have

I_{1}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}}{2}\right\}\cdot\left[1+2\cdot\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon_{t}}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho_{t}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}}{2}\right\}\right].

For
I_{2}
, we use the bounds (
4
) and (
7
) on the Poisson probabilities to obtain

I_{2}\leq 3\sum_{m=1}^{\rho_{t}-1}e^{-\rho_{t}}\sqrt{2\pi m}\left(\frac{e\rho_{t}}{m}\right)^{m}\leq 20\rho_{t}\sum_{m=1}^{\rho_{t}-1}\frac{e^{-\rho_{t}}}{\sqrt{2\pi(m-1)}}\left(\frac{e\rho_{t}}{m-1}\right)^{m-1}\leq\\
\leq 60\rho_{t}\sum_{m=1}^{\rho_{t}-1}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Po}(\rho_{t})=m-1)\leq 60\rho_{t}.

Lastly, we turn to the bound for
I_{3}
, which will be the dominating part of the sum. Upper bounding the nominator by
1
, and using (
7
) for the denominator, we have that

I_{3}\leq 3\sum_{m=\rho_{t}}^{k-1}\sqrt{2\pi m}\cdot e^{\rho_{t}}\cdot\left(\frac{m}{e\rho_{t}}\right)^{m}.

The
\sqrt{m}
factor is at most some small constant away from
\sqrt{\rho}_{t}
due to the range. Then, by using the monotonicity of the function
(x/n)^{x}
for
x\geq n
, the standard integral bound gives

I_{3}\leq 10\sqrt{\rho_{t}}\sum_{m=\rho_{t}}^{k-1}e^{\rho_{t}}\left(\frac{m}{e\rho_{t}}\right)^{m}\leq 10\sqrt{\rho}_{t}\int_{\rho_{t}}^{k}e^{\rho_{t}}\left(\frac{x}{e\rho_{t}}\right)^{x}{\rm d}x.

Substituting
x=\rho_{t}(1+y)
gives

I_{3}\leq 10\rho_{t}^{\frac{3}{2}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{\rho_{t}}\left(\frac{1+y}{e}\right)^{\rho_{t}(1+y)}{\rm d}y=10\rho_{t}^{\frac{3}{2}}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\left(1+y\right)^{(1+y)}}{e^{y}}\right)^{\rho_{t}}{\rm d}y\leq 10\rho_{t}^{\frac{3}{2}}\cdot\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{\frac{\rho_{t}y^{2}}{2}}{\rm d}y,

where in the second step we used the inequality
(1+y)^{1+y}/e^{y}\leq e^{y^{2}/2}
.
Lastly, let
y=\varepsilon\cdot\left(1-\frac{v}{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
to get

I_{3}\leq\frac{10\rho_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}\exp\left\{\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{v}{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}\right\}{\rm d}v=\frac{10\rho_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}}}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}\exp\left\{-v+\frac{v^{2}}{2\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}\right\}{\rm d}v.

For
x\leq n
, the bound
-x+x^{2}/(2n)\leq-x/2
holds, which we may apply to the integral here. The resulting integral can further be upper bounded by expanding the domain of integration up to infinity, and explicitly evaluating the integral. Overall, this yields

I_{3}\leq\frac{10\rho_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}}}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{v}{2}}{\rm d}v\leq\frac{20\rho_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}}}{\varepsilon}.

As a consequence, we have

q^{(k)}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right\}

and thus

\frac{q^{(k)}}{p^{(k)}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{t}\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

To conclude the proof, consider the expression (
12
) obtained for
p^{(k)}
.
Choosing
