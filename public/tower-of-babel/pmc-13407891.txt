Nationwide spread of multidrug resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae across US communities
Authors: Jianping Jiang, Austin J Terlecky, Kevin J Rome, Elena Shashkina, Claudia Manca, Meghan W Starolis, Julia Larsen, Akinyosoye Olufunmi, Ariel A Aptekmann, Tara Lozy, Kelly K Yen, Mia J Bucich, Robert S Jones, Barun Mathema, Johann D D Pitout, Liang Chen, Barry N Kreiswirth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditionally viewed as a hospital pathogen, multidrug-resistant (MDR) Klebsiella pneumoniae is emerging in community settings, yet its population structure and spread are currently undefined. A nationwide genomic survey of 2006 ceftriaxone-nonsusceptible outpatient isolates from 42 US states reveals that the spread of resistance is primarily driven by the blaCTX-M-15 extended-spectrum-β-lactamase (ESBL) gene across diverse K. pneumoniae lineages. This gene, harbored by a family of IncFIB(Kpn3) plasmids, has spread to hundreds of lineages, coupling antibiotic resistance with stress- and metal-tolerance traits that may enhance survival beyond human hosts. Nearly half of isolates formed transmission clusters spanning multiple states, underscoring active community dissemination. The majority originated from elderly women with urinary tract infections, where oral treatment options are increasingly ineffective. Capsule and O-antigen profiling reveal broad antigenic diversity, yet a manageable subset of serotypes could offer wide coverage for vaccine development. Our findings expose a rapidly evolving, plasmid-driven epidemic of community-associated MDR K. pneumoniae across the United States.Subject terms: Clinical microbiology, Bacterial infection

Introduction

Carbapenem- and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales, especially Klebsiella pneumoniae, pose a critical global threat by undermining the effectiveness of essential antibiotics1. The horizontal spread of plasmid mediated drug resistance and the clonal spread of multidrug resistant (MDR) K. pneumoniae strains, in both healthcare and community settings, underscores the urgent need for improved surveillance and molecular epidemiological studies to guide effective public health interventions2.

β-lactam agents (e.g., cephalosporins, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems) are the mainstay of treating Enterobacterales infections3. Among K. pneumoniae, extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) remain the most clinically important causes of resistance to third-generation cephalosporin (e.g., ceftriaxone, ceftazidime)4. During the 1990s/2000’s, SHV and TEM ESBLs were frequently detected among K. pneumoniae5. The CTX-M ESBL type increased in prevalence during the 2010s and are currently the most common ESBL type identified among K. pneumoniae6. The CTX-M family includes more than 250 different enzymes and CTX-M-15 is the most widely distributed with the highest global frequencies among K. pneumoniae7.

A 2007 report from a US healthcare system revealed a concerning rise in ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, particularly those with CTX-M-type enzymes8. Castanheira et al. further investigated this trend and revealed the prevalence of CTX-M-15 has increased from 16% in 2007 to 33.8% in 20109. A similar trend was observed in a surveillance study conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which found a 53.3% increase in incidence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales between 2012 and 201710. The CDC’s finding raises concerns about an expanding and underrecognized reservoir of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in the community. Supporting this hypothesis, Chen et al. identified CTX-M-15 in 71% of K. pneumoniae isolates from six community hospitals in North Carolina and Virginia between 2010 and 2012, with urinary tract infections (UTIs) as the predominant source11. These findings suggest that ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (ESBL-Kp) may be transitioning from a primarily healthcare-associated pathogen to one increasingly disseminated in the community.

In contrast to the well-documented carbapenem resistance epidemic in the United States, largely driven by clonal group 258 (CG258) K. pneumoniae and the acquisition of various blaKPC plasmids1,12,13, the molecular epidemiology of ESBL-Kp remains poorly characterized despite its increasing prevalence. In addition, knowledge of community-associated isolates is particularly scarce, and the burden and the genetic diversity remain largely unexplored. To address this gap, we conducted a nationwide study to genomically characterize the ESBL-Kp isolates from across the United States. Unlike most surveillance studies, which focus on hospital infections, our study design emphasized outpatients in the community, a potential reservoir that has not been systematically sampled. Over a 13-month period from July 2023 to July 2024, ten Quest Diagnostic central laboratories, serving primarily community outpatients, collected 2006 unique ceftriaxone-non-susceptible K. pneumoniae isolates from 42 US states for genomic analysis, in order to elucidate the genetic diversity, transmission dynamics, and emerging lineages driving ESBL dissemination in the community.

Results

Community-acquired multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (MDR-Kp) study population

A total of 2006 unique ceftriaxone-non-susceptible K. pneumoniae isolates from 10 Quest Diagnostic laboratories were collected for whole genome sequencing and analysis (Fig. 1a). Of these, 1928 were K. pneumoniae sensu stricto, 45 were K. quasipneumoniae, and 33 were K. variicola (Fig. 1b). Over two-thirds (67.4%, 1352/2006, Fig. 1c) were cultured from female patients, and three-quarters from patients (73.6%, 1476/2006, Fig. 1d) over 60 years old. The majority were obtained from outpatients (82%) with UTIs (89.9%, 1803/2006, Fig. 1e).

The collected Klebsiella spp. isolates exhibited high prevalence of non-susceptibility to all tested cephalosporins: ceftriaxone (100%, 2006/2006), cefazolin (100%, 1986/1986), ceftazidime (66.9%, 1301/1945) and cefepime (63.3%, 1266/2001), and to β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors: piperacillin/tazobactam (36.4%, 719/1975) and ampicillin/sulbactam (93.8%, 1877/2002). High non-susceptibility was also observed for oral agents commonly used to treat UTIs: levofloxacin (89.7%, 1592/1775), ciprofloxacin (89.2%, 1774/1988), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (85.7%, 1715/2002), and nitrofurantoin (86.3%, 1362/1578) (Fig. 1f). Alarmingly, 100% were classified as MDR and 69.5% (1081/1555) of isolates were non-susceptible to all four oral antibiotics, underscoring the urgent need for new oral treatment options. Non-susceptibility to the carbapenems imipenem and meropenem was 6.7% (133/1992) and 8.3% (92/1112), respectively.

Across 10 Quest Diagnostic laboratories, patient demographics characteristics were similar (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), but specimen sources (Supplementary Fig. 1c) and antibiotic non-susceptibility (Supplementary Fig. 2) varied markedly by region (e.g., cefepime 99.2% at Site-4 vs. 32.3% at Site-9 and imipenem 27.7% at Site-3 vs. 0.4% at Site-4), underscoring the need to use local epidemiology to guide empiric therapy.

Of the 2006 isolates, 1895 (94.5%) harbored at least one ESBL gene: blaCTX-M in 1821 (96.1%), blaSHV in 36 (1.9%), and both in 38 (2.0%) of ESBL-positive isolates. Among the blaCTX-M genes, blaCTX-M-15 was the most prevalent, accounting for 95.9% (1783/1859) of the blaCTX-M-positive isolates and 88.9% (1783/2006) overall, followed by blaCTX-M-14 (27/1859; 1.5%) and blaCTX-M-3 (14/1859, 0.8%). Among blaSHV ESBLs (n = 74), blaSHV-12 (27.0%, 20/74) and blaSHV-187 (21.6%, 16/74) were the most common. Carbapenemases were detected in 203 (10.1%) isolates, primarily blaKPC-2 (57.1%, 116/203) and blaKPC-3 (26.6%, 54/203). Sixteen isolates encoded a metallo-β-lactamase carbapenemase, all of which were NDM variants, including blaNDM-1 (n = 8), blaNDM-5 (n = 3), blaNDM-6 (n = 2), and blaNDM-7 (n = 3). Acquired ampC β-lactamase genes were present in 18 isolates, most commonly blaDHA-1 (n = 7) and blaCMY-2 (n = 6). Sixty isolates lacked ESBL, carbapenemase, and AmpC genes, and the basis of their ceftriaxone resistance remains to be defined.

Genomic characteristics and phylogenetic profile of MDR-Kp isolates

Genomic analysis showed substantial diversity among the 2006 MDR-Kp isolates, resolving 267 sequence types (STs), including 228 known and 39 novel STs. Among these isolates, ST307 (19.3%, 388/2006) was the most prevalent ST, followed by ST45 (8.1%, 162/2006) and ST405 (5.2%, 104/2006) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Notably, key STs associated with carbapenem resistance (e.g., ST258 [1.1%, 23/2006], ST11 [1.0%, 20/2006], and ST147 [1.0%, 21/2006]) or hypervirulence (e.g., ST23 [0.1%, 2/2006], ST86 [0%, 0/2006], and ST65 [0%, 0/2006]) were rare among our collection. The top 25 STs accounted for 70.3% (1411/2006) of all the MDR-Kp isolates (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Regional distributions (Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 1) and infection sources (Supplementary Fig. 4b) varied by STs (e.g., ST13 was enriched at Site-1 and in wound/respiratory samples, ST307 was widespread, ST45 was overrepresented at Sites-4/9, and ST219 at Site 3), underscoring geographic stratification of MDR-Kp in the United States. The distribution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene classes also differed across STs (Supplementary Fig. 4c). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4d, carbapenemases (predominantly KPC) were enriched in CG258 lineages, particularly ST258 (65.2%, 15/23; P < 0.01) and ST11 (35.0%, 7/20; P < 0.01) compared with non-CG258 (9.2%, 181/1963). Genomic analysis also revealed diversity in virulence loci, including ybt, outer-membrane porin mutations (predominantly OmpK35 truncations), metal, biocide, and heat-resistance genes (Supplementary Fig. 4d).

The phylogeny, shown in Fig. 2a illustrates the population structure and diversity of all 2006 sequenced isolates. blaCTX-M-15 occurred across all major STs (Fig. 2b), reaching 100% in ST1774, ST469, ST2004, and ST353, but only 4% in ST258. Plasmid replicon distributions among the predominant STs are shown in Fig. 2c, d. IncFIB(Kpn3) was the most abundant replicon (93.6%, 1877/2006) and was present in 95.5% (1702/1783) of blaCTX-M-15-positive isolates.

blaCTX-M-15 pKPN plasmid in MDR-Kp

To characterize the blaCTX-M-15-carrying IncFIB(Kpn3) plasmids in MDR-Kp, we selected 39 isolates from 22 representative STs for Nanopore sequencing and established closed assemblies (Supplementary Data 2). Plasmid pangenome analysis resolved these pKPN backbones into two major groups, pKPN-US-1 and pKPN-US-2. All pKPN-US-1 plasmids carry an intact conjugative tra operon, which is absent in pKPN-US-2. The pKPN-US-1 group was further subdivided into four sublineages, pKPN-US-1.1, -1.2, -1.3, and -1.4, distinguished by genes linked to metal, heat resistance, and iron transport (Supplementary Fig. 5). Except for pKPN-US-1.3, pKPN-US-1 plasmids exhibit a hybrid architecture bearing both IncFIB(Kpn3) and IncFIIK7 replicons.

The five pKPN plasmids were compared to two published structures, pKPN3-307 Type A14 (Fig. 3a) and pMV-ul-SK2-O-a15 (Fig. 3b). Among these plasmids, the AMR region is a highly dynamic mosaic of resistance determinants bracketed by mobile and transmissible units, jointly conferring resistance to β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, trimethoprim, and sulfonamides. Notable genes include blaCTX-M-15, blaTEM-1, blaOXA-1, dfrA, qnr, aac(3)-IIa, aph(3’)-Ia, aadA2, sul2, strAB, and mph, and most plasmids also carry heavy-metal resistance loci (silver, copper, arsenic). Beyond resistance and transfer modules, pKPN-US-1.1 and pKPN-US-2 encode environmental-survival genes, including the lacZYI operon and a Fec-like iron(III)-dicitrate system for lactose catabolism and iron uptake, respectively. pKPN-US-1.1 and pKPN-US-1.2 additionally harbor genes implicated in heat tolerance (clpK and hsp20) and urea transport (urt), while pKPN-US-1.1 alone carries glutathione ABC transport systems (gsi) and the glycogen-synthesis operon (glg). The blaCTX-M-15 gene on each plasmid is flanked by ISEcp1, an insertion sequence known to mediate transposition and modulate gene expression16. Consistent with this mobility, ISEcp1–blaCTX-M-15 was also found integrated into the chromosomes of some isolates from ST219 and ST1774. The ISEcp1–blaCTX-M-15 insertion mapped to yphB in 71.3% (57/80) of ST219 isolates (Supplementary Fig. 6a) and to benC in 70% (32/46) of ST1774 isolates (Supplementary Fig. 6b), suggesting descent from independent transposition events into distinct genetic backgrounds.

The five major pKPN plasmids, pKPN-US-1.1 (30.3%, 607/2006), pKPN-US-1.2 (15.3%, 307/2006), pKPN-US-1.3 (22.1%, 444/2006), pKPN-US-1.4 (1.0%, 20/2006) and pKPN-US-2 (14.9%, 298/2006), were identified in 1676/2006 (83.5%) isolates across various STs (Fig. 4a), consistent with plasmid-mediated horizontal transmission coupled with clonal spread. By contrast, pKPN-US-2, which lacks conjugative capacity, is largely identified in ST219, ST1774, and ST469. The distribution of these plasmids within and across predominant STs (Supplementary Fig. 7) suggests an active interplay between plasmids and their hosts. To investigate the global spread of pKPN-US sublineages, we screened 60,619 plasmids from PLSDB (latest version, 2025), including 19,886 plasmids from E. coli, 15,414 from K. pneumoniae, and 25,319 from other species (Supplementary Data 3). The screening identified 473 pKPN-US plasmids, which were predominantly restricted to K. pneumoniae (92.6%, 438/473), with only four detected in E. coli. pKPN-US-1.1 (n = 218) and pKPN-US-1.2 (n = 152) were the dominant types (Supplementary Data 4). In addition, most identified pKPN-US plasmids (67.2%, 318/473) carried blaCTX-M-15. Among the four pKPN-US plasmids detected in E. coli, two were pKPN-US-1.3, one was pKPN-US-1.1, and one was pKPN-US-1.2. Three carried blaCTX-M-15, and one carried blaCTX-M-173. Three of the four plasmids carried the tra locus. Taken together, the expansion observed in K. pneumoniae is more likely attributable to the spread of a K. pneumoniae-adapted plasmid lineage rather than transmission from common E. coli plasmids.

Gene content in AMR and other plasmid modules was largely conserved relative to the corresponding reference plasmids (Table 1). Notably, isolates carrying pKPN-US-1 harbored more AMR classes (Fig. 4b) and additional β-lactamase genes (Fig. 4c), including blaOXA-1 and blaTEM-1, than other isolates. The prevalence of conjugative pKPN-US-1 showed an increasing trend over time (slope = 0.005, P = 0.11), although this trend was not statistically significant. In contrast, the prevalence of non-conjugative pKPN-US-2 significantly decreased over the study period (slope = −0.006, P = 0.04) (Fig. 4d).

Experimental conjugation assays showed that inter-species transfer of pKPN-US plasmids to E. coli was highly restricted. Four of five tested variants failed to produce transconjugants, with conjugation frequencies below the detection limit (<10−7), whereas only pKPN-US-1.1 exhibited detectable transfer (6 × 10−3 ± 1.5 × 10−4). Despite this, the transfer rate of pKPN-US-1.1 remained substantially lower than that reported for highly promiscuous blaCTX-M plasmids (~10−1)17. These findings are consistent with in silico analysis of PLSDB and indicate that pKPN-US plasmids are largely adapted to K. pneumoniae, with limited capacity for inter-species dissemination.

Phenotypic impact of pKPN plasmid

To evaluate the contribution of the blaCTX-M-15-harboring pKPN-US-1 influencing the strain’s phenotypes, we precisely cured the lone plasmid in an ST307 isolate (BK70163), using the CRISPR-Cas-mediated pCasCure platform18. The creation of an isogenic strain pair ensured that phenotypic differences were attributable to plasmid carriage, enabling assessment under defined stress conditions. Susceptibility testing results (Table 2) showed that the parent strain BK70163 shifted from resistant/intermediate to fully susceptible in the plasmid-cured derivative BK70163-ΔpKPN across second to fifth-generation cephalosporins, which is consistent with the finding that pKPN-US-1 encodes CTX-M-15, TEM-1, and OXA-1. Likewise, the 4-fold decrease in ciprofloxacin MIC (32 to 8 µg/ml) is consistent with removal of qnrB1 and aac(6’)-Ib-cr on pKPN-US-1. However, BK70163-ΔpKPN remains ciprofloxacin-resistant (MIC 8 µg/ml), attributable to chromosomal substitutions in GyrA (codon 83) and ParC (codon 81). Interestingly, curing the Fec-encoding plasmid pKPN-US-1 resulted in an eightfold decrease in cefiderocol MIC (0.25 to 0.03 µg/ml), confirming a role for the Fec iron transport system in cefiderocol susceptibility. We additionally tested a second isolate, BK72421, which carries the same pKPN-US-1 plasmid as BK70163 but also harbors a chromosomal copy of blaCTX-M-15. As expected, MIC differences were less pronounced; however, the overall phenotypic trends remained consistent with those observed in BK70163 (Supplementary Data 5). Whole-genome sequencing confirmed plasmid loss without chromosomal mutations in both strain pairs, demonstrating that the observed phenotypic effects are attributable to plasmid carriage.

Beyond antibiotic resistance, pKPN-US-1 encodes determinants for heavy-metal tolerance (ars, cop, and sil), heat stress tolerance (clpK and hsp20), and iron acquisition (fec operon). Susceptibility testing showed eightfold higher MICs for arsenic and twofold higher MICs for silver in the parental strain relative to BK70163-ΔpKPN. In thermal-stress assays at 53 °C (Fig. 5a), survival of BK70163-ΔpKPN was 6%, significantly lower than the 43% observed for BK70163 (P = 0.005); assays at 45 °C yielded similar results (Fig. 5b), whereas 37 °C showed no difference (Fig. 5c). Growth-curve analysis in iron-depleted media (Fig. 5d) indicated that the doubling time of BK70163-ΔpKPN was twice that of BK70163, consistent with pKPN-US-1 enhancing iron-uptake efficiency. Together, these data highlight the multifaceted role of pKPN plasmids in AMR, heavy-metal resistance, and environmental-stress resilience.

Capsular polysaccharide and lipopolysaccharide diversity in MDR-Kp

Capsular polysaccharide (CPS, the “K” antigen), a key virulence factor and vaccine target in K. pneumoniae, was profiled by characterizing the CPS operon. A total of 102 distinct KL types were identified, revealing extensive serotype diversity. ST-KL associations varied presumably due to capsular switching. Several major STs showed conserved KL types (e.g., ST307-KL102, ST405-KL15, ST219-KL114, ST25-KL2), consistent with stable CPS operons within those lineages. By contrast, ST37, ST661, and ST147 displayed broad KL heterogeneity, indicative of frequent capsular switching and potential immune evasion (Supplementary Data 6). Notably, 70.3% (1410/2006) of isolates belonged to the 19 most prevalent KL types (Fig. 6a). However, regional differences in KL distributions (Supplementary Fig. 9a) were also observed, highlighting the overall need to evaluate capsule diversity when strategizing vaccine design and species coverage.

The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) outer-core polysaccharide (O antigen) was also profiled. Compared with CPS, O antigen diversity was less, with 13 OL types identified. O1/O2v2 was most prevalent (39.1%, 784/2006), followed by O1/O2v1 (23.1%, 464/2006) and O4 (11.6%, 232/2006). OL types were broadly shared across STs, for example, O1/O2v2 occurred in ST45, ST219, and ST15. The top three OL types comprised 73.8% (1480/2006) of all isolates, and the top six comprised 92.1% (1847/2006) (Fig. 6b). Across US regions, the four most common OL types would cover >70% of isolates (Supplementary Fig. 9b), a finding that could inform effective vaccine development strategies.

Transmission of MDR-Kp and blaCTX-M-15-harboring pKPN plasmids

We analyzed transmission patterns for 22 prevalent STs (≥20 isolates each). Using a cgSNP threshold of ≤21, we identified 140 transmission clusters (“transmissible strains”) encompassing 55.0% (749/1362) of the characterized isolates (Supplementary Data 7). Within ST307, 153 isolates formed 37 clusters (2-21 isolates each). Similarly, ST45 yielded 12 clusters from 101 isolates (2–47 each). The largest cluster was ST405-3 with 75 isolates (Supplementary Data 8). Phylogeographic analysis indicated interstate spread of ST405-3 (Supplementary Fig. 10), likely originating in Florida and disseminating to Georgia, Texas, and Connecticut. Additional clusters showed regional enrichment in coastal states (Florida, California, Connecticut; Supplementary Fig. 11a–e). Beyond interstate dissemination, we also detected a local cluster of 21 ST13 isolates in Georgia (Supplementary Fig. 11f).

The blaCTX-M-15-carrying pKPN plasmids, predominantly pKPN-US-1.1, -1.2, and -1.3, which harbor the plasmid conjugation tra operon, have disseminated across major STs (Supplementary Fig. 7). While horizontal plasmid transfer clearly contributes to the observed diversity in MDR-Kp (Fig. 3a), molecular epidemiology also supports vertical transmission. To differentiate the two routes, we extended genotyping of transmission strains to the accessory genome (acquired plasmids and chromosomal traits). Clusters sharing a conserved core genome and near-identical accessory content (≤10 gene differences) were classified as “emerging clones”, reflecting vertical spread. In total, 141 emerging clones were identified, comprising 64.2% (481/749) of isolates within transmission strains (Supplementary Data 8), with the largest (ST405-3B) including 22 isolates. Geographic mapping of nine emerging clones showed both local and interstate dissemination (Supplementary Fig. 12). Notably, most clones were detected in multiple states, for example, ST405-3B in 13 states and ST353-1A in 9, indicating extensive clonal spread.

Population structure and transmission patterns of sequence type ST307

Given the predominance of ST307 in a previous Houston hospital study19 and its success in the community (≥19% in our MDR-Kp cohort), we assessed the phylogeography of 1924 ST307 US genomes (1536 public genomes from US hospital-based studies and 388 from this study, Supplementary Data 9). The analysis resolved ST307 genomes into five closely related clades (Fig. 7). Clade 1 was distinguished by a unique GyrA-87N substitution and was enriched in Texas, particularly Houston hospitals (Fig. 7). When we contrasted US hospital collections with our community-derived ST307 genomes, clades 1 and 5 were markedly underrepresented, whereas clades 2–4 showed robust community spread (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. 13). The paucity of clades 1 and 5 in the community likely reflects their hospital associated populations.

Among our 388 ST307 isolates, 153 formed transmission clusters, all within clades 2–4 (Supplementary Data 10). Movements of three representative clusters, ST307-30, ST307-3, and ST307-14, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 14, illustrating both regional/statewide spread (ST307-3) and multi-state dissemination (ST307-14, ST307-30). Within these clusters, 100 isolates met criteria for emerging clones, ranging from 2 to 15 isolates each (Supplementary Data 10). The mapping of the isolates in Supplementary Fig. 15 shows examples of local spread, such as the isolates making up clones ST307-3A, ST307-3B, and ST307-14B that were localized to Florida. Of potential public health concern, the clone ST307-30B, comprising 10 isolates detected across 8 states, could represent the emergence of a novel predominant clone.

In this study, TransCluster identified consistent transmissible clusters based on genomic similarity and sampling time. A simplified cutoff of ≤21 pairwise SNP differences yielded highly concordant clustering patterns (Supplementary information), supporting its use as an operational definition. Phylogeographic analyses with TreeTime and PastML revealed structured regional dissemination of major lineages, although inferred routes should be interpreted as putative rather than direct transmission events.

Discussion

MDR-Kp is a major global health threat, yet the epidemiology of community-associated K. pneumoniae in the United States remains poorly characterized. Leveraging the nationwide Quest Diagnostics network, which primarily serves an outpatient population, we investigated the population structure, resistance mechanisms, and transmission dynamics of MDR-Kp in the community setting. We generated a geographically diverse collection of ceftriaxone-non-susceptible isolates from across the United States, revealing that the community, particularly elderly women with urinary tract infections, represents an important and underrecognized reservoir for MDR-Kp.

Unlike hospital-centric molecular epidemiological studies, our predominantly community-derived dataset enables resolution of the genomic structure of MDR-Kp beyond the healthcare setting. Phylogeographic analyses reveal emerging clones with conserved chromosomal and plasmid architectures that are widely dispersed across geographically distant regions, arguing against localized transmission and indicating widespread, underrecognized community reservoirs. The recurrence of identical clones across geographically dispersed sites suggests that these lineages are broadly established rather than recently transmitted. Together, these findings support a convergence of community and hospital populations, with high-risk clones circulating across both ecological niches. Their expansion may be driven by selective pressures in the community, including extensive use of oral antibiotics for urinary tract infections. Integrated genomic surveillance across clinical settings will be essential to delineate the transmission dynamics and evolutionary trajectories of these emerging lineages.

Our nationwide genomic survey reveals a genetically diverse MDR-Kp population shaped by both plasmid exchange and clonal expansion. Across 42 states, we identified 267 STs, with no single lineage exceeding 20% of isolates, and hypervirulent (e.g., ST23) or classical carbapenem-resistant clones (e.g., ST258) were infrequent. With the exception of ST307 (19%), the prevalent STs in our collection are not widely reported as dominant K. pneumoniae lineages in the literature20. Nearly half of isolates belonged to clonally expanding “transmissible strains” distributed across US communities, a finding that could reflect a fitness or survival advantage of these genetically related isolates. Several large transmissible strains spanned multiple, often distant, states, highlighting gaps in current surveillance and suggesting that the prevalence of some clusters is underestimated. More comprehensive sampling of both community and hospital isolates will likely better resolve regional and long-distance transmission networks. The population structure of ST307 is particularly notable. Cladistic analysis showed widespread dissemination of clades 2–4 and a relative absence of clade 1 (Houston hospital clade) strains in community isolates, suggesting a distinction between MDR-Kp circulating in community versus hospital settings.

The success of MDR-Kp is the result of the horizontal spread of mobile elements carrying diverse environmental adaptation genes, which enable transmissible strains to evolve into emerging clones. This trajectory reflects the interplay between a conserved core genome and the stepwise acquisition of accessory genes. The predominance of blaCTX-M-15 (89% of all isolates) in our dataset indicates a single dominant ESBL mechanism in US community settings. Notably, this gene is borne largely on a set of closely related IncFIB(Kpn3) plasmids, typified as pKPN-US-1 and pKPN-US-2. pKPN-US-1, which encodes an intact conjugation apparatus, is broadly distributed across sequence types and states. Its increasing prevalence over this one-year study period suggests active, ongoing horizontal dissemination and raises concern that conjugative ESBL plasmids may seed new successful clones in the community.

Analysis of the antibiograms underscores a major clinical concern: extensive drug resistance among predominant urinary isolates from community patients. Nearly 70% of isolates were non-susceptible to routinely used oral agents for treating UTIs (e.g., nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and fluoroquinolones), and >20% were resistant to all tested agents except carbapenems and amikacin. Thus, AMR is spreading both vertically and horizontally. The rise in oral-drug resistance can be compounded by co-selection on complex pKPN plasmids, where determinants for fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole are maintained alongside other AMR and environmental-adaptation genes (Fig. 3). These resistance profiles, together with high community prevalence, sharply limit empiric outpatient options for suspected Gram-negative infections. For community-acquired UTIs, clinicians may consider potentially effective but underused oral agents, such as fosfomycin and pivmecillinam, and healthcare systems need to recognize local and regional epidemiological variability in resistance and tailor guidance for empiric therapy accordingly.

In addition to antimicrobial resistance, pKPN plasmids encode determinants associated with heat tolerance, metal resistance, and iron acquisition, indicating a broader role in ecological fitness. Collectively, these traits enhance bacterial survival under environmental pressures, including elevated temperatures, and could contribute to the persistence and dissemination of this plasmid lineage beyond antibiotic selection alone.

Historically, CTX-M–producing Escherichia coli emerged globally in the late 1990s to mid-2000s21–23, whereas ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae became CTX-M–dominant later and remained largely associated with healthcare settings. Community-associated ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae infections were relatively uncommon, and earlier outbreaks were typically driven by localized clones rather than globally disseminated lineages5. In this context, our findings demonstrate the widespread distribution of CTX-M-15–producing K. pneumoniae across 42 US states, with the global clone ST307 accounting for approximately 20% of isolates. These data suggest broad dissemination of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae in community-associated settings, primarily driven by a group of blaCTX-M-15-harboring pKPN plasmids. To this end, our study also provides a reference standard for future global genomic studies on CTX-M-producing K. pneumoniae from the community.

The need for effective treatments against MDR-Kp extends beyond antibiotics, and our KL (capsular) and OL (O antigen) typing provides actionable leads for vaccine design. Our catalogue of prevalent CPS types offers up-to-date seroepidemiology to inform multivalent formulations, revisiting a strategy first explored in the 1980s24, when a 24-valent K. pneumoniae vaccine, following the Streptococcus pneumoniae model, was evaluated for safety and immunogenicity25. Unfortunately, the unfounded optimism of the antibiotic pipeline, which halted vaccine development, has now come full circle. Although the extensive CPS diversity we observe complicates vaccine development, the most common KL types remain viable targets for a multivalent approach. Moreover, the top three OL types provide >70% predicted coverage across all US regions (Supplementary Fig. 9B), suggesting an O antigen-based strategy with broad reach26. A protective or therapeutic vaccine, irrespective of antibiotic resistance, could address a critical unmet need, particularly for older women with drug-resistant UTIs.

This study has limitations related to the scope and composition of the collection. Because this study was designed as a targeted genomic characterization of CTX-M–associated isolates rather than a comparative case–control analysis, ceftriaxone-susceptible isolates were not included, limiting direct comparisons between CTX-M–positive and CTX-M–negative populations. The 2006 characterized isolates represent ~20% of the ceftriaxone-non-susceptible K. pneumoniae cultured at the ten Quest laboratories during the study year and were drawn predominantly from community patients. Consequently, we lack a comprehensive view of hospital-derived ESBL-producing or ceftriaxone-non-susceptible K. pneumoniae in the United States, and it remains unclear how those populations compare with the phylogeographic patterns reported here. Because the collection primarily targeted outpatients tested at commercial laboratories, patient-level metadata (e.g., prior antibiotic exposure, comorbidities, healthcare contact) were unavailable, limiting our ability to distinguish community- from healthcare-associated infections.

In conclusion, MDR-Kp circulating in US communities appears to be shaped by the convergence of potent MDR plasmids with the expansion of transmissible strains and the subsequent spread of emerging clones. The dominance of blaCTX-M-15-bearing pKPN-US plasmids, coupled with their stress- and survival-enhancing cargo, provides a genomic platform well suited to both horizontal and vertical spread. Ongoing genomic surveillance that integrates plasmid and chromosomal epidemiology will be essential to detect and ideally control the emergence of the next high-risk MDR K. pneumoniae clone.

Methods

The study

Between July 2023 and July 2024, ten Quest Diagnostic regional laboratories identified over 10,000 ceftriaxone-non-susceptible (≥2 µg/ml) K. pneumoniae isolates. For urine isolates, only Klebsiella spp. isolates with a colony count ≥100,000 colony-forming units/ml were included in this study. The collected ceftriaxone-non-susceptible Klebsiella spp. isolates were all multidrug-resistant (MDR)27 and were therefore designated as MDR-Kp in this study. Bacterial identification and susceptibility testing were performed by each contributing microbiology laboratory using Vitek MS and/or Vitek 2 ID & AST cards (N808/XN15) (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA) with the exception of one laboratory (Chantilly, VA). Identification at that site was performed with Biotyper (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) for identification, followed by susceptibility using Vitek 2 ID & AST cards (N808/XN15) (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA).

Whole genome sequencing and analysis

Among the MDR-Kp identified at the Quest laboratories, a total of 2006 random isolates (one isolate per patient) from 42 states were selected for whole genome sequencing (WGS) and analysis (Supplementary Data 6). Whole genome sequencing was performed as previously described using the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads1. Illumina raw sequencing reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.3928. Draft genomes were assembled using SPAdes v3.13.029. The multilocus sequence type (MLST) was determined in silico using mlst v2.23.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst). Additionally, 39 strains (Supplementary Data 2) selected from 22 ST clones were further sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore MinION platform, and assembled via Unicycler v0.4.930 leveraging both short and long sequencing reads. Kleborate v2.331 was employed to screen the genome assemblies of K. pneumoniae for the prediction of capsular type and the detection of virulence loci, including hypermucoidy genes rmpADC, rmpA2, and siderophore systems, such as yersiniabactin (ybt), colibactin (clb), aerobactin (iuc), salmochelin (iro), as well as their associated mobile elements (ICEKp). Resistance genes were called by AMRFinderPlus v3.10.2032, and ARIBA v2.14.633. The position of blaCTX-M-15 was inferred by using Platon v1.734. KL and OL types were called by Kaptive v2.0.935. The heavy metal resistant genes were detected by abritamr v1.0.1736.

Plasmid type analysis

Plasmid replicons were identified by ABRicate v1.01 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) using the PlasmidFinder37 database with 90% identity and 80% query coverage cutoffs. The IncFIIK7 type was identified by using pmlst37 with the plasmid MLST database38. The pKPN plasmids harboring IncFIB(Kpn3) replicon with complete sequence were classified into two categories, pKPN-US-1 and pKPN-US-2, according to the accessory genome tree. The pKPN-US-1 was further subdivided into four subcategories by the presence of IncFIIK7 replicon, arsenic resistance genes, and iron transport genes as pKPN-US-1.1 (IncFIIK7+/ars+/fec+/sil+), pKPN-US-1.2 (IncFIIK7+/ars+/fec-/sil+), pKPN-US-1.3 (IncFIIK7-/ars-/fec-/sil+), and pKPN-US-1.4 (IncFIIK7+/ars+/fec-/sil-). The accessory genome tree of these plasmids was built by using Roary v3.13.039 with the genes predicted by using Prokka v1.14.540. The linear alignment of plasmids with complete sequences was visualized by using Easyfig v2.141. To detect the five types of plasmids among all the isolates, the contig sequences of all the isolates were aligned to the five reference plasmids, including p71200-2 (pKPN-US-1.1), p69112-2 (pKPN-US-1.2), p70050-2 (pKPN-US-1.3), p70428-2 (pKPN-US-1.4), and p69850-2 (pKPN-US-2) by BLASTn v2.13.0+. The cutoff for the plasmid coverage was 0.65. If the alignment displayed more than 0.65 coverage to multiple plasmids, the highest alignment was selected. The isolates with a coverage lower than 0.65 and harbored a IncFIB(Kpn3) replicon were distinguished as carrying a plasmid other than pKPN.

K. pneumoniae phylogeny

For the phylogeny of the entire K. pneumoniae collection, core-genome alignments were used to construct the tree. The genes were predicted by using Prokka v1.14.540. The core-genome alignments based on the concatenation of core genes were obtained using panaroo v1.5.042, and using default parameters and core genes defined as those being present in more than 98% of the genomes. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed by using FastTree v2.1.11 (model GTR + GAMMA)43. For the phylogeny of each MLST clone, the paired-end raw fastq reads were mapped to each reference genome (Supplementary Data 7) using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). Recombination regions were detected using Gubbins v3.0.044. The maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed with the recombination-free whole genome alignment by using RAxML v8.2.12 (model GTR + GAMMA)45. The tree was visualized by using iTOL v646.

Transmissible strain and emerging clones

A “transmissible strain” is defined as being a member of a collection of strains that share a most recent common ancestor (MRCA) based on phylogenetic analysis of recombination-free regions and a fixed cutoff of ≤21 pairwise SNP differences47 in the core genome. Clustering was performed using the setSNPThresholds and makeSNPClusters functions in the R program TransCluster v0.1.048, which generate SNP-based clusters. These transmissible strain clusters have the same genome backbone. The consistency between the fixed SNP-based clustering and the probabilistic clustering inferred by TransCluster was evaluated by using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) (Supplementary information). The transmissible strains are further distinguished as “emerging clones” on the basis of containing the same array of accessory gene content, both chromosomal and plasmid encoded. Those isolates with a fixed cutoff of ≤10 pairwise gene content differences were considered as members of an emerging clone. The gene content differences were calculated based on the clusters of orthologous genes constructed by panaroo v1.5.042. The spatiotemporal dynamics for each transmissible clone were inferred by TreeTime v0.11.449 and visualized by using Auspice v2.3950.

ST307 K. pneumoniae phylogeny in the United States

A total of 1536 ST307 isolates collected from the United States were retrieved from the NCBI whole genome sequence database or short reads archive (dated as 5/2025), comprising all the genomes publicly available at the time of the study. The 388 ST307 isolates from our collection were also included in the analysis. The accession number, host, isolation source, geographical origin, and genotype data are listed in Supplementary Data 6. The paired-end raw fastq reads were mapped to the reference genome BK68976 using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). If raw sequencing data of genomes from the public database were not available, genome assemblies were used. To get the recombination regions among all the ST307 strains, an initial tree was built based on the whole genome alignment by using RAxML v8.2.1245. The nodes of the initial tree were reduced to 600 by using Treemmer v0.351, which reduces the size of datasets with different phylogenetic structures and levels of redundancy while maintaining a sub-sample that is representative of the original diversity. Gubbins v3.0.044 was then used to detect recombination regions among the 600 strains, representing the recombination regions of the entire ST307 population. The maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed with the recombination-free whole genome alignment by using RAxML v8.2.12 (model GTR + GAMMA)45. The clades were inferred by using fastbaps v1.0.852.

Plasmid curing

To eliminate the lone IncF hybrid plasmid pKPN-US-1 from the ST307 isolate BK70163 and from isolate BK72421, we employed the CRISPR-Cas-based vector pLCasCureT as previously described18. This construct carries a guide RNA targeting selected plasmid replicons and encodes Cas9 under the control of the arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter. The vector was introduced into the parental isolates by conjugation, after which Cas9 expression was induced. Transconjugants were screened for plasmid loss by PCR targeting the replicon sequence. Following confirmation of plasmid curing, pLCasCureT was removed through sacB-mediated counterselection. The final mutant strains retained an unaltered chromosome and were fully cured of the pKNP-US-1 plasmid.

Minimum inhibitory concentration

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using the broth microdilution method in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Briefly, bacterial cultures grown in LB medium were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.2. Subsequently, 5 × 105 CFU were inoculated into the appropriate assay medium containing serial two-fold dilutions of the antibiotic or metal compound in 96-well plates. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16–20 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that prevented visible bacterial growth.

Heat shock assays

Heat shock assays were performed by diluting stationary-phase bacterial cultures 1:10 in sterile 1× phosphate-buffered saline. Cell suspensions were incubated at the indicated temperatures, and aliquots were withdrawn at defined time intervals. To determine survival rates, samples were serially diluted and plated on LB agar. Colony-forming units (CFU) were enumerated after overnight incubation at 37 °C. Survival was expressed as the ratio of CFU at each time point to the CFU at time zero (prior to heat shock exposure). Survival curves for individual strains were generated accordingly.

Growth assays in iron-depleted medium

Iron-depleted cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (IDMH) was prepared as previously described (CLSI M23, 4th ed.). The final iron concentration was ≤0.03 μg/mL, as verified using the Viscolor® assay. An inoculum of 5 × 104 CFU/mL was dispensed into 96-well plates containing IDMH and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. OD600 measurements were recorded every 20 min using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted by using R v4.2.3. The t-test was used to calculate P values of comparisons of resistance classes and resistance genes. ANOVA was used to compare the survival rate and growth curve. Fisher’s Exact Test or Chi-squared Test was used to compare the proportion data. Temporal trends were assessed using linear regression analysis.
