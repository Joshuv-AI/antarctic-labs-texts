Real-World Analysis of Switching Between Ranibizumab Biosimilars: Safety and Results - a Multicenter Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Debdulal Chakraborty, Tushar Kanti Sinha, Rupak Kanti Biswas, Aniruddha Maiti, Manab Jyoti Barman, Ranabir Bhattacharya, Subhendu Boral, Arnab Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo describe disease-specific visual, anatomical, treatment-exposure, and documented safety outcomes over 24 weeks among eyes undergoing availability-driven switching between ranibizumab biosimilars in routine retinal practice in India.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective observational cohort study reviewed records from January to December 2024. Baseline was the index-switch visit, before administration of the substituted ranibizumab biosimilar. Only one eye per patient was included; when both eyes were eligible, the right eye was selected by convention. Eligible eyes had at least one documented availability-driven biosimilar-to-biosimilar switch and complete 24-week follow-up. Outcomes were summarized descriptively because there was no non-switch comparator group.ResultsOf 742 screened eyes, 595 eyes from 595 patients met the inclusion criteria and received 1625 intravitreal ranibizumab biosimilar injections over 24 weeks (mean, 2.73 injections per eye). One switch was recorded in 466 eyes (78.3%) and two switches in 129 eyes (21.7%). Mean BCVA improved from 0.57 logMAR at baseline to 0.26 at 12 weeks and 0.32 at 24 weeks; the early visual improvement therefore attenuated modestly by week 24 but remained better than baseline. Mean central macular thickness decreased from 490.4 micrometers at baseline to 273.6 micrometers at 24 weeks. At final follow-up, 369 eyes (62.0%) gained at least two lines and 488 eyes (82.0%) maintained or improved vision. No endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, or treatment-related systemic adverse event was documented in the available clinical records.ConclusionEyes exposed to availability-driven switching between ranibizumab biosimilars showed favorable short-term visual and anatomical outcomes over 24 weeks. Because this retrospective study lacked a non-switch comparator and protocol-mandated safety or immunogenicity surveillance, it cannot establish an effect attributable to switching, comparative safety, equivalence, or formal interchangeability.

Introduction

Retinal vascular diseases, including neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular edema (DME), retinal vein occlusion (RVO), and myopic choroidal neovascularization (mCNV), remain major causes of potentially irreversible visual impairment worldwide. The introduction of intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy has transformed the management of these disorders, with ranibizumab and other anti-VEGF agents demonstrating substantial visual and anatomical benefits across multiple VEGF-driven retinal diseases.1,2 However, the effectiveness of anti-VEGF therapy in routine practice depends not only on pharmacological efficacy, but also on sustained treatment intensity, adherence to follow-up, and timely retreatment. Real-world studies have consistently shown that undertreatment and reduced visit adherence may limit visual outcomes compared with pivotal clinical trials, particularly in chronic diseases requiring repeated intravitreal injections.3,4

The challenge of long-term anti-VEGF therapy is particularly relevant in resource-constrained healthcare systems. In India, intravitreal therapy is frequently influenced by out-of-pocket expenditure, variable insurance coverage, travel burden, and institutional drug availability.5,6 The emergence of ranibizumab biosimilars has therefore been clinically important, offering a regulated and more affordable alternative to reference ranibizumab while potentially improving access to treatment. Unlike off-label repackaged agents, ranibizumab biosimilars undergo analytical comparability evaluation, non-clinical assessment, and clinical testing to demonstrate similarity to the reference biologic in quality, efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity.7,8

Over the last decade, the evidence base supporting ranibizumab biosimilars has expanded substantially. Indian evidence includes prospective, Phase 3, and real-world studies of individual products and comparisons between biosimilar and reference ranibizumab.9–13 Globally, randomized trials of ranibizumab biosimilars such as SB11 and FYB201 have demonstrated efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity profiles comparable to reference ranibizumab in neovascular AMD.14,15 Contemporary ophthalmic reviews have further supported the clinical comparability of approved anti-VEGF biosimilars to their reference products when used within appropriate regulatory frameworks.16

Despite this growing evidence, most ophthalmic biosimilar studies have evaluated either a single biosimilar agent, reference-to-biosimilar switching, or comparisons between biosimilar and innovator ranibizumab. The clinical scenario of biosimilar-to-biosimilar switching remains less well characterized. This distinction is important because biologic agents are complex molecules produced through living systems, and biosimilars are not identical copies of either the reference product or of one another. Although approved biosimilars are expected to have no clinically meaningful differences from their reference biologic, sequential exposure to different biosimilars represents a pragmatic real-world situation that may raise questions regarding maintained efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity.17,18

The regulatory concept of interchangeability also differs across jurisdictions and clinical settings. In the United States, interchangeability has historically been linked to pharmacy-level substitution, while in Europe, the European Medicines Agency and Heads of Medicines Agencies have stated that approved biosimilars may be considered interchangeable with their reference medicine or with an equivalent biosimilar, subject to national implementation.19,20 Ophthalmology presents an additional nuance: intravitreal anti-VEGF injections are administered by retina specialists rather than dispensed directly to patients through pharmacies. Therefore, switching in ophthalmic practice is usually physician-supervised and often driven by clinical, economic, institutional, formulary, procurement, or supply-chain considerations rather than automatic substitution.17,18

India provides a particularly relevant setting in which to study this question. Multiple ranibizumab biosimilars are available in routine retinal practice, and high-volume eye-care systems may encounter drug substitutions due to procurement cycles, tendering, cost considerations, or temporary product availability. In such settings, patients may receive more than one ranibizumab biosimilar during the course of treatment, even in the absence of treatment failure or safety concerns. Understanding clinical outcomes in this setting is important not only for Indian retinal practice, but also for other healthcare systems where biosimilar adoption, formulary-driven substitution, and cost-containment policies are likely to become increasingly relevant.

Against this background, we conducted the REASSURE Study, a multicenter retrospective observational study of eyes with different retinal vascular diseases that underwent at least one availability-driven switch between ranibizumab biosimilars during routine clinical care. The research question was: among eyes with different retinal vascular diseases undergoing biosimilar-to-biosimilar ranibizumab switching, what disease-specific visual, anatomical, treatment-exposure, and documented safety outcomes were observed over 24 weeks? The study was not designed to establish formal interchangeability, equivalence, non-inferiority, or comparative performance of individual products.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a multicenter, retrospective, observational cohort study conducted across three ophthalmic care networks in India. The study evaluated real-world clinical outcomes in eyes with retinal vascular diseases that underwent switching between ranibizumab biosimilars during routine clinical care.

Medical records of patients receiving intravitreal ranibizumab biosimilars between January 2024 and December 2024 were reviewed. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and the principles of Good Clinical Practice. Institutional ethics committee approval was obtained before data collection, including approval from the central ethics committee of Disha Eye Hospitals, Kolkata, India (IRB Reg. No. ECR/846/Inst/WB/2016/RR-24). Written informed consent for intravitreal treatment had been obtained from all patients at the time of routine clinical care. Data were anonymized before analysis.

Study Population

Medical records of patients diagnosed with retinal vascular diseases and treated with intravitreal ranibizumab biosimilars during the study period were screened. The included disease categories were neovascular age-related macular degeneration, diabetic macular edema, retinal vein occlusion, myopic choroidal neovascularization, and other VEGF-mediated chorioretinal vascular disorders. Only one eye per patient was included. When both eyes fulfilled the eligibility criteria, the right eye was selected by convention. Accordingly, each patient contributed only one longitudinal outcome trajectory.

Eligible eyes were required to have received a ranibizumab biosimilar immediately before the index switch, undergone at least one documented switch to a different ranibizumab biosimilar, and completed 24 weeks of follow-up. BCVA and OCT data had to be available at the index-switch visit before administration of the substituted product and at follow-up. The total duration and number of anti-VEGF injections before the index switch were not uniformly available and were not analyzed.

Inclusion Criteria

Eyes were included if they fulfilled all of the following criteria: diagnosis of a retinal vascular disease requiring intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy; treatment with a ranibizumab biosimilar available within the participating centers; documentation of at least one switch from one ranibizumab biosimilar to another during routine clinical care; availability of BCVA, ocular examination, and spectral-domain OCT data at the index-switch visit and follow-up; and completion of the 24-week assessment.

Exclusion Criteria

Eyes were excluded for incomplete clinical or imaging records; follow-up of less than 24 weeks; intraocular surgery within the preceding 3 months; active or previous intraocular inflammation or endophthalmitis; coexisting retinal, optic nerve, or media pathology likely to confound visual outcomes; media opacity preventing reliable examination or OCT assessment; or missing documentation of the administered ranibizumab biosimilar.

Ranibizumab Biosimilars Evaluated

The products available during the study period were Razumab (ranibizumab; Intas Pharmaceuticals, Ahmedabad, India), Ranieyes (ranibizumab; Lupin Pharmaceuticals, Mumbai, India), and Oceva (ranibizumab; Sun Pharmaceutical Industries Ltd., Mumbai, India). For subsequent presentation, these are designated biosimilars A, B, and C, respectively. Other ranibizumab biosimilars marketed in India were not included because they were not available within the participating hospital networks during the study period.

Definition of Biosimilar Switching

For the purpose of this study, biosimilar switching was defined as administration of a ranibizumab biosimilar different from the ranibizumab biosimilar administered at the immediately preceding injection visit.

The first documented change from one ranibizumab biosimilar to another was the index biosimilar switch. The reason was documented for every switch and was temporary product unavailability or institutional procurement. Under routine hospital protocol, the same ranibizumab biosimilar was otherwise continued. When inadequate response warranted escalation, treatment was changed to another anti-VEGF molecule, such as aflibercept or faricimab, rather than to a different ranibizumab biosimilar. Eyes with more than one availability-driven biosimilar switch were retained, and the number and sequence of switches were recorded.

Treatment Protocol

All intravitreal injections were administered by trained retina specialists under aseptic precautions in an operating room, according to institutional protocols. A standard dose of 0.5 mg/0.05 mL ranibizumab biosimilar was injected intravitreally. Treatment regimens reflected routine real-world clinical practice. Eyes with neovascular age-related macular degeneration received loading injections followed by pro re nata retreatment based on disease activity assessment carried out every 4 to 6 weeks. Eyes with diabetic macular edema, retinal vein occlusion, and myopic choroidal neovascularization were treated using loading or 1 + PRN regimens at the discretion of the treating physician.

Retreatment decisions were based on one or more of the following: reduction or worsening of best-corrected visual acuity attributable to disease activity, persistence or recurrence of intraretinal fluid or subretinal fluid on OCT, increase in central macular thickness, persistent or recurrent macular edema, new hemorrhage, or treating physician assessment.

Clinical Assessments and Data Collection

At the index-switch visit, demographic and clinical data were collected from medical records before administration of the substituted biosimilar. These included age, sex, diagnosis, selected eye, BCVA, central macular thickness, ranibizumab biosimilar administered, injection exposure during follow-up, switching sequence, reason for switching, and documented adverse events.

Best-corrected visual acuity was recorded using Snellen charts during routine care and converted to logMAR units for descriptive analysis. Anatomical assessment was performed using the spectral-domain OCT platform routinely available at each center, including Cirrus 5000 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) and Topcon Maestro (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). An individual eye was assessed on the same device whenever feasible. Central macular thickness was recorded in micrometers.

Follow-up assessments were evaluated at approximately 4, 8, 12, and 24 weeks after the index switch. No prospective visit windows were imposed; the closest available routine visit was used. The 24-week horizon was prespecified to capture several routine anti-VEGF treatment visits while retaining comparable multicenter documentation and should be interpreted as short-term follow-up.

Outcome Measures

The primary effectiveness outcome was change in best-corrected visual acuity from baseline to 24 weeks. The primary safety outcome was the incidence of documented ocular and systemic adverse events during the 24-week follow-up period.

Secondary outcome measures included change in central macular thickness from baseline to 24 weeks, change in BCVA and central macular thickness at intermediate follow-up visits, number of injections administered during the 24-week study period, proportion of eyes gaining two or more lines of vision, proportion of eyes maintaining or improving vision, and biosimilar switching patterns and sequences.

Safety Assessment

Safety outcomes were assessed by reviewing clinical records at each follow-up visit. Ocular adverse events included ocular pain, burning sensation, transient blurring of vision, subconjunctival hemorrhage, anterior chamber inflammation, vitreous hemorrhage, hyphema, retinal pigment epithelial tear, retinal detachment, and endophthalmitis.

Systemic adverse events, including thromboembolic events or other systemic complications temporally associated with intravitreal therapy, were recorded where documented.

Adverse events were classified descriptively. Because this was a retrospective real-world study, adverse-event reporting was based on documentation in routine clinical records rather than protocol-mandated active surveillance.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Given the retrospective single-arm design and absence of a non-switch comparator, the revised analysis was restricted to descriptive reporting. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous demographic variables were summarized using available means and standard deviations, whereas the principal BCVA, CMT, and injection-exposure outcomes are presented as observed means and mean changes without unsupported dispersion or causal estimates.

No head-to-head comparison between individual ranibizumab biosimilars was undertaken because product exposure was availability-driven, nonrandomized, and markedly imbalanced. Injection-level observations were not treated as independent clinical outcome units.

The primary analysis included one eye from each patient. When both eyes were eligible, selection of the right eye by convention prevented inter-eye clustering. Each selected eye contributed one longitudinal outcome trajectory irrespective of the number of injections or switching events.

Small subgroups, including central retinal vein occlusion and myopic choroidal neovascularization, were interpreted as exploratory descriptive cohorts because no a priori sample size calculation was performed and their sample sizes were limited.

Only eyes with complete BCVA and OCT data at the index-switch and 24-week visits were included. No missing outcome values were imputed. All eligible complete cases during the study period were included; therefore, no a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Methodological Clarification

This study was not designed as a randomized clinical trial, equivalence study, non-inferiority study, or formal interchangeability study. No predefined non-inferiority or equivalence margin was specified. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive real-world evidence of clinical outcomes observed in eyes undergoing pragmatic biosimilar-to-biosimilar ranibizumab switching, rather than proof of formal interchangeability between individual ranibizumab biosimilars.

Results

Study Cohort and Patient Selection

A total of 742 eyes receiving intravitreal ranibizumab biosimilars across participating centers between January and December 2024 were screened. Exclusions comprised incomplete clinical or imaging records in 69 eyes, follow-up shorter than 24 weeks in 27 eyes, coexisting pathology likely to confound visual outcomes in 35 eyes, and intraocular surgery within the preceding 3 months in 16 eyes. The final complete-case cohort included 595 eyes from 595 patients, all of which underwent at least one availability-driven biosimilar-to-biosimilar switch. Baseline Characteristics and Treatment Exposure

The mean age of the 595 included patients was 52.1 ± 11.8 years, and 64.9% were male. The cohort included diabetic macular edema, branch retinal vein occlusion, neovascular age-related macular degeneration, central retinal vein occlusion, myopic choroidal neovascularization, and other heterogeneous chorioretinal vascular disorders.

Diabetic macular edema was the most common diagnosis, accounting for 238 eyes (40.0%), followed by branch retinal vein occlusion in 187 eyes (31.4%) and neovascular age-related macular degeneration in 132 eyes (22.2%). Central retinal vein occlusion, myopic choroidal neovascularization, and miscellaneous chorioretinal disorders accounted for 11 eyes (1.8%), 5 eyes (0.8%), and 22 eyes (3.7%), respectively.

A total of 1625 intravitreal ranibizumab biosimilar injections were administered over 24 weeks, corresponding to a mean of 2.73 injections per eye. The mean was 3.71 in nAMD, 2.72 in DME, 2.15 in BRVO, 2.18 in CRVO, 2.20 in myopic CNV, and 2.23 in the miscellaneous group. Biosimilar Switching Patterns

All eyes included in the final analysis had undergone at least one documented switch between ranibizumab biosimilars. One switch, defined as exposure to two different ranibizumab biosimilars, was recorded in 466 eyes (78.3%). Two switches, defined as exposure to all three ranibizumab biosimilars available within the participating centers, were recorded in 129 eyes (21.7%).

Immediately before the index switch, 577 eyes had received biosimilar A and 18 had received biosimilar B; none had received biosimilar C. The most common index sequence was A to B (382 eyes; 64.2%), followed by A to C (195 eyes; 32.8%) and B to C (18 eyes; 3.0%). Aggregate Initial-Treatment Context

The separate initial-treatment dataset comprised 742 treatment-naive eyes: 179 with nAMD, 295 with DME, 212 with BRVO, 16 with CRVO, 9 with myopic CNV, and 31 with miscellaneous disorders. A total of 1,847 injections were administered during the initial 24-week period, corresponding to 2.49 injections per eye. Functional Outcomes

Mean BCVA improved from 0.57 logMAR at the index-switch visit to 0.26 at 12 weeks and 0.32 at 24 weeks. Thus, the magnitude of early visual improvement attenuated modestly between weeks 12 and 24, while the 24-week mean remained better than baseline. Safety Outcomes

No cases of endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, or treatment-related systemic adverse events were documented during the 24-week follow-up period.

Minor ocular adverse events were infrequent and generally self-limiting. Mild ocular pain was documented in 57 eyes (9.6%), of which 11 eyes required oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication. Transient burning sensation was recorded in 41 eyes (6.9%), temporary blurring of vision in 21 eyes (3.5%), and subconjunctival hemorrhage in 18 eyes (3.0%). Mild anterior chamber inflammation occurred in 5 eyes (0.8%) and resolved with topical corticosteroid therapy. Hyphema was recorded in 1 eye (0.2%), vitreous hemorrhage in 2 eyes (0.3%), and retinal pigment epithelial tear in 3 eyes (0.5%). None of the recorded adverse events resulted in permanent visual loss or required surgical intervention. Discussion

The REASSURE study evaluated outcomes in 595 eyes from 595 patients undergoing availability-driven switching between ranibizumab biosimilars during routine retinal practice in India. Over 24 weeks and 1625 injections, favorable descriptive functional and anatomical outcomes were observed. Mean BCVA changed from 0.57 logMAR at the index-switch visit to 0.26 at 12 weeks and 0.32 at 24 weeks, demonstrating partial attenuation after the early response. Mean CMT changed from 490.4 to 273.6 micrometers. At final follow-up, 369 eyes gained at least two lines and 488 maintained or improved visual acuity. No endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, or treatment-related systemic adverse event was documented. These observations describe short-term outcomes during continued anti-VEGF care and should not be interpreted as an effect caused by switching.

The principal contribution of this study is its focus on sequential biosimilar-to-biosimilar exposure rather than the more commonly studied individual product or reference-to-biosimilar transition.6,9–16,21–24 In this cohort, every switch was documented as arising from temporary product unavailability or institutional procurement rather than inadequate response. The same biosimilar was normally continued under routine hospital protocol, while inadequate response prompted escalation to a different anti-VEGF molecule rather than substitution with another ranibizumab biosimilar. REASSURE therefore addresses a pragmatic supply-chain scenario identified as an emerging issue for retina specialists.17,18

A critical distinction should be made between biosimilar switching in routine practice and formal regulatory interchangeability. Biologic medicines are complex molecules produced through living systems, and biosimilars are not identical copies of either the reference product or of one another. Regulatory approval is based on the totality of evidence demonstrating that there are no clinically meaningful differences from the reference biologic in quality, efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity.6,7 However, sequential exposure to different biosimilars represents a distinct real-world scenario. In ophthalmology, switching also differs from pharmacy-level substitution in systemic specialties because intravitreal anti-VEGF injections are administered directly by ophthalmologists. Thus, biosimilar switching in retina practice is generally physician-supervised and often influenced by clinical, economic, institutional, or supply-chain factors.17–20 The findings of REASSURE should therefore be interpreted as observational evidence from a physician-supervised real-world setting, not as proof of formal interchangeability, equivalence, or non-inferiority between individual ranibizumab biosimilars.

The functional outcomes are consistent with the expected response to ongoing anti-VEGF therapy in VEGF-mediated retinal diseases. The greatest mean improvement occurred by 12 weeks, followed by partial regression at 24 weeks. In the absence of a non-switch comparator, this pattern cannot be assigned to switching. It may reflect ongoing treatment, disease chronicity, diagnostic heterogeneity, variable PRN intensity, or delayed visits.3,10,22–24 The mean injection exposure of 2.73 per eye over 24 weeks also reflects routine PRN care rather than fixed monthly treatment.

Anatomical outcomes paralleled the functional improvement. The pooled mean CMT reduction of 216.8 µm over 24 weeks supports continued biological activity of ranibizumab biosimilar therapy in reducing VEGF-mediated vascular leakage and macular edema. Anatomical improvement was observed across diagnostic subgroups, with particularly large reductions in eyes with high baseline macular thickness, such as DME and CRVO. However, subgroup outcomes require cautious interpretation. DME, BRVO, and nAMD formed the major diagnostic categories in this cohort, whereas CRVO and myopic CNV included small numbers of eyes. Therefore, the latter subgroup trends should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of disease-specific efficacy.

The available studies provide supportive context but answer different questions. ASSET evaluated an individual biosimilar in nAMD, Ratra et al compared biosimilar and reference ranibizumab with bevacizumab, and Indian DME and nAMD studies compared biosimilar with innovator ranibizumab.9,10,22,23,24 The BRESER and Phase 3 Sun Pharma studies evaluated individual products rather than sequential switching.11,12 These studies support the clinical activity of ranibizumab biosimilars but do not directly evaluate availability-driven exposure to more than one biosimilar.

The BRIO Study compared one ranibizumab biosimilar with innovator ranibizumab across several retinal vascular diseases over 24 months.24 Bommakanti et al examined a reference-to-biosimilar transition in nAMD, DME, and RVO.21 In contrast, REASSURE includes sequential biosimilar-to-biosimilar exposure across multiple diagnoses. Its unique setting is balanced by important weaknesses: it is uncontrolled, has only 24 weeks of follow-up, uses nonstandardized PRN treatment, and includes no direct immunogenicity assessment.

Randomized trials of SB11 and FYB201 demonstrated comparable efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity relative to reference ranibizumab in nAMD.14,15 Contemporary reviews emphasize analytical comparability, pharmacovigilance, and careful interpretation of biosimilar evidence.6,16 These data establish biosimilarity to a reference product; they do not establish the causal effect of switching between different biosimilars in an uncontrolled real-world cohort.

The index-switch patterns - A to B, A to C, and B to C - primarily reflected product chronology, procurement cycles, and temporary institutional availability. Immediately before switching, exposure was markedly imbalanced (577 eyes receiving A and 18 receiving B), with no eye receiving C. A product-level comparison could therefore create a misleading ranking driven by nonrandom treatment sequence and disease mix. The study consequently reports disease-specific longitudinal outcomes and does not compare individual biosimilars.

The safety observations in REASSURE were reassuring within the important limits of retrospective clinical documentation. No cases of endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, or treatment-related systemic adverse events were recorded during the 24-week observation period. Minor ocular events, including ocular pain, transient burning sensation, temporary visual blurring, subconjunctival hemorrhage, mild anterior chamber inflammation, hyphema, vitreous hemorrhage, and RPE tear, were infrequent and did not result in permanent visual loss or surgical intervention. These findings are broadly consistent with prior real-world and clinical trial evidence supporting the ocular safety of approved ranibizumab biosimilars.9–16,24 The large CRIBS study also reported a comparable real-world safety profile between innovator and biosimilar ranibizumab in chorioretinal vascular diseases, further supporting the safety experience of biosimilar ranibizumab in Indian retinal practice.13 The favorable safety profile observed in REASSURE is also consistent with the larger BRIO cohort, in which serious ocular and systemic adverse events were rare in both biosimilar and innovator ranibizumab groups, with no new safety signal over 24 months.24 Nevertheless, the absence of a documented safety signal in this cohort should not be interpreted as evidence that rare, delayed, or immune-mediated events cannot occur. Larger datasets, longer follow-up, active pharmacovigilance, and direct immunogenicity testing are required to detect uncommon inflammatory or immune-mediated events.

India represents one of the most clinically relevant settings for evaluating ranibizumab biosimilars because multiple biosimilars are available, anti-VEGF therapy is frequently influenced by out-of-pocket expenditure, and institutional procurement can affect continuity of drug availability.4,5,8 In such settings, switching between biosimilars may occur to avoid treatment interruption when a specific product is temporarily unavailable. The present study therefore reflects a real-world scenario with direct relevance to high-volume retina practices and healthcare systems seeking to balance access, affordability, and continuity of care. As biosimilar anti-VEGF adoption expands globally, similar questions regarding formulary-driven or procurement-driven switching may become increasingly relevant beyond India.

This study has several strengths. It includes a relatively large multicenter cohort in an under-studied biosimilar-to-biosimilar switching scenario, reflects routine practice across high-volume Indian retina centers, documents the reason for every switch, uses an index-switch baseline, and includes functional, anatomical, treatment-exposure, switching-sequence, and documented safety outcomes. Restricting the cohort to one eye per patient also avoids inter-eye clustering.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective single-arm design precludes causal inference, and the absence of a non-switch comparator means that the observed changes may reflect ongoing anti-VEGF therapy, disease course, or treatment intensity rather than switching. Although baseline was anchored to the index-switch visit, previous treatment duration and exposure were not uniformly available. The cohort included diseases with different natural histories and treatment regimens; CRVO and myopic CNV were particularly small, while the miscellaneous group contained heterogeneous poor-prognosis disorders and was not interpreted as a single efficacy subgroup. Treatment and visit timing were not standardized, Snellen acuity was converted to logMAR, and OCT platforms varied across centers. Only complete 24-week cases were analyzed, which introduces potential attrition bias. Follow-up was limited to 24 weeks, adverse-event ascertainment depended on retrospective documentation, and anti-drug antibody testing was not performed. The study therefore cannot establish switching-related efficacy, comparative safety, equivalence, or interchangeability.

Despite these limitations, REASSURE contributes descriptive evidence from a clinically relevant procurement-driven setting. Future prospective studies should use standardized switch-anchored visits, a suitable non-switch comparator, longer follow-up, protocol-based adverse-event and immunogenicity surveillance, and longitudinal models appropriate to the available patient-level data.

Conclusions

In this multicenter real-world cohort, eyes exposed to availability-driven switching between ranibizumab biosimilars showed visual and anatomical improvement over 24 weeks, although the magnitude of visual improvement attenuated between weeks 12 and 24. These outcomes cannot be attributed specifically to switching because there was no non-switch comparator and treatment continued according to routine disease activity. No obvious new safety signal was identified in the available retrospective records, but the study cannot establish switching-related efficacy, comparative safety, equivalence, or formal interchangeability.
