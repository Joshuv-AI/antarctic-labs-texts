Bifidobacterium longum inhibits Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans– associated gingival epithelial ferroptosis and protects cellular junctions
Authors: Xiaojiao Sun, Zehui Wang, Dan Qiu, Di Yan, Kun Cao, Sasaki Jun-Ichi, Imazato Satoshi, Xu Qin, Xiaojuan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeriodontal pathogens disrupt the gingival epithelial barrier, but the molecular links among junctional damage, ferroptosis, and inflammation remain unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate whether Bifidobacterium longum (BL) counteracts Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa)-induced junctional injury via regulation of ferroptosis in human gingival epithelial cells (HGECs).DesignOral microbiota differences between periodontitis patients and healthy controls were analyzed using 16S rRNA sequencing, combined with GSE16134 bioinformatics analysis. HGECs were exposed to Aa (1 × 10⁴ CFU/ml) and treated with BL (1 × 10⁸ CFU/ml) or ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 2 μM). Cell viability, mitochondrial morphology, ROS, junction proteins (CDH1, CLDN1), ferroptosis markers (SLC7A11, GPX4, NFE2L2), and inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, TNF) were assessed.ResultsBioinformatics revealed enrichment of junction-related pathways associated with ferroptosis. Aa induced mitochondrial damage, ROS accumulation, suppression of ferroptosis-protective signaling and junction proteins, and pro-inflammatory cytokine imbalance. BL significantly restored mitochondrial integrity, ferroptosis-related signaling, epithelial junctions, and inflammatory homeostasis, with effects comparable to or exceeding Fer-1.ConclusionAa disrupts gingival epithelial integrity through ferroptosis-mediated oxidative and inflammatory damage. BL effectively suppresses this cascade and protects epithelial junctions, highlighting its therapeutic potential for periodontitis.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease primarily driven by pathogenic bacteria. It causes progressive destruction of the tooth-supporting tissues, ultimately leading to tooth mobility and loss. Junctional complexes between gingival epithelial cells constitute a critical barrier to microbial invasion and are essential for maintaining epithelial cohesion and tissue integrity [1]. However, pathogens such as Aa can disrupt junctional proteins, including E-cadherin (CDH1) and claudin-1 (CLDN1), thereby compromising epithelial barrier function [2]. This disruption facilitates bacterial penetration and exacerbates the inflammatory response, contributing to periodontal tissue damage [3]. Current therapeutic strategies for periodontitis primarily involve mechanical debridement, adjunctive antibiotic administration and surgical intervention [4]. While these approaches can temporarily mitigate disease progression, they present notable limitations, including the risk of antibiotic resistance, high recurrence rates and insufficient capacity for tissue regeneration [5]. Consequently, the identification and development of novel therapeutic targets remain a critical need in periodontal therapy [6–8].

Based on our 16S rRNA sequencing results and recent studies, BL, a probiotic commonly present in the oral microbiome, has emerged as a promising candidate for therapeutic intervention. BL has demonstrated potential in modulating microbial homoeostasis, attenuating inflammatory responses and preserving the integrity of epithelial junctional structures [9].

Moreover, ferroptosis – a regulated form of cell death – has garnered increasing attention for its involvement in the pathogenesis of periodontitis [10,11]. The accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during ferroptosis triggers lipid peroxidation, ultimately resulting in cell membrane disruption. Activation of this pathway has been shown to aggravate periodontal tissue destruction [12].

In all, this study aims to investigate whether BL can inhibit Aa-associated ferroptosis in human gingival epithelial cells via modulation of the ferroptosis signalling pathway. Through integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we seek to determine whether BL attenuates the inflammatory response and preserves epithelial junctional integrity. Ultimately, our findings may offer a novel microecological strategy for the prevention and treatment of periodontitis [13,14].

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Subgingival plaque and gingival crevicular fluid samples were collected from natural teeth of fifteen patients with periodontitis and fifteen periodontally healthy individuals, who were randomly selected from the Department of Oral Prosthodontics and the Department of Periodontology at the Dental Hospital of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. The study protocol complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. KYLL-2024-0358).

16S rRNA sequencing

Gingival crevicular fluid was collected from 15 patients with periodontitis (PD) and 15 periodontally healthy controls (NC) for 16S rRNA gene sequencing of the V3–V4 region using universal primers 338F/806R. Bacterial DNA was extracted with the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs; ≥95% sequence identity) were clustered with USEARCH and taxonomically annotated with QIIME. Functional profiles were inferred with Tax4Fun2, and downstream analyses were performed using the Biomarker BioCloud platform.

GEO dataset

The transcriptomic dataset GSE16134 was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), comprising 310 gingival tissue samples (241 from individuals with periodontitis and 69 from periodontally healthy controls). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined using adjusted p value (adj. p < 0.05) and an absolute log fold change (|logFC| > 0) as thresholds. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.1) [15].

GOKEGG enrichment

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed with the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) after converting gene identifiers using the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database (Homo sapiens). Over-representation analysis (ORA) was applied. Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were visualised using ggplot2 (v3.4.4). Pairwise similarities among enriched terms were computed with the Jaccard index based on shared genes and subjected to hierarchical clustering (hclust). Enrichment maps were then constructed and visualised using igraph (v1.4.1) and ggraph (v2.1.0).

GSEA enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed with the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) using gene sets retrieved from the molecular signatures database (MSigDB) via the msigdbr package. Gene identifiers were mapped with the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database (Homo sapiens). GSEA was conducted on pre-ranked gene lists, and enrichment results were visualised using ggplot2 (v3.4.4). Pairwise Jaccard similarity coefficients based on shared genes were used to quantify functional similarity among enriched gene sets, followed by hierarchical clustering (hclust) and network visualisation with the igraph (v1.4.1) and ggraph (v2.1.0) packages.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was performed in R (v4.2.1) to evaluate pairwise associations among selected genes, including ferroptosis-related genes (SLC7A11, GPX4, NFE2L2), inflammation-related genes (IL6, IL10 and TNF) and cell junction-related genes (CDH1, CLDN1). Correlation matrices were visualised using ggplot2 (v3.4.4), while network diagrams were constructed using igraph (v1.4.1) and ggraph (v2.1.0).

PPI network

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed to explore the interactions among CDH1, CLDN1, SLC7A11, GPX4, NFE2L2, IL6, IL10 and TNF using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/).

Antibacterial zone experiment

The antibacterial inhibition zone test was conducted according to the CLSI M02 standard operating protocol. A total of 100 µL of 0.5 McFarland bacterial suspension (approximately 1 × 10 × 10⁸ CFU/ml) was applied using a sterile L-shaped glass rod. The rod was moved in multiple directions (front, back, left, right) while rotating the plate to ensure uniform distribution of the bacterial suspension across the entire surface to make sure Aa was evenly spread onto agar plates. After inoculation, the plate was incubated on a laminar flow hood for 10 minutes to allow complete absorption of the bacterial suspension by agar and surface drying [16]. A 6 mm puncher was used to create holes in the culture medium. Each well received 50 µL of the following treatments: PBS (negative control), 1.0 × 10, 1.0 × 10², 1.0 × 10⁴, 1.0 × 10⁶ and 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/ml BL. The plates were then placed in anaerobic culture bags and incubated at 37 °C for 48 hours.

Human gingival epithelial cells (HGECs) were obtained from the Shanghai Rongmin Biotechnology Centre. Cells were cultured in DMEM-H high-glucose basal medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days.

For the Aa group, bacteria in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and resuspended in PBS. Similarly, BL in the logarithmic phase was collected and resuspended in PBS for the BL group. An in vitro co-culture model was established with the following groups:

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from sixth-passage HGECs cultured in the NC, Aa, BL and Fer-1 groups. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with rapid blocking buffer, followed by incubation with the following primary antibodies: NFE2L2 (Proteintech), GPX4 (Proteintech), SLC7A11 (Proteintech), CDH1 (zenbio) and CLDN1 (zenbio), IL6, IL10 and TNF (abclonal). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Abclonal, China). Protein bands were visualised using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sixth-passage HGECs were cultured and assigned to the NC, Aa, BL and Fer-1 groups. Total protein was extracted from each group, and the expression levels of CDH1, CLDN1, SLC7A11, GPX4, NFE2L2, IL6, IL10 and TNF were quantified using ELISA kits (Yabscience, Nanjing, China). Absorbance was measured at 480 nm.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Sixth-passage HGECs were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips (24 mm diameter) at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per coverslip and divided into four groups: NC, Aa, BL and Fer-1. After appropriate treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with a suitable detergent (e.g., 0.1% Triton X-100), and incubated with primary antibodies against CDH1, CLDN1, SLC7A11, GPX4, NFE2L2, IL6, IL10 and TNF. Subsequently, cells were incubated with fluorescently labelled secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan), and three random fields per group were recorded for analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

HGECs (1 × 10⁶ cells) were seeded into 60 mm culture dishes and divided into four groups: NC, Aa, BL and Fer-1. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 hours. Samples were then post-fixed with 2% uranyl acetate, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in resin and stained with lead citrate. Ultrathin sections were prepared using a Leica ultramicrotome (Leica UC7, Germany). Cellular ultrastructure was examined and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800/HT7700, Hitachi, Japan).

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). HGECs from the NC, Aa, BL and Fer-1 groups were incubated with 2 ml of DCFH-DA working solution at 37 °C for 30 minutes. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove excess probe. Fluorescence intensity was then measured by flow cytometry at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. Fluorescent signals were also observed using a fluorescence microscope, and data were analysed using GraphPad Prism.

Statistical methods

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons among three or more groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Significance levels were denoted as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***) and p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

16S rRNA sequencing identified the periodontitis pathogen Aa and the probiotic BL

As shown in Figure 1A, with the increase in the number of sequences, the rarefaction curves of all biological samples tended to plateau, indicating that the experimental data can be used for further bioinformatics analysis and ensuring sufficient sequence diversity and accuracy of the results. Principal component analysis showed significant changes in the composition of gingival crevicular fluid microbiota between the NC and PD groups (Figure 1B). The Venn diagram also showed that the number of shared operational taxonomic units (OTUs) between the two groups was 879, while the number of unique OTUs was 5505 for the NC group and 4864 for the PD group (Figure 1C).

Figure 1D compared the relative abundance of microbial taxa between the NC and PD groups. Unclassified Actinomyces showed an increased relative abundance in the PD group, suggesting its potential expansion and involvement in the dysbiotic shifts associated with periodontitis. In the co-occurrence network (Figure 1E), Actinomyces occupied a node of moderate degree and showed positive correlations with multiple bacterial genera, consistent with a stable, synergistic role within the oral microbial community [18].

In Figure 1F, the predicted functional composition was analysed at the phylum level. Both the pathogenic bacterium Actinomyces and the probiotic Bifidobacterium and exhibited relatively high abundance in the microbial community. Cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis represented one of the core functions of Actinobacteriota. Consistent with the findings reported by Abdulkareem Aa et al., Aa was closely associated with the development of periodontitis [19]. Based on the abundance levels of the top 50 genera in the total abundance of all samples, similar results were obtained in the heatmap (Figure 1G).

Figure 1H indicated KEGG level-3 functional predictions for the PD and NC groups based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing data. Among the enriched pathways, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis and bacterial lipid metabolism (p < 0.05) were of particular interest because they were closely associated with ferroptosis. While LPS-driven inflammatory signalling has been reported to increase reactive oxygen species and labile iron levels and to compromise epithelial barrier integrity, thereby creating a microenvironment that may favour overactivation of ferroptosis-related pathways. These observations provided one of the rationales for focusing on ferroptosis signalling in the present study.

The relative abundance of common probiotics in the PD and NC was compared separately. Compared to the NC group, the genus with a higher relative abundance of probiotics in the periodontitis group was Bifidobacterium (Figure 1I). Among them, the relative abundance of the genus Bifidobacterium showed significant difference between the two groups, and the species level of Bifidobacterium can be divided into: BL and Bifidobacterium breve, etc.

According to our plaque microbiota analysis and in accordance with studies by Kaicen Wang, BL was identified as one of the most abundant species within the Bifidobacterium genus [20]. Consequently, BL was selected as the probiotic candidate for subsequent experiments to evaluate its antimicrobial activity against common pathogens [21]. The inhibition zone assay demonstrated that while the PBS control group failed to exhibit any growth suppression against Aa, all BL treated groups showed statistically significant inhibitory effects (p < 0.05), with optimal efficacy observed in the BL 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml group (p < 0.0001) (FIgure 1J and K). Therefore, this concentration was selected for further investigation.

BL alleviates Aa-associated ferroptosis in oral epithelial cells

First, to determine an effective concentration of the pathogenic bacterium – Aa on epithelial cells, we performed cytotoxicity assays. In Figure 2A, we evaluated the cytotoxic effects of Aa on oral epithelial cells at four different concentrations. A concentration of 1.0 × 10⁴ CFU/ml induced consistent and statistically significant cytotoxicity at 2, 6, 12 and 24 h without causing excessive cell death. We therefore selected 1.0 × 10⁴ CFU/ml as the working concentration of Aa for subsequent experiments.

Additionally, in Figure 1J and K, we observed that 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml of BL exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect against Aa at 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml. However, to further assess whether BL exerts cytotoxic effects on Aa-damaged oral epithelial cells, cell viability assays were conducted (Figure 2B). The results demonstrated that although oral epithelial cells were subjected to Aa-associated cytotoxicity, the addition of BL at concentrations ranging from 1.0 × 104 CFU/ml to 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml significantly mitigated Aa-associated cytotoxicity (p < 0.05). Notably, the most pronounced protective effect was observed at 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml of BL (p < 0.0001).

Therefore, we selected the following four groups to study the pathogenicity of Aa and the therapeutic properties of BL: the NC group, the Aa group (Aa: 1.0 × 104 CFU/ml), the BL group (Aa: 1.0 × 104 CFU/ml + BL: 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml) and the Fer-1 group (Aa: 1.0 × 104 CFU/ml + Fer-1: 2 uM).

As shown in Figure 2C, at 6, 24 and 48 hours, the cell viability of the other three groups was significantly enhanced compared to the Aa group, with statistical significance (p < 0.05). Compared with the BL and Fer-1 groups, the Aa group showed significantly lower cell viability at 6, 12, 24 and 48 hours (p < 0.05). When comparing the BL group and the Fer-1 group, the cell viability of the BL group was upregulated (p < 0.05) at 48 h. Also the cell viability of the BL group was higher than that of the other three groups, with significant statistical significance (p < 0.05). This proves that BL can alleviate the cytotoxicity associated by Aa. However, merely verifying the cytotoxicity of Aa through cell viability assays was insufficient to determine whether it could induce ferroptosis. Therefore, we employed the gold standard for validating ferroptosis occurrence, the morphology of mitochondrial cristae and used Flow Cytometry to detect ROS as a supplementary verification [22,23]. In Figure 2D, the mitochondrial structure in the NC group was clear, exhibiting a normal elliptical shape, with intact membranes, regularly arranged cristae, abundant in number and without significant swelling or fragmentation, serving as a reference for normal mitochondrial morphology. In contrast, the Aa group showed disordered mitochondrial morphology, with fractured or blurred cristae, marked swelling and focal membrane disruption, features that were consistent with activation of ferroptosis-related pathways.

The mitochondrial morphology in the BL group and the Fer-1 group was close to normal, with intact membrane structures and clear cristae and no significant characteristics of ferroptosis were observed. This indicates that BL and Fer-1 have a good inhibitory effect on ferroptosis, and it verifies the involvement of Aa in the occurrence of ferroptosis. In Figure 2E and F, the Aa group significantly upregulated the expression of ROS (p < 0.01), showing a strong oxidative stress response compared to the control group. The ROS levels in the BL group and the Fer-1 group were close to NC, indicating a certain alleviating effect on oxidative damage. The difference between the BL group and the Aa group was significant, suggesting that both Aa and BL might be involved in regulating ROS levels and reducing oxidative stress in different direction. The Fer-1 group, as an inhibitor of ferroptosis, also significantly reduced ROS like BL group, which was consistent with its anti-ferroptosis mechanism [24].

Figure 3I demonstrateed the Spearman correlation coefficients of key genes with CLDN1, revealing significant positive correlations between CLDN1 and CDH1, NFE2L2 (p < 0.05), suggesting that CLDN1 and CDH1, as cellular junction structures, might be involved in regulating ferroptosis or inflammatory responses. Aa–associated cell junctional disruption in HGECs and its association with ferroptosis

We next validated these findings at both the transcriptional and translational levels. At the mRNA level (Figure 4A–H), we focused on the key comparisons between NCand Aa, Aa and BL or Aa and Fer-1 groups. CDH1 and CLDN1 were used as markers of epithelial cell junctions. In Figure 4A, CDH1 expression was significantly reduced in the Aa group compared with NC (p < 0.05). BL or Fer-1 groups significantly increased CDH1 expression relative to Aa (p < 0.001). In Figure 4B, CLDN1 expression was markedly suppressed in the Aa group compared with NC (p < 0.001), consistent with impaired barrier function. BL and Fer-1 treatment significantly upregulated CLDN1 relative to Aa (p < 0.05), partially restoring intercellular junctions towards NC levels.

Inhibition of ferroptosis as a relevant mechanism for BL-mediated protection against Aa-associated epithelial barrier disruption

Figure 6 illustrates the protective role of BL against Aa-associated ferroptosis in gingival epithelial cells by NEF2L2/GPX4/SLC7A11 axis.

Associations of Aa with ferroptosis activation and of BL with its inhibition in periodontitis. A. Aa stimulates the production of pro-inflammatory factors, disrupts the epithelial barrier and promotes ferroptosis. The subsequent disruption of junctional structures and inflammatory damage in gingival epithelial cells induced by Aa ultimately leads to periodontitis. B. BL inhibits ferroptosis by activating the Nrf2/GPX4/SLC7A11 signalling axis, enhances mitochondrial function, thereby alleviating Aa-associated periodontitis and improving the microenvironment.

Under Aa stimulation, inflammatory factors (such as IL6 and TNF) in gingival epithelial cells are significantly upregulated, while the expression of cell junction proteins CDH1 and CLDN1 is downregulated, leading to the impairment of the epithelial barrier structure. Additionally, the expression of key regulatory factors of ferroptosis, SLC7A11, GPX4 and NFE2L2, is significantly downregulated, GSH synthesis is blocked and ROS accumulation increases, ultimately triggering mitochondrial damage and the ferroptosis phenotype. These changes collectively contribute to the deterioration of the local periodontal microenvironment and increase the risk of periodontitis (Figure 6A). After BL treatment, the expression of NFE2L2 was enhanced, which further activated the transcription of its downstream targets GPX4 and SLC7A11, restoring glutathione metabolism and ROS scavenging functions. Concurrently, there was a restoration in the expression of CDH1 and CLDN1, a downregulation of inflammatory factors and a significant increase in the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins. These effects ultimately improved mitochondrial function, reduced programmed cell death and restored epithelial barrier integrity, thereby effectively alleviating the periodontal pathological processes induced by Aa (Figure 6B). By synthesising all results, this study is the first to clarify from the perspective of ferroptosis that Aa-associated ferroptosis and inflammatory damage in HGECs are relevant mechanisms leading to epithelial barrier dysfunction and the occurrence of periodontitis.

Meanwhile, BL exerts multi-target protective effects by inhibiting the ferroptosis signalling pathway, demonstrating potential therapeutic value in restoring cellular connections, anti-inflammatory responses and anti-ferroptosis.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study is to confirm that Aa induces the loss of cellular connections and inflammatory responses through the ferroptosis signalling pathway, and that this process can be reversed by BL. Firstly, Figure 1 validated BL as a potential probiotic capable of antagonising Aa, based on its relative abundance and synergistic interactions within the gingival crevicular fluid microecology associated with periodontitis. In addition, functional prediction identified LPS biosynthesis and bacterial lipid metabolism (p < 0.05) as enriched microbial pathways closely associated with ferroptosis-related signalling. Therefore, we tested whether Aa could regulate ferroptosis and oxidative stress responses, and confirmed that BL has an inhibitory effect on this disruption. The TEM image (Figure 2D) indicates that Aa treatment induced significant mitochondrial damage, with typical ferroptosis characteristics.

The BL group and Fer-1 group significantly alleviated these mitochondrial structural damages, further supporting that the damage was caused by ferroptosis rather than other forms of cell death such as apoptosis or necrosis [36]. The elevation of ROS levels in Figure 2E is one of the key characteristics of ferroptosis. The Aa treatment group induced significant ROS accumulation, indicating that it can trigger oxidative stress and potentially initiate the ferroptosis pathway. Both the BL group and the Fer-1 group effectively inhibited ROS accumulation, validating their protective effects against ferroptosis and also supporting the involvement of ROS in the ferroptosis process [37]. In the bioinformatics analysis of GSE16134, we found that genes associated with periodontitis were enriched across several key pathways, particularly those related to cell connection and adhesion structures – such as cadherin binding, cell junction organisation and adherens junction pathways. These enrichments suggested that disruption of epithelial barrier integrity is a prominent molecular feature of the disease [38,39].

GO/KEGG and GSEA analysis further revealed significantly enriched pathways, including inflammatory cytokine signalling and extracellular matrix degradation. These findings suggested that microenvironmental alterations occurring under chronic inflammation may share molecular mechanisms with periodontal tissue destruction, particularly those involving cell junction and adhesion structures (Figure 3A–H) [40]. CDH1 and CLDN1 may act as bridging molecules connecting cell adhesion, ferroptosis regulation and inflammatory responses, playing a critical role in the development and progression of periodontitis. This indicates their potential involvement in the regulation of oxidative stress and cell death pathways. Through correlation analysis of cell junction-related genes (CDH1, CLDN1), ferroptosis key genes (SLC3A2, SLC7A11, NFE2L2, GPX4) and inflammation-related genes (IL6, IL10), combined with PPI network, the functional connections among these core genes were further validated, indicating a highly intertwined network regulatory mechanism involving cell junction damage, ferroptosis activation and inflammatory signalling in periodontitis (Figure 3I–N).

Subsequently, we performed multi-dimensional validation at both the transcriptional and translational levels. The Aa group generally exhibited features associated with ferroptosis activation, upregulation of inflammatory factors and disruption of cell junctions. Both BL and Fer-1 groups significantly reversed these changes at the mRNA and protein levels. Moreover, in restoring antioxidant capacity, anti-inflammatory activity and junctional integrity, showed slightly better performance than Fer-1 across most of indicators (Figures 4 and 5).

This study preliminarily analysed the pathogenic mechanisms of (Aa) on human gingival epithelial cells (HGECs), and the results revealed that Aa significantly increased ferroptosis phenotypes and disrupted the epithelial barrier function. Specifically, Aa stimulation significantly downregulated the expression of cell junction-related proteins CDH1 and CLDN1, leading to the disruption of intercellular junction structures, suggesting impairment of epithelial barrier integrity [35]. Concurrently, the expression of key regulators of ferroptosis, SLC7A11, GPX4 and NFE2L2, was significantly reduced in the Aa group, with increased ROS levels and damage to mitochondrial ultrastructure, exhibiting typical characteristics of ferroptosis [41].

In this study, we primarily described an association between Aa-associated epithelial barrier disruption and ferroptosis-related signalling; however, we did not establish ferroptosis as the principal driving mechanism underlying this biological phenomenon. For example, under the same experimental conditions, we did not systematically assess key markers from other programmed cell death pathways, such as cleaved caspase-3 for apoptosis, phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) for necroptosis, or cleaved gasdermin D (cleaved GSDMD) for pyroptosis [42].

In summary, BL effectively reversed Aa‑associated damage across most key indicators, demonstrating its potential for clinical applications. Next, we are expected to continue to explore the application prospects of BL in oral clinical materials in combination with oral biomaterials.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the pathogenic bacterium Aa was associated with the activation of ferroptosis-related signalling in HGECs, accompanied by decreased CDH1 and CLDN1 expression, impaired epithelial barrier integrity, increased IL6 and TNF and reduced IL10. The probiotic BL attenuated these ferroptosis-associated changes, restored junctional proteins, alleviated inflammation and promoted barrier reconstruction, with effects that surpassed those of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. These findings suggest that BL serves as a promising ecological intervention for periodontitis and as a potential candidate for the development of oral biomaterials [43,44].
