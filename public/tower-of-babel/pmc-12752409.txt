Replay: a GPU-accelerated tool for temporal contact network epidemiology
Authors: Harrison J. Greenlee, Assefaw H. Gebremedhin, Eric T. Lofgren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding disease transmission through human contact networks is critical for public health decision-making. Traditional epidemic models often assume homogeneous mixing or static contact patterns, misrepresenting real-world infection dynamics. The increasing availability of empirical human mobility data presents an opportunity to overcome these limitations. However, working with temporal network data introduces computational challenges, particularly for large populations.ResultsWe introduce Replay, a GPU-accelerated framework for efficiently simulating infectious disease spread over temporal contact networks. Replay transforms timestamped empirical contact data into duration-weighted contact graphs and employs sparse matrix operations to account for each exposure event that occurs. By literally “replaying” observed contacts across various scenarios, it enables realistic, data-driven epidemic simulations. This matrix-based approach facilitates large-scale Monte Carlo simulations, executing thousands of parallel runs per second on commodity hardware. Benchmarks demonstrate substantial GPU-based performance gains compared to equivalent CPU-based algorithms. Crucially, Replay responds to localized outbreaks and superspreader events without requiring reparameterization.ConclusionsReplay offers a practical solution for modeling disease transmission using empirical contact data in an efficient and realistic way. It is especially valuable for public health planning, hospital infection control, and retrospective outbreak analysis.

Background

Contact patterns are fundamental to epidemic modeling [4], yet often overlooked due to limited data, leading modelers to assume homogeneous mixing behaviors [4, 17, 20]. However, human interactions are inherently structured and heterogeneous, with frequent close interactions within small circles and infrequent contacts with broader groups [8, 12]. Accurately modeling this complexity is challenging because real-world behaviors are influenced by factors such as time, weather, financial status, and culture, which often makes simplistic mixing assumptions inadequate [22].

Epidemic models often rely on static contact networks or homogeneous mixing assumptions, which fail to reflect the time-dependent, heterogeneous nature of real human interactions [14]. Temporal network models address this by encoding not just who was in contact, but also when, which enables more realistic simulations. In this setting, transmission parameters are interpretable as the probability of infection per unit of contact time rather than abstract rates [22, 25]. Temporal models also account for bursty and clustered behavior in a more realistic way [14, 16].

High–resolution contact data now exist for classrooms, offices, events, and health care settings. These datasets often record person–person interactions with start and end times, and sometimes with location identifiers. They can be large and must be integrated in sequence across time to preserve who met whom and when. Many general purpose epidemic simulators do not operate on contact-by-contact timelines. Instead they rely on generalized mixing patterns, which can be computationally efficient but moves the analysis away from the measured contact paths that often shape outbreak dynamics in specific places. Replay targets the case where the user provides measured person–person contacts and wishes to replay transmission over those contacts while preserving the temporal ordering.

Agent-based models (ABMs) generalize contact as a modeling strategy. A synthetic population is built, agents are grouped into households, schools, workplaces, and other settings, and mixing is produced by rules or social mixing matrices. Examples include large-scale ABMs used for influenza and COVID-19 policy analysis [3, 5, 10]. ABMs commonly scale to very large populations because they avoid loading and integrating every empirical contact event, and thus their mixing patterns are simpler to compute. They advance time in regular steps or in event-driven kernels that operate on a small set of setting-level interactions per step, rather than iterating over a long list of time-stamped dyads. Location-based designs also support domain decomposition across nodes, which improves cache locality and communication patterns on clusters [5]. The tradeoff is that fidelity to measured dyads is reduced, since contacts are synthesized from generalizations rather than taken from sensors or logs.

Empirical contact–driven models instead operate on empirical contact sequences. The simulator consumes measured contacts with start and end times and a duration, and infections are evaluated only when and where contacts occur. This preserves the exact order of interactions and their overlap with infectious periods. It is attractive when the goal is to evaluate hypotheses or interventions on a specific dataset, such as a hospital or school, without replacing the observed mixing with a model. The main challenge is scale. Processing millions of time-stamped contacts can dominate runtime and memory because the simulator must scan events in order, compute overlap with infectiousness, and propagate infection state updates in lockstep with the data.

These empirical data are represented in several ways. Windowed snapshots aggregate contacts into a sequence of static graphs, for example one graph every five minutes, and simulation proceeds by stepping through these windows. This format supports vectorized operations. Tools then calculate the overlap between infectious and susceptible states during each event. Holme describes an event–driven approach for SIR on temporal networks that maintains correctness while improving speed relative to naive time stepping [13]. In general, applying the full contact duration as a single probability update at the start of a long contact front–loads risk and can bias infection timing. Event–driven implementations that integrate risk across the contact, or that subdivide long contacts into smaller evaluation intervals, avoid that artifact. The tool Tacoma exposes epidemic processes on temporal networks with APIs that work directly with event lists and with sliced networks, and documents model classes for SIR, SEIR, SIS, and variants [21]. Location occupancy sequences encode contacts implicitly by place. EpiSimdemics groups people by place, computes who is present during periods of constant occupancy, and evaluates transmission during those periods [5]. This location-based format can be reduced to raw event list format or windowed snapshot format, but not vice versa.

Established tools reflect these different modeling choices. Multiple open–source ABMs are widely used for policy and scenario analysis. JUNE builds a geographically granular virtual population and mixes within households, schools, workplaces, and communities [3]. OpenABM-Covid19 implements detailed age structure, social networks, and non–pharmaceutical interventions with performant kernels and user–facing interfaces [10]. Epiabm re–implements an Imperial College CovidSim–style workflow with Python and a higher performance C++ backend, including an exposed compartment to support latent periods [9]. These systems are designed to run simulations on large populations with complex mixing mechanics, but they are not intended to ingest high–volume temporal contact lists as primary input which places them in a different category of software.

Several libraries also target diffusion processes on graphs. EpiFire provides a C++ API and GUI for SIR and related processes on networks, with percolation and chain–binomial solvers [11]. NDlib is a Python library for diffusion on networks and lists SIR, SIS, and SEIS among supported models, but it does not include a standard SEIR implementation in the documented model set and it is not optimized for large temporal contact event streams [24]. EpiModel in R supports epidemic models on static and dynamic networks, including separable temporal ERGMs for partnership turnover [15]. Pathogen.jl targets transmission–network individual–level models in continuous time, with emphasis on Bayesian inference via MCMC rather than high–throughput forward simulation over temporal contact logs [2]. These libraries are valuable when the contact network is static or generated statistically. They generally do not solve the core problem of replaying a long sequence of measured dyadic contacts.

Working with temporal networks is computationally intensive, especially for large populations or superspreader events. To manage this, we rely on temporal slicing and GPU–accelerated sparse matrix operations to efficiently represent and process the data. We convert input to a sequence of time windows and build sparse adjacency matrices that encode measured person–person contact minutes in each window. We then compute exposure for all susceptible individuals by applying these matrices to the current infection states. Multiple Monte Carlo realizations are batched as columns so the GPU carries out both within–simulation parallelism and across–simulation parallelism in the same kernels. Infections are sampled from the accumulated exposure and the process advances to the next window. This design exploits linear algebra operations and sparse data structures to evaluate many contacts at once while preserving the temporal order of the original dataset.

GPU acceleration has also been explored through edge–parallel kernels on clusters. Zou et al. propose a GPU–cluster method that parallelizes infection updates across contact edges, partitions the network, and uses software caching and asynchronous queues to hide latency [30]. The primary focus is accelerating a single realization across many devices with careful memory management and message passing. Our approach differs in three ways. First, we work from measured temporal windows rather than static partitions of a synthetic graph. Second, exposure is computed by bulk matrix operations that integrate contact duration within each window rather than per–edge kernels that process one contact at a time. Third, we use batching to accelerate many realizations concurrently on a single GPU, whereas Zou et al. focus on distributing a single realization across multiple GPUs [30].

Working with temporal windows introduces tradeoffs that we make explicit. If windows are too long, infection timing within the window is coarsened. If windows are too short, matrix build cost increases. Event–driven tools like Tacoma operate directly on contact lists and avoid windowing, which can be preferable when evaluating detailed timing within long contacts [21]. Our results show that simulations remain tractable even at scale. GPU benchmarks demonstrate order–of–magnitude speedups over CPU–based models, particularly for high–contact or dense scenarios. Performance scales linearly with contact volume and sub–linearly with the number of simulations, enabling large–scale Monte Carlo analysis without sacrificing temporal detail.

There are also empirical tradeoffs to modeling with measured contact data. In practice, datasets are often assembled from wearable proximity sensors (active-RFID badges), smartphone Bluetooth logs, and contact diaries [18, 26]. RFID badges resolve face-to-face proximity at 1–1.5 m with 20 s temporal granularity and high detection probability, making them well suited for replay on temporal networks; by design, this emphasizes true interpersonal encounters over coarse co-location [6]. At the same time, diaries and sensors do not capture identical events: brief contacts are systematically under-reported in diaries, reported durations tend to be biased, and the two methods embody different contact definitions (conversation/physical touch vs. short-range proximity) [26]. Infrastructure and behavior also matter: sensor coverage gaps (receiver placement, rooms not instrumented) and carry/wear practices (e.g., tags shielded by clothing or bags) can lead to missed contacts [26]. Bluetooth-based proximity detection is based on received signal strength, which is known to be extremely noisy; distance varies with handset model, human-body occlusion, orientation, and the built environment (walls, reflections). For example, pockets vs. table placement alone can shift measured strength by tens of dB [18].

Despite these limitations, high-coverage, well-calibrated contact data is uniquely descriptive. It describes complex contact patterns that mixing assumptions cannot capture and preserves epidemic features like clustered immunity and superspreading events that may otherwise be overlooked. Taken together, these considerations motivate our simulation framework, Replay, which runs epidemic simulations on empirical contact data while maintaining computational efficiency. The remainder of the paper introduces the data pipeline and disease–state model, details the matrix–based exposure calculation and Monte Carlo batching strategy, and evaluates performance and behavior on synthetic benchmarks and an empirical case study. We conclude by discussing limitations and practical guidance on when to prefer temporal contact replay versus other modeling approaches.

Implementation

Overview and system architecture

Our software performs epidemiological simulations using a temporal contact network representation of a population. Each individual is modeled as a node and edges represent periods of contact between people. By explicitly encoding not only who is in contact, but also when those contacts occur, this approach avoids many of the problematic assumptions made in traditional epidemiological models (e.g., homogeneous mixing [27]). This added fidelity enables realistic simulation of how disease may propagate through a population over time, based on empirical or synthetic contact data.

The simulation engine supports large-scale Monte Carlo modeling, in which multiple stochastic simulations are executed independently to approximate a probability distribution over epidemic outcomes. Rather than executing one infection trajectory at a time, the system is designed to run hundreds or thousands of simulations in parallel using matrix-matrix operations on the GPU.

The process begins with input data from either a Generator (synthetic) or Empirical Data, as illustrated at the top of the figure. The central data structure is Tabular Data, from which Individual Level Statistics are derived. To efficiently query contact relationships at each time step, the data is compiled into a Nested Containment List (NCList) structure [1], implemented via the PyRanges library [28]. This structure enables rapid interval overlap queries to identify who shared a location during the relevant time intervals and for how long. Although this approach sacrifices fine-grained temporal ordering within each slice, it enables efficient matrix operations that scale well on parallel hardware. Static graphs for each time slice are built on-the-fly, reducing memory requirements.

Disease state modeling

The infection state of each person in the system is tracked in a vector of integers, using a countdown-based model. This approach deviates from the more common use of probabilistic transitions between disease states, which typically assume an exponentially distributed infectious period. While the exponential assumption offers mathematical convenience and is widely used in compartmental modeling frameworks, it has been shown to introduce unrealistic variability in infectious durations and can distort epidemic persistence and dynamics [19].

Our use of a countdown-based model reflects a deliberate design choice: it departs from conventional analytic assumptions, but enables more interpretable simulation parameters and aligns more closely with how empirical estimates of disease progression times are often presented. With our countdown-based approach, each individual progresses through a fixed sequence of stages (e.g., exposure, infectious, resistant), and their state counter decrements at each step until recovery. Algorithmic comparison: Replay vs Zou and EPIHIPER

Replay converts raw contacts into exposure by building, for each time window, a sparse duration-weighted adjacency A(t) that sums overlap seconds for every pair within the window. Exposure for person i is the i-th entry of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X(t)=A(t)S(t)$$\end{document}, which equals total time in contact with infectious people during that step. If the simulation step is finer than the window, we divide the window matrix by the number of steps κ so that the sum across steps reproduces the window total. Infection is then sampled with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i=1-(1-p_c)^{X_i/\tau}$$\end{document}. This is a bulk path: accumulate durations into A, multiply once, apply a vectorized transform, and sample. In EPIHIPER, contacts are kept as distinct directed edges with a duration field rather than being pre-summed [7]. During a time step, the engine iterates over edges grouped by target node, performs CPU computations for each edge, accumulates these contributions for that target, and then performs a Direct Gillespie selection over the accumulated sequence [7]. Zou’s GPU kernels take a different route: each thread processes one edge at a time and performs a Bernoulli trial for each edge [30].

Parallel execution also differs at the level of loops and memory layout. Replay performs the hot step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X=A\,S$$\end{document} as a GPU sparse-matrix × dense-matrix multiply. Threads are scheduled over nonzeros inside one simulation, and we stack m simulations as columns of S so one SpMM advances all m runs in a single call. Elementwise GPU kernels then compute probabilities, draw uniforms, and tick integer countdown clocks. There is no message passing interface (MPI) in this path and no explicit graph traversal inside the step. EPIHIPER parallelizes by splitting the target-ID range across MPI ranks and keeping edges sorted by target. Each rank owns a contiguous block of targets and loops through its edges to accumulate the per-target sums, with OpenMP dividing those target ranges among cores [7]. The design is CPU-centric and operates on edge lists rather than matrices. Zou’s implementation is edge-parallel on GPUs with device-resident edge tuples for coalesced loads, a software-managed cache to mitigate random reads of person states, and host–device streams that feed updated Wt arrays [30]. Their pipeline is tuned to speed up a single simulation realization across multiple GPUs, while REPLAY uses linear algebra to accelerate many simulation realizations on one device.

Algorithm 2: Each step builds infectious indicators from countdown clock (Fig. 2), multiplies by the per-step matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{A}^{(t)}$$\end{document} to obtain exposure X via sparse matrix multiplication (SpMM), converts exposure (seconds) to an infection probability using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P = 1-(1-p_c)^{X/\tau}$$\end{document}, and then samples new infections before decrementing clocks. Because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{A}^{(t)}$$\end{document} is already scaled, CPU and GPU paths apply identical per-step weights; the GPU accelerates the SpMM and elementwise kernels.

D3 GPU as linear-algebra engine. Algorithm 3 introduces inter-simulation parallelization to Algorithm 2. We show how Monte Carlo runs are stacked as columns so one SpMM with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{A}^{(t)}$$\end{document} advances all m realizations in parallel, while the elementwise steps operate over an n × m grid. This new design exploits both intra- and inter-simulation parallelism: runtime is primarily driven by contact volume (nonzeros) and scales sub-linearly with m.

Intra: SpMM parallelizes over nonzeros/rows; elementwise steps over people. Inter: stacking m realizations as columns advances all runs in one call; scaling is sub-linear in m.

Algorithm 4 iterates over windows, forms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{A}^{(t)}$$\end{document} once, and executes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa^{(t)}$$\end{document} steps so that the cumulative exposure across steps matches the total duration in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A^{(t)}$$\end{document}. This preserves exposure totals under finer simulation step sizes.

For numerical consistency, the static window length Δw must be an integer multiple of the simulation step size. If Δw is not evenly divisible by the step size, the final substep within a window would correspond to a fractional duration, breaking the assumption that the per-step matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{A}^{(t)} = A^{(t)}/\kappa^{(t)}$$\end{document} evenly partitions contact time. Requiring even divisibility ensures that each substep represents an equal fraction of the window’s duration and that cumulative exposures are conserved exactly across the full simulation timeline.

Synthetic benchmarking

We use synthetic networks to explore and validate the behavior of the epidemic simulator. Synthetic networks offer a controlled environment to isolate the performance impact of specific graph properties, such as modularity or density, without introducing confounding factors from real-world datasets. These parameterized scenarios test simulator scalability under growing computational and structural complexity. All code and configuration files used for these benchmarks are included in the public repository to support reproducibility. All benchmarks were executed on a workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU and an AMD Ryzen 79800X3D processor. In all cases, data initialization times are included in benchmarks.

Structural benchmarks

Population scaling benchmark

To evaluate the performance of our simulation system under a variety of conditions, we construct a collection of synthetic contact networks designed to expose the simulator to a broad range of structural configurations. Rather than literal settings, these networks reflect abstract features common to many social systems. These synthetic graphs allow us to benchmark against graph dynamics like clustering and mixing in a systemic way.

Parallelism benchmark

To evaluate how Replay scales under increasing computational demands, we use the same infection hyperparameters as in the previous subsection (Villages scenario; 100 steps with a step size of 3600 seconds, a 100% infection probability per time step, an exposure period of 3600 seconds, an infectious period of 3600 seconds, and no resistance period), but we systematically vary two key parameters: the number of edges in the contact network (reflecting contact frequency and density) and the number of parallel simulations executed concurrently (reflecting stochastic workload). These factors directly influence the size and complexity of the underlying sparse matrices used in each time step. By sweeping across both dimensions, we assess the performance limits of the GPU-accelerated engine and identify scaling behavior as contact volume and simulation throughput.

Runtime comparison across scenarios

We benchmarked GPU and CPU performance using four synthetic scenarios: Villages, Cities, Airport, and Dense. Each test was performed across increasing population sizes using 10,000 parallel simulations over 100 time steps.

Across all scenarios, GPU runtimes are roughly an order of magnitude faster than CPU runtimes. In the Villages and Cities scenarios (Figs. 3 and 4), this scaling is mostly linear with respect to population size due to sparse connectivity and low intergroup mixing. These are ideal conditions for sparse matrix acceleration on GPU.

The Airport scenario (Fig. 5) includes more cross-group contact, which increases matrix density and leads to longer runtimes on CPU. The GPU handles this more gracefully, though runtime begins to rise earlier than in other sparse cases.

In the Dense scenario (Fig. 6), the number of edges grows quadratically with population size. CPU performance deteriorates rapidly in this case, while the GPU maintains a significantly lower runtime.

Discussion

The results in Sections “Runtime Comparison Across Scenarios” and “Runtime Scaling with Contact Density and Parallelism” show a clear performance advantage for the GPU path in our window–based implementation. This follows from the structure of the algorithm. For our approach, the dominant cost is computing the total contact duration with infectious neighbors for each window. Notably, scenarios where few are infected are easy to compute. However, in scenarios where infection is prevalent, using GPU–accelerated sparse matrix operations allows us to compute sufficient stochastic realizations to characterize the distribution, even when graphs contain many edges.

Figures 3, 4, 5 and 6 show that the gap between CPU and GPU widens with population size. In the Villages and Cities scenarios, runtime grows roughly linearly with population on GPU, while CPU time rises much faster as graphs become less sparse. In the Dense scenario, CPU runtime deteriorates quickly, whereas GPU runtime remains far lower across the tested sizes. At the smallest population sizes, the difference is modest, which is consistent with kernel–launch and data–movement overheads that matter more when contact volume is low. However, as the population grows, potential edges increase quadratically in the worst case, so dense windows create large matrices. Parallelism allows the GPU path to handle larger populations and denser windows than the CPU path, in our observation. Figures 7 and 8 summarize scaling trends with contact volume and batching. Total runtime increases roughly linearly with the number of simulated contacts, while growth with the number of concurrent simulations is sub–linear. This reflects reuse of the same per–window matrices across columns during a single sparse matrix–dense matrix multiplication. In summary, our results show that when contact density is high or when many realizations are required, the GPU path provides order-of-magnitude scaling advantages for our computational approach.

Case study

To demonstrate Replay in practice, we simulate a simple respiratory pathogen using RollerNet, a public human-mobility dataset from a biweekly rollerskating event in Paris. In this setting, Bluetooth motes captured the durations of close contact between skaters [29]. In order to demonstrate behavior at a larger time scale, we make the assumption that each recurrence in the time series produces the same network structure. We also assume that these individuals are isolated when not participating in the rollerskating event. These assumptions are not necessarily realistic, but the resulting network establishes a consistent and reproducible baseline for evaluating different models. True empirical contact datasets are challenging to obtain, as human mobility data at sufficient scale is rarely publicly accessible, and patient records cannot be shared due to privacy concerns. We compare simulation results and performance benchmarks against two other tools in the literature: NDlib and Tacoma. These were selected for their similarity to Replay and code availability. These tools only support SIR modeling, but we include a version of Replay with SEIR for demonstration purposes. Note that in all cases, data initialization times are included in benchmarks.

Comparison with NDlib

Next, we configure the same scenario with NDlib. To ensure consistency across simulation frameworks, parameters from the continuous-time Replay model (Table 2) were converted into the discrete-time form expected by NDlib’s DynSIRModel. The conversion assumes a continuous-time Poisson infection and recovery process, where the probability of an event over a time interval Δt is given by: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ p = 1 - e^{-\lambda \Delta t}, $$\end{document}

with λ denoting the corresponding continuous-time rate (infection or recovery). This formulation allows a straightforward mapping from Replay to NDlib.

Infection probability

The Replay configuration specifies an infection probability of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\text{hour}} = 0.20$$\end{document} per hour of exposure. We first obtain the continuous infection rate λ by inverting the exponential hazard function: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \lambda = -\frac{\ln(1 - p_{\text{hour}})}{3600} = -\frac{\ln(1 - 0.20)}{3600} = 6.20 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}^{-1}. $$\end{document}

Because we are using a 5-minute (300 s) network snapshot for NDlib, the per-snapshot infection probability is obtained by integrating the continuous hazard over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t = 300$$\end{document} s: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \beta_{\text{snap}} = 1 - e^{-\lambda \Delta t} = 1 - e^{-(6.20\times10^{-5})(300)} \approx 0.0184. $$\end{document}

Hence, an infection probability of 20% per hour corresponds to approximately 1.84% infection chance per 5-minute snapshot in NDlib.

Recovery probability

The infectious duration in Replay is given as 14 days, which corresponds to a mean recovery rate of: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \rho = \frac{1}{14 \times 24 \times 3600} = 8.27 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{s}^{-1}. $$\end{document}

Applying the same exponential conversion over the 5-minute snapshot interval yields: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \gamma_{\text{snap}} = 1 - e^{-\rho \Delta t} = 1 - e^{-(8.27\times10^{-7})(300)} \approx 2.48 \times 10^{-4}. $$\end{document}

Thus, at each snapshot, an infected individual has a 0.0248% chance of recovery, corresponding to an expected infectious period of 14 days. NDlib supports a wide variety of state transition models including a countdown model similar to the one described in Section “Disease State Modeling”. Although it requires extra numerical conversions, we use the default (probabilistic state transitions) rather than the countdown model to fairly represent the behavior of this modeling software for general usage.

Comparison with Tacoma

By running the Tacoma simulation with parameters as specified in Table 4, we produce Fig. 11. Tacoma mitigates some performance limitations of discrete-time frameworks by using a Gillespie-style, continuous-time simulation that operates directly on event lists rather than time windows. However, edges remain unweighted, inheriting the drawbacks noted in Subsection “Comparison with NDlib”. In a continuous formulation this forces a single static transmission probability to represent contacts of any length. The model treats short and long encounters as equally risky, which can overestimate risk for brief contacts and underestimate risk for prolonged ones. In addition, triggering infections at contact initiation rather than integrating hazard over the full encounter can bias transmission timing. Discussion

Our RollerNet case study highlights three modeling choices that shape infection dynamics: representing an exposed (latent) stage, weighting contacts by their observed duration, and how long contacts are handled in continuous-time kernels. We also note limitations of our own countdown-based state model and of the case-study setup itself.

Including an exposed category matters whenever a pathogen has a latent period that is non–negligible with respect to contact frequency. In bursty, clustered contact settings, assuming newly infected people become infectious immediately allows multiple generations to propagate within the same burst, inflating early growth. An explicit exposure stage forces a minimum delay between exposure and infectiousness, which dampens that within–burst amplification and changes which encounters are even temporally capable of transmitting. In our simulation, the SEIR trace (Fig. 12) differs from the SIR trace (Fig. 9) in a way that highlights the importance of supporting a latent infection period for network propagation simulations.

Weighting contacts by their observed duration is likewise important. Without weights, a brief contact counts the same as a full 5–minute face–to–face conversation; short encounters are over–represented and longer ones are under–represented or effectively clipped [25]. Replay aggregates per–pair contact minutes within each slice and converts accumulated exposure time into an infection probability, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P = 1-(1-p_c)^{X/\tau}$$\end{document}, tying risk directly to measured exposure rather than to a binary edge. In the NDlib comparison the initial infection pace is faster, consistent with short contacts being treated as full–window exposures. Using duration weights reduces the need to re–tune probabilities whenever the mix of brief and sustained contacts changes and represents the data more accurately.

Event-driven simulation is slightly faster and avoids windowing but can be problematic for long contacts if the infection hazard is applied as a single event at contact start: this “front–loads” risk and can bias transmission timing. Tacoma’s event–driven API evaluates infections directly on contact lists. On RollerNet, where most contacts are short, this artifact is not especially visible. It would be more consequential in settings with protracted overlaps (e.g., inpatient wards or roommates) unless the hazard is integrated over the contact or long contacts are subdivided into smaller intervals.

Finally, our integer countdown clocks are an acceptable simplification for Replay, chosen for interpretability and speed, but they do constrain realism. Stage durations are fixed globally in the current implementation: they cannot be customized on a per–person basis and they cannot reproduce general transition–time distributions (e.g., gamma or log–normal) or recovery times based on demographic. Fixed duration state transitions cause later state transitions to spike suddenly after the state transition timer triggers in a way that is somewhat unrealistic.

Conclusions

We introduce a GPU-accelerated infection simulator that efficiently models disease spread using temporal contact networks. Unlike traditional compartmental or static models, this framework explicitly captures real-world human interactions by incorporating precise timing and durations of contacts. This approach facilitates realistic, empirically-driven simulations.

By reformulating infection dynamics as sparse matrix operations, our simulator enables large-scale Monte Carlo simulations on commodity GPUs. Benchmarks show that GPU runtimes consistently outperform CPU-based implementations by roughly an order of magnitude across various network structures and population sizes. Performance scales sub-linearly with the number of simulations and linearly with contact data volume, making the system suitable for large-scale public health applications. Empirical comparison against other network-based models produces similar epidemic curves, but the parameters used by Replay are especially interpretable and can often be transferred to other networks with minimal reparameterization [23].

The simulator provides a robust and reproducible platform for practical uses in public health planning, hospital infection control, and retrospective outbreak analysis. All code, benchmarking configurations, and datasets are publicly available to support transparency and facilitate further research.

Future work will focus on further optimizing performance and extending scalability. For example, high-density contact events, such as large gatherings, can be approximated more efficiently by estimating group-level exposure rather than computing all pairwise interactions. Partitioning network interactions into clustered subgroups (e.g., cities or towns) may also enhance memory efficiency and enable distributed GPU processing. Finally, simulations with identical intermediate states could be temporarily merged to avoid redundant calculations, reducing overhead in large-scale Monte Carlo runs. These strategies would further improve scalability for high-resolution modeling.
