Revisiting the foundation era of plant genomics with a commentary on the pioneering contributions of Prof. Chittaranjan Kole
Authors: Madhugiri Nageswara‐Rao, Sarita Pandey, Poulami Bhattacharjee, Dinesh Kumar Saini, Jaya R. Soneji, Bashasab Fakrudin, Lohithaswa Hirenallur Chandappa, Amir Ali Khoddamzadeh, Daniel A. Tucker, Jinfa Zhang, Shamseldeen Eltaher, Jauhar Ali, Robert Henry, Ilga Mercedes Porth, Aleksandra Radanović, Kiyosumi Hori, Maria Stefanie Dwiyanti, Ferdinando Branca, Shalini Tiwari, Matthew Blair, Yong Pyo Lim, Renate Horn, Olga Danilevskaya, Sunita Adhikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolution of plant genomics has been shaped by several pioneering milestones, beginning with the introduction of restriction fragment length polymorphism–based genetic linkage maps in the mid‐1980s. Among the global contributors, Prof. Chittaranjan Kole stands as a distinguished figure whose work fundamentally shifted the trajectory of plant genomics and molecular breeding. This tribute highlights his scientific journey and groundbreaking contributions, from being the first Indian scientist to physically map and sequence a plant gene in barley to establishing the foundations of molecular cytogenetics, comparative genomics, and molecular evolution and phylogenetic relationships in plants. His landmark research on Brassica genomics, including high‐resolution mapping, Mendelization of quantitative trait loci (QTLs), and innovative use of recombinant inbred lines, enabled unprecedented insights into trait evolution, stress biology, and genome homology between Brassica species and Arabidopsis. Prof. Kole's work on mapping genes and QTLs associated with flowering time, biotic stress resistance, abiotic stress tolerance, and genome evolution has provided a framework now integral to marker‐assisted selection, genomic breeding, and climate‐resilient crop development. This article offers a scholarly reflection on his pioneering contributions, establishing Prof. Kole as a founding architect of plant genomics research in India and one of its most influential contributors globally.

INTRODUCTION

The subject of plant genomics, in a broad sense, was born in 1986 coinciding exactly with the introduction of the term “genomics” by Tom Roderick and marked by the construction of the first genetic linkage maps using the DNA‐based molecular marker, restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) for the first time applied for genetic maps in maize and tomato (Helentjaris et al., 1986) and tomato by Bernatzky and Tanksley (1986) followed up the first work on using RFLPs in studies of plant diversity and development in maize and tomato by Helentjaris et al. (1985). These two linkage maps were immediately followed by another linkage map in maize (Helentjaris, 1987), a linkage map in lettuce (Landry et al., 1987), and a linkage map in tomato (Osborn et al., 1987), all using RFLP markers and simply inherited trait loci. RFLP linkage maps in two other major crops and maize again were reported in the next year, one in rice (McCouch et al., 1988) and another in potato (Bonierbale et al., 1988), along with another RFLP map in maize (Burr et al., 1988). The first RFLP maps in Brassica rapa (Song et al., 1991) and two polyploid crops, including Brassica napus (Landry et al., 1991) and wheat (Y. Liu & Tsunewaki, 1991), were constructed in the next 2 years. A few genes and quantitative trait loci (QTLs) were also positioned on some of these linkage maps by a few scientists during this period. However, there was no information on the nucleotide sequence of the genes and the flanking RFLP markers. The physical positions of these genes on the chromosomes were also obviously not available from these genetic mapping efforts. Simple sequence repeat or microsatellite markers, constituting DNA stretches consisting of short, tandemly repeated di‐, tri‐, tetra‐, or penta‐nucleotide motifs, were first applied to plants only in 1992 (Akkaya et al., 1992) and used in genetic map construction in the latter period (Devey et al., 1996; Cadalen et al., 1997).

In the same period, a large group of about 40 scientists was working on completely different frontiers of plant genomics under the supervision of the legendary scientist, Dr. Evgenii V. Ananiev, in the N.I. Vavilov Institute of General Genetics of the USSR Academy of Sciences. One of his teams, including scientists as Dr. Alexander Kolchinsky, Dr. Elena Yakovleva, and Dr. Anna Gazumyan, started working on genes constituting repetitive elements in cereals. In 1997, this group received a significant boost with the arrival of a bright young scientist from India, Dr. Chittaranjan Kole.

It is worthwhile to mention that the Department of Biotechnology (DBT) of India was established in 1986. Before that, scientists in India, like in any other country, particularly in the Global South, were engaged in research on optimizing in vitro culture development, isozyme markers, and seed protein analysis using SDS‐PAGE, DNA‐DNA hybridizations, etc. The Government of India planned to develop human resources in the field of biotechnology and started deputing scientists abroad for training, and Dr. Kole was one of the first scientists to participate in this program.

After 2 years of intensive research, Dr. Kole presented his report on mapping the 5S‐RNA genes in barley at the 15th EMBO Annual Symposium on “Molecular Communication in Higher Plants” held at the European Molecular Biology Laboratory, Heidelberg, on behalf of the team. Thus, this group became one of the first laboratories to physically map a plant gene, and by this Dr. Kole became the first Indian to “map a gene” in any plant or crop by any means. This group sequenced the 5S‐RNA gene (301 bp) and thus pioneered plant gene sequencing (Kolchinsky et al., 1990; Ananiev et al., 1991; Kole et al., 1992), and Dr. Kole became the first Indian to “sequence a gene.” Sequencing a 301 bp gene using the Maxam and Gilbert (1980) method was not easy in the late 1980s (1987–1989). Follow‐up research focused on interspecific homology of barley 5S‐RNA genes as “markers” with wheat and rye, thereby marking the beginning of “comparative genome homology” in plants, paving the way for the frontiers of “molecular evolution” and “molecular phylogenetic relationships.” Dr. Kole was also a pioneer in this aspect among Indians. Tanksley et al. (1988) reported comparative mapping between pepper and tomato but only using molecular genetic mapping. The use of repetitive elements of the 5S‐RNA gene as a “molecular marker” for these studies was also a first effort. An extension of this mapping work was “molecular cytogenetics,” again done for the first time, using in situ hybridization with a DNA probe to confirm the position of the 5S‐RNA genes on the 2nd chromosome of barley. Obviously, Dr. Kole pioneered “molecular cytogenetics” in India. Some of these pioneering works are presented in Figure 1.

Early molecular characterization of 5S‐rRNA genes in cereals by Dr. Chittaranjan Kole and coworkers. These autoradiographs illustrate the structural organization, interspecific variation, and hybridization patterns of 5S‐rRNA gene repeats in barley and wheat. Distinct banding profiles obtained through restriction enzyme digestion and blot hybridization demonstrate species‐specific signatures, confirm chromosomal localization, and provide early molecular evidence for comparative genome relationships among cereals. Together, these results formed the foundation for Dr. Kole's pioneering contributions to plant gene mapping, comparative genomics, and molecular cytogenetics (figures reproduced from Kolchinsky et al. [1990] in Theoretical and Applied Genetics).

In another project, this team of Dr. Ananiev used “repetitive DNA” as a source of molecular markers, the so‐called “genomic dactyloscopy” for studying “interspecific and intervarietal polymorphism” in major cereals including barley, wheat, and maize (Kolchinsky et al., 1991) (Figure 2). They successfully used small families of repetitive genes, such as histones and hordein, in depicting both interspecific and intervarietial polymorphism. This was another pioneering research on the detection of molecular markers based on repetitive DNAs and their use in elucidating evolution and phylogenetic relationships. All these are the first works on plant genomics and biotechnology by an Indian scientist, and this is why Prof. Kole is regarded as the “Father of Plant Genomics in India,” and broadly “Father of Plant Biotechnology in India.”

This figure, from Dr. Chittaranjan Kole's early work on repetitive DNA, shows high‐resolution electrophoretic patterns generated by end‐labeled cereal DNA digested with restriction enzymes. The distinct banding profiles of barley, wheat, and multiple maize varieties demonstrate characteristic species‐ and variety‐specific patterns, providing the first evidence that repetitive DNA sequences could be used as reliable molecular fingerprints. Through these patterns, Dr. Kole established the concept of “genomic dactyloscopy,” showing that the organization of repetitive DNA varies sufficiently among species to enable their identification and comparative analysis (figures reproduced from Kolchinsky et al. [1991] in Nucleus).

The next phase of Indian biotechnology research marks the golden period for India, when Dr. Kole worked on the broad project entitled “Molecular Mapping in Oilseed Brassicas” at the University of Wisconsin‐Madison from 1993 to 1997, for his DBT Overseas Research Associateship project. Here too, Dr. Kole made many discoveries that were unique worldwide, obviously as the first Indian to do so, with the assistance of his host professor, Dr. Thomas C. Osborn, his wife and colleague, Phullara Kole, and others.

More than 50 molecular maps were reported in 25‐plus crop plants during 1986–2000. So, the development of a molecular map for B. rapa by Dr. Kole's team (Kole, Kole, et al., 1996a; Kole, Teutonico, et al., 1996; Kole, Kole, Vogelzang, et al., 1997) was nothing unusual in itself. However, the molecular genetic linkage map of B. rapa, first reported in 1995, was the first “complete molecular map” developed by an Indian scientist. The next molecular map constructed by an Indian scientist was created by Dr. M. Maheswaran, whose team produced a molecular map for rice in 1997 (Maheswaran et al., 1997). Dr. Kole used “recombinant inbred lines (RILs)” as the mapping population, and that was the first time an Indian developed an RI population for molecular mapping, followed by Sharma et al. (2002) and the third instance in the world, following those in maize (Burr et al., 1988) and lentil (Tahir & Muehlbauer, 1994). Dr. Kole used this molecular map to map the genes (AC2/ACA1) conferring resistance to the most destructive disease, white rust of oilseed Brassicas caused by the fungus, Albugo candida race 2 (Kole, Kole, et al., 1996a; Kole, Teutonico, et al., 1996). He also mapped a gene controlling leaf pubescence (PUB1) close to the ACA1/AC2 locus, leading to the scope of indirect selection of lines for white rust resistance, and a gene controlling seed color (YLS1) (Figure 3). Previously, a gene controlling resistance to white rust Brassica carinata pathotype (ACA1) was mapped in B. napus (Ferreira et al., 1995), and the genes controlling seed color and leaf pubescence were mapped in B. rapa (Teutonico & Osborn, 1994). But the gene controlling resistance to A. candida race 2 was identified for the first time in Brassica crops and resistance to A. candida of any race for the first time in B. rapa by Kole, Kole, et al. (1996a), and Kole, Teutonico, et al. (1996). Thus, Dr. Kole became the first Indian to “map any gene in plants” using RFLP markers. It must be noted that during this period, two Indian teams reported molecular “tagging” of major genes that also confer disease resistance in rice (Hittalmani et al., 1995) and mustard (Prabhu et al., 1998), but by using random amplified polymorphic DNA markers—a type of anonymous markers. Therefore, Dr. Kole became also the first Indian to “map” any gene and in addition the first Indian to map any gene controlling “disease resistance” or “biotic stress” resistance, and a gene related to “crop quality.”

Linkage map of B. rapa linkage group 4 constructed from F3 families derived from the cross Per × R‐500. The resistance locus ACA1, conferring resistance to Albugo candida race 2, is shown to be mapped among restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) markers detected using Brassica genomic (wg, tg) and cDNA (ec) probes, along with heterologous probes. The gene controlling leaf pubescence (PUB1) is also shown. Genetic distances in centimorgans are indicated on the left. This map provided one of the earliest molecular characterizations of white rust resistance loci in Brassica and formed the basis for subsequent comparative and high‐resolution mapping studies (figure reproduced from Kole, Teutonico, et al. [1996b] in Phytopathology).

On their B. rapa map, Dr. Kole and coworkers also mapped 32 marker loci, detected by 22 orthologous cloned genes of Arabidopsis thaliana and B. napus, most of them controlling flowering time and stress resistance, marking the first instance of using markers detected by heterologous gene probes. This pragmatic inclusion of orthologous probes in molecular mapping opened a new vista for elucidation of the molecular evolution of genes underlying flowering time and stress resistance in plants. Dr. Kole observed clustering of marker loci skewed to either of the parents in different genomic locations, evidencing their involvement with biological fitness (Kole, Kole, Vogelzang, et al., 1997). These particular genomic locations were therefore assumed to contain loci with some deleterious alleles inherited from either of the parents. He also discovered frequent clustering of duplicated loci, even when detected early on with cDNA probes or cloned genes and conjectured the existence of “paleoploidy” that was substantiated much later by his peers.

Later, Dr. Kole and colleagues constructed an enriched molecular map of B. napus with 480 loci by the addition of 268 amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) loci (Osborn, Kole, Parkin, et al., 1997b) to a map developed earlier by using a double haploid population and 203 RFLP loci, five isozyme loci, two disease resistance loci, and two erucic acid loci (Ferreira et al., 1994; Thormann et al., 1996). Dr. Kole's team members became the first scientists to develop an “enriched” molecular map, which also used “AFLP markers” for the first time, and then used it for mapping genes and QTLs. Molecular maps based on AFLPs in addition to RFLPs were reported next in rice (Maheswaran et al., 1997; Penner et al., 1998).

Dr. Kole is also the first Indian to map any QTL when his team reported the mapping of QTLs controlling resistance to white rust race 2 and 7, flowering time (FR) and vernalization responsive flowering time (VFR) and winter survival in B. rapa (Kole, Kole, et al., 1996a; Kole, Kole, & Osborn, 1997; Osborn, Kole, Parkin, et al., 1997b; Osborn, Kole, & Butruille, 1997a). He was also the first Indian scientist to conduct “comparative mapping” using molecular markers while studying “genome homology” among B. rapa, B. napus, and A. thaliana. Through this research, his team was one of the first teams in the world to use QTLs in comparative mapping along with molecular markers (Osborn, Kole, Parkin, et al., 1997b; Osborn, Kole, & Butruille 1997; Kole, Kole, & Osborn, et al., 1997), and along with others (Pereira et al., 1994; Lin et al., 1995; Paterson et al., 1995). They detected two QTLs, named VFR1 and VFR2, for vernalization‐responsive flowering time in B. rapa. These two genomic regions containing these QTLs were shown to be homologous to two regions in B. napus, which contain QTLs (VFN1 and VFN2) controlling vernalization‐responsive flowering time. The Brassica regions carrying VFR2 and VFN2 were also shown to be homologous to a region at the top of chromosome 5 in the related crucifer A. thaliana, where in addition the flowering‐time genes CONSTANS (CO), FY (homologous to the yeast polyadenylation factor Pfs2p), and FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) are located, and at the top of chromosome 4 where FRIGIDA (FRI) is located. Later, Kole et al. (2001) reported elaboratively of the genome homology of the VFR2 QTL of B. rapa and the FLC gene of Arabidopsis, suggesting evolution of polygenes from single genes (Figure 4).

Comparative mapping of the vernalization‐responsive flowering‐time locus VFR2 in Brassica rapa and its homologous region containing FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) on Arabidopsis thaliana chromosome 5. The figure shows a portion of B. rapa linkage group 8 (Br8) harboring the flowering‐time QTL VFR2, aligned with the corresponding genomic region on A. thaliana chromosome 5 (At5). Positions of restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) markers and genetic distances (cM) are indicated along both linkage groups. Homologous loci detected using A. thaliana DNA probes, including FLC, CO, mi174, and mi438, are connected by lines between the two species, demonstrating conserved synteny. The exon–intron structure of FLC is shown, and an RFLP detected in B. rapa by exons 2–6 of FLC co‐segregates precisely with the VFR2 phenotype. This comparative map provided key evidence for the evolutionary homology between VFR2 in B. rapa and FLC in A. thaliana (figures reproduced from Kole et al. [2001] in Theoretical and Applied Genetics).

India and the world witnessed another outstanding discovery—the “Mendelization of QTL.” In 1997, Dr. Kole and colleagues reported “high‐resolution mapping” around the polygenic cluster VFR2 in such a way that flowering time exhibited Mendelian (monogenic) segregation (Kole, Kole, & Osborn, et al., 1997). This work was extended further, which laid the foundation of “molecular evolution of flowering time genes” in plants, and precisely, “evolution of polygenes from single genes.” In 2001, Dr. Kole and his colleagues depicted the perfect homology of the VFR2 locus with the flowering locus C (FLC) of A. thaliana, elucidating the molecular pathway of evolution of the VFR2 polygenic cluster from the FLC gene, probably through a series of tandem duplications (Kole et al., 2001). This discovery led to the depiction of homology of another two clusters, VFR1 and VFR3, with FLC genes, and the use of the “FLC genes” (FLC1, FLC2, and FLC3) in breeding programs in an array of crop plants. This strategy became widely popular to practice marker‐assisted monitoring of QTLs during breeding (Figure 4).

Stress resistance has recently reemerged in the genomics era as one of the most important breeding objectives, especially for climate resilience, and stupendous efforts have been made toward molecular mapping and genomics of biotic and abiotic stresses. It is amazing to imagine that even in the early 1990s, Prof. Kole thought about it and mapped the R‐gene for resistance to white rust (Kole, Teutonico, et al., 1996b). He also detected the QTLs controlling resistance to both the races AC2 and AC7 of this fungus in B. rapa (Kole, Teutonico, et al., 1996b; Kole, Williams, et al., 2002). Resistance to these two races was found to be controlled by a single major gene or two tightly related genes on linkage group 4 (BR4). Another minor QTL on BR2 was also found to control resistance to race 2. Comparative mapping of these map positions with corresponding regions on the B. napus molecular map and the A. thaliana “physical map” did not show any distinct genome homology (Figure 5).

Mapping of the gene Aca1 onto the linkage group BR4 conferring resistance to the races AC2 and AC7 of the fungus Albugo candida, causing the white rust disease, and a quantitative trait locus (QTL) (shown by cross‐hatching) conferring resistance to the race AC2 on linkage group BR2 of Brassica rapa. Comparative mapping with the corresponding chromosomes of B. napus, BN19 and BN9 carrying the Aca1 gene, conferring resistance to the B. carinata pathotype of A. candida, B. napus, and the physical positions of A. thaliana did not show any clear homology (for details of markers and maps see Kole, Williams, et al. [2002]).

In another pioneering research, Dr. Kole and his colleagues mapped QTLs controlling an important abiotic stress trait, winter survival (Kole, Kole, et al., 1996a, 1996b), and thus he became one of the first scientists to map genes controlling abiotic stress tolerance genes and obviously the first Indian to do so. His team conducted extensive studies on molecular mapping of winter survival and freezing tolerance (acclimatized and non‐acclimatized) in B. rapa and B. napus (Kole, Thormann, et al., 2002) (Figure 6). In addition, he demonstrated linkage of genes controlling winter survival and freezing tolerance in each of these crops and in addition genes underlying vernalization responsive flowering time and flowering time per se, and homology of some genetic regions in these two species concerning the two abiotic stresses. Interestingly, he also showed an association of some of the genomic regions conferring stress tolerance to cold‐related genes of A. thaliana.

The figure shows the positions of QTLs for winter survival (WS), acclimated freezing tolerance (FTA), and flowering time (V/F) mapped in corresponding linkage groups of B. rapa and B. napus. Comprehensive research by Dr. Kole on molecular mapping in oilseed Brassicas led to the emergence of several novel concepts (see Figure 7). The linkage of genes controlling flowering time with those underlying winter survival and freezing stress in his research demonstrated for the first time that flowering is not a beautiful happening as depicted in poems, but a clear symptom of stress. The linkage of biotic and abiotic stress‐related genes, as evidenced in his research, substantiated the clustering nature of these genes, indicating a shared or overlapping molecular mechanism. His research also detected some genes underlying abiotic stress tolerance that are contributed even by the sensitive parent genotype (R‐500 in B. rapa and Stellar in B. napus). This amazing observation led to the search for favorable genes in unfavorable genotypes by scientists in later years (for example, Xiao et al. [1998]).

Overview of the major scientific contributions of Prof. Chittaranjan Kole to plant genomics. The schematic highlights pioneering advances in cereals (5S‐rRNA sequencing, molecular cytogenetics, genomic fingerprinting, and phylogenetic analysis) alongside genetic mapping innovations in Brassica rapa and Brassica napus, including restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP)– and amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP)–based maps, recombinant inbred lines, and heterologous Arabidopsis probes. These efforts enabled mapping of R‐genes, QTLs for biotic and abiotic stress, flowering‐time loci, and quality traits. Comparative analyses among Brassica species and A. thaliana revealed conserved synteny and FLC–VFR2 homology, leading to the Mendelization of QTL and insights into the molecular evolution of polygenes. Collectively, these contributions shaped numerous frameworks for modern marker‐assisted and climate‐resilient breeding.

In addition to his pioneering contributions to plant genomics in the formative period, Prof. Koles’ contributions to genome sciences have also been outstanding through his later works in India and in the United States. Works of Prof. Kole and his colleagues covered almost all crop types including rice among cereals (Tiwari et al., 2005; Padmavathi et al., 2007; Singh et al., 2011; Khera et al., 2012; Upadhyay et al., 2012), Indian rape among oilseeds (Panigrahi et al., 2009, 2011), chickpea among grain legumes (Danakumara et al., 2024), apricot (Olukolu et al., 2009) and peach among fruit crops, tomato among vegetable crops (Kole et al., 2006), and Cannabis sativa among medicinal plants (J. Liu et al., 2023). Contributions of his teams from molecular mapping to genome sequencing in cultivated and wild bitter gourd have been pioneering (Kole et al., 2010, 2012; Vinay et al., 2024) among many others. It is during this period that his team pioneered in chromosome‐level de novo genome assemblies using over 100 plant species (Shirasawa et al., 2021). Prof. Kole's team also led to the birth of the new discipline of “agri‐nanotechnology” when they successfully demonstrated the use of nanoparticles to increase plant biomass, fruit yield, and phytomedicine content in bitter gourd (Kole et al., 2013). The pioneering contributions of Prof. Kole and his team to the foundation of plant proteomics through their enormous works on seed proteins have already been enumerated recently by a group of scientists (Pandey et al., 2025). As a legendary scientist, Prof. Kole has mentored and inspired innumerable scientists across the globe directly or indirectly.

Prof. Kole is highly regarded in India for his contributions to capacity building, primarily by developing facilities for research on genome sciences. He established the Laboratory of Molecular Biology and Biotechnology in the Odisha University of Agriculture & Technology at Bhubaneswar in 1990. He was also the Founder Head of the Department of Genetics & Plant Breeding and the Department of Seed technology of the Allahabad Agricultural Institute, presently named as the Sam Higginbottom University of Agriculture, Technology & Sciences at Allahabad; Founder Project Coordinator of the Indo‐Russian Centre of Biotechnology at the Indian Institute of Information Technology at Allahabad and the Founder Director of the Regional Nuclear Agriculture Center in Bidhan Chandra Krishi Viswavidyalaya, Mohanpur.

Prof. Kole is an internationally reputed science leader. He established three scientific organizations in the field of genome sciences alone. He has been acting as the Founder President of the Genome India International since 2005, International Phytomedomics and Nutriomics Consortium since 2011, and the International Climate Resilient Crop Genomics Consortium since 2012. He led a group of 40 scientist members of the International Climate Resilient Crop Genomics Consortium from 13 countries including Australia, Brazil, China, Columbia, India, Italy, Japan, Malaysia, Nigeria, Philippines, Sweden, United Kingdom, and United States to draft the White Paper on climate change genomics and breeding as a guide to scientists, policy makers and funding agencies for preparation of action plans on future research and its funding on climate change (Kole et al., 2015).

All these multifarious contributions of Prof. Kole beyond the formative stage of plant genomics as a scientist, scientific capacity builder, science leader, and as an editor of over 200 books on genome sciences have been only briefed here but obviously deserve detailed deliberations in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

Prof. Chittaranjan Kole's legacy represents a transformative era in plant genomics, one defined by scientific rigor, innovation, and vision. His pioneering work, beginning with the first physical gene mapping and extending to comparative genomics and QTL evolution, laid the foundation for modern molecular breeding and accelerated the global transition from classical genetics to genomics‐driven crop improvement (Figure 7). The conceptual advances emerging from his research, such as genomic dactyloscopy, Mendelization of QTLs, and linkage between stress biology and flowering networks, continue to influence current approaches to genome mapping, molecular diagnostics, and climate‐resilient agriculture. His work remains highly relevant in the era of genomic selection, pan‐genomics, and precision breeding, reflecting his role not only as a legendary scientist but also as a visionary leader. This tribute stands as recognition of a career that shaped the discipline and inspired generations to pursue excellence in plant genomics research.
