1:1 observation and support: a quality improvement approach to reducing cost and improving therapeutic input
Authors: David Thomas, Marie Little, Jessica Pawley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur quality improvement (QI) project aimed to reduce reliance on external temporary staff used for 1:1 observation and support within a recently merged acute, community and mental health National Health Service (NHS) Trust. Temporary staffing costs reached £2.7 million in the previous financial year. Between February 2022 and June 2024, we aimed to establish a more efficient and consistent approach, particularly within acute physical healthcare settings. The project focused on four main patient cohorts and the goal was to reduce temporary staffing costs by 25%. A multidisciplinary team, guided by the 7 Steps to QI methodology, developed and tested two key tools to standardise decision-making and reduce the need for temporary staff. The project was implemented in nine pilot areas across the Trust, leading to a reduction in staffing costs by £600,000 in the first year, with a further £1.2 million in the second year. Additionally, patient falls with harm decreased by 8%. Challenges arose due to communication barriers and the need for cultural change. By empowering local teams to make more informed decisions, the project demonstrated the value of standardised tools and staff training in enhancing both patient care and organisational sustainability.Keywords: Communication, Cost-effectiveness, Mental health, Patient safety, Quality improvement

Problem

As a recently merged and large acute, community and mental health National Health Service (NHS) Trust in England (47 wards staffed by 2750 colleagues), the organisation was concerned about the cost of agency and temporary staff for 1:1 observation and support. Also referred to as 1:1 care, enhanced care, enhanced supervision or, historically, ‘specialling’, 1:1 observation and support is provided for patients considered at risk of harm to themselves or others. Traditionally, this support has been delivered by temporary registered or unregistered staff.

The quality of 1:1 care provided at the bedside was inconsistent and poorly defined. Following the merger of physical and mental health services, it became clear that approaches to 1:1 observation and support in acute settings varied widely with little focus on de-escalation of risk. Over time, nursing shift leaders had become accustomed to requesting mental health trained or experienced colleagues to provide 1:1 observation and support, often resulting in the use of costly temporary staff, registered and unregistered, supplied through agencies. In some cases, up to five staff were required to support a single patient.

Colleagues in the Mental Health Service Group observed that the requirement for 1:1 observation and support was disjointed in its approach and lacked any formal decision-making in deciding who should be sitting next to the patient and for how long. Accordingly, the total spend for temporary nursing staff in the acute physical health environment was at £2.7 million for the previous financial year. While finance was not the primary driver, it was a measurement that we were able to track and enabled us to observe the reduction in sourcing external support for this cohort of patients. The aim of this quality improvement (QI) project was to reduce the reliance on external temporary staff by 25% through a sustainable decision-making process that enabled correctly trained colleagues to deliver 1:1 observation and support, building on the work undertaken by St George’s University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust.1 We further aimed to improve health outcomes, patient experience, colleague satisfaction and to lower costs by testing our ideas and enabling wards and patients to receive the appropriate levels of support when needed, in nine pilot areas totalling approx. 450 colleagues across the Trust.

Background

There is limited evidence in this area and no clear consensus on best practice or decision-making.

During the initial assessment, staff shared concerns about patient safety and uncertainty about how best to provide support, particularly for the identified patient groups. Some believed that all mental health patients automatically required 1:1 observation and support by a registered mental health nurse, regardless of clinical need.

Awareness of available resources was limited, and there was no consistent process for deciding who needed 1:1 observation and support. As a result, the highest level of care was often requested by default. Due to existing workforce pressures, this increased reliance on agency staff, and reduced continuity of care, with limited opportunities for therapeutic interaction with the patient.

An evidence search was conducted across the PubMed and Turning Research into Practice (TRIP) databases, Google Scholar and a range of grey literature sources.

Free-text keywords were used in the PICO framework and combined with Boolean operators to generate various search strings to interrogate each source (see online supplemental table 1). PICO stands for Patient/Problem/Population, Intervention, Comparison/Control, Outcome.

Not all results have been referenced for the purposes of this paper due to not being deemed contextually relevant. Please note this was not a systematic review and so did not follow any corresponding review guidelines such as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) tool. Critical appraisal of the papers and context of the project enabled the final results selection.

A 2019 systematic review2 showed video monitoring to be the most effective method for seeing a reduction in falls. No studies in the review reported an outcome of reduction in wandering, suicide or self-harm. Equally, no studies reported on the cost-effectiveness of using ‘sitters.’

The challenges associated with providing 1:1 observation and support have been recognised internationally.3 4 Complexities involved in understanding how and when to request 1:1 observation and support lack significant peer review and as such little background can be used in each individual organisation’s approach to personalised 1:1 observation and support.5–7 There is an evidence base showing the need for specialised training, as colleagues used for specialling are often without clinical experience.8

The evidence base further acknowledges the cost impact of using colleagues in this way,9 supporting the findings from our own organisation.

As our work was to enhance the current pathway, we did not seek ethics approval.

Measurement

Our outcome measure was taken from our colleague staffing system which allowed us to extract the number of staff, role, banding and hours worked per ward for 1:1 enhanced care or CAMHS/Mental Health and Specialling each day. The data were calculated on a fortnightly basis, and statistical process control (SPC) charts were created. Estimated average bank and agency spend was £12,000 per week across our nine pilot areas. This is indicative of inappropriate levels of temporary staff being used.

We wanted to check that requests for temporary agency cover were being reduced, which would indicate that more appropriate requests were being made. We tracked the number of requests received by the registered mental health nurse (RMN) and the healthcare assistant (HCA), using the same electronic system. Total requests for RMN and HCA were, on average, 51 per week.

We were concerned that our changes might have a negative impact on the number of incidents on wards if the appropriate care was not being received and so monitoring the number of incidents and extracting data from our incident reporting system was imperative. We included falls, assault and disruptive behaviour and violence and aggression. On average, there were 22 incidents per fortnight.

We originally wanted to include security costs in our calculations but were unable to obtain reliable information from our systems to track this on a fortnightly basis.

Design

The Trust’s 7 Steps to QI approach was used to undertake the programme of work (figure 1). The project commenced in February 2022 and ran until June 2024.

Somerset NHS Foundation Trust’s 7 Steps to Quality Improvement methodology.

A multidisciplinary team consisting of security, nursing, dementia and delirium specialists and mental health and learning disabilities representatives was formed, supported by an improvement advisor.

The iceberg model was used to explore the underlying factors contributing to the problem and to understand the behaviours, processes and mindsets that were causing and perpetuating it. Alongside this, we used the Problem Understanding Prompts tool, developed by the Trust, to understand how it would benefit the system if the problem was solved.

When developing our aim and measures, we focused on wards who were engaged across two acute sites, enabling us to more easily spread across these sites later.

We considered what good practice would look like and collated ideas that we could test to achieve our aim. The group came up with over 25 ideas which were themed to form the driver diagram. To help prioritise our ideas, we used the hierarchy of actions by colouring the most reliable and strongest green, the somewhat reliable yellow and the least reliable pink.

The team met virtually on a weekly basis for 45 min over a month followed by two face-to-face workshops to collate ideas and findings, then prioritise and plan. This enabled the team to advance swiftly through steps 1 to 5 in figure 1.

We chose to create and test an Escalation Prevention Checklist, which was categorised as somewhat reliable, as the checklist made it easier to do the right thing. Following this, our 1:1 observation and support assessment form (adapted model) was categorised as most reliable, as it changed the process demonstrating the appropriate support was being requested.

To inform the design of our decision-making tool, an away day was arranged for every ward across our acute sites (n=47), including experts by experience. Everyone was invited to hear about the project and asked to record likes, dislikes and changes they would like to make during group activities.

Posters were created to encourage colleagues to engage in the project using the Head, Hearts and Hands model.10 This formed an elevator pitch with three elements: facts for analytical people (the head); a reason why it matters for more emotional people (the heart) and what you want done for the more practical people (the hands). The posters were put up in the wards that were testing.

During the testing phase, the steering group was scaled down to support the wards testing the changes and formal meetings were scheduled monthly.

Strategy

The changes tested focused on ensuring wards could provide appropriate levels of support when needed for patients including those in mental health crisis, CAMHS, those with cognitive impairment, learning disabilities and unpredictable behaviour. This was initially undertaken in nine pilot areas across the Trust. The aim was to reduce the cost of 1:1 observation and support (agency and bank) by 25% from an average of £12,000 to £9,000 per week by the end of the trial.

Trial 1

The Escalation Prevention Checklist was created by the team to support individualised care to prevent distress, which ultimately reduces the need for enhanced support. We expected wards to engage through enthusiasm; however, we felt they might wait until escalation before using it, which would be too late. We anticipated that it could increase requests for training, and that we might receive pushback around asking colleagues to do additional work, especially if they do not see the value. It was first tested on two wards.

Cycle 1

Tick boxes were added to a poster. Following clinical review, it became a checklist, rather than a poster as teams fed back that there were too many posters on the ward, thus making it easier to use.

Cycle 2

It became apparent through feedback that wards needed training around high-risk care plans and ‘What matters to me’ or ‘This is me’ documentation. The Operational Service Manager for Liaison and Psychiatry and the Dementia and Delirium Leads agreed to do some impactful training on the pilot wards.

Cycle 3

The first ward started to see a reduction using the checklist which was being prioritised within the ward. The average fortnightly spend reduced from an average of £684 to £55.

Cycle 4

We started to see a reduction in spending on the second ward. Average fortnightly spending reduced from £4570 to £1450. Talking to the ward manager, we learnt that this was down to a change in nursing, to nursing in bays rather than as a result of our Plan Do Study Act (PDSA) cycle. As focus diminished on the first ward and the admission of more complex patients increased, spending increased to £1529, but with the input from the checklist remained significantly lower than pre-intervention.

Conclusions from trial 1

Although the checklist encouraged good practice on the ward, it did not have the full impact we desired on our agency and temporary staff costs, nor on the quality of care delivered. Use of the checklist was spread to three other wards before being rolled out to all acute wards.

Trial 2. The 1:1 observation and support assessment tool (adapted)

The 1:1 observation and support decision-making tool was adapted from the St George’s Model to signpost colleagues to the right levels of support through a series of questions. Alongside the tool, there was a process change in how requests for 1:1 observation and support were authorised which meant that the tool had to be completed before requests could be signed off. We expected a reduction in 1:1 observation and support costs of 25%. It was first tested on two wards before expanding to four more and then to the rest of the acute wards.

Cycle 1

Not all service contacts were correct, which varied across the Trust due to the recent merger. These were amended.

Cycle 2

Changes in terminology caused confusion, and so a calculated communication plan was implemented alongside briefing sessions.

Cycle 3

Site and Bank Teams were not fully aware of the process change and so a new operating procedure was instigated to enable temporary staff team members to prompt for the use of the decision-making tool outcomes.

Cycle 4

Started to see changes, and at this point, we added in the observation and support 5-day training programme, which requires further evaluation, but which also created a greater skill set in those assigned observation and support roles at ward level.

Conclusions from trial 2.

The decision-making tool, which first signposts to the completion of the Escalation Prevention Checklist, enabled the Trust to achieve a reduction in our agency and temporary staff spend. Embedding it into the agency and temporary staff process provided assurance that all options had been considered as well as patient needs before any temporary staff solutions were agreed. It provided greater emphasis on local teams undertaking 1:1 observation and support, which in turn led to backfill temporary staff costs only at general levels. The observations and support 5-day training programme for healthcare workers in therapeutic intervention was required to enhance the 1:1 observation and support that had been prescribed by the decision-making tool (figure 2).

Results

Following the first trial, an initial reduction in spend was observed on the trial wards. However, this was not sustained and required the second trial, which included the assessment form. This embedded the changes and supported a sustained reduction in spending. By the end of year one, use of external temporary staff reduced by £600,000. In year two, a further reduction of £1.2 million was achieved.

From a quality metric perspective, total incidents with harm have reduced by 12.5% and have maintained this level since we rolled out the project. An improved incident picture was noted, with falls (with harm) reducing by 8% and being sustained at a lower level consistently since the trial ended. Our concern of a detrimental effect on incidents of violence and aggression was unfounded. In fact, these incidents have reduced; however, there have been a plethora of other interventions around this, so there is no evidence to link it directly to this project.

Total shift requests significantly reduced, as wards began to manage their own requirements. With internal colleagues looking after the patients in a therapeutic way through the observations and support 5-day training programme, we negated the high-cost temporary staff who were adding little in the way of therapeutic input (figure 3).

Lessons and limitations

In retrospect, it became apparent that behavioural change required time and engagement at all stages. The initial checklist had limited impact when used in isolation but became more effective when integrated into the wider process. Introducing a ‘hard stop’ in the process increased pace and consistency of adoption.

Within the programme, we had to change terminology widely across a complex organisation. Communication to a dispersed population of colleagues provided challenges in the adoption of 1:1 observation and support understanding. The ambiguity in the language used to describe job roles provided a cultural challenge, with many colleagues misunderstanding what support was required. Through the length of the project, the name and terms to describe specialling changed to become enhanced care, then latterly to 1:1 observation and support. This may have created confusion; however, this reflects the journey of how views and understanding of the care required have evolved.

Managing the oversight of the safety and quality metrics was challenged during the programme; two merged organisations had to adopt one reporting system, meaning the level of data was mixed during the programme. During the later stages, we were able to review in retrospect and confirm anecdotal understanding of bedside level safety metrics.

A key message is the coproduction of this tool. All 47 inpatient wards were represented and inputted to the final assessment document. Wards shared their likes, dislikes and what they wanted to change, ensuring that the tool was more user-friendly. Experts by experience also joined the day and reviewed our tools and gave valuable insights into how it feels to be on the receiving end of 1:1 observation and support.

An interactive data review session (data mingle) where data were shared and discussed collaboratively proved successful so that wards could see how they were performing. They were encouraged to add annotations to their SPC charts for special causes and shifts which provoked great conversations about what was happening in their ward.

Conclusion

Peer-developed assessment criteria enabled nursing leaders to allocate 1:1 observation and support more effectively. This also supported less experienced leaders to make consistent decisions enabling the allocation of the right person next to the patient.

Increasing confidence in delivering 1:1 observation and support, while reducing reliance on restrictive approaches where appropriate, improved patient experience and contributed to a reduction in incidents.

Sustainability was supported through the development of an enhanced education package designed to facilitate an in-depth understanding of the process. Further work to evaluate the training package is required.

Our work has been cited as a best practice example within the national enhanced therapeutic observations of care programme (ETOC).11 We are continuing to develop as learning emerges joining the national programme’s fourth cohort on 11 February 2026.
