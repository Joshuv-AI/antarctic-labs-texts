Abstract
. Let Σ b \Sigma_{b} be a compact Riemann surface of genus b ≥ 2 b\geq 2 and let 𝖯 2 ​ ( Σ b ) = π 1 ​ ( Σ b × Σ b − Δ ) \mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})=\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b}-\Delta) be the corresponding pure braid group on two strands. A finite quotient φ : 𝖯 2 ​ ( Σ b ) ⟶ G \varphi\colon\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})\longrightarrow G is called admissible if φ \varphi does not factor through π 1 ​ ( Σ b × Σ b ) \pi_{1}(\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b}) . In this work we classify all admissible quotients of 𝖯 2 ​ ( Σ b ) \mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b}) such that | G | ≤ 127 |G|\leq 127 .



1.
Introduction

Let
\Sigma_{b}
be a compact Riemann surface of genus
b
. We write
\mathsf{P}_{k}(\Sigma_{b})
for the pure braid group on
k
strands of
\Sigma_{b}
, defined as the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Conf}_{k}\Sigma_{b})
of the configuration space

(1)

\operatorname{Conf}_{k}\Sigma_{b}=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in(\Sigma_{b})^{k}\;|\;x_{i}\neq x_{j}\;\operatorname{for}\;i\neq j\right\}.

For every choice of
b
and
k
, surface braid groups are finitely presented, and several presentations appear in the literature (e.g.
[
Bir69
,
Scott70
,
Bel04
,
GG04
]
). These groups play a relevant role in low-dimensional topology and related fields, bridging ideas from algebraic topology, geometric topology, and algebraic geometry.

A series of recent works
[
CaPol21
,
Pol22
,
PolSab22
,
PolSab23
,
PolSab24
]
has clarified how certain finite quotients of
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})=\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b}-\Delta)
, where
\Delta\subset\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b}
is the diagonal, relate to the theory of double Kodaira fibred surfaces. More precisely, let
A_{12}
denote the homotopy class of the geometric braid that loops once around the diagonal. A surjective homomorphism

(2)

\varphi\colon\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})\longrightarrow G

onto a finite group
G
, such that
\varphi(A_{12})
has order
n\geq 2
, will be called an
admissible epimorphism
of
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
. We will also say that the finite group
G
is an
admissible
(
pure
)
braid quotient of tipe

(b,\,n)
.

Since
n\geq 2
, a surjective homomorphism as in (
2
) is admissible if and only if it does not factor through
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b})
. Moreover, being
A_{12}
a commutator in
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
, an admissible braid quotient
G
is necessarily
non-abelian
, see Remark
3.6
.

By Grauert-Remmert’s extension theorem and Serre’s GAGA, an admissible braid quotient as in (
2
) determines a Galois cover

(3)

\mathbf{f}\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b},

where
S
is a smooth projective surface. The Galois group of this cover is
G
and its branch locus is exactly
\Delta
, with branching index equal to
n
.

Composing the map
\mathbf{f}\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b}
with the two projections onto the natural factors of
\Sigma_{b}\times\Sigma_{b}
, and performing a simultaneous Stein factorization if necessary, we get to smooth fibrations

(4)

f_{i}\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma_{b},\quad i=1,\,2,

each with connected fibres. When
b\geq 2
, these fibrations are non-isotrivial, i.e. the fibres of
f_{i}
vary in moduli; in the terminology of
[
CatRol09
]
, the surface
S
is therefore a
double Kodaira surface
, equipped with the two distinct Kodaira fibrations
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.

Moreover, all the topological and biregular invariants of the surface
S
can be recovered from the admissible epimorphism
\varphi\colon\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})\longrightarrow G
used in its construction. For a comprehensive discussion of this correspondence, we refer the reader to the works cited above, whereas for further information about double Kodaira surfaces one can see
[
Rol10
,
Cat17
,
LLR20
]
and the references contained therein.

The previous discussion shows that it makes sense to classify admissible quotients
G
of
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
. In the case
b=2
, this classification was carried out in
[
PolSab22
]
for admissible quotients of order at most

, and subsequently extended in
[

]
for admissible quotients of order up to

.

Let us emphasize that, although the surface braid groups

\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
are known to be residually

(see
[

), the present paper—together

We now turn to a description of the techniques underlying the proof of our Main Theorem. Our approach combines techniques from Combinatorial Group Theory with explicit computations carried out using the computer algebra system
GAP4
, see
[
GAP4
]
. The group
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
admits a finite geometric presentation with
4b+1
generators, as established in
[
GG04
, Theorem 7]
. Applying an admissible epimorphism to these generators yields an ordered tuple

(5)

\mathfrak{S}=(\mathsf{r}_{11},\,\mathsf{t}_{11},\ldots,\mathsf{r}_{1b},\,\mathsf{t}_{1b},\,\mathsf{r}_{21},\,\mathsf{t}_{21},\ldots,\mathsf{r}_{2b},\,\mathsf{t}_{2b},\,\mathsf{z}),

consisting of
4b+1
elements that generate
G
. Among these, the element
\mathsf{z}
(which is the image of
A_{12}\in\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
via
\varphi\colon\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})\longrightarrow G
) is a commutator satisfying
o(\mathsf{z})=n
, and the entire sequence is subject to a system of relations induced by those of
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
. Such a configuration is called a
diagonal double Kodaira structure
of type
(b,\,n)
on the group
G
, a terminology motivated by the connection with double Kodaira surfaces described earlier. Consequently, the geometric problem of constructing an admissible epimorphism of
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
onto
G
can be reformulated as the algebraic-combinatorial problem of determining a diagonal double Kodaira structure of type
(b,\,n)
on
G
.

Let us give a brief description of the computational tools employed in our analysis. While groups of order
|G|\leq 63
could still be handled manually, the use of
GAP4
became essential for the study of groups of order
|G|\geq 64
. To facilitate these computations, we implemented a dedicated procedure, denoted
CheckStructures
, which was used extensively throughout our work. For the reader’s convenience, all relevant scripts have been made available in an accompanying GitHub repository, see
[
GAP4DDKS
]
. In addition, selected excerpts of the
GAP4
code are included in the paper itself, in order to illustrate the nature of the computational methods involved.

Let us emphasize that significant computational complexity issues arise when
considering admissible braid quotients of order
|G|\geq 128
. Even in the case

b=2
, the computer algebra tools currently available are not suitable for a
systematic or exhaustive analysis at this level. Addressing the problem for
larger groups will therefore require new theoretical input to complement the
computational approach.

We conclude this Introduction by outlining the structure of the paper.

Section
2
collects the algebraic background required for the subsequent sections, with particular emphasis on CCT groups and monolithic groups.

Section
3
introduces the surface braid groups
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
and their admissible quotients.

In Section
4
we develop the combinatorial group-theoretic framework underlying our approach and we introduce diagonal double Kodaira structures and some related combinatorial objects, that we call
prestructures
. We recall that such structures cannot occur on CCT groups (Remark
4.7
), while for non-monolithic groups every diagonal double Kodaira structure arises as the lift of a structure from a proper quotient (Proposition
4.8
). In this sense, diagonal double Kodaira structures arising in monolithic groups may be regarded as “genuinely new”.
We also provide a sufficient condition ensuring that all diagonal double Kodaira structures in
G
(if any) are of type
(2,\,n)
, see Corollary
4.11
.

The results described above show that, in our investigation, we can exclude both non-CCT groups and non-monolithic groups whose proper quotients do not admit diagonal double Kodaira structures. The remaining groups of order
|G|\leq 127
are then examined individually in Section
5
, with the help of our
GAP4
routine
CheckStructure
, leading to the proof of the Main Theorem.

Finally, in Section
6
we make some final considerations and we present some open problems.

The paper concludes with an Appendix, which collects presentations and key
properties of all finite groups admitting prestructures that appear in our
analysis, and provides additional details on the computations carried out in
each case.

Definition 2.1
.

A non-abelian finite group

is said to be a

center commutative-transitive group

(
or a CCT-group, for short

if commutativity is a transitive

The CCT-property is equivalent to the condition that the centralizer

is abelian for every non-central element

Let

\boldsymbol{(1)}

-group.

\boldsymbol{(2)}

If

is a

-group.

\boldsymbol{(3)}

If

contains an abelian normal

-group.

2.2.
Monolithic groups

Given a finite group
G
, we define its
monolith

\operatorname{mon}(G)
as
the intersection of all non-trivial, normal subgroups of
G
. The group
G
is called
monolithic
if
\operatorname{mon}(G)\neq\{1\}
. Equivalently,
G
is monolithic if it contains precisely one minimal non-trivial, normal subgroup.

Example 2.6
.

The group
G
is simple if and only if
\operatorname{mon}(G)=G
. In particular, every non-trivial finite simple group is monolithic.

Example 2.7
.

A non-trivial product of the form
G=H\times K
is never monolithic, since
H\times\{1\}
and
\{1\}\times K
are two non-trivial normal subgroups intersecting only at the identity of
G
.

Example 2.8
.

A finite
p
-group
G
is called
extra-special
if its center
Z(G)
is cyclic of order
p
and the quotient
V=G/Z(G)
is a non-trivial, elementary abelian
p
-group. Every extra-special group
G
is monolithic, with
\operatorname{mon}(G)=Z(G)
. Indeed, since
Z(G)\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is normal in
G
, we always have
\operatorname{mon}(G)\subseteq Z(G)
. On the other hand, every non-trivial, normal subgroup of an extra-special group contains the center (see
[
Rob96
, Exercise 9
p. 146]
), hence
Z(G)\subseteq\operatorname{mon}(G)
.

Actually, a more general result holds true:

Proposition 2.9
.

If
G
is monolithic and
Z(G)\neq\{1\}
, then
\operatorname{mon}(G)
is cyclic of prime order. In particular, if
G
is a monolithic
p
-group then
\operatorname{mon}(G)\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Proof.

If
Z(G)
is non-trivial, by definition we have
\operatorname{mon}(G)\subseteq Z(G)
, hence
\operatorname{mon}(G)
is abelian and all its subgroups are normal in
G
. Thus the conclusion follows by using the minimality of
\operatorname{mon}(G)
and the fact that a finite
p
-group has a non-trivial center.
∎

6.
Final Considerations and open problems

Theorem
5.8
suggests that admissible braid quotients are extremely sparse.
Indeed, among the
1036
non-abelian groups of order at most
127
, only
11
occur
as admissible braid quotients. Let
a_{n}
denote the number of admissible braid
quotients
G
with
|G|\leq n
.

Question 6.1
.

What can be said about the asymptotic behaviour of the sequence
(a_{n})
?

For each
b\geq 2
, let
G_{b}
denote the smallest possible order of an admissible
quotient
G
of
\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{b})
.

Question 6.2
.

Can the integer
G_{b}
be computed explicitly, or at least effectively bounded?

As a first example, by
[
PolSab22
]
we know that
G_{2}=32
. Moreover, the results of
[
CaPol21
]
,
which show that certain extra-special groups arise as admissible braid quotients,
yield the upper bound

(74)

G_{b}\leq\min\bigl\{p^{2b+1},\,5^{4b+1}\bigr\},

where
p
denotes the smallest prime dividing
b+1
. At present, however, it is
unclear how sharp this bound is.

Note that (
74
) implies
G_{b}\leq 2^{2b+1}
whenever
b
is odd, and in
particular
G_{3}\leq 128
. On the other hand, Corollary
5.9
shows that

G_{3}\geq 128
. It follows that
G_{3}=128
; in other words, for
b=3
equality
holds in (
74
).

Question 6.3
.

Are the two extra-special groups of order
128
the only admissible quotients of

\mathsf{P}_{2}(\Sigma_{3})
having minimal order?

Further examples in higher genus would be needed to clarify whether extra-special
groups play a distinguished role for all values of
b
. We conclude the discussion of the invariant
G_{b}
by stating the following

Question 6.4
.

Is it true that, for every integer
b\geq 2
, we have
G_{b}=2^{2b+1}
?

At present, the evidence supporting an affermative answer to Question
6.4
is limited to the cases
b=2
and
b=3
. Furthermore, the computational techniques used in this paper are not suited to a brute-force investigation for
b\geq 4
,
suggesting that new conceptual tools will be needed to address the problem in
higher genus.

A further, and somewhat surprising, observation is that all admissible braid
quotients discovered so far are nilpotent of class
2
, that is, they are
non-abelian groups whose commutator subgroup is contained in the center. It would
be interesting to determine whether genuinely new families arise as the genus or the order increases. In particular, one may ask:

Question 6.5
.

Do there exist admissible braid quotients that are nilpotent of class at least
3
?
Are there non-nilpotent admissible braid quotients?

A related problem is to understand to what extent the existence of an admissible
braid quotient is governed by group-theoretic properties alone, independently of
the genus
b
. We expect that further progress in this direction will require a
deeper interaction between combinatorial group theory and the geometry of surface
braid groups.

More generally, we conclude by posing a broad–and perhaps overly ambitious–
question:

Question 6.6
.

To what extent is it possible to classify finite groups that arise as admissible
braid quotients?
