Identification of silage bacterial clusters and analysis of their microecological characteristics
Authors: Mao Li, Shuo Wu, Xuejuan Zi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnterotypes refer to the different bacterial clusters in the gut microecosystem, which are closely related to host physiology, digestion, disease, and other phenotypes. However, whether there are clear clusters in the silage microecosystem, and the fermentation quality and characteristics of unique cluster silage remain unknown. To determine whether distinct bacterial clusters exist in the silage microecosystem and to characterize their fermentation properties, we analyzed the bacterial community composition and fermentation quality of 156 silage samples, and further explored their underlying microbial ecological features.ResultsWe confirmed three distinct clusters in the silage microbiome, which were named according to their dominant bacterial taxa: the E-cluster (characterized by a higher abundance of unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (UG)), the P-cluster (enriched with Pseudomonas and Janthinobacterium), and the L-cluster (dominated by Lactobacillus). These microbial clusters were closely associated with fermentation quality: the L-cluster exhibited superior silage quality compared to the E- and P-clusters. Meanwhile, the microbial functional profiles differed significantly among the three clusters of silage. Numerous pathways were significantly enriched in the P-cluster, such as the Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, etc. Moreover, bacterial co-occurrence networks of three clusters silage displayed cooperative interactions mainly, P-cluster silage network was more complex and tighter, E-cluster silage has more functional microbial units and more stable. Furthermore, the assembly of microbial communities in the three silage clusters was dominated by stochastic processes. Specifically, the E-cluster and L-cluster were governed by ecological drift, while dispersal limitation was more influential in the P-cluster.ConclusionsOverall, we found in our study that the silage microbiome can be divided into three clusters, and different clusters have significant differences in fermentation quality, microbial diversity and compositions, functional profiles, microbial network characteristics and community assembly mechanisms. These results could broaden our comprehension of the silage microbial ecology processes and also provide a scientific basis on which to develop a method to precisely regulate silage quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00891-x.Keywords: Silage microbiome, Community cluster, Fermentation quality, Co-occurrence networks, Community assembly

Introduciton

Enterotypes, first described by Arumugam et al. [1], represent distinct gut microbial community types that can be classified according to the relative abundance of dominant genera. In humans, three enterotypes have been identified: Bacteroides (enterotype 1), Prevotella (enterotype 2), and Ruminococcus (enterotype 3), with each type characterized by the predominance of one genus in the gut microbiota. Enterotypes are the different functional composition types of human gut microbiomes, which refers to the dimensionality reduction and classification of complex gut microbiota into several distinct clusters with clear characteristics, and bears a high similarity to the phenomenon where individual blood types remain unchanged [1, 2]. The proposal of this game-changing concept has simplified the interpretation of the traditional data on gut microbiota, and many related studies have quickly splintered off regarding the interaction between enterotypes and diet, disease, host phenotype and the environment [3–5]. Prior to this, due to the complexity and variability of gut microbiota, it was difficult to associate human diseases, health, diet, and other phenotypic or environmental factors with gut microorganisms, thus neglecting the important role of microbiomes. The enterotype concept provides a scientific basis for disease intervention or treatment through precise regulation of gut microbiota and has thus contributed to outstanding progress in the field of medicine [6].

In clarifying human enterotypes, Arumugam et al. [1] also prospectively proposed that enterotypes may exist in animal intestines as well, and it may similarly correlate tightly with their health status. A few years later, Li et al. [7] described two recurring enterotypes found in bumblebees: enterotype 2 had abundant Gilliamella and Snodgrassella, and enterotype 1 had more Serratia and Hafnia. In more recent years, the enterotype concept has also been applied in livestock and poultry research. Yuan et al. [8] identified chicken gut enterotypes and also further explained their effects on gut microbiota and metabolism. Tröscher-Mußotter et al. [9] revealed that cow feces had three microbiome clusters, with each cluster contributing to the condition and productivity of the animals in unique ways. They reported that in particular, Bifidobacterium plays a crucial role. In pikas, Yu et al. [10] observed two types of gut microbiota. These may be related to the altitude of the pika’s living environment. Wang et al. [11] reported on two clusters occurring in the rumen of goats bacteria and found a close correlation between growth performance and rumen metabolites. These above studies indicated that gut microecosystem enterotypes closely relate to host microbiome metabolism and growth performance. Similarly, silage is an output of an intricate microecosystem process, so we suspect that there is also a certain clustering of silage microorganisms.

Silage is the most important component of ruminant nutrition [12], and the production of silage dates back around 3000 years [13]. In recent decades, silage has been especially promoted in Asia, especially in Japan and China, where mature silage has been applied to Grass-based Livestock Husbandry [14, 15]. Ensiling is an intricate process of microbial ecology where lactic acid bacteria facilitate the production of beneficial organic acids, including lactic acid, that inhibit spoilage microorganisms from growing, thereby allowing fresh forage to retain nutrients for a long time [16]. Quality of silage fermentation is the ultimate reflection of the microbial ecological process, which emphasizes that microbial ecology characteristics are crucial for regulating the condition of silage [17, 18].

Research on microbial ecology usually includes several aspects, such as microbial community diversity, structure, function, assembly mechanism, interaction between community composition and environmental factors, microbial network topological properties, and understanding network characteristics such as modularity, complexity, and stability of microbial networks [19–21]. In recent years, microbial ecology theory has also been applied in silage research. For instance, both plant materials and the methods of their preparation may affect silage microbial communities, community succession, co-occurrence networks and community assemblage [15, 18, 22–24]. However, so far no one has studied the microbial clustering of silage, let al.one the fermentation quality and microbial ecological characteristics of different silage cluster types.

Thereby, we propose the following scientific questions: Are there clear silage cluster types in the silage microecosystem? How is the fermentation quality of the unique types? What is the microbial taxonomy and function of each unique type and what are its characteristics of molecular ecology? Here, we identified for the first time the silage bacterial cluster types, and then we examined the differences in silage quality, microbial diversity, composition, functional profiles, community assembly processes, and molecular ecological networks between the different silage cluster types that had been identified. We believe that this current study will broaden our understanding of the unique microbial ecological processes found in different silages, as well as provide novel insights that will assist in the development of a method to precisely regulate silage quality.

Methods

Preparation of silage material

For this study we collected 156 forage samples (Supplementary Table 1) from the National Tropical Forage Germplasm Repository of the Tropical Crop Genetic Resources Institute, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences, Danzhou, Hainan, China. For each forage, about 500 g of material was placed in a specialized plastic silage bag and then vacuum sealed for indoor storage. After 60 days of ensiling at room temperature, the bags were opened, and samples were extracted to determine the silage fermentation parameters and microbial diversity metrics.

Silage fermentation quality analysis

From each silage sample, 50 g was removed for immersion in 200 mL of distilled water. Next, the soaked silage sample was stored at 4℃ for 24 h before being filtered through four gauze layers so only the extract remained. Half of the extract was used in the fermentation quality analysis, and the other half was stored at -80℃ until the silage microbiome could be analyzed. Next, an electronic precision pH meter was used to determine pH value. Ammonia nitrogen (AN) content was assessed according to the phenol hypochlorite method. High performance liquid chromatography was performed to determine how much lactic acid (LA), acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA) and butyric acid (BA) was present in the silage. The experimental steps followed the design of Li et al. [18], with optimizing improvements made according to the experimental conditions. Evaluation of silage fermentation quality was performed using V-Score, which is calculated based on both the ratio of ammonium nitrogen to total nitrogen and on the ratio of organic acids to total acids [25].

DNA extraction and sequencing

The silage extracts mentioned above were utilized in microbial community analysis. The Stool DNA Kit (OMEGA Bio Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) was used to perform genomic DNA extraction on 156 silage microbial samples, and the DNA extracted was appropriately diluted and saved in storage at -20 ℃. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTRCGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACCVGGGTATCTAAT-3′) [11]. PCR Conditions: 95℃ for 2 min, followed by 25 cycles at 95℃ for 30 s, 55℃ for 30 s and 72℃ for 30 s with a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. Controls: Negative controls were included during both the DNA extraction and PCR amplification steps to monitor for potential contamination. No positive controls were used, as the primary focus was on the comparative analysis of the natural microbial community across a large number of silage samples (n = 156), and the standardized protocol ensured consistent processing.The Entrust Biomarker Technologies Company (Beijing, China) made use of Illumina’s MiSeq 2500 platform to run high-throughput sequencing of PCR amplification products. The raw sequencing data were deposited into the European Molecular Biology Laboratory Nucleic Acid Sequence Database under the accession number PRJEB80301. The link is as follows: http://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/data/view/PRJEB80301.

Bioinformatic assessment

The resulting sequences were processed with Trimomatic V 0.33 software to remove primers and for quality filtering using the following parameters: SLIDINGWINDOW:5:30 (trimming when the average quality score dropped below Q30 within a 4-base window) and MINLEN:200 (discarding sequences shorter than 200 bp). The quality was reviewed and corrected using QIIME2 software [26]. The raw sequences were demultiplexed and quality-filtered. We applied stringent criteria: sequences were required to have a minimum length of 300 bp and a minimum Phred quality score of Q30. Chimeric sequences were identified and removed using Usearch 7.0. The resulting high-quality, non-chimeric sequences were then used for all downstream analyses. The subsequent optimized sequence was then divided into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on 97% similarity. High-quality sequences were annotated using taxonomy information based on the Naive Bayes classifier that was obtained from the SILVA reference database (Release 138.1) [27]. QIIME2 was used to calculate alpha diversity (Observed species and Shannon indices), which was subsequently visualized using R software. Prior to calculating alpha and beta diversity metrics, all samples were rarefied (subsampled without replacement) to an even sequencing depth of 166,656 clean reads per sample to ensure fair comparisons, as recommended for microbiome data analysis. QIIME was used to calculate Beta diversity (the principal coordinate analysis, PCoA) to assess the differences in microbial community distribution between the three silage clusters.

To identify the ideal community cluster count across the silage microbial samples, Calinski Harabasz (CH) and corresponding silhouette scores were calculated using the “Cascade km” program in the “vegan” community ecology R package that draws from Bray-Curtis distance [28, 29]. The maximum CH value usually indicates the ideal community cluster count. PCoA graphs based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity were created to visualize the variation in community structure between silage clusters. Predictions of silage bacterial function were based on the database from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), in combination with the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) software [30]. We calculated the differences in silage microorganismal gene functions in different clusters at the 2nd and 3rd level. Then, a visual PCoA graph was created to assess how the gene functional profiles differed between clusters based on the Bray-Curtis distance of predicted gene function at KEGG level 3 [31].

We constructed molecular ecological networks for three clusters of forage silage microorganisms to explore the differences in microbial community co-occurrence patterns. The SparCC method (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data), which is designed for compositional data, was used to calculate correlations among the top 200 OTUs by abundance. Only OTUs with |SparCC correlation| > 0.3 and p < 0.05 (based on 100 bootstraps) were retained for network analysis. Gephi v.0.9.2. was used for visualization. Some parameters of network topology features (clustering coefficient, edges, graph density, and average degree) were calculated. This analysis is based on previous studies and has been appropriately improved [32, 33]. Both the connectivity within modules (Zi) and the connectivity between modules (Pi) were used to characterize the role of nodes. By enacting the Pi and Zi thresholds of the simplified network, the entire ecological network can be categorized into the following: connectors, network hubs, module hubs, and peripheral nodes. The former three are commonly known as Keystone taxa [34].

The nearest taxon index (NTI) and the mean nearest taxon distance (MNTD) were calculated using the weighted ses and the “picante” R package. Both are indicators reflecting the assembly process of microbial communities. MNTD values reflect the phylogenetic relationships and relative abundance of OTUs. The NTI equals the opposite ses, and is calculated by dividing the difference between the measured MNTD and the random average MNTD by the standard deviation of the random MNTD value. When the NTI (or - ses. MNTD) of clustering exceeds 0, phylogenetic clustering primarily controls community assembly, but if the NTI is under 0, phylogenetic dispersion plays a major role in microbial assembly. MNTD or NTI quantify to what extent deterministic processes matter. We also evaluated the phylogenetic relationship between samples by calculating the β approximate taxon index (βNTI) and Bray-Curtis-based Raup-Crick metric (RCbray), and further clarified which processes dominate community assembly in different silage clusters. A |βNTI| value > 2 indicates that deterministic processes dominate community assembly. When the βNTI value is less than − 2, homogeneous selection dominates, and when it is greater than 2, heterogeneous selection dominates. However, if the |βNTI| value is between − 2 and 2, stochastic processes are considered the dominating influence on community assembly. When the RCbray value is under − 0.95, this indicates homogenized dispersal. A value exceeding 0.95 suggests dispersal limitation, and the intermediate values indicate drift. The analysis and calculation process of the above index refers to these studies [35–37].

Using a partial least squares path model (PLS-PM), we determined the effects of silage microbial community types on species diversity, functional diversity, community assembly (betaNTI) and microbial interaction. Then we selected the model with all paths significant as the optimal one, and calculated the corresponding path coefficients and goodness of fit. The former represents the strength and direction of linear correlation between two variables. The latter is a standard measure of the accuracy of metamodel prediction. During the application of PLS-PM, a GOF > 0.5 is acceptable. The final model was then constructed using the “inner plot” program in R package “plspm”. The analysis procedure was based on that described by Yu et al. [10].

Statistical analysis

Using SPSS 26.0, a Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare the silage fermentation parameters and the microbial alpha diversity of the three silage clusters. A Bray-Curtis and Jaccard distance-based Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) matrix was employed to assess how the bacterial community structures differed between the three silage clusters. PERMANOVA was used to quantify how beta diversity differed and was run in the R package “vegan” (R v 4.1.2). The statistical significance threshold was considered either P < 0.05, P < 0.01 or P < 0.001.

Results

Cluster identification of silage microbial communities

In the 156 silage microbiome samples, there were 35,881,660 paired-end reads and 25,998,350 clean reads were obtained. Meanwhile, 166,656 clean reads and 147,828 effective reads were generated on average per sample.

To identify the optimal clusters of the 156 silage microbiome samples, the Calinski–Harabasz (CH) values were calculated based on the Bray–Curtis distance of the silage microbiome at the OTU level, as were the analogous silhouette scores (Fig. 1a). CH peaked at a silage microbial community count of three clusters. There are cluster I (N = 52), cluster II (N = 81) and cluster Ⅲ (N = 23). A corresponding PCoA plot indicates that the silage microbial community structure was significantly distinct in each cluster (P < 0.001, Fig. 1b). The silage microbiota phylogenetic tree similarly indicates that the three clusters clearly diverge from each other (Fig. 1c).

Microbial diversity and taxonomy of different silage clusters

Through one-way ANOVA, the alpha diversity was contrasted among the different clusters. cluster II had significantly higher alpha diversity than cluster I and cluster III (P < 0.001, Fig. 2a, b). Phyla Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes dominated in cluster I and cluster II, with average relative abundances of 76.68%, 18.59%, and 1.44% in the former and 60.53%, 13.55%, and 12.34% in the latter, respectively (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, Firmicutes (81.62%), Proteobacteria (9.70%) and Bacteroidetes (3.73%) were the dominant bacteria in cluster III (Fig. 2c). Genera Enterobacteriaceae (UG) (64.65%) and Lactobacillus (11.36%) dominated in cluster I, Pseudomonas (23.59%) and Janthinobacterium (5.73%) dominated in cluster II, and Lactobacillaceae (UG) (48.92%) and Lactobacillus (22.52%) dominated in cluster III (Fig. 2d).

We further contrasted the three clusters in their bacterial composition. Phylum Proteobacteria was most profuse in cluster I, Firmicutes dominated cluster III, and Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria were most abundant in cluster II (Fig. 2e). Genus Enterobacteriaceae (UG) was most profuse in cluster I, Pseudomonas, Janthinobacterium and Brevundimonas dominated cluster II, and Lactobacillus (UG) and Lactobacillus were most abundant in cluster III (Fig. 2f). Figure 2(d, f) shows the relatively higher abundance of Enterobacteriaceae (UG), Pseudomonas and Lactobacillaceae (UG) in the three clusters. Based on this, we named the three clusters E-cluster (cluster I), P-cluster (cluster II) and L-cluster (cluster Ⅲ).

Fermentation quality of different silage clusters

The differences between the three clusters in their silage fermentation parameters are shown in Fig. 3. The pH value was lowest in the P-cluster(4.55) and highest in the E-cluster(5.46) (P < 0.01), and mean of L-cluster pH value was 4.81. The LA content was lowest in the E-cluster and highest in the L-cluster (P < 0.01). The AA content did not differ significantly between the three clusters (P>0.05). The PA content was lowest in the L-cluster and highest in the P-cluster (P < 0.0001). The BA concentration was lowest in the L-cluster and highest in the E-cluster (P < 0.01). The AN concentration was significantly higher in the E-cluster than in the P-cluster (P < 0.01). The V-score value, which is an indicator of silage fermentation quality, was significantly higher in the L-cluster than in the E-cluster or P-cluster (P < 0.001). Based on the high lactate content, low propionate and butyrate content, and the highest comprehensive evaluation index V-score value exhibited by L-cluster, we believe that L-cluster was superior in its silage quality over the E-cluster and P-cluster.

Analysis of functional differentiation between the silage microbial communities of different clusters

We used 16 S rRNA gene data to assess estimated gene functional profiles (Fig. 4). In Fig. 4a, the Bray-Curtis distance-based PCoA analysis displays significant differences between the functional profile of the P-cluster and those of the L-cluster and E-cluster (PERMANOVA test, P < 0.001). At KEGG level 2, 35 total functional pathways were uncovered. Figure 4b shows the ten most abundant pathways. The following pathways of the P-cluster were enriched significantly (P < 0.001): Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites; Protein family: signaling and cellular processes; Xenobiotics of Biodegradation and metabolism; Carbohydrate metabolism; Cellular motility; Metabolism of terpenoides and polyketides; Signal transduction; Lipid metabolism; Protein family: metabolism; Amino acid metabolism.

Analysis of co-occurrence networks of different silage cluster microbial communities

Network topological property analysis was conducted to explore interactions between the microbial taxa of the three silage clusters (Fig. 5a). The P-cluster had more edges than the E-cluster or L-cluster, which indicates that the relationships between microorganisms were most complex in the P-cluster. In addition, the positive edge rate was significantly higher than the negative edge rate in all three clusters, with the E-cluster having the highest proportion of positive edges. This indicates that the silage bacterial networks were mainly cooperative. Furthermore, the average path length, average degree, and graph density were obviously greater in the P-cluster network than in the E-cluster or L-cluster networks. This suggests that the P-cluster silage network was tighter than the others. It is also worth noting that the E-cluster network was more modular than the P-cluster or L-cluster networks, which indicates that the E-cluster silage had more functional microbial units and was more stable. Network analysis was also run to classify the ecological types of all the nodes (Fig. 5b). All nodes were organized into three categories: module hubs, peripherals and connectors. A total of 84 keystone taxa were identified across the three silage cluster networks, with one module hub (Streptococcus) and thirty six connectors found in the E-cluster network, sixteen connectors found in the P-cluster network and thirty-one connectors found in the L-cluster network. All remaining OTUs were considered peripheral nodes.

Assembly process of different silage cluster microbial communities

To explore the community assembly process of the silage microbiota, we calculated which ecological processes shaped the microbiota in each silage cluster. The NTI (- ses. MNTD) values of the E-cluster and P-cluster were significantly higher than zero, while that of the L-cluster was close to zero. This indicates that phylogenetic clustering dominated these silage microbial communities. Meanwhile, the P-cluster was shaped by a higher proportion of deterministic processes according to its greater absolute value of MNTD. The E-cluster community was mainly characterized by ecological drift, and by variable selection to a lesser extent, while the L-cluster was also mainly characterized by ecological drift. These two clusters were thus dominated by a stochastic process. The assembly process of the P-cluster community was mainly dispersal limitation, with ecological drift playing a lesser role. Dispersal and ecological drift are both stochastic processes. Therefore, our data showed that the microbial communities were dominated by stochastic processes in all three silage clusters.

The linking among silage community type, species diversity, functional diversity, community assembly (NTI) and microbial interaction

We assessed how silage microbial community types influenced species diversity, functional diversity, community assembly(betaNTI) and microbial interaction by running a partial least squares path model (PLS-PM). The results indicate that the community types correlated significantly and negatively with species diversity and functional diversity. In contrast, there was also a significant positive correlation of community type with microbial interaction and community assembly. In addition, community assembly processes were mainly positively influenced by community type, species diversity and functional diversity. Further, community type and functional diversity both had a strong positive effect on microbial interaction, while species diversity and community assembly showed weak negative effects. In summary, ensiling type significantly impacts the silage microbial community structure, assembly mechanism and function.

Discussion

Here, we assessed the community clusters of 156 silage microbiome samples, and three cluster types were identified: E-cluster, P-cluster and L-cluster. The taxonomic results show that species of the genus Enterobacteriaceae (UG) were most abundant in the E-cluster, while Pseudomonas, Janthinobacterium and Brevundimonas were most abundant in the P-cluster, and Lactobacillaceae (UG) and Lactobacillus dominated the L-cluster. The silage microbial cluster type related closely to fermentation quality, and our findings indicate that the L-cluster was superior in its silage quality over the E-cluster and the P-cluster. In addition, the microbial functional profiles differed significantly between the three silage cluster types. Many pathways were enriched significantly in the P-cluster, including Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites etc. Moreover, bacterial co-occurrence networks mainly displayed cooperative interactions in all three silage cluster types. The P-cluster silage network was the tightest and most complex, and the E-cluster silage network was more stable and had more functional microbial units. Furthermore, microbial community assembly was dominated by stochastic processes in all three silage cluster types, with the E-cluster and the L-cluster governed by ecological drift, and dispersal limitation being more influential in the P-cluster.

Ensiling is a microbially-driven anaerobic fermentation process where lactic acid bacteria are dominant [15]. As an intricate process in the microecosystem, we had ample reason to suspect a certain clustering of silage microorganisms here as well. In this study, we identified a confirmed three cluster types in the silage microbiome: the E-cluster, P-cluster and L-cluster, with the cluster identity closely tied to fermentation quality. The E-cluster silage was characterized by a higher pH value, and greater butyric acid and ammonium nitrogen concentrations, but the lactic acid concentrations were lower, meaning that the fermentation quality was unsatisfactory. The E-cluster silage was dominated by microorganisms of Enterobacteriaceae family. Among them, Enterobacter, which is a common and undesirable silage bacterium because it competes with lactic acid bacteria for fermentation substrates and produces acetic acid and ammonium nitrogen that reduce fermentation quality, was the main representative genus [38]. Therefore, using silage additives or mixing silages to reduce the abundance of Enterobacter can elevate its fermentation quality, and these are common silage preparation methods. He et al. [39, 40] found that adding gallic acid or a Moringa oleifera leaf mixture could lower the abundance of Enterobacter and improve stylo silage quality. Zi et al. [41] showed that different sugars reduced Enterobacter in stylo silage, overall improving its fermentation performance. Similarly, Wu et al. [42] reported that adding both molasses and Lacticaseibacillus casei to silage can also inhibit the growth of Enterobacter and achieve good fermentation.

Meanwhile, we found that the P-cluster silage fermentation quality mirrored that of the E-cluster, as evidenced by its great propionic acid and acetic acid contents. Interestingly, its lactate content was significantly higher than that of the E-cluster, while the pH value and ammonium nitrogen content were lower. The poor silage quality of the P-cluster may be related to the dominance of Pseudomonas, a microorganism that is generally considered to play a role in protein degradation and is involved in the generation of biogenic amines and lactic acid, which is not conducive to favorable silage quality [38, 43]. Further, some researchers have indicated that the genus Pseudomonas contributes to the production of propionic acid, which is harmful to silage fermentation [44]. It is generally believed propionic acid is a fermentation product of undesirable bacteria in silage, which consumes the fermentation substrate of lactic acid bacteria, reduces fermentation quality, and also affects animal palatability and intestinal health [45]. Recently, genus Pseudomonas has been found to be abundant in poorly fermented or gramineous silage [45–47]. Therefore, reducing Pseudomonas abundance appropriately can promote silage fermentation. One such highly effective method involves adding lactic acid bacteria inoculants [46, 48]. However, several reports have suggested that Pseudomonas produces numerous antimicrobial metabolites (AMs) that can inhibit pathogens and fungi [49, 50]. Currently, a few antimicrobial agents (AMs) (Phenazines, Pyrrolnitrin, Pyoluteorin, etc.) related to antibiotic activity are being used in agriculture and human medicine [51]. Similarly, several undesirable bacteria and fungi that reduce fermentation quality exist in the silage system [15]. Therefore, the scientific and rational utilization of Pseudomonas and its AMs has great potential in optimizing microbial community structure and promoting silage quality.

The L-cluster silage was characterized by the lowest amount of propionic acid and butyric acid and the greatest amount of lactic acid. According to its V-score value (77.40), the quality of the L-cluster silage approached the high-quality benchmark for silage (a V-score of 80), and this was in the cluster dominated by lactic acid bacteria. The Lactobacillaceae family, and especially genus Lactobacillus, were the main producers of lactic acid in the silage. Thus, increasing Lactobacillus abundance in silage is highly effective for enhancing quality [15]. Adding LAB inoculants is the most common method of silage preparation that achieves this end, with Lactobacillus plantarum being the most widely used LAB strain, followed by L. buchneri, L. bulgaricus, L. helveticus, L. paracasei, L. acidophilus, Pediococcus pentosaceus and P. acidilactici, in no particular order [17]. Various water-soluble carbohydrates, including molasses, glucose and sucrose, have also been applied in silage processing to enhance lactic acid bacteria fermentation substrate [41, 42, 52, 53]. Organic acids (formic acid, propionic acid, hexanoic acid, gallic acid, citric acid, malic acid etc.) and their derivatives acidify their surroundings and inhibit harmful bacteria, which thereby fosters lactic acid bacterial growth and reproduction and improves the quality of fermentation [54–57]. In addition, mixing several types of forage silage is also an effective measure for facilitating Lactobacillus growth. For instance alfalfa and maize, Sesbania cannabina and sweet sorghum, and cassava and corn stalk are all research-backed mixes [18, 58, 59]. Further, with the prohibition of antibiotics in recent years, a large number of medicinal plants with active ingredients have also been used for silage preparation. This venture has great potential for optimizing silage microbial ecology and improving fermentation quality [60–63].

In recent years, with the widespread application of single-molecule sequencing technology, many studies have analyzed the silage microbial community structure at the species level. A large number of lactic acid bacteria species were discovered, such as L. plantarum, L. buchneri, L. bulgaricus, L. helveticus, L. paracasei, L. acidophilus, L.acetotolerans, Leuconostoc lactis, L. suionicum, Pediococcus pentosaceus, P. acidilactici, Weissella cibaria, Enterococcus faecalis, E. mundtii and E. faecium etc [15–17]. The representative species of undesirable Enterobacter in silage mainly include Enterobacter asburiae, E. aerogenes, E. cobei and Klebsiella variicola etc [16, 23, 63]. Pseudomonas is a recently reported microorganism in silage, and there is still some controversy regarding its role [38, 43]. The main species include Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Pseudomonas syringae [18, 60, 62]. The above results indicate that the composition of silage microbial species is highly complex. Adopting appropriate methods to simplify the classification of silage microorganisms is of great significance for deepening the understanding of silage microbial processes and regulating fermentation quality. To sum up, regulating silage fermentation helps avoid the creation of E-cluster or P-cluster silage, and instead aims for L-cluster silage as much as possible. Therefore, unique personalized silage modulation methods need to be adopted for different forage materials.

In recent years, the theory of microbial ecology has also been applied in the study of silage, especially in the analysis of microbial networks and community assembly mechanisms. These studies have revealed that silage microbial network characteristics may be influenced by ensiling micro-environment, processing methods, fermentation time and silage materials. Bai et al. [22] indicated that storing a silage comprised of the entire corn plant at a high temperature could improve fermentation quality and also reduce network complexity, but a simultaneous LAB inoculation could influence network complexity and stability to an even greater degree. Wang et al. [58] claimed that Lactobacillus comes to dominate as the silage fermentation progresses. The microbial network also simplifies and stabilizes significantly. Here, we established that the P-cluster silage network was tighter and more complex than the E-cluster and L-cluster silage networks, possibly because different microbial species dominated in different silage clusters. The composition of P-cluster microorganisms was also more complex, with Pseudomonas (23.59%) and Janthinobacterium (5.73%) being the dominant microorganisms. The E-cluster and L-cluster were dominated by bacteria from the Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae families, respectively, each with an abundance exceeding 60%. This also explains why the P-cluster microbial network was more complex and compact. Still, some research reports have been inconsistent. Dong et al. [24] observed a lower silage fermentation quality when the forage was harvested at noon or in the afternoon. Even the microbial network complexity and stability were significantly lower than of silage from forage harvested in the morning. This difference may be attributed to the difference in the epibiotic microbe of the silage materials. Harmful bacteria (Pantoea dispersa, Enterobacter and Klebsiella varicola) were significantly more abundant in the noon and afternoon materials and were less abundant in the materials harvested in the morning. Our previous research also noted a reduction in the silage microbial network complexity as the dominant Lactobacillus increased in abundance [62].

As with water, environmental and rhizosphere microbial processes, silage fermentation also involves the assembly of microbial communities [64, 65]. Yet, the mechanisms underlying how these silage bacterial communities form and develop are largely uncertain. Exploring the assembly processes of silage microbial communities can begin to offer an explanation behind the changes in microbial diversity. In their analysis of the oat silage microbial community assembly mechanism, Dong et al. [24] applied the null model. The results showed that stochastic and deterministic processes alike were present in silage microorganismal assembly, with stochastic processes dominating early on in fermentation and deterministic processes dominating later. Here, we found that both the E-cluster and L-cluster communities were mainly characterized by ecological drift, while the P-cluster community assembly process was primarily dispersal limitation. Our data showed that the silage microbial communities were dominated by stochastic processes in all three clusters. Interestingly, the greater absolute MNTD value observed in the P-cluster reflected a higher proportion of deterministic processes in this group, thus indicating that stochastic and deterministic processes importantly influence the silage fermentation process. These findings are, to some extent, consistent with those of Dong et al. [24]. Wang et al. [66] also proposed that microbial community assembly is governed by stochastic processes overall, but at the same time biofilters, heterogeneous selection, dispersal limitation and homogeneous selection play important roles during different stages of fermentation. Conversely, ecological drift and dispersal limitation were the dominant factors in the current study. These discrepancies may be attributed to the use of different microbial diversity indices. Xun et al. [67] indicates that, as soil bacterial species richness and functional diversity lowers, bacterial community assembly mechanisms shift from stochastic to more deterministic. In summary, the role of key silage microorganisms and the contribution of their diversity to community assembly still warrants additional exploration.

Here, we preliminarily identified and characterized clusters of silage microorganisms, enterotypes if you will, in terms of their fermentation quality and microbial ecology. Some areas that will require continued attention in future research include: clustering, fermentation quality and microbial composition in larger-scale silage microbial samples. Applying metagenomic sequencing to investigate microbial species-level clustering and metabolic functions. Core microbial community composition in different silage clusters and functional validation based on synthetic microbial communities. Clarifying these scientific problems will help us deepen our understanding of the silage fermentation process and exert control over quality of silage in a precise way.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the silage microbiome can be categorized into three enterotype-like clusters, each exhibiting distinct fermentation quality, microbial composition, functional potential, network topology, and assembly mechanisms. These insights advance our understanding of silage microbial ecology and provide a foundational framework for developing precision strategies to regulate silage quality (Figs. 6, 7).
