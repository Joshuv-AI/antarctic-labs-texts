Changes in effectiveness and safety in patients with Lennox–Gastaut syndrome transitioning from the fenfluramine randomized controlled trial to open‐label extension study
Authors: Rima Nabbout, Orrin Devinsky, Lieven Lagae, Ingrid E. Scheffer, Renzo Guerrini, Joseph Sullivan, Antonio Gil‐Nagel, Sameer M. Zuberi, Kate Riney, Patrick Healy, Jayne Abraham, Rebecca Zhang Roper, Mélanie Langlois, Amélie Lothe, Kelly G. Knupp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the phase 3 randomized controlled trial (RCT; NCT03355209) of fenfluramine in Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS), patients in fenfluramine treatment groups (0.2 mg/kg/day, 0.7 mg/kg/day) experienced greater reduction from baseline in frequency of seizures associated with a fall versus placebo, which was sustained in the open‐label extension (OLE) study (NCT03355209). In this post hoc analysis, trajectories of fenfluramine effectiveness and safety, along with dose changes over time, are described for patients with LGS randomized to placebo in RCT who switched to fenfluramine in OLE (PBO‐FFA) and those who received fenfluramine in both RCT/OLE (FFA‐FFA). Among patients who completed 12 months in OLE (N = 151), numerical improvements in effectiveness outcomes were seen in the PBO‐FFA group (n = 59) after initiating fenfluramine and were similar to those in the FFA‐FFA group (n = 92). Regression to the mean was not observed in the PBO‐FFA group, suggesting that changes were due to fenfluramine. Incidence of the most commonly reported treatment‐emergent adverse events increased in the PBO‐FFA group after fenfluramine initiation but decreased in the FFA‐FFA group in OLE. These data demonstrate rapid improvement in seizure frequency and global functioning in both groups with continued clinical improvement as the mean fenfluramine dose was increased. These results confirm that sustained fenfluramine treatment is effective and tolerable.Plain Language SummaryThis study assessed the change over time in the number of seizures, overall improvement, and side effects in patients with LGS receiving placebo (no active medicine) or fenfluramine in a 14‐week study; all patients later received fenfluramine in the extension study. Overall, the number of seizures (associated with a fall) decreased once patients initially receiving placebo changed to fenfluramine (optimal effect around Month 4 while receiving a higher dose), but as expected, common side effects were reported more frequently once patients began fenfluramine treatment. Patients, parents, and doctors should be aware of this time course to allow fenfluramine enough time to work.

INTRODUCTION

Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) with a heterogenous etiology and is associated with pharmacoresistant seizures of multiple types, specific electroencephalographic characteristics, developmental delay, and cognitive and behavioral deficits. 1 , 2 Onset is typically between 18 months and 8 years old, but a broad range of symptoms, including seizures, persist into adolescence and adulthood, 2 causing a detrimental impact to patient, caregiver, and family quality of life. 3 Seizure management involves polytherapy: valproate as first‐line, then adjunctive therapies, including but not limited to lamotrigine, rufinamide, clobazam, cannabidiol, and fenfluramine. 4

Fenfluramine oral solution is approved for treatment of seizures associated with LGS in patients ≥2 years old in the US 5 and as add‐on treatment for management of seizures in many other countries. 6 , 7 , 8 Fenfluramine differs from other treatments to manage seizures in DEEs in its multimodal mechanism involving serotonin release and signaling at multiple serotonin and sigma‐1 receptors. 9 In a phase 3, 14‐week randomized controlled trial (RCT; ZX008‐1601/EP0214 Part 1, NCT03355209), 187 pediatric (2 to <18 years) and 76 adult (≥18 years) patients with LGS (median of 7 prior antiseizure medications [ASMs]) were randomized to fenfluramine or placebo. 10 Adjunctive fenfluramine 0.7 mg/kg/day significantly reduced the frequency of seizures associated with a fall (previously referred to as “drop seizure”) by a median 26.5% versus 7.6% in the placebo group (p = 0.001); this effect, as well as global functioning improvement, was sustained in the open‐label extension study (OLE; ZX008‐1601/EP0214 Part 2, NCT03355209). 11 Treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) reported in ≥10% of patients in the RCT included decreased appetite, somnolence, fatigue, pyrexia, diarrhea, and vomiting. 10 In the OLE, decreased appetite, fatigue, and pyrexia were also the most commonly reported TEAEs, along with nasopharyngitis and seizure. 11

In this post hoc analysis, we describe changes in effectiveness and safety in pediatric and adult patients with LGS who transitioned from RCT to OLE and the impact of fenfluramine dose changes. We aimed to examine changes in seizure frequency and global functioning in patients who received placebo in the RCT and initiated fenfluramine in the OLE. Long‐term safety/tolerability and effectiveness of fenfluramine are further described in patients who received fenfluramine in both RCT and OLE.

METHODS

The RCT and its OLE, published previously, 10 , 11 complied with current International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and were approved by applicable regulatory authorities and independent ethics committee/institutional review board. All patients or legal representatives provided signed informed consent before enrollment and assent where applicable.

Patients with a clinical LGS diagnosis (aged 2–35 years at RCT entry) enrolled in the 14‐week RCT and were randomized to receive fenfluramine (0.7 or 0.2 mg/kg/day; maximum daily dose, 26 mg/day) or placebo (Figure S1). Patients could then continue onto the OLE; all patients transitioned to fenfluramine 0.2 mg/kg/day at OLE start. Patients were included in this analysis if they completed ≥12 months of fenfluramine treatment in the OLE. Groups were categorized as follows: PBO‐FFA (patients who received placebo in RCT and transitioned to fenfluramine in OLE) or FFA‐FFA (combined dose group of patients who received fenfluramine [either dose] in RCT and continued flexibly dosed fenfluramine in OLE).

Effectiveness outcomes are reported for patients in PBO‐FFA and FFA‐FFA groups in the modified intent‐to‐treat (mITT) population who completed ≥12 months of OLE; mITT population comprised all patients who received ≥1 fenfluramine dose, had a valid pre‐RCT baseline (hereinafter referred to as “baseline”) estimated frequency of seizures associated with a fall, and provided ≥30 days of valid seizure data during the OLE. Trajectories of the following effectiveness outcomes are reported: median percentage change from baseline in frequency of seizures associated with a fall; mean change from baseline in seizure‐free days (SFDs) for seizures associated with a fall; and global functioning as measured by ratings of clinically meaningful improvement (‘much improved’ or ‘very much improved’) on the Clinical Global Impression–Improvement (CGI–I) scale relative to baseline by parent/caregiver and investigator. Safety was assessed in all patients who received ≥1 fenfluramine dose (safety population); for this analysis, the incidence of most commonly reported TEAEs (≥10% of patients) in both RCT/OLE is described.

Descriptive statistics were used. Adverse events were coded using MedDRA (applicable versions based on the time of the study 10 , 11 ).

RESULTS

Of 263 patients enrolled in the RCT, 247 (93.9%) patients continued into the OLE. Of those, 151 (61.1%) patients completed 12 months of the OLE and comprised both mITT and safety populations for this analysis (59 [PBO‐FFA group]; 92 [FFA‐FFA group]).

Most baseline characteristics were consistent between the groups (Table 1). Median baseline frequency of seizures associated with a fall/28 days was higher in the FFA‐FFA group (82 [range, 4–2943]) than in the PBO‐FFA group (62 [range, 4–1761]). Median number of concomitant ASMs while in the OLE was 3 for both groups (PBO‐FFA range [1–5] and FFA‐FFA range [1–6]).

Effectiveness

In both study groups, the mean fenfluramine daily dose increased steadily from 0.2 mg/kg/day (at OLE Month 1) to 0.5 mg/kg/day and remained stable from Month 4 through Month 12 of the OLE (Figure 1).

At OLE Month 1 (all patients received fenfluramine 0.2 mg/kg/day), the median percentage change in seizures associated with a fall compared with baseline was −32.1% in the PBO‐FFA group and −48.5% in the FFA‐FFA group (Figure 1A). In the PBO‐FFA group, the median percentage change in seizures associated with a fall continued to improve: −37.8% at Month 2 and −48.2 over Months 4–6. In the FFA‐FFA group, the median percentage change was sustained: −46.0% and −44.2% at Month 2 and Months 4–6, respectively (Figure 1A).

At OLE Month 1, a mean 10.3 SFDs was reported for patients in both study groups (Figure 1B); this was a mean (SD) change from baseline of 4.8 (7.8) and 4.3 (6.8) days for the PBO‐FFA and FFA‐FFA groups, respectively. Over Months 4–6, the mean (SD) change from baseline in SFDs was 6.2 (7.2) in the PBO‐FFA group and 5.1 (7.1) in the FFA‐FFA group.

For patients in the PBO‐FFA group, during Month 1 of OLE, 16/52 (30.8%) and 14/51 (27.5%) patients were rated as demonstrating clinically meaningful improvement on CGI–I by parent/caregiver and investigator, respectively; by Month 12, those proportions increased to 30/57 (52.6%) and 25/54 (46.3%) (Figure 1C,D). In the FFA‐FFA group, at Month 1 of OLE, 40/90 (44.4%) and 31/89 (34.8%) patients demonstrated clinically meaningful improvement as rated by parent/caregiver and investigator, respectively; similar to the PBO‐FFA group, the proportion of patients with those ratings increased to 46/92 (50.0%) and 46/91 (50.5%) at Month 12 (Figure 1C,D).

Safety

In the 14‐week RCT, ≥1 TEAE was reported in 45/59 (76.3%) patients in the PBO‐FFA group and 76/92 (82.6%) patients in the FFA‐FFA group. During the OLE, ≥1 TEAE was reported in 51/59 (86.4%) patients in the PBO‐FFA group and 81/92 (88.0%) patients in the FFA‐FFA group. The changes from RCT to OLE in the incidence of TEAEs reported in ≥10% of patients in either study are displayed by OLE study group in Figure 2. In the PBO‐FFA group, the incidence of TEAEs reported by ≥10% of patients increased once patients initiated fenfluramine in the OLE (Figure 2A). For patients receiving fenfluramine in both the RCT and OLE, the incidence in 4 of the 6 commonly reported TEAEs (decreased appetite, somnolence, fatigue, diarrhea) was lower in the OLE portion (Figure 2B).

DISCUSSION

In this post hoc analysis, effectiveness and safety trajectories among 151 pediatric and adult patients who completed 12 months in the OLE portion of the LGS phase 3 study were evaluated. Numerical improvements in all effectiveness outcomes were rapidly observed after patients initially randomized to placebo started fenfluramine in the OLE, and effectiveness continued to improve as fenfluramine doses steadily increased. Additionally, the incidence of the most commonly reported TEAEs decreased over time with continued fenfluramine use.

The minimal change in seizure frequency in PBO‐RCT patients during the RCT highlights that 4‐week baseline period provided an accurate depiction of seizure frequency. Additionally, regression to the mean, or natural variation in seizure frequency that can be mistaken for treatment response, was not observed in the PBO‐FFA group during the 14‐week RCT. Once fenfluramine was initiated at 0.2 mg/kg/day in the PBO‐FFA group, improvement in seizure reduction was observed as early as Month 1. Further reductions were observed in subsequent months consistent with those noted in patients in the FFA‐FFA group. Mean change in SFDs in the OLE was numerically greater in the PBO‐FFA group versus FFA‐FFA potentially due to the difference in baseline seizure frequency. Seizure effectiveness aligned with the gradual dose increase to a stable mean fenfluramine dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day. As described in published reports, after fenfluramine initiation, experts suggest gradually increasing the dose by 0.1 mg/kg/day every week or by 0.2 mg/kg/day every 2 weeks until fenfluramine 0.4 mg/kg/day is achieved; additional increases can be planned based on patient response. 12 Weekly titrations allows a target to be achieved by Week 6–8, 13 which is quicker than was done in the OLE. Alternatively, a “start low, go slow” approach may be useful for minimizing adverse events. 13 It is important to recognize that the maximally effective and tolerated dose should be targeted.

In many patients (especially pediatric patients weighing <37.5 kg), fenfluramine at 0.5 mg/kg/day still allows for up‐titration (max: 0.7 mg/kg/day or 26 mg/day) and with minimal risk of drug–drug interactions. Fenfluramine is not associated with as many CYP450‐related drug–drug interactions as other medications to manage seizures in DEEs and can generally be added to LGS treatment regimens without dose adjustments. 13 , 14 , 15 Fenfluramine dose adjustments may be needed when co‐administered with strong CYP1A2 or CYP2D6 inhibitors and CYP1A2, CYP2B6, or CYP3A4 inducers, although many of these are not typically prescribed for LGS management. Caution and monitoring for serotonin syndrome is advised if concomitant use of serotonergic agents is needed for treatment of psychiatric and behavioral symptoms. 5 , 16 Unlike fenfluramine, other therapies (eg, valproate, cannabidiol) act as CYP enzyme inhibitors and/or inducers, potentially necessitating treatment modifications when used concomitantly with other medications. 16

Effective LGS management includes addressing non‐seizure outcomes, as these also contribute to patient and family quality of life. 17 Benefits beyond seizure effectiveness, as demonstrated by patient global functioning (CGI–I), were observed as soon as patients in the PBO‐RCT group began receiving fenfluramine. At OLE Month 12, ≥40% of patients in both groups were rated as demonstrating clinically meaningful improvement by parent/caregiver and investigator.

In this analysis, we presented changes in the most commonly reported TEAEs from the phase 3 studies. As expected, when patients in the PBO‐FFA group transitioned to fenfluramine in the OLE, a higher incidence of many of the most common TEAEs (Figure 2A) was reported versus the incidence in the OLE in the FFA‐FFA group (Figure 2B). Many medications used to manage seizures in LGS can affect appetite and have gastrointestinal and sedating side effects. 18 , 19 , 20 Due to the serotonergic part of its mechanism, fenfluramine is associated with decreased appetite; results of this analysis show decreased appetite was reported in 29% and 24% of patients when fenfluramine was first initiated (FFA‐FFA in RCT and PBO‐FFA groups in OLE, respectively). Data from clinical trials and real‐world studies in LGS, as well as in Dravet syndrome, however, highlight that in the vast majority of patients, decreased appetite does not lead to fenfluramine discontinuation and it resolves over time without necessarily translating to weight loss. 10 , 13 , 21 , 22 , 23 Similarly, while some patients receiving fenfluramine may report somnolence or fatigue, improvement in overall sleep quality with fenfluramine treatment has also been reported. 13 It is reassuring that incidence of decreased appetite, somnolence, fatigue, and diarrhea among patients in the FFA‐FFA group decreased in the OLE, suggesting an acceptable long‐term tolerability. The only TEAEs that were higher in the FFA‐FFA group in the OLE portion were those associated with illness (pyrexia, nasopharyngitis) and were likely incidental findings.

LIMITATIONS

Patients who received fenfluramine ≥12 months in the OLE were included in this analysis; those who discontinued the study prior to OLE Month 12 were not considered. Due to the open‐label design, titrations were not standardized and may have been different from clinical practice. This study did not account for the influence of concomitant ASMs on effectiveness or safety.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE

In this post hoc analysis, data for patients who completed 12 months of the LGS OLE demonstrated the time course of fenfluramine effectiveness and tolerability. Onset of effectiveness was rapid (within 1 month), and continued clinical improvement aligned with increasing fenfluramine doses. Fenfluramine was well tolerated, with a decrease in incidence of commonly reported TEAEs with continued use. These results emphasize advantages of sustained use, adequate dose titration, and underscore benefits that extend beyond seizure reduction. In clinical practice, assessment of fenfluramine effectiveness should extend to ≥4 months of treatment if possible. These results provide practical guidance on time course of long‐term effective and tolerable fenfluramine treatment in patients with LGS.
