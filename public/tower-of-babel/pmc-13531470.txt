Estradiol mitigates lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and MAPKs activation in NASH rat model with bilateral orchidectomy
Authors: Fatist Okrit, Maneerat Chayanupatkul, Natcha Wanpiyarat, Prasong Siriviriyakul, Duangporn Werawatganon, Brent Stockwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) is characterized by hepatocyte injury and lobular inflammation driven by lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Low testosterone levels are associated with increased steatosis risk, particularly in transgender women undergoing feminization. To investigate the effects of estradiol supplementation and its mechanisms involving lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling (ERK, JNK, and p38) in testosterone-deficient rats fed a high-fat, high-fructose (HFHF) diet. Twenty-one male Sprague-Dawley rats were randomized into three groups (n = 7/group): (i) control (standard diet), (ii) ORX + HFHF (bilateral orchidectomy plus HFHF diet), and (iii) ORX + HFHF + E2 (ORX + HFHF plus oral estradiol, 1.6 mg/kg/day). After 6 weeks, the ORX + HFHF group exhibited the greatest hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, NAS scores, and Oil Red O staining. Lipid accumulation markers (PLIN2 and Foxa1), oxidative stress markers (MDA, 4-hydroxynonenal, HO-1, and iNOS), and Nrf2 expression were significantly increased versus controls. Testosterone deficiency combined with HFHF feeding induced apoptosis primarily through the intrinsic pathway, evidenced by increased Bax, Bcl-2, caspase-3, and caspase-9, while caspase-8 showed an increasing trend. Estradiol supplementation significantly attenuated NASH pathology by reducing lipogenesis, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, with these effects strongly associated with suppression of all MAPK subtypes. Estradiol mitigates NASH progression in testosterone-deficient rats by modulating lipid metabolism, restoring antioxidant defenses, suppressing intrinsic apoptosis, and inhibiting MAPK signaling. These findings provide mechanistic insight into hormone therapy in transgender women and identify GPER/MAPK signaling as a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: estradiol supplementation, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, bilateral orchiectomy, high-fat and high-fructose diet

Transgender women face an increased risk of severe nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), a condition exacerbated by testosterone deficiency and high-fat high-fructose diet. While estradiol supplementation is a vital feminizing treatment, its precise impact on NASH progression remains unclear. This study investigated the underlying mechanisms of NASH in transgender women using an animal model with a specific focus on lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and the related mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. Our findings demonstrated that estradiol improved NASH by inhibiting lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and MAPK activation, providing critical mechanistic insights for safe and effective hormone therapy in this specific population.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) is a rising global health priority, with expected prevalence rates of 18.90% (males) and 18.41% (females) by 2030. The condition is defined by its clinical evolution from isolated steatosis to hepatic inflammation, which can culminate in advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis (1). As the prevalence of male-to-female gender transition increases, bilateral orchidectomy (ORX) is becoming a more frequent surgical procedure. The resulting testosterone deficiency may exacerbate the progression of NASH by impairing metabolic homeostasis and intensifying oxidative stress and apoptosis (2, 3). While the consumption of a high-fat, high-fructose (HFHF) diet is a primary driver of metabolic syndrome, emerging evidence suggests that the endocrine environment, specifically testosterone deficiency, plays a synergistic role in exacerbating liver injury and promoting the progression to NASH (4, 5). This intersection is especially pertinent to transgender women, a population that frequently undergoes feminizing transition characterized by profound testosterone deprivation, even in the absence of exogenous estradiol supplementation. Despite its clinical significance, a critical gap remains in in vivo research evaluating the risk of NASH in the transgender female population. Specifically, this investigation is required to determine how estradiol treatment influences hepatic health within the physiological contexts of transgender women and to establish effective therapeutic strategies.

Estrogens, particularly 17β-estradiol, are critical regulators of hepatic metabolism and health, mediating their effects through nuclear receptors (ERα and ERβ) and the membrane-bound G-protein estrogen receptor (GPER) (6). To replicate established antiobesity effects, the present study utilized 1.6 mg/kg of oral estradiol. This dosage was chosen not only for its proven performance in estrogen-deprived models but also to simulate the physiological exposure of standard human oral therapy (typically 1–2 mg daily) (7). This hormone modulates lipid homeostasis by suppressing lipogenesis and promoting fatty acid oxidation, while simultaneously attenuating hepatic inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis (8, 9). Previous research indicates that estrogen replacement has been shown to attenuate fatty acid synthesis and mitigate steatosis specifically within liver zone 3 (the pericentral region) via an ERα dependent signaling pathway (10). Thus, estrogens appear to have a hepatoprotective effect, highlighting the importance of considering sex-based differences in the development and treatment of NASH.

Emerging evidence highlights that hormonal dysregulation significantly influences hepatic metabolic remodeling. Most experimental studies have focused either on diet-induced NASH or testosterone deficiency models separately; however, little is known about the synergistic impact of dietary stress and testosterone deficiency. While estrogen signaling has been shown to modulate hepatic lipid metabolism and inflammation, its mechanistic interaction with MAPK pathways and intrinsic apoptotic machinery under testosterone-deficient conditions remains poorly characterized. Therefore, a critical knowledge gap exists regarding how estradiol therapy affects NASH progression in a hormonal context that resembles transgender women.

Current literature indicates that the “double hit” of dietary stress and testosterone deficiency triggers complex molecular cascades. Regarding lipid dysregulation, perilipin 2 (PLIN2) plays a role in coating intracellular lipid droplets and promoting fat storage (11). Epigenetic modification of fork head box A1 (Foxa1) normally inhibit triglyceride synthesis and stimulates fatty acid oxidation. In NASH, Foxa1 expression is typically reduced, facilitating lipid accumulation (12). The potential interaction between Foxa1 and the storage-driven PLIN2 remains a key area for investigating how testosterone deficiency accelerates lipid imbalance. Moreover, the major end-products of lipid peroxidation, such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), malondialdehyde (MDA), alongside the protective antioxidant factors, including heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), exert context-dependent roles that maintain hepatic homeostasis through the modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways (13–15). The loss of testosterone in transgender women (modeled experimentally via orchidectomy and given a HFHF diet) exacerbates lipotoxicity-induced liver injury. However, the precise mechanisms governing the initiation of apoptosis, specifically the synergistic crosstalk between mitochondrial-dependent and death-receptor-mediated pathways remains incompletely understood. Thus, the alterations in pro- and antiapoptotic factors (Bax and Bcl-2), as well as the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways (caspase-3, -8, and -9), may be implicated in this setting (16, 17). Central to this pathogenesis is the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. Activation of MAPK subtypes, such as extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK), c-Jun N-terminal protein kinase (JNK), and p38, serves as a critical mediator linking dysregulated lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and apoptosis (18, 19).

Therefore, our work aimed to establish an in vivo model that recapitulates the physiological context of transgender women by combining testosterone deficiency with a HFHF diet, reflecting contemporary human dietary patterns. We specifically examined lipid metabolism (PLIN2 and Foxa1), oxidative stress markers (MDA, 4-HNE, and the Nrf2/HO-1 axis), intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways (Bax, Bcl-2, and caspases), and MAPK signaling (ERK, JNK, and p38). By elucidating the mechanistic interplay between estradiol signaling, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and MAPK activation, this study seeks to provide foundational insight into liver health in testosterone-deficient states and to inform safer hormone therapy strategies for transgender women and other androgen-deficient populations.

Results

Overview of rats’ general appearance, body weight, food intake, and seminal vesicle weight changes

We evaluated the impact of testosterone deficiency, a HFHF diet, and estradiol supplementation on body weight (BW) changes and food intake (Fig. 1A and B). After 6 weeks, the control group, which received a standard diet ad libitum, showed the greatest increase in BW. Notably, the ORX + HFHF group exhibited a higher BW compared to the ORX + HFHF + E2 group, a difference that correlated with food intake levels. We observed no significant changes in general appearances between groups, though the ORX + HFHF group showed slightly inferior hair quality compared to both control and ORX + HFHF + E2 groups (Fig. 1C). To assess BW changes, the delta BW (ΔBW) was calculated for each rat as the difference between the BW recorded at the end and the start of the experiment (ΔBW = Bwend − BWstart). We found that the delta body weights in the ORX + HFHF group were significantly lower than those in the control group (78.4 ± 27.01 vs. 272.7 ± 22.60, P < 0.05). Furthermore, estradiol supplementation resulted in an even greater reduction in the delta body weights compared to both the control and ORX + HFHF groups (3.7 ± 16.57 vs. 272.7 ± 22.60 vs. 78.4 ± 27.01, respectively, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D).

A marked reduction in seminal vesicle weight and subsequent atrophy served as hallmark indicators of complete bilateral orchidectomy and a testosterone-deficient state. Normal seminal vesicle morphology was observed in the control group. However, bilateral orchidectomy caused significant atrophy of the seminal vesicles in both the ORX + HFHF and ORX + HFHF + E2 groups compared to the control group (seminal vesicle weight; 0.06 ± 0.005 vs. 0.08 ± 0.009 vs. 1.14 ± 0.11, respectively, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E).

The gross liver appearance in the control group showed a normal reddish color, while livers from the ORX + HFHF group were enlarged with a pale yellowish color, indicating hepatic steatosis. Estradiol supplementation significantly improved overall liver morphology compared to the ORX + HFHF group (Fig. 2A).

As a transmembrane estrogen receptor, G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) is involved in regulating multiple biological functions. When bound to estradiol, it rapidly triggers estrogen signaling by activating G-proteins (Gαs and Gαi/o), which subsequently launch intracellular cascades, including the MAPK and ERK1/2 pathways (20). In this study, we found that GPER gene expression was significantly increased in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to both the control and ORX + HFHF groups (0.014 ± 0.002 vs. 0.007 ± 0.004 vs. 0.008 ± 0.002, respectively, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2F). However, analysis of Esr1 and Esr2 gene expression did not reveal statistically significant differences among the experimental groups. Thus, activation of GPER appears to be a key regulatory mechanism for estradiol binding in the estradiol-treated group.

Estradiol supplementation decreased hepatic lipid accumulation and silenced the epigenetic modification of Foxa1 in a NASH rat model induced by bilateral orchidectomy plus HFHF diet

This study focused on quantifying lipid accumulation within hepatocytes using two established methods: Oil Red O (ORO) staining and western blot analysis (measuring the levels of PLIN2 and Foxa1). ORO solution was used for the specific visualization of both macro- and microvesicular lipid droplets. Since PLIN2 is the predominant protein coating the surface of lipid droplets in hepatocytes, its expression was also determined as an indicator of lipid deposition. As shown in Fig. 3A, ORO staining revealed extensive deposition of lipid droplets in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group. Conversely, treatment with estradiol markedly reduced the accumulation of lipid droplets in hepatocytes when compared to the ORX + HFHF group. Quantitatively, the ORX + HFHF group exhibited the highest percentage of ORO-positive staining area compared to the control group (53.40 ± 2.72 vs. 2.76 ± 0.90). After treatment with estradiol, the percentage of positive area of ORO staining significantly decreased in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared with the ORX + HFHF group (2.69 ± 1.24 vs. 53.40 ± 2.72).

Consistent with the ORO staining results, the relative protein expression levels of PLIN2 were significantly higher in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (0.50 ± 0.17 vs. 0.14 ± 0.04). Importantly, estradiol supplementation effectively reversed this effect, resulting in a significant decrease in PLIN2 protein expression in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group when compared to the ORX + HFHF group (0.27 ± 0.02 vs. 0.50 ± 0.17) (Fig. 3B). Foxa1 acts as a critical regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism, offering a protective defense against liver injury and pathological steatosis. Interestingly, we found that Foxa1 protein expression was significantly upregulated in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group. When treated with estradiol, Foxa1 protein expression was significantly downregulated in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group as compared to the ORX + HFHF group (Fig. 3C). Original images of PLIN2 and Foxa1 western blot results were provided in Figs. S3 and S4.

Estradiol supplementation modulated hepatic oxidative stress and antioxidant balance in a NASH rat model induced by bilateral orchidectomy plus HFHF diet

The ORX + HFHF group had significantly increased hepatic levels of MDA compared to the control group (0.47 ± 0.10 vs. 0.24 ± 0.08 nmol/mg protein, P < 0.05), indicating elevated oxidative stress. Estradiol treatment significantly reduced hepatic MDA levels compared to the ORX + HFHF group (0.34 ± 0.07 vs. 0.47 ± 0.10 nmol/mg protein, P < 0.05). Furthermore, hepatic glutathione (GSH) levels showed a decreasing trend in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (2.71 ± 0.51 vs. 3.48 ± 1.07 nmol/mg protein, P > 0.05). Treatment with estradiol elevated hepatic GSH levels in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (5.91 ± 2.44 vs. 2.71 ± 0.51 nmol/mg protein, P < 0.05. Protein expression of oxidative stress (4-HNE, iNOS) and antioxidative markers (Nrf2, HO-1) was evaluated. 4-HNE protein expression was significantly elevated in the livers of the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (2.90 ± 0.43 vs. 1.22 ± 0.19, P < 0.05). Estradiol treatment effectively reduced hepatic 4-HNE expression in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (2.04 ± 0.64 vs. 2.90 ± 0.43; P < 0.05). Nevertheless, the 4-HNE levels in the estradiol-treated group remained significantly higher than those in the control group (2.04 ± 0.64 vs. 1.22 ± 0.19, P < 0.05). iNOS expression was higher in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (5.08 ± 3.04 vs. 2.23 ± 1.17; P > 0.05) and lower in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group, although this did not reach statistical significance (2.58 ± 0.85 vs. 5.08 ± 3.04; P > 0.05). Analysis of antioxidant protein expression revealed a consistent pattern for both Nrf2 and HO-1. Nrf2 and HO-1 protein expressions were significantly upregulated in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (Nrf2; 0.016 ± 0.009 vs. 0.002 ± 0.002, and HO-1; 0.88 ± 0.39 vs. 0.45 ± 0.02, respectively; P < 0.05). Original images of 4-HNE, Nrf2, HO-1, and iNOS western blot results were provided in Figs. S5–S8, respectively.

Moreover, we measured the mRNA expression levels of key antioxidants (Fig. 4H–L). Both Nrf2 and heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) gene expression in the liver tissue were significantly increased in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (Nrf2; 0.16 ± 0.07 vs. 0.05 ± 0.01, and HMOX1; 0.18 ± 0.03 vs. 0.10 ± 0.03, respectively; P < 0.05). Estradiol supplementation nonsignificantly lowered the levels of both Nrf2 and HMOX1 gene expression as compared to the ORX + HFHF group (Nrf2; 0.11 ± 0.03 vs. 0.16 ± 0.07, and HMOX1; 0.13 ± 0.01 vs. 0.18 ± 0.03, respectively; P > 0.05). Conversely, a significant decrease in superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1), and glutathione S-transferase 1 (GSTA1) gene expression was observed in the ORX + HFHF group as compared with the control group (SOD1; 1.63 ± 0.27 vs. 3.68 ± 0.88, GPX1; 0.20 ± 0.04 vs. 0.46 ± 0.04, and GSTA1; 3.20 ± 0.34 vs. 5.81 ± 1.10, respectively; P < 0.05). Estradiol supplementation significantly increased the levels of SOD1, GPX1, and GSTA1 gene expression compared to the ORX + HFFH group (SOD1; 4.09 ± 1.43 vs. 1.63 ± 0.27, GPX1; 0.43 ± 0.13 vs. 0.20 ± 0.04, and GSTA1; 7.28 ± 1.85 vs. 3.20 ± 0.34, respectively; P = 0.05).

To corroborate these findings, the expression and localization of Nrf2, iNOS, and HO-1 in rat liver tissue were further evaluated using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. We observed a distinct cellular localization pattern for these markers. Both Nrf2 and iNOS were primarily localized to damaged, lipid-laden hepatocytes (steatotic cells), while HO-1 expression was specifically detected in Kupffer cells surrounding the sinusoidal space. This finding confirms that these oxidative stress and antioxidant markers are activated in a cell-type specific manner within the disease state. Positive staining was quantified as the ratio of positive pixels to the total number of pixels within the analyzed area. We found a similar pattern of expression of Nrf2, iNOS, and HO-1. The ORX + HFHF group exhibited a significant increase in Nrf2, iNOS, and HO-1 positivity compared to the control group (Nrf2; 0.15 ± 0.01 vs. 0.03 ± 0.01, iNOS; 0.18 ± 0.01 vs. 0.04 ± 0.01, HO-1; 0.58 ± 0.02 vs. 0.20 ± 0.06, respectively; P < 0.05). In contrast, a significant reduction in Nrf2, iNOS, and HO-1 positive staining areas was observed in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared with the ORX + HFHF group (Nrf2; 0.05 ± 0.01 vs. 0.15 ± 0.01, iNOS; 0.05 ± 0.01 vs. 0.18 ± 0.01, HO-1; 0.25 ± 0.04 vs. 0.58 ± 0.02, respectively; P < 0.05) (Fig. 4M–P).

Estradiol supplementation modulated extrinsic and intrinsic apoptosis pathways in a NASH rat model induced by bilateral orchidectomy plus HFHF diet

Apoptotic markers were evaluated using western blotting, IHC, and real time PCR quantification of mRNA expression (Fig. 5A and B). This study demonstrated that the levels of caspase-9 protein expression were significantly elevated in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (0.64 ± 0.26 vs. 0.23 ± 0.05, P < 0.05). Treatment with estradiol significantly reduced caspase-9 protein expression in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (0.31 ± 0.18 vs. 0.64 ± 0.26, P < 0.05). Corroborating these findings, IHC analysis revealed that the degree of positive brown staining for caspase-9 was significantly higher in the ORX + HFHF group compared with the control group (0.70 ± 0.04 vs. 0.22 ± 0.13, P < 0.05). Likewise, estradiol supplementation significantly decreased caspase-9 positivity in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (0.50 ± 0.07 vs. 0.70 ± 0.04, P < 0.05), albeit the levels in the treated group remained significantly higher than those observed in the control group (0.50 ± 0.07 vs. 0.22 ± 0.13, P < 0.05). Gene expression analysis confirmed the detection of mRNA for key regulators of apoptosis, specifically Bax, Bcl-2, and caspases-3, -8, and -9, covering both the intrinsic and extrinsic signaling cascades. We demonstrated that three major proapoptotic related genes such as Bax, caspase-9 and caspase-3 were significantly upregulated in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (Bax; 0.006 ± 0.004 vs. 0.001 ± 0.001, caspase-9; 0.043 ± 0.023 vs. 0.011 ± 0.004, caspase-3; 0.076 ± 0.012 vs. 0.035 ± 0.010, respectively; P < 0.05). After estradiol supplementation, Bax, caspase-9, and caspase-3 mRNA expression were significantly downregulated in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (Bax; 0.002 ± 0.002 vs. 0.006 ± 0.004, caspase-9; 0.015 ± 0.003 vs. 0.043 ± 0.023, caspase-3; 0.066 ± 0.005 vs. 0.076 ± 0.012, respectively; P < 0.05). The expression of antiapoptotic gene, Blc-2 was significantly reduced in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (0.004 ± 0.001 vs. 0.010 ± 0.004, P = 0.05). On the other hand, estradiol supplementation restored the expression levels of Blc-2 in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (0.013 ± 0.001 vs. 0.004 ± 0.001, P < 0.05). Additionally, caspase-8 mRNA expression did not differ significantly among the experimental groups. Nevertheless, a nonsignificant increase was noted between the ORX + HFHF and control groups, and a trend toward reduction was observed in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group.

DNA fragmentation and resultant cell death were assessed and quantified in rat liver tissues using the well-established TUNEL assay. Hepatocyte apoptosis was confirmed by the presence of TUNEL-positive nuclei exhibiting dark brown staining. The number of TUNEL-positive cells was highest in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (16.83 ± 0.64 vs. 0.22 ± 0.10, P < 0.05). Estradiol supplementation significantly reduced the severity of hepatocyte apoptosis, as evidenced by the marked decrease in TUNEL positivity in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF group (3.02 ± 1.79 vs. 16.83 ± 0.64, P < 0.05). However, the number of TUNEL-positive cells remained significantly higher in the estradiol-treated group than in the control group than in (3.02 ± 1.79 vs. 0.22 ± 0.10, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5I and J).

Estradiol supplementation attenuated hepatic MAPK activation in a NASH rat model induced by bilateral orchidectomy plus HFHF diet

The MAPK signaling pathway, known for its role in regulating cellular survival, is hypothesized to be involved in the development and progression of NASH. This study examined the involvement and signaling configuration of the three key MAPK subtypes (ERK, JNK, and p38) within the context of testosterone deficiency and HFHF diet-induced NASH in a rat model. The protein levels of all three MAPK subtypes significantly increased in the ORX + HFHF group compared to the control group (ERK; 4.24 ± 1.25 vs. 0.031 ± 0.010, JNK; 0.427 ± 0.162 vs. 0.093 ± 0.045, p38; 1.570 ± 0.660 vs. 0.344 ± 0.130, respectively; P < 0.05). After treatment with estradiol, the protein levels of ERK and p38 were significantly decreased in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared with the ORX + HFHF group (ERK; 0.017 ± 0.015 vs. 4.240 ± 1.256, p38; 0.064 ± 0.026 vs. 1.570 ± 0.660, respectively; P < 0.05). Although JNK expression decreased in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group compared to the ORX + HFHF, the difference did not reach statistical significance (0.225 ± 0.041 vs. 0.427 ± 0.162, P > 0.05) (Fig. 6A–D). Discussion

This study investigated the effects of estradiol supplementation on PLIN2-coated lipid-droplet dynamics, Foxa1 transcriptional activity, oxidative stress, apoptotic cascades, and MAPK signaling alterations. We evaluated their roles in NASH pathogenesis using a testosterone-deficient rat model fed a HFHF diet to simulate NASH in transgender women. In this study, no significant weight gain was observed in rats fed a HFHF diet. This phenomenon has been documented in a previous in vivo study, which suggested that a HFHF diet may paradoxically lead to diminished body mass compared to a standard diet (21). We hypothesized that our HFHF regimen may cause a methionine deficiency, thereby driving hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation even in the absence of significant weight gain.

Besides its ability to modulate lipid metabolism and inflammation, testosterone has been found to serve many protective functions in the human body; therefore, lower levels of testosterone are associated with several features of metabolic syndrome (22). In combination with a HFHF diet, testosterone deficiency and overconsumption of fructose could produce a more severe form of NASH. In accordance with previous reports, our findings demonstrated that rats in the ORX + HFHF group had the highest degree of hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning and NAS scores. Quantitative analysis via Oil Red O staining revealed significant lipid accumulation in the ORX + HFHF group, which correlated with higher expression levels of the lipid-droplet protein PLIN2 and the metabolic regulator Foxa1 compared to the control group. A previous study reported that PLIN2 has been reported to play a role in promoting steatogenesis via its association with the surface of intracellular lipid droplets. This protein–lipid interaction serves as a primary driver of hepatic steatosis in NASH (23). Conversely, prior studies have suggested that Foxa1 might exert protective effects against NASH development through estrogen receptor-mediated estrogen signaling pathways (12, 24). We found a significant increase in Foxa1 expression in the ORX + HFHF group, followed by a significant decrease in Foxa1 expression in the estradiol-treated group. The upregulation of Foxa1 expression observed in rats with NASH in our study might represent a defensive mechanism aimed at silencing the transcription of lipogenic genes, such as SREBP1c and FAS (25). Therefore, when estradiol supplementation improved hepatic steatosis and inflammation, the requirement for this defense mechanism decreased, thereby reducing Foxa1 expression.

Our model aligned with the multiple-hit hypothesis of NAFLD by increasing hepatic steatosis and rendering the liver more vulnerable to various second hits, including inflammatory cytokines, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and partially activating the MAPK signaling pathway (ERK, JNK, and p38). We hypothesized that these factors could contribute to the development of NASH, and ultimately, fibrosis. Oxidative stress is one of the key processes involved in the progression from simple steatosis to NASH. The balance between oxidative stress (MDA) and antioxidant (GSH) levels was investigated in this study. We found a significant elevation of MDA in the ORX + HFHF group with a trend toward decreased GSH levels compared to the controls. Consistent with our findings, previous research similarly showed elevated MDA and reduced GSH levels in a rodent model of NASH induced by bilateral ovariectomy and HFHF diet (26, 27). The ORX + HFHF group exhibited a significant upregulation of 4-HNE, Nrf2, HO-1, and iNOS protein levels compared to the control group, as confirmed by western blotting. Interestingly, the IHC revealed that ORX + HFHF rats exhibited a significant elevation in Nrf2, iNOS, and HO-1 levels. While Nrf2 and iNOS were localized within the hepatocytes, HO-1 expression was detected specifically in Kupffer cells. These pro-oxidant and inflammatory shift were further supported by a marked increase in the mRNA expression of Nrf2 and HMOX1. The significant upregulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis in the ORX + HFHF group likely represented a compensatory antioxidant response to burgeoning oxidative stress, as indicated by 4-HNE accumulation (28). However, this induction appeared insufficient to neutralize the excessive pro-oxidant load, as evidenced by the concurrent depletion of GSH, SOD1, GPX1 and GSTA1 levels. Cai et al. (3) reported that low testosterone levels triggered liver inflammation and oxidative stress by weakening antioxidant defenses, such as SOD and CAT, leading to hepatic steatosis and impaired liver function. We can infer from these data that in the absence of testosterone, the liver's endogenous protective mechanisms are functionally overwhelmed, facilitating the transition from simple steatosis to severe steatohepatitis.

As expected, when the Nrf2 response could no longer maintain the redox balance, it triggered the intrinsic apoptotic pathway via caspase-9 and caspase-3. In the current study, activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway was increased in the ORX + HFHF group, characterized by the upregulation of Bax, caspase-9, and caspase-3 (at both protein and mRNA levels) and the downregulation of the antiapoptotic marker Bcl-2. However, we did not observe a significant change in caspase-8 expression between the ORX + HFHF group and the control group. Our findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that low testosterone impaired mitochondrial function, resulting in oxidative stress, cytochrome C release, an increased Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, and activation of caspase-3 and -9, leading to apoptosis in the gastric mucosa of orchidectomized rats (29, 30). Thus, the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway observed in ORX + HFHF rats is consistent with the intracellular origin of oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction. Our findings suggested that activation of the extrinsic apoptotic pathway might not play a major role in this NASH model.

MAPKs coordinate cellular processes through dynamic protein interactions that reflect and drive physiological changes. These kinases (ERK, JNK, and p38) play a prominent role in hepatic inflammatory responses under both physiological and diseased conditions (31). By phosphorylating diverse substrates, activated ERK1/2 translates external signals into nuclear responses that govern cell growth, differentiation, inflammation and apoptosis (32, 33). We demonstrated that ERK1/2 protein expression was significantly upregulated in the ORX + HFHF group. A previous in vivo study revealed that disrupting the IL-11 receptor in western diet-fed mice supplemented with fructose in drinking water exacerbated ERK activation, contributing to NASH development (34). On the contrary, although reduced testosterone levels have been linked to the change in the p38 MAPK pathway, ERK1/2 remained unchanged in the testes of aging mice (35). Moreover, in a mouse model of acetaminophen overdose, NAPQI caused mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress that triggered hepatocyte necrosis via the ERK pathway (36). To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the combined impact of testosterone deficiency and a HFHF diet that resembled transgender women with NASH. Based on our results, we hypothesized that testosterone deficiency combined with overnutrition activates the ERK pathway, thereby promoting inflammation and oxidative stress, and the progression toward NASH.

Another MAPK subtype, the JNK cascade has a property in controlling hepatic metabolism, inflammation and apoptosis. Because JNK3 is absent in hepatic tissue, research into liver inflammation primarily examines the roles of JNK1 and JNK2 (37, 38). A previous publication demonstrated that a reduction in M1 tissue macrophages was linked to significantly lower hepatic inflammation in JNK-knockout mice fed a high-fat diet compared to wild-type controls (39). These data revealed that JNK is essential for proinflammatory macrophage polarization. These findings underscore a critical requirement for JNK in the pathogenesis of obesity-induced inflammation and the subsequent development of insulin resistance. Prolonged JNK activation, a downstream effect of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, promotes apoptosis after 12 h, despite playing a protective role during the initial stages of the stress response in NASH (40). In line with prior studies, our results showed that JNK activation in ORX + HFHF rats caused greater hepatic injury, inflammation and apoptosis than in normal rats. Thus, the JNK signaling cascade, particularly through the JNK1 and JNK2 isoforms in the liver, serves as an important regulator of metabolic homeostasis, inflammation, and apoptosis.

The p38 MAPKs are activated by a wide range of environmental and intracellular stressors, and numerous studies have established their critical role in regulating hepatic inflammation (37). There has been uncertainty regarding the role of p38 in the development of NASH in testosterone-deficient rats fed with a HFHF diet. Recent evidence suggested that hepatocytes isolated from wild-type mice that progressed to NASH showed elevated MKP1 protein expression, which was associated with diminished p38 and JNK activity. In MKP1-knockout mice, the resulting elevation of p38 and JNK activities appeared to offer protection against the development of NASH (41). Conversely, a prior mouse model reported that p38α acted as a proinflammatory mediator activated by specific genetic mutations and played a key role in fatty liver disease and chronic inflammation (42). Mice with p38α-deficient macrophages demonstrated resistance to steatohepatitis when received a high-fat/high-cholesterol (HFHC) diet (43). In addition, p38 was implicated in the aging of Leydig cells triggered by both advanced age and obesity. Inhibiting this pathway could delay the aging process and mitigate the associated decline in testosterone levels (35). These observations were in alignment with our findings that p38 expression was significantly increased in ORX + HFHF rats, supporting its proinflammatory role.

NASH occurs more frequently in men and postmenopausal women compared to premenopausal women. Experimental findings indicate that estradiol, a key female hormone, offers protective effects against liver diseases, especially NAFLD and NASH (44). In this study, we evaluated the effects of estradiol supplementation on oxidative stress, antioxidants, apoptosis, and MAPK signaling pathways in a rat model that mimics NASH in transgender women. Evidence suggests that estrogen derived from testosterone aromatization is a necessary metabolic modulator of many target tissues in males. A recent study demonstrated that increased abdominal fat deposition was observed in patients who received an aromatase inhibitor suggesting that E2 generated by testosterone aromatization protected against intra-abdominal fat formation in men (45). Estradiol has beneficial effects in reducing body fat mass, downregulating lipid synthesis genes, and alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation, consequently leading to an improvement in NASH. We demonstrated that estradiol improved overall NASH histological parameters, ameliorated hepatic fat accumulation, and decreased oxidative stress markers (4-HNE, MDA) and MAPK pathway activation (ERK, JNK, p38) compared with the ORX + HFHF group. It should be noted that estradiol did not significantly reduce hepatocyte ballooning in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group. We speculated that estradiol may primarily modulate lipid metabolism and inflammation rather than directly mitigating hepatocyte ballooning. In liver-specific estrogen-related receptor alpha (ERRα) knockout mice, deletion of this gene has been shown to precipitate hepatic steatosis and drive progression toward NASH by intensifying ER stress and inflammatory responses within the liver (46). The persistent hepatocyte ballooning in our study may result from insufficient ERα activation or a weaker therapeutic effect compared with estradiol's effects on inflammation and lipid metabolism. Localized ERRα dysfunction in the ORX + HFHF model may disrupt critical signaling pathways, which could explain why estradiol improved lipid and inflammatory changes but did not fully resolve cellular swelling.

We found that estradiol supplementation not only attenuated overall NASH features but also increased hepatic antioxidant protein and mRNA markers (GSH, SOD1, GPX, and GSTA1) and inhibited the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis (Bax and caspase-3, -9). Even though we observed no significant differences in the mRNA expression of Esr1 and Esr2, GPER represents a compelling alternative target for investigating estradiol interaction and activation. Li et al. (47) proposed that GPER1 contributes to the protective mechanisms of estrogen by mitigating lipid deposition, oxidative damage, and inflammatory responses through activated AMPK signaling in hepatocytes isolated from estrogen deficient mice fed high-fat high-cholesterol diet-induced NASH model. These data suggested that GPER1 might be a potential therapeutic target for the prevention or treatment of NAFLD and NASH. We hypothesized that estradiol had potentially alleviated lipid dysregulation, inflammation and oxidative stress production through its interaction with GPER. Similarly, the study by Sprouse et al., found that the administration of estradiol could suppress gastric inflammation and apoptosis in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic female mice. Their results indicated that E2-mediated restoration of the intracellular ER/p38MAPK/Nrf2/nNOSα signaling pathway reduced inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, thereby improving gastric emptying in the gastroparesis model (48). Mechanistically, apoptosis is governed by the balance of Bcl-2 family proteins, with antiapoptotic Bcl-2 and proapoptotic Bax acting as the primary molecular switches (49). According to the study conducted by Lin et al. (50), estradiol suppressed hepatocyte apoptosis by increasing the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, lowering the release of cytochrome c into the cytosol, and diminishing the enzymatic activities of caspases-9 and -3 in a rat model of reduced-size hepatic ischemia/reperfusion injury. Emerging evidence indicated that GPER facilitates rapid, nongenomic responses to estrogen in both normal and cancerous tissues. Specifically, the activation of GPER by estradiol triggers a diverse array of intracellular signaling events, including the transactivation of EGFR, which induces rapid ERK1/2 phosphorylation, as well as the activation of PLC, PI3K, and adenylate cyclase, alongside the mobilization of intracellular calcium. Together, these pathways act as critical regulators of both cellular proliferation and apoptotic signaling (51). Consistent with findings in R2C Leydig cell tumors (LCTs), GPER activation via the agonist G1 initiated a mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic cascade. This process is characterized by sustained ERK1/2 phosphorylation, which subsequently triggered DNA fragmentation, a reduction in Bcl-2, an increase in Bax, cytochrome c release, and the ultimate activation of the caspase-3 and Parp-1 signaling axis (52). Although GPER's influence on apoptosis is well-documented in cancer research, its function within a model of testosterone deficiency and HFHF-induced NASH remains largely unexplored. GPER is a cell surface receptor that responds to diverse external signals and controls numerous bodily functions. The influence of 17β-estradiol is facilitated by GPER-mediated nongenomic signaling, specifically involving MAPK/ERK transduction. This receptor modulates a multifaceted signaling architecture, playing a pivotal role in maintaining metabolic equilibrium, protecting against metabolic dysfunction and regulating hepatocyte apoptosis (53). We speculate that the elevated mRNA expression of GPER observed in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group facilitated estrogen signaling that enhanced the antiapoptosis activity. This receptor-mediated pathway likely serves as a critical mechanism for suppressing apoptosis. Here, we found that liver tissues from ORX + HFHF rats exhibited reduced Bcl-2 and elevated Bax mRNA levels. In contrast, control rats and those in the ORX + HFHF + E2 group showed a reversal of this pattern, with higher Bcl-2 and lower Bax expression. We also found that estradiol supplementation decreased hepatic mRNA levels of caspase-3 and caspase-9 in ORX + HFHF + E2 rats. These results point toward a functional link between GPER activation and the regulation of intrinsic apoptotic machinery.

We also sought to determine whether estradiol engages in crosstalk with all three MAPK signaling pathways. Existing research indicated that upon ligand binding, GPER can engage all MAPK subtypes to modulate intracellular processes. Particularly, GPER can selectively activate different MAPK branches, such as ERK versus JNK or p38 depending on the cell type and the nature of the stimulus; this determines whether the signaling cascade promotes cell survival, often via the GPER/ERK axis, or facilitates cell death through stress-induced JNK/p38 pathways (54, 55). Another study demonstrated that estradiol inhibited the activation of MAPK pathways, including ERK, p38, and JNK, along with their associated transcription factors activator protein-1 and nuclear factor κB. This suppression induced apoptosis in activated hepatic stellate cells, ultimately reducing liver fibrosis (56). Nevertheless, estradiol also has anti-inflammatory effects that act through the inhibition of p38, leading to reduced hepatic inflammation (57). Similar to other studies, we found the levels of hepatic ERK, JNK, and p38 to be upregulated in ORX + HFHF rats, whereas they were downregulated after receiving daily estradiol administration in ORX + HFHF + E2 rats. Additionally, evidence suggested that the upregulation of certain MAPK signaling pathways correlated with a significant decrease in proinflammatory cytokines and a boost in antioxidant defenses, ultimately leading to a reduction in oxidative stress (58). Our findings support this evidence, as estradiol treatment successfully normalized markers of oxidative stress (MDA, 4-HNE, Nrf2, HO-1, and iNOS) and apoptosis, while simultaneously replenishing antioxidant defenses such as GSH, SOD1, GPX1, and GSTA1. Notably, the ability of estradiol to mitigate both oxidative damage and programmed cell death appears to be a protective mechanism preserved in GPER-expressing liver tissues. By virtue of its pleiotropic effects, including the attenuation of lipogenesis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, estradiol could potentially ameliorate NASH in transgender women experiencing testosterone deficiency.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results offered novel insights into the molecular mechanisms linking inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in testosterone-deficient rats with HFHF-induced NASH to the estradiol-mediated activation of the GPER/MAPK signaling axis. This study demonstrates that testosterone deficiency synergizes with a HFHF diet to exacerbate NASH progression through dysregulated lipid metabolism, oxidative stress imbalance, activation of the intrinsic apoptosis, and MAPK pathway stimulation. Estradiol supplementation significantly attenuated these pathological processes, primarily via the modulation of GPER-associated signaling and the suppression of ERK, JNK, and p38 activation. These findings extend the multiple-hit theory of NASH by incorporating endocrine deficiency as a critical pathogenic amplifier (Fig. 7).

Implications for theoretical contribution and practice

This study advances current understanding of NASH pathophysiology by identifying the GPER/MAPK signaling axis as a mechanistic link between testosterone deficiency and metabolic liver injury, thereby integrating endocrine disruption into the multiple-hit model of disease progression. By demonstrating that oxidative stress-induced intrinsic apoptosis operates downstream of MAPK activation under hormone-deficient conditions, our findings provide a refined and integrative mechanistic framework for hepatocellular injury. Clinically, these results suggest that estradiol therapy may confer metabolic and hepatoprotective benefits in transgender women with testosterone deficiency. Furthermore, these data highlight MAPK signaling as a potential therapeutic target and underscore the importance of routine liver monitoring in patients undergoing long-term hormonal treatment.

Key lessons learned

Our findings demonstrate that testosterone deficiency significantly sensitizes the liver to dietary metabolic stress, accelerating the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis through enhanced oxidative stress, intrinsic apoptosis, and MAPK activation. Estradiol supplementation exerts hepatoprotective effects that extend beyond basic lipid regulation, effectively restoring antioxidant defenses and suppressing apoptotic signaling. Notably, the inhibition of MAPK pathways appears central to this protective mechanism, emphasizing that the hormonal milieu is a critical determinant of NASH severity. Consequently, hormonal status should be carefully considered in both experimental modeling and clinical risk assessment.

Strengths and limitations

It is essential to consider the strengths and limitations of this research. To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to utilize an animal model that specifically mimics the physiological conditions of transgender women to evaluate the therapeutic effects of estradiol on NASH. We demonstrated that a 1.6 mg/kg BW dose of estradiol valerate effectively reduced lipid accumulation, hepatic inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis while simultaneously inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway. However, several limitations must be noted. First, our study design did not allow for the isolation of the precise molecular mechanisms by which estradiol and MAPK interact to regulate oxidative, apoptotic and inflammatory damage, a relationship that necessitates further exploration using in vitro gene silencing or targeted inhibitors. Second, evaluating multiple doses of estradiol will be warranted in future studies to determine potential dose–response effects and identify the optimal therapeutic range. Additionally, food intake was not individually monitored, making it impossible to confirm that caloric consumption was perfectly uniform across all experimental groups. Consequently, further clinical research is required to validate these findings and determine their translational significance in human cohorts.

Materials and methods

Animals, estradiol preparation, and diets

Seven-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–250 g BW) were purchased from Nomura Siam International Co. Ltd. (Thailand). All animal care and experimental protocols complied with the guidelines for experimental animals from the National Research Council of Thailand and received official approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (License No. 006-2564). The methodology and results are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Rats were maintained under standard housing conditions (25 °C, 12:12-h light-dark cycle) and acclimated to the facility for 1 week. Subsequently, rats assigned to the ORX groups underwent bilateral orchiectomy performed using isoflurane inhalation for anesthesia.

The HFHF diet was prepared using an established formulation (59) containing 55% fat, 35% carbohydrate (20% fructose, 15% starch), and 10% protein. This profile significantly differed from the standard diet, which contained 7% fat, 47% carbohydrate, 27% protein (Perfect Companion Group Co., Ltd, Thailand). The HFHF feeding regimen was initiated in the ORX + HFHF and ORX + HFHF + estrogen groups after the acclimatization period.

Estradiol valerate (1.6 mg/kg; Bayer Pharma AG, Germany), diluted in 0.1% DMSO, was delivered by daily oral gavage to the ORX + HFHF + estrogen group for six weeks. Rats in the control and ORX + HFHF diet groups received 1 mL of the 0.1% DMSO vehicle via the same route and for the same duration. All treatments were initiated the day following the orchiectomy procedure.

Experimental design

Twenty-one male Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into three groups: (i) control group fed with a standard diet, (ii) ORX + HFHF group, and (iii) ORX + HFHF diet with estradiol treatment (1.6 mg/kg BW oral gavage once daily). To determine the appropriate sample size, a power analysis was conducted using G*Power software based on hepatic signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) protein expression levels reported in our previous research (Fig. S2). Given that estradiol inhibits STAT1 signaling, a pathway linked to the progression of NASH in testosterone-deficient rats on a HFHF diet, thereby STAT1 expression served as an essential marker related to steatohepatitis. As determined by western blotting, in each group as follows: (i) the ORX + HFHF group (7.13 ± 3.17) and (ii) the ORX + HFHF + E2 group (2.75 ± 1.30). With an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.90, the analysis indicated that a sample size of seven animals per group (total n = 21) was sufficient to detect significant differences.

Rats had free access to food and water ad libitum for a 6-week experimental period. At the end of the experiment, all animals underwent an overnight fast (6–8 h) before being humanely euthanized via intraperitoneal injection (IP) of an overdose of thiopental sodium (>50 mg/kg BW). Whole livers were immediately harvested and rinsed with ice-cold normal saline solution. Seminal vesicles were collected and weighed as an indicator of systemic testosterone status. Liver tissue was partitioned for histopathological examination, gene expression analysis, and protein quantification. For the histological examination, liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin overnight, transferred into paraffin embedding and cut at 5-µm thickness before staining with a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) method. Paraffin-embedded tissues were also used to evaluate caspase-9, iNOS, HO-1, and Nrf2 expression via IHC. Hepatic apoptosis was assessed using a terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay kit. The remaining parts of liver tissue were used to examine the protein expression levels of PLIN2, 4-HNE, Nrf2, HO-1, iNOS, caspase-9, and all MAPK subtypes (ERK, JNK, and p38). Hepatic lipid peroxidation (MDA) and antioxidative (GSH) levels were assessed using thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and glutathione assay kits, respectively. Gene expression profiles were quantified using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) technique, and the degree of hepatic lipid deposition was evaluated by ORO staining.

Bilateral orchiectomy

Upon completion of the acclimatization period, rats in the ORX groups were subjected to bilateral orchiectomy using the method proposed by Idris et al. (60) (Fig. S1). Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane inhalation (2% in 2.5 L/min oxygen), with adequate sedation confirmed by the tail-pinch technique. The surgical procedure, which began with a ventral scrotal incision, involved the ligation of major blood vessels with sterile absorbable silk prior to the excision of the testis and epididymis. Incisions were closed using nonabsorbable silk and swabbed with povidone–iodine to ensure asepsis. Postoperatively, rats were singly housed for 24 h and administered ketorolac (0.5 mg/kg, subcutaneously) for effective pain relief.

Histopathological examination and quantification (H&E and ORO staining)

Histological analysis of all liver sections was performed by a pathologist who was blinded to the treatment groups. The severity of NAFLD was quantified using the NAFLD Activity Score (NAS), which is the sum of scores for hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning. Each component was graded according to the established Brunt criteria (61) using the following scale:

Steatosis (0–3): Graded based on the percentage of hepatocytes showing fat deposition (0 = <5%, 1 = <33%, 2 = 33–66%, and 3 = >66% of fat deposition in the hepatocytes).

Lobular inflammation (0–3): Scored by the number of inflammatory foci per 20 × field (0 = absence, 1 = <2 foci, 2 = 2–4 foci, and 3 = >4 foci per 20 × field).

Hepatocyte ballooning (0–2): Evaluated by the presence and prominence of ballooning degeneration (0 = absence, 1 = few ballooning hepatocytes, and 2 = many prominent ballooning hepatocytes).

To quantify hepatic lipid accumulation, ORO staining was performed. Frozen liver samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound. Sections 5 µm thick were cut and mounted onto adhesive microscope slides (Matsunami, Japan). The slides were stained with fresh ORO solution (Fluka) and counterstained with hematoxylin. Following a rinse in running tap water and air drying, sections were mounted (Dako, CA, USA). Ten random fields per sample were captured under a light microscope at 40× magnification. The percentage of hepatic fat deposition was subsequently quantified using color histogram analysis with Image J software (NIH, USA) (62).

Hepatic MDA (lipid peroxidation) and glutathione measurements

Lipid peroxidation, indicated by MDA levels, was quantified in liver tissue using a TBARS assay kit (Cayman Chemical Company, USA). Briefly, liver tissue was homogenized and sonicated on ice for 15 s. The resulting homogenate was centrifuged at 1600×g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain the supernatant. The collected supernatant was mixed with a reagent solution composed of 20% acetic acid, 0.8% thiobarbituric acid (TBA), and 8.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate to facilitate the reaction. The solution was heated in a 95 °C heat block for 60 min, followed by centrifugation at 1,600 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant fraction's absorbance was read at 532 nm, and the final concentrations were expressed as nmol per mg protein.

Hepatic GSH levels were assessed using a Glutathione assay kit (Cayman Chemical Company, USA). Briefly, liver tissue was washed, homogenized, and then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant, which was subsequently deproteinated. The assay relies on the reaction between the glutathione's sulfhydryl group and DTNB to produce TNB, with the resulting absorbance measured spectrophotometrically at 405 nm. Final concentrations were determined by applying the sample's deproteination dilution factor and expressed in nmol per mg protein.

TUNEL assay

Tissue microarray (TMA) is an efficient histopathological technique, originally described by Battifora H. in 1986, that facilitates the simultaneous analysis of numerous tissue samples. This method serves as a foundation for high-throughput histological investigations, including H&E staining, IHC, or even its application in the TUNEL assay (63). To create a TMA, small tissue cores (∼0.6 mm in diameter) are precisely extracted from various original donor paraffin blocks using an automated instrument, such as the Quick-Ray Tissue Microarrayer. These cores are then systematically arrayed and inserted into a single recipient paraffin block. Core selection is guided by an expert pathologist, who marks the target area on the corresponding H&E-stained slide. The resulting TMA block can then be thinly sectioned for subsequent staining and analysis.

Cellular apoptosis within the liver tissue was assessed using the TUNEL assay, following a previously published protocol (64) with the ApopTag In Situ Apoptosis detection kit (Chemicon; EMD Millipore). Briefly, deparaffinized liver sections from TMA blocks (seven rats per group) were washed with distilled water and subjected to protease treatment for 15 min at 37 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treating the sections with 3% H2O2. The sections were then incubated with the working strength terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) enzyme, and the reaction was terminated using the provided wash buffer. Subsequently, an antidigoxigenin conjugate was applied and incubated on the sections for 30 min at room temperature. Apoptotic nuclei were visualized via a diaminobenzidine (DAB) reaction, and the slides were finally counterstained with hematoxylin. The percentage of immunoreactive cells was determined using the Positive Pixel Count V9 algorithm. This involved counting hepatocytes exhibiting positive brown nuclear staining across ten randomly selected high-magnification fields per sample. The total number of positive cells was determined by counting 100 hepatocytes in each of 10 randomly selected 20× fields (1,000 cells in total). Data are expressed as the percentage of immunoreactive hepatocytes.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC was performed on paraffin-embedded liver sections to evaluate the expression of caspase-9, iNOS, HO-1, and Nrf2 across all experimental groups. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using sequential washes in xylene and graded ethanol concentrations. Antigen retrieval was carried out by heating the slides in a microwave using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). To inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The slides were then incubated with the primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature: caspase-9 (1:100), iNOS (1:300), HO-1 (1:300), and Nrf2 (1:300). This was followed by incubation with a secondary antibody (Dako, USA) for 30 min. Signal detection was achieved using DAB to generate a positive brown stain, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Digital images of the stained sections were captured using a light microscope. Protein expression was quantified using the Aperio ImageScope software program (Leica Biosystems Imaging, Inc., MD, USA). The ratio of strongly positive pixels to the total number of pixels was calculated in ten random high-magnification fields per sample using the Positive Pixel Count V9 algorithm, focusing on hepatocytes with positively stained nuclei and/or cytoplasm (64).

qRT-PCR analyses

qRT-PCR was used to assess hepatic gene expression, following a previously established protocol. Immediately after excision, rat liver tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was isolated from the frozen tissue using the TRIzol method according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 1 µg aliquot of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using iScript reverse transcription (RT) supermix (BioRad). The reverse transcription reaction was performed under the following thermocycler conditions: priming at 25 °C for 5 min, followed by the reverse transcription step at 46 °C for 20 min. The reaction was concluded with an RT inactivation process at 95 °C for 1 min. Subsequent qRT-PCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad). Gene expression was quantified using qRT-PCR detection on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR machine (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The amplification program was initiated with one cycle of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles of 95 °C for 15 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 1 min (annealing/extension). The relative expression levels of the target mRNA transcripts were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method, based on the obtained cycle threshold (CT) values. Quantification was achieved by comparing the target genes to the expression of an internal control, β-actin. The specific sequences for all qRT-PCR primers are provided in Table S1.

Immunoblot analysis

Liver tissue lysates containing equal amounts of protein (30 µg protein) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by semidry transfer onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating the membrane with 1% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Antibodies against the total and phosphorylated forms of all MAPK subtypes (ERK, JNK, and p38), Nrf2, and HO-1 (dilution 1:1,000) were obtained from Cell Signaling. Antibodies for PLIN2 and iNOS (both 1:1,000) were purchased from Abcam. Antibodies for 4-HNE (1:1,000) and caspase-9 (1:1,000) were purchased from R&D systems and Bioss antibodies, respectively. Antibodies for loading controls, cyclophilin B (CPB; 1:20,000) and β-actin (1:1,000) were obtained from Abcam and Santacruz biotechnology, respectively. Following primary antibody incubation and sequential PBST washes, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies as per the manufacturer's protocol. To assess the relative activity of the MAPK subtypes, band densities for phosphorylated and total proteins were normalized to their corresponding CPB. Then, the data were expressed as a ratio of phosphorylated to total proteins. Protein signals were generated using a chemiluminescence kit, and band densities were quantified using the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (BioRad Laboratories, CL, USA). Target protein expression levels were normalized by calculating the ratio of each protein's band density to that of the CPB and β-actin loading controls.

Statistical analysis

All statistical computations and graphical charts were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.3 (Windows). Results are reported as the mean ± SD. Mean differences across experimental groups were evaluated via a one-way ANOVA, followed by the Tukey post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Histological examination scores were assessed using descriptive statistics. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
