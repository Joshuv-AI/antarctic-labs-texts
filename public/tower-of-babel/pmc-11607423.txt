Impact of alcohol use disorder on antiretroviral therapy adherence in adults with HIV/AIDS at University of Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Gizachew Kassahun Bizuneh, Zemenu Wube Bayleyegn, Abera Dessie Dagnew, Yeniewa Kerie Anagaw, Liknaw Workie Limenh, Yonas Sisay Aragie, Mihret Melese, Wondim Ayenew, Alemante Tafese Beyna, Habtamu Semagne Ayele, Gashaw Sisay Chanie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Alcohol use disorder in people living with the human immunodeficiency virus is an important public health problem associated with high rates of HIV infection and lower antiretroviral treatment adherence. However, little is known about the prevalence of alcohol use disorders and its impact on ART adherence among people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA). Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the magnitude of alcohol use disorder and its impact on medication adherence among PLWHA attending the ART clinic. Methods A hospital based cross sectional study design was conducted at University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UOGCSH) ART clinic from September 30, 2023 to February 30, 2024. The World Health Organization’s Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Tool (AUDIT) was utilized to assess alcohol use disorder. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to investigate the relationship between AUDs and other variables. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. Results The overall prevalence of AUDs was 38.8%, with hazardous use, harmful use, and alcohol dependence accounting for 24.6%, 6.1%, and 8.1% of the total, respectively. In addition, 67.8% of the participants had medication adherence above 95%. Factors associated with alcohol use disorder were male AOR = 1.52; 95% CI (1.01, 2.30), cigarette smoking AOR = 2.384, 95% CI (1.183, 4.80), educational status AOR = 1.96, 95% CI (1.016, 3.764), and age 45–57 years AOR = 3.05, CI (1.587, 5.871). In present study, compared with social drinkers, harmful drinkers and alcohol dependents showed low medication adherence under 95% (AOR = 4.08 and AOR = 8.89, respectively). Conclusion The study reveals a high prevalence of alcohol use and dependence in PLWHA, underscoring the need for effective detection and intervention strategies particularly for males, cigarette smoker, and age between 54 and 75 years in healthcare and community settings. Alcohol use is strongly associated with poor adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART). Individuals with hazardous alcohol use are four times more likely to have low adherence, while alcohol-dependent individuals are nearly nine times more likely to have low adherence compared to social drinkers.Keywords: Alcohol use disorders, Antiretroviral adherence, Ethiopia, HIV/AIDSSubject terms: Epidemiology, Population screening

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) has grown into a major epidemic in today’s world. Combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) has proven to be the most effective in improving clinical outcomes for people living with HIV infection and reducing viral transmissions when taken regularly1,2. However, it is reported that poor medication adherence is associated with low virological suppression, decreased CD4 cell count, and an increased risk for antiretroviral drug resistance, which allows HIV transmission3,4. The adherence rate of medication should be maintained at 95% or above to optimize antiviral outcomes and enhance viral suppression5. Various causes of non-adherence to ART have been identified, including: Patient-related factors (low or no education, gender, and alcohol use), condition-related factors (being away from home), psychosocial factors (stigma, poor support, depression, and secrecy), and treatment-related factors (a lack of an inventory during visits, multiple-tablet regimens, side effects to medication, and missed clinic visits)6–8. Of these reasons, alcohol consumption is reported as one of the strongest and most consistent risk factors for poor antiretroviral adherence9. Individuals who are taking ART and drink alcohol are at higher risk of early mortality compared with nondrinkers10. Various mechanisms have been proposed by which alcohol can disrupt ART adherence: enhancing the toxicity of antiretroviral treatment (ART), increasing liver damage from concurrent infection with the hepatitis C virus, and increasing the risk of opportunistic infection due to decreased effectiveness of ART and exacerbation of immune suppression11,12. Alcohol use is also found to be associated with an increased risk of developing lipodystrophy13and exacerbate antiretroviral therapy-induced neuropathic pain in patients with HIV/AIDS14. People living with HIV are mostly exposed to stress in life compared to non-infected people, and they use alcohol as a short-term coping mechanism for the perceived stress that results in the prevalence of Alcohol use disorders (AUD) appearing high in PLHIV2,15,16. Alcohol use disorders (AUD) are characterized by compulsive heavy alcohol use or an inability to control alcohol use despite its negative impact on an individual’s health, social, or functional wellbeing17. AUD is a growing problem in developing countries, and several studies have been conducted in Africa on the prevalence of AUD in peoples living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA). The prevalence of AUD among PLWHA varies from 11.3% in Tanzania18, 28.77% in South Africa19, and 39.4% in Nigeria20. Similarly, studies conducted in Ethiopia have shown varying rates of AUD among PLWHA, with figures of 32.6% at Jimma University specialized hospital21, 31.8% at Hawassa University comprehensive hospital22, 24.8% at Assela teaching hospital23, and 14.2% at Bishoftu general hospital24. Despite this high drinking rate in Ethiopia, little investigation was conducted related the prevalence of AUDs and its impact on ART adherence in the country. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the prevalence of AUD and its impact on ART adherence among HIV-infected patients at the University of Gondar comprehensive and specialized hospital (UOGCSH). By examining the therapy adherence of people infected with HIV in relation to their alcohol use, this research has the potential to enhance ART adherence and stimulate the creation of effective programs for HIV-infected individuals who engage in alcohol consumption in the country. Furthermore, the findings from this study may offer valuable insights for the development of community-based initiatives focused on reducing alcohol misuse among individuals living with HIV/AIDS and establishing a supportive environment for healthcare providers to ensure optimal treatment.

Methods

Study design, setting and populations

A hospital based cross-sectional survey was conducted at University of Gondar Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital (UOGCSH) ART clinic, from September 30, 2023 to February 30, 2024. Gondar is a historical town located in the northwest part of Ethiopia, 725 km from the capital city Addis Ababa. In Gondar town, there is one teaching referral hospital providing specialized clinical services, 8 health centers, and 15 private clinics serving the population. All adult patients on ART who are on follow-up in University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital ART clinic were considered as source population and adult people living with HIV attending the UOGCSH ART clinic for follow up care during the study period were our study population.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula, where 1.96 used for Z α/2, 50% for P and 5% for d because there is no previous study in the study area. Where: d is margin of error; p is proportion of treatment outcome and Z α/2 is the 95% confidence interval.

Level of significance (Za/2) = 5% (with confidence level of 95%).

P=0.5

Z = 1.96 was used at 95% confidence interval.

N = sample size.

By considering a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size that we used was 423 participants.

A survey sampling technique was employed to select the study participants. The patient record charts were obtained from the patients during each follow-up in the hospitals for additional recorded data.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Participants with the age of 18 years or older, with HIV-positive status, who had been on antiretroviral treatment for at least 3 months, and those willing to participate were included in the study. Patients who were unable to communicate due to severe psychiatric disorders or had incomplete medical records were excluded.

Measurements

Alcohol Use disorders

The WHO’s Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT) a structured questionnaire, was used to measure AUDs25. The AUDIT is a 10-item self-report measure, with items scored on a 0–4 scale. Higher values represent greater alcohol consumption or more problems associated with alcohol use. The total score ranged from 0 to 40. In accordance with the recommended scoring, cut-off score of eight or more was used to define probable ‘alcohol use disorder’ (AUD). A total AUDIT score of 1–7 indicated social drinking, a score of 8–15 indicated “hazardous drinking” a score of 16–19 indicated “harmful drinking” and a score of 20 or above indicated probable alcohol dependence. Harmful drinking and alcohol dependence are both indicative of severe AUD. The total score on the AUDIT reflects the extent of alcohol involvement along a broad continuum of severity.

Antiretroviral adherence

Medication adherence was calculated as follows;

The number of doses prescribed for a month–the number of times dose not taken in one month X 100.

The number of doses prescribed for a month.

The obtained values were then divided into two groups: adherence < 95% and adherence 95%26.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Age, gender, educational level, marital status, employment status, living conditions, ethnicity, religion, smoking status, khat use, monthly income, duration since the start of ART drug (years), comorbidity, and daily medication frequency were collected to understand the characteristics of the participants.

Study variables

Dependent variables

Alcohol use disorder and ART Adherence are the main outcomes categorized as AUD and no AUD and adherent and non-adherent, respectively.

Independent variables

Independent variables include sociodemographic variables (gender, age, residence, marital status, religion, living status, education level, employment, smoking status, khat use), clinical characteristics (duration since the start of art drug, comorbidity, time of the day medication is taken).

Data collection and data quality assurance

Four data collectors with prior data collection experience were recruited, along with two professional supervisors, and data collectors were instructed to define the aim of the study. Supervisors implemented intensive follow-up in each study site. Participants in the study were contacted during their outpatient visits to the ART center at UOGCSH.

Data was collected through face-to-face interviews with study participants who provided informed consent. Data collecting technologies were tested at 15 patients living with HIV attending the ART clinic at UOGCSH to examine the completeness, correctness, and accuracy of the patients’ charts. On a daily basis, the data was checked for completeness and consistency. Each patient file’s data abstraction form was issued an identification code. The filled forms were examined for accuracy, consistency, and completeness by the principal investigator. Completed forms were kept in a secure location, protecting patient confidentiality and data from tampering. The data gathering instrument was translated into Amharic, a widely spoken local language.

Data entry and analysis

The data were checked for completeness and consistency, then edited, coded, and entered into EpiData. Afterward, the dataset was exported to SPSS Window version 26 for analysis. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as mean with standard deviation. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate predictors of AUDs and nonadherence.

Independent variables associated with AUDs in bivariate logistic regression with a P value less than or equal to 0.25 were entered into multivariate logistic regression analysis to control potential confounding variables. The results were considered statistically significant P values less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The Ethical approval Review Committee of the University of Gondar College of Medicine and Health Sciences School of Pharmacy approved this study with reference number SOP/088/2023. All respondents provided informed consent for participation in the survey. Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from each study participant to confirm their willingness to participate. All participants were given the option to withdraw from the study at any time if they chose not to continue. Confidentiality was ensured by removing any identifying information and securely storing the questionnaires in a locked area after data collection. All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations based on the Helsinki Legislation.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants

In the current study the majority 227 (53.7) of the participants were male. The average age of the participants was 45.5 ± 12.5. Majority (65.5%) of the participants lived in urban and 26.9% had college and above education. Of all the participants, more than half (59%) were married and 86.3% were orthodox Christians by their religion. Out of the total participants involved in this study, half (49.4%) of the participants lived with their families and nearly 40.4% work in their private job. The duration since the start of ART drug was 1–3 years for 35% of the participants. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants are shown in (Table 1).

Approximately two-thirds (38.2%) of the participants were identified as social drinkers (AUDIT score 1–7). The overall prevalence of AUDs was 38.9%, with hazardous use, dependence, and harmful use accounting for 24.8%, 8.0%, and 6.2% of the total, respectively. In addition, 67.8% of the participants had therapy adherence of above 95% (Table 2).

When asked about the reasons for drinking alcohol who were categorized as AUD (Score > 8), they responded that 35.5% of the participants drink alcohol due to the ease availability of alcohol and 25.7% started drinking because of peer pressure. Factors associated with AUDs among people living with HIV attending services at UOGCSH ART Clinic

In the bivariate analysis there was a significant association between AUDs and demographic variables like male, age, educational status, cigarette smoking. The multivariate analysis was used to identify factors that were predictive of alcohol use disorder. Age, gender male, smoking cigarettes and educational status were independently associated with Alcohol use disorder. Being male was 1.52 times (AOR = 1.52; 95% CI = 1.01, 2.30) more likely to develop AUD than their counterpart. Smoking in PLWHA was positively associated with AUDs in which those who smoke cigarette was more than two times at risk for AUDs when compared to non-smokers (AOR = 2.38, 95% CI = 1.18, 4.81). Patients who were unable to read and write were two times at risk of AUD as compared to College And Above (AOR = 1.96, CI = 1.02, 3.76). Age has also a significant association with alcohol use disorder. Patients at the age of 54-75yrs were three times at risk of AUD as compared to patients at the age of 20-36yrs (AOR = 3.05, CI = 1.59, 5.87). The details are illustrated in Table 3.

The effects of sociodemographic variables and alcohol use on ART adherence among people living with HIV attending services at UOGCSH ART Clinic

The relationship of alcohol use to adherence is important because alcohol consumption is common among PLWHA. In the current study, harmful alcohol use was associated with a fourfold increase in the likelihood of ART non-adherence (AOR = 4.08, 95% CI = 1.724–9.641) compared to social drinkers (Table 4). Moreover, individuals with alcohol dependence were found to be eight times more likely to be non-adherent to ART (AOR = 8.89, 95% CI = 3.732–21.217). This relationship highlights the influence of alcohol dependence on adherence behaviors. Additionally, comorbidity was another factor significantly associated with ART non-adherence. Those with coexisting health conditions were nearly four times more likely to be non-adherent (AOR = 3.796, 95% CI = 1.611–8.943).

Discussion

There is minimal information on the effects of alcohol used disorder on ART therapy adherence, particularly in low and lower-middle-income countries such as Ethiopia, The purpose of this study was to investigate the effect of alcohol use on antiretroviral therapy adherence in people living with HIV in Gondar University specialized and comprehensive hospital. The result showed that around one third of the participants had AUDs, with 24.6% of the study participants were hazardous drinkers, 6.1% harmful drinkers, and 8.1% were alcohol dependent. Previous studies reported that the prevalence of AUDs in PLWH is higher than in people without retroviral infection or the general public19,27. In Nigeria the prevalence of AUD among PLWHA revealed 39.4%20and another cross sectional study from Jimma and Hawassa, Ethiopia, reported 32.6% and 31.8% prevalence, respectively21,22. In the present study the prevalence of alcohol dependence was in line with the previous studies in Ethiopia 5.1%21.

In the present study, hazardous alcohol drinking was found in 24.6% of participants, which is in line with the study done in Ethiopia (24.7%). This is higher than the prevalence of hazardous drinking found in similar studies from Uganda (6%)28, and Nigeria (7.3%). However, the result is low as compared to the study done in South Africa (33%)29. One potential reason for this variation could be attributed to differences in sample sizes and the assessment tools used to evaluate AUD. Additionally, socio-economic, cultural, and contextual factors may also contribute to the discrepancies observed in prevalence rates across different studies.

Males were found to be 1.52 times more likely than females to have AUD, which is consistent with research conducted in Ethiopia27,30,31, Africa32, Brazil33and Vietnam34. Mostly, heavy drinking occurs among male when contrast to female and biological differences among male and female could contribute to this difference35,36. There was a statistically significant correlation between AUD and cigarette smoking. This is consistent with findings from other Ethiopian investigations24,30,37–39. This is likely due to the correlation between alcohol use and cigarette smoking, whereby stimulation from cigarettes reduces the intoxicating and depressant effects of alcohol40,41.

Low level of education was found to be significantly associated with AUDs. HIV infected patients who were unable to read and write were 1.96 at risk of AUD as compared to college and above. This result was concordant with the study conducted in Nigeria42, Korea26, and Brazil33. This could be due to the fact that individuals with more education are more protected from alcohol abuse. This may be related to these individuals’ ability to better cope with problems and stressful situations due to their easier access to information and health services, which leads them to adopt a healthier lifestyle42. The prevalence of AUDs in patients in the age group of 54–75 years was higher as compared to patients in the age group of 20–36 years, which is in line with a study conducted in Nigeria43. In the present study medication adherence was very poor, with 67.8% of the participants having medication adherence above 95% which is in line with the study conducted in Korea26. In this study, it is evident that alcohol consumption significantly affects medication adherence among HIV-infected individuals. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 4.08 for harmful drinkers and 8.89 for alcohol dependents demonstrates a progressively stronger association between the severity of alcohol use and reduced adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART). This indicates that alcohol use is a key determinant of non-adherence, with those classified as alcohol-dependent being nearly nine times more likely to exhibit low adherence compared to social drinkers.

These findings are consistent with previous research, where alcohol use was identified as a common factor contributing to poor ART adherence. The range of AORs, from harmful drinking to alcohol dependence, underscores the need for targeted interventions that address alcohol use in HIV-infected populations to improve treatment outcomes7. Despite the fact that AUD has a proven detrimental effect on HIV treatment results, the prevalence of these disorders has not been extensively studied among PLHIV in Ethiopia following the more widespread delivery of ART. Our study provides estimates of the prevalence of AUD in Ethiopia using standard measures of alcohol use (AUDIT) among patients taking ART.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Both alcohol use and adherence levels were assessed using a self-reported adherence scale, which relies on the honesty and trustworthiness of respondents. This could lead to overestimation or underestimation of alcohol use and medication adherence. Additionally, the cross-sectional survey’s self-reported nature may have introduced biases, such as social desirability bias and recall bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the responses. Furthermore, the study was conducted at the University of Gondar, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions or populations in Ethiopia or other countries. Despite these limitations, the study addresses a gap in the literature regarding the impact of alcohol use disorder on ART adherence.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study has revealed a high prevalence of alcohol use and dependence among PLWHA compared to the general population. Gender, age, educational status, and cigarette smoking were independently associated with alcohol use disorder. The study also highlights that alcohol consumption significantly affects medication adherence among HIV-infected individuals. Therefore, regular screening for alcohol use should be integrated into routine HIV care to identify individuals with AUD early and enable timely interventions to prevent poor ART adherence. Additionally, collaboration between HIV care providers and addiction specialists should be encouraged to offer comprehensive care that addresses both HIV and AUD, which can improve ART adherence and overall health outcomes.
