A randomized clinical trial of app cognitive behavior therapy vs. HealthWatch for obsessive compulsive disorder
Authors: Sabine Wilhelm, Jennifer L Greenberg, Ryan J Jacoby, Hilary Weingarden, Susanne S Hoeppner, Dalton Klare, Ivar Snorrason, Adam C Jaroszewski, Thomas H McCoy, Oliver Harrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractApp-based cognitive behavioral therapy (app-CBT) addresses many treatment barriers. We randomized 120 U.S. adults with primary OCD (82.4% female, 77.5% White, mean age 40.1) between 7/2021 and 7/2023 to 12-weeks of coach-guided Perspectives (n = 60; app-CBT) or coach-guided HealthWatch (n = 60; an online well-being program). OCD severity (Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale; primary outcome) analyzed using a pre-registered mixed linear model showed a trend level group difference at post-treatment (ES = −0.47; p = 0.079). However, results from secondary and sensitivity analyses showed significantly better outcomes in Perspectives after adjusting for baseline differences or varying missing data assumptions (ps < 0.05). Response rates among completers were 65% in Perspectives and 41% in HealthWatch. Perspectives was also associated with greater pre-to-post-treatment improvement in functioning (ES = −0.55; p = 0.014). 83% of completers in Perspectives were mostly/very satisfied and 91% recommended the program vs. 43% and 53% in HealthWatch. Results highlight app-CBT as an effective and feasible treatment option for OCD that addresses dissemination obstacles.Clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT04136626 (Registration Date: 10/17/2019).Subject terms: Psychology, Health care

Introduction

Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by recurring intrusive thoughts, images, or impulses (obsessions) and repetitive coping behaviors (compulsions)1. In a given year, OCD is estimated to affect approximately 3% of the global population2 and is often associated with reduced quality of life, functional impairment, and delayed access to care3. Clinical guidelines4,5 recommend selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) or clinician-delivered cognitive behavior therapy (CBT) as first-line interventions for OCD. CBT for OCD is a structured, evidence-based treatment. It typically includes psychoeducation, identifying and modifying self-defeating thoughts through cognitive strategies, and addressing avoidance behaviors and compulsions through Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP). Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) examining clinician-delivered CBT for OCD typically result in high response and remission rates (i.e., ~70% and ~40%, respectively)6; with meta-analyses showing large treatment effects (e.g., g = 1.13)7,8.

Despite the compelling efficacy of CBT, however, access to this first-line treatment is severely limited9. Data on treatment delay (i.e., prolonged time between the onset of symptoms and the start of effective treatment) are variable but suggest an average duration of untreated OCD of 10 years, with longer duration leading to poorer outcomes10. Barriers to CBT include high treatment costs and lack of trained clinicians, rigid and time-consuming scheduling of in-person sessions, and travel requirements, which can make it challenging for individuals to participate11. Digitally-based treatments (e.g., internet or smartphone) can address these barriers as they offer more flexibility for patients, are scalable and cost-effective11,12, and offer standardized therapeutic content while significantly reducing clinician effort13. Several meta-analyses of RCTs showed that remotely delivered CBT for OCD and anxiety disorders (e.g., internet-based) is as efficacious as traditional face-to-face CBT14–16. Moreover, smartphone-based applications (“apps”) may offer additional advantages over internet-based treatments that deliver longer, more structured sessions on a computer, and connect users with a licensed therapist through telehealth (e.g., text messaging, video or audio calls). Apps are widely adopted and enable the incorporation of therapeutic interventions into daily life17. Such apps include the same core treatment elements as traditional face-to-face or internet-CBT (i.e., psychoeducation, cognitive strategies, and tools to guide users through ERP exercises)18, but content is delivered through short, interactive exercises and may feature additional elements such as goal-setting, reminders, and gamified components to enhance engagement. App CBT can be offered as a standalone or alongside varying levels of human support.

While many self-guided OCD apps and related studies only focus on subclinical OCD symptoms18–20, some apps focus on clinical OCD and integrate treatment sessions from licensed clinicians with promising results21. For example, Gershkovich et al. (2021) examined the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of combining an app designed to facilitate ERP (with features such as customizable fear hierarchies, exposure reminders, and assistance with response prevention) with 3-5 90-min in-person sessions of ERP (N = 23)13. The app was well-received by patients, and the integrated intervention was associated with significant reductions in OCD symptoms. Additionally, a small RCT (N = 27; n = 12 app-OCD) evaluated the efficacy of a gamified CBT app for OCD22. The results showed that CBT delivered via app with shorter meetings with a psychiatrist (six 40-min sessions) was as effective as traditional in-person CBT (6 weekly 1-h sessions) in reducing OCD symptoms.

However, while apps might have enhanced the impact of the clinicians in these studies, providers still dedicated a substantial amount of time to treatment. Thus, solutions that pair clinicians with apps do not immediately address the issue of the current mental health provider shortage. To address this major barrier to care, recent clinical trials have shown that smartphone-app delivered CBT combined with light support from a non-specialist (i.e., bachelor’s level) coach (who provides education, encouragement, and accountability via asynchronous chat and is supervised by a licensed clinician) is safe, acceptable, and effective in the treatment of disorders related to OCD (e.g., body dysmorphic disorder)23,24. However, no large-scale RCTs have examined app-based CBT for OCD supported by non-clinician coaches, even though this approach is likely less expensive than traditional treatments and provides flexible access to care anywhere and at any time.

Accordingly, the aim of the present RCT was to compare the efficacy of Perspectives, an app delivering CBT for OCD with light-touch coach support, to HealthWatch, an internet-delivered, coach-supported wellbeing program. HealthWatch was chosen as a minimally therapeutic control condition to control for non-specific therapeutic factors (e.g., attention, therapeutic alliance, etc.) and regular interaction with technology, given that non-specific therapeutic factors can significantly improve outcomes in psychotherapies for psychiatric disorders, including OCD25,26. Given the previously described strong efficacy of CBT for OCD7,8, we hypothesized that, compared to HealthWatch, Perspectives would be associated with significantly lower OCD severity, depression, and psychosocial impairment, and higher quality of life at post-treatment (week 12).

Results

Participants

Of the 158 participants enrolled/consented, 38 were excluded, and 120 were randomized (Perspectives n = 60; HealthWatch n = 60) (Fig. 1). The most common reason for exclusion was failure to meet criteria for primary DSM-5 OCD (18/38; 47%). Other reasons included (1) lifetime bipolar disorder or psychosis (10/38; 26%); (2) psychotropic medication changes within 2 months of enrollment (4/38; 11%); (3) current posttraumatic stress disorder (4/38; 11%); (4) past participation in 4 or more sessions of CBT for OCD (3/38; 8%); (5) current severe substance use disorder (3/38; 8%); (6) acute, active suicidal ideation (3/38; 8%); (7) current severe comorbid major depression (3/38; 8%); (8) current or past use of an app-based CBT for OCD program (e.g., nOCD; 3/38; 8%); and (9) concurrent psychological treatment (1/38; 3%). Participants could be found ineligible based on multiple criteria; thus, numbers do not add up to 100%.

Efficacy of Perspectives vs. HealthWatch on OCD symptom severity

When comparing Y-BOCS scores (our primary outcome) of the two interventions at week 12 (post-treatment), with a mixed linear model (MLM), we found a trend level difference (Estimated Marginal Mean [EMM] difference [95% Confidence Interval (CI)]: −2.1 [−4.4, 0.2], p = 0.079; Effect Size (ES) = −0.47]; Table 2; Fig. 2). However, two secondary analyses, one adjusting for baseline Y-BOCS differences using a longitudinal analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model and another testing the group difference in Y-BOCS change over time in the original repeated measures model, showed that the Perspectives group had lower Y-BOCS scores at post-treatment than the HealthWatch group (longitudinal ANCOVA, Y-BOCS EMM difference [95% CI]: −2.8 [−5.1, −0.6], p = 0.014, ES = −0.64; Supplementary Table 1) and exhibited a significantly greater decrease in Y-BOCS scores from baseline to post-treatment compared to HealthWatch (Y-BOCS EMM difference in change [95% CI]: −3.4 [−5.7, −1.1]; p = 0.004; ES = −0.77; Table 2), with moderate to large effect sizes.

Sensitivity analyses generally also showed better outcomes with Perspectives, with moderate effect sizes ranging from −0.43 to −0.75 for end-of-treatment Y-BOCS differences between groups and large effect sizes ranging from −0.75 to −1.05 for the group difference in Y-BOCS change over treatment (Supplementary Table 1). In the controlled multiple imputation sensitivity analyses, end-of-treatment differences emerged when we assumed that individuals who withdrew from treatment had a worse response (δ = 50% or worse) than that predicted under missing at random assumptions (see Supplementary Results).

Among treatment completers, response rates at week 12 were 40% (23/57) in Perspectives vs. 26% (12/46) in HealthWatch for full response and 25% (14/57) vs. 15% (7/46) for partial response. Twenty-six percent (15/57) of completers in Perspectives and 17% (8/46) in HealthWatch were in remission from OCD at post-treatment. The majority of responders (86% Perspectives, 79% HealthWatch) maintained response at 12-month follow-up (See Fig. 3).

Efficacy of Perspectives vs. HealthWatch on secondary outcomes

We did not detect significant cross-sectional differences between Perspectives and HealthWatch at week 12 in our pre-registered MLM secondary outcome analyses. However, similar to the primary outcome, we found significant group differences in change from baseline to week 12 (Table 2) as well as post-treatment differences in most longitudinal ANCOVA models adjusting for baseline differences (Supplementary Table 1). Compared to HealthWatch, Perspectives was associated with a significantly greater pre- to post-treatment decrease in Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS) scores (EMM difference in change [95% CI]: −4.5 [−8.2, −0.9]; p = 0.014; ES = −0.55) and increase in Quality of Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire (Q-LES-Q) scores (EMM difference in change [95% CI]: 6.6 [0.2, 13.0]; p = 0.043; ES = 0.44), but no significant difference in Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology—Self Report (QIDS-SR; EMM difference in change [95% CI]: −1.3 [−3.2, 0.7]; p = 0.22; ES = −0.29); this might be due to a floor effect, as QIDS-SR scores were mild at baseline and remained mild during the trial for both groups. Sensitivity analyses broadly agreed, indicating moderate end-of-treatment effects in WSAS favoring Perspectives, mixed results for Q-LES-Q, and not detecting any end-of-differences or change differences in QIDS-SR (Supplementary Table 1).

Engagement, credibility, expectancy, and satisfaction

Perspectives participants self-reported more time on their program than those in HealthWatch at mid-point (median [IQR] minutes/week: app 30 [15–49]; HealthWatch 19 [10–30]; p = 0.002). However, at post-treatment, there was no difference (app 15 [6–30] vs. HealthWatch 15 [8–30] minutes; p = 0.96). Participants in Perspectives vs. HealthWatch spent more time on telephone calls with a coach (total call minutes, mean (SD): 36.4 (15.5) vs. 19.1 (8.8); EMM difference [95% CI]: 17.3 [12.6, 21.9]; p < 0.0001; ES[95% CI]: 1.36 [0.96, 1.8]), but groups did not differ on weekly asynchronous messaging time with a coach (chat minutes per participant per week, mean (SD): 3.3 (1.9) vs. 3.9 (2.2); EMM difference [95% CI]: −0.6 [−1.3, 0.2]; p = 0.14; ES[95% CI]: −0.28[−0.6, 0.1]).

Participants in Perspectives vs. HealthWatch perceived the treatment to be more credible and expected larger gains (Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire [CEQ]-Credibility mean (SD): 19.1 (3.9) vs. 16.8 (4.2); EMM difference [95% CI]: 2.3 [0.8, 3.7]; p = 0.003; ES[95% CI]: 0.56 [0.19, 0.93]; CEQ-Expectancy mean (SD): 16.4 (4.9) vs. 14.2 (4.5); EMM difference: 2.2 [0.5, 3.9]; p = 0.012; ES: 0.47 [0.11, 0.83]). Treatment satisfaction was also significantly higher in Perspectives vs. HealthWatch as measured by the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ; mean (SD): 25.6 (5.1) vs. 20.0 (5.8); EMM difference [95% CI]: 5.6 [3.4, 7.7]; p < 0.0001; ES[95% CI]: 1.21 [0.78, 1.63]). Among those who completed the survey, 83% (44/53) in Perspectives (vs. 43% (19/44) in HealthWatch) reported being mostly or very satisfied with the treatment and 91% (48/53) (vs. 53% (24/45) in HealthWatch) would recommend the program to a friend.

Attrition

Of the 120 randomized participants, 17 (14.2%) dropped out before completing the 12-week assessment. Dropout was significantly higher in HealthWatch vs. Perspectives (14 (23%) vs. 3 (5%); Odds Ratio (OR) [95% CI]: 5.78 [1.46, 22.85]; p = 0.012; see Fig. 1). Neither baseline Y-BOCS scores (1 SD increase, OR[95% CI]: 1.35 [0.78, 2.34]; p = 0.28) nor CEQ (1 SD increase, OR [95% CI]: 0.97 [0.55, 1.70]; p = 0.90) was associated with dropout after adjusting for treatment group. Of the 17 participants who dropped out prior to the end of treatment, 8 (1 Perspectives; 7 HealthWatch) provided no data after baseline; most of these had not accessed the treatment materials at all or only very little of it. The remaining 9 participants (2 Perspectives, 7 HealthWatch) accessed some (3–9) of their respective treatment modules and provided 1–4 weeks of self-rated Clinical Global Impressions Improvement scale (CGI-I) OCD ratings as part of patient safety monitoring. Of these 9 participants who provided at least one weekly CGI-OCD rating, 1 (HealthWatch) reported feeling minimally worse, 6 (2 Perspectives, 4 HealthWatch) reported no change in symptoms, and 1 (HealthWatch) reported a minimal improvement in symptoms. No participants were withdrawn from the study by the study team.

Concomitant treatment and adverse events

Eight participants (all in Perspectives) reported meaningful psychotropic medication changes during treatment (i.e., changes that were long enough and of high enough dose change to potentially affect outcomes); two of these participants also initiated disallowed therapies. These participants were excluded from per-protocol analyses. Overall, 10% (6/60) of Perspectives OCD and 3% (2/60) of HealthWatch participants reported one or more adverse events at least possibly related to study procedures: increased OCD symptoms (Perspectives: 2; HealthWatch: 1); increased anxiety (Perspectives: 3; HealthWatch: 1); increased irritability (Perspectives: 1); sleep difficulties (Perspectives: 1); and increased depression (Perspectives: 1). There were no serious adverse events related to study procedures (see Supplementary Results).

Discussion

In this RCT, we compared the efficacy of Perspectives vs. HealthWatch in treating OCD over a treatment period of 12 weeks. In our pre-registered primary outcome MLM analysis, we only found a trend-level difference between the two interventions at post-treatment. However, results from OCD severity secondary analyses and sensitivity analyses showed significantly better outcomes in Perspectives vs. HealthWatch when adjusting for baseline differences in OCD severity via longitudinal ANCOVA, when comparing change over time, and when revising assumptions about data from treatment dropout missing at random to assuming that patients who dropped out were more likely to have smaller to no improvement compared to those who finished the post-treatment assessment. Encouragingly, both groups showed improvement in OCD severity from pre- to post-treatment. However, Perspectives participants showed significantly greater pre- to post-treatment OCD improvement, with a large effect size (ES = −0.77). Furthermore, 65% of Perspectives completers showed a partial or full treatment response vs. 41% in HealthWatch; the majority of responders maintained gains through one-year follow-up. These improvements are promising and largely in line with results observed in trials examining guided app-based interventions for other psychiatric disorders23,24,27 and full course in-person CBT for OCD7. This is notable given that recruitment for this study occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have led to a smaller effect size due to increased distress and restricted exposure opportunities28.

Additionally, both groups showed substantial pre-to post-treatment improvements in depression, functioning, and quality of life. This suggests that the therapeutic effects across both treatments extended beyond OCD symptoms. When comparing treatment group differences in improvement, Perspectives had significantly greater improvements in functional impairment compared to HealthWatch. Results were mixed for quality of life, and did not detect any treatment differences in depression (perhaps due to depression symptoms being generally mild throughout the trial).

These results are in line with a large previous literature showing that while non-specific therapeutic factors can be quite effective in the treatment of psychiatric symptoms29, including OCD25, CBT tends to outperform non-specific minimally therapeutic comparison conditions8. The efficacy of HealthWatch underscores the importance of promoting non-specific therapeutic factors (e.g., alliance, expectancy, motivation) in digital interventions for OCD. However, the superior performance of Perspectives suggests that guided app-based CBT may also improve OCD symptoms through specific mechanisms such as inhibitory learning within ERP and belief change associated with cognitive restructuring, beyond the effects of non-specific factors. Future experimental therapeutic trials are needed to better understand the mechanisms of change in guided app-based CBT for OCD.

The lack of cross-sectional differences at week 12 may stem from insufficient statistical power. In our sample size calculation, we had anticipated a larger post-treatment effect size (ES = −0.76) than the post-treatment effect size we observed in our sample (ES[95% CI] = −0.47 [−0.83, −0.11]). Our a priori estimate was based on a meta-analysis estimating CBT effects compared to minimally therapeutic control interventions (i.e., “psychological placebo” conditions; e.g., relaxation)7, but might have underestimated the efficacy of HealthWatch when we designed the study. In the current study, HealthWatch ended up being a relatively active control condition, perhaps due to the inclusion of OCD-specific psychoeducation, which is a potent, evidence-based treatment component30, as well as the positive impact of having a supportive coach. It is also possible that the general health-related information might have resulted in lifestyle changes such as healthier eating choices or increased exercise, which might have, in turn, had a positive impact on mental health symptoms, including OCD, although we did not directly measure this. Thus, the effect size of our adapted version of HealthWatch on outcomes was larger than anticipated.

Additionally, by chance, there was an imbalance in baseline OCD severity between groups. In an alternative analysis adjusting for baseline symptoms with a longitudinal ANCOVA model, the week 12 difference became significant, with the Perspectives group showing lower symptom severity than the HealthWatch group (see Supplementary Results). The dropout rate was higher in the HealthWatch group, and higher attrition in this group might also have biased the estimated treatment effects. In our pre-specified analysis, we assumed that data were missing at random. However, study dropouts tended not to interact with the treatment material or only accessed very little of it, and most self-reported no symptom change before dropping out (see Supplementary Results). Thus, they probably did not drop out randomly but might have dropped out because they did not find the treatment acceptable and/or they did not improve. In sensitivity analyses that assumed that people who dropped out experienced reduced symptom improvements (25–100% of average overall change less during treatment), post-treatment group differences in OCD severity and functional impairment emerged favoring Perspectives over HealthWatch.

In addition to (1) underestimating the effect size of the enhanced HealthWatch treatment arm and (2) not expecting that groups might be imbalanced at baseline, we (3) might have also slightly overestimated the effects of Perspectives when we calculated the power for our study. However, given the substantial symptom severity drop and the high response rate in this group this might be the least important factor in explaining our findings. Regardless, it is possible that the inherent symptom heterogeneity and cognitive rigidity in OCD31 may have hindered participants’ ability to tailor strategies from Perspectives (e.g., exposure and response prevention) to their unique symptoms, even with support, thereby diminishing its effect. Additionally, anecdotal reports from patients to coaches suggested that Perspectives participants may have spent more time on preferred (e.g., mindfulness) vs. more distressing (but likely more effective) exposure-based strategies32. Future research should examine whether certain individuals require more focused or frequent human support to encourage the use of exposure-based treatment strategies.

Perspectives were highly feasible and acceptable. Compared to HealthWatch, participants in Perspectives rated the treatment as more credible, expected more benefits, and were less likely to drop out. Only 5% of Perspectives participants dropped out, which is much lower than the average attrition (19%) seen in face-to-face exposure-based OCD treatments33. Perspective participants were also more satisfied, with over 90% stating they would recommend Perspectives to a friend. These results add to a growing literature showing that coach-supported app CBT is an acceptable and effective treatment approach13,23,27.

The results also highlight the potential cost-effectiveness of light-touch digital services. Participants in both conditions improved, and most Perspectives participants achieved meaningful reductions in OCD symptoms with only a fraction of the specialized human effort required by traditional face-to-face therapies. A recent mega-analysis34 estimated that a course of evidence-based (e.g., CBT) therapy for OCD consisting of 17 sessions (~12–13 h of face-to-face clinician time) resulted in an average 12-point reduction in Y-BOCS OCD severity. In comparison, Perspectives was associated with a 9-point reduction of OCD severity over 12 weeks with only ~75 min of coach support (35 min of phone calls; 40 min of asynchronous chat). These findings suggest that guided app-based CBT may be a cost-effective and scalable psychiatric service. In particular, Perspectives may serve as an adjunct or standalone treatment for OCD in remote or underserved settings, single-practice clinics, and situations where geographical or financial barriers limit access to care. However, future research is needed to develop strategies for dissemination and implementation of coach-supported digital interventions in real world settings.

The study has several limitations. As described above, our small sample size might have been too small to detect a true effect against an active control group. Additionally, there was an imbalance in OCD severity between the two groups at baseline. Although not statistically significant, the difference may have confounded the treatment effect35. Coach call time was greater in Perspectives vs. HealthWatch, and this may have affected outcomes by enhancing engagement with and the credibility of Perspectives. Differences in platform type (app vs. website) could also influence user attention and engagement. Future studies could compare Perspectives with more widely used interventions for OCD (e.g., face-to-face CBT for OCD). In addition, the sample was recruited online and had on average mild depressive symptoms, which may not be fully representative. Mitigating this concern, however, was the fact that OCD severity in our sample was moderate to severe.

Further, to reduce assessment burden on participants, secondary outcomes were measured with self-report measures only, which introduces an element of subjectivity. Treatment delay data were not collected. Comorbid diagnoses were assessed in a brief structured diagnostic interview (Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview 7.0.2 [MINI 7.0.2]), and some potentially relevant comorbid diagnoses were not assessed (e.g., obsessive-compulsive personality disorder [OCPD]). Despite randomization, group differences in baseline characteristics (e.g., comorbidity profiles) may have further influenced intervention outcomes. The absence of OCPD data, in particular, represents a limitation, as OCPD may influence OCD symptom presentation and treatment outcomes. Thus, future studies may benefit from more comprehensive evaluation of comorbidities, treatment delays, and secondary outcomes. Finally, although ~25% of the sample identified as a sexual/gender minority and 64% had median or lower household incomes, the sample was primarily college educated and non-Hispanic White. Future research should replicate the study with more diverse samples.

In conclusion, the high cost and scarcity of trained clinicians are major barriers to the dissemination of face-to-face evidence-based therapies. There has been a recent proliferation of generic, non-specialized programs, which may have limited effectiveness for targeted conditions, like OCD. Our results suggest that both a coach-guided digital health and well-being program (HealthWatch) enhanced for OCD and coach-guided smartphone-delivered CBT for OCD app (Perspectives) were effective in reducing OCD severity. Both interventions only required limited time from a coach, which enhances their scalability. However, Perspectives was more highly acceptable, tended to be associated with greater improvements in OCD and functional impairment over time, and could reduce human support required for the gold-standard psychological treatment of OCD from an estimated 750 min per patient to 75 min. Future research should focus on further improving the efficacy of app-based CBT for OCD, for example, by adding just-in-time adaptive interventions and enhancing personalization (e.g., increasing coach training, identifying predictors and moderators of treatment response, and matching patients with interventions accordingly). Additionally, future research is needed to better test Perspectives in real-world settings with larger samples, better understanding the potential role of digital health and well-being programs for OCD, including durability of effects, and elucidating the mechanisms of change for Perspectives, including understanding the role of coaching on engagement and efficacy.

Methods

Participants

The trial was conducted at the Massachusetts General Hospital Center for OCD and Related Disorders, a specialty OCD clinic in Boston, MA. Adults with OCD were recruited throughout the U.S. between 7/2021 and 7/2023 through social media (e.g., Instagram, Facebook, etc.) in addition to traditional sources (e.g., clinic intake, referrals, flyers, listings on clinicaltrials.gov, institutional websites, etc.). Inclusion criteria were: ≥18 years old, living in the U.S., owning a smartphone with data plan, and a primary diagnosis of DSM-5 OCD. Exclusion criteria were: (1) concurrent psychological treatment or use of an OCD app, (2) ≥4 sessions of previous CBT for OCD, (3) recent (past 2 month) initiation of, or changes in, psychotropic medication (stable medication was allowed), (4) current severe substance use disorder, (5) lifetime bipolar disorder or psychosis, (6) acute, active suicidal ideation (clinical judgment and/or score ≥2 on the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale suicidal ideation subscale), (7) current severe major depression, (8) current posttraumatic stress disorder, and (9) limited technological literacy interfering with ability to engage with a digital treatment (i.e., unable or uncomfortable operating their smartphone).

Study design and procedures

Participants were randomized 1:1 (in parallel; superiority framework) to Perspectives or HealthWatch using computer-generated block randomization (block size 4 to ensure balance between conditions over time), stratified by stable psychotropic medication use at baseline (yes/no), to ensure balanced treatment assignment and maintain blinding. The randomization sequence was created prior to the trial start by the statistician using PROC PLAN in SAS and was uploaded to the randomization module in REDCapTM (Research Electronic Data Capture) by the study statistician prior to the start of the trial. Randomized treatment assignments uploaded to REDCap in this way could not be pre-viewed by database users. Allocation was concealed from participants until assignment. Participants were given descriptions of both treatment conditions as two potentially beneficial approaches, provided informed consent, and agreed to be randomized to either condition. The study was approved by the Mass General Brigham institutional review board (#2018P001669), registered with clinicaltrials.gov (#NCT04136626) on 10/17/2019, and followed the CONSORT guidelines. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan can be accessed at clinicaltrials.gov. There were no major protocol deviations. Changes to the protocol after the study commenced were minor (e.g., adding more detailed demographics questions, clarifying or adding questions about treatment history) and did not impact the pre-registered analysis plan or study procedures.

Assessment

Clinician-rated assessments were conducted at baseline, mid-treatment (Week 6), and post-treatment (Week 12) via videoconferencing by doctoral-level independent evaluators (IEs). The IEs were blinded to the treatment conditions. Participants were repeatedly reminded not to disclose any details about their treatment during assessment interviews. In the case of accidental unblinding, a new IE was assigned for future assessments.

The Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview 7.0.2 (MINI)36 and the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)37 were administered at baseline to characterize the sample and determine eligibility. The primary outcome was OCD severity, measured by the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)38. Severity of OCD symptoms at baseline was categorized by Y-BOCS total scores into mild (0–13), moderate (14–25), moderate-severe (26–34), and severe (35–40)39. IEs reported clinical improvement using the Clinical Global Impressions Improvement scale (CGI-I)40. Treatment response was defined as a three-category variable at week 12 and week 64 (12-month follow-up)23: (a) full response: ≥35% reduction in Y-BOCS total scores plus CGI-I rating of 1 (“very much improved”) or 2 (“much improved”), (b) partial response: <35% but ≥25% reduction in Y-BOCS plus CGI-I ≤ 3 (at least “minimally improved”), and (c) no response: <25% reduction in Y-BOCS and/or CGI-I rating > 3 (no change or OCD symptom worsening). Remission was defined as a Y-BOCS total score of ≤12 at week 12 and week 6441.

Secondary outcomes were self-reported using the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS)42 to assess functional impairment, the Quality of Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire—Short Form (Q-LES-Q-SF)43 to assess overall quality of life, and the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology—Self Report (QIDS-SR)44 to assess depression severity. Feasibility and acceptability were assessed via attrition, Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire (CEQ)45, and Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ)46. Internal consistency across measures and timepoints in this sample was acceptable to excellent, α = 0.73–0.92. Demographic data were self-reported at baseline. Participants were compensated $25 (week 6, 12) and $50 (12-month) for post-baseline assessments.

Interventions

Treatment Structure. Perspectives and HealthWatch were designed to be structurally equivalent with respect to dosing of treatment and level of coach support. Specifically, program content was delivered via the app or website, respectively, over 12 weeks. Participants could progress through the modules at any pace but were instructed to use their program daily. Each participant was assigned a coach with a bachelor’s degree in psychology or a related field who provided support throughout the treatment with the goal of facilitating motivation, engagement with the interventions, and problem-solving via asynchronous messaging and two brief ~5–20-min phone calls (onboarding and mid-treatment). At the onboarding call, the coach explained how they could be reached through the messaging system or email, respectively, oriented the participant to the program, gave suggestions about how to get the most out of the program, provided information about what to do in an emergency, and answered questions. At the mid-treatment call, the coach checked in on the participant’s progress and assisted them in updating goals for the remainder of treatment. Coaches completed a standardized, internationally available 4-h online continuing education (C.E.) training by the MGH Psychiatry Academy in CBT for OCD developed by our team and attended a 1.5 h live training that covered the Perspectives OCD app, HealthWatch program, key study procedures, and brief training in motivational interviewing and supportive accountability. To ensure ongoing high-quality treatment, two expert licensed psychologists provided weekly supervision. Coaches were instructed to reach out to each participant at least once weekly. Coach call and chat times were tracked in minutes per participant for the 12-week treatment. Both programs’ content included a notice about suicide risk, and information on what to do in case of suicidal thoughts was always available via a “safety” tab in the app/website. The safety tab included links to 988, 911, and a national suicide hotline.

Perspectives OCD. Perspectives is a digital service that provides smartphone-delivered CBT combined with light-touch support from the coach via asynchronous chat. The Perspectives app was developed via user-centered design24 with technology experts, expert clinician-researchers, and OCD patient consultants. Each treatment module included brief psychoeducation followed by short interactive exercises to engage the user and teach the specific CBT skill(s) from that module. Exercises could be repeated as often as desired.

Specifically, Perspectives includes seven core modules assigned to all participants receiving the app, which cover: (1) Discovering the Program, during which participants were oriented to the CBT model of OCD (i.e., psychoeducation) and selected personalized treatment goals, (2) Working With Thoughts, which taught stepping back and considering another perspective (i.e., cognitive restructuring), (3) Approaching Exposures (exposure and response prevention, ERP) which guided users through completion of exposure to OCD triggers while refraining from engaging in compulsions, (4–5) Mindfulness and Mindful Grounding and Letting Go, which included audio exercises to teach mindfulness and attentional retraining skills to gently and nonjudgmentally refocus attention away from obsessions, (6) Value-Based Living and Self-Esteem, which taught users to set activity goals that align with their values, and to fill time previously spent ritualizing; users also learned to identify and modify core beliefs (e.g., “I am worthless”) and enhance self-esteem through exercises (e.g., self-esteem pie); and (7) Staying Well, which focused on relapse prevention and maintenance of gains. See Fig. 4 for a schematic of the participant journey in the Perspectives app.

Schematic of the participant journey in the Perspectives app.

At treatment start, participants selected at least one personalized goal from the following symptom domains: (1) contamination, (2) over-responsibility, (3) perfectionism, (4) aggressive thoughts, (5) sexual thoughts, and (6) scrupulosity. For each goal selected, participants were directed to corresponding ERP modules covering symptom-specific graded exposure hierarchies in the app. Participants could follow the tasks in any order (gradually or variably), skip irrelevant tasks, and add customized exposures. The ERP module provides general guidelines for exposure implementation (e.g., structuring exposures in advance, repeating exposures frequently and in different contexts) as well as step-by-step instructions (e.g., identifying feared outcomes, specifying compulsions to reduce/eliminate, setting exposure goals, summarizing exposure outcomes and lessons learned).

HealthWatch. HealthWatch is an internet-based program that provides educational content on wellbeing topics. For the current study, HealthWatch was combined with light-touch support from the coach via asynchronous, text-based chat. Each of the 12 modules provided psychoeducation on various health and wellness-related topics, including the impact of the personal environment on health, information about weight loss and nutrition myths, back pain prevention, heart health, etc. Additionally, each module included an interactive exercise related to the topics. See Fig. 5 for a schematic of the participant journey in HealthWatch. HealthWatch has been used in previous clinical trials47,48, and controls for non-specific treatment effects, including attention, structured engagement, and sense of agency/empowerment. We added an OCD-specific psychoeducation and treatment rationale module to enhance treatment engagement, quality, and credibility.

Schematic of the participant journey in the HealthWatch program.

Quality assurance

Coaches were monitored for disallowed strategies (e.g., discussing exposure with HealthWatch participants). Coach calls were recorded and reviewed regularly by a doctoral-level adherence rater. Ratings of adherence (M = 6.99, SD = 0.07; on a scale from 1 = “not at all” to 7 = “completely adherent”; e.g., providing an overview of app or website modules, allowing participants to ask questions) and competence (M = 5.0, SD = 0.0; 1 = “not at all” to 5 = “completely competent”; e.g., expressing empathy, not reassuring participants about OCD fears) were excellent for both conditions.

IEs received training in the MINI and Y-BOCS from a gold-standard rater and had to demonstrate 100% agreement on MINI OCD diagnosis and .80 ICC reliability on the Y-BOCS. The primary diagnosis of OCD on the MINI was reviewed with a gold-standard expert rater for consensus prior to study enrollment. To maintain inter-rater reliability (IRR), IEs attended twice monthly supervision with a licensed psychologist with expertise in the assessment of OCD. Assessments were recorded, and 15% of randomly selected recordings were rated for IRR. IRR was excellent [Y-BOCS total scores (ICC(1,1) = 0.90].

Adverse events monitoring

All adverse events were documented using a standardized Adverse Events Form administered by a research assistant and reviewed by the IE at week 6 (mid-treatment), week 12 (post-treatment), and 12-month assessments. Clinical deterioration and suicidality were monitored by asking participants to complete weekly self-report measures (i.e., CGI-I and questions about suicidal ideation). Per protocol, participants who reported suicidality or significant worsening in symptoms (“much” or “very much” worse on the participant-rated CGI-I specific to OCD) over 3 consecutive weeks were evaluated by a licensed clinical psychologist. Participants could be withdrawn if, in the judgment of the principal investigator, remaining in the study posed a substantial risk to the participant or if a higher level of care was needed. No participants were withdrawn from the study.

Data analysis

Sample size. The sample size was calculated to detect a moderate to large mean between-group effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.76 at the end of treatment, based on the 95% lower confidence limit of the effect size of CBT for OCD versus minimally therapeutic control interventions (i.e., “psychological placebo” conditions; e.g., relaxation) in a meta-analysis7. Based on descriptions of HealthWatch as an attention-matched, internet-based control condition with expected minimal effects on therapeutic outcomes in prior trials47,49,50, we expected HealthWatch to have some therapeutic effects for OCD, but less than CBT. We assumed equal allocation of participants, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, power of 90%, and a 20% attrition rate comparable to previous RCTs for OCD7,51.

Analysis. The intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses included all randomized participants. To evaluate the effects of treatment on primary and secondary outcomes, mixed linear models (MLMs) were used. Categorical fixed effects included time (baseline (Bsl), mid-, and end-of-treatment (EoT)), the time by treatment interaction, stratification (stable baseline psychotropic medication use vs. not), and the time by stratification interaction52. This repeated measures model did not include a main effect of treatment (Perspectives (P) vs. HealthWatch (HW)); this modeling approach uses the beta-coefficient of the treatment group by time interaction at post-treatment to estimate the treatment effect, which limits the impact of baseline confounding with the treatment effect35. Time was modeled as a repeated measure using a heterogeneous variance first-order autoregressive covariance matrix, which was determined to fit the data better (based on AIC, BIC values) than alternatives considered (i.e., unstructured, Toeplitz, compound symmetry). Tests of group differences were based on a priori contrasts of end-of-treatment estimated marginal means (EMMs) of the time by treatment interaction, derived from the MLMs. Effect sizes (ES; Cohen’s d equivalent) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated based on EMM differences divided by raw pooled baseline standard deviations53 and show differences compared to the control group (HealthWatch; i.e., negative ES indicates greater outcome reductions in Perspectives compared to HealthWatch). Using the EMMs from the same model, we also compared the treatment difference in symptom change from baseline to end-of-treatment as a separate contrast (i.e., (EMM HW-EoT – EMM HW-Bsl) – (EMM P-EoT – EMM P-Bsl)). Noting the differences in OCD symptom severity at baseline, we also analyzed outcomes using longitudinal analysis of covariance models (ANCOVA), which modeled the effect of treatment, time (mid-treatment, end-of-treatment), and the treatment by time interaction, as well as baseline symptom severity and stratification as covariates (including time by covariate interactions); this model provided an alternative estimate of the treatment effect after adjusting for baseline differences35.

To assess the sensitivity of our results to protocol deviations and missing data assumptions, we additionally conducted six sensitivity analyses: (1) MLM analysis with per-protocol completers only; and (2–6) MLM analyses of controlled multiple imputation datasets using delta-based offsets to explore the possibility of the unobserved having a poorer response than those observed. In the analysis of the per-protocol sample, we used the same MLM as in the ITT analysis but excluded participants who dropped out during the 12-week treatment phase (n = 17), initiated prohibited treatment(s) during the study (n = 8), or self-reported not using their assigned intervention (n = 3). For the multiple imputation models, we created 150 imputed datasets; the imputation model included all primary and secondary outcome variables, OCD symptom severity and symptom number over time (as measured by the Obsessive Compulsive Symptoms Rating Scale; OCSRS)54, treatment group, and 18 auxiliary baseline variables, including socio-demographics, US geographical region, clinical characteristics, treatment history, and treatment readiness and expectancy variables. For each controlled imputation scenario, different δ-based offsets were added to the imputed values for the n = 17 dropouts: (2) δ = 0% (i.e., no offset; data from drop-outs is assumed to be missing at random); (3) δ = 25% (i.e., drop-outs are assumed to have 25% worse symptom improvement than people who did not drop out); (4) δ = 50%; (5) δ = 75%; and (6) δ = 100%. Delta-based offsets were calculated as a proportion of the average increase (or decrease) of (overall) observed data at each time point for each outcome separately. Group differences in credibility, expectancy, and satisfaction were derived from repeated measures ANOVA or independent samples t-tests for normally distributed, continuous variables. Differences in dropout (yes/no) were examined using a logistic regression with treatment group, baseline symptom severity, and baseline treatment credibility as predictors. Statistical significance was evaluated at a 2-tailed p < 0.05. Effect estimates are presented as model-adjusted least square means (estimated marginal means, EMMs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were performed using SAS System for Windows, version 9.4 (IBM).
