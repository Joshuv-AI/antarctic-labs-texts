Precision Prevention Is a Long Game
Authors: Jamal S Rana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: Asia, cost-effectiveness, polygenic risk score, precision medicine, primary prevention, statin therapy

We have known for nearly a decade how to measure a patient's inherited risk of coronary artery disease. We still do not know what that knowledge is worth.

The clinical validity of polygenic risk scores (PRSs) is well established. Across biobanks on several continents, individuals in the top quintile of the score distribution carry roughly twice the risk of incident disease.1, 2, 3, 4 The gradient persists after adjustment for conventional risk factors and is detectable decades before imaging registers a signal. But validity is not utility, and utility is not value. The 2026 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Multisociety Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia reflects that distinction: PRSs are recognized but not formally recommended for routine clinical decision-making; instead, they are positioned as an emerging risk enhancer that may help personalize atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk assessment.5 Evidence on cost is one of the things standing between that description and a recommendation.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Ooi et al6 supply some of that evidence from a setting where the question has rarely been asked.

What the Study Shows

The investigators built a state-transition Markov model in a hypothetical cohort of 45-year-olds at intermediate (7.5% to 20.0%) 10-year ASCVD risk by the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCEs). Under standard care, 52% of the cohort begin statins because they have risk-enhancing factors. The PRS-guided strategy treats those patients too and adds anyone in the highest-scoring 20% of the polygenic risk distribution who would otherwise go untreated. Costs and utilities were attached to each health state and projected from the payer perspective over 2 to 40 years.

The central finding is temporal (Figure 1). Over 2 to 20 years, the PRS strategy is dominated: it costs more than standard care and delivers slightly fewer quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). At 30 years, the picture inverts, with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of Singapore dollar (SGD) 60,334 per QALY, falling to SGD 23,667 by 40 years, comfortably below the SGD 75,000 threshold used by Singapore's Agency for Care Effectiveness. Probabilistic analysis placed the probability of cost-effectiveness at 53.5%.

What This Paper Contributes

Two contributions stand out. The first is geographic: this is among the very few economic evaluations of PRS-guided prevention outside North America and Europe, and the first grounded in Singapore-specific costs, life tables, and assessment thresholds. Canadian and U.S. analyses reached similar conclusions,7,8 but their transportability to Asian systems was assumed rather than shown.

The second contribution is conceptual and merits emphasis: the economics of PRS are governed by the time horizon, not by the price of the test. Genotyping costs SGD 100 once. Statin therapy and the associated primary care visits cost roughly SGD 280 per year, for decades. Whether a PRS costs SGD 100 or SGD 50 is largely beside the point. What determines value is how long one waits for avoided events to accumulate, and how far a payer's horizon extends. Health systems should judge genomic prevention against a patient's life course, not the length of a budget cycle. That is the premise of value-based care: returns accrue over a lifetime, not a fiscal year.

What the 2 Scenario Analyses Imply

Two scenario analyses deserve more attention than they will get. Substituting PREVENT (Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs) for the PCE lowered the ICER to SGD 6,204 per QALY; beginning testing at age 30 lowered it to SGD 15,893.

Together, these analyses suggest that the primary model, the base case against which the scenarios are compared, evaluates PRS under the 2 assumptions least favorable to its economic performance: relatively late testing and a risk equation that identifies a lower-risk population. Age 45 is a late start. Genetic risk needs time to act, and the length of that window is what generates benefit. The PCE compounds this. It has since been retired by the 2026 guideline in favor of PREVENT-ASCVD,9 and it places in the intermediate-risk band a population with a lower annual event rate than PREVENT does (0.010 vs 0.0164), leaving less absolute benefit to gain from treatment. Relax either assumption and the ICER falls by more than half.

The useful question is not whether PRS-guided statin therapy is cost-effective, but at what age and against which risk equation. Ooi et al6 answer the least favorable version and still finish below threshold, a stronger result than the base case alone conveys.

Where the Analysis Is Vulnerable

The authors are transparent about limitations. Four limitations bear on how the results should be read.

The first concerns where the numbers came from. Two inputs drive the model: the extra risk that a high score confers (HR: 1.62) and how well statins work in that group (relative risk: 0.56).10 Both were measured in mostly European populations, as was the score itself, an important limitation in a study motivated by Singapore's ethnic diversity. Substituting an HR from a large Chinese cohort11 raised the ICER to SGD 49,724 without changing the conclusion, which is reassuring but incomplete. When a score performs less well in a population, the problem is not simply a smaller HR; the score sorts patients less accurately, and sorting patients is the strategy.

The second is the size of the benefit: 0.012 QALYs over 40 years, about 4 to 5 extra days of healthy life, with a 95% uncertainty interval (−0.062 to 0.087 QALYs) that includes no benefit and a 53.5% probability of cost-effectiveness. Small per-person gains are the currency of prevention, so this is not disqualifying. But these figures belong to the base case, and the authors report no probabilistic results for the more favorable scenarios, so we cannot tell how much of the uncertainty belongs to PRS and how much to the configuration they chose.

The third explains why PRS looks harmful in the early years. The model applies to a small quality-of-life penalty for a daily tablet, charged to every treated patient every year, while benefits appear only later. That is defensible, but the early harm here is the nuisance of a pill, not clinical harm.

The fourth is the choice of threshold. The top quintile is conventional but ultimately arbitrary, and the most consequential policy lever, determining how many are genotyped and treated. Threshold optimization would have made the result actionable. Nor was subgroup analysis by sex or ethnicity performed, in a study whose premise is ethnic heterogeneity.

Where the Field Should Go Next

Multi-ancestry PRS development and local validation, through SG100K (Precision Health Research Singapore 100,000) and comparable efforts,12,13 remain the rate-limiting steps. Without calibration and percentile-based reporting in the intended population, economic projections remain provisional.14 Economic models should treat genotype data as a 1-time, reusable asset whose value extends beyond cardiovascular prevention to pharmacogenomics and cancer risk.

Models should also place PRS alongside the tools it will work with: coronary artery calcium (CAC), lipoprotein(a), and apolipoprotein B. PRS and CAC are complementary rather than rival, because PRS informs lifelong susceptibility whereas CAC quantifies disease already accrued.15,16 Sequencing them would serve clinicians better than modeling alone.

Finally, the therapeutic landscape cuts both ways. Patients flagged as high risk by PRS are those in whom clinicians will be reluctant to accept a statin-only result, and earlier combination therapy may reduce their cumulative exposure to low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).17 Future models should therefore assume a treatment sequence, a statin first with add-on therapy where goals are not met, rather than 1 drug held constant for 40 years.

What This Means for Practice

My reading is more affirmative than the base case alone suggests, and the right patients are already identifiable. They are in their thirties or forties, with a family history of premature disease, or with moderate LDL-C and a risk profile that looks reassuring on a 10-year calculator that will not justify treatment for another 2 decades. In them a score changes the conversation, because it speaks to lifetime rather than short-term risk. Identifying them early is the ambition of precision medicine, and PRS is the only tool that can do so decades in advance.5,16

Conclusions

Ooi et al6 frame their result cautiously, and prudence is warranted, but caution is not a negative finding. Their model shows that in the right patient, and over a long enough time frame, PRS-guided therapy delivers value well within accepted thresholds, even under conservative assumptions.

One qualification now matters more than when the model was built. With the 2026 guideline setting stricter LDL-C goals, more of the patients' PRS identifies who will need treatment beyond a statin, and ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, PCSK9 inhibitors, and small interfering RNA agents cost many times what atorvastatin does. That does not weaken the case for testing. It raises the premium on testing accurately, because the cost of treating the wrong patient rises with the price of treatment. Prevention pays most when it is aimed well and given time to work. Precision prevention is a long game, and the horizon that makes it pay lies within a patient's lifetime.
