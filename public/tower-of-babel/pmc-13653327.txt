Selective control of HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 transcription through DR/DQ super enhancer microelements
Authors: Parimal Majumder, Joshua Lee, Royce Butler, Christopher D. Scharer, Jeremy M. Boss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMHC-II gene expression requires promoter-proximal and distal organizing elements. A super enhancer located between the HLA-DR and -DQ genes was dissected into five microdomains (regions A-E) to define its mechanism of action. Regions A and B were required for maximal expression of HLA-DQA1. Region A facilitated HLA-DQA1 expression and substituted for region B in its absence, as well as mediating longer-range interactions with distal CTCF sites. Region D modulated HLA-DRB1 through specific 3D chromatin interactions, whereas a local insulator element (XL9) broadly interacted with the SE. Through deletion of HLA-DRB1 proximal-promoter elements, HLA-DQ expression increased by co-opting interactions with region D, indicating that competition between microelements regulates the absolute levels of MHC-II genes. Together, these data define an additional layer of control for MHC-II genes encoded in one of the most polymorphic and disease-associated regions of the human genome.

INTRODUCTION

The human major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) locus contains a dense cluster of highly polymorphic genes encoding the alpha and beta chains of the classical MHC-II molecules, HLA-DR, -DQ and -DP. MHC-II proteins present peptide antigens to CD4+ T cells, thereby initiating adaptive and humoral immune responses.1-3 MHC-II genes are co-regulated and expressed in a cell-type-specific manner, with constitutive expression in B-lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells (reviewed in).3,4 Non-lymphoid/myeloid cells express MHC-II genes following IFN-γtreatment,5 enabling these cells to present antigens and modulate immune responses.

Promoter proximal, conserved DNA sequences, termed X1, X2, and Y boxes, are essential for MHC-II expression. The X1, X2, and Y box elements bind the RFX complex, CREB, and NF-Y, respectively (reviewed in).6 These DNA-binding factors interact with each other to recruit the class II transactivator, CIITA. CIITA is expressed constitutively in B cells and various antigen-presenting cells and can be induced by IFNγ, explaining the general mechanism by which IFNγ controls MHC-II expression.7,8 Together, this complex recruits chromatin modifiers and the general transcription machinery, leading to expression of MHC-II genes.6,9 CIITA recruitment is essential for expression and the placement of active chromatin marks on MHC-II promoter region nucleosomes.9-12

CCCTC binding factor (CTCF) has been shown to insulate regions of regulatory activity within the genome by functioning as an enhancer blocker13,14 or by preventing the spread of heterochromatin into active genes.13,15-17 CTCF functions in part by forming the nexus of long-range chromatin loops that organize chromatin into higher-order structures.18 In addition, through the binding and loop extrusion action of cohesin subunits, some CTCF binding sites function to orchestrate interactions between gene promoters and distal enhancer elements.19 Previously, we identified a series of CTCF-binding sites that surrounded each major MHC-II α/β gene pair, suggesting that they were part of the evolutionary duplication process and necessary for efficient MHC-II expression.20 Indeed, CTCF binding and cohesin were found to be required for maximal expression of all MHC-II genes.20,21 XL9, a CTCF site located in the intergenic region between HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA122 formed chromatin interactions with the promoter regions of these genes.23 The interactions were dependent upon the presence of both CTCF and CIITA. CTCF depletion also decreased HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 expression and reduced histone acetylation at the promoters.22,23 CIITA and CTCF co-immunoprecipitated, suggesting that the two factors interacted directly or indirectly through a complex.21 These observations demonstrated a role for CTCF in regulating HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQB1 expression and in orchestrating the spatial and topological architecture of these genes.

Intriguingly, multiple human diseases have been linked to genetic variation in the HLA-DRB1/DQA1 intergenic region, including those in Vitiligo24 and SLE.25 Additionally, the levels of histone H3K27 acetylation in the HLA-DR/DQ intergenic region were found to be strongly associated with SLE.26 These findings and our previous observations raise the question of how this intergenic region could control MHC-II expression and contribute to human diseases.

Super enhancers (SEs) are characterized by their structural positions and sizes, dense associations of modified histones, particularly H3K27ac, transcription factors and co-factors, and strong interactions with specific gene promoters.27 They are also associated with high numbers of disease-associated SNPs.28,29 Based upon bioinformatic analyses, two SEs were identified in the intervening sequences between HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 in B cells.27 Although one of these maps to XL9, the other was not previously characterized. Deletion of this SE resulted in reduced expression of HLA-DRB1 and -DQA1, as well as CIITA binding and active histone modifications at their promoters.30 It was termed the DR/DQ-SE.30 XL9 and DR/DQ-SE interacted with each other, suggesting that this region was responsible for globally controlling the spatial architecture of the region. Further analyses through ATAC-seq and histone ChIP-seq data suggested that the DR/DQ-SE may have distinct features and functions. To determine if this is the case and understand how the SE functioned, the region was subdivided into 5 microdomains (regions A-E), and multiple CRISPR/Cas9 mutants were generated and analyzed. The results defined at least four microdomains, with two specifically focused on driving full expression of either HLA-DRB1 or HLA-DQA1; another was the main element for XL9 interaction; and the fourth functioned to facilitate or substitute for the actions of region B in driving HLA-DQA1 expression. Thus, while MHC-II genes are coordinately regulated by their promoter proximal regulatory regions, microdomains within the SE fine-tune MHC-II gene expression and provide a mechanism for varying antigen presentation across the human population.

RESULTS

Identification of microdomains within the DR/DQ-SE

Human MHC-II genes are regulated by a specific set of transcription factors (RFX, CREB, NF-Y, and CIITA) and homologous promoter proximal cis-regulatory elements (X-Y boxes), yet their expression is considerably different. RNA-seq of the Burkitt lymphoma B cell line Raji,31 a model line used to study MHC-II transcription,10,22,32 showed a >20-fold difference in steady-state transcripts across all MHC-II genes (Figure 1A). One reason for this may be the strength of CIITA binding to the proximal promoter X-Y box regions. We previously defined a motif model for the X-Y box from CIITA-ChIP-seq data that incorporated its spacing requirements.33 To estimate CIITA binding potential, we used this model and scored how well each of the HLA-DR and HLA-DQ gene proximal promoter and DR/DQ-SE X-Y boxes matched this consensus sequence (Figure 1B). HLA-DRA, which is expressed at the highest levels, had the top X-Y box score. Although the other genes showed similar X-Y box scores, there was no obvious direct correlation with expression, suggesting that other features may play a role in their regulation. Intriguingly, two X-Y boxes with lower X-Y box scores also resided in the DR/DQ-SE. Previous studies with the DR/DQ-SE suggested a role for CIITA,30 suggesting the possibility that differences in expression may lie within the ability to use a novel regulatory element: the DR/DQ-SE.

Previously, an 18.5 kb region encompassing the DR/DQ-SE (Figure 1C) was deleted from Raji cells, resulting in reduced expression of both HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 genes, as well as a reduced ability to stimulate T cells in a mixed lymphocyte reaction.30 Additionally, each of the above promoters interacted with the DR/DQ-SE in a CIITA-dependent manner. To identify potentially functional regions within the SE in Raji cells, H3K27ac ChIP-seq data were analyzed33 in conjunction with other epigenetic/chromatin data from a number of sources, including GM12878 (a B cell lymphoblastoid line) and ATAC-seq from resting nä ive (rN) primary human B cells34 and dendritic cells.35-37 (Figure 1D). A strong correlation of H3K27ac enrichment within the SE was observed. Additional chromatin marks, including H3K4me1 and eRNAs,37 were found (Figure 1D). Previous CIITA ChIP-seq data33 revealed two CIITA-binding regions (Figure 1D) that resided in the highest H3K27ac peaks in the DR/DQ-SE.33 To test different regions of the DR/DQ-SE for regulatory activity, the SE was subdivided into five microdomains based solely on the H3K27ac and ATAC-seq peaks and termed regions A, B, C, D, and E (Figure 1D). The SE was then subjected to CRISPR/Cas9 deletion mutagenesis/editing38,39 within the genome of Raji cells (Figure 1E).

To start, four progressive homozygous deletion mutants were generated (Figure S1): ΔA-E, a 10.7 kb deletion encompassing the entire DR/DQ-SE and spanning all active H3K27ac chromatin marks; ΔA-C, a 7.6 kb deletion encompassing regions A, B, and C; ΔA-B, a 5.7 kb deletion encompassing regions A and B; and ΔA, a 1.5 kb deletion encompassing only region A. Although Raji is heterozygous at its MHC, all CRISPR guides were chosen such that both alleles could be targeted and repaired using a target sequence that would ensure deletion between the guides. Between 25 and 58 homozygous clones, as determined/verified by PCR and/or Southern blot analysis, were isolated, expanded, and/or combined for downstream analysis (Figure S1).

Flow cytometry for HLA-DR and HLA-DQ surface expression and qRT-PCR for HLA-DRB1, -DQA1, and -DRA1, RFX5, and CIITA mRNA expression were performed on up to 30 randomly chosen homozygous clones from each of the above groups to account for clonal variation. For each mutant, individual flow cytometry histograms and the combined geometric mean fluorescent intensities (gMFI) were plotted (Figure 2A; Figure S2). The ΔA-E mutant clones showed modest but consistent losses in both HLA-DR and -DQ surface staining, confirming a role for the entire SE in controlling MHC-II expression.40 QRT-PCR of the clones confirmed that the decrease in surface expression was correlated directly with decreases in HLA-DRB1 and -DQA1 steady-state transcript levels (Figure 2B). HLA-DRA, RFX5, and CIITA mRNA expression did not change, demonstrating that their expression was not dependent on the DR/DQ-SE. The great majority of ΔA-C mutant clones displayed wild-type levels of HLA-DR surface expression and HLA-DRB1 RNA levels. In contrast, in all ΔA-C mutants, the levels of HLA-DQ were greatly reduced, as were the levels of HLA-DQA1 mRNA. The ΔAB and ΔA mutants also showed no changes in HLA-DR surface or HLA-DRB1 mRNA expression but instead displayed a marked decrease in HLA-DQ and HLA-DQA1 levels. Together, these data demonstrate that regions D or D-E are likely specific to the regulation of HLA-DRB1 and that region(s) A or A-B regulate HLA-DQA1.

Regions B, C, and D engage in higher-order chromatin interactions with the promoters of HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DRB1 and XL9

To define the molecular interactions between the DR/DQ-SE regions, MHC-II promoters, and XL9, a quantitative chromatin conformation capture (3C) assay developed previously for this system was employed on wild-type Raji cells.23,41 Three different restriction enzymes were used in separate 3C assays to derive a high-resolution interaction map. This approach divided the DR/DQ-SE into 14 overlapping restriction fragments that could be used to assess interactions between the promoter fragments and portions of the SE (Figure 3A). The restriction fragments were designated as follows: for BglII, B3, B5, B7, B9, B10, B14; PstI, P1, P4, P6, P8, P12; and EcoRI, E2, E11, E13 (Figure 3B). For each enzyme, unique restriction fragments that contained the promoters of HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, and XL9 were chosen40 and used as 3C anchors to test interactions between these features and the restriction fragments encoded in the DR/DQ-SE. In each 3C experiment, crosslinked (C) and non-crosslinked chromatin (NC) were used to demonstrate that observed interactions were dependent on crosslinking. Additionally, the self-ligated product that formed at the anchor fragment (Figure 3C, gray highlight) was used to confirm comparable restriction digestion and ligation efficiency for crosslinked and non-crosslinked samples. Multiple interactions across the SE were observed (Figure 3C). The HLA-DRB1 promoter region anchors showed interactions with DR/DQ-SE fragments B14, P8, and E11, which encompass region D. Fragments principally including regions A, B, C, and E showed no interactions with the HLA-DRB1 promoter, implying that region D interacts with HLA-DRB1. As expected,30 HLA-DRB1 promoter interactions with XL9 and the HLA-DQA1 promoter were also observed and validated in these assays. XL9 anchor fragments showed broad interactions with many of the DR/DQ-SE restriction fragments encompassing regions B, C, and D, including B5, B7, B9, B10, P1, P4, P6, P8, and E11. Interactions with regions A (B3 and P1) or E (E13 and P12) were not observed, suggesting that XL9 interacts with regions B, C, and D. The HLA-DQA1 promoter interacted with region B restriction fragments, including B5, B7, P1, and E11 (Figure 3C). To compare/summarize the critical interaction positions across experiments, seven restriction fragments with clear interactions were chosen; their 3C values were normalized and then plotted in summary plots in the linear order that they would occur in the SE (Figure 3D).

To verify that these results occurred in other B cells, the GM12878 B cell line, which has been sequenced and used extensively,37 was examined. GM12878 cells express both HLA-DR and DQ surface proteins, as well as HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 transcripts, although these are at lower levels than Raji cells (Figures S3A and S3B). 3C analysis using a subset of the interacting fragments, including region B (B5 and B7), region D (P8), and control (B3), was performed with each promoter and XL9 (Figure S3C). The results confirmed that the HLA-DRB1 promoter interacted with region D and that the HLA-DQA1 promoter interacted with region B. XL9 interacted with both promoters of the genes, as well as regions B, C, and D in a manner that was similar to Raji cells. As with Raji cells, interactions between the HLA-DRB1 and -DQA1 promoter regions were also observed.

To address whether the key interactions between the promoters and regions B and D occur in primary B cells, 3C assays were performed on primary human peripheral blood B cells. Due to the sequence polymorphisms, a limited set was examined. 3C fragments B5 and B14, which span regions B and D, respectively, recapitulated the data from Raji and GM12878 B cell lines (Figure S3D). Interactions between 3C products were further verified by sequencing of the 3C amplicons. Together, these 3C analyses provide a quantitative map of DR/DQ-SE interactions with neighboring MHC-II promoters and reveal a complex pattern, with the HLA-DRB1 promoter interacting with region D; the HLA-DQA1 promoter with region B; and XL9 with regions B, C, and D. Moreover, these interactions are universal across human cell lines and primary B cells.

Regions B and D are required for interactions with MHC-II promoters

To validate the specificity of interactions between MHC-II promoters and the respective microdomains within the DR/DQ-SE, CRISRP/Cas9 mutants encompassing regions B and D were generated and analyzed (Figure 1D). Twenty and 37 ΔB and ΔD region homozygous mutants were obtained, and thirteen and ten, respectively, were analyzed by flow cytometry and qRT-PCR (Figure 2; Figures S1 and S2). Although there was some variability in the clones isolated, ΔB mutants showed a decrease in HLA-DQ and HLA-DQA1 expression and wild-type levels of HLA-DR and HLA-DRB1. In contrast, ΔD mutants showed modest decreases in HLA-DR and HLA-DRB1 expression but no changes in HLA-DQ or HLA-DQA1 expression. 3C assays were conducted to determine if new interactions appeared in the absence of regions B and D (Figure 4). The results showed that no new promoter interactions with the SE occurred in the absence of either region B or D. This was also the case for interactions between XL9 and the DR/DQ-SE. Along with the genetic data above, these data demonstrate that regions B and D are specific to the regulation of HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DRB1, respectively.

Region A facilitates interactions between HLA-DQA1 and the locus

Above, we showed that deletion of regions A, B, or both reduced expression of HLA-DQA1 to a similar extent, suggesting that both regions were essential for HLA-DQA1 expression (Figure 2). However, whereas there were clear interactions between the HLA-DQA1 promoter and XL9 with region B, such interactions were absent with region A (3C fragments B3 and E2, which extend into region B) (Figure 3), implying that another mechanism may be operating. This raised the question as to whether region A could influence other interactions within the locus. Thus, the chromatin interaction pattern in the absence of region A (ΔA) was examined. In ΔA mutants, no changes were observed for interactions between the HLA-DRB1 promoter and region D (3C fragment P8) (Figure 4). In contrast, a striking loss of interactions between the HLA-DQA1 promoter and region B (3C fragments B5 and B7) was observed in ΔA mutants (Figure 4). Additionally, decreased interactions between XL9 and regions B (B5) and C (B9) were observed. These results demonstrate that region A facilitates HLA-DQA1 promoter and XL9 interactions with the DR/DQ-SE.

MHC-II promoters and CTCF insulators, including XL9, interact, forming a complex chromatin architecture.20 One way in which region A may have an effect is by altering the overall architecture of the locus. To test this, additional 3C reactions were performed with the MHC-II insulators C1, XL9, and C2. In wild-type Raji cells, the HLA-DRB1 promoter interacted with C1, XL9, and C2; and XL9 interacted with both C1 and C2 as expected (Figures 5A and 5B). Although there was a slight decrease in the ΔA mutant for interactions between HLA-DRB1 and C1, this was not statistically significant. In contrast, in the absence of region A, HLA-DQA1 promoter interactions with XL9 and C2 were reduced, suggesting that region A facilitates these interactions.

To examine the role of region A in the absence of the rest of the DR/DQ-SE, a deletion spanning B-E (ΔB-E) was created (Figure 1E). Expectedly, HLA-DR and HLA-DRB1 expression was reduced in this mutant as region D was missing (Figure 2). Surprisingly, HLA-DQ and HLA-DQA1 expression was normal. 3C analysis was performed and showed decreases in HLA-DRB1 promoter interactions with XL9, C1, and C2 (Figure 5B). Interactions between XL9 and the C1 and C2 insulators were also decreased. The loss of these interactions is likely due to a loss of regions C and D within the ΔB-E mutant. While interactions between the HLA-DQA1 promoter and C1 and XL9 were reduced in ΔB-E cells, new interactions with region A (3C fragment E2) were now present.

To further understand how region A is contributing to HLA-DQA1 expression, traditional H3K27ac ChIP assays were performed on the ΔA and ΔB-E mutants described above. The results showed that in the absence of region A (ΔA), H3K27ac modifications were reduced at the promoter of HLA-DQA1, region B, region C, and XL9, providing a mechanistic role for region A in promoting interactions with region B and enhancing H3K27ac at the HLA-DQA1 promoter (Figure 5C). A slight decrease in the HLA-DRB1 promoter region was observed. No changes were observed at regions D and E. Deletion of B-E (ΔB-E), resulted in reduced H3K27ac at HLA-DRB1 and XL9, corresponding with the reduced interactions and expression of HLA-DRB1. In contrast, H3K27ac at region A was enriched in the ΔB-E mutant, providing a mechanism for how region A can substitute for region B. Thus, in the absence of regions B-E, region A is a compensatory functional element that redirects the overall chromatin architecture, resulting in the restoration of HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DQ expression.

Deletion of the HLA-DRB1 promoter leads to elevated HLA-DQA1 expression through an additional chromatin interaction with region D

The above 3C data associated with wild-type Raji cells suggest distinct preferences for MHC-II gene promoters to interact with one DR/DQ-SE region versus another. This raises two questions. Does this preference govern the expression levels of the two genes, and is there a competition for one region versus another? To answer these questions, mutations deleting the X-Y box regions located in the proximal promoters of HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 genes were constructed (termed ΔDRB1 and ΔDQA1, respectively). At least 3 independent clones were isolated (Figure S1) and tested as above.

Flow cytometry confirmed that both mutants lost expression of their respective genes when compared to Raji and RJ2.2.5 (CIITA-deficient) cells (Figures 6A-6C). Surprisingly, deletion of the HLA-DRB1 promoter-X-Y boxes showed significantly increased HLA-DQ expression, whereas deletion of the HLA-DQA1 promoter had no reciprocal effect on HLA-DR expression. Analysis of steady-state mRNA expression revealed a complete loss of HLA-DRB1 or HLA-DQA1 expression in their respective mutant cell lines. Intriguingly, the ΔDRB1 mutant exhibited a near 50% increase in HLA-DQA1 expression, confirming the results obtained by flow cytometry. No reciprocal increase in HLA-DRB1 in the ΔDQA1 mutant was observed, nor were changes in the expression of the HLA-DRA1 gene or expression of the essential transcription factors CIITA and RFX5 (Figures 6B and 6C). The increased HLA-DQA1 expression in the ΔDRB1 mutant suggests the possibility that there is, in fact, a competition between the genes for a regulatory element or factor. To determine if the lack of the HLA-DRB1 promoter results in a reorganization of HLA-DQA1 promoter-DR/DQ-SE interactions, 3C assays were performed on the ΔDRB1 and ΔDQA1 mutants (Figure 6D). Strikingly, we observed two distinct patterns of interactions in the mutants. In the ΔDRB1 mutant, the HLA-DQA1 promoter showed new interactions with P8 (region D) and increased interactions with B5 (region B). In the ΔDQA1 mutant, 3C analysis detected expected interactions between the HLA-DRB1 promoter and P8, albeit at a reduced level compared to wild-type cells. No significant changes were observed using the XL9 anchor in either mutant (ΔDRB1 or ΔDQA1). Overall, these data demonstrate that the increased expression of HLA-DQA1 in the ΔDRB1 mutant correlates with enhanced interactions, including a novel engagement with region D. In contrast, the lack of change in HLA-DRB1 expression in the ΔDQA1 mutant corresponds to the absence of additional chromatin interactions. These data argue that there is competition for region D interactions and that such interactions could lead to higher levels of gene expression.

DR/DQ-SE CIITA sites do not contribute to MHC-II expression

Regions B and D each contain an X-Y box motif that binds CIITA that reside in locations that have high levels of chromatin accessibility and are in regions that are surrounded by peaks in H3K27ac (Figure 1D). Computational scores for CIITA binding indicated that while the region D site was twice the score of the region B site, both were weaker than each of the promoters (Figure 1B). To examine if these sites participate in regulating HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1, CRISPR/Cas9 deletions encompassing the X-Y box sequences of regions B and D (104 and 105bp, respectively; Figure S1) were constructed and tested (termed B−XY and D−XY). Four mutants were isolated for each. The deletions resulted in a loss of CIITA binding at their respective sites as expected (Figures S4A and S4B). Assessment of H3K27ac at these regions showed a decrease for the sites in region D, but not in region B (p = 0.09), suggesting that these regions contribute in part to the acetylation of the SE (Figure S4B). However, flow cytometry and qRT-PCR analyses showed no differences in steady-state surface or mRNA levels of MHC-II genes or CIITA and RFX5 transcripts (Figures S4C and S4D). Moreover, 3C assays were conducted to determine if there were changes in the interactions between the MHC-II promoters and the regions in these mutants (Figure S4E). No significant differences were observed. Thus, despite potential contributions to chromatin modifications, these sites do not appear to regulate these MHC-II genes.

IFNγ induces specific interactions within the DR/DQ-SE

Expression of MHC-II genes in non-immune cell types is induced by IFNγ through the induction and expression of CIITA.5,42 The human epithelial tumor cell line A-431 is highly responsive to IFNγ treatment, resulting in the induction of CIITA and subsequent MHC-II gene expression.23,43 Previously, we showed that in response to IFNγ, the promoter regions of HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 genes and DR/DQ-SE gain active histone modifications (H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and H3K4me3), suggesting that the DR/DQ-SE was active following IFNγ treatment and CIITA induction.23,40,43 To determine if the DR/DQ-SE was functioning in a similar manner, A-431 cells were treated with IFNγ, and flow cytometry and qRT-PCR were performed. As expected, flow cytometry showed an increase in HLA-DR and -DQ expression at 48 and further increases at 72 h following IFNγ treatment (Figure S5A). HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, and CIITA mRNA also showed proportional increases at the two IFNγ-induced time points (Figure S5B). 3C assays were also conducted following 72 h of IFNγ treatment. HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 promoters and XL9 region fragments were used as anchors, with specific interacting regions B (B5, B7), region D (P8), and XL9 fragments, as well as control fragments (B3) (Figures S5C and S5D). The results revealed that the HLA-DRB1 promoter interacts with region D (P8) and that the HLA-DQA1 promoter interacts with region B (B5) in a manner analogous to Raji and GM12878 cells, but only under IFNγ-induced conditions. Increased interactions between the HLA-DQA1 promoter and B7 were observed compared to Raji and GM12879 cells. Similar interactions between XL9 and the MHC-II promoters and between the promoters were also detected under IFNγ-induced conditions. Thus, these results demonstrate that specific interactions with microdomains within the DR/DQ-SE are universally used to regulate MHC-II gene expression under constitutive and inducible expression conditions.

DISCUSSION

The MHC-II locus represents one of the most polymorphic regions in the human genome, a feature that underscores its essential role in adaptive immunity. Highlighting its potential as a major regulatory hub for MHC-II gene expression, the DR/DQ-SE was ranked 1st of 689 SE in CD19+ B cells, 49th of 971 in CD20+ B cells, and 98th of 258 in the GM12878 cell line, based on H3K27ac signal intensity.27 Here, targeted deletion mutagenesis and 3C analyses were used to understand how it functions using the B cell line Raji,31 which has served as a model for understanding MHC-II expression.20,23,30 Although functioning differently, regions A and B were required for HLA-DQA1 expression, whereas region D was important for maximal expression of HLA-DRB1. The mechanism for regions B and D was related to their ability to interact directly with the promoters of the genes that they regulate (Figure 7). Additionally, these regions were able to interact with the CTCF XL9 region, which was previously described as an SE.27,40 Region A was found to be distinct in its mechanism of action. In the absence of region A, expression from HLA-DQA1 was reduced; interactions between region B and HLA-DQA1 were decreased, as were interactions between region C and XL9. When only region A was present, HLA-DQA1 expression was similar to wild-type. This paradox was found to be the result of novel interactions between region A and HLA-DQA1, XL9, and C2 that occurred in the absence of the rest of the SE. Additionally, region A’s presence was critical for full histone H3K27ac activation marks at regions B and C, XL9, and HLA-DQA1. This implies that region A has the capacity to facilitate an active chromatin structure in the locus with respect to HLA-DQA1 transcription when these other elements are missing. Also notable is the fact that no specific ATAC peak in B cells or dendritic cells was observed over region A, suggesting that the region by itself does not alter chromatin accessibility and may not have transcription factors bound in the wild-type state. Together, this suggests that region A’s activity is overshadowed in the full context of the other elements. No activity for region E was found. Even though the detailed mutagenesis experiments were performed in Raji cells, the universality of the above microdomain interactions was demonstrated further through the analyses in the B cell line GM12878, primary human peripheral B cells, and through the induction of MHC-II expression in an epithelial cell line A-431 by IFN-γ. Thus, four of the DR/DQ-SE subelements have unique functions in modulating MHC-II transcription.

The dissection of SEs in other systems has revealed that they are clusters of regulatory elements with some of their elements functioning as classical enhancers, while other elements have novel functions. For example, the mouse Vsx2 SE has elements that differentially regulate its expression in retinal progenitor cells and bipolar neurons.44 The Wap SE, which is progressively regulated during lactation and responsive to STAT5, showed that its sub-elements functioned differentially.45 The term facilitator was recently used to describe specific sub-elements within the SE of the mouse α-globin gene. The α-globin facilitators had no intrinsic enhancer activity but were instead required for the full activity of the other elements within the SE, including mediator recruitment and histone H3K27ac. Region A may have some of this activity in that it was required for the full activity of region B in modulating HLA-DQA1 transcription, including histone acetylation and interactions with XL9 and the HLA-DQA1 promoter. Regions B and D appear to function as enhancerlike elements, although this has not been formally tested. Region C appears to promote interactions with XL9 and may be important for organizing the chromatin structure into an active form.

Our previous data suggested that the MHC-II locus was organized into several higher-order chromatin structures in B cells.30 A first-order set of structures is formed by CTCF-CTCF interactions across the locus in which CTCF sites were found encompassing all MHC-II α/β gene pairs.20 The CTCF elements C1, XL9, and C2 represent such elements in the HLA-DR and -DQ loci. Similar interactions were conserved in the mouse class II locus.46 These interactions may allow for coordinate regulation of MHC-II α/β gene pairs. Intriguingly, in mouse plasma cells, which do not express MHC-II genes, all interactions were altered, suggesting that this first-order structure is linked with MHC-II expression. A second order of interactions is CIITA-dependent and involves the promoters of MHC-II genes interacting with the local CTCF sites: C1, XL9, and C2.20 A third order interaction is demonstrated herein with the MHC-II promoters interacting with microdomains within the DR/DQ-SE that function to regulate final expression levels. Together, we hypothesize that the CTCF-promoter interactions provide a mechanism to bring the SE regulatory elements in close proximity to each promoter to help achieve maximal transcription. A mechanism for this action likely involves the cohesin complex, which was shown to associate with MHC-II CTCF sites and was important for MHC-II expression.21 The SMC1 and SMC3 subunits of the cohesin complex are ATPase-driven motors that aid in the formation of chromatin loops as well as the ability to extrude DNA.47,48 While the ability to extrude DNA in vivo is debated,48,49 chromatin capture and loop formation is likely a major part of the mechanism by which distal and proximal regulatory elements regulate genes. Here, such activity would bring all known MHC-II regulatory regions to MHC-II promoters.

As one integrates the three different orders of interactions associated with MHC-II genes, the question regarding whether the interactions are all concurrent or are dynamic arises. In this regard, one possibility is that the three sets of interactions could bring all elements into a single hub coordinating simultaneous expression of all MHC-II genes. Alternative possibilities include the concept that some, if not all, the interactions observed are transient and rearrange to allow expression of each MHC-II gene. Two sets of data support the latter possibility. Fluorescence in situ hybridization assays on HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 transcripts in Raji cells showed that the HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 genes were not actively transcribed all the time.23 Although all cells expressed at least one HLA-DRB1 allele, all other combinations of transcriptional activity of the two genes were present, with some cells expressing no HLA-DQA1 transcripts, despite all cells being positive for both surface HLA-DR and HLA-DQ protein. While this could suggest that there is a competition for transcription factors and RNA polymerase II, another interpretation is that the right chromatin loop formation is required and that interactions with the DR/DQ-SE are critical for expression. The latter would imply that the interactions observed are transient in nature. Several studies have examined the dynamics of CTCF and cohesin using super-resolution live-cell imaging. In a study examining the Fbn2 topological associated domains (TADs) in embryonic stem cells, the authors found that CTCF-cohesin loops were dynamic, served to extrude DNA, and lasted 10–30 min50 In another study, CTCF and cohesin were found to have distinct but quite short residence times on DNA, with CTCF between 1 and 2 min and cohesin at ~22 min51 The reassociation of CTCF with its sites was more rapid than cohesin. Although these studies examined TADs and major chromatin loops, the data strongly suggest that the formation of these structures is very dynamic. For at least the first-order interactions between MHC-II CTCF sites, this could mean that multiple architectural conformations exist and, in a single cell, these may be different on its sister chromosomes. The 3C data collected here record all the interactions across all the cells; thus, it is a blended account and cannot distinguish individual cells or alleles. Given the potential dynamics of transcription factor occupancy and loop dynamics, all combinations of interactions observed are therefore possible over time.

In the absence of the HLA-DRB1 promoter, HLA-DQA1 expression increased, and the HLA-DQA1 promoter was now able to interact with region D. This suggests that region D is transcriptionally more active and a potentially favored site for MHC-II transcription. This conclusion is further supported by several results. The first is the fact that HLA-DRB1 did not interact with region B when the HLA-DQA1 promoter/X-Y box region was deleted, indicating that additional interactions with region B were not needed. Additionally, accessible chromatin and H3K27ac values were highest in region D within the SE. Previous ChIP-seq identified CIITA binding sites within both region B and D, as well as X-Y box homologous regions33; although these sequences had much lower scores than the promoter regions (Figure 1B). Surprisingly, while deletion of these X-Y boxes resulted in the loss of CIITA binding and, for region D, a significant reduction in histone acetylation in the region, their loss did not alter transcription of either of the MHC-II genes. This suggests that other factors regulate the activity of these elements. Sequence homology searches revealed the presence of RELB and IKAROS binding sites within the ATAC peaks of the SE. However, additional evidence for their role is not known and is the subject of future investigation.

Genetic variants underlying complex diseases are often within non-coding gene regulatory elements.52,53 Many autoimmune diseases are associated with genetic variation in the HLA-DR and -DQ genes.54,55 Although polymorphisms across the coding regions of the HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 genes are well documented, it is intriguing that an even greater number appear within the SE.30 As mentioned, in SLE, a clear association of genetic variation in XL9 that correlated with MHC-II expression in monocyte-derived macrophages25 was observed, as was an association with histone acetylation patterns with SLE HLA-risk alleles.26 These observations suggest that genetic and epigenetic variation may alter DR/DQ-SE function, particularly through region B and region D, thereby modulating the levels of MHC-II gene expression and influencing the efficiency and robustness of cells to present antigens.

In summary, the MHC-II DR/DQ-SE is a complex regulatory element with distinct microdomains that regulate HLA-DRB1 or HLA-DQA1 expression and govern complex spatial/architectural interactions across the entire locus. Thus, while all MHC-II genes are coordinately regulated by their promoter proximal regulatory regions, these data provide evidence for independent MHC-II regulation of antigen presentation and add an additional layer of control that involves one of the most polymorphic and disease-associated regions of the human genome. The genetic variation of the microdomains within this SE in regulating MHC-II expression may ultimately determine the ability to protect individuals from infection versus autoimmunity.

Limitations to the study

Although interactions were found to be comparable across multiple cell sources, the mutations were made in only one B cell line, limiting some of the conclusions, as haplotype differences are likely to dictate how or the magnitude by which each of the microelements is used. While we screened for many interactions, each was a one-to-one comparison. Thus, it is not clear how many interactions are occurring simultaneously or whether they were occurring in each cell. If developed with enough sensitivity for short distances, a single-cell 3C or multi-C approach could address this. Additionally, we could not determine how CIITA functions in driving these interactions, as deletion of its binding sites within the DR/DQ-SE did not result in a phenotype. It is possible that other transcription factors function to interact with the CIITA-bound promoters or that in CIITA’s absence, MHC-II promoters are inactive.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

The Burkitt’s lymphoma B cell line Raji31 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (CCL-86). RJ2.2.5 was generously provided by Dr. R. Accolla, University of Insubria, Varese, Italy.56 This cell line was derived from Raji cells following mutagenesis and loss of MHC-II gene expression due to deletion mutations within the CIITA gene.56-59 GM12878 B cells were obtained from Coriell Institute (www.coriell.org). The cells were not tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Primary human peripheral blood B cells were isolated from three deidentified healthy donors recruited through the “Phlebotomy of healthy adults for the purpose of evaluation and validation of immune response assays” protocol of Emory Vaccine Center. The Emory University Institutional Review board approved this protocol (CR004-IRB00045821). All donors provided their consent. Because they were deidentified, no information is available.

METHOD DETAILS

Cell culture and purification

Raji, RJ2.2.5, and GM12878 cells were grown in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (Sigma F2442), 5% bovine calf serum (Hyclone SH30073.03), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 4.5 g/L glucose, 1.0 mM sodium pyruvate, and 10 mM HEPES. A-431 (ATCC, CRL-1555) is an epithelial carcinoma cell line that is negative for MHC-II expression. A-431 cells were grown in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum and the above supplements. Where indicated, A-431 cells were treated with 100 U/mL IFN-γ for 48 and 72 h to induce CIITA and MHC-II gene expression.5,23,43 Human PBMCs were purified using Ficoll density-gradient centrifugation. Total B cells were subsequently isolated from PBMCs using the STEMCELL Technologies Human Pan B Cell Enrichment Kit (Cat#19554) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

Cells were harvested, washed twice with FACS buffer (PBS with 1% BSA and 2 mM EDTA) and resuspended at 106 cells/mL in the same buffer. Cells were stained for 1 h with anti-HLA-DR (PE) (BD Pharmingen, Cat. 555812) and anti-HLA-DQ (APC) (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. 130–104–497) antibody cocktails, followed by fixation in 1% paraformaldehyde. Flow-cytometric data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X-20 with FACSDiva software and analyzed with FlowJo 10.8.2. Student’s t tests were used to determine significance.

CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis

Two approaches using CRISPR/Cas9 technologies were employed to generate the mutants shown. For both approaches, at least two independent transfections of gRNA complexes were performed prior to the single cell sorting of individual clones. For the ΔB-E, a two-step PCR-based strategy was employed as described.60 Guide RNA (gRNA) sequences targeting the SE were designed using the CRISPR design tool (http://crispr.mit.edu/). The pX330-U6-Chimeric_BB-CBh-hSpCas9 plasmid (Addgene, 42230) served as template, a forward primer (U6) and a reverse primer containing the specific guide sequence and a portion of tracrRNA were used for the first PCR. The first PCR amplicon was used as template for the second PCR. The same forward primer (U6) from the first PCR and a new reverse primer containing full length tracrRNA were used to produce the final sgRNA expression cassette. The sgRNA expression cassette, a 90 bp synthetic oligonucleotide repair-homology arm and GFP reporter plasmid were co-transfected into the Raji cells using Nucleofector II (Lonza). All sequences, primers, and oligonucleotides are provided in Table S1.

All other mutant cell lines were created using the modified CRISPR/Cas9 method as follows. CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) containing guide RNAs specific to the targeted sequences were designed using the UCSC Genome Browser CRISPR/Cas9-NGG tool which integrates CRISPOR gRNA predictions.37 The crRNAs, TrueCut Cas9 Protein v2 (Invitrogen, A36499), and a pair of 45 bp sequences flanking either side of the targeted region (used as repair homology sequences) were gently mixed and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The resulting complex was co-transfected into Raji cells using the 4D-Nucleofector (Lonza). Four days post-transfection, GFP-positive cells or live cells (Zombie NIR dye negative, BioLegend, 423105) were single-cell sorted into 96-well plates. After three weeks, each well was screened for evidence of deletion by PCR. For all mutant clones analyzed, genomic PCR using internal and external primers (with respect to the target region were used to confirm the deletions. Some of the clones were analyzed by DNA sequencing. As indicated, for 3C and ChIP, up to 10 independent clones were randomly pooled into one sample (Figure S1).

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy RNA isolation kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA). cDNA synthesis was performed using Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, Inc., Carlsbad, CA) with 2 μg of total RNA in a 20 μL reaction volume in PCR II buffer containing 5 mM MgCl2 (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Following reverse transcription, cDNA was diluted to 200 μL with nuclease-free water and 3 μL of the diluted cDNA was used for quantitative real-time PCR. To control between samples, expression levels were normalized to 18s rRNA. PCR primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Student’s t tests were used to determine significance.

Quantitative 3C assay

A modified 3C assay protocol was performed as described previously.23,40,41 107 cells were washed in cold PBS and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Glycine (final concentration 125 mM) was added to stop the crosslinking reaction. Nuclei were isolated from the crosslinked cells, the DNA digested overnight at 37°C with EcoRI, PstI, or BglII, followed by heat inactivation of the enzymes at 65°C for 10 min. Samples were diluted 1:40 in ligation buffer and ligated overnight with T4 DNA ligase (20,000 NEB units) at 16°C. Proteinase K (final concentration to 200 μg/mL) was added to the ligation reactions and incubated overnight at 65°C to reverse the cross-links and digest the proteins. The DNA was purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. 3C products were quantified by real-time PCR using a five-point standard curve. Standard curve templates were generated in vitro by restriction enzyme digestion and ligation of purified genomic DNA, which was isolated from the cells used in the 3C assays. All primer sets (Table S1) were validated for >90% amplification efficiency prior to use in the 3C assay. Non-crosslinked chromatin and negative control restriction fragments were included to assess interaction specificity. Data were normalized as crosslinking and non-crosslinking frequencies relative to input ligated DNA used for the standard curve, which was the same amount for all primer sets. Due to sequence polymorphisms in primary human B cells associated with the region, additional sets of primers were tested and chosen for the 3C analysis of these cells and are listed in Table S1. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc corrections was used validate the significance.

Because the genomic sequence of A-431 cells was not available, primers were designed based on Raji cell sequences. To verify that these primers specifically amplified the corresponding regions in A-431 genomic DNA, DNA was first digested and religated and tested using the Raji derived primers. Primer specificity for A-431 genomic DNA was further confirmed by the standard curve generated with A-431 genomic DNA. Although the direct sequence for GM12878 is not available, SNPs information for this cell line is provided in the UCSC genome browser. Therefore, we substituted the B-cell genomic DNA sequences with the SNPs associated with GM12878 to reconstruct the MHC-II locus (HLA-DRB1-HLA-DQA1) specific to GM12878. From here, restriction sites were assessed and primers were designed for the fragments indicated.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were performed as described previously.10,40 Briefly, cells were cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min and a chromatin lysate was prepared and sonicated to generate fragments averaging ~500 bp in length. Antibodies used included anti-H3K27Ac (Millipore Sigma. Cat. 07–360), or anti-IgG (Millipore Sigma, Cat. 12–370). Protein G magnetic beads (Invitrogen Cat#2019–12–31) (15 μL/sample) were used to isolate the chromatin-antibody complexes. Following washes, the immunoprecipitated chromatin was eluted in elution buffer (50mM NaHCO3 and 1% SDS) and incubated overnight at 65°C to reverse the formaldehyde-induced crosslinks. The DNA was purified and quantitated by real-time PCR using a five-point genomic DNA standard curve using a CFX96 Real-Time System, C1000 Thermal Cycler (BIO-RAD). PCR reactions contained 5% DMSO, 1xSYBR Green (Cambrex Cat#50513), 0.04% gelatin, 0.3% Tween 20, 50 mM KCl, 20 mM Tris [pH 8.3], 3 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTP, and 100 nM of each primer. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Student’s t tests were used to determine significance.

RNA-seq

Raji cells were lysed in RLT buffer (79216; Qiagen) supplemented with 1% 2-mercaptoethanol and RNA purified using the Zymo Quick-RNA MicroPrep Kit (11-328M; Zymo Research). cDNA was synthesized using the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (634894; Takara Bio) kit with 10ng of input RNA and 12 cycles of PCR. Final libraries were produced using 200 pg of cDNA as input for the NexteraXT kit (Illumina, FC-131–1024) with 12 cycles of PCR amplification. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 using a PE150 run. Fastq files were mapped to the hg38 version of the human genome with STAR v2.7.6a61 and the GENCODE reference transcriptome. Duplicate reads were flagged and removed using the PICARD markduplicates v2.23.8 function (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Reads mapping to exons for all ENTREZ genes were computed and normalized to reads per kilobase per million (RPKM) using GenomicRanges v1.38.0.61,62 Raji RNA-seq data was deposited in GEO, Accession Number GSE343577.

Bioinformatic analysis

Scripts generating the genome plots across the DR/DQ locus are available at https://github.com/cdschar or at Zenodo https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21921707 using previously published datasets as indicated. To generate X-Y box scores a previously generated motif model33 was used as input for the GLAM2Scan software63 to scan DNA sequences of CIITA bound regions in MHC-II gene promoters and the DR/DQ-SE. The location of the top annotated motif was further validated using RFX5 (ENCFF015ASS) and NFYB (ENCFF814DIE) ChIP-seq data from the ENCODE Consortium data portal in GM12878 cells.64

Raji H3K27ac ChiP-seq data was from our published data33 deposited in GEO GSE52941. Human primary B cell H3K27Ac and H3K4me1 ChIP-seq data was downloaded from ENCODE.65 Human primary B cell PRO-Cap seq enhancer RNA data was downloaded from ENCODE.37 Human plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) ATAC-seq data was downloaded from published study by Arroyo Hornero et.al.35 Human pDC histone ChIP-seq data on H3K27ac and H3K4me1 were downloaded from Pacis et.al.36 Primary B cell PRO-cap data was downloaded from ENCODE for enhancer RNA.37 Hg38 processed bigwig files were uploaded as a track-hub on the UCSC genome browser for visualization.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For all PCR based and flow cytometry assays, Student’s t tests were used to determine significance. For 3C assays, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc corrections was used validate the significance. p values less than 0.05 were considered significant.
