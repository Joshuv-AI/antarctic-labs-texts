The solitary skull mass: An atypical presentation of anaplastic large cell lymphoma
Authors: Max Gevorkian, Ellen Y Chang, Reza Assadsangabi, Juliana H Kim, Jack Harlan, Bryce D Beutler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a rare type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by neoplastic proliferation of CD30-positive T lymphocytes. Three distinct genotypic subtypes have been described, each of which demonstrates distinct biomolecular features and phenotypic manifestations. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-positive ALCL typically affects adolescents and young adults and has a favorable prognosis. ALK-negative ALCL is more common in middle-aged adults and has a relatively poor prognosis with higher rates of treatment resistance and relapse. Breast implant-associated ALCL (BIA-ALCL) is an uncommon subtype that arises within the fibrous capsule or pericapsular fluid surrounding breast implants. Individuals with ALK-positive ALCL usually present with lymphadenopathy, fever, night sweats, and fatigue. Osseous involvement is rare. We present the case of a 21-year-old male who presented with a single tender, palpable skull mass and was subsequently found to have ALK-positive ALCL. In addition, we review the differential diagnosis for calvarial lesions as well as imaging features and management of ALK-positive ALCL. This case emphasizes that lymphoma should remain an important diagnostic consideration when evaluating an aggressive transcalvarial mass.Keywords: ALCL, Anaplastic large cell lymphoma, Lymphoma

Introduction

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a subtype of T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by large pleomorphic lymphoid cells with strong and uniform expression of CD30. The main rearrangement which defines ALCL involves the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene on chromosome 2p23, commonly resulting in the formation of the NPM-ALK fusion protein, which drives constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways involved in cellular survival and proliferation [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

ALK-positive ALCL most commonly affects children, adolescents, and young adults and demonstrates a strong male predominance. Patients typically present with lymphadenopathy and B symptoms. However, clinical manifestations may vary, and atypical presentations can occur. Despite characteristic morphologic and marker expression, such as the presence of “hallmark cells” and ALK positivity, overlap with other lymphoid conditions may complicate accurate identification in unusual cases. As compared to its ALK- negative counterpart, ALK-positive ALCL shows a relatively favorable prognosis [5,6].

Extensive lymphadenopathy represents the imaging hallmark of ALK-positive ALCL. Extranodal involvement is less common, but may present with soft tissue masses or skin lesions. Osteolytic lesions are rare. However, although uncommon, lymphoma should remain an important consideration in the differential diagnosis of an aggressive transcalvarial lesion, particularly when CT demonstrates permeative osseous destruction with minimal periosteal reaction.

We present the case of a 21-year-old male with a single tender, palpable skull mass who was found to have ALK-positive ALCL. In addition, we review the differential diagnosis for calvarial lesions as well as imaging findings and management of ALK-positive ALCL.

Case report

A 21-year-old male with no significant past medical history presented for evaluation of a progressively enlarging tender scalp mass. The patient endorsed ipsilateral headaches of 3 weeks duration, but denied any other symptoms, including fever, dizziness, visual disturbances, nausea, and vomiting. However, further history elicited from family members revealed a 60 pound weight loss over the past year; although weight loss was initially intentional, the patient had discontinued diet and exercise but continued to lose weight over the preceding 2 months.

Vital signs were within normal limits (heart rate: 80 beats per minute; blood pressure: 126/77 mmHg; respiratory rate: 18 breaths per minute; oxygen saturation: 100%). Physical examination revealed a soft but exquisitely tender right parietal scalp mass. A comprehensive neurologic examination was normal. Laboratory studies, including complete blood count, complete metabolic panel, coagulation studies, and infectious disease assays, were normal.

A noncontrast CT scan of the head showed a hyperattenuating mass centered within the diploic space of the right parietal calvarium and infiltrating the underlying epidural space as well as the overlying soft tissues. There was lucency and permeative change of the underlying bone without evidence of an aggressive periosteal reaction (Fig. 1).

A contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain revealed an avidly enhancing transcalvarial mass measuring approximately 2.9 × 6.9 × 4.0 cm (CC × AP × TR) with associated diffusion restriction centered within the right frontoparietal diploic space and involving the scalp soft tissues and underlying dura. The mass was T2-hyperintense and demonstrated elevated signal on arterial spin-labeled sequences. An underlying right holohemispheric subdural collection containing fluid and blood products measured approximately 1.1 cm in maximal thickness and contributed to leftward midline shift with medialization of the uncus (Fig. 2). The extensive pachymeningeal enhancement likely reflected direct dural involvement by the adjacent transcalvarial lymphoma rather than isolated reactive inflammatory change. The accompanying subdural collection was favored to represent secondary reactive or hemorrhagic change related to adjacent dural involvement rather than a primary hemorrhagic process.

A CT-guided biopsy of the scalp mass was performed (Fig. 2). Histopathologic examination of a tissue specimen showed with diffuse soft tissue infiltration by large lymphoid cells with indented nuclei and moderate amounts of cytoplasm. Hallmark cells were present. The large abnormal lymphoid cells were positive for uniform CD30, uniform ALK1, CD45, CD43, CD4, CD2, CD25, EMA (partial weak staining), perforin, granzyme B, and cytotoxic molecules; they were negative for CD3, CD5, CD7, CD20, CD138, CD1a, factor 13a, fascin, MPO, and S100 as well as histiocytic antigens. There were no large cells reminiscent of Reed-Sternberg cells or variant forms. The Ki67 proliferation index was approximately 50% of mononucleated cells. The morphologic features and immunohistochemical profile were consistent with a diagnosis of ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma.

A PET/CT scan was acquired for complete staging and showed diffuse, homogeneous and relatively low-grade hypermetabolic activity throughout the axial and appendicular skeleton; the SUVmax measured up to 5.6 in the sacral ala (Fig. 3). There were no lymphadenopathy or discrete focal osseous lesions. A bone marrow biopsy was performed. Histopathologic examination showed normocellular bone marrow with trilineage hematopoiesis and no morphologic or immunophenotypic evidence of lymphoma involvement. Iron studies demonstrated low serum iron and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) with elevated ferritin. Correlation of the imaging findings, bone marrow analysis, and laboratory studies suggested that the PET/CT findings reflected reactive bone marrow activation rather than lymphomatous infiltration. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was performed and revealed no evidence of leptomeningeal dissemination.

Maximum-intensity projection (MIP) FDG PET image obtained for initial staging demonstrates diffuse, homogeneous radiotracer uptake throughout the axial and appendicular skeleton without discrete focal hypermetabolic osseous lesions or metabolically active lymphadenopathy. The diffuse marrow activity (SUVmax of 5.6 within the sacral ala) initially raised the possibility of bone marrow involvement by lymphoma. However, subsequent bone marrow biopsy demonstrated no morphologic or immunophenotypic evidence of lymphoma, and correlation with laboratory studies favored reactive bone marrow activation rather than lymphomatous infiltration. Integration of the PET/CT findings with histopathology established localized Ann Arbor stage IEA disease.

The patient was diagnosed with stage IE (Ann Arbor stage IEA) systemic ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma and started on brentuximab vedotin, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone (BV-CHP). Intrathecal methotrexate, cytarabine, and hydrocortisone were also administered prophylactically to prevent central nervous system involvement. The patient will continue BV-CHP for a total of 6 cycles.

Discussion

ALK-positive Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma (ALK+ ALCL) is a subtype of T-cell lymphoma characterized by large lymphoid cells with strong expression of CD30 [1]. A chromosomal translocation involving the ALK gene on chromosome 2p23 is implicated in the pathogenesis of ALK+ ALCL, resulting in the formation of an oncogenic fusion protein when combined with various partner genes on the same or different chromosomes [2]. The most common translocation results in the NPM-ALK fusion gene, which drives constitutive activation of ALK tyrosine kinase and downstream oncogenic signaling pathways [[2], [3], [4]].

The clinical presentation of ALK+ ALCL is variable and typically includes lymphadenopathy, fever, night sweats, and fatigue [1]. It most commonly affects children, adolescents, and young adults under 30 years of age and demonstrates a male predominance with a reported male-to-female ratio of approximately 2:1 to 3:1 [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Although considered an aggressive lymphoma that is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage (stage III or IV), ALK+ ALCL is highly treatment-responsive and associated with a favorable prognosis compared with ALK-negative subtypes, with reported 5-year overall survival rates of approximately 67% or higher in contemporary series [10,11].

Imaging manifestations of ALK+ ALCL include multistation lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and extranodal soft tissue involvement [1,12]. Osseous involvement is uncommon, and primary osseous presentation is particularly rare [12,13]. Reported cases of ALCL involving bone have demonstrated osteolytic lesions that may clinically and radiographically mimic entities such as infection, metastatic disease, eosinophilic granuloma, or primary bone tumors [13].

Our patient presented with a solitary markedly T2-hyperintense, hypervascular transcalvarial mass. The imaging features initially suggested a primary bone neoplasm, with differential considerations including Ewing sarcoma, eosinophilic granuloma, and other primary osseous tumors (Table 1). However, marked diffusion restriction on MRI and absence of a florid periosteal reaction on CT were atypical for several primary bone neoplasms. Although lymphoma was therefore considered in the differential diagnosis, the absence of lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly on staging CT was initially unexpected. Nevertheless, as evidenced by this case, lymphoma should remain a differential consideration in the setting of an aggressive calvarial lesion given its highly variable imaging characteristics and behavior. In our case, the robust pachymeningeal enhancement likely reflected direct dural involvement by the adjacent transcalvarial lymphoma rather than isolated reactive inflammatory change. The accompanying subdural collection was favored to represent secondary reactive or hemorrhagic change related to adjacent dural involvement rather than a primary hemorrhagic process.

A CT scan serves as an essential complement to MRI in evaluating solitary calvarial lesions and may assist in narrowing the differential diagnosis. Although ALK+ ALCL typically appears as an aggressive osteolytic lesion with marrow destruction, cortical breakthrough, and an associated soft tissue mass, characteristic osseous features described in competing entities may not always be present [12]. In comparison, eosinophilic granuloma more commonly demonstrates beveled-edge lesions or a “button sequestrum,” findings that were absent in this case. Similarly, Ewing sarcoma and osteosarcoma more frequently demonstrate aggressive periosteal reaction and/or matrix mineralization, features that were not identified. However, these imaging features should be interpreted in context, as overlap exists among aggressive pediatric calvarial lesions, and imaging alone is not diagnostic [12,13].

The imaging manifestations of ALK+ ALCL on FDG PET/CT are variable. The hallmark of systemic disease is extensive hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy. Limited data show that the average SUVmax of involved lymph nodes is significantly higher in ALK+ ALCL as compared to its ALK-negative counterpart (22.1 vs 15.1, respectively). Extranodal diseases involving the spleen, liver, and/or bone may occur in up to 50% of patients. Muscle and cutaneous involvement have also been described. Marrow infiltration classically manifests with patchy or geographic regions of hypermetabolism, often without a discrete anatomic correlate; notably, the degree of metabolic activity described in the literature is typically above an SUVmax of 10, which is significantly greater than was observed in our patient [14,15].

Definitive diagnosis requires histopathologic evaluation. The characteristic finding is the presence of “hallmark cells,” large pleomorphic cells with kidney- or horseshoe-shaped nuclei and abundant cytoplasm [5,6]. Tumor cells exhibit strong expression of CD30 and ALK proteins, with variable or reduced expression of T-cell markers [[5], [6], [7]].

The clinical, histologic, and imaging features of ALK+ ALCL overlap with ALK-negative ALCL, primary cutaneous ALCL, and breast implant–associated ALCL; however, these entities differ in epidemiology, anatomic distribution, and immunophenotype. Early and accurate diagnosis is essential given the therapeutic implications and potential for curative treatment [9,10].

Multiagent systemic chemotherapy is the mainstay of management for ALK+ ALCL [10,11]. Brentuximab vedotin is a cornerstone of therapy, targeting CD30-positive tumor cells and inducing apoptosis via intracellular release of monomethyl auristatin E [9]. Standard regimens often include cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone [9]. Intrathecal chemotherapy may be considered in select high-risk patients for central nervous system prophylaxis. Radiation therapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation are reserved for relapsed, refractory, or residual disease [10,11].

In conclusion, ALK+ ALCL is a rare but clinically significant cause of a solitary calvarial mass. Radiologic evaluation, particularly CT correlation with MRI findings, may help suggest lymphoma in the appropriate clinical context; however, imaging features overlap with more common primary bone tumors. Histopathologic confirmation remains essential, and early diagnosis is critical to ensure favorable outcomes with modern multiagent chemotherapy. Lymphoma should always remain in the differential diagnosis of an aggressive transcalvarial mass, particularly in the setting of marked diffusion restriction, as early recognition and tissue diagnosis can expedite initiation of effective systemic therapy.
