Large deviations at the origin of random walk in random environment
Alexander Drewitzlabel=e2, mark]adrewitz@uni-koeln.de, Alejandro F. Ramírezlabel=e1, mark]ar23@nyu.edu, Santiago Sagliettilabel=e4, mark]sasaglietti@mat.uc.cl, Zhicheng Zhenglabel=e3, mark]zz4230@nyu.edu

Abstract
We consider a random walk in an i.i.d. random environment on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} and study properties of its large deviation rate function at the origin. It was proved by Comets, Gantert and Zeitouni in dimension d = 1 d=1 in 1999 and later by Varadhan in dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 in 2003 that, for uniformly elliptic i.i.d. random environments, the quenched and the averaged large deviation rate functions coincide at the origin. Here we provide a description of an atypical event realizing the correct quenched large deviation rate in the nestling and marginally nestling setting: the random walk seeks regions of space where the environment emulates the element in the convex hull of the support of the law of the environment at a site which minimizes the rate function. Periodic environments play a natural role in this description.

Introduction

The model of random walk in random environment, describing the motion of a particle in a highly disordered medium,
has been an important cornerstone in the microscopic description of homogenization phenomena
[
R11
]
and has more recently been
shown to fall within the KPZ universality class
[
BC17
]
. Several fundamental questions about this model have not been fully answered yet, such as,
for example, the validity of the law of large numbers
[
DR14
]
or the relation between directional transience and ballisticity.
Some understanding has, however, been achieved with regards to the large deviations of the random walk, both at the quenched
and the annealed level. We refer to
[
GdH94
]
for the first proof of the quenched large deviation principle for i.i.d. environments in dimension
d=1
,
[
CGZ00
]
for an extension of the quenched large deviation principle to ergodic environments and the first proof of the annealed large deviation principle in dimension
d=1
, and
[
Z98
]
for the quenched large deviation principle in dimensions
d\geq 1
for the nestling case. The general proof of both a quenched and an annealed large deviation principle for uniformly elliptic i.i.d. random environments was established by
Varadhan
[
V03
]
in 2003 for all dimensions
d\geq 1
.
In
[
CGZ00
]
and
[
V03
]
, it was also shown that at the origin both, the annealed and quenched large deviation rate functions coincide, and furthermore,
a variational formula for their common value was provided.

In this article, we describe a dominant behavior giving rise to the correct rate of exponential decay as
n\to\infty
of the large deviation probability that the random walk returns to
the origin after
n
steps in the so-called nestling or marginally nestling cases. In the quenched setting, we prove the following properties of this behavior, which we call
the
dominant behavior event
or
dominant event
, and of the rate function at
0
: (
i
) the rate
function at
0
is equal to the rate function at 0 of a random walk in a certain periodic environment, which we shall refer to as the

optimal environment
, whose site marginals are
all identical and correspond to a point in the boundary of the convex hull of the support of the law of the random environment; (
ii
) on this dominant event, the random walk moves quickly to a region where the
environment is close to a periodic environment whose rate function coincides with the rate function of the random walk in the optimal environment; (
iii
) this periodic environment uses jump probabilities which are close to jump probabilities of the support of the law of the random environment. These results
complement previous ones, giving insight into the mechanism under which atypical behavior of the random walk occurs. In particular, they complement what is known in the nestling case, where the random walk looks for a trap or nest (see Zerner
[
Z98
]
for the introduction of this terminology and Sznitman
[
S00
]
for further developments), and stays there for most of the time. A homogenization result of a viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation in a periodic potential is implicit in our results, including a representation of the homogenized Hamiltonian at
0
(see
[
LPV87
]
and
[
E89
]
for the proof of homogenization of the viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation in a periodic potential).

To provide some context about these questions, we now describe some of the main results about large deviations of random walk
in random environment. In 1997, Greven and den Hollander
[
GdH94
]
proved a quenched large deviation principle for random
walks in a uniformly elliptic i.i.d. random environment in dimension
d=1
. In 1999, Comets, Gantert and Zeitouni
[
CGZ00
]

extended this quenched large deviation principle to ergodic environments and, in addition, proved an annealed large
deviation principle for i.i.d. uniformly elliptic environments in dimension
d=1
. Zerner in 2000
[
Z98
]
proved a multidimensional
large deviation principle for the so-called random walks in nestling
environment. Finally, in 2003, Varadhan
[
V03
]
proved a quenched
large deviation principle in dimensions
d\geq 1
for random walk in a uniformly elliptic ergodic environment, together with
an annealed large deviation principle for random walk in uniformly elliptic i.i.d. environment. Furthermore, there he also showed that,
for any dimension
d\geq 1
, the quenched and the averaged rate
functions at
0
are equal. Subsequently, Yilmaz in
[
Y11
]
proved
that in dimensions
d\geq 4
, whenever the so called ballisticity condition
(T)
(cf.
[
S01
,
BDR11
]
) is satisfied,
there is a neighborhood of the velocity where the quenched and
annealed large
deviation rate functions are equal. As a complementary result, in

[
BMRS23
]
and
[
BMRS23b
]
, it
was proven that in dimensions
d\geq 4
, given any compact set in the
interior or in the boundary of the domain of the rate functions not containing
0
, if the disorder or
randomness of the environment is low enough, the quenched and annealed
large deviation rate functions coincide in this compact set.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
We will first introduce in Section
2
the notations and state the main results. Then we continue by investigating large deviations for a random walk in time-periodic environment so that it necessarily spends prescribed fractions of the time in certain environments in Section
3.1
. This analysis is comparatively easy but serves as a main building block for our more complex setup of space-periodic environments investigated in Section
3.2
. Indeed, space-periodic environments will play an important role since their mesoscopic approximations can naturally be found in large spatial balls due to a Borel-Cantelli type argument and our independence assumption. Using a refined analysis we can then – again approximately – reduce the random walk in certain space-periodic environments to the one investigated in Section
3.1
. This, among other tools such as local central limit theorems for RWRE, will be a key ingredient in the proof of the main result which is conducted in Section
4
.

2
Notation and results

To state our results, we begin by defining the models of random walk in random environment and random
walk in a periodic environment (or RWRE and RWPE, for short) on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For
p\in[1,\infty],
denote by
|x|_{p}
the
\ell^{p}
-norm of any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and introduce the set

\mathbb{V}:=\{e\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|e|_{1}=1\}
of all unit vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Furthermore, we define

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}:=\Big\{\vec{p}=(p(e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{V}}:\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}p(e)=1\Big\},

the simplex of all probability vectors on
\mathbb{V}
, and the product space
\Omega:=(\mathcal{P_{\mathbb{V}}})^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
endowed with the product topology. Any element
\omega\in\Omega
will be called an
environment
, i.e., each
\omega=(\omega(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a family of probability vectors
\omega(x)=(\omega(x,e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}
on
\mathbb{V}
, indexed by the sites
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Now, given some
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, we define the
random walk in the environment
\omega
starting from
x

as the discrete time Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with law
P_{x,\omega}
given by
P_{x,\omega}(X_{0}=x)=1
and, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
e\in\mathbb{V}
,

P_{x,\omega}(X_{n+1}=y+e|X_{n}=y)=\omega(y,e).

Now, if the environment
\omega
is chosen at random according to some probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega
, we obtain a probability law
P_{x}
on
(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\times\Omega

(endowed with the product
\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
of the underlying Borel
\sigma
-algebras)
given by their semi-direct product, i.e. for any
A\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
,

P_{x}(A\times B)=\int_{B}P_{x,\omega}(A)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega).

We will refer to
P_{x,\omega}
as the
quenched law

and
P_{x}
is the
averaged

(or
annealed
) law of the
random walk in random environment
(RWRE), and we call
\mathbb{P}
the
environment law
of the RWRE. Throughout the sequel, we will say that the random environment is i.i.d. if the random probability vectors
(\omega(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are i.i.d. under
\mathbb{P}
and, moreover, say it is
uniformly elliptic
if there exists
\kappa>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\omega(x,e)\geq\kappa)=1\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}$ and $e\in\mathbb{V}$},

i.e., if there exists some
\kappa>0
such that the environment law
\mathbb{P}
has marginals which are supported on the set

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}^{(\kappa)}:=\big\{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}:p(e)\geq\kappa\text{ for all }e\in\mathbb{V}\big\}.

(1)

Finally, in order to introduce periodic environments, fix some
{\bf n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and define the box
\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:0\leq x_{i}\leq n_{i}-1\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq i\leq d\}
. In addition, for
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
set

x\ {\rm mod}\ {\bf n}=(x_{1}\ {\rm mod}\ n_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\ {\rm mod}\ n_{d})\in\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}},

and, given
{\bf p}=(\vec{p}(x))_{x\in\mathbf{B}_{\bf n}}\in(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}})^{\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}}
, define the environment
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}\in\Omega
by the formula

\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x)=\vec{p}(x\ {\rm mod}\ {\bf n})\quad\text{ for each }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

(2)

Now, given
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the
random walk in the periodic environment (RWPE)
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
starting from
x
is the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with law
P_{x,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}
. We call the pair
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
the
period
of
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
.

Both RWRE and RWPE satisfy large deviation principles, which we describe next. For this purpose, let

\mathrm{D}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|_{1}\leq 1\}

denote the unit
\ell^{1}
unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In
[
V03
, Theorems 2.3 and 3.2]
, Varadhan established both a quenched and an averaged large deviation principle for random walks in i.i.d. (actually, for ergodic environments in the quenched case) uniformly elliptic
environments on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for any dimension
d\geq 1
:

(i)

There exists a rate function
I_{a}:\mathrm{D}\to[0,\infty)
, which is both convex and continuous on the interior
\mathrm{D}^{\circ}
of
{\rm D}
, such that,
for every Borel set
A\subseteq D
,

-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}I_{a}(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}\bigg(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in A\bigg)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}\bigg(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in A\bigg)\leq-\inf_{x\in\bar{A}}I_{a}(x).

(ii)

There exists a deterministic rate function
I_{q}:\mathrm{D}\to[0,\infty)
, convex and continuous on
\mathrm{D}^{\circ}
, such that, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega
, given any Borel set
A\subseteq D
one has

-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}I_{q}(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}\bigg(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in A\bigg)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}\bigg(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in A\bigg)\leq-\inf_{x\in\bar{A}}I_{q}(x).

In addition, in
[
V03
]
it has been established that both rate functions agree at the origin, i.e.
I_{a}(0)=I_{q}(0)
. More precisely, given an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}
, let
\mathcal{K}_{S}
denote its
convex hull and, given an environment law
\mathbb{P}
, define
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}:=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})}
where
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})
denotes the essential support of
\omega(0)
. Then, in
[
V03
]
the following was shown:

(iii)

For any dimension
d\geq 1
,

I_{a}(0)=I_{q}(0)=-\log\Big(\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}}\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}e^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}p(e)\Big).

(3)

Furthermore, given a RWPE with law
P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}
, there exists a convex function
I_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
such that for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\begin{split}-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}I_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x)&\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\bigg(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in A\bigg)\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\bigg(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in A\bigg)\leq-\inf_{x\in\bar{A}}I_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x).\end{split}

Indeed, this is an immediate consequence of the quenched large deviation principle
[
V03
, Theorem 2.3]
applied to RWRE with the ergodic environment distribution

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}}\delta_{\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(\cdot+x)}.

Finally, to state the main result of this article, we need to introduce first the concept of nestling, marginally nestling and non-nestling RWRE. To this end, given any
\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}
, we define the
drift
of
\vec{p}
as

\mathrm{d}(\vec{p}):=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}p(e)e

(4)

and denote by
\mathcal{D}_{0}:=\{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}:\mathrm{d}(\vec{p})=0\}
the set of all zero drift probability vectors on
\mathbb{V}
.
Then, given a RWRE with environment law
\mathbb{P}
, we will say that the RWRE is
nestling
if
\text{int}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}})\cap\mathcal{D}_{0}\neq\emptyset
,
marginally nestling
if
\text{int}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}})\cap\mathcal{D}_{0}=\emptyset
but
\partial\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{0}\neq\emptyset
and
non-nestling
if
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{0}=\emptyset
.

For
\varepsilon,\delta>0
chosen to be small enough later on, given
any period
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}})^{\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}}
and
N\geq 1
,
we define the event
G^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:=\bigcup_{x_{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}}G^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},x_{\mathbf{n}}}
, where for
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

G^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},y}:=\Big\{\omega\in\Omega:|\omega(x)-\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x)|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\text{ for all }x\in\mathrm{B}(y,\delta(\log N)^{1/d})\Big\},

(5)

and we recall that
\mathrm{B}(x,r)
denotes the
\ell^{1}
-ball on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
of
radius
r>0
centered at
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Furthermore, let us set
a_{N}:=\lfloor\frac{N}{\log N}\rfloor
and, for
x_{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}
, define the following events:

i.

A_{1}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}})}
, the event that the
random walk
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
moves from
0
to
x_{\mathbf{n}}
in
a_{N}
steps;

ii.

A_{2}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}})}=A_{2}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}}),\delta}
, the event that
X_{a_{N}}=x_{\mathbf{n}}
,
X_{n}\in\mathrm{B}(x_{\mathbf{n}},\delta(\log N)^{1/d})
for all
n\in[a_{N},N-a_{N}]
and
X_{N-a_{N}}=x_{\mathbf{n}}
;

iii.

A_{3}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}})}
, the event that
X_{N-a_{N}}=x_{\mathbf{n}}
and
X_{N}=0
.

With this, define now the event
A^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:=\bigcup_{x_{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}}A^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},x_{\mathbf{n}}}
, where

A^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},x_{\mathbf{n}}}:=G^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},x_{\mathbf{n}}}\cap A_{1}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}})}\cap A_{2}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}})}\cap A_{3}^{(N,x_{\mathbf{n}})},

i.e.,
A^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},x_{\mathbf{n}}}
is the event that the random walk moves in
a_{N}=o(N)
steps to a ball of
radius
\delta(\log N)^{1/d}
(centered at
x_{\mathbf{n}}
) within which the environment is at a distance
\varepsilon
to the periodic environment
\omega_{\bf n,\bf p}
, stays there for a time that is strongly asymptotic to
N
, and then
returns to
0
in
a_{N}
steps (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1:
Graphic representation of the event
A^{\varepsilon}_{N,\bf n,\bf p}
. The random walks starts at
0
, moving quickly through the upper red line to the ball, which is at a distance smaller than
N/\log N
, stays in the ball a time of order
N
and then moves back through the second red line to the origin. The environment inside the ball, which has a radius
\delta(\log N)^{1/d}
, is periodic, formed by alternating parallel strips each one composed of a different homogeneous environment belonging to the support of the law of
\omega(0)
.

We are now ready to state our main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider a random walk in a uniformly elliptic i.i.d. environment.
Then, the following assertions hold true:

(i)

There exists
\vec{p}_{\ast}=(p_{\ast}(e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}

such that

I_{q}(0)=I_{a}(0)=-\log\Big(\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\sqrt{p_{\ast}(e)p_{\ast}(-e)}\Big)=:I(0).

(6)

Furthermore, if the walk is either non-nestling or marginally nestling, then
p_{*}\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}
.

(ii)

Suppose that the random walk is either non-nestling or marginally nestling. Then, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists some period
(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{p}_{\varepsilon})
such that

|I(0)-I_{(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{p}_{\varepsilon})}(0)|\leq\varepsilon.

(iii)

Suppose that the random walk is either non-nestling or marginally nestling. Then, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists some period
(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{p}_{\varepsilon})
and a
\delta_{0}>0
such that for every
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\liminf_{N\to\infty}G^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{N,\bf n_{\varepsilon},\bf p_{\varepsilon}}\big)=1

(7)

and, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,

-I(0)-\varepsilon\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log P_{0,\omega}(A^{\varepsilon}_{N,\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{p}_{\varepsilon}})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log P_{0,\omega}(X_{N}=0)\leq-I(0).

Remark 2.2
.

Part (i) of Theorem
2.1
was originally proven for dimension
d=1
by Comets, Gantert and Zeitouni in
[
CGZ00
]
and for any dimension by
Varadhan in
[
V03
]
. We include it in the statement of the theorem for completeness.

Remark 2.3
.

The smallness of
\delta_{0}
in part
(iii)
of Theorem
2.1
depends on the dimension
d
and on
\varepsilon
through the probability per unit volume of realizing the periodic environment
\omega_{\bf n_{\varepsilon},\bf p_{\varepsilon}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

A statement analogous to part (iii) in the case of nestling random walks was proven by Zerner in
[
Z98
]
. In that case
I(0)=0
and essentially a good strategy for the random walk to realize the event
\{X_{n}=0\}
is to move in time
O(N/\log N)
from
0
to a ball of radius
O((\log N)^{1/d})
within a distance
N/\log N
where the environment is constructed to produce a trap (a
nest
in the language of
[
Z98
]
), stay there a time of order
O(N)
, and come back to
0
again in a time of order
O(N/\log N)
.

Remark 2.5
.

In part
(iii)
of Theorem
2.1
, the
periodic environment can be chosen as alternating parallel strips perpendicular to the velocity of the RWRE, where each strip is composed of a homogeneous environment so that the jump probabilities are all equal to some given element of the support of
\omega(0)
(see Figures

and
2
).

3
Large deviations for RWPE

In this section, we derive an explicit formula for the large deviation rate
function at the origin of random walks in (space-)periodic environments. As a first step, we shall study the analogous problem for random walks in time-periodic environments.

3.1
Large deviations for random walks in time-periodic
environments

Throughout this section we will work with random walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
evolving according to a time-periodic jump rule. More precisely, let us fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a
k
-tuple
\mathbf{q}:=(\vec{q}_{0},\dots,\vec{q}_{k-1})\in(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}})^{k}
of (not necessarily different) probability vectors and define the sequence of environments
\omega=(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subseteq\Omega
by the formula

\omega_{n}(x,e):=q_{n\,\text{mod}\,k}(e)\qquad\text{ for each $x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}$, $e\in\mathbb{V}$}.

We then define the
random walk in the time-periodic environment
\omega
(RWTPE)
starting from
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as the (time inhomogeneous) Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with law
Q_{x,\mathbf{q}}
given by
Q_{x,\mathbf{q}}(X_{0}=x)=1
and, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
e\in\mathbb{V}
,

Q_{x,\mathbf{q}}(X_{n+1}=y+e|X_{n}=y)=\omega_{n}(y,e).

For convenience, we shall sometimes call
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
a
\mathbf{q}
-RWTPE. We define the
support
of the

\mathbf{q}
-RWTPE as the set
\mathcal{S}
consisting of the different elements in the finite sequence
\vec{q}_{0},\dots,\vec{q}_{k-1}
, and we enumerate its different elements by
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j}
so that we can write

\mathcal{S}=\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}\},

(8)

where we note that
\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma_{i^{\prime}}
for all
i\neq i^{\prime}
. For each
i=1,\dots,j
, we define the
multiplicity
of
\sigma_{i}
as the number of times
k_{i}

that
\sigma_{i}
appears in the
k
-tuple
\mathbf{q}
and the
frequency
of
\sigma_{i}
as
\lambda_{i}:=\frac{k_{i}}{k}
. Note that

k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j}=k

and
\overline{\lambda}:=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{j})\in\Delta_{j}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{j}
, where
\Delta_{j}
denotes the
j
-simplex

\Delta_{j}:=\Big\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{j})\in[0,\infty)^{j}:\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}=1\Big\}.

(9)

Given a vector

\overline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{j})\in\Delta_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}^{j}
as above,
we will say that the RWTPE has
frequency

\overline{\lambda}.

Observe that when
\mathcal{S}=\{\sigma\}
is a singleton, the resulting RWTPE is merely a simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with jump distribution
\sigma
, or
\sigma
-RW, for short. Recall that, if we consider the logarithmic moment generating function
\Lambda_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
given by the formula

\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta):=\log\Big(\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}\sigma(e)\Big)

(10)

for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then, by Cramér’s theorem, the
\sigma
-RW satisfies a large deviation principle with rate function
I_{\sigma}
given for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the formula

I_{\sigma}(x):=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\langle\theta,x\rangle-\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta)\big).

In particular, it is straightforward to verify that, for any
non-degenerate

\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}
, i.e., such that
\sigma(e)>0
for all
e\in\mathbb{V}
, one has that

I_{\sigma}(0):=-\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta)=-\log\Big(\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\sqrt{\sigma(e)\sigma(-e)}\Big),

(11)

since in this case
\Lambda_{\sigma}
is finite, continuous and satisfies
\lim_{|\theta|\to\infty}\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta)=+\infty
, so that it must attain its global minimum at its unique critical point (which can be immediately computed and thus yields (
11
)). Furthermore, if
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is any
\mathbf{q}
-RWTPE with support
\mathcal{S}=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j}\}
and frequency
\overline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{j})
, then a simple computation gives that the limit

\Lambda_{\mathbf{q}}(\theta):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log Q_{0,\mathbf{q}}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,X_{n}\rangle}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{i}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)

exists and is finite for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
Q_{0,\mathbf{q}}[X]
denotes the expectation of
X
with respect to the probability measure
Q_{0,\mathbf{q}}
. Furthermore,
\Lambda_{\mathbf{q}}
is everywhere differentiable as a function of
\theta
. It then follows from the Gärtner-Ellis theorem
[
DZ98
, Exercise 2.3.20]
, that the
\mathbf{q}
-RWTPE satisfies a large deviation principle with rate function
I_{\mathbf{q}}
given by

I_{\mathbf{q}}(x):=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\langle\theta,x\rangle-\Lambda_{\mathbf{q}}(\theta)\big).

Recalling the notation
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}^{(\kappa)}
from (
1
), we are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix
\kappa>0
and a nonempty set
\mathrm{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}^{(\kappa)}
of probability vectors. Then, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists some period
\mathbf{q}_{\varepsilon}
such that the corresponding
\mathbf{q}_{\varepsilon}
-RWTPE satisfies

\left|I_{\mathbf{q}_{\varepsilon}}(0)-\inf_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{A}}}I_{\sigma}(0)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

For the proof of Proposition
3.1
we will need the following lemma (recall
\Delta_{j}
is the
j
-dimensional simplex, cf. (
9
)).

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
\kappa>0
and let
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}^{(\kappa)}
be all distinct. Define the mapping
\Lambda:\Delta_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by the formula

\Lambda(t,\theta):=\Lambda_{\langle t,\sigma\rangle}(\theta)=\log\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}\sigma_{i}(e)\Big),

where
\langle t,\sigma\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}
for
t\in\Delta_{j}
. Then,
\Lambda
has at least one saddle point, by which we refer to the (global) property that there exists some
(t^{*},\theta^{*})\in\Delta_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\Lambda(t,\theta^{*})\leq\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})\leq\Lambda(t^{*},\theta)
for all
t\in\Delta_{j}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Observe that such
(t^{*},\theta^{*})
necessarily
satisfies

\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})=\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda(t,\theta)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\Lambda(t,\theta).

Proof.

By direct computation, it is straightforward to verify that:

\bullet

(t,\theta)\mapsto\Lambda(t,\theta)
is continuous on
\Delta_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bullet

t\mapsto\Lambda(t,\theta)
is concave for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bullet

\theta\mapsto\Lambda(t,\theta)
is convex for all
t\in\Delta_{j}
.

Therefore, by Sion’s minimax theorem
[
Si58
]
, it follows that the minimax equality holds, i.e.,

\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda(t,\theta)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\Lambda(t,\theta).

Now let us define the function
R_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
via

R_{1}(\theta):=\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\Lambda(t,\theta)

and observe that

R_{1}(\theta)=\log\Big(\max_{i=1,\dots,j}\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}\sigma_{i}(e)\Big)=\max_{i=1,\dots,j}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta).

Hence, since it is straightforward to check from (
10
) that each
\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}
is finite, continuous and satisfies
\lim_{|\theta|\to\infty}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)=+\infty
, it follows that
R_{1}
satisfies these three properties as well and, as such, the set

X_{*}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:R_{1}(\theta)=\inf R_{1}\}

of minimizers is non-empty.

On the other hand, if we consider the function
R_{2}:\Delta_{j}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

R_{2}(t):=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda(t,\theta),

then, since
\Lambda(t,\theta)=\Lambda_{\langle t,\sigma\rangle}(\theta)
, by (
11
) we see that

R_{2}(t)=\log\left(\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\sqrt{\sum_{i,k=1}^{j}t_{i}t_{k}\sigma_{i}(e)\sigma_{k}(-e)}\right).

In particular,
R_{2}
is finite and continuous, so that, since
\Delta_{j}
is compact, we obtain that

Y_{*}:=\{t\in\Delta_{j}:R_{2}(t)=\sup R_{2}\}

is non-empty. It now follows from
[
B09
, Proposition 3.4.1]
that any point in
X_{*}\times Y_{*}
is a saddle point.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We start by noting that given any
\varepsilon>0
we may choose
j\in\mathbb{N}
and distinct
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j}\in\mathrm{A}
such that, by definition of convex hull,

\Big|\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{A}}}I_{\sigma}(0)-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0)\Big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

(12)

where we define
\mathcal{S}:=\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}\}
.

Next, let
\Lambda:\Delta_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be the mapping from Lemma
3.2
(for this particular choice of
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j}
). Then, by this very same lemma, there exists
(t^{*},\theta^{*})
such that

\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\Lambda(t,\theta)=\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda(t,\theta).

(13)

In particular, upon noticing that
-I_{\langle t,\vec{\sigma}\rangle}(0)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda(t,\theta)
, we see that

\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})=-\min_{t\in\Delta_{j}}I_{\langle t,\vec{\sigma}\rangle}(0)=-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0).

Now, if we write
t^{*}=(t_{1}^{*},\dots,t_{j}^{*})
and define
\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by the formula

\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta):=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}^{*}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta),

(14)

then our next step will be to show that
\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}
has a global minimum at
\theta^{*}
which satisfies
\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta^{*})=\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})
, so that

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta)=-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0).

(15)

To this end, we first observe that,
\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta^{*})=\max_{k=1,\dots,j}\Lambda_{\sigma_{k}}(\theta^{*})
for all
i
with
t^{*}_{i}>0
. Indeed, since for all
t\in\Delta_{j}
we have

\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda(t,\theta^{*})}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta^{*})},

(16)

if there existed
i,k
such that
t^{*}_{i}>0
and
\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta^{*})<\Lambda_{\sigma_{k}}(\theta^{*})
then this would imply that

\Lambda(t^{*}+t^{*}_{i}e_{k}-t^{*}_{i}e_{i},\theta^{*})>\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*}),

which would contradict the fact that
(t^{*},\theta^{*})
is a saddle point.
Thus, it follows from this observation and (
16
) that, if we write
\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*}):=\max_{k=1,\dots,j}\Lambda_{\sigma_{k}}(\theta^{*})
, then

\frac{\partial\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{k}}\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^{*}}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}^{*}\mathrm{e}^{-\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*})}\frac{\partial\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)}}{\partial\theta_{k}}\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^{*}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*})}\frac{\partial\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda_{\langle t^{*},\vec{\sigma}\rangle}(\theta^{*})}}{\partial\theta_{k}}=0

since
(t^{*},\theta^{*})
is a saddle point of
\Lambda
and thus
\theta^{*}
minimizes
\Lambda_{\langle t^{*},\vec{\sigma}\rangle}
. Arguing as with
R_{1}
in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we can show that
\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}
is finite, continuous and also satisfies
\lim_{|\theta|\to\infty}\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta)=\infty
, which implies that its critical point
\theta^{*}
must be a global minimum. In addition, by this same observation and (
14
), we obtain that
\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta^{*})=\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*})
. Finally, since we have that

\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})=\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\Lambda(t,\theta^{*})=\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*})

as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we conclude that
\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta^{*})=\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})
and hence (
15
) holds.

To conclude the proof we observe that, since each
\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}
is finite, continuous and verifies both
\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(0)=0
and
\lim_{|\theta|\to\infty}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)=\infty
, it is straightforward to verify that, as
n\to\infty
, for any
t^{(n)}=(t_{1}^{(n)},\dots,t_{j}^{(n)})\in\Delta_{j}
with
t^{(n)}\rightarrow t^{*}
we have

\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}^{(n)}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)\longrightarrow\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}t^{*}_{i}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)=\Lambda^{*}_{\vec{\sigma}}(\theta^{*})=-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0).

In particular, we see that for each sequence
(t^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Delta_{j}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{j}
with
t^{(n)}\rightarrow t^{*}
as
n\to\infty
, we have that for any
\mathbf{q}_{n}
-RWTPE with support
\mathcal{S}
and frequency
t^{(n)}
,

-I_{\mathbf{q}_{n}}(0)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda_{\mathbf{q}_{n}}(\theta)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}t^{(n)}_{i}\Lambda_{\sigma_{i}}(\theta)\longrightarrow-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0).

In combination with (
12
), the result now immediately follows.
∎

3.2
Large deviations for random walks in space-periodic

Consider a random walk
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in the space-periodic environment induced by a period
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
, which we assume to be uniformly elliptic. Associated to this walk we can consider the Markov chain
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

defined as

Y_{n}:=X_{n}\,\text{mod}\,{\bf n}

with finite state space
\mathbf{B}_{\bf n}
. The uniform ellipticity assumption entails that
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is irreducible. By choosing
{\bf n}
with the correct parity, we can also assume it is aperiodic. It therefore has a unique
invariant measure on
\mathbf{B}_{\bf n}
which we will denote by
\mu_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
.

In the sequel, we will need to construct a specific type of space-periodic environment, generated by different
environments on parallel strips. To this end, given

u\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with
r_{1}<r_{2}

(r_{1},r_{2})
-strip perpendicular to
u

S_{u,r_{1},r_{2}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:r_{1}\leq\langle x,u\rangle<r_{2}\}.

Next, we fix
r_{0}=0<r_{1}<\cdots<r_{j}
and define a partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
given by the strips
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
where
S_{i}:=S_{u,nr_{j}+r_{l-1},nr_{j}+r_{l}}
if
i=nj+l
with
l\in\{1,\ldots,j\}
.
We call the partition
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
a
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip periodic tiling
.

This tiling itself can be decomposed into finite blocks or
tiles
, which are translated copies of a particular hyper-rectangle
R
which we introduce next. We define recursively the set of vertices of one face
of
R
as follows: let
x_{0}=0
and, for
0\leq n\leq d-1
, we choose its
(n+1)

x_{n+1}\in{\rm argmin}\left\{|x|_{2}:x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\langle u,x\rangle=0,\langle x_{i},x\rangle=0,x\neq x_{i}\ {\rm for}\ 0\leq i\leq n\right\},

and observe that the argmin above exists precisely because
u
has rational coordinates.
We then define the
base

F
of the hyper-rectangle
R
as

F:=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}s_{n}x_{n},0\leq s_{n}<1,\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}s_{n}=1\Big\}.

(17)

We now define the hyper-rectangle
R
from its base
F
via the formula

R:=\big\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:y=x+\alpha u\,,\,x\in F\,,\,0\leq\alpha<r_{j}\big\}.

(18)

We call
R
the
basic tile
of the
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip-periodic tiling
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}

(see Figure
2
).

Finally, let us fix
u\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and consider distinct vectors
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}^{(\kappa)}
. Given a
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip-periodic tiling, we define a (space-)periodic environment
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
via

\omega^{*}(x)=\sigma_{i\,\text{mod}\,j},

whenever
x\in S_{i}
. We call this a
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip periodic environment
generated by
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}
. Observe that we can write
\omega^{*}=\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
for some specific period
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
depending only on the
j
-tuple
(r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
. In particular,
\omega^{*}
defines
a particular type of RWPE, which we shall denote by
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
(see Figure
2
). Also, if we project
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
onto the hyper-rectangle
R
via periodicity, we obtain a random walk
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on the finite state space
R
, i.e.,
Z_{n}

is given by

Z_{n}:=\hat{X}_{n},

(19)

where
\hat{x}
denotes the equivalence class defined by the basic tile
R
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

u

Strip periodic tiling in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
composed of

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix
\kappa>0
,
j\geq 1
and
\mathcal{S}:=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}^{(\kappa)}
with all
\sigma_{i}
’s distinct. Assume that there exists some
u\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, with
|u|_{2}=1
, such that

\langle\mathrm{d}(\sigma_{i}),u\rangle=0

(20)

for all
1\leq i\leq j
, where
\mathrm{d}(\sigma_{i})
is the drift of
\sigma_{i}
as defined in (
4
). Then, given
\varepsilon>0
, for any
\overline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{j})\in\Delta_{j}
there exists some periodic environment
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
with
\mathbf{p}=(\vec{p}(x))\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{n}}}
such that
P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}
-almost surely, for every
1\leq m\leq j
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n}1_{\{X_{i}\in S_{m}\}}}{n}-\lambda_{m}\right|\leq\varepsilon,

(21)

where
S_{m}
is the set of sites
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x)=\sigma_{m}
. As a matter of fact,
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
can be chosen as a
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip periodic environment generated by the
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}
for some
r_{1},\dots,r_{j}
.

Proof.

Consider some fixed
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip periodic environment generated by
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}
. Observe that the strip
S_{i}
in the corresponding
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip periodic tiling has width
r_{l}-r_{l-1}
if
i=nj+l
for some
l\in\{1,\dots,j\}
. We will assume throughout that
r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. This periodic environment defines a RWPE, which we denote by
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. We claim that this is the desired RWPE satisfying (
21
). We will carry out the proof of this in a number of steps, which we outline below.

Step 1.
First, we will define a one-dimensional random walk given by the projection of the RWPE
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
onto the line generated by
u
. Let
R
be the basic tile in the chosen
(u;r_{1},\dots,r_{j})
-strip periodic tiling (see Figure
2
) and let
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the random walk induced by
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

on
R
, see (
19
). Now define
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by the formula

W_{n}:=\langle Z_{n},u\rangle.

Note that
W
takes values in the set
\mathbb{W}:=\{\langle x,u\rangle:x\in R\}
and that, if we think of
\mathbb{W}
as having periodic boundary conditions, then
W
has independent increments, each supported on the set
\{\langle e,u\rangle:e\in\mathbb{V}\}
and thus bounded from above in absolute value by some constant
\ell_{u}
depending only on
u
.
Observe that, by the fact that the drifts
\mathrm{d}(\sigma_{i})
are all perpendicular to
u
, (cf. (
20
)),
W

is a martingale. Notice also that, since
u
is a unit vector, the number of points per unit length in
\mathbb{W}
is some constant integer
L\in\mathbb{N}
depending only on
u
.
In particular, we have that

|\mathbb{W}|=Lr_{j}.

We will show in the next steps that
W
has an invariant measure which is
approximately constant on each region of constant jump probabilities, i.e. on each set
R\cap\langle S_{i},u\rangle
.

Step 2.
To estimate the invariant measure of
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we will have to work with a renormalized random walk corresponding to the exit times of
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
from intervals of a fixed length
2N
. In order to define such random walk, we will first divide
\mathbb{W}
into overlapping intervals of length
2N
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
2N
divides each of the numbers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}
(which will be chosen accordingly later on) so that

\nu_{l}:=\frac{r_{l}-r_{l-1}}{2N},

are all integers. Now, let us set
\nu:=\sum_{l=1}^{j}\nu_{l}=\frac{r_{j}}{2N}
,
\overline{\nu}:=2L\nu
and define for
i=0,\dots,\overline{\nu}-1
the intervals

J_{i}:=[N(i-1),N(i+1)]\subseteq\mathbb{R}.

(22)

Let
V_{0}:=0
and set

T_{0}:=\min\{n>0:W_{n}\notin J_{0}\}.

Observe that, by definition of
W
, the point
W_{T_{0}}
must either lie in
J_{1}
(if
W
exits
J_{0}
to the right) or in
J_{\overline{\nu}-1}
(if
W
exits
J_{0}
to the left or, to be more precise, if
\langle X_{n},u\rangle
jumps to the left of
0
). Thus, we may set

\displaystyle V_{1}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $W_{T_{0}}\in J_{1}$},\\
\overline{\nu}-1&\text{ if $W_{T_{0}}\in J_{\overline{\nu}-1}$},\end{cases}

and then, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define recursively

T_{k}:=\min\Big\{n>T_{k-1}:W_{n}\notin J_{V_{k-1}}\Big\}

together with

V_{k+1}:=\begin{cases}V_{k}+1\,\,\text{mod}\,\,\overline{\nu}&\text{ if $W_{T_{k}}\in J_{V_{k}+1\,\text{mod}\,\overline{\nu}}$}\\
V_{k}-1\,\,\text{mod}\,\,\overline{\nu}&\text{ if $W_{T_{k}}\in J_{V_{k}-1\,\text{mod}\,\overline{\nu}}$}.\end{cases}

In other words,
V=(V_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the process taking values on the set
I_{\overline{\nu}}:=\{0,1,\dots,\overline{\nu}-1\}
with periodic boundary conditions which starts from
0
and, at time
k
, jumps either one step to the right (modulo
\overline{\nu}
) if
W
exits the interval
J_{V_{k}}
through its right endpoint or one step to the left otherwise.

Step 3.
Using the fact that
(W_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
martingale, by the optional stopping theorem we have that, as
N\to\infty
,

P(V_{k+1}=i+1|V_{k}=i)=\frac{1}{2}+O_{N}(N^{-1}).

(23)

In particular, by continuity we have that, for any fixed values of the ratios
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
,
V
has an invariant measure
\pi
which satisfies the asymptotics

\pi(i)=\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}}+o_{N}(1),

(24)

for all
i\in I_{\overline{\nu}}

where
o_{N}(1)
is an error term tending to
0
as
N\to\infty
.

Step 4.
We next show that the average number of steps that

W
takes to exit the interval
J_{i}
starting at most distance
\ell_{u}
from
iN
is of order
C_{i}N^{2}
, for some positive constant
C_{i}
. To do this,
consider the martingale

W_{n}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(W_{i}),

where

f(x)=f_{l}:=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\sigma_{l}(e)\langle e,u\rangle^{2}\qquad{\rm whenever}\ r_{l-1}\leq\langle x,u\rangle<r_{l}.

Define for each
i\geq 0
,

\tau_{i}:=T_{i}-T_{i-1},

with the convention
T_{-1}=0
. Note that the random variables

are not identically distributed, since the exit point of the walk

where we recall that

are bounded above by
\ell_{u}
. Next, for each
i\in I_{\overline{\nu}}
we let

be an independent copy of the

, the point in
\mathbb{W}
which is closest to
iN
, and define

Finally, for each
i\in I_{\overline{\nu}}
let
(\tau^{+}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables distributed as
\tau^{+}(i)
and notice that we may couple
(\tau^{+}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
so that, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, on the event
\{V_{k}=i\}
one has that

\tau_{k}\leq\tau^{+}_{N^{k}_{i}}(i),

(25)

where

denotes the total number of visits of
V
to site
i
until time
k
. Indeed, if
W
exits the interval
J_{i}
by reaching some site

\overline{\nu}
), then, since

2N

\tau^{+}(i^{\prime})
.

Now, notice that there are two types of sites

:

•

Type A:

\tilde{J}_{i}\subseteq[r_{l-1},r_{l})
for some

.
Here, by optional stopping we have that, for all

E[\tau^{+}(i)]=\frac{1}{f_{l}}E\Big[(W^{(i)}_{\tau^{+}(i)}-x_{i}^{(N)})^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{(N+3\ell_{u})^{2}}{f_{l}}.

(26)

•

Type B:

J_{i}
is not contained in any of the intervals
[r_{l-1},r_{l})
for

E[\tau^{+}(i)]\leq a_{i}N^{2},

for some positive constant
a_{i}
. Notice that there are at most
2j
sites of type

.

Step 4.
To continue, we first need to introduce some notation. Define
for each
1\leq l\leq j
,

\mathbb{H}_{l}:=\left\{i\in I_{\overline{\nu}}:J_{i}\cap[r_{l-1},r_{l})\neq\emptyset\right\}.

Now note that, for each

1\leq l\leq j
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the total number of visits of the walk
W
up to time
T_{m}
to the set

\mathbb{W}_{l}:=\{x\in\mathbb{W}:r_{l-1}\leq x<r_{l}\},

can be bounded from above using the coupling (

) by

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{H}_{l}}\sum_{k=1}^{N^{m}_{i}}\tau^{+}_{k}(i),

where we recall that

N^{m}_{i}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}1(V_{r}=i).

Observe that by the convergence theorem for Markov chains, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for each
i\in I_{\overline{\nu}}

P\left(\left|\frac{1}{m}N_{i}^{m}-\pi(i)\right|>\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}}\right)^{2}\right)\leq Ce^{-Cm}.

By employing the previous display in combination with the first Borel-Cantelli lemma, it follows that eventually in
m
we have that

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{H}_{l}}\sum_{k=1}^{N^{m}_{i}}\tau^{+}_{k}(i)\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{H}_{l}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{i}^{m}}\tau^{+}_{k}(i),

where

n_{i}^{m}:=m\left(\pi(i)+\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}}\right)^{2}\right).

By the law of large numbers and (
24
), it follows that
P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}
-a.s. for each
l=1,\dots,j
we have

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=0}^{T_{m}}1_{\mathbb{W}_{l}}(W_{n})\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{H}_{l}}\left(\pi(x)+\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}}\right)^{2}\right)E[\tau^{+}(i)].

If
i\in\mathbb{H}_{l}
is of type
A
, then the summand corresponding to
i
on the right-hand side of the last display is bounded above by
\big(\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}}+\big(\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}}\big)^{2}+o_{N}(1)\big)\frac{(N+3\ell_{u})^{2}}{f_{l}}
. On the other hand, if
i
is of type
B
then its corresponding summand is bounded above by
(\frac{2}{\overline{\nu}}+o_{N}(1))a_{i}N^{2}
. Since there are at most
2j

sites of type
B
and at most
2L\nu_{l}+4
sites in
\mathbb{H}_{l}
of type
A
, a straightforward computation using that

allows us to conclude that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=0}^{T_{m}}1_{\mathbb{W}_{l}}(W_{n})\leq\left(\frac{\nu_{l}}{\nu}+\frac{10}{\overline{\nu}}+5\overline{\nu}o_{N}(1)\right)\frac{1}{f_{l}}(N+3\ell_{u})^{2}+2\left(\frac{2}{\overline{\nu}}+o_{N}(1)\right)ja_{i}N^{2}.

Similarly, by suitably bounding the sequence
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
from below stochastically, one can show that

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=0}^{T_{m}}1_{\mathbb{W}_{l}}(W_{n})\geq\left(\frac{\nu_{l}}{\nu}-\frac{10}{\overline{\nu}}-5\overline{\nu}o_{N}(1)\right)\frac{1}{f_{l}}(N-3\ell_{u})^{2}.

Step 5.

From Step 4 we conclude that
P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}
-almost surely, for each
l=1,\dots,j
,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=0}^{m}1_{S_{l}}(X_{n})\leq\frac{\left(\frac{\nu_{l}}{\nu}+\frac{10}{\overline{\nu}}+5\overline{\nu}o_{N}(1)\right)\frac{1}{f_{l}}(N+3\ell_{u})^{2}+2\left(\frac{2}{\overline{\nu}}+o_{N}(1)\right)ja_{i}N^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{j}\left(\frac{\nu_{i}}{\nu}-\frac{10}{\overline{\nu}}-5\overline{\nu}o_{N}(1)\right)\frac{1}{f_{i}}(N-3\ell_{u})^{2}}.

Hence, by setting
\nu_{l}:=\lfloor M\lambda_{l}f_{l}\rfloor
for each

, then choosing

large enough and finally taking

21
).
The lower bound can be derived similarly.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\mathbb{P}
is non-nestling or marginally nestling then, given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a period
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
such that
\mathbf{p}=(\vec{p}(x))_{x\in B_{\bf n}}
is such that for all
x\in B_{\bf n}
,
p(x)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})
and the corresponding
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
-RWPE satisfies

\left|\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(X_{n}=0)-\left(-\inf_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{P}}}I_{\sigma}(0)\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

Again, we subdivide the proof into various steps.

Step 0
. We begin by proving that, for any periodic environment
\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
with support
\mathcal{S}
, we have the upper bound

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(X_{n}=0)\leq-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0).

(27)

To this end, note that, for
any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the exponential Chebychev inequality together with the Markov property supplies us with the upper bound

P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(X_{n}=0)\leq E_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,X_{n}\rangle}\big]\leq\Big(\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta)}\Big)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{n\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta)}.

As a consequence,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(X_{n}=0)\leq\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}\Lambda_{\sigma}(\theta)=-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0),

where in the last equality we have again used Sion’s minimax theorem as in Lemma
3.2
. Hence, we obtain (
27
).

Step 1.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and, recalling from above (
3
) that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})
denotes the essential support of
\omega(0)
under
\mathbb{P}
, choose

\mathcal{S}:=\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}\}\subseteq\Big\{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{V}}:\exists\,\vec{p}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})\text{ with }\sup_{e\in\mathbb{V}}|\sigma(e)-p(e)|<\varepsilon\Big\}

(28)

such that

\Big|\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0)-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}}I_{\sigma}(0)\Big|\leq\varepsilon.

(29)

Since the infimum in
\inf_{{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{P}}}I_{\sigma}(0)
is
attained in
\partial{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{P}}
(see Lemma
4.2
below), we can assume
without loss of generality that the convex hull of the set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}:=\{\mathrm{d}(\sigma):\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\}
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension smaller than or equal to
d-1
and thus we may take

.
By Lemma
3.2
, there exist

and
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(t^{*},\theta^{*})
is a saddle point of the mapping

from Lemma

so that, in particular, we have

\Lambda(t^{*},\theta^{*})=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\Lambda(t,\theta)=\max_{t\in\Delta_{j}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda(t,\theta)=-\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0).

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
t^{*}=(t_{1}^{*},\dots,t_{j}^{*})
satisfies

for all
i
, as otherwise we may replace
\mathcal{S}
by
\mathcal{S}^{*}:=\{\sigma_{i}:t_{i}^{*}>0\}
which, by definition of
\Lambda
and the fact that
(t^{*},\theta^{*})
is a saddle point of
\Lambda
, verifies that
\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}}I_{\sigma}(0)=\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}I_{\sigma}(0)
. In particular, under this assumption, it follows from the proof of Proposition

that

.
Therefore, for
i=1,\dots,j

\sigma_{i}^{*}

We now observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}^{*}\mathrm{d}(\sigma_{i}^{*})=0.

(30)

Indeed, since

has rational coordinates. We can now apply Lemma

where

is the

such that

.

Step 2.
Next, we give a construction of the RWPE from Step 1 which will be
convenient for the proof of the lower bound. For each

1\leq i\leq j
, let

be an independent random walk jumping according to

. Set

,
\tau^{(i)}_{0}:=1_{S_{i}}(X_{0})
and, for each

Clearly

from Step 1.

Step 3.
Finally, consider the space-periodic environment

defined on each

Hence, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for some constant

(depending only on the dimension

) we have that

\mathrm{e}^{n\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*})}P_{0,\omega^{*}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\left(A^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}\cap B^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{n\overline{\Lambda}(\theta^{*})}P_{0,\omega^{*}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\left(|X_{n}|\leq n\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{cn\varepsilon}P_{0,\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(|X_{n}|\leq n\varepsilon).

This immediately gives (
32
).

Step 4.
From the law of large numbers and (
31
) in Step 1, for every

,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Big|\frac{\tau^{(i)}_{n-1}}{n}-t_{i}^{*}\Big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\qquad P_{0,\omega^{*}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}-a.s.

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Z^{(i)}_{n}}{n}=\mathrm{d}(\sigma_{i}^{*})\qquad P_{0,\omega^{*}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}-a.s.

It follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{0,\omega^{*}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(A^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}\cap B^{(\varepsilon)}_{n})=1
.
Therefore, from Step 3, we see that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

where the last inequality follows from the proof of Proposition

.
Now, note that for each

(

there exists
z_{n}\in\mathrm{B}(0,\frac{n}{\log n})
such that for all

x\in B_{n}:=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|x^{\prime}-z_{n}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{\frac{1}{d}}\}
we have

|\omega(x,e)-\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x,e)|\leq\varepsilon.

(37)

Let
a_{n}=o(n)
. Now, consider the following events:

(1)

A_{1}
, the event that the
random walk
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
moves from
0
to the center
z_{n}
of the ball

B_{n}
in
a_{n}
steps;

(2)

A_{2}
the event that the random walk

(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
stays between times
0
and
n-2a_{n}
at
\infty
-distance at most
\delta(\log n)^{\frac{1}{d}}
to
z_{n}
, and that at time
n-2a_{n}
it ends up in the center
z_{n}
of the
ball
B_{n}
;

(3)

A_{3}
, the event that the random walk
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}

moves in
a_{n}
steps, from the center
z_{n}

of the ball
B_{n}
to the center of the box
C_{n}
.

By the Markov
property, we then have, for appropriate choices of
a_{n}
, and
n
even, that

P_{0,\omega}(X_{n}=0)\geq P_{0,\omega}(A_{1})P_{z_{n},\omega}(A_{2})P_{z_{n},\omega}(A_{3})\geq\kappa^{2a_{n}}P_{z_{n},\omega}(A_{2}),

where we have used the uniform ellipticity property and the fact that
the side of the box
C_{n}
is of length
o(n)
.

We will now lower bound the probability
P_{z_{n},\omega}(A_{2})
. Indeed, bearing in mind (
37
) we note that

P_{z_{n},\omega}(A_{2})\geq e^{-c\varepsilon n}P_{z_{n},\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\Big(X_{n-2a_{n}}=z_{n},\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-2a_{n}}|X_{m}-z_{n}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{1/d}\Big).

(38)

Now note that for
n
even, we have that

P_{z_{n},\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}(X_{n-2a_{n}}=z_{n})>0.

Furthermore,
writing
\theta_{*}
for the argmin of the Laplace transform
\Lambda_{X_{1}}
of
X_{1}
, so that

E_{z_{n},\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\big[e^{\langle\theta_{*},X_{1}\rangle}\big]=\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{z_{n},\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\big[e^{\langle\theta,X_{1}\rangle}\big],

we denote by

P_{*}
the law under which the random walk
X_{1}
jumps from
x
to
x\pm e_{i}
with probability

\frac{e^{\theta_{*,i}}\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x,e_{i})}{\Lambda_{X_{1}}(\theta_{*})}
and

\frac{e^{-\theta_{*,i}}\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(x,-e_{i})}{\Lambda_{X_{1}}(\theta_{*})}
, respectively. Writing furthermore

I_{\bf n,\bf p}
for the rate function of RWPE in the environment

\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P_{z_{n},\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}\Big(X_{n-2a_{n}}=z_{n},\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-2a_{n}}|X_{m}-z_{n}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{1/d}\Big)\\
&=\Lambda^{n-2a_{n}}_{X}(\theta_{*})P_{*}\Big(X_{n-2a_{n}}=0,\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-2a_{n}}|X_{m}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{1/d}\Big)\\
&=e^{-(n-2a_{n})I_{\bf n,\bf p}(0)}P_{*}\Big(X_{n-2a_{n}}=0,\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-2a_{n}}|X_{m}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{1/d}\Big).\end{split}

(39)

From (
38
) and (
39
), we see that it is enough to prove that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{*}\Big(\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-2a_{n}}|X_{m}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{1/d},X_{n-2a_{n}}=0\Big)=0.

To do this, we will consider paths of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
which return to

0
every
L_{n}=2\lfloor\delta(\log n)^{1/d}\rfloor
steps. Without loss of
generality, we will choose
a_{n}
so that
L_{n}
is a divisor of

n-2a_{n}
. Hence,

P_{*}\Big(\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-2a_{n}}|X_{m}|\leq\delta(\log n)^{1/d},X_{n-2a_{n}}=0\Big)\geq\big(P_{*}(X_{L_{n}}=0)\big)^{(n-2a_{n})/L_{n}}.

Next, we will apply the local limit theorem for (centered) RWPE, Theorem 1.1, proved by Takenami in
[
T02
]
. While this is proved for aperiodic RWPE, it can be easily adapted to
the nearest neighbor case using the fact that the even sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. While the remaining assumptions of that source are easy to check in our setting, let us note that the second part of Assumption 1.3 of
[
T02
, Theorem 1.1]
(centered increments under stationary distribution) follows from the above transition from
P_{z_{n},\omega_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}}
to
P_{*}
.
As a consequence,

[
T02
, Theorem 1.1]
then in particular entails the
existence of a constant

such that

P_{*}(X_{L_{n}}=0)=\frac{C}{(L_{n})^{d/2}}+o\left(L_{n}^{-d/2}\right).

Hence, for
n
even,

P_{*}\Big(\sup_{0\leq m\leq n-a_{n}}|X_{m}|_{\infty}\leq\delta(\log n)^{\frac{1}{d}},X_{n-a_{n}}=0\Big)\geq Ce^{-C\frac{n\log\log n}{(\log n)^{1/d}}}.

Finally, we construct a deterministic path from the center of the
neighborhood to the origin, which happens with probability at least

\kappa^{a_{n}}
. Putting this together, we conclude that for
n
even,

P_{0,\omega}(X_{n}=0)\geq C\kappa^{2a_{n}}e^{-(n-a_{n})I_{\bf n,\bf p}(0)}e^{-C\frac{n\log\log n}{(\log n)^{1/d}}}.

Thus, by (
36
),

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{2m}\log P_{0,\omega}(X_{2m}=0)\geq-I_{\bf n,\bf p}(0)\geq\log\Big(\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}}\sum_{e}e^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}\sigma(e)\Big)-h(\varepsilon).

∎

4.3
Proof of part (i) (maximizer at the boundary of the convex hull of the law)

Lemma 4.2
.

In the non-nestling or marginally nestling cases, the supremum

\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}p(e)

is attained on the boundary of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}
.

Proof.

We know that
\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}p(e)=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}},
where
p_{i}:=p(e_{i})
and
q_{i}:=p(-e_{i})
. Thus, it is enough to study
\sup_{p\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}
subject to the constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{d}(p_{i}+q_{i})=1
and
p_{i},q_{i}>0
, as the environment is uniformly elliptic. Applying the method of Lagrange multipliers, we start by
defining the Lagrangian function of the
p_{i}
,
q_{i}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

L(p,\lambda):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}+\lambda\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(p_{i}+q_{i})-1\Big).

Thus,

\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}}+\lambda=0

\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}}+\lambda=0

\sum_{i=1}^{d}(p_{i}+q_{i})=1

Solving these equations, we obtain that
\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}
and
p_{i}=q_{i}
for all
i
. Hence, the only local maximum of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}
corresponds to the zero drift case. Now, suppose that
\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}p(e)
is achieved at some
\vec{p}_{0}\in\textrm{int}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}})
.
In the non-nestling case, this would imply that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}
would have a local maximum at
\vec{p}_{0}
where
\vec{p}_{0}
has a non-zero drift, leading to a contradiction. On the other hand, in the marginally nestling case, we know that there exists some
\vec{p}_{1}\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{P}}
, such that the
\mathrm{d}(\vec{p}_{1})=0
. But it is easy to see that for this
\vec{p}_{1}=(p_{1,1},\ldots,p_{1,d},q_{1,1},\ldots,q_{1,d})
one has that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{1,i}q_{1,i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{1,i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{1,i}=\frac{d}{2},

which is the common value at all points with zero drift.
This completes the proof.
∎
