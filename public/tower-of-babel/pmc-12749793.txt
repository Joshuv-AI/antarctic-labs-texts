Disrupted rest-task shift of multi-band electroencephalography complexity in patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Shunsuke Tamura, Tsuyoshi Iwasaki, Osamu Ichikawa, Kantaro Nishigori, Yoshihiro Iwamura, Takako Fujimoto, Tatsuhiro Tamada, Hidetaka Nagata, Yoji Hirano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Brain activity undergoes intrinsic fluctuations with complexities specific to both the resting state and the task state. However, in patients with schizophrenia (SZ), where the boundary between internal and external realms is blurred, optimal modulation of these complexities between rest and stimulus phases is likely disrupted. Here, we present a frequency-dependent rest-task shift in electroencephalography (EEG) complexity in normal controls (NCs) and the associated abnormal patterns in patients with SZ. Specifically, we examined EEG complexity in different frequency bands, quantified by multiscale entropy (MSE), during the resting state and a 40 Hz auditory steady-state response (ASSR) task in 77 normal controls (NCs) and 103 patients with SZ. We compared the ASSR/resting MSE ratio between the NC and SZ groups. In the NC group, transitioning from the resting state to the ASSR state caused increased alpha-band entropy and decreased gamma-band entropy, whereas no such shifts were observed in patients with SZ. This study suggests that in NCs, alpha-band oscillatory complexity, which influences cortical response properties, increases during the transition from intrinsic to extrinsic information processing, while gamma-band oscillatory complexity decreases, likely due to concentration on auditory processing of a temporal enriching stimulation with high salience. Patients with SZ, which are marked by impaired neural synchrony, lacked the NCs’ pronounced changes in neural complexity across frequency bands. This indicates that in patients with SZ, disruptions in the spontaneous activity that shapes the internal mental state may alter the properties of cortical responses and entrainment to external stimuli.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a complex psychiatric syndrome characterized by various symptoms, including auditory hallucinations, delusions, thought disorders, ego disturbances, passivity phenomena, and cognitive deficits. Although no objective biological markers for the diagnosis or treatment of SZ have been identified, considerable evidence concerning brain dysfunctions in SZ patients has been obtained1–4. Recently, a comprehensive review by Northoff and Gomez-Pillar5 suggested that altered spontaneous (resting-state or prestimulus) brain activity in SZ patients could lead to reduced neural differentiation of task-related activity from resting-state or prestimulus activity, i.e., a reduced rest–task shift. The reduced rest-task shift in SZ patients could be linked to the abnormal differentiation of internally and externally oriented cognition in these patients.

Complexity analysis to evaluate the degree of irregularity of signals6 would enable analyses of the dynamical network characteristics of both resting-state and task-related brain activity. Considering that electroencephalography (EEG) signals are summations of electrical activities generated from multiple brain regions, EEG complexity might reflect the number of distinct neural generators and degree of phase synchronization among the neural generators7–9. Specifically, high EEG complexity indicates that neural generators tend to be widely distributed and desynchronized. In contrast, low EEG complexity indicates that neural generators tend to be narrowly localized and synchronized. In addition, neural systems are considered to dynamically shift among different brain states with varied functional brain connectivity patterns over time. Thus, more frequent shifts among brain states might cause greater EEG complexity10.

Several studies have investigated whether the reduced rest-task shift in EEG complexity is observed in SZ patients11,12. Notably, Ibáñez-Molina et al. reported that EEG complexity in the left temporal‒parietal regions increased from the resting state to an image naming task in healthy controls, whereas such a change was not observed in SZ patients. Based on this result, SZ patients may not be able to modulate dynamic network states in response to changing demands. Although most previous neuropsychiatry studies have performed complexity analyses using full-band EEG signals13, applying this approach to down-sampled signals with different sampling frequencies allows for a separate assessment of EEG complexity across various frequency bands14–16. Specifically, when the sampling frequency is lower, low-frequency signals become more prominent. In several previous studies, this approach has been employed to investigate altered EEG complexity in different frequency bands in SZ patients in the resting and task states (e.g.,17–19). However, to our knowledge, no studies have examined whether reduced the rest-task shift in EEG complexity in SZ patients is specific to certain frequency bands.

To address this issue, we examined the reduced rest-task shift in EEG complexity in SZ patients from the perspective of frequency dependency by applying a multiscale entropy (MSE) analysis technique to both resting-state and task-state EEG data collected within the framework of the Asian Consortium on EEG studies in Psychosis (ACEP, http://acep.saturn.bindcloud.jp). We adopted an auditory steady-state response (ASSR) task using a 40 Hz click-train stimulus that elicits gamma-band oscillations throughout the brain20,21 because several meta-analysis studies22,23 and a scoping review24,25 have suggested that 40 Hz ASSR is a robust task for detecting neural oscillatory deficits in SZ patients. Although most previous studies, including our own (e.g.,26–31), analyzed resting- and ASSR-state neural activities separately, comparisons of these activities in our recent study31 revealed that the modulation of alpha-band spontaneous power from resting- to ASSR-state was reduced in patients with SZ. The present study aimed to extend our previous findings by investigating the rest-to-ASSR shift in terms of EEG complexity. We first identified MSE differences between the resting state and ASSR task in healthy subjects, focusing on the gamma frequency band. Next, we investigated whether this difference was observed in SZ patients.

Methods and materials

Participants

In this study, 180 participants, including 77 normal controls (NCs) and 103 patients with schizophrenia (SZ), were recruited. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) history of neurological illness or major head trauma; (ii) history of electroconvulsive therapy; (iii) history of alcohol/drug dependence or abuse; and (iv) verbal IQ below 75. All NC participants were recruited from the local community in the Fukuoka metropolitan area, and SZ patients were recruited from Kyushu University Hospital. All NCs were screened using the Structured Clinical Interview (SCID)-nonpatient edition, and the participants and their first-degree relatives did not have Axis-I psychiatric disorders. All patients were diagnosed based on the DSM-5 criteria, with diagnostic verification by two experienced psychiatrists, and their symptom severity was evaluated using the PANSS32. The Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS)33 and Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART)34 were performed for evaluating cognitive functions and verbal IQ, respectively. EEG recording and procedures

During the resting-state EEG and ASSR recordings, the participants were instructed to close their eyes and relax in the supine position in a shielded room free from external interference and noise. During ASSR recordings, 40 Hz trains of 1 ms white noise clicks with a 500 ms duration were presented 150 times with a 600 ms interstimulus interval via inserted earphones (ER-3, Etymotic Research, Inc.). The sound pressure level was set to 80 dB using a sound level meter (type 2260; Brüel and Kjær), a 1/2-inch condenser microphone (type 4192; Brüel and Kjær), and a custom-made coupler (AD-0213; Kyushu InterTech).

EEG measurements were performed with two EEG systems (EEG-1000 or EEG-1200; Nihon-Koden Co., Tokyo, Japan). All continuous EEG data were acquired using a 19-electrode silver/silver chloride (Ag–AgCl) passive electrode setup (Nihon-Koden Co.) with standard 10–20 system electrode placement. The impedance was maintained at <20 kΩ for all the electrodes. EEG data were amplified using Neurofax amplifiers (Nihon-Koden Co.) and digitized at 500 Hz with an online 1–250 Hz bandpass filter.

EEG preprocessing

EEG data were preprocessed using MNE Python (https://mne.tools/dev/index.html). An offline 1–100 Hz bandpass filter and 60 Hz notch filters were applied to the EEG data. Ocular, cardiac, and muscle artifacts were removed using independent component analysis. The artifact-free EEG data were average-referenced, and 1100 ms epochs were created separately for the resting-state and ASSR task data (epochs with peak-to-peak amplitudes > 200 mV were rejected). Epochs during the ASSR task started at 400 ms prior to the stimulus onset (referred to as “−400 ms”) and lasted for 700 ms after the stimulus onset. We created the resting-state epochs by segmenting the resting-state EEG data every 1100 ms with no overlap. More than 100 epochs were obtained in both the resting-state and ASSR tasks for all participants. The stimulus-evoked data at each electrode were obtained by averaging epochs across trials.

We calculated each time scale of entropy (τ = 4–50) at each electrode separately for the resting-state and ASSR data by calculating the MSE value from each epoch and averaging across epochs. Considering that the sampling rate of the EEG time series for the MSE analysis was 400 Hz (the sampling frequency of the EEG signal was down-sampled from 500 to 400 Hz before MSE analysis), the time scales of 33–40, 14–32, and 4–13 in the MSE analysis corresponded to the alpha (8–10 Hz), beta (12–30 Hz), and gamma (30–80 Hz) frequency bands, respectively. We calculated an ASSR/resting ratio of the entropy at each time scale by dividing the entropy value during the ASSR stimulation by that in the resting state. We obtained entropy values for the alpha, beta, and gamma frequency bands separately for each state by averaging the entropy values across the time scales of 33–40, 14–32, and 4–13 before performing statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.6.3) and Python (version 3.7.6). Unpaired t tests and chi-square tests were used for group comparisons of demographic variables. For the statistical analysis of the demographic data, p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

We analyzed the phase-locking factor (PLF) and evoked power, which are the most robust measures for detecting 40 Hz ASSR deficits in SZ patients. The analysis procedure of the PLF and evoked power is described in the Supplementary Methods. We calculated the mean 40 Hz PLF and evoked power within the time window (200–500 ms) at the electrodes of interest (Fz, O1, and O2). We conducted a group comparison using unpaired t tests for the mean PLF and evoked power at each electrode. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction on the basis of the number of electrodes, resulting in the uncorrected p value being multiplied by 3.

The time scales for which a significant main effect of state (resting or ASSR state) was confirmed separately for the NC or SZ group data were determined by applying cluster-based permutation t tests to the entropy data at the Fz, O1, or O2 electrodes (the rationale for selecting these electrodes is described in the Results section)35. The detailed procedures for the cluster-based permutation t-tests are described in the Supplementary Methods. Based on the results of cluster-based permutation t-test for NC group (see Results for details), we focused on alpha- and gamma-band entropies. In addition, entropy measures were averaged across O1 and O2 electrodes for further analysis.

We performed a four-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA) with three within-subject factors (state (resting or ASSR), frequency band (alpha or gamma), and electrode (Fz or occipital (O1 and O2))) and one between-subject factor (group (SZ or NC)) based on the entropy of each frequency band. We conducted two- and three-way rmANOVAs and unpaired t tests as post hoc tests (see details in the Results). Multiple comparisons were adjusted in post hoc analyses via the Bonferroni method to correct the p values. For the three-way rmANOVA for each electrode, the obtained p value was multiplied by two. For the two-way rmANOVAs, which were performed for different combinations of frequency bands and electrodes, a total of four tests were conducted. Thus, the obtained p value was multiplied by four. Unpaired t-tests on entropy value were performed eight times separately for each frequency band, each electrode, and each state. Thus, the obtained p value was multiplied by eight. Lastly, the p-values of unpaired t-tests on ASSR/resting ratio of entropy values were multiplied by four because they were performed four times separately for each frequency band and each electrode.

Demographic data

There were no significant group differences in terms of age (t = 0.53, p = 0.594) or sex (χ2 = 0.05, p = 0.829). The education year and socioeconomic status (SES) were significantly lower in the SZ group than in the NC group (t = 4.47, p < 0.001 and t = 7.00, p < 0.001, respectively), whereas there was no significant difference between groups in terms of the parental SES (t = 0.72, p = 0.473).

To identify the frequency bands with significant differences in entropy between the resting and ASSR states, we applied cluster-based permutation tests to the entropy data as a function of the time scale at the electrodes of interest (Fz, O1, and O2) (Fig. 2B). The Fz electrode was chosen because the maximum 40 Hz ASSR is obtained at this electrode, whereas the O1 and O2 electrodes were selected as the main sources of strong alpha band activity in the resting state. At all electrodes, we found two significant clusters in the time scales associated with the alpha and beta bands (O1: corrected cluster-p = 0.003; O2: corrected cluster-p = 0.003; Fz: corrected cluster-p = 0.003) and beta and gamma bands (O1: corrected cluster-p = 0.003; O2: corrected cluster-p = 0.003; Fz: corrected cluster-p = 0.003). Additionally, we found significant clusters in the time scales corresponding to the beta band at the O2-corrected level (corrected cluster-p = 0.009). A reversal in the magnitudes of the entropy between the ASSR and resting states was observed in the beta band at all electrodes; therefore, we focused mainly on state-dependent changes in entropy in the alpha and gamma bands in our comparisons between the NC and SZ groups.

Figure 2C shows topographic maps of alpha-, beta-, and gamma-band entropy in the resting state and ASSR states for the SZ group. In contrast to the NC group, no clear differences in the entropy between the two states were observed in any frequency band. The cluster-based permutation tests for the entropy data at the electrodes of interest did not reveal any significant clusters across time scales (Fig. 2D).

The results of the post-hoc unpaired t-tests for each frequency band, electrode, and state revealed a significant difference between the NC and SZ groups in the resting-state alpha-band entropy at the occipital electrode (NC < SZ, t = −2.93, p = 0.031) (Fig. 4), whereas no significant group differences were observed in the other tests. Lastly, unpaired t tests based on the ASSR/resting state MSE ratio between groups revealed that the alpha band ratio in the NC group was significantly greater than that in the SZ group at the Fz (t = 2.72, corrected p = 0.029) and occipital electrodes (t = 4.23, corrected p < 0.001), whereas the gamma band ratio in the NC group was significantly lower (Fz: t = −4.46, corrected p < 0.001; occipital: t = 5.14, corrected p < 0.001). These results indicate that the state-dependent shift in EEG complexity across multiple frequency bands observed in the NC group did not occur in SZ patients.

No significant correlations were observed between the ASSR/resting state entropy ratio and clinical symptoms. Detailed results were shown in Supplementary Results.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated a frequency-dependent rest-task shift in EEG complexity in NCs and the associated abnormal patterns in patients with SZ. The results revealed increased alpha band complexity and decreased gamma band complexity from the resting state to the ASSR state in the NC group (Fig. 2), whereas this rest-task shift was diminished in patients with SZ (Figs. 3 and 4). Although several previous studies have reported that SZ patients exhibit reduced rest-task shifts in EEG complexity12,13, we are the first to reveal the frequency bands in which the rest-task shift in EEG complexity is diminished in patients with SZ.

First, we discuss the possible mechanism underlying the normal rest-task shift in EEG complexity observed in NCs. EEG complexity reflects not only the intrinsic complexity arising from a single brain source but also a composite signal arising from multiple sources. In addition, the different time scales of EEG complexity reflect the characteristics of networks at different spatial scales. Specifically, the EEG complexity at finer time scales reflects the extent to which localized brain regions near certain electrodes fluctuate dynamically, whereas the degree of complexity at coarser time scales indicates the number of independent signal sources contributing to the formation of EEG signals36–38. Thus, on the basis of the above interpretation of EEG complexity, the decreased gamma-band entropy during 40 Hz ASSR stimulation in the NC group may reflect state-dependent modulation of neural activity within localized brain regions near the electrodes of interest. Considering that greater EEG complexity may result from more frequent transitions among different types of information processing in the brain10, the lower EEG complexity during ASSR stimulation could be attributed to temporally enriched stimulation, which facilitates gamma-band oscillations for auditory information processing within the brain regions near the Fz and occipital electrodes. Notably, 40 Hz auditory stimulation is known to amplify auditory salience compared with rhythmic auditory stimuli at different frequencies, resulting in gamma-band oscillations across broad brain regions20. Few prior studies have revealed rest-task shifts in gamma-band EEG complexity. Notably, Kaur et al.39 analyzed the MSE in the eyes-closed and eyes-open resting states and visual task states in healthy young adults. Their findings revealed that the entropy at finer time scales was lower in the eye-open resting and visual task states compared with that in the eyes-closed resting state, which is consistent with the gamma-band entropy shift observed in the 40 Hz ASSR task in this study.

On the other hand, alpha-band EEG complexity may stem from a complex mixture of alpha-band oscillations originating from multiple cortical and subcortical sources, as alpha-band oscillations have been reported to propagate from higher- to lower-order cortical regions and from the cortex to the thalamus during quiet wakefulness40. As high EEG complexity is known to reflect the number of desynchronized neural generators8,9, the lower alpha-band entropy observed in the resting state could be attributed to strong functional connectivity among thalamocortical regions. Furthermore, the desynchronization of thalamocortical network activity may underly the increased EEG complexity observed during ASSR stimulation.

Our prior study31 also demonstrated frequency dependency in the rest-task shift of spontaneous power from the resting-state to the ASSR-state. Specifically, alpha-band spontaneous power was reduced during ASSR stimulation relative to the resting state in the NC group. Similar to EEG complexity, it is conceivable that the rest-task shift of alpha-band power results from network-level control of thalamocortical regions. In contrast, in the present study, we succeeded in identifying a rest-task shift of gamma-band EEG complexity, although no significant rest-task shift of the gamma-band spontaneous activity was observed in our previous work. Compared with spectral power, EEG complexity at a specific electrode is more strongly influenced by the degree of synchronizations among widely distributed neural generators8,9. Therefore, our findings provide new insights into the underlying mechanisms of state-dependent control of gamma-band neural activity from a network perspective.

We next discuss the mechanisms underlying the reduced gamma-band and alpha-band rest-task shifts in EEG complexity in patients with SZ. As described above, the decreased gamma-band EEG complexity during ASSR stimulation in the NC group may be attributed to an enhanced focus on auditory processing in response to highly salient auditory input. In this context, the diminished rest-task shift in EEG complexity observed in SZ patients likely occurs due to aberrant salience signaling related to auditory processing. Although few studies have investigated the neural mechanisms underlying auditory salience in SZ patients, several studies have reported that patients with SZ exhibit altered neural responses to high-salience (aversive) auditory stimuli, primarily in limbic brain regions41,42. The gamma-band entropy in each state was compared between groups, and SZ patients tended to exhibit greater entropy during ASSR stimulation than did NCs (Fig. 4). However, no significant group differences in gamma-band entropy were observed for any state or electrode, suggesting that the diminished rest‒task shift in gamma-band entropy arises from altered EEG complexity during both the resting state and ASSR state.

If the rest-task shift in alpha-band EEG complexity observed in the NC group results from state-dependent regulation of thalamocortical network activity, it is conceivable that SZ patients may be unable to modulate the synchronization state of the thalamocortical network according to situational demands. Our previous study using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)43 revealed abnormal patterns of thalamocortical functional connectivity in SZ patients in both the resting and ASSR states. However, we were unable to directly compare functional connectivity between the two states because of the difference in the fMRI scan design between the resting-state and auditory experiments. Therefore, our present findings on alpha-band EEG complexity may elucidate state-dependent dysregulation of the thalamocortical network in SZ patients. We next focused on group differences in alpha-band entropy separately for each state. Although no significant differences were detected between the NC and SZ groups for any state or electrode, the SZ patients exhibited greater entropy in the occipital regions in the resting state and lower entropy at the Fz electrode during ASSR stimulation than did the NCs (Fig. 4). Taken together, the diminished shift in EEG complexity from the resting state to the ASSR state may result not only from high complexity in the resting state but also from low complexity in the ASSR stimulation state or a combination of both factors.

Direct comparison of neural oscillatory features between the resting- and ASSR-states enabled us to identify state-dependent control deficits that have often been overlooked in SZ studies using the ASSR paradigm (e.g.,26–31). Notably, control deficits of gamma-band oscillations in patients with SZ were identified for the first time through EEG complexity analysis. Hirano et al.26 reported that gamma-band spontaneous activity during ASSR stimulation was abnormally elevated in patients with SZ, whereas no significant difference in resting-state gamma-band power was observed between the SZ and NC groups. Such ASSR-specific gamma-band oscillatory deficits may be attributed to impaired network-level control among widely distributed neural generators.

Although we obtained meaningful findings regarding abnormal patterns of EEG complexity at multiple scales and the associated rest-task shifts in SZ patients, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we could not fully rule out pharmacological effects on the results of the present study. Although we found no significant correlations between ASSR/resting ratio of alpha- and gamma-band entropy ASSR/resting ratio with the CPZ equivalent dose44 (alpha-band at Fz electrode: rho = 0.06, p = 0.565; alpha-band at O electrode: rho = −0.00, p = 0.962; gamma-band at Fz electrode: rho = −0.06, p = 0.568; gamma-band at O electrode: rho = −0.02, p = 0.875), a previous study reported that EEG complexity across different frequency bands reflects the therapeutic effects of antipsychotic treatment18. Second, we cannot exclude the effects of maturation or aging on EEG complexity in the present study. In healthy brains, entropy at fine and coarse time scales increases and decreases, respectively, during early maturation45,46. Conversely, aging is associated with decreased entropy at fine time scales and increased entropy at coarse time scales47. Future investigations on the abnormal MSE patterns associated with maturation and aging could elucidate disease progression patterns in neural oscillations associated with SZ. Finally, we were unable to identify the neural sources contributing to EEG complexity in the present study. Further investigations using magnetoencephalography with higher spatial resolution are warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the diminished rest-task shift from a network-level perspective.

In conclusion, we identified a frequency-dependent reduction in the rest-task shift in EEG complexity in SZ patients by applying MSE analysis to EEG data obtained in the resting-state and during 40 Hz ASSR stimulation. The disrupted rest-task shifts in the alpha and gamma band complexity reflect state-dependent control dysfunctions of global and local network circuits, respectively. Beyond the present study, reduced rest-task shifts in EEG complexity in SZ patients have been consistently observed regardless of the task type, neuroimaging modality, or analytical measure5. Therefore, altered state-dependent regulation of brain activity, which may blur the boundary between internal and external cognition, is considered a key mechanism underlying the pathophysiology of SZ.
