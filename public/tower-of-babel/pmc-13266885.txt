Clinical characteristics and risk factors associated with mortality in patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia in the intensive care unit
Authors: Kang Zhang, Wenshuai Ji, Hao Yang, Xinyu Miao, Xiaoxuan He, Kai Xie, Haifeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mortality rate among patients in the intensive care units (ICUs) with severe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is high. Identification of severe CAP early in the course and transferring to appropriate setting seem favorable. This study aimed to identify clinical characteristics and the risk factors associated with mortality of severe CAP in the ICU (ICU-CAP).MethodsA multi-center, prospective study was conducted at 11 teaching hospitals in China from December 2017 to October 2021. Patients who met the inclusion criteria were assigned to the ICU group and the non-ICU group according to whether they were admitted to the ICU.ResultsA total of 170 patients with severe CAP were included, 111 patients were admitted to the ICU and 59 patients were admitted to the ward. Among patients in the ICU, 91.9% of patients were with respiratory failure, 65.8% of patients with consciousness disturbance, 23.4% were in shock state, and 73.0% (81/111) of patients had at least one comorbidity. In-hospital mortality for ICU-CAP was 34.2% (38/111), 28-day mortality was 27.9% (31/111), and 7-day mortality was 10.8% (12/111). Mortality in patients with pneumonia severity index class V (PSI-V) was 40.0% (18/45), mortality in patients with invasive mechanical ventilation was 40.2% (33/82). In the ICU subgroup, invasive mechanical ventilation [odds ratio (OR) =3.35; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.14–9.81; P=0.02] and age ≥60 years (OR =2.64; 95% CI: 1.07–6.53; P=0.03) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality.ConclusionsIn this multicenter prospective cohort, severe CAP patients admitted to the ICU exhibited substantial disease severity, with high rates of treatment failure and mortality. Invasive mechanical ventilation and age ≥60 years were associated with in-hospital mortality in the ICU subgroup.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this multicenter prospective cohort of 170 patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), intensive care unit (ICU)‑admitted patients had substantial disease severity. The in‑hospital mortality rate was 34.2%, 28‑day mortality 27.9%, and 7‑day mortality 10.8%. Invasive mechanical ventilation [odds ratio (OR) =3.35; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.14–9.81] and age ≥60 years (OR =2.64; 95% CI: 1.07–6.53) were independently associated with in‑hospital mortality in the ICU subgroup.

What is known and what is new?

• Severe CAP carries high mortality, particularly among ICU patients. Advanced age and the need for mechanical ventilation have been previously linked to poor outcomes.

• This study provides contemporary, multicenter prospective data from China, reporting early (7‑day) mortality in ICU‑CAP patients. It also reframes invasive mechanical ventilation as a severity‑associated prognostic factor rather than a purely causal risk factor, acknowledging the observational nature of the evidence.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should recognize that age ≥60 years and the need for invasive mechanical ventilation identify a very high‑risk ICU population. Early, close monitoring and protocolized supportive care are warranted in these patients.

• Given the absence of standardized ICU admission criteria and the potential for confounding, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously. Future prospective studies with uniform admission protocols are needed to validate these findings and to refine risk stratification tools.

Introduction

Severe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a leading cause of admission to intensive care units (ICUs) worldwide and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality (1,2). Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, patients with severe CAP frequently develop life-threatening complications, including septic shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) (3-5), resulting in mortality rates ranging from 20% to 50% among those requiring ICU admission (6,7). The high burden of mortality is further exacerbated by the emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens and the challenges of managing invasive mechanical ventilation in critically ill patients (8,9).

Timely identification of severe CAP and appropriate triage to the ICU setting are critical to improving patient outcomes (2). However, the decision to admit a patient with severe CAP to the ICU remains complex and is often guided by clinical judgment, supported by prognostic scoring systems such as the pneumonia severity index (PSI) and CURB-65 (confusion, urea, respiratory rate, blood pressure, age ≥65 years) (10,11). While these tools are useful for identifying patients at risk of death, they were not specifically developed for ICU populations and may not fully capture the dynamic clinical course of patients with severe CAP after ICU admission (12). Moreover, most existing studies on risk factors for mortality in severe CAP have been conducted in Western populations or have focused on mixed settings, leaving a gap in understanding the clinical characteristics and outcomes of ICU-admitted patients with severe CAP in China, where pathogen profiles and healthcare practices may differ (13,14).

Given the persistently high mortality and the need for context-specific evidence, we conducted a multicenter prospective study to describe the clinical characteristics and identify risk factors associated with in-hospital mortality among patients with severe CAP admitted to ICUs in China. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0531/rc).

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a multi-center, prospective study conducted at 11 teaching hospitals in China. All adult patients with severe CAP were screened from December 2017 to October 2021. Patients who met the criteria were assigned to the ICU group and the non-ICU group according to whether they were admitted to the ICU, to demonstrate the clinical characteristics and prognosis of ICU-CAP. Patients admitted to the ICU were divided into survival group and death group according to whether they died in the hospital, to determine the risk factors associated with mortality. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Chinese Medicine (No. 2017HL-002-01). All participating hospitals were informed and agreed with this study (see Appendix 1 for the list of participating hospitals). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study population

Patients were eligible if they met the following criteria: age ≥18 years, and met severe CAP criteria (defined with the modified American Thoracic Society criteria) (15,16).

Patients were excluded if they had one of the following criteria: (I) pregnant and lactating women; (II) patients with COVID-19; (III) patients with hematologic malignancies, various solid tumors; (IV) patients who are unwilling to cooperate; (V) patients with severe immunosuppression [due to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, immunosuppressive conditions, or medication]; (VI) patients unwilling to sign the informed consent.

Study outcomes and data collection

The following clinical data on day 1 of admission were collected: demographics, comorbidities, primary symptoms and vital signs, microbial etiology, smoking status, vaccination history, prior antibiotic use within 72 hours before admission, time from symptom onset to antibiotic administration and severity of illness scores including PSI score, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, and CURB-65 score. If any laboratory examination was recorded more than once on day 1, the most severe value was recorded for analysis. Two trained researchers independently reviewed the medical records and collected the data. Any disagreement was solved by consultation with the third reviewers until consensus was reached. Patients were followed up on until they were discharged from the hospital. The primary outcome of prognosis was mortality, including in-hospital mortality, mortality in PSI class V (PSI-V), mortality in invasive mechanical ventilation and 28-day mortality. The secondary outcomes included treatment failure, length of stay (LOS). Treatment failure included both early and late treatment failure. Early treatment failure was defined as clinical deterioration within 72 hours of treatment initiation, meeting any one of the following criteria: (I) shock (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg for >30 minutes with lactate ≥2 mmol/L or vasopressor requirement); (II) new requirement for invasive mechanical ventilation (absent at baseline); (III) death. Late treatment failure was defined as meeting any one of the following criteria after 72 hours: (I) radiographic progression (≥50% increase in pulmonary infiltrates compared to baseline); (II) persistent severe respiratory failure [PaO2/FiO2 <200 mmHg with respiratory rate ≥30 breaths/min in non-intubated patients]; (III) new requirement for invasive mechanical ventilation (absent at baseline); (IV) death 72 hours after treatment initiation (17).

Bias control

To address potential sources of bias, several measures were implemented in this study. First, to minimize selection bias, all consecutive adult patients with severe CAP presenting to the participating hospitals during the study period were screened for eligibility, and enrollment was performed prospectively according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Second, to reduce information bias, data collection was standardized using a unified case report form. Two trained researchers independently extracted data from medical records, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third senior investigator until consensus was reached. Third, to mitigate confounding bias, multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to adjust for potential confounders, and variables were selected based on clinical relevance and univariate screening. Fourth, inter-rater reliability was assessed for key variables (e.g., PSI score, SOFA score) to ensure consistency across sites. Finally, as this was a multicenter study, regular online meetings were held among site investigators to harmonize data collection procedures and clinical definitions, thereby reducing inter-center variability. Despite these efforts, the observational nature of the study means that residual confounding from unmeasured or unknown factors cannot be completely excluded.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation: the sample size was calculated based on the events-per-variable (EPV) principle for logistic regression analysis (18). Assuming a mortality rate of approximately 30% among patients with severe CAP admitted to the ICU, as reported in previous studies (19), and anticipating up to five candidate predictors to be entered into the multivariable model, a minimum of 50 outcome events was required (5 predictors × 10 events per predictor). This corresponded to a required sample size of approximately 167 ICU patients (50/0.30). After accounting for an anticipated 10% rate of incomplete data or loss to follow up, the target sample size was set at 185 ICU patients. In the final cohort, 111 patients with severe CAP were admitted to the ICU, among whom 38 died (in-hospital mortality 34.2%). With only two variables retained in the final multivariable model, the EPV ratio was 19, which exceeds the recommended threshold of 10, indicating adequate statistical power for the primary analysis.

Handling of missing data: the proportion of missing data for each variable was assessed prior to analysis. Variables with less than 5% missing values were imputed using the median (for non-normally distributed continuous variables) or mean (for normally distributed continuous variables). For the SOFA score, which had 7.2% missing components due to unavailable laboratory tests, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed under the missing at random assumption. Five imputed datasets were generated, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Multivariable analysis for risk factors: descriptive statistics and group comparisons: the variables of clinical features and prognosis were subjected to a descriptive statistical analysis. We calculated the number and proportion for categorical variables, the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables with a normal distribution, and the median and interquartile range for those with a non-normal distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the t-test or the nonparametric Mann-Whitney test. All statistical tests were two-sided. P<0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to explore the risk factors associated with in-hospital mortality in ICU-CAP patients. The univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to screen for potential risk factors, and all potential risk factors that had a P value <0.1 were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. We used SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to analyze the data statistically.

Results

General information

A total of 170 patients with severe CAP were included. The ICU group accounted for 111 (65.3%) cases and the non-ICU group 59 (34.7%) cases. Among the patients with CAP in the ICU, 38 (34.2%) patients died during hospitalization (Figure 1). Males comprised 67.6% (75/111) of the ICU group. The mean age of patients in the ICU was 64.1±16.4 years, respectively. Compared with the non-ICU group, patients in ICU group were more likely to have a higher body mass index (BMI; 23.8 vs. 22.1 kg/m2, P=0.043), higher temperature (37.6 vs. 36.7 ℃, P<0.001), higher heart rate (105 vs. 97 beat/min, P=0.02), and lower diastolic blood pressure (66.9 vs. 76.3 mmHg, P<0.001) (Table 1).

Laboratory data

There were significant differences in median levels for red blood cell (RBC), haemoglobin (HGB), platelets (PLTs), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total bilirubin (TBIL), PaCO2, and PaO2/FiO2 between ICU group and non-ICU group (P<0.05). The levels of ALT (27.0 vs. 17.0 U/L, P=0.01), AST (32.0 vs. 24.0 U/L, P<0.001), BUN (9.3 vs. 6.9 mmol/L, P=0.009), PaCO2 (38.3 vs. 35.9 mmHg, P=0.02) were significantly higher in the ICU group than those in the non-ICU group. The levels of RBC (3.8×1012/L vs. 4.0×1012/L, P=0.02), HGB (113.5 vs. 124.0 g/L, P=0.03), PLT (157.5×109/L vs. 198.0×109/L, P=0.004), TBIL (11.7 vs. 15.2 µmol/L, P=0.02), PaO2/FiO2 (172.2 vs. 239.2, P<0.001) were significantly lower in the ICU group than those in the non-ICU group (Table 1).

Severity criteria

In the ICU group, 91.9% (102/111) patients had respiratory failure, 65.8% (73/111) of patients had consciousness disturbance, 23.4% (26/111) were in the shock state, and 78.2% (88/111) of patients were classified into PSI classes IV or V. Compared with the non-ICU group, patients in ICU group were more likely to have higher rate of confusion (65.8% vs. 30.5%, P<0.001), shock (23.4% vs. 8.5%, P=0.01), respiratory failure (91.9% vs. 76.3%, P=0.005), invasive mechanical ventilation (73.9% vs. 10.2%, P<0.001), and higher level of SOFA score (7 vs. 3, P<0.001) and CURB-65 score (2 vs. 2, P<0.001) (Table 1).

Comorbidities

In the ICU group, 73.0% (81/111) of patients had at least one comorbidity, including hypertension (36.9%, 41/111), diabetes mellitus (28.8%, 32/111), cerebral apoplexy (27.9%, 31/111), cardiac functional insufficiency (24.3%, 27/111), coronary artery disease (21.6%, 24/111). No remarkable differences in comorbidities between the two groups (Table 2).

Etiology

Microbiological examinations was performed at the time of clinical presentation by collecting samples of sputum and blood. The microbial etiology was established in 60 out of 111 cases (54.1%) in ICU group, including Acinetobacter baumannii (20.0%, 12/60), Klebsiella pneumoniae (15.0%, 9/60), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (13.3%, 8/60), and Staphylococcus aureus (13.3%, 8/60). Notably, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was significantly more common in the non-ICU group compared to the ICU group (46.2% vs. 13.3%, P=0.01) (Table 3).

Prognosis outcomes of ICU-CAP

In the ICU group, in-hospital mortality was 34.2% (38/111), 28-day mortality was 27.9% (31/111), and 7-day mortality was 10.8% (12/111). The mortality among patients with PSI-V was 40.0% (18/45), and among those receiving invasive mechanical ventilation was 40.2% (33/82). There were significant differences of prognostic outcomes between the two groups. Compared with the non-ICU group, patients in ICU group had higher in-hospital mortality (34.2% vs. 11.9%, P=0.002), 28-day mortality (27.9% vs. 11.9%, P=0.01), and a significantly higher rate of treatment failure (48.6% vs. 15.3%, P<0.001). However, there were no significant differences in LOS between the two groups (Table 4). The median survival time in ICU group was 29 days, which was significantly shorter than that in non-ICU group as shown by Kaplan-Meier analysis (P=0.02) (Figure 2).

Risk factors associated with mortality in patients with CAP in the ICU

First, possible risk factors were screened by univariate logistic regression analysis, including invasive mechanical ventilation, CURB-65 score, PSI class ≥ IV, SOFA score, heart rate, and age ≥60 years. A multifactorial analysis with the presence of death as the dependent variable and the potential risk factors as independent variables showed that the factors significantly associated with in-hospital mortality were invasive mechanical ventilation [odds ratio (OR) =3.35; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.14–9.81; P=0.02] and age ≥60 years (OR =2.64; 95% CI: 1.07–6.53; P=0.03) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this multicenter prospective study, patients with ICU-CAP exhibited greater disease severity on admission than those treated in general wards, as evidenced by higher BMI, temperature, and heart rate, lower diastolic blood pressure, a greater burden of comorbidities, and higher rates of treatment failure and mortality. Multivariable analysis further identified invasive mechanical ventilation and age ≥60 years were independently associated with in-hospital mortality.

Consistent with previous reports (20,21) patients with ICU-CAP in our cohort experienced a poor prognosis. The in-hospital mortality rate was 34.2%, which aligns closely with the 31.2% reported in a previous ICU-based study (22) and falls within the range of 21.9% to 47.6% documented in earlier investigations (23,24). The 28-day mortality in our study was 27.9%, comparable to the wide range of 11% to 56% observed in prior epidemiological surveys (25,26) Notably, rapidly progressive pneumonias have recently been defined as fulminant pneumonia, a subset characterized by early clinical deterioration and death within a short time frame (27,28). In our ICU cohort, the 7-day mortality rate was 10.8%, indicating that a notable proportion of deaths occurred early in the disease course. This observation aligns with the concept of fulminant pneumonia and highlights the need for early clinical attention in the first days after ICU admission.

Multivariable analysis identified age ≥60 years and invasive mechanical ventilation as independent predictors of in-hospital mortality in ICU-CAP patients. The association between advanced age and poor outcomes in infectious diseases is well established (29,30), particularly among critically ill populations (31). Older adults typically present with diminished physiological reserves and a higher burden of comorbidities, which collectively impair immune function, increase susceptibility to severe infections, and prolong recovery (32). These observations have also been supported by evidence from the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID19) pandemic (33). Our study extends this well-recognized association to the Chinese ICU population, confirming its applicability in this specific healthcare context.

Invasive mechanical ventilation is a critical life-sustaining intervention for severe respiratory failure (34). In our ICU cohort, 73.9% of patients required invasive mechanical ventilation, with a mortality rate of 40.2%, aligning with existing global evidence (35,36). Crucially, invasive mechanical ventilation functions less as an independent risk factor and more as a surrogate marker for profound respiratory compromise (37). While the elevated mortality observed in ventilated patients may partially stem from complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia, diaphragmatic dysfunction, and barotrauma, it is predominantly driven by the underlying disease severity (38,39). Therefore, invasive mechanical ventilation should be regarded not merely as a clean independent causal risk factor but rather as a severity-associated prognostic factor.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study conducted in real-world clinical settings, there were no standardized criteria for ICU admission. Although severity assessment tools such as PSI and CURB-65 were available for reference, admission decisions ultimately relied on physicians’ clinical judgment—a potential source of selection bias. However, the high disease severity in our ICU cohort (median SOFA score 7, 79.2% with PSI class ≥ IV) suggests that patient selection was generally appropriate and aligned with objective measures of illness severity. Second, despite being a multicenter study with standardized data collection protocols, variations in clinical practice across participating hospitals and physicians may have introduced treatment heterogeneity. Such variability is inherent to real-world studies and may influence outcomes, though it also enhances the generalizability of our findings to routine clinical practice. Third, the sample size was smaller than the initial target calculated based on the EPV principle. Nevertheless, with only two variables retained in the final multivariable model, the EPV ratio was 19, exceeding the recommended threshold of 10 and ensuring adequate statistical power for the primary analysis. However, the modest sample size may limit the ability to detect additional independent predictors or to perform meaningful subgroup analyses; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In this multicenter prospective cohort of Chinese patients with severe CAP, ICU‑admitted patients presented with substantial disease severity. Multivariable analysis identified invasive mechanical ventilation and age ≥60 years as factors associated with in‑hospital mortality in this population. These findings suggest that these readily available clinical indicators may have prognostic value. However, given the observational study design and the lack of standardized ICU admission criteria, these associations should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective studies with standardized protocols are needed to validate these findings.
