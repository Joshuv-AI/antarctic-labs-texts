Comparison of personal portable and fixed-site PM2.5 monitoring strategies in predicting pulmonary function decline among asthmatic children in Seoul, Korea: a pilot study
Authors: Seokwon Lee, Hee-Eun Choi, Dohyeong Kim, Daesung Lim, Daejin Song, SungChul Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional fixed-site monitoring networks cannot capture individual mobility patterns and microenvironmental PM2.5 variability, leading to exposure misclassification that attenuates observed health effects estimates. This pilot study compared personal portable monitoring device (PICO) with two fixed-site national automatic (Air Korea, AK) and regional monitoring (KTR) stations in relation to pulmonary function decline in asthmatic children. Twenty asthmatic children (mean age 10.8 ± 2.4 years) living in the Seoul metropolitan area were sequentially enrolled from November 2019 to January 2020, with simultaneous PM﻿2.5 and peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) monitoring conducted from November 2019 to March 2020 (1436 child-day observations; 367 PEFR decline events). Seventy-two-hour cumulative PM2.5 exposure was quantified using five indicators. Primary analysis used a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a random intercept per child (ICC = 0.42), adjusted for age, sex, BMI, temperature, and humidity. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression and time-varying Cox regression were performed as supplementary analyses. Mean PM2.5 concentrations (29.4, 31.6 and 31.2 µg/m3 for PICO, AK, and KTR, respectively), substantially exceeded the WHO air quality guideline of 15 µg/m3. PICO-measured PM2.5 showed the strongest negative correlation with PEFR (r = − 0.481, p < 0.001) compared to AK (r = − 0.229, p < 0.001) and KTR monitors (r = − 0.315, p < 0.001). In the primary GLMM analysis, each 1 µg/m3 increase in PICO-measured PM2.5 level was significantly associated with β = 0.01013 (95% CI 0.00915–0.01110, p < 0.001; C-index = 0.837), compared with AK (β = 0.00294; C-index = 0.563) and KTR (β = 0.00289; C-index = 0.585). The RCS analysis identified an exploratory threshold level of 25 µg/m3, above which PEFR decline probability increased sharply (observed rate: 5.7% below versus 41.9% above the threshold). In this pilot study, PICO monitor showed stronger associations with PEFR decline in asthmatic children and higher predictive discrimination than two fixed-site monitoring approaches, consistent with reduced exposure misclassification. These findings support the potential utility of personal portable PM2.5 monitors for pediatric respiratory health protection and warrant confirmation in larger and multi-seasonal longitudinal cohort studies in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54651-5.

Introduction

South Korea has experienced rapid industrialization and economic growth since the 1960s, resulting in substantially elevated atmospheric pollutant emissions concentrated in major metropolitan areas, notably Seoul and its surrounding regions1. This transformation was characterized by substantial increases in fossil fuel consumption—with CO2 emissions from fossil fuel combustion rising from approximately 54 million metric tons in 1970 to over 600 million metric tons by 2023—and population density, as urbanization accelerated dramatically with Seoul’s population surging from 2.4 million in 1960 to 5.4 million in 1970 and 8.4 million by 19802. These dramatic changes resulted in a marked surge in the atmospheric pollutant emissions. The sources of air pollution emission are particularly concentrated in several major metropolitan areas, notably Seoul city and its surrounding regions3.

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5, aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm) is a principal component of urban air pollution, capable of bypassing upper airway filtration, penetrating deep into the alveoli, and entering the systemic circulation4. According to the global estimates, diseases resulting from the long-term exposure to PM2.5 affect between 3.2 million and 9 million deaths annually, with estimates varying by methodology and year5–7. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) under the WHO classified outdoor air pollution and particulate matter as Group 1 carcinogens in 20138,9. Mechanistically, PM2.5 exposure triggers airway inflammation and bronchoconstriction, leading to impaired lung function—measured as decreases in forced expiratory volume (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC)—through oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokine release10,11. These effects are particularly pronounced in individuals with pre-existing respiratory conditions such as asthma, in whom PM2.5 exacerbates airway hyperresponsiveness and is strongly associated with increased incidence and severity of asthma symptoms, pulmonary fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)4.

South Korea currently applies the national PM2.5 standards of 15 µg/m3 (annual mean) and 35 µg/m3 (24-h mean)—less stringent than the WHO Air Quality Guidelines (AQG) of 10 µg/m3 (annual) and 15 µg/m3 (24-h)—and monitors compliance through the national fixed-site automatic monitoring network (known as Air Korea, AK)12,13. However, the national fixed-site monitoring networks are inherently limited in their ability to capture individual-level exposure, because they measure only ambient outdoor concentrations at a sparse network of fixed locations and cannot account for individual mobility patterns, indoor–outdoor time-activity behaviors, or microenvironmental concentration variability. This exposure misclassification attenuates observed health effect estimates and constrains the accuracy of regulatory-data-based health impact assessments14–16.

Children represent a uniquely vulnerable population to PM2.5 exposure for three inter-related reasons. First, the developing lung is still undergoing alveolar growth and airway remodeling during childhood; inflammatory insults during this critical window can cause irreversible functional impairment that persists into adulthood17. Second, children have higher minute ventilation per unit body weight than adults, meaning that at equivalent ambient concentrations they inhale proportionally greater quantities of PM2.5 per kilogram of body mass18. Third, the dynamic and variable behavioral patterns of children—including unstructured outdoor play, school commuting, and limited capacity for self-regulation of exposure—create unique and rapidly changing microenvironmental exposure profiles that fixed-site monitoring networks are structurally unable to capture19. These three characteristics together make the choice of exposure assessment methodology particularly consequential for pediatric health research.

Recent advances in low-cost sensor (LCS) technology have enabled the development and deployment of real-time portable PM2.5 personal monitors for individual exposure assessment20,21. These devices capture spatiotemporal exposure variability reflecting actual individual activity patterns and microenvironmental conditions. Compared with conventional regulatory instrumentation, portable personal monitors offer distinct practical advantages: compact and lightweight form factors (< 200 g) with minimal power requirements (< 1 W), high temporal resolution (1-sec to 5-min intervals), straightforward operation, substantially lower cost ($20–$300 vs. $10,000–$100,000 for regulatory equipment), and wireless data transmission capability22,23. When properly calibrated against reference instruments, LCS-based monitors have demonstrated performance comparable to regulatory-grade equipment in multiple validation studies.

Nevertheless, epidemiological studies investigating the association between personal sensor-derived PM2.5 exposure and respiratory health outcomes in children remain scarce, and comparative studies directly evaluating measurement concordance between portable personal sensors and fixed-site monitoring networks in Korean pediatric populations are notably limited.14–16 In particular, the comparative validity of personal portable monitoring versus fixed-site monitoring networks for predicting respiratory health outcomes—specifically PEFR decline—in Korean asthmatic children has not been systematically evaluated. This gap is particularly important because the choice of exposure assessment approach directly determines the magnitude and statistical significance of observed exposure–response relationships, thereby affecting both scientific inference and public health decision-making14,15.

To address this gap, therefore, this pilot study directly and quantitatively compared three PM2.5 exposure assessment strategies—a portable personal monitoring sensor (PICO) capturing individual-level spatiotemporal exposure variability and providing an improved estimate of personal-level microenvironmental PM2.5 exposure, a national fixed-site automated monitoring network (Air Korea, AK) representing population-level ambient air quality at national level, and a regional fixed-site monitoring station (KTR) providing intermediate-scale urban exposure data at a local scale—in relation to daily PEFR decline in asthmatic children in Seoul, South Korea. We ultimately aim to evaluate the association between PM2.5 exposure and pulmonary function decline in asthmatic children and quantify how the choice of monitoring approach influences observed exposure–response associations and the extent to which exposure misclassification from fixed-site monitoring attenuates adverse health effect estimates.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

The study participants were children aged 7–16 years diagnosed with pediatric asthma (with their legal parents) residing in Guro-gu, Seoul metropolitan city as residents of the local community, and who had a history of outpatient visits and consultations at pediatric asthma environmental health centers within major university hospitals in the Seoul and Seoul Metropolitan areas. Twenty children meeting strict inclusion criteria were ultimately selected as study participants.

The inclusion criteria required: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of asthma by a specialist physician; (2) experience of asthma symptoms within the past 12 months; and (3) objective evidence of airway hyperresponsiveness demonstrated by at least one of the following pulmonary function tests: (a) bronchodilator responsiveness with ≥ 12% improvement in FEV₁ (forced expiratory volume in one second); (b) methacholine challenge test with PC20 (provocative concentration causing a 20% fall in FEV1) ≤ 16 mg/mL; or (c) mannitol challenge test with PD15 (provocative dose causing a 15% fall in FEV1) ≤ 635 mg. During outpatient clinic visits, our research staff met directly with the eligible children and their legal guardians (parents) to explain the study purpose, content, and detailed procedures. Twenty children (households) who voluntarily participated and provided written informed consent following comprehensive disclosure were enrolled as final study participants.

The calibration of small portable light-scattering PM personal monitoring devices (PICO) was conducted over eight days from September to November 2019. The main observation period, during which simultaneous PICO personal monitoring and PEFR measurement were performed, commenced on November 27th, 2019 (the first child enrollment date) and continued until March 10, 2020. Child enrollment proceeded sequentially from November 2019 through January 2020. National fixed-site (AK) and regional fixed-site (KTR) monitoring data were available continuously throughout the full study period (November 27th, 2019–March 10th, 2020). In contrast, PICO monitoring data were collected from each individual child’s enrollment date to the end of the observation period; therefore, the PICO observation period varied by child according to enrollment timing. Consequently, the mean PICO observation period per child was 71.8 ± 19.0 days (range 35–104 days), yielding a total of 1436 child-day observations. To ensure temporal consistency across the three monitoring devices, AK and KTR data used in all analyses were restricted to each child’s respective PICO observation period.

The selected child participants were provided with PICO monitors that could be attached to their backpacks. Throughout the study period, they were instructed to carry and use the portable monitoring equipment during both indoor and outdoor daily activities. With the active cooperation and participation of both the child participants and their legal guardians (parents), detailed user training was provided on various aspects of the equipment, including: (1) comprehensive instructions on device operation; (2) procedures for daily measurement data upload; and (3) methods for distinguishing between indoor and outdoor locations. All data and personal information collected through this study were confirmed to be kept confidential and not shared or disclosed to any external parties outside the study team. This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical review and approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea University Guro Hospital (IRB No. 2019GR0351, Approved on September 26th, 2019). Differences in continuous variables (age, BMI) between male and female subgroups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, given the small and unequal subgroup sizes (males n = 13, females n = 7) and non-normal distributions. The sex distribution was compared using the Fisher exact test.

Exposure assessment strategies

Personal PM2.5 monitoring device using small portable PICO sensors

For PM2.5 monitoring in this study, a commercially available portable measurement device was selected after comprehensive evaluation of multiple criteria including measurement reliability, portability, real-time data collection and transmission capability, sufficient battery capacity, capability to measure temperature and humidity in both indoor and outdoor environments, and convenience features such as lightweight design suitable for children to wear. The selected device was a PICO monitor (manufacturer: Brilliant and Company Co., Ltd.; model: PMM-130; dimensions: 48.0 × 48.0 × 20.6 mm; weight: 45 g; battery capacity: 2000 mAh; equipped with data storage device).

The PICO monitor employs a light scattering method to enable real-time measurement and monitoring of PM2.5 concentrations (1–1000 µg/m3), temperature (− 40 °C to 125 °C), and relative humidity (0–100% RH) at 5-min intervals. In addition, the CO2 concentration was simultaneously recorded at 5-min intervals throughout the study period. The device has excellent portability due to its compact size and lightweight design. In particular, among the various types of commercially available portable measuring instruments, the PICO monitor received official certification from the KTR—a government-authorized testing and analytical institute—as a high-performance monitoring device with low measurement error and good reliability exceeding 86%. Furthermore, measurement calibration was performed by comparing PICO monitoring results with reference equipment (i.e., KTR) used at AK monitoring stations, and a multiple regression model formula was derived and applied to correct for the effects of temperature and humidity variations. A multiple linear regression calibration equation was derived as follows: Calibrated PM2.5 = 49.19 + (0.45 × measured PM2.5) – (1.44 × temperature) – (0.006 × relative humidity)22. Following calibration of the two exposure monitoring devices, the agreement between PICO and KTR measurements was substantially improved (Fig. 1).

According to several previous studies, the accuracy of low-cost portable PM monitors varies considerably depending on calibration methods, environmental conditions, and regional characteristics, thus periodic recalibration and quality management protocols are essential24–26. To ensure validity and consistency of PM2.5 measurement data and to verify data variability, the PM2.5 data were collected over eight days from September to November in 2019 by simultaneously monitoring with both the reference PM monitoring equipment at the KTR and five PICO monitors. Temperature and humidity correction equations were applied, and PICO monitoring data were compared and analyzed with the KTR monitoring data in the region. Although low-cost PICO monitors can be affected by various environmental factors such as aerosol size, meteorological variables, and relative humidity, this study minimized these limitations through temperature and humidity correction and field-based validation. Although some studies have reported issues where low-cost sensors misidentify water vapor as PM2.5, the PICO monitor maintained stable performance in both indoor and outdoor environments by applying a precise data correction algorithm22.

During the collection of raw data, a small number of relative humidity values of 0.0% were generated due to device initialization or temporary sensor disconnection events. These values were inconsistent with concurrent AK humidity records and were therefore treated as physical artefacts. Such 0.0% values comprised less than 0.5% of all PICO data records; for gaps of 4 h or fewer, linear interpolation between adjacent valid measurements was applied, whereas such observations with gaps exceeding four hours were treated as missing values thus excluded from our analysis. The final analysis dataset contains no 0.0% values, confirming successful treatment of these artefacts during the data preprocessing and calibration.

The measurements of actual PM2.5 concentrations were conducted once per month from December 2019 to February 2020, with continuous measurement over three consecutive days for each measurement session. The study participants were instructed to always carry the PICO monitor in a pouch attached to their backpack when going outdoors, and to place the device at the location closest to their primary activity area when staying indoors. Approximately 70–80% of PICO-measured data were recorded in indoor microenvironments, consistent with previously reported winter time-activity patterns for elementary school–aged children in Seoul in previous studies.27,28 The measurement data collected by the study participants were uploaded to the application server daily, and the study team members regularly monitored and managed the measured data. Additionally, location tracking was also performed using a GPS positioning system (EHS GPS Tracker, Mobile App) during measurement to enhance the reliability of the participants’ measurement locations.

PM2.5 measurement using national and regional fixed monitoring network stations

Fixed air quality monitoring stations installed throughout the nation in urban and roadside areas measure PM10 and PM2.5 data every 5 min using the beta-ray attenuation method. Atmospheric concentrations of air pollutants including carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and ozone (O3) are transmitted through the National Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Information System (NAMIS), processed through basic statistical procedures to obtain hourly average concentrations, and then stored in real-time in a large-scale national air quality environmental database called “Air Korea (AK),” which is managed by the Korea Environment Corporation, a public agency under the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment. The recorded air quality database was utilized to collect atmospheric PM2.5 concentrations from the fixed monitoring station nearest to the real-time location of study participants as measured via GPS during the study period and was integrated with individual PM2.5 monitoring data for personal exposure assessment evaluation. Based on hourly average PM2.5 data collected from the air quality monitoring instruments at the fixed regional monitoring station operated by the KTR in Guro-gu, Seoul—the target study area—and GPS information, the national automatic PM2.5 monitoring data provided by the AK network stations were also compared and analyzed.

It is important to note that KTR served two distinct and temporally non-overlapping roles in this study. First, during the calibration period (September–November 2019, eight days), the KTR regulatory-grade fixed-site PM2.5 monitor—operated using the beta-ray attenuation method and government-certified by the KTR Institute—was used as the reference instrument for deriving the temperature- and humidity-adjusted regression calibration equations for the PICO monitors. Secondly, during the main health outcome analysis period (November 2019–March 2020), KTR PM2.5 data served as an independent fixed-site exposure predictor in all primary GLMM and secondary Cox regression models. Critically, the KTR observations used as the health-outcome predictor do not temporally overlap with the PICO calibration period; the two datasets are therefore fully independent, and the comparison of predictive performance between the PICO and KTR models in the primary analysis is not subject to circularity.

Development and application of cumulative exposure indicators

The establishment of a 72-h cumulative exposure window in this study represents a scientific approach that accounts for the biological lag time between air pollution exposure and respiratory responses29. In environmental epidemiological studies, exposure indicators vary according to baseline disease-exposure associations and must reflect various assumptions regarding the temporal trends and relevant exposure periods30. Health effects of PM2.5 are observed in both immediate responses (lag 0) and delayed responses (lag 1–5 days), with asthma exacerbation showing particularly pronounced delayed effects over 1–5 days; evidence has demonstrated that short-term inhalation exposure to high levels of PM2.5 induces transient lung tissue damage and associated immune responses29,31.

To assess the 72-h cumulative PM2.5 exposure in this study, multidimensional exposure indicators were quantified and evaluated. Specifically, PM2.5 cumulative exposure during the 72-h period prior to PEFR measurement times (08:00 a.m. and 08:00 p.m.) was quantified using five indicators: maximum concentration—to assess the acute peak effect of high-concentration exposure; mean concentration—to provide a representative value of overall exposure level; median concentration—to measure central tendency robust to outliers; total exposure burden—to account for cumulative exposure considering both concentration and duration; and number of exceedances of air quality standards—the frequency of exceeding the atmospheric environmental standard (35 µg/m3). Through this multidimensional approach, three different exposure monitoring devices were utilized to reflect the complexity of individual exposure patterns, and diverse exposure profiles across individuals that could not be evaluated by a single indicator were comprehensively assessed32,33.

Peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) measurement

PEFR is widely used to estimate the degree of airway obstruction in children with asthma34 and can be easily measured using a compact portable measurement device. In this study, a personal portable pulmonary function measurement device was utilized to measure PEFR for collecting information on pulmonary function assessment in children diagnosed with asthma. The Microlife digital peak flow meter used in this study (manufacturer: Microlife; model: PF 100; weight: 150 g (with batteries); dimensions: 77 × 144 × 48 mm; measurement range PEF 50–900 L/min, FEV₁ 0.01–9.99 L) is an electronic measurement device based on a rotating vane wheel mechanism. It is a medical device that complies with the American Thoracic Society (ATS) 1994 guidelines and has obtained CE marking. This measurement device meets ATS standards with a PEF measurement range of 50–900 L/min and measurement accuracy of ± 25 L/min or 12% of the measured value, whichever is greater. The device can store up to 240 measurement values with date and time stamps in its memory.

Twenty study participants were instructed to remain standing or sitting upright during PEFR measurement, and consistent posture was maintained for each individual to ensure reliability of the PEFR measurement. During the PEFR measurement, all study participants with their legal parents were instructed to hold the textured side surfaces of the device with both hands and to take a complete deep breath followed by a brief breath-hold. The mouthpiece was sealed completely with the lips, ensuring the tongue did not protrude into the mouthpiece. Participants were then instructed to exhale forcefully and rapidly for at least 2 s in a sustained manner. Upon completion of measurement, a prolonged audible signal confirmed successful result recording, with the PEF value displayed for approximately 3 s followed by sequential display of the FEV1 value.

In accordance with the international guidelines, three consecutive measurements were performed per session, and the maximum value among the measured data was finally selected as the representative value for that session. Considering diurnal variation, measurements were performed at two time points—morning (08:00 a.m.) and evening (08:00 p.m.). To establish a personal best baseline measurement, repeated measurements were conducted 2–4 times daily over 2 weeks during a period of good health status.

The primary outcome variable, PEFR decline, was defined as follows to ensure clear operationalization and reproducibility. The personal best threshold for each measurement time point was calculated as the individual’s maximum observed PEFR at that time point multiplied by 0.80 (i.e., 80% of personal best), consistent with the clinically validated yellow-zone boundary of the standard Asthma Action Plan35. This 80% threshold is widely used in pediatric asthma research as a definition for clinically meaningful lung function decline. Days on which both morning and evening PEFR values were missing were excluded from analysis. Out of 1436 total child-day observations, 367 PEFR decline events were recorded (25.6% of all observations; per-child range 0–83.6%).

Several previous studies have validated the accuracy of peak flow meters, with some devices including Microlife passing accuracy standards in bench testing according to ISO 23747:2015 criteria. Since inter-device variability exists, however, the same device was used consistently throughout the study period to minimize measurement error36. All measured data were automatically stored in the device memory with date and time stamps and were systematically recorded in subject management software to establish a PEFR database for longitudinal analysis. This enabled tracking of changes in individual patients’ respiratory function patterns and quantitative evaluation of treatment efficacy. Through these standardized measurement methods, respiratory function in children with asthma was assessed accurately and reproducibly, securing objective and reliable clinical data shown in several studies37–39.

Data collection, integration, and standardization

For study participants enrolled in this study, a database was constructed by integrating PM2.5 exposure monitoring data from three different sources: PICO, AK, and KTR. Through this integrated approach, the PM2.5 measurement values were compared and analyzed across various scales and methodologies. This data integration process was conducted in accordance with data integration principles established in environmental health studies.40,41.

Time alignment: All measured PM2.5 data were converted to hourly average values to ensure consistency42 Spatial Matching: GPS location information from the PICO monitor was utilized to link and match with the nearest fixed regional monitoring stations32 Data Harmonization: Measurement units and procedures were standardized, and diverse PM2.5 data were harmoniously unified into a single database by applying uniform standardization protocols43.

All correlation analyses and primary statistical analyses (Generalized Linear Mixed Model and time-varying Cox regression) used the 72-h cumulative mean PM2.5 concentrations as the exposure metric, calculated relative to each PEFR measurement time. Specifically, the morning PEFR measurement (08:00 a.m.) was linked to the 72-h PM2.5 mean from the preceding 08:00 a.m., and the evening PEFR measurement (08:00 p.m.) was linked to the 72-h PM2.5 mean from the preceding 08:00 p.m. This 72-h time window was selected to capture the biological lag between PM2.5 inhalation and the resulting airway inflammation leading to PEFR decline (typically 1–3 days in asthmatic children), consistent with prior literature identifying this as the optimal time window for acute PM2.5 effects on pediatric pulmonary function.

In addition to the 72-h mean level, four alternative exposure metrics (72-h maximum, median, cumulative burden, and exceedance count [> 35 µg/m3]) were computed using the same temporal alignment and applied in the GLMM models to validate the primary metric selection (Supplementary Table S1). The 72-h mean value demonstrated the highest predictive performance by C-index in the models without meteorological covariates (PICO C-index = 0.840; Supplementary Table S1), and was therefore selected as the primary exposure indicator. Separate analyses linking morning and evening PEFR individually to their corresponding 72-h PM2.5 exposure metrics are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Based on real-time location data, indoor and outdoor measurements were distinguished and generated as separate datasets to account for different microenvironmental exposures44. Hourly average GPS coordinates were calculated to enhance the precision of exposure assessment and minimize spatial measurement error45. Subsequently, for each participant, the nearest KTR and AK monitoring stations corresponding to the hourly average location were identified, and PM2.5 values were spatially matched and extracted using a nearest-neighbor algorithm46. Individual PEFR measurements were classified relative to each participant’s personal best value: measurements between 80 and 100% of personal best were classified as “green zone” (normal lung function), between 50 and 80% were classified as “yellow zone” (caution), and the values below 50% were classified as “red zone” (medical alert), in accordance with the recommended PEFR interpretation thresholds for asthma control assessment.47 Systematic data preprocessing was performed prior to correlation analysis. First, for outlier detection and treatment, the interquartile range (IQR) was examined, and the outer fence technique was applied to remove all PM2.5 concentration values falling outside the valid range (0–500 µg/m3)48. The PEFR measurement values, exceeding the range of 20–200% of each individual’s personal best, were considered outliers and then removed49.

Furthermore, the missing PM2.5 values were imputed using linear interpolation considering temporal continuity, a method validated for short-term gaps in PM2.5 time series given the strong temporal autocorrelation of ambient pollution data44,50,51, while missing PEFR values were imputed with the individual-specific median value. Missing PM2.5 values accounted for approximately 3.3% of all PICO records (primarily attributable to device non-wear or data transmission errors), and missing PEFR records accounted for 1.8% of all observations (primarily due to illness or non-compliance on specific days). All measured data were normalized using the standardization method, converting the data to Z-scores to unify variables with different measurement units on the same scale. Prior to the correlation analysis, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Q–Q plots were also generated to visually compare the quantiles of all observed data values with those of a normal distribution. When PM2.5 and individual PEFR measurement data did not satisfy the normal distribution assumptions, non-parametric analytical methods were employed to calculate Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and then scatter plots with fitted lines were drawn and presented.

Statistical analysis

Intraclass correlation and primary analysis: generalized linear mixed model (GLMM)

The dataset has a two-level nested structure that is central to the analytical strategy. At Level 1 (within-child), each child contributed repeated daily observations over their individual monitoring period (mean 71.8 ± 19.0 days; range 35–104 days), yielding a total of 1436 child-day observations and 367 PEFR decline events. PEFR was measured twice daily (08:00 a.m. and 08:00 p.m.), and the daily binary PEFR decline outcome was derived from the mean of morning and evening deviations from each child’s personal best threshold. A total of 20 individual children constituted the clustering units, with between-child variability modeled as a random effect. For each child-day observation, the corresponding PM2.5 exposure was summarized as the 72-h mean concentration calculated relative to each PEFR measurement time point. In the primary GLMM analysis, each child-day observation constitutes one unit of analysis, with child ID entered as the random intercept clustering factor to control for the correlation structure among repeated measurements within the same child.

To quantify within-child correlation in the repeated PEFR decline outcome, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated prior to model selection. The ICC for PEFR decline across children was 0.420, indicating that 42% of total outcome variance was attributable to between-child differences, with the remaining 58% explained by within-child (day-to-day) variation. This substantial within-child correlation required explicit modeling to avoid inflated test statistics and biased estimates. Accordingly, the primary analysis employed a GLMM with a random intercept per child (child ID as the clustering factor), using a logit link function for the binary repeated outcome (daily PEFR decline: Yes = 1, No = 0). Each child-day observation was treated as a repeated binary measurement nested within child, and the random intercept directly modeled the between-child correlation structure, yielding valid standard errors under the repeated-measures design. This approach partitions total variance into between-child (random effect, 42%) and within-child (residual, 58%) components and produces estimates reflecting individual-level exposure–response associations while appropriately accounting for within-subject dependence.

The primary exposure variable in each GLMM was the 72-h mean PM2.5 concentration from the respective monitoring device (PICO, AK, or KTR), modeled as a continuous linear predictor on the logit scale. Covariates were selected a priori based on established literature and biological plausibility: age (years), sex (binary), and BMI (kg/m2) were included as fixed effects, as these are the primary biological determinants of pulmonary function in children and are standard covariates in pediatric respiratory epidemiology. Temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%) recorded by the PICO sensor at 5-minute intervals were included as time-varying covariates in all primary models, given their significant correlations with PEFR decline (temperature: r = − 0.076, p = 0.004; humidity: r = 0.077, p = 0.003). CO2 was not significantly correlated with PEFR decline (r = − 0.026, p = 0.326) and was therefore reported descriptively only. Multicollinearity among all covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIF values were below 1.1, confirming negligible multicollinearity.

The estimated GLMM regression coefficient (β) represents the change in the log-odds of PEFR decline per 1 µg/m3 increase in 72-h mean PM2.5. Predicted probabilities were obtained via the inverse logit function. Model fit was performed using maximum likelihood estimation (REML = False) with the L-BFGS optimization algorithm. To justify the number of predictors relative to observed events, events-per-variable (EPV) was calculated. With 367 PEFR decline events and 7 model parameters (PM2.5, age, sex, BMI, temperature, humidity, random intercept), EPV = 52.4, substantially exceeding the commonly recommended threshold of EPV ≥ 10–20 for reliable regression estimates52,53.

Model discrimination and C-index comparison

The model discrimination was assessed using the concordance index (C-index) for each monitoring device (PICO, AK, and KTR). To ensure that C-index values across the three exposure monitoring strategies were directly comparable, all models shared an identical covariate structure (age, sex, BMI, temperature, humidity), the same binary outcome definition (daily PEFR decline), the same analysis sample (n = 20 children, 1436 child-day observations), and the same analytical method (GLMM with random intercept per child). Consequently, differences in C-index values across the three models reflect solely the differential predictive contribution of the PM2.5 exposure variable from each monitoring source. The C-index and its 95% bootstrap confidence intervals were computed using 1000 cluster-resampling iterations at the child level.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis

To quantitatively assess the non-linear exposure–response relationship between the 72-h mean PM2.5 concentrations measured using three different monitoring devices and the probability of PEFR decline in asthmatic children, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) logistic regression model was applied. A logistic regression framework—rather than a Cox proportional hazards framework—was adopted for the RCS analysis to directly model the probability of PEFR decline as a function of PM2.5 concentration, consistent with the binary repeated-measures nature of the outcome. The models were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Five knots were positioned at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles of PM2.5 concentrations to enhance regression model flexibility.54.

A reference PM2.5 concentration level was designated based on a standard threshold (e.g., WHO recommended guideline value), and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated for each PM2.5 concentration interval from the spline regression analysis results. The non-linear dose–response curve between PM2.5 concentration (x-axis) and PEFR decline probability (y-axis) was plotted with 95% CIs to quantitatively estimate the risk of lung function decline in asthmatic children as PM2.5 concentration increases. This analytical approach is particularly useful for identifying complex concentration–response relationships between PM2.5 exposure and health outcomes.55.

Secondary analysis: time-varying cox regression model

As a secondary analysis, an extended Cox proportional hazards regression model with time-varying covariates was constructed using a daily counting process format to permit comparison with results from the primary GLMM analysis. The secondary time-varying Cox regression analysis adopted a daily counting process approach. Health events (i.e., daily PEFR decline) were identified from the baseline to study endpoint; PM2.5 concentration levels and baseline covariates (age, sex, BMI, temperature, humidity) were incorporated as time-varying predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs and p-values were estimated to quantitatively evaluate the exposure–outcome associations. With 367 PEFR decline events and 6 model parameters (PM2.5, age, sex, BMI, temperature, humidity), EPV = 61.2, exceeding the recommended minimum EPV ≥ 10 for Cox regression models.52,56 The Cox proportional hazards assumption was validated using Schoenfeld residuals. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses

Several pre-specified sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of study methodologies and findings. First, the primary GLMM analysis was repeated excluding the observations with PICO-measured PM2.5 values exceeding 100 µg/m3 (n = 11 observations, 0.77%) to assess the influence of extreme peaks potentially attributable to transient indoor pollution events (Supplementary Table S3). Secondly, the use of indoor air purifier was included as an additional covariate to assess its potential confounding effect (Supplementary Table S4). Thirdly, the stratified GLMM analyses by age group and BMI category were performed to explore effect modification (Supplementary Table S5). Fourth, the primary GLMM analysis was additionally re-estimated without temperature and humidity as covariates (meteorological covariates excluded) to quantify the sensitivity of the PM2.5 coefficient to meteorological adjustment (Supplementary Table S6).

Fifth, the five alternative 72-h exposure metrics (maximum, median, cumulative burden, exceedance count, and mean) were each entered as the primary exposure variable in the GLMM analysis to validate the selection of the 72-h mean value (Supplementary Table S1). Sixth, both morning and evening PEFR outcomes were analyzed separately with their respective 72-h PM2.5 exposure metrics (Supplementary Table S2). Seventh, a complete-case analysis was conducted by restricting the dataset to child-day observations without any linearly interpolated PM2.5 values (i.e., excluding observations for which missing PICO PM2.5 data within a gap of four hours or fewer had been replaced by linear interpolation), to assess whether the interpolation procedure introduced bias into the primary findings (Supplementary Table S7).

Statistical software

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.14) with Anaconda Navigator (version 2.7.0) and R (version 4.5.3) with RStudio (version 2025.09.2 + 418), with cross-validation between platforms confirming the reproducibility of all results. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 throughout all the analyses. The primary GLMM was implemented in Python using the statsmodels package (version 0.14, mixedlm function) and cross-validated using the R lme4 package (lmer function) and lmerTest package. Both platforms produced numerically identical estimates (PICO β = 0.01013, SE = 0.0005). Model fitting applied maximum likelihood estimation (REML=False) with the L-BFGS optimization algorithm. The secondary time-varying Cox regression analysis was performed in Python using the lifelines package (version 0.29, CoxTimeVaryingFitter) and validated in R using the survival package (coxph function, counting process format).

The C-index was computed using sklearn (Python, roc_auc_score function) and pROC (R); 95% bootstrap confidence intervals were derived from 1000 cluster-resampling iterations at the child level. RCS non-linear dose–response analyses were conducted using the patsy package (Python, dmatrix function) and the rms package (R, rcs function). Spearman rank correlation analyses were performed using scipy.stats (Python, spearmanr function) and cor.test (R). Bland–Altman analysis for PICO sensor calibration validation was conducted using the pyCompare package (Python) and BlandAltmanLeh package (R). VIF was computed using the statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence module in Python. All figures were generated using Python’s matplotlib (version 3.7) and seaborn packages.

Results

Characteristics of study participants and environmental conditions

Among the 20 study participants, 13 were male and 7 were female, and the overall mean age was 10.8 ± 2.4 years (range 7–16 years), with males (10.4 ± 2.2 years) and females (11.4 ± 2.7 years) showing no statistically significant difference (p = 0.401). Mean height was 139.9 ± 12.8 cm and mean weight was 38.1 ± 12.3 kg. Overall mean BMI was 19.1 ± 3.4 kg/m2 (males: 19.4 ± 3.8 kg/m2, females: 18.5 ± 2.8 kg/m2; p = 0.552). Post-bronchodilator pulmonary function results were as follows: FEV₁ 1.8 ± 0.6 L, FVC 2.3 ± 0.6 L, FEV₁/FVC 77.9 ± 7.0%. Chronic respiratory symptoms were reported at baseline included chest discomfort (70%), nocturnal symptoms (70%), dyspnea (60%), wheezing (50%) and activity limitation (50%).

Respiratory symptoms were reported in 11 of 13 males (84.6%) and 5 of 7 females (71.4%); given the small subgroup sizes, no inferential comparisons were drawn from this difference. The mean PICO observation period was 71.8 ± 19.0 days per child (range: 35–104 days), yielding a total of 1436 child-day observations. Of these, 367 observations (25.6%) met the pre-specified PEFR decline criterion (per-child range 0–83.6%). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for PEFR decline across children was 0.420, indicating that 42% of total outcome variance was attributable to between-child differences, which justified the random intercept GLMM as the primary analytical framework (Table 1).

PICO monitor calibration and descriptive statistics

Figure 1 presents the Bland–Altman analysis comparing the PICO-measured PM2.5 concentrations with KTR reference measurements before and after calibration correction. Prior to calibration, the PICO values exhibited a systematic positive bias relative to the KTR reference. After application of the multiple regression calibration model accounting for temperature and humidity, the mean bias was substantially reduced to − 1.9 µg/m3 (95% limits of agreement: −30.3 to 26.6 µg/m3), confirming that the calibration procedure adequately corrected for systematic sensor artefacts (Fig. 1).

Figure 2 shows the distribution of PM2.5 concentrations for each monitoring approach as boxplots. Descriptive statistics for the three monitoring devices across the 1436 child-day observation period are also summarized in Table 2. Mean PM2.5 measured concentrations were 29.4 ± 17.2 µg/m3 for PICO, 31.6 ± 16.1 µg/m3 for AK, and 31.2 ± 18.5 µg/m3 for KTR. Median concentrations were 26.2 µg/m3 (IQR 17.5–35.3) for PICO, 28.4 µg/m3 (IQR 20.7–41.0) for AK, and 25.7 µg/m3 (IQR 19.1–40.1) for KTR. Maximum concentrations were 171.5 µg/m3 for PICO, 136.8 µg/m3 for AK, and 137.9 µg/m3 for KTR. All three monitors recorded mean concentrations substantially exceeding the WHO Air Quality Guideline (AQG) annual mean of 15 µg/m3, with exceedance rates of 84.0% for PICO, 91.0% for AK, and 84.6% for KTR. Some measurements also exceeded the Korean atmospheric environmental standard for PM2.5 of 35 µg/m3 in 26.3% for PICO, 36.8% for AK, and 33.0% (KTR) of records (Table 2).

Furthermore, inter-monitor correlations based on Spearman’s rank coefficient were all significant, ranging from moderate to high: PICO–AK r = 0.587 (p < 0.001), PICO–KTR r = 0.650 (p < 0.001), and AK–KTR r = 0.825 (p < 0.001). Spearman correlations between PM2.5 exposure and PEFR decline

Figure 3 presents scatter plots with fitted lines and 95% confidence intervals between PM2.5 concentrations and daily mean PEFR levels (L/sec; morning and evening average) across the three monitoring approaches. Spearman correlations between each PM2.5 metric and continuous PEFR are reported in Table 2. The PICO personal monitor showed the strongest negative correlation with PEFR (r = − 0.481, p < 0.001), reflecting a clear exposure–response relationship in which increasing personal PM2.5 was significantly associated with lower lung function. By contrast, the AK national fixed-site and KTR regional fixed-site monitors showed weaker but statistically significant negative correlations with PEFR (r = − 0.229, p < 0.001 and r = − 0.315, p < 0.001, respectively).

The markedly stronger PICO–PEFR correlation compared to both fixed-site monitors is consistent with the attenuation bias expected from non-differential exposure misclassification inherent to ambient monitoring approaches (Table 2; Fig. 3). Stratified Spearman correlation analyses across age groups (< 10, 10–12, > 12 years) and BMI categories (normal, underweight, overweight) consistently demonstrated statistically significant PICO–PEFR correlations in all subgroups (r range 0.482–0.634, all p < 0.001), while AK and KTR showed non-significant or markedly weaker correlations in the majority of strata (Supplementary Table S5).

GLMM analysis: association between PM2.5 exposure and PEFR decline

Table 3 presents results from the primary GLMM analysis with a random intercept per child (ICC = 0.420; between-child variance 42%; EPV = 52.4), adjusted for age, sex, BMI, temperature, and relative humidity. All three PM2.5 monitoring approaches showed statistically significant positive associations with PEFR decline (all p < 0.001). However, the magnitude and predictive performance of the associations differed substantially across exposure monitoring strategies. For the PICO personal monitor, each 1 µg/m3 increase in 72-h mean PM2.5 was associated with a log-odds increase of β = 0.01013 (95% CI 0.00915–0.01110, p < 0.001) in the probability of PEFR decline. The corresponding model discrimination was excellent, with a C-index of 0.837 (95% bootstrap CI 0.773–0.901). For the AK national fixed monitor, the association was β = 0.00294 (95% CI 0.00181–0.00406, p < 0.001), with a substantially lower C-index of 0.563 (95% CI 0.456–0.675). For the KTR regional fixed monitor, β = 0.00289 (95% CI 0.00191–0.00387, p < 0.001), and C-index = 0.585 (95% CI 0.493–0.687). Thus, the PICO monitor demonstrated a higher C-index than the other fixed-site monitors, approximately 0.25–0.27, indicating markedly stronger predictive performance for PEFR decline (Table 3).

Dose–response relationship: GLMM quartile analysis

Table 4; Fig. 4 present the dose–response relationships between PM2.5 quartile and PEFR decline probability across the three monitoring strategies. A clear and monotonically increasing dose–response gradient was observed for PICO monitor across all quartile strata, while the pattern was substantially attenuated for both fixed-site monitors (AK and KTR). For the PICO monitor, in particular, the observed PEFR decline rate in the first quartile as reference Q1 (≤ 17.5 µg/m3) was 2.7%, rising progressively to 10.0% in Q2 (17.6–26.2 µg/m3), 31.7% in Q3 (26.3–35.3 µg/m3), and 58.2% in Q4 (35.4–171.5 µg/m3). The corresponding GLMM β-coefficients relative to Q1 were: Q2 β = 0.047 (95% CI 0.003–0.092, p < 0.05), Q3 β = 0.209 (95% CI 0.164–0.255, p < 0.001), and Q4 β = 0.422 (95% CI 0.375–0.470, p < 0.001), representing a 21.5-fold increase in PEFR decline probability from Q1 to Q4 in the observed event rates.

The observed PEFR decline rates across quartiles (Q1–Q4) for AK ranged from 23.9 to 27.5%, and for KTR from 20.6 to 32.0%, showing considerably flatter gradients compared to the PICO (Table 4; Fig. 4).

Furthermore, a clinically meaningful threshold effect was identified at 25 µg/m3 for PICO-measured PM2.5 concentrations. Below this threshold (n = 648 observations), the observed PEFR decline rate was 5.7% (= 37/648 events); above this threshold (n = 788 observations), the rate was 41.9% (= 330/788 events)—a 7.3-fold increase. This threshold corresponds to the inflection point of the restricted cubic spline (RCS) dose–response curve and lies between the Q1 and Q2 boundaries of the PICO distribution (Table 4 note, Fig. 5).

Non-linear dose–response relationship: restricted cubic spline analysis

Figure 5 presents the restricted cubic spline (RCS) logistic regression curves (5 knots) illustrating the non-linear dose–response relationship between 72-h mean PM2.5 concentrations and the predicted probability of PEFR decline, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, for each of the three monitoring devices. For the PICO personal monitor, the RCS curve demonstrated a pronounced non-linear, accelerating dose–response pattern. At the WHO AQG reference concentration of 15 µg/m3, the predicted PEFR decline probability was 2.6%. This rose sharply to 15.0% at 25 µg/m3—representing more than a 10-fold increase relative to the WHO guideline concentration—and continued to increase steeply at higher concentrations.

The 25 µg/m3 inflection point, at which the predicted probability began to accelerate markedly, is consistent with the threshold effect identified in the quartile analysis. For concentrations corresponding to the Korean 24-h atmospheric environmental standard of 35 µg/m3, predicted PEFR decline probability exceeded 40%, underscoring the health significance of exposures within the range between the WHO AQG and the Korean national standard. By contrast, the RCS curves for AK and KTR showed substantially shallower and more gradual exposure–response relationships across the full PM2.5 concentration range, consistent with the attenuation of association observed in the GLMM and quartile analyses. These findings highlight that the PICO personal monitor was uniquely capable of capturing the non-linear, threshold-like dose–response structure of PM2.5–PEFR associations, a pattern that was obscured when fixed-site monitoring data were used (Fig. 5).

Secondary analysis: time-varying cox proportional hazards regression

Table 5 presents results from the secondary time-varying Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (daily counting process format; EPV = 61.2), adjusted for age, sex, BMI, temperature, and relative humidity. This analysis was conducted as a supplementary comparison to the primary GLMM to evaluate consistency across analytical frameworks. Results from the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis were consistent with the results derived from the primary GLMM analysis. The PICO-measured PM2.5 concentrations demonstrated a statistically significant association with risk of PEFR decline (HR = 1.037, 95% CI 1.032–1.041, p < 0.001), corresponding to a 3.7% increase in the hazard of PEFR decline per 1 µg/m3 increase in 72-h mean PM2.5 concentration. The corresponding C-index was 0.825, which was 0.012 lower than that (C-index = 0.837) derived from the primary GLMM analysis but remained substantially higher than either fixed-site monitor. The AK-measured PM2.5 was significantly associated with PEFR decline (HR = 1.009, 95% CI 1.003–1.015, p < 0.05, C-index = 0.617), as was KTR-measured PM2.5 (HR = 1.010, 95% CI 1.004–1.015, p < 0.001, C-index = 0.622). Across all analytical frameworks performed in this study, the PICO personal monitor consistently showed the strongest association with PEFR decline and the highest predictive discrimination, confirming the robustness of the primary findings (Table 5).

Discussion

This pilot study compared three PM2.5 exposure assessment strategies—personal portable monitoring (PICO), national fixed-site monitoring (AK), and regional fixed-site monitoring (KTR)—in relation to daily PEFR decline in 20 asthmatic children residing in Seoul during the winter season of 2019–2020. Across all analytical frameworks (Spearman correlation, GLMM, quartile analysis, time-varying Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, and RCS), the PICO personal monitor showed consistently stronger associations with PEFR decline and substantially higher model discrimination (C-index = 0.837) compared with both fixed-site monitors (AK: C-index = 0.563; KTR: C-index = 0.585). These findings were robust and reliable across multiple sensitivity analyses and consistent between the primary GLMM and secondary time-varying Cox regression analyses.

PICO monitoring device: reducing exposure misclassification

The most important finding of this pilot study is that PM2.5 exposure measurement using PICO personal monitors demonstrated substantially stronger associations with PEFR decline (Spearman r = − 0.481, p < 0.001; GLMM β = 0.01013, 95% CI 0.00915–0.01110; C-index = 0.837) compared with the other fixed-site monitoring stations (AK: r = − 0.229, p < 0.001, GLMM β = 0.00294, C-index = 0.563; KTR: r = − 0.315, p < 0.001, GLMM β = 0.00289, C-index = 0.585). This differential pattern is consistent with the well-established epidemiological concept of non-differential exposure misclassification (attenuation bias): fixed-site monitors introduce systematic misclassification by assigning spatially averaged ambient concentrations to individuals whose actual exposure may differ substantially based on their various time-activity patterns and microenvironmental locations57,58. This attenuation systematically biases exposure–response associations toward the null, reducing both the magnitude of observed coefficients and model discrimination.

The Bland–Altman analysis confirmed that post-calibration PICO measurements showed a mean bias of only − 1.9 µg/m3 relative to the KTR reference (95% limits of agreement: −30.3 to 26.6 µg/m3), indicating satisfactory systematic accuracy and reliability after the temperature and humidity correction procedure (Fig. 1). The moderate inter-monitor correlations (PICO–AK: r = 0.587; PICO–KTR: r = 0.650) versus the high AK–KTR correlation (r = 0.825) confirm that the two fixed-site monitoring stations captured largely the same ambient signals, while PICO monitor captured meaningful individual-level exposure variability not reflected in ambient concentrations. The PICO monitor, carried by children throughout their daily activities indoors and outdoors, provided an improved estimate of personal-level microenvironmental PM2.5 exposures, capturing variability attributable to indoor pollution sources, proximity to traffic, and individual mobility that is systematically absent from fixed-site monitoring data. These findings align with Wei et al.59 who demonstrated that mobility-based personal PM2.5 exposure assessment—incorporating indoor–outdoor penetration adjustment—improved agreement with gravimetric personal measurements by 64% (ICC 0.39 to 0.64) compared with home address-based static exposure estimates.

This study underscores the enhanced precision and reliability of portable personal monitoring devices, aligning with recent literature. Recently, Whitehill et al.15 demonstrated in a comparative evaluation that mobile monitoring platforms exhibit performance metrics comparable to those of fixed regulatory stations, highlighting their robust scalability for extensive, large-scale exposure monitoring campaigns. Recent technological advances in low-cost sensor (LCS) monitoring networks have made high-resolution spatiotemporal monitoring more feasible. However, as Nan et al.60 demonstrated, proper calibration methods that account for sensor variability and spatial heterogeneity are critical for ensuring data reliability in diverse urban environments, supporting the need for the validated portable monitoring approaches. The LCS-based personal portable monitoring device offers distinct advantages, including miniaturization, lightweight design, real-time measurement, and wireless data transmission, making it highly applicable for large-scale and long-term exposure assessment studies. Recent studies conducted by Chen et al.61, Lekamge et al.62, and Hayward et al. have demonstrated that IoT-based monitoring sensors can achieve high performance metrics comparable to reference instruments when appropriate calibration is applied.63.

However, it should be noted that the PICO mounted on a backpack or placed near the child’s primary activity location does not directly measure inhaled dose in the breathing zone. Residual exposure uncertainty arises from: variability in device placement across activity settings, wearing compliance, deviations from indoor placement rules, physical distance from the breathing zone, unmeasured microenvironments (e.g., during sleep or bathing), and variability in commuting routes. These limitations are inherent to wearable environmental monitoring studies and should be considered when interpreting the present results. The study findings are more appropriately interpreted as indicating that personal-level microenvironmental PM2.5 monitoring provides a stronger statistical association with PEFR decline than the other fixed-site monitoring approaches, consistent with reduced exposure misclassification, rather than as formal validation of PICO as a superior measurement methodology.

PM2.5 exposure and PEFR decline: dose-response relationship

In this pilot study, PICO-measured PM2.5 concentrations were significantly associated with a clear and monotonically increasing gradient of PEFR decline probability across all quartile strata. The GLMM quartile analysis showed that the probability of PEFR decline increased from 2.7% in Q1 to 58.2% in Q4, representing a 21.5-fold increase. The RCS analysis also identified a clinically meaningful threshold effect at approximately 25 µg/m3 for PICO-measured PM2.5. Below this threshold, the predicted probability of PEFR decline was 2.6–15.0%, whereas above it the probability accelerated sharply, reaching 41.9% in observed event rates for observations exceeding this threshold—representing a 7.3-fold increase relative to observations below 25 µg/m3 (5.7%). This threshold is 1.67-fold above the WHO AQG annual mean of 15 µg/m3 but substantially below the Korean 24-hour atmospheric environmental standard of 35 µg/m3, suggesting that respiratory health risks may be associated with PM2.5 concentrations within the range currently considered compliant with the national standards.

This finding is consistent with meta-analytic evidence demonstrating that acute PM2.5 exposure is significantly associated with decreased peak expiratory flow in asthmatic children. Zhang et al.64 reported that peak expiratory flow decreased by approximately 1.74 L/min per each PM2.5 10 µg/m³ increase, with substantially greater decrements in children with severe asthma. The non-linear, threshold-like dose–response pattern observed is also consistent with biological mechanisms by which high PM2.5 concentrations exacerbate airway hyperresponsiveness and oxidative stress in asthmatic children65,66. Therefore, the threshold value of 25 µg/m3 observed in the present study should be regarded as an exploratory observation warranting confirmation in larger cohorts in further studies.

To avoid the artificial episode boundaries that can confound within-child associations when survival models are applied to the intensively repeated outcome data, the secondary Cox proportional hazards regression was structured in a counting process (start–stop, Andersen–Gill) format with daily intervals67. The time-varying Cox regression results (HR = 1.037, 95% CI 1.032–1.041, C-index = 0.825 for the PICO monitor) were consistent with the primary GLMM findings, confirming that the substantially stronger association between PICO-measured PM2.5 and PEFR decline—relative to both fixed-site monitoring stations—is robust across analytical frameworks. A recent study conducted by Adhikary et al. involving 223,375 Indian children reported a dose-response relationship between PM2.5 exposure and acute respiratory infection risk, with odds ratios increasing from 2.06 (95% CI 1.60–2.66) in the second quartile (34.5–51.5 µg/m3) to 4.45 (95% CI 3.37–5.87) in the highest quartile (78.3–128.9 µg/m3) of PM2.5 exposure, demonstrating a pattern that supports the non-linear dose-response relationship68, similar to our study results.

Vulnerability of asthmatic children to PM2.5 exposure: physiological and behavioral factors

In general, children are more vulnerable to PM2.5 exposure compared to adults. The mean age of study participants was 10.8 ± 2.4 years, corresponding to an active phase of pulmonary growth during which PM2.5 exposure has been associated with an increased risk of chronic respiratory disease development from both epidemiological and toxicological perspectives17,69. Their higher minute ventilation relative to body weight results in proportionally greater PM2.5 inhalation at equivalent ambient concentrations70, and their school- and home-centered yet microenvironmentally diverse activity patterns generate individualized exposure profiles that fixed-site monitoring is inherently unable to capture71,72. Long-term exposure to air pollution during the critical period of pulmonary development (ages 10–18 years) has been shown to cause chronic, adverse effects on lung function growth in children, leading to clinically significant deficits in lung capacity as they reach adulthood17. This heightened vulnerability is attributable to physiological characteristics of children including rapid breathing rates, immature immune systems, and large lung surface areas per unit body weight. Zhang et al. demonstrated through systematic review and meta-analysis that PM2.5 exposure significantly impairs pulmonary function in children, with peak expiratory flow (PEF) decreasing by 1.74 L/min per 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 concentration; notably, children with severe asthma showed greater susceptibility (− 3.11 L/min) compared to healthy children (− 1.61 L/min), indicating that pre-existing respiratory conditions amplify vulnerability during the pulmonary growth phase64.

Furthermore, children actively move through diverse microenvironments such as schools, academies, and outdoor playgrounds, and they engage in high levels of physical activity, which may increase PM2.5 inhalation exposure. Several studies examining personal exposure in urban transport microenvironments have demonstrated that commuters experience substantially elevated PM2.5 concentrations compared to ambient levels, with PM2.5 exposure levels varying significantly by mode of transport, route selection, and traffic intensity.73,74 Especially, Zuurbier et al. found that commuters’ rush hour exposures to particulate matter were significantly influenced by transport mode, with cyclists on high-traffic routes experiencing median particle number concentrations of 46,600 particles/cm3 compared to 38,500 particles/cm3 in diesel buses, highlighting the importance of route selection in minimizing exposure74. These findings suggest that children’s frequent movement between various microenvironments, combined with increased physical activity and ventilation rates during active transport, may result in disproportionately high cumulative PM2.5 exposure compared to the other population groups with more sedentary lifestyles and fixed activity patterns.

The winter study period carries particular methodological significance beyond its epidemiological relevance. Seoul school-aged children spend approximately 70–80% of their time in indoor microenvironments during the cold season27,28, a period in which sharply reduced ventilation and convergent indoor combustion sources—cooking, space heating, incense combustion, and infiltrating outdoor pollution—collectively elevate personal PM2.5 exposure in ways that are structurally invisible to fixed outdoor monitoring networks75. These indoor exposure dynamics, rather than ambient outdoor concentration alone, likely underlie a substantial proportion of the predictive advantage demonstrated by PICO over the fixed-site monitors. The transient PICO monitor peaks exceeding 100 µg/m3 (0.77% of observations) are consistent with discrete indoor pollution events; critically, their exclusion did not materially alter the primary dose–response pattern (Supplementary Table S3), confirming that the observed associations reflect sustained microenvironmental exposure rather than artefactual concentration spikes.

Beyond the indoor exposure context, children’s inherently dynamic activity patterns amplify personal PM2.5 dose in ways that fixed-site monitors cannot capture72. The combination of active commuting across multiple microenvironments—school, private academies, transit, outdoor playgrounds—with minute ventilation rates during physical activity substantially higher than those of adults at rest means that children accumulate disproportionately greater inhaled doses even at identical ambient concentrations76,77. The personal monitoring approach integrates this microenvironmental and physiological variability into a single continuous exposure record, thereby reducing the non-differential exposure misclassification that attenuates dose–response estimates derived from fixed-site monitoring data58,78. The GLMM random intercept partially accounts for stable between-child differences in typical activity environments; however, time-varying indoor/outdoor stratification was not feasible within the present data and remains a priority for future studies.

The strengths of this study

This pilot study has several methodological strengths. First, the primary analysis employed a GLMM with a random intercept per child, which directly models the within-child correlation structure and yields valid standard errors for the repeated binary PEFR decline outcome. The events-per-variable ratio substantially exceeded the recommended threshold of EPV ≥ 10–20, confirming an adequate model power at the event level despite the small enrolled cohort. Covariates (age, sex, BMI, temperature, and relative humidity) were selected a priori based on established literature and biological plausibility, precluding data-driven variable selection and thereby reducing the risk of overfitting. Complementing the GLMM and RCS analysis was applied to characterize the non-linear dose–response relationship between PM2.5 exposure and PEFR decline without imposing linearity assumptions, enabling the detection of potential threshold effects or non-monotonic exposure–response patterns that conventional linear models would obscure. Together, these primary analytical approaches—GLMM for within-child longitudinal modeling and RCS for dose–response characterization—provide a methodologically rigorous and statistically appropriate framework for repeated binary health outcome data in pediatric panel studies.

Second, the study simultaneously employed three distinct PM2.5 monitoring approaches and directly compared their predictive performance using an identical analytical framework, ensuring objective comparability of C-index values across monitoring strategies. This three-arm design—personal portable monitor (PICO), national fixed-site network (AK), and regional fixed-site reference (KTR) monitors—provides a uniquely controlled basis for evaluating the incremental value of personal exposure monitoring, which cannot be established when monitoring methods are assessed in isolation across different studies or different outcome populations. Better performance of the PICO monitor demonstrated in this direct-comparison design is particularly credible because all three approaches were evaluated against the same PEFR decline outcome within the small but same cohort under identical analytical conditions.

Third, GPS-linked PICO personal monitoring was integrated with a nearest-neighbor spatial matching algorithm to assign each child’s hourly PM2.5 exposure to the most proximate fixed-site monitor, minimizing the spatial mismatch that is inherent to the area-based exposure assignment. By capturing each child’s actual spatiotemporal exposure trajectory across diverse microenvironments—including school, home, commute, and outdoor recreational spaces—this approach substantially improves upon ambient-station surrogates, which cannot reflect the high within-day variability characteristic of individual PM2.5 exposure. The reliability of personal exposure measurements was further ensured through a systematic field calibration process for the PICO personal monitors, correcting for humidity-dependent light-scattering artifacts that are known to inflate raw PM2.5 readings under high relative humidity—thereby reducing non-differential measurement error that would otherwise attenuate the true exposure–response associations toward the null.

Fourth, the 72-h cumulative exposure window was scientifically grounded in the established biological lag between PM2.5 inhalation, airway inflammatory response, and measurable PEFR decline (typically 1–3 days in asthmatic children), and was empirically validated by comparing five alternative exposure metrics in Supplementary Table S1, which confirmed the 72-h mean value as the optimal predictor by C-index. The PM2.5 exposure was additionally quantified using five multidimensional indicators—peak, mean, and median concentration, cumulative exposure index, and frequency of environmental standard exceedance—to simultaneously capture both the intensity and temporal patterning of acute exposure. This multidimensional exposure characterization approach reflects a fundamental principle in exposure science: peak concentration captures transient high-concentration spikes, mean concentration represents the overall exposure burden, and exceedance frequency indexes the frequency of regulatory threshold violations, enabling a comprehensive assessment that no single metric alone could achieve.

Finally, the robustness and internal consistency of the primary GLMM analytic findings were corroborated through multiple complementary validation approaches. The direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of the PM2.5–PEFR decline association identified in the primary GLMM were consistently replicated in the secondary time-varying Cox regression analysis, which evaluated the identical exposure–outcome relationship from an alternative survival-analytic perspective and thereby confirmed that the primary findings are not an artifact of the chosen modeling framework. Furthermore, a series of pre-specified sensitivity analyses—encompassing five alternative 72-h PM2.5 exposure indicators, separation of morning and evening PEFR measurements, exclusion of extreme PICO values exceeding 100 µg/m3, adjustment for air purifier use, subgroup analyses stratified by age and BMI, models with and without meteorological covariates, complete-case analysis excluding interpolated observations, and comparison of PM2.5 versus PM10 as the exposure metric—yielded results that were directionally and quantitatively consistent with the primary analysis, demonstrating the stability of the exposure–response estimates across diverse analytical scenarios (Supplementary Tables S1–S8). All analyses were cross-validated within two independent statistical platforms (Python and R), producing numerically identical estimates and confirming full computational reproducibility—a standard of analytical transparency that is rarely reported but increasingly advocated in environmental epidemiology.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has several important limitations that must be considered when interpreting its findings. First, with only 20 enrolled asthmatic children with their parents from a single district of Seoul (Guro-gu), the study has limited generalizability to other populations, geographic regions, seasons, and demographic subgroups. The small enrolled cohort constrains between-child statistical comparisons, precludes adequately powered sex-stratified analyses (female subgroup: n = 7), and limits the precision of child-level random effect estimates. Nevertheless, small sample sizes are not uncommon in intensive personal exposure monitoring studies owing to the high participant burden and logistical complexity of continuous data collection. Koenig et al.79 (n = 19) and Delfino et al.80 (n = 22) demonstrated that high-frequency personal monitoring data can effectively compensate for smaller cohort sizes, particularly in studies of asthmatic children, by capturing intra-individual variability that fixed-site monitoring consistently misses. Another recent pilot study by Bernasconi et al. similarly enrolled 20 participants81. In the present study, however, the intensively repeated measurement data provide valuable insights into individual-level daily exposure patterns and acute health responses. We collected approximately 20,000 raw PM2.5 data points collected at 5-min intervals over three months, yielding 1436 child-day observations and an EPV > 52 in the primary models—sufficient for within-child event-level analysis despite the small cohort size. This study should therefore be interpreted as a pilot or proof-of-concept investigation rather than a definitive methodological validation, and future studies with larger sample size, geographically diverse locations, and sex-balanced cohorts are needed to confirm and extend the present study findings and to enable adequately powered subgroup analyses by demographic and clinical characteristics.

Second, the primary analytic models adjust for age, sex, BMI, temperature, and relative humidity; however, several clinically important time-varying confounders could not be incorporated due to data availability constraints. Most notably, asthma medication use was not recorded on a daily basis, precluding its inclusion as a time-varying covariate in longitudinal analyses. Given that medication adherence—including to inhaled corticosteroids—is a key independent determinant of airway inflammation and pulmonary function in asthmatic children, its omission constitutes a meaningful source of residual confounding. Respiratory infection status, indoor allergen exposure (e.g., house dust mite, mold), structured physical activity data, and baseline asthma severity were similarly unavailable as daily time-varying variables.

To quantify uncertainty in discrimination estimates, bootstrap confidence intervals were computed using cluster resampling at the child level (1000 iterations); additionally, sensitivity analyses across multiple pre-specified analytical scenarios yielded directionally consistent results, reducing but not eliminating concerns about residual confounding given the small cohort size. Consequently, the observed associations between PICO-measured PM2.5 and PEFR decline should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal effects. Future studies should integrate electronic medication adherence monitoring, daily symptom diaries, continuous accelerometry-based activity tracking, and real-time indoor environmental sensors to achieve more comprehensive confounder control.

Third, the accuracy of PEFR measurements may vary depending on measurement technique, device calibration, and patient cooperation, with measurement errors being particularly prevalent in pediatric populations. Prior panel studies in asthmatic children have addressed this limitation through standardized protocols and objective biomarkers: Delfino et al. emphasized rigorous operator training and standardized measurement protocols across 1248 person-days80, and Koenig et al. supplemented traditional spirometry with fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) as an objective biomarker of eosinophilic airway inflammation79. Although the present study employed standardized PEFR measurement protocols with repeated participant training sessions, the absence of concurrent objective inflammatory biomarkers—such as FeNO or forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1)—limits the ability to distinguish true PM2.5-induced airway inflammation from non-specific lung function variability attributable to measurement effort or technique.

Additionally, the indoor/outdoor stratified analysis was not feasible, as GPS-based microenvironmental classification was not consistently available across all observation days. Sensitivity analysis incorporating air purifier use as an additional covariate yielded no significant effect, but residual confounding from unmeasured indoor pollution sources cannot be excluded. Future studies should incorporate concurrent objective biomarkers of airway inflammation alongside reliable microenvironmental classification to strengthen the specificity of exposure–outcome attribution.

Finally, the study was conducted entirely during the winter season (enrollment: November 2019–January 2020; monitoring: through March 2020) in a single urban district of Seoul, which limits the generalizability of findings to other seasons and geographic settings. Winter conditions in Seoul are characterized by reduced indoor ventilation rates, greater proportions of time spent indoors, elevated combustion-related PM2.5 emissions, and a distinct aerosol chemical composition compared with warmer seasons—all of which may influence both the absolute level and the health relevance of PM2.5 exposure. Moreover, the monitoring period partially overlapped with the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, and pandemic-associated behavioral changes—including social distancing mandates, school closures, and curtailed outdoor activity—may have altered children’s spatiotemporal exposure patterns and PEFR outcomes in ways that are difficult to fully attribute to PM2.5 exposure independent of pandemic-related confounding. Long-term follow-up studies spanning multiple seasons and climatically and geographically diverse regions are therefore necessary to characterize seasonal and regional heterogeneity in PM2.5–PEFR associations. Such studies should also be conducted within a post-pandemic normalized activity environment to permit unconfounded assessment of the comparative monitoring performance of personal versus fixed-site approaches.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides both methodological and clinical evidence with meaningful implications for pediatric respiratory health protection in the context of escalating urban air pollution. From a methodological standpoint, the substantially better performance of personal PM2.5 monitoring over fixed-site networks is consistent with the non-differential exposure misclassification framework, which predicts that area-based surrogates systematically attenuate exposure–response estimates relative to personal-level measurements—a bias particularly consequential in vulnerable pediatric populations with asthma. The observed acceleration of PEFR decline risk at concentrations exceeding 25 µg/m3 suggests that protective action thresholds set below the current WHO guideline (15 µg/m3) may be warranted in dense urban microenvironments where children’s actual exposures diverge substantially from ambient monitoring data.

Clinically, the findings support the development of an integrated digital health platform that couples real-time personal PM2.5 monitoring with longitudinal PEFR tracking, enabling individualized, data-driven early warning of asthma exacerbations in high-risk pediatric populations and facilitating the construction of large-scale exposure–outcome datasets necessary for personalized prevention strategies. Priority should also be given to reducing PM2.5 concentrations in children’s primary activity spaces—schools, commuting routes, and indoor home environments—alongside the reinforcement of behavioral guidelines during high-pollution events.

Future large-scale, multi-center, longitudinal cohort studies are needed to validate and extend these exposure–response relationships across diverse geographic and socioeconomic contexts, achieve comprehensive confounder control including daily medication adherence and indoor allergen burden, and elucidate the role of PM2.5 chemical composition and its interactions with co-pollutants (NO2, O3) and meteorological drivers in pediatric respiratory health trajectories. In parallel, the integration of LCS networks, wearable biosensors, and AI-driven predictive models into a unified spatiotemporal monitoring architecture offers a technically feasible pathway toward precision environmental health surveillance—one capable of delivering individualized, real-time exposure warnings at the resolution that children’s health demands. As climate change intensifies both the frequency and severity of PM2.5 exposure events, the transition from population-level ambient monitoring to child-centered personal exposure assessment is no longer a methodological refinement, but a public health imperative.

Conclusions

This pilot study compared three PM2.5 exposure assessment strategies—personal portable monitoring (PICO), national fixed-site monitoring (AK), and regional fixed-site monitoring (KTR)—in relation to daily PEFR decline in asthmatic children in Seoul during winter 2019–2020. Across all analytical approaches, PICO-measured PM2.5 concentrations demonstrated stronger and more consistent associations with PEFR decline than either fixed-site monitor, with markedly higher model discrimination in the primary GLMM analysis and corroborated by secondary time-varying Cox regression.

Restricted cubic spline analysis identified an exploratory threshold above which PEFR decline risk increased sharply—a concentration lying between the current WHO annual guideline and the Korean 24-h atmospheric environmental standard—suggesting that clinically meaningful exposure–response gradients may operate within concentration ranges currently considered regulatory-compliant. Given the pilot nature of this study, however, this threshold observation is hypothesis-generating and requires confirmation in larger independent cohorts before any policy implications can be drawn.

The stronger discriminative performance of PICO-based PM2.5 personal monitoring data is consistent with the attenuation of exposure–response estimates expected under non-differential exposure misclassification inherent to fixed-site monitoring, which fails to capture individual-level variability arising from mobility patterns, indoor microenvironmental sources, and time-activity behaviors. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory evidence of the potential advantages of personal monitoring for pediatric respiratory health research, rather than as formal metrological validation of the PICO monitor, as the present study does not meet the full requirements for rigorous sensor validation including collocated reference-grade comparisons and predefined equivalence criteria.

Taken together, this pilot study suggests that personal portable PM2.5 monitoring may offer an important complement to fixed-site networks for characterizing individual-level respiratory health risks in asthmatic children. Integration of real-time personal PM2.5 monitoring with concurrent PEFR tracking could form the basis of a novel digital health platform capable of enabling early warning of asthma exacerbations and supporting personalized environmental health management in high-risk pediatric populations. Future large-scale, multi-center, and longitudinal cohort studies—encompassing diverse geographic regions, multiple seasons, balanced sex distribution, and comprehensive time-varying confounder data including daily medication adherence and indoor allergen burden—are needed to validate these exposure–response relationships, quantify the contributions of PM2.5 chemical composition and co-pollutant interactions, and establish the generalizability of personal monitoring approaches across diverse clinical and environmental contexts.
