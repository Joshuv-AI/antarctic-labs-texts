PMPIHGLL: predicting metabolite–protein interactions using dual hypergraph convolutional networks and large language models
Authors: Lei Chen, Zhitong Jin, Ying Shao, Bo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolite–protein interactions (MPIs) play pivotal roles in regulating cellular processes and metabolic pathways and have important implications for systems biology and drug discovery. Although several computational models have been proposed, alternative approaches are needed to broaden the methodological toolkit for MPI prediction. In this study, we developed a new deep-learning model for predicting MPIs. Chemical and protein large language models were used to generate informative representations of metabolites and proteins. For each entity type, we constructed two K-nearest-neighbor hypergraphs using different values of K, thereby capturing higher-order relationships at different scales and expanding the feature-learning space. A dual hypergraph convolutional network (HGCN) learned from these relationships. The resulting high-level features were further processed using a channel-wise attention mechanism and a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) to obtain integrated metabolite and protein representations. Finally, a fully connected layer generated the predictions. The model was evaluated on four MPI datasets using global five-fold cross-validation; AUC and AUPR exceeded 0.9 on most datasets. Local five-fold cross-validation and an independent test further indicated that the model could predict MPIs involving previously unobserved metabolites or proteins. The model outperformed several existing MPI prediction models and related models adapted to this task. Ablation studies supported the rationale for its design. Short abstractIn this study, we focused on the metabolite–protein interactions (MPIs), which play pivotal roles in regulating cellular processes and metabolic pathways, and designed an effective deep-learning model for predicting MPIs. A fully connected layer finally generated the predictions. The model shown competitive performance on four MPI datasets and outperformed several existing MPI prediction models. Introduction

Metabolite–protein interactions (MPIs) play critical roles in regulating protein function and metabolic processes, with broad effects on cellular phenotypes and physiological homeostasis [1]. As core components of dynamic metabolic-network regulation, MPIs contribute to diverse biological processes, including signal transduction, modulation of enzyme activity, and control of metabolic flux [2]. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks underlying these interactions can advance our understanding of complex metabolic regulation and provide foundational insights for systems biology, synthetic biology, and precision medicine [3]. Traditional biochemical experiments used to identify MPIs do not readily scale to large metabolite and protein datasets because they are time-consuming and expensive. Rapid and cost-effective methods are therefore needed for large-scale MPI detection.

Advances in computational science provide an alternative means of detecting MPIs rapidly and cost-effectively. However, computational methods typically require extensive metabolite and protein profile data. High-throughput metabolomics and proteomics technologies now generate large volumes of such data [4, 5], alleviating this limitation. Several computational models have been proposed to predict MPIs. Thieme and Walther developed a network model for predicting metabolic compound–protein interactions [6]. Their model used a biclique-extension approach to infer missing links in the metabolic networks of Escherichia coli and humans. Most MPI prediction models have used machine-learning algorithms that extract metabolite and protein features from multiple data sources and pass them to conventional classifiers [7, 8]. Zhao et al. replaced the conventional classifier with a deep neural network, thereby introducing deep learning into MPI prediction [9]. However, the remainder of its workflow resembled that of conventional machine-learning models. Wang et al. developed MPI-VGAE, a complete deep-learning framework for MPI prediction [10]. The model used a variational graph autoencoder with molecular features for metabolites and proteins and an MPI network as inputs. Although deep-learning algorithms and large language models (LLMs) have been widely applied to binary-association prediction, their use in MPI prediction remains limited. MPI-VGAE represents an early attempt in this direction. However, it does not model metabolite–metabolite or protein–protein associations, which may limit its performance.

In this study, we developed PMPIHGLL, a new deep-learning model for predicting MPIs. The chemical LLM ChemGPT [11] and the protein LLM ProtT5 [12] were used to extract initial metabolite and protein features. For each entity type, we then constructed two hypergraphs with the K-nearest-neighbor (KNN) algorithm to encode higher-order relationships among metabolites or among proteins; two hypergraphs used metabolites as nodes, and two used proteins as nodes. KNN and K-means are commonly used to construct hypergraphs; here, we selected KNN. Previous models have generally used a single KNN hypergraph, which can constrain the feature-learning space. PMPIHGLL instead constructs two metabolite hypergraphs and two protein hypergraphs using different values of K and therefore represents higher-order relationships at different scales. This design expands the feature-learning space and can yield more informative metabolite and protein features. A dual hypergraph convolutional network (HGCN) processed the hypergraphs to generate high-level features, which were then integrated using a channel-wise attention mechanism and a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN). A fully connected layer generated the final predictions. We evaluated the model on multiple datasets. For most dataset settings, AUC and AUPR exceeded 0.9 under global five-fold cross-validation. PMPIHGLL also outperformed several existing MPI prediction models and models adapted from related tasks.

Materials and methods

Datasets

In this study, we used four MPI datasets. The first three datasets, denoted D1, D2, and D3, were obtained from Soleymani Babadi et al.’s study [7] and covered two model organisms (E. coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae). The data-acquisition and cleaning procedures are described in that study [7]. D1 comprised 18 metabolites, 319 proteins, and 765 MPIs. D2 comprised 29 metabolites and 1079 proteins. MPIs in D2 were defined using the confidence scores reported in STITCH [13]. A medium confidence threshold of 0.4 yielded 3996 MPIs, whereas a high confidence threshold of 0.7 yielded 2319 MPIs. We refer to these datasets as D2(0.4) and D2(0.7), respectively. D3 comprised 41 metabolites and 958 proteins. As in D2, its MPIs were defined using STITCH confidence scores. The medium and high confidence thresholds yielded 4065 and 1879 MPIs, respectively, constituting D3(0.4) and D3(0.7). The fourth dataset, D4, was obtained from Zhao et al.’s study [9]. It contained 3829 human MPIs and was reported to cover five metabolites and 1677 proteins. Details of the four datasets are provided in Table 1.

Metabolite and protein representations

LLMs have recently been applied to many scientific problems. By learning statistical patterns from large volumes of unlabeled sequence-like data, they can generate informative embeddings for downstream tasks such as classification, clustering, and regression. Specialized LLMs for chemical structures and protein sequences have consequently been developed. In this study, we used one chemical LLM and one protein LLM to generate initial representations of metabolites and proteins.

Chemical structures can be represented as linear ASCII strings using the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES), making them amenable to language-model analysis. We used ChemGPT [11], a recently developed chemical LLM. ChemGPT is a GPT-3–style generative pretrained transformer based on GPT-Neo and uses a Self-Referencing Embedded Strings (SELFIES) tokenizer. It was trained on a large PubChem corpus and contains one billion non-embedding parameters. To our knowledge, ChemGPT is among the largest chemical LLMs developed to date. Performance evaluation and metrics

Model training required negative samples in addition to the validated MPIs described in Section 2.1. Unlabeled metabolite–protein pairs were treated as candidate negatives. Including all such pairs would have created severe class imbalance. We therefore treated the validated MPIs as positive samples and randomly sampled an equal number of unlabeled pairs as negative samples. This procedure yielded balanced versions of D1, D2, D3, and D4.

Cross-validation is widely used to evaluate model performance [26–30]. We used two forms of five-fold cross-validation. In global five-fold cross-validation, metabolite–protein pairs were randomly divided into five approximately equal folds. Each fold served once as the test set, while the remaining folds formed the training set. This setting assesses the prediction of missing interactions among metabolites and proteins represented in the dataset. In local five-fold cross-validation on metabolites or proteins, the entities of the selected type were randomly divided into five folds. For each held-out entity fold, all metabolite–protein pairs involving those metabolites or proteins formed the test set, and the remaining pairs formed the training set. Thus, the held-out metabolites or proteins were absent from model training. This setting evaluates predictions involving previously unobserved metabolites or proteins. Performance under both protocols was summarized using accuracy (ACC), F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR [31–37].

Because the four datasets covered three species (E. coli, S. cerevisiae, and humans), these findings suggest potential applicability across species, although validation on additional organisms is needed.

We further evaluated PMPIHGLL using local five-fold cross-validation on metabolites and proteins. Each protocol was repeated 10 times with independently sampled negative sets, and the mean ACC, F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR are reported in Table 5. Performance under local cross-validation was markedly lower than under global cross-validation. This difference was expected because information about held-out metabolites or proteins was excluded from training in the local setting but remained available in the global setting. Performance also differed between the two local protocols. Holding out metabolites produced lower performance than holding out proteins. Relative to global cross-validation, most metrics decreased by more than 10% when metabolites were held out but by <10% when proteins were held out. This contrast may reflect the pronounced difference between the numbers of metabolites and proteins in each dataset. Each dataset contained far fewer metabolites than proteins, so removing one metabolite fold substantially reduced the chemical diversity available for training. The decreases on D2 and D4 were also larger than those on D1 and D3 when metabolites were held out. To investigate this difference, we calculated structural similarities among the metabolites in each dataset. Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs) were generated with RDKit (http://www.rdkit.org/), and pairwise structural similarity was calculated using the Tanimoto coefficient. For each metabolite, we recorded its maximum similarity to another metabolite; the distributions are shown in Fig. 2. Metabolite similarities were lower in D2 and D4 than in D1 and D3. Because MPI prediction models commonly assume that similar metabolites tend to share protein partners, predicting interactions for chemically dissimilar held-out metabolites in D2 and D4 was more difficult. These results indicate that the current model generalizes more effectively to unobserved proteins than to unobserved metabolites.

Ablation study on features

PMPIHGLL used ChemGPT to generate raw metabolite features and ProtT5 to generate raw protein features. To assess whether these features were suitable for MPI prediction, we replaced them with alternative representations. For metabolites, we used molecular fingerprints [38] and molecular descriptors. PaDEL-Descriptor [39] generated 881 molecular-fingerprint features and 1444 molecular-descriptor features. For proteins, we evaluated three alternatives: (i) 343 conjoint-triad (CT) features [40]; (ii) 800 features derived from BLOSUM62 [41] and position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs) [42] using the Bigram method [43]; and (iii) 1280 LLM features generated by ESM2 [44]. Models using every combination of the metabolite and protein representations were evaluated on D2(0.4) in 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation. Mean performance is reported in Table 6. The model using LLM (ChemGPT) and LLM (ProtT5) features achieved the highest mean values for all four metrics, supporting their selection for PMPIHGLL. We also averaged ACC, F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR across models using each metabolite or protein representation (Fig. 3). LLM (ChemGPT) features consistently performed best among the metabolite representations, followed by molecular fingerprints and molecular descriptors (Fig. 3A). Among the protein representations, LLM (ProtT5) features performed best, followed by LLM (ESM2), BLOSUM62 + PSSM, and CT features (Fig. 3B). Thus, the selected LLM features performed best among the tested alternatives.

Ablation study on modules

PMPIHGLL contains several modules, including hypergraph construction, the HGCN, channel-wise attention, and the 1D-CNN. We assessed the contribution of each module by ablation.

First, cosine similarity was used to construct the metabolite and protein similarity matrices (Eqs. (4) and (6)). We replaced cosine similarity with Jaccard and Gaussian similarity functions and also used Euclidean and Manhattan distances to construct distance matrices. KNN hypergraphs were built from each new similarity or distance matrix. The resulting models were trained on D2(0.4) and evaluated in 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation. The ACC, F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR values are reported in Table 7. Models based on the different functions or distances performed at broadly similar levels. The performance of PMPIHGLL using cosine similarity is shown in the final row. Although its advantage was modest, it achieved the highest mean value for all four metrics, supporting the use of cosine similarity for metabolites and proteins.

PMPIHGLL exceeded these variants by more than 1 percentage point in ACC and F1-measure and by 0.5–1 percentage point in AUC and AUPR. These results suggest that HyperConv refined the raw metabolite and protein features more effectively than the tested alternatives. Conventional graph neural networks process pairwise relationships and therefore cannot directly exploit higher-order hypergraph structure, which may explain the lower performance of PMPIHGLL-GCN, PMPIHGLL-GAT, and PMPIHGLL-GraphSAGE. We next assessed the value of the dual-hypergraph design by constructing four models based on all combinations of one of the two metabolite hypergraphs and one of the two protein hypergraphs. These models were evaluated on D2(0.4) using 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation, and their mean performance is shown in Fig. 4. The combination and produced the highest ACC and F1-measure (Fig. 4A and B). The highest AUC was obtained with and (Fig. 4C), whereas the highest AUPR was obtained with and (Fig. 4D). Considering all four metrics, we selected and as the best single-hypergraph configuration and named this model PMPIHGLL-SHypergraph. Its performance, reported in Table 8, was lower than that of PMPIHGLL. The dual-hypergraph design enabled the HGCNs to extract information at complementary neighborhood scales, which improved performance.

Third, we removed contrastive learning to obtain PMPIHGLL-noCL. This model was evaluated on D2(0.4) using 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation. Its mean ACC, F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR were 0.848, 0.854, 0.913, and 0.881, respectively (Table 8). PMPIHGLL exceeded PMPIHGLL-noCL by more than 1.5 percentage points on every metric, supporting the contribution of contrastive learning.

Fourth, we assessed the channel-wise attention mechanism and 1D-CNN used to integrate the high-level features from the dual HGCN. We constructed three ablation variants. PMPIHGLL-noAttention omitted the attention mechanism and passed the two representations directly to the 1D-CNN. PMPIHGLL-noCNN omitted the 1D-CNN and averaged the two representations. PMPIHGLL-noACNN omitted both components and also fused the representations by averaging. All three variants were evaluated on D2(0.4) using 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation. Their performance is reported in Table 8. Each variant performed worse than PMPIHGLL, supporting the contributions of both the channel-wise attention mechanism and the 1D-CNN.

Finally, the refined metabolite and protein features were combined with their raw counterparts to form the final representations. We removed the raw features and passed only the refined features to the fully connected layer, yielding PMPIHGLL-noResidual. We also constructed PMPIHGLL-RF, which passed only the raw features to the prediction stage. Both models were evaluated on D2(0.4) using 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation. The mean ACC, F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR were 0.847, 0.854, 0.914, and 0.886 for PMPIHGLL-noResidual and 0.852, 0.855, 0.918, and 0.897 for PMPIHGLL-RF, respectively (Table 8). PMPIHGLL performed better than both variants on all four metrics, supporting the combination of raw and refined features.

Collectively, the ablation results indicate that each evaluated module contributed to PMPIHGLL performance, supporting the overall model design.

Comparison with existing models

Several computational models have been proposed for MPI prediction [6–10]. Based on dataset compatibility, we selected two MPI models, SARTRE [7] and the model of Zhao et al. [9], for comparison with PMPIHGLL. We also included EnzRank [51] and the MEI model [52], two deep-learning models developed for the related task of enzyme–substrate interaction prediction. These models were adapted to MPI prediction and evaluated by global five-fold cross-validation on the four datasets described in Section 2.1.

On D1, D2, and D3, we compared PMPIHGLL with SARTRE [7], EnzRank [51], and the MEI model [52] (Table 9). On D1, PMPIHGLL exceeded SARTRE by ~0.08 in ACC, F1-measure, and AUC. EnzRank performed similarly to SARTRE and remained inferior to PMPIHGLL. The MEI model outperformed SARTRE and EnzRank but remained below PMPIHGLL. On D2 and D3, PMPIHGLL also achieved higher ACC, F1-measure, and AUC, with improvements ranging from 0.02 to 0.09. Overall, PMPIHGLL outperformed SARTRE, EnzRank, and the MEI model on these datasets. SARTRE used shadow prices derived from metabolic networks as metabolite and protein features and passed them to a random-forest classifier. This architecture follows a conventional machine-learning pipeline with feature extraction separated from model training, whereas PMPIHGLL refines its representations during end-to-end training. EnzRank and the MEI model do not explicitly use metabolite–metabolite or protein–protein relationships, which may partly explain their lower performance.

Independent evaluation of PMPIHGLL

To assess the generalizability of PMPIHGLL, we constructed an independent test dataset. We downloaded 1675 validated E. coli MPIs from PMI-DB [9], reported to involve 20 metabolites and 617 proteins. D1 was selected as the training dataset because it represented the same species. After removing the D1 MPIs from the 1675 interactions, 910 MPIs remained. These positive interactions and an equal number of randomly sampled unlabeled metabolite–protein pairs formed the independent test dataset, which was reported to cover 18 metabolites and 347 proteins. Figure 5 shows the overlap of metabolites and proteins between the training and independent test datasets. Two metabolites and 301 proteins in the independent test set were absent from the training dataset and therefore did not participate in PMPIHGLL training. The test dataset was intended to assess generalization to previously unobserved entities.

Venn diagrams showing the overlap of metabolites and proteins between the training (D1) and independent test datasets. Two metabolites and 301 proteins in the independent test dataset do not occur in the training dataset.

PMPIHGLL trained on D1 was applied to the independent test dataset and achieved an ACC of 0.701, F1-measure of 0.676, AUC of 0.759, and AUPR of 0.744. Figure 6 compares these results with performance on D1 under global and local five-fold cross-validation. Performance on the independent test set was markedly lower than that under global cross-validation. This difference was expected because global cross-validation assessed missing MPIs among entities represented in the dataset, whereas the independent test set included metabolites and many proteins absent from the training data. The four metrics decreased by 0.05–0.085, indicating that performance remained moderate on the independent test set. Independent-test performance was similar to that obtained under local five-fold cross-validation on metabolites or proteins. Because local cross-validation also holds out entities from training, this similarity supports the relevance of the local protocol. These findings suggest that PMPIHGLL can predict candidate MPIs involving previously unobserved metabolites or proteins, although performance is lower than for observed entities.

Effect of negative-sample selection on model performance

PMPIHGLL training required negative samples, for which we randomly selected unlabeled metabolite–protein pairs. Because some unlabeled pairs may be true but unvalidated MPIs, treating them as negatives can bias model evaluation. To assess sensitivity to this issue, we created perturbed datasets by relabeling randomly selected unlabeled pairs as positive samples; the number relabeled was equal to 1% of the original positive samples. We trained one model on each perturbed dataset using the PMPIHGLL procedure and refer to these models as pseudo models. Each pseudo model was evaluated in 10 runs of global five-fold cross-validation. Figure 7 compares the results with those of PMPIHGLL. The pseudo models performed similarly to PMPIHGLL on D1, D2, and D4. On D1 and D2, ACC, F1-measure, AUC, and AUPR decreased by no more than 0.008; on D4, these metrics increased slightly. On D3, all four metrics decreased by 0.02–0.04. Overall, the small changes on most datasets suggest that performance was not highly sensitive to this limited relabeling perturbation. However, this experiment does not eliminate potential bias from latent positives among sampled negatives.

Performance of PMPIHGLL and the pseudo models across datasets. Each pseudo model is trained after randomly selected unlabeled metabolite–protein pairs are added to the positive class. PMPIHGLL and the pseudo models show broadly similar performance on most datasets.

Study limitations

PMPIHGLL performed well in MPI prediction but has several limitations. First, treating unlabeled metabolite–protein pairs as negative samples may introduce false negatives and bias model evaluation. The analysis in Section 3.6 suggests limited sensitivity to a 1% relabeling perturbation, but residual bias remains possible. Second, the model performed less well when predicting MPIs involving unobserved metabolites under local five-fold cross-validation. The small number and limited diversity of metabolites may contribute to this result; future studies should evaluate larger and more chemically diverse datasets. Third, PMPIHGLL has limited interpretability. Although it can prioritize candidate MPIs, it does not elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. Future work will address these limitations.

Conclusion

This study developed a new deep-learning model for MPI prediction. Chemical and protein LLMs generated the initial metabolite and protein representations, which were refined and integrated using a dual HGCN, a channel-wise attention mechanism, and a 1D-CNN. The model performed well across four MPI datasets and outperformed several existing MPI prediction models and models adapted from related tasks. Ablation studies indicated that each evaluated component contributed to performance. PMPIHGLL may therefore support the prioritization of candidate MPIs and research on metabolic regulation. The datasets and code used in this study are available at https://github.com/ztjin958/PMPIHGLL.
