Context‐informed personalized segmentation from radiology reports: A fully automated framework for long‐term liver tumor follow‐up
Authors: Jaehee Chun, McKell Woodland, Austin Castelo, Caleb O'Connor, Mais Altaie, Aashish Gupta, Shanli Ding, Allyson Nguyen, Eugene J Koay, Kristy K Brock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePersonalized segmentation has been shown to improve performance over generalized models in follow‐up imaging; however, its robustness across large temporal gaps remains unclear. We propose a fully automated, context‐informed personalization framework that integrates radiology report‐derived clinical context with imaging features to improve longitudinal liver tumor segmentation.MethodsThis retrospective study included 171 patients for generalized model training and 41 longitudinal patients with follow‐up CT scans acquired 3–15 months after baseline for personalization and evaluation. We developed a U‐Net‐based vision‐language segmentation framework that automatically summarizes free‐text radiology reports, encodes them into language embeddings, and integrates them into intermediate image features to provide clinical context. A generalized model was trained using paired CT‐report data. For each longitudinal patient, personalization was performed at the 3‐month follow‐up using either image‐only input (vision‐only personalization) or paired image–report input (context‐informed personalization). Models were then evaluated on subsequent 6–15‐month follow‐up scans without further adaptation. Patients were stratified into Stable and Dynamic cohorts based on relative GTV volume variability. Performance was assessed using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and LogPenalty Score (LPS), with additional ablation studies examining language models and report components.ResultsIn the Dynamic cohort, vision‐only personalization exhibited diminishing gains at later follow‐up, whereas context‐informed personalization maintained statistically significant improvements in ΔLPS at 9–15 months (p < 0.05). In the Stable cohort, both personalization strategies showed sustained performance with comparable segmentation accuracy, indicating that incorporating report‐derived clinical context did not degrade performance in cases with limited anatomical variability. Ablation studies demonstrated that domain‐specific encoder‐based language models (BioClinicalBERT) outperformed larger generative models and that effective context integration did not require complex architectural modifications.ConclusionIncorporating report‐derived clinical context improves the longitudinal robustness of personalized liver tumor segmentation, particularly in patients with dynamic tumor evolution. These findings suggest that clinically grounded language representations can stabilize personalization across extended follow‐up while enabling fully automated and scalable deployment.Keywords: liver tumor segmentation, longitudinal follow‐up imaging, personalized AI, radiology report integration, vision‐language model

INTRODUCTION

Longitudinal medical imaging, in which repeated scans are acquired over time for the same patient, plays a central role in the management of liver malignancies, including treatment response assessment, disease surveillance, and adaptive radiotherapy. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Across these applications, accurate liver and gross tumor volume (GTV) segmentation is required to quantify three‐dimensional tumor burden, evaluate interval change, and compare disease status across serial follow‐up scans. However, manual segmentation is labor‐intensive and rarely feasible in routine longitudinal imaging, particularly in follow‐up studies. As a result, longitudinal assessment in clinical practice is often reduced to simplified two‐dimensional measurements, such as Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1‐based long‐axis evaluations. 5 While RECIST provides standardized and reproducible criteria, its reliance on unidimensional measurements limits its ability to capture true three‐dimensional tumor burden and complex morphological changes over time. 6 , 7 , 8 These limitations have motivated growing interest in automated, AI‐based segmentation methods that can provide scalable and consistent delineation of anatomical structures across longitudinal imaging studies, thereby reducing the manual burden of repeated contouring and supporting more reproducible volumetric assessment during follow‐up. 9 , 10 , 11

While generalized segmentation models are trained across patient cohorts to learn shared imaging features for liver and GTV segmentation, they are inherently limited in their ability to accommodate substantial inter‐patient variability, including differences in anatomy, disease phenotype, and imaging characteristics. 12 , 13 To mitigate this limitation, personalized segmentation strategies have been proposed, in which a pretrained generalized model is adapted using patient‐specific prior imaging, thereby shifting the modeling focus from population averages to individual anatomy. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Such personalization has been shown to substantially improve segmentation accuracy compared with generalized models, particularly in low‐data settings and early follow‐up scenarios, by effectively resolving inter‐patient discrepancies that are poorly captured during generalized training. 14 , 18 , 19 , 20

Despite these advances, existing personalization strategies exhibit limitations in long‐term follow‐up settings. In practice, longitudinal imaging is often acquired at irregular or widely spaced intervals, reflecting clinical constraints, patient compliance, and disease‐dependent follow‐up schedules. As a result, personalization is commonly anchored to the most recent available prior scan, even when substantial temporal gaps exist between consecutive follow‐up studies.

Under these conditions, progressive anatomical changes, treatment‐induced effects, and evolving tumor characteristics can accumulate beyond what image‐based personalization can reliably extrapolate from prior data. 12 , 14 , 21 , 22 , 23 Consequently, the benefits of personalization may diminish as the temporal separation between scans increases, particularly in patients with dynamic tumor evolution. These observations suggest that while personalization effectively mitigates inter‐patient variability, it does not inherently guarantee robustness to large temporal gaps between follow‐up scans, highlighting a critical challenge for sustaining patient‐specific segmentation performance over extended longitudinal horizons.

A key reason for the breakdown of image‐based personalization under large temporal gaps is that medical images provide only a static snapshot of anatomy at a given time point. While longitudinal imaging enables repeated structural assessment, the interpretation of temporal change, such as disease progression or treatment response, is not explicitly encoded in the image itself but instead arises from comparative and contextual clinical assessment across examinations. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 24 , 25 , 26

In routine clinical practice, this temporal interpretation is formalized through radiology reports, where imaging findings are summarized relative to prior studies and integrated with clinical and treatment context. By contrast, image‐based personalized segmentation models operate directly on pixel‐level appearance and lack access to higher‐level temporal descriptors, instead relying on visual extrapolation from prior scans. 14 As temporal gaps between follow‐up studies increase, this reliance on image‐only information becomes increasingly fragile, leaving clinically available longitudinal context unexploited in existing personalization pipelines.

Recent advances in natural language processing have enabled the incorporation of radiology reports into data‐driven imaging models. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Because free‐text radiology reports are heterogeneous and not directly compatible with imaging models, an effective formulation is to first use large language models to normalize and summarize clinical narratives, yielding structured descriptions of disease status, interval change, and treatment context. 33 , 34 These structured summaries can then be encoded using either encoder or decoder‐based language models pretrained on clinical text, producing compact feature representations suitable for integration with imaging‐derived features. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41

Importantly, such language‐derived context is inherently longitudinal: radiology reports explicitly describe interval change, progression status, and treatment response relative to prior examinations, even when imaging is sparsely sampled. If incorporated appropriately, this information may help constrain patient‐specific model behavior across large temporal gaps, reducing reliance on direct visual extrapolation from prior scans alone. This perspective motivates the exploration of context‐informed personalization strategies that integrate imaging and report‐derived representations to improve robustness in long‐term follow‐up segmentation.

In this work, we propose a fully automated, context‐informed personalization framework for longitudinal liver tumor segmentation that integrates imaging features with report‐derived clinical context. Free‐text radiology reports are first processed using an automated language modeling pipeline to generate structured representations of patient‐specific temporal information, without manual curation or expert annotation. These representations are then incorporated into a vision‐based segmentation model to condition patient‐specific adaptation beyond what can be inferred from imaging alone.

The proposed framework is evaluated on a longitudinal liver tumor dataset with follow‐up intervals up to 15 months, enabling a systematic assessment of personalization robustness across increasing temporal separation. Through quantitative analysis and ablation studies, we demonstrate that incorporating report‐derived context improves the stability of personalization in patients with dynamic tumor evolution, particularly at later follow‐up stages where image‐only personalization tends to degrade. Together, these results suggest that leveraging clinically available language data provides a principled mechanism for sustaining personalized segmentation performance across long‐term follow‐up, while remaining fully automated and scalable for clinical deployment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Liver tumor dataset with radiology reports

This retrospective study included patients with liver tumors who underwent liver‐directed radiation therapy at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center between 2011 and 2023. This study was approved by the institutional review board, and informed consent was waived due to its retrospective design. All imaging data, structure sets, and radiology reports were de‐identified before analysis in accordance with institutional data protection procedures. Patient identifiers were removed from the analytic dataset, and each case was assigned a study‐specific identifier for model development and evaluation. Patients were identified through an institutional database query. Patients without appropriate follow‐up imaging or with incomplete clinical or imaging data were excluded. The final study cohort (N = 212) comprises all patients who met these eligibility criteria.

The cohort comprised a heterogeneous spectrum of liver malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC, n = 69), cholangiocarcinoma (CC, n = 90), and metastatic liver disease (n = 53), reflecting diverse pathological characteristics and clinical courses. Patients underwent various treatment strategies, such as radiation therapy, systemic therapy, and locoregional interventions, depending on disease type and stage.

In total, 376 diagnostic CT scans from these 212 patients were collected, along with corresponding radiology reports, expert‐annotated liver contours, and liver‐associated GTVs. These CT scans were acquired on scanners from GE Medical Systems, Philips, and Siemens Healthineers, including 16 scanner models. Tube voltage ranged from 100 to 140 kVp, with 120 kVp used for the majority of scans, reflecting the heterogeneous acquisition conditions of this retrospective clinical dataset. All CT scans were manually annotated in accordance with institutional standards, with liver and GTV contours delineated by an experienced radiologist with 7 years of abdominal imaging experience. Primary CT scans were excluded, as they typically lack temporally matched radiology reports and are therefore not suitable for automated report–image integration.

For AI model development and evaluation, the dataset was organized based on the availability of longitudinal follow‐up imaging. Among the 212 patients, 171 had a single CT scan at the 3‐month (3 m) follow‐up time point, while the remaining 41 had longitudinal CT scans at 5 time points (3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 months). This partitioning was based solely on data availability and was not associated with differences in patient characteristics or treatment strategies.

To evaluate the robustness of personalization under different longitudinal trajectories, the 41 longitudinal patients were further stratified into Dynamic (n = 20) and Stable (n = 21) cohorts. This stratification was based on the coefficient of variation (CV), calculated as the standard deviation of GTV volume normalized by the patient‐specific mean GTV volume across the five longitudinal follow‐up scans. This relative variability metric ranged from 0.08 to 1.74 across patients. The 20 patients with the highest variability (0.47–1.74) were assigned to the Dynamic cohort, whereas the remaining 21 patients (0.08–0.45) were assigned to the Stable cohort. The liver GTV volume was 73.0 ± 62.3 cubic centimeters (cc) in the Dynamic cohort and 92.5 ± 25.1 cc in the Stable cohort.

Fully automated vision‐language framework for personalized segmentation

The proposed framework is designed as a fully automated pipeline that translates raw radiology reports and CT images into segmentation outputs, eliminating the need for manual intervention at any stage. For each follow‐up time point, the framework takes a contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT image and its corresponding free‐text radiology report as inputs. It produces voxel‐wise segmentation masks of the liver and GTV as outputs. An overview of the complete architecture is illustrated in Figure 2.

Overview of the proposed fully automated vision‐language personalization framework for long‐term liver tumor segmentation. Raw free‐text radiology reports are first processed using an automated large language model (LLM)‐based summarization module to normalize and condense heterogeneous clinical text without human intervention. The summarized reports are encoded using BERT to generate fixed‐dimensional language embeddings, which provide patient‐specific clinical context. Imaging features extracted from a U‐Net‐based backbone are combined with language embeddings within a vision‐language model (VLM) to guide personalized segmentation. The framework enables end‐to‐end automation from radiology reports and imaging data to personalized segmentation outputs across longitudinal follow‐up.

Raw free‐text radiology reports were processed using an automated language model‐based summarization module (Phi‐4). 28 This step was intended to normalize and condense heterogeneous report text into a consistent representation suitable for downstream modeling, rather than to perform clinical interpretation or decision‐making. Automated summarization reduced variability in report length and structure while preserving clinically relevant longitudinal information.

Specifically, each report was summarized into a structured representation containing nine components: tumor type, anatomical location, lesion count, morphology, progression status, non‐tumoral liver changes, regional lymph nodes, peritoneal findings, and treatment history. These components were designed to capture clinically relevant contextual information commonly described in longitudinal liver imaging reports while maintaining a consistent format across patients and time points.

The summarized reports were subsequently encoded using BioClinicalBERT to generate fixed‐dimensional language embeddings. 30 BioClinicalBERT was selected due to its pretraining on large‐scale clinical corpora and its suitability for representing medical narratives. Language embeddings were extracted from the final‐layer [CLS] token of BioClinicalBERT and served as contextual inputs to the context‐informed segmentation model.

In parallel, CT images were processed by a U‐Net‐based segmentation backbone to extract spatial imaging features. Following the vision‐language segmentation framework proposed by Oh et al., 41 the report‐derived language embeddings were incorporated into the image encoder's intermediate feature representations to provide patient‐specific clinical context during segmentation. Rather than replacing image‐based representations, language information was used to condition image features, allowing the model to modulate segmentation behavior based on longitudinal cues encoded in the radiology reports while preserving the spatial fidelity of CT‐derived features.

Importantly, the entire framework—from raw free‐text reports and CT images to segmentation outputs—operated without manual curation, report section selection, or expert annotation of textual data, ensuring true end‐to‐end automation. The quality of automated report structuring was additionally assessed using an independent third‐party manual audit, with the sampling strategy, component‐level scoring, and error‐category analysis provided in the Table S2 and the accompanying Supplementary Materials text. This design enables scalable deployment of the context‐informed segmentation in longitudinal imaging workflows. Implementation details of the vision‐language framework, including network architecture and training configurations, are provided at https://github.com/jchun‐ai/context‐informed‐seg.

Baseline training and personalization protocol

Figure 3 summarizes the study design, including the generalized model development cohort, the longitudinal personalization/evaluation cohort, the patient‐specific personalization time point, and the subsequent inference/evaluation protocol. A generalized baseline model was first trained on a single‐time‐point cohort of 171 patients with CT scans acquired at the 3 m follow‐up. These cases were split into 136 training and 35 validation patients. Two generalized models were trained under matched settings: a vision‐only generalized model using CT images alone and a context‐informed generalized model using paired CT‐report inputs. The vision‐only generalized model served as the reference baseline for ΔLPS calculations. For each context‐informed case, the multimodal framework described in Section 2.2 was applied using paired inputs of the 3 m follow‐up CT image and its corresponding radiology report, with expert‐annotated liver and GTV contours as supervision.

Study design and longitudinal personalization workflow. The full study cohort included 212 patients with 376 CT‐report pairs. A generalized baseline model was developed using 171 patients with a single 3‐month follow‐up scan, split into 136 for training and 35 for validation. The longitudinal personalization/evaluation cohort included 41 patients with follow‐up scans at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 months. For each longitudinal patient, personalization was performed using the 3‐month CT‐report pair and corresponding contours, and inference/evaluation was performed on the 6‐, 9‐, 12‐, and 15‐month scans without further adaptation.

For the longitudinal cohort (n = 41), personalization was performed independently for each patient. Starting from the generalized baseline model, a separate patient‐specific model was fine‐tuned using a single 3‐month follow‐up CT volume, its matched radiology report, and the patient's corresponding liver and GTV annotations. No additional validation set was used during patient‐specific personalization. As a result, 41 personalized models were generated, one for each longitudinal patient. This design reflects a clinically realistic personalization scenario in which patient‐specific adaptation is performed using limited early follow‐up data.

Each patient‐specific model was then evaluated on the same patient's subsequent follow‐up scans acquired at 6, 9, 12, and 15 months, without any further parameter updates. This evaluation protocol enabled assessment of how well personalization based on an early follow‐up time point generalizes to later scans with increasing longitudinal divergence.

DICOM CT images were then converted to NIfTI format without in‐plane resizing. Original voxel spacing ranged from 0.66 × 0.66 × 0.63 mm3 to 1.17 × 1.17 × 5.00 mm3, with a median spacing of 0.94 × 0.94 × 2.50 mm3. The original superior–inferior (SI) image coverage ranged from 53 to 664 slices, with a median of 143 slices, corresponding to 185 to 662 mm in physical length, with a median of 357 mm. During the image preparation step, the SI extent of each volume was restricted to the liver‐bounding region, with an additional margin of 5 slices above and below the manually defined liver contour to reduce irrelevant anatomical coverage. These image preparation procedures were conducted using MATLAB R2024b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA).

Before AI model training and inference, the prepared CT volumes were further processed within the Python/PyTorch‐based preprocessing pipeline. CT images were resampled to a voxel spacing of 1.50 × 1.50 × 2.00 mm3. During training, random patches of size 192 × 192 × 64 were extracted from the resampled volumes. During evaluation, sliding‐window inference with a patch size of 192 × 192 × 64 and an overlap of 0.5 was applied. Intensity values were clipped to [−175, 250] Hounsfield units and normalized to [0, 1]. Patch‐based training and sliding‐window inference with an overlap of 0.5 were then applied during model optimization and evaluation.

The segmentation backbone was optimized using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10− 4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10− 5. A Dice‐cross‐entropy loss was used for supervision. Training was performed for up to 250 epochs, with a linear warm‐up followed by a cosine annealing learning rate schedule. Mixed‐precision training was enabled to improve computational efficiency. Sliding‐window inference with an overlap of 0.5 was applied during evaluation. Detailed architectural configurations, training scripts, and personalization settings are provided in a publicly available GitHub repository.

Ablation analyses

To evaluate the contribution of report‐derived contextual representations, two ablation analyses were performed. First, we compared alternative language models for encoding the summarized radiology reports, including BioClinicalBERT, BioClinicalModernBERT, RadBERT, LLaMA‐2, Phi‐4, and Seed‐OSS. These models were evaluated using the same personalization and inference protocol to assess whether the choice of language representation affected longitudinal segmentation performance. Second, we performed a leave‐one‐component‐out report ablation analysis, in which each structured report component was excluded individually from the summarized report input. This analysis evaluated the relative contribution of tumor type, anatomical location, lesion count, morphology, progression status, non‐tumoral liver changes, regional lymph nodes, peritoneal findings, and treatment history to context‐informed personalization.

Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis

Segmentation performance was evaluated using a combination of standard spatial accuracy metrics and a composite performance score. Specifically, the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), mean surface distance (MSD), and the LogPenalty Score (LPS) were used to assess volumetric overlap and boundary accuracy.

DSC was used to quantify volumetric agreement between the predicted segmentation and the reference annotation, with higher values indicating better overlap. Boundary accuracy was evaluated using HD95 and MSD, which capture worst‐case and average surface discrepancies, respectively. Lower values of HD95 and MSD indicate improved boundary alignment.

In addition to these conventional metrics, segmentation quality was summarized using the LogPenalty Score (LPS), a composite metric that jointly reflects volumetric overlap and boundary accuracy within a single bounded scale. 42 LPS is defined as

This formulation applies logarithmic penalties to boundary errors while preserving the influence of volumetric agreement through the DSC term. As a result, LPS reduces sensitivity to extreme outliers in surface distance metrics while remaining bounded between 0 and 100. Higher LPS values indicate better overall segmentation performance. Unlike HD95 and MSD, which may become unstable or undefined for small or fragmented structures, LPS provides a robust, interpretable summary metric suitable for comparing segmentation performance across patients and follow‐up time points.

All metrics were computed at the patient level. Performance differences between paired methods were assessed using one‐sided Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, reflecting the directional hypothesis that personalization improves segmentation performance. In addition to comparisons between personalized models and the generalized vision‐only baseline, differences between context‐informed and vision‐only personalized models were evaluated using the same statistical framework to test whether incorporating report‐derived clinical context yielded further performance gains. The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used because it does not require normally distributed paired differences and is therefore appropriate for the relatively small longitudinal cohorts analyzed in this study. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05, with additional significance levels indicated as * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001.

Although the proposed framework predicts both liver and GTV masks, the primary objective of this study is to evaluate the longitudinal robustness of tumor (GTV) segmentation across large temporal gaps. Therefore, quantitative liver segmentation results are reported in the Table S3.

In the Dynamic cohort, where substantial anatomical and tumor changes accumulate with increasing follow‐up, vision‐only personalization exhibited limited robustness. Although statistically significant ΔLPS gain was observed at the 6‐month follow‐up, vision‐only personalization failed to achieve statistically significant improvements over the generalized vision‐only baseline from the 9‐month follow‐up onward (Figure 4a). This progressive loss of significance indicates that image‐derived features alone are insufficient to compensate for pronounced longitudinal variations in tumor appearance and extent.

In contrast, context‐informed personalization consistently yielded positive ΔLPS values and achieved statistically significant improvements over the generalized vision‐only baseline across all evaluated follow‐up time points, including 6, 9, 12, and 15 months (p < 0.05, one‐sided Wilcoxon signed‐rank test; Figure 4a). The persistence of ΔLPS gains across increasing temporal separations from the baseline scan suggests that incorporating report‐derived clinical context enables more robust personalization under dynamic longitudinal conditions, capturing complementary information not fully encoded in image features alone.

For the Stable cohort, in which anatomical and tumor characteristics remained relatively consistent across follow‐up, both vision‐only and context‐informed personalization achieved stable, statistically significant ΔLPS gains at all evaluated time points (Figure 4b). In this setting, the performance difference between the two personalization strategies was less pronounced, indicating that when visual appearance remains stable, image‐based personalization alone may be sufficient to maintain reliable segmentation performance.

These time‐resolved trends are consistent with the absolute performance metrics summarized in Table 1. When pooled across all follow‐up scans (6–15 months), context‐informed personalization achieved the highest overall segmentation accuracy in the Dynamic cohort, whereas both personalization strategies performed comparably in the Stable cohort. Together, these findings indicate that the primary benefit of incorporating report‐derived clinical context emerges at later follow‐up stages in patients with dynamic tumor evolution. Representative qualitative examples illustrating these cohort‐specific longitudinal behaviors are presented in the following section.

Reference contours are overlaid on axial CT slices (green: liver, magenta: GTV). For each follow‐up scan, quantitative metrics, including LogPenalty Score (LPS), Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), and GTV volume, are annotated, enabling direct comparison of segmentation accuracy, contour plausibility, and volumetric change over time. At the 6‐month follow‐up, both vision‐only and context‐informed personalization achieve similarly high performance (LPS: 24.42 vs. 25.27), with a relatively large residual GTV volume (114 cc) that remains relatively similar in scale to the 3‐month personalization scan. Under these conditions, tumor boundaries remain visually discernible, and image‐derived features appear sufficient to support effective personalization for both approaches.

In contrast, at the 9‐month follow‐up, a marked divergence in performance becomes evident. The GTV volume decreases substantially to 52 cc, representing a pronounced relative regression from the 3‐month personalization scan. At this time point, context‐informed personalization maintains robust performance (LPS: 21.45), whereas vision‐only personalization exhibits a notable drop in accuracy (LPS: 13.56). This divergence indicates that rapid volumetric regression introduces a scale mismatch that begins to limit the effectiveness of image‐only personalization, even before overt appearance ambiguity becomes visually prominent.

At later follow‐up, however, the combination of pronounced size reduction and changing image contrast introduces a more challenging segmentation setting. At the 12‐month follow‐up, the CT appearance differs noticeably from earlier scans: the surrounding liver tissue appears darker, and the contrast between the lesion and the liver background is substantially reduced. This reduced lesion‐liver contrast, together with the continued regression relative to the 3‐month personalization scan (GTV volume: 46 cc), makes boundary delineation more ambiguous. Under these conditions, vision‐only personalization exhibits a sharp performance collapse (DSC ≈ 0.03), consistent with over‐adaptation to mismatched appearance and scale relative to the initial personalization scan.

In contrast, context‐informed personalization maintains substantially higher DSC and LPS values at the same time point, despite the large discrepancy in tumor size and visual appearance relative to the early follow‐up scans. By conditioning personalization on report‐derived longitudinal context, the model appears less sensitive to relative longitudinal changes in tumor scale and appearance, enabling more stable segmentation when direct visual correspondence to the initial personalization scan is diminished.

By the final follow‐up, where the GTV volume has further decreased (29 cc), context‐informed personalization continues to produce anatomically plausible contours, whereas vision‐only personalization remains less stable. Collectively, this case highlights that large relative changes from the personalization scan, together with appearance change, can limit the effectiveness of image‐only personalization, and that incorporating longitudinal clinical context helps preserve segmentation robustness when tumor size and contrast deviate substantially from the initial personalization state.

Ablation study on language models

Table 2 summarizes the results of an ablation study comparing different language models for generating report‐derived embeddings in the context‐informed personalization framework. Among the evaluated models, BioClinicalBERT achieved the most consistent performance gains following personalization, particularly in the Dynamic cohort, where it yielded a statistically significant improvement over the personalized vision‐only reference model in both LPS and DSC. RadBERT and Seed‐OSS also showed statistically significant improvements in the Dynamic cohort, although their overall gains were less consistent than those observed with BioClinicalBERT. This result indicates that report‐derived representations extracted using BioClinicalBERT provide complementary information beyond image‐based personalization under conditions of substantial longitudinal variation.

These findings suggest that generative language capacity alone does not directly translate to improved representation quality for downstream voxel‐wise segmentation tasks.

Overall, this ablation study indicates that clinically aligned language representations can improve context‐informed personalization in dynamic longitudinal settings, with BioClinicalBERT providing the most consistent performance among the evaluated models. The results also suggest that representation alignment, rather than model size alone, is a key factor for effective vision‐language personalization.

Quality audit of automated report summaries

The independent manual audit demonstrated high agreement between automated summaries and original radiology reports. Across 80 audited reports and 9 structured components per report, 692 of 720 components were judged accurate, yielding an overall component‐level accuracy of 96.1%. The most common discrepancies involved lesion‐location abstraction, such as mapping central, hilar, caudate, or bilobar disease into fixed location categories. DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that fully automated, language‐informed personalization improves long‐term liver tumor segmentation compared with vision‐only approaches. Across multiple follow‐up intervals, context‐informed personalization maintained positive ΔLPS values at later follow‐up stages, whereas vision‐only personalization showed diminished or non‐significant gains over time, particularly in patients exhibiting dynamic tumor evolution (Figure 4). These results indicate that vision‐only personalization becomes increasingly unstable as longitudinal appearance changes become more pronounced, whereas context‐informed personalization maintains consistent performance under the same conditions.

A mechanistic interpretation of this divergence is that radiology reports encode patient‐specific temporal context that cannot be recovered from a single imaging snapshot alone. In particular, reports describe interval changes relative to prior examinations, clinically interpreted progression status, and treatment‐related context, whereas each CT scan provides only a static representation of anatomy at a given time point. Incorporating such report‐derived information may therefore help constrain patient‐specific segmentation trajectories over time, supporting more temporally consistent predictions as disease appearance evolves across follow‐up scans.

The representative longitudinal cases further clarify the mechanism by which image‐only personalization breaks down under dynamic conditions. In the illustrated Dynamic case (Figure 5 ), vision‐only personalization exhibited severe performance degradation at later follow‐up, including near‐complete failure at 12 months, whereas context‐informed personalization remained stable across the same scans. Importantly, this collapse coincided with substantial changes in tumor scale and appearance relative to the initial personalization scan, rather than with errors in the generalized baseline. These observations indicate that rapid volumetric and contrast changes expose a scale and appearance mismatch inherent to image‐only personalization, while report‐informed models remain guided by longitudinal clinical context that is less dependent on direct visual correspondence to the initial personalization scan.

The ablation studies provide additional evidence that the benefit of language‐informed personalization depends on the nature of longitudinal variation rather than on architectural complexity. In the Dynamic cohort, most leave‐one‐component‐out configurations retained statistically significant gains relative to vision‐only personalization, though the significance varied across report components (Table 3). The most notable change occurred when treatment history was excluded, after which the context‐informed model no longer showed a statistically significant improvement in either LPS or DSC. In contrast, the Stable cohort showed limited sensitivity to report‐component ablation, consistent with the smaller marginal contribution of contextual conditioning when appearance remains relatively stable over time.

The manual audit also supports the reliability of the automated report‐structuring step. The overall component‐level accuracy was 96.1%, and the most frequent discrepancies involved lesion‐location abstraction, such as mapping central, hilar, caudate, or bilobar disease into fixed location categories. This pattern is relevant when interpreting the report‐component ablation analysis: excluding the location component did not substantially alter the overall performance trend of context‐informed personalization (Table 3), suggesting that the model was not highly dependent on this coarse location descriptor alone. Therefore, although minor location‐abstraction errors were observed, they are unlikely to materially explain the observed segmentation improvements. Future work could reduce this ambiguity by using more anatomically explicit location encoding or lesion‐level structured extraction.

Representative examples of the structured report components, including treatment history, are provided in Table S1. In the leave‐one‐component‐out ablation, excluding treatment history produced one of the largest performance changes in both cohorts. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that treatment history alone is dominant or sufficient. Because each component was evaluated by removing it from the full contextual representation, the observed effect may reflect interactions between treatment history and other report components. A definitive assessment would require additional single‐component analyses and systematic evaluation of mixed‐component combinations, which are beyond the scope of the present study. Therefore, the current ablation analysis is interpreted as identifying report components that can affect model output, while a more detailed analysis of component‐specific and combinatorial effects is left for future work.

Finally, the comparison across language models further supports the interpretation that representation alignment, rather than model scale or generative capacity, governs the effectiveness of context‐informed personalization. Encoder‐based clinical language models, particularly BioClinicalBERT, yielded more stable and consistent gains than larger generative models, including LLaMA‐2 and Phi‐4 (Table 2). Despite their expressive power, these generative models did not improve personalization performance and showed greater variability in the Dynamic cohort. This suggests that compact, semantically grounded representations trained on clinical narratives are better suited for conditioning voxel‐wise segmentation than representations optimized for text generation.

This study has several limitations. First, the proposed framework assumes the availability of paired radiology reports during model development, as the segmentation model is trained to incorporate report‐derived contextual signals. While such image‐report paired datasets are increasingly common in large clinical archives, this requirement may limit direct application to segmentation models trained solely on imaging data without associated clinical narratives. Second, because contextual conditioning relies on radiology reports, segmentation inference is performed after report availability rather than immediately after image acquisition. In typical clinical workflows, however, radiology reports are routinely generated as part of standard follow‐up assessment, and segmentation tasks used for longitudinal monitoring or retrospective analysis are often performed after report generation. As a result, the proposed framework remains compatible with common clinical workflows while enabling additional contextual guidance beyond image‐only models. Third, the longitudinal evaluation was conducted on a relatively limited cohort (n = 41) from a single institution, which may introduce institutional bias and limit the generalizability of the findings across different patient populations, imaging protocols, and clinical settings. However, this controlled setting enables a focused assessment of longitudinal personalization behavior under consistent imaging and reporting conditions.

Taken together, these results reframe the role of vision‐language models in longitudinal segmentation. Rather than serving primarily as multimodal feature enhancers, report‐informed models act as mechanisms for governing personalization behavior over time. By embedding patient‐specific clinical trajectories into the personalization process, the proposed framework reduces susceptibility to longitudinal drift and catastrophic failure in dynamic disease settings, while remaining fully automated and scalable for clinical deployment.

CONCLUSION

This study presents a fully automated, language‐informed personalization framework for long‐term liver tumor segmentation. By integrating automated radiology report summarization with clinically aligned language representations, the proposed approach improves segmentation performance and longitudinal consistency compared with vision‐only personalization strategies.

The results demonstrate that radiology reports provide complementary temporal clinical context that enhances personalization, particularly during dynamic follow‐up. Importantly, this study shows that domain‐specific, encoder‐based language models can outperform larger generative language models for vision‐language fusion in medical image segmentation, underscoring the importance of task‐aligned representation learning.

Overall, the proposed framework enables scalable and clinically practical personalization without the need for manual curation of textual data. These findings highlight the potential of language‐informed approaches for longitudinal imaging analysis and adaptive radiotherapy, while emphasizing that effective personalization can be achieved through the principled use of clinically grounded language representations rather than increased model complexity.
