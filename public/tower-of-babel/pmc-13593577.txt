Optimizing Ocular Surface Disease (OSD) Management in Patients with Glaucoma: Expert Strategies from Asia
Authors: Vilavun Puangsricharern, Jin Rong Low, Manchima Makornwattana, Eng Hui Gan, Chien-Chia Su, Ha Thi Thu Pham, Astrianda Nadya Suryono, Chungkwon Yoo, Joseph Anthony Tumbocon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOcular surface disease (OSD) commonly affects glaucoma patients and can occur due to the disease itself and multiple contributing factors, such as the long-term use of preserved topical medications, thereby impacting adherence and treatment outcomes. In Asia, rising glaucoma burden and variability in regional guidelines underscore the need for a practical synthesis of current literature in the region. This review summarizes the OSD treatment landscape in Asia and proposes a pragmatic approach for identifying and managing concomitant OSD in patients with glaucoma.Material and MethodsEight glaucoma specialists and a cornea expert representing eight Asian countries convened on 8th March 2025 at an industry‑sponsored expert meeting to discuss the current treatment landscape of concomitant OSD in glaucoma. A practical approach for identifying and managing concomitant OSD in glaucoma patients in daily clinics was subsequently developed based on published literature and expert input.ResultsRegional studies estimate high OSD prevalence among glaucoma patients, with country-level estimates ranging 32.1–99.1% based on symptoms or signs. Risk factors for OSD are similar between Asian and global populations, but Asian ethnicity is a recognized risk factor for dry eye disease. Regional and country-level guidelines for OSD are available but vary in terms of diagnostic criteria, and local awareness of OSD remains lacking. The 3-step approach (Step 1: Assess symptoms and risk factors, Step 2: Slit-lamp examination and staining, Step 3: Tailored management by severity) offers a pragmatic pathway for application in daily clinics.ConclusionThe proposed framework serves as a first step towards harmonizing OSD management in glaucoma patients across Asia, with the potential to enhance the quality of glaucoma care. Further validation of the framework, and increased consensus and awareness may help improve the management of OSD in Asia.

Introduction

Ocular surface disease (OSD) is a multifactorial disorder of the conjunctiva, cornea, lacrimal glands and meibomian glands, resulting in affected tear production, decreased visual clarity and discomfort.1–3 OSD leads to bothersome ocular symptoms such as inflammation, grittiness and itching, with dry eye disease (DED) being one of the most prominent manifestations. DED is characterized by tear film instability resulting in ocular symptoms and/or visual impairment.1,3,4

OSD can occur more frequently in patients with glaucoma than the general population, either following topical glaucoma treatment or as a pre-existing condition exacerbated by topical treatment.1 This predisposition can be due to a combination of factors such as glaucoma itself, increased intraocular pressure (IOP), history of previous OSD treatment, number of glaucoma topical medications and eye drops instilled daily, and prolonged use of preserved glaucoma medications.5,6 Glaucoma is a chronic disease requiring long-term treatment, with topical eye drops being the most commonly used treatment.7 However, due to the preservatives and/or active ingredients in these eye drops, long-term usage causes significant changes in the ocular surface and chronic low-grade inflammation, leading to various clinical manifestations.7 In combination with other contributing factors, prolonged exposure to preserved, multi-drop topical medications can exacerbate tear film instability and ocular surface inflammation, worsening DED symptoms.1,8,9

Within Asia, the burden of glaucoma is expected to grow, with the number of patients projected to increase to 80.87 million by 2040 due to aging populations.10 Therefore, the prevalence of concomitant OSD in the region is also expected to rise accordingly. Although high rates of ocular symptoms and signs have been reported among patients, OSD is often unrecognized and not addressed.7,11

It is important to recognize that OSD is not a mere side effect or tolerability issue in patients with glaucoma. If left untreated, it can result in a host of undesirable complications in glaucoma treatment, such as medication non-adherence, loss of IOP control and even surgical failure.7,12 Hence, there is a need for ophthalmologists and clinicians to effectively identify and manage concomitant OSD in patients with glaucoma.

To address this unmet need, this review aims to optimize the management of concomitant OSD in patients with glaucoma by providing an Asia-specific overview of the local treatment landscape and challenges. We also propose an expert-led, stepwise approach for identifying and managing concomitant OSD to enhance glaucoma patient care, while respecting real-world limitations that Asian ophthalmologists face in daily clinics.

Materials and Methods

The decision to develop this review article was made by a panel of experts from Asia who were selected based on their clinical expertise in glaucoma and OSD, while ensuring representation across countries and diverse healthcare settings in Asia. The panel comprised eight glaucoma specialists and one cornea specialist, providing a broad, multidisciplinary, multinational Asian perspective.

Published literature on concomitant OSD in glaucoma was identified through a pragmatic structured desk search and reviewed by the expert panel. Key screening and management considerations/approaches were decided via expert panel voting through a digital survey. A roundtable meeting was then held on 8th March 2025 for the experts to align on the panel’s recommendations, based on a review of the available evidence and their local clinical experience. During the roundtable, differences in opinions were discussed and the recommendations were refined accordingly, until consensus was achieved among the panel. The panel also focused on identifying practical challenges to managing OSD in everyday clinics and methods to overcome them.

Subsequently, the expert recommendations from this meeting were compiled into a stepwise practical guide intended for ophthalmologists to screen and manage concomitant OSD in patients with glaucoma. This review article summarizes the OSD treatment landscape in Asia, the management recommendations from the authors, and their expert insights on future directions for managing concomitant OSD in patients with glaucoma.

Discussion

Epidemiology of OSD in Glaucoma Patients Across Asia

Prevalence of OSD and DED

OSD is common among glaucoma patients, though prevalence rates vary depending on the diagnostic criteria used. Globally, the prevalence of OSD among glaucoma patients ranges between 48–59% when based on symptoms and 22–78% when based on signs.7

However, there are no studies describing OSD prevalence in glaucoma patients across Asia as a region specifically. Consequently, the epidemiology of DED in the general population may serve as an indicator of OSD prevalence, given the overlap between the two conditions. A 2022 systematic literature review and meta-analysis of DED in general Asian populations estimated the pooled prevalence to be 20.1% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 13.9–28.3) and the incidence to be 16.7% (95% CI: 0.0–34.9) in any population in Asia.13 From the Tear Film Ocular Surface Society (TFOS) Dry Eye Workshop (DEWS) II Epidemiology Report, general population-based studies across Southeast Asia have reported higher prevalence of symptomatic DED (between 20.0–52.4%, with Singapore as an exception ranging 6.5–12.3%).14 However, the DEWS II report also cautioned that the studies were heterogenous in terms of population characteristics, disease definitions and diagnostic methods.14

A few country-level epidemiology studies have been conducted in glaucoma patients in Asia, reporting OSD prevalence among glaucoma patients to be at least 30%. A multicenter observational study among patients with mild-to-moderate glaucoma in Malaysia between 2020–2021 estimated OSD prevalence to be 69.2% based on symptoms and 33.0% based on Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) score. The study also noted high prevalence of OSD signs, with 90.4% of patients having abnormal fluorescein tear film break-up time (F-TBUT; defined as ≤10 seconds in this study) and over two-thirds having an abnormal Schirmer’s test (≤10 mm).15 From Thailand, a single-center study estimated 32.1–99.1% and 38.5% of glaucoma patients had OSD, based on signs and OSDI score respectively.16 Despite the limited Asian epidemiological data on OSD, existing evidence suggests it is common in the region.

Risk Factors for OSD and DED

Several risk factors for OSD in glaucoma patients have been identified in global populations. These include older age, pre-existing meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), autoimmune or systemic disease, number of glaucoma medications used, and prolonged exposure to preservatives such as benzalkonium chloride (BAK).6,7,17 Similar risk factors have been identified in DED diagnosis frameworks from Indonesia, Hong Kong, Philippines and Singapore, suggesting that global and Asian populations do not differ substantially in terms of risk factors.18–21 Additionally, patients with more severe glaucoma may be at higher risk of developing OSD. A study in Nigerian patients has reported a significant, positive correlation between OSD prevalence and glaucoma severity (mild: 43.4%, moderate: 65.1%, severe: 85.0%; p<0.01).22 Further validation of this correlation is needed in Asian populations.

Notably, it is increasingly recognized that Asian populations are at higher risk of DED, compared to Western populations. The DEWS II Epidemiology report identified Asian ethnicity as a significant risk factor for DED after controlling for age and gender, with an odds ratio of 1.5–2.2 times that of non-Asian ethnicity.14 DEWS III also reported Asian ethnicity as a consistent risk factor for DED, noting higher prevalence of DED and MGD in Asian countries than Western countries.23 Among co-located Asian and non-Asian populations, Asians were more likely to have DED symptoms (74% vs 51%, p=0.002), poorer OSDI scores and more severe DED signs than their non-Asian counterparts.24

While the cause of Asian predisposition towards DED is not well-understood, a systematic literature review has suggested this may be due to ethnic differences in the ocular surface physiology.25 Craig et al similarly noted that Asian individuals showed poorer meibomian gland function and higher degree of incomplete blinking than co-located non-Asian individuals, which could also contribute to this trend.24

Treatment Challenges Faced by Patients with OSD and Glaucoma

Preservatives in Glaucoma Medications

It has been established that preserved topical glaucoma medications can have negative impacts on ocular surface health.6,7,12,26 The antimicrobial activity of the preservative is generally inversely proportional to its compatibility with the ocular surface.27 In a Korean study, patients receiving preserved prostaglandin analogs (PGAs) showed significantly worse meibomian gland parameters, greater ocular surface staining and shorter F-TBUT than healthy controls and those on preservative-free (PF) PGAs. However, the study sample size was small.28

Medication Non-Adherence

As glaucoma is a chronic disease requiring long-term care, medication adherence is crucial for helping to prevent disease progression and visual impairment.1 Despite this, suboptimal adherence is a widespread issue affecting 5–80% of glaucoma patients worldwide.31

The tolerability of glaucoma medications plays a major role in influencing adherence. Medications that are poorly tolerated may impact patients’ quality of life (QoL) and lead to poor adherence, which can contribute to poorer disease control.7 The discomfort and irritation from concomitant OSD symptoms can thus exacerbate this tolerability issue and subsequently contribute to poorer adherence.31

Impact on Patients’ Quality of Life and Economic Burden

For glaucoma patients, QoL is affected by several closely linked factors such as visual dysfunction severity, impact on vision-related daily living activities and psychological impact of the disease.7 Concomitant OSD can thus negatively impact patient QoL on multiple levels.

In the short-term, OSD symptoms can cause discomfort, irritation and visual disturbances, which may directly affect patients in their everyday lives. As reported in DEWS II, dry eye symptoms have negative psychological and physical impacts on patients’ lives, and impair visual function which disrupts many crucial daily activities (eg, reading, driving, using smartphones).14 In the long term, the tolerability issues from OSD may reduce medication adherence, leading to disease progression and visual dysfunction, and consequently further worsening of QoL.7

From a Malaysian study by Gan et al, OSD severity is correlated with the extent of QoL impairment. Glaucoma patients with better Glaucoma Quality of Life-15 (GQL-15) scores tend to have low OSDI scores, with a significant positive linear correlation between GQL-15 and OSDI (p<0.001). The study noted that the patient cohort had relatively good QoL based on GQL-15, with most experiencing no or little glaucoma-associated discomfort; however, this may be because only patients with mild-to-moderate glaucoma were included.15 While the impact of OSD on glaucoma patients’ QoL requires further research to accurately quantify, studies on DED patients suggest the impact can be substantial. From DEWS II, utility assessments suggest that patients with mild and severe DED experience QoL reductions on a similar level to patients with mild psoriasis and moderate-to-severe angina, respectively.14

Studies on the economic burden of OSD in patients with glaucoma are limited in the Asia-Pacific region; an Australian study estimated that the annual economic burden of moderate/severe OSD in patients with glaucoma as AU$330.5 million.32,33 However, global studies on glaucoma or DED individually suggest that both conditions are associated with substantial economic burden.33,34 Consequently, it is plausible that this burden is compounded in patients with concomitant glaucoma and OSD.

Diagnosis and Management of OSD in Asia

Although OSD is common in glaucoma patients, management guidelines with regional consensus and validation are lacking globally and within Asia. The terms “OSD” and “DED” are also used variably and sometimes interchangeably to refer to the same disease and/or overlapping conditions. For clarity, the DEWS III Diagnostic Methodology defines DED as a symptomatic subset of OSD, with the latter defined by ocular surface signs only, whereas DED involves both signs and symptoms.35

This section aims to provide an overview of OSD diagnosis and management recommendations across Asia. Due to the large degree of overlap between OSD and DED, recommendations pertaining to DED have also been included.

Discrepancies in Disease Definition and Diagnosis Criteria

Within Asia, the Asia-Pacific Glaucoma Guidelines (APGG) 4th edition and Asia Dry Eye Society (ADES) consensus report are the only regional guidelines addressing OSD and/or DED. Several country-specific frameworks for DED have also been developed in recent years. At the regional level, disease definitions from APGG and ADES largely align, with both recognizing OSD/DED as a multifactorial disease resulting in symptoms, tear film abnormality and visual impairment.1,4 Notably, the APGG 4th edition is the only guideline that specifically distinguishes DED as a manifestation of OSD, with other manifestations including superficial punctate keratitis, tear film instability, allergic conjunctivitis and dermatitis, pseudopemphigoid and vortex keratopathy.1 This aligns with the DEWS III definition of DED as a symptomatic subset of OSD.35 At the country-level, most guidelines follow disease definitions from ADES and/or DEWS II. Disease definitions are generally consistent across Asia.

All Asian guidelines share two key diagnostic criteria for OSD/DED: 1) presence of ocular symptoms and 2) abnormal F-TBUT. Majority of DED frameworks, from Indonesia, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Thailand, Philippines, Singapore and South Korea, share a common 3-step pathway: presence of symptoms, confirmation with at least one objective clinical test (F-TBUT or ocular surface staining), followed by severity assessment.18–21,36,38,39 In contrast, the Philippine DED criteria from Siong et al are the simplest, requiring only a single positive result on OSDI, F-TBUT, Schirmer’s test or basal tear secretion test.37 There are also points of divergence across the guidelines. Symptom assessment may be done with different questionnaires (OSDI, DEQ-5, and SPEED being most frequently recommended), or without a questionnaire. The F-TBUT cut-off threshold also varies, ranging between <5 to <10 seconds across guidelines. Most country-level guidelines were also not specific to OSD/DED in glaucoma patients but patients in general, with the Taiwanese guidelines providing additional guidance for patients undergoing cataract surgery.38

For comparison with global guidelines, DEWS III uses a two-step approach of OSDI questionnaire plus either a tear film marker assessment (F-TBUT <5 seconds, or tear osmolarity) or ocular surface staining.35 The European Glaucoma Society Guidelines 6th edition, while not specifying diagnostic criteria, suggests identifying OSD through signs such as redness of the lid margin, corneal and conjunctival fluorescein staining, and reduced F-TBUT (no threshold specified).41

The variation in guidelines poses advantages and limitations. Less stringent criteria are easier for ophthalmologists to apply in their practice but may lead to overdiagnosis. For example, Siong et al reported that only 53.2% of patients with DED according to the Philippine criteria would be diagnosed based on ADES criteria.37 The presence of an objective test for signs is also important as symptoms may be discordant with signs. This is illustrated in a Taiwanese study by Wu et al in which glaucoma patients reported improving OSDI scores, despite showing increased ocular staining over time.42

Beyond medications, other recommendations include surgical treatment (eg, punctal occlusion, selective laser trabeculoplasty [SLT], minimally invasive glaucoma surgery [MIGS]), patient education, environmental modification to reduce aggravating factors, improving lid hygiene, and referral to specialists for severe/uncontrolled cases. Most guidelines recommend follow-up every 6 months. However, guidelines from Indonesia and Philippines suggest more frequent follow-up (at 4- and 12-weeks, and every 3–6 months, respectively) and the Thai framework proposes follow-up as needed according to the patient’s severity.18,19,21,39 Overall, existing guidelines across Asia recommend a wide variety of treatment and follow-up options. This may offer advantages in allowing for a flexible, tailored management plan. However, it may also be overwhelming for clinicians to quickly identify appropriate treatment options in busy clinic settings.

Expert Recommendations

Unmet Needs in OSD Management Within the Region

Despite the available guidelines, ophthalmologists in Asia still face various challenges in identifying and managing concomitant OSD in patients with glaucoma. First, there is a general lack of awareness of OSD within the region. This may be attributable to the lack of overarching regional guidelines on OSD, or the variation in disease terminology and diagnostic criteria. Ophthalmologists in Asia also often face heavy workloads in their daily clinics, limiting their time and capacity to screen for OSD. This could also contribute to OSD being overlooked during patient consultations as ophthalmologists may prioritize treating glaucoma over additional diagnostic tests for OSD.

Also, glaucoma management options that consider OSD (eg, PF topical medications, extended-release medications, drug delivery devices and surgical options) may not be readily available in every country. Local availability and reimbursement policies vary widely across Asia, and this may further propagate OSD development. For example, if PF medications need to be paid out-of-pocket, patients might prefer to use preserved medications, which in turn may exacerbate OSD symptoms.

A Practical Approach to Identifying and Managing Concomitant OSD in Asian Glaucoma Patients

To address the challenges in the region, eight glaucoma specialists and a cornea specialist from eight Asian countries (Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Taiwan, Thailand, Vietnam) convened and discussed strategies for OSD in daily practice. Subsequently, a simplified 3-step approach to screening and managing concomitant OSD was developed (Figure 1), to provide a harmonized guide that considers limitations faced by Asian ophthalmologists in daily practice. A 3-step practical approach for screening and managing OSD in glaucoma patients.

This 3-step practical approach is based on two overarching principles: Addressing the major contributing factors of OSD, generally by reducing preservative load and treating the symptoms of OSD with ocular lubricants and medications as needed. In brief, the guide involves two steps to diagnose OSD via 1) assessment of key symptoms and risk factors with simple observation and Q&A, and 2) slit-lamp examination with fluorescein staining (F-TBUT <5 seconds and/or ocular staining pattern; the exact F-TBUT cut-off can be adapted based on local clinical guidelines and practices), followed by 3) tailored management according to the severity of OSD. The third step provides options to reduce preservative load (for example, by using preservative-free medications, medications with lower BAK concentrations or fixed dose combinations) and tailor OSD management, including suggested follow-up frequency.

The 3-step approach can also be complemented by other management methods, such as patient education to recognize OSD symptoms, or environmental modification to reduce aggravating factors. Lastly, while reducing preservative exposure may benefit ocular surface health, PF formulations and alternative treatment options may not be readily available or affordable across all healthcare settings. Treatment decisions should therefore be individualized to each patient, considering clinical need, local availability of treatment options, reimbursement policies and patient preferences.

Of note, this practical guide reflects the authors’ expert opinions and clinical experience, and it was not developed with a formal consensus methodology or prospective clinical validation. In a preliminary assessment of the guide’s practical applicability, six general ophthalmologists from Hong Kong, Thailand and Singapore were invited to evaluate the 3-step approach in routine clinical practice. However, formal validation and further studies are warranted to validate these recommendations and evaluate their impact across different clinical settings.

Future Directions for OSD Management in Glaucoma Patients

Beyond the current recommendations, other methods of helping to manage issues associated with OSD may emerge in the future. For example, latanoprost delivered via a novel PF cationic eye drop emulsion has recently demonstrated dual benefits of effectively lowering IOP while also significantly reducing signs of OSD (as measured by CFS score), compared to preserved latanoprost.44 Alternative drug delivery devices, such as sustained release systems and intraocular implants, could help mitigate the need for topical administration. Such devices may reduce patients’ need for self-administration, thus improving adherence and QoL, and supplementing OSD management.26

As emphasized by existing guidelines, patient education of OSD is important for them to recognize symptoms early. This can be achieved through dissemination of patient education leaflets or public talks to improve awareness. In addition, the education of ophthalmologists is equally important. Information around OSD and its impact on glaucoma treatment can be integrated more formally into glaucoma training materials and events. This might be achieved by distributing educational materials to glaucoma clinics and local ophthalmology training boards across Asia. As severe OSD cases are often referred to specialists, interdisciplinary collaboration between ophthalmologists and cornea specialists would also be useful for enhancing disease management.

Lastly, the proposed 3-step practical approach could be a step towards a more refined, straightforward screening and management algorithm, which may facilitate AI integration. Machine learning and AI applications could potentially streamline OSD screening by identifying existing symptoms, signs and risk factors among patients with glaucoma.

Conclusions

To conclude, OSD can negatively impact glaucoma treatment outcomes, both as a tolerability issue and, in some patients, by affecting IOP control and surgical success. Effective management of OSD is crucial for preserving vision and QoL in patients with glaucoma. However, it is frequently overlooked in daily practice due to a lack of awareness and practical limitations. Despite the availability of various Asian guidelines for OSD, a standardized pathway for managing OSD would enhance the quality of care in Asian glaucoma patients.

The proposed Asian expert-led 3-step approach to screening and managing OSD in patients with glaucoma represents expert opinion intended to complement existing local guidelines, and may be adapted according to local clinical practice and regulations. Further consensus and awareness across ophthalmology disciplines in Asia would be valuable to support early identification and appropriate management of OSD.
