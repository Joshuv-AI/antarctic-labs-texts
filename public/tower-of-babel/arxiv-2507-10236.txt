Navigating the Challenges of AI-Generated Image Detection in the Wild: What Truly Matters? Journal: Pattern Recognition
Despina Konstantinidou, Dimitrios Karageorgiou, Christos Koutlis, Olga Papadopoulou, Emmanouil Schinas, Symeon Papadopoulos

Abstract
The rapid advancement of generative technologies presents both unprecedented creative opportunities and significant challenges, particularly in maintaining social trust and ensuring the integrity of digital information. Following these concerns, the challenge of AI-Generated Image Detection (AID) becomes increasingly critical. As these technologies become more sophisticated, the quality of AI-generated images has reached a level that can easily deceive even the most discerning observers. Our systematic evaluation highlights a critical weakness in current AI-Generated Image Detection models: while they perform exceptionally well on controlled benchmark datasets, they struggle significantly with real-world variations. To assess this, we introduce ITW-SM , a new dataset of real and AI-generated images collected from major social media platforms. In this paper, we identify four key factors that influence AID performance in real-world scenarios: backbone architecture, training data composition, pre-processing strategies and data augmentation combinations. By systematically analyzing these components, we shed light on their impact on detection efficacy. Our modifications result in an average AUC improvement of 26.87% across various AID models under real-world conditions.

1
Introduction

The rapid evolution of generative artificial intelligence has revolutionized digital media. These advancements enable the creation of photorealistic visual content on demand, using nothing more than a natural language description
[
25
]
. While such tools offer immense creative potential, they also pose significant risks, as they can be maliciously exploited to spread disinformation, facilitate impersonation, or enable fraudulent activities
[
43
]
. The high fidelity and realism of generated images make them nearly impossible to detect through visual inspection alone, as even the most discerning observer can be deceived
[
30
,
34
]
. Furthermore, the sheer volume of online content limits the feasibility of rigorous manual examination, highlighting the need for AI-Generated Image Detection (AID) approaches that can be robustly deployed in the wild.

Existing AID approaches typically fall into several categories: pixel-level detection methods, which operate directly on raw pixel data
[
44
,
15
,
7
,
13
]
, and fingerprint-based detection methods, which leverage statistical artifacts in the image’s frequency domain or reconstruction errors
[
2
,
14
,
28
]
. More recently, zero-shot detection has emerged as a new paradigm, offering the potential for more generalized and scalable solutions without requiring task-specific training
[
17
,
10
]
. While many AID methods exist, achieving robust performance in real-world scenarios remains a challenge, as most AID models perform exceptionally well on benchmark datasets
[
37
]
, which are typically generated in controlled environments, but catastrophically fail when unconditionally tested on content shared online
[
19
,
6
,
9
]
. This disparity in performance highlights the need for a systematic study of the factors influencing AID robustness in real-world scenarios. Through our analysis, we identified four critical factors that significantly affect the performance of detectors in-the-wild:

1.

Backbone architecture
: Maps input images
x
to a feature space through a function
f_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
, aiming to extract discriminative features that expose generative artifacts
[
2
]
. Its effectiveness can be quantified by the expected classification loss:

L_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\left[\ell\left(\mathcal{D}(f_{\mathcal{B}}(x)),y\right)\right],

where
\ell
is a classification loss function,
\mathcal{D}
is a decoder mapping features to the decision space,
y
is the true label and
\mathcal{X}
represents the dataset. Lower
L_{\mathcal{B}}
implies a more effective backbone for modeling
\mathcal{X}
.

2.

Training data
:
In AID, the challenge lies in ensuring that the training data reflects the diversity and complexity of real-world scenarios. If the training distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\text{train}}(x)
is derived from controlled, synthetic settings, it may diverge from the actual distribution encountered at deployment
\mathcal{P}_{\text{actual}}(x)
. This mismatch can be quantified using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence:

\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathcal{P}_{\text{actual}}(x)\,\|\,\mathcal{P}_{\text{train}}(x)\right),

where a high divergence indicates a substantial shift between the training and real-world data distributions.

3.

Cropping methods
: Pre-processing methods play a crucial role in ensuring that models can handle input data effectively, as most require a standard input size. With image resolutions increasing drastically and resizing no longer being a common approach—due to its tendency to erase subtle high-frequency traces left by the generation process—cropping becomes essential
[
22
,
20
]
. By segmenting the image into smaller regions, models can analyze these crops individually, enabling them to focus on localized details that may carry important information about the presence of synthetic artifacts.

4.

Data augmentations
: Data augmentation techniques are crucial for improving the robustness of models, as they simulate real-world variations. Data augmentation can be seen as a set of transformations
\mathcal{T}
applied to the training data
D
, where each transformation
t\in\mathcal{T}
maps an image
I\in D
to a new image
t(I)
. By introducing these perturbations, the model is exposed to a wider range of potential inputs, improving its generalization capabilities.

Our study systematically evaluates these components to identify optimal strategies for improving AID robustness in the wild. Each of these factors contribute to the overall expected error in AI-Generated Image Detection, which can be expressed as
E_{\text{AID}}\sim g(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
, where g is a function that models their interactions. To evaluate under realistic conditions and address gaps in existing resources, we introduce
ITW-SM
, a new in-the-wild test dataset of 10,000 real and generated images collected from major social media platforms. Using both benchmarks and ITW-SM, we provide a structured evaluation to clarify why current methods fail in the wild and how to improve them. Our contributions include::

1.

A systematic experimental evaluation that reveals the strengths and weaknesses of various AID approaches under real-world conditions.

2.

A comprehensive analysis of the impact of backbone architecture, training data, cropping strategies, and data augmentation on AID performance in the wild.

3.

The introduction of
ITW-SM
, a new in-the-wild AID benchmark dataset collected from four major social media platforms, designed to support evaluation under realistic and unconstrained conditions.

4.

A set of recommendations for designing more robust AID models capable of handling in-the-wild variations, derived from our systematic evaluation of the four key factors.

2
Related Work

The state-of-the-art methods for AI-Generated Image Detection can be categorized into five fundamental categories. These categories encompass the key approach utilized for detecting synthetic images and are the following: End-to-End Supervised, Clip-based, Heuristic-based, Reconstruction and Zero-Shot methods.

End-to-End Supervised models are trained on labeled datasets, allowing them to learn distinguishing features between real and synthetic images through supervised learning techniques. They form the foundation of AI-Generated Image Detection. In
[
44
]
a pre-trained ResNet50
[
16
]
is trained on 20 different object classes of real images from LSUN
[
47
]
and ProGAN
[
21
]
images. To better capture generative artifacts, some methods focus on local image patches
[
3
]
or combine local and global feature analysis
[
18
]
. Another approach avoids downsampling in the early network layers to better preserve generative inconsistencies
[
15
]
. Building on this,
[
7
]
fuse networks trained separately on GAN- and diffusion-based images to enhance generalization.

Clip-based models have gained significant traction in recent years. They utilize features extracted from foundational models like the Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training (CLIP) model
[
35
]
, which has proven highly effective in distinguishing real from generated images. Ohja et al.
[
32
]
use the same training protocol as in
[
44
]
, but employ CLIP, as a feature extractor instead of training a ResNet50. Another approach
[
39
]
integrates image captions for joint analysis during training, leveraging CLIP’s pre-trained visual and textual embeddings to improve detection of images from text-to-image diffusion models. The RINE method
[
23
]
further advances CLIP-based detection by extracting features not only from the final layer of CLIP’s image encoder but also from intermediate layers.

Heuristic-based models employ predefined rules and techniques based on the properties of images to detect synthetic content. These models incorporate strong inductive biases that exploit known discrepancies between real and synthetic images.
In
[
41
]
, Learning on Gradients (LGrad) is introduced that leverages gradients from pretrained CNNs as artifact representations for classification. The work in
[
40
]
explores AI-Generated Image Detection through neighboring pixel relationships. Pixel dependencies introduced by upsampling in GANs and variational autoencoders (VAEs) are used to detect synthetic content. A more recent work,
[
46
]
, proposes AIDE (AI-generated Image DEtector with Hybrid Features), which consists of a module for computing low-level statistics from texture and smooth patches, a high-level semantic embedding module, and a discriminator to classify whether an image is generated or real.

Reconstruction models, focused on reconstructing images, identify discrepancies and artifacts that may indicate whether an image is AI-generated. By comparing the original and reconstructed images, they can highlight areas where generative artifacts are present.
Diffusion Reconstruction Error (DIRE)
[
45
]
measures the discrepancy between an input image and its reconstructed version generated by a pre-trained ablated diffusion model (ADM)
[
12
]
, utilizing this differences to train a ResNet50 classifier. In contrast, AEROBLADE
[
36
]
avoids classifier training and directly uses reconstruction errors from a latent diffusion model’s (LDM) autoencoder (AE), noting that synthetic images reconstruct more accurately. More recently SPAI
[
20
]
introduces a spectral detection method, learning the spectral distribution of real images in a self-supervised manner and identifying generative artifacts via reconstruction similarity.

Zero-shot models have the capability to detect synthetic images without prior training on synthetic data, by relying only on real images and learning their underlying distribution. There are only a few recent methods that avoid training on synthetic data altogether. In
[
17
]
, the authors introduce RIGID, a training-free, model-agnostic method based on the observation that real images are more robust to small perturbations in the embedding space of large pre-trained models. Inspired by advancements in machine-generated text detection, ZED
[
10
]
, estimates how "surprising" an image is using a lossless image encoder to model pixel-wise probabilities.

While the aforementioned works have significantly advanced the field of AI-Generated Image Detection, a growing body of research has also investigated the robustness of these methods to various perturbations
[
7
,
6
,
37
]
. These studies often reveal a significant drop in detection accuracy when models trained on unprocessed synthetic data are evaluated on images with real-world degradations, such as resizing and compression. A key observation is that certain perturbations affect synthetic images from the training distribution less severely, indicating that the learned detection features might be overly specific to the artifacts of those particular generation processes and less effective against real-world distortions.

3
Methodology

We define a structured experimental framework that varies four key components: the backbone architecture used for feature extraction, the composition of the training data, the cropping strategy employed during pre-processing, and the data augmentations applied during training. By varying one factor at a time while keeping others fixed, we assess the individual effects on AID performance across both benchmark and in-the-wild datasets.

3.1
Backbone Architectures for AID

The backbone of a AI-Generated Image Detection model plays a crucial role in feature extraction, influencing its ability to detect subtle synthetic artifacts. Traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as ResNet
[
16
]
and EfficientNet
[
42
]
, have been widely used
[
8
,
15
,
7
,
18
,
31
,
13
]
due to their strong spatial feature extraction capabilities. More recently, numerous methods
[
1
,
32
,
9
,
23
]
have relied on foundational models, such as CLIP
[
35
]
, as they have demonstrated superior performance in capturing both low-level details and high-level semantic patterns, making them promising for the AID task. Our goal is to assess how well each architecture captures synthetic artifacts and whether they can generalize to in-the-wild scenarios, where images show variations in lighting, resolution, and post-processing artifacts.

3.2
The Role of Training Data Diversity in AID Generalization

The composition of training datasets significantly affects the robustness of AID models. Most studies predominantly rely on datasets composed of synthetic images generated in controlled environments, often from a limited number of generative models, which do not accurately reflect the complexity of real-world online media. Moreover, training set diversity in terms of both generative models and semantics has been found to significantly improve a model’s ability to generalize to previously unseen generative architectures
[
44
,
7
]
. Furthermore,
[
44
]
indicates that while larger and more diverse datasets improve detection rates, the benefits plateau beyond a certain threshold. Additionally, it has been established that maintaining a balanced distribution of real and synthetic images is crucial for preventing biases that could hinder generalization. By training models on different datasets encompassing both benchmark and in the wild data, we evaluate how training set diversity affects generalization.

3.3
Cropping Strategies and their Impact on AID

Cropping strategies play a vital role in AID by directing models to focus on the most informative regions of an image. Earlier works relied on resizing in order to adapt images to fixed input layers. However,
[
7
]
, emphasizes that resizing should be avoided, as it involves image resampling and interpolation, which may erase the subtle high-frequency traces left by the generation process. Currently, the most commonly used cropping methods in AID are center cropping and random cropping. Center cropping selects a fixed-size region from the middle of the image, while random cropping extracts a randomly chosen region.
Another approach that has been explored for inference in AID is ten cropping, which was used in
[
23
]
. This method selects the center, four corners, and their horizontal flips to enhance inference performance.
Finally, texture-based cropping, introduced in
[
22
]
, targets high-frequency regions such as edges and fine textures, where generative models often introduce artifacts, and outperforms resizing, center cropping, and ten cropping across multiple detectors in the AID task.
We systematically evaluate each cropping method under a consistent training pipeline to isolate its effect on model accuracy and robustness.

3.4
Data Augmentations for Real-World Robustness

Data augmentations are widely used to simulate real-world distortions and improve AID model generalization. The most common augmentations in AID include compression-based augmentations, like JPEG and WEBP compression that are applied to introduce artifacts commonly found in online images. Geometric transformations such as random cropping, rotating, and flipping are applied to ensure robustness to variations in framing and composition. Noise and filtering techniques, like adding Gaussian noise, blur, and sharpening improve resilience to environmental and post-processing distortions. Following prior findings, that support the tight relation between augmentation and detectors’ reliability
[
44
,
31
]
, we evaluate whether augmentations improve robustness also when the images tested are heavily processed images retrieved in-the-wild.

4.2
Collection Procedure

Data collection was performed using a custom crawler that respects the terms of service of each platform. For each platform real content was scraped from verified accounts to ensure authenticity. Synthetic content was scraped from user accounts and community pages dedicated to sharing AI-generated visuals. All images were saved in their original resolution, preserving native compression artifacts. A multi-stage filtering pipeline was employed to ensure quality and consistency. First, images with heavy text overlays, watermarks, or non-photographic content (e.g., memes, screenshots) were removed. Then duplicates were eliminated using similarity scores and all samples were manually reviewed to verify label correctness.

5
Experimental Setup

5.1
Datasets

We use two datasets for re-training our models, a dataset generated under lab-controlled conditions and a dataset of images collected in the wild. For the lab-controlled dataset, we used the LDM Training Dataset introduced in
[
7
]
, while for the in the wild data we relied on the TWIGMA dataset
[
4
]
. This choice was motivated by the limitations of earlier datasets like ProGAN
[
44
]
, which contain low resolution images generated using an outdated architecture. Although widely used in earlier AID work, models trained on ProGAN data have been shown to struggle with generalizing to more recent, high-fidelity generators. To better reflect the current landscape of generative models, we use the LDM dataset, which includes images generated by a more recent Latent Diffusion model, providing a more relevant training distribution. Additionally, TWIGMA is, to the best of our knowledge, the only large-scale dataset composed of in-the-wild synthetic images collected from the web. Its inclusion allows our models to better learn features that generalize to real-world scenarios, which are often underrepresented in clean, benchmark-style datasets.

For evaluation, we assess our methods on images from three datasets: Synthbuster
[
2
]
, a benchmark dataset containing synthetic and real images, Chameleon
[
46
]
, an in-the-wild dataset of AI-generated and real images and our own In The Wild Social Media (ITW-SM) dataset, curated from a wide variety of social media platforms to capture diverse real-world image distributions. The Synthbuster dataset contains 1000 AI-generated images from each one of 9 different models and 1,000 uncompressed and unprocessed real images from the RAISE dataset
[
11
]
. The Chameleon dataset consists of more than 11K high-fidelity AI-generated images and more that 14K real images, from platforms and online communities providing high-quality, free-to-use images contributed by photographers worldwide. In The Wild Social Media (ITW-SM) is a dataset we created that contains 10,000 images (balanced between real and generated), sourced from four social media platforms: Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, and X. By incorporating both benchmark datasets and real-world, in-the-wild data, we aim to bridge the gap between controlled experimental setups and real-world conditions.

5.2
AID Detectors

For our experiments, we utilize one representative model from each of the End-to-End Supervised, Clip-based, Heuristic-based, and Reconstruction categories. However, we do not include a model from the Zero-Shot category, as our focus is on improvements that can be made in the training process, which is closely tied to the training data.

1.

DMID

[
7
]
, which—as mentioned before—fuses the logits of two independently trained models, one originally trained on GAN-generated images and the other on diffusion-generated images. Each model is a ResNet50 with intense augmentation that avoids down-sampling in the first layer.

2.

RINE

[
23
]
, that leverages image representations extracted from intermediate Transformer blocks of CLIP’s image-encoder to retain more fine-grained visual information. A lightweight network then maps these features into a forgery-aware vector space that generalizes well across datasets. By incorporating a trainable module to weight the importance of each Transformer block, the detection of synthetic images is improved.

3.

NPR

[
40
]
, that relies on artifacts introduced by the up-scaling layers of the generative models for detection. The proposed Neighboring Pixel Relationships are employed to train detector as artifact representation and focus on the local interdependence between image pixels caused by up-sampling operators.

4.

SPAI

[
20
]
, that employs masked spectral learning with the pretext task of frequency reconstruction. As generated images fall outside the distribution expected by this model, spectral reconstruction similarity is employed to detect these discrepancies, while spectral context attention is also introduced in order to effectively capture subtle spectral inconsistencies in images of varying resolutions.

5.3
Evaluation Protocol

To ensure a fair comparison of different AID methods, we retrained each model following the training details provided in their respective papers. This process required significant computational resources, as each model was retrained under the specified conditions to guarantee consistency and accuracy in the comparison. In total, our experiments resulted in more than 1000 GPU hours invested, ensuring that the analysis was as rigorous and exhaustive as possible. The scale of this effort underscores the resource-intensive nature of evaluating AID techniques and serves to highlight the challenges associated with conducting such a thorough evaluation.

While we adhered to the original training strategies, we made controlled adjustments to the backbones (when applicable), datasets, preprocessing methods, and augmentations to align with our experimental setup. To ensure computational feasibility across all methods, we applied similar resource constraints during training. This adjustment was necessary to balance feasibility with experimental consistency while still enabling a meaningful performance evaluation.

6.1
Backbone

Recognizing the significance of selecting effective foundation models for AID tasks, we conducted an ablation study centered on the CLIP-based RINE model trained on LDM data. We selected RINE because it exemplifies a representative and competitive approach within the recent generation of AID methods, leveraging powerful pre-trained vision-language models to detect synthetic content.
In our study, we replaced the CLIP L/14 backbone
[
35
]
in RINE with various alternative image encoders to systematically assess the impact of backbone choice on detection performance.
It is important to note that the backbone is characterized by three main components: the architecture itself, the pretraining objective, and the dataset used during pretraining. In this ablation, we primarily focus on Vision Transformer (ViT)-based architectures, but the key variations lie in the pretraining objectives and the diversity of the pretraining datasets. The encoders tested were:

1.

OpenCLIP’s CLIP L/14

[
5
]

This open-source implementation of CLIP is designed to provide greater flexibility and transparency in large-scale vision-language modeling. OpenCLIP L/14 is trained on the LAION-2B dataset
[
38
]
and also extends the CLIP architecture with improvements in training.

2.

BLIP2

[
26
]
: This model integrates frozen pre-trained image encoders with large language models (LLMs) by employing a lightweight 12-layer Transformer encoder in between, trained on a 129M image dataset introduced in
[
27
]
, achieving state-of-the-art results on various vision-language tasks.

3.

CLIP H/14

[
5
]
: This variant of the CLIP model employs advanced scaling techniques to enhance performance across different applications. It is a larger and more powerful version of CLIP L/14 trained also on the LAION-2B dataset
[
38
]
.

4.

DINO-V2-L/14

[
33
]
: This image encoder is pretrained on large, curated datasets without supervision. It incorporates an optimized training recipe, increased model scale, and a larger curated dataset, LVD-142M
[
33
]
, along with a distillation process that enables smaller models to benefit from the capabilities of the most powerful ViT architecture.

With the rising resolution of modern AI-generated images, naive cropping (e.g., center crop) risks discarding key artifacts. Alternatives like ten-crop and texture-based cropping better preserve detail but increase training time nearly tenfold. Inspired by the promising results of
[
22
]
, we adopt TextureCrop during training, randomly selecting one of the 10 crops per image to reduce overhead. It is important to note that the SPAI model is not included in this analysis, as it operates on patches of the original image rather than a single crop.

Based on our results, texture cropping appears to significantly boost performance compared to center cropping on the DMID and RINE methods, as the images used for training are no longer 256x256 (due to the TWIGMA dataset). Texture cropping appears to be more effective as it targets specific areas with the most texture information, capturing generative artifacts without the randomness associated with standard cropping methods. However, this is not always the case, as we can see in the NPR method, where center cropping seems to perform better than texture cropping in most cases.

By artificially expanding the diversity of the training dataset, augmentation techniques help the model recognize generative artifacts across varying conditions and scenarios. 7
Conclusion

Our study highlights the critical challenges that AI-Generated Image Detection (AID) models face in real-world applications and identifies key factors that influence detection performance. By analyzing the role of backbone architectures, training data composition, cropping methods, and data augmentations, we provide actionable insights for improving AID robustness. The findings demonstrate the need for more adaptive detection techniques that account for real-world variations, ensuring that AID methods remain effective in practical applications. Addressing these issues will enhance the ability of AID models to discern synthetic from real images more accurately, reducing the risks posed by AI-generated media. An analysis like this should be conducted on any new model that aims to achieve good performance in the wild, as different models exhibit different behaviors and may require tailored strategies for optimal detection.
