Protocol for conditional mapping of proximal interactomes of dimeric protein complexes using a BioID-based complementation approach
Authors: Abishankari Rajkumar, Antoine Gaudreau-Lapierre, Curtis LF Anthony, Laura Trinkle-Mulcahy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we provide a protocol for conditional mapping of proximal interactomes of dimeric protein complexes using a BioID-based complementation approach. We describe steps for fusing amino- and carboxyl-terminal fragments of the biotin ligase TurboID to proteins known to interact within cells. This interaction drives re-formation of an active ligase and biotinylation of nearby proteins. We then detail procedures for capturing biotinylated proteins on a streptavidin affinity matrix for identification by mass spectrometry and Python-based scripts for identifying high-confidence proximal interactors.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Rajkumar et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cell-based Assays, Proteomics

Before you begin

This protocol provides a detailed workflow for performing splitTurboID conditional proximity labeling experiments using cultured human cells. Originally designed to map the conditional interactomes of SUMOylated proteins,2 we recently extended the approach to map the conditional interactomes of other dimeric protein complexes.1 In the original SUMO-ID approach, the group identified a split site within the TurboID biotin ligase (T194/G195) that results in fragments of a roughly equivalent size (22 and 15 kDa) that are catalytically inactive on their own but re-form an active ligase when brought into close proximity by the interaction of proteins to which they are fused.

We applied this approach to analyze the large panel of catalytic-regulatory subunit pairs that govern the subcellular localization and activity of the PP1 serine/threonine protein phosphatase.1 As proof-of-concept, we mapped conditional interactomes for holophosphatase complexes with both distinct and overlapping subcellular localization patterns and demonstrated the reliability and high spatial resolution of the technique. We also developed an analysis pipeline that overlaps datasets to highlight unique and enriched near neighbours for individual holophosphatases above shared common contaminants. Gene ontology analysis confirmed enrichment of biological processes and molecular functions consistent with their subcellular localization and previously described roles in the cell, and known interactors and substrates were identified. We also confirmed the general applicability of splitTurboID to other types of protein-protein pairs, such as the TRIM28-HP1ɣ interaction involved in epigenetic silencing1

Innovation

A challenge in identifying functionally relevant interactions for PP1 holophosphases by mapping the interactomes of the subunits on their own is that each can play roles in the cell that are independent of their association. We demonstrated the ability of splitTurboID-based conditional mapping to distinguish PP1-dependent and -independent associations for the regulatory subunit c20orf27/ADISSP.1 Indeed, several hundred proteins have now been identified as “moonlighting proteins”, meaning that they perform two or more distinct functions within the cell.3 This can complicate their analysis by classic immunoprecipitation/mass spectrometry (IP/MS) and BioID approaches. As we’ve shown, a complementation-based splitTurboID approach provides an additional layer of specificity for proximal interactome mapping of protein-protein complexes.

Here, we walk through our standard workflow for a splitTurboID experiment from cloning through initial small-scale testing and optimization in cells to a larger-scale affinity purification/mass spectrometry (AP/MS) experiment and data analysis (Figure 1). All the steps are readily accessible to any cell biology laboratory with access to a fee-for-service proteomic facility. That said, there are experimental factors that should be considered before beginning this protocol. Below, we describe these experimental considerations in greater detail.

Cloning genes into splitTurboID plasmids

Timing: 1 week

1.Choose fusion strategy.

Choose fusion strategy.

Note: Our in-house splitTurboID mammalian expression vectors (available through Addgene) support fusion of Flag-TurboID NTD (N-terminal aa1-194) or myc-TurboID CTD (C-terminal aa 195-320) to either the N-terminus (C1 frame plasmids) or C-terminus (N3 frame plasmids) of the protein of interest.

Note: As with any fusion protein strategy, the tag and/or its position has the potential to impact protein localization and function. We’ve found that replicating the fusion strategy (tag placement, linker length) that works for fusion of a protein to popular fluorophore tags like EGFP and mCherry also works for splitTurboID fragments, in that the fusion proteins localize correctly and associate with known interactors.

Formaldehyde fixative solution (4%)

Heat 60 ml of PBS to 60°C on a stir plate in a fume hood. Add 4 g of PFA powder while stirring and then add 1N NaOH dropwise until the solution clears.

Let cool, adjust pH to 7.4 and add ddH2O to a final volume of 100 mL. Filter sterilize through a 0.22 μm filter.

Note: Can store in the dark at 4°C for up to one week, or in aliquots at −20°C for up to 3 months.

Polyethylenimine solution (1 mg/ml)

Add 100 mg of PEI 25K™ to 90 ml ddH2O and stir to dissolve, while adding HCl to pH 7.0. Add ddH2O to bring the final volume to 100 ml and filter sterilize through a 0.22 μm filter.

Note: We test the efficiency of a new batch by transfecting pEGFP into HEK293 cells and confirming fluorescence in >70% of cells.

Note: Can store in 1 ml aliquots at −80°C for several years. Thawed aliquots can be stored at 4°C for up to 3 months.

Biotin stock solution (50 mM)

Note: Can prepare in advance and store at 4°C for several months.

Step-by-step method details

Assessment of biotinylation in fixed cells by streptavidin-fluorophore staining

Timing: 3 days

These steps describe how to assess the subcellular localization of proteins biotinylated by a splitTurboID protein-protein pair expressed in HEK293 cells. This staining also assesses the efficiency of plasmid co-transfection and ligase re-formation, reporting the percentage of cells with a detectable biotinylation signal above background staining (see Figure 2A).

Expected outcomes

Provided the co-expression of the splitTurboID-tagged interaction partners is sufficient to support reformation of the ligase and a biotinylation signal above background in at least 40% of cells, the AP/MS workflow should identify proximal interactors with high sensitivity and spatial resolution. We have successfully applied this technique to the analysis of multiple dimeric PP1 complexes that localize to different regions throughout the cell and demonstrated its applicability to other types of dimeric protein-protein complexes.1

Overlapping a splitTurboID dataset with control datasets can increase confidence in the validity of the proximal interactors identified as unique or enriched, while overlapping splitTurboID datasets for different protein pairs, particularly those found within the same cellular compartment, can further increase confidence in the specificity of proximal interactors detected for each pair.

Here, we utilize the workflow to compare a splitPP1:RRP1B dataset to two different ligase-only controls (TurboID and miniTurboID; Figure 3C and Supplemental Data File). Previously published interactors related to ribosome biogenesis5 were detected as unique to or enriched in the splitPP1:RRP1B dataset when it was overlapped with the ligase only controls (Figures 3C and 3D), and also when it was overlapped with datasets for other nuclear (splitPP1:NIPP1), cytoplasmic (splitPP1:TPRN) and nucleolar (splitPP1:NOM1) holophosphatases (Figure 3D).

Limitations

SplitTurboID relies on well-matched expression levels for both fusion proteins and the ability of the cells to tolerate this expression. As noted above, some protein pairs may not express well by co-transfection, necessitating alternate approaches such as lentiviral delivery (standard, or utilizing bidirectional promoters or bicistronic expression), inducible systems and/or establishment of stably expressing cell lines.

We utilize HEK293 cells for the majority of our splitTurboID experiments, given their amenability to plasmid delivery and high levels of protein expression. While this may preclude identification of cell type-specific interactions, the assumption is that key functional roles and hence associations are conserved and can be validated by follow-up analysis in more relevant cell lines. We have also carried out splitTurboID experiments in U2-OS, HeLa, HCT116 and RPE-1 cell lines.

Although our gel-based fractionation strategy significantly increases proteomic depth, caveats include the increased cost per experiment (5 MS runs vs 1) and the increased coverage of environmental contaminants such as keratins due to additional sample manipulation.

In standard BioID experiments, the ligase alone is normally used as a negative control. This is more complicated than an IgG control for a classic IP/MS experiment, as the free ligase usually expresses at a much higher level. For splitTurboID, we’ve found that the re-formed ligase is less active than full-length TurboID1 (Figure 2D). It remains unclear whether this is due to inefficient recombination of the two halves of the ligase, poorly matched levels of each half or other factors. We use a 4 hr labeling time for our splitTurboID experiments, achieving signals more comparable to that of 1 hr TurboID or 4 hr miniTurboID labeling1 (Figure 2D). We thus recommend that if TurboID alone is used as a control, labeling is limited to 1 hr.

For our analyses of different PP1 holoenzyme complexes, often localized to the same subcellular compartment, we overlap the datasets directly. This highlights shared contaminants that cluster in the middle, and unique and high-confidence hits with a higher proportional representation in a specific dataset. In the case of two PP1 holophosphatases (PP1:RRP1B and PP1:NOM1) that accumulate in similar localization patterns within the nucleolus, for example, this overlap revealed not only a shared role in ribosome biogenesis, but distinct roles in large vs small ribosome subunit biogenesis, respectively.1

Validating hits identified in a proximity labeling experiment is challenging, as the technique captures transient and low affinity interactions that may not be retained in a classic co-immunoprecipitation assay. We suggest an initial splitTurboID AP/western blot-based confirmation of enrichment of a specific protein in the dataset (particularly if it was identified by MS with low peptide coverage), but this should always be followed by complementary validation experiments that can capture the interaction in vivo, such as Proximity Ligation Assays (PLA) and Fluorescence/Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET).

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The subcellular biotinylation pattern of a splitTurboID protein pair, as assessed by streptavidin-fluorophore cell staining in steps 3-5 does not match the known (or expected) localization pattern of the complex.

Degradation, tag placement and/or linker length can impact proper localization of the fusion proteins, as can overexpression itself. For example, we previously demonstrated that overexpression of the microtubule- and actin-binding protein SPECC1L, which can regulate localization of the PP1:MYPT1 holophosphatase, induces a microtubule bundling phenotype.4 When we co-express CTD-tagged SPECC1L and NTD-tagged MYPT1 in a splitTurboID experiment, the biotinylation pattern reflects that overexpression phenotype (predominantly accumulated at bundled microtubules). This can help us study that phenotype but does not accurately reflect the distribution of the complex at endogenous levels.

Problem 4

Initial testing confirmed a strong biotinylation signal in the cells (by streptavidin-fluorophore staining in steps 3–5 and streptavidin-HRP western blotting of lysates in steps 6–11) but few proteins are identified by MS analysis.

Although our AP protocol typically results in depletion of >90% of biotinylated proteins from the lysate, we confirm this for every experiment by subjecting the input and flowthrough samples to western blot analysis with streptavidin-HRP (as per steps 8-11). If <50% of the total biotinylated proteins in the lysate are depleted, the resulting MS dataset may be sub-optimal. Note that if the in-gel digestion strategy is followed, the amount of Coomassie-stained protein detectable in the eluate lane also serves as an indicator of capture/elution efficiency (see Figure 2C for an example of an eluate lane for which >1000 proteins were identified by MS).

Potential solution

•If using a different affinity matrix than the one noted here, you will need to test and determine the conditions that result in the best depletion efficiency, as they may differ.

•If depletion efficiency is high, the issue could be inefficient elution of biotinylated proteins from the beads. This can be tested by using a highly stringent denaturing method to elute any proteins remaining on the beads for western blot analysis with streptavidin-HRP. After elution (step 22) add a bead equivalent volume of 8M urea, vortex, spin 3 s in a mini-centrifuge to ensure no beads are stuck to the sides of the tube and incubate at 95°C for 10 min. Vortex again, spin 3 s in a mini-centrifuge and transfer the liquid to a new tube containing 5 μl of 4x LDS (or SDS) sample buffer. If streptavidin-HRP western blot analysis detects a significant amount of protein that had been left on the beads following the initial SDS/biotin elution, this step may need to be further optimized (e.g., test increased biotin and/or SDS in the elution buffer, or different affinity matrices).
