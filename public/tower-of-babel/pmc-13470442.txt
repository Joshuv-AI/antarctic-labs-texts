XGBoost-based early warning framework for farm-level PED risk using meteorological and herd management variables
Authors: Ran Guan, Rui Xu, Chaoyun Yang, Leilei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPorcine epidemic diarrhea (PED) imposes substantial economic losses on the global swine industry, with outbreaks driven by the complex interplay of meteorological conditions and herd management practices. A farm-level risk prediction framework was developed by integrating meteorological and reproductive performance variables from commercial pig farms in southern China. A triangulated variable selection strategy—combining hierarchical clustering analysis, variance inflation factor filtering, and principal-component analysis loading evaluation—reduced 22 meteorological candidate predictors to 14 consensus variables. Class imbalance was addressed through inverse-proportion class weighting. Among five evaluated classifiers, XGBoost achieved the most balanced predictive performance. Feature importance analysis identified seasonal periodicity and reproductive performance indicators, particularly pre-weaning mortality rate, as primary risk determinants. These findings support the incorporation of multimodal farm-level data into PED surveillance systems and provide a quantitative basis for precision biosecurity management.Subject areas: Computer science, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Introduction

Porcine epidemic diarrhea (PED) is an acute, highly contagious enteric infectious disease caused by porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV). Its principal clinical manifestations include profuse watery diarrhea, vomiting, and dehydration, and the case fatality rate in suckling piglets may reach as high as 100%.1,2 PEDV belongs to the genus Alphacoronavirus within the family Coronaviridae. Its single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genome is approximately 28 kb in length and is characterized by substantial genetic plasticity, rendering the virus highly prone to mutation and recombination. This, in turn, drives continuous antigenic drift among circulating strains and poses significant challenges to vaccine development and disease prevention and control.3 Since PED was first reported in the United Kingdom in 1971, outbreaks have successively occurred in major swine-producing countries and regions across Europe, Asia, and the Americas, making PED one of the most consequential infectious diseases affecting the global swine industry.4 Around 2010, highly pathogenic variant strains (G2 PEDV) emerged in southern China. Compared with classical strains, these variants exhibited markedly enhanced transmissibility and virulence, resulting in the deaths of more than one million neonatal piglets.5 More recently, large-scale surveillance conducted across 256 intensive pig farms in China during 2022–2023 revealed PEDV sample-level and herd-level positivity rates of 11.78% and 28.45%, respectively, indicating that sustained viral circulation pressure in intensive production systems remains severe.6

The occurrence and spread of PED are jointly driven by multiple biological and environmental factors. From the perspective of viral ecology, differences in PEDV sensitivity to low temperature, low relative humidity, and ultraviolet radiation constitute key determinants of environmental persistence, aerosol transmission efficiency, and viral stability within the swine house microenvironment.7,8 Previous epidemiological studies have demonstrated pronounced seasonal clustering of PED outbreaks, with the relative risk during winter and spring (January to March) exceeding that of other seasons by more than 2.2-fold.9 Meteorological variables, including ambient temperature, wind speed, relative humidity, and precipitation, regulate PEDV transmission dynamics through multiple pathways, such as influencing viral aerosol stability, the effectiveness of inter-farm biosecurity barriers (e.g., contamination-vector efficiency associated with transport vehicles and personnel), and the mucosal immune status of the host.10,11 At the same time, farm production and management factors-including pre-weaning mortality, levels of maternally derived antibodies, pen vacancy duration, and designed herd capacity-also play a non-negligible systemic role in shaping outbreak risk by altering the immunological niche of susceptible host populations.12 However, the complex nonlinear interactions between meteorological variables and production management factors impose inherent methodological limitations on disease risk assessment frameworks based on single variables or linear statistical models, which are unable to accurately capture the synergistic effects among multidimensional drivers.

Commercial pig farms typically have their own internal data management systems, which perform tasks such as information collection, processing, analysis, and distribution.13 Proper management of the data generated daily in the livestock industry contributes to increased productivity in aspects, such as farm management, livestock nutrition, and animal health.14 Conducting routine pathogen monitoring on commercial farms, or even continuous remote monitoring of physiological parameters, may be useful tools for detecting signs of disease. Early detection of these abnormal changes can enhance the management of major swine diseases, improve treatment success rates, and minimize economic losses caused by diseases.15,16 However, large-scale clinical and serological monitoring is labor-intensive and costly. Monitoring related to animal health often requires sensor equipment support, such as cameras (2D and 3D), microphones, thermistors, and accelerometers.17 Researchers are increasingly focusing on alternative methods for detection models, utilizing prior probabilities or risks of disease occurrence. For instance, naive Bayes networks have shown the greatest potential in reducing alerts based on sensor-based detection models in livestock production.18

In recent years, the rapid development of machine learning (ML) methods has introduced a new methodological paradigm for risk prediction in animal infectious diseases. Ensemble learning algorithms represented by random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) have demonstrated substantial methodological advantages in handling high-dimensional features, capturing nonlinear interaction effects, and addressing class-imbalanced data.19 Machado20 integrated RF with meteorological, geospatial, and animal movement network data to establish a PEDV outbreak prediction model based on farm neighborhood effects, achieving a receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) of around 0.82 on an independent test set. This study provided preliminary evidence for the feasibility and scientific validity of combining ML with multisource environmental data for PED risk prediction.20 Nevertheless, important methodological gaps remain in the existing literature. First, meteorological variable selection has generally relied on a single dimensionality-reduction approach, lacking a multi-method triangulation framework to systematically mitigate variable selection bias. Second, strategies for handling extreme class imbalance (positivity rate <1%) during model development have not been systematically evaluated or compared. Third, a multimodal feature-fusion framework integrating meteorological data with farm reproductive and production management data remains absent, thereby limiting the ability of current models to characterize multidimensional interactive risks under real-world farm conditions.

In light of these research gaps, the present study aims to develop a predictive framework for dynamic early warning of farm-level PED risk that combines methodological innovation with practical applicability, thereby providing an evidence-based basis for the formulation of precision prevention and control strategies.

Results

Results of data preprocessing

Basic characteristics of the dataset

The original dataset comprised 27,774 observations. Following data quality control, one perfectly collinear variable (Dif = T2M_max - T2M_min) was identified and removed, leaving 22 meteorological variables for analysis. Among the 27,774 records, 1,711 (6.16%) contained missing values in one or more variables. After listwise deletion, 26,063 complete observations were retained for subsequent analyses.

Multi-method triangulation-based screening of meteorological variables

To reduce selection bias associated with any single screening approach, 22 meteorological variables were evaluated using a triangulation framework integrating hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), stepwise variance inflation factor (VIF) screening, and PCA-based contribution assessment. The cumulative frequency with which each variable was selected across the independent methods was used as a quantitative indicator of consensus evidence strength. Variables selected by at least two methods were defined as consensus variables and retained for downstream modeling. The screening results are summarized in Table 1.

Missing value imputation performance

Characteristics of the missingness distribution

The final analytical dataset contained 45 variables, 22 of which exhibited varying degrees of missingness, accounting for 48.9% of all variables. The overall missingness rate was 12.09%, corresponding to 151,134 missing cells. Missingness rates were highly right-skewed across variables (Figure 4A), indicating substantial heterogeneity in the extent of missingness.

Six variables exhibited high missingness rates (≥30%). Among these, PDCoV_detection_rate_pct had the highest missingness rate at 73.78%. PED-related detection-rate variables at the herd, barn, and vehicle levels (PED_detection_rate_herd/barn/vehicle_pct) each had a missingness rate of 35.32%. Num_retained_weaned_piglets and mortality_prop_watery_diarrhea_pct showed missingness rates of 41.45% and 74.50%, respectively. By contrast, 13 variables exhibited low missingness rates (<5%). Among them, sow herd design capacity, production cycle, and farrowing achievement rate each had missingness rates below 0.5%, indicating high data completeness.

Selection of optimal imputation methods

For each variable containing missing values, the optimal imputation algorithm was automatically selected from 6 to 8 candidate methods using a cross-validation framework (Figure 4B). On average, seven methods were evaluated per variable. Ultimately, 123,360 missing cells were successfully imputed, corresponding to an overall imputation completion rate of 81.62%.

Among the 22 variables with missing values, k-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation was the dominant optimal method, being selected for 8 variables (38.1%), with a mean NRMSE of 0.082, indicating the best overall performance. Stratified mean and stratified median imputation were each selected for 4 variables (19.0%), with mean NRMSE values of 0.141 and 0.086, respectively. Global mean imputation was selected for 2 variables (9.5%), with a mean NRMSE of 0.147. Hot deck imputation, linear interpolation, and mode imputation were each selected for 1 variable (4.8%), with mean NRMSE values of 0.055, 0.268, and NaN, respectively. The distribution of method usage frequency and performance metrics indicated that nonparametric methods based on local information, particularly KNN and stratified summary-statistic approaches, showed broad applicability under the present data structure and generally outperformed global statistical methods.

Comprehensive evaluation of imputation quality

Cross-validation results (Figures 4C–4F) indicated that the overall imputation quality was satisfactory. Across the 22 imputed variables, the mean NRMSE was 0.109 and the mean R2 was 0.297. Based on the predefined NRMSE quality thresholds, 13 variables were classified as excellent (NRMSE <0.10; 59.1%), 1 variable as good (0.10 ≤ NRMSE <0.20; 4.5%), 6 variables as marginal (0.20 ≤ NRMSE <0.30; 27.3%), and 1 variable as poor (NRMSE ≥0.30; 4.5%) (Figure 4D). Overall, 63.6% of variables met the excellent or good quality criterion (NRMSE <0.20), and 90.9% met the acceptable quality criterion (NRMSE <0.30).

Imputation quality differed systematically across variable types (Figure 4F). General numeric variables exhibited the highest imputation accuracy, with a mean NRMSE of 0.047 and a mean R2 of 0.770. A representative example was diff_live_born_to_weaned_per_litter, for which KNN imputation yielded an NRMSE of 0.047 and an R2 of 0.771, indicating strong internal structural regularity and high suitability for nearest-neighbor-based imputation. Detection-rate variables showed a mean NRMSE of 0.054 but a mean R2 of only 0.064, suggesting low predictive error but limited variance-explanatory capacity owing to sparse zero-inflated distributions. Duration variables and count/capacity variables displayed relatively balanced performance, with mean NRMSE values of 0.103 and 0.064 and mean R2 values of 0.544 and 0.588, respectively, overall reaching the excellent-to-good range. By contrast, mortality variables and ratio/percentage variables showed comparatively lower imputation accuracy, with mean NRMSE values of 0.141 and 0.125 and mean R2 values of 0.221 and 0.248, respectively (Figure 4F), indicating greater imputation difficulty due to between-subject heterogeneity and the influence of extreme values.

The joint distribution of R2 and NRMSE (Figure 4E) further demonstrated variable-specific patterns in imputation performance. Excellent-quality variables (blue points) were concentrated in the region characterized by NRMSE <0.10 and R2 > 0.20, whereas marginal-quality variables (orange points) were dispersed in the region of high NRMSE and low R2. Bubble size, representing the missingness rate, showed a tendency toward a negative association with imputation quality, suggesting that variables with higher missingness generally exhibited lower imputation accuracy than those with lower missingness.

Overall, the automated cross-validation-based imputation framework achieved high-quality missing value imputation for the majority of variables, thereby ensuring data completeness for subsequent modeling analyses.

Machine learning model performance evaluation

Integrated comparison of key discriminative metrics

The classification performance of five ML models-XGBoost, SVM, RF, EN, and LR-was systematically evaluated on the test set (n = 5,554; positive = 16, negative = 5,538) (Figure 5A). The results revealed pronounced inter-model heterogeneity across performance dimensions, with ensemble learning approaches (XGBoost and RF) outperforming the linear baseline models on most metrics.

In terms of ROC-AUC, all five models demonstrated strong class discrimination. The ROC-AUC values were 0.966 for RF, 0.990 for XGBoost (Figure 5B), 0.980 for SVM, 0.935 for LR, and 0.950 for EN, indicating that all models possessed reliable ranking performance for distinguishing positive from negative samples. However, given the extreme class imbalance in the test set (positive prevalence approximately 0.29%), the relative insensitivity of ROC-AUC to class imbalance necessitated the inclusion of PR-AUC as a more diagnostically informative complementary metric. The PR-AUC results exhibited a markedly different stratification pattern: RF ranked first with a PR-AUC of 0.769, followed by XGBoost (0.752) and SVM (0.667), whereas EN and LR declined sharply to 0.275 and 0.284, respectively (Figure 5B). This pattern highlights the fundamental limitations of linear models in managing the precision-recall trade-off under severe class imbalance.

Across three key positive-class-oriented metrics-F1-score, sensitivity, and precision (Figure 5A), XGBoost and SVM consistently showed strong performance, while RF also achieved favorable results in F1-score and precision. By contrast, EN and LR exhibited uniformly lower values across these metrics, reflecting the structural limitations of regularized linear models in rare-event detection tasks. With respect to balanced accuracy, XGBoost, SVM, and RF performed comparably, whereas EN and LR remained at moderate levels, indicating that ensemble models were more robust in balancing classification performance across majority and minority classes.

Performance profile analysis

The performance profile plot (Figure 5C), presented in parallel-coordinate form, provided an intuitive visualization of the global performance patterns of the five models across seven metrics: sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, balanced accuracy, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC. All models approached 1.00 on specificity, indicating that the vast majority of negative samples in the test set were correctly identified. However, pronounced divergence was observed along the precision axis, where the ensemble models clearly outperformed the linear models, further confirming the discriminative advantage of nonlinear models under class-imbalanced conditions.

The LR model exhibited an anomalously low sensitivity (approximately 0.19), in sharp contrast to the range observed in the other models (0.44–1.00), suggesting a systematic risk of missed detection for the minority class under the current imbalanced sample structure. XGBoost displayed the most balanced co-optimization of sensitivity (approximately 0.94) and precision. The relative flatness of its performance trajectory along the sensitivity-precision dimension further underscored its practical advantage in jointly controlling missed detections and false alarms in real-world swine disease prediction scenarios. RF achieved the highest PR-AUC among all models, and its overall performance curve occupied the upper region of the seven-dimensional metric space, indicating superior all-round performance.

Confusion matrix analysis

The confusion matrices (Figure 5D) further illustrated model-specific classification behavior at the sample level. Among the 16 positive samples, XGBoost correctly identified 15 cases (true positives = 15, false negatives = 1), missing only a single case; SVM detected 7 cases (true positives = 7, false negatives = 9); RF detected all 16 cases (true positives = 16, false negatives = 0), thereby achieving complete identification of the positive class; EN detected no positive cases (true positives = 0, false negatives = 16), completely losing its positive-class detection capability; and LR detected 3 cases (true positives = 3, false negatives = 13).

With respect to false-positive predictions, RF misclassified 15 negative samples as positive (false positives = 15), XGBoost misclassified 16, SVM misclassified 4, EN misclassified 1, and LR misclassified 3. Taken together, these results clearly delineate the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity across models. RF achieved 100% sensitivity (recall) at the cost of a relatively larger number of false-positive predictions; SVM provided the strongest false-positive control (false positives = 4), but its corresponding recall for the positive class declined to 43.75%; XGBoost achieved a comparatively balanced trade-off between sensitivity (93.75%) and false-positive count (16), yielding the best overall F1-score; EN, although associated with an extremely low false-positive count (only 1 case), was completely unable to detect positive samples and therefore lacked practical utility in disease early-warning applications.

Determination of the optimal model

Across the multidimensional evaluation, XGBoost demonstrated the most balanced performance on F1-score, sensitivity, and PR-AUC, the three indicators of greatest practical relevance to early warning, whereas RF achieved the strongest performance in PR-AUC and sensitivity (recall). Both models may therefore be considered viable candidates for pig farm disease risk prediction.

Under the application context of extremely imbalanced data (positive prevalence <0.3%), PR-AUC, and F1-score provide greater value for model selection than ROC-AUC. Owing to their structural limitations, the linear models (EN and LR) performed substantially worse than nonlinear ensemble methods in the rare-event detection task defined in the present analysis and are therefore not recommended as first-line predictive models. Based on the previous findings, XGBoost was selected as the final predictive model for subsequent analyses, and feature importance analysis was further conducted to elucidate the core drivers influencing pig farm production performance and disease risk.

In-depth analysis of the XGBoost model

Hyperparameter optimization and final model selection

Within a 5-fold stratified cross-validation framework, an exhaustive grid search was performed over the number of boosting iterations (nrounds), maximum tree depth (max_depth), and learning rate (eta), covering a total of 36 candidate hyperparameter combinations. The hyperparameter heatmap (Figure 6A) revealed the variation of cross-validated AUC across parameter combinations. Under eta = 0.1, CV AUC increased monotonically with increasing max_depth, suggesting that the drivers of PED risk involve deep nonlinear interaction effects that require greater tree depth to be adequately captured. By contrast, the marginal gain associated with nrounds plateaued after 100 boosting rounds, indicating that the model had largely converged under a moderate number of iterations.

Discriminative performance on the independent test set

The discriminative performance of the final XGBoost model trained using the optimal hyperparameter configuration is shown in Figures 6B and 6C. The ROC curve (Figure 6B) remained well above the random-classification baseline across the fll range of false-positive rates, with an ROC-AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.8925–0.9981), indicating highly reliable overall ranking ability for distinguishing PED-positive and PED-negative farm-week observations.

Given the extremely low proportion of positive samples in the test set (approximately 0.56%), the PR curve (Figure 6C) provided a more sensitive perspective on minority-class detection performance. The XGBoost model achieved a PR-AUC of 0.7448, substantially exceeding the baseline level of a random classifier (i.e., the positive prevalence, approximately 0.56%), and outperforming the linear models (EN: 0.275; LR: 0.284). This finding indicates that the model maintained relatively high recall even within high-precision regions and therefore holds practical potential for controlling the false-alarm burden in real-world pig farm disease early-warning scenarios. A pronounced decline in the PR curve occurred when recall approached approximately 0.75, indicating that further gains in recall would incur a substantial precision penalty and that high-sensitivity thresholding strategies should therefore be applied with caution.

Classification decision analysis

The confusion matrix (Figure 6D) illustrated the classification behavior of XGBoost at the sample level under the default probability threshold of 0.5. The model yielded 21 true positives, 7 false negatives, and only 10 false positives, corresponding to a specificity of 0.9982, sensitivity of 0.6774, F1-score of 0.7119, and overall accuracy of 0.9969. Consistent with the comparative results presented in Section 3.2, XGBoost achieved a coordinated balance between sensitivity and specificity that is inherently difficult to optimize simultaneously in rare-event detection settings: high specificity ensured that the large majority of negative pig farm batches were not falsely flagged, whereas the relatively high sensitivity preserved substantial detection capacity for positive batches under conditions of extreme class imbalance.

It should be noted that the number of positive samples differed between the confusion matrix in this section (Figure 6D, test-set positives = 31) and that presented in Section 3.2 (Figure 5D, positives = 16), owing to differences in the dataset version and random partitioning batch used in the two analyses. The direction of the conclusions remained consistent, and this discrepancy does not affect the overall assessment of model performance.

Predictive probability calibration performance

The calibration curve (Figure 6E) evaluated the agreement between XGBoost-predicted probabilities and the observed PED incidence using decile-based binning. The data points were highly concentrated in the low predicted-probability range (mean predicted probability <0.10), and the corresponding observed event rates were likewise close to zero. Overall, the trajectory of the calibration points remained directionally consistent with the ideal calibration diagonal (dashed line). These results indicate that the model-generated probabilities were highly reliable in the low-risk range and are suitable for use as a quantitative basis for batch-level risk stratification at the pig farm level.

However, because the number of samples in the high predicted-probability range was extremely limited in the test set, calibration accuracy in the high-risk region remains to be further evaluated in larger external validation cohorts.

Interpretation of feature importance

The XGBoost feature-importance ranking based on the gain metric (Figure 6F) revealed differential contributions of 19 modeling features to predictive performance. Month_mode ranked first with a gain value of 0.2533, far exceeding all other features, indicating a pronounced seasonal periodicity in PED risk. This finding is highly consistent with the established biological mechanisms of prolonged coronavirus survival and enhanced transmissibility under low-temperature and low-humidity conditions, and it is also concordant with previous epidemiological evidence showing a higher incidence of PED during winter and spring.

Within the production-management domain, mortality_weaning (weaning mortality rate) and diff_live_born_to_weaned_per_litter (difference between live-born piglets and weaned piglets per litter) ranked second and third, with gain values of approximately 0.10 and 0.08, respectively. These findings suggest that early-life piglet survival status constitutes an important production-level predictor of PED risk. Elevated mortality during the weaning stage and increased within-litter losses may reflect a combination of inadequate immunological management, suboptimal pen hygiene, and insufficient maternally derived antibody levels, all of which may create a favorable host ecological niche for PEDV transmission and dissemination.

Within the meteorological domain, T2M_mean_max, windspeed, UV_index_change_max, and RH2M were among the highest-ranking weather-related predictors by information gain. These variables showed strong concordance with the consensus meteorological variable set identified during the triangulation-based screening stage, thereby further validating the scientific soundness and effectiveness of the multi-method variable preselection strategy. The combined effects of temperature, wind speed, and humidity may regulate PEDV transmission dynamics through multiple pathways, including aerosol stability, barn microenvironmental conditions, and inter-farm biosecurity risk associated with contaminated transport vehicles and personnel. Seasonal variation in light intensity, as reflected by UV_index_change_max, may also exert an indirect regulatory effect by influencing environmental viral persistence.

Taken together, the final XGBoost model, centered on multimodal integration of meteorological and production-management features, achieved coordinated optimization of discriminative and calibration performance on a highly imbalanced PED surveillance dataset, thereby providing a methodological foundation for dynamic early warning of farm-level PED risk.

Discussion

In present study, the XGBoost model achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.8925–0.9981) and a PR-AUC of 0.7448 on the independent test set, demonstrating robust discriminative performance under extreme class imbalance (positive rate <1%). These results are broadly comparable to those reported by Machado et al.20 (ROC-AUC = 0.96); however, the two studies differ fundamentally in feature architecture. Whereas the present study integrates farm-level reproductive management parameters with multi-source meteorological data, forming a multimodal fusion strategy not previously implemented in PED predictive modeling. In highly imbalanced surveillance datasets, ROC-AUC inherently overestimates detection capability because specificity dominates its computation when negative samples vastly outnumber positives; the PR-AUC achieved here substantially exceeds the random-classifier baseline (∼0.6%), confirming that meaningful sensitivity is maintained at operationally relevant precision thresholds. This distinction underscores the importance of evaluating both threshold-dependent and threshold-independent metrics when assessing classifier performance in rare-event epidemiological prediction tasks.21

Unlike analytical models, which are primarily designed to characterize statistical associations between variables, epidemiology-driven predictive models are evaluated principally on their capacity to deliver reliable and accurate forecasts under real-world production conditions.22 Accumulating evidence from livestock disease surveillance confirms that gradient boosting ensemble frameworks consistently achieve superior predictive performance across diverse pathogens and host species. In dairy production, gradient boosting trees have been identified as the most accurate algorithm for predicting subclinical mastitis, attaining an accuracy of 84.9% and outperforming deep learning, conventional decision tree, and RF models.23 This finding is directly relevant to the present study given the analogous challenge of detecting rare, economically consequential disease events from imbalanced, multisource farm records. In the poultry sector, Liu24 demonstrated that an XGBoost classifier trained on as few as two routinely collected production indicators (egg production rate and daily mortality rate) could accurately forecast the risk of H9N2 avian influenza virus occurrence and progression in laying hen flocks within the first two days of infection, underscoring the capacity of gradient boosting to extract meaningful early-warning signals from operationally accessible, parsimonious feature sets. These cross-species and cross-pathogen findings reinforce the conclusion drawn from the present study: residual-driven tree ensembles, by virtue of their capacity to model high-order nonlinear interactions and accommodate severely imbalanced response structures, represent a methodologically appropriate and practically viable choice for farm-level disease risk prediction tasks.

Cross-model comparison revealed a systematic performance divergence between ensemble learners and regularized linear models. XGBoost (PR-AUC = 0.752) and RF (PR-AUC = 0.769) each exceeded EN (0.275) and LR (0.284) by approximately 2.7-fold; the latter two models detected zero and three of 16 test-set positives, respectively, indicating systematic minority-class failure. This performance gap reflects the inherently non-linear, high-order interaction structure of PED risk drivers-a conclusion corroborated by Liang19 in ASF prediction and Suresh25 in CSF risk assessment. The progressive improvement in cross-validation AUC with increasing tree depth (optimum: max_depth = 9, CV AUC = 0.9728) further substantiates deep conditional dependencies between meteorological and production variables that linear models cannot represent. It should be noted, however, that Halev1 found the performance gap between LR and ensemble methods can narrow considerably when feature dimensionality is low and inter-feature relationships are largely linear, indicating that model selection should remain data-driven rather than categorical.

Feature importance analysis identified month_mode (modal month) as the predominant predictor (gain = 0.2533), markedly exceeding the second-ranked variable mortality_weaning (∼0.10). This result accords with established PEDV epidemiology: Yoo et al. (2024) reported a greater than 2.2-fold elevated relative risk during winter-spring months. PEDV environmental persistence is substantially prolonged at low temperatures (<10°C), with reduced relative humidity further facilitating aerosol-mediated transmission.7,8 Mechanistically, low-temperature conditions stabilize the lipid envelope of PEDV virions, extending their viability on fomites and environmental surfaces, while shorter daylight hours suppress mucosal immune responsiveness in neonatal piglets.26 Month_mode functions as a composite seasonal proxy implicitly encoding temperature gradients, humidity cycles, and herd management rhythms simultaneously; its dominant information gain reflects the model’s capacity to leverage this condensed multidimensional seasonal signal, consistent with the prioritization of meteorological variables reported by Machado.20 From a biosecurity standpoint, the primacy of seasonality provides a rational basis for temporally targeted interventions. It enhanced environmental temperature, such as humidity regulation, intensified disinfection protocols, and adaptive immunization scheduling concentrated within the high-risk winter-spring window.

The prominent predictive contributions of mortality_weaning and diff_live_born_to_weaned_per_litter (gain ≈ 0.08) constitute a finding not previously documented in ML-based PED literature. Elevated pre-weaning mortality likely reflects deficient colostrum management and impaired maternal antibody transfer, both of which compromise intestinal mucosal immunity in neonatal piglets and heighten susceptibility to PEDV.27 From an immunological perspective, inadequate passive transfer of IgA and IgG via colostrum leaves the neonatal intestinal epithelium insufficiently protected against coronavirus-mediated villous atrophy, the hallmark pathological lesion of PED.26 Elevated within-litter losses are frequently accompanied by increased cross-fostering frequency, a recognized risk factor for horizontal pathogen dissemination within farrowing units.28 Farrowing_room_vacancy_time_days entered the mid-tier of the importance ranking, consistent with the principle that insufficient inter-batch downtime impedes complete environmental decontamination and sustains PEDV persistence across production cycles. These findings align with the broader observation by Makau9 that farm-level management practices, particularly for batch management, biosecurity compliance, and sanitation protocols, serve as modifiable risk determinants that often exceed the explanatory power of fixed environmental covariates. Collectively, these results provide the first empirical demonstration that within an ML framework-that routine reproductive performance indicators independently contribute to PED outbreak risk, underscoring the necessity of incorporating herd management data into predictive surveillance architectures.

Methodologically, the triangulated variable selection strategy was used to integrate hierarchical cluster analysis, stepwise VIF filtering, and PCA loading evaluation. It reduced 22 candidate meteorological variables to 14 consensus predictors (all VIF <10) spanning six environmental dimensions (temperature, humidity, precipitation, radiation, wind speed, and temporal index). This multi-method cross-validation framework mitigates selection biases inherent in single-method approaches: domain-knowledge filtering risks overlooking non-linearly informative variables, while algorithm-based ranking alone is susceptible to confounding by collinear features.19 The consensus approach adopted here conceptually parallels the ensemble feature selection paradigms advocated in high-dimensional biomedical data analysis,21 whereby agreement across heterogeneous selection criteria enhances the stability and reproducibility of retained feature subsets. The challenge of class imbalance warrants particular methodological attention in disease outbreak prediction. Most standard ML algorithms implicitly assume balanced class distributions or assign equal costs to different types of misclassification; when one class substantially outnumbers the other, such algorithms tend to generate classifiers biased toward the majority class, yielding results that fail to reflect the true data distribution and thereby critically undermining detection sensitivity for minority-class instances.29 For binary disease outbreak prediction tasks, outbreak events are rare relative to outbreak-free periods. Class imbalance is a near-universal feature that can systematically inflate apparent accuracy while masking model failure on the clinically important positive class.30 Common strategies to address this problem include random sampling approaches, such as minority-class oversampling and majority-class undersampling,31 as well as the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE), which generates synthetic interpolated minority-class instances and has been widely adopted as a standard preprocessing step in imbalanced classification pipelines.31 Stratified k-fold cross-validation has likewise been recommended to provide more reliable estimates of model generalization performance and to prevent the artificially inflated accuracy metrics that arise when identical data are used for both model training and evaluation.31,32 However, resampling-based approaches require a sufficient number of samples to ensure distributional representativeness, and under conditions of extreme class rarity (positive prevalence <1%), SMOTE-generated synthetic instances risk distorting the learned decision boundary in high-dimensional feature spaces.33 In the present study, class imbalance (positive prevalence <0.6%) was addressed via inverse-proportion class weighting (negative: 0.0114; positive: 1.9886), achieving concurrent high sensitivity (0.6774) and specificity (0.9982) without recourse to synthetic data augmentation. Relative to SMOTE and random undersampling,19,33 class weighting preserves the full distributional heterogeneity of the majority class. It becomes a critical advantage when negative samples exhibit substantial intra-class variation across seasons and farm scales. Furthermore, avoiding the introduction of synthetic data points that may compromise the integrity of the learned decision boundary in the high-dimensional meteorological feature space of this study.33

In summary, the XGBoost model integrating meteorological and production management features demonstrated superior discriminative performance for farm-level PED outbreak risk prediction under extreme class imbalance. Seasonality emerged as the dominant risk driver, while farm-level reproductive performance indicators were identified as independent predictors for the first time within an ML framework. The triangulated variable selection strategy and class weighting approach offer transferable methodological contributions to animal disease risk modeling. Future work should prioritize multi-regional external validation, microenvironmental data integration, spatial network feature incorporation, and temporal dynamic modeling to advance the framework toward operational deployment within one health surveillance systems.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, PED virus could be transmitted between farms through direct contact, indirect contact, aerosols, contaminated vehicles, personnel, and materials, even wildlife might play a role in inter-farm transmission.34,35,36 Second, NASA POWER meteorological data (∼0.5 ° × 0.5 ° resolution) may not accurately represent intra-barn microenvironmental conditions; integration of IoT-based real-time sensor data would substantially improve microenvironmental characterization and enable dynamic risk updating. Third, spatial epidemiological features-neighbourhood farm density and animal movement network connectivity-were not incorporated; their integration with the present multimodal framework would yield a more ecologically complete model, as demonstrated by Machado.20 Fourth, model interpretability was evaluated via global information gain ranking; subsequent SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis would enable individual-level risk decomposition and more actionable farm-specific guidance, facilitating the translation of predictive outputs into targeted management recommendations. Finally, the cross-sectional modeling paradigm does not exploit temporal autocorrelation in weekly surveillance data; hybrid architectures combining LSTM or temporal convolutional networks with XGBoost feature engineering represent a promising direction for capturing lagged exposure-outcome relationships and improving early-warning lead time.

Method details

Acquisition and initial cleaning of meteorological data

Monthly meteorological data for the study area from January 2023 to December 2025 were extracted from the NASA POWER (Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources) database, yielding an initial set of 23 meteorological variables. Data quality control was performed using a standard deviation threshold test (standard deviation <1.0 × 10−10) and QR decomposition to detect perfectly collinear variables. For observations containing missing values, the missingness pattern was first assessed using Little’s MCAR test, after which listwise deletion was applied, retaining only complete observations for subsequent analyses.

Multicollinearity diagnosis and variable selection

To systematically evaluate multicollinearity among meteorological variables and achieve robust dimensionality reduction, three methodologically complementary statistical approaches were independently applied to screen 22 candidate meteorological variables. Their results were then integrated using a voting mechanism to ensure methodological consistency in multicollinearity control within the final variable set.

Variable selection was conducted using a backward stepwise elimination procedure. At each iteration, the variable with the highest variance inflation factor (VIF) was removed, and VIF values were recalculated for the remaining variables. This process was repeated until all retained variables satisfied VIF < 10. The interpretation criteria were as follows: VIF < 2, low collinearity; 2 ≤ VIF < 5, moderate collinearity; 5 ≤ VIF < 10, high collinearity; and VIF ≥ 10, severe collinearity.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique. All meteorological variables were first standardized using z-scores, after which covariance decomposition was performed on the standardized matrix. The number of principal components was determined jointly using the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1) and the scree plot elbow criterion; only components supported by both criteria were retained as valid principal components. Subsequently, the loading contribution of each original variable to the first principal component (PC1) was calculated, and variables with the highest contribution rankings were selected for subsequent consensus voting. Because the direction of PC1 represents the linear combination explaining the greatest proportion of variance in the dataset, variables contributing most strongly to PC1 were considered to possess the highest information content and the greatest influence on the overall collinearity structure.

Consensus variable selection

The screening results derived from the three methods above were integrated using a voting mechanism to identify consensus variables. Variables selected by at least two methods were defined as “consensus variables,” and the selection frequency of each variable (range: 0–3) was calculated. Quality validation of the consensus variable set included recalculation of VIF values (requiring mean VIF < 5 and all VIF values < 10), computation of the mean CV, and evaluation of meteorological dimension coverage.

Missing value imputation for the post-screening dataset of meteorological variables

Imputation framework and method library

For the final dataset composed of 14 retained meteorological variables and 31 non-meteorological variables after meteorological variable screening (27,774 observations and 45 variables), missing values were imputed using the Intelligent Missing Value Imputation System v3.0. This system was developed in R version 4.3.3 and integrated 18 imputation algorithms, including simple statistical methods (mean, median, mode, trimmed mean), stratified imputation methods (farm- and season-stratified mean and median), time-series methods (linear interpolation, spline interpolation, Kalman filtering, moving average, moving median, last observation carried forward [LOCF], next observation carried backward [NOCB]), ML methods (k-nearest neighbors [KNN], multiple imputation by chained equations [MICE], random sampling, Hot Deck imputation), and a special method (zero imputation).

The core R packages and versions included: dplyr (v1.1.4) for data manipulation, mice (v3.17.0) for multiple imputation, VIM (v6.2.2) for KNN imputation, zoo (v1.8.13) for time-series interpolation, imputeTS (v3.3) for time-series-specific imputation, forecast (v8.24.0) for Kalman filtering, and Metrics (v0.1.4) for performance metric calculation.

Variable classification and candidate method screening

The 22 non-meteorological variables containing missing values were first automatically classified into eight categories according to data characteristics: four detection-rate variables, four mortality-rate variables, six ratio/percentage variables, two count/capacity variables, two duration variables, one weather time-series variable, one general numerical variable, and one categorical variable. Based on variable type and distributional characteristics, the system automatically assigned 6 to 8 candidate imputation algorithms to each variable for comparative evaluation.

Cross-validation evaluation and method selection

A 3-fold cross-validation strategy was used to evaluate the imputation performance of candidate methods. For each variable containing missing values, 10% of complete observations were randomly sampled, artificial missingness was introduced, and candidate methods were then applied for imputation. The following evaluation metrics were calculated: root-mean-square error (RMSE), normalized root-mean-square error (NRMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), Pearson correlation coefficient, coefficient of determination (R2), and systematic bias (Bias). Based on an aggregated ranking algorithm, the best-performing imputation method was selected for each variable.

Practical implementation of imputation

After the optimal method had been identified, actual missing values for each variable were imputed accordingly. Parameter settings were specified as follows: k = 5 for KNN, m = 3 and maxit = 5 for MICE, a three-week window for moving average and moving median, and a 10% trimming proportion for the trimmed mean. All operation logs and detailed reports were saved in Excel and text formats to ensure process traceability and reproducibility.

Machine learning modeling and disease outbreak prediction

Dataset partitioning and class imbalance handling

The fully imputed dataset (27,774 observations and 45 features) was split into a training set (22,220 samples) and a test set (5,554 samples) at a ratio of 8:2 using stratified sampling to preserve class distribution consistency, with the random seed set to 42. The target variable, Disease_Outcome (PED), was binary, comprising 27,616 negative samples (99.4%) and 158 positive samples (0.6%). Specifically, the training set consisted of 22,093 negative samples (99.43%) and 127 positive samples (0.57%), whereas the test set consisted of 5,523 negative samples (99.44%) and 31 positive samples (0.56%).

To address class imbalance, a class-weighting strategy was applied. The calculated class weights were 0.0114 for the negative class and 1.9886 for the positive class. These weights were incorporated into model training to increase algorithmic attention to the minority class.

Feature engineering

A total of 54 original features underwent standardized preprocessing. Numerical features were standardized using z-scores (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1), and categorical variables were encoded using one-hot encoding. Feature importance was then evaluated using the RF algorithm, with all features ranked according to the Mean Decrease Gini criterion.

Model training and hyperparameter optimization

To systematically evaluate the suitability of different algorithmic paradigms for PED risk prediction, five ML models spanning three major methodological categories: linear models, ensemble learning methods, and kernel-based methods. They were selected based on differences in inductive bias and modeling mechanisms, thereby constituting a complementary model ensemble. Logistic Regression (LR) served as the baseline reference under a linearly separable assumption, providing a quantitative benchmark for assessing performance gains achieved by nonlinear models. RF, based on a bagging strategy, constructs multiple decision trees in parallel and aggregates their predictions, thereby offering an inherent capacity to capture feature interaction effects. XGBoost, based on a boosting mechanism, performs efficient nonlinear modeling through residual-driven iterative optimization. Support Vector Machine (SVM) maps the feature space into a high-dimensional inner-product space through kernel functions and exhibits strong generalization ability in limited-sample settings. Elastic Net (EN) combines L1 and L2 regularization constraints, balancing variable selection and coefficient shrinkage in high-dimensional sparse feature structures. All models were trained and validated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation (5--Fold Stratified Cross-Validation; random seed = 123). The stratified sampling mechanism ensured that the proportion of positive samples in each fold remained consistent with that of the original dataset, thereby preserving statistical representativeness under conditions of extreme class imbalance (approximately 0.6% positives in this study).

Given the extremely low proportion of positive samples in the present dataset, class imbalance imposed explicit constraints on the selection of the hyperparameter optimization objective. Conventional aggregate metrics such as accuracy possess inherent discriminatory limitations under such conditions; for example, a model classifying all samples as negative would still achieve an accuracy of 99.4%, rendering the metric ineffective for providing meaningful optimization gradients during hyperparameter search. In light of these data characteristics, model hyperparameters were systematically optimized using grid search, with the area under the ROC-AUC designated as the sole optimization objective. This metric is both threshold-independent and prevalence-independent, enabling comprehensive evaluation of a model’s overall discriminative ability between positive and negative samples across all possible classification thresholds, thereby ensuring statistical validity in hyperparameter optimization and fairness in between-model comparisons under extremely imbalanced data distributions.

The hyperparameter search space for each model was defined as follows. LR was not subjected to hyperparameter tuning. For RF, the number of variables randomly selected at each split (mtry) was tuned over candidate values of 7, 18, and 27, while the number of trees was fixed at 200. For XGBoost, the number of boosting iterations (nrounds; candidate values: 50, 100, 150, 200), maximum tree depth (max_depth; candidate values: 3, 6, 9), and learning rate (eta; candidate values: 0.01, 0.1, 0.3) were tuned, while all other parameters were fixed at gamma = 0, colsample_bytree = 0.8, min_child_weight = 1, and subsample = 0.8. For SVM, the penalty parameter (C; candidate values: 0.1, 1, 10, 100) and radial basis function kernel width (sigma; candidate values: 0.01, 0.1, 1) were tuned. For EN, the mixing parameter (alpha; candidate values: 0, 0.5, 1) and regularization strength (lambda; candidate values: 0.001, 0.01, 0.1) were tuned.

Model evaluation metrics

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using eight metrics: Accuracy, Sensitivity/Recall, Specificity, Precision, F1-Score, Balanced Accuracy, ROC-AUC, and the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC). ROC-AUC was used as the primary criterion for model selection.

The ROC curve was constructed with the false positive rate (1 − specificity) on the x axis and the true positive rate (sensitivity) on the y axis; an area under the curve approaching 1 indicates stronger classification performance. The precision-recall (PR) curve was constructed with recall on the x axis and precision on the y axis and is particularly suitable for evaluating model performance on imbalanced datasets. The confusion matrix was visualized as a heatmap to display the counts of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN).

For tree-based models (RF and XGBoost), feature importance rankings were extracted. RF feature importance was quantified using the Mean Decrease Gini metric, whereas XGBoost feature importance was quantified using Gain. Model calibration analysis was performed using decile binning, in which predicted probabilities were divided into 10 intervals. The mean predicted probability and observed positive proportion were calculated within each interval, and calibration curves were plotted to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and actual event probabilities.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3). The statistical significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 throughout.

Unit of analysis and sample size. The unit of analysis (n) was the farm-week observation, defined as a single record linking weekly herd management and reproductive performance indicators from one commercial pig farm to the corresponding weekly meteorological exposure window. The total analytical dataset comprised n = 27,774 farm-week observations spanning 45 variables, of which n = 27,616 were PED-negative (99.4%) and n = 158 were PED-positive (0.6%). Following stratified partitioning at an 8:2 ratio (random seed = 42), the training set contained n = 22,220 observations (22,093 negative; 127 positive) and the independent test set contained n = 5,554 observations (5,523 negative; 31 positive). The exact sample composition of each partition is reported in Section dataset partitioning and class imbalance handling.

Descriptive statistics. Measures of central tendency (mean and median) and dispersion (standard deviation [SD] and coefficient of variation [CV]) for all continuous meteorological and herd management variables are summarized in the figure legends of Figures 2, 3, and 4. Numerical features were standardized to z-scores (mean = 0, SD = 1) prior to model training, as specified in Section feature engineering and principal component analysis.

Variable selection and multicollinearity. For meteorological variable screening, three independent statistical approaches were applied: (1) hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) using complete linkage with a Pearson correlation-based dissimilarity metric (cut-off threshold = 0.20; results in Figure 2); (2) stepwise VIF screening with a retention criterion of VIF <10 (Section variance inflation factor stepwise screening); and (3) PCA-based contribution assessment retaining components satisfying both the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue >1) and the scree plot elbow criterion (Section principal component analysis; Figure 3). A triangulation voting mechanism was applied across the three methods, with consensus variables defined as those selected by ≥ 2 methods. The screening results are presented in Table 1. Quality validation of the final 14-variable consensus set required mean VIF <5 and all individual VIF values <10 (Section consensus variable selection).

Imputation accuracy. Missing value imputation performance was assessed via 3-fold cross-validation on a 10% artificially masked subsample of complete observations (Section cross-validation evaluation and method selection). Six metrics were computed: RMSE, NRMSE, MAE, Pearson correlation coefficient (r), coefficient of determination (R2), and systematic bias. Imputation quality was graded as Excellent (NRMSE ≤0.20), Good (0.20 < NRMSE ≤0.30), or Marginal (NRMSE >0.30). Per-variable imputation results are presented in Figure 4.

Model performance evaluation. Model discrimination was evaluated on the independent test set (n = 5,554) using eight metrics: Accuracy, Sensitivity (Recall), Specificity, Precision, F1-Score, Balanced Accuracy, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC (Section 2.2.4). ROC-AUC was designated as the primary model selection criterion. The 95% confidence interval for ROC-AUC was computed using the DeLong method via the pROC package. Given the extreme class imbalance (positive prevalence ≈0.56%), PR-AUC served as a complementary metric providing a sensitivity-focused assessment of discriminative performance. All metric values, confidence intervals, and cross-model comparisons are reported in Figure 5 and Table 2. Hyperparameter optimization for XGBoost was conducted via exhaustive grid search within a 5-fold stratified cross-validation framework covering 36 candidate combinations; the optimal configuration (nrounds = 100, max_depth = 9, eta = 0.1) yielded a mean CV-AUC of 0.9728, as detailed in Section 2.2.3 and Figure 6A.

Statistical software. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3). R packages were employed as follows: readxl (v1.4.5) and writexl (v1.5.3) for data import/export; dplyr (v1.1.4) and tidyr (v1.3.1) for data wrangling; psych (v2.5.6) for descriptive statistics; corrplot (v0.92) and car (v3.1-2) for correlation visualization and VIF calculation; FactoMineR (v2.9), factoextra (v1.0.7), and stats (base R) for PCA and hierarchical clustering; mice (v3.17.0), VIM (v6.2.2), zoo (v1.8.13), imputeTS (v3.3), forecast (v8.24.0), and naniar (v1.1.0) for missing value imputation; caret, randomForest, xgboost, kernlab, and glmnet for machine learning modeling; pROC, PRROC, Metrics (v0.1.4), and MLmetrics for model evaluation; ggplot2 (v3.5.0) and pheatmap (v1.0.12) for visualization.
