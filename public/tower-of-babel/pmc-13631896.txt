Ellagic acid in poultry nutrition: emerging roles in gut health, immunity, and sustainable production
Authors: Kanglei Zhang, Zhonggui Gong, Min Chen, Muhammad Asif Arain, Muhammad Zohaib, Lei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ellagic acid (EA), is a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound abundant in pomegranate, berries, walnuts, and various agro-industrial by-products, and has attracted increasing interest as a potential phytogenic feed additive for poultry. Its importance arises from the need for effective natural alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters that can simultaneously support bird health, productivity, and production sustainability. This review synthesizes evidence from peer-reviewed studies identified through systematic searches of major scientific databases using combinations of keywords related to EA, poultry, gut health, immunity, oxidative stress, inflammation, growth performance, meat quality, and sustainable production. Relevant peer-reviewed studies were selected based on their direct relevance to EA-mediated physiological, intestinal, immunological, and productive responses in poultry. Literature indicates that EA exerts antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory activities through regulation of oxidative stress responses, inflammatory signaling, intestinal barrier function, and gut microbial communities. Emerging evidence further suggests potential benefits for growth performance, feed efficiency, meat quality, and product stability, while nutrigenomic studies indicate that EA can modulate genes involved in immunity, metabolism, antioxidant defense, and intestinal function. However, its practical application is constrained by limited bioavailability, variable responses, dose optimization, and insufficient long-term evidence. Therefore, this review aims to critically evaluate the mechanistic basis and poultry-specific evidence for EA as a phytogenic feed additive, with particular emphasis on gut–microbiota interactions, immune regulation, oxidative stress and inflammation, productive performance, and highlights the prospects and limitations for sustainable poultry production.

Introduction

The poultry industry plays a pivotal role in global food security by supplying affordable, high-quality animal protein through meat and eggs production. However, intensive production systems increasingly expose birds to multiple challenges, including gut health disorders, emerging infectious diseases, oxidative and heat stress, and compromised feed efficiency which can collectively impair productivity, health, and welfare (Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Chen et al., 2023). Antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) have historically been used to enhance growth performance and disease resistance; nevertheless, their indiscriminate use has raised serious concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance and food safety, resulting in increasing restrictions and bans worldwide (Salim et al., 2018; Li et al., 2026b). These challenges have accelerated the search for natural, safe, and sustainable feed additives that can simultaneously support productivity and physiological resilience in poultry and other livestock species (Arain et al., 2018; Nabi et al., 2020b; Liu et al., 2023). Among the emerging alternatives, plant-derived bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols, have attracted considerable interest because of their diverse antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory activities (Du et al., 2024; Yatao et al., 2018; Yuan et al., 2025a).

Among dietary polyphenols, ellagic acid (EA) has emerged as a potent bioactive compound with significant potential poultry nutrition. EA is widely distributed in fruits and plant-derived materials, including pomegranates, berries, walnuts, and oak-derived products, predominantly as ellagitannins that release EA following hydrolysis (Lorenzo et al., 2019). After ingestion, EA undergoes extensive biotransformation by gut microbiota into urolithins, which generally exhibit greater bioavailability and diverse biological activities (Leng et al., 2025). Thus, the physiological effects EA extend beyond its direct molecular actions and involve complex interactions among EA metabolites, the intestinal microbiota, and host tissue. Importantly the increasing availability of EA-rich agro-industrial by-products provides an additional opportunity for their incorporation into sustainable poultry feeding strategies, linking functional nutrition with waste valorization and circular-bioeconomy principles.

The biological relevance of EA is largely associated with its capacity to regulate oxidative stress inflammation and microbial homeostasis. EA can directly scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and enhances endogenous antioxidant defenses, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), thereby limiting oxidative damage to cellular and tissues components (Zhang et al., 2023). Consequently, EA influences major redox- and inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), which coordinate antioxidant defense and inflammatory responses, respectively (Aslan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). These mechanisms are highly relevant to poultry because rapid metabolic activity, high production intensity, and environmental stressors can disrupt intestinal barrier function, immune homeostasis, and nutrient utilization (Li et al., 2025). In addition, the antimicrobial activity of EA against selected pathogenic bacteria suggest that it may contribute to microbial equilibrium within the gastrointestinal tract (Aljassim et al., 2022). These properties provide a mechanistic basis for investigating EA as a multifunctional nutritional strategy rather than merely as a conventional antioxidant additive.

Gut health is a fundamental determinant of nutrient digestion and absorption, growth performance, immune competence, and production efficiency in poultry (Yu et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026). Emerging evidence indicates that dietary EA may improve intestinal architecture by promoting villus development, maintaining crypt integrity, and enhancing tight junction protein expression, thereby supporting epithelial barrier function (Li et al., 2024). EA may also modulate the intestinal microbiota by favoring beneficial microbial populations while limiting potentially pathogenic bacteria, with downstream implications for digestive efficiency and immune regulation (Ren et al., 2026a). Despite these promising findings, evidence for EA supplementation in poultry remains fragmented, particularly regarding dose optimization, physiological mechanism, duration-dependent effects, bioavailability, and responses under different production or environmental conditions. Therefore, this review critically evaluates the current evidence on EA in poultry nutrition, with particular emphasis on its mechanistic roles in gut health, immune regulation, oxidative-stress mitigation, and sustainable production, while identifying major knowledge gaps and priorities for precision-oriented application of EA in poultry systems.

Search strategy used for data collection

The literature search was conducted to identify peer-reviewed studies investigating the biological, nutritional, physiological, and productive effects of EA in poultry. Major scientific databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar, were searched using combinations of keywords and Boolean operators. The primary search terms included ellagic acid, ellagic acid supplementation, poultry, broiler, laying hen, gut health, intestinal barrier, gut microbiota, immunity, immunomodulation, oxidative stress, inflammation, feed efficiency, growth performance, egg production, and heat stress. Search combinations were adapted to the indexing requirements of each database to maximize retrieval of relevant literature.

Studies were considered eligible when they were published in peer-reviewed journals and provided direct or mechanistically relevant evidence concerning EA or EA-rich dietary sources in relation to poultry health, productivity and product quality. Experimental studies involving poultry species were prioritized, while relevant mechanistic and supporting evidence from non-avian models was considered only when necessary to explain the molecular basis of EA activity. Studies with insufficient information on EA exposure, outcomes, or experimental relevance to the review objectives were excluded.

Chemical structure and physicochemical properties of EA

Ellagic acid is a naturally occurring polyphenolic phytochemical belonging to the class of hydrolyzable tannins and is widely distributed in numerous fruits, nuts, and medicinal plants. Chemically, EA is generated through the oxidative dimerization of two gallic acid molecules, yielding a dilactone structure with the molecular formula C₁₄H₆O₈ and a molecular weight of 302.19 g/mol (Tokutomi et al., 2018). Its molecular architecture is characterized by a planar fused biphenyl framework containing four hydroxyl groups and two lactone moieties (Fig. 1). In plants, EA exists either in its free form or, more commonly, as a constituent of ellagitannins, a subclass of complex tannins (Fraga-Corral et al., 2021). Following ingestion, ellagitannins undergo hydrolysis within the gastrointestinal tract to release free EA, which can subsequently be transformed by intestinal microbiota into biologically active metabolites known as urolithins (Espín et al., 2013). These microbial derivatives exhibit enhanced bioavailability and are considered key mediators of many physiological effects attributed to EA, thereby expanding its nutritional and therapeutic relevance.Fig. 1Chemical structure of ellagic acid.

The physicochemical properties of EA play a pivotal role in determining its stability, absorption, and biological efficacy. EA is typically observed as a pale-yellow crystalline powder with extremely low aqueous solubility (approximately 9-10 μg/mL at room temperature), a characteristic that substantially limits its oral bioavailability. Nevertheless, its solubility increases under alkaline conditions and in selected organic solvents, including methanol and dimethyl sulfoxide (Williams et al., 2016). The compound exhibits a high melting point (>350°C), reflecting considerable thermal stability during feed processing and storage. The abundance of phenolic hydroxyl groups enables efficient hydrogen donation and radical scavenging, thereby protecting cellular lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids from oxidative. Furthermore, the conjugated aromatic system and hydroxyl functionalities facilitate interactions with transition metals, biological membranes, and intracellular signaling molecules, contributing to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and metal-chelating activities (Alfei et al., 2020). Collectively, these structural and physicochemical attributes provide the mechanistic basis for the increasing application of EA as a functional nutraceutical and feed additive.

Major dietary sources of EA

EA is primarily acquired through the consumption of ellagitannin-rich plant foods, although its distribution and concentration vary considerably among dietary sources. Pomegranate is recognized as the most abundant source, containing high levels of ellagitannins, particularly punicalagins, which range from approximately 2,000-5,000 mg/kg in the peel and 300-1,500 mg/kg in the juice fraction, thereby serving as a major precursor for EA release upon hydrolysis (Seeram et al., 2005; Saeed et al., 2018). Berries such as raspberries and blackberries also contribute substantially, providing around 100–300 mg/100 g fresh weight of ellagitannins (Marquez-Lopez et al., 2019). In addition, tree nuts, including walnuts and pecans, represent notable dietary sources, with ellagitannin contents of approximately 20–60 mg/100 g, predominantly localized in the skin and pellicle tissues (Maestri et al., 2026).

Potential pharmaceutical properties of EA

EA naturally acquire broad pharmacological potential, exhibiting antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, neuroprotective, and antiproliferative activities. Its therapeutic effects are associated with ROS scavenging, enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defenses, suppression of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling, modulation of lipid metabolism, and regulation of apoptosis and angiogenesis (Evtyugin et al., 2020). EA also shows hepatoprotective effects through regulation of antioxidant enzymes, inflammatory cytokines, lipid homeostasis, and fibrotic pathways, supporting its potential as a complementary pharmacological agent.

Antioxidant properties of EA

Evidence from poultry species

Poultry evidence supports EA as a multifunctional antioxidant that acts beyond direct radical scavenging by strengthening endogenous redox defenses and coordinating antioxidant, mitochondrial, inflammatory, and gut-barrier responses. Across heat-stressed broilers, laying hens, and coccidial challenge models, dietary EA consistently reduced oxidative damage, reflected by lower malondialdehyde (MDA) and ROS accumulation, while SOD, CAT, GPx, and GPX4 activities or expression (Yang et al., 2022a; Yuan et al., 2025b). Mechanistically, activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway appears central to EA-mediated antioxidant protection, whereas suppression of ferroptosis-related signaling and preservation of mitochondrial function may further limit cellular oxidative injury in poultry (Yuan et al., 2025b). Importantly, the potentiation of Nrf2/HO-1 signaling by EA combined with Lactobacillus brevis suggests that interactions between polyphenols and the intestinal microbiota can amplify antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects during Eimeria infection (Yang et al., 2022b). Increased jejunal CAT activity following 300 mg/kg EA supplementation further indicates tissue-specific reinforcement of intestinal antioxidant capacity in broiler (Wang et al., 2025b). These findings position EA as a promising feed-derived redox modulator in poultry, although responses remain dependent on dose, physiological stressor, and biological context.

Supportive evidence from non-poultry species

Evidence from non-poultry models provides mechanistic support for the potential antioxidant relevance of EA in poultry (Fig. 2), although species-specific validation remains necessary. In rainbow trout, dietary EA (50–150 mg/kg) enhanced SOD, CAT, and GPx activities, increased GSH, and reduced MDA, demonstrating reinforcement of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses (Mişe Yonar et al., 2014). Similarly, EA (0.1–0.3%) activated the Nrf2–ARE pathway in C57BL/6J mice, increasing phosphorylated Nrf2, HO-1, and NQO1 while decreasing hepatic MDA (Xu et al., 2021). Comparable protection against oxidative injury was observed in rat skeletal muscle following ischemia/reperfusion, where EA restored SOD activity and suppressed lipid peroxidation (Ekinci Akdemir et al., 2016). At the cellular level, EA reduced ROS and MDA and enhanced Nrf2 signaling through the miR-223/Keap1 axis in high-glucose-stressed HepG2 cells (Ding et al., 2019). Moreover, chitosan-encapsulated EA reduced hepatic and renal oxidative and nitrosative damage while restoring GPx, CAT, and GST activities, highlighting the importance of bioavailability (EL Barky et al., 2020). EA also protected zebrafish embryos from H₂O₂-induced ROS and DNA damage (Mottola et al., 2020). Conclusively, these findings are physiologically relevant to poultry because oxidative challenges associated with rapid growth, high metabolic activity, heat stress, inflammation, and intensive production can compromise intestinal integrity, immunity, hepatic function, and muscle quality (Arain et al., 2024). Anti-inflammatory properties

Evidence from poultry species

The available poultry evidence suggests that EA may function as a multilevel anti-inflammatory phytochemical, integrating redox regulation, innate immune signaling, epithelial barrier protection, and tissue remodeling. In broilers, dietary EA or EA-rich pomegranate products consistently attenuated inflammatory responses by suppressing TLR/NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and NLRP3-associated pathways, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOS (Yang et al., 2022b; Zhao et al., 2026). Importantly, these effects are physiologically relevant because intestinal inflammation compromises epithelial integrity, nutrient absorption, microbial homeostasis, and growth efficiency in poultry (Arain et al., 2025). The concurrent activation of Nrf2/HO-1 indicates functional coupling between antioxidant and inflammatory pathways, suggesting that EA may interrupt the oxidative-stress–inflammation feed-forward loop during enteric challenge (Yang et al., 2022b). Moreover, modulation of gut microbiota and microbial metabolites may provide an additional interface through which EA-rich products reinforce intestinal immune homeostasis (Tang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2026; Li et al., 2022). Beyond the intestine, attenuation of TGF-β/Smad-mediated fibrotic remodeling suggests potential protection of muscle structural integrity and meat quality during inflammatory stress (Jiang et al., 2026). These findings position EA as a promising immunomodulatory component of poultry nutrition, although direct EA-specific evidence remains limited and further dose-response, bioavailability, and mechanistic studies are required to distinguish its effects from those of other pomegranate polyphenols.

Supportive evidence from non-poultry species

Research evidence from non-poultry species indicates that EA exerts anti-inflammatory effects through an integrated redox-inflammatory signaling network, rather than a single molecular target. Across intestinal, gastric, systemic, and neuroinflammatory models, EA consistently activates Nrf2/HO-1/NQO1, restores GSH and SOD activity, and limits lipid peroxidation, while concurrently suppressing NF-κB/COX-2 and downstream cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Xiao et al., 2022; Eldesoqui et al., 2025; Bains et al., 2022). Activation of PPAR-γ further suggests transcriptional regulation of intestinal inflammatory responses and barrier protection (Chen et al., 2020), whereas rabbit evidence demonstrates modulation of hypothalamic NF-κB/COX-2 signaling and systemic oxidative status (Xie et al., 2024). These mechanisms are physiologically relevant to poultry because intestinal oxidative stress, epithelial barrier disruption (Sun et al., 2026), and excessive NF-κB-mediated cytokine production are closely associated with impaired nutrient absorption, gut integrity, immune activation, and growth efficiency in poultry birds (Fig. 2). Thus, although direct poultry evidence remains comparatively limited, the conserved Nrf2–NF-κB–PPAR regulatory axis provides a strong mechanistic rationale for investigating EA as an anti-inflammatory phytochemical in poultry, particularly under oxidative and enteric stress conditions.

Immunomodulatory properties

Ellagic acid and their derivatives demonstrate context-dependent immunomodulatory activity, through attenuating excessive NF-κB/MAPK activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production while preserving protective immune responses (Čolić et al., 2022; Safdar et al., 2024). This mechanism is particularly relevant to poultry, in which rapid growth, high metabolic activity, environmental stress, and continuous exposure to enteric pathogens can increase oxidative and inflammatory burdens, impairing intestinal integrity and production efficiency. Experimental evidence from other biological systems further supports this pleiotropic capacity: EA can enhance macrophage antimicrobial activity during intracellular infection, whereas under chronic inflammatory conditions it promotes IL-10-associated resolution and limits TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ-driven tissue injury (Alves et al., 2017; Allam et al., 2016). In poultry, such immune balancing could theoretically improve pathogen resistance while minimizing the metabolic costs of persistent inflammation. Moreover, enhanced bioavailability through advanced delivery systems may strengthen these effects (Allemailem 2023). Thus, EA represents a promising phytogenic immunomodulator capable of integrating antioxidant defense, cytokine regulation, intestinal immune homeostasis, and tissue protection, although poultry-specific mechanistic validation remains essential.

Potential anti-bacterial properties

Ellagic acid demonstrates broad-spectrum antibacterial activity across both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, with efficacy extending from Helicobacter pylori to multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Comparative evidence indicates that EA not only exerts direct bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects but also functions as a potent anti-virulence and antibiotic-adjuvant molecule. For instance, in H. pylori infection models, EA reduced gastric colonization and inflammation in vivo, highlighting its dual role in microbial suppression and host protection (De et al., 2018). Similarly, EA exhibited measurable MBC values against MDR pathogens, with stronger activity against S. aureus and E. coli, while showing slightly reduced potency against P. aeruginosa, likely due to its intrinsic outer membrane permeability barriers (Aljassim et al., 2022). In addition, EA-derived phytochemicals from Rubus ulmifolius significantly inhibited biofilm formation and enhanced antibiotic susceptibility, reinforcing its role in disrupting bacterial community resilience and persistence (Quave et al., 2012).

Mechanistically, EA’s antibacterial efficacy is multifactorial and involves direct membrane disruption, metabolic interference, oxidative stress induction, and suppression of virulence regulation. The polyphenolic structure of EA enables interaction with lipid bilayers, causing membrane destabilization and leakage of intracellular components, while also interfering with essential enzymatic systems required for bacterial survival. A more advanced mechanism is observed in nanotechnology-based systems, where EA-mediated synthesis of AgNPs amplifies antimicrobial activity through ROS overproduction, mitochondrial-like stress in bacteria, and irreversible cellular damage (Ekrikaya et al., 2021). Furthermore, EA-functionalized magnetic nanoparticles downregulate efflux pump genes (acrB, tolC), thereby increasing intracellular antibiotic accumulation and reversing resistance phenotypes (Norouzalinia et al., 2025). Complementary studies show that EA also inhibits NorA-type efflux systems in S. aureus, restoring antibiotic susceptibility (Macêdo et al., 2022). Beyond direct antibacterial action, EA modulates host-pathogen interactions by enhancing mucosal immunity and regulating immunoglobulin responses during Aeromonas hydrophila infection (Abuelsaad et al., 2013). In summary, these converging mechanisms position EA as a multifunctional antimicrobial agent with direct bactericidal, anti-biofilm, efflux inhibitory, and host-immunomodulatory properties.

Biological effects of EA in poultry

Ellagic acid exerts multiple biological effects that support poultry health and productivity. Substitute EA positively influences gut health by promoting intestinal barrier integrity, improving villus morphology, and modulating the composition of beneficial gut microbiota, which collectively enhance nutrient digestion and absorption. Impact on gut health and intestinal morphology

Dietary EA consistently demonstrates multifaceted role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and enhancing poultry performance under pathogenic, metabolic, and environmental stress conditions (Fig. 3). Across broilers, quails, pigeons, and laying birds, EA supplementation (typically 100-400 mg/kg) converges on a central mechanism involving the reinforcement of intestinal barrier integrity, largely through upregulation of tight-junction proteins (claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1) and suppression of epithelial permeability indicators such as serum diamine oxidase (Li et al., 2026a). In E. coli K88 and Clostridium perfringens challenge models, EA mitigates mucosal damage by reducing oxidative stress (lower MDA levels) while enhancing endogenous antioxidant defense systems (Li et al., 2024). Mechanistically, these effects are strongly linked to EA-mediated inhibition of NF-κB signaling, leading to downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and attenuation of inflammation-driven epithelial disruption, thereby preserving mucosal architecture and nutrient absorption capacity (Tang et al., 2022).Fig. 3Mechanistic framework illustrating the protective effects of dietary ellagic acid supplementation in poultry by improving gut health, immune status and, and stress mitigation. Ellagic acid exerts multifaceted protective effects by modulating the gut-immune-redox axis. It enhances intestinal morphology, strengthens barrier integrity via tight-junction proteins, improves microbial balance, increases SCFA production, and reduces pathogenic bacteria and endotoxin leakage. EA also supports immune organ development, enhances immunoglobulins, suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines while elevating IL-10, and improves macrophage-mediated pathogen clearance. Additionally, it activates Nrf2 signaling, upregulates antioxidant enzymes, and reduces oxidative damage. These coordinated actions improve growth performance, nutrient utilization, and stress resilience.

Comparatively, in non-infectious stress models such as heat stress and growth transition phases, EA exerts similar protective outcomes but primarily through redox-microbiota-metabolism crosstalk. In heat-stressed broilers and quails, EA reduces lipid peroxidation and restores systemic biochemical balance, indicating its role as a potent phenolic antioxidant that directly scavenges ROS, while simultaneously activating endogenous enzymatic defenses (Yang et al., 2022a; MUTLU and Güler 2021). Moreover, EA-driven modulation of gut microbiota, characterized by increased beneficial taxa such as Alistipes and lactic acid bacteria, along with enhanced short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, emerges as a key upstream regulator of gut integrity, immune competence (elevated IgA, IgG, IgM), and metabolic efficiency (Wang et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2026). SCFAs further strengthen epithelial barrier function and serve as signaling metabolites linking microbial activity to host gene expression (Ren et al., 2026a). Collectively, these converging pathways, antioxidant activation, NF-κB suppression, microbiota reshaping, and SCFA-mediated epithelial signaling, explain the broad-spectrum efficacy of EA in improving growth performance, nutrient utilization, and intestinal health across poultry species.

Impact on growth performance and feed efficiency

In feed supplementation of EA demonstrates significant growth-promoting effect in poultry, with improvements in average daily gain, feed conversion ratio, and nutrient digestibility primarily driven by enhanced digestive enzyme activity (chymotrypsin, pepsin, and lipase) and improved intestinal absorptive capacity (Fig. 3). Mechanistically, these responses are closely linked to EA-mediated reinforcement of gut barrier integrity, characterized by improved villus height and villus-to-crypt ratio, alongside reduced intestinal permeability and systemic endotoxemia (Wang et al., 2024b). Comparative evidence from avian species further indicates dose-dependent and tissue-specific responses, where moderate supplementation (around 200 mg/kg) produces the most balanced improvements in growth and gut microbial stability, while lower doses preferentially enhance immune organ development and higher doses favor structural growth traits such as muscle deposition and skeletal dimensions (Ren et al., 2026a). These findings suggest that herbal bioactives acts as a multifunctional phytochemical modulator integrating digestive efficiency, immunometabolic regulation, and oxidative stress resilience, thereby optimizing nutrient partitioning toward growth (Zheng et al., 2026; Lin and Li 2026).

In parallel, plant-derived by-products rich in EA and hydrolyzable tannins, such as pomegranate peel, grape pomace, and eucalyptus leaves, exhibit complementary but partially overlapping mechanisms in enhancing poultry productivity. Pomegranate peel supplementation improves growth performance and lipid metabolism by reducing serum cholesterol, triglycerides, and hepatic enzyme activity, while simultaneously enhancing protein metabolism and immune responsiveness, indicating a systemic metabolic reprogramming toward improved nutrient efficiency (Mostafa and Ameen 2026; Choudhari et al., 2026). Similarly, hydrolyzable tannins combined with organic acids exert synergistic effects by improving villus architecture, increasing absorptive surface area, and modulating gut microbiota through suppression of pathogenic Escherichia coli and enrichment of Lactobacillus spp., thereby reinforcing mucosal immunity and reducing abdominal fat deposition (Khorasgani et al., 2025). EA enriched grape pomace extract further extends these benefits through early-life (in-ovo) programming of antioxidant defenses and humoral immunity, leading to sustained improvements in post-hatch growth performance (Madkour et al., 2024). When comparatively integrated, these phytogenic interventions converge on three central biological axes: (i) enhancement of gut morphology and microbiota stability, (ii) modulation of systemic antioxidant and inflammatory signaling, and (iii) optimization of metabolic efficiency and nutrient utilization. However, EA appears more mechanistically targeted toward intracellular redox signaling pathways (SIRT1/Nrf2), whereas tannin-rich and fiber-associated by-products exert stronger ecological effects on gut microbial ecology and lipid metabolism. This functional complementarity highlights the potential of precision phytogenic nutrition, where strategic combination and dose optimization of polyphenol-rich feed additives could synergistically maximize growth performance while maintaining gut health and metabolic homeostasis in poultry production systems.

Impact on egg production

EA has emerged as a potent nutraceutical for improving egg production through multi-targeted regulation of ovarian function, redox balance, and cellular metabolism in laying birds. At dietary levels of 100–500 mg/kg, EA alleviates ovarian aging by suppressing ferroptosis, as indicated by reduced iron overload, lipid peroxidation, and downregulation of pro-ferroptotic genes (TFRC, PTGS2), alongside upregulation of GPX4 and endogenous antioxidant systems (Yuan et al., 2025b). Mechanistically, this protection preserves granulosa cell viability, maintains mitochondrial integrity, and supports follicular development, collectively translating into improved ovarian endocrine activity and laying persistence. Similar antioxidative and anti-apoptotic effects have been observed under toxicological stress conditions (Rehman et al., 2025), where EA supplementation in quail exposed to lead mitigated oxidative damage, restored cellular integrity, and enhanced productive resilience (Mutlu et al., 2021). These findings indicate that EA acts primarily through reinforcement of redox homeostasis and inhibition of ferroptosis-driven ovarian degeneration, thereby sustaining reproductive efficiency under both physiological aging and environmental stressors.

Comparatively, pomegranate-enriched EA (peel extracts, seed pulp, and juice) exhibit broader but more variable effects on egg production, largely dependent on dose, formulation, and bioavailability. Moderate inclusion levels (≈500-750 mg/kg) consistently improve feed efficiency, nutrient digestibility, antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, CAT), hormonal balance, and gut microbiota composition, ultimately enhancing egg production performance and metabolic health (Xu et al., 2026; Hongrui et al., 2026). In contrast, higher or poorly standardized inclusions may produce neutral or even adverse outcomes, as seen with pomegranate juice (5-10%) showing minimal performance benefits (Gultepe et al., 2022), and pomegranate seed pulp increasing oxidative stress and serum cholesterol at higher levels (Saki et al., 2014). These inconsistencies likely reflect differences in polyphenolic composition, fiber content, and enzyme interactions affecting nutrient absorption and lipid metabolism. Mechanistically, while EA exerts a more targeted mitochondrial-ferroptotic regulatory effect, pomegranate by-products act through multifactorial pathways involving gut-liver axis modulation, lipid metabolism regulation, and systemic antioxidant enhancement. Together, these evidence-based comparisons highlight that precise phytochemical standardization and optimized dosing are critical to harness the full reproductive benefits of EA-based interventions in laying poultry systems.

Impact on heat stress mitigation

EA-enriched bioactives consistently demonstrate a multifaceted protective role against heat stress-induced physiological dysregulation in poultry, primarily through reinforcement of antioxidant defense networks and suppression of stress-mediated signaling cascades. Mechanistically, EA activates the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway, thereby upregulating endogenous antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GPx), while concurrently inhibiting NF-κB-driven inflammatory responses and downstream apoptotic signaling, collectively preserving cellular integrity under thermal challenge (Wang et al., 2024b). In heat-stressed broilers and laying quails, these molecular adaptations translate into improved redox homeostasis, reduced lipid peroxidation (e.g., MDA), and enhanced systemic resilience, indicating that EA functions not merely as an antioxidant supplement but as a transcriptional modulator of stress-response pathways (Yang et al., 2022a; MUTLU and Güler 2021). Comparative evidence further suggests that EA’s efficacy is amplified under co-stress conditions such as heavy metal exposure (e.g., lead toxicity), where it mitigates productivity losses by restoring antioxidant capacity and limiting cellular injury (Mutlu et al., 2021), highlighting its broad-spectrum cytoprotective potential.

At the physiological and integrative level, EA and related pomegranate derivatives (peel extract and pulp) exert convergent benefits on performance, gut integrity, lipid metabolism, and immune competence, though with variability depending on dose, formulation, and stress intensity. In heat-stressed poultry, EA and herbal extract improves growth performance, feed efficiency, and egg production while enhancing nutrient digestibility and modulating gut microbiota toward beneficial taxa, thereby strengthening intestinal barrier function (MUTLU and Güler 2021; Su et al., 2023). Similarly, pomegranate peel extract and pulp supplementation, reduce pathogenic bacterial load, and enhance humoral immunity (e.g., increased antibody titers), collectively reinforcing systemic adaptation to heat stress (Sharifian et al., 2019; Hosseini-Vashan and Raei-Moghadam 2019). Notably, lipid metabolism is also favorably modulated, evidenced by reduced plasma cholesterol, LDL, and abdominal fat deposition, indicating a shift toward improved metabolic efficiency under thermal stress. Conclusively, these integrative effects suggest that EA-enriched supplements act at the interface of redox regulation, gut–immune crosstalk, and metabolic remodeling, positioning them as promising nutrigenomic interventions for enhancing thermotolerance and productivity in poultry systems.

Synergistic effects of EA with other feed additives

Synergistic nutritional strategies combining with phytogenic compounds, plant extracts, or probiotics consistently demonstrate superior bio-efficacy compared with single supplementation, primarily due to complementary mechanisms targeting oxidative stress, gut integrity, and microbial homeostasis (Wang et al., 2026b; Ren et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2025a). In broiler chickens, pomegranate peel infusion enhanced growth performance and feed efficiency by improving nutrient utilization and reinforcing endogenous antioxidant defenses, thereby reducing oxidative stress–mediated metabolic constraints and restoring physiological homeostasis (Ghosh et al., 2020). Similarly, combined supplementation of EA with Punica granatum mesocarp extract in laying hens improved productive traits, including feed intake, body weight, and egg production, while simultaneously enhancing reproductive performance and hatchability. These effects were mechanistically linked to reduced lipid peroxidation (MDA) and upregulation of key antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPx, TAC), indicating a coordinated redox-regulatory response that supports both somatic and reproductive efficiency under metabolic demand (Gul et al., 2022; Nabi et al., 2020a). These findings suggest that polyphenol-rich botanical matrices may potentiate EA bioactivity through additive antioxidant capacity and improved bioavailability of phenolic metabolites (Chen et al., 2022).

EA-based combinations also exhibit cross-species and microbiota-mediated synergism that integrates metabolic, immunological, and intestinal signaling pathways. In poultry disease models, co-administration of EA-enriched pomegranate peel extract with probiotics (Bacillus subtilis and Lactobacillus spp.) significantly reduced Eimeria tenella burden, lesion scores, and inflammatory responses, reflecting enhanced gut resilience through microbial restoration and competitive exclusion (Wang et al., 2026a). Mechanistically, Lactobacillus brevis 23017 combined with EA activated the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signaling pathway, thereby strengthening endogenous antioxidant defenses and suppressing inflammation during coccidial infection (Yang et al., 2022b). Collectively, these integrative interactions indicate that EA acts not only as a direct antioxidant but also as a microbiota-modulating and signaling-regulatory molecule, providing a mechanistic rationale for its use in precision nutrition strategies aimed at improving productivity and disease resistance.

Sustainability and circular feed strategies for livestock

EA offers a promising sustainability dimension when recovered from agro-industrial residues, particularly pomegranate peel, rind, pulp, and pomace generated during juice and fruit processing. Pomegranate peel constitutes approximately 30-40% of the fruit and contains substantial amounts of hydrolysable tannins, including ellagitannins that can yield EA, making this biomass an important feedstock for bioactive recovery rather than disposal (Xiang et al., 2022). Integrating green extraction or biorefinery approaches could enable sequential recovery of EA-rich phenolics, dietary fiber, oils, and other coproducts, thereby improving resource efficiency and reducing processing waste (Arun et al., 2022). The residual biomass can subsequently be incorporated into livestock and poultry diets as a functional feed ingredient, supporting circular feed systems while potentially improving antioxidant, immune, and productive responses (Natalello et al., 2020). Thus, converting EA-rich fruit residues into standardized feed additives can create a waste-to-feed-to-value pathway that simultaneously reduces agro-industrial waste, lowers dependence on conventional feed resources, and generates additional economic value within circular bioeconomies.

Conclusion and future prospects

In conclusion, available poultry evidence indicates that phytogenic EA can attenuate oxidative and inflammatory disturbances, strengthen intestinal barrier function, modulate microbial communities, and support nutrient utilization and productive performance. These interconnected actions provide a mechanistic basis for its potential to improve growth, feed efficiency, immune competence, egg production, meat quality, and resilience to environmental and infectious challenges. The conversion of EA to microbiota-derived urolithins further suggests that its biological activity should be considered within a broader diet-microbiota-host framework rather than attributed exclusively to the parent compound. Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains heterogeneous, with substantial variation in dose, dietary matrix, EA source and purity, formulation, bird genotype, age, production system, and experimental stress conditions along with aqueous solubility and limited bioavailability.

Future research should therefore move from proof-of-concept supplementation studies toward mechanism-driven and standardized investigations. Priority areas include defining dose-response relationships and safety margins across poultry species and production stages; characterizing EA metabolites, particularly urolithins, and their contribution to biological responses; and developing delivery and formulation technologies that improve stability, bioavailability, and reproducibility. Integrative multi-omics approaches combining metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics should be used to resolve diet-microbiota-host interactions and identify reliable molecular biomarkers of response. Particular attention should also be given to standardized EA-rich agro-industrial by-products, allowing simultaneous evaluation of phytochemical efficacy, product quality, circular feed-resource utilization, and environmental benefits.
