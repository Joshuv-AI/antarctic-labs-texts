Finite-Particle Rates for Regularized Stein Variational Gradient Descent


Abstract
We derive finite-particle rates for the regularized Stein variational gradient descent (R-SVGD) algorithm introduced by He et al. 2024 that corrects the constant-order bias of the SVGD by applying a resolvent-type preconditioner to the kernelized Wasserstein gradient. For the resulting interacting N N -particle system, we establish explicit non-asymptotic bounds for time-averaged (annealed) empirical measures, illustrating convergence in the true (non-kernelized) Fisher information and, under a W 1 ​ I \mathrm{W}_{1}\mathrm{I} condition on the target, corresponding W 1 \mathrm{W}_{1} convergence for a large class of smooth kernels. Our analysis covers both continuous- and discrete-time dynamics and yields principled tuning rules for the regularization parameter, step size, and averaging horizon that quantify the trade-off between approximating the Wasserstein gradient flow and controlling finite-particle estimation error.



1
Introduction

Given a potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we consider the problem of sampling from the target density
\pi(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}
where
Z=\smallint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V(x)}\,dx,

where the normalizing constant
Z
is typically unknown or intractable. This task is ubiquitous in Bayesian statistics, machine learning, and numerical integration. A useful modern viewpoint interprets sampling as
optimization over probability measures

(
Jordan et al. 1998
;
Wibisono 2018
)
based on the Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF) of the relative entropy,

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot\!\left(\rho_{t}\nabla_{W_{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\rho_{t}\mid\pi)\right)=\nabla\cdot\!\left(\rho_{t}\nabla\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right).

(1)

Exact space–time discretization of (
1
) by empirical measures is impossible due to the diffusion term, motivating deterministic approximations via mollification and related constructions.

Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) is a prominent deterministic approximation scheme proposed by
Liu and Wang 2016
;
Liu 2017
, that has been widely adopted in machine learning and applied mathematics as a deterministic discretzation of (
1
). It corresponds to a kernelized, constant-order approximation of the WGF, often written as the Stein Variational Gradient Flow (SVGF)

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot\!\left(\rho_{t}\,\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu_{t}}\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\right),

(2)

where
\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho}
is the integral operator induced by the reproducing kernel
k
; see Section
F
for a short overview of the
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
background required for this work. While this kernelization yields a practical deterministic particle dynamics, it also introduces a systematic bias: for common bounded translation-invariant characteristic kernels,
\|\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu_{t}}-I\|_{\mathrm{op}}
need not be small, so the quality of the approximation depends critically on the kernel choice.

To address this,
He et al. 2024
proposed the
Regularized
-Stein Variational Gradient Flow (R-SVGF) that applies a resolvent-type correction,

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot\!\left(\rho_{t}\,\big((1-\nu)\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho_{t}}+\nu I\big)^{-1}\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho_{t}}\nabla\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right),

(3)

with
\nu\in(0,1]
. The factor
\big((1-\nu)\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho_{t}}+\nu I\big)^{-1}\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho_{t}}
“de-biases” the kernelized gradient: whenever
\nabla\log(\rho_{t}/\pi)
lies in (or is sufficiently regular relative to) the range of
\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho_{t}}
, this correction converges to the true Wasserstein gradient as
\nu\to 0
, with a rate that improves under additional smoothness.

Rates of convergence to equilibrium in under various assumption for the WGF is relatively well-studied
(
Ambrosio et al. 2005
)
. Recently, several works established similar rates for the SVGF
(
Liu 2017
;
Lu et al. 2019
;
Chewi et al. 2020
;
Duncan et al. 2023
;
Carrillo et al. 2024
)
and R-SVGF
(
He et al. 2024
)
. Convergence rates for time-discretizations of SVGF have also been studied by various authors including
Korba et al. 2020
,
Salim et al. 2022
, and
Sun et al. 2023
.

Practical implementations of SVGF and R-SVGF approximate the evolving density with
N
interacting particles and a time discretization, so a complete convergence theory ultimately requires
finite-particle, discrete-time
rates—yet such results for SVGD have been notoriously difficult. As a first step toward this goal, several works studied
mean-field
convergence (finite-particle dynamics to the limiting flow in continuous time) using PDE and propagation-of-chaos techniques
(
Lu et al. 2019
;
Gorham et al. 2020
;
Korba et al. 2020
;
Carrillo and Skrzeczkowski 2025
)
. Moving to discrete time,
Shi and Mackey 2024
obtained the first finite-particle rates in the KSD metric (cf. (
6
)), but only at the sub-optimal order
1/\sqrt{\log\log N}
;
Liu et al. 2024
proved improved parametric rates in the special
Gaussian SVGD
setting (Gaussian target and initialization, bilinear kernel); and most recently,
Banerjee et al. 2025
established state-of-the-art
1/\sqrt{N}
finite-particle, continuous and discrete-time rates, in KSD (a double-exponential improvement over
Shi and Mackey 2024
) and, leveraging
Kanagawa et al. 2025
, also derived
W_{2}
guarantees with Matérn kernels.

Our main results provide
finite-particle, non-asymptotic
guarantees for regularized SVGD in both continuous and discrete time, and they clarify how regularization enables control in
true
non-kernalized Fisher information rather than only kernelized (Stein) quantities. In continuous time, we show that an annealed (time-averaged) version of the empirical law enjoys quantitative decay of the Fisher information along the interacting particle dynamics. Concretely, the average Fisher information over a time horizon
[0,T]
can be bounded by a sum of (i) an initialization term that decreases as the run time increases, (ii) an interaction term of order
1/N
, and (iii) a regularization-induced term that grows with
T
but is suppressed by larger
N
and larger
\nu
. Choosing an appropriate averaging window (of order
N^{2/3}
) balances these effects and yields explicit rates for the time-averaged measure
\mu_{av}^{N}
, showing that the Fisher information (and, under a standard
\mathrm{W}_{1}\mathrm{I}
inequality for the target, also the
\mathrm{W}_{1}
distance) decreases with
N
at rates that are
kernel-agnostic in the exponent
. This stands in contrast with classical SVGD analyses, which typically control only the weaker Stein Fisher information and may require specialized structured kernels to obtain Wasserstein guarantees.

In discrete time, we complement the continuous-time theory with step-size and regularization schedules that make the finite-
N
bounds explicit for the practical R-SVGD algorithm. A resulting corollary identifies two regimes: when
\nu
is kept very close to
1
, the method behaves like SVGD and achieves an
N^{-1}
-type accuracy in the regularized Stein metric (up to dimension- and kernel-dependent factors), matching the usual Monte Carlo-type rates for averaged quantities. Moreover, when
\nu
is allowed to
decrease
with
N
, the dynamics become progressively closer to the Wasserstein gradient flow, and we can guarantee convergence in the
true
non-kernelized Fisher information and in
\mathrm{W}_{1}
for the annealed iterate—at the price of a slower rate that explicitly captures the estimation cost of inverting the regularized operator. The theory pinpoints a sharp trade-off:
\nu
should not decay too fast (roughly, slower than
N^{-1/3}
in the stated regime) to ensure the finite-particle error still vanishes, thereby providing principled guidance for tuning
\nu
,
h
, and the averaging horizon in practice.

2
Preliminaries on Regularized Stein Variational Gradient Descent

We now provide a brief description of the derivation of the
Regularized Stein Variational Gradient Flow
(R-SVGF) by
He et al. 2024
, and clarify how it connects to both the Stein Variational Gradient Flow (SVGF) and the Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF). The derivation proceeds from the standard variational characterization of steepest descent of
\mathrm{KL}(\rho|\pi)
under infinitesimal transport maps. Consider a perturbation of the form
T(x)=x+h\phi(x)
, where
h>0
is small and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth vector field. The key object is the
Stein operator
associated with a density
p
,
\mathcal{A}_{p}\phi(x)=\phi(x)\otimes\nabla\log p(x)+\nabla\phi(x),
whose trace controls the first variation of the KL divergence: for
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
Liu and Wang 2016
showed that
\left.\nabla_{h}\,\mathrm{KL}(T_{\#}\rho\mid\pi)\right|_{h=0}=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\!\left[\mathrm{trace}\big(\mathcal{A}_{\pi}\phi(x)\big)\right]
. Thus, choosing
\phi
to maximize
\big(\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathrm{trace}(\mathcal{A}_{\pi}\phi)]\big)^{2}
over a unit ball yields the direction of steepest KL descent. If the unit ball is taken in
L_{2}^{d}(\rho)
, then an integration-by-parts argument identifies the maximizer with the Wasserstein gradient
\nabla\log(\rho/\pi)
, recovering the WGF. SVGD instead restricts
\phi
to the unit ball of the vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}
, which yields the kernelized direction
\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho}\nabla\log(\rho/\pi)
and hence the SVGF.

He et al. 2024
chose to use a regularization that interpolates between these two geometries by maximizing the same Stein objective over an
interpolated norm
:
\nu\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}}^{2}+(1-\nu)\|\phi\|_{L_{2}^{d}(\rho)}^{2}\leq 1,\nu\in(0,1]
. This constraint trades off RKHS regularity and
L_{2}(\rho)
fidelity, and leads to a closed-form optimal vector field expressed via the RKHS inclusion operator
\iota_{k,\rho}:\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}\to L_{2}^{d}(\rho)
and its adjoint
\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}
. Under mild assumptions (finite Fisher information and
\int k(x,x)\rho(x)\,dx<\infty
), the maximizing direction can be written equivalently as

\phi_{\rho,\pi}^{*}\ \propto\ -\Big((1-\nu)\,\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho}+\nu I_{d}\Big)^{-1}\,\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\!\left(\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right),

which reduces to SVGD as
\nu\to 1
and approaches the Wasserstein gradient as
\nu\downarrow 0
(in the regimes quantified later). Plugging
\phi_{\rho_{t},\pi}^{*}
into the continuity equation yields the mean-field R-SVGF PDE:

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot\!\left(\rho_{t}\,\iota_{k,\rho_{t}}\Big((1-\nu)\,\iota_{k,\rho_{t}}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho_{t}}+\nu I_{d}\Big)^{-1}\iota_{k,\rho_{t}}^{*}\!\left(\nabla\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)\right).

Finally, the operator appearing above admits an important
alternative form
that makes the connection to SVGF explicit. Using the identity

\iota_{k,\rho}\Big((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho}+\nu I_{d}\Big)^{-1}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}=\Big((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}+\nu I\Big)^{-1}\iota_{k,\rho}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*},

and recalling
\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho}=\iota_{k,\rho}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}
, we obtain the compact expression introduced in (
3
). This equivalent formulation highlights R-SVGF as a resolvent-corrected version of SVGF: the flow applies
\big((1-\nu)\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho}+\nu I\big)^{-1}
to “undo” the non-invertibility and constant-order bias induced by
\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho}
, while preserving the kernel-based structure that enables deterministic particle implementations.

Particle-based spatial discretization (R-SVGD dynamics).

To obtain an implementable algorithm, we discretize the mean-field R-SVGF PDE by restricting the evolving law
\rho_{t}
to the class of empirical measures supported on finitely many particles. Concretely, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
we consider
\rho_{t}^{N}\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}(t)},
where
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are deterministic particles. This choice can be viewed as a spatial discretization of the continuity equation associated with the R-SVGF: the velocity field is obtained by substituting
\rho_{t}
with
\rho_{t}^{N}
in the
optimal
steepest-descent direction derived from the regularized Stein variational problem. The key idea is to choose the transport direction that most rapidly decreases
\mathrm{KL}(\rho\mid\pi)
while balancing smoothness (through the RKHS norm) and fidelity to the current distribution (through the
L_{2}(\rho)
norm), which leads to a resolvent-preconditioned Stein update that admits equivalent formulations in terms of either RKHS embedding operators or the associated kernel integral operator; see
(
He et al. 2024
, Section 3)
. As a result, each particle moves according to the same regularized Stein vector field evaluated at its current location, leading to the interacting ODE system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x}^{i}(t)&=-\left((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho^{N}(t)}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho^{N}(t)}+\nu I_{d}\right)^{-1}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}-\nabla_{2}k\left(x^{i}(t),x^{j}(t)\right)+k\left(x^{i}(t),x^{j}(t)\right)\nabla V(x^{j}(t))\bigg)\\
x^{i}(0)&=x^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 9.24994pti=1,2,\ldots,N\end{aligned}\right.,

where
\nu\in(0,1]
,
\{x^{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
is the set of
N
particles,

\nabla_{2}k(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the gradient of
k
with respect to its second argument, and
\iota_{k,\rho_{t}^{N}}:\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}\to L_{2}^{d}(\rho_{t}^{N})
is the RKHS embedding induced by the empirical measure (with adjoint
\iota_{k,\rho_{t}^{N}}^{*}
). The term inside the parentheses is the familiar SVGD-type “Stein force”: it combines an
attractive
drift term (through
\nabla V
) that pushes particles toward regions of high target density, and a
repulsive
interaction term (through
\nabla_{2}k
) that discourages particle collapse. The distinctive feature of R-SVGD is the preconditioning by the regularized inverse
\big((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho_{t}^{N}}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho_{t}^{N}}+\nu I_{d}\big)^{-1}
, which corrects the non-invertibility of the kernel operator and mitigates the constant-order bias present in SVGF. In particular, as
\nu\to 1
the preconditioner reduces to
I_{d}
and we recover the usual SVGD particle dynamics, whereas as
\nu\downarrow 0
the dynamics increasingly “de-kernelizes” the gradient direction, bringing the particle flow closer to the Wasserstein gradient flow in the regimes quantified later.

R-SVGD Algorithm.

Let
(X_{n}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the particle locations at iteration
n
, and write

\underline{X}_{n}\coloneqq[X_{n}^{1},\ldots,X_{n}^{N}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
for the stacked particle vector. For any vector field
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the evaluation operator
L_{n}f\;\coloneqq\;[f(X_{n}^{1}),\ldots,f(X_{n}^{N})]^{\intercal}.

The R-SVGD update is obtained by an explicit time discretization of the regularized particle dynamics, where the kernel interaction is encoded through the Gram matrix. Specifically, let
K_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be the Gram matrix with entries
(K_{n})_{ij}=k(X_{n}^{i},X_{n}^{j})
for
i,j\in[N]
, and let
I_{N}
denote the
N\times N
identity. Given a step-size sequence
(h_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and a regularization schedule
(\nu_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in(0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
, the particles are updated according to

\underline{X}_{n+1}=\underline{X}_{n}-h_{n+1}\left(\frac{1-\nu_{n+1}}{N}K_{n}+\nu_{n+1}I_{N}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}K_{n}(L_{n}\nabla V)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}L_{n}\nabla k(X_{n}^{j},\cdot)\right),

(4)

where
\nabla k(X_{n}^{j},\cdot)
denotes the map
x\mapsto\nabla_{2}k(X_{n}^{j},x)
evaluated at the particle locations via
L_{n}
. The term
\frac{1}{N}K_{n}(L_{n}\nabla V)
corresponds to the attractive drift induced by the target potential
V
, while
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}L_{n}\nabla k(X_{n}^{j},\cdot)
is the repulsive interaction that promotes particle diversity. The matrix factor
\left(\frac{1-\nu_{n+1}}{N}K_{n}+\nu_{n+1}I_{N}\right)^{-1}
plays the role of the regularized inverse (a resolvent-type preconditioner), correcting the kernelization bias: when
\nu_{n+1}\approx 1
the update reduces to SVGD, whereas smaller
\nu_{n+1}
increasingly de-kernelizes the direction and moves the method closer to the Wasserstein gradient flow regime analyzed later.

3
Convergence of Finite-Particle R-SVGF

We start with the following assumptions on the kernel
k
and the potential function
V
.

Assumption 1
.

The kernel function
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies the following conditions: [(1)]
k
is symmetric, [(2)]
k
is positive semi-definite, i.e.,
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)k(x,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and [(3)] (Boundedness)
k
and all its partial derivatives up to order
2
are uniformly bounded by
B\in(0,\infty)
.

Assumption
1
holds, for example, for the Gaussian/RBF kernel
k(x,y)=\exp\!\big(-\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}/(2\ell^{2})\big)
, the rational quadratic kernel
k(x,y)=\big(1+\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}/(2\alpha\ell^{2})\big)^{-\alpha}
, Matérn kernels with smoothness parameter
\nu>1
(ensuring bounded derivatives up to order two), the inverse multiquadric (IMQ) kernel
k(x,y)=(c^{2}+\|x-y\|_{2}^{2})^{-\beta}
, and sufficiently smooth compactly supported Wendland kernels. In contrast, unbounded kernels such as polynomial kernels do not satisfy (3) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 2
.

The target potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is twice continuously differentiable and smooth, and there exists a constant
C_{V}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\|\nabla^{2}V(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\,C_{V}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
denotes the operator norm.

This assumption enforces global
C^{2}
-smoothness of
V
with a uniformly bounded Hessian, i.e., the gradient
\nabla V
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant at most
C_{V}
. We now introduce the following discrepancy measures which are used in the subsequent analysis:

\displaystyle I(\rho|\pi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi},\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right\rangle_{L_{2}^{d}(\rho)},

(5)

\displaystyle I_{\text{Stein}}(\rho|\pi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi},\iota_{k,\rho}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right\rangle_{L_{2}^{d}(\rho)},

(6)

\displaystyle I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho|\pi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi},\left((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho}+\nu I_{d}\right)^{-1}\iota_{k,\rho}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right\rangle_{L_{2}^{d}(\rho)}.

(7)

These quantities measure the discrepancy between
\rho
and
\pi
through the score
\nabla\log(\rho/\pi)
under different geometries.
The standard Fisher information
I(\rho|\pi)
is the
L_{2}(\rho)
-squared norm of this score and corresponds to the “full” Wasserstein gradient magnitude, while
I_{\mathrm{Stein}}(\rho|\pi)
(also known as the
\text{KSD}^{2}
distance) replaces it by its kernelized version via the integral operator
\iota_{k,\rho}\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}=\mathcal{T}_{k,\rho}
, matching the SVGF geometry.
Finally,
I_{\nu,\mathrm{Stein}}(\rho\mid\pi)
, which was introduced by
He et al. 2024
and referred to as the
regularized Fisher information
, inserts the resolvent-type preconditioner
\big((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho}+\nu I_{d}\big)^{-1}
, interpolating between the Stein and Wasserstein scales and recovering the latter as
\nu\downarrow 0
under suitable regularity.

3.1
Convergence of regularized Fisher information

The convergence of the continuous-time finite particle system depends on the interaction between the kernel function
k
and the potential function
V
. As in
Banerjee et al. 2025
, the (regularized) gradient flow structure of the dynamics leads to the Kullback-Leibler (
\mathsf{KL}
) distance between the
joint density

p^{N}(\cdot)
of the particle locations and the
N
-fold target
\pi^{\otimes N}
behaving as an
approximate Lyapunov function
with a dominant decreasing component in time, characterized in terms of the regularized Fisher information
I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}
. However, the dependence of the driving vector field of any particle on its own location, which is negligible in the population limit, does exert a non-trivial effect on the finite particle dynamics. This is captured through the
self-interaction coefficient

C^{*}
described as follows. For
\underline{x}=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, let

K\;=\;\big(k(x^{i},x^{j})\big)_{i,j\in[N]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\qquad K_{\nu}\;\coloneqq\;\Big(\tfrac{1-\nu}{N}K+\nu I_{N}\Big)^{-1},

and define (with
\nabla_{1},\nabla_{2}
and
\Delta_{2}
denoting derivatives with respect to the first/second argument)

\displaystyle C^{*}(\underline{x})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}K^{\nu}_{ij}\big(\Delta_{2}k(x^{j},x^{i})-\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\nabla V(x^{i})-k(x^{j},x^{i})\Delta V(x^{i})\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\nu}{\nu}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j,l=1}^{N}k(x^{i},x^{l})K^{\nu}_{li}\big(\nabla_{1}\cdot\nabla_{2}k(x^{i},x^{j})-\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\nabla V(x^{j})\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\nu}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{N}K^{\nu}_{ij}K^{\nu}_{ik}\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\big(\nabla_{2}k({x^{k}},x^{l})-k({x^{k}},x^{l})\nabla V(x^{l})\big)

\displaystyle-\frac{(1-\nu)^{2}}{\nu}\frac{1}{N^{3}}\sum_{i,j,k,l,r=1}^{N}K^{\nu}_{ri}K^{\nu}_{jk}k(x^{i},x_{r})\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\big(\nabla_{2}k({x^{k}},x^{l})-k({x^{k}},x^{l})\nabla V(x^{l})\big).

(8)

Unlike in the usual SVGD framework
(
Banerjee et al. 2025
)
, this functional takes a significantly more complex form in the regularized set-up, as described below. The first line approaches the “SVGD-type” self-interaction term when
\nu\rightarrow 1
, while the remaining lines are
purely regularization-induced higher-order interactions
that originate from differentiating and coupling the resolvent preconditioner with the Stein drift. When
\nu=1
, the higher-order interactions disappear recovering the
C^{*}
from
Banerjee et al. 2025
. We also remark that the presence of these higher-order interaction terms make our proofs significantly more involved compared to the usual SVGD case in
Banerjee et al. 2025
, and we will highlight the differences in the rest of the draft.

As in
Banerjee et al. 2025
, the uniform boundedness of the SVGD type self-interaction term plays a crucial role which we capture in the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

Assume there exists
c^{*}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\Delta_{2}k(z,z)-\nabla_{1}k(z,z)\cdot\nabla V(z)-k(z,z)\Delta V(z)\big)\right|=c^{*}d.

(9)

In the following theorem, we exhibit the relationship between the decay rate of
\mathsf{KL},I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}
and the interaction-coefficient
C^{*}
which, in turn, furnishes rates of convergence in
I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}
under suitable assumptions.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
1
- (1) and (2), let
\{x^{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
be the set of
N
particles along the R-SVGD dynamics,
p^{N}(t)
be the joint distribution of
\underline{x}(t)\coloneqq(x^{1}(t),\cdots,x^{N}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
and
\rho^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{j}(t)}
be the empirical distribution at time
t
. Then for any
T>0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho_{av,T}^{N}|\pi)]\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(t)|\pi)\big]\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{NT}+\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[C^{*}(\underline{x}(t))]\mathrm{d}t}{NT},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(p^{N}(T)|\pi^{\otimes N})\leq\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[C^{*}(\underline{x}(t))]\mathrm{d}t,

(11)

where
C^{*}(\underline{x})
is given in (
8
),
p^{N}(0)=p_{0}^{N}
and let
\rho^{N}_{av,T}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\rho^{N}(t)\mathrm{d}t
, the time-averaged empirical measure of the particle system over the horizon. If we further assume Assumption
1
-(3) and Assumptions
2
and
3
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\underline{x}\sim p^{N}(t)}[C^{*}(\underline{x})]
can be estimated appropriately (see Lemma
1
and Remark
1
), and we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho_{av,T}^{N}|\pi)]\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(t)|\pi)\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{NT}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}+(1-\nu)\left(\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}]d^{\frac{1}{2}}}{N}+\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}dT^{2}}{\nu N^{2}}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(p^{N}(T)|\pi^{\otimes N})

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\mathsf{KL}(p^{N}_{0}|\pi^{\otimes N})+c^{*}dT+(1-\nu)\left(\beta_{1}dT+\beta_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}]d^{\frac{1}{2}}T+\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}dT^{3}}{\nu N}\right),

(13)

where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
are defined as

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=B(1+C_{V})+\frac{2B^{2}}{\nu^{2}}+\frac{B^{3}}{\nu^{3}}(1-\nu),\ \beta_{2}=B+\frac{2B^{2}}{\nu^{2}}+\frac{B^{3}}{\nu^{3}}(1-\nu).

(14)

Remark 1

(Challenge in estimating
\mathbb{E}[C^{*}(\underline{x}(t))]
)
.

Unlike in
Banerjee et al. 2025
, the quantity
\mathbb{E}[C^{*}(\underline{x}(t))]
in (
10
) and (
11
) cannot be simply bounded by a uniform constant that is linear in dimension
d
, when
\nu<1
. Instead, we bound
\mathbb{E}[C^{*}(\underline{x}(t))]
in terms of constants as well as the regularized Fisher information, in the form of
\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(s)|\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}]\mathrm{d}s
, along the trajectory of the process. Then the explicit bounds in (
12
) and (
13
) follow by a recursive integral inequality. See Lemma
1
and the proof of Theorem
1
in Appendix
B
for details.

Corollary 1
.

Define
\rho_{av}^{N}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}}\int_{0}^{T=(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}}\rho^{N}(t)\mathrm{d}t
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho_{av}^{N}|\pi)]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{5}{3}}}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}+(1-\nu)\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}}}{N}+\frac{\nu^{-1}\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}d}{(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}}.

In particular, if
\nu=\nu_{N}=1-\frac{1}{N}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu_{N},\text{Stein}}(\rho_{av}^{N}|\pi)]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{N^{2}}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}+\frac{\beta_{1,N}d+\beta_{2,N}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}}}{N^{2}}+\frac{\nu_{N}^{-1}\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}d}{N},

where
\beta_{1,N},\beta_{2,N}
are defined in (
14
) taking
\nu_{N}
in place of
\nu
.

Remark 2
.

Under the natural assumption that
\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})=\mathcal{O}(N)
, the choice of
T=(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}
above is made in order to match the order of the first and fourth terms in (
12
) (ones involving
T
). This ‘optimizes’ the bound (in the sense of maximizing decay rate order) and gives a decay rate for
\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho_{av}^{N}|\pi)]
of order
(1-\nu)^{\frac{1}{3}}N^{-\frac{2}{3}}
.

Remark 3

(Matching SVGD bounds under regularization for
\nu
close to
1
)
.

From the above result, we note that the bounds match the performance of SVGD in
Banerjee et al. 2025
when
\nu=1
. However, to match the order of the bounds, it suffices to take
\nu=\nu_{N}=1-\frac{1}{N}
. This choice will ensure that the regularization effect of R-SVGD still persists for every finite
N
, while its performance is at least as good as SVGD. However, as we will see in Section
3.2
(see Remark
5
), the efficacy of R-SVGD as an approximation to the Wasserstein gradient flow worsens as
\nu\rightarrow 1
.

Remark 4

(Convergence rate for small
\nu
)
.

Under the natural scaling
\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})=\mathcal{O}(N)
, the bound simplifies when
\nu\to 0
, as
(1-\nu)^{1/3}=1+o(1)
, to

\mathbb{E}\!\left[I_{\nu,\mathrm{Stein}}(\rho_{av}^{N}\mid\pi)\right]\;\lesssim\;\mathcal{O}\!\left({N^{-2/3}}\right)\;+\;\mathcal{O}\!\left({N^{-1}}\right)\;+\;\mathcal{O}\!\left({\nu^{-1}\,N^{-2/3}}\right).

Therefore, along any joint limit
N\to\infty
and
\nu=\nu_{N}\to 0
, the right-hand side goes to
0

if and only if

\nu_{N}\,N^{2/3}\to\infty

or equivalently,
\nu_{N}=\omega(N^{-2/3})
. For example, choosing
\nu_{N}=N^{-\alpha}
with any
\alpha\in(0,2/3)
and the near-minimal choice
\nu_{N}=N^{-2/3}\log N
respectively ensure convergence and yield the rates

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[I_{\nu_{N},\mathrm{Stein}}(\rho_{av}^{N}\mid\pi)\right]=\mathcal{O}\!\left(N^{-(2/3-\alpha)}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\!\left[I_{\nu_{N},\mathrm{Stein}}(\rho_{av}^{N}\mid\pi)\right]=\mathcal{O}\!\left({1}/{\log N}\right).

This shows that as
\nu
goes to zero, while the metric is strong (compared to
I_{\text{Stein}}
), the rates get worse.

Under exchangeability of the law of initial particles, Theorem
1
implies weak convergence to the target distribution of the time-averaged single-particle marginals.

Theorem 2
.

Under the conditions in Theorem
1
, suppose that the law
p_{0}^{N}
of the initial particles
x^{1}(0),x^{2}(0),\cdots,x^{N}(0)
is exchangeable for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{N}<\infty
.
Define
\bar{\rho}^{N}(\cdot)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}}\int_{0}^{(1-\nu)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}}\mathbb{P}(x^{1}(t)\in\cdot)\mathrm{d}t
. Then
I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\bar{\rho}^{N}|\pi)\to 0
and
\bar{\rho}^{N}\rightharpoonup\pi
as

, where ‘

\nu=\nu_{N}=1-\frac{1}{N}

3.2
Convergence in Fisher information and Wasserstein distance

In this section, we analyze convergence in Wasserstein-1 distance (
\mathrm{W}_{1}
) along the R-SVGD dynamics. Our analysis is performed in 2 parts: (1) deriving convergence in Fisher information based on the convergence in regularized Fisher information in Theorem
1
and the equivalence relation between Fisher information and the regularized Fisher information as studied in
He et al. 2024
; (2) deriving convergence of
\mathrm{W}_{1}
under the transport-information inequality assumption on the target distribution, relating the Fisher information convergence in (1) to
\mathrm{W}_{1}
-convergence.

Assumption 4

(Transport-Information
(\mathrm{W}_{1}\mathrm{I}
) inequality)
.

A distribution
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with parameter
C_{\mu}
if
W_{1}(\rho,\mu)\leq C_{\mu}\sqrt{I(\rho|\mu)},~~\forall\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We start from part (1) by stating the equivalence result in
He et al. 2024
. It is worth noting that the Fisher information
I(\cdot|\pi)
is not well-defined for empirical distributions. Therefore, instead of looking at
I(\rho^{N}(t)|\pi)
, we look at
I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
where
\mu^{N}(t)
is the population expectation of the empirical distribution
\rho^{N}(t)
defined as

\displaystyle\mu^{N}(t,A)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\underline{x}(0)\sim p_{0}^{N}}[\rho^{N}(t,A)]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{\underline{x}(0)\sim p_{0}^{N}}[x^{i}(t)\in A],\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(15)

When the initial condition
p_{0}^{N}
admits a continuous differentiable density,
\mu^{N}(t)
would admit a continuous differentiable density, hence making
I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
well-defined. Following
(
He et al. 2024
, Proposition 3)
, for any
t\in[0,T]
, assume that there exists
\gamma(t)\in(0,1/2]
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}(\mu^{N}(t),\pi)\|_{L_{2}^{d}(\mu^{N}(t))}<\infty,\quad\text{for }\mathcal{J}(\mu^{N}(t),\pi)\coloneqq(\iota_{k,\mu^{N}(t)}^{*}\iota_{k,\mu^{N}(t)})^{-\gamma(t)}\nabla\log\frac{\mu^{N}(t)}{\pi}.

This is essentially a smoothness assumption on
\mu^{N}(t)
and can be verified in special cases. Moreover, we assume
\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)}{2\|\mathcal{J}(\mu^{N}(t),\pi)\|_{L_{2}^{d}(\mu^{N}(t))}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma(t)}}>0
, in which case, there exists a regularization parameter
\nu\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\leq\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)}{2\|\mathcal{J}(\mu^{N}(t),\pi)\|_{L_{2}^{d}(\mu^{N}(t))}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma(t)}}.

(16)

Then we have that
I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
and
I_{\nu_{N},\text{Stein}}(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
are equivalent, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1-\nu)^{-1}I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)\leq I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)\leq(1-\nu)^{-1}I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi).

(17)

Combining the equivalence relation in (
17
) and the convergence results in Theorem
1
, we get convergence of the Fisher information along the R-SVGD dynamics.

Theorem 3
.

Under the conditions in Theorem
1
, for any
T>0
, consider
(\mu^{N}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
defined by (
15
). Moreover, suppose (
16
) is satisfied. Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{NT}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}+\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}]d^{\frac{1}{2}}}{N}+\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}dT^{2}}{\nu N^{2}}.

(18)

Furthermore, defining
\mu_{av}^{N}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{N^{2/3}}\int_{0}^{N^{2/3}}\mu^{N}(t)\mathrm{d}t
, we have

\displaystyle I(\mu_{av}^{N}|\pi)\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{N^{\frac{5}{3}}}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}+\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}}}{N^{2}}+\frac{\nu^{-1}\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}d}{N^{\frac{2}{3}}}.

(19)

If
\pi
satisfies the
\mathrm{W}_{1}\mathrm{I}
with parameter
C_{\pi}
, we have
\mathrm{W}_{1}(\mu_{av}^{N},\pi)^{2}\leq C_{\pi}^{2}I(\mu_{av}^{N}|\pi)
. Then immediately we obtain convergence of the time- and population- averaged empirical distribution
\mu_{av}^{N}
in
\mathrm{W}_{1}
along the R-SVGD dynamics from (
19
) in Theorem
3
.

Theorem 4
.

Under conditions in Theorem
3
, if the target distribution
\pi
satisfies
\mathrm{W}_{1}\mathrm{I}
with parameter
C_{\pi}
, then for any
\nu
satisfying (
16
), we have with
\tfrac{1}{N^{2/3}}\int_{0}^{N^{2/3}}\mu^{N}(t)\mathrm{d}t
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}_{1}(\mu_{av}^{N},\pi)^{2}\lesssim C_{\pi}^{2}\bigg(\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{N^{\frac{5}{3}}}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}+\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}}}{N^{2}}+\frac{\nu^{-1}\beta_{2}^{2}C_{V}^{2}d}{N^{\frac{2}{3}}}\bigg).

Remark 5
.

Theorems
3
and
4
highlight the main advantage of working with regularized SVGD. In contrast with the classical SVGD algorithm, we obtain convergence in (true) Fisher information for the annealed empirical measure
\mu^{N}
. In comparison, the rates in
Banerjee et al. 2025
are established only for the weaker
I_{\text{Stein}}
metric.

Observe that the left hand side of (
16
) diverges as
\nu\rightarrow 1
. This indicates that
I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
and
I(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
become more ‘singular’ with respect to each other as
\nu\rightarrow 1
. Thus, although R-SVGD matches the performance of SVGD in this regime in
I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\mu^{N}(t)|\pi)
, it will have a possibly worse convergence rate with respect to the Fisher information and Wasserstein metric.

In comparison to
Banerjee et al. 2025
, where dimension-dependent
W_{2}
rates were obtained for a special (Matérn) type of kernel for the true empirical measure, we obtain
W_{1}
rates with dimension-independent exponents which are agnostic to the choice of the kernel, but for the annealed measure.

4
Convergence of R-SVGD Algorithm

In this section, we study the convergence of the actual R-SVGD algorithm (
4
), in which the time-discretization with varying step-size and regularizers is introduced. We study the convergence by quantifying the decay rate of
\mathsf{KL}
in every single step of the algorithm.

In each step, we understand the R-SVGD algorithm as a transportation map on particles that push the empirical distribution of the particles towards the target distribution. For any
n\geq 1
, we define a transportation map
\mathsf{T}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\to\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, as follows

\displaystyle{\mathsf{T}}_{n}(\underline{x})=\big(\frac{1-\nu_{n}}{N}K(\underline{x})+\nu_{n}I_{N}\big)^{-1}\big(\frac{1}{N}K(\underline{x}){\nabla V}(\underline{x})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\nabla_{2}k}(\underline{x},x^{j})\big),

(20)

where
K(\underline{x})\coloneqq(k(x^{i},x^{j}))_{i,j\in[N]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the Gram matrix and
\phi(\underline{x})\coloneqq(\phi(x_{1}),\cdots,\phi(x_{N}))^{\intercal}
for all
\phi
. Then the
n^{th}
step of R-SVGD in (
4
) is exactly

\displaystyle\underline{X}_{n}=\Psi_{n}(h_{n},\underline{X}_{n-1})\text{ where }\Psi_{n}(t,\underline{x})\coloneqq\underline{x}-t\mathsf{T}_{n}(\underline{x}),\quad\forall(t,\underline{x})\in[0,h_{n}]\times\mathbb{R}^{dN}.

(21)

Therefore, the single-step decaying property of
\mathsf{KL}
along R-SVGD is equivalent to the decaying property
\mathsf{KL}
after applying a sequence of maps
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
in (
21
).

Our strategy of analyzing the decay of
\mathsf{KL}
after applying
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
follows from
Korba et al. 2020
. Denote
{p_{n}^{N}}=\text{Law}(\underline{X}_{n})
for all
n\geq 0
. We express
\{p_{n}^{N}\}_{n\geq 1}
as a sequence of push-forward measures through
\{\Psi_{n}(h_{n},\cdot)\}_{n\geq 1}
starting from
p_{0}^{N}
. For all
n\geq 0
and
t\in[0,h_{n+1}]
, define the probability measure
\nu_{n,t}\coloneqq\Psi_{n+1}(t,\cdot)_{\#{p_{n}^{N}}}
, hence
\nu_{n,0}={p_{n}^{N}}
and
\nu_{n,h_{n+1}}={p_{n+1}^{N}}
. Denote the density of
\nu_{n,t}
by
q_{n,t}
. We study the decay of
\mathsf{KL}(q_{n,t}|\pi^{\otimes N})
within the interval
[0,h_{n+1}]
for a single iteration and then use telescoping property to analyze the decay property for multiple iterations.

Like many existing works studying SVGD
(
Korba et al. 2020
;
Banerjee et al. 2025
)
, compared to continuous-time analysis, our discrete-time analysis requires extra (mild) assumptions on the kernel
k
and target potential
V
.

Assumption 5
.

We make the following assumptions; [(a)] Positivity:
\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(z)>0
, [(b)] Growth: There exist
A>0
and
\alpha\in[0,1/2]
s.t.
\|\nabla V(x)\|\leq AV(x)^{\alpha}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and [(c)] Initial entropy bound:
\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})\leq C_{\mathsf{KL}}Nd
for some constant
C_{\mathsf{KL}}>0
.

Remark 6
.

Condition (b) in Assumption
5
is related to the tail-heaviness of the target density
\pi
. When the tails of
\pi
decay slower than exponentially (including heavy tails), this corresponds to the choice
\alpha=0
, and
\alpha=1/2
corresponds to
\pi
having close to Gaussian tails. In our results, this tail decay rate will play a crucial role in tuning step sizes, regularization parameters and eventually in our convergence rates.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
, pick
\{(h_{n},\nu_{n})\}_{n=1}^{T}
s.t. for all
n=1,2,\cdots,T
,

\displaystyle\frac{h_{n}}{\nu_{n}}=\frac{\theta}{2}C_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{16(1-\alpha)^{2}C_{V}A^{2}B^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}h_{l}\nu_{l}^{-1}}\wedge\frac{N}{C_{V}B\nu_{n}+8Bd(1-\nu_{n})^{2}\nu_{n}^{-2}}\wedge\frac{1}{B}

(22)

for some
\theta\in[0,1]
and

\displaystyle C_{n}=18\max\{\nu_{n}^{-4}A^{2}B^{4}NdM^{2\alpha}\left(d^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}+K\right)^{2\alpha}(\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}h_{l}\nu_{l}^{-1}\vee 1)^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}},\nu_{n}^{-4}B^{4}Nd^{2}+B^{2}C_{V}^{2}d\}.

(23)

If the initial N-sample distribution
p_{0}^{N}
satisfies
\int_{\mathcal{S}_{K}}{p^{N}_{0,K}}(\underline{z})\mathrm{d}\underline{z}\geq 1/2

\displaystyle{p^{N}_{0,K}}\coloneqq p_{0}^{N}|\mathcal{S}_{K},\ \text{where }\ \mathcal{S}_{K}\coloneqq\left\{\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}:N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x^{i})\leq K\right\},

(24)

and
\gamma\coloneqq 2C_{\mathsf{KL}}+\log 2
, then we have, writing
\rho^{N}_{n}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{n}^{i}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{p^{N}_{0,K}}}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n=1}^{T}h_{n}I_{\nu_{n},\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}_{n-1}|\pi)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\gamma d}{T}+\frac{2c^{*}d\sum_{n=1}^{T}h_{n}}{NT}+\frac{2d\sum_{n=1}^{T}\beta_{1,n}(1-\nu_{n})h_{n}}{NT}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\theta\sum_{n=1}^{T}\beta_{2,n}(1-\nu_{n})\nu_{n}^{3}}{B^{2}N^{\frac{3}{2}}T}+\frac{\theta^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\nu_{n}^{2}}{4NT},

where
\beta_{1,n}
and
\beta_{2,n}
are given in (
14
) with
\nu=\nu_{n}
.

To derive the convergence of Fisher information along the dynamics of R-SVGD, we follow the strategy in Section

which is not well-defined, we look at

{\mu^{N}_{n,K}}

\displaystyle\mu^{N}_{n,K}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\underline{X}_{0}\sim{p^{N}_{0,K}}}[\rho_{n}^{N}]=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\underline{X}_{0}\sim{p^{N}_{0,K}}}[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{n}^{i}}].

(25)

When the initial condition
{p^{N}_{0,K}}
admits a continuous differentiable density,
{\mu^{N}_{n,K}}
will also admit a continuous differentiable density. Hence,

is well-defined. According to

\displaystyle R_{n}\coloneqq\|\mathcal{J}({\mu^{N}_{n,K}},\pi)\|_{L_{2}^{d}({\mu^{N}_{n,K}})}<\infty,\quad\text{for }\mathcal{J}({\mu^{N}_{n,K}},\pi)\coloneqq(\iota_{k,{\mu^{N}_{n,K}}}^{*}\iota_{k,{\mu^{N}_{n,K}}})^{-\gamma_{n}}\nabla\log\frac{{\mu^{N}_{n,K}}}{\pi}.

(26)

Then we can derive the convergence of the Fisher information from the convergence of

in Theorem

and the equivalence between Fisher information and regularized Stein Fisher information. We state the decay of Fisher information along the R-SVGD dynamics, and in
W_{1}
under

Theorem 6
.

Under conditions in Theorem

K>0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{h_{n}}{1-\nu_{n}}I({\mu^{N}_{n-1,K}}|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\gamma d}{T}+\frac{4c^{*}d\sum_{n=1}^{T}h_{n}}{NT}+\frac{4d\sum_{n=1}^{T}\beta_{1,n}(1-\nu_{n})h_{n}}{NT}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2\theta\sum_{n=1}^{T}\beta_{2,n}(1-\nu_{n})\nu_{n}^{3}}{B^{2}N^{\frac{3}{2}}T}+\frac{\theta^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\nu_{n}^{2}}{2NT},

where

and

. Furthermore, if the target distribution

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu^{N}_{T,av},\pi)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2C_{\pi}^{2}\theta\sum_{n=1}^{T}\beta_{2,n}(1-\nu_{n})\nu_{n}^{3}}{B^{2}N^{\frac{3}{2}}T}+\frac{C_{\pi}^{2}\theta^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\nu_{n}^{2}}{2NT},

where
\mu^{N}_{T,av}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{h_{n}}{1-\nu_{n}}{\mu^{N}_{n-1,K}}
.

Corollary 2

(Rates under constant

.

The optimal scalings in Theorems

and

(1)

Picking

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+(1-\nu)\left[\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]}{N}t+\frac{\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}}{\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}N}\int_{0}^{t}\left(s\int_{0}^{s}\mathbb{E}[I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(u)|\pi)]\mathrm{d}u\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}s\right],

\displaystyle\sqrt{G(t)}\leq(1-\nu)\frac{\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}}{2\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}N}t^{\frac{3}{2}}

which implies that

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})+2c^{*}dt}{N}+2(1-\nu)\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]}{N}t+(1-\nu)\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}dC_{V}^{2}}{\nu N^{2}}t^{3}.

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{NT}+\frac{c^{*}d}{N}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+(1-\nu)\left[\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]}{N}+\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}dC_{V}^{2}T^{2}}{\nu N^{2}}\right].

C^{*}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathsf{KL}(p^{N}_{0}|\pi^{\otimes N})+c^{*}dT+(1-\nu)\left[\beta_{1}dT+\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]T+\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon T^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 9.24994pt+\epsilon^{-1}\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}^{N}|\pi^{\otimes N})}{N}+\frac{\beta_{1}d+\beta_{2}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]}{N}T+\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}dC_{V}^{2}}{\nu N^{2}}T^{3}\right)T\right].

(Expectation bound for

)
.

Let Assumptions

-

For any fixed

Proof of Lemma
1
..

Recall the expression of
C^{*}(\underline{x})
in (
8
):

\displaystyle C^{*}(\underline{x})=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}K^{\nu}_{ij}\big(\Delta_{2}k(x^{j},x^{i})-\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\nabla V(x^{i})-k(x^{j},x^{i})\Delta V(x^{i})\big)}_{\coloneqq A_{1}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{+\frac{1-\nu}{\nu}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j,l=1}^{N}k(x^{i},x^{l})K^{\nu}_{li}\big(\nabla_{1}\cdot\nabla_{2}k(x^{i},x^{j})-\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\nabla V(x^{j})\big)}_{\coloneqq A_{2}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{+\frac{1-\nu}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{N}K^{\nu}_{ij}K^{\nu}_{ik}\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\big(\nabla_{2}k({x^{k}},x^{l})-k({x^{k}},x^{l})\nabla V(x^{l})\big)}_{A_{3}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{(1-\nu)^{2}}{\nu}\frac{1}{N^{3}}\sum_{i,j,k,l,r=1}^{N}K^{\nu}_{ri}K^{\nu}_{jk}k(x^{i},x_{r})\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\big(\nabla_{2}k({x^{k}},x^{l})-k({x^{k}},x^{l})\nabla V(x^{l})\big)}_{\coloneqq A_{4}}.

We bound the 4 terms
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},A_{4}
in the decomposition respectively. According to Assumptions
1
and
2
:

\displaystyle|k|\leq B,\quad\|\nabla_{1}k\|\leq\sqrt{d}B,\quad|\nabla_{1}\cdot\nabla_{2}k|\leq dB,\quad|\Delta_{1}k|,|\Delta_{2}k|\leq dB,\quad\|\nabla^{2}V\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{V},

and the last bound also implies
|\Delta V|\leq dC_{V}
.
Since
K\succeq 0
, we have the following bound for the
i
th row for any
i\in[N]
:

\displaystyle\big\|K^{\nu}(\underline{x})_{i,\cdot}\big\|_{2}=\big\|\left(\big((1-\nu)K/N+\nu I\big)^{-1}\right)_{i,\cdot}\big\|_{2}\leq\nu^{-1}.

Moreover, observe that

\displaystyle K^{\nu}(\underline{x})=\left(I_{N}-(1-\nu)\left(I_{N}-\frac{K}{N}\right)\right)^{-1}=I+(1-\nu)R,

where

\|R_{i,\cdot}\|_{2}\leq 1

for any
i\in[N]
.
Write

\displaystyle A_{1}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\Delta_{2}k(x^{i},x^{i})-\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{i})\cdot\nabla V(x^{i})-k(x^{i},x^{i})\Delta V(x^{i})\big)+\hat{A}_{1}

where

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{1}:=-\frac{1-\nu}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}R_{ij}\big(\Delta_{2}k(x^{j},x^{i})-\nabla_{1}k(x^{i},x^{j})\cdot\nabla V(x^{i})-k(x^{j},x^{i})\Delta V(x^{i})\big).

For each term of
\hat{A}_{1},A_{2},A_{3},A_{4}
, we count the numbers of occurrences of factors
K^{\nu}
,
R
,
k
and its derivatives, and track their contributions to the magnitude using the bounds above.

Taking
\hat{A}_{1}
for example, we express
A_{1}
as average over indices
i
, i.e.,
\hat{A}_{1}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{A}_{1,i}
. For each
i
, the block
\hat{A}_{1,i}
has one row of
R
multiplying a column that is bounded in
L^{2}
-norm by
dB+B\sqrt{d}\|\nabla V(x^{i})\|+dBC_{V}
.
Therefore, Cauchy–Schwarz in the
j

\|R_{i,\cdot}\|_{2}\leq 1

\displaystyle|\hat{A}_{1,i}|\leq(1-\nu)\left(dB+B\sqrt{d}\|\nabla V(x^{i})\|+dBC_{V}\right).

Averaging over

and taking expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\hat{A}_{1}|\leq B(1-\nu)\left((1+C_{V})d+\sqrt{d}\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\right).

Similarly, for
A_{2},A_{3},A_{4}
, using
\big\|K^{\nu}(\underline{x})_{i,\cdot}\big\|_{2}\leq\nu^{-1}
instead of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|A_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\nu}{\nu}\frac{1}{\nu}\big(dB^{2}+B^{2}\sqrt{d}\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|A_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\nu)\frac{1}{\nu^{2}}\big(dB^{2}+B^{2}\sqrt{d}\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|A_{4}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\nu)^{2}}{\nu}\frac{1}{\nu^{2}}\big(dB^{3}+B^{3}\sqrt{d}\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\big).

Combining the 4 estimations, and using Assumption
3
, we obtain
(
31
).

The second part of the proof relies on bounding
\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
based on the
C_{V}
-smoothness of
V
and the fact that
x^{i}(t)
follows the R-SVGD dynamics. Denote
F(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(t)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]
. Then we have

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla V(x^{i}(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\nabla^{2}V(x^{i}(t))\nabla V(x^{i}(t)),\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x^{i}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{V}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\nabla V(x^{i}(t))\|\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x^{i}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{V}F(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\left((1-\nu)\iota_{k,\rho^{N}(t)}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho^{N}(t)}+\nu I_{d}\right)^{-1}\iota_{k,\rho^{N}(t)}^{*}\iota_{k,\rho^{N}(t)}\nabla\log\frac{\rho^{N}(t)}{\pi}\|_{L^{2}(\rho^{N}(t))}

\displaystyle\leq 2\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}F(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(t)|\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where in the second last inequality, the notation

represents a term as a whole, which is well-defined through integration by parts even though

.
In the last identity, we used the spectrum expression of

introduced in

. From the above differential inequality, we obtain

\sqrt{F(t)}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}\int_{0}^{t}I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(s)|\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}s.

With the above bound and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\nabla V(x^{i}(t))\|]\leq\mathbb{E}[\big(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(t)}[\|\nabla V(y)\|^{2}]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}]=\mathbb{E}[F(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\rho^{N}(0)}[\|V(y)\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}]+\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{V}\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{t}I_{\nu,\text{Stein}}(\rho^{N}(s)|\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}s].

Appendix F
Preliminaries on Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS)

This appendix collects RKHS facts used in the analysis. We refer to
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
,
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2011
,
Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
for detailed expositions.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be a separable reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with reproducing kernel
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. Recall the reproducing property:

f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\qquad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The next proposition summarizes standard embedding and operator-theoretic properties that we repeatedly use, under Assumption
1
; see
[
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Lem. 4.23, Thms. 4.26–4.27]
.

Proposition 1

(Basic RKHS properties
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
.

(i)

(
Boundedness and
L_{\infty}
-embedding
) The kernel
k
is bounded (i.e.,
\sup_{x}k(x,x)<\infty
) if and only if every
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
is bounded. In this case, the inclusion (embedding)
\iota_{\infty}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous and

\|\iota_{\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}\to L_{\infty}}=\|k\|_{\infty},\qquad\|k\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{k(x,x)}.

Intuition:

k(x,x)
controls the size of the point-evaluation functional
f\mapsto f(x)
via the reproducing property.

(ii)

(
L_{2}(\mu)
-embedding and adjoint
) Let
\mu
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume
k
is measurable and satisfies

\|k\|_{L_{2}(\mu)}:=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,x)\,d\mu(x)\Big)^{1/2}<\infty.

Then
\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
and the inclusion
\iota_{k,\mu}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to L_{2}(\mu)
is continuous with

\|\iota_{k,\mu}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}\to L_{2}(\mu)}\leq\|k\|_{L_{2}(\mu)}.

Moreover, the adjoint
\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}:L_{2}(\mu)\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
is the (kernel) integral operator

(\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,y)\,g(y)\,d\mu(y),\qquad g\in L_{2}(\mu).

Intuition:

\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}
maps an
L_{2}(\mu)
function to a smoother RKHS element by averaging it against
k(\cdot,y)
.

(iii)

(
Density vs. injectivity
) Under (ii),
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is dense in
L_{2}(\mu)
if and only if
\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}
is injective. Equivalently,
\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}
has dense range in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
if and only if
\iota_{k,\mu}
is injective.

(iv)

(
Hilbert–Schmidt embedding and compactness
) Under (ii), the inclusion
\iota_{k,\mu}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to L_{2}(\mu)
is Hilbert–Schmidt with

\|\iota_{k,\mu}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|k\|_{L_{2}(\mu)}.

Consequently, the associated integral operator

\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}:=\iota_{k,\mu}\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}:L_{2}(\mu)\to L_{2}(\mu)

is compact, positive, self-adjoint, and trace-class (nuclear).

Vector-valued inner products.

For
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}
with
f=[f_{1},\dots,f_{d}]^{\top}
and
g\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, we use the componentwise pairing

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad(\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})_{i}:=\langle f_{i},g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}.

Similarly, for
f\in L_{2}^{d}(\mu)
and
g\in L_{2}(\mu)
,

\langle f,g\rangle_{L_{2}(\mu)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad(\langle f,g\rangle_{L_{2}(\mu)})_{i}:=\langle f_{i},g\rangle_{L_{2}(\mu)}.

Range of
\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}^{1/2}
.

Since
\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}=\iota_{k,\mu}\iota_{k,\mu}^{*}
, one can identify

\mathrm{Ran}(\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}^{1/2})=\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset L_{2}(\mu),

see, e.g.,
Cucker and Zhou 2007
. Intuitively,
\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}^{1/2}
links the
L_{2}(\mu)
geometry to the RKHS geometry.

Spectral representation.

Let
(\lambda_{i},e_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be eigenpairs of
\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots>0
and
\{e_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
orthonormal in
\mathrm{Ran}(\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu})
. Then, for all
f\in L_{2}(\mu)
,

\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\,\langle f,e_{i}\rangle_{L_{2}(\mu)}\,e_{i}.

(45)

Computing this decomposition for general
(k,\mu)
is typically difficult and is only known in special cases (see, e.g.,
Minh et al. 2006
,
Scetbon and Harchaoui 2021
). In this work, the spectral form (
45
) is used purely as an analysis tool; the practical algorithm does not require explicit knowledge of
(\lambda_{i},e_{i})
.

Remark 11
.

The notation above is simplest when
\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu}
has a trivial null space, in which case
\overline{\mathrm{Ran}(\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu})}=L_{2}(\mu)
and the eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(\mu)
. Our arguments do
not
require this: if
\textsf{k}(\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu})\neq\{0\}
, then
\overline{\mathrm{Ran}(\mathcal{T}_{k,\mu})}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
, and one may extend
\{e_{i}\}
(by adding a basis of the null space) without affecting the conclusions.
