The prevalence of depression and anxiety and associated factors among school-going adolescents in poverty and conflict-affected settings in Uganda
Authors: James Mugisha, Philip B. Ward, Eugene Kinyanda, Japheth Nkiriyehe Kwiringira, Mpungu Steven Kiwuwa, Wadiembe Symon, Tine Van Damme, Davy Vancampfort
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchools could play a vital role in mental health care, particularly in low-income countries such as Uganda. An understanding of the prevalence and associated factors of mental health symptoms among school-going adolescents is essential for designing effective school-based interventions in Uganda This is important given Uganda’s regional disparities, where adolescents in post-conflict areas may face higher exposure to trauma and limited access to mental health care compared to those in non-conflict regions. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence rates of depressive and anxiety symptoms among school-going adolescents aged 14 to 17 years in both post-conflict and non-conflict settings. It also examines factors associated with moderate depressive and anxiety symptoms in both settings.MethodsIn total, 2845 school-going adolescents (1,273 boys; 16.3 ± 1.0 years) were screened using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 – adolescent version, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, and self-report items on food insecurity, health and wealth status, history of childhood abuse and neglect and level of physical activity. Logistic regression models were applied to examine predictors of moderate to severe levels of depression and anxiety symptoms.ResultsFindings indicate that 67.5% (n = 1905) reported at least mild, 34.1% (n = 962) at least moderate, 12.7% (n = 359) at least moderate-severe and 4.9% (n = 138) severe symptoms of depression, while 65.6% (n = 1860) at least mild, 27.5% (n = 777) at least moderate and 7.8% (n = 219) severe symptoms of anxiety. In the multivariable analyses, female sex, food insecurity, poor self-reported health, co-morbid anxiety symptoms, and a history of abuse and neglect were all significantly associated with a higher odds of moderate symptoms of depression, while female sex, living in a post-conflict setting, food insecurity, poor self-reported health, co-morbid depressive symptoms, and a history of abuse were all significantly associated with a higher odds of moderate anxiety symptoms.ConclusionsA substantial proportion of school-going adolescents in Uganda report at least moderate symptoms of depression and anxiety. Government and public sector agencies should re-evaluate their strategies at both family and school levels, particularly in underserved settings. Schools can serve as platforms for screening-and-referral pathways and group-based psychosocial programs, while the health system should strengthen capacity for co-occurring conditions.

Background

Adolescent mental disorders are a major global public health concern [1], often emerging during late childhood and early adolescence [2]. These conditions can impair developmental trajectories, disrupt educational and social functioning, and increase the risk of morbidity and premature mortality, including suicide [3]. Early identification and access to low-cost, scalable interventions are essential to mitigating these outcomes and reducing the long-term burden on health systems [4]. This is particularly critical in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the prevalence of mental health problems is high and service delivery systems are often severely under-resourced [5].

For example, in Uganda, the mental health system faces considerable constraints in addressing the needs of adolescents. Over 80% of young people do not access formal mental health services, and there is currently no dedicated national policy for adolescent mental health [6]. Mental health care in Uganda remains highly centralized, with Butabika National Referral Hospital receiving more than 90% of the national mental health budget and functioning as the sole facility offering specialized services for children and adolescents. Despite this critical role, Butabika’s child and adolescent mental health unit is small, under-resourced, and disproportionately burdened with the responsibility of serving the entire country. While mental health units exist at regional referral hospitals, they lack child and adolescent specialists [6, 7]. Psychosocial services are often delivered by non-governmental organizations, but these are typically fragmented, inconsistent, and lack specialized personnel. In addition to these systemic challenges, multiple barriers limit access to care, including geographic inaccessibility, insufficient funding and leadership, a shortage of trained providers, and widespread stigma associated with mental illness [5, 8].

Given these challenges, schools offer a promising platform for strengthening adolescent mental health care in Uganda [9, 10]. Schools provide a stable and structured environment where adolescents spend a significant portion of their time, and they may be perceived as safe, familiar spaces where students feel more comfortable disclosing mental health concerns. In contexts where stigma inhibits help-seeking, schools can play a role in normalizing mental health care and facilitating early identification, prevention, and referral [11]. Despite this potential, schools have not been systematically integrated into the national mental health response. Efforts to integrate mental health into Uganda’s primary health care system have focused primarily on the community level, leaving school settings largely overlooked [10, 12]. Even at the community level, integration efforts face critical barriers, including workforce shortages, negative provider attitudes, and insufficient training in mental health care [13]. Schools, however, can serve as accessible hubs for mental health promotion and support. They can provide psychoeducation, basic screening, and structured referral mechanisms while fostering an environment that promotes well-being and reduces stigma. Teachers and school staff can also be equipped to recognize signs of psychological distress and offer basic support or referral. To inform the development of such interventions, it is essential to understand the specific mental health burden among school-going adolescents and the contextual factors that influence symptom presentation and access to care.

To date, only two studies have examined mental health symptoms among school-going adolescents in Uganda. A pre-COVID study by Nabunya et al. [14] conducted among 1,260 adolescent girls aged 14 to 17 years in 47 secondary schools in southern Uganda found that 16.3% reported severe depressive symptoms, while 29.7% reported moderate symptoms, with higher rates observed among older adolescents. Protective factors included better family relationships, higher social support, and positive psychological attributes, while hopelessness was associated with greater symptom severity. A post-COVID study by Mugisha et al. [9] conducted in one private school in central Uganda among 205 adolescents of both sexes found that 64.4% reported mild depressive or anxiety symptoms, with physical inactivity identified as a significant risk factor.

While these studies provide valuable insights, our study advances the literature in several important ways. First, unlike Nabunya et al. [14], our sample includes both boys and girls and is drawn from both post-conflict and non-conflict regions, offering a broader geographical and contextual perspective [15, 16]. Second, unlike Mugisha et al. [9], who studied a single private school shortly after COVID-19 closures, our study encompasses multiple public schools across different regions 18 months after school reopening, capturing more generalized and temporally relevant data. Third, based on prior evidence, we selected key psychosocial risk factors a priori for inclusion in multivariable analyses, including food insecurity [17, 18], self-reported health status [19], mental health co-morbidity [19], and histories of childhood abuse and neglect [20, 21], that have not been systematically examined in previous Ugandan school-based adolescent mental health studies. Together, these distinctions enable a more comprehensive understanding of the mental health problems and its associated contextual factors, thereby better informing scalable, school-based interventions tailored to Uganda’s diverse adolescent populations.

This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of symptoms of depression and anxiety among adolescents aged 14 to 17 years attending secondary schools in both post-conflict and non-conflict regions of Uganda, 18 months after schools reopened following the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, the study sought to: (a) estimate the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms in this population; (b) examine associations between moderate symptoms of depression and anxiety and key demographic and psychosocial factors, including age, sex, household wealth, food insecurity, self-reported health status, mental health co-morbidity, childhood abuse and neglect, physical inactivity, and residence in post-conflict versus non-conflict regions. The study focuses on school-going adolescents aged 14 to 17 years, representing middle to late adolescence, a developmental stage characterized by heightened academic and social pressures, identity formation, and increased susceptibility to mental health challenges [2]. Although the study does not include younger adolescents or out-of-school youth, concentrating on this subgroup offers critical insight into a population segment that is both vulnerable and reachable through existing school infrastructure, making it a practical and strategic focus for intervention planning in the Ugandan school context.

Methods

Study design and research setting

This was a cross-sectional survey. Data collection was carried out over a one-month period in each participating school by a trained research team comprising three members with clinical experience in mental health care. Study participants were school-going adolescents aged 14–17 years, recruited between August and December 2023 from the two largest (as confirmed by the district education office) secondary mixed (boys and girls) public day schools in Masaka district situated in central Uganda and the two largest (as confirmed by the district education office) secondary mixed (boys and girls) public day schools in Gulu (a post-conflict district in Northern Uganda). We focused on adolescents aged 14–17 years as this age group typically corresponds to lower and upper secondary school students in Uganda, who are at higher risk for mental health problems [15] and more likely to reliably complete self-report measures. The number of students in each school ranged between 1292 and 1573.

HIV prevalence in Masaka is almost 12%, the highest rate in the country [22]. The population of Masaka district is predominantly semi-literate, and involved in subsistence farming [23]. Masaka district has multiple hot spots for high-risk sexual activity located in fishing communities along Lake Victoria and several trading centres along the Trans-African Highway connecting Kenya to the Democratic Republic of Congo and Rwanda through Uganda [22]. In Masaka district, schools provide education to a mix of rural and urban students, though schools face challenges related to overcrowding and limited resources. On the other hand, Gulu district is a post-conflict district in northern Uganda. It was one of the most affected districts during the civil strife by the Lord’s Resistance Army (LRA), a civil conflict between the Government of Uganda and LRA rebels, and this conflict lasted for over two decades. Moreover, Gulu is located within northern Uganda, which is the poorest region in the country. There are both government and private sector efforts to deliver post-recovery programs though their impact is still minimal in both social and health service sectors. In Gulu district, schools are still recovering from the impacts of the conflict, with many families facing challenges related to displacement and trauma, while there is inadequate infrastructure and limited access to educational materials.

Data collection tools

Socio-demographic variables

Socio-demographic variables collected included age (years), sex (boy vs. girl), religion (Catholic, Protestant, Born Again / Pentecostal, Muslim, Seventh-day Adventist or another religious background).

Food security

In accordance with previous research in Uganda [15], food security was assessed with the question: “In the last month, did you or your family have enough food?”.

Health status

Health status was assessed with the question: “Do you perceive your current health as positive or negative?”

Wealth index

In line with the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) wealth index recommendations [24], we calculated, through principal components analysis, a composite score of the living standard of a household using a combination of local assets indicators. Principal components analysis was used to generate a weighted index that captures the relative importance of each asset, providing a more robust measure of household wealth than simple summation. Each variable contributed to the PCA-derived index, reflecting relative household wealth within the study population. In this study, we assessed the availability of electricity (yes vs. no), availability of tap water (yes vs. borehole, protected well, unprotected well or river), having a car (yes vs. no), bicycle (yes vs. no), television (yes vs. no), refrigerator (yes vs. no), telephone (yes vs. no), a cupboard (yes vs. no), and the quality of the materials of the floor (tiles vs. mud and cement), wall (burnt bricks vs. mud and wattle and unburnt bricks), and roof (tiles vs. thatch and corrugated iron). In line with DHS recommendations [24], the household wealth status was divided into five quintiles (i.e., poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest).

Patient health questionnaire-9 – adolescent version (PHQ-9-A)

The PHQ-9-A is a nine-item depression scale which is widely used to screen for depressive symptoms among adolescents [25]. It is adapted from the adult PHQ-9 by using simpler language and adolescent-appropriate phrasing to enhance comprehension and relevance for younger populations, for example, wording such as “Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless” is adapted to “Feeling sad, down, or hopeless” to better match adolescents’ vocabulary and experiences. Each item asks the individual to rate the severity of their symptoms over the past two weeks. Each item is scored on a Likert scale with symptoms rated as 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days) and 3 (every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 27. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The optimal cut-off for the presence of mild symptoms of depression in those aged 10 years or older is ≥ 5, for moderate symptoms ≥ 10, for moderate severe symptoms ≥ 15, and severe symptoms ≥ 20 in Uganda [25]. The PHQ-9-A has been validated for assessing depressive symptoms in rural Uganda [26]. The Cronbach’s alpha in the current study was 0.85 indicating a good reliability.

Generalized anxiety disorder − 7 (GAD-7)

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7) [27] is a seven-item instrument, used in both adolescent and adult populations, to measure the severity of generalized anxiety disorder. Each item asks the individual to rate the severity of his or her symptoms over the past two weeks. Each item is scored on a Likert scale with symptoms rated as 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days) and 3 (every day). The optimal cut-off score for presence of mild, moderate and severe symptoms of anxiety is respectively 5, 10, and 15 in adolescents [27]. The GAD-7 has been used before in Uganda [28]. The Cronbach’s alpha in the current study was 0.89 indicating a good reliability.

Childhood trauma questionnaire-short form (CTQ-SF)

Traumatic experience in childhood was evaluated by the CTQ-SF [29]. The CTQ-SF is a 28-item self-report questionnaire with five subscales: physical neglect, emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical abuse, and sexual abuse. The survey comprises five items that measure each of the five types of trauma, along with an additional three items that were included for validity evaluation (e.g., “perfect childhood”). The trauma level is estimated by each item ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always) with several items requiring counter-scoring. Each subscale score ranges from 5 to 25, while the overall score ranges from 25 to 125. Participants were deemed to have had childhood trauma if their scores were above any of the following internationally recommended subscale thresholds: physical neglect ≥ 10; emotional abuse ≥ 13; emotional neglect ≥ 15; physical abuse ≥ 10 and sexual abuse ≥ 8. Cronbach’s alpha values for the subdomains ranged from 0.75 for physical neglect to 0.89 for sexual abuse demonstrating acceptable to good reliability.

Physical activity vital sign (PAVS) – adolescents’ method

The PAVS consists of two simple questions [30]. The first question is: “On average how many days per week do you engage in moderate to vigorous physical activity like a brisk walk?” It was explained to the participants that this means that due to being active their heart rate increased, and they breathed more deeply and faster than normal, with some even experiencing sweating. The second question is: “On those days, how many minutes do you engage on average in physical activity at this level?” Next, the two responses were multiplied to calculate the minutes per week of self-reported moderate to vigorous physical activity and verified whether adolescent were achieving the internationally recommended target of 420 min per week of moderate to vigorous physical activity. The tool has been validated and used before in Uganda [31, 32].

Statistical analysis

Prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms at different severity levels was calculated using descriptive statistics. Logistic regression models were used to assess univariable and multivariable associations between the dependent variable, i.e., moderate to severe symptoms of depression and anxiety (defined as a score of ≥ 10 on the PHQ-9-A and GAD-7, respectively) and the independent variables, including age (14–15 vs. 16–17 years), sex (boys vs. girls), setting (non-conflict vs. conflict), wealth index quintiles, food security (enough food vs. not enough food), self-reported health status (good vs. poor), comorbid anxiety or depression (present vs. not present), history of childhood abuse (yes vs. no), history of childhood neglect (yes vs. no), and physical inactivity (yes vs. no). Consistent with best practices and to address potential confounding, the multivariable models included variables selected based on theoretical considerations and prior literature, rather than solely on univariable significance. Specifically, our analysis incorporates a range of psychosocial risk factors, i.e. food insecurity [17, 18], self-reported health status [19], mental health co-morbidity [19], and histories of childhood abuse and neglect [20, 21], that have been identified in prior studies. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors, all of which were below 5, indicating no evidence of problematic collinearity. Unadjusted odds ratios and those adjusted for significant associations in the univariable analyses are reported. We chose to focus our analysis on at least moderate level of severity (cutoff score ≥ 10), as mild symptoms were expected to be highly prevalent and risk inflating associations, while severe symptoms alone were likely too infrequent to allow for reliable statistical analysis. By including both moderate and severe cases, we aimed to capture clinically meaningful symptomatology while maintaining sufficient statistical power. For analyses involving self-reported health status and the wealth index, participants who did not know their health status or whose responses were insufficient to determine wealth quintile were excluded; these participants were included in all other analyses. To account for potential clustering of participants within schools, we conducted sensitivity analyses using cluster-robust standard errors at the school level. These analyses yielded results consistent with our primary models. A priori, significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Ethical considerations

Adolescents were informed that non-participation would not affect their relationship with the school. All participants gave their written informed assent and the legal representatives of the participating adolescents their written informed consent. While all students took part in the mental health screening as part of a broader school-based outreach initiative, only data from those with both assent and parental consent were included in the study analysis. Participants were not financially compensated but received a refreshment during completion of the interviewer-administered questionnaires. All interviews were done in English as this is the language used at the schools. All interviews were conducted face-to-face within school facilities, using a designated classroom made available specifically to ensure privacy and minimize disruptions. No audio recordings were made, and each interview lasted approximately 30 min per participant. Participants who scored in the moderately severe or severe range on the PHQ-9-A or GAD-7 (total score ≥ 15) were referred to local health services for additional mental health support, ensuring that those with the highest levels of symptoms received timely care.The study was approved by the ethical committee of Mildmay Uganda (Ref nr: MUREC 0501–2023).

Results

Participants

In total, 2845 school-going adolescents were invited to participate. None refused, but we did not obtain informed assent and consent forms from 24 adolescents, i.e. a response rate of 99.1%. We did not explore differences between these non-consenting adolescents and the rest of the sample, and while this may have introduced some unmeasured bias, the small number (less than 1%) is unlikely to have systematically influenced the findings. The information used in this study is therefore derived from 2821 adolescents [45.1% (n = 1273) boys; 16.3 ± 1.0 years). Most of the adolescents were Catholic (51.9%, n = 1462), followed by Protestant (18.6%, n = 524), Born Again / Pentecostal (15.9%, n = 447), Muslim (11.8%, n = 333), Seventh-day Adventist (1.1%, n = 30) and another religious background (0.8%, n = 23). When looking at the prevalence of depressive symptoms, 67.5% (n = 1905) reported at least mild symptoms, 34.1% (n = 962) at least moderate symptoms, 12.7% (n = 359) at least moderate severe symptoms and 4.9% (n = 138) severe symptoms of depression. With regards to anxiety, 65.6% (n = 1860) reported at least mild symptoms of anxiety, 27.5% (n = 777) at least moderate and 7.8% (n = 219) severe anxiety symptoms. A history of significant abuse was reported in 59.0% (n = 1665) of the participants. In more detail, 29.8% (n = 841) experienced emotional abuse, 40.1% (n = 1131) physical abuse and 34.0% (n = 958) sexual abuse. When looking at a history of neglect, 67.5% (n = 1905) experienced neglect with 59.7% (n = 1684) physical and 46.4% (n = 1308) emotional neglect. The mean PAVS score is 267.9 ± 221.8 min per week. In total, 2363 adolescents (83.7%) reported less than 420 min of physical activity per week and were classified as physically inactive. Discussion

The present cross-sectional study examined the prevalence of symptoms of depression and anxiety and their associated factors among school-going children in post-conflict and non-conflict districts of Uganda (Gulu and Masaka, respectively), approximately 18 months after the reopening of schools and following the world’s longest lockdown. Our findings highlight a substantial mental health burden among adolescents: over 67% reported at least mild depressive symptoms, with more than 34% exhibiting symptoms at or above the moderate level and nearly 13% reaching moderate-severe levels of depression. Approximately 5% of adolescents reported severe depression. For anxiety, more than 65% experienced at least mild symptoms, over 27% had symptoms at or above moderate severity, and about 8% reported severe anxiety. These post-COVID-19 prevalence rates remain comparable to earlier reports from the pandemic period in Uganda, where 64.4% of adolescents aged 14 to 17 exhibited at least mild depressive or anxiety symptoms using the same instruments [28]. This aligns with global meta-analytic data [33] showing a significant increase in anxiety and depression among children and adolescents following the COVID-19 pandemic, underscoring an urgent public health concern. Left untreated, these mental health problems threaten not only psychological well-being but also physical health [34, 35] and long-term educational outcomes [36], potentially exacerbating already high school dropout rates in both districts [37]. To better understand the underlying mechanisms driving these high prevalence rates, we explored a range of individual and structural correlates.

Our study found that 41.3% of adolescents self-reported poor health status, which was significantly associated with at least moderate levels of both depressive and anxiety symptoms. This finding underscores the interplay between perceived physical health and psychological distress, which is consistent with prior literature on the bidirectional relationship between mental and physical health [35, 38]. Additionally, comorbid anxiety and depression symptoms were strongly interrelated in our sample, suggesting that adolescents often experience overlapping symptoms rather than distinct syndromes [39]. These findings emphasize the need for integrated and holistic mental health approaches that can screen for and address both mental and physical health complaints within school or community settings. However, the critical shortage of trained professionals and psychotropic medications in Uganda limits the feasibility of specialized services [39]. In this context, resource-efficient, transdiagnostic interventions, such as those delivered by trained lay providers [8, 40] or embedded within school health programs [41], may offer feasible and scalable solutions to address the complex mental health needs of adolescents.

Beyond individual health perceptions, structural and socioeconomic factors also emerged as key contributors to mental health symptoms. In our study, food insecurity, a proxy indicator of poverty, was significantly associated with at least moderate levels of both depression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents. This finding aligns with previous research [15, 16] linking household food insecurity to increased risk of mental health disorders, including major depressive disorder, in post-conflict settings. The relationship between poverty and mental health is well established, and our findings support calls for the inclusion of psychosocial dimensions in poverty reduction strategies. In northern Uganda, still recovering from decades of armed conflict, households face persistent challenges in meeting basic needs, including access to food, water, and electricity. These material hardships may contribute to elevated psychological distress among adolescents. Other studies have found that greater household asset ownership is associated with lower depressive symptoms [42] and with better access to health care among adolescents [43]. Our findings therefore suggest that efforts to address adolescent mental health must be coupled with policies aimed at improving food security and household resilience in vulnerable regions.

In addition to poverty, sex disparities also emerged prominently, with girls showing higher odds of depressive and anxiety symptoms. This may be attributable to entrenched societal and cultural expectations in Uganda that assign girls physically demanding and time-consuming domestic roles, such as cooking, cleaning, fetching water, and caregiving, which reduce their time for schoolwork, rest, and socialization [44]. This added burden has been linked to psychological distress, poor academic outcomes, and increased risk of early school dropout across sub-Saharan Africa, including Uganda [45, 46]. Gender-sensitive interventions are essential to address these disparities. In Uganda, programs like the Empowerment and Livelihood for Adolescents initiative and school-based menstrual hygiene management interventions have shown promise in improving girls’ psychosocial well-being and school retention [47–49]. Tailored approaches such as gender-sensitive curricula, extracurricular programs fostering peer support, mentorship initiatives promoting girls’ education, and community engagement encouraging shared household responsibilities are urgently needed. However, exclusive focus on girls may risk overlooking boys’ mental health needs and perpetuating gender imbalances in program access. Interventions should strive to be inclusive, recognizing that boys may also face gendered pressures and unaddressed psychosocial needs. Additionally, it is important to consider that higher symptom reporting among girls could partially reflect greater emotional awareness or expression, or even measurement bias, rather than solely true differences in prevalence [50].

Finally, psychosocial adversity in the form of abuse and neglect emerged as a prominent factor associated with poor mental health outcomes. A high prevalence of abuse (59.0%) and neglect (59.7%) was reported, both significantly associated with symptoms of anxiety and depression among adolescents. The COVID-19 lockdown in Uganda coincided with a rise in child abuse and labor [51], although high levels of childhood abuse and neglect have been documented for over a decade in these regions. These findings may reflect a transgenerational transmission of mental disorders, as caregivers with abuse histories and related mental health problems are more likely to perpetuate maltreatment. This cycle poses a substantial barrier to mental health in both districts, but mainly in post-conflict settings [52]. Despite the Ugandan government’s multi-sectoral framework for child protection, our results suggest limited effectiveness of current interventions. Schools, as integral community units, represent an underutilized platform for interventions aimed at building self-esteem and resilience among children affected by abuse and neglect [53]. Some evidence-based school-based programs have been piloted in Uganda and similar contexts, such as the Good School Toolkit, which was found to reduce physical violence from school staff and improve student well-being [54], and the INSPIRE framework, which provides a set of strategies for preventing and responding to violence against children across sectors, including education [55]. Such programs offer promising models for adaptation and scaling within Ugandan schools.

Interestingly, although physical inactivity was significantly associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms in univariable analysis, it did not retain significance in the adjusted models. This suggests that physical inactivity may be confounded by or mediated through other psychosocial factors, such as abuse, neglect, or food insecurity, which more directly impact mental health in this population. Additionally, measurement limitations and the complex interplay between physical activity, mental health, and contextual adversities may obscure direct associations. Future research using longitudinal designs and objective measures of physical activity could clarify these relationships and inform interventions [56].

Limitations

Limitations of the study must be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the use of screening tools for symptoms of depression and anxiety means that our data do not constitute clinical diagnoses; further research using diagnostic criteria is warranted. Second, reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias, including potential underreporting of sensitive issues such as abuse and neglect, especially in school settings. Third, our focus on adolescents enrolled in mixed public day specifically only two large schools in the selected districts, limits generalizability to adolescents in other school types (e.g., private or boarding schools) and out-of-school youth, who may have different mental health profiles, thereby introducing potential selection bias. Fourth, measurement bias related to sex or setting differences cannot be fully excluded, as gender norms may influence adolescents’ willingness to report psychological distress. For example, boys in the Ugandan context may be less likely to report feelings of distress due to cultural expectations that discourage males from showing vulnerability. In addition, school-level factors can shape students’ sense of safety and trust within the school environment. Adolescents who perceive the school as supportive and fair may feel more comfortable disclosing feelings of distress, whereas those who experience rigid, punitive, or inconsistent policies may be less willing to share their psychological concerns. These dynamics may have shaped reporting patterns and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and restricts conclusions on outcomes. Follow-up studies are planned as part of ongoing intervention evaluations.

Conclusions

This study provides critical, comprehensive data on the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms among Ugandan adolescents nearly two years post-COVID-19 lockdown, revealing persistently high symptom levels that represent a major public health concern. Our findings call for renewed governmental and public sector efforts to improve mental health strategies at family and school levels. In particular, schools should be leveraged as accessible platforms for action, including structured screening-and-referral pathways, group-based psychosocial programs, and integration with community resources. Health systems must be strengthened to address complex comorbidities that extend beyond what community- and school-based programs alone can achieve. As this is a cross-sectional study, the observed associations cannot be interpreted as causal; rather, they highlight priority targets for future research and intervention. Confirmatory studies using diagnostic assessments are vital to guide policy and practice aimed at reducing adolescent mental health burden in Uganda.
