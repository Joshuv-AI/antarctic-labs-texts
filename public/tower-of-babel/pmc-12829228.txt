Exploring the transmission mechanism of low-carbon city pilot policies on enhancing agribusiness ESG performance: an empirical study using the PSM-DID model
Authors: Xue Zhu, Xiwu Shao, Chengjie Li, Chunmai Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Amid China’s sustained medium-high speed economic growth, the extensive development model has caused severe climate deterioration and environmental pollution. The implementation of low-carbon city pilot policies provides a strategic direction for the sustainable development of agribusinesses. Using unbalanced panel data from 781 agriculture-related listed enterprises in China between 2007 and 2023, this study employs the PSM-DID model to estimate the average treatment effect and heterogeneity of low-carbon city pilot policies. In addition, a mediation model is applied to examine the mechanism through which digital transformation enhances the Environmental-Social-Governance (ESG) performance of these enterprises under policies. The empirical results indicate that: (1) low-carbon city pilot policies simultaneously improve the environmental, social, and governance performance of agribusiness enterprises, thereby enhancing their overall ESG performance. (2) The impact of policies on ESG, environmental, social, and governance performance varies across regions, with the western region showing the most significant improvement in ESG performance. (3) Digital transformation plays a significant mediating role in the effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG, environmental, social, and governance performance. Based on these findings, agribusiness should leverage policy advantages to enhance sustainable development through multi-dimensional performance. The government should introduce targeted policies to help enterprises define development directions and formulate localized strategies. Furthermore, agribusinesses must strengthen digital transformation as a core driver for improving ESG performance.

Introduction

While the concept of sustainable development has gained global recognition, corporate Environmental-Social-Governance (ESG) principles have also been widely adopted and implemented across the corporate sector [3, 22, 24]. As a vital link between agribusinesses and the market economy, agribusinesses ESG performance is crucial not only to their own sustainability but also to the green transformation of the entire agricultural value chain (He [16],; You et al., [56]. In response to climate change and environmental challenges, China proposed its “carbon peaking and carbon neutrality” goals in 2020 [17, 30]. In this context, enhancing the ESG performance of agribusinesses—an essential component of China’s economy—has become increasingly important for high-quality economic development [31, 54]. In 2021, China’s Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (MARD) issued a directive titled Opinions on Promoting Agricultural Industrialization Leading Enterprises to Become Bigger and Stronger, which emphasized enhancing green development capacity and improving environmental governance in agribusinesses through policy guidance and financial support [6, 62]. According to the China Agricultural Enterprises ESG Development Report 2024, agricultural enterprises need to deal with greenhouse gas emissions. Some enterprises have taken emission reduction measures, but the overall disclosure rate has been greatly improved. The report points out that agricultural listed companies must strengthen ESG practices in supply chain management in order to realize green transformation and sustainable development of the whole industrial chain [8, 37, 43]. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into agribusiness ESG performance holds significant theoretical and practical value for advancing China’s high-quality and sustainable economic development.

The introduction of low-carbon city pilot policies reflects national efforts to address the global climate crisis, while also offering policy support and a practical platform for enterprises to implement ESG principles [21, 32]. This policy system incorporates carbon emission quotas, carbon trading markets, and low-carbon certification, thereby establishing a multi-level pressure transmission mechanism for emissions reduction (Yang & Liu [53]),. Through policy guidance and market incentives, agribusinesses are encouraged to achieve notable outcomes in energy conservation, emissions reduction, and green production, thereby improving ESG performance and laying a foundation for the agricultural value chain’s green transition [15]. According to Porter’s hypothesis, environmental regulation can stimulate innovation, offset compliance costs, and lead to a win-win scenario for both economic and ESG performance [39]. Since agribusinesses are typically positioned midstream, policy pressure is often weakly transmitted downstream, and their small scale limits their capacity to adopt green technologies [18, 38]. The implementation of the low-carbon city pilot policy provides an important opportunity for agriculture-related enterprises to increase their investment in green technology and innovation, which is conducive to the overall improvement of ESG performance.

In recent years, ESG has become one of the hot topics in economics and management research. A large number of literatures have discussed the influencing factors of corporate ESG performance, including internal and external factors [27, 40, 46]. The impact of policy implementation on corporate ESG performance has increasingly drawn academic attention. Concurrently, research on how low-carbon city pilot policies influence corporate behavior and performance continues to expand and evolve [46]. These pilot policies not only significantly reduce corporate carbon emissions, curb pollution practices, and alleviate environmental pressures, but also substantially promote technological innovation and enhance production efficiency—factors that collectively elevate corporate ESG performance [36]. Scholars primarily investigate the relationship between policy and ESG performance through two key dimensions: First, some researchers analyze policy impacts on agriculture-related enterprises from a policy perspective. Glowinkel et al. [11] argue that the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) may lead to uneven resource allocation of small and medium-sized enterprises, and hinder the sustainable development of the agribusiness sector. Savastano et al. [41] suggest that understanding green determinants can help policymakers identify key areas for enhancing the performance of the green agricultural sector. Chen et al. [4] find that the National Nutrition Programme (2017–2030) boosted overall agri-food sales among listed firms. On the other hand, some scholars have investigated the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on corporate ESG performance. Wang [46] finds that these policies enhance ESG disclosure by improving firms’ ESG performance. Yu et al. [58] shows that the policies have a positive effect, particularly in high-emission industries and regions.

The impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on corporate ESG performance is typically achieved through multiple mechanisms. Existing research analyzes the effects of these policies on ESG outcomes from perspectives such as green finance, technological innovation, and industrial restructuring. Technological innovation channels are reflected in the research and development of cleaner production technologies and energy efficiency improvement under policy incentives, which directly reduces the carbon emission intensity per unit output; while industrial structure upgrading reshapes the underlying support logic of enterprises’ ESG performance from the supply side by phasing out high-energy-consuming industries and cultivating green emerging industrial clusters. Such as, Wan et al. [44] identified two mediating factors in the policy’s enhancement effect: corporate dynamism and analyst attention. He and Wang [15] found that low-carbon city pilots improve corporate ESG performance by strengthening internal controls, driving green innovation, and curbing emission behaviors. Notably, the interaction between corporate internal controls and green innovation significantly elevates ESG performance. Wang and Yao [49] demonstrated that low-carbon city policies effectively alleviate financing constraints and address environmental issues. Both green technological innovation and the adoption of embedded technologies play crucial roles in economic performance, significantly boosting environmental and ESG metrics.

Based on the above analyses, existing studies have examined the impact of policies on agribusiness development using quasi-natural experiments. However, there is still a large space for expansion in this field. First, although extensive research exists on the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance of enterprises, few studies specifically focus on agribusinesses. Second, most of the existing studies tend to analyze the influencing factors and heterogeneity of the improvement of corporate ESG performance, but ignore the specific paths and mechanisms of environmental performance, social performance and governance performance of agriculture-related enterprises. Third, although existing studies have mainly focused on the effects of policies on ESG improvement, few have examined whether digital transformation significantly influences the relationship between policy implementation and ESG performance in agribusinesses.

In view of this, this paper uses the unbalanced panel data from 781 agriculture-related listed enterprises in China between 2007 and 2023, to explore the impact effect and mechanism of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance of agriculture-related enterprises. This study contributes marginally in three key dimensions. First, by analyzing 781 agricultural enterprises using the propensity score matching-differences in differences (PSM-DID) model, we explore how low-carbon city pilot policies affect their ESG performance, thereby enriching both theoretical foundations and research perspectives in this field. Second, through examining environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance from three dimensions respectively, we provide actionable insights for advancing high-quality agricultural development. Third, by focusing on digital transformation as a catalyst, we analyze policy impacts on ESG outcomes while mapping their interrelationships across these dimensions. These findings offer practical references for governments to develop tailored development strategies for agricultural enterprises.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

To analyze the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on the agribusiness ESG performance, this paper proposes three research hypotheses based on the logical framework in Fig. 1, providing a theoretical foundation for subsequent empirical testing.

The impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance

Urban green and low-carbon transformation is a key component of high-quality economic development. It not only addresses ecological sustainability but also directly influences long-term socio-economic progress [28, 59]. In his 1993 book Economics, American economist Joseph Eugene Stiglitz explicitly stated that government intervention in the economy has significant positive effects. By relying on legally regulated government measures, the market can effectively allocate resources [45]. This theory demonstrates that implementing low-carbon city pilot policies will enhance energy efficiency, reduce emissions during production processes, and drive industrial transformation toward low-carbon development [1]. Such policies not only boost short-term economic benefits for agricultural enterprises but also create opportunities for their long-term sustainable growth. Since the ESG performance of agricultural enterprises is measured through three dimensions—environmental, social, and governance—improvements in each dimension collectively contribute to enhancing corporate ESG outcomes [42]. Accordingly, this study posits that low-carbon city pilot policies significantly influence agribusinesses ESG performance across three key dimensions: environmental, social, and governance.

First, from the perspective of environmental performance. Pilot policies for low-carbon cities will influence agricultural enterprises’ costs through environmental regulation tools (Niu & Liu [35]),. While environmental regulations may increase corporate costs in the short term, they can drive long-term optimization of resource allocation and enhance environmental performance in agriculture. On one hand, carbon trading markets internalize the social cost of emissions, making high-carbon enterprises face increasing marginal costs that compel them to optimize energy structures [15]. On the other hand, policies restricting the expansion of high-energy-consuming industries force enterprises to adopt renewable energy. These measures collectively demonstrate that implementing low-carbon city pilot policies significantly improves environmental performance in agricultural sectors [25].

Second, from the perspective of social performance. The low-carbon city pilot policy establishes carbon disclosure systems and green certification frameworks to create new legitimacy standards, thereby optimizing the social performance of agribusiness enterprises [34]. Specifically, these enterprises enhance brand image and consumer trust by disclosing environmental information. Simultaneously, the policy drives technological innovation, encouraging companies to upgrade existing technologies and develop green solutions aligned with low-carbon development needs [61]. Consequently, through green transformation, agribusinesses not only reduce carbon emissions but also achieve dual improvements in social reputation and brand value by expanding into new sectors like organic agricultural products. This comprehensive approach effectively elevates their overall social performance.

Third, from the perspective of governance effectiveness. The low-carbon city pilot policy empowers agricultural enterprises to enhance digital and information technologies, thereby optimizing their governance structures and refining management models [59]. Specifically, by implementing carbon quota trading mechanisms, the policy streamlines cash flow management and reduces financing costs. State-owned enterprises demonstrate greater responsiveness to policy incentives compared to private businesses [9]. Through supply chain transmission, this policy drives large state-owned enterprises to lead small and medium-sized farmers in adopting green technologies and establishing waste management systems. This creates a closed-loop green supply chain that elevates governance efficiency across the entire industrial chain [26].

Based on the above analysis, it can be found that the low-carbon city pilot policy is conducive to the improvement of environmental performance, social performance and governance performance of agriculture-related enterprises, and thus significantly improves the ESG performance of agriculture-related enterprises. Therefore, the first research hypothesis of this paper is proposed:

Hypothesis 1

Low-carbon city pilot policies simultaneously enhance the environmental, social, and governance performance of agribusinesses, thereby exerting a significant positive impact on their overall ESG performance.

Heterogeneity of ESG performance improvement

The Second Law of Geography posits that spatial variations in geographical elements are inherently uncontrollable, emphasizing their pronounced spatial heterogeneity [52]. China’s current economic development reveals regional disparities in economic foundations, resource endowments, and institutional environments. These differences result in significant regional variations in how low-carbon city pilot policies affect environmental performance, social impact, and governance effectiveness of agribusiness enterprises [19]; Yang & Liu [53]),. Consequently, the policy’s effects on the three-dimensional performance of agribusiness enterprises—environmental, social, and governance aspects—show distinct regional variations across eastern, central, and western economic zones.

First, although the eastern region, as China’s most economically developed area, leverages its robust economic strength and technological innovation capabilities to actively implement low-carbon city pilot policies, effectively enhancing the environmental-social-governance (ESG) triple performance of agricultural enterprises through introducing advanced eco-friendly technologies and optimizing resource allocation [13], it faces challenges in resource constraints and transformation pressures during development. This is due to characteristics such as a low rural population ratio and limited arable land reserves in the eastern region. Consequently, agricultural enterprises in the eastern area exhibit low impact effects when improving their ESG triple performance.

Second, as a crucial transitional zone in China’s agricultural economy, the central region boasts abundant agricultural resources and a substantial rural population base, providing vast development opportunities for agribusiness enterprises [47]. Under policy incentives, these enterprises are leveraging green transformation opportunities to develop eco-friendly organic products, enhance corporate social responsibility, and optimize governance structures. This has effectively reduced carbon emissions, promoted rural community development, and improved management efficiency and transparency [50]. Consequently, the environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance of agribusinesses in the central region may exhibit distinct characteristics when influenced by low-carbon city pilot policies.

Third, due to their remote geographical location, relatively weak economic foundations, and limited resource endowments, agricultural enterprises in western China face multiple challenges when responding to low-carbon city pilot policies [7]. However, the region boasts abundant renewable energy resources such as solar and wind power, which enable agricultural enterprises to develop green agriculture and circular economy. This not only helps reduce carbon emissions but also promotes sustainable utilization of agricultural resources and ecological improvement. Driven by low-carbon city pilot policies, agricultural enterprises in western China possess relatively significant advantages in low-carbon transition [60]. Therefore, the impact of these pilot policies on the environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance of agricultural enterprises in western China may be notably substantial.

This indicates that the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on the environmental performance, social performance and governance performance of agribusinesses shows significant regional heterogeneity, and thus the impact on the ESG performance of agribusinesses also shows significant regional differences. Therefore, the second hypothesis of this paper is proposed:

Hypothesis 2

There is significant regional heterogeneity in the impacts of low-carbon city pilot policies on the environmental, social, and governance performance dimensions of agribusinesses, which leads to substantial heterogeneity in their overall ESG performance.

The mediation effect of digital transformation

The development, promotion, and application of “economically viable and ecologically sustainable” agricultural technologies form the foundation for green agricultural development. Based on this premise, Hayami Yujiro proposed the renowned induced technological change theory [23]. This theory posits that agricultural productivity growth depends on continuous advancements in modern agricultural technologies, which manifest through two forms: mechanical innovation and physicochemical progress [20]. As a crucial component of modern agricultural technology, digital transformation has gradually become a key driver for enhancing ESG performance in agribusiness enterprises.

From the perspective of how low-carbon city pilot policies influence digital transformation in agricultural enterprises, these initiatives systematically enhance digital capabilities through policy guidance and institutional incentives [40]. First, the policies provide clear direction and support for digital transformation in agricultural enterprises. They encourage adopting advanced digital technologies and intelligent management systems to optimize production processes, improve resource efficiency, and reduce carbon emissions and environmental pollution. This policy orientation drives enterprises to prioritize digital transformation, increasing investments and R&D efforts in this field [48]. Second, financial support and tax incentives lower costs and risks associated with digitalization. This motivates more agricultural enterprises to embrace digital transformation, accelerating industry-wide adoption [2]. Finally, these policies promote widespread application of digital technologies. As policies deepen implementation, IoT, big data, and AI are increasingly integrated into agricultural production and management. The adoption of digital technologies has significantly improved operational efficiency and product quality in agricultural enterprises.

Digital transformation serves as a crucial pathway for agricultural enterprises to respond to low-carbon city pilot policies and enhance ESG performance. By optimizing resource allocation, improving production efficiency, strengthening environmental governance capabilities, and enhancing social responsibility and transparency, digital transformation positively impacts the environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance of agricultural enterprises [55]. Specifically: First, digital transformation enables precise management of agricultural resources, reduces waste and pollution, thereby improving environmental performance [29]. Second, it facilitates communication and collaboration between agricultural enterprises and partners, strengthens corporate social responsibility awareness, and enhances social performance. Third, it improves corporate governance structures, decision-making efficiency, and transparency, thereby elevating governance performance. Consequently, low-carbon city pilot policies indirectly boost the ESG performance of agricultural enterprises by driving their digital transformation (Fig. 1).

Accordingly, the third research hypothesis of this paper is proposed:

Hypothesis 3

Digital transformation significantly mediates the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on the environmental-social-governance performance of agribusinesses, thus influencing their ESG performance.

Research design

Samples and data resources

This paper studies the impact effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on the environmental, social and governance performance of agriculture-related enterprises. Therefore, the listed enterprises from 2007 to 2023 are taken as the research objects. The selection process of specific sample enterprises is as follows: First, agribusinesses are defined according to Fan et al. [10] and include those involved in ‘agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, and fishery’ as per the Guidelines for Industry Classification of Listed Companies (Revised in 2012). Additionally, it covers industries such as ‘agro-food processing’, ‘food manufacturing’, ‘wine, medical and refined tea manufacturing’, ‘textile industry’, ‘leather, fur, feather, and their products manufacturing’, ‘wood processing and wood, bamboo, rattan, palm, and grass products’, and ‘chemical raw materials and chemical products manufacturing’, which primarily engage in agricultural machinery, fertilizers, and other agricultural elements. This collected 7,389 sample data entries from 800 agricultural-related listed companies spanning 2007 to 2023. Second, considering the potential impact of abnormal data on empirical analysis, we excluded samples from ST (Stock Exchange’s designation for companies with financial irregularities or significant risks) and *ST (Stock Exchange’s designation for companies at delisting risk) listed companies, removing a total of 484 entries. This resulted in 6,905 data entries from 790 remaining listed companies. Third, to mitigate the influence of extreme values (outliers) on statistical analysis, we applied 1% truncation to all continuous variables. Fourth, we aligned processed data with ESG rating data, eliminating samples with severe data gaps, which totaled 278 entries. Notably, ESG rating data only became available starting from 2007. Therefore, the final dataset consists of unbalanced panel data from 781 listed agribusinesses with 6,627 observations from 2007 to 2023. Additionally, this study applies a 1% shrinkage to all continuous variables.

Variables

Explained variable

This study’s explanatory variable is the Environmental-Social-Governance (ESG) performance of agribusinesses. As domestic economic development began later than in other countries, the more comprehensive ESG evaluation systems now include those of CSI, Wind Company, Allied Wave, Business Way Ronggreen, and FTSE Russell. The CSI ESG evaluation system, based on the above-mentioned frameworks, incorporates indicators focused on reducing income inequality and participating in poverty alleviation with Chinese characteristics, alongside mainstream ESG indicators. This index covers all enterprises listed on Shanghai and Shenzhen A-shares in China and is currently the most recognized by academics. This paper uses the CSI ESG ratings, which assess the environmental, social, and governance performance of agribusinesses (ESG_E, ESG_S, and ESG_G, respectively). The CSI ESG performance is also used to measure the overall environmental-social-governance performance of agribusinesses. In this case, all the data were log transformed.

Core explanatory variable

The core explanatory variable in this study is the implementation of low-carbon city pilot policies, measured using a difference-in-differences (DID) approach: DID = treat × post. Here, treat is a dummy variable indicating whether a firm is located in a low-carbon pilot city. It takes the value of 1 if the enterprise is located in a pilot city and is thus subject to the policy; otherwise, it takes the value of 0. The low-carbon city pilot policy was first introduced in July 2010, designating 13 provinces and cities, including Guangdong and Yunnan, as initial pilot zones. In 2012, 29 more regions—including Hainan Province and Shijiazhuang City—were added, followed by an additional 45 cities, such as Dalian and Beijing, in 2017. Accordingly, this study sets 2010 as the policy implementation year. The variable post equals 1 for years t ≥ 2010, indicating the post-policy period, and 0 otherwise. The interaction term treat × post captures the differential impact of the policy over time, and its coefficient reflects the effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance of agribusinesses, which is the primary focus of this study.

Mediator variable

The mediating effect of digital transformation on the ESG performance of agricultural enterprises under low-carbon city pilot policies primarily refers to leveraging data resources and digital technologies like artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and big data to drive enterprise development. Consequently, digital transformation plays a crucial role in enhancing the ESG performance of agricultural enterprises through these policy initiatives. The mediating variable in this study is digital transformation (denoted as Digital). Following the approach of Luo et al. [33], this study measures the degree of digital transformation based on the frequency of digital-related keywords appearing in corporate annual reports. This method reflects not only the emphasis agribusinesses place on digital transformation, but also their strategic direction and development trends. Building upon Yu et al. [57] research foundation, this study employs Word2Vec machine learning to identify highly relevant terms from existing lexicons in the 14th Five-Year Plan for Digital Economy Development and Overall Layout Plan for Digital China Construction. The process expanded the existing lexicon to include 76 keywords related to digital transformation, covering fields such as artificial intelligence, blockchain, big data, digital technology, and cloud computing. Through text analysis of annual reports from listed agricultural enterprises, this paper extracted key terms to quantify the frequency of digital transformation- related expressions. Therefore, the digital transformation level of each enterprise is then calculated as the natural logarithm of the total keyword frequency plus one, i.e., Digital = ln(Frequency + 1).

Control variables

This study controls for several corporate-level factors that may influence both the implementation of low-carbon city pilot policies and the ESG performance of agribusinesses, following the approaches of Yu et al. [58]. The control variables include: the gearing ratio (Lev), asset turnover ratio (Ato), inventory-to-asset ratio (Inv), proportion of independent directors (Indep), management expense ratio (Mfee), cash flow ratio (Cashflow), and fixed asset ratio (Fixed). Specifically, the Lev is defined as the ratio of year-end total liabilities to year-end total assets; the Ato is the ratio of operating revenue to average total assets; the Inv is the ratio of net inventories to total assets; the Indep is calculated as the number of independent directors divided by the total number of board members; the Mfee is the ratio of overhead expenses to operating revenue; the Cashflow is the ratio of net cash flow from operating activities to total assets; and the Fixed is the proportion of net fixed assets to total assets. Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for the main variables used in this study. The results show that the mean ESG performance score is 3.49, with a maximum value of 4.374 and a minimum of 0.840, indicating substantial variation in ESG performance across agribusinesses. Similar observations apply to the core explanatory, mediating, and control variables, all of which exhibit reasonable distributions without apparent outliers.

Models

The PSM-DID model

In this study, referring to the research of He et al. [14] Propensity Score Matching (PSM) method is first applied to identify appropriate samples for the treatment group. Unmatched samples are subsequently excluded, resulting in a dataset suitable for PSM and Difference-in-Differences (DID) regression analysis. Enterprises located in areas designated as low-carbon city pilot zones between 2010 and 2023 comprise the treatment group, while the remaining enterprises constitute the control group. The matching variables include the seven control variables described earlier: the gearing ratio (Lev), asset turnover ratio (Ato), inventory-to-asset ratio (Inv), proportion of independent directors (Indep), management expense ratio (Mfee), cash flow ratio (Cashflow), and fixed asset ratio (Fixed). The empirical analysis

The results and examinations of propensity score matching (PSM)

The parallelism test

To ensure the unbiased estimation of the effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance of agribusinesses, the Difference-in-Differences (DID) regression model must satisfy the parallel trend assumption, which requires that the treatment and control groups follow similar trends prior to policy implementation. Accordingly, this study adopts an event study approach to test the parallel trend hypothesis, and the results are illustrated in Fig. 2. In the figure, the short vertical lines represent the 95% confidence intervals of the estimated coefficients for the interaction terms between time dummies and the treatment indicator. As shown, the coefficients before the policy implementation year (2010) are statistically insignificant, with confidence intervals crossing the horizontal zero line. In contrast, the coefficients become largely significant after the policy takes effect. These results indicate that the parallel trend assumption is satisfied, thereby validating the use of the DID regression model to examine the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance.

The result of parallel trend test

The balance test

To effectively address potential endogeneity arising from self-selection bias and to accurately estimate the net effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance of agribusinesses, the sample data were processed using the PSM model after confirming the parallel trend assumption. This approach helps ensure that the treatment and control groups exhibit comparable characteristics prior to policy implementation. The matching covariates used in the PSM procedure are the same control variables included in the above DID model. A 1:1 nearest neighbor matching algorithm was applied to identify the most comparable pairs between the treatment and control groups for subsequent DID analysis. Figure 3 presents the standardized mean differences of the covariates before and after matching. As shown, most covariates exhibited standardized deviations exceeding 10% before matching, indicating considerable differences between groups. However, after matching, all standardized deviations fell below the 10% threshold, suggesting that the matching procedure successfully reduced intergroup differences. Therefore, it can be concluded that the balance test is satisfied, and the matched samples of the treatment and control groups are statistically comparable.

The common support test

To further confirm the absence of significant differences between the treatment and control groups, a common support test was conducted following the balance test.

The kernel density distribution of the propensity scores

Figure 4 illustrates the kernel density distribution of the propensity scores for both groups after matching. Prior to nearest neighbor matching, the distributions of the two groups exhibited substantial divergence. However, after matching, the kernel density curves of the treatment and control groups closely overlap, indicating that the common support assumption is satisfied. Therefore, it can be concluded that the final matched sample lies within the region of common support.

The result of DID benchmark regression

To assess the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance of agribusinesses, this study first conducts a regression analysis of Model (1), yielding the results of the DID benchmark regression, as shown in Table 2. Table 2 presents the effects of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance, as well as on environmental, social, and governance performance, of agribusinesses. Columns (1) to (4) present the model’s analysis of low-carbon city pilot policies’ impact on ESG performance without control variables. The data analysis in the table reveals significant positive effects of these policies on ESG performance, environmental performance, social performance, and governance performance. Columns (5) to (8) demonstrate the regression results after incorporating control variables into the model. The regression results column reveal that the coefficient for the effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance is 0.239, which is statistically significant at the 1% level. This indicates a significant positive impact of the policies on agribusiness ESG performance. Simultaneously, the regression coefficients for the impacts of low-carbon city pilot policies on environmental performance, social performance, and governance performance are 0.603, 0.507, and 0.109, respectively, all of which are statistically significant at the 1% level. This shows that after the inclusion of control variables, the influence effect of low-carbon city pilot policy does not change, and the value of R-squared is significantly increased, which further verifies that the selected control variables in this paper are reasonable and scientific. These results indicate that the implementation of low-carbon city pilot policies significantly enhances agribusinesses environmental, social, and governance performance. The most pronounced effect is observed in environmental performance, thereby confirming hypothesis H1.

Therefore, analyzing the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance in agricultural enterprises, combined with the average values of each dimension, reveals that the relative improvement effect of ESG performance is 6.83%. The relative improvement effects for environmental performance, social performance, and governance performance are 22.14%,15.88%, and 3.43% respectively. This indicates that enhancing environmental performance serves as the primary pathway for improving ESG outcomes in agricultural enterprises, while the enhancement of governance performance shows relatively weaker momentum. The primary reason for this phenomenon is the dual-driven effect of policy constraints and market incentives. Specifically, the implementation of low-carbon city pilot policies strengthens regional carbon emission and intensity limits, compelling agribusinesses to adopt cleaner energy technologies in place of traditional high-pollution equipment. For agribusinesses, while initial investment is required, in the long run, this approach reduces energy costs and improves resource utilization. This creates a virtuous cycle of ‘reducing emissions – lowering costs – increasing efficiency’, thereby improving the environmental, social, and governance performance of enterprises. On the other hand, the implementation of low-carbon city pilot policies mandates that enterprises establish traceable green industrial supply chains, optimizing the entire supply chain during enterprise development to achieve upstream-downstream synergies in low-carbon development. For instance, when agricultural processing enterprises collaborate with planting bases to promote organic fertilizers, short-term costs may rise significantly. However, the improved product quality generates substantial premiums, with some products seeing a 30–50% increase in selling price. Additionally, this satisfies international carbon footprint certification requirements, expanding export share.

The placebo test

The above analyses reveal that low-carbon city pilot policies significantly promote ESG performance, as well as environmental, social, and governance performance, of agribusinesses. However, the empirical results of the benchmark regression may still be influenced by unobservable factors or omitted variables. To further test the robustness of these results, this study employs a placebo test. The study randomized the experimental group by selecting 500 random samples of the experimental group variables. In each instance, 58 samples were randomly drawn from the total of 781 agribusinesses to serve as pseudo-treatments, with the remaining samples used as pseudo-control groups. Since the pseudo-treatment groups are generated through random sampling, the average treatment effect should be distributed around zero. Any deviation from this suggests that the benchmark experiment is influenced by unmeasured factors or omitted variables. The kernel density distribution of the 500 randomized experiments (Fig. 5) indicates that the t-value estimates of the ‘experimental group × experimental year’ interaction are centered around zero. Most of the policy effect coefficients are smaller than the 0.239 observed in the baseline regression, suggesting that the PSM-DID estimates in the previous section pass the placebo test, and the baseline estimates are not affected by unobservable factors or omitted variables.

Regarding environmental, social, and governance performance, the policies have a significant impact on the environmental and social performance of agribusinesses in the central region. Data show that the environmental information disclosure rate of firms in the East is 40% higher than that in the Midwest, yet the improvement in governance performance is only 5% points higher, highlighting the difficulty of institutional change.

Analysis of the results of the mediation effects test

To further analyze the role of low-carbon city pilot policies in influencing the environmental-social-governance performance of agribusinesses, this paper constructs a mediation model based on the DID approach to empirically test the mediating effect of digital transformation on the relationship between low-carbon city pilot policies and ESG performance. The empirical results are presented in Table 7. First, the regression coefficient of low-carbon city pilot policies on digital transformation is 0.715, indicating that these policies significantly promote the level of digital transformation. Moreover, in the overall regression model, the mediating effect of digital transformation in low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance accounted for 30.51% of the total effect. Specifically, the mediating effect on environmental performance, social performance, and governance performance accounted for 36.76%, 15.23%, and 36.76%, respectively. These results indicate that digital transformation significantly mediates the effect of low-carbon city pilot policies on the ESG performance of agribusinesses, thus verifying hypothesis H3. From the perspective of social benefits, digital technology promotes precision agriculture and resource recycling, reducing energy consumption per unit of GDP. Data show that energy consumption of agribusinesses adopting digital management drops by 15–20%, resulting in cost advantages and a significant improvement in carbon performance. Second, digitalization enhances transparency in the agricultural supply chain, compelling enterprises to adopt green technologies, such as blockchain traceability systems that make the carbon footprint of agricultural products traceable, thus enhancing consumer trust and increasing the premium price of green products. Third, digital tools such as carbon accounts and carbon credits quantify emission reduction targets and link them to financing and tax incentives, allowing agribusinesses in pilot areas to generate additional revenue through carbon trading, forming a positive cycle of ‘emission reduction – revenue – reinvestment’.

Discussion

This study investigates the impact pathways of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance in agricultural enterprises. Through systematic analysis, three key conclusions emerge: First, these policies demonstrate significant positive effects on ESG performance in agriculture-related firms, though their capacity to enhance governance effectiveness remains relatively limited. This finding aligns with findings from Eccles et al. [9, 12], which indicate that listed companies exhibit moderate efficiency gains in governance improvements, primarily reflecting structural inertia in governance mechanisms that typically respond more slowly to policy changes. Second, there exists notable heterogeneity in how low-carbon city policies affect ESG performance across agricultural enterprises. Wan et al. [44] highlight that such policies make substantial contributions to corporate ESG practices, particularly benefiting firms located in eastern regions, those with stable institutional investors, larger enterprises, and those demonstrating superior internal control quality. Furthermore, the impact of low-carbon city pilot policies on governance performance in agriculture-related enterprises in central China shows statistically insignificant results. This phenomenon stems from the incomplete manifestation of policy effects in central cities, imperfect transmission mechanisms, and difficulties in translating short-term measures into tangible outcomes. In contrast, eastern regions leverage their geographical, capital, and technological advantages to rapidly absorb policy benefits, while western areas, despite economic underdevelopment, benefit from green agricultural policies through resource allocation and ecological compensation mechanisms [49]. Third, green technological innovation acts as a mediating factor in the policy’s impact on ESG performance. Xu et al. [51] propose that green technological innovation serves as a crucial mechanism for improving corporate ESG quality under low-carbon city policies, showing dual improvements in both quantity and quality. Notably, energy-saving green technologies demonstrate enhanced effectiveness in elevating corporate ESG standards within this policy framework. Based on the above analysis, it can be found that the conclusion of this study is correct and reasonable, which is consistent with the existing research conclusions.

Although this study analyzed the impact mechanism of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance of agricultural enterprises through sample data from 2007 to 2023, and explored the mediating effect of digital transformation, it holds significant theoretical and practical implications. However, the research still has three limitations and future research directions: First, the measurement of the mediating variable, digital transformation, through keyword frequency in annual reports raises validity concerns. This study evaluates digital transformation through keyword frequency analysis in corporate annual reports. While this approach can partially reflect the digitalization level of agricultural enterprises, its validity may be compromised due to methodological limitations in capturing actual enterprise-level digital adoption and transformation progress. Future research should establish an evaluation index system for enterprise-level digital transformation, measuring comprehensive indices from dimensions such as digital infrastructure development and technological innovation capabilities to achieve more precise assessment of digital transformation levels. Secondly, the impact mechanisms of low-carbon city pilot policies on ESG performance in agricultural enterprises involve complex interrelationships. This study only examines the mediating role of digital transformation while neglecting mediating effects from variables like green technology innovation, green finance, and industrial structure upgrading. Future research could expand mediating effect analysis dimensions to better understand how low-carbon city pilot policies enhance ESG performance in agricultural enterprises. Thirdly, the empirical data and model construction in this study are relatively simplistic. The use of the PSM-DID model and mediation effect models to explore relationships between low-carbon city policies, digital transformation, and agricultural enterprise ESG performance may introduce positive bias due to small sample estimation. Therefore, future studies should employ dual-machine learning and explainable machine learning models to investigate the mechanisms through which low-carbon city policies influence agricultural enterprise ESG performance.

Results and findings

Research results

With growing awareness of sustainable development, ESG performance has increasingly become a focal point for agribusinesses and their stakeholders. Using unbalanced panel data from 781 listed agribusinesses in China between 2007 and 2023, this paper employs DID and PSM-DID models to estimate the average treatment effect of low-carbon city pilot policies and verify their direct impact on ESG performance. Second, the agribusinesses are categorized into eastern, central, and western regions to examine regional heterogeneity in the impact of these policies on ESG performance; Finally, a mediating effect model is constructed to empirically examine the mechanism through which digital transformation affects ESG performance. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Low-carbon city pilot policies significantly enhance the environmental, social, and governance performance of agribusinesses, thereby improving their overall ESG performance. Specifically, the regression coefficient of the policies’ impact on ESG performance is 0.239 and is statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating a strong positive effect. Furthermore, the policies also exert significant positive impacts on environmental, social, and governance performance individually, with the magnitude of impact decreasing in the order of environmental, social, and governance performance.

(2) Regional heterogeneity is evident in how low-carbon city pilot policies influence the ESG performance—specifically, the environmental, social, and governance dimensions—of agribusinesses. Specifically, in the central region, these policies significantly enhance environmental and social performance, but have no notable effect on governance performance. Moreover, the impact of these policies on ESG and environmental performance is more pronounced in the western region than in the eastern region, whereas their influence on social and governance performance is stronger in the eastern region. Among the three ESG dimensions, environmental performance shows the most significant improvement across all major economic regions, followed by social performance, while governance performance exhibits the least impact.

(3) Digital transformation plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between low-carbon city pilot policies and the ESG performance—including environmental, social, and governance dimensions—of agribusinesses. Specifically, the mediating effect of digital transformation on the ESG performance of agribusinesses accounts for 30.51% of the total effect. For environmental, social, and governance performance individually, the mediating effects of digital transformation account for 36.76%, 15.23%, and 36.76% of the total effect, respectively.

Policy recommendations

Based on the above findings, this paper proposes the following policy recommendations:

(1) Leveraging policy advantages to strengthen the sustainable development capabilities of agricultural enterprises and achieve multi-dimensional efficiency optimization. The low-carbon city pilot policies can implement differentiated measures based on the characteristics of agribusinesses, driving their transition to low-carbon, green, and sustainable models. Firstly, for crop cultivation-oriented agribusinesses, supporting low-carbon agronomic innovations, standardizing agricultural non-point source pollution disclosure, and establishing carbon sink trading mechanisms can significantly enhance corporate carbon performance while reducing carbon emission intensity per unit output value. Secondly, for livestock breeding enterprises, reducing carbon emissions from manure production and improving environmental quality at production sites contribute to agricultural ecosystem sustainability. This also encourages companies to develop organic agricultural products and carbon footprint traceability services. Thirdly, policies incentivize enterprises to strengthen full-chain management across planting, breeding, and processing. By building digital agricultural parks and promoting smart farming technologies to improve operational efficiency, these measures ultimately promote green transformation and industrial upgrading in agriculture through optimized energy structures and enhanced environmental quality.

(2) Develop differentiated regional policy frameworks based on distinct resource endowments and economic foundations. Eastern China should prioritize enhancing carbon trading mechanisms through pilot programs for paid allocation of carbon quotas and cross-regional transactions, guiding agricultural enterprises to incorporate carbon assets into financing collateral systems. Leveraging industrial clusters in the Yangtze River Delta and Pearl River Delta regions, establish green technology innovation alliances to accelerate R&D and commercialization of smart agricultural machinery and low-carbon farming technologies. Central China should focus on core grain production chains by implementing standardized farming practices, establishing regional monitoring networks for agricultural non-point source pollution, and reducing implementation costs for fertilizer and pesticide substitution through fiscal subsidies and tax incentives. This approach also prevents high-energy-consuming processing sectors from relocating to ecologically fragile areas. Western China should capitalize on its ecological barrier functions by strengthening dual channels of ecological compensation and technology transfer. Through policies like national key ecological function zone transfers and carbon sink subsidies, support the development of specialty industries such as grassland ecotourism and organic forestry.

(3) Strengthening the pivotal role of digital transformation in enhancing agricultural enterprises ‘ESG performance requires dual policy considerations: cost-effectiveness and efficiency. First, agricultural enterprises should enhance data-driven decision-making capabilities through digital infrastructure deployment such as IoT sensors and blockchain traceability systems, which improves decision quality and transparency. However, small and medium-sized enterprises face challenges in technology procurement costs and digital talent development. Governments should reduce initial investment barriers through targeted subsidies and tax incentives. Second, digital transformation should prioritize energy structure optimization and supply chain integration. Intelligent monitoring platforms enable real-time energy tracking and emission reduction, though fragmented agricultural production scenarios may increase data collection costs. Establishing regional agricultural data sharing mechanisms could help distribute infrastructure expenses. Additionally, while integrating supply chain resources through digital platforms improves resource utilization efficiency, attention must be paid to cybersecurity investments and farmers’ digital literacy training costs during platform development.
