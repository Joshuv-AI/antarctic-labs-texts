Treatment outcome and its predictors among children with epilepsy on chronic follow-up in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Gebremariam Wulie Geremew, Yilkal Abebaw Wassie, Gebresilassie Tadesse, Setegn Fentahun, Abebaw Setegn Yazie, Sisay Sitotaw Anberbr, Gebremariam Genet, Abaynesh Fentahun Bekalu, Gashaw Sisay Chanie, Tekletsadik Tekleslassie Alemayehu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeventy percent of epileptic patients may not experience seizures if they receive proper treatment with antiepileptic drugs (AEDs). However, many children and adolescents face poor seizure control (PSC). Therefore, the purpose of this review is to systematically and quantitatively summarize the pooled prevalence of PSC and its predictors among children with epilepsy in Ethiopia.MethodsThe following databases were used to conduct a thorough literature search: Africa Journal of the Online Library, Hinari, Google Scholar, PubMed, Science Direct, EMBASE, Cochrane Database, Sci-Hub, and Scopus. To evaluate the quality of the studies, Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS) checklists were utilized. The data on study characteristics and prevalence estimates were then combined via random effects meta-analysis, followed by subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Both visual and statistical analyses were employed to assess for any potential publication bias.ResultsThis review analyzed a total of 12 main studies and reported that the overall prevalence of PSC was 42.42% (95% CI: 33.42–51.42). However, there was significant heterogeneity in the results based on the study region, design, and seizure-free period. The subgroup analysis revealed that the highest prevalence of PSC was found in Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ (SNNPs) studies (61.88%; 95% CI: 35.91–87.85), studies with a cross-sectional design (46.73%; 95% CI: 36.83–56.62), and studies with a seizure-free period < 6 months (44.69%; 95% CI: 34.51–54.86). However, the lowest prevalence was observed in the Amhara region (35.54%; 95% CI: 27.40–43.67), cohort studies (29.53%; 95% CI: 21.26–38.21), and studies with a seizure-free duration of six months or more (41.64%; 95% CI: 29.94–53.35). The results also revealed a significant correlation between PSC and medication nonadherence (4.64, 95% CI: 2.84–7.58), comorbidities (2.08, 95% CI: 1.01–4.26), and seizure type (3.59, 95% CI: 1.18–10.8).ConclusionBased on this review, the findings suggest a notable prevalence of poorly controlled seizures (PSC) among children with epilepsy who are receiving antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) in Ethiopian outpatient epilepsy clinics. Seizures of tonic‒clonic status, comorbidities, and medication nonadherence were associated with poor seizure control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20441-9.Keywords: Children, Epilepsy, Predictors, Treatment outcomes, Ethiopia

Introduction

More than seventy million people in the world, ranging in age from infants to adults, suffer from epilepsy, a devastating neurological illness [1]. At least one epileptic seizure is expected in approximately 10% of the population who live normal lifespans. Approximately sixty-five million people worldwide suffer from epilepsy, 80% of whom reside in developing nations [2]. African countries are among the most badly affected regions, with an estimated 10 million individuals on the continent suffering from epilepsy [3, 4]. According to studies, every year, approximately 150,000 children worldwide will experience their first unprovoked seizure. Among these, 30,000 patients develop epilepsy [5]. The first year of life, or between the ages of 1 and 12, is when epilepsy has its highest incidence, which is 102 cases per 100,000 cases annually. The incidence of epilepsy is 21–24 cases per 100,000 between the ages of 11 and 17 years [6, 7]. Childhood epilepsy has an unjustified societal stigma in some regions of Africa because affected children are sometimes mistakenly classified as having a communicable condition or are mistakenly believed to be possessed by an evil spirit [8]. Avoidance and isolation at school could arise from this. Accordingly, the societal stigma associated with pediatric epilepsy in sub-Saharan Africa may contribute to an underreporting of the disease’s genuine burden [8]. Similarly, epilepsy affects Ethiopia, with a yearly incidence rate of 64 per 100,000 people and a prevalence rate of 5.2 per 1000 people [9, 10].

Seventy percent of these individuals may not experience seizures if they receive proper treatment with antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) [2, 11]. However, many children and adolescents have unanswered questions about their medications and may not fully understand the serious consequences of missing doses [12]. The International League against Epilepsy defines seizure remission as the absence of all seizures for at least one year, although other definitions may vary in the literature. This is the most commonly used term [13]. Poor seizure control (PSC) results in a decreased quality of life, severe physical harm, compromised cognitive functioning, lower rates of marriage, lower levels of education, lower employment, a decreased ability to engage in social activities, and a shorter lifespan [14, 15]. A precise diagnosis of the type of seizure, knowledge about medication, and adherence to treatment are crucial for a patient’s response [16]. In Ethiopia, there is a discrepancy in treatment outcomes among children with epilepsy who are receiving AEDs, with reported rates ranging from 22.9 to 53% [17, 18]. Nonadherence to antiepileptic medication [14, 17, 19–22], caregiver relationships [21], frequent seizures (≥ 4 episodes per week) before starting AEDs, and a history of head injury before the onset of seizures [22] have been identified as potential causes of poor treatment outcomes. Other factors that may contribute to poor outcomes include negative beliefs about medication, comorbidities, alcohol consumption [14], a history of increased seizure frequency before starting treatment, a history of head injury, being female [17], an age of onset of less than 20 years, a family history of epilepsy, the need for monotherapy, and social support [23], which could be the causes of poor treatment outcomes. It can be challenging for physicians and information users to develop effective interventions and make informed decisions due to this variability. Thus, the goal of this review was to compile the differing outcomes of multiple investigations about treatment outcomes for children with epilepsy into a single, complete document that would allow the findings of these studies to be examined and reviewed. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this review is the first to address treatment outcomes and their determinants among Ethiopian children with epilepsy. To improve treatment outcomes and enhance clinical practices by reducing the incidence of seizure episodes nationwide, the data from this study will be used to inform the design of evidence-based solutions by clinicians and child health policymakers.

Methods and materials

Reporting, protocol registration, and search strategy

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines for observational studies [24] were followed in the conduct and reporting of this systematic review and meta-analysis. The review procedure was registered with the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number “CRD42024567977.” Two of the authors (GWG and TTA) conducted an electronic search between April 1, 2024, and June 15, 2024. We searched for free publications via databases medical and health science databases (PubMed, EMBASE (Excerpta Medica Database), the Cochrane Library, and Hinari (Health Internetwork Access to Research Initiative), scientific and academic database (Science Direct, Scopus, and Google Scholar), and Open access database (Sci-Hub (Scientific Hub), Africa journals online, and Hinari). Additionally, we searched the institutional repositories of Addis Ababa, Jimma, and Gondar Universities to include any relevant unpublished works from Ethiopia. The key terms used to search primary articles were treatment outcome OR treatment effectiveness OR clinical effectiveness OR patient-relevant outcome OR rehabilitation outcome OR treatment failure OR progression-free survival OR disease-free survival OR seizure control AND antiepileptic drugs OR anticonvulsant drugs OR antiseizure medications AND epilepsy OR epileptic Syndromes OR seizure AND Children OR pediatrics OR adolescent AND Ethiopia. In addition, searching reference lists of all the included studies (snowball technique) was done to retrieve other studies that are not addressed by our searching strategies.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria for the studies were incomplete information, records without an outcome of interest, conclusions drawn from individual viewpoints, editorial reports, letters to the editor, case reports and series, systematic reviews, and qualitative investigations. A quality assessment tool was used to evaluate studies and exclude those that did not meet a predefined quality threshold.

Furthermore, articles that were only available in abstract form were disqualified because it was challenging to assess their quality and extract all relevant data.

To ensure the consistent application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria for all the studies in this review, the following methods were applied. A detailed protocol outlining clear inclusion and exclusion criteria was developed. Train reviewers on the protocol and criteria to ensure that they understand and apply them correctly. A process of independent review in which multiple reviewers assess each study was implemented. This will help to identify any discrepancies and allow them to be resolved through discussion or consensus. Moreover, regular audits should be conducted by periodically reviewing a sample of included and excluded studies. This confirms that the criteria are being consistently applied. Overall, it is important to have a clear and detailed protocol, well-trained reviewers, and a process for resolving discrepancies and auditing the application of criteria. By following these steps, we can ensure that our application process is consistent and reliable.

Study selection

Using the PRISMA standard [25], two authors (GWG and TTA) independently assessed whether primary studies were eligible to be included in this review.

Using Endnote citation management, duplicate articles from all of the detected studies were first avoided. The titles and abstracts of the articles were then reviewed to assess their relevance. Studies reporting at least one of the following (antiepileptic medicine, antiepileptic drugs, antiepileptic medication, treatment outcome, seizure control, seizure-free period, epilepsy, or seizure) were taken into consideration for full-text reading evaluation in the title and abstract evaluation. Following the reading of the full texts of the chosen articles, studies that met the eligibility requirements were added to this meta-analysis and systematic review. Conflicts between the two assessors were resolved by the authors working together to reevaluate eligibility.

Data extraction

Reviewers GWG and TTA used Microsoft Excel 2019 to create a standardized data abstraction form for extracting the necessary data. The form included key outcomes such as the prevalence of poor seizure control, its predictors, and the effect size (OR) with lower and upper confidence intervals. Other important information, such as study design, region, sample size, primary and secondary variables/outcomes, seizure-free period, and year of study and publication, was also included in the form. The authors carefully extracted these significant data, and any disagreements between the two assessors were resolved during a joint reevaluation of eligibility.

Outcome measurement

The pooled prevalence of poor seizure control and its predictors among children with epilepsy was the outcome of this meta-analysis and systematic review. In a controlled seizure, the patient had not experienced any seizures within the past year. In contrast, poor seizure control refers to a patient who has had one or more seizures during the previous year’s follow-up period [26, 27]. The direct reports of the primary studies were used to measure poor seizure control. We also monitored poor seizure control in studies where the seizure control of individuals receiving epilepsy outpatient treatment in Ethiopia was recorded as an explanatory variable of other outcome variables. Seizure attack was measured in the primary studies by organizing appointments to an outpatient clinic.

Quality assessment

With the use of the Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS) as a quality assessment tool [28], both authors (GWG and TTA) independently assessed the overall characteristics of the original articles. The NOS quality assessment tool is composed of three main components: outcome, selection, and comparability. The selection component includes four subheadings, the comparability component consists of one subheading, and the outcome component contains three subheadings. Each numbered item within the selection and outcome categories allows a study to earn up to one star, while the comparability component allows for a maximum rating of two stars. Cross-sectional studies can receive up to 6 stars in total, while cohort studies are eligible for a maximum of 9 stars. Disagreements between the two assessors were resolved by discussing and calculating the average of the two assessments’ scores. This review included articles that scored six or above on the quality assessment criterion, indicating a low risk of bias.

Statistical procedures

Data extracted from Microsoft Excel 2019 were imported into STATA version 14 for analysis. The standard error for the prevalence of poorly controlled seizures in each original study was calculated using the binomial distribution formula. To assess variability across the primary studies, a chi-square test was applied. Chi-square values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% corresponded to no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [29].

To maintain consistency across the different effect estimates in our meta-analysis, we adjusted certain variables to evaluate their relationship with poorly controlled seizures (PSC) using appropriate reference categories. For instance, to analyze medication nonadherence as a categorical variable, we extracted or computed the odds ratios (ORs) for PSC in nonadherence groups compared to adherence groups. When studies reported continuous factors, we presented their effect estimates separately.

In this meta-analysis, which included both cohort and cross-sectional studies, standardized methods were used to combine effect sizes. Odds ratios were predominantly used for cross-sectional studies, while risk ratios and hazard ratios were utilized for cohort studies. To ensure comparability, we converted all effect measures, when necessary, into common metric using statistical methods such as the inverse variance method. This allowed us to accurately estimate a pooled effect size, capturing the relationship between risk factors and outcomes across different study designs. Notably, no major difficulties were encountered in harmonizing the effect sizes.

Using DerSimonian and Laird’s random-effects meta-analysis, we estimated the pooled effect of poorly controlled seizures and the pooled odds ratios for associated factors. Meta-regression was employed to explore the influence of study-level characteristics, such as publication year and sample size, on effect sizes and outcomes. This analysis helped identify variables that could explain heterogeneity across the included studies. Additionally, subgroup analysis was conducted using screening tools for poor seizure control to reduce random variations in point estimates from the primary studies. Publication bias was assessed through Egger’s test, with a 5% significance level, and visually inspected using a funnel plot [30].

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses are helpful in examining differences between groups or determining how the features of a particular group impact the prediction of the pooled prevalence and the reason for heterogeneity among studies. By breaking down the region, research design, and seizure-free period, it was possible to assess the prevalence of poor seizure control in children with epilepsy. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated, along with percentages representing the prevalence of poor seizure control among children with epilepsy in Ethiopia. Meta-analysis, publication bias assessment, and sensitivity analysis were conducted by GWG.

Results

Properties of the studies that were part of the analysis

A total of 100 primary articles were identified through manual and database searches of the literature. Following the removal of duplicate papers and a careful examination of the abstracts of the articles, 21 articles were evaluated using the qualifying criteria. Nine irrelevant articles were thus eliminated since they did not meet the established qualifying requirements.

Because they satisfied the inclusion criteria, a total of 12 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The articles were released between 2014 and 2024, although one was obtained from the Haramaya University repository and was never published. Approximately half of the investigations (n = 6) were conducted in the Amhara region, while the remaining were carried out in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ (SNNPs) (n = 2), Oromia region (n = 1), and Addis Ababa (n = 3). The sample sizes of the included studies varied widely, ranging from 82 to 385 individuals. The quality of the primary studies was assessed via the Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale, a widely accepted method for evaluating study quality. Studies with a score of six or higher out of ten were considered to be of acceptable quality [31]. For this analysis, a total of 12 main studies were used to calculate the pooled prevalence of treatment outcomes for 2738 children receiving antiepileptic medication and accessing follow-up services for epilepsy in Ethiopia (Table 1). The most commonly used AEDs were phenobarbitone (71%), phenytoin (42.9%), and valproic acid (12.4%) [17] (55.8%), (28.1%), and (8.1%) [21]; phenytoin (53.5%), (30.5%), and (9%) [32]; (41.0%), (27.2%) [33]; (31.4%), (33.6%), and (25.6%) [18]; (39%), (15.9%) [34]; (19.6%), (10.8%) [35]; (20.2%), (50.6%) [36]; (63%) [37]; (42.7%) [38]; and (62.3%), (30.9%) [39], respectively. Only seven studies reported that the type of seizure among these generalized tonic‒clonic seizures was the most common seizure, followed by the focal type of seizure [18, 32, 34, 36, 37, 39, 40]. Among the 12 studies, only five reported secondary causes of seizures; among these secondary causes, the most common are central nervous system (CNS) infections, such as meningitis, CNS tuberculosis, and viral meningitis; febrile illness; hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy; electrolyte disturbance; posttraumatic, head injury; and a history of prenatal insult [18, 21, 32, 34, 38] (Table 2).

Study quality

Among the 12 studies, nine out of the twelve investigations, or 75% of the total [23, 32, 33, 35–37, 39, 41], were cross-sectional studies. Two of the studies were retrospective follow-up studies [17, 21], and one prospective cohort study [38] scored at least 6 points out of 9 on the NOS [28] and was considered high quality. The median score was 8 (range 6–9). All of the included studies were considered high-quality (scoring ≥ 6). The low-quality studies lacked suitable statistical procedures, did not specify the sampling process, and did not control for potential confounders in the design and statistical analysis.

Poor seizure control among children receiving AEDs

According to this review, the total pooled prevalence of poor seizure control among Ethiopian children receiving AEDs among children was 42.42% (95% CI: 33.42–51.42) (Fig. 2). Individual studies conducted in Ethiopia revealed significant variations in the prevalence of poor seizure control. Among these patients, 22.9% had the lowest prevalence of inadequate seizure control, according to a study performed at UOGCSH [17]. An investigation carried out at the Gurage Zone in SNNPs [23] revealed the highest prevalence of poor seizure control (75%).

Heterogeneity and publication bias

The current systematic review and meta-analysis included 12 studies, all of which had high levels of heterogeneity, as indicated by the test statistics (I-squared = 96.0%, p value = 0.000). Two techniques were used to assess whether publication bias existed in the included studies. First, a funnel plot (Fig. 3) was constructed to show that the included studies had a symmetrical distribution and were not biased by publication. Furthermore, the value in Table 3, p = 0.38, shows that Egger’s test was performed to confirm that there was no publication bias. Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis were used to distinguish between the reasons for heterogeneity.

Poor seizure control prevalence as determined by subgroup analysis

The degree of poor seizure control varied significantly depending on the study region, study design, and seizure-free period, according to a subgroup analysis of the primary studies. The Amhara region had the lowest pooled prevalence of poor seizure control (35.54%) in the subgroup analysis of poor seizure control by study region, whereas SNNPs had the greatest prevalence (61.88%) (Fig. 4). In terms of the subgroup analysis by study design, the cohort study design had the lowest pooled prevalence of poor seizure control (29.73%), whereas the cross-sectional study design had the highest pooled prevalence (46.73%) (Fig. 5). Most of the studies (75%) defined controlled seizures as having seizure control for at least six months, whereas the other studies (25%) defined controlled seizures as having seizure freedom for fewer than six months. The length of the seizure-free period affected the pooled prevalence of seizure control, with a shorter seizure-free period (less than six months) resulting in a greater pooled prevalence of poor seizure control (44.69%) (Fig. 6). A meta-regression analysis was conducted to assess the effects of publication year on the PSC. However, the results indicate that there is no strong evidence to suggest that years since publication significantly influences the outcome (p = 0.348). Additionally, a Meta regression was conducted to assess the effects of sample size on PSC. However, the results indicate that there is no strong evidence to suggest that sample size significantly influences PSC (p = 0.403) (Table 4).

Factors associated with poor seizure control (PSC) among children treated with AEDs

Among the 12 articles included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, nine reported both the magnitude and the factors associated with poor seizure control, whereas three studies reported only the magnitude of PSC. Multiparity [38], family history, a lack of social support, multiple seizures, monotherapy, duration of AED use, urban residence, education level, and recurrent seizures have been reported to be associated with PSC. Variables identified as predictors of PSC among at least two original studies were taken into consideration for this meta-analysis, although several characteristics were reported as determinants of PSC. Thus, to assess their association with PSC, the following factors were included in this meta-analysis: medication nonadherence, young age, comorbidities, female sex, seizure frequency, seizure type (tonic‒clonic), and multiple AEDs.

Nine [17, 18, 21, 32, 34, 35, 37, 38, 41] studies were included to identify the associations between nonadherence and poor seizure control. Six of these studies [17, 21, 32, 35, 37, 41] reported a statistically significant association between medication nonadherence and poor seizure control. According to the random effects model estimate, the pooled odds of developing poor seizure control among nonadherent children were 64% greater than those among adherent children [OR = 4.64, 95% CI (2.84–7.58)]. In addition, three studies [18, 32, 34] reported a statistically significant association between comorbidities and PSC. According to the random effects model estimate, the pooled odds of developing PSC among children with comorbidities were that these children were 8% more likely to develop PSC than noncomorbid children [OR = 2.08, 95% CI (1.01–4.26)]. Moreover, two studies [37, 38] reported statistically significant associations between seizure type (tonic‒clonic) and PSC. According to the random effects model estimate, the pooled odds of developing PSC among children with tonic‒clonic seizures were 59% greater than those among children with other types of seizures [OR = 3.59, 95% CI (1.18–10.8)] (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Children with epilepsy face a variety of challenges, such as poor academic achievement and school withdrawal, injuries and cerebral trauma, prejudice and stigma, behavioral disorders, delayed cognitive development, and early death [42, 43]. Research has shown that with the use of proper antiseizure medication, almost 60% of children with epilepsy can stop experiencing seizures [39, 44–47]. This systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to estimate the pooled prevalence of poor seizure control and its determinants among children with epilepsy in Ethiopia.

The findings of this review suggest that 42.42% of Ethiopian children with epilepsy who were treated with antiepileptic medication still experienced inadequate seizure control. An investigation carried out in Nigeria [8] and Ethiopia [14] supported this finding. This finding contrasts with other clinical guidelines, the WHO report [48], and the Cochrane review [49, 50]. There are several factors that could contribute to this discrepancy, including variations in the study population, medication availability, research design, seizure type, lack of awareness, limited diagnostic resources resulting in treatment delays, comorbidity, the definition of uncontrolled seizures, and the degree of medication nonadherence among the primary studies included. Notably, these results were lower than those of a previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia [51]. This could be due to differences in the study population, as the previous review focused mainly on adults, with only one study including children and two studies including both age groups. The current systematic review suggests that pharmacoresistant epilepsy may be the underlying cause of PSC [52, 53]. This finding was verified by an Ethiopian systematic review that identified nonadherence to antiepileptic medication as a contributing factor [51, 54]. To maximize the effectiveness of therapy, it is crucial to recognize pharmacoresistant epilepsy and implement suitable management strategies, in addition to promoting medication adherence among epileptic children.

Subgroup analysis of this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a significant difference in reports of poor seizure control among primary studies on the basis of differences in study region, seizure-free period, and study design. As a result, the study design, which is cross-sectional (46.73%), studies using a seizure-free period of less than six months (44.69%), and SNNPs (61.88%), had the highest prevalence, followed by the Oromia region (43.3%). This discrepancy can be explained by the possibility that the higher prevalence of poor seizure control may be detected earlier in the follow-up period rather than later because, over the long term, there may be an increased risk of drug resistance, comorbidities, exposure to seizure-triggering factors, and other factors [14, 55]. In this review, nine articles (or 75%) defined a poorly controlled seizure as one that lasted at least six months, as advised by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) [56]. The remaining papers (or 25%) defined a poorly controlled seizure as one that lasted less than six months. Regional differences in poor seizure control may be attributed to a variety of factors, such as variations in healthcare infrastructure, cultural and societal influences, and healthcare provider training. In addition, poor seizure control may vary across different study designs, possibly because of differences in the selection of subjects, temporal relationships, and outcome measurements.

The second goal of this systematic review was to identify the factors that contribute to poor seizure control in children with epilepsy in Ethiopia. The results revealed that poor seizure control was significantly correlated with comorbidity seizure type (tonic‒clonic) and nonadherence to antiepileptic medication. Compared with noncomorbid children, the pooled effect of comorbidities in epileptic children was a 2.08-fold greater risk of having inadequate seizure control. This is in line with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [22, 32, 57]. This could be partially explained by the fact that people with comorbidities often take multiple drugs, which can lower medication adherence and increase the adverse effect profile of AEDs [58, 59]. Compared with children with other seizure types, children with tonic‒clonic seizures are 3.59 times more likely to have poor seizure control, possibly because tonic‒clonic seizures are less likely to respond to AEDs [60]. This result is in line with prior studies [37, 38]. Furthermore, compared with children who were adherent to AEDs, children who did not take their medication as prescribed were 4.64 times more likely to have poor seizure control. Numerous investigations in Ethiopia [14, 20], the United Kingdom [61], and the USA [62], which support our findings, have demonstrated that poor seizure control is caused mostly by medication nonadherence.

Overall, the results of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that many children visiting Ethiopia’s epilepsy clinics’ outpatient departments have poorly controlled epilepsy. This highlights the need for a team effort to identify key contributing factors, such as comorbidities and medication nonadherence, and make informed decisions during routine clinical care to prevent negative impacts on social interactions, damage peer connections, lower self-esteem, deteriorate cognitive functions, and prevent self-reliance. These factors should be continuously assessed during therapy because they have a significant impact on each child’s unique development [63]. Consequently, professionals are advised to consider the vital role of the psychosocial assistance network and how it integrates with medication intervention [64]. Medical professionals, policymakers, and clinical pharmacists may find it useful to identify risk factors for inadequate seizure control to develop effective education campaigns and suitable interventions.

Strengths and limitations

The purpose of this review was to investigate the treatment outcomes and predictors for Ethiopian children who have epilepsy. We conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis using rigorous methodology, incorporating subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and adjusted odds ratios. The quality of the study results was ensured through the use of a quality indicator, which allowed us to select only reliable and well-conducted studies.

Several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, key variables such as the cause of epilepsy and patient age were inconsistently reported across the included studies, making it challenging to evaluate their impact on treatment outcomes. This lack of data may have influenced the overall conclusions of the review. Second, the review was limited to articles written in English, potentially introducing language bias and limiting the generalizability of the findings. However, no language-related issues were encountered during the search process. Additionally, significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies, which may have affected the accuracy of the pooled prevalence estimates. Suggestions for explaining the observed heterogeneity include variations in the study design, study region, and seizure free period.

While one gray literature source [36] was included, gray literature often lacks the rigorous peer-review process that published articles undergo, potentially affecting the quality of the evidence. Nevertheless, the inclusion of gray literature can help mitigate publication bias and provide a broader perspective on the topic.

It is important to discuss these limitations in greater detail, particularly how they may have influenced the results. Future research should focus on addressing these shortcomings, such as by ensuring the consistent reporting of key variables and incorporating studies in multiple languages to enhance the generalizability of findings.

Conclusion

Based on this review, the findings suggest a notable prevalence of poorly controlled seizures (PSC) among children with epilepsy who are receiving antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) in Ethiopian outpatient epilepsy clinics. The primary studies that revealed this issue showed considerable heterogeneity in the prevalence of PSC. This is because these studies used different definitions of PSC, had varying study designs, and were conducted in different regions.

In Ethiopia, factors such as tonic‒clonic seizures, comorbidities, and medication nonadherence were found to be associated with poor seizure control among children with epilepsy. Moreover, we suggest that risk factors for PSC, such as nonadherence to medication and comorbidities, should be prioritized by health planners, policymakers, and the community in general.
