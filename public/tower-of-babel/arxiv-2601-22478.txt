Transform-Augmented GRPO Improves Pass@k
Khiem Le, Youssef Mroueh, Phuc Nguyen, Chi-Heng Lin, Shangqian Gao, Ting Hua, Nitesh V. Chawla

Abstract
Large language models trained via next-token prediction are fundamentally pattern-matchers: sensitive to superficial phrasing variations even when the underlying problem is identical. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was designed to improve reasoning, but in fact it worsens this situation through two failure modes: diversity collapse , where training amplifies a single solution strategy while ignoring alternatives of gradient signal, and gradient diminishing , where a large portion of questions yield zero gradients because all rollouts receive identical rewards. We propose TA-GRPO (Transform-Augmented GRPO), which generates semantically equivalent transformed variants of each question (via paraphrasing, variable renaming, and format changes) and computes advantages by pooling rewards across the entire group. This pooled computation ensures mixed rewards even when the original question is too easy or too hard, while training on diverse phrasings promotes multiple solution strategies. We provide theoretical justification showing that TA-GRPO reduces zero-gradient probability and improves generalization via reduced train-test distribution shift. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show consistent Pass@k improvements, with gains up to 9.84 points on competition math (AMC12, AIME24) and 5.05 points on out-of-distribution scientific reasoning (GPQA-Diamond).



1
Introduction

What Pretraining Creates: A Pattern-Matcher.

Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained via next-token prediction: given a context, predict the probability distribution over the next token. This objective teaches the model which tokens tend to appear in which contexts. Fundamentally, it creates a sophisticated
pattern-matcher
rather than a system that truly “understands” language or reasoning.
This pattern-matching nature explains a well-known phenomenon: LLMs are sensitive to superficial variations in input phrasing. Small changes in wording
(
Salinas & Morstatter 2024
)
, question format
(
Hua et al. 2025
)
, variable names, or even the order of information
(
Guan et al. 2025
)
can dramatically change model outputs, even when the underlying problem is semantically identical. A model that truly understood would be invariant to such surface-level changes; a pattern-matcher is not.

What GRPO Is Supposed To Do.

To bridge the gap between token prediction and problem-solving, researchers turn to Reinforcement Learning (RL): optimize directly for task success rather than next-token likelihood.
For reasoning tasks like mathematics or coding, rewards can be verified automatically: the answer is either correct or not. This setting is known as Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
(
Lambert et al. 2025
)
. Among RLVR algorithms, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
is the most widely used. While traditional policy gradient methods require a separate value function, GRPO computes advantages by comparing rollouts within each question group. The success of DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
demonstrates that this simpler approach is effective, and GRPO has since been widely adopted for training reasoning models.
The hope is that GRPO will teach the model to “reason better”, to develop robust problem-solving strategies that generalize beyond the training distribution.

What GRPO Actually Does: Making Pattern-Matching Worse.

In practice, GRPO does not fix the pattern-matching limitation of pretrained models; it worsens it.

(1) Diversity Collapse.
GRPO amplifies whatever solution pattern currently achieves the highest reward. If the model has a dominant strategy for a question (e.g., solving algebraically rather than geometrically), GRPO reinforces this pattern while starving alternative valid patterns of gradient signal. The result is a model that collapses from knowing multiple solution strategies to knowing only one
(
Yue et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Dang et al. 2025
)
. This manifests as suboptimal Pass@k performance, the probability of finding at least one correct answer in
k
attempts. Because the model repeatedly produces the same answer rather than exploring diverse solutions.

(2) Gradient Diminishing.
GRPO’s group-relative normalization creates a problematic case: when all rollouts for a question receive identical rewards (all correct or all incorrect), the computed advantages are zero, yielding no gradient signal. Questions that are “too easy” (the model always succeeds) or “too hard” (the model always fails) contribute nothing to learning. As shown in
Figure

1
, over 80% of training questions yield zero gradients for standard GRPO, with only medium-difficulty questions contributing to learning.

Figure 1

TA-GRPO consistently reduces zero-gradient questions compared to GRPO, maintaining a 12–16 percentage point advantage throughout training (e.g., 75% vs 86% at convergence).

Our Insight: Diversify the Training Signal.

The root cause of both failures is that GRPO trains on each question in only one phrasing, reinforcing a single dominant pattern. Our insight is simple: present each question in multiple semantically equivalent forms. This forces the model to encounter diverse patterns for the same underlying problem, breaking the single-pattern trap.

We propose
transform augmentation
: training on multiple phrasings of each question. By presenting “What is
2+3
?”, “Calculate
2+3
”, “Find the sum of 2 and 3”, and “If
x=2+3
, what is
x
?” as variations of the same problem, we expose the model to diverse solution patterns rather than reinforcing just one.
Transform augmentation directly addresses these issues. First, different phrasings trigger different dominant patterns, so training on diverse phrasings ensures multiple solution strategies receive gradient signal. Second, different phrasings have different difficulty, so grouping a question with its transforms produces non-zero advantages even for otherwise “too easy” or “too hard” questions. Third, training on diverse phrasings reduces the train-test distribution shift.
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

TA-GRPO Algorithm
(
Section

4
): We propose TA-GRPO (Transform-Augmented GRPO), which groups each question with its transformed variants for joint advantage computation, directly addressing gradient diminishing while promoting diversity.

•

Theoretical Framework
(

any

•

On out-of-distribution scientific reasoning (GPQA-Diamond), the improvement reaches 5.05 points, confirming the generalization benefits of transform augmentation.

2
Related Work

GRPO and Variants.

GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
has become the dominant method for training reasoning models due to its simplicity and efficiency. However, its limitations have spurred numerous modifications. DAPO
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
addresses gradient diminishing through dynamic sampling, over-sampling rollouts and filtering out questions where all rollouts receive identical rewards. While effective, this approach discards potentially valuable training signal. DCPO
(
Yang et al. 2025b
)
extends this with adaptive clipping bounds. Dr. GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
removes the standard deviation normalization entirely to avoid difficulty-dependent biases, while GRPO-LEAD
(
Zhang & Zuo 2025
)
employs difficulty-aware weighting to rebalance the contribution of easy versus hard questions. Other variants address different aspects: GRPO-MA
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
handles the mismatch between rollouts and final answers, SEED-GRPO
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
shapes advantages with semantic entropy, and ExGRPO
(
Zhan et al. 2025
)
leverages human-annotated data. Unlike these approaches that modify how advantages are computed, we enrich the training data through transformation, addressing the root cause of both gradient diminishing and diversity collapse.

Diversity Collapse in RL.

The tendency of RL fine-tuning to reduce output diversity is well-documented in the language model literature.
O’Mahony et al. 2024
trace mode collapse to specific components of the fine-tuning process, while
Yun et al. 2025
quantify how formatting templates suppress output diversity. Most relevant to our work,
Dang et al. 2025
provide theoretical analysis showing that RLVR optimization inherently drives models toward lower-entropy policies.
Yue et al. 2025
and
Zhang et al. 2025
empirically demonstrate that RLVR primarily sharpens pre-existing capabilities rather than developing new reasoning skills, with diversity collapse being a key mechanism. Our theoretical framework explains why transform augmentation can counteract this collapse: by ensuring diverse correct answers appear in training rollouts, we prevent the optimization from converging to a single mode.

Data Augmentation for Robustness.

The sensitivity of LLMs to superficial prompt variations is extensively documented. Small changes in wording
(
Salinas & Morstatter 2024
)
, question format
(
Hua et al. 2025
)
, or even the order of information
(
Guan et al. 2025
)
can dramatically alter model outputs.
Chatterjee et al. 2024
propose POSIX to quantify this sensitivity. Prior work has used augmentation mainly to improve robustness: training on diverse phrasings so the model becomes less sensitive to surface variations. We show that augmentation provides benefits beyond robustness: it actively improves exploration and Pass@k performance by promoting diverse solution strategies.

3
Background

3.1
GRPO Formulation

Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote the policy model parameterized by
\theta
. Given a question
q
, GRPO samples
G
rollouts
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
from the current policy and assigns binary rewards
R_{i}\in\{0,1\}
based on answer correctness. The key is computing advantages by normalizing rewards within each question group:

A_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\text{mean}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\text{std}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,G\}.

(1)

This eliminates the need for a separate value function. The policy is updated to increase the probability of high-advantage rollouts (see
Section

A.1
for the full objective).

3.2
The Gradient-Diminishing Problem

A critical issue arises from the advantage computation in
Equation

1
: when all
G
rollouts receive the same reward, either all correct (
R_{i}=1
for all
i
) or all incorrect (
R_{i}=0
for all
i
), both the mean and each individual reward are equal, yielding
A_{i}=0
for all rollouts. This produces zero gradients, meaning the question contributes nothing to learning.

This is not a rare edge case. Questions that are “too easy” for the current model (high pass rate) will likely have all rollouts correct. Questions that are “too hard” (near-zero pass rate) will likely have all rollouts incorrect. Our experiments show that over 80% of training questions fall into these categories for standard GRPO (
Figure

1
), representing substantial wasted computation and missed learning opportunities.

3.3
Pass@k Metric

Definition 3.1

(Pass Rate and Pass@k)
.

Let
\mathcal{O}^{*}(q)
be the set of correct answers for question
q
. The
pass rate
is
\rho_{q}=\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}^{*}(q)}\pi_{\theta}(o|q)
. The
Pass@k
metric measures the probability of obtaining at least one correct answer in
k
independent samples:

\text{Pass@k}(q)=1-(1-\rho_{q})^{k}.

(2)

Pass@k is particularly relevant for reasoning tasks: (1) users often regenerate responses if the first attempt fails; (2) it measures solution space coverage rather than just the mode; (3) it is sensitive to diversity collapse—a model that always produces the same answer has Pass@k
\approx
Pass@1.

4
TA-GRPO

We propose TA-GRPO (Transform-Augmented GRPO), which groups each question with semantically equivalent variants for joint advantage computation. This addresses the gradient-diminishing problem while promoting solution diversity. We provide theoretical justification in
Section

5
.

Transform Augmentation.

For each training question
q
, we generate
N
transformed variants using three types of semantic-preserving transformations: paraphrasing, variable renaming, and format changes. All variants share the same correct answer. The resulting neighbors exhibit varied difficulty—some phrasings are easier (matching pretraining patterns), others harder (unfamiliar formulations). See
Section

C.1
for generation details.

Pooled Advantage Computation.

The core innovation is treating each question and its
N
transforms as a unified
group
for advantage computation. Rather than normalizing rewards separately for each variant, we normalize across the entire group:

A_{i,j}=\frac{R_{i,j}-\mu_{\text{group}}}{\sigma_{\text{group}}+\epsilon},

(3)

where
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
indexes group members,
j\in\{1,\ldots,G\}
indexes rollouts, and
\mu_{\text{group}},\sigma_{\text{group}}
are computed over all
(N+1)\times G
rewards. We show in
Section

5.5
that this normalization is theoretically principled: for binary rewards, the pooled success follows a Bernoulli distribution whose variance determines the correct whitening.

This directly addresses gradient diminishing: even if the original question is “too easy” (all rollouts correct), a harder transform variant produces mixed rewards across the group, yielding non-zero advantages. Conversely, an easier variant can provide gradient signal for “too hard” questions.

5
Theoretical Framework

We now provide theoretical justification for TA-GRPO (
Section

4
), explaining why transform augmentation is a principled approach through three mechanisms: (1) reduced gradient-diminishing via mixed rewards, (2) improved generalization from training to testing, and (3) a principled derivation of the pooled advantage formula. We leave empirical validation of Pass@k improvement to
Section

6
.

5.1
Preliminaries and Notation

Let
q
denote an original question from the training distribution
\mathcal{D}
, with
\mathcal{O}^{*}(q)
denoting the set of correct answers. We define
N
semantic-preserving transformations
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}\}
, where each
T_{i}(q)
produces a semantically equivalent variant of
q
(e.g., paraphrasing, variable renaming). Let
\pi_{\theta}(o|q)
denote the probability that the model generates output
o
given question
q
, and
r(q,o)\in\{0,1\}
the binary reward indicating correctness.

We compare two training approaches:
Standard GRPO
trains only on original questions
q
;
TA-GRPO
trains on
N+1
variants per question (the original
q
and transforms
\{T_{1}(q),\ldots,T_{N}(q)\}
), sampled uniformly. At test time, questions may appear in different phrasings; we denote the test distribution by
P_{\mathrm{te}}
and analyze how
P_{\mathrm{tr}}
affects the train-test gap.

We define success rates at multiple levels:
per-transform

\rho(q,T_{i};\theta):=\Pr_{o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|T_{i}(q))}(r(q,o)=1)
;
pooled

\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta):=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\rho(q,T_{i};\theta)
averaging over original (
T_{0}=\text{identity}
) and transforms; and
aggregate

\bar{\rho}_{\text{tr}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D}}[\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta)]
averaging over questions. We use superscript
(0)
for initialization, e.g.,
\rho^{(0)}(q,T_{i}):=\rho(q,T_{i};\theta_{0})
.

5.2
Assumptions

Assumption 5.1

(Baseline Solvability)
.

The baseline model has non-zero pooled pass rate:
\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta_{0})>0
.

Assumption 5.2

(Transformation Consistency)
.

Transformations preserve the answer set:
\mathcal{O}^{*}(T_{i}(q))=\mathcal{O}^{*}(q)
for all transformations
T_{i}
.

Assumption 5.3

(Transformation Diversity)
.

Different transformations induce different success rates:
\rho(q,T_{i};\theta)\neq\rho(q,T_{j};\theta)
for some
i\neq j
.

Assumptions
5.1
and
5.2
are standard requirements. Assumption
5.3
states that transformations affect model performance, which is well-supported by research on prompt sensitivity: the same question phrased differently can be easier or harder for the model to solve.

5.3
Gradient Signal Analysis

Transform augmentation addresses gradient-diminishing through two mechanisms: ensuring non-vacuous initialization, and producing mixed rewards within groups.

Non-Vacuous Initialization.

Standard GRPO requires
\rho(q,T_{0};\theta_{0})>0
on the original question. TA-GRPO relaxes this: it suffices that
any
transform succeeds.

Proposition 5.4

(Non-Vacuous Initialization)
.

If there exists any transformation
T_{i}
with
\rho^{(0)}(q,T_{i})>0
, then
\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta_{0})\geq\frac{1}{N+1}\rho^{(0)}(q,T_{i})>0
.

This ensures Assumption
5.1
is satisfied more reliably than training on original questions alone, since the model only needs to succeed on
some
phrasing rather than the specific original phrasing. This connects to recent theoretical work:
Mroueh 2025
showed that Mirror-GRPO drives per-prompt success probability to 1, provided the initial success probability is non-zero. Proposition
5.4
shows that transform augmentation makes this non-degeneracy condition easier to satisfy.

Mixed Rewards Prevent Zero Gradients.

The gradient-diminishing problem occurs when all rollouts receive the same reward (all correct or all incorrect), yielding zero advantage and no learning signal. By Assumption
5.3
, different transforms have different success rates. Even if the original
q
is “too easy” or “too hard,” some transforms may have intermediate difficulty, producing mixed rewards within the group.

Theorem 5.5

(Reduced Zero-Gradient Questions)
.

Under Assumptions
5.1
–
5.3
, the probability that a question contributes zero gradient is lower for TA-GRPO than for the standard GRPO:

\Pr(\text{zero gradient}\mid\text{TA-GRPO})\leq\Pr(\text{zero gradient}\mid\text{GRPO}).

(4)

Proof.

Let
\rho_{i}:=\rho(q,T_{i};\theta)
denote the success rate on transform
T_{i}(q)
. A question contributes zero gradient when all rollouts in its group receive the same reward (all correct or all incorrect).

Standard GRPO:
The probability of uniform outcomes across
G
rollouts is
P_{\text{zero}}^{\text{std}}=\rho_{0}^{G}+(1-\rho_{0})^{G}
.

TA-GRPO:
With
(N+1)\times G
rollouts across the group, the probability becomes
P_{\text{zero}}^{\text{TA}}=\prod_{i=0}^{N}\rho_{i}^{G}+\prod_{i=0}^{N}(1-\rho_{i})^{G}
.

Comparison:
By Assumption
5.3
, there exist
i\neq j
with
\rho_{i}\neq\rho_{j}
. For the “all correct” case, if
\rho_{i}<\rho_{0}
for some
i
, then
\prod_{i=0}^{N}\rho_{i}^{G}\leq\rho_{0}^{(N+1)G}\leq\rho_{0}^{G}
. Similarly, if
\rho_{j}>\rho_{0}
for some
j
, then
(1-\rho_{j})<(1-\rho_{0})
, so
\prod_{i=0}^{N}(1-\rho_{i})^{G}\leq(1-\rho_{0})^{G}
. Combining:
P_{\text{zero}}^{\text{TA}}\leq P_{\text{zero}}^{\text{std}}
.
∎

The key insight is that different transforms have different difficulty levels. Even if the original question is “too easy” (high
\rho_{0}
) or “too hard” (low
\rho_{0}
), transformed variants provide mixed rewards, preventing uniform outcomes across the group. This means questions that would be “wasted” under standard GRPO can contribute meaningful gradients under TA-GRPO.

Remark 5.6

(Empirical Validation)
.

Our experiments confirm this: TA-GRPO consistently reduces zero-gradient questions compared to GRPO by 12–16 percentage points throughout training (
Figure

1
), directly validating the theoretical prediction.

5.4
Generalization Analysis

Why should training diversity help at test time? We establish that transform augmentation reduces distribution shift between training and testing. Following the definition of
\bar{\rho}_{\text{tr}}(\theta)
in
Section

5.1
, let
\bar{\rho}_{\text{te}}(\theta)
denote the aggregate success rate on the test distribution
P_{\mathrm{te}}
.

Proposition 5.7

(Train-Test Gap via Pinsker’s Inequality)
.

Assume
P_{\mathrm{te}}\ll P_{\mathrm{tr}}
. Then for any policy
\theta
:

\bar{\rho}_{\text{te}}(\theta)\geq\bar{\rho}_{\text{tr}}(\theta)-\sqrt{2\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{te}}\|P_{\mathrm{tr}})}.

(5)

Test performance is at least training performance minus a penalty controlled by the KL divergence between distributions (proof in
Section

B.3
). Standard GRPO trains only on original questions; if test questions appear in different phrasings not seen during training, then
\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{te}}\|P_{\mathrm{tr}})=\infty
. TA-GRPO trains on diverse phrasings, yielding finite KL divergence and meaningful generalization guarantees.

Corollary 5.8

(Non-Vacuous Test Bound)
.

If training achieves success rate
\bar{\rho}_{\text{tr}}(\theta)\geq 1-\eta
and
\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{te}}\|P_{\mathrm{tr}})\leq\delta^{2}/2
, then
\bar{\rho}_{\text{te}}(\theta)\geq 1-(\eta+\delta)
.

5.5
Pooled Objective Derivation

The Pinsker bound (
Proposition

5.7
) motivates optimizing the
pooled
probability of success
\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta)
across transforms, rather than per-prompt success on original questions only. We now derive the correct advantage normalization for this objective.

Lemma 5.9

(Bernoulli Variance of Pooled Reward)
.

Fix
q
and
\theta
, uniformly sample a transform
T_{i}
from
\{T_{0},\ldots,T_{N}\}
, then sample
o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|T_{i}(q))
. The reward
R:=r(q,o)\in\{0,1\}
is Bernoulli with:

\mathbb{E}[R]=\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta),\qquad\mathrm{Var}(R)=\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta)(1-\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta)).

(6)

Correct Advantage Normalization.

Lemma
5.9
implies the natural whitening for binary rewards is:

A_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(q,o)=\frac{r(q,o)-\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta_{\text{old}})}{\sqrt{\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta_{\text{old}})(1-\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta_{\text{old}}))+\varepsilon}},

(7)

where
\varepsilon>0
prevents division by zero. This is precisely the pooled advantage computation in TA-GRPO (
Section

4
), now derived from first principles rather than heuristics.

Corollary 5.10

(Augmentation Improves Pooled Success)
.

If there exists
T_{i}
with
\rho(q,T_{i};\theta)>\rho(q,T_{0};\theta)
, then pooled success strictly exceeds original-only success:
\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta)>\rho(q,T_{0};\theta)
.

Remark 5.11

(Why Pooled Optimization)
.

Optimizing pooled success has three advantages: (1) initialization is more likely non-zero since it suffices that
any
transform succeeds (Proposition
5.4
), (2) it directly minimizes the train-test gap (
Proposition

5.7
), and (3) augmentation can strictly improve over original-only training (Corollary
5.10
).

5.6
Summary

Our theoretical analysis justifies TA-GRPO through four mechanisms: (1)
non-vacuous initialization
: transform diversity ensures at least one variant succeeds (Proposition
5.4
); (2)
reduced gradient diminishing
: different success rates produce mixed rewards and non-zero gradients (Theorem
5.5
); (3)
better generalization
: diverse training reduces distribution shift (Corollary
5.8
); and (4)
principled objective
: the pooled advantage formula is derived from Bernoulli variance (Lemma
5.9
). These explain
why
TA-GRPO is principled; we validate
that
it improves Pass@k in
Section

6
.

6.3
Analysis: Rollout Diversity

Figure

3
directly validates that TA-GRPO increases rollout diversity. We measure diversity as the average pairwise cosine distance between rollouts in the embedding space (using Gemma-300M embeddings). At every training checkpoint, TA-GRPO’s distribution is shifted right compared to GRPO, indicating more diverse outputs.

The diversity gap widens as training continues. At initialization, both methods start with similar diversity distributions. However, standard GRPO progressively narrows its output distribution as it reinforces the dominant solution pattern for each question: a phenomenon known as mode collapse. In contrast, our TA-GRPO maintains diversity throughout training because different transforms trigger different solution patterns, preventing any single pattern from dominating.

This diversity directly explains the Pass@k results. Consider a model that produces identical outputs for all
k
samples versus one that produces diverse outputs. The former gains nothing from additional samples (Pass@k
\approx
Pass@1), while the latter increases its chance of finding a correct answer with each attempt. The rightward shift in
Figure

3
corresponds directly to the widening gap in
Figure

2
: more diverse rollouts mean more independent attempts at solving the problem, and thus higher Pass@k for larger
k
.

6.4
Analysis: Gradient-Diminishing Reduction

As shown in
Figure

1
, TA-GRPO consistently reduces zero-gradient questions compared to GRPO, maintaining a 12–16 percentage point advantage throughout training (e.g., 75% vs 86% at convergence). This answers
RQ3
and validates
Theorem

5.5
: even if the original question is “too easy” or “too hard,” transformed variants may have different difficulty, producing mixed rewards and non-zero gradients.

This reduction is substantial: TA-GRPO recovers useful gradient signal from questions that would otherwise contribute nothing to learning. Standard GRPO wastes the vast majority of questions; TA-GRPO significantly reduces this waste. Furthermore, the remaining zero-gradient questions in TA-GRPO are the truly intractable ones where all transforms are uniformly easy or hard; standard GRPO wastes gradients on many recoverable cases.
Importantly, TA-GRPO achieves this while maintaining competitive Pass@1 (
\pm
1–2 points) and improving Pass@32 by +3–10 points. This demonstrates that diversity is achieved
without
sacrificing first-try accuracy, the model retains its best solution while learning additional strategies.

6.5
Ablation: Effect of Pooled Advantages

We investigate whether the improvement is due to having more diverse training data (data augmentation), or the pooled advantage computation. We ablate by training “TA-GRPO w/o pooling”: same transforms, but advantages computed per-variant rather than pooled across the group.

Figure

4
reveals that:
data augmentation alone does not guarantee success
. Without pooling, gains over GRPO are minimal (AIME24: +2, GPQA: +1) or even negative (AIME25:
-3
). Only with pooled advantages does TA-GRPO consistently outperform GRPO across all benchmarks.
This finding directly validates our theoretical analysis. Recall from
Section

5.3
that the gradient-diminishing problem occurs when all rollouts receive identical rewards. Without pooling, each variant is treated independently: if the original question is “too easy,” it still yields zero gradients regardless of what happens on the transforms. With pooling, rewards are mixed across the group: even if the original question is too easy, a harder transform produces mixed rewards within the group, yielding non-zero gradients.

The AIME25 result is particularly informative. Without pooling, performance
drops
below GRPO, suggesting that naively adding transformed variants can weaken the training signal when each is computed separately. Pooling resolves this by ensuring the group collectively contributes meaningful gradients. This confirms that TA-GRPO’s effectiveness stems not from data quantity but from the principled pooled objective derived in
Section

5.5
.

7
Limitations

Transformation Quality.
Our theoretical analysis assumes semantic-preserving transformations where
\mathcal{O}^{*}(T_{i}(q))=\mathcal{O}^{*}(q)
. Poorly designed transforms that inadvertently change the answer or introduce ambiguity would violate this assumption and degrade performance. In practice, we use GPT-4 for transformation generation with careful prompt engineering, but automated verification of semantic equivalence remains an open challenge.

Hyperparameter Choices.
We use
N=3
transformations throughout our experiments, which already yields consistent improvements across all benchmarks. While different values of
N
or adaptive selection strategies could potentially yield further gains, our results demonstrate that even a simple fixed configuration is effective. The theoretical framework applies for any
N\geq 1
.

Evaluation Scope.
We focus on mathematical reasoning, where correctness can be verified automatically. This is the standard setting for RLVR methods, including GRPO. Our theoretical analysis is domain-agnostic and applies to other verifiable domains such as coding or formal reasoning. The strong out-of-distribution results on GPQA-Diamond (science) suggest TA-GRPO generalizes beyond mathematics.

Model Scale.
We validate on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B. The larger relative gains for the smaller model (+5.28 vs +4.45 average) suggest that TA-GRPO may be particularly beneficial for smaller models prone to solution collapse, though experiments on larger scales would be valuable to confirm scaling behavior.

8
Conclusion

We introduced TA-GRPO, a simple yet principled approach to improve Pass@k in reasoning models by training on semantically equivalent question variants with pooled advantage computation. Our theoretical analysis explains why this works: transform augmentation reduces zero-gradient questions by producing mixed rewards within groups, and decreases the train-test gap through reduced distribution shift. Experiments validate these predictions, showing consistent Pass@k improvements across competition math and out-of-distribution benchmarks. The ablation study confirms that pooled advantages, not merely data augmentation, drive the improvement. Our work provides both theoretical grounding and practical guidance for using transformation-based augmentation in RL training.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning, specifically improving reasoning capabilities in large language models. The techniques developed could enhance AI systems’ ability to solve mathematical and scientific problems, with potential applications in education, scientific discovery, and automated theorem proving. We do not foresee specific negative social consequences beyond those generally associated with more capable AI systems.

Full GRPO Objective

The complete GRPO objective (omitting clipping and KL penalty for clarity) is:

\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\{o_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}A_{i}\right].

(8)

A.2
The Gradient Diminishing Problem

See
Section

3.2
in the main paper for a complete description of the gradient-diminishing problem. In brief: when all rollouts receive the same reward, the advantage becomes zero and the question contributes nothing to learning. Our experiments show over 80% of questions fall into this category for standard GRPO.

A.3
Pass@k Examples

The Pass@k formula
\text{Pass@k}(q)=1-(1-\rho_{q})^{k}
follows from noting that
(1-\rho_{q})^{k}
is the probability that all
k
samples are incorrect. For example, with
\rho_{q}=0.3
: Pass@1
=0.3
, Pass@5
\approx 0.83
, Pass@10
\approx 0.97
.

\rho^{(0)}(q,T_{i})>0

\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\rho^{(0)}(q,T_{j})\geq\frac{1}{N+1}\rho^{(0)}(q,T_{i})>0.

(9)

∎

Proof of Corollary
5.10
.

Suppose
\rho(q,T_{i};\theta)>\rho(q,T_{0};\theta)
for some

. Let
\delta=\rho(q,T_{i};\theta)-\rho(q,T_{0};\theta)>0
. Then:

\rho_{\text{tr}}(q;\theta)-\rho(q,T_{0};\theta)=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\left[\rho(q,T_{j};\theta)-\rho(q,T_{0};\theta)\right]\geq\frac{\delta}{N+1}>0,

(10)

j=i

\delta
and all other terms are

Remark B.1

(Connection to Mirror-GRPO Dynamics)
.

Mroueh 2025
showed that Mirror-GRPO with KL regularization drives per-prompt success probability to 1, provided the initial success probability is non-zero (the only fixed points are at
\{0,1\}
, with 0 absorbing only when
\rho^{(0)}=0
). Proposition
5.4
shows that transform augmentation makes this non-degeneracy condition easier to satisfy: it suffices that
any

B.2
Extended Discussion of Theorem
5.5

The proof of Theorem
5.5
appears in the main paper (
Section

5.3
). Here we provide additional intuition and discuss when strict inequality holds.

Remark B.2

(Strict Inequality Conditions)
.

By Assumption
5.3
, the success rates vary across transforms. If some transform is harder (
\rho_{i}<\rho_{0}
) and some is easier (
\rho_{j}>\rho_{0}
), then both the “all correct” and “all incorrect” probabilities are strictly smaller for TA-GRPO, giving strict inequality
P_{\text{zero}}^{\text{TA}}<P_{\text{zero}}^{\text{std}}
.

Remark B.3

(Practical Interpretation)
.

The key insight is that different transforms have different difficulty levels. Even if the original question is “too easy” (high
\rho_{0}
) or “too hard” (low
\rho_{0}
), transformed variants provide mixed rewards, preventing uniform outcomes across the group. This means questions that would be “wasted” under standard GRPO can contribute meaningful gradients under TA-GRPO.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
5.7

Define the success function
g_{\theta}(t,q):=\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|t(q))}[r(q,o)]\in[0,1]
, representing the expected reward when question
q
is transformed by
t
. The gap between test and training success is:

\bar{\rho}_{\text{te}}(\theta)-\bar{\rho}_{\text{tr}}(\theta)=\int g_{\theta}\,d(P_{\mathrm{te}}-P_{\mathrm{tr}}).

(11)

Since
g_{\theta}
is bounded in
[0,1]
, we can bound the integral:

|\bar{\rho}_{\text{te}}(\theta)-\bar{\rho}_{\text{tr}}(\theta)|\leq\int|d(P_{\mathrm{te}}-P_{\mathrm{tr}})|=\|P_{\mathrm{te}}-P_{\mathrm{tr}}\|_{1}.

(12)

Pinsker’s inequality gives
\|P_{\mathrm{te}}-P_{\mathrm{tr}}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{te}}\|P_{\mathrm{tr}})}
, completing the proof.
