Preliminary Bereaved Family Satisfaction Questionnaire for Hospice Care: A Mixed-Methods Feasibility and Acceptability Study
Authors: Hyunok Yun, Shinyoung Kwon, Jieun Choi, Dae-kyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to develop a preliminary hospice bereaved-family satisfaction questionnaire that captures families’ experiences and is comprehensible to respondents and to assess its feasibility and acceptability.MethodsAn exploratory sequential mixed-methods design guided by the COSMIN framework was used. In-depth interviews were conducted with bereaved family members (n=12) to identify key areas for evaluation. A draft item pool was developed by integrating these areas with existing experience-assessment tools and refined through two Delphi rounds with hospice experts (n=20) and a comprehensibility review. A multi-institutional pilot survey assessed feasibility (completion time and item-level missing data) and preliminary measurement properties (item distributions and internal consistency).ResultsQualitative analysis identified four overarching categories choice of hospice and palliative care services; experience with hospice and palliative care (satisfaction and unmet needs); end-of-life preparation and post-bereavement experience; and experience with previous satisfaction surveys. In the second Delphi round, all items met the content-validity threshold (CVR ≥0.42) for importance and appropriateness. Service-specific questionnaires comprised 25 items each for inpatient and home hospices and 23 items for consultative hospices. Item-level missingness was low, the mean completion time ranged from 10~12 minutes, and the inpatient version showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.95).ConclusionThe questionnaire demonstrated acceptable content validity and feasibility in the Korean hospice context. A large-scale validation of the factor structure, criterion validity, and other measurement properties is required before nationwide use in quality monitoring and policy evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Hospice and palliative care services in Korea have steadily expanded with government support and institutionalization [1]. Since enactment of the Act on Decisions on Life-Sustaining Treatment for Patients in Hospice and Palliative Care or at the End of Life, the number of designated hospice institutions has increased, and inpatient, home, and consultative hospice services have been established. These developments have improved access and expanded service coverage. However, quality assessment systems have not developed at the same pace [2]. Improving end-of-life care requires indicators that systematically assess the quality of care provided. As patients near the end of life are often unable to participate directly in surveys or interviews because of physical or psychological deterioration, evaluating their service experiences presents practical and ethical challenges [3]. Therefore, bereaved relatives who were closely involved in patient care are widely used as proxy respondents [4]. They can report on both the patient’s care and the support received by the family, providing information to guide service improvement and policy development. Bereaved relatives are therefore considered important informants not only about patient’s experiences near the end of life but also about whether families received adequate preparation, education, emotional care, and support after death.

Several countries use bereaved-family experience instruments for quality management and policy improvement (Table 1). These include the US Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) Hospice Survey [5,6], the UK Views of Informal Carers–Evaluation of Services (VOICES) [7], Singapore’s National Caregiver Response Survey (CaRES) [8] and FAMCARE-10 [9], and Australia’s Victorian Palliative Care Satisfaction Instrument (VPCSI) [10]. Although their purposes and applications differ, they assess multidimensional domains such as communication, symptom and pain management, dignity, emotional and spiritual support, and family and bereavement support. Because these instruments were developed within different healthcare systems and cultural settings, their direct application may not adequately reflect Korea’s differentiated hospice service types and family-centered decision-making culture. Although the international instruments differ in their specific purpose and level of policy use, they share an experience-based, multidimensional approach that links survey findings to service quality improvement. Their strengths informed this study, while their contextual limitations highlighted the need for a Korean instrument.

In Korea, bereaved-family satisfaction surveys are used as operational reference data [11]. Designated hospice institutions currently use the Comprehensive Evaluation Scale (CES) for inpatient services and FAMCARE for home and consultative services, together with the Good Death Inventory (GDI) to assess quality of life near the end of life [12]. Before the present study, a nationwide consultation with hospice practitioners identified important limitations: an inpatient-oriented tool was inappropriate for consultative hospices because it did not reflect their counseling, education, and coordination functions; redundant, abstract, or contextually inappropriate items were difficult for older respondents; and using satisfaction results as institutional performance indicators raised concerns. These limitations may compromise response reliability and usefulness of the result for policy. Practitioners also noted that differences in respondents’ literacy and familiarity with end-of-life care could reduce consistency when items were abstract or redundant. Therefore, a revised measure needed to distinguish service quality from constructs such as quality of death, be answerable based on families’ direct experiences, and avoid items that could induce guilt or require families to judge matters they could not observe.

Therefore, Korea requires a standardized bereaved-family experience questionnaire that reflects service-specific characteristics and actual family experiences and is comprehensible to respondents. The items should be easy for the intended respondents to understand and answer while remaining sufficiently specific to support quality improvement and inform policy.

This study aimed to develop preliminary questionnaire for Korea’s differentiated inpatient, home, and consultative hospice and palliative care services to support quality management and inform policy. Ease of comprehension was treated as a design requirement because the intended respondents included older adults and non-medical family members. Specifically, we sought to identify core domains, develop service-specific preliminary items with expert-confirmed content validity and evaluate feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary internal consistency among actual respondents.

METHODS

1. Study design

We used an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design comprising qualitative exploration, expert consensus, and a pilot survey. In-depth interviews were conducted to explore experiences with hospice care and previous satisfaction surveys. We also consulted practitioners at designated hospice institutions about the limitations of existing instruments and the need for improvement. Qualitative findings, practitioner input, and established international measures informed the draft item pool and service-specific modifications. A multidisciplinary Delphi panel assessed item appropriateness and clarity, followed by a pilot survey to evaluate feasibility, acceptability, response characteristics, and preliminary internal consistency.

2. Ethics

The Institutional Review Board of Incheon St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea, approved the interview and pilot survey procedures (OIRB-신20250808-031 and OIRB-신20260313-013). Interview participants were recruited through bereavement groups or clinical staff at five hospice institutions. Before participation, the researchers explained the purpose and procedures, voluntary participation, withdrawal, anonymity, and confidentiality. Written informed consent was obtained from all interview and pilot survey participants. Pilot participants received the same explanation of the study purpose and procedures before providing consent.

3. COSMIN-based content-validity approach

This study followed the COSMIN methodology for content validity [13], which defines content validity as the extent to which an instrument adequately reflects the construct it is intended to measure and identifies relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility as its core elements. Qualitative inquiry was used to capture important content and implementation considerations in the Korean context and support comprehensibility. The Delphi process assessed item importance, appropriateness, and clarity, supporting relevance. An expert comprehensibility review and pilot test were conducted to examine whether respondents could understand and complete the questionnaire. Figure 1 summarizes the item development and preliminary evaluation processes. Across these phases, qualitative inquiry supported comprehensiveness, the Delphi rounds addressed relevance, and the comprehensibility review and pilot administration assessed whether respondents could understand and complete the questionnaire.

4. Qualitative exploration

Bereaved family members of patients who had received hospice and palliative care within the previous 1~3 years were recruited. Stratified purposive sampling sought variation by service type and family relationship, with approximately three to four participants recruited per service type. Prior research indicates that major themes may reach saturation with 6~12 participants in relatively homogeneous groups [14], with 9~17 participants reported as an appropriate range [15]. Therefore, we targeted approximately 12 participants for one-time interviews to capture diverse service experiences while allowing code saturation.

Individual interviews followed a semi-structured guide covering care during hospice use, family support, perceptions of existing satisfaction-survey items, and areas participants believed should be evaluated. Individual interviews allowed relatives to describe sensitive experiences in depth without influence from other participants. A hospice and palliative care researcher conducted a 40~60 minute interview with each participant in a private institutional room or another preferred location. After consent was obtained, the interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed.

Two trained researchers independently conducted the inductive content analysis. They repeatedly read each transcript, identified meaning units, grouped similar units into codes, and organized the codes into subcategories and overarching categories. Coding results were compared, disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a final categorical system was established. Saturation was reached when repeated concepts yielded no new codes or categories. The qualitative phase was intended to identify important domains and shortcomings of existing measures rather than generate items verbatim. The researchers organized the final themes into main areas of hospice service experience and used them as the foundation for service-specific evaluation domains. Items were constructed by integrating these domains with standardized international measures and refined through Delphi review. This distinction was deliberate: interview accounts provided the conceptual basis and contextual adaptations, whereas established instruments provided tested domain coverage and item formats. This approach avoided treating isolated quotations as questionnaire items while keeping development grounded in bereaved families’ experiences. Thus, the qualitative findings established conceptual domains and identified gaps and user-perceived problems. They were not used as direct item-generation data.

5. Delphi survey and comprehensibility review

The Delphi panel comprised 20 physicians, nurses, and social workers with hospice and palliative care experience, familiarity with patient or bereaved-family experience assessments, and direct involvement in at least one hospice service type. The draft items combined interview domains with common domains from international measures while reflecting Korean services differences, family-centered decision-making, and the counseling and coordination functions of consultative hospices. The initial versions contained 32 inpatient, 30 home hospice, and 29 consultative hospice items covering information provision, communication and participation in decision-making, symptom management, healthcare-professional attitudes, emotional and spiritual support, family support, and end-of-life and bereavement support. Common international domains provided the basic framework, while wording and composition were adapted to Korean service differences, family-centered decision-making, and the advisory rather than treatment-delivery role of consultative teams. Two Delphi rounds were conducted, with a 100% response rate.

Experts rated importance, clarity, appropriateness, and feasibility on a nine-point Likert scale. Importance and appropriateness determined item retention, while clarity and feasibility guided wording and implementation. Median, interquartile range (IQR), and content validity index (CVI; proportion rating an item ≥7) were assessed. Because no universally accepted consensus threshold exists for Delphi studies, decision criteria should be specified before data analysis [16]. Accordingly, drawing on the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method [17], we defined the item-processing criteria a priori: Items were retained when median importance and appropriateness were ≥7, IQR ≤1.5, and the CVI was ≥0.70. Items were removed when either median was ≤3 or CVI <0.70; and otherwise revised. After round 1, redundant items were combined, ambiguous wording was revised, and items inconsistent with the service’s role were removed. Round 2 reassessed the revised items and additionally applied Lawshe’s content validity ratio (CVR), using 0.42 as the critical value for 20 panelists [18].

After the Delphi process, an advisory group comprising a patient advocate, bereavement-care specialist, broadcast scriptwriter, medical communication expert, and patient experience evaluation specialist reviewed the terminology, sentence structure, and response formats. Abstract wording was replaced with concrete, behavior-based descriptions, and items were refined for older, non-medical respondents. The advisory group reviewed word choice, sentence structure, and response formats to ensure that respondents could understand and answer each item.

6. Pilot survey and analysis

Eligible participants were adults aged 19~74 years whose relatives had received hospice and palliative care and who had been bereaved for at least three months. Respondents answered only about the service type (inpatient, home, or consultative hospice) used at the time of death. With support from the National Hospice Center’, participants were recruited through hospice institutions throughout Korea. Each institution provided study information through its bereavement-family group, and the survey was administered after the researcher explained the study purpose and procedures and obtained written consent.

The pilot survey examined item comprehension, completion time, response distribution, feasibility, and acceptability. The purpose was to identify application and response-behavior problems before a definitive psychometric testing rather than establish a national score.

Descriptive analyses included item-level missingness, means and standard deviations, skewness and kurtosis, and floor and ceiling effects. Preliminary reliability analyses included Cronbach’s α, item-total correlations, and α if an item was deleted, overall and by domain where appropriate. Items were classified as retained, revised, or removed based on the combined findings. Items with satisfactory comprehension and response behavior were retained, those with wording or response-structure problems were revised, and those with probable response errors or redundancy were considered for removal. The resulting instrument was intended as a foundation for subsequent full psychometric validation rather than as a finalized national indicator. Because the home and consultative samples were small, internal consistency was evaluated primarily for the inpatient version.

RESULTS

1. Qualitative findings

Twelve bereaved family members recruited from five institutions participated. All were women were aged 40~70 years, mainly spouses, daughters, or daughters-in-law who had served as primary caregivers. Participants had experiences with inpatient, home, or consultative hospice alone or with combinations of inpatient hospice and home or consultative hospice. Duration of hospice care ranged from less than one week to more than eight weeks, most commonly three to six weeks. Including both single-service and combined-service pathways allowed the interviews to capture differences and transitions across settings. Participants had encountered different existing measures, including portions of the CES and GDI, depending on the institution and service type. The interviews identified the domains required for hospice evaluation from lived experiences, informed item development and wording, and also explored participants’ reactions to items and expressions they had encountered.

The analysis identified four overarching categories: (1) choice of hospice and palliative care services; (2) experiences of hospice and palliative care (satisfaction and unmet needs); (3) end-of-life preparation and post-bereavement experiences; and (4) experiences with previous bereaved family satisfaction surveys (Table 2).

Service choice encompassed motivations, decision-making, and expectations. Worsening symptoms and caregiver burden were common motivations, while clinician recommendations or urgent crises prompted hospice use. Decisions were made by patients, their families, or jointly. Rather than directly generating satisfaction items, this category provided context for understanding how satisfaction developed and how responses should be interpreted. Some patients expressed their own preferences, whereas families made decisions when patients were too ill; in other cases, patients and relatives decided jointly. Expectations commonly concerned symptom relief and family support.

Care experiences included physical care, family caregiving support, emotional and spiritual care, clinical and multidisciplinary team approaches, and services that did or did not meet expectations. Physical care comprised pain and symptom control and basic nursing care. Family support included teaching practical caregiving skills and allowing relatives to rest. The multidisciplinary team domain reflected whether staff understood patient’s conditions and provided professional and respectful care. Participants valued symptom management, caregiver education, and opportunities for respite. They identified the physical environment, insufficient explanation of the dying process, and restrictions on service duration as areas for improvement.

End-of-life preparation and post-bereavement experiences included support for end-of-life preparation, facilitation of a positive grieving process, and post-bereavement support. Explanations about approaching death and support for spending time at the end of life facilitated more positive bereavement experiences, while post-death contact and emotional support were also valued.

Participants reported that some existing items inadequately reflected actual care or Korean culture and created confusion or burden. CES items on cost adequacy could evoke guilt about not having done enough for a parent, whereas items concerning prognosis explanations, immediate admission, and interdisciplinary collaboration were difficult for relatives to observe or judge. Some GDI wording was abstract or ambiguous, and including quality-of-death items under a “satisfaction survey” label was inconsistent with its stated purpose and content. Response options were considered insufficiently intuitive and in need of a more symmetrical, easily understood format. Participants noted that questions about collaboration among healthcare professionals required judgments about processes families could not directly observe.

Participants recommended assessing families’ care experience; contributions of hospice assistants, volunteers, and therapists; emotional and psychological support; and hospice programs and therapies. Some preferred written or online self-administration to telephone interviews because these modes allowed more time to consider emotionally difficult questions. They believed that a long or repetitive questionnaire increased response burden and that concrete questions about observable actions and experiences were easier and more reliable than abstract, feeling-centered questions. They emphasized fewer redundant items and concrete, experience-based wording rather than abstract emotional expressions. Interviewees also distinguished between patient and family care. They regarded caregiver instructions, respite from caregiving, emotional reassurance, and post-death follow-up as service experiences worthy of direct assessment. These accounts supported including family and bereavement support domains rather than restricting the questionnaire to symptom control and staff behavior.

2. Delphi findings and final questionnaire structure

The 20 panelists comprised 10 nurses, six social workers, and four physicians. They were 33~58 years old and had 2~25 years of hospice and palliative care research or practice experience, supporting assessment of clinical appropriateness and practical applicability. Inpatient experts predominated, although home and consultative experts were also represented.

In round one, most items showed high agreement for importance and appropriateness (CVI 0.50~1.00 and 0.40~1.00, respectively; median scores generally ≥8). Concerns involved abstract expressions such as “emotional support” and “supportive relationship”; ambiguous spiritual-care language that could be difficult for nonreligious respondents; rigid time frames such as “the final two days”; incompatibility of some items with consultative hospice; and item redundancy. Items were then revised, combined, or removed. Specifically, panelists questioned whether “supportive relationship” conveyed an observable experience, whether spiritual-care wording presupposed religious belief, and whether relatives could accurately recall a rigid period such as “the final two days.” They also emphasized that questions about treatment delivery could misrepresent consultative teams whose principal functions are counseling, education, support, and referral. Five recurring issues were identified: abstract emotional-support language; ambiguous spiritual-care wording, particularly for respondents without religious affiliation; rigid definitions of the final days; mismatch between some items and consultative hospice functions; and excessive item number or overlap in symptom management, emotional support, and family support.

In round two, all 31 reassessed items met the core content-validity criteria for importance and appropriateness, with CVRs of 0.50~1.00 (all ≥0.42) and CVIs of 0.75~1.00, and median scores of 8~9. Importance and appropriateness were the principal criteria for item adoption, whereas clarity and feasibility guided wording and practical administration rather than automatic deletion. Most clarity and feasibility ratings also met the criteria (clarity CVI 0.65~1.00; feasibility CVI 0.70~1.00), but three items had lower CVRs for clarity (0.30~0.40) and feasibility (0.40) with wide IQRs. Because these items remained important and appropriate, they were retained and reworded during the comprehensibility review rather than deleted (Table 3). Further consolidation reduced redundancies in symptom management and emotional support. Conceptually similar symptom items were merged, and overlapping emotional support items were combined. Consultative items were reframed around advice, support, and coordination rather than direct treatment delivery.

The finalized preliminary instruments contained 25 inpatient, 25 home hospice, and 23 consultative hospice items after duplicate items among the 31 reassessed in round two were combined or removed. The service-specific structure was retained, while individual items were adjusted to match the responsibilities of each setting. Domains included information provision, communication and decision participation, symptom management, staff attitudes, emotional and spiritual care, family support, dying and bereavement support, accessibility and responsiveness, team professionalism, and overall evaluation. The inpatient version included one facility-environment item. The home and consultative versions each included one service-support item: information about equipment and welfare services for home hospice and transfer coordination for consultative hospice. The consultative hospice used fewer information and communication items to reflect its distinct role: three information items and two communication/decision-participation items, compared with more items in the inpatient and home versions. Each version combined five-point items with a separate overall rating of 0~10. All items are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

After the Delphi review established content appropriateness, the advisory group separately examined comprehensibility and clarity. The comprehensibility review replaced abstract statements with observable behaviors. For example, “maintained a supportive relationship” became a question asking whether the team listened to and comforted the patient and family. “Meaning and beliefs” was reframed as respect for the religion or values important to the patient or family. Fixed periods such as “the final two days” became “as death approached” or “during the last few days of life.”

3. Pilot findings

Nineteen institutions in 14 regions participated in the study. Respondents included 142 inpatient, 11 home-hospice, and 19 consultative-hospice bereaved family members. Women predominated in all groups, and older adults comprised the majority of respondents (Table 4). Spouses comprised the largest relationship group, and most respondents lived with and directly cared for the patients.

In the inpatient version, item-level missingness ranged from 0% to 2.8%. One missing response in the small home hospice sample resulted in 9.1% missingness for some items (1 of 11 respondents), whereas the consultative version had no item-level omissions and one missing overall score. No particular item showed consistently higher missingness.

Inpatient item means ranged from 4.51 to 4.86, and the mean overall rating was 9.46 (SD 1.38). The corresponding overall means were 9.40 (SD 0.52) for home hospice and 9.67 (SD 0.59) for consultative hospice. Responses were concentrated near the upper end, with several items showing negative skewness and high kurtosis. Similar patterns appeared in home and consultative groups, although small sample sizes precluded stable item-level comparisons (Table 5). No items showed consistent patterns of identical or repeated responses.

Mean completion times were approximately 10~12 minutes (median, 10) across all service types, with little variation between types, supporting the operational acceptability of the 23~25 item versions. Mean ratings of how well the questionnaire reflected respondents’ experiences exceeded 4.2 in all groups, with 84.2~93.5% reporting positive responses. Mean difficulty ratings for item comprehension were 2.0~2.4; the proportions reporting difficulty were 26.6% of inpatients, 20.0% of home-hospice respondents, and 15.8% of consultative hospices respondents reported difficulties.

For the inpatient version, Cronbach’s α was 0.95. The item-total correlations ranged from 0.301 to 0.787, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.30. Deleting individual items resulted in only slight increases in Cronbach’s α. All item-total correlations met the recommended threshold, supporting the preliminary internal consistency of the inpatient item set.

DISCUSSION

This study developed and preliminarily evaluated service-specific bereaved family experience questionnaires for inpatients, homes, and consultative hospice services in Korea, considering both families’ experiences and respondents’ comprehension. Because patients with severe physical or cognitive impairment near the end of life may be unable to participate directly in experience assessments, retrospective reports from bereaved families are widely used internationally [3]. The principal contribution of this study is an item structure adapted to Korea’s differentiated service models, family-centered decision-making, and the operational role of consultative hospices.

First, the development process integrated qualitative exploration, expert consensus, comprehensibility review, and pilot testing to address the COSMIN elements of relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility [13]. Treating comprehensibility as an explicit stage helped refine items for older respondents and reinforced COSMIN’s emphasis on incorporating target population perspectives alongside expert judgment. This approach also addressed the practical problem that a questionnaire may have adequate conceptual content validity yet remain difficult to use in practice.

Second, the questionnaires retained strengths of established international tools while addressing limitations associated with direct adaptation. The standardized US CAHPS Hospice Survey is a representative national instrument used for public reporting and quality management that systematically measures caregivers’ experiences [6]. VOICES-SF uses a retrospective mortality follow-up to assess care across multiple settings [7]. However, in Korea, the attending teams, ward staff, and hospice team may have overlapping roles, while consultative hospices primarily provide advice, education, support, and coordination rather than direct treatment. Service-specific wording reduces uncertainty about whom and what respondents are evaluating and aligns items with actual service delivery. The inpatient facility item and the home and consultative service-support items illustrate this principle: rather than forcing all settings into an identical form, the questionnaire retain common domains while accommodating differences in care environment and responsibilities.

Third, comprehensibility was treated as a core requirement of measurement development. Even conceptually valid items can generate missing or inaccurate responses when older bereaved relatives have difficulty understanding them [19]. The advisory review converted abstract concepts, such as support, belief, and meaning, into observable experiences and reduced lengthy or double-barreled wording. This strategy may improve response quality in future large-scale applications by reducing omissions and misinterpretations. This approach addresses the common gap between content judged valid by professionals and language readily understood by bereaved family members.

Fourth, the pilot findings support feasibility and provide preliminary evidence of internal consistency. Missing data were limited, and completion required approximately 10~12 minutes. The inpatient version demonstrated high internal consistency, with all item-total correlations exceeding 0.30. Nevertheless, the high mean, negative skewness, and upper-end concentration warrant further investigation of ceiling effects and discrimination. High scores may reflect excellent care but may also be influenced by gratitude bias, respondent characteristics, time since bereavement, or mode of administration [3]. A Cronbach’s α of 0.95 may also indicate item redundancy. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, along with potential item-response-theory refinement, should examine the domain structure and opportunities for item reduction.

This study has several limitations. First, the home and consultative samples were small, limiting statistical stability for each service type. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of questionnaire structure and operational feasibility rather than as justification for immediate adoption as a national performance indicator. Second, retrospective reports are subject to recall bias and respondents’ emotional states, a recurrent concern in bereaved family research. Third, substantial institutional variations in programs, bereavement services, and consultative team involvement should be assessed and incorporated into subsequent analyses. Fourth, only approximately three to four qualitative participants represented each service type; therefore, the service-specific items remained preliminary rather than definitive. Although separate questionnaires were developed, an alternative structure comprising common core items and service-specific modules should be evaluated in a larger sample. Adequate samples for each service type are required to determine whether separate questionnaires or core-plus-module designs provide better comparability and validity.

This was a preliminary instrument-development study intended to establish content validity and evaluate comprehensibility and feasibility, rather than a definitive measurement-validation study. Therefore, service-specific questionnaires should be regarded as preliminary item sets that require further testing. Nonetheless, this study provides a practical development process integrating content validity, comprehensibility, and feasibility and proposes questionnaires aligned with Korea’s multiple hospice models. A nationwide, multi-institutional study should evaluate the factor structure, measurement invariance across service types, criterion validity, and scoring interpretation. Feedback formats that link the results to institutional quality improvement systems should also be developed. Scoring and reporting rules will be essential if the results are used for policy, and feedback should identify actionable areas without prematurely treating a preliminary questionnaire score as a punitive institutional ranking.
