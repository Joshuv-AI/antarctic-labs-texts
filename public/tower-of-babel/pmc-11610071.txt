Developing a model for clinical teaching with emotional intelligence
Authors: Athar Omid, Fariba Haghani, Nahid Toufan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical teaching is an essential part of medical education and clinical teacher must acquire the necessary competencies to perform such a role. In this regard, there is emphasis on emotional intelligence as an essential competency. The aim of this study was to develop a model for clinical teaching with emotional intelligence based on the experiences of clinical teachers.MethodsA workshop on teaching with emotional intelligence was designed and conducted three times. One month after, a sample of workshop participants (N = 18) was selected through purposive sampling and their experiences of clinical teaching with emotional intelligence were described using focus groups (n = 3) and deductive content analysis.ResultsA model consisting of four themes: preparation and start communication before round, sustained communication during rounds, promotion after the round, and managing social interactions throughout the presence in the ward were presented for describing clinical teachers’ experiences.ConclusionIn this study, a model for using emotional intelligence in clinical setting was extracted. This model can be used in faculty development programs. A suggestion for future research is to examine the effect of using the model on learning outcomes and patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06349-3.

Background

Emotional intelligence is a sign of a person’s health and an important factor for improving job performance [1, 2]. Emotional intelligence means recognizing and managing your own emotions, recognizing and empathizing with others’ emotions, and being able to manage relationships with others [3, 4]. The combination of these competencies may leads to effective performance. Obviously, all professions in which leadership skills or management skills as well as communication skills are important, such as teaching and providing health services, require higher emotional intelligence [2].

Clinical teaching means teaching that takes place in various clinical environments, such as inpatient, outpatient, morning report, etc. In the educational clinical environment, patient, student, health team members, and clinical teacher are present together [5]. This environment is very complex because of the interaction between these people. According to the Dundee three-circle model, the clinical teacher must have special competencies in the form of three circle, which include the technical competencies such as clinical skills, practical procedures, patient and communication, the intellectual, emotional and analytical competencies and the personal intelligences [5]. In such an environment, the presence of a competent clinical teacher as a mentor creates a positive educational atmosphere that include positive, supportive relationships between him and students. This provides an ideal situation for effective student learning [6]. In order to present their roles properly, medical teachers depend on non-cognitive and social emotional characteristics such as the ability to communicate supportively with students and enthusiasm for teaching and medicine [7].

A teacher with high emotional intelligence communicates well with the students, manages the classroom appropriately, and is successful in teaching social emotional competencies. By controlling these three factors, such a teacher seems to improve a positive classroom atmosphere that leads to increased academic achievement [8, 9]. Conversely, if a teacher does not use his or her emotional intelligence, the value of his or her expertise in the subject and his or her knowledge of teaching and learning methods will be severely diminished, leading to widespread failure [10, 11]. Teachers with high emotional intelligence are less vulnerable to stress [12–16]; as a result, burnout among them is reduced and they have more well-being [12, 13, 17, 18]. Such teachers are known for behaviors such as self-confidence, more flexible, intentional reflective, more optimistic, and commitment ethics [19].

In addition, the clinical teacher should act as a role model in patient care and has the role of management and leadership in the clinical environment [20] This role requires effective communication between not only physician and the patient to provide patient centered care, but also physician and other disciplines to deliver good healthcare. Such communication leads to patient adaptability, gaining their trust, and ultimately improving clinical outcomes [12, 21–23]. Providing patient-centered care reduces unnecessary referrals and paraclinical tests and increases patient satisfaction [24]. Additionally, if we expect physicians to function effectively as a member of a team, they must learn the necessary team skills as a student and be aware and respect the various professions and their roles in providing health care. Therefore, there is international agreement that medical students should be prepared for practice by experiencing Interprofessional Education (IPE) [25]. Emotional intelligence is effective in establishing such an effective relationship between physician and patient. A physician with high emotional intelligence understands and empathizes with the patient by listening to him/her and paying attention to his/her non-verbal behaviors [26–29]. Such physicians are more successful in treating patients by being aware of their emotions [21].

As mentioned, there are many studies that have pointed to aspects of emotional intelligence such as communication and empathy as the competencies of a clinical teacher and considered these social and emotional competencies necessary to fulfill his roles [5, 20, 30, 31], but no study was found that clearly and practically stated what clinical teaching based on emotional intelligence is like. Therefore, this study was conducted with the aim of providing a practical model and a guide for clinical teachers to show what activities should be done step by step in different stages of clinical teaching. This model can be used as a guide in clinical teacher empowerment programs.

Methods

This study was performed in Isfahan University of Medical Sciences by directed or deductive content analysis. Directed content analysis is used when the researchers have an existing theory or framework that they want to base their research on it [32]. In this study, for interview questions, coding and text analysis, the characteristics of a person with high emotional intelligence presented by Goleman were used. Goleman’s model includes four domains of competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and relationship management. This model includes competencies, emotional self-awareness, accurate self-assessment, self-confidence, empathy, organizational awareness, service orientation, emotional self-control, adaptability, initiative, optimism, achievement orientation, transparency, conflict management, influence, change catalyst, developing others, inspirational leadership, teamwork and collaboration [3, 26]. In addition, the general teaching model was used as a framework for this stage of the study and this model was used in interview questions, coding and data analysis. In this model, a teacher’s activities are divided into four stages, before the teaching, before and during the teaching, during the teaching and after the teaching.

Two researchers (AO, NT) are general practitioners. In addition, the researcher (AO) has many years of experience in teaching and research in the field of clinical education. Two authors (FH, AO) have years of experience in teaching and research in the field of emotional intelligence. Therefore, the link between these two fields (clinical education and emotional intelligence) was considered in data analysis and preparation of educational content.

The research environment was the university hospitals of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. This university is one of the top universities in Iran, with 280 clinical medical teachers and general, specialized and sub-specialized departments in most fields. The study population consisted of clinical teachers participating in workshops that taught emotional intelligence and teaching with emotional intelligence.

The workshops were held for 6 h in one day. The participants of the workshops were 68 clinical teachers who participated in these workshops in three groups. Clinical medical teachers who wished to attend the workshop were included in the study. In these workshops, the concept of multiple intelligence, emotional intelligence and its 18 competencies [3, 26] were first presented, and participants were introduced to the importance of these competencies in clinical teaching. Participants worked in small group to extract examples of the use of each of these competencies in in daily life and teaching. The teaching method in this workshop was the lecture method and student-centered methods such as scenario-based learning. An expert in the field of emotional intelligence conducted the workshop. She has a lot of educational and research experiences in the field of emotional intelligence (her experiences and workshop schedule was attached).

One month after the workshops, the participants were invited to present their experience of using emotional intelligence. At this stage, the focus group was used. Purposeful sampling was performed and teachers were invited who were willing to participate in these sessions, gave the most information, and also showed their interest in using emotional intelligence in teaching during the workshop.

The participants of the focus groups consisted of 18 clinical teachers (8 females and 10 males) from Isfahan University of Medical Sciences with a mean work experience of 14.08 ± 9.74 which included, surgical specialties (obstetricians and gynecologists, urologists, ophthalmologist, surgeons, orthopedist) (N = 9), specialists from different fields of internal medicine and cardiovascular specialist (N = 3) and other specialties (toxicologist, ‌psychiatrist, anesthesiologist, pediatricians) (N = 6). Among them, 8 were assistant professors, 6 were associate professors and 4 were professors. Therefore, appropriate samples were selected for interviews (Table 1). Demographic characteristics of study participants

In this study, the focus group was used due to the need to create a comfortable environment to extract different views and behaviors of participants. Each session was attended by 6 participants, one facilitator (FH) and one note taker (AO). The meeting environment was calm and without disturbing factors such as staff traffic. This place was chosen with the consent of the participants in the hall near their place of work. Workshops were also held in the same place. A round table was used to make eye contact between participants. The interaction of the participants in each group was easily established due to the interactions they had with each other in the workshop. In addition, it was tried to homogenize the people of each group according to the field of study. At the beginning of the focus group the research objectives were explained. Focus group discussions were recorded with the permission of the participants. The duration of each session was from 60 to 90 min and the repetition of the focus group sessions was stopped after 3 sessions after reaching the data saturation.

Participants were first asked to consider a day of teaching in the clinical environment and to reflect on their experience in using emotional intelligence in recent weeks. Then, more specifically, they continued with three open-ended questions that asked them to describe the experience of using each of the emotional intelligence competencies during clinical teaching before, during, and after the patient encounter. In addition, during the session, the use of each of the competencies of emotional intelligence was probed.

Interviews were conducted verbatim, and after reading the text, sentences were coded that matched the emotional intelligence competencies. The codes were then categorized and the operational definition of each category was based on Goleman’s emotional intelligence competencies. Then the results were categorized in the form of activities before, before and during, during and after the round and throughout were presented in the form of a schematic model.

In this study, based on four Lincoln & Guba criteria, different methods were used to ensure the accuracy of the data. Sufficient time has been allotted for data collection to ensure data credibility. The member check procedure was also used, which after analyzing the data, participants reviewed and approved the final themes and model. Confirmability of the data was obtained despite the clinical teaching experience of one of the researchers in this study as well as the research background of all researchers in this study who were related to clinical education. Transferability was considered with the full description of the study and Dependability was obtained by writing a memo and coding the texts by two researchers with experience in qualitative research (AO, FH). The participants were informed to participate in the research. The interviews were conducted with their permission and the text of the interviews was analyzed anonymously, the principle of confidentiality and confidentiality of information and the right to withdraw were observed in each stage of the research.

Preparation and start communication before round

According to the participants’ point of view, emotional intelligence was regarded imperative before preparing for the teaching process owing to their busy work schedule and daily stress. They prepared themselves by recognizing and reflecting on their emotions and attitudes toward students and patients, and thereby maintained composure. In addition, at this stage, they try to cultivate enthusiasm in themselves and be optimistic about the results of their efforts.“A few days ago, when I was at work, I was angry about the breakdown of my car and I was still tired from the day before. Had I not been aware of this frustration, I would be very upset to see any sort of misbehavior of students and I might upset others, but “I tried to figure out my reasons for anger before the round and put it aside.”

Emotional intelligence is used to prepare before the rounds, while establishing initial rapport and stimulating students’ and HCPs’ enthusiasm. Participants stated that it is necessary to turn formal teaching into a friendly relationship with students initially. In fact, they considered it essential to establish an intimate relationship with the student and the staff in order to engage them in teaching and caring for the patient. They do this by greeting, breaking the ice, remembering their names, explaining the goals and nature of the round, having the patients’ consent, and showing respect and affection towards them.“When I see a student, I genially greet them or when I go to the patient’s bed, I greet, then I let the students to talk about whatever they like and I take a few minutes to break the formal air, ‌or to make the patient feel good, for instance, I say how good the weather is while they are hospitalized here and ... ”

Establishing and sustaining communication during the round

According to the participants, effective teaching during the round is possible by establishing initial rapport with patient, attending to students’ and patient’s physical comfort and personal safety, actively determining and appropriately responding others concerns and feelings, extending the relationship with the people present in the round, adapting to changes in the teaching conditions of the round and by being a role model.

Participants stated that students’ and patients’ concerns and feelings are identified once the relationship has been initiated. To do this, they listen to what they have to say, pay attention to the non-verbal signs, and encourage people to express their concerns. Then, by expressing empathy, they try to create a supportive environment for them. They considered this step necessary to get people to engage in the teaching process.“Once I noticed that one of the residents looked pale and seemed exhausted. They stated that at the end of the round, they evaluate patients’ reactions and satisfaction in order to improve the next programs.“After the round, I ask the patient their opinion on the ward staff and ward schedule. Managing social interaction throughout the presence at the ward

Managing social and light atmosphere was another theme of this study, which is a vital part of the whole the clinical education process starting from pre-round to post-round. They noted that appropriate interactions between students, staff, colleagues, and patients are necessary to succeed in patient care and student education. Assigning tasks to each person in the group in a way that makes them feel empowered and important, spreading enthusiasm in teamwork, sensing unspoken feelings and messages conveyed through interactions, committing to managing conflicts, and building friendly relationships between people based on trust building was all reported by the participants of this study as imperative.“I explain to the students that this head nurse has her own rules and her territory must be respected, and I tell the head nurse to warn me whenever I interfere in her duties, this makes her understand that I respect her territory and as a result we have less conflict.”“Dividing tasks helps a lot as it keeps everyone occupied and responsible and makes them feel important. Discussion

The results of this study showed that a clinical teacher must learn to use emotional intelligence before attending the ward, and should be able to handle and resolve intense emotions such as anger and hatred beforehand, and enter the ward with a positive attitude towards students. In addition, excitement and optimism about the outcome of the training also occurs at this phase. In fact, self-management is the first step and key to better communication and effective teaching.

This capability is imperative to clinical teachers as it lets them with cope challenges like unpredictability and diversity of subject matter [33–36], different levels and interests of students, the severity of the patient and unpredictable environmental conditions [37–39], in spite of their many responsibilities [33]. Moreover, low student motivation, lack of reward system for educational activities and lack of proper management of the educational system brings down their motivation for teaching [34, 40, 41]. So emotional intelligence is a pre-requisite for effective clinical teaching [7] which enables teachers to cope with various circumstances. This helps the person recognize his emotions, create enthusiasm and motivation for himself [8, 42] and becomes more committed to his job [43].

Another finding of this study was the friendly relationship between the teacher, students, patient care team and patient. Kristina Mikkonen et al., in a systematic review has examined the core competences of health science teachers. They believed that interpersonal and communicative skills are as important as their clinical knowledge and skills in promoting effective learning in the clinical practice area [44]. The relationship between teacher and student has multiple effects on the quality of teaching and learning and explains almost half of the variance in effective teaching [45, 46]. Further still, most teaching takes place as a one-on-one interaction between a supervisor or mentor and a student in a clinical setting. The effectiveness of this supervision is highly dependent on the quality of the teacher-student relationship and the interaction between them plays an important role in creating positive experiences [47]. This type of communication that facilitates learning is termed an educational alliance, the same way, effective communication between patient and physician has been termed therapeutic alliance [45, 48, 49] which is crucial for providing a relationship-centered care [50].

A teacher with high emotional intelligence is successful in initiating and managing an effective relationship. Such a teacher welcomes the student, the patient, and others in the clinical setting and, with the patient’s permission, demonstrates that his or her opinions and feelings are respected. Here, he tries to use the right words to start the communication and addresses people by their names, listens to them and scans people’s non-verbal signals to understand their unspoken messages. Such an environment encourages people to be comfortable in using all forms of verbal and non-verbal forms of communication to easily express their concerns and ask their questions. Identifying their concerns and empathizing and supporting them emotionally sustains the relationship.

Maintaining the physical comfort of students and patients was another issue that was that concerned the teachers. According to them, the prolonged stay of students on foot in the crowded rooms of wards and clinics, lack of ventilation and physical space, hinders the comfort of the patient and the student. Teaching sessions can be especially frustrating for critically ill patients. Just as a tired and worried learner cannot be forced to participate in educational activities by any means [42] and a tired teacher cannot be expected to do his / her educational task properly [51]. In addition, patients’ worries about disclosing their secrets and the risk of physical harm/pain makes them less interested in the participation of education [52, 53]. The clinical teacher with emotional intelligence is better able to understand these and comes up with new ways to positive emotional states in patients and students. With this in mind the teachers choose a physical environment that best suits his objectives and circumstance and conducts the rounds at the patients’ bedside, hallway, living room, corridors, conference rooms, etc. In addition, proper adjustment of residents ‘and interns’ working hours and planning for mid-day naps in long shifts are reported as helpful [54–57].

Another result of this study was creating enthusiasm among students and staff. A good teacher can motivate and produce active students [7, 58]. A teacher with high emotional intelligence has the ability to influence others with his [19]. He can motivate others by displaying his enthusiasm for teaching and medicine. Such a teacher accepts the differences and students’ different needs, understands their emotions and knows how to motivate each student [59]. For example, he tries to design a suitable learning environment based on learning theories that improve students’ motivation and performance [60, 61]. Also, a person with high emotional intelligence is optimistic and can pave the way for a great career for future students.

Study participants emphasized on flexibility in clinical teaching. Like with other studies, owing to the large changes in medical education, unpredictability and complexity of the clinical environment, the need for a flexible teacher has been prioritized [62]. These studies emphasize the importance of choosing a teaching method appropriate to the characteristics of students and learning objectives and creatively presenting different models of education [63].

The prospect of a clinical teacher, setting the standards for appropriate interactions, respect and effective communication was another theme that emerged from this study. In a study setting, a teacher with high emotional intelligence eliciting characteristics such as good communication, optimism, self-esteem, flexibility and patience becomes a positive role model for students [64, 65]. The teacher with such qualities highly impacts others and has the power to gain their respect [26].

A vital part of emotional intelligence is self-assessment and reflecting upon one’s own experiences and experience working with others. In the process of self-assessment, a teacher judges the efficacy and adequacy of his / her knowledge, performance and beliefs, and becomes aware of his / her strengths and weaknesses [66, 67]. He sincerely asks the remarks of others (colleagues, staff and learners) on his clinical teaching skills [68, 69]. According to Golman, a person with high emotional intelligence uses this to improve themselves and achieve the highest standard [3] and optimistically uses every opportunity to enhance their performance. According to Goleman, the added benefit of emotional intelligence is the ability to help others develop/progress as they are capable of identifying the talents in other and provide appropriate feedback [69]. Since a teacher with emotional intelligence opens conversations in a non-threatening atmosphere with all due respect to the feelings and emotions of students [70–73], students become more open to feedback and criticisms [74], since the teacher listens attentively to the students prior to providing feedback [75] and also by recognizing those, is able to guide the student to manage their emotions.

Another theme revealed by the present study is the availability of the teacher even after the rounds. Goleman considers Service Orientation and readiness to help and serve others as another grand quality of a person possessing emotional intelligence. Sutkin et al., in their study described availability as a notable quality of a great teacher, since, he/she is eager to teach and ready to help students and patients and takes personal responsibility for meeting their needs. He/she spends a lot of time observing how students perform physical examinations and take medical history, answers their questions and discusses with them [7]. Also, Also, he/she pays attention to the satisfaction of the patient during instrucational planning. [7, 58].

Another competency of emotional intelligence was the ability to incorporate the events of the rounds into teaching. In addition to portraying these abilities during the round, the teachers completed their teaching after the completion of the round by recalling these events.

Another theme unfolded by this study was managing a friendly and a social atmosphere. A teacher with high emotional intelligence can create formal and extensive relationships [76]. A person with such quality encourages the members of the team to cooperate and creates a passion for teamwork in them [8]. He seeks to shape group identity, induce collective spirit, and a sense of commitment [77]. Such a person seeks to establish relationships that are mutually beneficial and try to resolve conflicts when they arise [76]. At the initial phase of when interactions and student groups are being formed, the assignment of responsibilities whilst, clarifying duties and rules, prevents misbehaviors and creates a stable society. Meanwhile, this encourages students to take responsibility for their own behavior [78].

The teacher with high emotional intelligence pays attention to the relationship between students and other people in the department and by analyzing and discussing, recognizes and manages the challenges that students face. Moreover, these informal interactions do impact the efficacy of teaching [79].

The strengths and limitations of the study

One of the strengths of this study was that the use of emotional intelligence competencies in clinical teaching was emphasized. In addition, a practical model for clinical teaching based on emotional intelligence was presented. In this model, which is presented based on the clinical teachers’ experiences, strategies for teaching based on emotional intelligence were explained. This model can be used for faculty development workshops.

One of the limitations of this study is that this research was conducted in the actual clinical environment. The clinical environment is an unpredictable environment, and other factors in this environment may influence the results of this study that may not have been considered. Another limitation of this study was the participation of clinical teachers from merely one university. With focus group studies, the possibility of bias and the influence of interviewers ‘opinions on participants’ opinions exists, but attempts were made to reduce this possibility. For this purpose, we had interviewers refrain from presenting their views and dedicate on conversations and questions that would only clarify the statements of participants. In addition, there was a frequency of using such strategies influenced by teachers ‘emotional intelligence that was not examined in this study. We used a standard SRQR checklist for reporting this studies.

Lessons learned and implications for practice and future researchers.

The current study examined teachers’ experience in using emotional intelligence but the overall effect of increasing teachers’ emotional intelligence on students’ learning outcomes and the quality of patient care was not examined. We recommend further studies to determine the effect of improving teachers ‘emotional intelligence skills and using this model on the effectiveness of clinical teaching, learners’ learning outcomes, and the quality of patient care and satisfaction. The consequences of learning, including the extent to which clinical knowledge is memorized, clinical reasoning, communication skills, professional character, and student satisfaction, should also be examined.

Conclusion

In this study, based on the statements of the participants, a model for using emotional intelligence in clinical setting was extracted. This model could be useful in making clinical teaching more effective and can be used in faculty development programs.
