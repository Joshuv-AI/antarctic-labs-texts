Regularized Gradient Temporal-Difference Learning
Hyunjun Na, Donghwan Lee

Abstract
Gradient temporal-difference (GTD) learning algorithms are widely used for off-policy policy evaluation with function approximation. However, existing convergence analyses rely on the restrictive assumption that the so-called feature interaction matrix (FIM) is nonsingular. In practice, the FIM can become singular and leads to instability or degraded performance. In this paper, we propose a regularized optimization objective by reformulating the mean-square projected Bellman error (MSPBE) minimization. This formulation naturally yields a regularized GTD algorithms, referred to as R-GTD, which guarantees convergence to a unique solution even when the FIM is singular. We establish theoretical convergence guarantees and explicit error bounds for the proposed method, and validate its effectiveness through empirical experiments.



1
Introduction

Temporal-difference (TD) learning
(
27
)
constitutes a foundational approach for policy evaluation in reinforcement learning (RL)
(
24
)
. Despite its effectiveness in on-policy settings, it is now well understood that instability and divergence may arise when TD learning is combined with function approximation and bootstrapping in an off-policy setting.
This combination, commonly referred to as the
deadly triad
, has been identified as a fundamental source of instability in RL.

(
28
)
.

To resolve the convergence issue, gradient temporal-difference (GTD) was introduced as an alternative to TD learning
(
26
)
. Unlike TD learning, which relies on semi-gradient updates, GTD is derived from the true stochastic gradient of a well-defined objective function known as the mean-square Bellman error (MSBE), whose minimization is equivalent to solving the policy evaluation problem by enforcing the Bellman equation in a least-squares sense. Unlike the TD learning, GTD is known to guarantee convergence under off-policy training with linear function approximation.
However, it still faces limitations with linear function approximation.
Because the Bellman operator typically maps a value function outside the class of functions representable by the linear function approximation.
As a result, even the optimal solution of the MSBE may exhibit a nonzero residual Bellman error. To address this issue, GTD2
(
25
)
considers the projected Bellman equation (PBE), where the Bellman update is projected onto the feature space. Specifically, GTD2 minimizes the mean-square projected Bellman error (MSPBE), which measures the squared residual of the PBE.
By enforcing the Bellman equation within the feature space, the projected Bellman error can be driven to zero, and it allows GTD2 to obtain a more accurate approximation of the value function that can be represented by the chosen linear features.

From an optimization perspective, the MSPBE objective admits an equivalent
saddle-point formulation, under which GTD2 can be interpreted as solving a
convex–concave saddle-point problem
(
16
)
.
This perspective allows the use of analytical tools such as Fenchel duality
(
4
)
and Lagrangian methods
(
7
)
. Recent work in
13
has extended this perspective by proposing new saddle-point formulations and further GTD2 variants that exploit these structural insights.
Complementary to the saddle-point viewpoint,
control-theoretic analyses have interpreted divergence in GTD2 and related
off-policy TD methods as dynamical instabilities and proposed stabilizing modifications
(
15
)
. Alongside these stability-focused perspectives, techniques such as TDRC
(
8
)
have introduced
regularization to improve the practical robustness and stability of GTD methods.

However, GTD methods typically require a nonsingularity assumption on a certain matrix which is associated with feature interaction.
Under this assumption, the solution to the PBE is well defined and unique, which in turn ensures the existence of
a unique minimizer of the MSPBE. In this paper, for notational convenience,
we refer to this matrix as the

feature interaction matrix
(FIM).
When the FIM is singular, the PBE admits multiple solutions; as a result, the MSPBE no longer has a unique minimizer.
As a consequence, the convergence guarantees of GTD2 no longer apply,
and the algorithm may exhibit instability in practice.
This limitation motivates the development of algorithms that remain
stable even when the FIM is singular.

In this paper, we propose regularized GTD (R-GTD), a new variant of GTD2 that
introduces a regularized convex–concave saddle-point formulation with a unique
solution without imposing the nonsingularity assumption on the FIM.
Finally, the main contributions are summarized as follows:

(a)

We propose R-GTD, which removes the nonsingularity assumption on the FIM required for the convergence analysis. This advantage allows us to establish convergence guarantees even in singular settings, where GTD methods lack theoretical justification.

(b)

We study the theoretical properties of R-GTD from two complementary perspectives. First, we analyze the convergence of R-GTD toward the saddle point of the associated convex–concave formulation using primal–dual gradient dynamics (PDGD) in
21
and the
ordinary differential equation (ODE) method in
3
.
Second, we establish explicit error bounds between the R-GTD solution and the true projected solution in both nonsingular and singular settings, which precisely characterize the limiting behavior of the R-GTD solution.

(c)

We empirically validate the proposed algorithm in a representative setting where the FIM is singular. While GTD2 exhibits instability in this regime, R-GTD converges consistently to the saddle-point solution with more stable behavior.
Additional
experiments in
Appendix

J
extend this evaluation to diverse scenarios.

Preliminaries

2.1
Markov decision process

A Markov decision process (MDP) is defined as a tuple

{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},P,r,\gamma)
,
where
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}
denote the finite state and action spaces, respectively.
The transition probability
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
specifies the probability of moving from state
s
to
s^{\prime}

when action
a
is taken, and

r:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

is the reward function.
The discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
determines the relative importance of future rewards. When an agent in state
s
selects action
a
, it transits to the next state
s^{\prime}

with probability
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
and receives an immediate reward
r(s,a,s^{\prime})
.
A stochastic policy
\pi:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,1]

specifies the probability
\pi(a|s)
of selecting action
a
at state
s
.
Let
P^{\pi}
denote the state transition matrix induced by policy
\pi
and

D
denote a diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements

d(s)
for
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Here,
d
can be any state distribution satisfying
d(s)>0
for all

s\in\mathcal{S}
.
As a particular case,
d
may be chosen as the stationary state distribution

d^{\beta}
induced by a behavior policy
\beta
.
The expected reward under policy
\pi
at state
s
is denoted by
R^{\pi}(s)
. The infinite-horizon discounted value function associated with
\pi
is defined as

J^{\pi}(s):={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k},a_{k},s_{k+1})\,\middle|\,s_{0}=s,\;\pi\right],

where
{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation with respect to the
state–action trajectories generated by
\pi
. Given a set of basis (feature) functions

\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{q}:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
we define the
feature matrix

\Phi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|\times q}

whose
s
-th row is given by

\phi(s):=[\phi_{1}(s)\;\cdots\;\phi_{q}(s)]
. Here,
q
is the number of
feature functions and
|{\cal S}|
denote the cardinality of the state. Throughout this paper, we assume that
\Phi
has full column rank. Under this setting, the
policy evaluation problem
aims to estimate the value function

J^{\pi}
corresponding to a fixed policy
\pi
.

2.2
Saddle-point problem

We briefly review the saddle-point problem

(
18
;
21
)
.
Let
L:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a convex–concave function.
We begin with the standard definition of a saddle point.

Definition 2.1

(Saddle point)
.

A pair
(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is a saddle point of
L
if

L(\theta^{*},\lambda)\leq L(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})\leq L(\theta,\lambda^{*})\quad\forall(\theta,\lambda).

When
L
is convex in
\theta
and concave in
\lambda
, the saddle-point
condition is equivalent to the following min–max problem:

Problem 1

(Min–max problem)
.

\min_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}L(\theta,\lambda)=\max_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\min_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}L(\theta,\lambda).

This equivalence requires the convex–concave structure of
L
together with mild regularity conditions such as coercivity

(
23
;
22
)
.
Under these conditions, a pair
(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})
is a saddle point if and
only if it satisfies the first-order stationarity conditions

\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})=0,\;\nabla_{\lambda}L(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})=0.

(1)

This characterization motivates gradient-based methods for finding such saddle points. A standard approach is the primal–dual gradient dynamic (PDGD) method
(
18
)
, whose discrete-time iteration is given as

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{k},\lambda_{k}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}+\alpha_{k}\nabla_{\lambda}L(\theta_{k},\lambda_{k}).

Moreover, we can also consider a continuous-time counterpart of PDGD given as

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t}=-\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{t},\lambda_{t}),\;\dot{\lambda}_{t}=\nabla_{\lambda}L(\theta_{t},\lambda_{t}),

(2)

which converges to a saddle point under standard assumptions

(
18
;
21
)
.
When only stochastic approximations of the gradients are available, the corresponding stochastic formulation is given as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla_{\theta}\bigl(L(\theta_{k},\lambda_{k})+v_{k}\bigr),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=\lambda_{k}+\alpha_{k}\nabla_{\lambda}\bigl(L(\theta_{k},\lambda_{k})+w_{k}\bigr),

where
(v_{k},w_{k})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are i.i.d. zero-mean noise terms. We refer to this version as stochastic
PDGD. The stochastic PDGD is also known to converge to a saddle point in a probabilistic sense
(
6
;
29
)
.

3
Review of GTD2

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the GTD2 algorithm
introduced in
25
which tries to solve the policy evaluation problem.
Specifically, when the state space is large, linear function approximation is commonly
used, and the objective of policy evaluation is to find a parameter vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that

\Phi\theta
approximates
J^{\pi}
, where
\Phi
denotes a given feature matrix.
However, under linear function approximation, the Bellman equation
\Phi\theta=R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta
generally does
not admit a solution within the feature subspace.
This motivates the use of the projected Bellman equation (PBE), defined as

\Phi\theta=\Pi_{\mathcal{R}(\Phi)}\bigl(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta\bigr),

(3)

which seeks a solution
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Here,
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}(\Phi)}
denotes the projection onto the range space of

\Phi
, denoted by
\mathcal{R}(\Phi)
:
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}(\Phi)}(x):=\argmin_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(\Phi)}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{D^{\beta}}^{2}
. Since
\Phi
has full column rank, this projection admits a closed-form
solution, which can be written as a matrix operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}(\Phi)}:=\Phi(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}.

GTD2 formulates the policy evaluation problem by minimizing the mean-square projected Bellman error (MSPBE), whose minimizer coincides with the solution of the PBE in (
3
)

Problem 2
.

Solve for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
the optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}{\rm MSPBE}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\|\Pi_{\operatorname{\cal{R}}(\Phi)}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta)-\Phi\theta\|_{D^{\beta}}^{2}.

Here,
\|x\|_{D^{\beta}}
is defined as
\sqrt{x^{\top}D^{\beta}x}
for any positive-definite matrix
D^{\beta}
. Note that minimizing the objective means minimizing the error of the PBE in (
3
)
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{D^{\beta}}
. Moreover, in the objective of
2
,
d^{\beta}
depends on the behavior policy,
\beta
, while
P^{\pi}
and
R^{\pi}
depend on the target policy,
\pi
, that we want to evaluate. This structure allows us to obtain an off-policy learning algorithm through the importance sampling
(
20
)
or sub-sampling techniques
(
26
)
.

Although GTD2 is a promising algorithm with convergence guarantees
under off-policy training, its theoretical analysis relies on the following assumption:

Assumption 3.1
.

\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi
is nonsingular, where
I
denotes the identity matrix with an appropriate dimension.

Note that
3.1
is common in the literature, and is adopted in
26
;
25
;
8
for convergence of GTD methods.
In this paper, we refer to
\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(I-\gamma P^{\pi})\Phi
as the
feature interaction matrix
(FIM), and we say the problem is
ill-conditioned
when the FIM is singular or nearly singular. Under
3.1
, the MSPBE minimization in
2

has a unique solution. In particular, the GTD2 solution is given by

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}=-\bigl(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}R^{\pi},

(4)

as shown in
16
, which coincides with the unique solution of the PBE under
3.1
.
To find this solution in an incremental and model-free manner,
25
derived the GTD2 algorithm with a convergence analysis. More recently, to provide a deeper optimization-theoretic perspective,

13
showed that the MSPBE minimization
in
2
can be equivalently expressed as the following
constrained optimization problem:

Problem 3
.

Solve for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
the optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}0\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad 0=\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta-\Phi\theta).

It can be easily proved that the equality constraint in
3
is equivalent to the PBE in (
3
). Specifically, this constraint can be rewritten as
\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(I-\gamma P^{\pi})\Phi\theta=\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}R^{\pi}
, which leads to the same solution as the GTD2 algorithm in (
4
). This constrained formulation not only characterizes the PBE solution but also naturally admits an equivalent saddle-point representation.
A standard approach to solving constrained optimization problems with equality constraints
is to introduce a Lagrange multiplier and consider the associated Lagrangian
(
4
)
,
which is given by

L(\theta,\lambda)=\lambda^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta-\Phi\theta).

This Lagrangian provides a saddle-point characterization of the PBE as

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}\lambda^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta-\Phi\theta)

and it forms the basis for primal–dual GTD-type algorithms. Moreover, the solution of the saddle-point problem is equivalent to that of the
equality-constrained formulation in
3
.

4
Regularized GTD

To provide a foundation for our algorithm, we reformulate the policy evaluation problem by extending the constrained optimization in
3
. First, we observe that adding a quadratic term
\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\theta
to the objective of
3
does not shift the optimal solution
\theta_{\text{GTD2}}
as long as the FIM is nonsingular (
3.1
). This term serves as a base for regularization without altering the solution.
However, when the FIM is singular, the added quadratic term
selects a particular solution among them, thereby
altering the optimal solution.
Next, to ensure the problem remains well-defined even when FIM is singular, we introduce an auxiliary variable
w\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
into the constraint. This
w
acts as a slack variable that allows for a small error in the PBE.
Finally, to prevent this error from becoming large, we incorporate a penalty term
\frac{c}{2}w^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi w
into the objective, where
c>0
is a regularization coefficient. This leads to the following regularized optimization problem:

Problem 4
.

Solve for
\theta,w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
the optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\theta,w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}\frac{c}{2}w^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi w+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\theta

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\quad 0=\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta-\Phi\theta+\Phi w).

(5)

Here, we allow a weighted error term

\Phi w
in the constraint compared to
3
, which effectively introduces a small error in the PBE in (
3
).
The variable
w
represents an auxiliary variable capturing the error of the
PBE, and
c>0
is a regularization parameter that
controls its penalty.

Based on this formulation, we derive the R-GTD algorithm in this paper. The first step in this derivation is to recast
4
as a min–max saddle-point problem in
1
, for which we now introduce the corresponding Lagrangian function

\displaystyle L(\theta,w,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2}w^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi w+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\theta

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta-\Phi\theta+\Phi w),

(6)

where we can prove that
L(\theta,w,\lambda)
is strongly convex in the primal variable
\theta
and
w
, concave in the dual variable
\lambda
, and coercive due to the positive definite weighting matrix. Therefore, by
Lemma

A.4
of Appendix, the problem satisfies the structural conditions of the min–max formulation in
1
and admits a saddle point.
Moreover, due to the strong convexity of
L(\theta,w,\lambda)
in the primal
variables
(\theta,w)
and its concavity in the dual variable
\lambda
,
the min–max problem admits a unique
saddle-point solution.
The corresponding min-max saddle-point problem is summarized below for convenience.

Problem 5
.

Solve for
\theta,w,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
the optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\theta,w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}\max_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}}L(\theta,w,\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\frac{c}{2}w^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi w

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\theta+\lambda^{\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}

\displaystyle\quad\times(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta-\Phi\theta+\Phi w).

Solving the stationary conditions in (
1
) yields a closed-form characterization
of the optimal solution
(\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}},\lambda_{\mathrm{RGTD}},w_{\mathrm{RGTD}})

to the min-max problem in
5
.
For clarity, the explicit expressions of the optimal solution are summarized below.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}},\lambda_{\mathrm{RGTD}},w_{\mathrm{RGTD}})

denote the optimal solution to the min-max problem in
5
.
Then the optimal solution admits the following closed-form expressions:

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\bigl(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}R^{\pi}}_{\theta_{GTD2}}

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{-\bigl(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\,w_{\mathrm{RGTD}}}_{\text{error}},

(7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{RGTD}}

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi\bigr)^{-\top}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\,\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}},

(8)

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{RGTD}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}\,\lambda_{\mathrm{RGTD}}.

(9)

The proof of
Proposition

4.1
is given in
Appendix

B
.
The characterization in
Proposition

4.1
reveals that the R-GTD solution

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}
does not exactly coincide with the GTD2 solution

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}
for finite values of the parameter
c
.
In particular, compared to
\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}
in (
4
), the
\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}

contains an additional bias term

-(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\,w_{\mathrm{RGTD}},
which originates from the auxiliary variable
w_{\mathrm{RGTD}}

introduced in (
5
).
Moreover, by direct manipulations of (
7
)–(
9
), one can easily prove that
\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}
is explicitly given by the following equation:

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}

\displaystyle=-\Bigl((\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi)^{\top}(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi)^{-1}

(10)

\displaystyle\times(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi)+\tfrac{1}{c}\,\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\Bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi)^{\top}

\displaystyle\times(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}R^{\pi}.

We can see that as
c\to\infty
, the solution (
10
) converges to the GTD2 solution in (
4
) provided that the FIM,
\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi
, is nonsingular.
When FIM is singular, then still the term inside
(\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(\gamma P^{\pi}-I)\Phi)+\tfrac{1}{c}\,\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi)^{-1}
is nonsingular. Therefore, the R-GTD solutions is still well defined in this case. Consequently, under
3.1
,
letting
c\to\infty
eliminates the effect of the auxiliary variable,
yielding

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}\rightarrow\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}},w_{\mathrm{RGTD}}\to 0,
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{RGTD}}\to 0.

Having characterized the optimal solution and its properties, we now derive the practical update rules for R-GTD. As discussed earlier, a standard approach for solving such convex–concave
saddle-point problems is to apply the PDGD.
Accordingly, we first consider the continuous-time PDGD in (
2
) associated with the
Lagrangian
L(\theta,w,\lambda)
in (
4
), given by

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{t},w_{t},\lambda_{t})

\displaystyle=-\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\,\theta_{t}-\big((\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi)^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi-\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\big)\lambda_{t},

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{w}L(\theta_{t},w_{t},\lambda_{t})=-c\,\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\,w_{t}-\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\,\lambda_{t},

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\lambda}L(\theta_{t},w_{t},\lambda_{t})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta_{t}-\Phi\theta_{t}+\Phi w_{t}).

(11)

Its discrete-time counterpart (by Euler discretization) is

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\theta_{k}+\alpha_{k}(-\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\theta_{k}-((\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi)^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi

\displaystyle-\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi)\lambda_{k}),

\displaystyle w_{k+1}=

\displaystyle w_{k}+\alpha_{k}(-c\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi w_{k}-\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}\Phi\lambda_{k}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}+\alpha_{k}\Phi^{\top}D^{\beta}(R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}\Phi\theta_{k}-\Phi\theta_{k}+\Phi w_{k}),

(12)

where the scalar
\alpha_{k}>0
denotes the step size at iteration
k
and
e_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
denotes the standard basis vector associated with
state
s
, whose
s
-th entry is one and all other entries are zero.
Considering
s_{k}\,\sim\,d^{\beta}
,
a_{k}\,\sim\,\pi(\cdot\,|\,s_{k})
, and

s_{k}^{\prime}\,\sim\,P(\cdot\,|\,s_{k},a_{k})
,
The corresponding stochastic PDGD can then be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\theta_{k}+\alpha_{k}(-\Phi^{\top}e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}}^{\top}\Phi\theta_{k}

\displaystyle-((\gamma e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}^{\prime}}\Phi)^{\top}e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}}^{\top}\Phi-\Phi^{\top}e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}}^{\top}\Phi)\lambda_{k}),

\displaystyle w_{k+1}=

\displaystyle w_{k}+\alpha_{k}(-c\Phi^{\top}e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}}^{\top}\Phi w_{k}-\Phi^{\top}e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}}^{\top}\Phi\lambda_{k}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}+\alpha_{k}\Phi^{\top}e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}}^{\top}(e_{s_{k}}r(s_{k},a_{k},s_{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\gamma e_{s_{k}}e_{s_{k}^{\prime}}^{\top}\Phi\theta_{k}-\Phi\theta_{k}+\Phi w_{k}),

where
s_{k}
is the state sampled at iteration
k
, and
s_{k}^{\prime}
is the next state observed after taking an action
a_{k}
according to the behavior policy
\beta
.
The resulting stochastic PDGD updates constitute the R-GTD algorithm,
summarized in
Algorithm

1
, which incorporates importance sampling
for off-policy data
(
20
)
.

Algorithm 1

R-GTD

1:

Set the step-size
(\alpha_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
and regularization parameter
c
. Initialize
(\theta_{0},w_{0},\lambda_{0})
.

2:

for

k\in\{0,\ldots\}

do

3:

Observe
s_{k}\sim d^{\beta}
,
a_{k}\sim\beta(\cdot|s_{k})
, and
s_{k}^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|s_{k},a_{k})
,
r_{k+1}:=r(s_{k},a_{k},s_{k}^{\prime})
.

4:

Update parameters according to

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+\alpha_{k}((\phi_{k}-\gamma\rho_{k}\phi_{k}^{\prime})(\phi_{k}^{\top}\lambda_{k})-\phi_{k}(\phi_{k}^{\top}\theta_{k})),

\displaystyle w_{k+1}=w_{k}+\alpha_{k}(-c\phi_{k}^{\top}w_{k}-\phi_{k}^{\top}\lambda_{k})\phi_{k},

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=\lambda_{k}+\alpha_{k}(\delta_{k}+\phi_{k}^{\top}w_{k})\phi_{k},

where
\phi_{k}:=\phi(s_{k}),\phi_{k}^{\prime}:=\phi(s_{k}^{\prime})
,

5.1

Conclusion

This paper introduced R-GTD, a regularized variant of GTD2 derived from a
convex–concave saddle-point formulation.
The proposed approach relaxes the convergence assumptions of GTD2 while preserving the core theoretical structure of GTD2. As a result, the method achieves improved stability in ill-conditioned settings.
We proved asymptotic convergence to the saddle point and established error bounds
with respect to the true projected solution.
In particular, the analysis reveals how regularization affects the asymptotic behavior of the solution.
Future work includes extensions to nonlinear function approximation and
finite-time convergence analysis.

Proof.

,

x^{\top}G_{\alpha}x=x^{\top}Gx+\alpha x^{\top}Hx.

If

, then

because

and the zero eigenvalues occur only along the nullspace directions.
Thus,
x^{\top}G_{\alpha}x>0
. If

, then

for some nonzero vector

x^{\top}Hx=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(v_{i}^{\top}x)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}^{2}=\|c\|^{2}>0.

Thus,

x^{\top}G_{\alpha}x=\alpha\|c\|^{2}>0.

Since
x^{\top}G_{\alpha}x>0
for all
x\neq 0
, the matrix
G_{\alpha}
is
positive definite and hence nonsingular.
∎

Lemma

To derive explicit expressions and error bounds in the singular case,
however, it is necessary to further characterize the structure of this
regularized matrix. To this end, we require several auxiliary results from linear algebra.
In particular, the following lemmas describe the geometric decomposition
induced by a symmetric matrix, the structure of orthogonal projections,
and the interaction between the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse and the

.

(

Lemma A.10

\Pi_{\mathcal{N}(G)}G^{\dagger}=0

G=M^{\top}B^{-1}M
) and also define

K:=G^{-1}BG^{-1}M^{\top}B^{-1}b
.
Then, we obtain

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}=\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}+\frac{1}{c}K+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{c^{2}}\right).

(b) Singular case.

Suppose that
M
is singular, and hence

G=M^{\top}B^{-1}M
is positive semidefinite but not invertible.

Although
G^{-1}
does not exist, the regularized matrix

G+\tfrac{1}{c}B

is invertible for every
c>0
because
B\succ 0
implies

G+\tfrac{1}{c}B\succeq\tfrac{1}{c}B\succ 0
.
Therefore, the R-GTD solution

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}=(G+c^{-1}B)^{-1}M^{\top}B^{-1}b

is well-defined for all
c>0
.

To relate this solution to the GTD2 solutions, we first decompose any

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}\in\Theta_{GTD2}
into its nullspace and orthogonal components:

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}=v+v_{\perp},\qquad v\in\operatorname{Null}(G),\qquad v_{\perp}\in\operatorname{Null}(G)^{\perp}.

Because
Gv=0
, the GTD2 equation
G\theta_{GTD2}=M^{T}B^{-1}b
reduces to

Gv_{\perp}=M^{\top}B^{-1}b.

To express
v_{\perp}
explicitly, we introduce a small regularization
in the nullspace direction:

\left[G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right]v_{\perp}=M^{\top}B^{-1}b,

where
{c^{-1}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{{v_{i}}}v_{i}^{\top}{v_{\bot}}=0
.
By
Lemma

A.6
of Appendix,
G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}
is invertible, and thus, we can derive

v_{\perp}=\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\right)^{-1}M^{T}B^{-1}b.

Note that although the auxiliary parameter
c
is introduced to regularize the
operator and ensure nonsingularity, the orthogonal component
v_{\perp}
of the
GTD2 solution is in fact independent of
c
.
This can be seen by explicitly characterizing the inverse of the regularized
operator.

Recall that
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
is a basis of

\operatorname{Null}(G)
.
Then the matrix

\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}

coincides with the orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{Null}(G)
.
Indeed, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}x=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}x,

which implies

\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}=\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}.

It follows from
Lemma

A.11
that the inverse of the regularized operator admits
the explicit form

(G+c^{-1}\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)})^{-1}=G^{\dagger}+c\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)},\qquad c>0.

Since the GTD2 equation
G\theta=M^{\top}B^{-1}b
is solvable, we have

M^{\top}B^{-1}b\in\mathrm{Range}(G)
, and hence

\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}\,M^{\top}B^{-1}b=0.

Consequently,

\bigl(G+c^{-1}\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}\bigr)^{-1}M^{\top}B^{-1}b=\bigl(G^{\dagger}+c\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}\bigr)M^{\top}B^{-1}b=G^{\dagger}M^{\top}B^{-1}b,

which shows that the orthogonal component
v_{\perp}
is independent of
c
.

Thus,

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}=v+v_{\perp}=\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}M^{T}B^{-1}b+v.

Then,

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}-\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}(\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}})=\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}M^{T}B^{-1}b.

Next, we decompose the R-GTD solution as

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}=u+u_{\perp},\qquad u\in\operatorname{Null}(G),\qquad u_{\perp}\in\operatorname{Null}(G)^{\perp}.

From the definition of
\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}
,

u_{\perp}=(G+c^{-1}B)^{-1}M^{\top}B^{-1}b-u.

To compare this expression with the GTD2 decomposition, we rewrite

G+c^{-1}B=G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}+c^{-1}\!\left(B-\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right).

Factoring out the first term gives

(G+c^{-1}B)^{-1}=\left[I+c^{-1}\!\left(B-\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)\right]^{-1}\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}.

Let
c_{0}:=\|B-\Pi_{\mathcal{N}(G)}\|_{2}
. For all
c>c_{0}
, the bracketed term is a small perturbation of the identity since
\|\frac{1}{c}(B-\sum v_{i}v_{i}^{\top})\|_{2}<1
. Thus, its inverse admits the first-order expansion:

\left[I+c^{-1}\!\left(B-\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)\right]^{-1}=I+O(1/c).

Substituting this into the expression for
u_{\perp}
yields

u_{\perp}=\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}M^{\top}B^{-1}b+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{c}\right)-u.

Therefore,

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}=u+u_{\perp}=\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}M^{T}B^{-1}b+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{c}\right).

Using the earlier identity for the GTD2 orthogonal component,

\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}-\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}(\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}})=\left(G+c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}M^{T}B^{-1}b,

we conclude that

\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}=\left(\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}-\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}(\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}})\right)+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{c}\right).

Finally, letting
c\to\infty
yields the limit

\lim_{c\to\infty}\theta_{\mathrm{RGTD}}=\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}-\Pi_{\cal{N}(G)}(\theta_{\mathrm{GTD2}}),

which shows that the R-GTD solution removes the nullspace component
of the GTD2 solution as the regularization parameter grows.

Multiplying both sides by
