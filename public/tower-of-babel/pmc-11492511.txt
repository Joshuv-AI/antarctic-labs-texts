Associations between the geriatric nutritional risk index and the risk of and mortality associated with chronic kidney disease in older individuals
Authors: Peilin Zou, Yucong Zhang, Liangkai Chen, Man Liu, Hao Nie, Hongyu Gao, Cuntai Zhang, Jinhua Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition is a complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Whether malnutrition, assessed via the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), is associated with the incidence and risk of CKD in older individuals remains unclear.MethodsData from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and the UK Biobank database were used. Older participants over 60 years old with available data for GNRI assessment and CKD diagnosis were enrolled. Logistic regression models and Cox regression models were used to assess associations between the geriatric nutritional risk index and the risk of and mortality associated with CKD.ResultsThis study enrolled 13,162 participants from the NHANES and 66,326 participants from the UK Biobank. We identified 6,135 and 16,662 CKD patients in the NHANES and UK Biobank, respectively, with the majority being male (74% in the NHANES and 52% in the UK Biobank). The average age of CKD patients was 72.3 (SD 7.2) years in the NHANES and 64.9 (SD 2.9) years in the UK Biobank. The median follow-up times of older CKD patients were 81 months and 162 months in the NHANES and UK Biobank, respectively. According to the cross-sectional analysis, individuals with a lower GNRI had an increased likelihood of having CKD, with odds ratios of 1.38 (95% CI: 1.05–1.80, P = 0.020) in the NHANES and 2.35 (95% CI: 1.89–2.92, P < 0.001) in the UK Biobank. According to our analysis of the risk of incident CKD in the UK Biobank, a lower GNRI was associated with a greater incidence of CKD (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.04–1.18; P = 0.002). According to the analysis of the risk of mortality, a lower GNRI was associated with an increased risk of death among older CKD patients (NHANES: HR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.13–2.53, P = 0.011; UK Biobank: HR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.94–2.69, P < 0.001).ConclusionMalnutrition assessed by the GNRI was significantly and independently associated with the incidence of CKD. Moreover, CKD patients with malnutrition also have a high risk of mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20283-5.

Introduction

The incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is rapidly increasing worldwide. This may lead to cardiovascular disease (CVD), the need for dialysis or kidney transplantation, or even death. Notably, the risk of CKD increases with age. Owing to the progressive aging of the population worldwide, CKD will be the 5th most common cause of mortality by 2040 and the second leading cause of mortality by the end of this century in aging countries [1]. The identification of risk factors for the development and progression of CKD is highly important for its prevention and management.

Malnutrition is highly prevalent in older individuals, even in developed countries [2]. Malnutrition in older individuals can lead to complex adverse outcomes, including mood changes, muscle wasting, osteoporosis, decreased immunocompetence [3], frailty [4], decreased quality of life, cognitive impairment [5], and even premature mortality [6]. Several chronic diseases are exacerbated by malnutrition [4, 5], which is also associated with high health-care costs [7–9].

Malnutrition is a main complication of CKD and may further promote the progression of CKD and lead to poor outcomes [10]. Most previous studies have focused on the assessment, prevention and management of malnutrition in CKD patients, especially in Asian populations [11]. Limited evidence has been established regarding the associations between malnutrition and the incidence and risk of CKD. Currently, several screening tools, such as the Nutritional Risk Screening-2002 [12], Malnutrition Screening Tool [13], Mini Nutritional Assessment and Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria [14], are used for nutritional status assessment in clinical settings. Some tools require brief patient interviews, which depend on the patient’s ability to provide accurate data. However, it can be troublesome for older individuals, especially those who suffer from cognitive impairment [10]. In addition, the GLIM criteria even require modern techniques for body composition measurement, which further limits their use for large-sample screening of malnutrition in older community-dwelling individuals [15].

The geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) is an objective technique for assessing malnutrition on the basis of simple indications such as the serum albumin level and the ratio of actual to ideal body weight. It can be used for the prognosis estimation of older in-hospital patients [16]. Among the several available nutrition screening instruments, the GNRI is believed to be the most straightforward and reliable for detecting patients who are malnourished [17, 18]. In the present study, we investigated the associations between malnutrition, which was assessed by using the GNRI, and the risk of CKD in older individuals by independently using data from two large national cohorts. The association between malnutrition and the risk of mortality in older CKD patients was also analysed.

Method

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the associations between the GNRI and the incidence and mortality outcomes of CKD by utilizing data from two significant sources: the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the UK Biobank study.

The NHANES is characterized as a continuous, cross-sectional survey that captures a broad spectrum of information from a representative cohort of U.S. individuals, a noninstitutionalized population. This survey was implemented biennially, initiating with household interviews before the participants were invited to a mobile examination center (MEC). At these centers, participants undergo a series of diagnostic tests and provide blood samples for further examination. Given that the participants contributed to the data collection, in addition to death data, only once between 1999 and 2023, the NHANES data were restricted to cross-sectional analyses regarding the association between the GNRI and the likelihood of having CKD. Additionally, longitudinal analysis was performed to analyse the association between the GNRI score and mortality risk in CKD patients from the NHANES cohort.

The UK Biobank represents an expansive prospective cohort study, initially recruiting over 500,000 individuals from 2006 to 2010. During the enrolment phase, participants completed a touch-screen questionnaire to provide insights into their demographics, lifestyle choices, and historical medical issues; this questionnaire was complemented by the collection of anthropometric data and biological specimens. The methodologies and structural design of the study have been meticulously detailed in prior publications [19]. The data from the UK Biobank were used in all relevant analyses.

Together, these datasets afford a profound opportunity to explore the associations of nutritional risk, kidney disease, and mortality within diverse and large-scale populations, providing researchers with invaluable insights into the epidemiological and clinical impacts of these variables.

Our research comprehensively included subjects from the NHANES, spanning from January 1, 2003, to December 31, 2018, and participants from the UK Biobank with accessible clinical data requisite for the computation of the GNRI and the assessment of CKD status. We included participants over the age of 60, excluded subjects with incomplete data regarding the GNRI and CKD-related variables, and further excluded subjects without mortality details for the analysis of mortality. For the longitudinal analysis concerning the incidence of CKD within the UK Biobank cohort, we included individuals without CKD at baseline. To analyse mortality risk among CKD patients, our subset selection was confined to individuals with baseline CKD.

The Ethics Review Board of the U.S. National Center for Health Statistics provided ethical clearance for the NHANES. All the subjects provided written informed consent during the recruitment phase. Similarly, the UK Biobank obtained authorization from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was likewise obtained from every participant. Data access for this research endeavor was solicited through a tailored application to the UK Biobank (application number 63454). Our study constituted a secondary data review of preexisting, publicly available datasets, thereby circumventing the requirement for distinct ethical approval.

GNRI evaluation and grouping

As a modified tool, the GNRI is considered the simplest method for assessing nutritional risk. It is calculated on the basis of three parameters: height, body weight, and serum ALB levels [16]. The detailed calculation of the GNRI is provided in the supplementary file. In our study, the participants were divided into two groups, the high-GNRI group (GNRI ≥ 98) and the low-GNRI group (GNRI < 98), according to a previous study [20].

Ascertainment of outcomes

Ascertainment of CKD

CKD was classified into five stages according to the KDIGO guidelines on the basis of an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min and/or evidence of renal impairment, which was defined as the presence of albuminuria (urinary albumin‒creatinine ratio [ACR] ≥ 30 mg/g) [21]. The detailed methods for calculating the eGFR and urinary ACR are provided in the supplementary file.

In our study, individuals with stage 1–5 CKD were considered CKD patients, which also included those receiving dialysis or kidney transplantation. In addition, in the UK biobank cohort, individuals were also considered to have CKD according to inpatient hospital data on the basis of ICD-10 codes and OPCS-4 codes (Supplementary Table 1).

Death data

The mortality outcomes of NHANES patients were determined on the basis of records in the U.S. National Death Index. Death data from the UK biobank were obtained from the NHS Central Register (for Scotland) and the National Health Service (NHS) Information Centre (for England and Wales).

Covariates

Covariates, including age (continuous), sex (male or female), race (white or nonwhite), BMI (≤ 25 or > 25), educational level (higher education or not), alcohol intake (never or ever), smoking status (never or ever), diabetes status (yes or no), hypertension status (yes or no), blood biochemical indicators—triglyceride (continuous, mg/dl), serum creatinine (continuous, mg/dl) and total serum cholesterol (continuous, mg/dl)—and economic status, were assessed at baseline. In the NHANES cohort, economic status was assessed on the basis of the ratio of family income to poverty. In the UK Biobank, economic status was assessed by the Townsend deprivation index [22]. In the mortality analysis, we added the number of comorbidities as a covariate. The detailed definitions of long-term comorbidities are shown in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Statistical analysis

In the current study, we performed a complete analysis in each model and therefore excluded participants who had missing values for any covariates of interest. On the basis of the characteristics of the databases, we performed a cross-sectional analysis of the association between the GNRI and the likelihood of having CKD in older individuals and a longitudinal analysis of the association between the GNRI and the mortality risk of older CKD patients via both NHANES and UK Biobank data. However, the longitudinal analysis regarding the association between the GNRI and the risk of incident CKD in older individuals was performed only by using the UK Biobank.

In the UK Biobank and NHANES, baseline characteristics were given for participants with and without CKD. Continuous variables are shown as the means (plus standard deviations [SDs]). Categorical variables are shown as absolute values with corresponding percentages. We conducted our analysis considering the sampling weights and complicated sampling design in accordance with the NHANES analytical criteria. Three models were considered in all analyses. Model 1 was a crude model without adjusting for confounding factors. Model 2 was adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. Model 3 was adjusted for all covariates.

In the cross-sectional analysis of the association between the GNRI and CKD incidence, logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) (and associated 95% CIs). In the prospective analysis, Cox regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% CIs for the associations between the GNRI and CKD and all-cause mortality. Survival analysis began at baseline (2003 in the NHANES, 2006–2010 in the UK Biobank). The dose‒response relationships between the GNRI and the risk of CKD and all-cause mortality were assessed via restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with full adjustment for confounders.

Subgroup analysis was performed for each confounding factor to assess the robustness of the results.

All the statistical analyses were performed by using R 4.3.1. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline

Among the 80,312 participants recruited to the NHANES from 2003 to 2018, data for CKD status were missing for 30,012 (37.4%) participants, and data for the GNRI were missing for 576 (0.7%) participants. Moreover, we selected participants over 60 years old. As a result, we included 13,162 participants from the NHANES in our cross-sectional analysis, 6135 of whom were considered to suffer from CKD. A total of 617 (39%) malnourished patients suffered from stage III-V CKD. The mean age of the CKD patients was 72.3 years (SD 7.2), whereas that of the non-CKD participants was 68.7 years (SD 6.8). Among the population affected by CKD, 4,524 (74%) were male, and among the non-CKD participants, 2,067 (29%) were male. For survival analysis of CKD patients, we excluded an additional 7034 (8.8%) individuals without available death information, resulting in a sample of 6128 NHANES CKD patients.

Among the 502,371 participants in the UK Biobank, 66,326 participants were over 60 years old, with available information on the GNRI and CKD (Fig. 1). A total of 226 (20%) malnourished patients suffered from stage III-V CKD. The mean age of the CKD patients in the UK Biobank included in our cross-sectional analysis was 64.9 years (SD 2.9), and that of the non-CKD participants was 64.3 years (SD 2.9). There were 8,595 (52%) males with CKD and 26,493 (53%) in the non-CKD population. For the CKD incidence analysis, we selected 49,664 people without CKD at baseline, while 16,662 CKD patients were included in the mortality analysis. During more than 16 years of follow-up, 4012 incident cases of CKD were documented. At baseline, in both studies, compared with participants without CKD, participants with CKD were typically older, more likely to be male, and had lower educational backgrounds. Additionally, they presented higher levels of triglycerides and serum creatinine, along with lower total serum cholesterol. People with CKD also had a greater Townsend deprivation index and a lower ratio of family income to poverty and were more likely to have hypertension and diabetes (Supplementary Table S4 and S5).

Cross-sectional analysis of the likelihood of having CKD

In investigating the association between the GNRI score and the presence of CKD, RCS analysis revealed a statistically significant association between a lower GNRI (< 98) and an increased likelihood of having CKD (Fig. 2; NHANES: P-overall = 0.022, P-nonlinearity = 0.995; UK Biobank: P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinearity < 0.001). A cross-sectional assessment of the NHANES cohort indicated that individuals with a GNRI less than 98 experienced a 38% increase in the likelihood of having CKD (P = 0.02) after adjusting for potential confounders (Table 1). Moreover, in the analysis of cross-sectional data from the UK Biobank, it was observed that subjects exhibiting a lower GNRI demonstrated a substantially greater likelihood of having CKD (Table 1, OR: 2.35, 95% CI: 1.89–2.92, P < 0.001).

Prospective analysis of the incidence of CKD

In a prospective analysis of the UK Biobank cohort, a dose–response function revealed a statistically significant linear correlation between the GNRI score and the risk of incident CKD (Fig. 3, P overall = 0.001, P nonlinearity = 0.198). According to the regression analysis, a GNRI greater than 98 was not significantly associated with the risk of incident CKD (Table 2, HR: 1.35, 95% CI: 0.93–1.96, p = 0.11). A GNRI greater than 108, for which the threshold was chosen according to RCS analysis, was significantly associated with the risk of incident CKD (Table 2, HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.04–1.18, p = 0.002). According to the subgroup analysis, the associations between the GNRI score and the risk of incident CKD remained significant in the following subgroups: male sex, aged < 65 years, white race, smoking status, alcohol status, low education status, nondiabetic status, hypertension status, BMI > 25 kg/m2, triglycerides > 150 mg/dL, normal serum creatinine (males ≤ 1.2, females ≤ 1.1) and serum total cholesterol > 200 mg/dL or ≤ 200 mg/dL (Supplementary Table S7).

Mortality analysis

RCS analysis revealed a marked nonlinear association between the GNRI and all-cause mortality in the UK Biobank (Fig. 4, P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinearity = 0.021). Within the dataset of the UK Biobank, a GNRI less than 98 was significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality among CKD patients (Table 3, HR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.94–2.69, p < 0.001). According to the subgroup analysis, the associations remained significant in most subgroups except for the non-White race subgroup and the noncomorbidity subgroup.

Similarly, RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear association between GNRI scores and all-cause mortality in the NHANES (Fig. 4, P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinearity = 0.031). Within the NHANES dataset, a GNRI less than 98 was significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality among CKD patients (Table 3, HR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.13–2.53, p = 0.011). According to the subgroup analysis, there were significant relationships between a low GNRI and death in the following subgroups: male, aged ≥ 65 years, nonwhite, smoking, any level of education, ever or never consuming alcohol, diabetes or hypertension, poor economic conditions, BMI > 25 kg/m2, triglyceride > 150 mg/dL or triglyceride ≤ 150 mg/dL, abnormal serum creatinine (male > 1.2, female > 1.1), serum total cholesterol ≤ 200 mg/dL, and no comorbidity or over 1 (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

The current investigation examined the relationship between the GNRI and the risk of CKD via cross-sectional and prospective analyses of data from the UK Biobank and NHANES cohorts. Although there are differences in age distribution, sex distribution, race distribution, education level, and lifestyle between the participants from the NHANES and the UK biobank, our study still indicated that high nutritional risk in elderly individuals was significantly associated with the risk of CKD in these two cohorts. Moreover, a high nutritional risk in CKD patients was associated with a high risk of mortality. Overall, our study demonstrated that high nutritional risk could increase the risk of both the onset and progression of CKD.

CKD is a serious public health problem globally. Furthermore, owing to the irreversible nature of CKD, the identification of modifiable risk factors is key for preventing its development and progression, which is highly important for reducing the burden on society caused by CKD. However, the risk factors, especially modifiable risk factors, for CKD remain unclear.

Malnutrition is highly prevalent in community-dwelling elderly individuals and has also been recognized as a common complication of CKD due to the sustained leakage of protein and other pathophysiological mechanisms in CKD [11]. Malnutrition, including dialysis, kidney transplantation, cardiovascular events and even mortality, is also a risk factor for poor outcomes in CKD patients. Unfortunately, there is limited evidence regarding the association between malnutrition and the incidence or risk of CKD. A cross-sectional study of 9897 older (≥ 60 years old) individuals with hypertension revealed that malnutrition is associated with a greater incidence of CKD [23]. However, such associations in community-dwelling elderly individuals and the potential causal relationships should be further validated. Moreover, some studies have explored the association between the GNRI and CKD in older populations [10, 24], but these studies focused only on the outcomes of CKD patients. However, our study also reported an association between a lower GNRI and an increased risk of incident CKD in non-CKD individuals. In addition, our study provided additional evidence supporting the association between the GNRI and the outcome of CKD by using data from the NHANES and the UK Biobank, which included different individuals from those studies.

The GNRI is calculated on the basis of the serum albumin level and body weight, which are two key diagnostic criteria for protein energy wasting in CKD patients [25]. In our study, we enrolled 13,162 and 66,326 older participants from the NHANES cohort and the UK Biobank cohort, respectively, for cross-sectional analysis. Although the GNRI was developed on the basis of in-hospital patients, we found that malnutrition determined by the GNRI was significantly associated with a high likelihood of having CKD in general individuals ≥ 60 years old after full adjustment for all covariates. We also found that some malnourished individuals may suffer from severe CKD (Stages III–V). However, further studies are still needed to identify the causal association between malnutrition and CKD.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to assess the causal association between malnutrition and the risk of incident CKD in non-CKD individuals. On the basis of the data from 49,664 individuals without CKD at baseline, we first found that malnutrition determined by the GNRI, with a threshold of 108, was an independent risk factor for incident CKD. Therefore, assessing the nutritional status of older individuals may help identify the risk of incident CKD, and improving nutritional status may help reduce the incidence of CKD. Notably, the association between a decreased GNRI and an increased risk of incident CKD was not significant when the GNRI threshold was 98, which was the recommended threshold for the ascertainment of malnutrition in a previous study. The GNRI threshold for the prediction of CKD risk should be further investigated and validated. However, both cross-sectional analyses of the two databases and prospective analysis of the UK Biobank demonstrated that a lower GNRI was associated with a higher risk of CKD and an increased mortality risk among CKD patients. In addition, according to the results of the subgroup analysis, such associations remained significant in only some of the subgroups. However, the trends of the relevant results were consistent. Most of the ORs in the subgroup analysis were greater than 1. Larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to verify the association between a decreased GNRI score and an increased risk of incident CKD in the subgroup population.

Similar to previous studies [11], our study also demonstrated that a decreased GNRI was independently associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in CKD patients older than 60 years, both in the NHANES cohort and the UK Biobank cohort, which was independent of the number of long-term comorbidities, including cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, heart diseases, and metabolic disorders. Thus, monitoring the nutritional status of elderly CKD patients might help prevent death. Moreover, this association remained significant in most subgroup analyses of the UK Biobank cohort. The differences in the results among subgroups and cohorts highlight the need for further validation in specific subpopulations. Notably, previous studies were conducted mainly on Asian dialysis patients with limited sample sizes (hundreds to more than a thousand participants). Our study first enrolled 6128 general CKD patients from the NHANES cohort and 16,662 general CKD patients from the UK Biobank cohort from the United States and the United Kingdom, respectively.

A previous study indicated that both hypoalbuminemia and low body weight are signs of malnutrition. Both hypoalbuminemia and a lower BMI could be important indicators of protein-energy wasting [26]. Epidemiological studies have previously demonstrated a significant association between lower serum albumin levels and increased renal insufficiency risk [27, 28]. Moreover, serum ALB has anti-inflammatory [29] and antioxidant effects [30]. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [31] are closely related to the development and progression of CKD [32]. Moreover, weight change plays an essential role in CKD risk. Both underweight and overweight individuals can increase the risk of CKD [33, 34]. Overall, the GNRI may reliably determine an older patient’s nutritional status when paired with anthropometric and blood biochemical markers. These findings may help nutritional management prevent CKD and related cardiovascular diseases in older individuals.

The long survey and follow-up periods and large sample size are the main strengths of our study. Several limitations still need to be noted. First, although some confounding variables were controlled or adjusted, residual confounders or unmeasured confounders caused by measurement errors may still introduce bias to the results. Second, we only assessed nutritional status via the GNRI at baseline, and the time-varying changes in nutritional status were not investigated. Furthermore, the HRs we reported should be regarded with caution since they were computed using a single timepoint throughout the follow-up period, and the period-specific HRs were influenced by selection bias. Third, in the NHANES cohort, CKD was ascertained on the basis of the eGFR and ACR, and a history of CKD diagnosis and treatment was unavailable, which may introduce bias in the determination of CKD. In addition, in the UK Biobank cohort, incident CKD during the follow-up period was diagnosed on the basis of hospital inpatient records; thus, latent incident CKD patients who did not visit the hospital owing to less severe clinical symptoms may be excluded. Information regarding the treatment of older CKD patients was not available in either the NHANES or the UK Biobank cohort, which may introduce bias in the analysis of mortality risk. Fourth, the sample sizes of some subgroups were limited. Further studies are needed to establish whether our results can be generalized to other subpopulations. Fifth, owing to the observational nature of our study, direct causal associations between a decreased GNRI score and the development and progression of CKD could not be established. Sixth, for easier ascertainment of the nutritional status of the participants, the GNRI was dichotomized, which may reduce the statistical power and increase residual confounding. Moreover, a potential limitation of this study is the age range of individuals in the UK Biobank cohort, which includes volunteers aged between 40 and 69. Given that the focus of the analysis is on older individuals, the absence of data from individuals aged 70 and above introduces a potential bias. This age group is particularly relevant for studies on aging and chronic conditions such as CKD, as the prevalence of both malnutrition and CKD increases with age. Therefore, the findings of this study may not fully represent the older population at risk of CKD. Future research should aim to include participants over 70 years of age to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between the GNRI and CKD in geriatric populations. Finally, the GNRI was initially developed to measure the nutritional status of in-hospital patients, but in our study, we used the GNRI to assess the nutritional status of the general population to determine the associations between malnutrition and the prevalence and risk of CKD. Our study demonstrated the potential ability of the GNRI to predict the risk of CKD in the general population, which needs to be further validated.

Conclusion

In this large multicenter study, malnutrition assessed by using the GNRI was significantly and independently associated with the incidence of CKD. Moreover, CKD patients with malnutrition also have a high risk of mortality. The assessment and management of malnutrition may help prevent and manage the development and progression of CKD. Future studies are needed to investigate direct causal inference and the role of malnutrition management in the prevention of CKD.
