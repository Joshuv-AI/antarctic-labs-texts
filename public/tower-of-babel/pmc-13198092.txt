From Complete Heart Block to Torsades de Pointes
Authors: Vasiliki-Chara Mystakidi, Panteleimon Pantelidis, Andreas Gerostathis, Polychronis E. Dilaveris, Gerasimos Siasos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplete atrioventricular block is a recognized cause of acquired QT prolongation and Torsades de Pointes.Case SummaryAn 80-year-old woman with no known prior cardiac history presented to the emergency department with acute confusion and altered mental status. There was no reported history of syncope, palpitations or structural heart disease. Baseline electrocardiography demonstrated complete atrioventricular block with a stable junctional escape rhythm and marked corrected QT prolongation (QTc 545 ms). Continuous electrocardiography monitoring revealed sudden-onset polymorphic ventricular tachycardia consistent with Torsades de Pointes.Take-Home MessagesCardiac memory–induced repolarization abnormalities can trigger Torsades de Pointes in complete complete atrioventricular block despite stable RR intervals. Sudden changes in QRS morphology and axis due to alteration of the escape rhythm during atrioventricular block with persistent T-wave orientation represent an important electrocardiographic warning sign (cardiac memory) because they may lead to further prolongation of the QTc interval and the onset of Torsades de Pointes.

Answer

Correct Answer: C. Cardiac memory–induced repolarization abnormality following abrupt axis change.

Careful inspection of the initiating beats demonstrated a striking change in QRS axis between the fourth and fifth escape complexes. While the RR interval remained constant, the QT interval following the fifth beat was markedly prolonged (QTc up to 545 ms), immediately preceding Torsades de Pointes (TdP) onset (Figure 1).Figure 1Patient Electrocardiography Demonstrates Complete Atrioventricular Block With a Stable Junctional Escape Rhythm, Marked Corrected QT Prolongation, and Episode of Torsades de Pointes

The first 4 QRS complexes showed left-axis deviation, followed by an abrupt shift to a normal axis, likely reflecting a transient change in the junctional pacemaker focus within the atrioventricular (AV) junction. Despite this shift in depolarization, the T-wave axis remained unchanged, consistent with cardiac memory, defines as the persistence of a T-wave vector reflecting prior abnormal ventricular activation after restoration or alternation of conduction, where repolarization patterns lag behind changes in QRS morphology. This dissociation between depolarization and repolarization vectors is characteristic of cardiac memory. During cardiac memory, downregulation of repolarizing potassium currents reduces repolarization reserve and increases spatial dispersion of repolarization, increasing left ventricular repolarization gradients and decreasing repolarization reserve.1 The sudden alteration in depolarization was followed by exaggerated repolarization delay, creating a vulnerable substrate for triggered activity and TdP in the setting of profound bradycardia and female sex. The persistence of the exaggerated repolarization delay on subsequent beats even though the axis is similar to the first 3 complexes likely reflects abnormal restitution of the repolarizing potassium currents which increases left ventricular repolarization gradients and diminishes the repolarization reserve.

Discussion and Rationale

Bradycardia due to complete AV block is a well-established cause of acquired QT prolongation and TdP. Established mechanisms linking chronic AV block and bradycardia with QT prolongation and TdP include reduced repolarization reserve from potassium current downregulation, augmentation of late sodium current prolonging action potential duration, and increased spatial dispersion of repolarization facilitating early afterdepolarization.2, 3, 4

In this case, however, cardiac memory represents the most plausible explanation based on the electrocardiography morphology. Alternative or concomitant mechanisms—including regional repolarization heterogeneity, subtle pause-dependent influences, or autonomic modulation—cannot be fully excluded. Instead, TdP followed an abrupt change in QRS axis with persistence of the T-wave axis, a hallmark of cardiac memory.5 The patient already had a prolonged QT interval, but the cardiac memory event led to a frank long QT and QT-related ventricular arrythmia.

Cardiac memory reflects altered repolarization following changes in ventricular activation and is thought to involve disrupted regulation of repolarizing potassium currents. The resulting exaggerated QT prolongation created a substrate for early afterdepolarizations and TdP. Female sex further increased vulnerability.

Ischemia (A) and catecholamine excess (D) were unsupported clinically. Electrolyte disturbance (E) was not documented. Option B is incomplete because classic pause-dependent early afterdepolarizations require RR prolongation, which was absent, and the trigger was temporally linked to QRS morphology change rather than the pause duration.

Recognition of extreme QT prolongation, polymorphic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, and electrocardiography evidence of cardiac memory should prompt urgent intervention in patients with an AV block.
