Nudix hydrolase 15 deficiency and azathioprine toxicity in a liver transplant recipient: A case report
Authors: Jenny Q. Nguyen, Natalie Rodriguez, Kimberly A. Chapman, Matthew A. Deardorff, Keith Z. Hazleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThiopurines, such as azathioprine (AZA), are among the immunosuppressants used to prevent allograft rejection. Thiopurines are metabolized by thiopurine S‐methyltransferase (TPMT) and nudix hydrolase 15 (NUDT15) enzymes, and their functions are highly influenced by genetic variation. We present a 6‐year‐old female with recurrent acute cellular rejection and steroid‐induced glaucoma due to high‐dose corticosteroid therapy. Consequently, AZA was incorporated into her calcineurin‐inhibitor‐based regimen to reduce steroid dose. Within 33 days of initiating AZA, alopecia and pancytopenia developed despite confirmed TPMT enzyme activity with normal phenotype and low 6‐Thioguanine and 6‐Methylmercaptopurine levels in the blood. Pharmacogenomic analysis revealed an NUDT15 *3/*3 genotype, indicating two “no function” alleles, classified as NUDT15 poor metabolizer at high risk of myelosuppression with standard thiopurine doses. Liver donor pharmacogenomic testing was not performed. This case highlights the consideration of pharmacogenomic testing for recipient and donor prior to thiopurine use in liver‐transplant recipients.

INTRODUCTION

Post‐transplant immunosuppression is crucial for the preservation of a transplanted organ. Multiple immunosuppressants exist, each with varied adverse effects: for example, tacrolimus: renal and neurotoxicity; thiopurines: hepatotoxicity and bone marrow suppression; prednisone: metabolic side effects. 1

Relevant to this, thiopurine S‐methyltransferase (TPMT) deactivates thiopurine metabolites, and nudix hydrolase 15 (NUDT15) detoxifies cytotoxic thioguanine triphosphate to thioguanine monophosphate. 2 Loss‐of‐function variants in TPMT and NUDT15 decrease enzyme function and lead to increased myelosuppression risk at standard doses. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guideline informs TPMT and NUDT15 genotype‐guided recommendations for thiopurines. 2 TPMT and NUDT15 testing are not routinely performed prior to thiopurine initiation for liver transplant patients. This case reports a pediatric liver transplant patient with azathioprine (AZA)‐induced severe myelosuppression and alopecia found to be a NUDT15 poor metabolizer.

CASE REPORT

A 6‐year‐old female with a deceased‐donor left lateral segment liver transplant at age 4 due to biliary atresia presented with acute‐onset alopecia and pancytopenia in the setting of recent AZA initiation. Her post‐transplant course was notable for acute cellular rejection (ACR) at 21 and 26 months post‐transplantation, with each episode requiring high‐dose corticosteroids, causing glaucoma necessitating tube shunt implants.

Consequently, following her second ACR episode, AZA was added as a third immunosuppressant alongside tacrolimus and maintenance corticosteroids. This approach aimed to reduce the corticosteroid dosage. A complete blood count (CBC) confirmed normal cell line levels prior to initiating AZA. TPMT activity indicated a normal phenotype (Table 1), and CBC results remained normal and stable while on AZA dosed at 0.5 mg/kg/day (Figure 1). Therefore, after approximately 2 weeks since initiation, the dose was adjusted to the full therapeutic dose of 1 mg/kg/day. One week following the dosage increase, outpatient monitoring lab results revealed mild leukopenia, neutropenia, and anemia. Subsequent outpatient labs within 4 days demonstrated that leukopenia, neutropenia, and anemia had progressed to severe levels despite AZA discontinuation, prompting the patient's referral to the emergency department. There, she was found to have acute‐onset alopecia, symptomatic anemia, and febrile neutropenia, which led to her hospitalization.

Upon admission, AZA and marrow‐suppressing medications (sulfamethoxazole trimethoprim, valganciclovir) were held. The hematology consult team suspected AZA‐induced bone marrow suppression. Notably, thiopurine metabolites, 6‐thioguanine and 6‐methylmercaptopurine, levels were low (Table 1). Subsequently, NUDT15 genotyping of the recipient revealed NUDT15 homozygous c.55_56insGAGTCG and c.415C>T (previously denoted *2, since updated to be a sub‐allele for *3), 3 indicative of two “no function” alleles and phenotypically classified as NUDT15 poor metabolizer (Table 1). This confers a high risk of myelosuppression with standard thiopurine doses. TMPT and NUDT15 genetic testing were not performed on her donor. The patient required red blood cell and platelet transfusions during her hospitalization. Bone marrow recovery and alopecia improvement were noted approximately 40 days after AZA discontinuation (Figure 1).

DISCUSSION

While several sites evaluate TPMT activity prior to thiopurine administration in liver‐transplant patients. However, NUDT15 genotyping is not standard practice. Currently, there is no validated phenotypic assay (e.g., enzymatic activity) for NUDT15 in routine clinical practice; therefore, genetic testing remains the sole method. 2 Testing options range from targeted genotyping assays to broader sequencing techniques. 2 The 2022 Association for Molecular Pathology TPMT and NUDT15 genotyping recommendation guideline outlines the key alleles for clinical testing, including NUDT15*3 (Tier 1) and *4, *6, *9, *14 (Tier 2)—the most prevalent nonfunctional variants. 4 Numerous clinical laboratories provide combined TPMT/NUDT15 genotyping panels that assess the common nonfunctional alleles; therefore, testing is more efficient with the combined panel for both genes. NUDT15 no‐function variants have been reported in higher frequencies in Asian, Native American, and Hispanic populations. 2 Despite it being in higher frequencies in certain populations, every patient should be tested regardless of race/ethnicity, as there is a risk of a no‐function variant. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration drug label for AZA calls for testing of both TPMT and NUDT15 and does not specify testing in certain groups. 5

Our patient is of Hispanic descent, a population with a higher prevalence of NUDT15 loss‐of‐function variations compared to TPMT, 6 and was found to have two “no function” alleles denoted *3 (Table 1: homozygous c.55_56insGAGTCG and c.415C>T), which correlated with her toxicity caused by standard AZA dosing. We did not have pharmacogenomic results of the liver donor, but it is presumed that TPMT and NUDT15 were normal given the low prevalence in the general population (0.03%–0.3% for TPMT poor metabolizers, and 0.1%–0.7% for NUDT15 poor metabolizers 2 ). Donor's TPMT results would be relevant to understand the effect of first‐pass metabolism of thiopurines by TPMT enzymes. 7 TPMT deficiency would shunt the hepatic metabolic pathway toward conversion of the active metabolite, 6‐thioguanine nucleotides, further increasing the risk of myelosuppression. 7 Considering the likely non‐concordance in NUDT15 results, we suspect the non‐hepatic NUDT15 expression directly affected her bone marrow and hair follicles. Considering thiopurines' deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) synthesis inhibition, cell apoptosis can lead to a severe reaction despite her likely having normal hepatic expression. 8 However, more in‐depth studies are needed to confirm this.

The CPIC guideline for TPMT, NUDT15, and thiopurines does not explicitly specify how to consider both recipient and donor results in dosing recommendations for liver transplant patients. 1 However, the CPIC guideline for tacrolimus and CYP3A5 indicates that both recipient and donor genotypes may influence dosing and offers genotype‐guided recommendations when the genotypes match, but the guideline is inconclusive on how to adjust tacrolimus dosing for non‐concordance results. 9 Based on this guideline, a similar approach could be applied to thiopurine dosing if results match. Nonetheless, if non‐concordance is suspected, as in our case, limited guidance is available. To prevent similar situations in the future, institutions could consider TPMT and NUDT15 testing prior to starting AZA and, if possible, genotyping the donor. However, further investigations are needed to better understand the clinical impact and application of each result to develop a stronger dosing guideline in this patient population.

Thiopurine‐induced side effects from TPMT and NUDT15 deficiency have been reported several times but mostly in inflammatory bowel disease or leukemia. 3 , 10 , 11 To our knowledge, this is the first case report that describes the impact of NUDT15 deficiency with AZA in a liver transplant recipient.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the importance of recipient's non‐hepatic TPMT and NUDT15 expression influencing the risk of thiopurine toxicity when hepatic metabolism is suspected to be normal. Secondly, this showcases the importance of testing for both TPMT and NUDT15 prior to thiopurine administration, ideally for both recipient and donor. Liver transplant recipients are commonly excluded from pharmacogenomic testing, but this case shows the important impact of NUDT15 in this patient population. Furthermore, the case shows the importance of testing NUDT15 in Hispanic populations and not just TPMT alone.
