On the Feinberg-Piunovskiy Theorem and its extension to chattering policies Supported by grant PID2021-122442NB-I00 from the Spanish Ministerio de Ciencia e Innovación.
François Dufour, Tomás Prieto-Rumeau

Abstract
The Feinberg-Piunovskiy Theorem established in [ 14 , Theorem 3.8] asserts that for a discrete-time uniformly absorbing and atomless Markov Decision Process (MDP) with Borel state space and multiple criteria, the family of deterministic stationary policies is a sufficient class of policies. In this paper, we study some related problems and some extensions. In particular dropping the atomless hypothesis, we establish that the set of chattering stationary policies is a sufficient class of policies for uniformly absorbing MDPs with measurable state space and multiple criteria. We also prove the Feinberg-Piunovskiy Theorem in the context of a measurable state space in two different ways that differ from [ 14 ] . In particular, we show that the sufficiency of chattering stationary policies directly yields the sufficiency of deterministics stationary policies for atomless models. Our approach is partially based on the analysis of extreme points of certain convex sets of occupation measures satisfying integral type constraints. We show that for a uniformly absorbing model an extreme point of such sets is necessarily given by occupation measures induced by chattering stationary policies of order d + 1 d+1 where d d is the dimension of the vector of constraints. When in addition the model 𝖬 \mathsf{M} is atomless, then the extreme points of this constrained set of occupation measures are precisely the occupation measures generated by deterministic stationary policies satisfying these constraints.



Keywords:
Markov decision processes; absorbing model; extreme points of occupation measures, chattering policies, deterministic policies, atomless model, sufficiency of families of policies

Introduction

In this paper, we consider a discrete-time absorbing Markov Decision Process (MDP) denoted by
\mathsf{M}
and defined on a measurable state space
\mathbf{X}
and a Borel action space
\mathbf{A}
, with a fixed initial distribution.
We adopt a general measurable framework for the state space in order to encompass models commonly considered in game theory.
In particular, non-zero sum games have been recently studied in the context of constraints by using the notion of occupation measure, a concept we study here in details.
The set of pairs of state and feasible action denoted by
\mathbf{K}
satisfies some technical measurability properties and throughout the paper, the hypotheses on
\mathsf{M}
will be strengthened
by also considering (uniformly) absorbing as well as atomless models.
The set of all policies will be denoted by
\mathbf{\Pi}
and we will also consider two specific families of policies: the sets of deterministic stationary policies
\mathbb{D}
and chattering stationary policies
\mathbb{C}_{n}

of order
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
that is stationary policies given by a randomization (or, in other words, a mixture) between at most
n
deterministic stationary policies (see Definition
2.2

for a mathematical description of these policies).
We have chosen to use the term of chattering policy which is borrowed from optimal control theory. Readers are referred to the discussion at the top of page 25 in
[
3
]
and the references therein for a discussion of this topic, as well as

[
13
]
that used it in the context of MDPs.

For a measurable subset
\Delta\subset\mathbf{X}
referred to as the absorbing set, a
\Delta
-absorbing MDP is characterized by the fact that, once the state process
enters
\Delta
, it remains there indefinitely generating no further reward or cost.
A standard assumption is that the expected entrance time of the state process to
\Delta
is finite under any admissible policy.
The notion of a uniformly absorbing MDP was introduced in
[
14
, Definition 3.6]
) and means roughly speaking that the series of the queues of the hitting time of
\Delta

go to zero uniformly over the set of policies.
For comprehensive discussions as well as recent results on (uniformly) absorbing MDPs, we refer the reader to
[
2
,
10
,
11
,
14
,
17
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
25
]

and references therein.
An MDP is called atomless if the initial distribution of the state process and the transition probabilities are atomless.

The notion of occupation measure will play a central role in this paper. An occupation measure generated (or induced) by a policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
denoted by
\mu_{\pi}
provides a concise representation
of the expected aggregated behavior of the state and action processes over time.
More precisely,
\mu_{\pi}(\Gamma)
gives the expected time spent by the state and action processes in the measurable subset
\Gamma
of
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}
under the policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
.
We will write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
for the set of occupation measures.

A bounded one-step reward vector-function denoted by
r
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
will be used to evaluate the performance of a policy of the model
\mathsf{M}
. More specifically, the performance vector of a policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
will be given by
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\pi)=\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}}r(x,a)\mu_{\pi}(dx,da).

A subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{\Pi}
will be called a sufficient class (family) of policies if
\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
.

A main, but not the only, purpose of this paper is to characterize the extremes points of sets of occupation measures satisfying
integral type constraints of the form
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}}r(x,a)\mu(dx,da)=\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Only a few results have been established on this subject, mainly dealing with the characterization of the extreme points of the unconstrained set of occupation mesures.
Let us mention
[
2
,
5
,
6
]
for finite or countable state spaces, and
[
17
,
22
]
for Borel state spaces.
If we focus on the literature for Borel MDPs, it has been shown that every extreme (finite) occupation measure is generated by a deterministic stationary strategy
for absorbing models in
[
17
, Lemma 4.6]
and for models having a costless (rewardless) absorbing state in
[
22
, Theorem 1]

(in this latter case, this type of model is more general than an absorbing model, in particular an occupation measure may be infinite).
As far as we know, all these results focus on unconstrained sets of occupation measures.
In the present work, we will establish the following results.

a)

We will first show that extreme points of the set of occupation measures satisfying these integral type constraints are necessarily given by occupation measures induced by chattering stationary policies of order
d+1
satisfying these constraints (where
d
is the dimension of the reward vector-function).
To get this result, we will show in Proposition

that the set of occupation measures
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
is linearly closed and linearly bounded.

b)

If in addition, the model
\mathsf{M}
is atomless, then the extreme points of this constrained set of occupation measures are precisely
the occupation measures generated by deterministic stationary policies satisfying these constraints.

We refer the reader to Theorems
3.4

As far as we know, they are both novel and will play a particularly important role in showing the sufficiency of certain classes of policies as explained now.

The second main objective of this paper is to study for a uniformly absorbing model, the sufficiency of the set of chattering stationary policies and also the sufficiency of the deterministic stationary policies but in assuming in addition that the model is atomless.
There are few results on the sufficiency of chattering policies for MDPs even in the case of a real valued reward function.
To the best of our knowledge, the only results of this type are related to optimization problems, see for example Theorem 2 in

There are more references on the sufficiency of deterministic policies for atomless and uniformly absorbing models

In

In the present paper, we will establish the following results:

1)

Assuming the model
\mathsf{M}
is uniformly absorbing, for any policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
there exists a chattering stationary policy

\gamma\in\mathbb{C}_{d+1}
of order
d+1
having the same performance vector, that is,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}}r(x,a)\mu_{\pi}(dx,da)=\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}}r(x,a)\mu_{\gamma}(dx,da)

or equivalently,
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C}_{d+1})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
, see Theorem
5.6
.
As far as we know, this result is to the best of our knowledge novel even in the case of a real valued reward function.
To get it, we will first show that the set of chattering Markov policies of order
2d+1
is a sufficient class of control strategies
(Theorem
4.2
(a)
).
Combined with the property stated in point
a)
, this yields the following important properties: the set
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C}_{d+1})

is convex and dense in
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
and have also the same relative interior as
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
(Theorem
5.3
(a)
).
The claim is then obtained by showing a technical property of uniformly absorbing models. Roughly speaking, for any policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
, there exists a subset

\mathbf{\Pi}^{*}
of
\mathbf{\Pi}
such that the model associated with
\mathbf{\Pi}^{*}
inherits the properties of
\mathsf{M}

and for which
\mu_{\pi}(r)
is in the relative interior of
r(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
(see Theorem
5.5
for a precise statement of this result).
This result partly builds on a generalization of a theorem established in
[
14
, Theorem 6.3]
under the assumption that the model is atomless, extending it to the setting where this assumption is not imposed and for
a general measurable state space.
All the properties of the set
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C}_{d+1})
we have outlined above appear to be new.

2)

If in addition the model is atomless, then the set of deterministic stationary policies is sufficient:

\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{D})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
, see Theorem
5.7
.
This is precisely the Feinberg-Piunovskiy Theorem in the context of a general state space.
We will propose two different proofs for that result.
One proof is based on Proposition
5.2
that states that
the sufficiency of chattering stationary policies directly yields the sufficiency of deterministic stationary policies
for atomless models by using the characterization of extreme points of sets of occupation measures satisfying integral type constraints.
The second proof of this claim proceeds along the same lines as for item
1)
.
We now highlight the main differences of this second approach with the one given in
[
14
, Theorem 3.8]
.
The same result is obtained here but for more general models since the state space is allowed to be a measurable space, which is a notable first difference.
Indeed, the approach used in
[
14
]
is based on
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
which depends heavily on the Borel structure of the state space
\mathbf{X}
.
This last point was emphasized in
[
20
, Remark 5]
.
To illustrate another important difference, observe that as mentioned in
[
14
]
:

one and nontrivial step is to prove that for an atomless model the set of performance vectors for all deterministic stationary policies is convex.
This fact is nontrivial even for the case of one criterion.

In this regard, it should be emphasized that the authors in
[
14
]
show that the convexity of the set
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{D})
is a consequence of their main result
(see Corollary 3.10 and the discussion above in
[
14
, Page 169]
).
The approach used in
[
14
]
is based on a dimensionality reduction technique as the authors themselves state.
It consists of showing that the set of occupation measures generated by deterministic stationary policies endowed with the setwise topology is path connected
and then proceed by induction on the dimension of the one-step reward vector-function
r
to get the sufficiency of the set of deterministic stationary policies, see section 9 in
[
14
]
.
The proofs of the convexity of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{D})
and the sufficiency of the set of deterministic stationary policies are intertwined through this recurrence.
As for us, the convexity of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{D})
is directly obtained by using the characterization of the extreme points of the constrained sets of occupation measures.
The sufficiency of the family
\mathbb{D}
of policies is then obtained by using the convexity of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{D})
and the fact that this set has the same relative interior of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})

highlighting the differences between our two approaches.
Another important difference is that the notion of
relative interior
of a convex set plays a central role in our work.

(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}},\mu)
be a measure space, a family of subsets
\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}}
of a Borel space
\mathbf{A}
. Consider

g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{0}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and a stochastic kernel
\kappa
on
\mathbf{A}
given
\mathbf{X}
satisfying
\kappa(\mathbf{A}(x)|x)=1
for any
x\in\mathbf{X}
and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}}|g(x,a)|\kappa(da|x)\mu(dx)<+\infty
.
Suppose also that the set
\mathbf{K}=\{(x,a)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}:a\in\mathbf{A}(x)\}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbf{A})
and there exists a function
\theta\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{0}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{X})
whose graph is a subset of
\mathbf{K}
.
Then, there exists a finitely supported kernel
\gamma
on
\mathbf{A}
given
(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})
of order
n+1
such that

(a)

For any
x
in
\mathbf{X}
,
\gamma(\mathbf{A}(x)|x)=1
.

(b)

For
\mu
-almost every
x
in
\mathbf{X}
,
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{A}}g(x,a)\kappa(da|x)=\int_{\mathbf{A}}g(x,a)\gamma(da|x).

2
The Markov control model

The main goal of this section is to introduce the parameters defining the control model with a brief presentation of the construction of the controlled process.
We will present different types of properties for a control model, such as being (uniformly) absorbing and atomless.
We will also introduce the notion of occupation measure, which has been studied in detail in
[
10
,
11
,
22
]
for absorbing models and
which will play a fundamental role in the proof techniques we will use in this paper.

2.1
The model.

A control model
\mathsf{M}
consists of the following parameters.

•

A state space
\mathbf{X}
endowed with a
\sigma
-algebra
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
. The set
\mathbf{X}
will always be endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
unless explicitly stated, in which case the context will be specified.

•

A Borel space
\mathbf{A}
, representing the action space.

•

A family of nonempty measurable sets
\mathbf{A}(x)\subseteq\mathbf{A}
for
x\in\mathbf{X}
. The set
\mathbf{A}(x)
gives the admissible actions in state
x
.
Let
\mathbf{K}=\{(x,a)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}:a\in\mathbf{A}(x)\}
be the family of feasible state-action pairs.

•

A stochastic kernel
Q
on
\mathbf{X}
given
\big(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbf{A})\big)
which stands for the transition probability function.

•

An initial distribution given by
\eta\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{X})
.

•

A bounded reward vector-function
r\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{0}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
for
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
that will be used to evaluate the performance of a control policy.

We often write a model
\mathsf{M}
as
\mathsf{M}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)
to specify the notations used for the parameters of this model.

Definition 2.1
.

We will say that a model
\mathsf{M}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)
satisfies the measurability conditions if the following properties holds.

(M.1)

\mathbf{K}
is a measurable subset of
\big(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbf{A})\big)
.

(M.2)

There exists a function
\theta\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{0}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}})
whose graph is a subset of
\mathbf{K}
.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{D}
the set of functions
\phi\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{0}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}})
satisfying
\phi(x)\in\mathbf{A}(x)
for any
x\in\mathbf{X}
and
by
\mathbb{S}
(respectively,
\mathbb{C}
) the set of (respectively, finitely supported) stochastic kernels
\varphi
on
\mathbf{A}
given
\mathbf{X}
satisfying
\varphi(\mathbf{A}(x)|x)=1

for any
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
We will write
\mathbb{C}_{p}
for the set of finitely supported kernels in
\mathbb{C}
of order
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

Let us consider now an arbitrary but fixed model
\mathsf{M}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)
satisfying the measurability conditions.
The space of admissible histories of the controlled process up to time
n\in\mathbb{N}
is denoted by
\mathbf{H}_{n}
. It is defined recursively by

\mathbf{H}_{0}=\mathbf{X}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathbf{H}_{n}=\mathbf{H}_{n-1}\times\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{X}
for
n\geq 1
, all endowed with their corresponding product
\sigma
-algebras.
A control policy
\pi
is a sequence
\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of stochastic kernels on
\mathbf{A}
given
\mathbf{H}_{n}
, denoted by
\pi_{n}(da|h_{n})
, such that

\pi_{n}(\mathbf{A}(x_{n})|h_{n})=1
for each
n\geq 0
and
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
.
The set of all policies is denoted by
\mathbf{\Pi}
.

Definition 2.2
.

We now present several specific families of policies that will be used in the remainder of this work.

•

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbf{\Pi}
is called a
randomized Markov policy
if there exists a sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{S}
satisfying

\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\gamma_{n}(\cdot|x_{n})
for any
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a
randomized stationary policy
if there exists
\varphi\in\mathbb{S}
satisfying

\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\varphi(\cdot|x_{n})
for any
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By a slight abuse of notation, we will write
\mathbb{S}
for the set of all randomized stationary policies.

•

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a
chattering Markov policy
if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists a finitely supported kernel
\varphi_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\varphi_{n}(\cdot|x_{n})
for any
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a
chattering stationary policy
if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists a finitely supported kernel
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\varphi(\cdot|x_{n})
for
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
A chattering stationary policy defined by
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}
will be said of order
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
if
\varphi
is of order
p
.
By a slight abuse of notation, we will write
\mathbb{C}_{p}
for the set of all chattering stationary policies of order
p
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

•

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbf{\Pi}
is called a
deterministic Markov policy
if there exists a sequence
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{S}
satisfying

\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\delta_{\phi_{n}(x_{n})}(\cdot)
for any
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbf{\Pi}
is called a
deterministic stationary policy
if there exists
\phi\in\mathbb{D}
satisfying

\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\delta_{\phi(x_{n})}(\cdot)
for any
h_{n}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We will identify the set of deterministic stationary policies with the set
\mathbb{D}
. We will therefore use
\mathbb{D}
to designate these two sets.

For each
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, we write

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}(D|x)=\int_{\mathbf{A}}Q(D|x,a)\sigma(da|x)

for
x\in\mathbf{X}
and
D\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
.
The compositions of
Q_{\sigma}
with itself are denoted by
Q^{t}_{\sigma}
for any
t\geq 0
, with the convention that
Q^{0}_{\sigma}(\cdot|x)
is the identity kernel
\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{X}}
on
\mathbf{X}
.
We also write
\displaystyle g_{\sigma}(x)=\int_{\mathbf{A}}g(x,a)\sigma(da|x)
for
x\in\mathbf{X}
and a measurable function
g
from
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbb{R}
provided this integral is well-defined.
For any Markov randomized policy
\pi=(\pi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we write
Q_{\pi}^{(0)}
for the identity kernel on
\mathbf{X}
and

Q_{\pi}^{(t)}
for the product of the transition kernels from step
0
to
t-1
for
t\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, that is,

Q_{\pi}^{(t)}=\begin{cases}Q_{\pi_{0}}Q_{\pi_{1}}\cdots Q_{\pi_{t-1}}&\text{for }t\geq 1,\\
\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{X}}&\text{for }t=0.\end{cases}

The canonical space of all possible sample paths of the state-action process is

\mathbf{\Omega}=(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})^{\infty}
endowed with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
.
The coordinate projection functions from
\mathbf{\Omega}
to the state space
\mathbf{X}
, the action space
\mathbf{A}
, and
\mathbf{H}_{n}
for
n\geq 0
are respectively denoted by
X_{n}
,
A_{n}
, and
H_{n}
.
We will refer to
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as the state process and
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as the action process.
It is a well known result that for every policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}

there exists a unique probability
measure
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
on
(\mathbf{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})

such that
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\mathbf{K}^{\infty})=1
and such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Gamma\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
, and
\Lambda\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbf{A})
we have

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(X_{0}\in\Gamma)=\eta(\Gamma)
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(X_{n+1}\in\Gamma\mid H_{n},A_{n})=Q(\Gamma\mid X_{n},A_{n})\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(A_{n}\in\Lambda\mid H_{n})=\pi_{n}(\Lambda\mid H_{n})

with

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
-probability one.
We will refer to
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
as to a
strategic probability measure
, and we will denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\}_{\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{\Omega})

the set of all strategic probability measures.
The expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
. For a
p
-dimensional random variable
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i},\ldots,Z_{p})
with
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
we will write
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Z]
for the vector
\big(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Z_{1}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Z_{i}],\ldots,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Z_{p}]\big)
when
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Z_{i}]
is well defined for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

2.2
Absorbing and atomless models

In this section, we consider an arbitrary but fixed model
\mathsf{M}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)
satisfying the measurability conditions.
Essentially, we are going to introduce different type of control models such as absorbing and atomless models. To do this, we will need to define the notion of occupation measures associated with a control policy.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that the control model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing if
\Delta\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
and the following conditions are satisfied

\bullet

Q(\Delta|x,a)=1
and
r(x,a)=0_{d}
for
(x,a)\in\Delta\times\mathbf{A}
.

\bullet

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[T_{\Delta}]
is finite for any
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
.

and we say that it is uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing if, additionally, the following condition holds

\bullet

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}}\sum_{t=n}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\{T_{\Delta}>t\}=0
.

where
T_{\Delta}:\mathbf{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined by

T_{\Delta}(\omega)=\min\{n\geq 0:X_{n}(\omega)\in\Delta\},
where by convention the
\min
over the empty set is defined as
+\infty
.

The notion of a uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing MDP was introduced in
[
14
, Definition 3.6]
).

We define now the occupation measures of a
\Delta
-absorbing control model
\mathsf{M}
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a
\Delta
-absorbing control model
\mathsf{M}
, the occupation measure
\mu_{\pi}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{+}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
of a control policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
is

\mu_{\pi}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{I}_{\{T_{\Delta}>t\}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{\{(X_{t},A_{t})\in\Gamma\}}\Big]\quad\hbox{for $\Gamma\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}\otimes\mathfrak{B}(\mathbf{A})$.}

Remark 2.5
.

(a)

The measure
\mu_{\pi}
is indeed a finite measure since
\mu_{\pi}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[T_{\Delta}]<+\infty

since
\mathsf{M}
is by assumption
\Delta
-absorbing.

(b)

Observe that for an arbitrary Markov randomized policy
\pi=\{\pi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the occupation measure
\mu_{\pi}
takes the form

\displaystyle\mu_{\pi}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\eta Q^{(j)}_{\pi}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\otimes\pi_{j}.

(2.1)

Most of the time we will use this equation without quoting it explicitly.

We will write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}=\{\mu_{\pi}:\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}\}
for the set of occupation measures. We will also consider the following subsets of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
denoted by

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\Lambda}=\{\mu_{\pi}:\pi\in\Lambda\}

for the set of policies
\Lambda
given by
\mathbb{S}
,
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}_{p}
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

Remark 2.6
.

(a)

Observe that the subset
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
of the vector space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
is convex.
This well known property has been established in the special case of a Borel state space see for example Corollary 4.3 in
[
17
]
and Proposition 3 in
[
22
]
.
For the general case of a measurable state space, we can proceed by using similar arguments in showing that the set of strategic measures
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
is convex implying the convexity of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
.

(b)

It is also important to recall the following well-known property:
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{S}},

see for example Theorem 8.1 in
[
2
]
for a countable state space, Lemma 4.1 in
[
17
]
or Lemma 2 in
[
22
]
for a Borel state space
and Proposition 3.3(ii) in
[
10
]
for a general measurable state space.

We will need of the following simple result that says that for two Markov policies which both apply the same fixed control, except at a certain stage where they apply a different control for each of them,
then the corresponding occupation measures are not equal provided that the distribution of the state process at this stage charges the set of states for which these policies are different.

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that the Markov controlled model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{S}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider two Markov policies
\sigma^{1}=\{\sigma^{1}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\sigma^{2}=\{\sigma^{2}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfying

\sigma^{1}_{k}(\cdot|x)=\sigma^{2}_{k}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)
for
k\neq N
. Define
\Gamma=\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\sigma^{1}_{N}(\cdot|x)\neq\sigma^{2}_{N}(\cdot|x)\}
.
We have
\Gamma\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
and if
\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}(\Gamma)>0
, then
\mu_{\sigma^{1}}\neq\mu_{\sigma^{2}}
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma^{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\eta Q_{\gamma}^{k}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\otimes\gamma+\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\otimes\sigma^{i}_{N}+\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}Q_{\sigma^{i}_{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Q_{\gamma}^{j}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\otimes\gamma

for
i=1,2
from equation (
2.1
).
If
\mu_{\sigma^{1}}^{\mathbf{X}}\neq\mu_{\sigma^{2}}^{\mathbf{X}}
then we get the claim. Now, if
\mu_{\sigma^{1}}^{\mathbf{X}}=\mu_{\sigma^{2}}^{\mathbf{X}}

then it follows that
\displaystyle\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}Q_{\sigma^{1}_{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Q_{\gamma}^{j}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}=\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}Q_{\sigma^{2}_{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Q_{\gamma}^{j}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}

and so,
\mu_{\sigma^{1}}-\mu_{\sigma^{2}}=\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\otimes[\sigma^{1}_{N}-\sigma^{2}_{N}].

From Theorem 8.10.39 in
[
4
]
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{A})
is countably separated since the points in
\mathbf{A}
can be separated by a countable family of continuous functions on
\mathbf{A}

(see Theorem 6.7.7 in
[
4
]
).
Therefore, there exists a countable family of functions
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\mathbf{A}
satisfying

\displaystyle\Gamma=\mathop{\cup}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\sigma^{1}_{N}h_{n}(x)\neq\sigma^{2}_{N}h_{n}(x)\}

and so,
\Gamma\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Since
\eta Q^{N}_{\gamma}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}(\Gamma)>0
, there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
either
\eta Q^{N}_{\gamma}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}(\Gamma_{j}^{+})>0
or
\eta Q^{N}_{\gamma}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}(\Gamma_{j}^{-})>0

where
\Gamma_{j}^{+}=\{x\in\Gamma:\sigma^{1}_{N}h_{j}(x)>\sigma^{2}_{N}h_{j}(x)\}

and
\Gamma_{j}^{-}=\{x\in\Gamma:\sigma^{1}_{N}h_{j}(x)<\sigma^{2}_{N}h_{j}(x)\}
.
Therefore, it follows that either
\mu_{\sigma^{1}}(\mathbf{I}_{\Gamma_{j}^{+}}h_{j})>\mu_{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{\Gamma_{j}^{+}}h_{j})
or

\mu_{\sigma^{1}}(\mathbf{I}_{\Gamma_{j}^{-}}h_{j})<\mu_{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{\Gamma_{j}^{-}}h_{j})

showing the result.
∎

Now, we introduce the so-called characteristic equation.

Definition 2.8
.

Suppose that the model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
A measure
\mu
in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{+}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
is a solution of the characteristic equations when

\mu(\mathbf{K}^{c})=0
and
\mu^{\mathbf{X}}=(\eta+\mu Q)\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}
.
We denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
the set of all solutions of the characteristic equations.

In the special case of a
\Delta
-absorbing model, the next lemma provides a link between occupation measures and the solutions of the characteristic equation.
We refer the reader to
[
10
]
for a more detailed analysis of these links.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that the Markov controlled model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
Let
\mu\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
. Then,

\mu\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\iff\Big[\mu\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\text{ and }\mu^{\mathbf{X}}\ll\mu^{\mathbf{X}}_{\pi}\text{ for some }\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}\Big].

Proof.

We have only to show the reverse implication since
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
by Proposition 3.3(i) in
[
10
]
.
From Theorem 3.7(i) in
[
10
]
there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\mu=\mu_{\sigma}+\vartheta
with

\vartheta\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{+}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
satisfying
\vartheta^{\mathbf{X}}=\vartheta Q\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}
.
By hypothesis
\mu^{\mathbf{X}}\ll\mu^{\mathbf{X}}_{\pi}
, therefore,
\vartheta=0
according to Proposition 3.8(i) in
[
10
]
and so,

\mu=\mu_{\sigma}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
.
∎

The performance of a policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
is evaluated by using the following vector-function.

Definition 2.10
.

Suppose that the model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing. The vector of expected rewards for the policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
denoted by
\mathcal{R}(\pi)
is given by

\mathcal{R}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}r(X_{t},A_{t})\Big].

This defines an application from
\mathbf{\Pi}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
called the the performance vector function.
A subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{\Pi}
will be called a sufficient class (family) of policies if
\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
.

Remark 2.11
.

Suppose that the model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
For a bounded real-valued measurable function
g
defined on
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}
satisfying
g(x,a)=0
for
x\in\Delta
and an arbitrary Markov randomized policy
\pi=\{\pi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

we have by using the identity
\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}g=g
and equation (
2.1
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}g(X_{t},A_{t})\Big]=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\eta Q^{(j)}_{\pi}g_{\pi_{j}}.

Therefore,
\mathcal{R}(\pi)=\mu_{\pi}(r)
and
\|\mathcal{R}(\pi)\|\leq\|r\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[T_{\Delta}]<+\infty

for a Markov randomized policy
\pi
.
Most often, these equations will be used implicitly without being mentioned.

Finally, we present a definition of atomless model which is inspired by the definition introduced in
[
14
, Definition 2.1]
but adapted to the general framework of a measurable state space.

Definition 2.12
.

The model
\mathsf{M}
is called atomless if the measure
\eta\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}
is atomless and if there exists a countably generated sub
\sigma
-algebra

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
such that
Q\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}(\cdot|x,a)
is atomless on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}
for any
(x,a)\in\Delta^{c}\times\mathbf{A}
.

Remark 2.13
.

Note that in the special case where
\mathbf{X}
is a Borel space, the previous definition coincides with Definition 2.1 in
[
14
]
.

Remark 2.14
.

There is no loss of generality to assume that the action space
\mathbf{A}
is compact. Indeed, if this is not the case, observe that
\mathbf{A}

is homeomorphic to a Borel subset of a compact metric space denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
.
The sets
\mathbf{A}(x)
for
x\in\mathbf{X}
are then measurable subsets of
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
.
The stochastic kernel
Q
can be extended to a Markov kernel
\hat{Q}
on
\mathbf{X}
given
\mathbf{X}\times\hat{\mathbf{A}}
by defining

\hat{Q}(dy|x,a)=Q(dy|x,a)
for
(x,a)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}
and
\hat{Q}(dy|x,a)=\eta(dy)
for
(x,a)\in\mathbf{X}\times(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\setminus\mathbf{A})
and the reward function
r
can be extended to a function
\hat{r}
given by

\hat{r}(x,a)=r(x,a)
for
(x,a)\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}
and
\hat{r}(x,a)=0
for
(x,a)\in\mathbf{X}\times(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\setminus\mathbf{A})
.
Clearly,
\mathbf{K}
is a measurable subset of
\mathbf{X}\times\hat{\mathbf{A}}
. Let us write
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}
for the set of strategic measures for the model

\hat{\mathsf{M}}=(\mathbf{X},\hat{\mathbf{A}},\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},\hat{Q},\eta,\hat{r})
.
Observe that for any
\hat{\mathbb{P}}\in\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}
, the restriction of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
to
\mathbf{K}^{\infty}
coincides with some
\mathbb{P}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
and conversely, for any
\mathbb{P}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
,
there exists
\hat{\mathbb{P}}\in\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}
such that the restriction of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
to
\mathbf{K}^{\infty}
coincides with
\mathbb{P}
.
Therefore, the models
\mathsf{M}
and
\hat{\mathsf{M}}
can be indentified.
It is also easy to show that if the model
\mathsf{M}
is (uniformly)
\Delta
-absorbing, so is
\hat{\mathsf{M}}
and that if
\mathsf{M}
is atomless, so is
\hat{\mathsf{M}}
.
In the rest of this paper we will assume that
\mathbf{A}
is compact.
This extension will allow us to use the results obtained in
[
10
]
for
\Delta
-absorbing models.

Throughout the rest of this paper, we will use a fixed model
\mathsf{M}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)
satisfying the measurability conditions.
To avoid presenting the results in a too cumbersome manner, we will not repeat in the following that the model
\mathsf{M}
is assumed to satisfy these conditions.
For technical reasons, we will also have to consider other models which will most often consist of modifying the space of admissible actions of the model
\mathsf{M}
.

3
Extreme points of constrained sets of occupation measures

n this section, we will characterize the extreme points of the set of occupation measures
\mu
satisfying integral type constraints of the form
\mu(r)=\alpha
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for
\Delta
-absorbing models but also for models that are both
\Delta
-absorbing and atomless.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and a bounded measurable function
g:\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, let us denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(g,\alpha)
the affine hyperplane of the vector space

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(g,\alpha)=\{\mu\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}):\mu(g)=\alpha\}.

We write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\mathop{\cap}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(g,\alpha)
and also,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\Lambda}(g,\alpha)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\Lambda}\mathop{\cap}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(g,\alpha)
for
\Lambda
given by
\mathbb{S}
,
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}_{p+1}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
In section
3.1
, we will establish that for a
\Delta
-absorbing model every extreme point of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha)
is necessarily given by an occupation measure induced by a chattering stationary policy of order
d+1
, that is,

\ext(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha))\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{C}_{d+1}}
(see the Main Theorem in
3.4
).
In section
3.2
, we will show that the extreme points of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha)
are precisely the occupation measures generated by deterministic stationary policies, that is,

\ext(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha))=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}(g,\alpha)
if in addition, the model
\mathsf{M}
is atomless.

The results of section
3.1
and
3.2
rely heavily on the following preliminary proposition.
The first item states that any stochastic kernel
\gamma\in\mathbb{S}
can be written as a convex combination of two stochastic kernels
\overline{\gamma}
and
\widehat{\gamma}
in
\mathbb{S}
such that
the set of points
x\in\mathbf{X}
satisfying
\overline{\gamma}(\cdot|x)\neq\widehat{\gamma}(\cdot|x)
are, loosely speaking, those for which
\gamma(\cdot|x)
is not given by a Dirac measure.
To establish it, we will use a general result by Castaing and Valadier (see Theorem IV.14 in
[
7
]
) that characterize the extreme points of the set of selectors of a multifunction with convex values.
The second item says that when this set is a
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-null set then
\mu_{\gamma}
is induced by a deterministic stationary policy, that is,
\mu_{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that the Markov controlled model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
For
\gamma\in\mathbb{S}
, let us write
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{\gamma}
for the
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-completion of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}

and define
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\big\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\gamma(\cdot|x)\in\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)\setminus\ext(\mathbf{\Gamma}(x))\big\}

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)=\{\lambda\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A}):\lambda(\mathbf{A}(x))=1\}
.

(a)

The set
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{\gamma}
-measurable and there exist
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in
\mathbb{S}
such that

\displaystyle\gamma+\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]\in\mathbb{S}\text{ and }\gamma-\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]\in\mathbb{S}.

(3.2)

Moreover,
\mathbf{\Xi}=\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\gamma_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\gamma_{2}(\cdot|x)\}
is a
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable subset of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}\setminus\mathbf{\Xi}

is a
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-null set.

(b)

If
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-null set then
\mu_{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}
, that is,

\mu_{\gamma}=\mu_{\phi}
for some
\phi\in\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

Let us show the first item.
We will suppose that the spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{A})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
are endowed with the weak topology.
Since
\mathbf{K}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbf{A})
, it follows from Theorem IV.12 in
[
7
]
that the graph of the multifunction
\mathbf{\Gamma}:\mathbf{X}\twoheadrightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
defined by

\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)=\{\lambda\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A}):\lambda(\mathbf{A}(x))=1\}

belongs to
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A}))
.
Let us denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
the set of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable mappings
\sigma
from
\mathbf{X}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
such that

\sigma(\cdot|x)\in\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)
for any
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
Recalling Assumption
(M.2)
,
\mathbf{\Gamma}
has non empty convex values in the Haussdorff locally convex space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{A})
.
Observe that the stochastic kernel
\gamma\in\mathbb{S}
gives rise to a mapping in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
defined by

x\rightarrow\gamma(\cdot|x)
and conversely,
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
yields to a stochastic kernel in
\mathbb{S}
given by

(x,\Lambda)\rightarrow\sigma(x,\Lambda)
for
x\in\mathbf{X}
and
\Lambda\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbf{A})
.
This follows easily from Theorem 3.1 in
[
18
]
(in this book, it is assumed that
(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}})
is a complete measurable space but actually the proof of Theorem 3.1 does not use this point).
Depending on the context, we will use
\mathbb{S}
to refer to control policies and we will use
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
when using results from
[
7
]
.
According to Theorem IV.14 in
[
7
]
and its proof, the set
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\big\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\gamma(\cdot|x)\in\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)\setminus\ext(\mathbf{\Gamma}(x))\big\}

is a
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{\gamma}
-measurable subset of
\mathbf{X}
and there also exist
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{\gamma}
-measurable selectors of the multifunction
\mathbf{\Gamma}
satisfying

\varrho_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\varrho_{2}(\cdot|x)
for
x\in\mathbf{\Lambda}
, and
\varrho_{1}(\cdot|x)=\varrho_{2}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)
for
x\in\mathbf{\Lambda}^{c}
,
and such that
\gamma+\frac{1}{2}[\varrho_{1}-\varrho_{2}]
and
\gamma-\frac{1}{2}[\varrho_{1}-\varrho_{2}]
are
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}_{\gamma}
-measurable selectors of the multifunction
\mathbf{\Gamma}
.
By using Lemma 1.2 in
[
8
]
there exist a
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable subset
\Xi
of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
, two
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable mappings
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
from
\mathbf{X}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathcal{N}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
such that

(\mathbf{\Lambda}\setminus\Xi)\mathop{\cup}\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\rho_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\varrho_{1}(\cdot|x)\}\mathop{\cup}\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\rho_{2}(\cdot|x)\neq\varrho_{2}(\cdot|x)\}\subset\mathcal{N}

with
\mu^{\mathbf{X}}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{N})=0
.
Define
\mathbf{\Xi}=\Xi\mathop{\cap}\mathcal{N}^{c}
. Then
\mathbf{\Xi}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
and
\mathbf{\Xi}\subset\Xi\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}\subset[\Xi\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N}]=\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{\Lambda}\setminus\mathbf{\Xi}
is a
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-null set. Let us define, the stochastic kernels
\gamma_{i}
on
\mathbf{A}
given
\mathbf{X}
for
i=1,2
by
\gamma_{i}(\cdot|x)=\rho_{i}(\cdot|x)
for
x\in\mathcal{N}^{c}
and
\gamma_{i}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)
for
x\in\mathcal{N}
.
Then,
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
are in
\mathbb{S}
and satisfy

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\gamma_{2}(\cdot|x)\text{ for }x\in\mathbf{\Xi},\quad\gamma_{1}(\cdot|x)=\gamma_{2}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)\text{ for }x\in\mathbf{\Xi}^{c}

showing that
\mathbf{\Xi}=\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\gamma_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\gamma_{2}(\cdot|x)\}\subset\mathbf{\Lambda}
.
Moreover, we have also

\displaystyle\gamma+\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\text{ and }\gamma-\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}}.

For the second item, observe that if
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-null set then
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{\Xi})=0
.
In this case, we have

\gamma(\cdot|x)=\delta_{\{\theta(x)\}}(\cdot)\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N}}(x)+\gamma(\cdot|x)\mathbf{I}_{(\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N})^{c}}(x)

for
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-almost every

since
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N})=0
where
\theta
is the
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable function from
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{A}
introduced in Assumption
(M.2)
.
The extreme points of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
are given by the Dirac measures from Theorem 15.9 in
[

]
.
Clearly, the mapping
x\mapsto\delta_{\{\theta(x)\}}(\cdot)\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N}}(x)+\gamma(\cdot|x)\mathbf{I}_{(\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N})^{c}}(x)
from
\mathbf{X}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})

is
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable and
\delta_{\{\theta(x)\}}(\cdot)\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N}}(x)+\gamma(\cdot|x)\mathbf{I}_{(\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N})^{c}}(x)\in\ext(\mathbf{\Gamma}(x))

by definition of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and recalling
\mathbf{\Lambda}\subset\mathbf{\Xi}\mathop{\cup}\mathcal{N}
.
The extreme points of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
are given by the Dirac measures from Theorem 15.9 in
[
1
]
.
Observe that
\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)
is a face of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A})
for any

and so,
\ext(\mathbf{\Gamma}(x))=\ext(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{A}))\mathop{\cap}\mathbf{\Gamma}(x)
.
Therefore, there exists a
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable mapping
\phi
from
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{A}
satisfying

for any

and

\mu_{\gamma}(dx,da)=\delta_{\phi(x)}(da)\mu^{\mathbf{X}}_{\gamma}(dx)
.
Proceeding as for the proof of Proposition 3.3(ii) in

]
, we get easily that
\mu_{\gamma}=\mu_{\phi}
showing the result.
∎

3.1
The case of a general absorbing model

We start with the following proposition which states that the set of occupation measures of an
\Delta
-absorbing model is linearly closed and linearly bounded, that is, its intersection with each line is closed and bounded
in the natural topology of the line. These are particularly important properties in the study of extremal points of convex sets, see for example
[
9
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume that the Markov controlled model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing. The set of occupation of measures
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
is linearly closed and linearly bounded.

Proof.

Let us first show that for
\nu\neq\gamma
in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
, the set
\mathcal{D}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}:(1-u)\nu+u\gamma\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\}
is a bounded interval of
\mathbb{R}
.
Recalling that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
is a convex set, it follows easily that for
s
,
t
in
\mathcal{D}
,
[s,t]\subset\mathcal{D}
and so,
\mathcal{D}
is an interval of
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\Gamma
be a measurable subset of
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}
such that
\nu(\Gamma)\neq\gamma(\Gamma)
. Then,

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{D}}|u|\leq\Big[\sup_{\mu\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}}\mu(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})+|\nu(\Gamma)|\Big]/|\gamma(\Gamma)-\nu(\Gamma)|.

By using Lemma 2.4 in
[
10
]
, this implies that
\mathcal{D}
is bounded.
Finally, let us show that
\mathcal{D}
is closed. Let us consider
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence in
\mathcal{D}
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}=u\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then it follows that the measure
\lambda_{u}
defined by
\lambda_{u}=(1-u)\nu+u\gamma
lies in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{+}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})

and also satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda_{u}^{\mathbf{X}}=\big[\eta+\lambda_{u}Q\big]\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\text{ with }\lambda_{u}(\mathbf{K}^{c})=0

showing that
\lambda_{u}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
. Write
\lambda_{u_{n}}=(1-u_{n})\nu+u_{n}\gamma
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. There is no loss of generality to assume that
\lambda_{u_{n_{0}}}\neq\lambda_{u_{n_{1}}}

for some
n_{0}
,
n_{1}
in
\mathbb{N}
.
Consequently, there exists
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathbb{R}
such that

\nu=(1-\alpha)\lambda_{u_{n_{0}}}+\alpha\lambda_{u_{n_{1}}}
and
\gamma=(1-\beta)\lambda_{u_{n_{0}}}+\beta\lambda_{u_{n_{1}}}
. This implies that

\lambda_{u}\ll(\lambda_{u_{n_{0}}}+\lambda_{u_{n_{1}}})/2=\lambda_{\pi}

for some
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
since
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
is convex. Therefore,

\lambda_{u}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
from Lemma
2.9
showing that
\mathcal{D}
is closed.
∎

We will need the following result established in
[
17
, Lemma 4.6]
for absorbing models and in
[
22
, Theorem 1]
for models having an absorbing
state in the framework of a Borel state space. It is possible to prove this property in the context of a general measurable state space by using Proposition
3.1
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that the model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
Then,
\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\big)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof.

To get the result, we will first show that
\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{S}}\big)\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}
.
Suppose that
\mu_{\gamma}\in\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{S}}\big)
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{S}
.
From Proposition
3.1
(a)
, there exist
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in
\mathbb{S}
such that

\gamma+\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]\in\mathbb{S}
and
\gamma-\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]\in\mathbb{S}

and
\mathbf{\Xi}=\{x\in\mathbf{\Lambda}:\gamma_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\gamma_{2}(\cdot|x)\}
is a
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{X}}
-measurable set.
Suppose that
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{\Xi})>0
, then there exists
N
such that
\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}(\mathbf{\Xi})>0
.
Therefore, we can define the Markov policies
\sigma^{1}=\{\sigma^{1}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\sigma^{2}=\{\sigma^{2}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
by

\sigma^{1}_{k}(\cdot|x)=\sigma^{2}_{k}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)
for
k\neq N
and
\sigma^{1}_{N}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)+\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]
and

\sigma^{2}_{N}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)-\frac{1}{2}[\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}]

for
x\in\mathbf{X}
. By using the expression (
2.1
) of an occupation measure generated by a Markov policy and the definition of
\sigma^{i}
for
i=1,2
, it follows that

\frac{1}{2}[\mu_{\sigma^{1}}+\mu_{\sigma^{2}}]
.
Now observe that
\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\sigma^{1}_{N}(\cdot|x)\neq\sigma^{2}_{N}(\cdot|x)\}=\{x\in\mathbf{X}:\gamma_{1}(\cdot|x)\neq\gamma_{2}(\cdot|x)\}=\mathbf{\Xi}
.
From Lemma
2.7
, we get that
\mu_{\sigma^{1}}\neq\mu_{\sigma^{2}}
yielding a contradiction with
\mu_{\gamma}\in\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{S}}\big)
.
Therefore,
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{\Xi})=0
and so, the set
\mathbf{\Lambda}
introduced in Proposition
3.1
is a
\mu_{\gamma}^{\mathbf{X}}
-null set and the result follows by
Proposition
3.1
(b)
. The converse inclusion is straightforward.
∎

The next Theorem is the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that the Markov controlled model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
Consider a bounded measurable function
g:\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying
g(x,a)=0_{p}
for
(x,a)\in\Delta\times\mathbf{A}
and a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Every extreme point of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha)
is a convex combination of at most
p+1
extreme points of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}
and so,

\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha)\big)\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{C}_{p+1}}(g,\alpha).

Proof.

Combining Proposition
3.2
and the Main Theorem in
[
9
]
, we easily get the first part of the claim.
Since by Proposition
3.3

\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\big)\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}
, it follows that if
\mu_{\pi}\in\ext\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha)\big)
for some
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
then

\mu_{\pi}(g)=\alpha
and

\displaystyle\mu_{\pi}=\sum_{i=1}^{p+1}\alpha_{i}\mu_{\phi_{i}}
with
\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{D}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}_{p+1}^{*}
and
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p+1})\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{S}}_{p+1}
.
By using a slight modification of Lemma 5 in
[
13
]
to a measurable state space, there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}_{p+1}
such that
\mu_{\pi}=\mu_{\gamma}
and so,

\mu_{\gamma}(g)=\alpha
showing the result.
∎

3.2
The case of an absorbing and atomless model

To establish the main result of this subsection, that is,
\ext(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}(g,\alpha))=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{D}}(g,\alpha)
we will need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that the Markov controlled model
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing.
Consider
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a bounded function
g\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{0}_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{A})
satisfying
g(x,a)=0_{p}
for
(x,a)\in\Delta\times\mathbf{A}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
For
\gamma
,
\pi^{1}
and
\pi^{2}
in
\mathbb{S}
, the
p
-dimensional signed measure
\nu
on
\mathbf{X}
defined by

\nu(dx)=\Big[g_{\pi}(x)+Q_{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{\gamma}^{k}g_{\gamma}(x)\Big]\>\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}(dx)

is finite and absolutely continuous with respect to
\eta Q_{\gamma}^{N}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}
where
g_{\pi}=g_{\pi^{1}}-g_{\pi^{2}}
and
Q_{\pi}=Q_{\pi^{1}}-Q_{\pi^{2}}
.

Proof.

Consider the Markov policy
\sigma^{i}=\{\sigma^{i}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
for
i=1,2
defined by

\sigma^{i}_{k}(\cdot|x)=\gamma(\cdot|x)
for
k\neq N
and

\sigma^{i}_{N}(\cdot|x)=\pi^{i}(\cdot|x)
, for
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
Since
\mathsf{M}
is
\Delta
-absorbing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{i}}\Big[\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}h(X_{j},A_{j})\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\eta Q^{j}_{\gamma}h_{\gamma}+\eta Q^{N}_{\gamma}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}\big[h_{\pi^{i}}+Q_{\pi^{i}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{\gamma}^{k}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}h_{\gamma}\big]<+\infty

(3.3)

for
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
h(x,a)=|g(x,a)|
.
Consequently, the components of the
p
-dimensional functions
g_{\pi^{i}}+Q_{\pi^{i}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{\gamma}^{k}g_{\gamma}
for
i=1,2

are
\eta Q^{N}_{\gamma}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}
-measurable, see Definition 2.1.10 in
[
4
]
and so, are the components of the function

g_{\pi}+Q_{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{\gamma}^{k}g_{\gamma}
.
By using again inequality (
3.3
), it is also
\eta Q^{N}_{\gamma}\mathbb{I}_{\Delta^{c}}
-integrable. Therefore, the
p
-dimensional signed measure

4
Sufficiency of chattering and deterministic Markov policies

The main objective of this section is to show that for a uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing model
\mathsf{M}
, the set of chattering Markov policies of order
2d+1
is a sufficient class of control strategies. In other words, for any control policy

of order

satisfying

.
To our best knowledge, this result is new and it will be reinforced in the following section by showing the stronger result that the set of chattering stationary policies of order
d+1
is in fact sufficient.
If in addition the model is atomless then we will show that the set of deterministic Markov policy is sufficient.
As already mentioned in the introduction, the sufficiency of deterministic Markov policies for an atomless model with Borel state space is well known. The reader is referred to the references
[
15
,
16
]
where the authors obtained this type of results in a very general context by considering a vector performance functional having the form of total rewards (
i.e.
the model under consideration are not necessarily absorbing).
Nevertheless, we believe it is worthwhile to propose this new statement, which deals with general state space,
but also because we are proposing a new direct approach to prove this result, based on the Young measure theory.

We start this section by showing that for an arbitrary Markov randomized policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}

To get these two results we will apply the finite support equivalence Theorem and the Dirac equivalence Theorem, two well known results coming from the Young measure theory, see Theorem 8.2 and Theorem 9.2 in
[

According to Theorem

, for any
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
, there
exists a uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing model
\mathsf{M}^{*}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}^{*}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)
satisfying the measurability conditions with
\mathbf{A}^{*}(x)\subset\mathbf{A}(x)
and such that

\mu_{\pi}(r)\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
where
\mathbf{\Pi}^{*}
denotes set of all control policies associated with the model
\mathsf{M}^{*}
.
Clearly,
\mathbf{\Pi}^{*}\subset\mathbf{\Pi}
.
Suppose that
\mu_{\pi}(r)
belongs to the relative boundary of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
. Then, there is a linear functional
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
not constant on
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
such that

G
achieves its maximum at
\mu_{\pi}(r)
by Corollary 11.6.2 in
[
24
]
.
However, we have by Theorem
5.4
that
G(\mu(r))=G(\mu_{\pi}(r))
for any
\mu\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}^{*}
.
This leads a contradiction and so
\mu_{\pi}(r)
belongs necessarily to the relative interior of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
.
∎

Now, we obtain the main results of this section.

Theorem 5.6
.

Assume that the model
\mathsf{M}
is uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing.
Then,
\mathbb{C}_{d+1}
is a sufficient family of policies, that is,
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C}_{d+1})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
.

Proof.

For an arbitrary policy
\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}
, there exists
\mathsf{M}^{*}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{A}^{*}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbf{X}},Q,\eta,r)

a uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing model satisfying the measurability conditions with

{\mathbf{A}}^{*}(x)\subset\mathbf{A}(x)
for which
\mu_{\pi}(r)
belongs to the relative interior of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
. Let us write
\mathbb{C}_{d+1}^{*}
for the set of chattering stationary policies for the model
\mathsf{M}^{*}
.
We can apply Theorem
5.3
(a)
to get that
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{*})
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C}_{d+1}^{*})
have the same relative interior.
Therefore, there exists a chattering stationary policy
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}_{d+1}^{*}
such that
\mu_{\pi}(r)=\mu_{\gamma}(r)
but
\mathbb{C}_{d+1}^{*}\subset\mathbb{C}_{d+1}
showing the result.
∎

We provide two different proofs for the second main result which is an extension of the Feinberg-Piunovskiy Theorem in the context of a general state space

Theorem 5.7

(The Feinberg-Piunovskiy Theorem)
.

Assume that the model
\mathsf{M}
is uniformly
\Delta
-absorbing and atomless then
\mathbb{D}
is a sufficient family of policies, that is,
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{D})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Pi})
.

Proof.

The first proof is obtained by combining Proposition
5.2
and Theorem
5.6
.

The second proof consists in proceeding exactly as in the proof of Theorem
5.6
by applying Theorem
5.3
(b)
and by noting that if
\mathsf{M}
is atomless so is
\mathsf{M}^{*}
and also
\mathbb{D}^{*}\subset\mathbb{D}
if
\mathbb{D}^{*}
denotes the set of deterministic stationary policies for the model
\mathsf{M}^{*}
.
∎
