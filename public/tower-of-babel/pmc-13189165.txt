REST—a deep learning tool for automated mouse sleep stage classification
Authors: Jun Wang, Sue Osting, Anna Goforth, Mathew V Jones, Rama Maganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesSleep research in rodents often involves large-scale electroencephalography (EEG) recordings that are traditionally scored manually—a labor-intensive process prone to scorer bias and inconsistency. We developed the REST-Rodent EEG Sleep Transformer, a neural network trained for automated sleep stage classification based on EEG and electromyography (EMG).Materials and MethodsEEG and EMG signals were segmented into 4-second epochs, transformed via short-time Fourier transform to generate representations with five time frames and 130 frequency bins per epoch. We used a two-stage model: the first stage analyzes individual epochs to extract meaningful features, while the second stage captures sleep patterns over time by modeling the relationships between consecutive epochs. The model was trained based on 116 days of artifact-free, pre-labeled recordings from 94 male Fmr1 knockout mice (C57BL/6 background). It was then tested on 38 separate 24-hour recordings from the same mice and on three other mouse strains (C57, DBA, and CD-1). REST was also tested against a convolutional neural network (CNN)–based model (Accusleep).ResultsREST achieved a Cohen’s kappa of 0.873 ± 0.005 and F1-scores for Wake (0.936 ± 0.003) and non-rapid eye movement sleep (0.926 ± 0.003), with strong performance for rapid eye movement sleep (0.913 ± 0.005). REST showed robust cross-strain compatibility and generalized effectively to external datasets.ConclusionREST is a fast, accurate, and accessible tool for automated sleep stage classification in mice. Its Transformer architecture provides long-range temporal awareness, enabling consistent and reliable analysis of rodent sleep in large-scale datasets. Statement of SignificanceManual scoring of rodent sleep is a major bottleneck in preclinical sleep research, especially for large-cohort studies with weeks-long recordings. REST, a Transformer-based neural network model, provides reliably accurate, automated sleep stage classification and is adaptable across different mouse strains and disease models. REST maintains high performance without retraining or manual fine-tuning, making it practical for translational research. Its application in mice subjected to traumatic brain injury revealed pharmacologically induced sleep changes, demonstrating its utility in both mechanistic and therapeutic studies. REST addresses a key gap in rodent sleep research by enabling fast, consistent sleep analysis. Future work will expand its capability to detect artifacts and extend to other species, enhancing its value for cross-strain investigations in neurological disorders.

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental behavior in animal species, including humans [1]. In sleep research, mice are often chosen as a model organism to characterize sleep features in different disease states. Sleep analysis in rodents has relied on human scoring over the last few decades [2]. Human scoring requires training, and a major obstacle is that human scoring is extremely labor-intensive, especially when research protocols involve a large number of animals and when the recordings are chronic. Even for an expert scorer, manual scoring of a 24-hour file would take 2–3 hours, and thus, thousands of person-hours are required to analyze sleep for a large study.

Semi-automated methods have evolved, and many laboratories use these methods to classify sleep into three sleep stages in rodents, including wake, non-rapid eye movement (NREM), or rapid eye movement (REM) sleep [3]. The semiautomated methods rely on the EMG to separate sleep from wake and theta/delta spectral power to distinguish REM from NREM sleep. However, the semiautomated methods require human curation as stage transitions and very brief wake episodes, artifacts, and events such as seizures are poorly distinguished [2].

Automated sleep stage classification has a long history of development [4]. Most classifiers are designed for human EEG [5, 6]. Automated sleep stage classification for rodent research also exists [7–10]. Most of these classifiers are based on neural networks such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or recurrent neural networks (RNNs). CNNs extract meaningful patterns from EEG and EMG signals through convolution, highlighting features like sleep spindles or slow waves [11]. RNNs process sequences of these features over time, using their memory-like structure to model the transitions between sleep stages [12]. While these models often achieve good overall accuracy, they typically require manual labeling of a section of each recording [8] or extensive training on large datasets [9, 10]. Most models also have limited temporal context [8, 10]. Temporal context refers to the ability of a model to take into account how sleep evolves over time, such as the transitions between stages and the continuity of sleep states across multiple epochs. Furthermore, these models were often developed and tested in a single strain of mice (C57s commonly). Though large sample sizes were used and validated, it is unclear if these same models can be used in different strains and treatment groups without extensive retraining.

To address these limitations, we set out to develop an automated sleep stage classification model tailored to our needs: Rodent EEG Sleep Transformer (REST). Here, we developed a Transformer-based neural network model for automated sleep stage classification of recordings into wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep. The Transformer is a deep learning architecture originally developed for natural language processing. Transformers are well suited for sleep classification because they can model both short-term features and long-range dependencies in EEG data, thus reducing sudden, unnatural transitions between sleep stages in prediction. Unlike CNNs, which focus on local patterns, or RNNs, which process sequences one step at a time, Transformers use the self-attention mechanism to evaluate relationships between time points (epochs) simultaneously. Self-attention assigns importance scores between every pair of epochs, allowing the model to weigh and combine information from across the entire sequence. This helps the model capture transitions between sleep stages and maintain context over extended periods—both of which are critical for accurate sleep scoring. Furthermore, because Transformers process all time points in parallel rather than sequentially, they can train more efficiently on large datasets. REST was trained using pre-labeled recording previously collected from a large dataset of male Fmr1 knock-out mice (C57 background). After the training, a robust series of tests was performed to measure the validity of the algorithm against human manual scoring in multiple mouse strains and treatment groups. Following this, the model was applied to score a large set of recordings collected as part of traumatic brain injury (TBI) research in CD-1 mice to assess the effect of a dual orexin antagonist (Lemborexant) on sleep in TBI mice.

REST is a practical, open-source sleep staging tool developed for routine use in our laboratory; this work focuses on its validation, limitations, cross-strain performance, and research application rather than proposing a new model architecture.

Materials and methods

Animal

Four separate mouse strains were used for this study: CD-1 IGS (CD-1), Fmr1tm4Cgr (Fmr1KO), C57BL/6 J (C57), and DBA/2 J (DBA) mice. Adult CD-1 mice (2 months old) and C57 mice (3 months old) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Adult DBA mice (3 months old) were obtained from Envigo (Indianapolis, IN). Adult Fmr1KO mice (4 months old) were sourced from a colony maintained locally at the University of Wisconsin-Madison [13]. All mice were singly housed in recording chambers at the start of EEG recording. Mice were housed in a 12:12-hour light–dark cycle, with access to food and water ad libitum. All animal procedures were approved by the University of Wisconsin IACUC, adhering to the US Department of Agriculture Animal Welfare Act and the National Institutes of Health Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

TBI induction, EEG/EMG implantation, and recordings

All mice in this study underwent a surgical procedure for EEG/EMG electrode implantation under isoflurane anesthesia in a stereotaxic frame, as described earlier [14]. CD-1 mice in the TBI study additionally underwent a 4–5 mm craniotomy (right parietal) after which a moderate-to-severe controlled cortical impact (CCI) was delivered using a precision cortical impactor (Hatteras Instruments, NC) (1.5 mm depth, 5 m/s impact velocity, and 100–200-millisecond dwell time) (n = 52). The sham injury group (n = 20) had only the craniotomy without CCI. The C57 and DBA mice underwent EEG/EMG electrode implantation, followed a week later by repeated low-dose systemic kainic acid (KA) or saline injections, whereas Fmr1KO mice had EEG/EMG electrode implantation alone.

All mice had right frontal and left parietal epidural screw electrodes (contacting the dura) for EEG, along with two stainless-steel wires inserted into the nuchal muscles for EMG recording. All procedures were performed during a single surgical session. Following TBI induction and electrode implantation, EEG and EMG signals were recorded continuously using the XLTEK (Madison, WI) EEG recording system at a sampling rate of 512 Hz for 6 weeks. All recorded EEG/EMG signals were saved in a European data format (EDF) for analysis. The EDFs were converted to Sirenia (Pinnacle Technologies, Lawrence, KS) sleep files for manual scoring of vigilance states.

Drug treatment in the TBI study

In the TBI study, Sham-injury (referred to as Sham) and CCI groups (referred to as TBI) received daily drug treatment with either Lemborexant, an FDA-approved dual orexin receptor antagonist (100 mg/kg suspended in methylcellulose as vehicle; TBI n = 26; Sham n = 10), or vehicle alone (methylcellulose only 0.3–0.4 mL; TBI n = 26; Sham n = 10). The drugs were administered daily via oral gavage at approximately 8–9 am each morning (shortly after lights-on, near sleep onset for rodents) for 6 weeks beginning on the day of surgery.

REST model design and training

Prior to training, preprocessing of training data was performed by bandpass filtering EEG signals between 0.1 and 30 Hz, while EMG signals were bandpass-filtered between 10 and 250 Hz with an additional 60 Hz notch filter to remove electrical interference. Both signals were normalized using z-score normalization and concatenated across the 180 days of 24-hour recordings.

Signals were then segmented into non-overlapping 4-second epochs. Each EEG epoch was resampled to 64 Hz (256 samples), and each EMG epoch (512 Hz) retained its native resolution (2048 samples). To extract time–frequency representations, we computed the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) for each epoch. EEG STFTs were computed using a 128-point window with a 64-point overlap. EMG STFTs were computed using a 1024-point window with a 512-point overlap. Both transforms produced five time frames per epoch with 0.5 Hz resolution. EMG STFT frequency bins were then resampled to match the EEG dimension (5 frames, 65 frequency bins) per epoch (Figure 1). Unlike the down-sampling of EEG signals—which removes higher-frequency components entirely via a pre-applied bandpass filter (0.1–30 Hz)—the down-sampling of EMG was performed after applying the STFT. Instead of reducing the sampling rate of the EMG signal directly, we compressed the STFT output by reducing the number of frequency bins, effectively condensing the power spectrum while preserving its overall shape. This approach retained relative spectral information, allowing the model to learn from the distribution of EMG power across a coarser but representative frequency range, rather than from specific high-resolution details. The down-sampling of both EEG (in the time domain) and EMG (in the frequency domain) was performed to reduce the input data size and meet system memory constraints while still preserving the critical features needed for sleep stage classification. EEG STFT and EMG STFT were then concatenated at the frequency bin dimension to form a matrix with shape 5 × 130 (time frames × frequency bins) per epoch. Input sequences to the model consisted of sliding windows of 90 consecutive 4-second epochs (6 minutes), with a 30-epoch overlap. Each input sequence thus had a shape of 90 × 5 × 130 (epochs × time frames × frequency bins).

The Transformer is a relatively recent deep learning architecture, first proposed in 2017 [15]. It serves as the foundation for generative Large Language Model (LLM) models such as ChatGPT. Transformer-based models have previously demonstrated high accuracy in classifying human scalp EEG for sleep staging [16]. Building on these findings, our model adopts a similar Transformer-based architecture tailored for mouse sleep stage classification.

The first stage of the model is the epoch-level encoder, which processes each individual 4-second epoch to generate a compact and informative summary of its features. In each of the five time frames within an epoch, the 130 frequency bins are passed through a linear projection layer, which transforms them into a 256-dimensional representation. This transformation allows the model to capture more complex combinations of frequency features by expanding them into a higher-dimensional space, making it easier to learn subtle patterns that differentiate sleep stages. A sinusoidal positional encoding is then added to preserve the temporal order across the five frames [15]. The Transformer architecture does not have any built-in mechanism to recognize the sequence or position of inputs; it treats all input frames as unordered. By adding positional information using sinusoidal functions, the model can learn to associate each frame with its correct place in time, allowing it to capture the temporal structure of the EEG signal within each epoch.

The positionally encoded sequence is then processed by a Transformer encoder consisting of four layers. Each layer includes a multi-head self-attention mechanism with eight attention heads, which allows the model to weigh and integrate information from all time frames based on their relevance to each other. This is followed by a position-wise feedforward network (feedforward size of 512, dropout rate of 0.1) with two fully connected layers and ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation, applied identically to each time frame. ReLU is a simple activation function that outputs the input if it’s positive and zero otherwise. This allows the model to capture more complex patterns while introducing nonlinearity and maintaining generalization (Figure 2A). The mathematical formulations and functions of sinusoidal positional encoding, scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, the feed-forward network, and the Transformer encoder have been thoroughly described in “Attention Is All You Need” by Vaswani et al., as well as in subsequent Transformer-based sleep classification studies [15, 16].

Transformer encoder components (A) and REST architecture (B). (A) depicts the key components of the Transformer encoder used in REST: Scaled dot-product attention (left) computes attention scores by comparing query (Q) and key (K) vectors derived from the same input using matrix multiplication (MatMul). These scores are scaled by the square root of the key dimension (Scale), optionally masked [Mask(opt)], passed through a softmax function to produce attention weights, and used to generate a weighted sum of the value (V) vectors using matrix multiplication (MatMul)—allowing the model to focus on the most relevant time frames. In this framework, queries represent what each time frame is looking for, keys represent what each time frame offers, and values contain the actual information to be combined. Multi-head attention (middle) enables the model to attend to different types of temporal relationships in parallel. The input data are linearly projected into multiple sets of Q, K, and V matrices, which are processed independently through parallel scaled dot-product attention heads. The outputs are then concatenated and passed through a final linear layer to integrate the information. Transformer encoder block (right), which stacks multi-head attention with a position-wise feedforward network. Feedforward network applies two fully connected layers with a ReLU activation to each time step individually, adding depth and non-linearity to the representation. Each sub-layer (attention and feedforward) is wrapped with an Add & Norm operation, which adds the original input back to the output (residual connection) and applies layer normalization. This stabilizes training and allows deeper stacking of layers. (B) depicts REST’s two-level structure: at the epoch level, individual 4-second epochs are encoded using their own positional encoding and Transformer encoder, while at the sequence level, temporal context is captured across multiple epochs with a separate positional encoder and transformer encoder. The output of the sequence-level Transformer encoder is passed through two fully connected layers to produce a set of logits, which are then passed through a softmax function to compute class probabilities.

The outputs of the final Transformer layer are combined using an attention pooling mechanism, which assigns a learned importance weight to each of the five time frames. These weights are then used to compute a weighted average, producing a single epoch embedding: a fixed-size vector (256 dimensions) that summarizes the entire 4-second epoch. The primary motivation for using an epoch-level encoder with multiple time frames is to better capture within-epoch transitions. In cases where a mouse changes vigilance state partway through a 4-second epoch, the self-attention mechanism allows the model to weigh the contribution of each time frame and identify the dominant state. This enables the model to classify the epoch based on where the majority of the activity occurs, rather than being misled by brief fluctuations or edge effects near state boundaries.

A similar positional encoding is applied to the sequence of epoch embeddings to preserve the temporal order of epochs within the 90-epoch-long sequence. These sequence-level embeddings are then passed to the second stage of the model: the sequence-level encoder. The sequence-level encoder captures longer temporal dependencies across the 6-minute-long window by applying another set of four Transformer encoder layers, each using the same architectural parameters as the epoch encoder. At this level, the self-attention mechanism enables the model to evaluate the relevance of each epoch in the context of its neighbors. This allows the model to weigh information across time and refine its understanding of each epoch’s sleep stage based on surrounding context. For example, ambiguous or noisy epochs can be more accurately classified by considering the sleep state transitions and temporal continuity in the surrounding sequence, leading to more robust and context-aware predictions.

Finally, a fully connected classification head is applied to the output of the sequence-level encoder to generate sleep stage predictions for each epoch. The classification head consists of two linear layers. The first maps the 256-dimensional encoder output to a 64-dimensional hidden layer, followed by a ReLU activation and dropout with a probability of 0.1. The second linear layer maps this to a vector of three logits, representing unnormalized confidence scores for the three sleep stage categories: Wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep. During training, these logits are passed through a softmax function to compute class probabilities, which are used to guide learning by comparing them to the reference label. During inference—that is, when the model is applied to new, unseen data—the logits are again passed through softmax to convert them into probabilities, and the sleep stage with the highest probability is selected as the model’s prediction through the argmax function. We used a total of 116 days of 24-hour, pre-labeled EEG/EMG data collected from 94 male Fmr1KO mice (22 mice contributed two recordings, while 72 mice contributed only one recording) as the training dataset. Fmr1KO mice were used instead of C57 mice primarily because they are the only strain for which a large pre-labeled dataset is available to us. The recordings are relatively clean and contain less than 1% artifact. The data were manually scored in 4-second epochs using Sirenia Sleep (Pinnacle Technologies, Lawrence, KS) as Wake, NREM sleep, or REM sleep. Only one EEG channel (right frontal) was used during both manual scoring and model training.

A dedicated Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) with CUDA support (RTX 4060 Ti, Nvidia, Santa Clara, CA) was used to meet processing requirements. Training was performed using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1e−4 and a weight decay of 1e−2. AdamW is a widely used optimizer that combines the benefits of adaptive learning rates with better handling of weight decay, helping improve generalization and stability during training [17]. To address class imbalance, particularly the underrepresentation of REM sleep, we employed focal loss with a focusing parameter γ = 2.0 and used class weights computed from the training data. Focal loss reduces the impact of well-classified examples and focuses learning on harder, less frequent cases—making it well suited for imbalanced classification tasks [18].

Additionally, training sequences that contained at least one REM sleep epoch were oversampled with a repeat factor of three. This ensured that the model was exposed to a more balanced representation of sleep stages during training, helping it better recognize rare REM sleep patterns [19]. During training, performance was validated on a separate dataset containing 29 days of recording that was not used for updating the model. The final model was selected based on the highest validation accuracy.

Validation of REST model

The performance of REST was evaluated by comparing its sleep stage classifications on six sets of recordings against manual scores. Prelabeled 24-hour recordings from Fmr1KO mice (n = 38, not used in training/validation, all male), CD-1 mice (n = 8, include both sham and TBI, 4 male, 4 female), C57 mice injected with saline (n = 7, 2 male, 5 female), C57 mice injected with KA (n = 5, 3 male, 2 female), DBA mice injected with saline (n = 5, 5 female), and DBA mice injected with KA (n = 6, 4 male, 2 female) were scored by REST. The REST sleep score is compared to the manual sleep score. To evaluate REST classification performance, we calculated precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, and Cohen’s kappa; the mathematical definitions and implementations of these standard machine learning metrics are detailed in Sokolova et al. [20].

REST’s performance was also compared to that of an established mouse sleep classifier, Accusleep, a CNN-based sleep classifier. Instead of requiring a large training dataset, Accusleep classifies 24-hour recordings using manual scores from a part of the recording [8]. An experienced scorer labeled the first hour of each of the seven CD-1 mice recordings used in the REST evaluation, and Accusleep then scored the remaining portions of each recording. The same evaluation metrics were calculated for Accusleep’s results.

In addition to comparing REST’s predictions to individual human scores, we also evaluated the agreement between human scorers themselves. The seven recordings from CD-1 mice were scored by three human scorers with varying levels of experience. This allowed us to contextualize REST’s performance by assessing whether its agreement with human scorers was comparable to the variability observed between human scorers. If REST’s predictions differ from human scores by a similar amount as humans differ from one another, it suggests that REST performs within the expected range of human variability.

Sleep analysis

EEG/EMG recordings from all CD-1 mice in the TBI research were scored using trained and tested REST. To ensure scored recordings had good signal quality and reasonable sleep stage classifications, a manual screening was performed based on delta (0.5–4 Hz) power, theta (5–7 Hz) power, EMG power, and predicted score from REST. The goal was to evaluate whether delta, theta, and EMG power varied across each recording as mice transitioned between sleep states, and whether REST scores fell within expected ranges (Figure 3). Recordings with poor overall signal quality (more than 10% artifact or significant drift in EEG or EMG amplitude) were excluded from further sleep analysis. Notably, recordings were excluded based on poor signal quality rather than poor REST classification output. For each mouse, any week with fewer than 4 days of acceptable recordings was excluded. Mice with fewer than 3 weeks of valid data were excluded from the final analysis.

Interface for manual quality screening. Displayed are theta (5–7 Hz), delta (0.5–4 Hz), and EMG power values calculated across 4-second epochs, alongside REST-predicted sleep stages (1 = Wake, 2 = NREM sleep, 3 = REM sleep). Users scroll through the full 24-hour recording to visually assess signal quality and verify scoring accuracy. In recordings accurately scored by REST, users can typically observe the following patterns: epochs with high EMG power are labeled as Wake; epochs with low EMG and elevated delta power are labeled as NREM sleep; and epochs with low EMG and low delta power are labeled as REM sleep. While these visual patterns do not necessarily reflect the internal decision-making process of REST, they provide a quick and intuitive way for reviewers to assess whether the predicted sleep stages align with expected physiological features.

The total time spent in Wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep was calculated in 2-hour bins for each 24-hour recording day. For each animal, the amount of time spent in each sleep state within each 2-hour bin was averaged across all days within a given week, resulting in one weekly value per time bin per animal. To assess group-level differences while properly accounting for the repeated-measures structure of the study, we analyzed the data using a linear mixed-effects (LME) regression model for each sleep state. The model included categorical fixed effects to estimate the mean percentage of sleep for every treatment group across each specific week and 2-hour time bin. To account for within-subject dependency and longitudinal variance, we specified random intercepts and continuous random slopes over time for each individual mouse. Group comparisons for each specific week and time bin were evaluated using F-tests on the model coefficients. Finally, to correct for multiple comparisons across the 72 time bins per sleep state, all p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. Significant differences were determined based on these FDR-corrected p-values, and results are reported using the model-derived estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals to reflect effect sizes and estimate precision.

Results

REST classification performance

Analysis of REST’s sleep stage classification on the 38 Fmr1KO recordings, which were the same mouse strain as the recordings REST was trained on, revealed that REST achieved high overall classification accuracy. A Cohen’s kappa value of 0.873 ± 0.005 (mean ± SEM) indicated a high overall level of agreement between manual scoring and REST predictions. F1-scores of 0.936 ± 0.003 for Wake and 0.926 ± 0.003 for NREM sleep demonstrated REST’s strong ability to distinguish between sleep and wakefulness. However, the F1-score for REM sleep was lower at 0.913 ± 0.005, indicating that REST was less effective at distinguishing between NREM and REM sleep. Nevertheless, because REM sleep is a relatively rare class and constitutes less than 10% of the recordings in most mouse strains [21], the overall weighted average F1-score and classification accuracy remained high at 0.931 ± 0.003 (Table 1).

It is important to note the fundamental difference in the operational deployment of these two models during this evaluation: REST utilized a global model pre-trained on a large dataset recording, whereas AccuSleep was calibrated using approximately 1 hour of manually scored data per recording day. This evaluation strategy was deliberately chosen to reflect the intended use case and standard operational pipeline of each tool. REST is designed to operate as a fully automated, globally generalized model, while AccuSleep is explicitly designed to function as a semi-automated, subject-calibrated system.

While it is technically possible to construct a one-to-one comparison by retraining the AccuSleep architecture on the entire REST training dataset, executing this would violate AccuSleep’s core design philosophy. The primary methodological innovation of AccuSleep is its use of Mixture Z-scoring, a normalization technique developed specifically to counteract distributional shifts in EEG/EMG signals when applying a model to a new mouse strain or a different laboratory environment [8]. Because Mixture Z-scoring fundamentally requires a small amount of subject-specific labeled data to accurately calibrate the signal distribution, deploying AccuSleep without this calibration step—and instead forcing it to rely solely on a massive global training set with standard normalization—would evaluate an artificial, sub-optimal implementation of the algorithm rather than the optimized tool intended by its authors. Therefore, evaluating both models in their respective intended operational states provides the most accurate and ecologically valid comparison of their utility for researchers. Overall, REST is better suited for large-scale fully automated sleep classification, while Accusleep is better for quick scoring of small datasets without the need for pre-training.

To further establish a comprehensive baseline and address potential concerns regarding evaluation fairness, we additionally applied the pre-trained REST model to the publicly available dataset provided in the original AccuSleep study [8]. As detailed in Table 3, REST achieved high classification accuracy on this independent dataset without requiring any subject-specific calibration or fine-tuning. This reciprocal evaluation demonstrates that REST’s globally trained architecture generalizes effectively to external data, maintaining robust performance even on recordings originating from a different lab.

Discussion

Advantages and disadvantages of automated sleep scoring

As noted in the introduction, sleep studies in mice can quickly accumulate large volumes of data—over 3000 days of recording in our case. Manually scoring this volume of data would require an enormous number of human labor hours. In contrast, REST required just over 48 hours to automatically score all 3000 days of recordings from the raw EDF files.

However, as demonstrated in our inter-scorer comparison using the CD1 mice, experienced human scorers achieved agreement levels (Cohen’s kappa ≈ 0.9) higher than REST’s agreement with the same scorers (kappa = 0.873 ± 0.012). As shown in Table 4, if the score from the most experienced scorer (Scorer 3) is treated as the ground truth, the other human scorers agreed with it slightly more often than REST did. Overall, REST disagrees with the reference score more often than other human scorers, particularly in identifying REM sleep. Nonetheless, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.88 and an overall accuracy of 0.93, we consider REST’s performance acceptable given the significant time savings it offers. It is also important to acknowledge that human scoring is subject to inconsistency, both due to variation among multiple scorers and the natural tendency of individuals to second-guess or change decisions over time. In contrast, as a pretrained deep learning model, REST offers a high degree of consistency—applying the same learned criteria uniformly across all data, free from scorer fatigue or subjective variation.

Like many deep learning models, REST functions largely as a black box, meaning that while we can observe the inputs (EEG/EMG features) and outputs (predicted sleep stages), the internal decision-making process is not easily interpretable. Neural networks—especially those with many layers and complex structures like REST—operate by transforming input data through a series of non-linear mathematical operations across hundreds of hidden units and parameters. Although Figure 3 shows that REST’s final predictions appear reasonable when compared to simple, interpretable features such as theta, delta, and EMG power, the actual mechanism by which the model processes and weighs the high-dimensional features extracted from the STFT of EEG and EMG signals remains unknown to observers. This lack of a directly observable decision-making process makes it challenging to trace back a specific prediction to a clear, human-understandable rationale, which is why such models are often referred to as black boxes. Despite this limitation, their ability to learn complex patterns directly from data makes them powerful tools for tasks like automated sleep scoring.

Comparing with other models

Significant advances in automated sleep classification have been made over the last decade, with newly developed models offering substantial improvements in accuracy, generalization, and accessibility.

The Sleep–Wake and Ictal State Classifier (SWISC) is a dual-purpose model capable of detecting seizures and postictal EEG suppression alongside sleep classification. Utilizing a bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) architecture, SWISC achieved an accuracy of 97.77%, one of the highest reported in the field. However, to achieve this performance and enable seizure detection, the model requires bilateral hippocampal depth electrodes in addition to standard frontal EEG and EMG [26]. Similarly, MLS-Net focuses on improving REM sleep classification—the most challenging stage to identify due to its relative rarity and less distinguishable EEG/EMG characteristics. By utilizing Bi-LSTM and electrooculogram (EOG) signals, MLS-Net achieved a REM F1-score of 87.3% [27]. While these novel approaches offer high accuracy, they often require complex surgical procedures and additional signal channels.

Cross-dataset generalization remains a significant challenge in automated sleep scoring. Models trained on a specific dataset often exhibit substantially lower accuracy when applied to unseen data. This performance degradation is primarily driven by distributional shifts in the underlying electrophysiological signals. Variations in mouse strains, experimental treatments, electrode placement, recording equipment, and environmental conditions all introduce signal variability that contributes to this drop in accuracy [8, 9]. Models such as Sleep-Deep-Learner [28] and Accusleep [8] address this by requiring researchers to manually score a small portion of each recording to tune the model to a particular subject. While this approach effectively mitigates the problem of cross-dataset generalization, it limits scalability by requiring human intervention for every recording. In contrast, with a traditional supervised model like REST, once the model is trained on a representative dataset, it can automatically score an unlimited number of new recordings without further human intervention.

In an effort to improve accessibility and reduce surgical trauma, single-channel systems (EEG only, no EMG) such as SAGER [29] and LG-Sleep [30], as well as non-invasive video-based models [31], have been developed. However, these models often struggle with REM sleep accuracy. Single-channel systems lack the muscle activity information from EMG necessary to effectively separate REM from wakefulness, while video-based models lack the brain activity data from EEG required to distinguish REM from NREM sleep.

Unsupervised models, such as FASTER2 [32] and SAGER [29], represent significant strides in improving the accessibility of automated sleep scoring by eliminating the need for extensive model training. However, while high accuracies have been reported for both models under controlled conditions, unsupervised approaches are inherently more susceptible to signal noise and amplitude drift. Because these models lack the learned feature robustness of supervised neural networks, environmental and physiological signal variations can easily distort their classification boundaries, thereby limiting their broad utility in diverse research settings.

While several complex architectures prioritize gains in overall accuracy, REST is designed to balance high performance with practical utility. The model offers several key advantages:

First, REST achieves an exceptional REM sleep F1-score of 0.913 when tested on the training mouse strain, representing one of the highest values among recent models. Furthermore, it maintains a robust REM sleep F1-score range of 0.765–0.912 across all tested non-seizure datasets. In research where the precise separation of NREM and REM sleep is critical, REST provides one of the most reliable REM classification tools currently available.

Second, REST maximizes practical utility by requiring only a single frontal EEG channel and one EMG channel. Unlike many high-performance models that rely on auxiliary signals—such as EOG or hippocampal local field potentials—REST achieves its reported accuracy using the most common electrode configuration found in rodent sleep studies. While the model still requires EMG input, it occupies a strategic “sweet spot” between high-fidelity classification and minimal surgical burden.

Cross-dataset generalization remains a significant challenge in automated sleep scoring, as models often exhibit a decline in accuracy when applied to unfamiliar data. While REST is not intended to replace the precision of a subject-specifically retrained model in all contexts, it offers a pragmatic alternative for common research scenarios where high-throughput analysis is required. As shown in Table 3, the model’s ability to maintain a Cohen’s kappa of 0.869 on external data indicates a degree of resilience to the signal variations introduced by different laboratory settings. This performance suggests that REST can serve as a functional baseline for investigators seeking to reduce the initial burden of manual data labeling without a catastrophic loss in classification quality. While retraining the model for a specific mouse strain remains the optimal approach, REST’s inherent generalization capabilities provide a “plug-and-play” option for researchers who do not possess extensive pre-labeled datasets. Furthermore, the intuitive graphical user interface (Figure 6) ensures that researchers without specialized expertise in neural networks and programming can easily process and score their sleep recordings.

Graphical interface (GUI) of REST inference. The REST inference interface allows users to process sleep recordings in EDF format, supporting both individual file analysis and batch processing. Users can manually select the desired EEG and EMG channels from the available signal inputs. This interface is specifically designed to be accessible for researchers who may find it difficult to navigate Python-based command-line tools.

Third, by utilizing a Transformer architecture, REST is structurally efficient and computationally accessible. In our implementation, REST was trained in under 24 hours using a mid-range consumer-grade GPU (NVIDIA RTX 4060 Ti), demonstrating that high-performance sleep stage classification is achievable without access to large-scale computing infrastructure. Once trained, REST can perform inference on new recordings using a standard Central Processing Unit (CPU), further enhancing its accessibility for laboratories without modern GPU hardware. While Bi-LSTM architectures—such as those used in MC-SleepNet [33], SWISC [26], and MLS-Net [27]—are popular for their ability to integrate forward and backward temporal context, REST’s Transformer architecture offers similar benefits through its multi-head attention mechanism. Furthermore, the Transformer’s parallelizable nature makes it computationally leaner and easier to deploy than recurrent architectures.

Application of REST on preclinical sleep investigations

Our analysis of CD-1 mice’s sleep based on the sleep score predicted by REST showed increased sleep duration that lasted about 2 hours following administration of Lemborexant. This observation closely aligns with the known characterization of Lemborexant when administered to mice [25]. This test demonstrates that REST can be a valid tool for preclinical sleep investigation in mice. REST-predicted sleep scores were accurate enough to enable detection of treatment-related changes in sleep architecture.

Limitation

REST was trained using a high-quality dataset containing minimal artifacts, representing less than 1% of all epochs. Consequently, the current model architecture is designed to classify three distinct physiological states: Wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep. Because the training data lacked a sufficient volume of labeled artifactual events, REST does not currently possess the native ability to distinguish signal artifacts from valid physiological data. This design choice was a strategic decision to establish a robust baseline for physiological sleep–wake architecture before introducing non-physiological signal noise, which is often subject to high inter-scorer variability and “label noise.”

This limitation necessitates that REST be applied primarily to EEG/EMG signals with high signal-to-noise ratios. In datasets where artifact contamination is prevalent, REST may misclassify these segments or fail to assign labels that accurately reflect the signal quality. To mitigate this in the current workflow, a threshold-based artifact detection algorithm can be implemented prior to REST to pre-label and exclude compromised epochs.

Furthermore, as demonstrated by the performance drop in DBA mice treated with kainic acid, REST does not currently generalize well to EEG signals characterized by prominent epileptiform activity. This performance degradation likely reflects the scarcity of such pathological patterns in the training set rather than a fundamental flaw in the Transformer architecture.

Future iterations of REST will focus on expanding its utility by incorporating large, pre-labeled datasets that include both diverse signal artifacts and pathological neural activity. Expanding the model to a four-class system—explicitly including artifact as a category—will broaden its applicability to studies where perfect signal quality cannot be guaranteed.

We did not perform a systematic ablation study of Transformer depth, the two-stage architecture, or STFT parameter choices. These design elements were adopted based on prior work in Transformer-based sleep classification and were selected to balance performance and computational efficiency. While ablation experiments could provide further insight into the contribution of individual components, they were beyond the scope of the present study, which focused on developing REST as a sleep classification tool with high accuracy.

REST was trained and validated only on mouse recordings. Therefore, its performance for sleep classification in other laboratory animals, such as rats, is unknown. Because differences in neurophysiology, sleep patterns, and EEG/EMG signals are typically larger between species than between different mouse strains or disease models [34], simply retraining without model tweaking might not be enough for REST to work with other species.

Conclusion

In summary, REST is a Transformer-based neural network model that can accurately and automatically classify mouse vigilance states into wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep using one EEG and one EMG channel. Although REST can differ from human scorers, it offers key advantages, including rapid scoring speed, consistency, and scalability. REST’s scoring accuracy is also on par with other established automated classifiers; however, it is to be noted that at the current state, REST lacks the native ability to detect artifacts. Furthermore, our application of REST to sleep analysis in a TBI model using CD-1 mice treated with Lemborexant demonstrates its practical utility as a preclinical research tool. REST was able to reliably detect treatment-induced changes in sleep architecture, highlighting its potential for use in pharmacological and disease-model studies where manual scoring would be prohibitively time-consuming.

In future work, we aim to extend REST’s utility beyond mouse recordings by adapting and evaluating the model on EEG/EMG datasets from other commonly used laboratory animals, such as rats. Given the physiological and spectral differences between species, we anticipate that additional training or model fine-tuning may be necessary to maintain classification accuracy. Another key goal is to improve REST’s robustness to signal artifacts. Future work will incorporate artifact-contaminated data into the training pipeline so that REST can learn to detect and label artifacts explicitly as an additional class. This enhancement would broaden REST’s usability to include studies where perfect signal quality cannot always be guaranteed.
