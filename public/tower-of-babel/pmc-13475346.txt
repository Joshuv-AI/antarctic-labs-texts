Spatial exploration of the impact of cattle on risk of malarial infection using data from a cluster randomised trial
Authors: Almudena Sanz Gutierrez, Rachel Otuko, Kexin Xie, Isaiah Omondi, Mercy Kariukiu, Paula Ruiz-Castillo, Caroline Wanjiku, Aurelia Brazeal, Mwanajuma Ngama, Truphena Onyango, Karisa Kazungu, Mary Mael, Winnie Wangari, Khadija Nuru, Isaac Ringera, Jamal Mbarak, Shadrack Mramba, Yegon Kibet, Naomi Nyambura, Scholastica Wanjiru, Felix Hammann, Francisco Saute, Joseph Mwangangi, N Regina Rabinovich, Marta Maia, Cassidy Rist, Carlos Chaccour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the context of stalled progress in reducing malaria burden and ongoing efforts to develop novel vector control methods, there is ongoing debate about the impact of livestock on malaria transmission. Zooprophylaxis refers to the use of livestock as an alternate source of blood that diverts mosquito vectors away from humans, potentially reducing malaria transmission. However, conflicting evidence highlights the concept of zoopotentiation, where livestock may instead increase vector populations and enhance malaria transmission. Prior studies that explore these concepts have examined livestock as a household asset, which does not account for the geospatial effect of animals owned by neighbours around the home.MethodsWe implemented a novel spatial approach to explore the impact of cattle on the risk of malarial infection in coastal Kenya. Using data with high granularity from a recent randomised controlled trial, we measured the presence of animals at increasing concentric areas around the household. We fit binomial regression models with generalised estimating equations to explore how cattle density and cattle-to-human-ratio measures were related to malaria prevalence in children 5–15 years old, accounting for relevant environmental and socio-economic variables.ResultsOur findings show that cattle density measured at 400, 500, and 600 m around the household (ORs: 1.63, 95% CI 1.20 to 2.21; 1.84, 95% CI 1.21 to 2.80 and 2.04, 95% CI 1.13 to 3.67, respectively) and cattle-to-human ratio at 500 and 600 m (ORs: 2.73, 95% CI 1.45 to 5.41 and 2.32, 95% CI 1.11 to 4.86, respectively) are significantly related to higher odds of testing positive for malaria. We did not find a significant association at closer distances (<400 m) or when cattle were evaluated only as a household asset.ConclusionTo better understand the impact of livestock on malaria transmission and potentially develop vector control strategies that account for these impacts, future research should consider animals as an environmental exposure and not only as a household asset.Keywords: Malaria, Global Health, Epidemiology, Kenya

Introduction

Malaria is a parasitic disease transmitted by Anopheles mosquitoes.1 Global efforts to control the disease led to a marked reduction in the disease burden from 2000 to 2015.1 However, progress has stalled since then, with the WHO reporting an increase of 52 million cases globally from 2015 to 2024.1 About 95% of malaria deaths occur in the African Region, where just over 75% of the deaths are among children under 5 years of age.1 The disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic to health systems worldwide can only partly explain the lost progress as there are many other factors hindering malaria control, including climate change, insecticide resistance, mosquito behavioural adaptations and antimalarial drug resistance, among others.1 Vector-related challenges are of special concern because previous successes in the fight against malaria have been largely attributed to the widespread implementation of vector control tools, mainly long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS).2 These core vector control tools work mostly against mosquitoes that bite humans indoors at night, which are the most efficient malaria vectors in most African settings.3 Nonetheless, most malaria vectors feed at least partially on animals (ie, they are partially zoophilic) and have important outdoor and crepuscular activity,4 being able to sustain residual malaria transmission even after good coverage and usage of all available vector control tools.3–5 Furthermore, the intensive use of LLINs and IRS has been associated with a shift towards outdoor, zoophilic behaviour.6–9 Considering this scenario, evidence suggests that the currently available tools will not be enough to control malaria and achieve elimination in all settings.10–12

Livestock keeping is an essential source of income and wealth, particularly in rural settings where malaria is more prevalent.13 Given the proclivities of some malaria vectors for animal hosts, there has been extensive exploration of the possibility of using livestock as zooprophylaxis for malaria, which means using the animals to attract mosquitoes, thus reducing the number of bites on humans. Some studies have found significant associations between livestock keeping and malaria reduction,14–16 supporting that approach. However, other studies have reported a significant increase in malaria prevalence related to owning cattle and/or pigs,17 18 and one study saw no association between malaria and livestock ownership at all.19 While mathematical models have explored the conditions under which a zooprophylaxis strategy might be efficacious,20 21 the two systematic reviews on the topic agree that there are context-specific key factors to consider, including the distance between where the humans sleep and where the animals are kept.22 23 The opposite effect of zooprophylaxis is zoopotentiation, where the presence of livestock enhances malaria transmission either by serving as an alternative and relatively unprotected blood source for mosquitoes, thus allowing to sustain the vector populations, or by contributing to the creation and sustainment of breeding sites where mosquitoes can reproduce, such as muddy pens full of puddles enriched with organic matter.24

The challenge with many studies investigating the role of livestock in malaria is that they examine the association based on livestock ownership at the household level, considering it similarly to other household-level variables such as housing materials or family assets. However, both zooprophylaxis and zoopotentiation require proximity of livestock to humans, which may not be directly associated with ownership. For example, in semiextensive livestock systems commonly found in malaria-endemic areas, families keep their livestock close to or within the homestead at night. As a result, where the houses are closely spaced, households that do not own livestock might still be impacted by the presence of animals owned by neighbours. Hence, it is perhaps more appropriate to shift the epidemiological framework and consider livestock as an environmental variable, similar to the presence of water bodies around the household, which impact vector life cycles and alter vector abundance. Livestock available as an alternative blood meal source for mosquitoes might contribute to changes in vector population size in a similar fashion. In other words, the impact of livestock on malaria transmission is more likely to depend on the number of animals living around humans than on the fact that the animals belong to any specific individual or household.

In this study, we use data collected during a community-level randomised controlled trial evaluating a novel vector control tool for malaria in coastal Kenya25 to explore the role of livestock on the risk of malaria infection and what factors might contribute to a zooprophylactic or zoopotentiation effect. We hypothesise that cattle presence around households is more predictive of malaria transmission than cattle ownership. To test this, we divert from previous studies on the topic and implement a novel spatial approach that considers cattle density and cattle-to-human-ratio in increasing concentric areas around the household, regardless of who owns the animals. That is, livestock is explored as an environmental exposure rather than a family asset.

Methods

Study area

Kwale county is located on the south coast of Kenya, bordering Tanzania to the south and divided into five administrative subcounties. The study was conducted in selected areas of Ramisi ward, in Msambweni subcounty and Pongwe Kikoneni ward, in Lunga-Lunga subcounty. Kwale experiences a predominantly semi-arid climate, with two rainy seasons, the ‘long rains’ from March to June and the ‘short rains’ from late October to December, which have significant variations each year and were subject to strong influence by El Niño phenomenon in 2023. The county has the highest malaria burden in coastal Kenya26 with infection prevalence in children under 5 years of age at 37.9% in 2017.27 The disease is driven by three main vectors: Anopheles arabiensis, Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) and Anopheles funestus,28 some of which have been shown to exhibit zoophagic behaviour.29 30 Vector control is solely based on LLINs. Prior to this study, Kwale had last received mass distribution of pyrethroid-only, WHO-prequalified LLINs in 2021. The county’s agriculture sector is based on subsistence farming and accounts for 80.6% of families’ income.31 The human population of Kwale was projected to be 1 914 796 in 2022.30 The county was estimated to hold 190 988 zebu cattle, 5475 dairy cross cattle, 3 371 126 goats, 54 578 sheep and 725 000 poultry in 2018. Livestock are kept for both food and income generation and contribute around 25% of the county’s income.31

Data collection

Our study was part of the BOHEMIA (Broad One Health Endectocide-based Malaria Intervention in Africa) trial, an open-label, assessor-blinded, controlled, cluster-randomised trial conducted in Kwale, Kenya. The trial’s objective was to determine the safety and efficacy of ivermectin mass drug administration (MDA) to reduce malaria transmission.32 The creation of the 84 clusters was started in random households and included a core area encompassing about 50 households and a 400-metre buffer around it. Cluster creation was not restricted nor stratified by location or any other criteria, making the study sample geographically representative. In June and July 2023, prior to the start of the trial, key sociodemographic data including livestock ownership and the location of all households within the borders of the trial clusters was collected. A wealth index was calculated for each household based on these data following methods presented by Xie et al.33 For this analysis, the index tertiles were used to categorise the population into low, medium and high wealth index ranks.

Outcome measure: malaria prevalence

During the trial, a cohort of randomly selected children between 5 and 15 years old living in the core of each cluster was followed prospectively with monthly rapid diagnostic malaria tests (RDTs) for 7 months starting with the first round of MDA. Given that the data on livestock ownership were collected before the trial started, we used infection prevalence by RDT at the baseline visit as a proxy for malaria infection risk as this was the closest epidemiological data point to the demography study. Using baseline prevalence as the outcome measure also avoids inclusion of any effect of the trial intervention on malaria transmission.

Cattle as an environmental exposure

We decided to focus this study on cattle, the second most common livestock species in the county, because they accounted for over twice the proportion of blood meals than goats, the only more abundant species, in the bordering county of Taita-Taveta (33.9% vs 13.8%).28 Variables collected during the demographic study included livestock ownership and the number of animals owned by the household. To explore the concept of exposure to cattle, we relied on that survey data and counted the number of cattle owned by homesteads around the households where children in the cohort being tested for malaria lived. From that information, cattle density (cattle per m2) and cattle-to-human ratio were calculated within the cluster and in concentric areas of 50-metre, 100-metre, 200-metre, 400-metre, 500-metre and 600-metre radiuses around each child’s household (figure 1).

These measures of livestock exposure assume that cattle stay close to the household and, therefore, their validity is impacted by cattle mobility. According to the demographic data, most households (>60%) kept their cattle within 50 m of the household at night (table 1) and most households (∼98%) had their animals grazing during the day. To inform the household proximity assumption, additional cattle mobility data was collected from 70 herds in the study area. Herds were tracked for 2 weeks with a Global Positioning System device, set to record precise locational information once every 2 min while moving and to turn off when stationary. Each herd was tracked for approximately 2 weeks.34 Preliminary analysis of data collected over 522 days, spanning 1–21 days of data collection per herd (mean: 8 days), found that on average herds stay 160.8 m from the household at night (median: 36.7 m), spend the day 102.6 m from the household while grazing (median: 38.8 m) and travel up to 777.2 m once a day to be watered (median: 635.5 m). In addition, 75% of the herds had a 90% utilisation distribution home range of 0.322 km2 or lower. This supports the assumption that cattle do spend most of their time either close to the household or within the spatial distances used in this study. These data are presented in more detail in the online supplemental materials (1. Substudy on herd mobility: methods and key findings, online supplemental Table S1).

The incremental radiuses were developed relevant to the anopheline flying ranges documented in a local mark–release–recapture experiment. In coastal Kenya, Midega et al recaptured most An. gambiae and An. funestus at 200 m and 400 m from the release spot, with minimum distances of 167 m and maximum of 661 m.35 Guerra et al generated a database gathering the results of mosquito mark–release–recapture experiments globally. In this database, the study by Midega et al is the only one reported for Anopheles species in Kenya.36 Because no data was collected from households outside the study clusters, areas that extended too far beyond the cluster border would not be informative. As a result, the maximum distance was set at 600 m around the household, as it covered up to the cluster border in most cases and was aligned with the local mark-release-recapture study.35

In summary, four livestock exposure-measuring variables were used in the analysis: (1) whether the household owned any cattle, (2) how many, (3) the cattle-to-human ratio, and (4) the number of cattle per m2, with the last two measured within the cluster and in 50-metre, 100-metre, 200-metre, 400-metre, 500-metre, 600-metre radius areas around the household. To account for human presence, the number of people per m2 living in the cluster and in the same concentric areas around each household was also calculated and incorporated into the analysis.

Environmental variables

To account for environmental factors influencing vector population, we included the following variables in the models: precipitation, temperature, Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and presence of water. Rainfall data was downloaded from National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)’s Integrated Multi-satellitE Retrievals for Global Precipitation Measurement, which provides monthly accumulation in mm with a resolution of 0.1 degrees (11.1 km × 11.1 km approximately).37 Average monthly day and night land surface temperature with a resolution of 0.5 degrees (5.6 km × 5.6 km) were also obtained online from Terra Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer.38 These monthly measures were assigned to each household based on their location. NDVI was calculated by measuring the difference between near-infrared and red light from data derived from the Landsat 7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus, and Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager/Thermal Infrared Sensor with 30 m × 30 m resolution. To determine the presence of water, Sentinel-2 Water Index (SWI) was calculated. SWI measures the difference between the shortwave infrared value and the red edge value in data derived from the Sentinel-2, MultiSpectral Instrument, Level-2A with 10 m × 10 m resolution. From SWI values, presence of humidity (SWI >0.0 and <0.2) or water bodies (SWI ≥0.2 and ≤1) was determined. For more details on the processing of these data see supplementary material (2. Processing of NDVI and SWI measures, online supplemental figures S3 and S4).

Data analysis

Binomial regression models with generalised estimating equation (GEE) and an exchangeable correlation structure were fitted to account for clustering. Due to the very low values (3– 5 orders of magnitude below one) of both the cattle and human density variables, they were rescaled by multiplying them by 104. This facilitates the interpretation of the results by allowing model coefficients to reflect changes over a more meaningful scale.

First, bivariate relationships were explored for all livestock-related variables, human density variables, environmental variables and other covariates which included sex, age, household size, whether the child slept under a bed net the previous night, wealth index rank and study assignment (treatment or control). Second, one multivariate model per livestock exposure variable—livestock ownership, number of animals owned, cattle densities and cattle-to-human ratios—was fitted, including the covariates that showed a statistically significant relationship with malaria prevalence in the univariate models. The models evaluating cattle density accounted for human density corresponding to the same concentric area too. This was done to ensure that models evaluating each measure of cattle exposure accounted for human presence, as both species (humans and cattle) contribute significantly to blood meals locally.29 All models were fitted using the function glmgee from the glmtoolbox package V.0.1.12 and the final models were selected using a backward elimination approach with Quasi-Likelihood Information Criterion Approximation using the stepCriterion function from the same package.

For all univariate and multivariate models, OR and 95% CIs are reported. This study tests independent models at various concentric ring distances using the same data, which can easily lead to false positives. To minimise this issue, the hypothesis testing was adjusted via Benjamini-Hochberg correction, which yields stricter p values. Significance was set at 0.05. Both receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and randomised quantile residuals were computed for all models. ROC curves are used in logistic regression to evaluate the model’s ability to discriminate between the two outcome classes, in this case positive versus negative malaria tests, by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate. The area under the curve (AUC) ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates poor discrimination ability, 1 indicates the model has perfect discrimination ability and 0.5 indicates random assignment. In this study, the models were fitted with a random selection of 70% of the dataset, while the other 30% of cases were used to compute the ROC curves of the models. Randomised quantile residuals provide robust measures of goodness of fit of models for which traditional residuals would not be useful, such as logistic regression models. Goodness of fit was assessed by plotting each model’s randomised quantile residuals against its fitted values, as well as their quantile–quantile plot. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess potential non-linearity and to confirm that spatial autocorrelation of malaria prevalence between nearby households was being addressed by the GEE approach. These are presented in the supplementary materials (8. Sensitivity analyses). All analyses were done in R (V.4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, http://www.R-project.org, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Engagement activities were done with guidance from the KEMRI community liaison group and a KEMRI Community Engagement Advise for Studies group. Investigators engaged with the Kwale county Ministry of Health officials first to describe the study. Once county approval was obtained, the team engaged other community stakeholders such as chiefs, ward administrators, public health officers and the community health worker platform. Community members were informed about the study through community ‘barazas’ in study villages where, respecting COVID-19 regulations and institutional health and safety guidelines, chiefs, subchiefs, community representatives and local officials were be informed about the study and community concerns were addressed.

Results

Table 1 shows the population characteristics as per the demography study conducted prior to the trial. Around 18% (n=1084) of the households owned cattle and, on average, cattle-owning households had seven animals (range 1–115). When asked about cattle management during the day and at night, most households reported that their cattle spent the day grazing (98.4%, n=1043) and were kept outdoors at night (99.06%, n=1050). Of the cattle that spent the day grazing, they mostly stayed within a 30-minute walk from the household (71.43%, n=745), while most households keeping the cattle outdoors at night reported doing so within 50 m of the household (61.24%, n=643). At the baseline visit of the trial, 71.65% (n=1749) of the children aged 5–15 years reported having slept under a net the previous night, although only 50.39% of them had done so consistently every night in the last week (table 1). During this visit, 30.19% of them tested positive for malaria. The short rains had not started when baseline data was collected and the temperature was homogeneous across the study area, while NDVI spanned from 0.46 to 0.78. The spatial variability of malaria prevalence and the environmental covariables are presented in the supplementary material (3. Spatial heterogeneity of study variables) (online supplemental figures S5 and S6).

Regarding the variables exploring exposure to livestock, cattle and human density were highest within a 50-metre radius of the household, with a median of 2.52 cattle per 10 000 m2 and 25.20 people per 10 000 m2 (figure 2). Both density measures then decreased as distance from the household increased, with 0.62 cattle per 10 000 m2 and 2.02 humans per 10 000 m2 at the 600-metre radius. On the contrary, the cattle-to-human ratio was lowest within a 50-metre radius of the household (0.07 cattle/humans) and increased with distance, reaching a median of 0.30 in the 600-metre radius area (figure 2). The heterogeneity of the cattle population across the study area is described in the supplementary material (3. Spatial heterogeneity of study variables) (online supplemental figure S7). Plots of livestock measures against malaria prevalence are presented in the supplementary materials (4. Plots of livestock predictors against malaria prevalence) (online supplemental figure S8).

The results of the univariate models are presented in supplementary materials (5. Univariate models) (online supplemental Table S2). Cattle ownership, number of cattle owned, cattle density and cattle-to-human ratio did not significantly correlate with malaria prevalence. Human density variables at all distances except at 50 m around the household had a negative correlation with malaria prevalence, with the most protective effect occurring at 600 m, where each unit increase in humans per 10 000 m2 was associated with 0.33 lower odds of testing positive for malaria (OR: 0.77, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.88). Presence of water bodies 500 m around the household (OR: 0.37, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.74), as well as sleeping under a bed net the night before (OR: 0.43, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.56), higher temperatures at night (OR: 0.28, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.56) and living in a medium wealth (OR: 0.68, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.92) or high wealth rank (OR: 0.67, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.91) household appeared to have a protective effect, while more rain accumulation (OR: 1.11, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.14) and being male (OR: 1.25, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.48) showed a harmful effect. Models evaluating land surface temperature during the day, NDVI, study assignment, age and number of household members did not yield statistically significant associations. The AUC of the ROC of these univariate models ranged from 0.47 to 0.67.

A total of 16 multivariate models were fitted, each one including one of the livestock exposure variables: cattle ownership, number of animals owned, cattle density at the cluster and 50-metre, 100-metre, 200-metre, 400-metre, 500-metre and 600-metre radius areas around the household; and cattle-to-human ratios in the same areas. All 16 models included the statistically significant covariates of the univariate models: presence of water, night temperature, sleeping under a bed the night before, wealth index rank, rain accumulation and sex. The models evaluating cattle density included variables measuring human density in the same area too. The final models for which the livestock predictor of interest was significant are shown in table 2. All final models can be found in the supplementary material (6. All multivariate models) (online supplemental Table S3). Cattle ownership and number of cattle owned remained unrelated to malaria prevalence when considering the covariables. However, cattle density and cattle-to-human ratio were significantly associated with higher odds of malaria prevalence when considering the covariables, although not in all concentric areas around the household. Cattle density per 10 000 m2 in areas of 400-metre, 500-metre and 600-metre radius around the household was associated with 0.63, 0.84 and 1.04 higher odds of malaria prevalence, respectively, keeping all other variables constant (OR:1.63, 95% CI 1.20 to 2.21; 1.84, 95% CI 1.21 to 2.80; and 2.04, 95% CI 1.13 to 3.67, respectively). In these models, higher rain accumulation and being male were also related to higher odds of malaria prevalence, while human density, land surface temperature at night and presence of water bodies 500 m around the household were protective. Children living in households with higher cattle-to-human ratios in the 500-metre and 600-metre radius areas had 1.73 and 1.32 higher odds of testing positive for malaria, respectively, after adjusting for the covariables (OR: 2.73, 95% CI 1.45 to 5.16; and 2.32, 95% CI 1.11 to 4.86, respectively). In this set of models, higher rain accumulation and being male were also related to higher odds of malaria prevalence, whereas presence of water bodies 500 m around the household and having slept under a bed net the night before were protective against testing positive for malaria. Wealth index rank was not significantly associated with baseline malaria infection after adjusting for other covariables. The AUC of the ROC of these multivariate models ranged from 0.71 to 0.74, reflecting their ability to better discriminate between positive and negative malaria cases. Based on the randomised quantile residuals plots for these multivariate models (online supplemental material 7. Multivariate models residuals diagnostic online supplemental figure S9), we were confident that they complied with the generalised linear model assumptions. Sensitivity analyses assessing the linearity assumption and spatial autocorrelation confirmed the robustness of these findings (online supplemental material 8. Sensitivity analyses, online supplemental Tables S4-S6).

Discussion

Leveraging data from a randomised controlled trial, we present a novel approach to evaluate exposure to livestock as a risk factor for malaria. To our knowledge, this is the first study that goes beyond ownership at the household level and considers livestock as an environmental exposure. Our hypothesis that cattle presence around the household would be more relevant for malaria prevalence than cattle ownership was confirmed. In this rural region of coastal Kenya, household level variables of livestock ownership did not have a significant association with malaria prevalence. However, by measuring cattle density (animals per m2) and cattle-to-human ratio, we uncovered a potential zoopotentiation effect of cattle on malaria prevalence, which was statistically significant in concentric areas of 400–600 m radius from the household, but not in closer ranges.

Despite the considerable amount of evidence on the topic, all studies investigating the role of livestock in malaria cases have focused solely on livestock owned by the household. When distances are regarded, they are limited to how far the animals are tethered or kept within the homestead (ie, a few metres away).19 39 One might assume that our finding of a zoopotentiation effect aligns with that of Bouma and Rowland17, where higher parasite prevalence was associated with families keeping cattle and villages with a higher proportion of cattle-owning families.17 Similarly, a cross-sectional study in Mozambique identified pig-keeping as a risk factor for malaria prevalence.18 Zooprophylaxis, however, was found in a trial in Ethiopia, where decreased malaria incidence rate was associated with livestock ownership,15 as well as in two other cross-sectional studies in Uganda14 and in the Democratic Republic of Congo.16 Because we do not observe any impact from cattle <400 m from the household, we can really only say that our results are consistent with a cohort study in Gambia that found no significant difference in the prevalence of malaria between children sleeping <20 m or >50 m from cattle.19

Entomological studies have also tried to assess the relationship between livestock and malaria vectors’ density and behaviour. While Anophelines were more commonly found in homes with cattle in Lao PDR,40 the impact of livestock is not homogeneous across species. An. arabiensis seems to consistently increase its numbers and reduce its human biting in households with animals, while An. funestus is similarly influenced by animals but to a lesser extent and An. gambiae is generally insensitive to livestock.39 41 42 Interestingly, a recent study in Tanzania looked at the distance between homes and livestock pens within the homestead but found no significant association with mosquito density,39 consistent with the lack of impact in the ranges under 400 m that we found here. Several modelling studies20 21 43 and systematic reviews22 23 on the topic coincide in pointing out that the relationship between malaria transmission and livestock is non-linear and highly context specific. In light of our results, we hypothesise that the influence of livestock on vector behaviour is spatially dependent and that this dependence is related to the mosquito flying range (figure 3). We found that cattle density per 10 000 m2 and cattle-to-human ratio 400 to 600 m around the home were associated with higher odds of testing positive for malaria, while this association was not significant in closer proximity to the household. Because these distances are not too different from local mosquito flying ranges,35 we theorise that higher cattle density and cattle-to-human-ratio may positively influence mosquito populations only in the area covered by the mosquito flying range. Higher mosquito density might then lead to an increase in malaria transmission (ie, zoopotentiation) limited to the population living in that area, regardless of whether they own the cattle. The figure presents a visual summary of study findings along with mosquito flying range based on Midega et al35 (yellow area), and a conceptual model for the epidemiological framework: higher cattle density and cattle-to-human-ratio may positively influence mosquito populations only in the area covered by the mosquito’s flying range. This might then lead to an increase in malaria transmission limited to the population living in that area, regardless of whether they own cattle.

In our study, the median cattle density in the cluster fell between those for the 400-metre and 500-metre radius areas around the household, and the median cattle-to-human-ratio in the cluster was similar to the ratio in the 600-metre radius area (figure 2). Surprisingly, these cattle exposure variables measured around the household showed significant associations with malaria prevalence while the ones at the cluster level did not (online supplemental Table S3). We suggest that this further supports the idea that livestock plays an environmental role in enhancing malaria transmission: although all children tested for malaria were selected from households in the cluster core, the full area of a cluster is not representative of the area around the household of a child living closer to the cluster edge, hence providing a diluted measure of the environmental effect of cattle.

We found a protective effect of human density (people per 10 000 m2) at all distances except for the 50-metre radius area models (online supplemental Table S3). The number of people living in the household also showed no association with malaria prevalence (online supplemental Table S2). Together, these results suggest that more populated areas have lower malarial infection risk, when keeping all other variables constant, even if short-range population density (ie, within the homestead) does not seem to make a difference. We also found that the presence of water bodies 500 m around the household was consistently associated with lower odds of testing positive for malaria (table 2), which is counterintuitive because water bodies serve as mosquito breeding sites. We explored potential multicollinearity concerns that might be masking the actual effect of water bodies but did not find any issues. Of the children who tested negative for malaria, 6.3% lived in a household with a water body 500 m around, while only 1.6% of the children who tested positive did so. We do not have a hypothesis to explain this phenomenon. Regarding the rest of covariables, the protective impact of net usage and harmful impact of higher monthly rain accumulation (table 2) were expected. Boys were consistently more likely to test positive for malaria than girls (table 2), which might be related to local gender roles. It seems that the potential for zooprophylaxis or zoopotentiation is context-specific, highlighting the need to understand local vector and social dynamics in their evaluation.

Several limitations have narrowed the impact of our analysis. To truly understand transmission dynamics, malaria incidence would have been the ideal outcome of interest. Having malaria prevalence as the outcome makes it impossible to reach any conclusions about transmission from these findings, and that should be the target of new studies on this topic. This study, however, provides a key foundational analysis for future work. We also do not present any entomological data to support our findings and help create a more accurate picture of how livestock may influence malaria transmission. By analysing cattle exposure only, we might be leaving out the effect of other livestock species such as small ruminants, pigs or poultry, which would have ideally been included here to achieve a comprehensive assessment of the impact of livestock on malaria. However, given local blood meal analyses29 that were performed prior to our study, we decided the selection of cattle was most appropriate for this initial analysis. In addition, because the data used for this study was collected before the short rains started, our results cannot be directly extrapolated to the long rainy season, when most malaria transmission takes place. We also acknowledge that, because some of the 600-metre radius areas expanded over clusters borders, this analysis might be impacted by the lack of data outside the clusters. Given all these limitations, and because this is an observational exploratory study, there might be residual confounding and, therefore, the findings presented here should be interpreted with caution.

Livestock husbandry is a crucial economic activity worldwide, especially in rural settings. Animals have direct impacts on human health, both negative when they mediate disease transmission and positive by alleviating food insecurity, supporting livelihoods and providing resources that can be reinvested towards the household’s health needs.13 44 The results presented here suggest that cattle presence at the community level might increase malaria risk. Therefore, populations where livestock keeping is prevalent may benefit from additional protection by means of targeted interventions. Beyond these findings, this study is the first one to use an environmental epidemiology approach to the question of livestock and malaria. While malaria transmission dynamics are always context-specific, here we have opened the door to a novel methodology that may prove valuable for assessing livestock-based malaria control strategies.45 Furthermore, this framework could inform the longstanding debate between zooprophylaxis and zoopotentiation by assuming that livestock is not only a household asset but also an environmental factor determining vector dynamics.
