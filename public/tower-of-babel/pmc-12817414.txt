The reliability of answers from four different AI chatbots on periodontology theoretical exam questions: an evaluation in dental education
Authors: Cenker Zeki Koyuncuoglu, Ahmet Hamdi Selcuker, Erdem Ozyilmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDentistry is a profession affected by modern technology, materials, and societal events like the pandemic, leading to an academic field that continuously evolves in both practice and education. Consequently, advancements include the extensive implementation of digital dentistry, the incorporation of remote instruction into dental training throughout the pandemic, and the investigation of optimal AI integration within the dental profession and education are necessary. This study evaluated the reliability of answers provided by four different major artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot using 125 periodontology exam questions administered between 2018−2023.MethodsThis study used closed-ended questions retrieved from the official archives of the Department of Periodontology, Faculty of Dentistry, Istanbul Aydin University originally included in exams given to 3rd, 4th, and 5th-year students between 2018 and 2023. These questions were then posed to AI chatbots for evaluation. These include 92 of the questions are true/false, 8 are fill-in-the-blank, 22 are multiple-choice, and 3 are calculation questions. Questions were asked to each AI chatbot (ChatGPT-4o mini, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini Advance, and CoPilot Pro) twice, with a one-month interval, and evaluated on a binary scoring system. Before the questions were asked to the AI chatbots, the chat histories and cookies were cleared from the user interfaces, and a previously unused e-mail address was used to log in. The questions were asked one at a time, and the next question was not asked until the previous one was answered. The NCSS (Number Cruncher Statistical System) 2007 (Kaysville, Utah, USA) program was used for statistical analyses. While evaluating the study data, descriptive statistical methods were used. In the comparison of qualitative data across three or more periods, the Cochran’s-Q test was used, and the Mc Nemar test was used for post hoc analyses. Statistical significance level was set at p < 0.01 and p < 0.05 levels.ResultsCoPilot Pro achieved the highest accuracy rate both on Day-0 (73.6%) and after one month (75.2%). When comparing the performance of AI chatbots on Day-0 and Month-1, no statistically significant difference was found. However, GPT-4o mini performed significantly worse than the other three AI chatbots at both time points (p < 0.05). The performance of GPT-4o was the most inconsistent, as 19 questions answered correctly in the first round were answered incorrectly in the second round.ConclusionThe findings underscore the need for critical evaluation of AI tools before their adoption in dental education. While AI chatbots can support dental education, their use should be carefully guided and complemented by clinical experience, critical appraisal of information sources, and academic oversight to ensure professional competence and responsible integration into learning processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07387-z.

Introduction

The ability of machines to exhibit a form of intelligence is called artificial intelligence (AI) [1]. Nowadays, artificial intelligence has created a new paradigm that affects not only fields like science and technology but also dentistry [2]. AI software is used to mimic the intellectual behaviors of humans, such as problem-solving and cognitive skills, in machines/computers.1 AI enables the simultaneous analysis of large amounts of data [3] and recently, it is frequently used in the field of medicine and in the education of medical fields [4]. Dentistry is a profession that is influenced by current technologies, materials, and social events such as the pandemic, making it a field that continuously updates itself in both practice and education. Therefore, developments such as the widespread adoption of digital dentistry and the integration of remote education into dental training during the pandemic, as well as the research into the most efficient integration of AI into the dental profession and education, are being explored [5].

The artificial intelligence chatbot named “ChatGPT” was introduced to the world on November 30, 2022, by the organization “Open AI” with the aim of improving human-AI interaction [6]. The wide range of applications of this AI allowed the chatbot named “ChatGPT” to quickly become popular among students and teachers shortly after its introduction to the market. Following this situation, many researchers have expressed concerns about the limitations of this AI chatbot and how students and teachers should interact with it in order to better and more accurately obtain the information they need [7, 8]. Google® launched the AI chatbot named “Bard” in May 2023, making it accessible in 180 countries. Later, Google® announced the advanced version of “Bard,” called “Gemini,” as its largest and most capable AI chatbot [9]. The use of these AI chatbots in the fields of medicine and dentistry, like in many other professions, is becoming increasingly widespread day by day [4]. Microsoft Copilot is an AI chatbot developed and launched in 2023, supported by technologies similar to those behind OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini [10].

The number of studies on the use of AI in healthcare has rapidly increased in the last 10 years, but there are not enough studies related to AI in dental literature. Although the development of AI is seen as promising, there is a significant question mark today regarding the accuracy of the answers provided by AI to the questions asked. The information obtained through AI can be far from accurate and potentially harmful [1]. Substantial discussions exist over the precision, neutrality, and ethical dependability of the outputs produced by AI models. McGrath, et.all [11] (2025) examine the ethical implications of artificial intelligence in oral health care, highlighting the necessity for awareness over data security, algorithmic bias, digital inequality, and collective accountability, especially for marginalized populations. This approach highlights the necessity for ethical consciousness and methodological prudence in employing artificial intelligence chatbots for academic evaluations in periodontology.

Various literatures show that the quality of answers and the accuracy rates of responses given by AI chat models to questions about different branches of dentistry vary significantly [12, 13]. Furthermore, it was emphasized that dentists must recognize the limitations of using AI chatbots and that these chatbots should not replace dental professionals [12].

At present, AI chatbots are being used for dentistry teaching and clinical decision-making procedures. AI chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini, or CoPilot help students in evaluating their theoretical knowledge, analyzing clinical cases, and preparing for examinations. Studies demonstrate that the accuracy, consistency, and scientific validity of replies generated by these systems differ markedly between fields [14, 15]. Periodontology presents a unique combination of theoretical knowledge and clinical decision-making, making it a suitable domain to assess the capabilities and limitations of generative AI in dental education.Recent researches indicate that various artificial intelligence models exhibit markedly varying accuracy rates on periodontology examination questions [16, 17].

Periodontology was selected as the focus of this study because it represents a specialized and clinically significant domain within dentistry. As periodontologists and educators, we were particularly interested in evaluating how generative AI performs in a field that combines theoretical depth with diagnostic complexity. This choice also aligns with our academic expertise and allows for meaningful benchmarking against expert-level expectations.

In accordance with this information, this research aim to evaluate the accuracy of the responses given by AI chatbots to the midterm and final periodontology exam questions from exams conducted between 2018 and 2023. The questions will be posed to the different AI chatbots “ChatGPT-4o,” “ChatGPT-4omini,” “Gemini Advance,” and “CoPilot Pro,” and the accuracy of their responses will be compared to determine which AI chatbot answered the questions correctly. Our secondary aim is to determine how much the answers given by AI chatbots to questions will change within a month.

Methods

The study did not require ethical committee approval and compliance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration as it did not involve human and animal subjects. This study used closed-ended questions retrieved from the official archives of the Department of Periodontology, Faculty of Dentistry, Istanbul Aydin University originally included in exams given to 3rd, 4th, and 5th-year students between 2018 and 2023. These questions were then posed to AI chatbots for evaluation. These include 92 of the questions are true/false, 8 are fill-in-the-blank, 22 are multiple-choice, and 3 are calculation questions. When repeated questions were identified, they were excluded from the evaluation. As a result, 25 questions were removed, and a total of 125 questions were included in the study. Closed-ended questions were prefered because they allow for objective and unambiguous evaluation. Each question has a clearly defined correct answer, which facilitates direct comparison across AI chatbot responses. In contrast, open-ended questions would require subjective interpretation, variable-length responses, and expert calibration to assess accuracy. This would necessitate blinded scoring by multiple experts and a more complex statistical framework, which was beyond the scope of this study.

All questions and their corresponding answer keys were originally prepared and validated by faculty members of the Department of Periodontology at Istanbul Aydın University during the respective exam periods. The correct answers used in this study were based on these official answer keys. To ensure consistency, all correct responses were re-verified by one of the authors (CZK) and involved in the original preparation and administration of the exams. Since the questions were closed-ended and the correct options were predefined, no additional expert consensus or calibration was required, and kappa statistics were not applicable.

ChatGPT-4o mini, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini Advanced, and Copilot Pro were selected because they represent the most widely accessible and commonly used AI chatbots among students and professionals. Three of these models are paid versions, chosen to evaluate their full capabilities. However, since many students tend to use free models, we also included the free version of ChatGPT to reflect real-world usage patterns. In addition, these chatbots are also frequently cited in the literature, making them suitable for comparative analysis and future reference [11, 13].

The AI chatbots included in the study were selected as ChatGPT-4o mini, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini Advance, and CoPilot Pro. Of these programs, only ChatGPT-4o mini is available for free public use.

The one-month interval was selected as a practical timeframe to observe whether any changes occurred in the responses of AI chatbots over time. While a longer interval could have been chosen, we aimed to assess whether even within a relatively brief period, updates or shifts in model behavior might be detectable. This decision was not based on a specific theoretical framework but rather on feasibility and relevance. We have acknowledged this limitation in the manuscript and consider longer intervals for future research.

Questions were asked to the AI chatbots on 15.12.2024 and 15.01.2025 by the researcher (AHS) with the addition of the text “Can you answer the following question?” at the beginning of the questions. No additional prompt engineering was used beyond this standardized phrasing. Before the questions were asked to the AI chatbots, the chat histories and cookies were cleared from the user interfaces, and a previously unused e-mail address was used to log in. The questions were asked one at a time, and the next question was not asked until the previous one was answered. The answers given by the AI chatbots have been evaluated on a 1 − 0 scale: A “1” response means correct, a “0” response means wrong. The incorrect answers were not evaluated based on how close they were to the correct answer, and all incorrect answers were considered equally unsuccessful. Similarly, study did not evulate the references used by AI chatbots when providing answers.

Statistical analysis

The NCSS (Number Cruncher Statistical System) 2007 (Kaysville, Utah, USA) program was used for statistical analyses. While evaluating the study data, descriptive statistical methods were used. In the comparison of qualitative data across three or more periods, the Cochran’s-Q test was used, and the Mc Nemar test was used for post hoc analyses. Statistical significance level was set at p < 0.01 and p < 0.05 levels.

Discussion

In recent years, AI chatbots have been frequently used in many areas of dentistry, just as in every field. The rapid developments in the field of AI have been shown to provide various advantages to medical personnel, including better diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of diseases [18–20]. It has been seen that AI chatbots are used not only in areas such as treatment and prosthesis planning, and patient follow-up, but also by students and teachers to create presentations, gain knowledge about lessons, and even prepare for exams [21–23]. However, since the main purpose of developing these AI chatbots is not to answer questions related to medicine and dentistry, they do not possess the necessary competence to solve the complex complicated problems in medicine and dentistry [24–26]. In our study, the accuracy of the answers provided by three leading paid AI chatbots (ChatGPT 4.0, Google Gemini Advance, Microsoft CoPilot Pro) and one free version (ChatGPT-4o mini) to periodontology exam questions were compared among themselves and over time. Additionally, in our study, the rate of self-improvement of the AI chatbots was evaluated by asking the same questions twice with a one-month interval to see if the accuracy rate changed. It is known that the responses given by AI chatbots to questions can vary based on previous conversations with the AI chatbot [13, 27]. To prevent this, questions were asked to AI chatbots in our study using an email address that had never been used before. Before asking questions to the AI chatbots, all browsing history and cookies were cleared.

In our study, when looking at the accuracy of answers given by AI chatbots to questions, ChatGPT-4o mini showed the weakest statistically significant result, followed by ChatGPT-4o, Google Gemini Advance, and Microsoft CoPilot Pro. After one month, the accuracy rates for ChatGPT-4o mini and ChatGPT-4o decreased, but those for Google Gemini Advance and Microsoft CoPilot Pro increased. The fact that other AI chatbots, apart from ChatGPT, have improved to some extent within a month, but both versions of ChatGPT provided lower accuracy in the second usage, indicates that the accuracy of the responses given by AI chatbots to questions should be carefully evaluated today. In the literature, studies comparing different AI chatbots have also reported significant differences between AI models [12, 13, 28].

In a study testing 4 AI chatbots (ChatGPT-4o, MedGebra GPT-4o, Meta LIama 3, and Gemini Advanced) with a one-week interval, no significant difference was found among the total correct answers of the AI engines within themselves [13]. In the study, 4-option multiple-choice endodontics questions were asked to AI chat engines. They reported that the most successful engine in terms of total correct answers was MedGebra GPT-4o with 93%, and only MedGebra GPT-4o showed a decrease in the total number of correct answers, while the remaining AI chat engines showed an increase in the total number of correct answers [13]. However, it is stated that the MedGebra GPT-4o AI chatbot used in this study was trained before the questions were asked [13]. In a study evaluating the Perio-GPT AI chatbot with five different AI chatbots (ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4, ChatGPT-4o, ScholarGPT, and Gemini AI) [18] AI chatbots were examined using a multiple-choice test and answers based on 71 exams on ‘Periodontal Etiology and Pathogenesis’ from the American Academy of Periodontology’s (AAP) 2023 and 2024 “In-Service Examination.” As a result of the study, it was observed that the PerioGPT AI chatbot, specifically trained for the field of periodontology, provided a higher percentage of correct answers (81.16%) compared to others. The accuracy rates of the other AI chatbots were observed to be lower compared to PerioGPT. The highest accuracy rate among the AI chatbots used in our study is 75.2% for CoPilot Pro in the first month. This significant difference in correct answer rates can be explained by the fact that the AI chatbots used in our study had not been previously trained, and in the tests conducted from Day-0 to Month-1, all browsing history and cookies were cleared, and tests were conducted using new email addresses with questions belonging to different fields.

In a study where 10 open-ended periodontology questions were asked to 4 AI models, it was concluded that the AI models’ answers were sufficient [12]. In another study [29] it has been argued that the answers produced by AI chatbots to open-ended questions are at least of the same quality as those given by humans, if not better. Additionally, this study noted that the responses of AI chatbots lack reasoning and personal experience compared to students’ answers. This result may lead to questioning the accuracy of the answers given by AI chatbots to reasoning-required questions (such as calculating attachment loss) in student exams. Additionally, the lack of critical thinking in AI chatbots has the potential to dull the critical thinking abilities of students who use these resources while studying [29]. However, there is also data in the literature showing that when AI chatbots are used analytically and clearly, they have the educational potential to help students or professionals develop skills such as communication, problem-solving, and critical thinking that are needed in clinical practices [12, 30–32].

In a study examining the success of Bard AI, the precursor of Google Gemini, and ChatGPT in various medical disciplines such as otolaryngology, cardiology, and urology [33], it was noted that AI chatbots provided correct answers to 57% of the questions asked. However, the same study also states that the accuracy rate of the AI chatbots in answering multiple-choice questions was 34%. In another study, it was reported that higher rates of correct answers were given to multiple-choice questions [13]. In our study, a comparison could not be made because there were not enough multiple-choice questions for comparison to obtain meaningful data.

In a study published in the field of oncology [34], it was noted that patients sought answers to their questions about their medical conditions and treatment through AI chatbots. In this study, a positive view of the potential capabilities of AI chatbots was expressed, and it was suggested that they are valuable tools for addressing the concerns of patients and their relatives in radiation oncology. However, other studies [35, 36] have indicated that AI chatbots are not a reliable source in the field of radiation oncology. In a study conducted in the field of radiology in dentistry [37], no difference was found in terms of comprehensibility between radiology assignments created by artificial intelligence and those written by students. However, it has been noted that the assignments generated by AI chatbots generally exhibit low levels of originality in their references, while also lacking important diagnostic information.

Despite all this information, our study is not without limitations; the limited number of questions asked to the AI chatbots, the potential for increasing the time between the first and second evaluations, the focus solely on questions related to the field of periodontology, the inclusion of only the ChatGPT-4o mini among free AI chatbots in the study, the questions being asked one by one rather than in a standard exam format, and the not being included of newly released AI chatbots from the study can be noted. We acknowledge the value of open-ended questions and plan to explore them in future research to assess AI performance in more nuanced and interpretive contexts.

Conclusion

AI chatbots should be considered as a complementary tool for students. Additionally, AI chatbots need to be academically supervised and developed. To better contribute to education, AI chatbots need to develop information verification mechanisms. This development will also enhance its contribution to education and enable students to use these robots more consciously and in accordance with academic standards.

In conclusion, there are many factors that dental students in the academic development process should consider before using AI chatbots as their primary source of information. Dental students and professionals need to question the relevance of the information provided by AI and its support in literature. In addition to this, students should not only focus on theoretical knowledge acquisition but also be supported by clinical observations and practical work, which are very important for professional competence.
