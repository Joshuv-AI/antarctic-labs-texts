Assessing stakeholder readiness for implementation of a diabetes prevention programme for patients with recent gestational diabetes: a short report
Authors: Katelyn Sushko, Apishanthi Sriskandarajah, Sameen Ali, Megan Racey, Ishraq Rahman, Sandhya Sahye-Pudaruth, Diana Sherifali, Kelly Smith, Aimen Zehra, Lorraine Lipscombe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImplementing guideline-based diabetes prevention programs (DPPs) for women with recent gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is critical, given the global rise in type 2 diabetes (T2D) and the established association between GDM and subsequent diabetes risk. Implementing new healthcare programs, however, is inherently challenging, as success depends on the engagement and commitment of frontline providers. Determining stakeholders' readiness for change was therefore critical before implementing a postpartum DPP for patients with recent GDM.MethodWe conducted a cross-sectional survey of healthcare providers across three DPP implementation settings in Ontario, Canada. Survey measures included the Organizational Readiness for Implementing Change (ORIC) scale and a measure assessing constructs from the inner setting domain of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). Due to our low sample size, we analyzed results descriptively.ResultsWe surveyed 36 stakeholders across three implementation settings. The ORIC scale and CFIR-based measurement tool scores were relatively high (greater than 4.00 out of 5.00) across most participants. Scores were similar across service types and professional roles.ConclusionsStakeholders across three settings appear ready to implement a postpartum DPP for patients with GDM. Although those from an integrated care setting tended toward higher readiness based on change efficacy and commitment, the results of the CFIR-based measurement tool underscore the importance of using tailored approaches when implementing programs in community-based settings with lower resources.Keywords: Healthcare quality improvement, Health services research, Transitions in care

Introduction

Diabetes prevention programmes (DPPs) that incorporate lifestyle modification are recommended for women after gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), to reduce their risk of type 2 diabetes.1,9 Implementing evidence-based complex interventions within healthcare systems, however, is challenging.10,13 A recent review of 252 publications on implemented healthcare interventions found that only 4% resulted in sustainable practice change.10 Implementation success depends on multiple factors, including readiness of frontline staff to adopt change within their organisations.11 12 Failing to determine readiness can hinder implementation, as strategies should be tailored to the readiness level.12 As postpartum DPP implementation is dependent on effective care transitions between prenatal and postpartum providers, centres that integrate prenatal and postpartum diabetes care may have higher readiness for implementation. In the current study, we explored whether organisational readiness varies among staff in integrated versus non-integrated settings in Ontario, Canada, before implementing a DPP for patients with recent GDM.14

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of healthcare providers and administrators in three DPP implementation settings in Ontario, Canada. Setting 1 is an integrated model of care wherein prenatal diabetes services and the postpartum DPP are within the same centre. In settings 2 and 3, prenatal diabetes care is within hospital-based clinics, and the postpartum DPP is in an outpatient centre. We received ethics approval from our institutional review boards. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement guided the reporting of this study.15

The Organizational Readiness for Implementing Change (ORIC) scale and a Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)-based measurement tool assessed readiness for organisational change and CFIR inner setting characteristics, respectively. The ORIC scale is a 12-item Likert scale that measures organisational confidence and commitment to change implementation.12 There are two subscales, change efficacy and change commitment, which examine the organisation’s belief in its ability to implement change and the commitment of an organisation to change implementation, respectively.12 The CFIR-based measurement tool is a Likert scale with 19 questions.16 It focuses on organisational readiness for implementation across four domains: learning culture, learning climate, leadership engagement and available resources.16 Both measures are valid and reliable.12 16 We did not have sufficient power to detect significant differences due to our low sample size. Thus, we analysed the results descriptively across settings and provider types, and we explored differences between setting 1 (integrated) and settings 2 and 3 (non-integrated).

Results

We sent survey invitations to 44 eligible participants between October 2024 and August 2025. The survey completion rate was 82% (n=36). One participant provided a partial response, only completing the ORIC scale. The participants varied across all three implementation settings (n=13, n=14 and n=9 from each setting, respectively) and professional roles (clinicians, n=21; administrators, n=15). Scores were similar across service types and professional roles.

Discussion

The implementation success of evidence-based complex interventions relies on the willingness and commitment of frontline staff to adopt change within their organisation. As such, we assessed readiness for organisational change and CFIR inner setting characteristics before implementing a DPP for patients with recent GDM. Our results showed that ORIC scale scores were relatively high (greater than 4.00 out of 5.00) across most participants, indicating an overall readiness for DPP implementation. However, participants within an integrated care setting tended to have higher readiness based on change efficacy and commitment. We hypothesised that scores may have been higher among these participants due to the fragmentation of care reported to affect the prenatal to postpartum transition.17,19 While prenatal GDM care involves frequent monitoring and follow-up, there is often a sudden discontinuation of care post partum without a clear handover from prenatal to postpartum providers.20,25 Indeed, we noted greater fragmentation of care among the settings where prenatal and postpartum diabetes services were separate. In the non-integrated settings, the usual practice is that patients with GDM are discharged from diabetes care after delivery and advised to follow-up with their primary care provider (PCP). Unfortunately, evidence indicates that barriers, such as time constraints and a lack of standardised testing, impact the ability of PCPs to manage postpartum diabetes preventive care.20,24 Additionally, new motherhood can impede engagement in diabetes prevention.20,2225 Strategies to improve care integration for the prenatal to postpartum transition are needed to optimise diabetes testing and prevention for patients with GDM.

For the CFIR-based measurement tool, the lowest overall mean (SD) score under 4.00 was for the available resources subscale. Previous research has identified resource limitations as a common barrier to implementation, particularly in outpatient community settings. Community centres typically have fewer financial reserves and staffing supports available to them compared with hospital settings.28,30 Thus, leveraging the community’s available assets and customising the implementation approach to local contextual factors is recommended when implementing evidence-based interventions in lower resource community settings.28,30

In summary, stakeholders appear ready to implement a DPP for patients with recent GDM, with possible higher readiness in an integrated care setting. CFIR-based measurement tool scores highlighted the need for a tailored approach in lower resource community settings to facilitate sustainability within the local context. This study allowed us to establish baseline readiness among those involved in programme implementation. Perhaps even more importantly, it is critical to have these data available for our evaluation phase to explore if baseline readiness was a predictor of implementation success. Further research with larger sample sizes will be needed to confirm initial findings.
