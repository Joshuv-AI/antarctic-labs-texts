Assessment of artificial intelligence chatbots in responding to dental occlusion questions: a comparative study
Authors: Hamod Alqahtani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) chatbots, which use large language models (LLMs) to comprehend user questions and respond to human conversations, are increasingly used in dental education, but their accuracy in core subjects like occlusion remains unexplored. This study evaluated the performance of four AI chatbots, namely, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA 3.2, in answering multiple-choice questions (MCQs) related to fundamentals of dental occlusion.MethodsEach chatbot was tested across two rounds on a standardized set of MCQs taken from a textbook on dental occlusion. Accuracy, intra-model consistency, and inter-model agreement were assessed using descriptive statistics, Cohen’s Kappa, and McNemar’s test.ResultsClaude 3.7 sonnet and GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy in round one at 90%, while in round two, Claude 3.7 sonnet had an accuracy of 91.4%, and GPT-4o scored 84.4%. Meta Llama3.2 recorded the lowest accuracy across both rounds, with 71.4% in the first and 64.3% in the second. The Kappa test showed the intra-model consistency between the two rounds to be highest for Claude 3.7 sonnet (0.746), with a McNemar P-value of 1. For inter-model consistency when comparing the chatbots in round 1, Claude 3.7 sonnet and GPT-4o displayed the highest consistency with a kappa value (p < 0.005) and McNemar P-value of 1. In round 2, Claude 3.7 sonnet with GPT-4o and with DeepSeek had the highest consistency, with kappa values of 0.272 and a McNemar P-value of 0.227.ConclusionsClaude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o show strong potential as reliable tools for dental education. DeepSeek offers moderate reliability, while Meta LLaMA 3.2 displayed inconsistent performance. Human oversight remains critical, and further refinement is essential before broader adoption in academic or clinical settings.Trial registrationNot applicable as it is not a clinical trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07573-z.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Accuracy, Dental occlusion, Dental education, Large language models, Technology, Multiple-choice tests

Background

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is transforming both healthcare and education by enabling machines to simulate human cognitive functions, including learning, decision-making, and reasoning.1 One of the most significant advancements in this field is the emergence of large language models (LLMs), which employ deep learning algorithms trained on extensive datasets to produce contextually appropriate, human-like responses [1].

In recent years, LLM-based AI chatbots have been incorporated into medical and dental education as interactive tools to support student learning. Given the increasingly content-dense curricula and limited instructional time, these technologies serve as valuable supplementary resources, offering immediate, interactive, and personalised assistance for students [1, 2]. Such systems can deliver real-time explanations, simulate clinical scenarios, and generate multiple-choice questions (MCQs), thereby enabling students to assess their knowledge and consolidate complex subject matter [3, 4]. The use of AI in education is becoming more popular among both students and educators because of its quick response times and ability to provide logical explanations, acting as a virtual tutor and offering a personalized learning experience tailored to each individual’s needs [5]. Additionally, it is increasingly being used in diagnostics and treatment planning in clinical settings. It helps interpret radiographic images, allowing for the detection of periodontal bone loss, periapical lesions, and carious lesions. Furthermore, it plays an important role in designing and developing CAD/CAM prostheses, identifying and planning implant sites, and improving orthodontic treatment planning [6]. AI-driven tools are currently being explored for their efficiency and their potential to enhance learning outcomes, foster learner autonomy, and improve clinical competence.

In dentistry, where mastery of complex anatomical, pathological, and procedural knowledge is essential, AI chatbots offer an innovative means for students to review key concepts, assess their understanding, and access immediate explanations [7, 8]. In prosthodontics, for instance, dental occlusion plays a critical role in the diagnosis, planning, and execution of treatments such as full-mouth rehabilitations and complete denture fabrication [9–11]. AI chatbots can support learning by generating clinically relevant MCQs, such as identifying the appropriate occlusal scheme for patients requiring complete dentures or full-arch fixed prostheses. These tools generally promote reasoning and support the retrieval of information from memory [12, 13]. Furthermore, by simulating a variety of question formats and adjusting difficulty levels, AI technologies can deliver standardised formative assessments and assist in the early identification of knowledge gaps—particularly in nuanced occlusal principles that influence long-term prosthodontic outcomes. The potential of AI chatbots to transform student learning, enhance content engagement, and improve knowledge assessment is considerable [14]. Nevertheless, despite their increasing integration into education, students remain cautious, often expressing concerns regarding the accuracy, reliability, and potential misinterpretation of AI-generated content [15].

Chatbot models such as GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA 3.2 represent the cutting edge of AI-based large language model (LLM) technology, offering advanced capabilities in content generation, problem-solving, and knowledge retrieval. Claude 3.7 Sonnet excels in front-end web development, utilising a modular, rule-based system to produce coherent and contextually relevant educational responses [16]. Its design facilitates the creation of engaging, interactive user interfaces that enhance the learning experience by integrating real-time feedback with user input. GPT-4.0, a large-scale LLM, employs deep neural networks trained on extensive datasets to comprehend and generate human-like language [17]. Its robust natural language processing abilities enable it to address complex queries effectively, making it particularly suited to specialised applications in dental education [18]. DeepSeek is a versatile AI system that combines supervised and unsupervised learning algorithms to perform a broad spectrum of tasks [19]. It is notably proficient in delivering detailed, context-sensitive explanations and responding to questions requiring nuanced understanding, thus supporting dynamic learning environments. Meta’s LLaMA 3.2 specialises in visual reasoning by leveraging multimodal AI techniques to analyse both textual and visual inputs [20]. This capability is especially valuable in contexts demanding detailed image-based assessments, such as identifying anatomical structures or evaluating occlusal schemes in prosthodontic treatments.

These four models were selected due to their widespread adoption and reported strengths. While some studies indicate that large language models (LLMs) can outperform human experts in summarising medical texts [21] and answering clinical questions [22], other research has highlighted concerns regarding ambiguous outputs [23], high computational requirements [24], and inherent biases [25]. Despite their increasing application in dental education [26, 27], there remains limited comparative research on their performance in specialised areas such as dental occlusion. Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate the performance of four AI chatbots—Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA 3.2—in answering multiple-choice questions related to dental occlusion, focusing on response accuracy, intra-model consistency, and inter-model agreement. The results will offer valuable insights into the strengths and limitations of AI chatbots within the context of dental education.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a comparative performance evaluation of four prominent AI chatbots in answering MCQs related to dental occlusion. The primary objective was to assess the accuracy, consistency, and concordance of the responses generated by the selected AI models: Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA 3.2. The MCQs were carefully selected to encompass a range of both fundamental and advanced topics within the domain of dental occlusion, thereby ensuring the questions were relevant and sufficiently challenging for the AI models.

Ethical considerations

This study was exempted from Institutional Review Board approval as no patient data was involved in this study, and all chatbots used were publicly available tools.

Selection of chatbots

The four chatbots selected for this evaluation—Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA 3.2—represent some of the most advanced and widely utilised AI tools powered by LLMs. These models were chosen based on their demonstrated performance across various domains and their accessibility for use in educational settings [28, 29]. To simulate real-world user interactions, the publicly available web interfaces of each chatbot were employed. A total of 70 MCQs related to dental occlusion were individually entered into each chatbot, with a new chat session initiated for each question to minimise memory bias. GPT-4o was accessed via https://chat.openai.com, Claude 3.7 Sonnet through the Poe platform (https://poe.com/Claude), DeepSeek via https://chat.deepseek.com, and Meta LLaMA 3.2 through Meta AI’s assistant on Facebook Messenger [16, 17, 19, 20]. All versions used were publicly accessible at the time of the study.

Multiple-choice questions

A total of 70 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) have been adapted from the textbook Fundamentals of Occlusion, a key reference for dental students. The textbook is structured into seven chapters which are: Glossary of occlusal terms, Anatomy and physiology, Occlusal instruments, Occlusal disorder, Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorder and management, Diagnosis and treatment planning, and Removable prosthodontic occlusion. At the end of every chapter, MCQs are provided, and 10 questions were selected per chapter, ensuring comprehensive coverage of all core occlusal principles and techniques. A set of three sample questions with correct answers are attached (see the additional file1).

Questioning procedure

Each AI chatbot was administered a set of MCQs in two rounds to evaluate its consistency and reliability in responding to the same questions over time. The same set of MCQs was utilized in both rounds of inquiry. In Round 1 (May 15, 2025), the chatbots were prompted to provide their responses. Following a seven-day interval, during Round 2 (May 22, 2025), the same set of questions was presented again, individually, to assess the models’ consistency and any potential improvements in their responses.

Data collection and evaluation

The accuracy of the responses was evaluated by comparing the chatbot-generated answers to a predefined answer key mentioned in the textbook. Correct responses were classified as “true” answers, while incorrect responses were classified as “false” answers. The number of true and false answers for each chatbot was recorded for each round, and performance metrics, such as the percentage of correct answers and the consistency of answers across rounds, were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were employed to present the number of correct and incorrect answers for each chatbot. McNemar’s test was used to assess the significant changes in responses between Round 1 and Round 2 for each chatbot, with Bonferroni correction applied to account for multiple comparisons. Additionally, Kappa statistics were calculated to measure the agreement between responses from different chatbots and the consistency within each model across rounds. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study assessed the performance of four AI chatbots (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA 3.2) in answering MCQs related to dental occlusion. The results were analyzed based on the accuracy of responses, intra-model consistency between two rounds of questioning, and inter-model agreement across the first and second rounds.

Accuracy of chatbot responses

As shown in Table 1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4.0 achieved the highest accuracy in the first round (90% each), followed by DeepSeek (80%) and Meta LLaMA 3.2 (71.4%). In the second round, Claude 3.7 Sonnet slightly improved (91.4%), while GPT-4.0 and DeepSeek each maintained an 84.3% accuracy. Meta LLaMA 3.2’s performance declined to 64.3%. Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of four AI chatbots, namely, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Meta LLaMA3.2, in answering dental occlusion-based MCQs, focusing on accuracy, intra-model consistency, and inter-model agreement. Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o were the most accurate, each scoring 90% in the first round. Claude also showed high internal consistency (κ = 0.746) and perfect agreement with GPT-4.0 (κ = 1.0).

These findings are consistent with a recent study using 100 endodontic MCQs, where ChatGPT-4o and MedGebra GPT-4o achieved the highest performance (93% and 90% across two rounds), while Meta LLaMA 3 scored the lowest (73% and 75%) [30]. Similarly, another study comparing ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4, and Google Bard on 40 endodontic questions found that ChatGPT-4 had the highest score and the lowest misinformation rate (P = 0.008), significantly outperforming Bard (P = 0.004) [31]. Furthermore, in a study involving 60 dichotomous endodontic questions, ChatGPT showed high consistency (85.44%) but only moderate accuracy (57.33%), with notable variation based on question difficulty [32].

Chatbot performance appears to vary significantly across dental domains [33]. A study analyzing 100 oral pathology MCQs found that ChatGPT-4o scored highest (96/100), followed by Claude (84), with Copilot scoring lowest (61). Differences between case-based and knowledge-based questions were significant (P < 0.001), demonstrating how question type impacts model performance [34]. In pediatric dentistry, a pilot study on 30 questions revealed that pediatric dentists (96.67% ± 4.3%) and general dentists (88.0% ± 6.1%) significantly outperformed AI models. ChatGPT scored highest among the chatbots (78% ± 3%), though only some models demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.7), indicating concerns about clinical applicability [35]. Likewise, human benchmarks have been used to compare with AI performance, specifically dentists or specialists in the field. Such comparisons provide a more realistic basis and validate the interpretation of results generated by AI. Expert evaluation creates a benchmark for objectively comparing AI-generated solutions, ensuring that their accuracy and reasoning meet professional standards. This is especially important in dentistry, where making well-informed choices is necessary for safe and effective patient care [28]. Human benchmarks also make it easier to interpret AI performance metrics, helping researchers assess whether improved chatbot outputs meet clinical standards rather than just showing that one AI model is better than another.

Our findings, which place Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4.0 as the most accurate and consistent models, align with previous research. For example, in an oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMFS) analysis, GPT-4 achieved 76.8% accuracy, surpassing Copilot and GPT-3.5, while Meta LLaMA2 scored significantly lower at 42.5% (P < 0.001), indicating potential gaps in domain-specific training [36]. A study evaluating the performance of ChatGPT-4.0 and GPT-3.5 on U.S. and U.K. dental licensing exam questions found that ChatGPT-4.0 outperformed GPT-3.5, achieving scores of 80.7% and 62.7%, respectively, while GPT-3.5 failed to surpass the passing threshold [37]. Another MCQ study on prosthodontics and restorative dentistry was evaluated for accuracy and rationale quality using GPT-4.0, Claude-2, and Llama 2. The results showed notable differences in performance, with GPT-4.0 outperforming the other chatbots in these assessments [28]. Additionally, GPT-4 excelled at open-ended OMFS questions, though its clinical usefulness was limited by a lack of significant differences across models [38]. Furthermore, in a study comparing the accuracy of ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4 Omni (4.0), Google Bard, and Microsoft Copilot on 123 multiple-choice oral radiology questions, ChatGPT-4 Omni achieved the highest accuracy at 86.1%, followed by Google Bard at 61.8%, ChatGPT-3.5 at 43.9%, and Microsoft Copilot at 41.5%, demonstrating its potential as a superior educational tool in healthcare [39].

An extensive evaluation of six LLMs, Copilot, Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Mistral, and LLaMA, across 1,490 dental MCQs revealed significant differences in performance (P < 0.001). Copilot (85.5%), Claude (84.0%), and ChatGPT (83.8%) performed best overall, especially on text-based questions, while LLaMA lagged at 72.4% [40]. Interestingly, some LLMs have even outperformed humans in specific areas. A recent study comparing AI models with second-year periodontics residents found that ChatGPT-4o, Claude 3 Opus, and Gemini Advanced outperformed residents, with ChatGPT-4o achieving an impressive 92.7% accuracy. These results support the potential for integrating LLMs into specialty dental education for concept reinforcement and self-directed learning [41]. Similarly, a study comparing three GPT models with an expert orthodontist in evaluating hand-wrist radiographs found strong correlations with reference bone ages, with GPT-o1-pro being the most consistent. Meanwhile, GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy in classifying growth stages, with the authors suggesting that its reasoning abilities and accessibility make it a promising tool for initial assessments [42].

In contrast to the earlier findings, a comprehensive evaluation of nine publicly available chatbots—Google Bard, ChatGPT 4, ChatGPT 3.5, Llama, Sage, Claude 2 100k, Claude-instant, Claude instant − 100k, and Google PaLM—assessed their performance on 25 true/false questions related to special needs dentistry and 15 questions for syndrome diagnosis. The average accuracy was 55.4%, with 37.6% for diagnosis and 67.8% for true/false. No statistically significant differences were observed among the chatbots, which demonstrated moderate accuracy on true/false questions and low accuracy on diagnosis questions [43].

Despite these promising outcomes, a recent systematic review of 45 studies assessing ChatGPT’s performance in medical licensing examinations reported considerable variability in results across models, domains, and question formats. GPT-4 outperformed GPT-3.5, achieving 81% overall accuracy and passing the majority of exams attempted. However, performance declined with open-ended or image-based questions and was influenced by question language and country of origin [44].

Implications for dental education

Our findings suggest the strengths and shortcomings of AI integration in dental education. Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4.0 showed strong performance and potential for curricular integration, especially in under-taught areas like dental occlusion, where their accurate, accessible feedback can support students’ self-directed learning. However, the inconsistent accuracy of Meta LLaMA 3.2 highlights the need for model-specific evaluation and strategic implementation based on curricular goals and question types. Echoing trends in medical education, AI output is sensitive to prompt structure and question complexity. Responsible use demands faculty oversight, alignment with evidence-based standards, and training students and staff to critically assess AI-generated content. Ethical concerns, such as misinformation, over-reliance, and data privacy, must also be addressed through transparency and human accountability [45].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This is the first study on AI’s accuracy related to dental occlusion. However, it had many limitations: relying on a single textbook for the questions, a limited number of questions that could not cover the extensive field of occlusion, and using only a few AI models. Another major limitation is the lack of a human benchmark, which hinders the comparison of chatbot performance with clinical standards. Comparing AI models reveals their strengths but does not guarantee they meet the dental field’s standards for accuracy and explanatory depth. Furthermore, this study utilized only text-based MCQs, with no clinical or diagnostic images were included. Since understanding dental occlusion often requires clinical assessment rather than theoretical knowledge alone, incorporating AI with image analysis capabilities and including clinical images of various normal occlusal patterns and malocclusions would improve students’ understanding. Future research should broaden its scope to include different dental disciplines and a wider range of question types and complexities, such as multimodal questions that integrate text and images to more accurately reflect real clinical scenarios.

Further studies should also explore the effectiveness of AI chatbots in interactive and case-based learning environments. Longitudinal studies examining student outcomes with and without chatbot integration would provide deeper insights into their educational impact. Additionally, the continued refinement of LLMs with domain-specific datasets is critical to improving their accuracy, reliability, and alignment with clinical guidelines.

Conclusions

Among the evaluated AI chatbots, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o demonstrated the highest accuracy and consistency in answering MCQs related to dental occlusion, suggesting their potential for application in dental education. DeepSeek showed moderate performance, indicating utility as a supplementary learning tool pending further optimization. Conversely, the lower consistency of Meta LLaMA3.2 demonstrates the need for cautious interpretation of its outputs. While AI chatbots offer promising opportunities to enhance educational experiences, their integration into academic settings must be accompanied by expert oversight and continuous validation to ensure both pedagogical and clinical integrity.
