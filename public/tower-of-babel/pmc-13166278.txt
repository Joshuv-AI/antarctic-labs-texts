Metagenomic next-generation sequencing for pathology-suspected fungal infections at rare anatomical sites: a case series
Authors: Xizhen Xu, Dong Chen, Nan Luo, Wendi Zhang, Liping Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Histopathology for suspected fungal infections lacks species-level identification and is prone to morphological mimics. The utility of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) at rare anatomical sites is underexplored. Methods:We retrospectively analyzed 10 cases with histopathology suggestive of fungal infection at rare sites (brain, cardiac valve, bone, etc.). All underwent mNGS testing on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples. Results:mNGS detected fungal DNA in 8/10 cases (80%), providing species-level identification (e.g., Cryptococcus, Candida, Fusarium, Rhizopus, Histoplasma). Polymicrobial infections were identified in 70%. mNGS corrected two misdiagnoses: one confirmed neurocysticercosis; another revealed only bacteria in a suspected fungal lesion. Antimicrobial resistance genes (ErmB) were identified in two cases. Conclusion:mNGS enhances diagnostic precision at rare sites by enabling species identification, uncovering polymicrobial infections, and correcting morphological misdiagnoses, supporting targeted therapy.Keywords: Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), invasive fungal infection, histopathology, rare anatomical sites, polymicrobial infection

Introduction

The diagnosis of invasive fungal infections, particularly those occurring at rare or atypical anatomical sites, presents a significant clinical challenge. Histopathological examination remains a cornerstone for diagnosis, often revealing fungal elements through characteristic morphological features on routine or special stains. However, this approach has inherent limitations, including the inability to provide species-level identification, the potential for morphological mimicry by non-fungal structures or other pathogens, and difficulty in detecting co-infecting microorganisms [1]. These shortcomings can directly impact patient management, as effective antifungal therapy is highly dependent on accurate speciation and an understanding of the complete microbial landscape.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of DNA has emerged as a powerful, hypothesis-free diagnostic tool capable of detecting a broad spectrum of pathogens directly from clinical samples. Its application has proven valuable in diagnosing infections of the central nervous system, bloodstream, and respiratory tract, especially in immunocompromised hosts or culture-negative cases [2]. However, its specific utility and diagnostic yield in patients with pathology-suspected fungal infections at rare anatomical sites—such as bone, cardiac valves, the gastrointestinal tract, and soft tissue niches—remain underexplored. The performance of mNGS in this unique context, where pretest probability and microbial background differ significantly from more common sites of infection, requires systematic evaluation [3].

This study aimed to assess the diagnostic value of mNGS in a cohort of patients with histopathological findings suggestive of fungal infection at rare anatomic locations. We performed a head-to-head comparison between traditional histopathology and mNGS to determine: 1) the concordance and discordance rates between the two methods; 2) the ability of mNGS to provide definitive species identification and uncover polymicrobial infections; and 3) the potential of mNGS results to refine diagnoses and guide targeted antimicrobial therapy. By focusing on this challenging patient population, we seek to elucidate the complementary role of molecular diagnostics in clarifying the etiology of complex infections where conventional methods may fall short.

Materials and methods

Processing of pathological samples and DNA nucleic acid extraction

Cut eight sections with a thickness of 4 μm from the Formalin-fixed and Paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sample, and place the wax rolls into a clean 1.5 mL EP tube. Add Deparaffinization Solution to the sample for deparaffinization, incubate at 56 °C with shaking at 400 rpm for 10 minutes, then centrifuge at 15,000 rpm for 2 minutes and discard the supernatant. Add 750 μL of ATL buffer and 20 μL of PK, and incubate overnight at 56 °C with shaking at 600 rpm. After lysis is complete, add PBS to make up to 740 μL, briefly centrifuge, and then add 250 μL of 0.5 mm glass beads for physical disruption. After bead milling, use the MataPure™ DNA Extraction Kit (magnetic bead method for pathogenic microbial total nucleic acid extraction, KS121-WSWTO-48) to extract total nucleic acids according to the kit’s instructions. The extracted total nucleic acids are used for library construction.

Library construction, enrichment, and sequencing

Use the MetaPath Pathogen Capture Metagenomics Detection Kit (KingCreate, China) to perform enzymatic fragmentation, end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR reaction on the extracted DNA for library construction. For the constructed libraries, pool every 8 libraries into one capture pool, and use specific probes for rapid hybridization capture, which only takes 2 hours. Use the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to quality control the fragment size of the captured library, which should be around 350 bp. Use the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) to quality control the concentration of the captured library. Based on the measured concentration, pool the constructed captured libraries by equal mass. Sequencing is performed on the Illumina Miniseq platform with single-end 100 bp reads, with a sequencing volume of 0.3 M per library.

Based on the RPM value relative to the dominant pathogen in the same sample, we semi-quantitatively categorized signals as “dominant” (RPM ratio >10× compared to the second most abundant microbe, or RPM >100 for fungi in sterile sites), “moderate” (RPM 10–100), and “low” (RPM 3–<10). These categories are not absolute cutoffs for clinical significance but serve as a guide for integrating with histopathological and clinical data. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was not performed in this retrospective study, and absolute quantification of fungal DNA was not available.

Negative and contamination controls

To monitor for potential reagent contamination and background microbial DNA signals, we included two types of negative controls in each batch of experiments. First, a blank extraction control (BEC) was processed in parallel with each set of FFPE samples, consisting of nuclease-free water subjected to the identical deparaffinization, lysis, and DNA extraction steps. Second, a histologically confirmed noninfectious FFPE tissue (normal liver parenchyma from a patient without any clinical or pathological evidence of infection) was used as a tissue negative control. Both BEC and the noninfectious tissue control were carried through library preparation, hybridization capture, and sequencing under the same conditions as the clinical samples.

For bioinformatics analysis, microbial reads detected in the negative controls were compiled into a “contaminant list” for each sequencing run. Any microorganism with a read count in a clinical sample that did not exceed five times the mean read count of that same organism in the negative controls (or with an absolute read count <3 unique reads for fungi/parasites and <10 unique reads for bacteria/viruses) was considered potential background or reagent contamination and was excluded from final reporting. This approach allowed us to distinguish true pathogen signals from environmental or kit-derived contaminants, thereby increasing the specificity of mNGS interpretation.

Bioinformatics analysis process

The sequencing data was first converted from base call files to raw FASTQ format files using the bcl2fastq software (v2.20.0.422). Then, Fastp (version 0.23.1) was used to trim adapter sequences and low-quality bases at the ends, with sequences shorter than 36 bp after trimming being filtered out. The quality-controlled sequences were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using BWA (0.7.17-r1188, Burrow-Wheeler Aligner), and reads aligned to the human reference genome were filtered. The remaining sequences were aligned to a taxonomic reference genome database downloaded from NCBI (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/), which included 11,958 bacteria, 7,373 viruses, 1,714 fungi, and 343 parasites. Finally, in-house developed software was used to calculate the number of reads per million (RPM) at the species or genus level, and the ratio of the same species in the negative control sample at the species level (RPM ratio). The threshold was established based on retrospective study results. If a species was detected in the negative control sample, an RPM ratio ≥10 was retained. For species not detected in the negative control sample, the requirements were RPM ≥3 and sequence distribution across ≥2 distinct genomic regions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort. Continuous variables were presented as medians with ranges, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The diagnostic performance of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was evaluated by calculating the detection rate of fungal DNA and the proportion of cases with species-level identification. Concordance between histopathology and mNGS findings was assessed descriptively. No formal statistical comparisons or hypothesis tests were performed due to the descriptive nature of the study and the small sample size.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

This study retrospectively analyzed 10 cases with a pathological diagnosis of fungal infection at rare anatomical sites. The cohort predominantly consisted of male patients (n = 8, 80%), with a median age of 55.5 years (range: 28–72). The involved rare sites included the brain parenchyma, cardiac valve, toe bone, spinal canal, appendix, and various segments of the colon. The pathological diagnoses were based on histomorphological findings on H&E staining (fungal hyphae, yeast-like structures, granulomatous inflammation with necrosis) and, in some cases, were supported by positive special stains (Gomori methenamine silver stain). Representative histopathological images of some selected cases were shown as Figure 1A–C. The panels showed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), and Grocott-Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) staining. The pathology reports described the presence of fungal elements but did not specify the fungal species. All cases underwent subsequent microbial DNA metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) testing, allowing for a direct comparison between histopathological findings and molecular pathogen detection.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) detection results

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of DNA was successfully performed on all 10 cases. The assay provided detailed microbial profiles that extended beyond the histopathological findings (Table 1). mNGS detected fungal DNA in 8 out of 10 cases (80%), offering species-level or complex-level identification that was not specified in the original pathology reports. The spectrum of fungi identified included Cryptococcus neoformans (Case 1), Candida parapsilosis (Cases 3, 5), Candida orthopsilosis (Case 9), Candida albicans (Case 10), Fusarium incarnatum-equiseti species complex (Case 4), Rhizopus microsporus (Case 5), Histoplasma capsulatum (Case 7), and Pichia kudriavzevii (formerly Candida krusei, Case 8). In two cases (Cases 6 and 10), despite a pathological suspicion of fungal infection, no fungal DNA was detected by mNGS; instead, bacterial pathogens (Citrobacter freundii, Escherichia coli, etc.) were identified.

Furthermore, mNGS revealed frequent polymicrobial scenarios. In 7 cases (70%), it identified concurrent bacterial (e.g., Staphylococcus capitis, Enterococcus faecium, various Bacteroides spp.) or viral (Epstein-Barr virus, Cytomegalovirus, Human Herpesvirus-7) nucleic acids alongside the primary fungal signal. This co-detection suggests potential complex infections or significant microbial background in immunocompromised niches. In two cases (Cases 7 and 10), mNGS also reported the presence of antimicrobial resistance genes (ErmB), providing adjunctive data for potential treatment challenges. The mNGS results corroborated the histopathological diagnosis of a fungal process in 7 cases (70%), provided a specific etiological agent in 8 cases (80%), and uncovered additional, potentially relevant microbes in the majority of cases. Two cases (Cases 2 and 6) showed significant discordance, where mNGS suggested a parasitic infection (Tenia solium) or identified only bacterial communities in the absence of fungal DNA, respectively.

Multimodal diagnostic correlation in a case of diagnostic discrepancy

Brain MRI in this patient revealed an abnormal signal intensity in the left precentral gyrus. The imaging findings were suggestive of an inflammatory or granulomatous lesion (Figure 2A and B), although a neoplastic process could not be entirely excluded. Notably, magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) demonstrated decreased levels of choline (Cho), creatine (Cr), and N-acetylaspartate (NAA) peaks without a significant elevation of the Cho peak at the lesion site in the left frontoparietal region (Figure 2C). This MRS pattern is more consistent with a non-neoplastic, inflammatory, or infectious etiology rather than a primary brain tumor, which typically exhibits elevated Cho.

The MRI findings of a possible granulomatous process aligned with the histopathological description of chronic granulomatous inflammation with suspected fungal elements on Grocott’s methenamine silver stain. However, the mNGS result identified Tenia solium (neurocysticercosis) DNA without detecting fungal nucleic acids, suggesting a parasitic etiology. This case highlights a diagnostic discrepancy between imaging, histopathology, and molecular testing: Firstly, the MRI and MRS features, while favoring an inflammatory condition, are not specific and can be seen in both granulomatous fungal infections and parasitic lesions such as neurocysticercosis, particularly in its granulomatous/nodular stage. Secondly, the histopathological observation of “fungus-like” hyphal structures on silver stain may represent morphological mimicry (Figure 2D and E), as degenerated parasitic cyst walls or sclerotic lesions can sometimes appear similar to fungal hyphae on routine stains. Thirdly, the positive mNGS for Tenia solium strongly supports neurocysticercosis, indicating that the granulomatous inflammation is likely a host response to the parasite rather than to a fungal pathogen.

In this patient, the integration of MRI (which ruled out a typical tumor profile), histopathology (which suggested but did not confirm fungi), and mNGS (which identified a parasite) was essential to clarify the etiology. The case underscores the importance of multimodal diagnostic approaches in atypical intracranial lesions, where imaging and histology may be inconclusive or misleading, and molecular testing can provide definitive pathogenic clarification, directly guiding targeted antiparasitic therapy.

Negative mNGS reclassifying a histopathology-suspected fungal appendiceal lesion

In Case 6, a diagnostic discrepancy emerged where histopathology of the appendix described mucinous material and rounded, fungus-like structures with granulomatous inflammation (Figure 3A), raising suspicion for Cryptococcus infection, while mNGS returned a negative result for fungi. Instead, mNGS identified a typical intestinal polymicrobial bacterial community. This discordance can be explained by the unique context of the appendix and the limitations of both methods. The blind-ending anatomy of the appendix makes it prone to obstruction, leading to mucocele formation and sterile inflammation. The observed “fungus-like” structures likely represented inspissated mucin or cellular debris—a common morphological mimic—and the granulomatous reaction was probably a nonspecific response to extravasated mucin rather than an infectious process. Concurrently, the necrotic or mucinous content of such a lesion may contain few or no viable fungal organisms, potentially placing the pathogen below the detection limit of mNGS, whereas the detected bacteria likely reflect secondary colonization or translocation in a damaged mucosal environment. This case highlights a critical diagnostic insight: in rare sites like the appendix, a histopathological suggestion of fungal infection has a lower pretest probability and requires robust microbiological confirmation. A negative mNGS result serves as a crucial validator, prompting a reevaluation of pathological mimics and refocusing the clinical diagnosis toward more common, noninfectious inflammatory or obstructive pathologies complicated by secondary bacterial presence rather than a rare invasive fungal disease.

The significance of mNGS in guiding antimicrobial therapy

Beyond its role in diagnostic clarification, mNGS provides potential insights into clinical management. Firstly, mNGS can unveil polymicrobial infections, which may inform rational combination therapy. Several cases demonstrated complex co-infections missed by conventional methods. Case 4 (foot infection, Figure 3B) showed concurrent detection of Fusarium incarnatum-equiseti complex, Staphylococcus capitis, and Candida parapsilosis, suggesting the potential need for combined antifungal (e.g., voriconazole) and anti-staphylococcal therapy. In Case 5 (spinal infection), the co-detection of Candida parapsilosis and Rhizopus microsporus—pathogens with differing antifungal susceptibilities—supported the use of a broad-spectrum regimen (e.g., liposomal amphotericin B plus fluconazole or isavuconazole). Case 7 (enteritis) further illustrated this by identifying Histoplasma capsulatum, Enterococcus faecium, and cytomegalovirus/EBV, indicating that effective management might require a triple-target approach against fungus, bacterium, and virus.

What’s more, the detection of antimicrobial resistance genes by mNGS provides an early warning for potential treatment failure. In Cases 7 and 10, mNGS reported the presence of the ErmB gene, which confers resistance to macrolides, lincosamides, and streptogramins B. This finding cautioned against the use of antibiotics like erythromycin or clindamycin if these bacteria were deemed pathogenic, guiding selection toward alternative classes. Furthermore, the explicit comment in Case 4 that the Fusarium complex is intrinsically resistant to fluconazole underscores how mNGS-coupled resistance data can prevent the use of inherently ineffective antifungals. Moreover, mNGS aids in interpreting microbial findings by providing semi-quantitative data and contextual background, helping to distinguish colonization from true infection. In gastrointestinal cases like 8 and 9, mNGS detected Candida species alongside diverse commensal bacteria. The interpretation required integration of clinical context—such as immune status and mucosal integrity—to decide whether the fungi represented pathogens requiring treatment or merely reflected dysbiosis or colonization. The relative sequence counts offered by mNGS added a valuable, though not definitive, dimension to this clinical-pathological correlation.

Collectively, mNGS may inform therapeutic precision by correcting misdirected diagnoses, revealing complex polymicrobial landscapes, flagging resistance threats, and assisting in the critical differentiation between pathogens and bystanders. This technology has the potential to facilitate a shift from broad empirical regimens to targeted, evidence-based antimicrobial strategies, particularly valuable in complex infections at rare anatomic sites.

Discussion

This study evaluated the diagnostic utility of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) in a challenging cohort of patients with histopathological suspicion of fungal infection at rare anatomic sites. Our findings demonstrate that mNGS serves as a powerful adjunct to traditional pathology, significantly refining etiological diagnosis and uncovering clinically relevant complexities that may inform directly impact therapeutic decision-making.

The primary strength of mNGS lies in its capacity to provide precise microbial identification where histopathology can only offer morphological clues. In our series, mNGS delivered species- or complex-level identification in 80% (8/10) of cases, identifying a diverse array of fungi including Cryptococcus neoformans, various Candida species, molds like Fusarium and Rhizopus, and the dimorphic fungus Histoplasma capsulatum. This precise identification is critical, as antifungal susceptibility and optimal therapeutic strategies vary substantially across these species. For instance, the intrinsic resistance of Fusarium species to fluconazole and the differential susceptibility patterns between Candida parapsilosis and Rhizopus microsporus underscore the necessity of accurate speciation. Our results align with and extend previous reports on mNGS for diagnosing invasive fungal infections [4], confirming its high diagnostic yield even in paucimicrobial samples from sterile sites.

Perhaps the most striking finding was the ability of mNGS to correct misdirected diagnoses, thereby preventing potentially futile or harmful therapy [5]. In Case 2, the definitive identification of Tenia solium DNA overruled the histopathological suspicion of fungal hyphae, redirecting therapy toward antiparasitic agents. This case exemplifies the phenomenon of morphological mimicry, where parasitic structures can closely resemble fungal elements on special stains, a known diagnostic pitfall [6]. Similarly, in Case 6, the absence of fungal DNA by mNGS alongside the detection of enteric bacteria prompted a reevaluation of the appendiceal pathology, favoring a diagnosis of obstructive mucocele with secondary bacterial colonization over primary cryptococcosis. These discordant cases highlight that histopathology, while invaluable, has a pretest probability conditioned by the clinical and anatomical context. In rare sites, the prior likelihood of certain infections changes, and mNGS acts as an unbiased arbitrator, validating or challenging the pathological impression.

Beyond correction, mNGS profoundly illuminated the polymicrobial nature of these infections. The high rate (70%) of co-detection of bacteria or viruses alongside fungi challenges the paradigm of monomicrobial etiology often assumed from histology [7]. Cases of osteomyelitis (Case 4), spondylodiscitis (Case 5), and enteritis (Case 7) revealed complex communities suggesting that the inflammatory pathology may be driven or compounded by multiple organisms. This has direct therapeutic implications, as effective management may require combination or sequential antimicrobial therapy targeting different kingdoms of life. The “one-bug, one-drug” approach becomes insufficient, and mNGS provides the roadmap for a more comprehensive antimicrobial strategy.

Furthermore, the incidental detection of antimicrobial resistance genes (ErmB) adds a proactive dimension to therapeutic planning [8,9]. While not replacing phenotypic susceptibility testing, this genomic signal may serve as an early warning against the use of certain drug classes. However, whether this translates into shorter time to effective therapy or improved outcomes requires further prospective validation [10,11].

Our study also sheds light on the interpretative challenges of mNGS, particularly in distinguishing colonization or background nucleic acid from true invasion. This was most apparent in gastrointestinal cases (Cases 8, 9), where the detection of Candida species and commensal bacteria required careful integration with clinical parameters such as immune status, mucosal integrity, and histologic evidence of invasion. mNGS provides a sensitive snapshot of the microbial landscape, but its clinical translation demands a nuanced, context-driven interpretation by a multidisciplinary team.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective design and small sample size, though focused on a rare clinical scenario, limit generalizability. Therefore, our results should be considered hypothesis-generating and descriptive rather than definitive. A prospective, multi-center study with a larger cohort is required to validate the diagnostic accuracy of mNGS at rare anatomical sites. The performance of mNGS can be affected by factors like pathogen load, nucleic acid extraction efficiency, and the presence of inhibitors [9]. A negative mNGS result, as seen in two cases, does not definitively rule out infection, as it may reflect sampling error, sequence database limitations, or pathogen loads below the assay’s limit of detection.In addition, the use of FFPE samples introduces inevitable DNA degradation, including fragmentation and crosslinking, which can bias mNGS results. Fungal genomes with high GC content or large size may be preferentially underrepresented. Although our hybridization capture panel may partially compensate by enriching target sequences, the impact of FFPE-induced damage on detection sensitivity cannot be fully eliminated. Future studies using fresh or frozen tissue, or optimized FFPE extraction protocols, would help validate our findings.What’s more, histopathological slides were not subjected to independent blinded review by external pathologists. Although two of the authors are pathologists who reviewed all cases, the lack of external validation may introduce interpretive bias. Future studies should include blinded external pathology review to strengthen the reference standard. Besides, This study did not compare the cost-effectiveness or turnaround time of mNGS with conventional diagnostic methods, as these were not the primary research objectives and the necessary data were not collected in this retrospective series. Future prospective studies should include such comparisons to assess the operational feasibility of mNGS in routine practice. Finally, we did not collect systematic post-treatment follow-up data. While mNGS results informed diagnostic and therapeutic decisions in individual cases (e.g., avoiding unnecessary antifungals in Case 6, enabling anti-parasitic treatment in Case 2), we cannot report clinical outcomes such as symptom resolution, microbiological cure, or mortality. The absence of outcome data precludes any conclusion about the impact of mNGS-guided therapy on patient prognosis. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating diagnostic and decision-support value rather than proven clinical benefit.

In conclusion, this study suggests that mNGS can augment the diagnostic evaluation of suspected fungal infections at rare sites. It can help move the diagnostic process from morphological presumption toward molecular precision, potentially correct diagnostic errors, reveal complex polymicrobial interactions, and flag potential resistance. Integrating mNGS into the diagnostic workflow for these challenging cases may promote a shift from empirical, broad-spectrum therapy toward targeted, evidence-based antimicrobial strategies. Future studies are needed to determine whether this diagnostic refinement translates into improved patient outcomes.
