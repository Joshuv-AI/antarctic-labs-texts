Abstract
. Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly framed as a collaborative partner in creative activities, yet children’s interactions with AI have largely been studied in AI-led instructional settings rather than co-creative collaboration. This leaves open questions about how children can meaningfully engage with AI through iterative co-creation. We present Tinker Tales , a tangible storytelling system designed with narrative and social-emotional scaffolding to support child–AI collaboration. The system combines a physical storytelling board, NFC-embedded toys representing story elements (e.g., characters, places, items, and emotions), and a mobile app that mediates child–AI interaction. Children shape and refine stories by placing and moving story elements and interacting with the AI through tangible and voice-based interaction. We conducted an exploratory user study with 10 children to examine how they interacted with Tinker Tales . Our findings show that children treated the AI as an attentive, responsive collaborator, while scaffolding supported coherent narrative refinement without diminishing children’s agency.

3.
Tinker Tales

3.1.
Design Rationale

3.1.1.
Combining Tangible Play and Voice Interaction

We combined tangible play with voice-based interaction to support children’s engagement and sense-making during story development. Prior work suggests that physical manipulation of objects can help younger children ground abstract ideas through embodied experience
(
19
)
, while voice interaction allows children to express ideas fluidly without the constraints of reading and writing
(
32
;
41
)
. By placing and moving story elements on the physical board, children make the story’s structure and progression visible and tangible. Voice interaction complements this tangible setup by allowing children to develop, elaborate on, or redirect the story in natural language, supporting expressive interaction with the AI. This combination of tangible and voice interaction was intended to lower interaction barriers, support multisensory expression, and create a playful setting in which children can engage with AI through familiar forms of play rather than screen-centric input alone.

3.1.2.
Structuring Collaborative Roles Between Children and AI

The collaborative roles between children and the AI were inspired by two complementary sources. First, we drew inspiration from common practices in adult–AI collaboration for creative writing, where people often contribute partial ideas, receive AI-generated content, and iteratively develop it over multiple turns
(
51
;
35
)
. Second, we were informed by established practices in adult–child storytelling, in which caregivers and educators support children’s narrative construction by eliciting ideas and encouraging elaboration while allowing children to retain primary control over narrative decisions
(
39
;
43
)
. We adapted these patterns to a child-centered storytelling context. In
Tinker Tales
, the AI opens the interaction by engaging children to imagine and talk about the kind of story they would like to create in this session. Children then initiate story generation by selecting story elements, beginning with characters and followed by the selection of a place, item, and emotion at each narrative stage. The AI uses these inputs to generate story segments, which children can then further develop and elaborate through subsequent voice interaction. This iterative loop positions children as the primary decision-makers, while the AI supports children’s thinking through elaboration questions and advances story progression by transforming their inputs into narrative content that remains open to further shaping.

3.1.3.
Designing AI Responses for Educational Scaffolding

We designed the AI’s conversational behavior to provide educational scaffolding grounded in Applebee’s narrative development model
(
2
)
and CASEL’s social–emotional learning (SEL) framework
(
7
)
. Rather than treating the AI as a free-form conversational partner, we structured its responses to support developmentally appropriate child–AI interaction within the narrative context.

Applebee’s model characterizes children’s narrative development as progressing from early forms, such as
Heaps
and
Sequence
, toward more coherent narrative structures, including
Primitive

Chain

True narratives
. In
Tinker Tales
, we used these stages as an analytic lens to guide the design of AI agent scaffolding. Specifically, we treated
Heaps
and

3.2.3.
Conversational AI Agent

The behavior of the AI is governed by a scripted prompting strategy that organizes interaction into a sequence of eight phases corresponding to the activity. These phases include: (1) Session opening, (2) Character selection, (3)–(6) Four narrative stages (Start, Journey, Climax, and End), (7) Post-story activity, and (8) Session closing. Each phase is implemented as a dedicated prompt that specifies a sequence of actions. Appendix
A.1
provides representative prompts for key phases, including Session opening (Figure

11

12

14
). Progression between phases occurs only after all required actions within the current phase are completed, using an explicit transition marker (e.g.,
##NEXT##

Although each phase follows a structured sequence of actions, the agent allows for flexible progression and can adapt its responses within the interaction. At the system level, we allow the agent to engage in brief exchanges beyond the scripted prompts when the child initiates questions, comments, or side talk. As specified in the system prompt (Appendix Figure
9
), the agent is instructed to follow the current phase script closely, while also being allowed to ask or answer unscripted questions when relevant. This design enables flexible, child-responsive conversation within a predictable interaction structure.

Within each narrative stage (3)–(6), the agent follows a consistent interaction loop to support children’s story creation. Children first select story elements by scanning NFC tokens corresponding to a place, an item, and an emotion. Based on these inputs, the agent generates a short story draft for the current stage. The agent then poses two follow-up questions to support further elaboration and refinement: one focused on narrative attribute and one focused on social-emotional reasoning, as introduced in Section
3.1.3
. The agent then updates the story by incorporating the child’s inputs and presents the updated story through voice output. Through repeated iterations of this loop across stages, children produce a complete story with a coherent narrative structure.

4.
User Study

4.1.
Selection and Participation of Children

Ten early elementary–aged children (ages 6–8, M = 7.5; 5 girls, 5 boys) participated in this study. Participants were required to have spoken English proficiency, as the system currently supports English-language interaction. They were recruited through online flyers distributed by a local community organization in the United States. No prior experience with storytelling activities or digital technologies was required. Caregivers were informed during recruitment that participation required access to a mobile device capable of running the study application. Access to the application was provided through caregiver-created accounts using caregiver email addresses, in accordance with child data protection requirements. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Study materials, including the game board and NFC-embedded toys, were provided to families for home use. Caregivers were instructed to have their child complete the co-creative storytelling session twice over a two-week study period. Each session typically lasted approximately 30 minutes. Children did not receive monetary compensation but were allowed to keep the board and toys after the study concluded as a small token of appreciation.

Prior to participation, caregivers were also provided with pre-study training materials outlining their supervision role, potential risks associated with AI responses (e.g., biased or unexpected content), and procedures for pausing sessions or reporting concerns. The training emphasized active supervision and procedures for responding to potential concerns. It was intended to support safe and age-appropriate child–AI interaction during home use. Caregivers were also informed about the purpose of the study and may request a summary of the research findings upon completion.

During the study, we collected text-based interaction logs derived from speech-to-text (STT) processing of child–AI dialogue, system interaction logs, and the stories created during the sessions. To protect participants’ privacy, no raw audio recordings were stored or retained, and speech input was processed in real time for interaction purposes only. Basic demographic and contact information (e.g., child’s age, gender, and parent contact details) were collected for consent and study coordination purposes, but were stored separately from the research data and not included in the analysis. All research data were anonymized prior to analysis, securely stored, and used solely for research purposes.

4.2.
Study Design

We conducted a home-based, within-subject study to explore children’s experiences with
Tinker Tales

Conditions and counterbalancing.

To examine how children engaged with different forms of scaffolding during co-creative storytelling with an AI agent, we designed two conditions that differed only in the form of elaboration questions posed by the agent, with each condition implemented as a separate session. In the
Structured

Practice sessions.

Before the study, caregivers received an onboarding guide for supervision, which included instructions for having their child complete a short in-app practice prior to the storytelling activities. Data sources and materials

To capture interaction patterns during co-creative storytelling, we logged turn-by-turn dialogue between the child and the AI as transcribed text. Children’s spoken input during each session was transcribed in real time, resulting in child–AI interaction transcripts that served as the primary data source for our analysis (see Section

4.4.
Analytic procedure

We analyzed child–AI interaction transcripts using an iterative, inductive qualitative coding approach to examine how child–AI collaboration unfolded. Our analysis focused on interaction patterns, with particular attention to how children contributed to and refined narrative ideas, how the AI responded, and how stories were jointly constructed over multiple turns.

Unit of Analysis

We analyzed the data at two complementary levels:
Turn-level,
coding individual child and AI utterances, and
Stage-level,
corresponding to each narrative stage (Start, Journey, Climax, and End). We excluded other activity phases (e.g., Character selection and Post-story activity) from the coding, as our analysis focused on interaction patterns during further story elaboration and refinement. Broader observations on children’s engagement across the overall activity flow, including other phases, are presented in Section
4.5.2
.

Coding procedure

We began with open coding on a subset of sessions across both conditions (
Structured
and
Generic
), identifying recurring patterns in child–AI interaction that supported iterative story development. Our coding focused on moments of story development across turns, capturing (1) the type of question posed by the AI agent, (2) the narrative content contributed by the child in response, and (3) whether and how that contribution was taken up in subsequent story updates. Through iterative discussion within the research team, these codes were refined into a shared coding scheme capturing:

•

(1)
Agent question framing
, distinguishing educationally scaffolded elaboration questions, either narrative or social-emotional, from generic elaboration questions.

•

(2)
Narrative functions of child input
, focusing on how children attempted to advance the story through the introduction of new events, causal reasoning, and the attribution of emotional states to characters.

•

(3)
Uptake of child contributions
, defined as whether and how children’s inputs were incorporated into subsequent story updates at each stage, thereby shaping the evolving storyline.

Because children’s responses could serve multiple narrative functions within a single utterance (e.g., providing a causal explanation by introducing a new event.), child inputs were allowed to receive multiple narrative function labels when applicable. and Section

4.5.1.
Children’s AI Literacy as Context

To contextualize children’s interpretations of the AI agent, we administered a short AI literacy questionnaire at the first app login, before the storytelling sessions. Appendix Figure
16
summarizes the distribution of responses across all items (N=10). Overall, children entered the study primarily framing AI as something they can talk to—often described in social terms (e.g., a friend or robot)—and expecting it to answer questions and generate stories, even as their understanding of how it works remained mixed.

When asked what AI is, children most often described it as a
computer
(40%), but many also framed AI in relational terms such as a
friend
(30%) or a
robot
(30%). Consistent with this framing, children were more likely to identify conversational technologies as using AI (e.g., a phone that talks to you: 60%; a smart speaker: 50%) than less visible AI applications such as recommendation systems (30%) or game characters (10%). Children’s expectations of AI’s capabilities further emphasized communication and creativity. Nearly all believed that AI can
answer questions
(100%) and
make a fun story
(90%), and many also expected AI to
help with homework
(90%). In contrast, fewer children attributed emotional capacities to AI, such as
feeling happy or sad
(40%) or
loving people like family
(40%). When asked what they would want from their own AI, children most frequently selected practical assistance, such as
cleaning their room
(60%), suggesting an instrumental view of AI alongside its creative role.

Children’s mental models of how AI works were mixed. Some endorsed a computational explanation (e.g., “changes words into numbers”: 20%), while others anthropomorphized AI (e.g., “it listens like a person”: 30%) or expressed uncertainty (“I don’t know”: 30%). Similarly, half of the children believed that AI
creates stories by itself
(50%), with fewer attributing AI’s knowledge to
learning from books
(20%) or
searching on a computer
(20%). Despite this uncertainty about underlying mechanisms, children demonstrated emerging normative awareness around responsible AI use. Most agreed that AI should
be fair to everyone
(60%) and selected safety-related cautions such as
not believing everything AI says
(60%),
not letting it be mean
(60%), and
not telling secrets
(50%). When considering how AI errors should be handled, children indicated that
people should fix it
(70%), rather than
leaving correction solely to the AI
(30%).

4.5.2.
Children’s Initiative and Narrative Control

Within
Tinker Tales
, children exercised narrative control at multiple levels: (1) proposing an initial story idea, (2) selecting and defining the main characters, (3) choosing a place, item, and emotion to ground each narrative stage, and (4) elaborating and refining the unfolding story. Collectively, these decisions created repeated points of control that enabled children to steer the narrative, resulting in an authoring experience in which children carried their own story through to the final outcome.

Children often expressed an initial story idea or theme during the session opening, and in some cases this early ideation served as an organizing thread for the full activity. For example, one child (
P1
) proposed
“a big monster with big eyes, long feet, and lots of arms”
and specified an ending:
“somebody kicks the monster and the monster dies.”
This premise carried through the story as the rabbit, bear, and lion moved across locations and ultimately fought and defeated the monster. In another session, a child (
P4
) introduced a magic-themed destination, describing
“candy land”
as a place with
“magical powers”
and
“a lot of candy to eat,”
and framing the story as a
“best friends”
adventure involving traveling to
candy land
together. The story likewise unfolded around this premise as the characters began by a river, took a boat toward
candy land
, and continued the adventure. However, early ideation did not always shape the story. Some children began with underspecified intents (e.g.,
“a funny one,” “very fun story”
), expressed uncertainty (e.g.,
“I don’t know”
), or introduced an initial idea that did not carry through subsequent story updates. Overall, children varied in how much they engaged with early ideation and whether they carried it through later control points.

Children also established early ownership over
who
the story is about through character selection and definition. Across sessions, children consistently engaged in selecting the main characters and describing their traits and relationships. We did not observe refusals during character description. For instance, in one session, a child (
P8
) selected Frog, Bird, and Bear and described them in their own words: Frog
“loves jumping and digging in the sand”
; Bird
“fell down”
and
“could not fly anymore”
; and Bear
“got lost.”
The child also framed the trio as
“friends.”
The agent then carried these descriptions forward by weaving them into subsequent story drafts (e.g., treating Bird as a character who
“could not fly anymore”
and portraying Bear as someone who
could get lost
, while depicting them as
supportive friends
).

Furthermore, story element choices served as repeated control points over
where
and
how
the story progressed. The board structure distributed narrative control across the four narrative stages by requiring children to commit to three concrete story elements—place, item, and emotion—at each stage. These choices shaped the draft for each stage, which children then elaborated and refined through follow-up dialogue (e.g., adding events, causal links, or character perspectives). We examine these dynamics in Sections
4.5.3
and
4.5.4
.

Finally, post-story activities allowed children to revisit the completed story and reflect on their interpretations. For example, when asked a social–emotional reflection question about how the characters should have responded to a shark attack, one child (
P5
) said they should
“work together and defend them”
, reframing the moment through a relationship-centered lens. While earlier scaffolded questions may also shape engagement in post-story activities, we do not compare scaffolding conditions here due to the study’s exploratory nature and small sample size.

4.5.5.
Breakdowns, Repair, and Interactional Dynamics

Across sessions, interactional breakdowns occasionally emerged, due to background household noise, speech-to-text (STT) errors, child responses interrupted by system turn-taking, NFC scanning mistakes, or momentary misalignment between children’s intentions and the AI agent’s interpretation. However, such moments did not terminate or stall the activity. Instead, they were resolved through lightweight repair sequences that allowed children to reassert meaning. Below, we describe how these breakdowns were repaired through child–AI interaction and how interactional continuity was maintained.

Background noise in home environments contributed to interactional breakdowns, particularly when children’s speech was masked by unrelated household conversation. In such cases, the AI agent typically responded with clarification rather than moving the interaction forward. For example, when asked
why the characters heard a loud noise in the bushes
, a child’s response was dominated by unrelated background conversation in the home (
P1
). The agent then rephrased the question and asked again, “
I’m not sure what you mean. Let me ask again: Why do you think they heard that loud noise in the bushes?
”. Such noise-related breakdowns were not evenly distributed across sessions, but instead occurred frequently in a small subset (two sessions in our data).

When breakdowns stemmed from STT errors rather than noise, children often took an active role in repairing misunderstandings. For instance, during the character selection phase, one child (
P5
) attempted to describe the Lion character, but the utterance was nonsensically transcribed as “
It a lying
,” which the AI agent tentatively interpreted as “
Lion is a liar?
” The child immediately corrected the error “
No, Lion is friendly and funny… not a liar
”. In another session, when the agent asked what would happen next in the cave, the child’s response was transcribed as “
soda
” (
P4
). The agent initially incorporated this interpretation “
So there’s soda in the cave! What do they do with the soda?
”, after which the child corrected it “
I didn’t say soda
”, leading the AI agent to re-ask the question. Rather than disrupting interaction, these STT-related errors became part of the conversational process, creating moments in which children asserted interpretive authority and clarified intended meaning.

Breakdowns also arose when children’s utterances were interrupted by system turn-taking. In these moments, the AI agent treated partially captured responses as openings for continuation rather than progressing immediately. For instance, when a child’s response was captured as “
scared and
” to describe a lion character (
P1
), the agent immediately followed up with “
Scared and.. what? Tell me more about Lion
”. Similarly, when a child (
P4
) began explaining character relationships with “
first the bird
,” the agent encouraged elaboration “
Yes, go on! What about the Bird?
”, enabling the child to complete the intended idea. These sequences show how partially captured child responses were not left unresolved, but instead became sites of interactional repair through which children completed and refined their contributions.

NFC tagging errors were another common source of interactional breakdown. When children scanned an incorrect token or responded with off-task speech instead of scanning, the AI agent consistently relied on its prompting structure to guide repair. For example, when a child (
P2
) scanned an item token instead of a place token, the agent reiterated the expected action “
I need you to scan a yellow place token first
” until the correct token was provided. Similarly, when children responded verbally instead of scanning the token, the agent redirected the interaction back to the required action. For instance, when the agent prompted the child (
P1
) to scan an emotion token and the child instead responded verbally “
happy
”, the agent restated: “
You need to scan the correct NFC toy to choose. Pick up and scan a red emotion token and place it on the red circle.
” These sequences illustrate how procedural breakdowns were handled through redirection, allowing the activity to proceed without interruption.

Children also intervened directly in the interaction flow after it had already progressed, particularly when the system’s output or progression did not align with their intentions. In one session, after the story advanced to a later narrative stage, the AI agent referred to a character in a way that conflicted with the child’s intended characterization from an earlier turn. The child (
P2
) corrected the AI agent by stating, “
It was not a kid turtle. It was a baby turtle
”, which led the agent to restore the child’s intended representation. In another instance, a child (
P4
) explicitly requested to undo an accidental input “
Can I change the yellow token to another one? Because I scanned it accidentally
” and the AI agent accommodated this request by allowing the child to rescan a different token. These moments highlight children’s active role in monitoring not only narrative content but also interaction state over time, intervening when needed to realign the system’s behavior with their intentions.

Together, these observations suggest that interactional breakdowns were a common feature of children’s engagement with the system in home settings, shaped by both technical and situational factors. Rather than interrupting collaboration, these moments became sites where children actively intervened to clarify meaning and guide the interaction forward. We did not systematically correct transcripts for such breakdowns; instead, we treated them as situated interactional phenomena rather than as noise to be removed.

4.5.6.

To complement the interaction-based observations, we also considered responses from a brief optional post-study survey (Appendix

All six children reported high enjoyment of making stories with the AI, with four rating the experience as 5 and two rating it as 4 on a 5-point scale (M=4.67). much easier
, two reported that it was
a little easier

about the same

making fun stories
, and five selected
thinking of new ideas
. In contrast, only two child selected
talking with the AI friend
, and one selected
understanding what the AI friend does
. Children’s responses also reflected how they perceived the AI agent’s role in the activity: three children described the AI as feeling like a

, two as a

, and one as a

. Open-ended responses further showed a sense of shared activity, with children commenting, for example,

and

Others emphasized their own authorship more directly (e.g.,

,

5.
Discussion

Our findings suggest that young children can engage with an AI as a co-creative partner in developing an evolving story, jointly shaping the outcome while retaining narrative agency throughout the interaction. In
Tinker Tales
, children iteratively advanced their own narrative intentions, while the AI primarily influenced
how
ideas were organized and extended rather than
what
story was told. In the following section, we interpret these patterns in relation to our research questions, derive design implications for child–AI co-creative systems, and discuss limitations and future directions.

5.1.
RQ1: Educational scaffolding supported narrative elaboration while preserving children’s agency

Our observations suggest that children treated the story as something they could actively
refine
in progress, using the AI’s story outputs as material to work with rather than as a finished product to accept. After seeing how their story element choices shaped an initial draft, children layered their own narrative ideas onto the story to better reflect their intentions. This process resembles adult co-writing with AI
(
51
;
35
)
, while also drawing on practices from adult–child storytelling
(
39
;
43
)
, offering children an experiential understanding of how to communicate with an AI and shape a shared outcome while maintaining narrative control. Children’s post-study survey provides additional context: almost all children reported learning to
make fun stories
and
think of new ideas
through
Tinker Tales
, suggesting that they viewed the activity as involving active idea development in shaping the story.

In this process, scaffolding played a critical role by serving as an entry point into story refinement. General elaboration questions did not consistently engage children in developing the story; in many cases, children accepted the existing narrative as complete. Entirely open-ended questions can be difficult for younger children to operationalize, and engagement is highly sensitive to question framing
(
12
;
55
)
. By contrast, pedagogically grounded questions oriented children toward specific aspects of the narrative—such as unfolding events or characters’ emotional states—encouraging elaboration without prescribing its direction. This form of scaffolding reduced the cognitive burden of deciding
how
to refine ideas, helping children stay engaged in the ongoing co-creative storytelling flow. Importantly, although these questions guided attention, they did not constrain narrative outcomes: children retained control over whether to make changes, what aspects to build on, and how to elaborate their ideas. Across sessions, children variously introduced new events, added causal links, adjusted emotional dynamics, or chose not to make changes at all, suggesting that scaffolding shaped the
process
of story development rather than its content
(
54
;
5
)
.

In addition, grounding scaffolding in educational frameworks such as narrative development and social–emotional learning provided a developmentally aligned and safe way to support younger children’s participation, guiding interaction in predictable, pedagogically grounded ways
(
28
;
39
)
. At the same time, our findings suggest that different forms of grounding may elicit different types of child contributions, shaping the structure of interaction during story development. This opens up opportunities for future work to explore how alternative educational framings might intentionally steer co-creative processes in distinct ways.

5.2.
RQ2: Children perceived the AI as a responsive collaborator

Our observations suggest that children oriented to the AI agent not as a one-way guide, but as a responsive collaborator—one that listened and adapted to their input in ways that felt consequential. This perception was grounded in two aspects of the interaction: (1) children’s ideas accumulated into an evolving story over time, and (2) the agent handled in-the-moment interactional breakdowns through repair, helping children redirect or re-align the interaction when needed. Children’s post-study survey responses provide additional interpretive context, with the
Tinker Tales
experience often framed as an activity done
together
.

First, children experienced responsiveness through the cumulative structure of the activity. Because choices made at earlier moments—such as character definitions or previously constructed narrative elements—were consistently taken up in later story segments, children could observe a direct and sustained link between their input and the AI agent’s output. This carryover across narrative stages allowed children to treat the AI as something that responded to their ongoing involvement, rather than as a sequence of isolated exchanges. In this sense, responsiveness was not only enacted turn-by-turn, but also emerged through the persistence of meaningful interaction over time. More broadly, this suggests that responsiveness can be designed at the level of activity flow: carrying children’s decisions forward across stages helps sustain their sense that the agent is listening and responding.

Second, children experienced responsiveness through interaction repair during the activity. This responsive orientation toward the AI was closely tied to how the agent responded to children’s interventions
(
40
;
11
)
. When children stepped in to adjust the interaction, the agent responded promptly and appropriately by accommodating requests or re-aligning the interaction. The agent also tolerated brief side talk or off-task contributions, responding at an appropriate level before guiding the interaction back to the activity. Such flexible and responsive interaction supported children in treating the AI as a partner that listened and adapted to their input. More broadly, these observations highlight the role of conversational design choices in shaping an AI agent’s interactional stance. Because conversational agents are inherently flexible and underspecified, how an agent’s participation in dialogue is framed and constrained can meaningfully shape how users engage with and relate to the system
(
38
)
. Our findings suggest that such a balance may be an important consideration for designing child–AI interactions that support interactional adjustment, agency, and collaborative engagement.

5.3.
RQ3: Design insights for child–AI co-creative systems

Drawing on our observations of how children engaged in co-creative activities with the AI in
Tinker Tales
, we highlight five design considerations for supporting child–AI collaboration.

5.3.1.
Externalize the current phase in the shared process.

Young children may benefit when the shared process is made visible, especially which part they are working on right now. Physical layouts can externalize the activity structure, so moving between layouts becomes a tangible cue for “where we are” and “what comes next.”

5.3.2.
Make children’s contributions consequential in the shared outcome.

Co-creative systems can be designed so that children’s contributions shape the outcome in ways that feel consequential. When children can see their inputs taken up repeatedly across updates, collaboration can feel like ongoing joint construction rather than transient turn-taking.

5.3.3.
Treat AI prompting as interaction design, not just activity instruction.

AI prompt design can be understood as interaction design rather than mere procedural instruction. Prompt framing sets expectations for responsiveness and participation, shaping how children orient to the AI as a conversational partner.

5.3.4.
Use pedagogically grounded scaffolding as entry points for contribution.

For young children, fully open-ended invitations to contribute can be difficult to operationalize in the moment. Scaffolding can support participation by offering concrete entry points—guiding how to engage while preserving children’s control over content and direction. Grounding scaffolding in pedagogical frameworks can also keep AI responses developmentally appropriate and shape different co-creative dynamics.

5.3.5.
Balance structure and flexibility, including repair and redirection.

Structured activity flows can provide predictability and help young children sustain engagement over extended interactions. At the same time, co-creative systems can accommodate lightweight repair and redirection as natural parts of collaboration, staying responsive to children’s initiatives, deviations, and side talk without breaking the overall flow.

5.4.
Limitations

This study is exploratory and has several limitations. First, the number of participants was small (N=10), which limits the range of experiences represented in the data and limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized beyond the study context. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as qualitative insights into interactional patterns rather than as broad claims. Second, sessions were conducted in children’s homes, and we did not collect audio or video recordings to protect participants’ privacy. While interaction logs and transcripts capture verbal inputs and system responses, they do not include nonverbal behavior, physical arrangement that may have shaped how interactions unfolded. Third, all children interacted in English and were between the ages of 5 and 8. The findings may not extend to children in other linguistic, cultural, or developmental contexts, or to older children who may engage differently with our co-creative activities. Finally, children’s interaction with the system was limited to a small number of storytelling sessions. As a result, we were unable to examine how collaboration patterns, perceptions of the AI, or the role of scaffolding might evolve through more extended or repeated use over time.

5.5.
Future Work

A key direction for future work is to examine how children’s perceptions of and relationships with AI evolve through repeated encounters over longer periods and with more diverse groups of children. Longer-term deployments would enable examination of how collaboration patterns and the role of scaffolding change over time as children continue to interact with the system. Future work could also systematically vary the pedagogical frameworks used to ground scaffolding, comparing how different framings shape interactional dynamics and the kinds of co-creative practices they invite. While our study focused on narrative development and social–emotional learning, alternative frameworks may lead children to engage with AI in qualitatively different ways. An important extension of this direction is to explore how scaffolding strategies might be made configurable by caregivers or educators, allowing adults to tailor the AI’s guidance to children’s developmental goals or learning contexts. In addition, future deployments could situate child–AI co-creative activities within school-supported programs, where storytelling kits are lent to children for home use and later brought back for small-group discussion and reflection at school. This model would create opportunities for children to share, compare, and talk through their experiences of collaborating with an AI. Peer discussion could surface differing expectations, and support collective sensemaking about the AI’s role in creative work. As a next step, we aim to study child–AI co-creative interaction along three complementary directions: (1) with larger participant groups and over longer periods of use, (2) under varied pedagogical framings that can be customized, and (3) in school-supported small-group activities where children share, compare, and reflect on their AI collaborations.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we presented
Tinker Tales
, a tangible co-creative storytelling system that introduces children to human–AI collaboration through iterative story development with a conversational agent. Through a home-based user study with 10 participants, we observed that children treated stories as evolving artifacts, actively contributing ideas through interaction with the AI. Our analysis shows that educationally grounded scaffolding supported children’s engagement in story development while preserving their narrative agency. We also found that responsive interaction—particularly the visible uptake of children’s inputs and support for repair—appeared to sustain collaboration under real-world conditions, including moments of breakdown. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of how an AI participates in interaction for children’s co-creative engagement. We hope this work contributes interactional insights for future research and design of AI systems that support children’s collaborative and creative activities.
