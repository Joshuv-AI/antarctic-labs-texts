Global strong solutions to a compressible fluid-particle interaction model with density-dependent friction force Thanks: ∗ ​ Corresponding author
Fucai Li, Jinkai Ni ∗, Man Wu

Abstract
. We investigate the Cauchy problem for a fluid-particle interaction model in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . This model consists of the compressible barotropic Navier–Stokes equations and the Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation coupled together via the density-dependent friction force. Due to the strong coupling caused by the friction force, it is a challenging problem to construct the global existence and optimal decay rates of strong solutions. In this paper, by assuming that the H 2 H^{2} -norm of the initial data is sufficiently small, we establish the global well-posedness of strong solutions. Furthermore, if the L 1 L^{1} -norm of initial data is bounded, then we achieve the optimal decay rates of strong solutions and their gradients in L 2 L^{2} -norm. The proofs rely on developing refined energy estimates and exploiting the frequency decomposition method. In addition, for the periodic domain case, our global strong solutions decay exponentially.

Introduction

1.1.
Fluid-particle models

Fluid-particle models, where dispersive particles are suspended within a dense fluid, have been extensively applied in various fields, such as
dynamics of sprays
[
2
]
, combustion theory
[
43
,
44
]
, chemical engineering
[
6
]
, and biomedical sprays
[
3
]
. From a statistical viewpoint,
the behavior of particles is typically characterized by a probability density distribution
function
F=F(t,x,v)\geq 0

t>0
, the position
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

, which typically adheres to the Vlasov–Fokker–Planck (VFP) equation (​​
[
34
]
):

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla F+\mathrm{div}_{v}[F_{d}F-\nabla_{v}F]=0.

Here
F_{d}
denotes the friction force (also known as drag force)

(1.2)

\displaystyle F_{d}=F_{0}(u-v)

with some constant
F_{0}>0
(usually being chosen as one for simplicity), and
the unknown function
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents
the velocity of the fluid.

The formula (
1.2
) is widely used in incompressible homogeneous fluids
[
7
,
19
,
20
,
10
,
18
,
24
,
26
]
, incompressible inhomogeneous fluids
[
17
,
13
,
37
,
38
,

,

16

41
,
36
,
35
,
34
,

.
As Mellet and Vasseur pointed out in
[

, the choice of (
1.2
)
may not be the most
precise from a physical perspective, especially when the density of the fluid is varying,
for example, incompressible inhomogeneous fluids and compressible fluids. Thus, it is more appropriate
to assume that
F_{d}
depends on the fluid density
n=n(t,x)
, say,

(1.3)

\displaystyle F_{d}=F_{0}n(u-v),

which carries greater physical significance. However, due to the fluid density

involved in (
1.3
),
some trilinear terms in fluid-particle models appear. Hence it is more challenging to obtain global solutions
of the fluid-particle models
in this situation and only a few rigorous mathematical results are available, see
[

,
4
,
12
,
29

Wang and Yu
[

]
obtained the global existence of weak solutions to the following system

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla F+n\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F]=0,\\
&\partial_{t}n+\mathrm{div}(nu)=0,\quad\mathrm{div}u=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(nu)+\mathrm{div}(nu\otimes u)+\nabla\pi=-n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(u-v)F{\rm d}v{,}\end{aligned}\right.

with large data in a bounded domain with the reflection boundary condition. Choi and Kwon
[
12

obtained the strong solutions on
[0,T]

in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for arbitrary

is sufficiently small and regular. Moreover, they also showed that the velocities of particles and fluid
tend to the aligned values exponentially provided that the local density
of the particles satisfies a certain integrability condition.
Baranger and Desvillettes
[
4
]
first obtained the local-in-time classical solutions
to the following model

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla F+n\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F]=0,\\
&\partial_{t}n+\mathrm{div}(nu)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(nu)+\mathrm{div}(nu\otimes u)+\nabla P=-n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(u-v)F{\rm d}v{,}\end{aligned}\right.

in
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{x}
when the initial data are sufficiently smooth and their support has suitable properties. Carrillo, Goudon, and Lafitte
[
9
]
introduced a modified version of (
1.5
)
by adding a viscous term in (
1.5
)
1
, i.e. changing
n\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F]

to
n\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F-\nabla_{v}F]
. However, no mathematical results on this modified model were presented in
[
9
]
. By adding a viscous term
-\mu\Delta u
in the fluid equation (
1.5
)
3
to the model introduced in
[
9
]
,
Li, Mu, and Wang
[
29
]
considered the following
model:

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla F+n\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F-\nabla_{v}F]=0,\\
&\partial_{t}n+\mathrm{div}(nu)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(nu)+\mathrm{div}(nu\otimes u)+\nabla P-\mu\Delta u=-n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(u-v)F{\rm d}v{,}\end{aligned}\right.

in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a torus

, respectively. The pressure

In this paper, we revisit the model (

to
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

1.2.
Briefly review of related results

As mentioned before, most studies on fluid-particle models focus on the friction force with the form
(
1.2
). Below we briefly review some results on both the incompressible and compressible
models.

First, we consider the following incompressible model:

(1.7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla F+\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F-\nabla_{v}F]=0,\\
&{\rm div}u=0,\\
&\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla\pi-\Delta u=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(u-v)F{\rm d}v.\end{aligned}\right.

This model (
1.7
) is known as the incompressible
homogeneous Navier-Stokes-Vlasov-Fokker-Planck system.
Goudon, et al.
[
18
]
established the global existence of classical solutions in Sobolev space
H^{s}
(
s\geq 2)
to (
1.7
) near an equilibrium on a torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.
Chae, Kang, and Lee
[
10
]
proved the global existence of weak solutions to (
1.7
) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(where
d=2,3
). Carrillo, Duan and Moussa
[
7
]
achieved the time-decay rate of smooth solutions to the inviscid case of (
1.7
) in
L^{2}
-norm.
If the
\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F-\nabla_{v}F]
in (
1.7
)
1
is changed to
\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F]
, i.e. the diffusion term
-\Delta_{v}F
is neglected, the system (
1.7
) becomes the so-called
Vlasov–Navier–Stokes system and there are substantial mathematical works on it.
Hamdache
[
23
]
first established the global existence and long-time behavior of solutions for the Vlasov-Stokes system in a bounded domain.
Boudin, et al.
[
5
]
obtained
the global existence of weak solutions to the
Vlasov–Navier–Stokes
system in a torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.
Recently, Han-Kwan, Moussa and Moyano
[
26
]
studied large time behavior of the Vlasov-Navier–Stokes system on the torus, assuming that the initial modulated energy is sufficiently small. Besides, large time behavior of small-data solutions to the Vlasov-Navier–Stokes system on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
was studied in
[
24
]
. Ertzbischoff, Han-Kwan, and Moussa
[
17
]

investigated small smooth solutions for the
Vlasov-Navier–Stokes system in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Danchin
[
13
]
obtained the global existence of Fujita-Kato type solutions and demonstrated that if the
L^{1}
-norm of initial velocity is sufficiently small, then the total energy of the system decays to
0
at the optimal rate of
t^{-{3}/{4}}
in
L^{2}
-norm.
Goudon, Jabin, and Vasseur
[
19
,
20
]
, and Han-Kwan and Michel
[
25
]
studied the
hydrodynamic limit to the Vlasov-Navier-Stokes system in different regimes, see also
[
38
,
37
]

on the recent progress on the inhomogeneous incompressible Navier-Stokes-Vlasov(-Fokker-Planck) system.

Next, we turn to the compressible model:

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla F+\mathrm{div}_{v}[(u-v)F-\nabla_{v}F]=0,\\
&\partial_{t}n+{\rm div}(nu)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(nu)+\mathrm{div}(nu\otimes u)+\nabla P-\Delta u=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(u-v)F{\rm d}v.\end{aligned}\right.

Mellet and Vasseur
[

Additionally,

Very recently, Wang

established the global strong solutions in the

framework and derived the optimal decay rates for all derivatives of the solutions to (

For more results regarding the compressible fluid-particle models, interested readers can refer

1.3.
Our results and the strategies in the proofs

The objective of this paper is to establish the global existence and optimal
time decay rate of strong solutions to the system (
1.6
) near the equilibrium state
(F,n,u)\equiv(M,1,0)
,
where

M=M(v)={(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}}

represents the global Maxwellian.
We supplement the system (
1.6
) with the initial data

(1.9)

(F,n,u)|_{t=0}=(F_{0}(x,v),n_{0}(x),u_{0}(x)),\quad(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},

By setting the standard
perturbation
n=1+\rho
,
F=M+\sqrt{M}f
, we can rewrite the system (
1.6
) and (
1.9
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+u\cdot\nabla_{v}f-{\frac{1}{2}}u\cdot vf-u\cdot v\sqrt{M}

(1.10)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathcal{L}f+\rho\Big(\mathcal{L}f-u\cdot\nabla_{v}f+\frac{1}{2}u\cdot vf+u\cdot v\sqrt{M}\Big),

(1.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+(\rho+1){\rm div}u+\nabla\rho\cdot u=0,

(1.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+{\frac{P^{\prime}(1+\rho)}{1+\rho}}\nabla\rho-\frac{\mu\Delta u}{1+\rho}={b-u-au},

with initial data

(1.13)

(f,\rho,u)|_{t=0}=(f_{0}(x,v),\rho_{0}(x),u_{0}(x))=\bigg(\frac{F_{0}(x,v)-M}{\sqrt{M}},n_{0}(x)-1,u_{0}(x)\bigg).

Here,
\mathcal{L}
is the linearized Fokker-Planck operator defined by

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathrm{div}_{v}\left[M\nabla_{v}\left(\frac{f}{\sqrt{M}}\right)\right]=\Delta_{v}f-\frac{|v|^{2}}{4}f+\frac{3}{2}f,

and
a=a^{f},b=b^{f}
are corresponding moments of
f
given by

\displaystyle a^{f}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{M}f(t,x,v){\rm d}v,\quad b^{f}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v\sqrt{M}f(t,x,v){\rm d}v.

The main results of this paper can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the initial data
(f_{0},\rho_{0},u_{0})

satisfy that
\|f_{0}\|_{H_{x,v}^{2}}+\|(\rho_{0},u_{0})\|_{H^{2}}
is sufficiently small and
F_{0}=M+

\sqrt{M}f_{0}\geq 0
. Then the Cauchy problem (
1.10
)–(
1.13
) admits a unique global strong solution

(f,\rho,u)
satisfying
F=M+\sqrt{M}f\geq
0 and

\displaystyle f\in C([0,\infty);H_{x,v}^{2}),\quad\rho,u\in C([0,\infty);H^{2}),

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\{\|f(t)\|_{H_{x,v}^{2}}+\|(\rho,u)(t)\|_{H^{2}}\big\}\leq C\big(\|f_{0}\|_{H_{x,v}^{2}}+\|(\rho_{0},u_{0})\|_{H^{2}}\big),

for some constant
C>0
.

Remark 1.1
.

When addressing the energy estimate of higher-order derivatives of solutions, as per
[
29
, Proposition 4.2]
, the energy structure of density is
\frac{\sqrt{P^{\prime}(1+\rho)}}{1+\rho}\|\partial^{\alpha}\rho\|^{2}
. This results in the estimate of
\partial_{t}\rho
. Consequently, a fourth-order regularity of solutions is necessary (refer to the estimate of
I_{14}
in
[
29
, Proposition 4.2]
). Unlike
[
29
]
, the energy we have chosen in Lemma
3.2
is
P^{\prime}(1)\|\partial^{\alpha}\rho\|^{2}
. By means of a refined energy method, we can restrict our estimates in the
H^{2}
framework.

Theorem 1.2
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1.1
, if we further assume that

\|(f_{0},\rho_{0},u_{0})\|_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}
is bounded, then

(1.15)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}_{v}(H^{2})}+\|(\rho,u)\|_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}},

(1.16)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{v}(H^{1})}+\|\nabla(\rho,u)\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{5}{4}},

(1.17)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{L^{2}_{v}(L^{2})}+\|\nabla^{2}(\rho,u)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{5}{4}},

for some constant
C>0
and any
t\geq 0
. Moreover,

(1.18)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}_{v}(L^{p})}+\|(\rho,u)\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{2}(1-\frac{1}{p})},

holds for
2\leq p\leq 6
, and while

(1.19)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}_{v}(L^{p})}+\|(\rho,u)\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{5}{4}},

holds for
6\leq p\leq\infty
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the local existence part is standard in some sense and can be established
by applying linearization method and Banach’s fixed point theorem, while the argument for global
existence part relies on establishing newly uniform-in-time a priori energy estimates.
Compared to the approach developed in
[
29
]
where
the
H^{4}
regularity assumption of smooth solutions simplifies
the energy estimate process, our constraints on
H^{2}
regularity of solutions bring more challenges and
require us develop new ideas to enclose the energy estimates, see Section 3 for more details.

For the proof of Theorem
1.2
, the nonlinear term
\rho\mathcal{L}f
in the equation (
1.10
) brings about significant difficulties in estimating the decay rates of the solutions.
For the friction force (
1.2
) considered in
[
7
,
41
]
, the nonlinear terms in the perturbation of the particle equation
f
do not include the term
v^{2}f
and their structures are relatively simpler than our case (
1.3
). Consequently, when conducting energy estimates, the a priori estimates can be
enclosed without performing energy estimates on the mixed space-velocity
derivatives of solutions, see
[
7
,
41
]
for more details. In contrast, our friction force (
1.3
) involves the density
\rho
, and the equation (
1.10
) includes the nonlinear terms
\rho v^{2}f
and
\rho\mathcal{L}f
, necessitating the energy estimates incorporating the mixed space-velocity derivatives of solutions to manage our nonlinear term
K_{5}
(see (
4.17
)).
By decomposing
\rho\mathcal{L}f
into
\rho\mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}\}f

and
\rho\mathcal{L}\mathbf{P}f
in Section 4,
and leveraging the results of Theorem
4.1
,
we successfully derive the decay rates of strong solutions
to (
1.10
)–(
1.13
) in
L^{2}
-norm under the assumption that the
L^{1}
-norm of the initial data
is sufficiently small. Since the nonlinear term contains the second derivative terms such as
\Delta u
and
\Delta_{v}f
, it is difficult to get decay rates of the higher derivative
of
(f,\rho,u)
by applying the classical energy method.
To overcome these difficulties, in Section 5, we adopt a more refined low-high frequency decomposition method and an improved energy method, thereby relaxing the requirement for the initial data of
L^{1}
-norm, i.e. only boundedness
of
L^{1}
-norm is needed.
A crucial step in the subsequent proof of Theorem
1.2
is
to establish the following inequality (see (
5.27
)):

(1.20)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t)+\lambda_{16}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t)\leq C\|\nabla^{2}(a^{L},b^{L},\rho^{L},u^{L})\|^{2},

where the functional satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(t)\backsim\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{L_{v}^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla^{2}(\rho,u)\|^{2}.

Here we develop a refined low-frequency
estimate in Theorem
5.4
, based on the Duhamel principle,
which exhibits more information than that in Theorem
4.1
.
By combining Theorem
5.4
with the inequality (
1.20
),
we derive the optimal time decay rates of strong
solutions and their gradients. In addition, by utilizing the interpolation inequality,
we also determine the optimal decay rate
(1+t)^{-{3}/{2}(1-{1}/{p})}
of solutions
in
L^{p}
-norm for
2\leq p\leq 6
, and a decay rate
(1+t)^{-5/4}
in
L^{p}
-norm for
6\leq p\leq\infty
.
In stark contrast to the density-independent friction force (
1.2
) studied in

[
41
,
31
]
, for our density-dependent friction force case (
1.3
), due to the fact that we cannot fully transform
the term
\rho\mathcal{L}f
into an absolute energy term
\mathcal{E}(t)
,
and consequently derive the corresponding dissipation
\mathcal{D}(t)
,
we are unable to establish the optimal time-decay rates
for the second-order spatial derivatives of strong solutions.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows.
In Section 2, we present some preliminary notations
and properties that will be used throughout the paper.
In Section 3, we establish a priori estimates
for the problem (
1.10
)–(
1.13
) and prove
the global existence of strong solutions for the Cauchy
problem. Time-decay estimates of the solutions are derived
in Section 4 by the classical energy method and in Section 5 by using low-frequency and
high-frequency decomposition techniques along with Fourier analysis.
In Section 6, we demonstrate that when particles lie
in a torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
, the time-decay rate of convergence
of strong solutions is exponential.
Finally, in the Appendix, we present several useful lemmas.

2.1.
Notations

The letter
C
denotes a generic positive constant which may change from line to line. The symbol

denotes the relation
\frac{A}{C}\leq B\leq CA

.
We set

for the Banach

, where the functions

and

belong to

.

We denote the weight function

and the norm

as

\displaystyle|g|_{\nu}^{2}:

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|\nabla_{v}g(v)|^{2}+\nu(v)|g(v)|^{2}){\rm d}v,\quad g=g(v).

For the corresponding norm concerning with the dissipation of
the Fokker-Planck operator, we denote by

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\nu}^{2}:

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|\nabla_{v}g(x,v)|^{2}+\nu(v)|g(x,v)|^{2}){\rm d}x{\rm d}v.

For simplicity of notation, we use

to denote the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L_{x}^{2}}
or
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}
, and

inner product over space

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle g,h\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}g(v)h(v)\mathrm{d}v,\quad g,h\in L_{v}^{2}.

For

\displaystyle\|g\|_{Z_{q}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|g(x,v)|^{q}\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{2}{q}}\mathrm{d}v.

For any multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
,

\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})
, we always denote
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\partial_{x_{3}}^{\alpha_{3}}\partial^{\beta_{1}}_{v_{1}}\partial^{\beta_{2}}_{v_{2}}\partial^{\beta_{3}}_{v_{3}}

. The lengths of

\displaystyle\|g\|_{H^{s}}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\|\partial^{\alpha}g\|,\quad\|g\|_{H_{x,v}^{s}}=\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq s}\|\partial_{\beta}^{\alpha}g\|.

For an integrable function

, its Fourier transform
is given by

\widehat{g}(k)=\mathcal{F}g(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-ix\cdot k}g(x){\rm d}x,\quad x\cdot k=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}x_{j}k_{j},

.
Here,

For two complex functions

Finally, we denote norms

,

6.
The periodic domain case

In this section, we deal with the Cauchy problem (
1.10
)–(
1.13
) in the spatial periodic domain
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. We have

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that the initial data
(f_{0},\rho_{0},u_{0})

satisfy that
F_{0}=M+

\sqrt{M}f_{0}\geq 0
,

\|f_{0}\|_{H_{x,v}^{2}}+\|(\rho_{0},u_{0})\|_{H^{2}}
is sufficient small, and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}a_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big(b_{0}+(1+\rho_{0})u_{0}\big)\mathrm{d}x=0,

where

\displaystyle a_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\sqrt{M}f_{0}(x,v)\mathrm{d}v,\ \ b_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v\sqrt{M}f_{0}(x,v)\mathrm{d}v.

Then, the Cauchy problem (
1.10
)–(
1.13
) admits a unique global strong solution
(f,\rho,u)
satisfying
F=M+\sqrt{M}f\geq 0
, and

\displaystyle f\in C([0,\infty);H_{x,v}^{2}),\quad\rho,u\in C([0,\infty);H^{2}),

\displaystyle\|f(t)\|_{H_{x,v}^{2}}+\|(\rho,u)(t)\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\big(\|f_{0}\|_{H_{x,v}^{2}}+\|(\rho_{0},u_{0})\|_{H^{2}}\big)e^{-\zeta_{0}t},

with some constant
\zeta_{0}>0
, for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

First, by a direct calculation,
the following conservation laws can be obtained from (
1.6
):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}}F\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}v=0,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}n\mathrm{d}x=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}nu\mathrm{d}x+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}}vF\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}v\right)=0.

Then, according to the assumption in Theorem
6.1
, we derive that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}a\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big(b+(1+\rho)u\big)\mathrm{d}x=0,

for any
t\geq 0
.

Due to the fact that the proof of local existence and uniqueness of
(f,\rho,u)
in

\mathbb{T}^{3}
is similar to that in the
whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
case, we only show the proof of the global a priori
estimates here. From the Poincaré’s inequality and (
6.1
), one gets

\displaystyle\|a\|_{L^{2}}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|\nabla a\|_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{L^{2}}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle\|u+b\|_{L^{2}}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|b+\rho(1+u)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\rho u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\big\|\nabla\big(b+\rho(1+u)\big)\big\|_{L^{2}}+\|\rho\|_{L^{3}}\|u\|_{L^{6}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|\nabla(b,u)\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{3}}\|\rho\|_{L^{6}}+C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}}+\|\rho\|_{H^{1}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}

(6.2)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|\nabla(b,u,\rho)\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus, we deduce from (
6.2
) and basic triangle inequality that

(6.3)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|u-b\|_{L^{2}}+C\|u+b\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|u-b\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\nabla(b,u,\rho)\|_{L^{2}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\|b\|_{L^{2}}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|u-b\|_{L^{2}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|u-b\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\nabla(b,u,\rho)\|_{L^{2}}.

Now, let us define the energy functional
\mathcal{E}(t)
and the corresponding dissipation rate functional
\mathcal{D}(t)
on
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{3}
, exactly as in the case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see (
3.61
)). And we also define

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{T}}(t):=\mathcal{D}(t)+\|(a,b,\rho,u)\|_{L^{2}}.

Applying
the similar process on getting (
3.62
) to the
\mathbb{T}^{3}
case, then combining
(
6.2
)–(
6.5
), and using the fact that
\mathcal{E}(t)\leq C\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{T}}(t)
, we eventually obtain

(6.6)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathcal{E}(t)+\lambda\mathcal{E}(t)\leq 0,

for some
\lambda>0
.
Then, from Gronwall’s inequality, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)\leq e^{-\lambda t}\mathcal{E}(0),

which leads to the exponential decay rate of
\mathcal{E}(t)
. Noticing that
\mathcal{E}(t)\backsim\|(f,\rho,u)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}^{2}
in time, hence, we complete the proof
of Theorem
6.1
.

, one has

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|\nabla_{x}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C\|g\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\|g\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

for
2\leq q\leq 6
.

Lemma A.3

(see
[
1
,
33
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer, then we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}(gh)\|_{L^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|g\|_{L^{r_{1}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}\|\nabla^{k}h\|_{L^{r_{2}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}+C\|h\|_{L^{r_{3}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}\|\nabla^{k}g\|_{L^{r_{4}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)},

and

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}(gh)-g\nabla^{k}h\|_{L^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla g\|_{L^{r_{1}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}\|\nabla^{k-1}h\|_{L^{r_{2}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}+C\|h\|_{L^{r_{3}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}\|\nabla^{k}g\|_{L^{r_{4}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)},

with
1<r,r_{2},r_{4}<\infty
and
r_{i}(1\leq i\leq 4)
satisfy the following identity:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{1}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}}=\frac{1}{r_{3}}+\frac{1}{r_{4}}=\frac{1}{r}.

Lemma A.4

(see
[
1
]
)
.

Suppose that
1\leq r\leq s\leq q\leq\infty
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=\frac{\zeta}{r}+\frac{1-\zeta}{q},

where
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
.
Assume also
g\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then
g\in L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{s}}\leq\|g\|_{L^{r}}^{\zeta}\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{1-\zeta}.

Lemma A.5

(See
[
7
, Lemma 3.2]
)
.

Given any
0<\beta_{1}\neq 1
and
\beta_{2}>1
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(1+t-s)^{-\beta_{1}}(1+s)^{-\beta_{2}}{\rm d}s\leq C(1+t)^{-\min\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}},

for all
t\geq 0
.

Lemma A.6

(See
[
7
, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

Let
\gamma>1
and
g_{1},g_{2}\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})
with

g_{1}(0)=0
. For
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, define
\mathcal{B}_{A}:=\{y\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+},(\mathbb{R}_{+})|\,\,y\leq A+g_{1}(A)y+g_{2}(A)y^{\gamma},\,\,y(0)\leq A\}
.
Then, there exists a constant
A_{0}\in(0,\,\min\{A_{1},A_{2}\})
such that for
any
0<A\leq A_{0}
,

\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{B}_{A}\Rightarrow\sup_{t\geq 0}y(t)\leq 2A.

Finally, for a function
g(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we define the
low and high frequencies decompositions

g^{L}
and
g^{H}

of
it as

\displaystyle g^{L}(x)=\phi_{0}(D_{x})g(x),\quad g^{H}(x)=\phi_{1}(D_{x})g(x).

Here,
D_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-1}}{(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{3}})}
, and
\phi_{0}(D_{x})

and
\phi_{1}(D_{x})
are two pseudo-differential operators
corresponding to the smooth cut-off functions
\phi_{0}(k)
and
\phi_{1}(k)
satisfying

0\leq\phi_{0}(k)\leq 1
,
\phi_{1}(k)=1-\phi_{0}(k)
, where

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(k)=\begin{cases}1,\quad|k|\leq\frac{r_{0}}{2},\\
0,\quad|k|>{r_{0}},\end{cases}

for a fixed constant
r_{0}>0
. It is obvious that

\displaystyle g(x)=g^{L}(x)+g^{H}(x),

which together with Plancherel Theorem gives rise to the following lemma.

Lemma A.7

(see
[
42
]
)
.

Let
g\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\|g^{H}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|g^{H}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\nabla^{2}g\|_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}g^{L}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\nabla g^{L}\|_{L^{2}},

for some constant
C>0
.
