Introducing tetracycline as a potential OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitor: an in silico and in vitro approach
Authors: Hourieh Kalhor, Mana Salehibarmi, Maryam Siroosi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of OXA-48 carbapenemase has significantly compromised the effectiveness of carbapenems in treating drug-resistant Gram-negative infections, highlighting an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies due to the limitations of currently available β-lactam inhibitors against this enzyme. To address this issue, the present study aimed to identify new inhibitors of OXA-48 carbapenemase. Structure-based virtual screening of a library of bacterial secondary metabolites, using molecular docking, was performed to evaluate the ability of these metabolites to inhibit OXA-48 carbapenemase. The screening discovered several promising lead compounds with favorable docking scores of − 9.8 kcal/mol or lower, which were further evaluated through molecular dynamics simulations. Structural analysis of the top lead compound, Tetracenoquinocin, revealed notable structural similarity between this compound and the Tetracycline class of antibiotics, leading to the investigation of Tetracycline as a repurposing candidate. Comparative docking scores and molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the OXA-48/Tetracycline complex exhibited superior binding stability, with a more consistent RMSD profile and stronger hydrogen-bonding interaction (4–6 H-bonds) compared to the Tetracenoquinocin complex. These computational findings were validated by in vitro analyses, where a combination of Tetracycline and Meropenem successfully reduced the minimum inhibitory concentration of Meropenem for clinical Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates harboring the blaOXA−48 gene. Introduction

β-Lactam antibiotics including Penicillins, Cephalosporins, Carbapenems, and Monobactams have a β-lactam ring in their chemical structure and they all belong to the group of bacterial cell wall synthesis inhibitors. They inactivate peptidoglycan trans-peptidases and prevent proper formation of peptidoglycan polymers. On the other hand, bacterial β-lactamases pose a serious threat to the effectiveness of these life-saving antibiotics by hydrolyzing them1. Since it was found that Enterobacteriaceae can develop molecular resistance through extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), they have been recognized as a public health concern2. Based on the Ambler classification system, β-lactamases are categorized in four groups (A, B, C, and D). Class B β-lactamases are metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), whereas classes A, C, and D are serine β-lactamases3,4. Class D β-lactamases, also known as oxacillinases, include all enzymes similar to OXA-48 β-lactamase. These OXA-48 like enzymes are able to hydrolyze carbapenems, but not (or only weakly) cephalosporins, and they are not inhibited by traditional β-lactamase inhibitors5. OXA β-lactamases have a carbamylated lysine at position 73 (KCX73) and a serine residue at position 70. They use this carbamylated lysine to activate Ser70 for attack on the β-lactam ring during acylation, and to activate the active-site water molecule required for hydrolysis of the acyl–enzyme intermediate6. The first OXA-48-producing bacterial isolate was recovered from a patient in Istanbul, Turkey. This isolate was a multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae with high resistance to all β-lactam antibiotics, including broad-spectrum Cephalosporins, Cephamycins, Monobactams, and Carbapenems. Analysis of this isolate revealed a plasmid-encoded gene, blaOXA−48, which expressed a β-lactamase that shared only 46, 36, 32, and 21% amino acid identity with OXA-10, OXA-23, OXA-40, and OXA-1, respectively. This indicates that this β-lactamase enzyme is distantly related to other β-lactamases7. After the initial discovery of OXA-48 in Turkey, numerous countries, including those in the Middle East and North Africa started to report this enzyme, followed by other countries around the world8. Globally, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) colonization or infection is becoming more common in hospital outbreaks. Among carbapenemases, OXA-48 is the most prevalent one in Iran and very common in other Middle East countries, an endemic area for carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales9. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) are resistant to several antibiotics, including Carbapenems. Their extensive drug resistance poses a serious problem in clinical settings10. There aren’t many alternatives for treating infections brought on by these resistant bacteria, and the treatment often require the use of newer or older medicines. Although Colistin and Polymyxin B are frequently used, their toxicity and inconsistent effectiveness limit their use to serious infections. Tigecycline has been used to treat CRE infections, but because of its limited effectiveness when taken alone, it is usually advised to use it in conjunction with other medications11. Ceftazidime-Avibactam is an approved treatment for Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales. However, the emergence of resistance to this therapy is a growing concern. Meropenem-Vaborbactam is another β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitor combination used to treat infections caused by Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales. However, Vaborbactam is ineffective against OXA-48 carbapenemase12.

Given the challenge of treating infections caused by OXA-48 carbapenemase-producing bacteria, particularly the limited effectiveness of current β-lactamase inhibitors, there is a need to identify new inhibitors targeting this enzyme. Recent advances in computational drug discovery have accelerated this process by reducing time and cost. These in silico approaches enable high-throughput and accurate screening of large chemical libraries to identify potential inhibitors, providing a powerful strategy to address antibiotic resistance13,14. Using these methods, millions of compounds can be assessed efficiently, and protein-ligand interactions can be analyzed rapidly and accurately15. Molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, binding free energy analysis, and pharmacokinetic analysis are among the techniques frequently used in these approaches16–18. Molecular docking is a widely used method for evaluating how ligands interact with protein receptors and for predicting both their binding modes and affinities19. MD simulation predicts the motions of all atoms in a biomolecular system over time to study protein and ligand dynamics, conformational changes, and responses to perturbations at atomic resolution20.

The present study employed structure-based virtual screening to identify novel natural products with potential inhibitory activity against OXA-48 carbapenemase. A library of natural products derived from Streptomyces spp. in StreptomeDB 4.0 database (https://streptomedb.vm.uni-freiburg.de/streptomedb) was screened using AutoDock Vina to rank the compounds based on their binding affinity and docking position to the enzyme’s active site to identify potential lead compounds. Additionally, MD simulation studies were performed to evaluate the conformational stability, flexibility, and behavior of the OXA-48 carbapenemase and lead compounds over a 200 ns simulation. The most promising lead compound, Tetracenoquinocin, showed structural similarity to the Tetracycline class of antibiotics. Consequently, further computational and experimental studies were performed to assess the effectiveness of Tetracycline against OXA-48 carbapenemase. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating Tetracycline as a potential inhibitor of OXA-48 carbapenemase.

Methods

Receptor preparation for molecular docking

The three-dimensional crystal structure of OXA-48 carbapenemase (PDBID: 3HBR)21 was retrieved from RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). The protein was then prepared as a receptor for non-covalent molecular docking using AutoDock Tools 4.222. This process involved several steps: first, all water molecules were removed; second, AutoDock atom types and bond orders were assigned; and third, hydrogen atoms and Gasteiger–Marsili charges were added. The final receptor structure was saved in PDBQT format, ready for subsequent docking simulations22.

The amino acid residues involved in the catalytic reaction of OXA-48 carbapenemase were identified to serve as the binding pocket for virtual screening and molecular docking studies. The active site was defined based on a previous study21 and confirmed by analyzing the crystallographic structure of the enzyme using the PDBsum database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/) and LigPlot+ software23.

The key catalytic residues, including Ser70, KCX 73 (which was replaced by a standard Lys residue), Ile 102, Ser118, Gln 124, Thr209, Arg214, Ser244, and Arg250, were used to define the grid box for molecular docking. AutoDock Tools 4.2 was employed to create a grid box encompassing these residues, with dimensions of 26 × 26 × 26 Å and a grid spacing of 1 Å. Prior to running the molecular docking, it was confirmed in both AutoDock Tools 4.2 and PyMOL 3.124 that the selected grid dimensions fully encompassed the entire catalytic cavity.

Ligand collection and preparation

The StreptomeDB 4.0 library, a unique and valuable resource of approximately 8,500 natural products (NPs) derived from 3,900 Streptomyces species25, was selected, and its ligands were prepared for molecular docking against the OXA-48 carbapenemase. The library was first filtered based on Lipinski’s rule of five as a primary drug-likeness criterion, which requires a molecular weight of ≤ 500 Da, a maximum of five hydrogen bond donors, a maximum of ten hydrogen bond acceptors, and a LogP value of ≤ 526. This filtration was performed using Open Babel software version 2.3.227 and NPs that met all these criteria were selected for subsequent steps.

Each compound in the filtered library was then prepared for molecular docking using the following steps: nonpolar hydrogens were merged, Gasteiger–Marsili charges were added, and AutoDock atom types and rotatable bonds were assigned. The final prepared ligands were saved in PDBQT format for use in the docking simulations.

Virtual screening and molecular docking studies

In the virtual screening step, AutoDock Vina 1.2.028,29 was employed to perform molecular docking of 2,500 NPs from the filtered StreptomeDB 4.0 library into the defined binding site of OXA-48 carbapenemase to evaluate the structural interactions between NPs and the enzyme16,30.

Moreover, Avibactam was used as a reference inhibitor, and re-docking was carried out to qualitatively evaluate the reliability of the docking protocol in reproducing ligand binding within the active site of OXA-48 carbapenemase.

The docking results were then ranked based on their docking scores, with lower (more negative) values indicating a higher predicted affinity between the ligand and the receptor31. The top-scoring ligands were subsequently selected as hit NPs for further analysis. The molecular interactions between the best-ranked NPs and OXA-48 carbapenemase were analyzed and visualized using PyMOL 3.1 and Discovery Studio software V24.1.033432. Consequently, the hit NPs exhibiting the most favorable binding poses, based on docking scores and molecular interactions (hydrogen bonds and other interactions), were chosen as lead compounds.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation analysis

MD simulations were performed to evaluate the conformational changes, dynamic behavior, and binding stability of the OXA-48 carbapenemase in complex with the selected lead compounds. All simulations were conducted for 200 ns using GROMACS software version 2020.633–35. The CHARMM36 force field was applied for the protein. To prepare the ligand files for MD simulations, the structures of the lead compounds were first converted from PDB to MOL2 format, and hydrogen atoms were added using Open Babel version 2.3.2. The ligand topology files were then generated using SwissParam (www.swissparam.ch)36, which provides CHARMM-compatible parameters derived from the Merck Molecular Force Field (MMFF). The ligand topology files generated by SwissParam were examined to ensure that no missing parameters or abnormal values were present.

The complex corresponding to the lowest binding energy pose from docking for each OXA-48 carbapenemase/ligand system was solvated in a cubic box with TIP3P water molecules, ensuring a minimum distance of 10 Å between protein atoms and the box edges. To neutralize the system, Na+ and Cl− ions were introduced. Following energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm to remove steric clashes, the system was equilibrated in two stages. First, a 1 ns NVT simulation was performed at 310 K using the velocity-rescaling thermostat. Second, a 1 ns NPT simulation followed at 310 K and 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Finally, MD simulations were performed for 200 ns. The MD trajectories were analyzed using the following parameters: root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bonds (H-bonds), and protein root mean square fluctuation (RMSF).

Binding free energy calculation

The molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) approach was employed to assess the binding free energy (ΔGbind) between OXA-48 carbapenemase and ligands using the gmx_MMPBSA tool37 for ranking ligands and evaluating their inhibitory potential. This approach is widely used to refine virtual screening results and rationalize experimental observations. However, it has inherent limitations, such as the omission of solute configurational entropy38.

To calculate the binding free energy for each system, snapshots were extracted at regular intervals of 2 ps from the final 50 ns of the MD simulation trajectories. Individual energy components, including van der Waals (DEvdw), electrostatic (DEele), polar solvation (ΔEGB), non-polar solvation (ΔESURF), gas-phase (ΔGGAS), total solvation (ΔGSOLV), and total binding free energy (ΔGbind) were calculated37.

Structural analysis of the most promising lead compound

The chemical structure of the most promising lead compound identified in this study, Tetracenoquinocin, was characterized based on its key chemical features. Its core scaffold and functional groups were visually examined using PyMOL, and its chemical classification was confirmed using the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to identify structurally related analogues.

Computational analysis of Tetracenoquinocin analogues

The potential inhibitory effect of several Tetracycline antibiotics, including Minocycline, Doxycycline, and Tetracycline, on OXA-48 carbapenemase was evaluated. Molecular docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina, following the methodology described above. The docking results and molecular interactions were analyzed to identify the most promising Tetracycline analogue. This selected analogue, Tetracycline, was further subjected to MD simulations and binding free energy calculations to assess its complex stability and binding affinity.

In vitro studies

To assess whether Tetracycline could inhibit OXA-48 carbapenemase and thereby increase bacterial susceptibility to Meropenem, a Carbapenem antibiotic, two Meropenem-resistant, clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae (Kp03 and Kp04), previously confirmed to harbor the blaOXA−48 gene and lacking other carbapenemase genes, were selected for in vitro experiments. Two additional Meropenem-sensitive, clinical K. pneumoniae isolates (Kp14 and Kp15), which were negative for carbapenemase genes39 were included as negative controls. All isolates were multi-drug resistant and identified by routine microbiological tests and khe gene amplification40. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Meropenem and Tetracycline for these isolates was determined in accordance with CLSI guidelines. According to CLSI, the MIC breakpoints for Meropenem are as follows: susceptible, ≤ 1 µg/mL; intermediate, 2 µg/mL; resistant, ≥ 4 µg/mL41. In comparison, EUCAST defines meropenem MIC breakpoints as: susceptible, ≤ 2 µg/mL; susceptible, increased exposure, 4–8 µg/mL; resistant, > 8 µg/mL42.

To evaluate the combined effect of Tetracycline and Meropenem, a checkerboard microdilution assay was conducted43. Tetracycline was serially diluted across the columns of a 96-well microtiter plate, reaching concentrations from 0.031 to 8 µg/mL. Subsequently, Meropenem was serially diluted across the rows, from 1/32 of its MIC up to the MIC value for each isolate. A bacterial suspension was then added to each well, resulting in a final concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/mL in a total volume of 100 µL. After incubation for 18 h at 37 °C, bacterial growth was determined by measuring the turbidity at 600 nm. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) for each combination of agents was calculated as: FICI = (MIC of Meropenem in combination/MIC of Meropenem alone) + (MIC of Tetracycline in combination/MIC of Tetracycline alone). For interpretation, FIC < 1 indicates synergy, FIC = 1 indicates additivity, and FIC > 1 indicates antagonism44.

The experiments were repeated three times and included a range of controls to ensure the accuracy of the results. These included a bacterial growth control, a sterility control, a Meropenem-only control, and a Tetracycline-only control.

Results

OXA-48 carbapenemase structure and the catalytic site

OXA-48 carbapenemase is a homodimeric enzyme with four chains (A, B, C, D), and each chain has 265 amino acid residues. According to the crystallographic structure of OXA-48 carbapenemase, the active site is located within a single monomer, positioned in a narrow intra-monomer crevice between the two structural domains of the same chain, with dimensions of 5 Å in width, 20 Å in length, and 10 Å in depth. As shown in Fig. 1, Ser70, Lys73 (KCX73), Ile102, Ser118, Gln124, Thr209, Arg214, Ser244, and Arg250 are important residues involved in the catalytic activity of OXA-48 carbapenemase21.

Structure-based virtual screening and identification of lead NPs as potential inhibitors

To identify NPs from the StreptomeDB 4.0 database with potential inhibitory activity against OXA-48 carbapenemase, a structure-based virtual screening was performed. The overall workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 2. Initially, the library containing 8,500 compounds was filtered based on Lipinski’s rule of five, resulting in a subset of 2,500 compounds. These were then docked into the active site of OXA-48 using AutoDock Vina software.

Integrated workflow for in silico screening, repurposing, and in vitro validation of a potential inhibitor against OXA-48 carbapenemase.

Molecular docking results were ranked by their predicted scores, and a threshold of -9 kcal/mol was applied to identify the most promising candidates. This process yielded 20 top-ranked hits, which were then further evaluated based on their molecular interactions with key catalytic residues. Discovery Studio software was used to analyze the molecular interactions between the hits and OXA-48 carbapenemase. For comparison, the known clinical inhibitor Avibactam was also docked.

The docking score analysis showed that 8-O-Methylrabelomycin (PubChem CID: 197268, formula: C20H16O6), N05WA963B (PubChem CID: 139587666, formula: C26H26O9), and Holyrine A (PubChem CID: 10503251, formula: C26H24N4O3) had the most favorable docking scores at − 9.9 kcal/mol, followed by Tetracenoquinocin (PubChem CID: 46210465, formula: C26H24O9) at − 9.8 kcal/mol. In contrast, Avibactam (PubChem CID: 9835049, formula: C7H11N3O6S) showed a less favorable score of − 6.8 kcal/mol. Based on their superior scores and favorable binding interactions, 8-O-Methylrabelomycin, N05WA963B, Holyrine A, and Tetracenoquinocin were selected as the final lead compounds. MD simulation analysis

MD simulation studies were carried out to investigate the dynamic interactions between OXA-48 carbapenemase in complex with the lead compounds 8-O-Methylrabelomycin, N05WA963B, Holyrine A, Tetracenoquinocin, as well as Avibactam by analyzing their MD trajectories and determining RMSD, Rg, H-bonds, and RMSF values.

To measure the deviation of each OXA-48 carbapenemase/ligand complex from the starting structure over the course of a 200 ns simulation and evaluate its structural stability and conformational changes, RMSD analysis of the protein backbone was performed. According to the RMSD profiles presented in Fig. 5A, all complexes exhibited a rapid increase in RMSD during the equilibration phase, reflecting the relaxation and adjustment of the systems to the simulation environment. Following this phase, the RMSD values for all complexes remained relatively low, fluctuating predominantly below 0.3 nm, mostly ranging between 0.1 nm and 0.25 nm. These low values suggest that the overall fold and structural integrity of the complexes were maintained throughout the simulation. The RMSD values for OXA-48 carbapenemase/8-O-Methylrabelomycin complex fluctuated between 0.07 and 0.27 nm throughout the simulation period, and an upward shift in RMSD values was observed at around 100 ns, resulting in relatively higher RMSD values until the end of the simulation, indicating conformational adaptations or a different stable state. Similarly, OXA-48 carbapenemase/Avibactam complex fluctuated between 0.08 and 0.31 nm, and a gradual shift to a higher fluctuation range started at around 90 ns, with large fluctuations in the later phases of simulation. The RMSD plots for Holyrine A and N05WA963B complexes exhibited profiles with values ranging from 0.07 to 0.25 and 0.07 to 0.26 nm, respectively. A continuous upward drift in RMSD of Holyrine A complex was observed throughout the simulation after the initial equilibration, which could suggest that the complex had not reached a stable conformation. N05WA963B complex showed initial stability and then a shift to a higher fluctuation range at around 90 ns, and relatively stable fluctuations in the later phase of simulation. Tetracenoquinocin-containing complex showed initial stability, an increase in the fluctuation between 70 and 80 ns, and then stable fluctuations for the remainder of the simulation, indicating minor conformational adaptations in the binding pocket and a relatively consistent structural conformation throughout the simulation.

The MD simulation analysis of four OXA-48 carbapenemase/selected lead compound complexes over 200 ns. (A) RMSD plot; (B) Rg plot; (C) H-bonds plot; (D) RMSF plot. To evaluate the protein structural compactness, Rg value for each OXA-48 carbapenemase/ligand complex was calculated, with higher values indicating a decrease in protein compactness. As shown in Fig. 5B, Rg values for OXA-48 carbapenemase in complex with 8-O-methylrabelomycin, Holyrine A, N05WA963B, and Tetracenoquinocin were in the range of 1.71–1.80, 1.71–1.79, 1.72–1.81, and 1.72–1.82 nm, respectively. Moreover, Rg values for OXA-48 carbapenemase/Avibactam complex were in the range of 1.71–1.83 nm. The Rg value for OXA-48 carbapenemase/8-O-Methylrabelomycin complex fluctuated for around the first 100 ns, and then an increase in Rg value was observed, which means the compactness of the protein had decreased. Similarly, N05WA963B- and Avibactam-containing complexes exhibited fluctuations at higher Rg values at specific time points, but before this point, they fluctuated around relatively stable Rg values. OXA-48 carbapenemase/Holyrine A showed a repetitive increase and decrease pattern in its Rg plot over the simulation period. Among all complexes, Tetracenoquinocin-containing complex showed the most stable Rg plot. Its plot started and remained relatively stable, although a transient increase was observed at 70–80 ns.

The number of H-bonds formed between OXA-48 carbapenemase and the ligands was analyzed to evaluate the stability of the complexes during the MD simulations. The total number of H-bonds observed during the simulations ranged between 0 and 5 for 8-O-Methylrabelomycin and Avibactam, while the range was between 0 and 6 for Holyrin A, N05WA963B, and Tetracenoquinocin. As seen in the H-bond plots (Fig. 5C), 8-O-Methylrabelomycin exhibited a gradual increase in the number of H-bonds over the 200 ns period. In the first half of the simulation, H-bonds fluctuated mostly between 1 and 3. From ~ 100 ns onwards, the complex maintained a more stable H-bond interaction, mostly showing 2–3 H-bonds with spikes up to 5. For the Avibactam complex, H-bond formation and breakdowns were highly dynamic throughout the entire 200 ns simulation, with the number of H-bonds fluctuating primarily between 1 and 4. Transient changes in the number of H-bonds of OXA-48 carbapenemase/Avibactam were observed at ~ 60–70 ns and ~ 120–130 ns. The MD simulation for Holyrin A started with a higher number of H-bonds, typically ranging from 3 to 4 H-bonds in the first ~ 20 ns. After this initial phase, the number of H-bonds fluctuated primarily between 1 and 2. Similarly, N05WA963B began the simulation with more stable H-bond formation, mostly fluctuating between 1 and 2, which lasted for ~ 60 ns. From ~ 60 ns onward, the interactions appeared less stable, with the number of H-bonds fluctuated primarily between 0 and 1. Finally, the number of H-bonds for Tetracenoquinocin remained above 0 for almost entire simulation period and generally fluctuated between 3 and 4, suggesting a more stable hydrogen bonding interaction. Generally, the complexes of 8-O-Methylrabelomycin and Holyrin A were stabilized by an average of three hydrogen bonds, while N05WA963B and Tetracenoquinocin complexes maintained an average of two H-bonds. These interactions remained highly stable throughout the MD simulations. Conversely, the Avibactam complex exhibited the highest conformational fluctuation over the simulation period, indicating less stability within the active site.

RMSF values were evaluated to assess the average fluctuation of each residue during the MD simulations, with lower values indicating fewer conformational changes of residues (Fig. 5D). RMSF analysis showed that residues fluctuated in a range of 0.05–0.4 nm in the 8-O-Methylrabelomycin complex, 0.05–0.32 nm in the Holyrine A complex, 0.05–0.30 nm in the N05WA963B complex, 0.04–0.29 nm in the Tetracenoquinocin complex, and 0.05–0.35 nm in the Avibactam complex. Generally, the fluctuation of residues in all systems was below 0.3 nm except for residues 145 and 146 in the 8-O-Methylrabelomycin complex, residue 200 in the Avibactam complex, and residues 146 and 147 in the Holyrine A complex. However, patterns of the RMSF plots in all complexes were very similar to each other. All five complexes demonstrated higher fluctuations in loop regions and residues near the active site (residues 145–150 and 200–250), which are critical for ligand interaction and enzymatic activity.

Binding free energy calculations

To analyze the interactions between OXA-48 carbapenemase and the lead compounds 8-O-Methylrabelomycin, Holyrine A, N05WA963B, Tetracenoquinocin, as well as Avibactam in detail, binding free energies (ΔGbind) were calculated using the MM/PBSA approach. These calculations were performed on the final 50 ns of the 200 ns MD trajectories, with a sampling interval of 2 ps. The calculated energy components included DEvdw, DEele, ΔEGB, ΔESURF, ΔGGAS, ΔGSOLV, and ΔGbind, which are summarized in Table 2.

For the OXA-48 carbapenemase/8-O-Methylrabelomycin complex, residues Arg214 (– 4.08 kcal/mol), Thr213 (– 1.20 kcal/mol), and Leu158 (– 1.66 kcal/mol) displayed the most favorable ΔG contributions, indicating their major role in ligand stabilization through van der Waals interactions. These residues primarily contributed through favorable van der Waals interactions.

Structural analysis of the most promising lead compound

Based on the results from the molecular docking and MD simulations, Tetracenoquinocin was identified as the most promising lead compound with the potential to inhibit OXA-48 carbapenemase. The structural analysis of Tetracenoquinocin revealed it to be a tetracene quinone. The compound’s core is a polycyclic aromatic scaffold composed of a four-fused-ring system, and the presence of two ketone groups on this ring system classifies it as a quinone. As a result of this structural analysis, the Tetracycline class of antibiotics, which also contains a polycyclic scaffold, including Minocycline, Doxycycline, and Tetracycline, was identified as a set of close structural analogues (Fig. 6).

Computational analysis of tetracenoquinocin analogues

Docking scores of Minocycline, Doxycycline, and Tetracycline as Tetracenoquinocin analogues are presented in Table 1. All three ligands showed strong binding affinities. Among them, Tetracycline had the most favorable docking score (− 9.4 kcal/mol), which was more favorable compared to Avibactam (− 6.8 kcal/mol) but slightly less favorable compared to Tetracenoquinocin (− 9.8 kcal/mol). Moreover, key molecular interactions between these Tetracenoquinocin analogues and OXA-48 carbapenemase are summarized in Table 1. Analysis of the binding poses revealed that Tetracycline formed the most hydrogen bonds within the OXA-48 carbapenemase active site with residues Ser70, Ser118, Thr209, Tyr211, and Arg250, more than those formed by Minocycline (Ser118, Thr209, Tyr211, and Arg250), Doxycycline (Ser70, Tyr211, and Arg214), and even Tetracenoquinocin, which formed hydrogen bonds with Lys116 and Thr209 residues.

Based on its superior docking score and docking pose compared to other analogues, the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex was selected for further validation through 200 ns MD simulations. RMSD, Rg, RMSF, and H-bond analyses of MD simulations of this complex are shown in Fig. 7. MD plots of Tetracenoquinocin are also presented for comparison. As shown in Fig. 7A, the RMSD profile for the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex exhibited an initial structural relaxation phase, with values increasing from approximately 0.07 nm to 0.14 nm within the first 60 ns. Following this initial adjustment, the complex underwent a period of conformational adaptation. Notably, the RMSD values began to stabilize after ~ 150 ns, fluctuating around a mean value of ~ 0.2 nm for the remainder of the simulation. This plateau in the RMSD profile indicates that the complex achieved a stable conformation within the binding pocket, suggesting that Tetracycline maintained a consistent binding pose throughout the later stages of the simulation.

The Rg plot for the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex exhibits a highly stable profile, fluctuating within a very narrow range of 1.71 to 1.79 nm. The absence of any significant upward trend or major conformational change indicates that the protein maintained its compact and stable tertiary structure over the entire simulation period. The consistency of the Rg values confirms that the binding of Tetracycline did not induce any major unfolding or structural rearrangements within the enzyme’s overall fold, suggesting a stable protein-ligand complex (Fig. 7B).

As shown in Fig. 7C, the H-bond profile can be divided into two distinct phases. During the initial phase (0–60 ns), the number of hydrogen bonds was highly dynamic, fluctuating primarily between 1 and 4. After approximately 60 ns, the complex established a more stable hydrogen-bonding pattern, with the number of hydrogen bonds significantly increasing and maintaining a consistent range of 4 to 6. This stable pattern of hydrogen bonds, with occasional spikes to 7, suggests that the Tetracycline ligand achieved a stable and energetically favorable binding pose within the active site for the remainder of the simulation. This finding further supports the overall stability of the complex as observed in the RMSD and Rg plots.

RMSF values showed that residues in the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex fluctuated in a range of 0.04–0.29 nm (Fig. 7D). Generally, the fluctuation of residues was below 0.25 nm except for residues 147, 148, 200, and 201, which are located in loop regions and near the active site.

ΔGbind for OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex was determined to be − 27.13 kcal/mol. The calculated energy components, including DEvdw, DEele, ΔEGB, ΔESURF, ΔGGAS, and ΔGSOLV are also summarized in Table 2. To further characterize the interaction pattern of Tetracycline within the active site, MM/GBSA per-residue free-energy decomposition was performed. In the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex, nearly all interacting residues including Ser70, Ile102, Trp105, Ser118, Val120, Thr209, Gly210, Tyr211, Leu247, Gly248, Arg250, and Gln251 showed weak per-residue ΔG contributions. The only exception was Ile102, which contributed a modest stabilizing energy of − 1.49 kcal/mol (Supplementary Fig. S2).

In vitro experiments

For the Meropenem-resistant isolates harboring the blaOXA−48 gene (Kp03 and Kp04), the MIC values for Meropenem and Tetracycline were 8 and 2 µg/mL for Kp03, and 16 and 2 µg/mL for Kp04, respectively. The combination of Meropenem and sub-MIC Tetracycline reduced the Meropenem MIC to 2 µg/mL for Kp03 and 8 µg/mL for Kp04. For both isolates, the FIC index was < 1, indicating synergy between Meropenem and Tetracycline.

For the Meropenem-sensitive isolates without the blaOXA−48 gene (Kp14 and Kp15), the MIC values of Meropenem and Tetracycline for both isolates were determined to be 1 and 1 µg/mL, respectively. The combination of these two antibiotics did not reduce the MIC of Meropenem for these isolates, indicating that no synergistic, antagonistic, or adverse interaction occurred between the drugs in these isolates.

Discussion

OXA-48 carbapenemase, a class D β-lactamase, is one of the major contributors to antibiotic resistance in Enterobacterales. This enzyme confers resistance by efficiently hydrolyzing Penicillins and Carbapenems, even at low concentrations. The presence of OXA-48 carbapenemase complicates clinical treatment due to its limited susceptibility to existing β-lactamase inhibitors, underscoring the urgent need for novel drug discovery and rational design strategies to combat these difficult-to-treat infections45,46. OXA-48 carbapenemase is highly prevalent across Middle Eastern countries, including a high incidence in Iran9,47. Specifically, a recent systematic review reported blaOXA−48 as the most common carbapenemase gene detected in Escherichia coli and K. pneumoniae isolates from Iranian ICUs47. These findings highlight the critical importance of research aimed at developing novel inhibitors for this enzyme.

Introducing novel and potential OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitors has been the subject of research efforts employing diverse strategies. These include screening libraries of synthetic chemicals48, flavonoids49, or microbiome-derived peptides50. The StreptomeDB 4.0 database25, used in this study, is a specialized library of NPs with diverse scaffolds originating from Streptomyces species, which were subjected to in silico screening to identify potential OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitors.

Following the molecular docking studies, four promising lead compounds (8-O-Methylrabelomycin, Holyrine A, N05WA963B, and Tetracenoquinocin) were successfully identified based on high docking scores and their potential to form stable initial complexes with the enzyme. These ligands primarily interacted with key catalytic residues through hydrogen bonds, π-π T-shaped, and van der Waals interactions. Of the four lead compounds, Tetracenoquinocin stands out due to its broad engagement with the enzyme’s active site. Its ability to interact with seven of the nine key catalytic residues suggests a robust and extensive set of interactions. The prominence of van der Waals interactions for Tetracenoquinocin indicates that its binding is not just limited to a few specific polar interactions but also involves strong hydrophobic forces, which are critical for stabilizing the ligand within the binding pocket.

MD simulations are widely used to explore protein-ligand interactions, assess complex stability, and understand dynamic behavior at the atomic level15,31,51. The structural stability and convergence observed in our 200 ns simulations are consistent with the methodological standards established in previous studies52–54. The MD simulations in this study revealed clear differences in the dynamic stability of OXA-48 carbapenemase and ligand complexes. While all the lead compounds were able to inhibit key catalytic residues, Holyrine A, N05WA963B, and 8-O-Methylrabelomycin in complex with OXA-48 carbapenemase showed reduced stability over time, with the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Holyrine A complex showing the least stable conformation throughout the simulation. In contrast, the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracenoquinocin complex demonstrated the most stable profile throughout the 200 ns simulation, supported by persistent hydrogen bonding and minimal structural fluctuations.

These findings, combined with a favorable docking score (− 9.8 kcal/mol for Tetracenoquinocin, compared to − 6.8 kcal/mol for Avibactam) and total binding free energy (− 33.37 kcal/mol for Tetracenoquinocin, compared to − 0.34 kcal/mol for Avibactam), strongly support Tetracenoquinocin as the most promising lead candidate.

The investigations into the binding mode of Tetracenoquinocin revealed its essential structural features: a linear, polyaromatic naphthacene core, a constellation of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and a polar region capable of forming interactions. This analysis led to a key finding: Tetracenoquinocin exhibits structural homology with the Tetracycline class of antibiotics, including Minocycline, Doxycycline, and Tetracycline. This unexpected similarity provided a compelling rationale for investigating these clinically approved drugs as repurposing candidates for OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibition, potentially offering an accelerated path to therapeutic application.

Molecular docking identified Tetracycline as the strongest binder among the tested Tetracycline class of antibiotics, forming the highest number of hydrogen bonds with key catalytic residues of OXA-48 carbapenemase. MD simulations revealed a more stable OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex compared to the OXA-48/Tetracenoquinocin complex. The OXA-48/Tetracycline complex established a stable hydrogen-bonding pattern with a consistent range of four to six hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation, indicating a strong and energetically favorable binding pose. The OXA-48/Tetracenoquinocin complex, conversely, maintained a lower average of two hydrogen bonds, fluctuating mostly between three and four. While this signifies a stable interaction, the significantly higher number of hydrogen bonds for Tetracycline suggests a more potent and stable interaction within the active site. In summary, while both Tetracenoquinocin and Tetracycline demonstrated stable binding to OXA-48, the Tetracycline complex showed superior stability across all metrics. This suggests that the Tetracycline scaffold is better suited for inhibiting OXA-48 carbapenemase by forming a more consistent and robust interaction.

The dynamic stability of the OXA-48 carbapenemase/Tetracycline complex was evaluated and contextualized against a recent 200 ns MD study on OXA-48 carbapenemase peptide inhibitors50. In that study, peptide complexes reached stable conformations with RMSD values stabilizing around 0.22–0.26 nm. Similarly, the OXA-48/Tetracycline complex demonstrated stable RMSD values around 0.2 nm. RMSF analysis further confirmed these results, showing higher flexibility in the loop regions in both studies. While the bulkier peptides formed a higher average number of hydrogen bonds, Tetracycline achieved comparable stability with fewer, yet energetically significant, hydrogen bonds. These comparisons support the reliability of our simulations and highlight Tetracycline’s potential as a stable OXA-48 inhibitor.

Structural and dynamic analyses provide a clear explanation for the relatively lower binding free energy of Avibactam compared to the other lead compounds, including Tetracycline. Avibactam contains a diazabicyclooctane core with polar functional groups, including a sulfonamide, carbamate, and amide moieties, capable of forming hydrogen bonds with Ser118, Thr209, Tyr211, and Arg250. However, it lacks strong lipophilic or aromatic groups, resulting in fewer hydrophobic contacts and π–π interactions with active site residues. In contrast, compounds such as Tetracenoquinocin, Tetracycline derivatives, Holyrine A, and 8-O-Methylrabelomycin possess larger, rigid or semi-rigid aromatic scaffolds with multiple polar groups, allowing for more extensive and persistent hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions. MD simulations further support these observations: Avibactam exhibits higher RMSD and RMSF values in the binding pocket, along with transient increases in Rg, reflecting conformational flexibility and reduced compactness. Hydrogen-bond analysis shows fewer persistent interactions during the simulation compared to other ligands. Together, these structural and dynamic features indicate that the lower binding free energy of Avibactam arises from weaker and less stable interactions within the OXA-48 carbapenemase active site.

The findings in this study provided a strong basis to prioritize the repurposing of Tetracycline for in vitro investigations using four clinical K. pneumoniae isolates. The combination of Meropenem and sub-MIC Tetracycline reduced the MIC of Meropenem for the OXA-48-positive, Meropenem-resistant isolates (Kp03 and Kp04). Conversely, this combination had no effect on the MIC for the OXA-48-negative, Meropenem-sensitive isolates. These experimental results are consistent with our computational findings, providing strong evidence that Tetracycline functions as a potential inhibitor of OXA-48 carbapenemase. The in vitro synergistic effect (FIC < 1) observed in the two Meropenem-resistant clinical isolates demonstrates the potential role of Tetracycline as an OXA-48 inhibitor. For isolate Kp03, the combination treatment reduced the Meropenem MIC by 4-fold (from 8 to 2 µg/mL), shifting the isolate from the resistant (≥ 4 µg/mL) to intermediate (2 µg/mL) category according to the CLSI breakpoints, and from susceptible, increased exposure to susceptible according to EUCAST breakpoints. For Kp04, the MIC was reduced by 2-fold (from 16 to 8 µg/mL), which remains within the resistant range by CLSI criteria but moves the isolate into the susceptible, increased exposure category according to EUCAST. Although Tetracycline itself has not previously been evaluated as an inhibitor of β-lactamases, including OXA-48 carbapenemase, there is evidence that certain Tetracycline derivatives may potentiate Meropenem activity. For example, a study examining Meropenem–Minocycline co-treatment in blaOXA−48-positive K. pneumoniae isolates reported synergistic interactions between the two antibiotics55, supporting the idea that Tetracycline-class compounds can modulate carbapenem resistance.

Although Tetracycline is an old antibiotic with well-known resistance mechanisms in Gram-negative bacteria, these mechanisms do not necessarily preclude its use as an OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitor. Specific efflux pumps for Tetracycline (Tet) remove this antibiotic from the cytoplasm (where its target, the ribosome, is located) to the periplasmic space56, which is also where β-lactamases, including OXA-48 carbapenemase, reside57. Other mechanisms, such as multidrug-resistant efflux pumps capable of transporting Tetracycline directly from the cytoplasm to the outside of the cell, as well as Tetracycline-inactivating enzymes, may interfere with the use of Tetracycline as an OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitor. However, these mechanisms are generally not as effective as Tet-specific efflux pumps58,59.

The findings of this study, demonstrating Tetracycline’s potential inhibitory effect on OXA-48 carbapenemase, align with the growing body of research focused on novel strategies to combat carbapenemase resistance. In one study evaluating the penicillanic acid sulfone LN-1-255 as a novel OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitor in combination with Meropenem against one Meropenem-resistant clinical strain of K. pneumoniae harboring blaOXA48, concentrations of 4 and 16 µg/mL LN-1-255 reduced the MIC value of the isolate by 2- and 4-fold, respectively60. These fold reductions are similar to the results of the present study. Molecular docking analyses of LN-1-255 with OXA-48 carbapenemase revealed interactions with the active site residues Ser70, Arg250, Lys208, Tyr211, and Thr209. Tetracycline also interacted with these residues, along with several additional residues, according to the docking results of the present work. In another attempt to find a specific OXA-48 carbapenemase inhibitor, a library of triazine-based compounds was screened against OXA-48, and compound CDD-97 was selected as the lead candidate. However, experiments evaluating the ability of this inhibitor to enhance the susceptibility of an OXA-48-positive bacterial strain to Imipenem demonstrated only poor activity. Concentrations of 8–128 µg/mL of CDD-97 reduced the MIC value of Imipenem by 2-fold, while higher concentrations did not change the MIC48. Moreover, a recent study identified a novel microbiome-derived peptide capable of inhibiting the OXA-48 enzyme, which similarly restored the efficacy of Meropenem against a Meropenem-resistant K. pneumoniae clinical isolate. Concentrations of 7–125 µg/mL of the peptide reduced the MIC of Meropenem by 4-fold, from 8 to 2 µg/mL 50. While such novel peptides provide valuable insight into potential binding mechanisms, their development for clinical application faces significant hurdles. Peptide synthesis is a notably expensive process compared to the production of small molecules, raising concerns about scalability and cost-effectiveness. In contrast, the present drug repurposing approach offers a distinct advantage. By identifying an already approved and widely available old drug like Tetracycline, we present a potentially more rapid and cost-effective path to clinical use61, making our findings particularly relevant for immediate therapeutic consideration.

Conclusions

This study identified Tetracenoquinocin as a potential inhibitor of OXA-48 carbapenemase through a combination of computational methods. The central finding of this research was the structural similarity between Tetracenoquinocin and the Tetracycline class of antibiotics, which provided the rationale for evaluating clinically approved Tetracyclines as repurposing candidates. A key takeaway of this work is that Tetracycline exhibits superior binding stability and a more favorable interaction profile with the OXA-48 enzyme compared to the lead compound, Tetracenoquinocin, acting as a potential non-β-lactam inhibitor. The inhibitory effect of Tetracycline was further validated by in vitro studies, where it successfully restored the efficacy of Meropenem against two clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae harboring the blaOXA−48 gene. This work offers a platform for future research by providing a validated structural template for rational drug design; specifically, the binding interactions mapped in this study can guide the chemical modification of the Tetracycline scaffold to develop high-affinity OXA-48 inhibitors. However, the small number of clinical isolates examined and the absence of enzymatic inhibition assays remain limitations of this work. Further experimental validation, including additional in vitro and in vivo studies, is needed to fully assess the therapeutic potential of this combination therapy. Future work should involve evaluating a larger set of clinical isolates, conducting enzyme kinetic analyses, and using animal infection models to determine the therapeutic potential of the Meropenem/Tetracycline combination in vivo.
