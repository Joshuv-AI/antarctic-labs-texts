Probiotic-derived EPS-ZnO nanoparticles: therapeutic potential in colorectal cancer gene regulation
Authors: Saeideh Afsharipoor, Razieh Nazari, Anoosh Eghdami, Mahdi Fasihi-Ramandi, Mohammad Reza Zolfaghari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC), the third most common and second deadliest cancer worldwide, arises from the abnormal growth of epithelial cells in the colon or rectum. This study focused on the oncogenes BCL9, KRAS, and WNT10A, alongside the tumor suppressor genes PDCD4, PTEN, and LRIG3, in the SW480 cell line. Probiotics, valued for their antioxidant and anti-cancer effects—especially due to their exopolysaccharides (EPS)—are promising in cancer prevention. Nanoparticles (NPs) hold great potential for diagnosing and treating various diseases, including cancer. Among them, zinc oxide NPs (ZnONPs) stand out for their unique physical and chemical properties, making them widely used in drug delivery, cancer diagnosis, and therapy. In this study, EPS were extracted and purified from the probiotic bacterium Lactiplantibacillus plantarum at a concentration of 301 ± 0.12 mg/L, and subsequently employed in the biosynthesis of ZnONPs, achieving an optimal yield of approximately 61.2%. Validation tests (UV-Vis, XRD, FTIR, FE-SEM, TEM, and zeta potential analysis) were conducted on the ZnONPs coated with exopolysaccharide (EPS-ZnONPs). The cytotoxicity of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs was assessed on the SW480 cell line, and their antioxidant properties were evaluated. Quantitative cycle variations (∆∆Cqs) measured via the Real-time PCR system, following the treatment of the cancerous cell line with the target metabolites, demonstrated reduced expression of oncogenes and increased expression of tumor suppressors. The results indicated that EPS extracted from probiotics, as well as EPS-ZnONPs, could serve as effective agents in the treatment of CRC by modulating the expression levels of genes involved in the development of this cancer.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04247-w.

Introduction

CRC has emerged as one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and continues to rank among the top causes of cancer-related deaths [1]. Its onset is driven by a cascade of genetic and epigenetic changes within epithelial cells, which ultimately disrupt normal growth regulation and lead to unchecked cellular proliferation [2]. Numerous genes involved in key signaling pathways have been implicated in CRC, with many showing abnormal activity or mutations that disturb their normal function [3]. Proto-oncogenes, such as BCL9, KRAS, and WNT10A, and tumor suppressor genes, including PDCD4, PTEN, and LRIG3, play crucial roles in the regulatory pathways of cell division. Mutations or alterations in these genes can lead to their activation, contributing to the malignant transformation of colorectal cells [4]. Research indicates that CRC arises from a complex interplay of risk factors, which can be broadly categorized into non-modifiable and modifiable types. Factors such as age, sex, ethnicity, a history of adenomatous polyps or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and familial predisposition are beyond individual control [5]. In contrast, modifiable risks are tied to lifestyle and behavioral patterns—including tobacco use, poor dietary habits, excessive alcohol intake, lack of physical activity, and obesity—which can significantly influence disease development [5, 6].

The most effective method for preventing CRC and reducing CRC-related mortality among the population is screening individuals at average risk, primarily through fecal occult blood test (FOBT) and lower endoscopy [7]. Treatment options for CRC may include a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy using monoclonal antibodies [8]. Modern chemotherapy regimens almost invariably lead to resistance in CRC patients, reducing the effectiveness of anticancer drugs and ultimately resulting in chemotherapy failure [5].

To enhance treatment efficacy and reduce the side effects associated with conventional chemotherapy, alternative therapies such as anti-inflammatory drugs, gold-based agents, agarose microbeads, and probiotics are currently under investigation [9].

Probiotics are live and non-pathogenic microorganisms that confer beneficial effects upon consumption by the host [10]. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are among the most commonly utilized probiotic strains, renowned for their resilience within the gastrointestinal environment, particularly their ability to withstand bile salts [11]. This resilience enables them to successfully reach and colonize the intestinal lining. Beyond their colonization capacity, LAB are recognized for their favorable impact on the immune system, their genetic stability and safety, and their ability to generate health-promoting metabolic byproducts [12].

Probiotics play a significant role in the prevention and effective treatment of various types of cancer, garnering attention due to their ability to modulate cancer signaling [13]. Probiotics can modulate cancer progression by inducing apoptosis, inhibiting mutagenic activity, downregulating oncogene expression [14], suppressing kinase activity [15], triggering autophagy [16], reactivating tumor suppressors [17], and prevention metastasis [18]. Numerous studies have also suggested potential mechanisms by which probiotics appear to prevent CRC development. Some of these mechanisms include modification of the gut microbiota composition, binding and degradation of carcinogenic compounds in the intestinal lumen, immune modulation, improvement of the intestinal barrier function, alterations in host physiology, and inhibition of cell proliferation and induction of apoptosis in cancer cells [19].

Furthermore, studies have shown that molecules and metabolites derived from probiotics can prevent tumor progression by modulating the host’s immune system [20]. EPS extracted from probiotics plays a crucial role in cancer prevention and treatment [13, 21]. EPS is one of the most important functional components of the metabolic products of LAB [22], which has been reported to have several physiological functions, including immunomodulatory effects [23], anti-inflammatory and inhibitory roles against tumor cell proliferation [24], and antioxidant activities [25].

The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) family comprises protein complexes that play a central role in regulating gene expression, orchestrating cytokine synthesis, and promoting cell survival. In cancerous cells, transcriptional regulators such as NF-κB, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) STAT3(, and hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha)HIF1A) become activated, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and various growth-promoting molecules [26]. EPSs inhibit the JAK/STAT3, NF-κB, and HIF pathways in tumor cells, as well as NF-κB and p38/MAPK (Mitogen-activated protein kinase) in immune cells. In summary, EPS blocks Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on macrophages, thereby inhibiting downstream MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, which prevents p38 phosphorylation — a component of the MAPK pathway — and subsequently reduces the production of inflammatory mediators [27]. In addition to these pathways, EPS also inhibits the production of β-catenin, a key component of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, Wnt/β-catenin [28].

On the other hand, nanoparticle technology and the production of NPs are considered promising approaches for the effective treatment and diagnosis of many diseases, including cancer [29]. Currently, the biological synthesis of metal and metal oxide NPs is a preferred method for producing these materials [30]; however, it is limited by certain practical drawbacks, such as long reduction times, agglomeration, and complex downstream processing [31]. Scientists have recently shifted their focus to researching the use of microbial EPSs as reducing agents to overcome these challenges [32]. Therefore, EPSs isolated from probiotics are currently being developed as excellent alternatives to improve the green synthesis and stabilization of metal NPs [33]. At times, EPSs and NPs interact in a complementary manner, enhancing each other’s effects. The EPS layer surrounding the NPs is thought to improve their biocompatibility, enabling these NPs to exhibit potent antimicrobial, antioxidant, anticancer, and cytotoxic activities. However, when designing EPS-derived metal oxide NPs for advanced biomedical applications—such as cancer treatment, targeted drug delivery, medical imaging, and biosensing—it is essential that the microbial EPSs used are devoid of any antigenic or pathogenic traits [34]. Among these, ZnONPs stand out due to their remarkable biomedical and therapeutic potential [35]. Their ability to selectively target and destroy cancer cells positions them as a promising candidate for future cancer therapies [36]. Zinc itself is a vital trace element in the human body, playing a foundational role in the activity of numerous proteins and enzymes, as well as in various biochemical and genetic pathways. It also contributes to the structural integrity of key biomolecules like proteins, DNA, RNA, and ribosomes, while actively participating in the regulation of gene expression [37]. The anticancer effects of zinc are attributed to its antioxidant properties, immune system enhancement, cell cycle regulation, and differentiation, as well as its role as a secondary messenger in regulating cellular signaling [38]. Zinc signaling targets include the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways within the MAPK pathway. Zinc also enhances T cell proliferation through the MAPK pathway by inhibiting the dephosphorylation of MAPK/ERK kinase (MEK) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK). Furthermore, zinc activates the STAT protein pathway, leading to the expression of genes related to differentiation, survival, cell proliferation, and apoptosis [38]. Zinc ions play an essential role in defending against tumor growth and development by inhibiting angiogenesis and regulating vascularization. Various metal oxides, such as TiO2, CuO, and ZnO, have been produced, but ZnONPs are the most interesting of these metal oxides due to their inexpensive, safe, and simple synthesis [39]. Reports have shown that microbially mediated ZnONPs exhibit significant antioxidant properties in the cytoplasm and nucleus, inducing oxidative stress, cell cycle progression, DNA replication, DNA repair, and ultimately, apoptosis in many cancer cell lines [40].

This study explores the extraction and purification of EPS from L. plantarum for the biosynthesis of ZnONPs. These NPs were characterized using multiple analytical techniques and tested for antioxidant activity (diphenylpicrylhydrazyl (DPPH) system) and cytotoxicity against the SW480 cancer cell line (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]−2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay). Additionally, molecular analysis via quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assessed the expression levels of oncogenes (BCL9, KRAS, WNT10A) and tumor suppressor genes (PDCD4, PTEN, LRIG3), providing insight into their potential biomedical applications.

Experimental procedures

Standard probiotic strains

The standard bacterial strains utilized in this study were sourced from the Iranian Biological Resource Center (IBRC). These included eight standard probiotic strains: L. plantarum ATCC 14,917 (IBRC-M 10817), Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus ATCC 7469 (IBRC-M 10754), Limosilactobacillus reuteri ATCC 23,272 (IBRC-M 10755), Lacticaseibacillus casei ATCC 393 (IBRC-M 10711), Lactobacillus gasseri ATCC 11,842 (IBRC-M 10944), Lactobacillus delbrueckii ATCC 11,842 (IBRC-M 10730), Levilactobacillus brevis DSM 6235 (CCM 7932, IBRC-M 1081), and Lactobacillus acidophilus DSM 20,079 (CCM 4833, IBRC-M 10815). These strains were cultured on De Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) agar and broth media. Subsequently, four bacterial strains—L. plantarum, L. rhamnosus, L. casei, and L. reuteri—were selected based on their more mucoid colonies and superior probiotic characteristics.

EPS isolation and purification

A 1000 mL culture medium was prepared, and L. plantarum, L. rhamnosus, L. casei, and L. reuteri were inoculated into MRS broth (QLab, Canada) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. To isolate EPS, the culture medium was boiled at 100 °C, treated with 80% trichloroacetic acid (TCA, Merck, Germany), and precipitated using ethanol. After centrifugation, the EPS was dissolved in deionized water, dialyzed against a 12 kDa membrane, freeze-dried for 36 h, and stored at 4 °C [41].

To purify and remove residual DNA and proteins, the crude EPS powder was dissolved in a solution containing Tris-HCl and MgSO4 (Merck, Germany). It was first treated with DNAase I (Kiagen Fanavar, Iran) and then with Proteinase E (Merck, Germany) at 37 °C. Subsequently, TCA was added to precipitate enzymes and residual materials. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected, and its pH was adjusted to 4–5 using 10 M NaOH for neutralization. Finally, the sample underwent freeze-drying [42].

Cytotoxicity of EPS, MTT assay

The human CRC cell line used in this study was SW480 (Code: C506), which was obtained as a prepared flask from the Pasteur Institute of Iran’s cell bank. Additionally, the normal human embryonic kidney cell line HEK-293 (ATCC® CRL-1573™), exhibiting epithelial morphology, was selected as a control cell line. All human cell lines used in the past three years were authenticated by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) profiling based on their official cell line names. SW480 and HEK-293 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic (Gibco, USA) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To assess the effect of EPS extracted from L. plantarum, L. rhamnosus, L. casei, and L. reuteri on the viability of SW480 and HEK-293 cell lines, the MTT assay was employed.

Fifty milligrams of MTT powder (DNA Biotech, Iran) were fully dissolved in 10 mL of RPMI-1640 medium, sterilized using a 0.2 μm filter, aliquoted into 1.5 mL portions, and stored at − 20 °C. For the assay, flasks with 80% cell confluence—indicating 80% surface coverage—were used. Selection of the superior bacteria

The bacterium L. plantarum, which demonstrated the highest EPS production, strongest cytotoxic effects, and lowest cell viability against the SW480 cell line, was selected for further investigation. A bile salt tolerance assay was subsequently conducted on this strain. To evaluate its tolerance, bacterial growth was monitored in media containing bile salt concentrations of 0.05%, 0.15%, and 0.3%, alongside a bile salt-free control. Optical density (OD) was measured at 620 nm using a spectrophotometer at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h. The strain was then re-cultured in a one-liter volume for subsequent experimental stages, including EPS confirmation assays.

Total carbohydrate determination

The total carbohydrate content was determined using the phenol-sulfuric acid method with glucose as the standard. In this method, to prepare the glucose stock solution, 10 mg of α-D-glucose was dissolved in 80 mL of distilled water, resulting in a 100 mL glucose stock solution. The absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 490 nm. The carbohydrate content was then calculated using the glucose standard curve.

Ultraviolet–visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy

Following the extraction and purification of EPS, its absorbance was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo, Biomate5, USA) over a wavelength range of 190–800 nm.

Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy (Thermo, AVATAR, USA) was used to identify the main structural groups of the purified EPS. FTIR is commonly used in polysaccharide analysis to determine glycosidic linkages, monosaccharide composition, and functional groups. The EPS was mixed with KBr powder and then compressed. Afterward, the spectrum was recorded and analyzed using an FTIR spectrophotometer in the spectral region of 500–3500 cm⁻¹.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) imaging

To examine the structure and morphology of EPS, FE-SEM microscopy (TESCAN, Model MIRA3, Czech Republic) was used. Samples were coated with an ultra-thin layer of gold and analyzed using FE-SEM at accelerating voltages of 10 kV and 15 kV and magnifications of 3.00 kx, 8.00 kx, 12.0 kx, and 16.0 kx.

Biosynthesis of L. plantarum EPS-coated ZnONPs

A 0.02 M zinc acetate dihydrate solution was prepared (Merck, Germany). 90 mL of the 0.02 M zinc acetate dihydrate solution was placed on a magnetic stirrer at 37 °C, and then 0.3 g of EPS was added. A 2 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was prepared. The 2 M NaOH solution was added dropwise to the zinc acetate and EPS solution until the pH reached 12. The mixture was then stirred on a magnetic stirrer at 37 °C for 2 h. The precipitation of white particles indicated the formation of ZnO-NPs by EPS. The resulting precipitate was washed three times with deionized water and then with 96% ethanol by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min each time. It was then dried at 70 °C (Fig. 1) [44].

Characterization of EPS-ZnONPs

The presence of EPS-ZnONPs was confirmed using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo, Biomate5, USA), with spectra recorded over a wavelength range of 300–500 nm. The FT-IR spectrum was prepared by forming a thin potassium bromide tablet containing dried NPs, using a Thermo spectrophotometer (USA) in the range of 500–3500 cm⁻¹. A portion of the EPS-ZnONPs colloidal solution was centrifuged and converted into powder using a freeze-dryer (VaCo 2, Germany) at −76 °C and 0.01 mbar. Additionally, EPS-ZnONPs were evaluated using a powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (GNR Co., Model EXPLORER, Italy) to examine the crystalline phase of the biosynthesized NPs, with XRD analysis performed at 40 kV using Cu-Kα radiation. The colloidal solutions were vortexed to create a homogeneous mixture, and the zeta potential of the biosynthesized NPs in the solution was determined using a Zeta Compact analyzer (CAD Co., France). Furthermore, a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) (PHILIPS, Model CM120, Netherlands) and a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) (TESCAN, Model MIRA3, Czech Republic) were employed to analyze the morphology and size of the biosynthesized ZnONPs powder. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was also utilized as an analytical method for the structural analysis of the samples [45].

Antioxidant activity assay via DPPH radical scavenging evaluation

Samples were initially dissolved in DMSO (DNA Biotech, Iran), and then sample concentrations of 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 µg/mL were prepared. 50 µL of each sample concentration was added to 5 mL of 0.004% DPPH solution in methanol, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. DMSO was used as a control. After 20 min, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 2 min. The absorbance of the samples was then measured at 517 nm using an ELISA reader [46]. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated using the following formula:

Scavenging activity (%) = (1- (A sample-A blank)/A control) × 100.

The cultured cells were collected, and the cell count was determined using a Neubauer hemocytometer. Twenty thousand cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well microplate. Gene expression analysis using quantitative real-time PCR

RNA extraction and complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis

For RNA extraction, cancer cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Upon reaching 80% confluence, cells were treated with the IC50 concentrations of EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and 5-FU. Untreated cells served as the control. RNA was extracted using a Total RNA extraction kit (Parstous, Iran) under RNase-free and isolated conditions. This method eliminates contaminants and enzyme inhibitors, allowing for rapid, convenient, and reliable RNA isolation. A Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at A260/A280 nm was used to quantify the extracted genome [49]. Synthesis of cDNA was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit (Parstous, Iran) after total RNA extraction.

Real-time PCR assay

To examine gene expression differences between treated and untreated (control) cells, real-time PCR was performed in duplicate for the KRAS, BCL9, WNT10A, PTEN, LRIG3, and PDCD4 genes using a real-time PCR machine (Long Gene, Q1000, China). Gene expression levels were measured relative to the β-actin reference gene, which served as an internal positive control. SYBR Green master mix with high ROX (Addbio, Korea) was used for qPCR. Each reaction mixture contained 5 µL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (2X), 2 µL of 100 ng cDNA template, 1 µL of 10 µM of each primer, and dH2O, making up a final volume of 11 µL. The thermal cycling conditions for real-time PCR consisted of an initial step at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s (denaturation), 60 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 45 s (extension). Specific primer sequences for each target gene and the reference gene were obtained from previously published articles, validated using Primer3 software and the NCBI website, and are listed in Table 1. Melting curve analysis was performed for each gene replicate to assess primer specificity. Calibration curves for each target gene were generated using serial dilutions, with calculated PCR efficiency ranging between 98 and 99%. Negative controls were also included in each run [50].

Statistical analysis

In this study, Statistical analysis of the results obtained from the tests was performed using GraphPad Prism software, version 8. Prior to applying parametric tests, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analyses were conducted using the t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Amount of EPS isolated from the desired probiotics

EPS production by LAB, measured in mg/L, varied across strains, with L. plantarum showing the highest yield: L. plantarum (EPS = 301 ± 0.12 mg/L) > L. rhamnosus (EPS = 257 ± 0.07 mg/L) > L. casei (EPS = 189 ± 0.05 mg/L) > L. reuteri (EPS = 147 ± 0.01 mg/L).

Growth in different bile salt concentrations

Bacterial growth in bile salt-containing media was evaluated based on OD and survival rate after 24 h. By comparing the initial OD with measurements taken at 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h, it was confirmed that L. plantarum was able to grow in media containing bile salt concentrations of 0.05%, 0.15%, and 0.3%. In all tested concentrations, the OD increased by more than 0.3 units relative to the baseline, indicating that the strain is bile-tolerant under these conditions. Furthermore, the survival rates after 24 h in media containing 0.05%, 0.15%, and 0.3% bile salts were 97.71%, 91.78%, and 74.97%, respectively.

Cytotoxicity evaluation

According to the results obtained, the EPS of L. plantarum exhibited the highest cytotoxicity against the SW480 cell line compared to that of other bacteria. Therefore, it was selected as the superior bacterium for investigating the characteristics of EPS, Synthesis of ZnONP using EPS and examining of their effect on the expression of genes involved in CRC (Tables 2 and 3).

Measurement of EPS carbohydrates extracted from L. plantaurm

The total carbohydrate content was measured using the phenol-sulfuric acid method, with glucose serving as the standard. The total carbohydrate content was determined to be 290.85 mg/mL, with a calculated purity of 96.63%, supporting the effectiveness of the purification process and the integrity of the final EPS product.

Characterization of EPS extracted from L. plantarum and biosynthesized EPS-ZnONPs

The successful synthesis of EPS-mediated ZnONPs was indicated by the formation of white precipitates at the end of the reaction, yielding 0.184 g of product from 0.3 g of EPS, 90 mL of 0.02 M zinc acetate dihydrate, and 4 mL of 2 M sodium hydroxide—corresponding to an optimal synthesis efficiency of approximately 61.2%.

UV-Vis spectroscopy of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs analysis

The absorbance of EPS was examined in the wavelength range of 190 to 800 nm. Spectrophotometric analysis confirmed the efficiency of the purification process. The polysaccharide exhibited a strong absorption peak at 201 nm (Fig. 2(a)), a characteristic feature of polysaccharides attributed to their structure. Furthermore, no distinct absorption peaks were observed at 260 and 280 nm, indicating that the EPS was not contaminated with proteins or nucleic acids.

The optical absorption properties of EPS-ZnONPs were determined by UV-Vis spectroscopy analysis. EPS-ZnONPs exhibited an absorption peak at 348 nm (Fig. 2(b)), indicating the formation of ZnONPs.

FTIR spectroscopy of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs

The FTIR spectrum of L. plantarum EPS exhibited a strong and broad absorption peak at 3448 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the stretching vibration of O-H groups. The spectrum also showed a medium peak at 2932.99 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the C-H bond. A strong peak at 1642.20 cm⁻¹ was attributed to the stretching vibration of the C = O bond. A strong peak at 1064.53 cm⁻¹ was associated with the stretching vibration of the C-O bond, and a peak in the 1414.24 cm⁻¹ region corresponded to the stretching vibrations of C-O [58] (Fig. 3 (a)).

FTIR analysis was performed to identify the potential functional groups involved in the synthesis of ZnONPs. The FTIR spectrum of EPS-ZnONPs showed strong peaks at 3486.31, 1644.43, 1414.94, 1084.12, 1047.61, and 549.90 cm⁻¹. The possible functional groups responsible for the efficient stabilization of EPS-ZnONPs were confirmed by FTIR spectral analysis. Antioxidant activity via DPPH radical scavenging

The percentage of DPPH radical scavenging by EPS and EPS-ZnONPs at concentrations of 15.62, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 µg/mL was studied using vitamin C as a standard, and their IC50 values were calculated. The results demonstrated that DPPH scavenging by EPS was concentration-dependent, with inhibition significantly increasing as EPS concentration rose. While no significant difference was observed at lower concentrations (15.62 µg/mL, 31.25 µg/mL, and 62.5 µg/mL) compared to the control, a marked inhibitory effect emerged at 125 µg/mL (P = 0.005) and became highly significant at 250 µg/mL, 500 µg/mL, and 1000 µg/mL (P < 0.0001). According to the results, EPS-ZnONPs exhibited weak inhibitory activity at concentrations of 15.62 µg/mL and 31.25 µg/mL. The inhibitory effect at these concentrations was not significantly different from the control (p > 0.05) (Fig. 7). Although ZnONPs are generally known for their antioxidant properties, the relatively weak DPPH scavenging activity observed for EPS-ZnONPs may be attributed to the EPS coating surrounding the NPs. This coating could limit the direct interaction between ZnONPs and DPPH radicals. Additionally, the structural conformation and surface chemistry of the EPS-ZnONPs complex may reduce the accessibility of active antioxidant sites, thereby diminishing their radical scavenging efficiency.

Discussion

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, and despite advances in treatment, there remains a need for novel anticancer agents, as current therapies affect normal cells in addition to cancer cells and have severe side effects [60]. In general, probiotics and their metabolites (postbiotics) play a significant role in cancer research by modulating signaling pathways related to proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [61]. Postbiotics have more advantages than live probiotics and do not carry the risk of transferring antibiotic resistance genes [62]. EPS produced by probiotic bacteria has antioxidant, antibiofilm, and antitumor effects and can inhibit the proliferation of cancer cells, including those of the colon, breast, pancreas, leukemia, and cervix, without affecting normal cells [63]. EPS likely binds to surface receptors of colon cancer cells, as probiotic lactobacilli are widely present in the human intestine and contribute to colonic mucosal homeostasis [27].

Evaluation of the EPS and NPs production

EPS production varies among different Lactobacillus strains. According to a report by Li et al., Bifidobacterium bifidum WBIN03 produced 241.20 mg/L of EPS, and L. plantarum R315 produced 290.17 mg/L of EPS [64]. In the present study, the highest EPS production was observed in L. plantarum, at 301 ± 12.0 mg/L.

The determination of total carbohydrate content by the phenol-sulfuric acid method indicates the purity of the extracted polysaccharide. Reports suggest that carbohydrates constitute 90 to 96% of the EPS composition produced by L. plantarum [65]. Bouzaiene et al. recently reported a carbohydrate content of approximately 187.0 g/L for L. plantarum NTMI20 in 2024 [66], whereas in our study, the total carbohydrate content of L. plantarum EPS was 290.85 mg/mL, with a purity of 96.63%, which is similar to the values reported by Tang et al. (97%) [67] and Lio et al. (96.48%) [68]. For evaluating the anticancer properties of EPS, high purity and the absence of protein and nucleic acid are important. Our data show that the EPS produced by L. plantarum was pure and had a characteristic polysaccharide absorption at 201 nm, with no absorption peaks at 260 and 280 nm, indicating the absence of protein and nucleic acid contamination [69]. These results collectively validate the efficiency of the purification process and the high purity of the final EPS preparation.

In the present study, the FTIR spectra of the purified EPS showed a strong and broad absorption peak at 3447.99 cm⁻¹, which, according to studies, corresponds to the stretching vibration of numerous hydroxyl (O-H) groups belonging to the sugar moieties of carbohydrates [70]. Additionally, a medium peak was observed at 2932.99 cm⁻¹, which is attributed to the C-H bond and is considered an indicator band for polysaccharides [71]. It has been shown that these carboxyl and hydroxyl groups are associated with the biological activities of EPS, including antioxidant and antibacterial activities [66]. Furthermore, FTIR spectroscopy analysis confirmed that EPS-ZnONPs contain functional groups associated with polysaccharides and zinc oxide; specifically, the absorption peaks in the 400–700 cm⁻¹ range can be attributed to the stretching modes of ZnO [72].

SEM can help study the microstructure and surface morphology of EPSs and EPS-ZnONPs, thereby aiding in understanding their physical properties [73]. In our study, the SEM image of the EPS extracted from L. plantarum showed uneven and irregular surfaces composed of irregular spherical bodies and networks with indentations and cavities. Conversely, EPS-ZnONPs in the SEM and TEM images exhibited spherical and agglomerated shapes with sizes ranging from 50 to 100 nm, which is similar to the results reported by Li et al. in 2023 [74]. However, Yu et al. (2023) reported the SEM-observed structure of L. plantarum HDC-01 EPS as an irregular, smooth, and continuous reticulated flake-like structure [75]. The differences in microstructure and surface morphology of EPS may be related to physicochemical properties, the chosen extraction method, the drying method, and the polysaccharide structure [76].

Compared to other metal or metal oxide NPs, ZnONPs possess unique selectivity, a strong capacity to target cancer cells, and biocompatibility while simultaneously performing several key functions, including inhibiting cancer proliferation, sensitizing drug-resistant cancers, and preventing metastasis and cancer recurrence [77].

In this study, ZnONPs were synthesized using EPS extracted from L. plantarum. EPS acted as a reducing and stabilizing agent in this process. Abinaya et al. introduced a novel and effective approach for the synthesis of ZnONPs using EPS from the probiotic strain Bacillus licheniformis Dahb1 [44]. Our results demonstrated that the combination of EPS and ZnONPs can have a significant anticancer effect in cancer treatment, reducing the expression of oncogenes. However, in the present study, EPS-ZnONPs did not show a significant inhibitory effect. This may be because NPs are delivered into cells randomly and non-selectively and non-intelligently disrupt the activity of cytosolic enzymes in metabolic processes and nucleic acid reactions, including replication, transcription, and translation [78].

The use of UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy has been introduced as a method for confirming the structure of NPs. ZnONPs exhibit an absorption peak between 300 and 380 nm in the UV-Vis spectrum due to oxygen transfer [79]. In the present study, the absorption peak of EPS-ZnONPs was observed at 348 nm, which was consistent with recent results reported by Saravanan et al. [80]. Abd El-Nour et al. [81]. Furthermore, the zeta potential analysis of EPS-ZnONPs (−21.63 mV) indicated their favorable stability in the colloidal system, likely due to the presence of the EPS coating.

Investigating antioxidant and cytotoxic effects

Numerous studies have shown that EPS produced by probiotic bacteria, particularly lactobacilli, exhibits significant antioxidant properties and plays a crucial role in reducing free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [82]. Tumor cells produce more ROS due to increased metabolic activity, leading to cell proliferation, migration, genetic mutations, and angiogenesis [83]. In an in vitro study, Elova et al. demonstrated the antioxidant activity of L. plantarum EB 2 EPS in the DPPH system, showing that radical scavenging activity increased with increasing EPS concentration. The maximum radical scavenging activity was identified at 4 mg/mL (42%) [84]. In the present study, the DPPH radical scavenging activity of EPS increased with increasing concentration, consistent with the results of Zhang et al. [85]; however, unlike the results obtained by Abinaya et al. [44], EPS-ZnONPs did not show any inhibitory effect in our study.

In a study conducted by Wang et al., the cytotoxic effect of EPS produced by L. plantarum on inhibiting the growth of colon cancer cell lines, including Caco-2, BGC 823, HT-29, and CT26, was evaluated in vitro using the MTT assay, and it was shown that inhibitory activities increased with increasing EPS concentration [86]. It has been established that most EPSs derived from LAB have lower or even no cytotoxicity on normal cells compared to tumor cells [87]. Ismail and Nampoothiri showed that L. plantarum EPS did not exhibit cytotoxicity in normal L929 fibroblast cells until the EPS concentration reached 50 mg/mL [88]. In the present study, it was found that EPS had a significant inhibitory effect on the proliferation and survival of SW480 cells, such that after 24 h of treating SW480 cells with five µg/mL of EPS, the survival rate was approximately 9%, and the IC50 value was approximately 1.5 mg/mL.

Although our study presents the concentration- and time-dependent effects of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs on SW480 and HEK-293 cell viability using descriptive statistics and significance testing, the underlying data include detailed measurements across multiple concentrations and time points. These data could be visualized as dose-response curves to better illustrate the cytotoxic profiles of each treatment, as demonstrated in the studies by Dhandapani et al. [89] and Kim et al. [90]. However, such dose-dependent curves are not included in the current figures of this work.

In recent years, significant work has been conducted on the high potential of ZnONPs as a nanomedicine agent. For example, Shilpa et al. reported in 2020 that the use of ZnONPs significantly reduced the proliferation and viability of MCF-7 cells compared to control cells [91]. Dulta et al. also reported that green-synthesized ZnONPs exhibited significant selective cytotoxicity against HeLa and HT-29 cell lines [92]. Parthasarathy et al. reported in 2021 that PMSS-1 Bacillus cereus-synthesized ZnONPs exhibited concentration-dependent cytotoxicity against human melanoma 375 A cells. ZnONPs also induced cell apoptosis, as evidenced by increased lipid peroxidation (LPO) and decreased antioxidant activity [93]. Manimaran et al. examined cytotoxicity in vitro, demonstrating significant cytotoxic effects of Pleurotus djamor-synthesized ZnONPs against A549 lung cancer cells, with an LC50 value of 42.26 µg/mL [94]. In the present study, similar to the results of previous studies, it was found that the survival rate was dependent on the concentration of the samples and the treatment time. The survival rate of SW480 cells was dependent on both the concentration of ZnONPs and the treatment time, such that as the concentration and treatment time increased, the survival rate of SW480 cells decreased. It was also found that the survival rate of SW480 cells treated with EPS-ZnONPs was dependent on both concentration and time, similar to that of ZnONPs. In the study of the effect of the positive control 5-FU, it was found that the survival rate of SW480 cells was dependent on the concentration of 5-FU and the treatment time, such that with increasing concentration and time, the survival rate of SW480 cells decreased.

Gene expression modulation

What is presented as an advantage and novelty in this study is the simultaneous investigation of the effects of EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and their synergistic relationship on the expression of genes involved in CRC, a phenomenon rarely observed in other studies.

KRAS is an oncogene that plays a role in CRC progression, and 40% of CRC cases have mutations in the KRAS gene [95]. The results of our study showed a significant decrease in KRAS gene expression in SW480 cells treated with EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and 5-FU, which is probably due to the influence on the TGF-β signaling pathway, leading to the inhibition of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and consequently suppressing metastasis. In the comparison between EPS-ZnONPs and EPS biological samples, it was found that EPS had a greater effect on reducing KRAS gene expression than EPS-ZnONPs. Therefore, for CRC treatment targeting the KRAS gene, the use of EPS would be a more appropriate and efficient choice. In a recent study by Emam et al. in 2021, EPS extracted from L. acidophilus (DSM20079) reduced KRAS gene expression in MCF-7 and Caco-2 cell lines but did not affect KRAS gene expression in the HepG2 cell line [96].

BCL9 is a novel oncogene that is overexpressed in colon cancer. BCL9 plays a crucial role in tumor progression by regulating the proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastatic potential of tumor cells [97]. In this study, the investigation of the effect of EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and 5-FU on BCL9 gene expression in SW480 cells showed a significant decrease in the expression of this gene. The reduction in BCL9 gene expression likely inhibits tumor growth by attenuating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and suppressing angiogenesis.

WNT10A is an oncogene that is highly expressed in the SW480 cell line. WNT10A may play an oncogenic role during CRC carcinogenesis by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [53]. The results of the present study showed that the WNT10A gene in SW480 cells treated with EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and 5-FU exhibited a significant decrease in relative expression. Since WNT10A is a member of the WNT protein family, the samples under investigation may prevent cancer progression by reducing the expression of this gene and affecting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.

PTEN is a tumor suppressor gene, and its protein acts as a phosphatase, serving as a negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. PTEN also modulates angiogenesis by regulating this pathway [98]. The present study showed that in SW480 cells treated with EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and 5-FU, the PTEN gene exhibited increased expression, which was significant under the influence of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs but not significant under the influence of 5-FU. In comparing the biological metabolites EPS-ZnONPs and EPS, it was found that EPS had a greater effect on increasing PTEN gene expression than EPS-ZnONPs. Therefore, for CRC treatment targeting the PTEN gene, the use of EPS would be a more appropriate and effective choice. Heydari et al. (2019) reported in an animal study that L. acidophilus regulated increased expression of the PTEN tumor suppressor gene compared to the control group [99], which was consistent with the results of the present study.

LRIG3 is another tumor suppressor gene known to inhibit cell motility and suppress CRC metastasis, and its expression is significantly reduced in cancer tissue and CRC cell lines [100]. This study found that in SW480 cells treated with EPS, EPS-ZnONPs, and 5-FU, LRIG3 gene expression increased. The increase was significant under EPS treatment but not under EPS-ZnONPs or 5-FU treatment. Based on these findings, EPS significantly upregulated LRIG3 gene expression, making it a promising candidate for CRC treatment strategies targeting LRIG3.

PDCD4 is a tumor suppressor gene that also functions as a protein translation inhibitor. It plays a crucial role in suppressing various cancer-related characteristics, including inflammation, proliferation, survival, invasion, and metastasis [101]. In the present study, the investigation of PDCD4 gene expression levels in SW480 cells treated with EPS and EPS-ZnONPs showed a significant increase in the expression of this gene. In comparing the biological samples EPS-ZnONPs and EPS, it was found that EPS had a greater effect on increasing PDCD4 gene expression than EPS-ZnONPs. Therefore, for CRC treatment targeting the PDCD4 gene, the use of EPS would be a more appropriate and efficient choice. Li et al. (2017) exposed A549 cell lines to 0, 25, and 100 µg/mL concentrations of ZnONPs but did not observe any significant changes in PDCD4 expression levels in ZnONPs-treated A549 cells at these concentrations [102].

In the present study, it was found that EPS significantly increased the relative expression of the tumor suppressor genes PTEN, LRIG3, and PDCD4, with LRIG3 exhibiting the most notable increase in expression. This is promising for the use of EPS in CRC treatment, as it significantly increases LRIG3 gene expression and, consequently, enhances strong tumor-suppressing functions. By examining the expression levels of tumor suppressor genes under the influence of the biological samples in this study, namely EPS and EPS-ZnONPs, it was observed that EPS caused a greater increase in expression compared to EPS-ZnONPs in all of these genes. Therefore, for cancer treatment aimed at increasing tumor suppression activities, EPS is a more appropriate and effective option than EPS-ZnONPs.

In examining the relative decrease in oncogene expression, EPS had a greater effect on reducing KRAS gene expression compared to EPS-ZnONPs. Still, for other genes, EPS-ZnONPs caused a slightly greater decrease in expression compared to EPS. Therefore, considering the results, it appears that both EPS and EPS-ZnONPs are suitable options for cancer treatment aimed at inhibiting tumorigenic activities.

Synergistic analysis of EPS-ZnONPs

Considering one of the key objectives of this study, which was to investigate the potential synergistic effects of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs on anticancer activity, the results did not indicate such synergy in antioxidant activity, cytotoxicity, or modulation of target gene expression when compared to EPS alone. In most cases, EPS exhibited superior performance, with EPS-ZnONPs showing slightly better effects only in a few instances—none of which were statistically significant. Several factors may account for the lack of observed synergy. First, although the EPS coating on ZnONPs enhances biocompatibility and structural stability, it may hinder the direct interaction of ZnONPs with cancer cell membranes, thereby reducing their inherent cytotoxic potential. Additionally, conjugating ZnONPs to EPS could alter the structural integrity or biological activity of the polysaccharide, potentially impairing its ability to engage with cellular targets. Second, the structural conformation and physicochemical characteristics of the EPS-ZnONPs complex—such as particle size, surface charge, and dispersion stability—may have compromised the accessibility of key functional groups, thereby limiting cellular uptake and reducing their ability to effectively interact with target sites or elicit synergistic cytotoxic responses. Third, encapsulation of ZnONPs within the EPS matrix may restrict their release and lower their reactivity within the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, the concentration ratios used in the formulation may have been suboptimal for inducing a synergistic response, highlighting the need for further optimization of both dosage and delivery parameters [103, 104].

While EPS-ZnONPs offer potential benefits such as controlled release and reduced toxicity, their anticancer efficacy appears to rely heavily on fine-tuning the formulation to strike an effective balance between structural stability and biological availability. It is also plausible that EPS exerts its anticancer effects through specific molecular pathways that are not significantly enhanced by ZnONPs, or that the presence of ZnONPs interferes with EPS-mediated signaling. Furthermore, the biological complexity of CRC cells and their variable responses to NPs-based treatments may also contribute to the observed outcomes [105].

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The gene expression analysis was limited to a select group of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. A broader transcriptomic or proteomic approach could provide deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms involved. Furthermore, the findings are based solely on in vitro assays. The in vivo behavior, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs remain unexplored. Future studies should include animal models to assess systemic effects, immune responses, and long-term safety profiles.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the anticancer potential of EPS and highlights the need for further investigation into nanoparticle-based delivery systems and their interaction with bioactive compounds. Investigating the effects of EPS and EPS-ZnONPs on the expression levels of key genes in various cancers presents a promising strategy for advancing cancer treatment. Moreover, utilizing a liposomal model and functionalizing biosynthesized NPs with aptamers and other biological receptors can facilitate the programmed, targeted, and precise delivery of NPs to specific genes in cancer cells. This approach permits the controlled modulation of oncogene and tumor suppressor gene expression, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion

The results obtained from the present study demonstrate that the application of EPS extracted from the probiotic bacterium L. plantarum and EPS-ZnONPs can serve as effective agents in the treatment of CRC by regulating the expression levels of genes involved in its development. In this study, molecular analysis using real-time PCR revealed that L. plantarum EPS and EPS-ZnONPs increased the expression of tumor suppressor genes while decreasing the expression of oncogenes in the SW480 cell line, suggesting that the application of these metabolites could hold promise for future patient treatment. Furthermore, L. plantarum EPS exhibits antioxidant properties, making it a potential candidate for cancer prevention.
