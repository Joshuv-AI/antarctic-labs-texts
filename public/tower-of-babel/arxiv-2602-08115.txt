Stability of L p L^{p} Dirichlet solvability under small bi-Lipschitz transformations of domains Thanks: J. Zhuge is partially supported by NNSF of China (No. 12494541, 12288201, 12471115).
Joseph Feneuil, Linhan Li, Jinping Zhuge

Abstract
. We show that small bi-Lipschitz deformations of a Lipschitz domain (with possibly large Lipschitz constant) preserve the solvability of the Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian with boundary data in L p L^{p} , for the same value of p > 1 p>1 . As a consequence, for all p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) p\in(1,\infty) , we obtain the solvability of the L p L^{p} Dirichlet problem for small Lipschitz perturbations of convex domains, thereby unifying two fundamentally different settings in which such results were previously known: convex and C 1 C^{1} domains. The key ingredient and novelty of our approach is a construction of a change of variables based on a non-constant basis derived from the Green function, which encodes the geometry of the base domain.



Keywords:

L^{p}
Dirichlet problem, Laplacian, bi-Lipschitz change of variables, perturbations, Green function, Lipschitz domains, convex domains, quasiconvex domains.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and statement of main results

We consider the Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The solvability of the Dirichlet problem with boundary data in
L^{p}
—or
(D)_{p}
for simplicity, see Definition
2.8
—is closely tied to the geometry of the underlying domain and its boundary. A substantial body of recent work (see e.g.
[
HM14
,
HMUT14
,
AHM+20
]
) has culminated in identifying the necessary and sufficient geometric conditions for solving the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for
some
(large)
p>1
. Roughly speaking, the right geometric condition is some weak connectivity of the domain in addition to uniform rectifiability of the boundary of the domain, the latter being a strictly more general notion than a union of Lipschitz graphs.
In contrast, the right geometric notion for the solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for
all

p\in(1,\infty)
is far from clear. It remains an open question:

Question 1.1
.

What are the necessary and sufficient conditions on the domain for solving the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem
(D)_{p}
for all
p>1
?

Noticeably, there are two fundamentally different types of domains on which the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian is solvable for all
p\in(1,\infty)
: convex domains, and domains with “very flat” boundaries. Let us be more precise.

For convex domains, as one has a pointwise bound on the gradient of the Green function up to the boundary, one can obtain
(D)_{p}
for the full range of
p
using the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function (this is observed in
[
She06
]
). Combining this idea with Schauder theory, we can extend the result slightly beyond convex domains, namely, to domains whose boundary is (locally) the image of a convex graph under a
C^{1,\alpha}
diffeomorphism (see Appendix
B
). However, since the argument relies heavily on boundary
C^{1}
estimates of solutions, reducing the regularity of the
C^{1,\alpha}
diffeomorphism to only
C^{1}
is beyond reach of this method.

On the other hand, it is known that for any
p>1
, the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable in Lipschitz domains with sufficiently small Lipschitz constant, and in particular, in bounded
C^{1}
domains. This follows from the result of
[
JK82b
]
, which shows that on
C^{1}
domains, the logarithm of the Poisson kernel (
\log k
) has vanishing mean oscillation (VMO) on the boundary. Alternatively, one can deduce this result from
[
DPP07
]
, which treats a more general class of elliptic operators. Also in this direction,
[
KT97
]
generalizes the result to a broader class of domains whose boundaries are not necessarily given (locally) by graphs of functions. Specifically, Kenig and Toro showed that on
vanishing chord arc domains
—which can be viewed as a generalization of Lipschitz graph domains with vanishing Lipschitz constant—the logarithm of the Poisson kernel is in
\rm{VMO}(\partial\Omega)
, which implies that the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian is solvable for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. Remarkably, there is also a converse to this result (
[
KT03
]
): assuming that the domain is a chord-arc domain with sufficiently small constant, if
\log k\in\rm{VMO}(\partial\Omega)
, then the domain is a vanishing chord arc domain.

We can see that “flatness” of the boundary—such as the vanishing chord-arc condition—
cannot
be the right geometric criterion characterizing the solvability of
(D)_{p}
for all
p>1
.
Indeed, convex domains can be Lipschitz domains with very large Lipschitz constants, and since the Lipschitz constant is invariant under dilation, the boundaries of such domains can remain far from flat at every scale—yet the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for all
p>1
. In particular, this means that neither convex nor vanishing chord-arc is a necessary condition for solving the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for all
p>1
. The first step to understand the right geometric condition would thus require unifying these two conditions.

One of our contributions in this paper is
identifying a class of domains which includes both convex domains and Lipschitz domains with vanishing Lipschitz constants, and on which the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for all
p>1
(see Corollary
1.10
). Notably, this class of domains is invariant under any
C^{1}
diffeomorphism. We postpone the precise definition and statement until the end of this section.

Our approach is to study the following question, which can be viewed as the first step toward understanding Question
1.1
.

Question 1.2
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. What kinds of domain perturbations preserve
(D)_{p}
for the same value of
p
?

While there are many perturbation results for
operators
that preserve the solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem—see e.g.
[
Dah86
,
Esc96
,
DPP07
,
MPT13
,
MPT14
,
CHM19
,
AHMT23
,
AAA+11
,
BHLG+24
]
, see also perturbation results for operators that do not preserve the same value of
p

[
FKP91
,
CHMT20
,
DSU24
,
FP22
,
Hof25
]
—to our knowledge there has been no perturbation theory concerning
domains
, or at least, it has not usually been viewed in this way. On the other hand, one can apply certain perturbation results for operators to obtain corresponding perturbation results for domains, which is likely one of the original motivations for studying perturbation theory for operators. However, even when restricted to small Lipschitz perturbations in the transversal direction of a Lipschitz graph domain, existing theory suffers from one of the following 3 limitations:

•

it fails to preserve the same value of

p
,

•

it does not yield results in the range
1<p<2
,
or

•

it requires the base domain to have a sufficiently flat boundary (such as Lipschitz domain with small constant).

We refer the reader to Section
1.2
for a detailed discussion on this.

Our main result gives an answer to Question
1.2
, overcomes all the limitations of previous methods, and includes a substantially broader class of perturbations. In fact, our result applies to any bi-Lipschitz transformation sufficiently close to the identity. Moreover, we show that these transformations of domains preserve
(D)_{p}
for the same value of
p
for any
p\in(1,\infty)
, and we do not require any smallness condition on the Lipschitz constant of the base domain.
Specifically, our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Lipschitz graph domain, that is, there exists
M>0
and a
M
-Lipschitz function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
such that

\Omega_{0}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},\,t>g(x)\}.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
be such that the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian is solvable in
\Omega_{0}
.

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that if
\Phi:\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bi-Lipschitz map whose Jacobian matrix
\nabla\Phi
satisfies
\|\nabla\Phi-I\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{0}
, then the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian is solvable in
\Omega=\Phi(\Omega_{0})
.

Moreover,
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}(n,p,C_{p},M)
depends only on the dimension
n
, the parameter
p
, the reverse Hölder constant
C_{p}
in (
2.13
), and the Lipschitz constant
M
.

Some remarks are in order.

Remark 1.4
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1.3
, the perturbed domain
\Omega=\Phi(\Omega_{0})
takes the form of

\Omega=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:t>g(x+\psi(x))+h(x)\right\},

where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
are mappings that satisfy
|\nabla\psi|+|\nabla h|\lesssim\epsilon_{0}
. We provide a proof of this observation in Appendix
A
.

Remark 1.5
.

In view of Remark
1.4
, the class of allowable domain perturbations includes, in particular, small Lipschitz perturbations in the transversal direction. More precisely, our result applies to any perturbation of
\Omega_{0}
of the form

(1.6)

\Omega=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:t>g(x)+h(x)\right\},

where
h:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is any
\epsilon_{0}
-Lipschitz function. On the other hand, the class of perturbations covered by our theorem is strictly more general than the class described by (
1.6
), as the function
g(x+\psi(x))-g(x)
may not have small Lipschitz constant even if
\psi
does.

Remark 1.7
.

When restricted to
p\geq 2
, Theorem
1.3
can be derived from known arguments and results. In fact, since both
\Omega_{0}
and the perturbed domain
\Omega
are Lipschitz domains, the result can be obtained by using the solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian in Lipschitz domains for all
p\in[2,\infty)
, which is established in
[
Dah77
]
and
[
Ver84
]
. However, none of the available methods apply to the range
1<p<2
—see further discussion in Section
1.2
. We take a new approach which, by contrast, works uniformly for every
p>1
and does not distinguish between the cases
p\geq 2
and
p<2
.

Equipped with Theorem
1.3
, we can now introduce the class of domains that unifies convex and
C^{1}
domains. Roughly speaking, these domains are locally the image of a convex domain under a bi-Lipschitz map close to the identity.
Specifically, we define the following.

Definition 1.8
.

We say that a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
strongly quasiconvex
if there exists
M>0
such that for any
\epsilon_{0}>0
, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for any
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, there exists an coordinate basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
, an
M
-Lipschitz convex function
g_{x_{0}}
and a bi-Lipschitz map
\Phi_{x_{0}}:\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose Jacobian matrix
\nabla\Phi_{x_{0}}
satisfies
\|\nabla\Phi_{x_{0}}-I\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{0}
such that

\Omega\cap B(x_{0},r_{0})=\Phi_{x_{0}}\Big(\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},\,t>g_{x_{0}}(x)\}\Big)\cap B(x_{0},r_{0}).

It is obvious from definition that any convex domain and any Lipschitz domain with vanishing Lipschitz constant (or any bounded
C^{1}
domain) are strongly quasiconvex. We refer to this class of domains as
strongly quasiconvex
because the condition resembles that of
quasiconvex domains
in the literature (see, for instance,
[
JLW10
,
Zhu20
,
ZZ23
]
).

Remark 1.9
.

The class of strongly quasiconvex domains is stable under any
C^{1}
diffeomorphism (not necessarily with small constants). We give a sketch of the proof of this claim in Appendix
A
.
In particular, a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism transforms a convex domain into a strongly quasiconvex domain. This suggests that the class of strongly quasiconvex domains is a natural setting to consider.

By an appropriate localization argument (see Section
4.2
), one can deduce the our main corollary from Theorem
1.3
.

Corollary 1.10
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded strongly quasiconvex domain. Then the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for
-\Delta
in
\Omega
is solvable for all
p\in(1,\infty)
.

By the duality between the Dirichlet problem and the Regularity problem (see Definition
2.10
) in Lipschitz domains
[
She07
]
, the
L^{p}
Regularity problem is solvable whenever the
L^{p^{\prime}}
Dirichlet problem is solvable and the
L^{q}
Regularity problem is solvable for some
q>1
. Since the latter is known to hold for the Laplacian in Lipschitz domains (
[
Ver84
,
DK87
]
), we obtain the same range of
p
for which the
L^{p}
Regularity problem is solvable as for the Dirichlet problem.

Corollary 1.11
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded strongly quasiconvex domain. Then the
L^{p}
Regularity problem for
-\Delta
in
\Omega
is solvable for all
p\in(1,\infty)
.

1.2.
Existing methods and their limitations

If one views Lipschitz domains with sufficiently small constant as a small Lipschitz perturbation of flat sets, and vanishing chord arc domains as a small perturbation of Lipschitz domains with vanishing constant, then the solvability results mentioned at the beginning of the previous section on
(D)_{p}
for all
p>1
on these two types of domains can be interpreted as perturbation results for domains that preserve the solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem, for the same value of
p>1
. One feature common to these two results is that both involve small perturbations from “flat sets”.

Since our base domain may have very large Lipschitz constant—and thus its boundary far from being flat—existing methods either fail to preserve the same value of
p
, or they do not produce the result in the range
1<p<2
. In fact, in most cases, it is not even clear whether any of the existing methods could be applied to the general perturbations considered in our Theorem
1.3
. Therefore, we shall often restrict our comparisons to a special case of our result: when the small Lipschitz perturbation occurs only in directions transversal to the boundary of the base domain, i.e., when the perturbed domain
\Omega
has the form (
1.6
).

One possible approach to study stability of solvability of
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem under domain perturbation is via the method of layer potentials, which requires studying the stability of the invertibility of layer potentials in
L^{p}
. This, in turn, requires not only the sovability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem in the base domain
\Omega_{0}
, but also that in its complement
\Omega_{0}^{\complement}
, together with the solvability of the
L^{p^{\prime}}
Regularity and Neumann problems in both
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{0}^{\complement}
.
Such assumptions are too strong for our purposes: they cannot be used to deduce our Theorem
1.3
(even in the special case when only transversal perturbation occurs) for
1<p<2
, since knowing that
(D)_{p}
is solvable in
\Omega_{0}
alone yields neither
(D)_{p}
in
\Omega_{0}^{\complement}
nor solvability of the
L^{p^{\prime}}
Neumann problem in
\Omega_{0}
or
\Omega_{0}^{\complement}
. For example, in a convex domain
\Omega_{0}
the Dirichlet and Neumann problems are solvable for the full range of
p\in(1,\infty)
; but
\Omega_{0}^{\complement}
is not convex and the Dirichlet and Neumann problems are generally not solvable for the full range of
p
. Note that when
p\geq 2
, the above assumptions are automatically satisfied, since both
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{0}^{\complement}
are Lipschitz domains. Indeed,
[
DK87
]
shows that for the Laplacian, the
L^{p}
Dirichlet, the
L^{p^{\prime}}
Regularity and Neumann problems are solvable in Lipschitz domains for all
p\in(2-\epsilon,\infty)
for some small
\epsilon>0
depending on the Lipschitz domain. Moreover, this range is optimal, in the sense that there are counterexamples for every
p<2
(see
[
Ken86
]
). Thus the method of layer potentials reveals a sharp dichotomy between the cases
p\geq 2
and
1<p<2
: it gives no new information in the former range, and it breaks down in the latter.

Alternatively, one might wish to relate perturbations of domains to corresponding perturbations of operators. However, the existing perturbation theory for elliptic operators does not apply to our setting via any standard change of variables. Let us be more precise.

A standard approach to treating a boundary value problem on a Lipschitz graph domain

\Omega=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:t>\varphi(x)\right\}

is to transform it into a boundary value problem on the upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
for a different operator via a bi-Lipschitz change of variables (see Proposition
2.17
for a precise formulation of such an example). There are two types of elliptic operators in the form
L=-\diver A\nabla
arsing from two different bi-Lipschitz change of variables:

(1)

“
t
-independent” operators, which are operators whose coefficients are independent of the transversal variable, that is,
A(x,t)=A(x)
;

(2)

“DKP” operators, which are operators whose coefficients satisfy a Carleson condition, namely,
\dist(\cdot,\partial\Omega)|\nabla A|\in\CMs
(see Definition
2.1
).

The “
t
-independent” operators are associated with the simple flattening (or lifting) map:

\rho_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\to\Omega,\quad(x,t)\mapsto(x,t+\varphi(x)),

and the “DKP” operators are associated with the Nečas-Kenig-Stein map (see
[
KP01
]
):

\rho_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\to\Omega,\quad(x,t)\mapsto(x,ct+\eta_{t}*\varphi(x)),

where
\eta_{\epsilon}
is a standard mollifier, and
c
is a constant chosen to make sure that the map is 1-1.

For the map
\rho_{0}
, the Lipschitz constant of the boundary is reflected as the
L^{\infty}
norm of the pull-back operator, and so Theorem
1.3
in the special case when the perturbed domain
\Omega
has the form (
1.6
) can be reduced to the stability of the solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem for symmetric, real,
t
-independent operators
L_{0}=-\diver A_{0}\nabla
and
L_{1}=-\diver A_{1}\nabla
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, under the perturbation

\left\|A_{0}-A_{1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\epsilon.

This question is studied for
p=2
in
[
AAA+11
]
and for general
p
in
[
HMM15
]
via the method of layer potentials. One of the remarkable features of both works is that their theory applies to operators with complex coefficients. When restricted to the real-coefficient setting, however, the method faces the same limitations discussed above for layer potentials. In particular, it cannot be applied, at least directly, to obtain the range
1<p<2
in our Theorem
1.3
.

We now discuss the map
\rho_{1}
. From its construction, one can check that the constant in the Carleson condition of the pull-back operator increases as the Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
increases. Keing and Pipher (
[
KP01
]
) showed that the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for some (typically large)
p>1
for DKP operators (with possibly large Carleson constant) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, and hence also on Lipschitz domains, since the DKP condition is stable under bi-Lipschitz changes of variables.
Later,
[
DPP07
]
showed that for any
p>1
, if the Carleson constant of the coefficients of a DKP operator is
sufficiently small
, then
(D)_{p}
is solvable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, and therefore on Lipschitz domains with
sufficiently small
Lipschitz constant. Both works employ clever and delicate integration-by-parts arguments that depend crucially on a distinguished variable
t
; as a result, one is inevitably led to the flat case
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. If we apply
\rho_{1}
to the two Lipschitz domains
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega
, we get two DKP operators with
large
Carleson constants, for which we only know
(D)_{p}
for some large
p
. Although their Carleson constants are close to each other in the special case when
\Omega
is a small Lipschitz perturbation of
\Omega_{0}
in the transversal direction, they are not the appropriate Carleson perturbations to each other. In particular, the existing perturbation theory on operators do not apply in this setting.

1.3.
Main ideas of our approach

The takeaway from the previous discussions is that we do
not
want to reduce a Lipschitz domain to the flat case
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
because the small perturbation of the domain is lost in the flattening process. The central idea—and the key novelty—of our approach is to construct a map
\rho
directly
from the Lipschitz graph domain

\Omega_{0}=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:t>g(x)\right\}

to the perturbed domain

\Omega=\Phi(\Omega_{0})
,
and to design
\rho
in such a way that the small perturbation of the domains is appropriately captured in the pull-back operator
L_{\rho}
so that it enjoys the same solvablity of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem as the Laplacian on
\Omega_{0}
.

The first challenge that we face here is that we lose the special variable
t
(or equivalently, the special direction
e_{n}
) when constructing a map directly from
\Omega_{0}
. Note that this issue is not due to the level of generality of the perturbation we consider: even when the perturbation occurs only in directions transversal to the boundary of
\Omega_{0}
, i.e., when the perturbed domain takes the form (
1.6
),
one can no longer consider maps such as
\rho_{0}
or
\rho_{1}
mentioned in Section
1.2
by viewing points in
\Omega
as the image of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.
Our idea is to use the Green function with pole at infinity (see Lemma
2.5
for the definition) for the Laplacian in the base domain—denoted by
G
—to capture the geometry of
\Omega_{0}
. This is not too surprising, as the zero set of the Green function is
\partial\Omega_{0}
, and the level sets of
G
gives a family of surfaces that covers
\Omega_{0}
. We therefore consider the vector field
\nabla G
on
\Omega_{0}
, and build an orthonormal basis
\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\}
for any point
X\in\Omega_{0}
, with
v_{n}=\frac{\nabla G}{|\nabla G|}
. To make sure that it is possible, we show in Lemma
3.2
that
\nabla G
is non-degenerate on
\Omega_{0}
, and moreover, that
\frac{G(X)}{|\nabla G(X)|}
behaves like the distance from
X
to
\partial\Omega_{0}
. What is even better is that the gradient of
\frac{G}{|\nabla G|}
is ‘almost parallel’ to
v_{n}
at every
X\in\Omega_{0}
, in the sense that their difference satisfies a Carleson condition— see Theorem
3.10
. Although the result in Theorem
3.10
looks delicate, we remark that little additional work is required to obtain it as the difficult estimates for the second derivatives of the Green function have already been established in
[
FL23
]
and
[
DLM22
]
, and can also be found implicitly in
[
Azz19
]
. This implies that we can think of the function

t(X):=\frac{G(X)}{|\nabla G(X)|}\quad\text{for }X\in\Omega_{0}

as the transversal variable
t
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, and hence we have an appropriate special direction for
\Omega_{0}
—although the direction depends on the location in
\Omega_{0}
.

Next, we carefully design
\rho
so that the pull-back operator
L_{\rho}=-\diver A_{\rho}\nabla
from the Laplacian on
\Omega
under the map
\rho
is a small Carleson perturbation of an intermediate elliptic operator
L_{0}:=-\diver A_{0}\nabla
on
\Omega_{0}
, and that
L_{0}
enjoys the same solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem as the Laplacian on
\Omega_{0}
. Since small Carleson perturbations of operators preserve solvability of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem (see Theorem
2.16
for a precise statement), this process ensures that
L_{\rho}
has the same Dirichlet solvability as the Laplacian on
\Omega_{0}
, and thus the Laplacian has the same Dirichlet solvability on
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega
. The major work and challenge is to construct the intermediate operator
L_{0}
so that it is close to both the Laplacian on
\Omega_{0}
and the pull-back operator
L_{\rho}
. This construction—together with the construction of the map
\rho
—is carried out in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, and the properties of
L_{0}
is summarized in Lemma
4.2
.

One of the key properties that we want for the coefficient matrix
A_{0}
of the intermediate operator
L_{0}
is the following: we construct another orthonormal basis
\left\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\right\}
from
\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\}
, so that
when written in these two bases
, the Jacobian matrix of
\rho
is close—up to a Carleson perturbation—to a matrix in block form. It will imply that
A_{\rho}
is close to
A_{0}
, which,
when written in the basis
\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\}
, is of the form

\begin{bmatrix}*&\mathbf{0}^{T}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{0}=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}=(0,\dots,0).

This implies that
A_{0}v_{n}=v_{n}
, which, thanks to the direct relation between
v_{n}
and the Green function, will be enough to show that
L_{0}
and
-\Delta
have the same
L^{p}
Dirichlet solvability. We note that reducing matters to a block-form matrix or to a small perturbation of such a matrix, is not new in this area (see e.g.
[
HKMP15
,
Fen22
,
DFM19
,
DHP23
]
) and is often the core of the matter, though the idea is sometimes obscured by intricate integration-by-parts arguments. But again, in all previous work, such reductions rely crucially on the structure of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and are based on the Euclidean basis
\left\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\right\}
that is constant.

Let us now discuss the main ideas behind the construction of the basis
\left\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\right\}
from
\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\}
, as well as the map
\rho
. We want
\left\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\right\}
to be a small perturbation of
\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\}
, capturing the perturbation caused by the bi-Lipschitz map
\Phi
. In particular, the vector
w_{n}

should be an analog of
v_{n}
for the perturbed domain
\Omega=\Phi(\Omega_{0})
, in the sense that it should play the role as a normal vector to the new boundary
\partial\Omega
that propagates into
\Omega
. This leads us to look at the perturbation of the boundary, which can be described by the vector-valued function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

h(z):=\Phi(z,g(z))-(z,g(z))
(see (
3.17
)). However, this function is not smooth enough (it is Lipschitz) to allow us control the resulting Jacobian, and so we introduce a smoothing of
h
for any point
X\in\Omega_{0}
, which is defined as
\lambda(X)
(see (
3.16
)).
We then define the map
\rho:\Omega_{0}\to\Omega
to be

\rho(X)=X-t(X)v_{n}(X)+\lambda(X)+a(X)t(X)w_{n}(X),

or equivalently, as in (
3.40
). Here, one should think of
X-t(X)v_{n}(X)
as the projection of
X\in\Omega_{0}
onto
\partial\Omega_{0}
, and so
X-t(X)v_{n}(X)+\lambda(X)
is near the boundary of
\Omega
smoothed at scale
t(X)
. The map
\rho
preserves the special direction of
\partial\Omega
due to our construction of
w_{n}
. The role of
a(X)
(defined in (
3.36
)) is essentially to make the
nn
-th entry of the matrix
A_{0}
equal to 1, which simplifies the step comparing
L_{0}
to
-\Delta
.

In the simpler case when the perturbed domain takes the form (
1.6
), experts familiar with
[
DFM19
]
may wish to compare our construction of
\rho
with the change of variables introduced there. In
[
DFM19
]
, a modification of the Nečas–Kenig–Stein map
\rho_{1}
is introduced to show that the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for some
p>1
for a class of degenerate elliptic operators in domains with low-dimensional boundaries. One of the key advantages of their modification, even in the co-dimension 1 case, is that it is better adapted to the geometry of the target domain, in that it tends to preserve the orthogonal direction. In this respect, our construction is similar in spirit, but many new ideas are required as the argument must now be carried out relative to a non-constant basis
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
, must also accommodate a perturbation of the domain, and we consider perturbations much more general than those occur only in directions transversal to the boundary of the base domain.

We conclude the introduction by noting that our approach appears robust enough to apply to more general elliptic operators. For instance, we are hopeful that our results could be extended to operators satisfying the DKP condition, although substantial modifications would be required. Our results may also extend to more general domains whose boundaries are not necessarily given by graphs, following the ideas of
[
KT03
]
. In a different direction, our method might be adapted to address Question
1.2
for the Neumann problem; however, due to the inherent difficulty of the Neumann problem, new ideas would be needed. Finally, a precise characterization of the necessary condition in Question
1.1
remains open and is likely to be highly challenging.

It is well-known that the elliptic measure and Green function with pole at infinity can be compared via the comparison principle (see, for example,

Lemma 2.11

(Lemmas 3.3 and 3.4 in
[

]
)
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
be a Lipschitz graph domain, and let

be a

such that the Green function with pole at infinity

and the elliptic measure

with pole at infinity verifies

Moreover, the constant

The elliptic measure and Green function with pole at infinity can be used to characterize the solvability of the Dirichlet problem.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
be a Lipschitz graph domain, and write
\sigma
for the surface measure on
\partial\Omega_{0}
. Let
L=-\diver A\nabla
be a symmetric uniformly elliptic operator, and
p\in(1,\infty)
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem
(D)_{p}^{L}
is solvable in
\Omega_{0}
.

(ii)

The elliptic measure with pole at infinity
\omega_{L}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\sigma
, and there exists
C_{p}>0
such that the function
\kappa
defined as

\kappa(x):=\limsup_{X\in\gamma(x)\atop X\to x}\frac{G_{L}(X)}{\delta(X)}

satisfies

(2.13)

\left(\fint_{B(x,r)\cap\partial\Omega_{0}}\kappa^{p^{\prime}}(y)\,d\sigma(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\leq C_{p}\fint_{B(x,r)\cap\partial\Omega_{0}}\kappa(y)\,d\sigma(y)\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\partial\Omega_{0},\,r>0.

Here
p^{\prime}
stands for the Hölder conjugate of
p
.

When
\Omega_{0}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, we take a point
X_{0}\in\Omega_{0}
such that
\dist(X_{0},\partial\Omega_{0})\approx\diam(\Omega)
. Then (i) is equivalent to

(ii’)

The elliptic measure
\omega_{L}^{X_{0}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\sigma
, and there exists
C_{p}>0
such that the function
\kappa^{X_{0}}
defined as

\kappa^{X_{0}}(x):=\limsup_{X\in\gamma(x)\atop X\to x}\frac{G_{L}^{X_{0}}(X)}{\delta(X)},

satisfies

(2.14)

\left(\fint_{B(x,r)\cap\partial\Omega_{0}}\left(\kappa^{X_{0}}\right)^{p^{\prime}}(y)\,d\sigma(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\leq C_{p}\fint_{B(x,r)\cap\partial\Omega_{0}}\kappa^{X_{0}}(y)\,d\sigma(y)\qquad\text{for }x\in\partial\Omega,\,1<r<\diam(\Omega).

Proof:
It is well-known that
(i)
is equivalent to
\omega_{L}\ll\sigma
and that there exists
C_{p}>0
such that the function
k:=\frac{d\omega_{L}}{d\sigma}
satisfies (
2.13
), or, in the bounded case,
k^{X_{0}}:=\frac{d\omega_{L}^{X_{0}}}{d\sigma}
satisfies (
2.14
). We claim that
k
and
\kappa
are comparable, and that
k^{X_{0}}
and
\kappa^{X_{0}}
are comparable, with the implicit constants depending only the dimension
n
, the Lipschitz constant
M
of
\Omega_{0}
, and the ellipticity constant
C_{ellip}
of
L
. Note that the equivalence follows once this claim is justified.

We only prove the unbounded (i.e. Lipschitz graph domain) case as the bounded case is similar.
For any
X\in\gamma(x)
, write
s_{X}
for
\delta(X)
. Then by Lemma
2.11
,

(2.15)

\frac{G_{L}(X)}{\delta(X)}\approx\frac{\omega_{L}(\Delta(x,s_{X}))}{\sigma(\Delta(x,s_{X}))}

since
\sigma(\Delta(x,s_{X}))\approx s_{X}^{n-1}=\delta(X)^{n-1}
. This implies that

k(x)=\liminf_{s\to 0}\frac{\omega_{L}(\Delta(x,s))}{\sigma(\Delta(x,s))}\lesssim\limsup_{X\in\gamma(x)\atop X\to x}\frac{G_{L}(X)}{\delta(X)}\lesssim\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{\omega_{L}(\Delta(x,s))}{\sigma(\Delta(x,s))}=k(x)\quad\sigma-\text{a.e. }x\in\partial\Omega_{0}

by the assumption that

We also need the stability of the Dirichlet problem under bi-Lispchitz change of variables.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega
be two Lipschitz domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\rho:\Omega_{0}\to\Omega
be a bi-Lipschitz map that satisfies
\rho(\partial\Omega_{0})=\partial\Omega
. Then the solvability of the

Dirichlet problem is stable under

be an elliptic operator on
\Omega
, and let

be the pull-back of

under

(a.k.a. the conjugate of

is solvable in

Dirichlet problem for

is solvable in
\Omega_{0}
.

Proof.

This result is folklore; we sketch its proof for completeness. We only show that if the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for
L
in
\Omega
, then the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for
L_{\rho}
in
\Omega_{0}
, as the other direction is similar. Suppose the Lipschitz constant of
\rho
and
\rho^{-1}
is bounded by
M
.
Let
g\in L^{p}(\partial\Omega_{0})
be arbitrary, and let
f=g\circ\rho^{-1}
. Then
f\in L^{p}(\partial\Omega)
, and so by assumption,

(2.18)

\left\|N_{b}(u_{f})\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}

for some

, where

is defined as in (

), and

is any aperture which will be determined later. One can verify that the solution

given by

satisfies

.
Our goal is to show that
\left\|N(v_{g})\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega_{0})}\leq C^{\prime}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega_{0})}
for some

. Observe that for any

, we have

N(v_{\rho})(x)=\sup_{Y\in\gamma(x)}|u_{f}(\rho(Y))|\leq\sup_{Y\in\gamma_{M^{2}}(\rho(x))}|u_{f}(Y)|=N_{2M^{2}}(u_{f})(\rho(x)),

where

with vertex

In fact, for

\left\|N(v_{\rho})\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega_{0})}\leq\left\|N_{M^{2}}(u_{f})\circ\rho\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega_{0})}\leq C_{M}\left\|N_{M^{2}}(u_{f})\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{M}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{M}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega_{0})},

which proves that the

in
\Omega_{0}

2.3.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\Delta u=0
in
\Omega_{0}
. Then there exists
C>0
such that

|\nabla u(X)|\leq\frac{C}{\delta(X)}\left(\fint_{B_{X}}|u(Y)|^{2}dY\right)^{1/2}\qquad\text{ for all }X\in\Omega_{0},

where
B_{X}:=B(X,\delta(X)/2)
. If in addition,
u\geq 0
in
\Omega_{0}
, then

|\nabla u(X)|\leq Cu(X)/\delta(X)\qquad\text{ for all }X\in\Omega_{0}.

In particular, if
\Omega_{0}
is unbounded and if
G
is the Green function with pole at infinity for the Laplacian, as defined in Lemma
2.5
, then there exists
C>0
such that

|\nabla G(X)|\leq CG(X)/\delta(X)\qquad\text{ for all }X\in\Omega_{0}.

Proof:
This lemma is a classical consequence of the mean value property of harmonic functions, and also holds for solutions to more general elliptic PDEs. We include the short proof for completeness. Since
\partial_{i}u
is harmonic in
\Omega_{0}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the De Giorgi-Nash-Moser estimate gives that

|\nabla u(X)|^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\partial_{i}u(X)|^{2}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\fint_{\frac{1}{2}B_{X}}|\partial_{i}u|^{2}dY=\fint_{\frac{1}{2}B_{X}}|\nabla u|^{2}dY.

Using the Caccioppoli inequality, we further get

|\nabla u(X)|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta(X)^{2}}\fint_{B_{X}}u^{2}(Y)dY,

which is the first claim. If
u\geq 0
in
\Omega_{0}
, then the right-hand side above is further bounded by
\frac{u(X)^{2}}{\delta(X)^{2}}

by the Harnack inequality. The lemma follows.

\square

.

3.
The change of variables - the heart of the matter

3.1.
Set-up estimates on Lipschitz graph domains

Throughout this section,
\Omega_{0}
is an unbounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
G
is the Green function for the Laplacian with pole at infinity in
\Omega_{0}
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that the domain
\Omega_{0}
satisfies the
\nabla G
’s non-degeneracy condition—called (
H1
)—if there exists
C_{1}>0
such that

(H1)

G(X)\leq C_{H}\delta(X)|\nabla G(X)|\qquad\text{ for all }X\in\Omega_{0}.

We say that the domain
\Omega
satisfies the preferred direction condition—called (
H2
)—if there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

(H2)

\left<\nabla G(X),e_{n}\right>\geq(C_{2})^{-1}|\nabla G(X)|\quad\text{ for }X\in\Omega_{0}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega_{0}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,t>g(x)\}
be a Lipschitz graph domain. Then
\Omega_{0}
satisfies (
H1
)–(
H2
).

Proof:
Recall that we write
\partial_{n}G
for
\left<\nabla G(X),e_{n}\right>
. We can assume without loss of generality that
g(0)=0
, that is,
0\in\partial\Omega_{0}
.

Step 1: Show that
\partial_{n}G\geq 0
in
\Omega_{0}
.
This step carries all the key arguments, and (
H1
)–(
H2
) is actually a corollary of this statement.

Step 1a: Convergence to
G
.
For
Y\in\Omega_{0}
, let
G^{Y}
be a smooth version of Green function with pole at
Y
. Specifically, if
G(X,Y)
is the Green function in
\Omega_{0}
, and if
\eta_{t}
is a mollifier, meaning that
\eta\in C^{\infty}(B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1/2))
,
\eta\geq 0
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta=1
, and
\eta_{t}(Z):=t^{-n}\eta(Z/t)
, then

G^{Y}(X):=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)G(X,Z)\,dZ.

Let
Y_{i}
be the point
(0,2^{i})
. From the construction of the Green function with pole at infinity, and in particular, (
2.6
), we have that

(3.3)

\frac{G(.,Z_{i})}{G(Y_{0},Z_{i})}\text{ converges to }\frac{G}{G(Y_{0})}\text{ uniformly on compacts of $\overline{\Omega_{0}}$}.

as
i\to\infty
, as long as
Z_{i}\in B(Y_{i},\delta(Y_{i})/2)
. Moreover, the rate of convergence does not depend on the choice of
Z_{i}\in B(Y_{i},\delta(Y_{i})/2)
. As a consequence

(3.4)

\frac{G^{Y_{i}}}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}\text{ converges to }\frac{G}{G(Y_{0})}\text{ uniformly on compacts of $\overline{\Omega_{0}}$}.

The De Giorgi-Nash-Moser estimate and the Caccioppoli inequality show then the convergence of the gradient, that is

(3.5)

\frac{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}\text{ converges to }\frac{\partial_{n}G}{G(Y_{0})}\text{ uniformly on compacts of $\Omega_{0}$}.

Indeed, take
K\Subset\Omega_{0}
be compact, and chose
i\in\mathbb{N}
large enough such that
|Y_{i}|\geq 4(\diam K+\dist(K,\partial\Omega_{0})
. Then for any
X\in K
, since both
G^{Y_{i}}
and
G
are solutions to
\Delta u=0
in
B(X,\delta(X))
, so is
\frac{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}-\frac{\partial_{n}G}{G(Y_{0})}
.
Therefore, by the De Giorgi-Nash-Moser estimate and the Caccioppoli inequality, we have

\left|\frac{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}(X)}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}-\frac{\partial_{n}G(X)}{G(Y_{0})}\right|\lesssim\left(\fint_{B_{X}/2}\left|\frac{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}(Z)}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}-\frac{\partial_{n}G(Z)}{G(Y_{0})}\right|^{2}\,dZ\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{\dist(K,\partial\Omega_{0})}\left(\fint_{B_{X}}\left|\frac{G^{Y_{i}}(Z)}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}-\frac{G^{Y_{i}}(Z)}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}\right|^{2}\,dZ\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{\dist(K,\partial\Omega_{0})}\sup_{Z\in K^{*}}\left|\frac{G^{Y_{i}}(Z)}{G^{Y_{i}}(Y_{0})}-\frac{G(Z)}{G(Y_{0})}\right|\longrightarrow 0\text{ as }i\to\infty.

where
K^{*}:=\bigcup_{Z\in K}\overline{B(Z,\delta(Z)/2)}
, and the final convergence is by (
3.4
).

Step 1b: Representation of
\partial_{n}G^{Y}
.

We write
H^{Y}
for the function

H^{Y}(X):=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(\partial_{n}\eta_{\delta(Y)}\right)(Y-Z)G(X,Z)dZ.

We claim that

(3.6)

\partial_{n}G+H^{Y}\text{ is a weak solution to $-\Delta u=0$ in $\Omega_{0}$.}

First,
G^{Y}
and
H^{Y}
are smooth functions, since there both weak solution to
-\Delta u=f
with some
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
. More precisely,
-\Delta G^{Y}=\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-\cdot)
, and
-\Delta H^{Y}=\left(\partial_{n}\eta_{\delta(Y)}\right)(Y-\cdot)
.
Hence,
\partial_{n}G^{Y}
and
H^{Y}
are in
W^{1,2}_{loc}(\Omega_{0})
. Second, for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have by the PDE satisfied by
G^{Y}
and
H^{Y}
,

\int_{\Omega_{0}}\nabla[\partial_{n}G^{Y}+H^{Y}]\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dZ=-\int_{\Omega_{0}}\nabla G^{Y}\cdot\nabla\partial_{n}\varphi\,dZ+\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(\partial_{n}\eta_{\delta(Y)}\right)(Y-Z)\varphi(Z)\,dZ\\
=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\big(-\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\partial_{n}\varphi(Z)+\left(\partial_{n}\eta_{\delta(Y)}\right)(Y-Z)\varphi(Z)\big)\,dZ.

Integration by parts in
Z_{n}
gives that

-\int_{\Omega_{0}}\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\partial_{n}\varphi(Z)dZ=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\partial_{Z_{n}}\left(\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\right)\varphi(Z)dZ=-\int_{\Omega_{0}}(\partial_{n}\eta_{\delta(Y)})(Y-Z)\varphi(Z)dZ,

which implies that
\int_{\Omega_{0}}\nabla[\partial_{n}G^{Y}+H^{Y}]\cdot\nabla\varphi=0
as claimed.

Since
\Omega_{0}
is a Lipschitz domain, the
L^{2}
Dirichlet problem is solvable for
-\Delta
in
\Omega_{0}
(see
[
Dah77
]
or
[
JK81
]
2
2

2

An attentive reader might notice that both references are for bounded Lipschitz domain, but the same result holds for unbounded domain. Alternatively, since we actually only need
L^{p}
estimate for some
p>1
, we can apply
[
HKMP15
]
for unbounded domains.
). So, by Theorem 1.22 in
[
MPT22
]
, the Poisson-Dirichlet and Poisson-regularity problem are also solvable in

. It implies that

\|N(\nabla G^{Y})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}+\|N(H^{Y})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}<+\infty.

In particular,
\partial_{n}G^{Y}
has a trace in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})
. The
L^{2}
solvability of the Dirichlet problem in
\Omega_{0}
also implies that the harmonic extension
u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}}
of this trace in
\Omega_{0}
verifies

\|N(u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}\lesssim\left\|Tr(\partial_{n}G^{Y})\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{0})}<+\infty.

Note that the non-tangential bounds of
\partial_{n}G^{Y}
,
H^{Y}
, and
u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}}
guarantees the almost everywhere non-tangential limit of the functions to their traces, and that

\Tr(\partial_{n}G^{Y}+H^{Y}-u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}})=\Tr(H^{Y})=0.

Consequently,
\partial_{n}G^{Y}+H^{Y}-u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}}
is a function with non-tangential bounds and zero trace, and is a solution to
-\Delta u=0
in
\Omega_{0}
by (
3.6
). Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem (see
[
Ken94
, Theorem 1.4.4]
, or
[
HLMP22
, Section 6]
for unbounded domains) gives the formula:

\partial_{n}G^{Y}=u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}}-H^{Y}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{0}.

Step 1c:
\partial_{n}G\geq 0
.
First, note that
\partial_{n}G
is non-negative on the boundary
\partial\Omega_{0}
. Indeed, for
x\in\partial\Omega
, since
G^{Y}
is positive in
\Omega_{0}
and since
x+he_{n}\in\Omega_{0}
for
h>0
, we have

\partial_{n}G^{Y}(x)=\lim_{h\to 0\atop h>0}\frac{G^{Y}(x+he_{n})}{h}\geq 0.

As a consequence, the harmonic extension
u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y}}
of
\Tr(\partial_{n}G^{Y})
- which is given by the integral of
\Tr(\partial_{n}G^{Y})
with respect to a positive measure (the harmonic measure) - is non-negative in
\Omega_{0}
.

Second, we compare
H^{Y}
and
G^{Y}
outside of
B_{Y}
.
Observe that

H^{Y}(X)=-\int_{B_{Y}}\partial_{Z_{n}}\left(\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\right)G(X,Z)dZ=\int_{B_{Y}}\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\partial_{Z_{n}}G(X,Z)dZ

by integration by parts. Therefore,

|H^{Y}(X)|\leq\int_{B_{Y}}\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)|\nabla_{Z}G(X,Z)|dZ.

If
B_{X}
and
B_{Y}
do not intersect, we apply Lemma
2.19
to
G(X,\cdot)
in
\Omega_{0}\setminus B_{X}
. For
Z\in B_{Y}
,
\dist(Z,\partial(\Omega_{0}\setminus B_{X}))=\min\left\{\delta(Z),\dist(Z,B_{X})\right\}
. Obviously,
\delta(Z)\geq\delta(Y)/2
. We check that
\dist(Z,B_{X})\geq\max\left\{\delta(X)/2,|Y-X|-\delta(X)/2-\delta(Y)/2\right\}
. If
\delta(X)<\delta(Y)/4
, then
|Y-X|\geq\delta(Y)-\delta(X)
, and so
|Y-X|-\delta(X)/2-\delta(Y)/2\geq\delta(Y)/8
. This implies that
\dist(Z,B_{X})\geq\delta(Y)/8
, and thus for
Z\in B_{Y}
,
\dist(Z,\partial(\Omega_{0}\setminus B_{X}))\geq\delta(Y)/8
. So we have that

|H^{Y}(X)|\lesssim\int_{B_{Y}}\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\frac{G(X,Z)}{\dist(Z,\partial(\Omega_{0}\setminus B_{X}))}dZ\lesssim\int_{B_{Y}}\eta_{\delta(Y)}(Y-Z)\frac{G(X,Z)}{\delta(Y)}dZ=\frac{G^{Y}(X)}{\delta(Y)}.

It means that for any
X\in\Omega_{0}
and any
c>0
, there exists
i_{0}:=i_{0}(X,c)
such that for
i\geq i_{0}

(3.7)

|H^{Y_{i}}(X)|\lesssim 2^{-i}G^{Y_{i}}(X)\lesssim cG^{Y_{i}}.

For
c>0
, using successively Step 1a, Step 1b, the positivity of
u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}}
and (
3.7
) we have then that

\frac{\partial_{n}G(X)}{G(X)}=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}(X)}{G^{Y_{i}}(X)}=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{u_{\partial_{n}G^{Y_{i}}}(X)-H^{Y_{i}}(X)}{G^{Y_{i}}(X)}\geq-c

Since the bound is for all
c>0
, we conclude that

\frac{\partial_{n}G(X)}{G(X)}\geq 0

as desired.

Step 2: Show that
\partial_{n}G(X)\geq cG(X)/\delta(X)
for
X\in\Omega_{0}
.
From Step 1, we get that for any Lipschitz graph domain
\Omega_{0}
and any Green function with pole at infinity
G
on
\Omega_{0}
, we have

(3.8)

\partial_{n}G>0.

Indeed,
\partial_{n}G
is a non-zero non-negative harmonic function in
\Omega_{0}
, so the Harnack inequality forces
\partial_{n}G
to be positive.

We want to prove that there exists
c>0
(depending only on
n
and the Lipschitz constant of
g
) such that

(3.9)

\partial_{n}G(X)\geq cG(X)/\delta(X)\qquad\text{ for all }X\in\Omega_{0}.

This claim easily yields (
H1
)–(
H2
).

To show (
3.9
), it suffices to show that there is
c>0
depending only on
n
and the Lipschitz constant of
g
, such that
\partial_{n}G(0,1)>cG(0,1)
(recall that
0\in\partial\Omega_{0}
). We can assume without loss of generality that
G(0,1)=1
. By the Harnack inequality,
0<G(X)\lesssim 1
for all
X\in B(0,2)\cap\Omega_{0}
, with the implicit constant depending only on the dimension and the Lipschitz constant. Moreover, since
G
is smooth (and in particular, continuous) in

, there exists
c_{0}>0
(depending only on
n
and the Lipschitz constant of
g
) such that
G(0,c_{0})\leq 1/2
. So we have that

\frac{1}{2}\leq G(0,1)-G(0,c_{0})=\int_{c_{0}}^{1}\partial_{n}G(0,s)ds.

By the mean value theorem, there exists
s_{0}\in(c_{0},1)
such that
\partial_{n}G(0,s_{0})\geq 1/2
. Finally, since

is harmonic and nonnegative, the Harnack inequality implies that

for some

.

\square

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\Omega_{0}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,t>g(x)\}

\delta\nabla\left(\frac{\nabla G}{|\nabla G|}\right)\in\CMs(M),

This theorem is a simple consequence of Theorem
2.20

|\nabla G|
never becomes 0, meaning that we can differentiate
\nabla G/|\nabla G|

\left|\delta\nabla\left(\frac{\nabla G}{|\nabla G|}\right)\right|\leq 2\frac{\delta|\nabla^{2}G|}{|\nabla G|}\lesssim\frac{\delta^{2}|\nabla^{2}G|}{G}\in\CMs

\left|\nabla\left(\frac{G}{|\nabla G|}\right)-\frac{\nabla G}{|\nabla G|}\right|\leq\frac{G|\nabla^{2}G|}{|\nabla G|^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\delta^{2}|\nabla^{2}G|}{G}\in\CMs

\square

We need now an orthonormal basis whose last element is
\nabla G/|\nabla G|

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
satisfy (
H1
)–(
H2
). There exists
n
vectors functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
on
\Omega_{0}
such that:

(i)

For each
X\in\Omega_{0}
, the collection
\{v_{1}(X),\dots,v_{n}(X)\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(ii)

The last vector function is
v_{n}:=\frac{(\nabla G)^{T}}{|\nabla G|}
, so in particular

v_{i}(X)\cdot\nabla G(X)=0\qquad\text{ for each }X\in\Omega\text{ and }i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}.

(iii)

There exists
C>0
such that, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
X\in\Omega_{0}
,

|\nabla v_{i}(X)|\leq C|\nabla v_{n}(X)|.

Proof:
The result by itself is not surprising, since we can simply see it as the variant of the Gram-Schmidt process, and can probably already be found in the literature. For completeness, we sketch the argument.

Let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
be the Cartesian orthonormal basis from condition (
H2
). For any fixed
X\in\Omega_{0}
, we define the vectors
v_{i}(X)
by a decreasing induction in
i
as follows. For
i\in\{n-1,\dots,1\}
,

(3.12)

\tilde{v}_{i}(X):=e_{i}-\sum_{i<k\leq n}\langle e_{i},v_{k}(X)\rangle v_{k}(X),\quad\text{ and }\quad v_{i}(X):=\dfrac{\tilde{v}_{i}(X)}{\left|\tilde{v}_{i}(X)\right|}.

The condition (
H2
) guarantees that

\left\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1},v_{n}(X)\right\}
is linearly independent, and so the construction automatically returns an orthonormal basis. Moreover, (
H2
) says that
\left<v_{n},e_{n}\right>\geq c
for some
c\in(0,1)
independent of
X
. By the Parseval’s identity,

(3.13)

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|\left<e_{i},v_{n}(X)\right>|^{2}\leq 1-c^{2}.

We want to prove that

(3.14)

|\tilde{v}_{i}(X)|\geq c\quad\text{for }X\in\Omega_{0},\,i\in\left\{1,\dots,n-1\right\}.

To this objective, note that by the Gram-Schmidt process,
|\tilde{v}_{i}|
is the distance from
e_{i}
to the linear space spanned by
\{e_{i+1},\cdots,e_{n-1},v_{n}\}
. Thus

\displaystyle|\tilde{v}_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R},i+1\leq k\leq n}\left<e_{i}-\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}\alpha_{k}e_{k}-\alpha_{n}v_{n},\ e_{i}-\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}\alpha_{k}e_{k}-\alpha_{n}v_{n}\right>

\displaystyle=\inf_{\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R},i+1\leq k\leq n}\Big\{1+\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\alpha_{k}^{2}-2\alpha_{n}\left<e_{i},v_{n}\right>-\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}2\alpha_{k}\alpha_{n}\left<e_{k},v_{n}\right>\Big\}.

Using
|2\alpha_{k}\alpha_{n}\left<e_{k},v_{n}\right>|\leq\alpha_{k}^{2}+\alpha_{n}^{2}|\left<e_{k},v_{n}\right>|^{2}
and
|2\alpha_{n}\left<e_{i},v_{n}\right>|\leq(1-c^{2})+(1-c^{2})^{-1}\alpha_{n}^{2}|\left<e_{i},v_{n}\right>|^{2}
, we then have

\displaystyle|\tilde{v}_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{1+\alpha_{n}^{2}-(1-c^{2})-(1-c^{2})^{-1}\alpha_{n}^{2}|\left<e_{i},v_{n}\right>|^{2}-\alpha_{n}^{2}\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}|\left<e_{k},v_{n}\right>|^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{c^{2}+\alpha_{n}^{2}-\alpha_{n}^{2}(1-c^{2})^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{n-1}|\left<e_{k},v_{n}\right>|^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq c^{2},

where we have used (
3.13
) in the last inequality.

It remains to justify
(iii)
. In fact, the uniform non-degeneracy of
\tilde{v}_{i}
(
3.14
) guarantees that (
3.12
) is well-defined and thus, each
v_{i}
is a
C^{\infty}
function of
v_{n}
. Then the chain rule implies that
|\nabla v_{i}|\lesssim|\nabla v_{n}|
. The lemma follows.

\square

3.2.
Quantifying small Lipschitz perturbations of Lipschitz graph domains.

In the rest of the section,

\Omega_{0}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},\,t>g(x)\}

is a Lipschitz graph domain, with a (possibly large) constant
M
.
G
is the Green function with pole at
\infty
for the Laplacian on
\Omega_{0}
.

On top of that, we set
\Omega:=\Phi(\Omega_{0})
, where
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bi-Lipschitz map satisfying
\|\nabla\Phi-I\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, where
\epsilon>0
will be small. We want to construct a “better” bi-Lipschitz change of variable
\rho
from
\Omega_{0}
to
\Omega
, which preserves the structure of
\Omega_{0}
. By preserving the structure of
\Omega_{0}
, we mean that at least the Jacobian
\nabla\rho
is close to identity, in the sense that the errors are Carleson measures on
\Omega_{0}
with small constant.

We first need a mollifier. We let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
be a
radial
function such that
\eta\geq 0
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\eta\,dx=1
, and
\eta\equiv 0
outside
B_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(0,1)
. Then, for
t>0
, we define
\eta_{t}(x):=t^{1-n}\eta(x/t)
.

We view
\Omega
as a perturbation of
\Omega_{0}
, and the quantity that we want to control is
\lambda:\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is a smooth version of the perturbation defined by

(3.16)

\lambda(X):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\,\eta_{t(X)}\big(y(X)-z\big)\,dz\quad\text{for }X\in\Omega_{0},

where

(3.17)

h(z):=\Phi(z,g(z))-(z,g(z)),

and

(3.18)

t(X):=\frac{G(X)}{|\nabla G(X)|},\qquad y(X):=\pi\Big(X-\frac{G(X)(\nabla G(X))^{T}}{|\nabla G(X)|^{2}}\Big)=\pi\Big(X-t(X)v_{n}(X)\Big),

and
v_{n}:=\frac{(\nabla G)^{T}}{|\nabla G|}
as in Lemma
3.11
. Recall that
\pi
is the orthogonal projection on the first
(n-1)
-coordinates.

We emphasize that in our definition,
t:\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function and should not be confused with the variable
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We deliberately choose this notation, as we wish to view the function as playing the same role, in
\Omega_{0}
, of the special variable
t
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. In fact, by Lemma
2.19
, (
H1
), Theorem
3.10
, and Corollary
3.15
, we have that

(3.19)

t\approx\delta:=\dist(.,\partial\Omega_{0});

(3.20)

t\nabla v_{i}\in\CMs(CM)\quad\text{for }i\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\},

and

(3.21)

\nabla t-v_{n}^{T}\in\CMs(CM).

Note that
h:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Lipschitz, since

(3.22)

|\nabla h(z)|\leq|\nabla\Phi((z,g(z))-I|\sqrt{1+|\nabla g(z)|^{2}}\leq C_{M}\epsilon.

The main result of this subsection is:

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
\Omega_{0}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,t>g(x)\}
, where
g:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
M
-Lipschitz function, and let
\Phi:\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bi-Lipschitz change of variable satisfying
\|\nabla\Phi-I\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. Then the quantity
\lambda
defined in (
3.16
)–(
3.18
) has the following properties:

(i)

\|\nabla\lambda\|_{\infty}\leq C_{M}\epsilon
.

(ii)

\partial_{v_{n}}\lambda\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)
.

(iii)

There exists a decomposition
\nabla\lambda=:\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}
verifying
\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{M}\epsilon
,
v_{n}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, and
|\mathbf{C}|+t|\nabla\mathbf{B}|\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)
.

The constant
C_{M}>0
in (i), (ii) and (iii) is independent of
\epsilon
, meaning that it depends only on
M
, the dimension
n
, the choice of
\eta
, and the constant in (
H1
).

Proof:
Step 0: Preliminaries.

In the proof, we write
t
for
t(X)
,
y
for
y(X)
,
v_{n}
for
v_{n}(X)
, and
G
for
G(X)
; this considerably lightens the notation. Now, (
3.16
) becomes

\lambda(X):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz.

Building on estimates (
3.20
), (
3.21
) and the observation that

\nabla y=\pi(\nabla X)-\pi(\nabla(tv_{n}))=I_{n\times(n-1)}-\pi\left((\nabla t-v_{n}^{T})v_{n}\right)-\pi(v_{n}^{T}v_{n})-\pi(t\nabla v_{n}),

where
I_{n\times(n-1)}
is the
n\times(n-1)
matrix that takes the value
1
on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere, we also have

(3.24)

\big[\nabla y-I_{n\times(n-1)}+(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big]\in\CMs(CM).

In particular, the estimates (
3.20
), (
3.21
) and (
3.24
) imply that (see Remark
2.2
)

(3.25)

\|\nabla t\|_{\infty}+\|t\nabla v_{n}\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla y\|_{\infty}\leq C(1+M^{1/2}).

Step 1: proof of (i).

Now, we can compute
\nabla\lambda
. Take
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be defined later. We have, since
\int\eta_{t}=1
,

(3.26)

\nabla\lambda(X)=\nabla(\lambda-a)=\nabla_{X}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}[h(z)-a]\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right)\\
=(\nabla t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}[h(z)-a]\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}[h(z)-a](\nabla y)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right),

where
(\nabla y)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}
is seen as a matrix multiplication (
\nabla y
has dimensions
n\times(n-1)
and
\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}
is a column vector of dimension
n-1
). Check that
\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}
and
\partial_{t}\eta_{t}
are both supported in
B_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(0,t)
, and verify
\|\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\|\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-n}
. So we obtain that

|\nabla\lambda(X)|\lesssim t^{-n}\left(\int_{B(y,t)}|h(z)-a|\,dz\right)(|\nabla t|+|\nabla y|)\lesssim\frac{M^{1/2}+1}{t}\fint_{B(y,t)}|h(z)-a|\,dz

by (
3.25
). We choose
a:=\fint_{B(y,t)}h\,dz
, and since
h
is
\epsilon
-Lipschitz, we get that
|h(z)-a|\leq\epsilon t
for all
z\in B(y,t)
. So

|\nabla\lambda(X)|\leq C_{M}\epsilon.

with a constant independent of
X
. Conclusion (i) of the Theorem follows.

Step 2: proof of (ii).
We need to be finer. We compute

\displaystyle v_{n}\nabla\lambda(X)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right)(\partial_{v_{n}}t)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)v_{n}(\nabla y)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

Observe that
\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)=-\nabla_{z}\eta_{t}(y-z)
, and so

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}v_{n}(\nabla y)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz=0,

which implies that

I_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\left(h(z)-a^{\prime}\right)\big[v_{n}(\nabla y)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\big]\,dz\quad\text{for any }a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Note that (
3.24
) gives that

v_{n}(\nabla y)=v_{n}\big(\nabla y-I_{n\times(n-1)}+(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big)\in\CMs(CM).

Hence

|I_{2}|\leq\underbrace{\left(\int_{B(y,t)}|h(z)-a^{\prime}||\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)|\,dz\right)}_{\leq C\epsilon\text{ as in Step 1}}|v_{n}(\nabla y)|\in\CMs(CM\epsilon).

As for
I_{1}
, we use (
3.25
) to get that

|I_{1}|\lesssim(1+M^{1/2})\Big|\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\,\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz}_{I_{11}}\Big|

To deal with
I_{11}
, we observe that

\partial_{t}\eta_{t}(z)=\diver_{z}[\tilde{\eta}_{t}](z),

with
\tilde{\eta}(z)=-z\eta(z)
and
\tilde{\eta}_{t}(z)=t^{1-n}\tilde{\eta}(z/t)
. Denote by
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1})
the coordinates of

. By the divergence theorem, the identity implies that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(a+z_{i})\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{z}z_{i}\cdot\tilde{\eta}_{t}(y-z)\,dz=0

since
\tilde{\eta}
is odd (because
\eta
is radial). We deduce that for any constant vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for any constant matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
,
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(a+zB)\,\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz=0,

and hence

I_{11}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}[h(z)-a-zB]\,\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz.

Consequently,

(3.27)

|I_{11}|=|I_{11}(X)|\lesssim\beta\left(y(X),t(X)\right),

where

\beta(y,t):=\frac{1}{t}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}}\fint_{B(y,t)}|h(z)-a-zB|\,dz.

Observe that the function
(y,t)\mapsto\beta(y,t)
enjoys the following property: for
(y,t)
and
(y^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty)
satisfying the inclusion of balls

for some
K\geq 1
and
t^{\prime}\geq ct
for some
c>0
, we have
\beta(y^{\prime},t^{\prime})\leq C_{n,K,c}\beta(y,Kt)
.
Moreover, since
h
is a
C\epsilon
-Lipschitz function, Dorronsoro’s Theorem (
[
Dor85
]
) gives that

\beta^{2}(y,t)dy\,dt/t
is a Carleson measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty)
, with Carleson norm smaller than
C\epsilon
, that is,

(3.28)

\sup_{(x,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty)}r^{1-n}\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(x,r)}\int_{0}^{r}\beta(y,t)^{2}\frac{dt}{t}dy\leq C\epsilon.

We want to show that (
3.28
) implies that
|I_{1}|\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)
.
To do that, consider that map
\Upsilon:(y,t)\mapsto(y,t+g(y))
. It is easy to see that
\Upsilon
is a bi-Lipschitz map from
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty)
to
\Omega_{0}
. For an arbitrary
X\in\Omega_{0}
, we reserve the notations
y=y(X)
,
t=t(X)
. We denote

X^{\prime}=\Upsilon(y,t)=(y,t+g(y))\in\Omega_{0},\quad y^{\prime}=y(X^{\prime}),\text{ and }t^{\prime}=t(X^{\prime}).

We claim that the point
X^{\prime}
is close to
X
in the sense that the coefficients
\beta(y^{\prime},t^{\prime})
can be controlled by a multiple of
\beta(y,Kt)
for some
K\geq 1
. In fact, since
g
is
M
-Lipschitz,
\delta(X^{\prime})\approx t
, which implies that
t\approx t^{\prime}
because
\delta(X^{\prime})\approx t^{\prime}
and
\delta(X)\approx t
by (
3.19
). Next, note that
\pi(X^{\prime})=y
, and so
y^{\prime}=y-t^{\prime}\pi(v_{n}(X^{\prime}))
. This implies that

|y^{\prime}-y|=|t^{\prime}\pi(v_{n}(X^{\prime}))|\leq t^{\prime}\approx t
,
and hence
B(y^{\prime},t^{\prime})\subset B(y,Kt)
for some
K\geq 1
. We deduce that
\beta(y^{\prime},t^{\prime})\lesssim\beta(y,Kt)
from the property of
\beta
.

We now consider an arbitrary point
Z\in W_{X^{\prime}}
, where
W_{X^{\prime}}=B(X^{\prime},\delta(X^{\prime})/4)
is a Whitney ball in
\Omega_{0}
centered at
X^{\prime}=\Upsilon(y,t)
. Obviously,
t(Z)\approx\delta(Z)\approx\delta(X^{\prime})\approx t^{\prime}
, and

|y(Z)-y^{\prime}|\leq|Z-X^{\prime}|+|t(Z)v_{n}(Z)-t^{\prime}v_{n}(X^{\prime})|\lesssim t^{\prime}.

This again implies that
B(y(Z),t(Z))\subset B(y^{\prime},Kt^{\prime})
for some
K\geq 1
, and that
\beta(y(Z),t(Z))\lesssim\beta(y^{\prime},Kt^{\prime})
. Therefore, by (
3.27
) and the above estimates on
\beta
, we have that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
r>0
,

\int_{B(x,r)}\int_{0}^{r}\sup_{Z\in W_{\Phi(y,t)}}|I_{11}(Z)|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\,dy\lesssim\int_{B(x,r)}\int_{0}^{r}\sup_{Z\in W_{\Phi(y,t)}}|\beta(y(Z),t(Z))|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\,dy\\
\lesssim\int_{B(x,r)}\int_{0}^{r}|\beta(y^{\prime},Kt^{\prime})|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\,dy\lesssim\int_{B(x,r)}\int_{0}^{r}|\beta(y,K^{2}t)|^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}\,dy\lesssim\epsilon\,r^{n-1},

where we have used (
3.28
) in the last inequality. Using again the fact that
\Upsilon
is bi-Lipschitz, we obtain that

|I_{1}|\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)

as desired. Step 2 follows.

Step 3: proof of (iii).
The computations are similar to Step 2, although more involved. Morally, we will proceed as in Step 2, but for all the coordinates instead just for
v_{n}
. The terms that we can control by Carleson measures at the ‘first derivative’ level will be put in
\mathbf{C}
. The others will be placed in
\mathbf{B}
and we shall differentiate them an additional time to get the control in terms of Carleson measure. We have

\displaystyle\nabla\lambda(X)=

\displaystyle(\nabla t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(\nabla y)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\nabla t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\big(\nabla y-I_{n\times(n-1)}+(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\big(I_{n\times(n-1)}-(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz\right)=:J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}.

We write
\mathbf{C}:=J_{1}+J_{2}
, and
\mathbf{B}:=J_{3}
. The control on
\mathbf{C}
is like in Step 2: specifically,
J_{1}
can be treated like
I_{1}
,
J_{2}
is treated like
I_{2}
(using, instead of
v_{n}(\nabla y)\in\CMs(CM)
like for
I_{2}
, the Carleson estimate (
3.24
)).

For the
L^{\infty}
-bound on
\mathbf{B}
, we observe that

0=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\big(I_{n\times(n-1)}-(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\,dz,

and so

\mathbf{B}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\big(I_{n\times(n-1)}-(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big)\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)[h(z)-a^{\prime}]\,dz\quad\text{for any }a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then the estimate
\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{M}\epsilon
is a simpler variant of the
L^{\infty}
-bound of
\nabla\lambda
treated in Step 1. Moreover, observe that

v_{n}\big(I_{n\times(n-1)}-(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})\big)=\pi(v_{n})-v_{n}(v_{n})^{T}\pi(v_{n})=0,

which entails that
v_{n}\mathbf{B}=0
.

We want now to derive a Carleson measure estimate for
\nabla\mathbf{B}
. Let
\{\mathbf{b}_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
be the
n
rows of
\mathbf{B}
, and we want thus to study the quantities
\nabla\mathbf{b}_{j}
. Write
v_{n}=:\big((v_{n})_{1},\dots,(v_{n})_{n}\big)
and let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
be the Cartesian orthonormal basis. Then

\mathbf{b}_{j}(X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}h(z)\Big<\pi(e_{j}-(v_{n})_{j}v_{n}),\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)\Big>\,dz.

Differentiating in
X_{i}
, we have by the chain rule that

|\partial_{i}\mathbf{b}_{j}(X)|\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz\right||\pi(e_{j}-(v_{n})_{j}v_{n})||\partial_{i}t|\\
+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}^{2}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz\right||\pi(e_{j}-(v_{n})_{j}v_{n})||\nabla y|\\
+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)h(z)\,dz\right||\partial_{i}[(v_{n})_{j}v_{n}]|=:K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3}.

The term
K_{3}
is simple: we have that

K_{3}=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)[h(z)-a]\,dz\right||\partial_{i}[(v_{n})_{j}v_{n}]|\quad\text{for any }a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and we can prove that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}[h(z)-a]\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)\,dz\right|\lesssim\epsilon,

with the same strategy as in Step 1, and (
3.20
) gives that
t\,\partial_{i}[(v_{n})_{j}v_{n}]\in\CMs(CM)
. Hence
t|K_{3}|\in\CMs(CM\epsilon)
as desired. For the two other terms
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, observe that
\|\pi(e_{j}-(v_{n})_{j}v_{n})\|_{\infty}\leq 2
and

\|\partial_{i}t\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla y\|_{\infty}\lesssim(1+M)

by (
3.25
). So proving that
t\left(K_{1}+K_{2}\right)\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)
reduces to proving that
t|K_{11}|+t|K_{21}|\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)
, where

K_{11}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz

and

K_{12}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}^{2}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)h(z)\,dz.

The strategy is the same as the one used for
I_{11}
. Observe that

\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)=-\nabla_{z}\eta_{t}(y-z)\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}\eta_{t}\,dz=0.

Hence, by the divergence theorem, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any constant matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(a+zB)\nabla_{y}\partial_{t}\eta_{t}(y-z)\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(a+zB)\nabla^{2}_{y}\eta_{t}(y-z)\,dz=0,

and thus

|K_{11}|\leq\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}\partial_{t}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\big[h(z)-(a+zB)\big]\,dz\right|\\
\lesssim\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\fint_{B(y,t)}|h(z)-(a+zB)|\,dz=\frac{\beta(y,t)}{t}

and similarly

|K_{12}|\leq\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla_{y}^{2}\eta_{t}\big(y-z\big)\big[h(z)-(a+zB)\big]\,dz\right|\\
\lesssim\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\fint_{B(y,t)}|h(z)-(a+zB)|\,dz=\frac{\beta(y,t)}{t}.

Then we can argue as in Step 2 starting from (
3.28
) to obtain that
t|K_{11}|+t|K_{21}|\in\CMs(C_{M}\epsilon)
. The theorem follows.

\square

The lemma is obtained again with the Gram-Schmidt process. We define by induction

(2)

holds and that

Initial Step:
We have
\tilde{w}_{1}=\bar{w}_{1}
, and so

\left<\tilde{w}_{1},v_{1}\right>=1+\left<v_{1}\mathbf{B},v_{1}\right>\geq 1-C\epsilon

Altogether,

\big||\left<w_{1},v_{1}\right>|-1\big|\leq\left|\frac{|\left<\tilde{w}_{1},v_{1}\right>|-1}{|\tilde{w}_{1}|}\right|+\left|\frac{|\tilde{w}_{1}|-1}{|\tilde{w}_{1}|}\right|\lesssim\epsilon

as long as

is chosen small enough such that

. Since

is a unit vector and

is an orthonormal base, the fact that

The case

Induction step:

by (iii) of Theorem

The induction step and the lemma follows.

\rho
is locally invertible at any point. This implies the degree of
\rho
defined as

N_{\rho}(\mathcal{Z}):=\#\{X\in\Omega_{0},\,\rho(X)=\mathcal{Z}\}

is locally constant (positive, integer, not necessarily finite). Therefore

is constant on connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\partial\Omega
.

We want to show that

on
\Omega
and
N_{\rho}\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
.
One strategy is to construct the family of bi-Lipschitz changes of variables

One can see from our explicit construction that
\rho=\rho_{\Phi}
is continuous in
\Phi
, so
\rho_{\Phi_{\theta}}
is continuous in
\theta
, and eventually that

is continuous in
\theta
. Since
\Phi_{0}
and then
\rho_{0}
are the identity maps, we have that

0&\text{ if }X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{0}}.\end{array}\right.

The theorem follows.

\square

) is equivalent to

(B.3)

\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Phi^{-1}(\Omega\cap B_{1/2}))}\leq C\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Phi^{-1}(\Omega\cap B_{1}))}.

But now
\Phi^{-1}(\Omega\cap B_{1})=\{(x,t):t>\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
. By assumption, the part
\{(x,t):t=\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
is convex. The gradient estimate (
B.3
) over convex boundaries is more or less well-known; see
[
GT83
, Chapter 14.2]
for elliptic operator in nondivergence form. We provide a proof for our elliptic operator in divergence form for the reader’s convenience.

Up to a translation and rotation, we can assume that
\Phi(0)=0
,
\phi(0)=0
, and by the convexity of
\phi
, that
\phi(x)\geq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
We claim: for
t>0
and
(0,t)\in\Phi^{-1}(B_{3/4})\subset\subset\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
, we have

(B.4)

|v(0,t)|\leq Ct\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Phi^{-1}(\Omega\cap B_{1}))}

This is proved by the comparison principle. We construct a barrier function in the following way. Let
M=\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Phi^{-1}(\Omega\cap B_{1}))}
. Let
w
be a barrier function satisfying
L_{\Phi}(w)=0
in
\{t>0\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
,
w=0
on
\{t=0\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
and
w=M
on
\{t>0\}\cap\partial\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
. Clearly,
0\leq w\leq M
in
\{t>0\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(\Phi)
. The key fact we will use from the convexity of
\phi(x)
is that
\{t>\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})\subset\{t>0\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
(due to the fact
\phi(x)\geq 0
). Therefore, we have
|v|=0\leq w
on the convex part of the boundary
\{t=\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
and
|v|\leq M=w
on the rest of the boundary
\{t>\phi(x)\}\cap\partial\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
. By the comparison principle, we have
|v|\leq w
in
\{t>\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1})
. By the Schauder estimate of
w
over a flat boundary
\{t=0\}
, we have
|w(x,t)|\leq CtM
for any
(x,t)\in\{t>0\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{3/4})
. This particularly implies (
B.4
).

Finally, by applying the previous argument for (
B.4
) to all the boundary points on
\{t=\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{3/4})
, we actually have
|v(X)|\leq C\delta(X)M
, where
\delta(X)
is the distance from
X
to the boundary
\{t=\phi(x)\}
. Hence, by the interior Schauder estimate, for any
X\in\{t>\phi(x)\}\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{1/2})
, we have

|\nabla v(X)|\leq C\delta(X)^{-1}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(B(X,c\delta(X)))}\leq CM.

This ends the proof of (
B.3
).
∎

Remark B.5
.

The previous lemma (and thus the next theorem) is also valid if
\Phi
is a
C^{1,{\rm Dini}}
-type diffeomorphism, thanks to the well-known gradient estimate for elliptic operators with Dini continuous coefficients; see
[
Lie86
]
. However, the argument does not work if
\Phi
is merely
C^{1}
, due to the classical failure of the boundedness of gradient for the elliptic operator
L_{\Phi}=-\diver(A_{\Phi}\nabla)
with continuous coefficients.

Theorem B.6
.

Let
\Omega
be either a bounded Lipschitz domain, or an unbounded Lipschitz graph domain in a form of
\Omega=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:t>\phi_{0}(x)+\phi_{1}(x)\right\}
, where
\phi_{0}
is a convex function and
\|\nabla\phi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}+\|\phi_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}+\|\nabla\phi_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}\leq M
. Suppose that there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for any
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, there exists a coordinate basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
, an
M
-Lipschitz convex function
g_{x_{0}}
and a
C^{1,\alpha}
diffeomporphism
\Phi_{x_{0}}:\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\nabla\Phi_{x_{0}}|+|\nabla\Phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}|\leq M
such that

\Omega\cap B(x_{0},r_{0})=\Phi_{x_{0}}\Big(\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},\,t>g_{x_{0}}(x)\}\Big)\cap B(x_{0},r_{0}).

Then
(D)_{p}
for
-\Delta
is solvable for all
1<p<\infty
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Case 1:
when
\Omega
is an unbounded Lipschitz graph domain. It suffices to prove that for any
q>1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(B.7)

\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta(X,r))}\int_{\Delta(X,r)}k(y)^{q}d\sigma(y)\right)^{1/q}\leq\frac{C}{\sigma(\Delta(X,r))}\int_{\Delta(X,r)}k(y)d\sigma(y)=C\frac{\omega(\Delta(X,r))}{\sigma(X,r)}

for all
X\in\partial\Omega
,
r>0
, where
k(y)=\frac{d\omega}{d\sigma}(y)
and
\omega
is the harmonic measure with pole at infinity (see Lemma
2.5
).

Since
\phi
is Lipschitz with constant at most
M
, for any
y=(y^{\prime},\phi(y^{\prime}))\in\Delta(X,r)
, we have

k(y)=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\omega(\Delta(y,s))}{\sigma(\Delta(y,s))}\lesssim\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{G(y^{\prime},s+\phi(y^{\prime}))}{s}

by Lemma
2.11
, where
G
is the Green function for
-\Delta
with pole at infinity. Apply Lemma
B.1
to
u=G
in
B(X,r_{0})\cap\Omega
, we get that (for
s>0
sufficiently small)

(B.8)

\frac{G(y^{\prime},s+\phi(y^{\prime}))}{s}\leq\frac{C\left\|G\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(X,r)\cap\Omega)}}{r},

for any
0<s<r\leq r_{0}
, where the constant
C
is independent of
X
and
r
. To extend the same estimate for
r>r_{0}
, we need a large-scale Lipschitz estimate, which has been established in
[
Zhu21
, Theorem 4.2]
if the boundary graph is a “convex + bounded” function (i.e.,
\partial\Omega=\{t=\phi_{0}(x)+\phi_{1}(x)\}
), and in our case can be formed as

\left\|G\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(X,r_{0})\cap\Omega)}\leq\frac{C\left\|G\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(X,r)\cap\Omega)}}{r}

for all
r>r_{0}
. Combing the local estimate with the large-scale Lipschitz estimate, we obtain (
B.8
) for all
r>s>0
.
By the Bourgain estimate (see e.g.
[
JK82a
, Lemma 4.4]
), one has
\left\|G\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(X,2r)\cap\Omega)}\leq G(A_{X,r})
, where
A_{X,r}
is a corkscrew point relative to
X
and
r
, that is,
A_{X,r}\in B(X,Mr)\cap\Omega
satisfies
\dist(A_{X,r},\partial\Omega)\approx|A_{X,r}-X|\approx r
. By Lemma
2.11
again, we have that
\frac{G(A_{X,r})}{r}\approx\frac{\omega(\Delta(X,r))}{\sigma(X,r)}
. Putting everything together, one has

\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta(X,r))}\int_{\Delta(X,r)}k(y)^{q}d\sigma(y)\right)^{1/q}\leq\frac{C\left\|G\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(X,2r)\cap\Omega)}}{r}\leq C\frac{\omega(\Delta(X,r))}{\sigma(X,r)}

as desired.

Case 2:
when
\Omega
is bounded. It suffices to show that for any
q>1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(B.9)

\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta(X,r))}\int_{\Delta(X,r)}k^{X_{0}}(y)^{q}d\sigma(y)\right)^{1/q}\leq\frac{C}{\sigma(\Delta(X,r))}\int_{\Delta(X,r)}k^{X_{0}}(y)d\sigma(y)=C\frac{\omega^{X_{0}}(\Delta(X,r))}{\sigma(X,r)}

for all
X\in\partial\Omega
,
0<r<\diam(\Omega)
, where
X_{0}\in\Omega
is a point satisfying
\dist(X_{0},\partial\Omega)\approx\diam(\Omega)
,
k^{X_{0}}(y)=\frac{d\omega^{X_{0}}}{d\sigma}(y)
, and
\omega^{X_{0}}
is the harmonic measure with pole
X_{0}
. By definition, there is
0<r_{0}<\diam(\Omega)
such that for any
X\in\partial\Omega
,

\Omega\cap B(X,r_{0})=\Phi_{X}(\left\{(x,t)\in B(X,r_{0}):t>g_{X}(x)\right\})

where
g_{X}
is convex with Lipschitz constant at most
M
. By a covering argument, it suffices to show (
B.9
) for
0<r<\min\left\{r_{0}/4,\dist(X_{0},\partial\Omega)/4\right\}
. Since for any
X\in\partial\Omega
and
0<r<\min\left\{r_{0}/4,\dist(X_{0},\partial\Omega)/4\right\}
,
G(\cdot,X_{0})
is a solution to
-\Delta u=0
in
B(X,2r)\cap\Omega
with
G(\cdot,X_{0})=0
on
\partial\Omega
, one can apply (
B.1
) to
G(\cdot,X_{0})
in
B(X,r)\cap\Omega
and the rest of the argument goes as in Case 1.
∎

Remark B.10
.

In the case of an unbounded Lipschitz graph domain considered in Theorem
B.6
, if
\phi_{1}=0
, then
\Omega
is convex. Then
\Phi_{x_{0}}
can be taken as identity transform and
g_{x_{0}}(x)=\phi_{0}(x)
for any
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.

Theorem B.11
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem (

Regularity problem
(R)_{p}

for any
1<p<\infty
.
