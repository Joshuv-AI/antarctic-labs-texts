A Superselection Rule for Quantum Causality
Issam Ibnouhsein

Abstract
We formalize the independent frame choice on connections between local laboratories as a symmetry principle for indefinite causal order. This induces a superselection rule that partitions process space into distinct sectors and leads to our main result: every bipartite covariant process—including marginals of multipartite circuits or reductions across cuts—is causally separable. Because covariant processes cannot be converted into noncovariant ones, all circuit-embeddable dynamics, such as the quantum switch, are forbidden from violating bipartite causal inequalities, even asymptotically.



Introduction.—
Quantum theory allows for indefinite causal order (ICO), where correlations are incompatible with any pre-defined causal structure. The process-matrix (PM) framework
Oreshkov et al. 2012
provides a rigorous formalism for these dynamics, defining a vast space of mathematically consistent possibilities. A central challenge is to identify which of these possibilities correspond to physical reality, as exemplified by a long-standing puzzle. The quantum switch (QS)
Chiribella et al. 2013
is a physically realized process with ICO
Procopio et al. 2015
;
Rubino et al. 2017
;
Goswami et al. 2018
, yet its observable correlations are reproducible by a classical mixture of definite orders
Purves and Short 2021
, so it cannot violate
any
causal inequality
Araújo et al. 2015
. By contrast, the Oreshkov-Costa-Brukner (OCB) process
Oreshkov et al. 2012
was designed to produce nonclassical correlations, violating a causal inequality—the definitive device-independent witness of ICO—but has never been observed. This stark contrast between experimental accessibility and nonclassical causal power motivates our search for a deeper structural principle.

Previous work has sought to delineate physical processes through diverse constraints. No-go results obtained from the purification postulate
Araújo et al. 2017
, causal box embeddings
Portmann et al. 2017
, and realizability within standard quantum circuits—that is, circuits built from local operations on distinct systems connected by independent quantum channels, as formalized by the quantum comb framework
Chiribella et al. 2009
—all preclude causal inequality violations. Nevertheless, tripartite unitary extensions on time-delocalized subsystems have been shown to enable such violations
Oreshkov 2019
;
Wechs et al. 2019
;
Wechs et al. 2023
. From a spacetime perspective, related no-go theorems argue that ICO either requires nonlocalized systems or collapses to definite order at finer scales
Barrett et al. 2021
;
Vilasini and Renner 2024
. While these approaches clarify the resource requirements for violating causal inequalities, a unified framework for characterizing which processes are genuinely physical remains elusive.

We propose a new guiding principle, inspired by the resource-theoretic treatment of reference frames
Bartlett et al. 2007
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2014
. There, the absence of a shared frame is elevated to a symmetry constraint, inducing a superselection rule that governs quantum information processing. We extend this methodology to the causal domain, focusing on a core operational feature of quantum theory: the notion of a
local laboratory
—an abstract agent performing an operation, whether a party in a Bell test or a gate in a circuit. In such settings, a foundational property is the operational independence of distinct agents. We formalize one aspect of this independence as the gauge freedom on each physical connection, or “wire”, linking the laboratories, and elevate it to a symmetry principle we call
independent wire covariance
.

As with reference frames, this principle induces a superselection rule that partitions all valid processes into distinct sectors. Each sector is consistent with quantum mechanics, yet they are structurally inconvertible: no composition of free operations—defined here as
covariant
maps—can transform one sector into another, even asymptotically. In particular, the canonical free operations,
Local Operations and Shared Randomness
(LOSR), act entirely within each sector and cannot convert a covariant process into a noncovariant one.

We prove that any bipartite process respecting independent wire covariance is causally separable—a class that includes the bipartite marginals of all multipartite circuits, such as the QS. More generally, any reduction across a cut with independent crossing wires lies in the covariant sector and is therefore separable. This
cut-separability
result explains why no bipartite causal inequality violation has ever been observed in circuit dynamics and resolves the QS/OCB puzzle: the QS lies inside the covariant sector where violations are symmetry-forbidden, while the OCB process belongs to a noncovariant sector inaccessible to circuits. In this light, any violation of a bipartite causal inequality requires a symmetry-breaking mechanism that correlates local reference frames across distinct wires, a feature absent from standard laboratory models.

Process Matrices.—
A bipartite process matrix
W
is a positive semidefinite operator on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A_{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{O}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{O}}
, describing interactions between two local laboratories, Alice (
A
) and Bob (
B
), each with an input and an output system. For any choice of local quantum
instruments
—collections of completely positive maps

\{M_{a|x}^{A}\}
and
\{M_{b|y}^{B}\}
such that
\sum_{a}M_{a|x}^{A}
and
\sum_{b}M_{b|y}^{B}
are
completely positive and trace-preserving—the joint probability distribution is given
by the generalized Born rule:

p(a,b|x,y)=\Tr[W\,(M_{a|x}^{A}\otimes M_{b|y}^{B})].

(1)

The process matrix
W
must satisfy a set of linear constraints ensuring that this rule defines a valid probability distribution for all local instruments: positivity (
p(a,b|x,y)\geq 0
), normalization (
\sum_{a,b}p(a,b|x,y)=1
), and causality constraints forbidding signaling to the future
Oreshkov et al. 2012
;
Araújo et al. 2015
.

A process is
causally separable
if it can be written as a convex combination of processes with a definite causal order:

W_{\text{sep}}=p\,W_{A\prec B}+(1-p)W_{B\prec A}\quad(p\in[0,1]),

(2)

where
W_{A\prec B}
denotes a process where Alice’s operation must occur before Bob’s, and
W_{B\prec A}
denotes the reverse. The canonical
fixed-order processes
are

\displaystyle W_{A\prec B}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{A_{I}B_{O}}\otimes|\Phi^{+}\rangle\langle\Phi^{+}|_{A_{O}B_{I}},

(3)

\displaystyle W_{B\prec A}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{B_{I}A_{O}}\otimes|\Phi^{+}\rangle\langle\Phi^{+}|_{B_{O}A_{I}}.

Quantum switch.
—The QS implements a coherent control over the causal order in which Alice and Bob act on a target system. Its natural representation is tripartite, with a control qubit
C
that selects the order:

\displaystyle W_{\text{QS}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(

\displaystyle|0\rangle\langle 0|_{C}\otimes W_{A\prec B}+|1\rangle\langle 1|_{C}\otimes W_{B\prec A}

\displaystyle+|0\rangle\langle 1|_{C}\otimes W_{AB,BA}+|1\rangle\langle 0|_{C}\otimes W_{BA,AB}\Big).

(4)

The first two terms correspond to definite orders conditioned on the control state,
while the off-diagonal terms
W_{AB,BA}
and
W_{BA,AB}
encode the coherence between them.
Tracing out the control yields the bipartite marginal

W_{\mathrm{marg}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(W_{A\prec B}+W_{B\prec A})
, which is causally separable.
Thus, the indefinite order manifests only when the control qubit is retained as a coherent degree of freedom. The QS cannot violate any causal inequality
Araújo et al. 2015
, and has been demonstrated experimentally on several platforms
Procopio et al. 2015
;
Rubino et al. 2017
;
Goswami et al. 2018
.

OCB process.
—The OCB process provides a bipartite example of indefinite order that violates a causal inequality
Oreshkov et al. 2012
. In the qubit case we adopt:

W_{\text{OCB}}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(Z_{A_{O}}Z_{B_{I}}+Z_{A_{I}}X_{B_{I}}Z_{B_{O}}\right)\right),

(5)

where
X
and
Z
are the standard Pauli matrices, and identities on remaining subspaces are implicit. Despite intense interest,
W_{\text{OCB}}
has no experimental realization to date.

This contrast between causal indefiniteness with and without causal inequality violation motivates the search for a structural principle that separates the two classes of processes, developed below.

Symmetry Framework.—
We analyze the structure of the process space by considering unitary symmetries that reflect operational invariances. For a group
G
acting on the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
via a unitary representation
\{U_{g}\}_{g\in G}
, the action on a process
W
is
W\mapsto U_{g}WU_{g}^{\dagger}
. This action is physically well-founded because each unitary
U_{g}
acts as an automorphism of the set of valid processes, preserving positivity and normalization constraints.

The key analytical tool is the
twirl
, a projection onto the subspace of
G
-invariant operators:

\mathcal{T}_{G}(X)=\int_{G}U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger}dg.

(6)

Since the twirl is a convex combination of these automorphisms, it necessarily maps the set of valid processes to itself. Operationally, this projection is a group average. As established in the study of quantum reference frames
Bartlett et al. 2007
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2014
, this leads to a dramatic simplification: the capabilities of all symmetry-respecting operations can be understood by studying the covariant sector alone (see Supplemental Material (SM)
SM
, Sec. I).

This restriction establishes a superselection rule: a process that is
G
-invariant cannot be converted by any covariant protocol into a process with components outside the covariant sector.
This rule can be certified by quantitative
witnesses
. Any observable
M
can be decomposed into its invariant and non-invariant parts,

M=M_{G}+M_{\perp},\qquad M_{G}:=\mathcal{T}_{G}(M),\quad\mathcal{T}_{G}(M_{\perp})=0.

(7)

For any process
W
we define the
noncovariance witness

\mathcal{N}_{M_{\perp}}(W):=|\Tr(M_{\perp}W)|,

(8)

a quantity that vanishes for all covariant processes. Every linear score functional
F_{M}(W)=\Tr[MW]
decomposes as

F_{M}(W)=\Tr[M_{G}W]+\Tr[M_{\perp}W]\leq B_{G}+\mathcal{N}_{M_{\perp}}(W),

(9)

where
B_{G}:=\max_{W_{\text{sep}}}\Tr[M_{G}W]
is the
covariant benchmark
.
Exceeding
B_{G}
thus directly certifies
\mathcal{N}_{M_{\perp}}(W)>0
,
i.e., a nonzero symmetry-breaking resource.
Accordingly, any causal inequality violation is a device-independent witness of symmetry breaking.

Independent Wire Covariance.—
Our guiding principle is to elevate the operational independence of local laboratories—manifested in quantum circuits as the independence of the “wires” linking their operations—into a fundamental symmetry of the process-matrix space.

In a standard quantum circuit, each wire connecting
d
-dimensional systems is modeled as an identity channel whose Choi state is the maximally entangled projector
\Pi:=|\Phi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\Phi^{+}|
with
|\Phi^{+}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle
. Since local basis choice is a gauge freedom, the physical description must be invariant under local frame changes
V\otimes V^{*}
where
V\in U(d)
. This invariance is enforced by the twirl

\mathcal{T}(X):=\int_{U(d)}(V\otimes V^{*})\,X\,(V^{\dagger}\otimes V^{*\dagger})\,dV,

(10)

which projects any operator
X
onto the commutant of
V\mapsto V\otimes V^{*}
. By Schur–Weyl duality, this commutant is the two-dimensional algebra spanned by
\{\Pi,\Pi^{\perp}:=\mathbb{I}-\Pi\}
. For multiple wires, the full invariant algebra is their tensor product, so any circuit-like dynamics belongs to this covariant sector.

Figure 1:
Independent wire covariance.
A bipartite process
W
connects Alice (
M^{A}
) and Bob (
M^{B}
) through two abstract, independent “wires”. Wire 1 (red,
A_{O}\!\to\!B_{I}
) is invariant under local frame changes
V_{1}\otimes V_{1}^{*}
, and Wire 2 (blue,
B_{O}\!\to\!A_{I}
) is independently invariant under
V_{2}\otimes V_{2}^{*}
. Processes in this covariant sector are causally separable and cannot violate bipartite causal inequalities. Violations (e.g., by the OCB process) require breaking this factorized symmetry by correlating the local reference frames of the two wires.

We now extend this principle from circuit wires to the abstract Hilbert space structure of any bipartite process with local dimension
d
. The connections
A_{O}\!\to\!B_{I}
and
B_{O}\!\to\!A_{I}
are treated as two independent abstract wires, each with symmetry group
U(d)
acting as
V\otimes V^{*}
. The total symmetry group is therefore
G_{\text{wire}}=U(d)\times U(d)
, represented on
\mathcal{H}
by

U_{V_{1},V_{2}}=V_{1,A_{O}}\otimes V^{*}_{1,B_{I}}\otimes V_{2,B_{O}}\otimes V^{*}_{2,A_{I}},

(11)

with action
W\mapsto U_{V_{1},V_{2}}WU_{V_{1},V_{2}}^{\dagger}
.
The corresponding projection is the
independent wire twirl
,

\mathcal{T}_{\text{wire}}(W):=\int_{U(d)\times U(d)}U_{V_{1},V_{2}}\,W\,U_{V_{1},V_{2}}^{\dagger}\,dV_{1}dV_{2},

(12)

whose image
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{cov}}
is a four-dimensional algebra generated by tensor products of the single-wire projectors
\{\Pi,\Pi^{\perp}\}
, reflecting the two irreducible representations (symmetric/antisymmetric) per wire (see SM
SM
, Sec. II).

This formal structure provides a genuine superselection principle for quantum causality: it partitions the process space into distinct symmetry sectors that cannot be connected by covariant operations (including LOSR).

Main Result.—
For arbitrary local dimension
d
, any bipartite process
W
that is independently wire-covariant,
i.e.

\mathcal{T}_{\text{wire}}(W)=W
, is causally separable.

Proof.
The argument has three steps. First, the wire twirl restricts the space of processes to a 4D algebra with basis

\{\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2},\ \Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp},\ \Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2},\ \Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}\}
.
Any
W
in this space has the expansion

\displaystyle W={}

\displaystyle\delta(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\beta(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp})

(13)

\displaystyle+\gamma(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\alpha(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}),

and, since these projectors are mutually orthogonal, the positivity constraint of the process-matrix framework implies
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\geq 0
.

Second, imposing the normalization and causality constraints reduces the coefficients to satisfy

\alpha+\delta=\beta+\gamma,\qquad\delta+\alpha(d^{2}-1)=1.

(14)

The feasible set is therefore the intersection of the positive orthant with this 2D affine plane, i.e. a compact convex polygon.

In two dimensions, a vertex must saturate at least two positivity constraints.
Substituting the above equalities shows that the only consistent solutions are obtained at

(\alpha,\delta)=(0,1)
or
(\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1},0)
, combined with either
\beta=0
or
\gamma=0
.
This yields exactly four vertices, corresponding to the processes

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{1}\otimes\mathbb{I},

\displaystyle\quad W_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\Pi_{2},

(15)

\displaystyle W_{3}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{I},

\displaystyle\quad W_{4}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}\mathbb{I}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}.

Each vertex satisfies the conditions for a definite causal order (
A\prec B
or
B\prec A
).
Since every point of the polygon is a convex mixture of its vertices, every covariant process is causally separable.
\square

Corollary (Cut Separability).
The bipartite marginal of any process realizable in a standard circuit is causally separable. More generally, the reduced process across any bipartition is separable whenever the crossing wires are independently covariant.

This general result provides a symmetry-based explanation of the QS/OCB puzzle, revealing the structural reason for the gap between causal nonseparability and causal inequality violation. The independent wire twirl confines all circuit-like dynamics to the covariant sector
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{cov}}
, so the QS remains strictly within this sector. Since every covariant process is causally separable, the OCB process—which is not—must lie outside, in a symmetry-breaking sector, and cannot be reached from the QS even asymptotically.

Illustration: OCB Witness.—

As an application of the symmetry framework, we examine the OCB causal game
Oreshkov et al. 2012
. Its score functional, operationally equivalent to the success probability in the original formulation
Araújo et al. 2015
, is

F_{\text{OCB}}(W):=\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\Tr[M_{\text{OCB}}W],\quad M_{\text{OCB}}=M_{1}+M_{2},

(16)

where
M_{1}=\mathbb{I}_{A_{I}}\otimes Z_{A_{O}}\otimes Z_{B_{I}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B_{O}}

and
M_{2}=Z_{A_{I}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{O}}\otimes X_{B_{I}}\otimes Z_{B_{O}}
.

Applying the wire twirl to each component reveals their different behaviors:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{wire}}(M_{1})\neq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{T}_{\text{wire}}(M_{2})=0,

(17)

so
M_{1}
plays the role of
M_{G}
and
M_{2}
of
M_{\perp}
in our decomposition. This yields the witness expression:

F_{\text{OCB}}(W)=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\Tr[M_{1}W]}_{\text{Covariant Benchmark}}+\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\Tr[M_{2}W]}_{\text{Resource Witness}}.

(18)

The covariant benchmark term yields at most
1/\sqrt{2}
for all causally separable processes (see SM
SM
, Sec. VI), while the witness term vanishes within the covariant sector. Hence
F_{\text{OCB}}(W)\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
for any separable process, establishing the causal bound. For the OCB process, by contrast, a direct calculation gives a benchmark contribution of
1/2
and a witness value of
\mathcal{N}_{M_{2}}(W_{\rm OCB})=1/2
, yielding the total score
F_{\text{OCB}}(W_{\rm OCB})=1
. The causal inequality violation is therefore traced directly to the presence of a nonzero symmetry-breaking resource.

Implications.—
Independent wire covariance provides a symmetry-based lens on bipartite quantum dynamics, partitioning the process space into distinct sectors with sharply different operational capabilities.

The covariant sector contains all processes respecting this symmetry, including all circuit-embeddable ones. Our theorem shows that every such process is causally separable and cannot violate any bipartite causal inequality, capturing exactly the dynamics accessible to operationally independent agents.

The noncovariant sectors, in contrast, host exotic causal structures such as the OCB process. Their correlations effectively “lock” the reference frames of the output–input connections between laboratories, thereby violating operational independence. Accessing such sectors requires introducing a resource that breaks wire covariance by correlating local reference frames, beyond what independent laboratories provide.

Various mechanisms have been identified that could supply the needed symmetry-breaking resource: a shared physical environment can supply a common phase reference that locks local bases; post-selection can condition on outcomes that retrospectively impose correlations
Lloyd et al. 2011
; time-delocalized subsystems can collapse multiple ports onto a single physical carrier
Wechs et al. 2023
; and in quantum gravity, a common spacetime or clock reference may play the same role
Zych et al. 2019
. This asymmetry is not merely a mathematical artefact but constitutes an operational resource
Guérin et al. 2016
, much as entanglement provides nonlocal advantages and is captured within a symmetry–resource framework
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Bartlett et al. 2007
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2014
.

While our proof applies to the bipartite case (including all cuts of multipartite processes), every known proposal for nonclassical causal correlations operates by breaking independent wire covariance. This motivates the conjecture that any causal inequality violation requires the breaking of some natural operational symmetry reflecting laboratory independence.

Symmetry thus offers a unified picture in which indefinite causal order appears as one of the quantum resources arising from its violation, with ultimate limits plausibly set by information-theoretic principles constraining asymmetric correlations.

Conclusion.—
By promoting the independent frame choice of local laboratories—manifested in standard circuits—to a fundamental symmetry principle, we derived a superselection rule that partitions the process space. Our main theorem shows that the entire bipartite covariant sector is causally separable. Hence every bipartite marginal or cut of a standard circuit lies within this sector, and violations of causal inequalities are excluded. The obstruction to nonclassical correlations is precisely the lack of a resource that aligns local reference frames, a possibility precluded by the operational independence of standard laboratories.

This perspective reframes the study of indefinite causal order, shifting the focus from listing examples of causal nonseparability to analyzing the symmetries of operational setups. Our results show that this method offers a systematic way to delineate the boundaries of physical dynamics in the causal domain. This points to a broader program: identifying further foundational symmetries, deriving their superselection rules, and charting the space of processes beyond definite causal structure.

S2
Symmetry, Superselection Rules, and Resource Witnesses

Task symmetry.

Let
G
be a compact symmetry group acting unitarily on the total Hilbert space via
U_{g}
.
A task is
G
-invariant if its score functional
F_{M}(W)=\Tr[MW]
satisfies
M=\mathcal{T}_{G}(M)
, so that
F_{M}(U_{g}WU_{g}^{\dagger})=F_{M}(W)
for all
g\in G
.

The admissible operations are assumed to be closed under group conjugation and convex mixing. We call an operation covariant if it commutes with the twirl, i.e.
\Lambda\circ\mathcal{T}_{G}=\mathcal{T}_{G}\circ\Lambda
.

Symmetrization lemma.

For any admissible operation
\Lambda
, define its group average

\Lambda_{c}:=\int_{G}U_{g}\,\Lambda\!\big(U_{g}^{\dagger}(\,\cdot\,)U_{g}\big)\,U_{g}^{\dagger}\,dg=\mathcal{T}_{G}\circ\Lambda\circ\mathcal{T}_{G}.

(S1)

Then
\Lambda_{c}
is covariant and admissible. Moreover:

(a)

If
W
is
G
-invariant, then
F_{M}(\Lambda_{c}(W))=F_{M}(\Lambda(W))
.

(b)

More generally, for any
G
-invariant task,

\sup_{\Lambda}F_{M}(\Lambda(W))=\sup_{\Lambda\ \text{covariant}}F_{M}(\Lambda(W)).

(S2)

Thus restricting to covariant operations is without loss of generality.

Point (a) follows immediately from
M=\mathcal{T}_{G}(M)
and the self-adjointness of
\mathcal{T}_{G}
.
Point (b) is the standard group-averaging argument from resource theories of asymmetry
Bartlett et al. 2007
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2014
.

Superselection rule.

If
W
is
G
-invariant, then for any covariant
\Lambda
,

\mathcal{T}_{G}(\Lambda(W))=\Lambda(\mathcal{T}_{G}(W))=\Lambda(W),

(S3)

so the invariant sector is closed under free operations: asymmetry cannot be created from symmetry.

Witness decomposition.

For any observable
M
, write
M=M_{G}+M_{\perp}
with
M_{G}=\mathcal{T}_{G}(M)
and
\mathcal{T}_{G}(M_{\perp})=0
.
Then

F_{M}(W)=\Tr[MW]=\Tr[M_{G}W]+\Tr[M_{\perp}W].

(S4)

The second term,

\mathcal{N}_{M_{\perp}}(W):=|\Tr(M_{\perp}W)|,

(S5)

vanishes for all
G
-invariant processes and therefore serves as a
resource witness
of symmetry breaking.
Accordingly, any causal-inequality violation (a device-independent certification of ICO) certifies
\mathcal{N}_{M_{\perp}}(W)>0
.

Invariant Subspace of the Wire Twirl

The symmetry group is
G_{\text{wire}}=U(d)\times U(d)
with action

U_{V_{1},V_{2}}=V_{1,A_{O}}\otimes V_{1,B_{I}}^{*}\otimes V_{2,B_{O}}\otimes V_{2,A_{I}}^{*}.

(S6)

It factorizes across the two “wires”
(A_{O},B_{I})
and
(B_{O},A_{I})

On one wire, the commutant of
V\mapsto V\otimes V^{*}
is two-dimensional, spanned by

\{\mathbb{I},\Pi\}
, where
\Pi=|\Phi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\Phi^{+}|

Thus the invariant algebra on one wire is two-dimensional; on two wires it is
2\times 2=4

Any
W
invariant under the twirl has the expansion

W=\delta(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\beta(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp})+\gamma(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\alpha(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}),

(S7)

with
\Pi^{\perp}=\mathbb{I}-\Pi
.
This is the 4D algebra quoted in the Letter.

Single-wire twirl formula.

For later use, for any operator
X
on one wire,

\mathcal{T}(X)=\alpha(X)\,\mathbb{I}+\beta(X)\,\Pi,

(S8)

where the coefficients are

\alpha(X)=\frac{\Tr X-\Tr(\Pi X)}{d^{2}-1},\qquad\beta(X)=\frac{d\,\Tr(\Pi X)-\tfrac{1}{d}\Tr X}{d^{2}-1}.

Reduction Preserves Wire Covariance

Let
W_{ABR}
be a process on parties
A,B
and a remainder
R
.
For
U_{V_{1},V_{2}}
acting trivially on
R

\Tr_{R}\!\left(U_{V_{1},V_{2}}W_{ABR}U_{V_{1},V_{2}}^{\dagger}\right)=U_{V_{1},V_{2}}\,\Tr_{R}(W_{ABR})\,U_{V_{1},V_{2}}^{\dagger}.

S5
Main Theorem: Wire Covariance Implies Causal Separability

We now give a detailed proof that any independently wire-covariant bipartite process is causally separable.

S5.1
Setup and Parametrization

A bipartite process
W
acts on
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A_{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{O}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{O}}
.
Group wire 1 as
(A_{O},B_{I})
and wire 2 as
(B_{O},A_{I})
.
By Schur–Weyl duality, the commutant algebra on one wire is spanned by
\{\Pi,\Pi^{\perp}\}
, with
\Pi=|\Phi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\Phi^{+}|
and
\Pi^{\perp}=\mathbb{I}-\Pi
.
Hence the invariant algebra on two wires is 4D with basis

\{\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2},\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp},\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2},\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}\}
.

Thus every wire-covariant process can be expanded uniquely as

W=\delta\,(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\beta\,(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp})+\gamma\,(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\alpha\,(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp})\,.

(S12)

Because these four basis elements are pairwise orthogonal projectors (positive and mutually orthogonal), positivity of
W
implies

\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\geq 0.

(S13)

S5.2
Process-Matrix Causality Constraints

A valid process must satisfy three linear causality constraints (see
Oreshkov et al. 2012
;
Araújo et al. 2015
):

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{A}_{O})(I-\mathcal{B}_{O})(W)

\displaystyle=0,

(S14)

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{A}_{O})\,\mathcal{B}_{I}(W)

\displaystyle=0,

(S15)

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{B}_{O})\,\mathcal{A}_{I}(W)

\displaystyle=0.

(S16)

Here
\mathcal{R}_{Y}(X):=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{Y}}{d}\otimes\Tr_{Y}(X)
is the trace-and-replace map on subsystem
Y
,
and we use the standard shorthand

\mathcal{A}_{O}:=\mathcal{R}_{A_{O}},\quad\mathcal{B}_{O}:=\mathcal{R}_{B_{O}}\,,\quad\mathcal{A}_{I}:=\mathcal{R}_{A_{I}},\quad\mathcal{B}_{I}:=\mathcal{R}_{B_{I}},

each tensored with the identity on the complementary subsystems.

Lemma.
For

S5.4
Feasible Region and Vertices

The feasible set is the intersection of
(i) the positivity cone
\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\geq 0\}
(a closed convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
)
with (ii) the affine plane imposed by the two linear equalities (

As an intersection of convex sets, it is convex.
Moreover, (
S18

0\leq\delta\leq 1
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1/(d^{2}-1)
,
hence the intersection is bounded; being closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional space,
the feasible set is a compact, 2D convex polytope (a polygon).

To identify its vertices, note that in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
two independent equalities leave a 2D affine slice;
a vertex of the intersection with the nonnegative orthant must saturate at least two of the
nonnegativity constraints. The extremal values of (
S18
) are
\alpha=0
(giving
\delta=1
) and

\alpha=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}
(giving
\delta=0
). In each case, (
S5.2
) fixes
\beta+\gamma=\alpha+\delta
,
and vertices occur when one of
\beta
or
\gamma

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=(0,1,0,1)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle W=\Pi_{1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2},

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=(0,0,1,1)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle W=\mathbb{I}_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2},

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1},0,\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1},0\right)

\displaystyle W=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2},

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1},\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1},0,0\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle W=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}\mathbb{I}_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}.

S5.5
Verification of Definite Order

Recall that a bipartite process
W
has definite order
A\prec B
if

(I-\mathcal{B}_{O})(W)=0\quad\text{and}\quad(I-\mathcal{A}_{O})\mathcal{B}_{I}(W)=0,

(S20)

and analogously for
B\prec A
.
We now check these conditions for each of the four vertices identified above.

Vertex 1:

W=\Pi_{1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}

Here
\mathcal{B}_{O}(\mathbb{I}_{2})=\mathbb{I}_{2}
and
\mathcal{B}_{I}(\Pi_{1})=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{1}
, so both conditions hold.
This vertex represents a process where Alice’s output is transmitted to Bob through a maximally entangled channel, while Bob’s output is unconstrained.

Vertex 2:

W=\mathbb{I}_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}

By symmetry of the roles of
A
and
B
, the conditions hold for order
B\prec A
.
This corresponds to a process where Bob’s output is transmitted to Alice through a maximally entangled channel, while Alice’s output is unconstrained.

Vertex 3:

W=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}

We compute
\mathcal{B}_{I}(\Pi_{1}^{\perp})=\tfrac{d^{2}-1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{1}
, so after rescaling the same conditions as for Vertex 1 are satisfied.
This vertex represents a process with order
A\prec B
, where the transmission from Alice to Bob occurs through the orthogonal complement of the maximally entangled subspace.

Vertex 4:

W=\tfrac{1}{d^{2}-1}\mathbb{I}_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}

By symmetry, this satisfies the conditions for order
B\prec A
.
This vertex corresponds to a process with order
B\prec A
, where the transmission from Bob to Alice occurs through the orthogonal complement of the maximally entangled subspace.

In summary, each of the four vertices corresponds to a process of definite causal order (two realizing
A\prec B
, two realizing
B\prec A
).
By convexity, any
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{cov}}
is a convex mixture of these vertices and is therefore causally separable.

S6
Twirl Action on the OCB Witness

The OCB witness is
M_{\text{OCB}}=M_{1}+M_{2}
with

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{A_{I}}\otimes Z_{A_{O}}\otimes Z_{B_{I}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B_{O}},

(S21)

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=Z_{A_{I}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{O}}\otimes X_{B_{I}}\otimes Z_{B_{O}}.

Reordering into wire pairs gives

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=(Z_{A_{O}}\otimes Z_{B_{I}})\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{B_{O}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{I}}),

(S22)

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{A_{O}}\otimes X_{B_{I}})\otimes(Z_{B_{O}}\otimes Z_{A_{I}}).

Applying the single-wire twirl:

\mathcal{T}_{A_{O}B_{I}}(Z\otimes Z)\neq 0,\qquad\mathcal{T}_{B_{O}A_{I}}(\mathbb{I})=\mathbb{I},

so that

\mathcal{T}_{\text{wire}}(M_{1})\neq 0.

(S23)

On the other hand,

\mathcal{T}_{A_{O}B_{I}}(\mathbb{I}\otimes X)=0\qquad\text{since }\Tr[X]=\Tr[X^{T}]=0,

which implies

\mathcal{T}_{\text{wire}}(M_{2})=0.

(S24)

Thus
M_{1}
is identified as the covariant component
M_{G}
, while
M_{2}
is the noncovariant component
M_{\perp}
.

S7
Causal Bound on the OCB Functional

The OCB score is defined as

F_{\text{OCB}}(W)=\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\Tr[(M_{1}+M_{2})W],

(S25)

and the wire twirl leaves
M_{1}
invariant while annihilating
M_{2}
(Sec. V).
The covariant component is therefore
M_{G}=M_{1}
, and the covariant benchmark is defined as the optimal value of this contribution over all causally separable processes:

B_{G}:=\max_{W_{\mathrm{sep}}}\ \tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\,\Tr[M_{1}W_{\mathrm{sep}}].

(S26)

Reduction to the covariant sector.

Since
M_{1}
is
G
-invariant and the twirl
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{wire}}
is self-adjoint,

\Tr[M_{1}W_{\mathrm{sep}}]=\Tr[M_{1}\,\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{wire}}(W_{\mathrm{sep}})].

(S27)

Moreover, the twirl preserves causal separability (by invariance of the fixed-order constraints and convexity). Hence the optimization in (
S26
) can be restricted to
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{cov}}
:

B_{G}\leq\max_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{cov}}}\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\,\Tr[M_{1}W].

(S28)

Optimization.

For the qubit case
d=2
, an explicit optimization over
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{cov}}
gives the maximum value
1/\sqrt{2}
.
This can be seen by parametrizing

W=\delta\,(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\beta\,(\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp})+\gamma\,(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2})+\alpha\,(\Pi_{1}^{\perp}\otimes\Pi_{2}^{\perp}),

(S29)

for which a direct calculation gives

\Tr[M_{1}W]=4(\beta-\alpha),

(S30)

so that

F_{\text{OCB}}(W)=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}.

(S31)

From the process-matrix constraints we obtained

\alpha+\delta=\beta+\gamma,\qquad\delta+\alpha(d^{2}-1)=1.

(S32)

For
d=2
, these become

\alpha+\delta=\beta+\gamma,\qquad\delta+3\alpha=1.

(S33)

Together with positivity
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\geq 0
, the feasible set is a compact polygon in
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
-space.

Maximizing
\beta-\alpha
under these constraints gives the optimum
at
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)=(0,1,0,1)
, yielding

F_{\text{OCB}}^{\max}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

(S34)

which corresponds exactly to the definite-order vertex
W=\Pi_{1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}
.
Since this vertex is causally separable, the bound is tight and the inequality in (
S28
) is saturated. Therefore

B_{G}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(S35)

In summary, the covariant benchmark for the OCB functional equals
1/\sqrt{2}
.
Within the covariant sector the noncovariant contribution
\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\Tr[M_{2}W]
vanishes, so
F_{\text{OCB}}(W)\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
for all causally separable processes, and this bound is attained already by a definite-order vertex.

S8
Conclusion

We have provided full derivations supporting the Letter’s claims.

First, we established the symmetrization lemma, justifying the restriction to covariant operations, and derived the associated superselection rule forbidding the creation of asymmetry from symmetric resources. We introduced the witness decomposition
M=M_{G}+M_{\perp}
, showing that every score functional splits into a covariant benchmark and a symmetry-breaking term. Consequently, a nonzero value
\mathcal{N}_{M_{\perp}}(W)>0
is necessary to surpass the covariant benchmark. Thus, any causal inequality violation—a device-independent certification of indefinite causal order—directly witnesses the presence of this symmetry-breaking resource.

Second, we determined that the invariant algebra of the wire twirl is four-dimensional, proved that reductions preserve wire covariance, and derived the feasible region of wire-covariant processes as a two-dimensional convex polygon whose vertices correspond to definite causal orders.

Finally, we analyzed the OCB witness, decomposing it into covariant and noncovariant parts, and established the bound
F_{\text{OCB}}\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
for all causally separable processes.

Together, these results provide the technical foundation for the Letter’s symmetry-based separation between covariant and noncovariant sectors of quantum causality.
