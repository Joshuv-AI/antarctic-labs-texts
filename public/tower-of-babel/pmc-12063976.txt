Aortic atresia with complex aortic arch collateralization through the subclavian arteries
Authors: Saumya Kapoor, Thomas Knapp, Peace Madueme, Katherine T. Braley, Peter D. Wearden, Jennifer S. Nelson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA term newborn boy was diagnosed with aortic atresia and ventricular septal defect with duplicated subclavian arteries bilaterally. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated highly unusual aortic arch vasculature. A diminutive aortic arch and complex head vessel anatomy precluded standard surgical palliation with a Damus–Kaye–Stansel, arch reconstruction, and systemic to pulmonary artery shunt. Instead, the patient underwent placement of bilateral pulmonary artery bands and stenting of the patent arterial duct (PDA). At 2 months of age, the patient underwent PDA stent dilation. At 4 months of age, he underwent balloon angioplasty of pulmonary artery bands with transverse aortic stenting. At the most recent follow-up at 15 months of age, there was no significant growth of the aortic arch, and the patient was mildly hypertensive but otherwise hemodynamically stable. He is currently being evaluated for heart transplantation due to his complex vascular anatomy.

INTRODUCTION

A term newborn boy with prenatal diagnoses of aortic valve atresia, ventricular septal defect (VSD), and aortic arch hypoplasia was referred to our institution for evaluation and treatment. The patient’s complex arch and coronary anatomy made him a high-risk candidate for a traditional surgical approach to staged palliation, and an alternate approach was pursued. Herein, we present a hybrid staged palliation strategy for neonates with a diminutive aortic arch and complex collateralization through the subclavian arteries.

CLINICAL SUMMARY

Upon admission, an echocardiogram was performed and confirmed prenatal diagnoses, including functional aortic atresia with a severely hypoplastic aortic root, ascending and transverse aortic arch, and a large posterior malalignment VSD with a left-to-right shunt [Figure 1a–c]. The left ventricular size and function were normal, and the mitral valve was mildly hypoplastic (annulus Z-score of −2.5). The main and branch pulmonary arteries (PAs) were dilated, and a widely patent arterial duct (PDA) was observed. The whole genome chromosomal single-nucleotide polymorphism microarray analysis revealed no significant DNA copy number changes. A baseline computed tomography angiography (CTA) scan was performed. The CTA revealed a diminutive aortic arch. The ascending aorta measured 2.7 mm in diameter, the transverse aortic arch measured 2.0 mm, and both the right and left subclavian arteries were found to form prominent collaterals that coursed caudally and connected with the medial and posterior aspects of the descending aorta, respectively [Video 1 and Figure 2a]. The left coronary artery origin was usual, from the left sinus of Valsalva. The right coronary artery (RCA) origin initially appeared to be atretic with a proximal RCA fistulous connection to the right pulmonary artery [Video 2 and Figure 2b]. This vascular anatomy implied coronary and cerebral perfusion from retrograde flow through the PDA and the two large collaterals. A surgical stage 1 Norwood procedure with Damus-Kaye-Stansel (DKS), arch reconstruction, and a right ventricle to pulmonary artery shunt was initially planned, with an alternate plan of hybrid palliation if needed.

Upon visualization of the anatomy intraoperatively, the ascending aorta and aortic arch were confirmed to be diminutive, and the isthmus was nearly atretic. Instead, the decision was made to perform banding of the right and left PAs with anticipated stenting of the PDA, and informed consent for those procedures was obtained. PA bands were secured with fine polypropylene sutures rather than titanium clips to allow for future balloon angioplasty. The PDA was successfully stented with an 8 mm × 26 mm VALEO® stent (Bard Inc., New Providence, NJ, USA), after which the patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged home on dual antiplatelet therapy and followed closely by the high-risk cardiology clinic.

After discharge, the patient was readmitted at 2 months of age with severely decreased right ventricular systolic function and PDA stent narrowing, for which he received an additional, more proximally placed PDA stent and medical treatment with digoxin and furosemide. His right ventricular systolic function subsequently recovered. At approximately 4 months of age, he was readmitted for oxygen desaturations with feeds. He underwent balloon angioplasty of the PA bands and PDA stent and stent placement in the transverse aortic arch to address a coarctation at the severely hypoplastic transverse arch causing retrograde obstruction. He was discharged home on clopidogrel and continued his digoxin and furosemide regimen. At the latest clinic follow-up at 15 months of age, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and is currently being evaluated for heart transplantation.

DISCUSSION

This patient was born with a rare constellation of vascular anatomical considerations that presented a significant surgical challenge. Although the standard approach to aortic atresia with hypoplastic aortic arch would involve a DKS and arch reconstruction, the diminutive size of this patient’s aorta was felt to be prohibitive, and the complex vascular anatomy visualized intraoperatively precluded typical (Norwood type) aortic arch patch augmentation. An interrupted aortic arch-style repair was briefly discussed, but given the great distance between the ascending and descending aorta, this approach was thought to require an interposition graft, which was undesirable. Another option could have been to replace the ductal arch with an interposition graft from the distal main PA to the proximal descending aorta to incorporate the collaterals and to provide an RV to PA shunt for a source of pulmonary blood flow. That approach would have resulted in a similar physiology to the hybrid approach (PDA stent and bilateral PABs) that was utilized, but it would have required cardiopulmonary bypass, hypothermic circulatory arrest, a ventriculotomy for the proximal RV to PA shunt anastomosis, and risked injury to or distortion of the hypoplastic aortic arch and head vessels. Based on the complex arch anatomy with diminutive size, the decision was made to perform banding of the right and left PAs with stenting of the PDA instead. Subsequent cardiac catheterization confirmed the presence of large collaterals between both subclavian arteries and the descending aorta. In addition, aortic root injection revealed antegrade filling of both right and left coronary arteries and a suspected small fistula between the RCA and right pulmonary artery, with anatomy better appreciated on CTA [Video 2, playback of axial images demonstrates arch and coronary anatomy].

To summarize, in a neonate with diminutive aortic arch and collaterals from bilateral subclavian arteries, palliation with bilateral PA banding, PDA stenting, and subsequent stenting of the aortic arch has provided clinical stability for 15 months and the opportunity to observe this child for growth of these structures over time while pursuing transplantation. Using fine sutures rather than clips to secure the PA bands facilitated successful band adjustment with angioplasty. At the last follow-up, the aorta demonstrated minimal growth, the main and proximal branch PAs were dilated, and the bands were well-positioned bilaterally, with moderate dilation of the left lower lobar and segmental PA branches.
