Comparative Study of Noncatheterized and Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections by ESBL-Producing Gram-Negative Bacilli in an Urban Tertiary-Care Center, Salem, Tamil Nadu
Authors: KT Revathi, Reena Rajan, S Mathavi, AV Raghavendra Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Urinary tract infections are frequently caused by Gram-negative bacilli, and timely detection and management of resistant strains and their antimicrobial susceptibilities are crucial.Methods:A six-month study examined Gram-negative bacilli from catheterized and noncatheterized patients, assessing antibiotic susceptibility, minimum inhibitory concentration, and Extended spectrum beta lactamase (ESBL) production genes using real-time Polymerase chain reaction (PCR).Results:Out of 72 isolates, 55.56% were ESBLs, with 65.0% from catheterized and 35.0% from noncatheterized samples. Out of 21 ESBL isolates, 47.62% had cefotaximase M (CTX-M) gene, while 4.76% had sulfhydril variant (SHV) and oxacillinase (OXA) 10/11 genes. Coproduction of CTX-M and SHV was observed.Conclusion:The study reveals multiple ESBL genes co-expression in clinical isolates from catheterized and noncatheterized patients, emphasizing the need for regular monitoring and antibiotic policy development.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infections are the predominant bacterial infections, constituting 40% of infections acquired in hospitals.[1] This condition is characterized by elevated body temperature, intense urination, abdominal discomfort, painful urination, pus presence in urine, and an increased number of white blood cells. Bacteria count is 105 CFUs/ml.[2]

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections are the predominant healthcare-associated infection, occurring in patients after catheter insertion within 48 h.[3] These infections can be short term (less than 28 days) or long term (over 28 days). Some examples of common pathogens are Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter, Proteus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and Candida.[4]

Antibiotic-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, particularly K. pneumoniae and E. coli, pose a significant challenge in treating urinary tract infections due to limited treatment options.[5]

Enterobacteriaceae isolates producing ESBL often display antibiotic resistance, with CTX-M, SHV, and TEM genotypes primarily found in clinical isolates, leading to treatment failure and changing UTI etiology patterns.[6]

The study aimed to identify ESBL producers linked to UTI in catheterized and noncatheterized patients and genotypically characterize them using multiplex PCR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A six-month study at VMKVMC&H, Salem, examined Gram-negative bacilli in urine from catheterized and noncatheterized patients, determining antibiotic susceptibility using the VITEK automated method.

Specimen collection

Urine samples were obtained from patients who had catheters inserted, short-term catheterized patients, long-term indwelling catheters, and symptomatic patients. Midstream samples were collected from noncatheterized patients, and significant bacteruria was defined as a colony count of 105 or 103 CFU/ml.

Culture

The study investigated bacterial growth in urine specimens using Macconkey agar culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the Kirby Bauer disc diffusion method. Antibiotic discs used included cefotaxime, ceftazidime, amoxicillin clavulanate, amikacin, gentamycin, ciprofloxacin, cefepime, levofloxacin, imipenem, meropenem, cefoperazone sulbactam, piperacillin tazobactam, cotrimoxazole, doxycycline, ofloxacin, norfloxacin, nalidixic acid, netilmicin, and nitrofurantoin.

The optimal PCR protocol involves denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of annealing and extension at 60°C for 30 s each. The reaction is concluded at 4°C. The study was approved by an ethical committee and statistical analysis was performed.

RESULTS

The study found that out of 130 urine samples, 55.38% were Gram-negative, with 40.56% being ESBLs, and 65.0% were from catheterized samples and 35.0% from noncatheterized samples.

DISCUSSION

Gram-negative ESBL bacteria, including bla SHV, TEM, and CTX-M, pose serious pathogens due to their ability to hydrolyze antibiotics and resistance to other antibiotic classes.[4] The study found that 55.38% of Gram-negative bacilli were ESBL producers, with 62.5% from noncatheterized patients and 20.83% from catheterized patients, a correlation with previous research. This correlated with the study conducted by Mulu Gebrestadik Gebremedhin et al.[7]

Fouzia Ashraf et al.’s[8] investigation found that the majority of ESBL isolates were collected from intensive care units and wards. E. coli and K. pneumoniae were found to be the predominant ESBL-producing organisms, as confirmed by studies by Suryarashmisahoo et al. and Mulu Gebrestadik Gebremedhin et al.[7] The study found UTI most commonly in individuals aged 31–60, consistent with previous research by Mulu Gebrestadik Gebremedhin et al. and Fouzia Ashraf et al.[8]

The investigation revealed that the CTX-M gene was detected in 47.62% of the isolates, making it the most prevalent gene. The SHV gene was detected in 4.76% of the isolates, while the OXA gene was found in 28.5% of the isolates. Three isolates were reported to have coproducers of CTX-M and SHV, whereas one isolate had coproducers of SHV and OXA.[9]

The study reveals that E. coli and K. pneumoniae exhibit varying levels of resistance to various antibiotics. E. coli has a 70.59% resistance to cotrimoxazole, while K. pneumoniae has a 75% resistance. Both strains are vulnerable to amikacin, imipenem, meropenem, piperacillin tazobactam, and cefaperazone sulbactam. Regular monitoring and surveillance of ESBL-producing organisms is crucial to prevent cross-infections in hospitals.

CONCLUSION

E. coli and K. pneumoniae are the most common extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) isolates in patients, with the CTX-M genotype being common. ICU patients are more susceptible to ESBLs. To prevent nosocomial infections, patients should be nursed with contact precautions, and hospitals should develop an antibiotic policy.
