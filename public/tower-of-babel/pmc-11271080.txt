The dynamics of funding for sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) advocacy and movement building
Authors: Rupsa Mallik, Eszter Kismodi, TK Sundari Ravindran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe article reports on a roundtable dialogue on Donor Funding for SRHR Advocacy and Movement Building, organised by Sexual and Reproductive Health Matters (SRHM) in collaboration with AmplifyChange on 14–15 September 2022. It explores the current scenario about the changing landscape for advocacy and movement building for SRHR and presents some key takeaways from the discussion. The roundtable examined the current funding architecture, drawing on the diverse perspectives of participants, including funders, and raises critical questions on whether current funding patterns are geared to help or hinder advancing a transformative SRHR advocacy agenda that can effectively counter opposition to and rollback of SRHR gains. It provides insights into current approaches to monitoring success and impact that, in turn, shape future funding strategies and priorities and offers preliminary suggestions and solutions for a way forward. The article ends with a call to infuse the SRHR advocacy and movement-building funding landscape with a new set of mutually agreed values, principles and strategies that are embedded in people and movement-centred approaches as a way to ensure not just more but better funding.Keywords: sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR), advocacy, movement building, funding, impact

Background

The funding landscape for SRHR advocacy and movement building has undergone profound changes over the past decade. While there have been efforts to monitor funding flows for SRHR, there is limited coherent information on the sources, extent, and nature of funding. There is also the critique that donor practices often try to fit advocacy and movement-building efforts into short-term, result-oriented evaluation frameworks. This, in turn, shapes donor funding priorities that continue to privilege short-term project-based, restricted funding over sustained and long-term financial commitments that are critical to ensure durable change outcomes.

In recognition of the urgent need to facilitate discussions between SRHR advocates and funders on some of these issues, Sexual and Reproductive Health Matters (SRHM) collaborated with AmplifyChange to create a dialogue and knowledge exchange platform. Preliminary consultations with selected SRHR advocacy organisations and donors were undertaken to map key issues, and this helped shape the agenda and identify participants for a roundtable dialogue titled “Donor Funding for SRHR advocacy and movement-building” held on 14–15 September 2022.

Spread over two sessions, the dialogue series included the following themes, explored from the perspective of donors as well as advocates and activists:

Around 30 participants, 15 from the global South and comprising advocates, donors and activists from various movements, such as the LGBTQI, disability and sex worker rights movements, participated in the roundtable dialogue. Participants spoke candidly in a spirit of finding solutions to shared concerns.

This article is a report on the roundtable dialogue. The authors conceptualised and convened the Dialogue on behalf of SRHM. Dialogue participants consented to the recording of the Dialogue and the use of transcripts to support the writing of the article. They consented to the publication of the article and agreed to have their views incorporated as part of the article including through anonymised quotes. It presents the gist of the discussions and critical recommendations for ensuring better donor support for SRHR advocacy and movement-building. The following section summarises participants’ views on the current realities, and section three presents the proposals for the way forward.

The current scenario

Advocacy and movement building for SRHR: the changing landscape

Although the two terms were twinned during the roundtable discussions, participants clearly articulated their understanding that advocacy and movement-building are mutually related but distinct areas of work. Movements often use advocacy to further their agenda, and advocacy efforts may rely on mobilising specific constituencies. At the same time, advocacy efforts are not necessarily linked to movements, especially at the international and national levels. Likewise, movement building may happen without advocacy as its aim and be focused on empowering and strengthening a marginalised community.

The International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) held in Cairo in 1994 was a watershed in advocacy by the SRHR movement and helped put the issue on the international agenda. A participant representing a regional non-governmental organisation (NGO) highlighted that

“the Nineties were about large conferences such as the International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) and the Beijing World Conference on Women (1995) serving as vehicles to bring together thousands of organisations to exert pressure on their government as a key strategy to advance SRHR”.

SRHR advocacy efforts stemming from these conferences were directed at multiple levels: globally, regionally, nationally and locally, and played an influential role in shaping the priorities of donors, governments and inter-governmental bodies, including regional human rights courts. However, the past decade has also seen a significant shift in how advocacy is conducted, as the head of a regional SRHR advocacy organisation explained:

“Advocacy in the past [followed a more linear trajectory … It meant] talk[ing] to decision-makers [and] those decision-makers can change their policies, their laws or their programs, in accordance to how we have advocated them around. But today, we are not the only group exerting pressure upon that decision-maker or that duty-bearer … . Now it is accepted across both donors and organisations [that it is about] pressure forces … these pressure forces [can] be individuals, they could be within the system, they could be outside of the system … we also know how social media too exerts pressure on government and decision-makers.”

The SRHR advocacy landscape has changed in many ways since 1994. The spaces and the processes are fragmented. In many countries, decentralisation processes have resulted in a multiplicity of duty-bearers and decision-makers, from the community to the sub-national levels. At the global level, SRHR advocacy is now happening at various United Nations spaces, such as at the Commission on the Status of Women and United Nations Treaty Monitoring Bodies, such as the Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), the Committee on the Rights of The Child, and the Human Rights Committee, and also in spaces such as the World Health Assembly of the World Health Organization. At the regional level, significant attention has shifted to regional human rights bodies such as the African Commission on Human and Peoples’ Rights, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights and the European Court of Human Rights. Activists and advocates have tough decisions on where they should focus their efforts so that they see the most significant rewards.

A significant development since 1994 is the emergence of new social justice movements – HIV, LGBTQI, disability and sex workers’ rights, and those working with refugee and indigenous populations – and focus on intersectional and justice-based approaches to advance SRHR has transformed the advocacy landscape. The SRHR advocacy agenda has diversified and expanded to include the “intersectional” advocacy issues of these movements and other issues such as decriminalisation of abortion, sex work and same-sex sexual conduct. A participant representing a SRHR organisation based in East Africa commented that

“ … some of the positive changes of incorporating movement building as part of SRHR advocacy has been the expansion of pressure groups that work outside the formal governance system in strong collaboration with community-based advocates. These groups have also successfully leveraged social media for wider outreach and collaboration.”

However, the diversification of advocacy actors and agendas has not been without challenges. The multiplicity of sites and issues on which to conduct advocacy has made the task of prioritisation complex not only for advocates but also for funders. In the view of one participant, this has “contributed to the fragmentation and dilution of the SRHR agenda within traditional advocacy spaces”. This view also stems from the limited resonance and acceptance of the role of diverse movements within the mainstream SRHR ecosystem. The lack of cohesion among the different movements advocating for SRHR has often been exacerbated by growing opposition to SRHR.

The positive momentum generated by the 90s conferences to advance a progressive SRHR advocacy agenda has been met with stiff opposition by a wide range of state and non-state actors. The successes of the SRHR movement have fuelled the emergence of these actors and a brand of SRHR advocacy driven by anti-gender and anti-rights ideologies. The results of this well-funded opposition can be seen in many countries where legislative, constitutional, and judicial processes are being deployed to restrict human rights overall and SRHR, including access to safe abortion and recognition of LGBTQI equality and rights. This is often accompanied by shrinking civil society space through legal prohibitions for receiving foreign funding. As one funder observed, “this makes the task of funding precarious and open to attack from both the opposition and governments opposed to a progressive SRHR agenda”.

At the global level, particularly within UN forums, there has been an increase in conservative opposition to gender equality and SRHR. Much attention is being paid to defending existing rights while advancing the overall sexual and reproductive rights agenda. At the national level, remarkable achievements have been gained in the decriminalisation and legalisation of abortion and decriminalisation of same-sex sex, for example, the development of laws, policies and health system measures on violence against women. At the same time, the recent reversal of Roe vs Wade by the US Supreme Court is symbolic of the enormous power and impact of these opposition forces achieving the national spaces. There is an urgent need to mobilise resources to support the recreation of effective SRHR strategies and counter opposition forces.

Helping or hindering? Funding for advocacy and movement building

Three global trends have profoundly impacted the SRHR funding landscape. These include

The Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015), followed by the Sustainable Development Goals (2015–2030), which have contributed to realigning governments and funders’ priorities to focus narrowly on specific SRH issues, with particular implications for SRHR overall.

Funding approaches need to align and seek to mitigate these trends. Yet, the funding landscape has not been able to respond fully to these emerging trends.

The significant sources of funding for SRHR continue (since 1994) to be bilateral, multilateral and institutional donors. In recent years, funds that provide dedicated support for specific issues (e.g. Safe Abortion Action Fund) and movements (e.g. Disability Rights Fund) have also become another funding channel supporting SRHR advocacy and movement building.

A major concern that funders and advocates cite is that the need for investment far exceeds availability. One of the funders shared that in the context of the agency they represented,

“it was initially intended to be something like 70 grants, but it ended up being 700. I think it was pretty clear that there was an awful lot of demand for support … There’s a 1:10 ratio and sometimes even a 1:15 ratio between applications for funding and the actual number of grants.”

An institutional donor highlighted that the current money fields for SRHR remain limited to traditional sources of funding that European governments and US-based private philanthropies provide. Research conducted by the same donor in 2020 found that 66% of the aggregate funding comes from direct grant sources in the sample of organisations surveyed. International non-governmental organisation contributions through partnerships increased this percentage by close to 90%. Only around 10% of the funding came from other sources.

Unfortunately, the current economic context has been profoundly impacted by the COVID pandemic, crises precipitated by Ukraine and other ongoing wars, and various economic crises in multiple spaces, making it difficult to build a strong political case for an increase in overseas development assistance. In addition, conservative, ideology-driven political changes in governments often result in the cut of development aid for sexual and reproductive health and rights issues.

Lack of adequate funding often resulted in trade-offs. A donor providing small-scale challenge funds to global South countries on SRHR pointed out that the demand to fund a larger number of organisations can reduce the scale and duration of funding to individual civil society organisations (CSOs). In some instances, donors committed to providing longer-term funding reduce the number of CSOs they fund and restrict the issues for which funds are provided. Donors also had to make choices about the priority countries for funding, leaving many low-income countries in dire need of external funding support.

A European bilateral donor acknowledged that funders needed to know their role in influencing movement-building processes. She said that while funders cannot

“force groups to work together … there are dynamics that we all need to be aware of … we are somehow historically responsible for the silos that we have made. And now to go and turn that around and say, okay, now we don’t want to fund single issues. We are going to fund intersectionality. [There is need for] also a conversation that needs to take place now to do this best.”

The roundtable dialogue allowed donors to reflect on their challenges in providing flexible, long-term funding for SRHR advocacy and movement building. Bilateral donors at the roundtable shared the constraints of programming taxpayers’ money as these often lead to strict due diligence requirements that make it challenging for global South organisations to access these resources. The agenda-setting for funding priorities is driven by domestic considerations and closely aligned with the ruling government’s political views. Bilateral donors also cited the lack of internal capacity to undertake extensive outreach in target countries and their reliance on intermediaries, prominent national and regional organisations, to channel funding to recipient countries. These all act as roadblocks to ensure that funding is more responsive to local needs.

The volume and nature of funding and donor funding practices dictated by their organisational structures can obstruct movement-building and leadership by local and community-based SRHR organisations. Participants from CSOs highlighted that fund shortages resulted in local advocacy organisations being in a constant trap to raise resources. In doing so, they often had to align to donor-driven priorities at the cost of their strategic advocacy and movement-building goals. In their view, this reinforces power imbalances between funders and advocates and contributes to creating fractures and disruptions in movement building.

Besides the lack of funding availability, the current approaches to grant-making limited the best utilisation of resources for advocacy and movement building. According to an SRHR advocate from the MENA region, one of the manifestations of this is the lack of donor interest to support nascent movements. Funders tend to prioritise assessment of the “readiness” of a movement to undertake advocacy rather than invest in long-term movement-building processes.

Another issue, especially for community-based and local organisations, is the power imbalance between better-established CSOs and smaller organisations. This impacts the ability of local organisations to tap into sources of funding. According to one CSO participant, large, well-funded organisations find it easier to meet and interact with donors and build their “reputational advantage”, which eases their access to funding. Also, donors often relied on the opinions of the CSOs they already funded to make decisions about a new applicant, as one participant representing an organisation working with women and girls with disabilities in Kenya described: “‘Do you think these people should get funded?’ And if they are in the good graces of these bigger groups that are getting funded, they will get some crumbs from the pie.”

A participant from a smaller CSO observed that smaller, under-funded organisations rarely share space with donors, particularly in high-level advocacy forums, where donors are present, which impacted their fundability:

“The efforts for underfunded CSOs to be present in the international and regional advocacy arena, to network where donors are current, or simply to find out about these spaces [has a bearing on their ability to raise resources] … Currently, the bigger you are, the easier it is to access funds continuously. And the more continuous flow of funding you have, the better internal structure you’ll build to manage larger sums and then keep fundraising … it is a political game. When we talk about decolonisation, this is the sort of dynamics that needs to be rethought.”

This participant felt that current funding models did little to

“ … disrupt the advocacy ecosystem and increase locally driven advocacy [even though they contribute to] more durable and sustainable change. Funding architecture, where we have intermediaries versus direct funding … essentially, they maintain hierarchies … that bring in not only agendas from the global North, but you’ve also got to take into account the priorities and various theories of change of the intermediary. So by the time you get to the [local] organisation, it is weighted against the realities in communities and within the advocacy structures they operate in.”

Monitoring success and impact in advocacy and movement building

Measurement of the impact of SRHR advocacy plays a critical role in shaping funding priorities and guiding decisions related to grant-making. One of the participants, representing a bilateral donor, illustrated the current state of the field in pointing out that “[t]he concept of monitoring advocacy and monitoring the impact of policy influence has always been at the forefront … [however] trying to force advocacy efforts into results frameworks or result matrices may not be very useful or fit for purpose”. She highlighted the need for increased understanding across funders making decisions about advocacy on what constitutes effective and reasonable advocacy outcomes and appropriate ways to measure these.

There was agreement among participants that profound transformations are taking place within the SRHR advocacy ecosystem, and approaches to measuring impact need to be responsive to these changes. With the growing integration of movement building as part of SRHR advocacy, there is an urgent need to develop evaluation frameworks best suited to measure complex change processes.

Participants also pointed out the increasing localisation of advocacy efforts and the diverse and often volatile contexts that influence outcomes. Ensuring sustainability, resilience, and strengthened leadership of local organisations needs to be seen as a measurement of success going beyond the usual understanding that tends to view funding impact only through the prism of legal and policy change.

A strategy and evaluation practitioner highlighted the

“[need to focus] on how we’re contributing to the strength of the advocacy community. So, how is power shared in that community? Is the leadership from directly impacted communities? Are we seeing global South leadership, global South setting of the agenda and leadership of marginalised communities?”

Localisation of advocacy is inherently linked to integrating movement building as part of SRHR advocacy. With the increased participation of diverse movements, assessment and attribution of “wins” to measure impact also become more challenging and limiting. As one participant representing a US-based philanthropic fund remarked,

“[W]e often count our victories and call that a form of evaluation. But I think depending on the window of time you’re looking at, it’s very tricky to determine a causal connection between what you’re funding and those particular victories … there’s an entire ecosystem of actors that can claim victory [therefore] we can’t attribute success to particular actors.”

In the view of a trans rights advocate, the context of setbacks and the role of opposition is vital. Funders supporting SRHR advocacy often measure impact in terms of the originality of projects and a narrative based on best practices. This is not always best suited to create a multiplier effect. Comparative analysis of how the opposition to sexual and reproductive rights is funding reveals what one participant representing a global South feminist alliance described as

“a complete divorce between how most of our movements are being funded by very small project grants attached to short-term and mid-term indicators … The opposition [on the other hand] is funding and investing in processes that go beyond 10 20 30 years”,

with a view to duplicating, scaling up and amplifying efforts.

A bilateral donor, agreeing with this, said that current indicators tend to be short-term, oriented towards specific outcomes, developed with a linear vision for change, and often insulated from the dynamic context within which actual change occurs. For example, in an increasing context of opposition and rollback of SRHR, success can also mean maintaining the status quo, like retaining a liberal abortion law in a given country. The donor reiterated that there is an urgent need to ensure funding patterns prioritise long-term, multi-year support to counter the opposition’s approach that is geared towards ensuring durable change. In addition, this shift in funding approach needs to be accompanied by impact measurement frameworks that go beyond short-term results-oriented indicators.

Investing in developing process indicators has significant value in capturing incremental stages of change. However, a participant representing a feminist funder highlighted that our current approaches to measuring impact

“hardly ever document what it takes to get there. So the lack of documentation is also the lack of guidance on what you would require to get there … if the focus is only on [the measurement] of the end game and not how we get there, people may get discouraged along the way, people may not understand the level of intensity that is required. And people might not be recognised for the good work being done.”

For example, if the impact of advocacy is only understood as the passage of progressive laws, it fails to account for other important outcomes. Even when progressive legal reform fails, there is tremendous success in ensuring significant scale mobilisation of people to advocate for change on an issue.

Several participants representing CSOs reiterated that successful outcomes are often the result of non-linear, incremental processes that include setbacks and are inherently cyclical. Given the dynamic and changing contexts in which multiple players are involved in bringing about change, one could not reasonably be expected to predict “what one may expect to achieve quarterly, or even annually”.

The way forward

Despite these challenges, participants also pinpointed solutions. For example, one of the donors at the roundtable shared that partnership with other funders with the experience, capacity and processes to fund movements has provided an alternative route to channel their funding to movements.

Examples were shared of alternative models to be found among small-scale donors:

“ … the fund whose board I am on … (its) objectives are shaped by grantees, around the idea of building assets, capacities and trust. And their work aims to foster these things. … [Should we be asking] what would it take to shift how they do things?”

The emergence of a community of feminist funders also contributes to shifts in funding practices that uphold resource justice. A feminist funder defined resource justice as being more than

“just equitable allocation of resources … it is also about a whole range of non-financial resources and knowledge creation, which needs to be driven by experiences of the global South and the global East and not left to theorising by other actors from [a] more privileged geopolitical situation”.

In their view, this approach can positively disrupt funding priorities, altering existing hierarchies and hegemonies and catalyse a resilient, connected and open advocacy ecosystem.

Participants, however, also cautioned that shifts in funding practices need to be balanced. There is a continued need to support organisations that work at the global and regional level and play an essential role in shaping worldwide advocacy and cross-regional work while attempting to channel substantive funding to local organisations and movements.

Several participants made important interventions about the key principles that must guide the development of alternative measurement approaches. Some practical approaches to monitoring and evaluating advocacy efforts outlined by participants are (a) being clear about what one wants to change, (b) asking ourselves periodically whether we are doing what we said we were going to do, (c) whether we are doing it effectively (d) examining if our strategy is moving us towards our long-term goal (e) Examining what worked and what didn’t; making sense of the reasons why; and recasting our strategies based on these lessons.

Some of the specific suggestions shared by participants included ways to assess movement building. These needed to integrate power analysis – how power is shared, and collaboration strengthened amongst diverse SRHR advocates, the type of representation and leadership from directly impacted communities, and whether global South actors lead local agenda-setting.

One of the participants representing a regional SRHR service delivery and advocacy organisation suggested that one of the core principles is the need to prioritise

“perspectives of inclusiveness and participation of the communities in the monitoring and evaluation that is done … it’s very important to involve the activists and the communities in the design of the interventions on movement building and advocacy … it allows [sharing of] power and building horizontal relationships and to have a shared understanding of what advancement in the political, legal or social level means. This will allow us to have a better political assessment of the environment we’re working on because we’re listening to the communities in that environment, and also enable conversation about what is at risk.”

A participant from a regional CSO emphasised that there is also a need to reframe the goals of advocacy change agendas differently to reflect a more graded approach to how success is measured – what we expect to see, what we like to see, and what we would love to see – to be able to assess better when and how change happens. In their view, this also ensures responsiveness to the dynamics of advocacy change processes beyond a singular focus on claiming victory. Several participants pointed out that measuring failure, too, needs to be incorporated into indicator development processes, as these can become critical tools for learning and reflection.

A feminist funder shared the need to elevate principles of collaboration and accountability as core tenets in the grant-making process. In their mind, this also entails shifting terminology that can reduce the power hierarchies inherent in donor-partner (grantee) relationships. In her view, one approach to doing this was for funders to have an overall institutional monitoring, evaluation and learning (MEL) framework. Shifting away from a project and funding cycle-based approach will allow funders to place their change agenda within an organisational rather than project- (individual grant) based context.

Co-creation was also mentioned as an essential principle that could foster horizontal – rather than top-down – relationships between donors and partners. It also helps create a shared learning space to develop a nuanced understanding of complex change processes within the context of material realities of people’s and communities’ lives. It also ensures a robust assessment of the political context within which to advocate for change.

There was an overall strong consensus amongst participants that infusing measurement approaches with granularity rather than a catch-all approach of attributing advocacy wins to specific actors and movements is vital to diffuse tension and fragmentation in movement spaces.

Conclusion

This article is an important step forward in bringing together diverse stakeholder perspectives, including that of funders, on the dynamics of funding for SRHR and current practices of measuring progress in advocacy and movement building. The SRHR ecosystem has undergone profound shifts mediated by a range of factors that include but are not limited to the diversification of pressure groups, including the emergence of an organised and well-funded opposition to SRHR; fragmentation of processes and sites for advocacy at the national and global level; and the growing role of movement actors advocating for integration of movement building and intersectional approaches in SRHR advocacy. In many places, the shrinking civic space accompanied by restrictions on foreign funding has created a precarious environment for grant-making where funders not only need to contend with the role of “other” funders that support conservative opposition to SRHR but also navigate legal restrictions on how and whom they can fund in the country.

There is a need to radically reshape the funding architecture for SRHR and adopt practices led by funders centred on resource justice. Adopting an intersectional and justice-based lens in funding includes the assumption of the leadership role of advocates, community leaders and global South movement actors in shaping the SRHR advocacy agenda and not being dependent on funding goals and objectives. Recreating advocacy and movement-building strategies and successfully countering the opposition to SRHR can only be achieved through resourcing movements – through long-term and flexible funding – that go beyond project-based funding approaches. In doing so, funders need also to work together to find ways to harmonise their funding strategies.

This article calls to infuse the SRHR advocacy and movement-building funding landscape with a new set of mutually agreed values, principles and strategies between funders and partners. Further conversation and action are needed to co-create a people- and movement-centred approach to SRHR philanthropy. This is the only way to advance a transformative SRHR agenda that is supported not just by more but by better funding.
