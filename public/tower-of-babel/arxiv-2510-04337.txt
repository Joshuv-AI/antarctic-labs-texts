Distinct Distances on Pfaffian Curves
Abhiram Natarajan, Adam Sheffer

Abstract
We generalize Pach and de Zeeuw’s bound for distinct distances between points on two curves, from algebraic curves to Pfaffian curves. Pfaffian curves include those that can be defined by any combination of elementary functions, including exponential and logarithmic functions, rational and irrational powers, trigonometric functions and their inverses, integration, and more. The bound remains Ω ⁡ ( min ⁡ { m 3 / 4 ​ n 3 / 4 , m 2 , n 2 } ) \Omega(\min\{m^{3/4}n^{3/4},m^{2},n^{2}\}) , as obtained from the proximity technique of Solymosi and Zahl.

Introduction

In recent decades, there has been an increasing interest in generalizing central Discrete Geometry theorems.
Results concerning incidences, distinct distances, and related topics often begin with lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and are then extended to algebraic curves, algebraic varieties in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, semi-algebraic sets, other fields, and more. For a few examples among many, see
[
2
,
5

23
,
28

\mathbb{R}
to include sets more general than semi-algebraic sets, yet retaining certain convenient topological properties. These properties — for example, having finitely many connected components and admitting stratifications — are usually referred to as
tame topological
. There is a growing body of work in discrete geometry which studies questions in this broader tame setting
[
4
,
7

In this paper we focus on Pfaffian functions, which were introduced by Khovanskiĭ
[
17
]
. Sets defined by Pfaffian functions, called
Pfaffian sets
, are significantly more general than semi-algebraic sets, allowing the use of exponential and logarithmic functions, rational and irrational powers, trigonometric functions and their inverses, integration, and more.

The use of sines and cosines is allowed only on a bounded domain.
At the same time, Pfaffian sets retain key properties of semi-algebraic sets, such as well-behaved intersections and a bounded number of connected and irreducible components.

An ambitious long-term goal is to extend the known bounds for expanding polynomials
[

,

]
and the Zarankiewicz problem
[
13

]
from the semi-algebraic case to the Pfaffian case.
The current work provides a modest first step in this direction, proving a distinct distances bound between two Pfaffian curves.
This is a special case of the expanding polynomials bounds of

Two other works which explore questions in extremal combinatorics involving Pfaffian sets are

For those curious about the model-theoretic origins of tameness, the framework of
o-minimal geometry
provides a natural setting for going beyond semi-algebraic sets while preserving their tame topological properties. The axioms of o-minimality guarantee that any collection of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying them — such a collection is called an
o-minimal structure
— exhibits tame behavior. Pfaffian sets (zero loci of Pfaffian functions) and semi-Pfaffian sets (defined by inequalities of Pfaffian functions) belong to the Pfaffian structure, which is the smallest collection containing all semi-Pfaffian sets. Also, the Pfaffian structure is closed under the usual structure operations (finite unions, complements, Cartesian products, and projections. Speissegger
[
30

established that the Pfaffian structure is an o-minimal structure. By providing a rich class of sets with tame topological behavior, the Pfaffian structure plays a central role in o-minimal geometry. Readers interested in further details regarding o-minimality can consult

The proof technique.

The proof of Theorem
1
begins by imitating the proof of Pach and de Zeeuw
[
21
]
for the algebraic case.
In particular, we define a bipartite distance energy, study isometries of curves, and obtain an incidence problem (these terms are defined in detail below).

However, since Pfaffian curves behave differently than algebraic curves, several parts of the proof need to be replaced.
For example, the projection of an algebraic variety to a lower dimensional space is well-behaved.
4
4

4

More precisely, the Zariski closure of the projection of an algebraic variety is algebraic.

On the other hand, projections of semi-Pfaffian sets need not be semi-Pfaffian, or even coverable by a fixed number of semi-Pfaffian sets. Such an example is provided in
[
20
]
.
Thus, we need to replace a step of the proof from
[
21
]
that projects curves from
\mathbb{R}^{4}
down to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Instead, we rely on a property of parameterizations of Pfaffian curves.
Algebraic curves do not satisfy this property.

When trying to generalize expanding polynomial results such as
[
23
,
24
]
to Pfaffian functions, we stumble upon similar issues.
In particular, these results rely on the property that a generic algebraic function is irreducible
[
31
]
.
It is currently unknown whether such a property holds for Pfaffian functions.

We also rely on the proximity technique of Solymosi and Zahl
[
29
]
.
This technique easily extends to the case of distinct distances between two curves.
However, it may not extend to other cases involving Pfaffian functions, due to the issues described above.

In Section

In Section

Pfaffian chains and Pfaffian functions.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set.
A
Pfaffian chain
over

of
order

r

chain-degree

\vec{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})\in(C^{\infty}(U))^{r}
that satisfies the following:
for every
1\leq i\leq r
and
1\leq j\leq d

P_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d},y_{1},\ldots,y_{j}]
of degree at most

such that

\frac{\partial q_{j}(\mathrm{x})}{\partial x_{i}}=P_{i,j}(\mathrm{x},q_{1}(\mathrm{x}),\ldots,q_{j}(\mathrm{x})).

(1)

A function
f\in C^{\infty}(U)
is called
Pfaffian
on an open domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to the Pfaffian chain
\vec{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})\in(C^{\infty}(U))^{r}
if there exists
Q_{f}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}]
of degree
\beta
such that

f(\mathrm{x})=Q_{f}(\mathrm{x},q_{1}(\mathrm{x}),\ldots,q_{r}(\mathrm{x})).

(2)

The
order
of
f
is the order
r
of
\vec{q}
.
We say that
f
has
format

(\alpha,\beta,r)
.
We denote by
\mathrm{Pfaff}_{d}(\beta,\vec{q})
the set of
d
-variate Pfaffian functions with respect to a Pfaffian chain
\vec{q}
defined by a polynomial of degree at most
\beta
from the ring
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}]
. Thus we have
f\in\mathrm{Pfaff}_{d}(\beta,\vec{q})
.

The above is currently the standard and most common definition of Pfaffian functions, although other definitions exist in the literature.
In particular, Khovanskiĭ’s original definition in
[
17
]
is slightly more general than the above. Both definitions lead to nearly the same class of Pfaffian functions, albeit sometimes with different formats.

Examples of Pfaffian functions:

•

A degree
D
polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is Pfaffian function of order 0 and chain-degree 0.
That is, we may use an empty Pfaffian chain.
In this case, the format is
(0,D,0)
.

•

For
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
\vec{q}=(q_{1})
with

is a Pfaffian chain of order 1 and chain-degree 1 given
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}q_{1}(x)=aq_{1}(x)

f\in\mathbb{R}[x,e^{ax}]
is a Pfaffian function with respect to
\vec{q}
.

•

Further generalizing the above, we note that
(e^{ax},e^{e^{ax}},e^{e^{e^{ax}}},\ldots)
is a Pfaffian chain.
Thus, every
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,e^{ax},e^{e^{ax}},e^{e^{e^{ax}}},\ldots]
is a Pfaffian function.

•

When
q_{1}(x)=\tan x
, the chain
\vec{q}=(q_{1})
is Pfaffian of order 1 and chain-degree 2, in the domain
U=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a+\pi/2\ :\ a\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. This is immediate from
\frac{\partial q_{1}(x)}{\partial x}=\mathrm{sec}^{2}x=1+q_{1}(x)^{2}
. Thus, every
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,\tan x]
is Pfaffian in
U
.

•

In
(0,\infty)
, for
q_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{x}
and
q_{2}(x)=\ln x
, we have that
\vec{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})
is a Pfaffian chain of order 2.
This is because
\frac{\partial q_{1}(x)}{\partial x}=-q_{1}(x)^{2}
and
\frac{\partial q_{2}(x)}{\partial x}=q_{1}(x)
.
Thus, every
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,\frac{1}{x},\ln x]
is Pfaffian in
(0,\infty)
.

•

In
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, for
q_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{x}
and
q_{2}(x)=x^{m}
,
\vec{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})
is a Pfaffian chain, for any real
m>0
.
This is immediate from the previous example and
\frac{\partial q_{2}(x)}{\partial x}=mq_{1}(x)q_{2}(x)
.
This implies that every
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,\frac{1}{x},x^{m}]
is Pfaffian in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

•

We can generalize the above examples to multivariate functions.
For example, for
m_{1},\ldots,m_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=x_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{m_{d}}
is Pfaffian due to the chain

Pfaffian sets.

For a
d

4.1
Symmetries of Pfaffian sets

We now study preliminaries that are required for the proof of Part (b) of Lemma
3
.
This is a variant of an analysis of Pach and de Zeeuw
[
21
]
, generalized to Pfaffian curves.

The
isometries
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the rotations, translations, and glide reflections (a reflection
combined with a translation).
A transformation
T
is a
symmetry
of a curve
\gamma
if
T
is an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that satisfies
T(\gamma)=\gamma
.
We now list facts about isometries in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For more information, see Conrad
[
11
]
.
Let
I_{2}
be the
2\times 2
identity matrix.
Let
O_{\mathbb{R}}(2)
be the group of orthogonal
2\times 2
matrices.

(
Iso-1
)

The isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the transformations

h_{A,\mathrm{w}}(\mathrm{v})=A\mathrm{v}+\mathrm{w},

where
A\in O_{\mathbb{R}}(2)
and
\mathrm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(
Iso-2
)

A complete classification of isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(a)

The
identity
is obtained when
A=I_{2}
and
\mathrm{w}=0
.

(b)

A nonzero
translation
is obtained when
A=I_{2}
and
\mathrm{w}\neq 0
.

(c)

A nonzero
rotation
is obtained when
\det(A)=1
and
A\neq I_{2}
. These are the matrices
A=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{pmatrix}
with
\theta\neq 0
. The center of the rotation is
p=(I_{2}-A)^{-1}\mathrm{w}
and the angle is
\theta
. We may also write
h(\mathrm{v})=A(\mathrm{v}-p)+p
.

(d)

A
reflection
is obtained when
\det(A)=-1
and
A\mathrm{w}=-\mathrm{w}
. In particular, it is the reflection across line
\frac{\mathrm{w}}{2}+\mathrm{ker}(A-I_{2})
. We may also write
h(\mathrm{v})=A\left(\mathrm{v}-\frac{\mathrm{w}}{2}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{w}}{2}
.

(e)

A
glide reflection
is obtained when
\mathrm{det}(A)=-1
and
A\mathrm{w}\neq-\mathrm{w}
.

(
Iso-3
)

The set of symmetries of a curve forms a group under composition.

We require an upper bound on the number of symmetries a curve may have.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Q
be a Pfaffian chain with chain-degree
\alpha
and order
r
.
Consider

such that
\gamma=Z(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an irreducible Pfaffian curve that is neither a line nor a circle. Then
\gamma
has
O_{\alpha,\beta,r}(1)
symmetries.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
\gamma
has
a translation symmetry

T
.
We write
T(\mathrm{v})=\mathrm{v}+\mathrm{w}
, with
\mathrm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
For a point
p\in\gamma
, we have that
f(p)=0
, which in turn implies that
f(p+\mathrm{w})=0
.
Inductively, this means that
f(p+n\mathrm{w})=0
for all

.
That is,
\gamma
has an infinite intersection with the line
\{p+t\mathrm{w}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
By Theorem
2
,
\gamma
is this line, contradicting the assumption.

Next, we assume that
\gamma
has two
rotation symmetries
.
Following
(Iso-1)
, we denote these rotations as
h_{1}=h_{A_{1},\mathrm{w}_{1}}(\mathrm{v})
and
h_{2}=h_{A_{2},\mathrm{w}_{2}}(\mathrm{v})
.
We denote the centers of the rotations as
p_{1}
and
p_{2}

By
(Iso-2)
, we have that

.
We note that

.
We recall that
A_{1},A_{2},I_{2}
are in the group

, which is Abelian.
Combining the above leads to

\displaystyle(h_{2}^{-1}\circ h_{1}^{-1}\circ h_{2}\circ h_{1})(\mathrm{v})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(h_{2}^{-1}\circ h_{1}^{-1}\circ h_{2})(A_{1}(\mathrm{v}-p_{1})+p_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(h_{2}^{-1}\circ h_{1}^{-1})(A_{2}A_{1}(\mathrm{v}-p_{1})+A_{2}p_{1}-(A_{2}-I_{2})p_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(h_{2}^{-1})(A_{2}(\mathrm{v}-p_{1})+(A_{1}^{-1}A_{2}-A_{1}^{-1}+I_{2})p_{1}-(A_{1}^{-1}A_{2}-A_{1}^{-1})p_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathrm{v}+(A_{1}^{-1}-A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}^{-1}+A_{2}^{-1}-I_{2})p_{1}-(A_{1}^{-1}-A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}^{-1}+A_{2}^{-1}-I_{2})p_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathrm{v}+(I_{2}-A_{2}^{-1})(I_{2}-A_{1}^{-1})(p_{2}-p_{1}).

By

, the above transformation is a symmetry of
\gamma
.
When

, this symmetry is a translation.
By the first paragraph of the current proof,
\gamma
cannot have a symmetry that is a translation.
Thus, all rotational symmetries of
\gamma
have the same center
p
.

Fix a point

By Lemma

This in turn implies that
\gamma
has
O_{\alpha,\beta,r}(1)

Assume for contradiction that
\gamma
has two
reflection symmetries
with parallel axes of symmetry.
Combining two such reflections leads to a translation.
By
(Iso-3)
, this translation is also a symmetry of
\gamma
, which contradicts the above.
Thus, each two reflection symmetries of
\gamma
have non-parallel axes.

Consider two reflection symmetries of
\gamma

By the preceding paragraph, the two respective axes of reflection intersect in a point

Following

We fix one such reflection symmetry

By

, it remains to consider the case of

We write

We thus conclude that
\gamma
