Aberrant activation of renal functional reserve and reduced tubule flow, but not obstruction, is associated with cystogenesis in Pkd2 mutant kidneys
Authors: Isis N. Thomas, Sneha Rajendran, Zhang Li, Courtney J. Haycraft, Mandy J. Croyle, Bradley K. Yoder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disruption of PKD2 function causes polycystic kidney disease (PKD) and leads to the formation of cysts in the kidney in both mice and humans. PKD is progressive, initiating with the formation of tubule dilations that expand into large cysts, causing the destruction of renal parenchyma and loss of kidney function. The mechanisms initiating tubule dilation and expansion are uncertain. Tubule obstruction has been proposed as a factor contributing to cystogenesis. Here, we utilize a proximal tubule fluorescent dextran uptake assay to evaluate changes in tubule flow that occur during cyst initiation or expansion and functional decline. We did not observe overt signs of tubule obstruction during cyst initiation or expansion, even in larger cysts; however, the rate of tubule flow within individual nephrons was greatly diminished in cystic kidneys. Surprisingly, along with reduced tubule flow rate, we observe an increase in newly labeled dextran cells associated with disease in the Pkd2 mutants. These cells are spatially distinct from those absorbing the initial dextran and are detected in both cortical and medullary regions of mutant kidneys. Marker analysis indicated that the dextran+ medullary cells were in the loop of Henle and interstitial macrophages. These cells are not present in wild-type controls. This may be due to tubule or vasculature leakage and utilization of renal reserve capacity in mutants that become exhausted, associated with declining glomerular filtration rate (GFR) as cysts expand.

INTRODUCTION

Polycystic kidney disease (PKD) is characterized by the development of numerous fluid-filled renal cysts (1). Although the genetic causes for PKD, mutations in Pkd1 and Pkd2, have been identified, factors that lead to cyst initiation and expansion have not been fully determined (2, 3). As early as 1892, it was suggested by Dr. Rudolf Virchow that in PKD, tubules lose their connections to the collecting duct system or detach from the tubule, supporting tubule obstruction as a factor involved in cystogenesis (4, 5). Furthermore, intraluminal micropuncture studies indicated that although the mean pressure in cysts was not significantly above normal tubules, some cysts do have elevated intratubular pressure, supporting this model (4). However, there was no direct correlation between the size of the cyst and the pressure measurements (4). Another possible model is that, rather than obstruction, narrowing of the tubule region downstream of the dilation causes increased resistance and luminal pressure, altering fluid flow dynamics as cyst initiation occurs. The altered tubule flow would impact primary cilia and polycystin function that extend into the lumen of the tubule leading to possible alterations in mechanosensory signals (6–8). These flow and mechanosensory signal changes are likely to be important for understanding mechanisms of cyst initiation.

In PKD, renal functional decline becomes evident well after cyst formation has initiated. The preservation of renal function in PKD may be related to the ability of the kidney to compensate as individual nephron function is compromised through what is referred to as renal functional reserve capacity (RFR) (9–11). In healthy kidneys, RFR can range between 20% and 30% of normal renal function and can be activated in response to injury or other stimuli that puts additional demands on the kidney (9). Activation of RFR is also associated with an expansion of the endocytic properties from predominantly the S1 into S2 and S3 segments of the proximal tubules (12–14). In disease states, such as PKD, the RFR likely allows baseline renal function to appear normal despite significant cyst formation; however, as the RFR becomes exhausted, the kidney is unable to buffer additional demand, manifesting in reduced GFR and kidney functional abnormalities (10, 11). A change in RFR may be an important subclinical disease risk factor and early indicator of future renal dysfunction associated with cyst development. Although the RFR is important for maintaining renal function, the mechanism regulating RFR capacity is not fully understood.

Here we use a sequential fluorescent dextran uptake assay in adult-induced Pkd2 conditional mutant mice to evaluate tubule obstruction, altered tubule flow dynamics, and RFR decline in association with cyst initiation or progression. Dextran is a marker for fluid-phase endocytosis (pinocytosis) that occurs predominantly in S1-S2 segments of the proximal tubule in normal kidneys. Using the dextran assay, we observe that fluorescent dextran uptake occurs even in large cysts, indicating they are not fully occluded or disconnected from the nephron. Although they remain patent with the rest of the nephron, the rate of flow in dilated tubules and cysts is greatly attenuated. We also show that there are cells that did not endocytose an initial fluorescent dextran but subsequently become competent to endocytose a second fluorescent dextran injected later during disease progression. These newly activated endocytic cells are evident shortly after loss of Pkd2 as tubule dilation occurs, well before overt cyst formation and the onset of renal function decline. The cells do not express megalin, increase with disease severity, and are located in the cortex and medullary regions, surprisingly including the loop of Henle segment of the kidney. A similar phenotype was recently reported in mice associated with glomerular disease (15). We also detect an increase in dextran uptake in interstitial macrophages in the medulla, but not cortex, suggesting the tubules or vasculature may become leaky. These new cells that are competent to uptake fluorescent dextran may represent the RFR capacity being accessed in more distal nephron segments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mouse Model

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Alabama at Birmingham (Approval No. 21072). Pkd2fl/fl mice were crossed with CaggCreER mice to establish Pkd2fl/fl; CaggCreER conditional mutants. To delete Pkd2, mice were injected intraperitoneally with three daily injections of tamoxifen (6 mg/40 g body wt) starting at 8 wk of age. Tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil, and the mixture was rocked at 37°C until it was dissolved. All mutant and control animals received tamoxifen. All results are from biological replicates of at least 3 independent animals/genotype. Reagents used in this study, their sources, and catalog numbers are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Glomerular Filtration Rate Measurements

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was measured in conscious mice using transcutaneous clearance of FITC-sinistrin (16) through the UAB O’Brien Center. Following intravenous injection of FITC-sinistrin, the fluorescence decay was recorded, and clearance curves were analyzed to determine elimination half-life and converted to GFR values. To allow comparison across animals of different sizes, GFR values were normalized to body weight and reported as µL/min/100 g. GFR readings were performed and recorded through the UAB Small Animal Surgery Core. Mice were delivered to Core the morning of each timepoint (1 wk postinduction (WPI), 6 WPI, 12 WPI, and 16 WPI) and returned to their home cage following recording.

Dextran Injection and Tissue Harvesting

Dextran injection was performed via retro orbital injection of 100–150 µL of 10 kDa low molecular weight dextran (20 µg/mouse). Dextran was diluted in sterile dPBS. Mice were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane to allow injection. Animals were monitored for 10–30 min postinjection before being returned to their home cage. For single dextran injection studies, animals were injected with dextran 1 day before kidney isolation and analyzed at 6, 12, and 16 WPI. Before isolation, mice were anesthetized with 2,2,2-tribromoethanol via intraperitoneal injection and checked for a toe pinch response before cardiac perfusion with room temperature dPBS followed by kidney isolation. Both kidneys were collected and placed on ice or stored at 4°C. The left kidney was directly placed into chilled RPMI 1640 for flow cytometry processing, and the right kidney was bisected and placed in chilled 4% PFA for later cryopreservation in 30% sucrose/PBS and paraffin preservation.

Dual dextran injections were performed via retroorbital injection as described earlier. Two independent timelines were followed. In group 1 (precystic to cyst initiation period), dextran 1 (Alexa 647) was injected immediately following tamoxifen induction. Dextran 2 (Alexa 488) was injected at 12 WPI, the cyst initiation timepoint. In group 2 (cyst initiation to expansion period), dextran 1 (Alexa 647) was injected at 6 WPI, at the precystic timepoint, and dextran 2 (Alexa 488) was injected at 16 WPI, the cyst expansion timepoint. Animals were isolated and analyzed 1 day after the last injection for all groups.

In our initial experiments, there was no significant difference based on the sex, therefore, we completed the remainder of experiments with female-only cohorts.

Histology and Cystic Index Quantification

Kidneys were isolated at the indicated timepoints, perfused with dPBS, and fixed in 4% PFA solution over night before storing ½ kidney in 70% ETOH for paraffin processing and sectioning. Postisolation cystic indices were calculated using hematoxylin and eosin-stained paraffin sections (10 µm). Full kidney sections were imaged, total kidney area and cystic lumen areas were recorded, and three standard deviations from wild-type tubules were used to define a cystic tubule. The cystic index was calculated by the sum of all cystic areas divided by the total kidney area (minimum of 3 sections per animal). The measurements were used to categorize 6 wk postinduction (WPI) as precystic, 12 WPI as cyst initiation, and 16 WPI as cyst expansion.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

After isolation from dPBS-perfused mouse, the left kidneys were minced and digested in the digestion buffer (1 mg/mL collagenase type 1 and 100 U/mL deoxyribonuclease 1 in RPMI medium) for 30 min at 37°C. The cell suspension was then washed with RPMI and passed through 70 μm cell strainers and centrifuged at 500 g at 4°C for 6 min. Supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. About 10 mL of RPMI was added to neutralize the lysis buffer, and the sample was centrifuged at 500 g at 4°C for 6 min. Samples were then blocked in Fc-blocking buffer diluted in FACS buffer (DPBS + Ca2+/Mg2+, 1% BSA) for 30 min at 4°C. About 250 µL of sample was added per well of a 96-well plate. Samples were centrifuged, washed with FACS buffer, and stained with antibody for 30 min on ice. All antibodies are listed in Supplemental Table S1. Samples were centrifuged at 500 g, washed, fixed with 2% PFA for 30 min, resuspended in FACS buffer, and kept at 4°C until sorted. Control mice injected with single color 647 or 488 dextran and one noninjected animal were used as single-fluorophore and no fluorophore controls. Proximal tubule cells were identified using 1:200 LTA, dead cell staining 1:500 live/dead, and CD45 immune cells using 1:200 cd45 bv786.

For analysis, samples were gated selecting singlet cells, then live cells only, and CD45-negative cells. To exclude macrophages, all CD45-positive (CD45+) cells were removed from analysis, and only CD45-negative cells were used for quantitative analysis of dextran uptake. Immunofluorescence imaging of kidney sections was used to independently evaluate dextran+ immune cells. Each dextran fluorophore was selected and analyzed. All gating and analysis were done in FlowJo (Java Version: 17.0.8 + 7-LTS).

Live Imaging

For intravital imaging studies, mice were injected, with dextran 488, 1–2 h before imaging, to label dextran-absorbing tubules. The confocal environmental chamber was allowed to equilibrate to 37°C (to maintain animal body temperature during imaging). Animals were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane, the skin was cleaned with 70% ethanol, and the left flank was opened to expose the kidney. A small incision was made, and the kidney was gently lifted out of the body cavity and stabilized with sterile gauze soaked in sterile dPBS. The kidney was kept hydrated during this procedure with sterile dPBS. The animal was positioned on the imaging stage custom-designed for kidney imaging. Using a water immersion objective (×20), kidney tubules were identified and imaging began. While recording, animals were kept under continuous sedation with 2% isoflurane and injected with dextran 647 via the retroorbital cavity to analyze flow through the tubules. The rate of flow was calculated by the time of dextran appearance in tubules minus the time of initial dextran appearance in blood vessels and compared between mutants and controls. Fluorescence imaging was captured on a Nikon Ti2 Eclipse, Yokogawa X1 spinning disk confocal microscope, and an Orca Flash 4.0 sCMOS camera (Hamamatsu). Images were processed and analyzed in Nikon NIS Elements software (5.0).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were done in GraphPad Prism 10. Specificities for each test are noted in the figure legends. All error bars represent the standard error (SE).

RESULTS

Time Course of Cyst Initiation, Progression, and Renal Functional Decline

To begin defining the relationship between polycystin-2, tubule flow, cyst development, and renal function, we used the Pkd2;CaggCreER conditional mutant mouse model and induced deletion of Pkd2 at 8 wk of age by injection of tamoxifen as per the methods. We analyzed histology at 6, 12, and 16 wk postinduction (WPI) and quantified disease severity by measuring the cystic indices (Fig. 1A). Dilations of the tubules were evident by 6 WPI, small cystic structures by 12 WPI, and expanding cysts by 16 WPI (Fig. 1B). We correlated the kidney phenotypes with renal function by monitoring FITC-sinistrin clearance and calculated the adjusted glomerular filtration rate (GFR) at 1, 6, 12, and 16 WPI (Fig. 1C). At 1 WPI, before any overt pathology, we observed a significant increase in GFR in mutants compared with the controls, suggesting mild hyperperfusion in the mutants. The reason for the increased GFR in the mutants is not known. At 6 and 12 WPI, the GFR was not significantly different between mutants and controls. By 16 WPI, GFR declined in the mutants, indicating a significant impairment of renal filtration function. Along with decreased GFR, urine creatinine concentration confirms a significant reduction in urine concentrating ability in mutants at the 16 WPI timepoint (Fig. 1D). Overall body weight remained comparable between control and mutant mice across all time points, indicating that differences in cyst burden and renal dysfunction were not attributable to systemic changes in body mass (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Dextran Uptake Is Preserved Across Genotypes During Cyst Progression

To address whether there is altered tubule flow or obstruction associated with tubule dilation, cyst initiation, or cyst expansion and the decline of renal function, we used a low molecular weight (LMW, 10 kDa) fluorescent dextran uptake assay. Dextran is absorbed into cells in proximal tubules through a fluid phase (pinocytosis) process (17). Dextran uptake occurs prominently in S1 and S2 segments of the proximal tubule but can also occur in S3 segments to provide a reserve endocytic capacity in disease states (14, 18). Endocytosed dextran enters the vacuolar and lysosomal compartment but is not degraded efficiently, allowing temporal analysis of flow based on dextran uptake (19). This approach allowed us to evaluate whether flow was occurring through the proximal tubules during the process of cyst initiation and progression.

At 6, 12, and 16 WPI, mice received a single dextran injection one day before kidney isolation (Fig. 2A). To quantify differences between mutants and controls, we performed flow cytometry and analyzed the percentage of dextran+ cells among CD45− nonimmune cells and within the LTA+ proximal tubule population at each time point. At all time points, mutants exhibited a significantly higher percentage of dextran+ cells within the CD45− population compared with controls (Fig. 2B). However, there was no difference in the percentage of LTA+ cells between genotypes from 6 to 16 WPI (Fig. 2C), nor was there a difference in the total number of dextran+ LTA+ cells (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that the increase in dextran-labeled cells in Pkd2 mutants was not due to an expansion of proximal tubule cells. Instead, the increased dextran uptake was observed in non-LTA+ populations (Fig. 2E), raising the possibility that these represent new cells capable of dextran uptake.

Using fluorescence microscopy, we analyzed the distribution of LTA+ and dextran+ cells to determine whether dilated tubules and cysts were receiving flow as indicated by dextran uptake. In the cortex of both mutants and controls, we detected dextran+, LTA+ and dextran−, LTA+ tubules (Fig. 2F). The S1 and, to a lesser extent, S2 proximal tubule segments possess well-developed endolysosomal systems and readily endocytose dextran and protein from the tubule lumen, whereas S3 segments have reduced uptake capacity (14). Accordingly, LTA+ tubules lacking dextran labeling likely represent S3 segments.

In mutant kidneys, dextran uptake was evident in LTA+ dilated tubules as well as in both small and large cysts, indicating that these structures remained connected to the glomerulus and continued to receive tubular flow (Fig. 2F). The presence of dextran in cystic structures across a range of sizes indicates that cyst initiation and progression are not primarily driven by tubule obstruction.

Sequential Dextran Labeling During Tubule Dilation and Cyst Initiation

One limitation of the above approach was the inability to detect obstructed tubules in which proximal tubule cells may have lost their identity and dedifferentiated, rendering them incompetent for dextran uptake. To address this, we used a sequential dextran injection assay (Fig. 3A). The sequential labeling method was first evaluated by injecting control mice with each dextran either 1 day or 11 days apart and isolating samples 24 h after the second dextran injection to quantify dextran+ cells and confirm colocalization (Supplemental Fig. S2). This information revealed the reproducibility of each fluorophore absorption and that there were minimal differences based on the injection of different fluorophore-tagged dextrans. We saw similar absorption patterns as the majority of cells that were dextran positive were dual labeled. For the remainder of the study, we performed all experiments with dextran 647 injected first and dextran 488 injected second.

For the sequential injection method, Pkd2 deletion was induced at 8 wk of age (0 WPI) along with a low molecular weight dextran (10 kDa, Alexa 647) to label the S1 and S2 proximal tubule segments. A second green fluorescent dextran (Alexa 488; dextran 2) was administered at 12 WPI, corresponding to the time of cyst initiation, 1 day before kidney isolation (Fig. 3A). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that ∼15% of nonimmune cells were positive for dextran 1 (injected at 0 WPI) when analyzed at the 12 WPI time point, with no significant differences between control and mutant mice (Fig. 3B, purple). A similar outcome was observed for dextran 2 in control mice, indicating that dextran uptake remained stable over the 12-wk interval. However, mutant kidneys exhibited a significant increase in the percentage of cells that endocytosed dextran 2 compared with controls (Fig. 3B, green).

Using flow cytometry, we analyzed the percentage of cells that were dextran 1+-only (purple), dextran 2+-only (green), or dual dextran 1+ and dextran 2+ (yellow) labeled out of total nonimmune populations at 12 WPI following Pkd2 loss and in controls (Fig. 3C). Mutant kidneys showed a marked increase in dextran 2+-only cells (green), accompanied by a reduction in dextran-negative (gray) cells. These findings confirm that additional nonimmune cells, including tubule epithelial cells, acquire dextran uptake capacity over time, resulting in an expanded population of dextran+ cells in mutant kidneys. One possible explanation is increased utilization of the S2 segment (hybrid S1/S2 population) or S3 segment during cyst initiation, consistent with activation of renal functional reserve mechanisms previously reported in this proximal tubule segment during renal disease and injury (14, 15).

To determine where these new dextran-labeled cells are located, we analyzed sections of the kidney by fluorescence microscopy. Most tubules in the cortex were positive for both the first and second dextran (Fig. 4A, gray box) in both mutants and controls. There were also tubules that endocytosed dextran 1 but not dextran 2 (Fig. 4A, red box) and partial tubules where cells were only positive for dextran 2 and not dextran 1 (Fig. 4A, yellow boxes). These mosaic tubules were evident more frequently in the mutants than in the controls. These data confirm the presence of new cells in tubules that are capable of endocytosing dextran 2. These cells likely account for some of the increase in dextran 2+-only cells detected in the flow cytometry analysis. No clear correlation was observed between dextran labeling and whether tubules appeared dilated, cystic, or morphologically normal.

One of the most striking spatial differences between mutants and controls was the appearance of dextran 2+ cells within the medullary region of mutant kidneys. Dextran 2, administered 12 wk after Pkd2 deletion, was detected in both cortical and medullary cells, whereas dextran 1, administered at the time of Pkd2 deletion, remained restricted to the cortical region as expected for S1/S2 proximal tubule segments (Fig. 4B). To define the new cell types that took up dextran 2, we stained sections with phalloidin to mark the apical side of epithelial cells, uromodulin to define the loop of Henle, F4/80 to label macrophages, and CD31 to identify the vasculature. These data show dextran uptake in phalloidin- and uromodulin-labeled tubule epithelia located in the medulla (Fig. 4C). Dextran was also present in F4/80 macrophages (Fig. 4D and Supplemental Fig. S3) in the medulla but not those in the cortex and not in CD31+ endothelial cells (Fig. 4E). Thus, the increase in dextran 2+-only cells detected by flow cytometry, which excluded CD45+ cells, is derived from epithelium in the cortex that are likely S3 proximal tubules along with epithelium in the loop of Henle. We were unsuccessful in confirming these cells as S3 due to difficulties with several S3 segment-specific antibodies.

Sequential Dextran Labeling Between Cyst Initiation and Expansion

To help define when these new medullary cells begin to uptake dextran and whether it is progressive, we performed another temporal dextran study where the initial dextran injection occurred at 6 WPI (dilations present) and the subsequent dextran 2 injection at 16 WPI (cyst expansion, Fig. 5A). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ∼20% of the nonimmune cells in the kidney of control mice endocytosed dextran 1 at 6 WPI or dextran 2 at 16 WPI (Fig. 5B). However, in the mutants at both 6 WPI and 16 WPI, the percentage of dextran 1+ cells is increased compared with the controls. This contrasts with the injections done at 0 WPI where there were no differences between the mutant and controls. These data suggest that the new cells capable of endocytosing dextran are present as early as 6 wk after Pkd2 gene loss, well before cysts form.

As in the previous cyst initiation paradigm, we observed a higher frequency of mosaic tubules and dextran 2+-only cells/tubules in the mutants. We used fluorescent microscopy to define the cells in the renal medulla of the mutants that endocytose dextran. To begin identifying these cells in the medulla, we stained with phalloidin (apical membrane) and uromodulin (loop of Henle). These data indicate that some of the epithelial cells in the LOH endocytose dextran and that this occurs in only the mutants (Fig. 7, A and B). As in the previous time point analysis, we found that many of the dextran+ cells located in the renal medulla were F4/80-positive (F4/80+) macrophages (Fig. 7C), indicating that a substantial portion of the medullary dextran signal corresponds to interstitial immune cells. These F4/80-labeled macrophages were tightly associated with the epithelium (Fig. 7D), and dextran was present in most of the interstitial macrophages in mutants. Macrophages in the control were not labeled with dextran.

Live Imaging Shows Reduced but Persistent Tubule Flow in Mutants

Although the studies above indicated that the cystic nephrons in the Pkd2 mouse model remain open based on dextran uptake, it does not address whether flow rates are affected during cyst formation. To evaluate this possibility, we conducted intravital imaging in the kidney of live animals. dextran 1 was injected 2 h before imaging to allow dextran to be endocytosed in proximal tubules that would serve as landmarks for imaging (Fig. 8A). A second dextran was then injected retro-orbitally while the kidney was simultaneously being imaged as described in the methods (20). In controls, dextran 1+ tubules rapidly filled with dextran 2 and all dextran 1+ tubules became positive for the second dextran within less than 20 s after injection (Fig. 8, B and C, Movie). In contrast, in mutants, dextran 2 first became evident in the proximal tubules at 20 s postinjection and did not fill all dextran 1-labeled proximal tubules until after 1 min. Thus, although tubules are not obstructed, the mutants exhibited a much slower flow rate compared with controls; however, uptake still occurred, consistent with the FACS results.

DISCUSSION

Here, we used adult-induced Pkd2 mouse mutants and low molecular weight fluorescent dextran to analyze tubule flow based on the endocytic uptake of dextran into proximal tubule cells. Single dextran injections were done at the time of gene deletion, when tubules began to dilate (6 WPI), at cyst initiation (12 WPI), and during a period of rapid cyst expansion associated with GFR decline (16 WPI). We found that once fluorescent dextran is endocytosed, the fluorescent signal is retained in the cells for at least 4 mo and that the fluorescent tag is not noticeably extruded or degraded, as the number of fluorescently tagged cells did not significantly decrease over the 12-wk period analyzed. The resistance of dextran to degradation in the kidney was reported previously (19). Based on these data, we reasoned that this approach could be used to fluorescently tag dextran-absorbing cells where flow was present to evaluate whether changes in tubule flow occur in proximal tubules during cystogenesis. In this study, we demonstrate that tubule dextran uptake patterns during PKD progression reflect dynamic remodeling of nephron function rather than simple obstruction of tubule flow.

We first asked whether tubule obstruction occurs at the dilation (6 WPI), initiation (12 WPI), or cyst progression (16 WPI) time points. Using kidney sections, we found that fluorescent dextran could be readily detected in proximal tubules at all stages of cyst development. This occurred in a majority of LTA+ cells of the proximal tubule and was evident in dilated tubules and in moderate and large cysts. These data indicate that tubule obstruction is not a major factor at any stage of cystogenesis, at least in the proximal tubules analyzed in the Pkd2 mutant mouse model.

Using intravital imaging approaches, we found that although cystic tubules can endocytose fluorescent dextran, the rate by which the tubules fill is greatly attenuated in the mutants. We observed this delay in flow in tubule dilations and in moderate to large-sized cysts. To distinguish altered tubular flow from endocytic capacity, we quantified the time between the initial appearance of dextran in renal blood vessels and the arrival of the dextran signal within the imaged tubule lumen. The decreased flow corresponded with a decrease in adjusted GFR as measured by clearance of FITC-sinistrin. Alternatively, the reduced flow could be due to a transition from an expanded region (dilation or cyst) into a very compressed outlet, which could explain the increased pressure observed in some cystic structures (4). One thing we did not observe was a complete lack of flow in cystic tubule segments.

Using flow cytometry, we found that the percentage of dextran-labeled cells among nonimmune cells was consistently higher in mutants compared with controls. This was not due to increases in the number of LTA+ cells in the mutants, as these remained consistent across genotypes. One possibility was a stochastic effect within a tubule where some cells endocytosed dextran, and others did not, resulting in more cells within a tubule capable of endocytosing fluorescent dextran in mutants. This new population of cells could be detected by at least 6 wk after induction of Pkd2 loss and was not observed in any of the control kidneys. As immune cells were excluded from the flow cytometry analysis, the large increase in dextran 2+-only labeled cells in mutants was due to activation of new epithelial cells in the cortex that we suspect are S3 segments. It is known that S1 cells readily endocytose filtered protein and fluorescent dextran and that S2 and, particularly, S3 cells are much less efficient (14, 15). Under pathological conditions, there can be large-scale changes in transcriptional profiles and differentiation states in the S2 and S3 segments, resulting in increased endocytic activity to reduce urinary protein loss (13–15, 21–23). Unfortunately, attempts to identify S3 segments using multiple different antibodies were not successful. Marker analysis indicates that some of these epithelial cells are in the loop of Henle based on uromodulin staining. This axial plasticity in endocytosis along the tubule is thought to be a component of renal functional reserve capacity (23, 24).

An important observation from this study is the identity of dextran+ cells within the renal medulla. Although we could detect uromodulin+, dextran+ tubule epithelia, a substantial portion of the medullary dextran signal was present in F4/80+ macrophages, indicating that interstitial immune cells contribute to dextran uptake in this region. These cells were excluded from flow cytometry analyses and became prominent in the later stages. The mechanism or pathology that leads to the release of dextran into the interstitium is not currently known, but suggests the tubules or endothelium may have become leaky. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the dextran+ macrophages access the luminal compartment, engulf dextran, and then deposit dextran across the basement membrane into the tubule epithelium.

Although we did not observe obstruction in any of the dilated tubules or cysts, there was a large decrease in tubule flow rate, which could be related to changes in tubule diameter. Classical fluid dynamic models such as Poiseuille’s law assume rigid cylindrical tubes with uniform geometry and constant downstream resistance. These assumptions are not met in renal tubules, which exhibit compliant epithelial walls, variable luminal geometry, extensive epithelial fluid transport, and dynamic regulation through glomerulotubular feedback. Consequently, changes in tubular diameter associated with dilation or cyst expansion do not necessarily correspond to predictable changes in flow rates within the nephron.

The changes in flow rates detected in mutants could alter endocytic responses along the nephron. Fluid flow across proximal tubule cells is known to influence both receptor-mediated (megalin) and fluid-phase endocytosis (25, 26). Studies in cultured proximal tubule cells subjected to fluid shear stress demonstrated a two- to threefold increase in albumin and fluid-phase uptake (25, 26). This response was associated with intracellular Ca2+ signaling mediated through ATP release and Cdc42 activation and was dependent on the presence of the primary cilium (25), the subcellular domain in which Pkd2 functions (27, 28). It remains to be determined whether cilia-localized Pkd2 participates in this response.

One possibility is that the reduced flow rates detected in proximal tubules of mutants alter shear-stress signaling, leading to reduced proximal tubule endocytic capacity and compensatory activation of renal functional reserve capacity through aberrant endocytosis in downstream nephron segments. Another possibility is that reduced flow allows dextran to dwell longer in distal tubules, revealing normally low endocytic activity that becomes detectable under these pathogenic conditions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, this analysis is largely restricted to proximal tubules and cannot directly address cyst initiation or expansion in more distal nephron segments. Second, it is unclear whether the fluorescent label is retained following cell proliferation. If the label were lost during proliferation and cells subsequently endocytosed the second dextran, some cells could appear newly labeled; however, this would not account for the large increase in dextran 2+-only cells in mutants or their predominance in the medullary region, as proliferation rates remain low at these early stages of cystogenesis (29). Third, intravital imaging is limited to superficial cortical regions due to optical constraints, preventing visualization of deeper corticomedullary nephrons.

Together, these findings demonstrate that tubule flow dynamics are altered during cyst progression in PKD models and that dextran uptake patterns reflect dynamic remodeling of nephron function rather than simple obstruction of tubule flow. These observations support a model in which nephron segments adapt to altered flow conditions during cyst expansion. Understanding how these alterations in tubule flow relate to changes in epithelial transport physiology during cyst initiation and progression may provide new insight into the mechanisms driving renal functional decline in polycystic kidney disease.
