Efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonists on obesity and metabolic profile in patients with inflammatory bowel disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fatemeh Ojaghi Shirmard, Seyed Morteza Pourfaraji, Mahyaar Omouri-Kharashtomi, Arash Amani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have shown an increase in obesity prevalence in the inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) population. The popularity of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RAs) has increased in recent years due to their effectiveness in weight management. In this comprehensive review, we evaluate the efficacy of GLP-1 RAs in the management of obesity in patients with IBD.MethodsWe performed a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science up to October 2025 for studies that reported changes in anthropometric variables in IBD cases treated with GLP-1 RAs. The primary outcome was weight loss after treatment, and the secondary outcome was changes in patients’ metabolic profiles. We pooled the mean raw changes (MRAW) in weight, body mass index (BMI), Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), Total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) from baseline to follow-up. This study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251063158).ResultsOverall, eight retrospective cohort studies involving 1236 patients were included. The pooled weight change following GLP-1 RAs treatment was − 5.71 Kg (95% CI, -9.56 to -1.86; P = 0.01; I² = 78.0%). The weight loss was also significant in the semaglutide subgroup (MRAW: -5.59 Kg; 95% CI, -10.37 to -0.81; P = 0.03; I² = 80.0%). Furthermore, the pooled MRAW of three studies on BMI changes showed a significant reduction (-2.18 Kg/m2; 95% CI, -3.66 to -0.69; P < 0.01), while heterogeneity was high (I² = 81.1%). The certainty of evidence was rated as “Low” for weight change and “Very Low” for all other outcomes, according to the GRADE framework.ConclusionThe GLP-1 RAs could be an effective option for obesity management in IBD. However, the robustness of the current study’s results is limited by potential bias in the included studies and small sample sizes in the secondary outcomes analysis. Further prospective studies are needed prior to broader prescription of GLP-1 RAs in the IBD population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04496-5.Keywords: Weight loss, Semaglutide, Ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, Pharmacotherapy

Introduction

Nearly 15%−40% of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) have obesity [1], and the obesity rate, along with other metabolic comorbidities, has risen over the past decade among IBD patients [1, 2]. In large prospective studies, obesity has been linked to a higher risk of Crohn’s disease (CD) [3, 4]. A meta-analysis further highlights the association between obesity and increased risk of IBD, especially CD [5]. Anthropometric measures, including waist circumference, body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio, body roundness index, and A body shape index, have also been indicated to increase the risk of IBD [6]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and adiponectin secreted from adipose tissue, gut microbiome dysbiosis, and enteroendocrine hormones like Glucagon-Like Peptide 1 (GLP-1) are thought to be the link between obesity and IBD [7]. In addition to increasing the risk of IBD, obesity also contributes to the course of the disease. Obesity is associated with increased odds of treatment failure with anti-TNF therapy in ulcerative colitis (UC) [8], longer length of hospitalizations, increased hospital charges, and overall complications [9, 10]. On the other hand, no association is observed between obesity and a higher risk of hospitalization, IBD-related surgeries, or hospital mortality [9, 11].

Obesity should be carefully assessed, and stepwise approaches from lifestyle modifications and pharmacological therapy, like GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), to surgical interventions should be applied for obesity management in IBD patients [12]. GLP-1 RAs, including semaglutide and liraglutide, are anti-diabetic medications that also exert weight loss effects through various mechanisms, including delaying gastric emptying, increasing satiety, reducing appetite, and improving eating habits [13]. Besides the metabolic effects of GLP-1 RAs, studies have reported the anti-inflammatory effect, mucosal repair, enteric immune response, and gut microbiome modulatory effect of GLP-1 RAs in IBD patients [14]. GLP-1 RAs further contribute to the improvement of the clinical course of disease, such as reducing IBD-related surgery, hospitalization, and disease severity [15].

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aim to consolidate the current data on the effect of GLP-1 RAs on obesity and metabolic parameters in patients with IBD and obesity. This study will advance the current understanding of administering GLP-1 RAs in IBD patients and their efficacy in the management of obesity and metabolic parameters to inform clinical and therapeutic approaches.

Materials and methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the 2020 version of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplemental Table 1). This study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251063158).

Research question and framework

The research question was formulated using the Patient, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcome (PICO) framework, Patient (P): Individuals diagnosed with IBD, including CD and UC., Intervention (I): GLP-1 RAs, Comparator (C): Non-GLP-1 therapies or placebo, Outcome (O): Changes in metabolic parameters (BMI, weight, and Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)) and lipid profiles (Total cholesterol (TChol), LDL, HDL, triglycerides (TG)). We included studies involving patients with IBD that assessed the effects of GLP-1 RAs therapies or placebo, reporting at least one metabolic parameter (e.g., BMI, weight, and HbA1c). Clinical trials, cohort studies, observational studies, and case series were selected. Non-human studies, preclinical studies, and conference abstracts were excluded from the study. While comparative studies with control or placebo groups (e.g., non-GLP-1 therapies) were prioritized, single-arm studies and case series that reported pre-treatment and post-treatment metabolic parameters were also included. The primary outcome was weight loss following GLP-1 RAs therapy in patients with IBD and obesity. Secondary outcomes included changes in body mass index (BMI), glycemic indices (HbA1c), and lipid profile parameters (Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and TG).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Scopus, from inception to October 2025. The following keywords and MeSH terms were used: “GLP-1 receptor agonist”, “inflammatory bowel disease”, “Crohn’s disease”, and “ulcerative colitis.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) and filters (e.g., English language) were applied to refine the search. The complete search strategy is available in the Supplementary File.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data (F.O.S. and A.A.) using a standardized data collection sheet. Extracted information included study characteristics (author name, year of publication, country, and study design), patient demographics, intervention details, and reported outcomes. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third author (S.M.P.). Baseline and post-intervention measures of BMI and total body weight were the primary outcome variables. Secondary outcome variables included HbA1c, Total cholesterol, HDL, and LDL. Data were extracted as means ± standard deviation (SD) at baseline and follow-up or as mean change and SD.

Risk of bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias of the eligible publications, two reviewers (F.O.S. and M.O.) independently used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool for Cohorts studies [16]. Each study was rated as having Good, Fair, or Poor quality based on three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome, using following scoring, Good (3 or 4 stars in selection domain AND 1 or 2 stars in comparability domain AND 2 or 3 stars in outcome domain), Fair (2 stars in selection domain AND 1 or 2 stars in comparability domain AND 2 or 3 stars in outcome domain), or Poor (0 or 1 star in selection domain OR 0 stars in comparability domain OR 0 or 1 stars in outcome domain). Any disagreements were resolved through a third reviewer (S.M.P.).

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence was assessed according to the five parts of the Grading of the Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [17]: (1) risk of bias (2), inconsistency (3), indirectness (4), imprecision, and (5) publication bias. The overall quality of the evidence for each outcome was ranked as high, moderate, low, or very low. The investigation was conducted independently by two authors (F.O.S. and M.O.), and a third reviewer (S.M.P.) resolved any disagreements.

Statistical analysis

Reported medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were converted to means and SDs using the approach proposed by Luo et al. and Wan et al. [18]. We calculated the effect size as the mean raw change (MRAW) between baseline and follow-up measurements within each study. Meta-analyses were performed using a random-effects model, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated via the Hartung-Knapp adjustment method. Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochrane’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic. For interpretation, I² values were categorized as low (< 25%), moderate (25–50%), and high (> 50%) heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was conducted for each reported outcome to evaluate the robustness of findings. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value less than 0.05, except for the chi-square test for heterogeneity, where a significance threshold of p < 0.10 was applied. All statistical analyses were conducted using the “meta” package in R software (version 4.5.0).

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 891 records were identified through a systematic search, and eight studies [20–27] that met the inclusion criteria were selected for the current meta-analysis (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 2). The included studies were published between 2024 and 2025, and primarily conducted in the USA (n = 7), with one study from Spain. The follow-up duration across studies ranged from 3 to 18 months. All studies employed a retrospective design. Overall, the studies involved 1236 patients with IBD, comprising 584 cases of UC and 652 cases of CD, with a mean of reported mean age of 55.01 years. Synthesized results

Anthropometric variables

A meta-analysis of five studies [20–24] revealed a statistically significant weight loss following GLP-1 RAs therapy in IBD cases (MRAW: −5.71 Kg; 95% CI, −9.56 to −1.86; P = 0.01). The included studies were heterogeneous (I² = 78.0%; P < 0.01) (see Fig. 2A). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis revealed that heterogeneity was reduced to 48% by excluding Anderson’s study [20] (Supplemental Fig. 3). Further analysis of three studies [20, 23, 24], including 371 patients, that reported the efficacy of semaglutide indicated significant reduced weight changes among the cases (MRAW: −5.59 Kg; 95% CI, −10.37 to −0.81; P = 0.03), with high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 80.0%; P < 0.01) (see Fig. 2B). In contrast, the subgroup analysis of three articles [20, 21, 23] that investigated liraglutide in 60 cases did not show significant weight loss after intervention (MRAW: −3.27 Kg; 95% CI, −18.81 to 12.26; P = 0.44). The small number of included patients limited the robustness of the Liraglutide subgroup findings (see Supplemental Fig. 2).

Four studies [22, 25–27], involving 494 cases, evaluated the impact of GLP-1 RAs therapy on BMI. The pooled MRAW of these studies was − 2.18 Kg/m2 (95% CI, −3.66 to −0.69; P < 0.01), indicating a significant reduction in BMI. However, heterogeneity was high (I2 = 81.1%, P < 0.01) (see Fig. 3). The sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method revealed that removing either the Levine [26] or St-Pierre [22] studies resulted in a nonsignificant pooled MD (see Supplemental Fig. 4).

Glycemic variable and lipid profile

Glycemic and lipid profile changes were reported in three studies. The pooled results of HbA1c indicated a nonsignificant alteration following treatment with GLP-1 RAs (MRAW: −0.28%; 95% CI, −0.56 to 0.05; P = 0.07), with low observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0; P = 0.77) (see Fig. 3A and Supplemental Fig. 5). A meta-analysis of studies that assessed the efficacy of GLP-1 RAs on lipid profiles demonstrated no significant changes in TChol (MRAW: −13.35 mg/dL; 95% CI, −78.56 to 51.84; P = 0.55), LDL (MRAW: −4.66 mg/dL; 95% CI, −39.61 to 30.28; P = 0.37), and HDL (MRAW: −4.34 mg/dL; 95% CI, −26.05 to 17.59; P = 0.48) after the treatment (see Fig. 3). Between-study heterogeneity was significantly high across studies in TChol (I2 = 93.4%; P < 0.01), HDL (I2 = 95.4%; P < 0.01), and LDL (I2 = 78.8%; P < 0.01) (see Fig. 4). The sensitivity analyses revealed the Levine [26] study as a source of heterogeneity in both Tchol, HDL, LDL analyses (see Supplemental Figs. 6–8). Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

Supplementary Table 5 presents the certainty of the evidence synthesized in the current meta-analysis according to the GRADE system. The certainty of evidence was low for weight outcome and very low for the remaining outcomes. The included studies were observational, and all initially had a low grade of certainty. For BMI, glycemic and lipid outcomes, the imprecision due to small sample size downgraded the certainty to very low.

Adverse events

Table 2 presents the most frequently reported adverse events in the IBD population treated with GLP-1 RAs, as reported in the included studies. The most common side effects were nausea and vomiting, occurring in 115 of 883 patients (13.7%), with reported incidence rates ranging from 3% to 39% across studies. Constipation and diarrhea were observed in 7% (48 of 672) and 5% (40 of 802) of cases, respectively. Some studies reported rare but serious adverse events, including pancreatitis (one case each in the studies by Anderson et al. [20] and Sehgal et al. [23]), IBD flare (one case each in the studies by Anderson et al. [20] and Sehgal et al. [23]), and Bowel obstruction (one case in the study by Weng et al. [27]). Another commonly reported symptom among the studies was abdominal pain. However, the prevalence of this adverse event was not consistently reported across the studies.

Discussion

Despite the conventional belief about the association of IBD with underweight, obesity was reported in 20–40% of these populations [28]. Recent studies reported a marked increase in obesity prevalence among IBD cases [28–30]. Furthermore, several articles showed that obesity significantly influenced the course of disease, response to therapy, and surgical outcomes in patients with IBD [12, 28, 31–33]. These observations highlighted the importance of obesity management options in IBD [31]. GLP-1 RAs, including semaglutide and liraglutide, were initially developed for managing hyperglycemia among patients with diabetes (DM). More recently, these agents emerged as one of the most effective and popular pharmaceutical options for weight loss in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [12, 34].

Several review articles studied various aspects of GLP-1 RAs in IBD populations [33, 35]. Yang et al. conducted a meta-analysis on the association between GLP-1 RAs and IBD-related surgery and suggested a potential protective role for GLP-1 RAs in reducing the need for surgery [36]. Similarly, Bayoumy et al. reported results of a meta-analysis of the effect of GLP-1 RAs on the risk of surgery and hospitalization in cases with IBD [35]. Despite valuable contributions from previous review articles, the current meta-analysis is the first to specifically evaluate changes in weight and key metabolic variables, including BMI, HbA1c, and lipid profiles, following GLP-1 RAs therapy in patients with IBD. The findings revealed significant improvement in anthropometric parameters, including weight and BMI, following the intervention. Weight changes remained significant within the semaglutide subgroup, while the pooled result of weight change in liraglutide was not significant. In contrast, the meta-analyses of HbA1c and lipid profiles demonstrated no significant alterations from baseline following GLP-1 RAs therapy. These findings suggest that, while GLP-1 RAs may offer benefits for weight management in IBD populations, their impact on glycemic and lipid outcomes appears limited in the studied cohorts. It is worth mentioning that a limited number of involved patients restricted the generalizability of analyses of glycemic and lipid outcomes.

The findings of the present study regarding weight and BMI reduction were consistent with the results of a meta-analysis of 47 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating GLP-1 RAs in non-IBD populations [37]. They reported a pooled mean weight loss of 4.57 kg in the overall population, with a significantly greater reduction of 9.19 Kg observed in the non-diabetic subgroup. Since the prevalence of DM among participants of our meta-analysis ranged from 30% to 60%, the pooled weight loss of −5.71 kg in our study is more comparable to the overall population estimate than to the non-diabetic subgroup. Similarly, the pooled BMI change in our study (−2.18 kg/m²) was consistent with the − 2.07 kg/m² reported in the previous meta-analysis. The greater BMI reduction in non-diabetic cases compared to patients with DM was observed in the Levine et al. study on IBD cases [26]. Furthermore, Desai et al. conducted a direct comparison between patients with IBD and those without IBD using propensity score matching (PSM). Their analysis found no significant difference in body weight change following GLP-1 RAs therapy between the two groups [24]. These observations support the comparability of GLP-1 RAs efficacy on anthropometric outcomes between IBD and non-IBD populations. Previous meta-analyses on non-IBD cases and limited investigations on IBD cohorts demonstrated that semaglutide is more effective than liraglutide for weight loss [20, 23, 37, 38]. Our pooled results further support the superior efficacy of semaglutide in reducing body weight among patients with IBD. However, the small number of cases in the liraglutide subgroups (n = 60) restricted the robustness of this observation.

Despite the well-established modification in HbA1c levels following GLP-1 RAs therapy in studies of non-IBD cases with DM, the pooled results of the current meta-analysis did not demonstrate significant improvement in glycemic control [39]. The lower baseline HbA1c levels in the included studies (ranging from 5.45 to 7.42) compared with those in RCTs in the DM population, where baseline HbA1c values often exceed 8.0%, may explain the disparity. The GLP-1 RAs are glucose-dependent, meaning a greater reduction occurs in higher glucose levels [40]. Furthermore, the heterogeneous nature of the study population, which included both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals with IBD, and the limited number of cases may have reduced the overall glycemic effect’s significance.

The most frequently reported adverse events in the included studies were gastrointestinal conditions, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation. The incidence of nausea and vomiting ranged from 10% to 38% in studies with various types of GLP-1 RAs and heterogeneous populations [21, 22, 24, 25]. Articles investigating the GLP-1 RAs side effects in non-IBD populations demonstrated comparable rates of gastrointestinal events [15, 20, 39, 41]. These GI side effects are also listed as common symptoms in the IBD population, independent of GLP-1 RAs intervention. The overlap of these IBD symptoms with GLP-1 RA-associated adverse effects initially raised concerns regarding the safety and tolerability of GLP-1 RAs use in this population, as well as its potential impact on disease course. However, none of the studies in IBD cases indicated associations between GLP-1 RAs therapy and worse disease outcomes [21, 24, 25, 42, 43]. A nationwide cohort study indicated no greater risk of ileus or intestinal obstruction, or potential severe adverse events, due to GLP-1 RAs therapy within the IBD population [44]. Moreover, several studies have found a beneficial role for GLP-1 RAs in individuals with IBD and DM cases. A larger cohort involving 982 IBD cases treated with GLP-1 RAs had a significantly lower rate of need for advanced therapy, hospitalization, and surgery [45]. Gorelik et al. indicated a reduction in IBD-related hospitalizations among similar populations who were treated with GLP-1 RAs compared to those receiving other antidiabetic drugs. The observed improvement was significant only in cases with coexisting obesity, suggesting that the enhanced outcomes may be attributed to weight loss achieved by GLP-1 RAs treatment [42]. A further PSM study on a similar population demonstrated a lower risk of undergoing IBD-related surgery within patients treated with GLP-1 RAs compared to other oral antidiabetic options [43]. These findings support the potential therapeutic role and safety of GLP-1 RAs in improving both disease outcomes and anthropometric measures in patients with IBD and DM, particularly in those with obesity. However, an important consideration is that GLP-1 RAs can alter GI motility and delay gastric emptying, thereby negatively impacting adequate bowel preparation for colonoscopy or video capsule endoscopy in the IBD population [15]. Several studies in the general population have revealed lower Boston Bowel Preparation Scores (BBPS) and higher rates of inadequate preparation in cases who received GLP-1 RAs [46–48]. These findings support concerns regarding the performance of screening and surveillance colonoscopies in cases with IBD treated with GLP-1 RAs. Some experts suggested holding treatment for better preparation in non-IBD cases. However, the evidence on this issue is unclear, and further studies are needed to investigate the association between GLP-1 RAs and colonoscopy quality, especially in the IBD population.

GLP-1 RAs alter the course of IBD through both direct anti-inflammatory effects and indirect mechanisms related to weight loss–mediated reduction in systemic inflammation. Experimental studies in rodent models have shown that GLP-1 can modulate intestinal inflammation by regulating the pro-inflammatory state via the cAMP/NF-κB signaling pathways [49, 50]. Additionally, preclinical research has identified several other anti-inflammatory mechanisms, including the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 (IL-1), as well as alterations in macrophage migration and function [51]. In human studies, reduced GLP-1 mRNA expression has been observed in inflamed colonic tissue from IBD patients compared to non-IBD controls, suggesting impaired endogenous GLP-1 production in active disease [52]. Furthermore, other studies have reported decreased expression of the GLP-1 receptor in patients with active IBD, indicating a possible dysregulation of the GLP-1 signaling axis in the context of intestinal inflammation [53]. These observations support the potential role of GLP-1 RAs in modulating immune responses and improving outcomes in IBD.

Limitations and future directions

The current study has several limitations. All included studies were retrospective in design, which inherently limited the robustness of the findings and prevented the establishment of causality. Furthermore, the lack of control groups treated with other weight loss interventions, including lifestyle modifications or other pharmacotherapy options, in most studies limited the determination of the efficacy of GLP-1 RAs relative to other treatments. Furthermore, the BMI meta-analysis showed high heterogeneity, and the sensitivity analysis indicated that the significant BMI reduction was not robust, as excluding some studies rendered the result non-significant. Due to the limited number of included studies (n < 10), publication bias could not be reliably assessed, and this should be considered a potential limitation for interpreting meta-analytic findings. Additionally, tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, was included alongside selective GLP-1 receptor agonists due to limited data availability and the lack of separate reporting, which may affect the specificity of our findings. Additionally, due to limited reports on the effect of GLP-1 RAs on the course of IBD, we were unable to conduct a meta-analysis of this association, and we suggest that future studies focus on this issue once sufficient reports are published. Next, most included studies did not report the outcomes stratified by subgroups, including CD versus UC cases, diabetic versus non-diabetic patients, or by specific GLP-1 RAs used. Therefore, conducting subgroup analysis for most outcomes was not possible in our meta-analysis. Sample sizes in glycemic and lipid outcomes were very small, potentially reducing the generalizability and statistical power of those outcomes. Moreover, despite prior articles indicating a more significant association between visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and adverse IBD outcomes [54, 55], none of the included studies reported changes in VAT-related parameters. Since recent studies indicated intestinal ultrasound (IUS) as an accurate modality for evaluating IBD activity and measuring VAT [28, 56], we recommend utilizing IUS as a rapid and cost-effective option in future studies. Further large, prospective studies and RCTs comparing the efficacy of different GLP-1 RAs with other obesity management approaches for enhancing IBD outcomes and anthropometric variables are warranted.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide evidence that GLP-1 RAs are effective in promoting significant weight and BMI reductions in patients with IBD. However, GLP-1 RAs did not demonstrate significant improvements in glycemic control or lipid profiles, as indicated by the available data. The GI adverse effects observed were consistent with those reported in non-IBD populations and did not correlate with worsened IBD outcomes. While findings suggest a favorable therapeutic role and safety profile of GLP-1 RAs in managing overweight and obesity in IBD patients, limitations related to retrospective designs, small sample sizes, and potential risk of publication bias in most included studies underscore the need for larger prospective studies and randomized controlled trials.
