Time Passes, The Valve Migrates
Authors: Hüseyin Ayhan, Betül Banu Karasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Case reports play a pivotal role in recognizing rare diseases, unusual clinical presentations, and previously unrecognized complications. The present report by Tadokoro et al1 highlights a rare but clinically important complication of Takayasu arteritis (TAK): progressive prosthetic aortic valve migration into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). Although only 1 similar case has been reported to date, each additional report contributes meaningfully to the growing body of evidence by increasing clinical awareness, facilitating timely diagnosis, and informing management strategies.2

Unlike the more familiar presentation of prosthetic valve dehiscence with overt paravalvular leak (PVL), the investigators describe 2 patients who developed progressive prosthetic valve migration into the LVOT without significant PVL. One patient presented with concealed prosthetic valve detachment, whereas the other developed functional mitral stenosis caused by mechanical impingement of the anterior mitral leaflet, ultimately requiring redo double-valve replacement. These observations broaden the spectrum of prosthetic valve complications associated with inflammatory aortopathy and remind clinicians that prosthetic dysfunction may present with atypical hemodynamic features.

TAK remains one of the most challenging forms of large-vessel vasculitis because of its chronic inflammatory involvement of the aorta and adjacent cardiovascular structures. Progressive inflammation may result in arterial stenosis, occlusion, aneurysm formation, aortic root remodeling, and valvular dysfunction, creating substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment supported by inflammatory markers and multimodality vascular imaging, because laboratory findings alone frequently underestimate disease activity.3 This experience illustrates that challenge well. In 1 patient, vasculitis remained unrecognized until recurrent prosthetic valve dysfunction prompted further investigation and histopathological confirmation, whereas the second patient had an established diagnosis before reoperation. Delayed diagnosis may permit ongoing vascular injury, progressive valvular destruction, and complex complications requiring repeat surgery.

The cases also underscore the value of multimodality imaging. Although echocardiography remains indispensable for hemodynamic assessment, it may fail to identify concealed prosthetic migration when significant PVL is absent. In such situations, computed tomography provides critical anatomical information for establishing the diagnosis, defining the extent of structural involvement, and guiding surgical planning. Together, these complementary imaging modalities enable a more comprehensive assessment of both prosthetic valve dysfunction and the underlying inflammatory process.

Immunosuppressive therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment in TAK, aiming to control vascular inflammation and prevent progressive structural damage. Glucocorticoids remain the initial therapy, whereas conventional and biologic disease-modifying agents are increasingly used to maintain remission and reduce relapses. Nevertheless, advanced cardiovascular complications frequently necessitate surgical intervention despite optimal medical therapy.4,5

These cases further emphasize that successful management depends not only on technically appropriate surgery but also on sustained control of inflammatory disease activity, underscoring the importance of close collaboration among cardiovascular surgeons, cardiologists, rheumatologists, and cardiovascular imaging specialists. The chronic granulomatous inflammation characteristic of TAK progressively disrupts the structural integrity of the aortic wall and periannular tissues, predisposing affected patients to late cardiovascular complications. Persistent vascular inflammation may weaken periannular tissues, promote aortic root remodeling, and compromise prosthetic valve stability over time, even after an initially successful operation. Accordingly, surgical planning—including the extent of root reconstruction, prosthesis selection, and the timing of reintervention—should be integrated with careful assessment of disease activity and optimization of immunosuppressive therapy to achieve durable long-term outcomes. Ultimately, these observations reinforce a fundamental principle in TAK: disease control and valve durability are inseparable.

Future research should focus on several unresolved issues. Strategies for comprehensive preoperative evaluation, ideally through a multidisciplinary heart team including rheumatologists, cardiologists, and cardiovascular surgeons, should be refined to ensure timely recognition of TAK and other inflammatory vasculitides before valve intervention. Comparative studies are needed to determine whether specific surgical techniques, prosthesis types, or annular reinforcement strategies can reduce the risk of prosthetic valve dehiscence and late migration in patients with inflammatory aortopathy. In parallel, prospective investigations should clarify whether earlier recognition of disease activity and timely initiation of intensive immunosuppressive, particularly biologic, therapies can prevent progressive annular destruction and subsequent prosthetic complications. Finally, standardized imaging surveillance protocols, including quantitative CT- or multimodality imaging-based assessment of prosthetic valve position, may enable detection of subclinical migration before clinically significant hemodynamic deterioration develops, allowing earlier intervention and potentially improving long-term outcomes.

These cases also underscore the importance of identifying the underlying etiology before and after valve surgery, particularly in younger patients. Recurrent valve dysfunction, progressive aortic root enlargement, or otherwise unexplained aortopathy should raise suspicion for TAK and prompt comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. Early recognition and appropriate immunosuppressive treatment, combined with carefully timed surgical intervention, may reduce the risk of recurrent prosthetic valve complications and the need for repeated reoperations.

In conclusion, this instructive report broadens our understanding of prosthetic valve complications in TAK by highlighting prosthetic aortic valve migration into the LVOT as a rare and likely underrecognized clinical manifestation rather than a typical presentation of prosthetic valve dehiscence. Beyond documenting an unusual complication, it reinforces that disease control and valve durability are inseparable in inflammatory aortopathy. Ultimately, this report reinforces a broader principle applicable to inflammatory aortopathies and vasculitides alike: durable surgical outcomes depend not only on technical excellence but also on sustained control of the underlying inflammatory process.
