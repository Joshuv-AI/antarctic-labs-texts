Cutting-edge developments in computer-aided anti-HIV drug design
Authors: Weiying Yang, Ye Wang, Chenda Feng, Wenjing Tang, Lichao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), caused by the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), remains a major global public health issue. Current antiretroviral therapy (ART) does not fully eradicate HIV infection, and the virus can establish latent reservoirs in the body that are challenging to eliminate. Consequently, the development of new anti-HIV medications remains a critical necessity. Traditional drug development methods are time-consuming, financially demanding, and carry a high risk of failure. In contrast, computer-aided drug design (CADD) offers a promising alternative by leveraging computational techniques and bioinformatics data to accelerate drug development, reduce costs, and improve the success rates. This literature review provides a comprehensive overview of the CADD process, identifies key proteins involved in the HIV life cycle that serve as drug targets. It highlights successful examples where CADD has contributed to the development of inhibitors targeting these proteins. Additionally, the review discusses emerging strategies and limitations of CADD in anti-HIV drug research, offering insights into its potential to revolutionize future therapeutic efforts.

Introduction

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), which was first recognized in 1981, is a sexually transmitted infection (STI) and a blood-borne disease affecting humans, currently ranking among the top three infectious diseases globally (1). The primary pathogen responsible for AIDS is human immunodeficiency virus type I (HIV-1) (2, 3). Since the onset of the AIDS epidemic, approximately 42.3 million individuals [ranging from 35.7 million to 51.1 million] have succumbed to AIDS-related conditions, according to World Health Organization (WHO) data (http://UNAIDS.org). The predominant mode of HIV transmission is sexual contact, which includes unsafe homosexual, heterosexual, and bisexual interactions (4, 5). Other transmission routes encompass mother-to-child transmission (which can occur during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding) and transmission through blood and blood products (such as sharing needles for intravenous drug use, unsafe medical practices, and tattooing) (6, 7). The WHO reports that HIV remains a significant global public health issue, having resulted in 40.4 million deaths to date [with estimates between 32.9 million and 51.3 million] and continues to spread worldwide.

HIV was first isolated and characterized in 1983 and is classified as a single-stranded ribonucleic acid (RNA) lentivirus with a genome comprising nine distinct genes, each transcribed into specific proteins (8–10). Based on genetic and antigenic differences, HIV is divided into two types: HIV-1 and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-2), with HIV-1 being the more virulent and widely transmitted strain (11–13). The HIV-1 genome is categorized into three major groups, M, N, and O, along with numerous subtypes and variants (14). While HIV-1 is the primary driver of the global pandemic, HIV-2 is mostly restricted to West Africa (15, 16). The viral life cycle of HIV involves several key components, including reverse transcriptase (RT), integrase (IN), protease (PR), glycoprotein 120 (gp120), and glycoprotein 41 (gp41), which serve as potential drug targets for treating HIV infections (17, 18). HIV-1 reverse transcriptase is a crucial enzyme that facilitates the conversion of single-stranded viral RNA (ssRNA) into double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (dsDNA), which then integrates into the host’s genomic DNA. This enzyme exhibits both RNA-dependent and DNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity, as well as RNase H activity, playing a vital role in the early stages of the viral life cycle. HIV-1 integrase is responsible for inserting the dsDNA of the viral genome, produced by reverse transcriptase, into the host’s chromosomal DNA. Additionally, HIV-1 protease, a dimeric aspartic acid protease, is tasked with breaking down Gag-Pol polypeptides into viral proteins. The envelope glycoprotein gp120 (Gp120) is found on the surface of the HIV envelope, while gp41 is involved in the fusion of the virus with the host cell.

As of now, the primary approaches for preventing and treating HIV consist of vaccination and antiretroviral therapy (ART). The scientific community largely agrees that the best strategy to manage and eventually eradicate the HIV epidemic is to create a safe, effective, affordable, and easily accessible preventive vaccine for AIDS on a global scale (19, 20). Recent progress in HIV-1 vaccines includes the ability to stimulate the production of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) against the HIV-1 virus. Additionally, the HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein (Env) trimer is identified as the protein responsible for starting viral entry and is the exclusive target for broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) (21). So far, the only Phase III clinical trial for an HIV-1 vaccine that has demonstrated effectiveness is RV144 (22). Nevertheless, the advancement of vaccine development has been significantly impeded by the considerable variability exhibited by HIV-1 reverse transcriptase. The typical duration for the development of conventional vaccines, commencing from the preclinical phase, exceeds a decade (23). It is estimated that the development of a new vaccine candidate utilizing current technologies would incur costs exceeding $500 million, in addition to supplementary expenses ranging from $50 million to $700 million for the establishment of necessary facilities and equipment. HIV exhibits a significant degree of genetic variability, with substantial differences observed among its subtypes, complicating the creation of a vaccine that can effectively address all variant forms of the infection. Furthermore, HIV possesses a remarkable capacity for immune evasion, allowing it to circumvent the host’s immune response. Consequently, no viable HIV vaccine has been developed to date. This underscores the urgent need for the development of effective antiretroviral drugs against HIV. However, the research and development process for new pharmaceuticals is inherently complex, characterized by substantial costs, considerable risks, and extended timelines. The development of an innovative drug typically requires an investment of billions of dollars and a timeframe of 10 to 15 years before it can be brought to market (24).

PR, RT, and IN represent the primary targets for the development of potential anti-AIDS pharmacological agents (25, 26). The objective of this review is to conduct a systematic examination of the utilization of computer-aided drug design (CADD) in the context of small-molecule drug research for HIV, alongside recent advancements in associated domains. This review will encompass an overview of the evolution of CADD methodologies and tools, an exploration of their applications in HIV drug research, and a comprehensive analysis of the biological characteristics and potential drug targets associated with HIV (Figure 1). Furthermore, we will highlight the significant role of CADD in virtual screening, drug design, and toxicity assessment. Additionally, we will address the contributions of CADD in emergency response scenarios during outbreaks and consider prospective future developments in this field.

Flow chart of commonly used computer-aided drug design (CADD) methods.

While recent reviews frequently discuss computational anti-HIV drug discovery, they generally concentrate on isolated targets or traditional docking methodologies. The academic community still lacks a systematic evaluation of methodological evolution in CADD. Specifically, current literature misses a comprehensive analysis of the transition from static docking to dynamic simulations, such as molecular dynamics (MD) and Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA). Furthermore, we need a clearer understanding of how researchers integrate emerging artificial intelligence-driven de novo design across all major HIV enzymes (RT, IN, and PR). Our review stands apart from previous publications because we do more than simply summarize recent active compounds. We critically assess validation deficits in current computational pipelines, notably the omission of absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiling. Ultimately, we provide a standardized methodological blueprint to bridge the gap between in silico predictions and in vitro/in vivo realities. This work offers researchers actionable insights to develop next-generation therapies against multidrug-resistant HIV strains.

Research methodology

This manuscript explicitly serves as a narrative review, aimed at systematically summarizing the application value of computer-aided drug design (CADD) in anti-HIV drug research and development. A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed and Web of Science. In addition, the official FDA website (www.accessdata.fda.gov) was used to retrieve relevant anti-HIV drug prescribing information. The publication date limits spanned from January 1, 2021, to April 30, 2026. The core search string with Boolean operators was: ((HIV OR AIDS) AND (computer-aided drug design OR CADD) AND (reverse transcriptase OR integrase OR protease OR gp120 OR gp41)). Only English-language publications were included. The inclusion criteria were original research articles, review papers, and official FDA prescribing information relevant to the application of CADD in anti-HIV drug development. Exclusion criteria comprised conference abstracts, preprints, non-English publications, and studies with no direct relevance to CADD techniques or HIV drug targets. Regarding the screening procedure, the initial database search yielded 789 potentially relevant records. After removing 28 duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened, and approximately 200 records were retained for full-text assessment. During full-text screening, each record was evaluated with respect to study content, target relevance, and the application of CADD techniques. Records with irrelevant themes, redundant information, or failure to meet inclusion criteria were excluded. Finally, 119 eligible records were included in this narrative review.

HIV life cycle

The HIV life cycle represents a multifaceted biological process encompassing several critical stages: attachment, fusion, and entry into the host cell; reverse transcription of viral RNA; integration; transcription; translation; assembly; and subsequent release of viral progeny (Figure 2). Throughout this cycle, various interactions between the virus and the host cell are established. Antiretroviral therapies often target specific proteins that are integral to distinct phases of the viral life cycle. Notably, the HIV envelope glycoprotein, gp120, engages with the CD4 receptor and chemokine co-receptors CCR5 or C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) located on the host cell surface. This interaction facilitates the fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane, allowing the virus to penetrate and infect normal cells within the organism (37, 38). The viral genome, along with essential enzymes including RT, IN, and PR, is released into the cytoplasm of the host cell. Subsequently, the HIV viral capsid interacts with the nuclear pore complex (NPC) and facilitates its entry into the nucleus of the cell (39). HIV viral RNA undergoes reverse transcription facilitated by intra-nuclear RT, after which IN incorporates the reverse transcribed viral DNA into the chromosomal DNA of the host cell (37, 40). The viral DNA that is integrated into the host genome is referred to as the provirus, which remains in a latent state until it is triggered by the host cell (41). The protovirus undergoes transcription into messenger RNA (mRNA) facilitated by the host cell’s RNA polymerase II. Subsequently, the mRNA is spliced and transported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (42). Viral mRNA is translated into viral proteins by the ribosomes of the host cell (43). Gag polyproteins undergo cleavage by viral proteases, resulting in the production of structural proteins such as matrix (MA), capsid (CA), and nucleocapsid (NC) (39, 44). The Gag-Pol polyprotein undergoes cleavage to yield viral enzymes, such as RT, IN, and PR (45). Viral proteins and RNA genomes are organized into viral particles at the membrane of the host cell. Proteins such as Gag and Gag-pol interact with the viral RNA genome to create the nucleocapsid core (46). The core is encased by a lipid bilayer that originates from the membrane of the host cell and incorporates the viral envelope glycoprotein. Ultimately, fully developed viral particles exit the host cell membrane, acquiring the envelope during this process (47–49). This process necessitates the collaboration of viral RNA, Gag-Pol precursors, various enzymes, and membrane proteins.

Schematic diagram of the human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) life cycle: Step 1: The virus attaches to the host cell surface via the gp120 protein on the surface of HIV-1, then fuses with and enters the cell; Step 2, viral RNA is reverse transcribed into DNA by the enzyme reverse transcriptase (RT), and the viral DNA is integrated into the host cell's genome by integrase (IN); Step 3, the integrated viral DNA, or provirus, is transcribed into RNA and translated into viral proteins by the host cell's machinery; Step 4, the newly synthesized viral proteins and RNAs self-assemble into new viral particles at the host cell membrane, and the viral protease (PR) cleaves the Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins into their functional forms; Step 5, once the viral particles are assembled, they bud from the host cell and are released, ready to infect additional cells.

Application of computer-aided drug design

CADD employs computational and bioinformatics techniques to facilitate drug discovery and development, significantly reducing research expenditures compared to conventional methodologies (50). The prevalent generic CADD approaches encompass structure-based drug design (SBDD), ligand-based drug design (LBDD), and de novo drug design (DNDD). Key techniques employed in anti-HIV drug discovery include MD simulation (51), molecular docking (52), MM/GBSA (53), virtual screening (VS) (54), pharmacological modeling (55), quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) modelling (56), and assessments of ADMET (57). These tools, used independently or in combination with experimental methods, have facilitated inhibitor repurposing, novel drug design, and binding affinity optimization (58).

For targets with known three-dimensional structures, SBDD leverages molecular docking and virtual screening informed by experimental or predicted structures from databases such as the Protein Data Bank (PDB), with AlphaFold and RosettaFold providing valuable structural predictions when experimental data are unavailable (59–61). Using repositories like PubChem (62), researchers can efficiently screen or design small-molecule ligands, construct complex structures through molecular simulations, and rigorously evaluate binding energies and physicochemical properties (63).

LBDD is a strategy employed in the identification of lead compounds through the analysis of the structural characteristics of ligands that exhibit specific binding affinity to a target, particularly in instances where the three-dimensional structure of the protein target remains undetermined (64). Widely utilized methods in ligand-based drug design (LBDD) encompass similarity searches, scaffold hopping, QSAR, and pharmacophore modeling (65).

Furthermore, recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have markedly progressed across various scientific fields. The convergence of these technologies with computer-aided drug design is transforming the drug discovery process. De novo drug design represents a significant domain that has experienced substantial advancements due to generative AI and diffusion models (66). Unlike traditional generative adversarial networks, diffusion models excel at generating 3D molecular structures by learning the complex probability distributions of molecular geometries, enabling the direct generation of novel, synthesizable anti-HIV scaffolds that are stereochemically stable. To further refine these generated structures, reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms are increasingly integrated with generative frameworks. By employing multi-objective reward functions, RL dynamically optimizes the molecules, simultaneously maximizing critical parameters such as target binding affinity, quantitative estimate of drug-likeness (QED), and synthetic accessibility (SA) to ensure the clinical viability of theoretical candidates (67). Additionally, graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly improved the extraction of molecular features by capturing complex inter-atomic topological connections, outperforming traditional 1D and 2D molecular representations in predicting drug-target binding affinities (68). Models powered by artificial intelligence, such as target-conditioned transformer models, can now utilize AlphaFold-derived latent protein embeddings to seamlessly generate target-specific molecules across diverse protein families (68). Furthermore, multi-step high-throughput virtual screening pipelines integrate graph neural networks with absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) predictions and drug-likeness rules to ensure optimal pharmacokinetic profiles (68).

Function and structure of target proteins for anti-HIV drug candidates

Reverse transcriptase

RT is a critical enzyme in the HIV infection lifecycle, serving two primary functions: it acts as an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase (RDDP) and a DNA-dependent DNA polymerase (DDDP) (Figure 3). Additionally, it possesses ribonuclease H (RNase H) activity, which enables it to degrade the RNA component of RNA/DNA double-stranded hybrids (69). The initiation of host cell infection involves the transcription of the viral single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) genome into double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) facilitated by the enzyme reverse transcriptase. Consequently, reverse transcriptase is regarded as a critical molecular target in the development of therapeutic agents aimed at combating HIV (70).

HIV reverse transcriptase (RT) is a heterodimer with a molecular weight of 117 kDa, composed of a 66 kDa subunit (p66) and a 51 kDa subunit (p51), which is derived from p66. The p66/p51 HIV-1 reverse transcriptase possesses a functional DNA polymerase activity as well as an active site for ribonuclease H (RNase H), both of which are situated in spatially distinct regions within the p66 subunit (71). The structural domain of the p66 polymerase within reverse transcriptase (RT) is categorized into four distinct sub-structural domains: the finger domain, comprising residues 1–84 and 119-154; the palm domain, encompassing residues 85–118 and 155-241; the thumb domain, which includes residues 242-313; and the linkage domain, consisting of residues 314-426 (72). The p51 subunit is derived from the proteolytic cleavage of p66, which does not possess the structural domain characteristic of RNase H (73). As illustrated in Figure 3, the inhibitor binds tightly within the active site. Specifically, key amino acid residues, including Lys101, Tyr181, Tyr188, and Trp229, form critical interactions within the binding pocket, determining the spatial conformation and therapeutic efficacy of the compound against the HIV-1 RT target. Antiretroviral medications primarily consist of NRTIs and NNRTIs. NRTIs function by competitively inhibiting the HIV reverse transcriptase enzyme, thereby obstructing the reverse transcription process of viral RNA into DNA (48, 74). Frequently utilized nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) encompass zidovudine (AZT) (75), lamivudine (3TC) (76), and abacavir (ABC) (77), among others. NNRTIs function by directly binding to the inactive domain of reverse transcriptase, resulting in a conformational alteration that inhibits its enzymatic activity. Notable examples of NNRTIs include nevirapine (NVP) (78), efavirenz (EFV) (29), and rilpivirine (RPV) (79). Nevirapine interacts with HIV-1 reverse transcriptase, inhibiting its catalytic site and consequently obstructing the replication of the virus (2, 80). Three frequently utilized non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs), namely efavirenz, nevirapine, and delavirdine, demonstrate efficacy in suppressing the replication of wild-type (WT) HIV strains. However, their effectiveness is significantly diminished against clinically relevant RT mutant strains, such as Y188C, Y181C, K103N, and L100I. These mutant viruses have acquired substantial resistance to the majority of currently available NNRTIs (81).

To address the challenges posed by clinically relevant reverse transcriptase mutant strains, such as K103N, researchers are increasingly turning to graph neural networks (GNNs) enhanced by self-supervised learning (SSL) pre-training strategies (68). By leveraging robust node-level and graph-level representations of molecular structures, computational screening can efficiently navigate data scarcity to rapidly identify novel non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) that bypass traditional resistance mutations (68). Furthermore, these ML-based predictions are successfully integrated with in-silico structure-based screening, including molecular docking and MD simulations, continuously refining repurposed candidate molecules to maximize binding affinity against mutant strains while preserving favorable pharmacokinetic profiles (68). To explore key interactions within the non-nucleoside inhibitor binding pocket (NNIBP), Sakakibara and colleagues employed a classical bioisostere strategy to design and synthesize 22 novel triazine-based NNRTIs, and conducted molecular docking studies using MacroModel software with the AMBER* force field (82). Their workflow generated over 3,000 ligand conformers and successfully identified key hydrogen bonds (e.g., between the 6-amino group and the Lys101 backbone) and hydrophobic contacts, including π-π stacking with Tyr181 and Tyr188 (82). However, this study exemplifies the limitations of the early Docking-only paradigm: its scoring function was not validated by MD simulations to assess complex stability, nor were binding free energies estimated using methods such as Molecular Mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area (MM/PBSA) or MM/GBSA. Moreover, the receptor was treated as rigid, completely neglecting protein flexibility. As a result, while the predicted binding modes could rationalize activity, they lacked dynamic validation and reliability.

Methodologically more advanced is the work by Su and co-workers. In their investigation of tetrahydroquinoline-based allosteric inhibitors, they integrated computational modeling with X-ray crystallography. Their workflow generated approximately 100 ligand conformations and employed energy minimization with the MMFFs force field, successfully predicting that a cis-cyclopropyl ring could serve as a structural surrogate for a sulfur-methylene linker, a hypothesis subsequently validated by experimental potency (the cis-enantiomer 16) and crystallographic structures (PDB: 3I0R and 3I0S) (83). Compared to pure docking approaches, this study demonstrates the higher reliability of a docking and crystallographic validation strategy. Nevertheless, its methodological limitations remain evident. The modeling still focused primarily on ligand conformational analysis without integrating receptor flexibility; MD simulations were not performed to assess the temporal stability of the complexes; and no free-energy calculations were employed to quantitatively rank binding affinities across the series.

Zhang and colleagues utilized AutoDock Vina for rigid docking of S-DABO compounds based on the 1rt1 crystal structure. Their study successfully mapped the hydrogen-bond network with key residues Lys101 and Lys103 and, through superimposition with the active compound 4j, qualitatively elucidated how C-6 substituents affect π-π stacking interactions. However, their computational workflow represents another widely adopted static docking paradigm: relying solely on empirical scoring functions to evaluate binding modes, without employing MD simulations to account for target flexibility or performing subsequent free-energy calculations. Consequently, the quantitative interpretation and validation of structure–activity relationship (SAR) trends remain severely constrained (84).

To address clinically relevant drug-resistant mutants, Chander and colleagues employed a ligand-based molecular hybridization strategy to design tetrahydroquinoline-piperazine derivatives (the specific chemical structures of compounds 6a-o are detailed in the original publication) (85). Their computational approach demonstrates significant progress at the level of docking and multi-mutant processing. Using the Glide module (XP mode) within the Schrödinger suite, they performed systematic cross-docking against wild-type (PDB: 3MEE) and two mutant structures (K103N: 3TAM; K103N/Y181C: 4I2Q), and validated the reproducibility of their protocol by redocking co-crystallized ligands (RMSD values of 0.6, 1.1, and 0.9 Å, respectively). More importantly, their analysis successfully correlated the disruption of a dual hydrogen-bond network (ligand with Lys101/Lys103) with mutation-induced structural disorientation, providing computational insights into resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, the integration of ADMET predictions via three platforms (QikProp, admetSAR, and FAF-Drugs) reflects a forward-looking awareness of early-stage drug-likeness filtering. Nevertheless, the depth of their simulations remains limited. The absence of MD simulations and MM/GBSA free-energy calculations to refine binding conformations, and the removal of crystallographic water molecules prior to docking, neglect receptor flexibility and water-mediated polar interactions, thereby limiting the precise quantitative evaluation of relative binding affinities.

Most studies stop at static docking or conformational searches, lacking MD simulation-supported dynamic conformational analysis and binding free-energy calculations (MM/GBSA), which means the predicted binding modes lack thermodynamic and kinetic cross-validation. Even more striking is that virtually none of the studies systematically performed pre-synthetic ADMET filtering (neither Su (83) nor Zhang (84) employed computational ADMET tools; only Su (83) provided experimental PK data post-synthetically). This directly contributes to the precipitous drop in cellular activity observed for some highly potent molecules [such as the cytotoxicity issues noted by Zhang et al. (86)]. This series of methodological discontinuities not only undermines the practical value of computational predictions in guiding synthetic optimization but also highlights the urgent need to establish a standardized docking, MD, MM/GBSA, and ADMET prediction validation and screening pipeline for future RT-targeted CADD research, in order to genuinely bridge the gap between computation and experimentation.

Integrase

HIV-1 IN plays a crucial role in the integration of viral DNA, which is generated through RT, into the host genome (Figure 4). This process is vital for the completion of the viral replication cycle, making integrase an indispensable enzyme in the life cycle of HIV-1 (87). INSTIs demonstrate efficacy in obstructing this mechanism. Frequently utilized INSTIs encompass polyenes, including dolutegravir (DTG) (88) and bictegravir (BIC) (89). In contrast to PR and RT, there are currently no identified functional analogs of IN, nor is there substantial evidence of cytotoxic effects associated with IN inhibitors in human cells (90).

HIV-1 IN is encoded by the pol gene and comprises 288 amino acids, resulting in a molecular weight of 32 kDa. This protein can be categorized into three distinct structural domains: the N-terminal domain (NTD), which spans residues 1 to 49; the catalytic core domain (CCD), encompassing residues 50 to 212; and the C-terminal domain (CTD), which includes residues 213 to 288 (91). The zinc finger located within the N-terminal domain (NTD) contributes to the stability of the integrase enzyme. Additionally, the effective chelation of the DDE motifs—specifically Asp64, Asp116, and Glu152—with the two magnesium ions (Mg2+) present in the central catalytic domain (CCD) is essential for sustaining optimal enzymatic activity. Furthermore, the C-terminal domain (CTD) functions as a nonspecific binding site for viral DNA (92, 93).

The structural modeling of HIV-1 integrase, particularly the functional intasome complex, has historically been challenging due to its high flexibility and the lack of complete crystallographic data. The advent of AlphaFold-enabled structure prediction has revolutionized this aspect of integrase inhibitor design (94). AlphaFold and its subsequent iterations allow for the highly accurate prediction of full-length integrase structures and their dynamic conformational states. By utilizing these AI-predicted models, structure-based drug design can now more reliably simulate the binding of novel integrase strand transfer inhibitors within the catalytic core domain and predict how the enzyme interacts with viral DNA, significantly reducing the reliance on static and often incomplete experimental models.

A total of ten primary inhibitors of HIV-1 IN have been identified in the literature. These include diketo acids, diazonaphthalene derivatives, quinolones, pyrimidinones, thiazathiazepines, polyhydroxyaromatic compounds, disulfides, benzenesulfonamides, coumarin derivatives, and salicylhydrazide derivatives, among others (95, 96). Diketo acids represent the most extensively researched and promising inhibitors of HIV-1 IN, exhibiting significant potency, selectivity, and minimal toxicity (97). Raltegravir was the inaugural integrase inhibitor to receive approval from the FDA in 2007 (98), subsequently followed by the approvals of second-generation agents with higher resistance barriers such as dolutegravir (99) and bictegravir (100), and more recently, the long-acting injectable formulation cabotegravir (101). As detailed in Table 2, several computational pipelines have been employed in recent years to optimize the design of IN inhibitors. Methodologically, Sharma and colleagues advanced beyond earlier Docking-only studies by employing a homology model of full-length HIV-1 IN bound to DNA, constructed from the PFV crystal structure-a clear improvement over their previous work using partial IN structures without DNA (97). Using this more biologically relevant model, the docking successfully identified a novel binding region that did not interact with resistance-associated residues Gln148 and Asn155. However, the docking protocol relied on a conventional scoring function without explicitly treating protein flexibility, and no MD simulations were performed to assess complex stability or refine binding poses. The absence of MM/PBSA or MM/GBSA free-energy calculations further limits the quantitative reliability of the predicted binding modes, representing a notable gap between computational prediction and rigorous validation.

Patel and colleagues employed a docking-based strategy using the PFV intasome structure (PDB: 3OYL) as the template to design bridged tricyclic pyrimidinone carboxamides as IN strand transfer inhibitors (96). Their docking models provided a clear structural rationale for the observed SAR, particularly highlighting the oxamide moiety’s role in hydrogen bonding with the 3’-OH of the terminal adenosine of viral DNA. The predictive utility of the docking received partial validation through correlation with measured antiviral activity. However, like Sharma et al., this study did not perform MD simulations to assess the dynamic stability of the complexes or binding free-energy calculations. Furthermore, the absence of rigorous redocking validation, such as RMSD quantification against known co-crystal structures, represents a notable methodological gap that limits confidence in the predicted binding modes.

Employing a similar docking-only approach, Oliaie and colleagues used AutoDock Vina with the PFV intasome structure (PDB: 3OYA) to design 2-hydroxyacetophenone analogues as IN inhibitors (102). A notable feature of their workflow was the superposition of compound 7’s docked pose with the co-crystallized ligand raltegravir, which demonstrated that the designed compound replicates the reference binding mechanism. The docking results aligned with biological activity data, as the most potent compounds (7 and 9) exhibited the expected chelation interactions with Mg2+ ions. However, this study also lacked MD simulations to account for receptor flexibility, MM/PBSA or MM/GBSA free-energy calculations, and redocking validation with RMSD quantification, all of which are essential for establishing the reliability of docking predictions and quantifying the confidence in binding mode hypotheses.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate a common pattern in computational IN inhibitor research: the frequent reliance on docking alone without subsequent dynamic refinement. The absence of MD simulations and free-energy calculations represents a significant limitation, as IN is a flexible enzyme, particularly in the CCD and CTD regions. The lack of rigorous validation protocols, such as redocking co-crystallized ligands to assess RMSD, also undermines confidence in the predicted binding modes. Furthermore, the treatment of receptor flexibility (or lack thereof) is a critical factor that distinguishes more reliable from less reliable computational workflows.

ADMET considerations: an assessment of specific studies. The integration of ADMET prediction into IN inhibitor discovery has been limited but is gaining attention. Among the studies reviewed, none of the above-mentioned IN inhibitor studies (96, 97, 102) performed systematic ADMET profiling of their synthesized compounds. S.S. Oliaie et al. (102) reported experimental cytotoxicity data (CC50 values) but did not provide in silico ADMET predictions or discuss drug-likeness parameters. Similarly, Sharma et al. (97) and Patel et al. (96) focused primarily on enzyme inhibition and antiviral activity without assessing pharmacokinetic properties such as absorption, metabolism, or toxicity. This represents a missed opportunity, as early ADMET prediction could have identified potential liabilities such as poor oral bioavailability, high plasma protein binding, or cytochrome P450 inhibition. For example, the limited antiviral activity observed for some compounds in cellular assays may partially reflect poor cell permeability or extensive protein binding, but without systematic ADMET profiling we cannot confirm this. Future studies should incorporate ADMET prediction tools such as QikProp, admetSAR, or SwissADME to provide a more comprehensive assessment of therapeutic potential, as recommended by current CADD best practices (102).

Protease

HIV-1 PR is a critical enzyme in the HIV life cycle, as it facilitates the cleavage of Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins, resulting in the generation of a singular mature protein (Figure 5) (103, 104). The inhibition of HIV PR plays a crucial role in obstructing the maturation and subsequent release of new viral particles, which in turn hinders the dissemination of the virus. Frequently utilized PIs encompass lopinavir (LPV) (86), ritonavir (RTV), among others (105).

HIV-1 PR is characterized as a homodimeric aspartic acid protease, featuring a substrate-binding pocket that comprises the catalytic triad formed by Asp25(25’), Thr26(26’), and Gly27(27’), along with a valve region (106). The active site of HIV-1 PR is organized into eight distinct subsites, designated as S4, S3, S2, S1, S1’, S2’, S3’, and S4’. These subsites correspond to eight substrate residues labeled as P4, P3, P2, P1, P1’, P2’, P3’, and P4’, with the cleavage bond situated between the residues P1 and P1’ (107). HIV-1 PR has emerged as a significant molecular target for the development of anti-HIV pharmacological agents. The HIV-1 PR domain consists of 99 amino acids and adopts a predominantly β-sheet structure, with each monomer containing one α-helix and two antiparallel β-sheets. The PR functions as a homodimer, and the active site is formed at the dimer interface and covered by flexible β-hairpin (flap) regions. The catalytic residues Asp25 and Asp125 are located within the active-site region, while the flap regions play an important role in substrate and inhibitor recognition and contribute to the formation of the inhibitor-binding pocket (Figure 6) (108). This domain corresponds to database entries such as Pfam PF00077 and PROSITE PS00141. Common resistance-associated mutations include V82A, I84V, and L90M, which are situated near the active site or the dimerization interface. These mutations can alter the geometric conformation of the subsites, thereby synergistically reducing inhibitor binding affinity, and represent a critical mechanistic basis for clinical multidrug resistance (MDR) (109).

In the context of HIV-1 protease, predicting how specific mutations confer multidrug resistance remains a significant hurdle. Protein language models (PLMs) have emerged as a cutting-edge solution to this problem (110). By training on vast databases of protein sequences, protein language models acquire rich representational capabilities and can capture the evolutionary constraints and mutational landscapes of the protease enzyme (110). These models can accurately predict the impact of distal minor mutations on the flap dynamics and the overall geometry of the active site (110). Consequently, generative AI methodologies can use these insights to design next-generation protease inhibitors that maintain their efficacy across a broad spectrum of clinical isolates by targeting evolutionarily conserved, mutation-resistant pockets.

To combat these resistance profiles, recent research has heavily relied on CADD, with the methodological characteristics of representative PR-targeted studies outlined in Table 2. A representative example of docking-based virtual screening applied to PR is the work of Zhou and colleagues, who designed pyrrolidine-derived P2 ligands and employed the Schrödinger suite for molecular docking and homology modeling against both WT and drug-resistant PR variants (111). Their most potent compound 34b exhibited exceptional enzyme inhibitory activity with an IC50 of 0.32 nM and maintained efficacy against multidrug-resistant variants with an EC50 of 1.90 µM. The docking models revealed extensive hydrogen bonding and salt bridge networks between the inhibitor and backbone residues of the protease. However, this study relied entirely on static docking models. The authors did not perform MD simulations to assess the stability of the protein-ligand complexes over time, nor did they compute binding free energies using MM/PBSA or MM/GBSA methods. Without these dynamic validation steps, the predicted binding modes lack rigorous confirmation, and the influence of receptor flexibility on inhibitor binding remains uncharacterized.

Recognizing the limitations of static docking for PR, Venkatachalam and colleagues emphasized in their review the critical importance of MD simulations and free-energy calculations for capturing the conformational dynamics of PR mutants (106). Their analysis demonstrates that computational workflows which explicitly account for receptor flexibility, including two-layered ONIOM quantum mechanics-molecular mechanics models, achieve substantially higher predictive reliability for resistance mechanism assessment than rigid docking alone. The review highlights that MD simulations can sample the open and closed conformations of the flaps, providing thermodynamic and kinetic information that docking methods cannot access. This methodological perspective directly addresses the deficiencies observed in docking-only studies and establishes a benchmark for what constitutes a rigorous computational validation pipeline.

As an alternative to computationally expensive MD simulations, Wang and colleagues employed a Mutation-dependent Biomacromolecular Quantitative Structure Activity Relationship (MB-QSAR) method to predict drug resistance across 59 HIV-1 PR mutants (103). Using Comparative Molecular Field Analysis and Comparative Molecular Similarity Indices Analysis with Partial Least Squares regression, they quantified the relative contributions of steric and electrostatic fields to resistance. Steric fields accounted for approximately 60 percent of the resistance-conferring effect, while electrostatic fields contributed roughly 40 percent. The principal advantage of this approach is its strong external predictive performance, with cross-validated correlation coefficients exceeding 0.7 for most inhibitors, combined with rapid computation that avoids the high cost of MD simulations. However, the method depends on pre-aligned static homology models that do not account for the extreme flap dynamics induced by distal minor mutations. This limitation means that while MB-QSAR can efficiently identify resistance-associated structural features, it cannot fully capture the conformational entropy changes that govern inhibitor binding in highly flexible systems.

Consistent with the crucial role of ADMET in CADD, evaluating the pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles of PR inhibitors is vital for predicting their therapeutic potential. In the aforementioned study by Zhou et al., while in vitro cytotoxicity was evaluated (demonstrating a favorable CC50>200µM), the computational workflow lacked systematic in silico ADMET predictions. Information regarding absorption, metabolism, membrane permeability, or drug-likeness parameters was not reported, leaving a significant gap in predicting in vivo bioavailability and potential drug-drug interactions. Future computational workflows for HIV-1 PR inhibitors must routinely incorporate ADMET prediction tools (e.g., QikProp, admetSAR, or SwissADME) to provide a more holistic assessment of new scaffolds, bridging the gap between high in vitro potency and clinical viability.

Future prospects and conclusions

AIDS, caused by HIV, continues to pose significant challenges for global healthcare. While ART remains the cornerstone of treatment by effectively suppressing viral replication and improving patient outcomes, the virus’s ability to develop resistance underscores the need for innovative drug discovery approaches.

Recently, multi-targeting and multivalent binding strategies have gained prominence in the design of anti-HIV drugs. These innovative approaches aim to enhance therapeutic efficacy and mitigate the emergence of drug resistance by concurrently targeting multiple critical stages or proteins within the HIV life cycle (112, 113). For example, some research teams are developing drugs that can inhibit both HIV RT and IN or designing multivalent inhibitors that bind to several different sites on the HIV surface protein (25, 114).

To achieve these complex polypharmacological goals, the field is transitioning from conventional docking and ligand-based screening to advanced, AI-driven methodologies. Traditional models have already enhanced the precision of QSAR analyses (115–117) and automated high-throughput screening (118). Building on this, cutting-edge technologies-such as GNNs, generative AI, diffusion models, AlphaFold-enabled structure predictions, and transformer-based PLMs-are now shaping the future. These advancements not only drive de novo drug design and uncover cryptic binding sites, but also allow for accurate drug-drug interaction predictions and the identification of synergistic effects from combination therapies (119).

The broader application of AI in CADD is currently limited by inconsistent data quality, opaque model interpretability, and high computational demands. Furthermore, predictive models frequently suffer from overfitting. A more fundamental issue is the persistent gap between in silico predictions and experimental reality, which underscores the risk of relying purely on virtual screening metrics without rigorous in vitro or in vivo validation.

To advance from algorithmic development to clinical utility, the field must adopt stricter validation workflows prior to chemical synthesis. Routine integration of molecular docking, MD simulations, MM/GBSA calculations, and ADMET profiling is necessary to confirm the structural stability and pharmacokinetic viability of generated compounds. Beyond validation, addressing the data deficit is critical. Expanding training databases to include comprehensive structural data on HIV mutant strains will directly improve the generalizability of these models against resistant variants. As these data pipelines mature, generative AI can more effectively accelerate the design of targeted combination therapies, tackling multiple viral targets (e.g., RT, IN, and PR) without compromising overall oral bioavailability.

In conclusion, realizing the full potential of AI in anti-HIV drug discovery requires moving beyond theoretical predictions. By prioritizing robust analytical validation, curating high-quality mutant databases, and focusing on rational combination strategies, researchers can position AI-driven CADD as a reliable foundation for developing next-generation treatments against resistant strains.
