Robust high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for trimmed sample means
Lucas Resende

Abstract
Robust mean estimation has largely focused on concentration guarantees under heavy tails and contamination. We study robustness from a different perspective: high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximations. We show that trimmed sample means admit Gaussian and bootstrap approximations under finite (p)-th moment assumptions, even in high-dimensional regimes and in the presence of adversarial contamination. Our bounds recover, up to the dependence on the moment parameter, the rates available for the empirical mean under light tails, while requiring substantially weaker moment assumptions. We further extend the Gaussian approximation to VC-subgraph classes and apply it to robust vector mean estimation under arbitrary norms, obtaining bounds with optimal Gaussian-width complexity. Finally, we develop uniform confidence intervals based on the bootstrap approximation and show empirically that they maintain coverage under heavy tails and adversarial contamination.

Introduction

Let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables taking values in some set
{\bf X}
according to a distribution
P
. Also let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions
f:{\bf X}\to\mathbb{R}
and denote by
Pf
the expectation of
f(X)
. Uniformly estimating the population mean
Pf
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a fundamental task in statistics; when
\mathcal{F}
has a single element it is the classical problem of mean estimation, when
\mathcal{F}
is finite it is related to the problem of vector mean estimation under the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm, when
\mathcal{F}
is infinite this problem is related to vector mean estimation under general norms, maximum likelihood estimation, generalization bounds, and others.

Since Catoni’s seminal paper
[
1
]
, a line of research has focused on designing mean estimators attaining, with few assumptions on
P
and sometimes also considering contaminated samples, high probability error bounds similar to those the empirical mean attains on sub-Gaussian samples. Such estimators are called sub-Gaussian mean estimators, see
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. For instance, taking
\mathcal{F}=\{f\}
and assuming only
\sigma^{2}:=\Ex(f(X)-Pf)^{2}<\infty
, it is possible to construct a family of estimators
\left\{\widehat{\mu}_{n,\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}_{\alpha\in(0,1)}
satisfying, for some absolute constant
C>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\widehat{\mu}_{n,\alpha}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-Pf\right|\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha.

(1.1)

This article looks at robustness in terms of another desirable property of the empirical mean under light-tailed distributions: its high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximations. We study high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for the trimmed mean, a mean estimator that is known to achieve optimal performance in several problems
[
7
]
. Let
k<\frac{n}{2}
be a non-negative integer, the trimmed mean estimate of
Pf
given
x_{1:n}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\bf X}^{n}
is simply

T_{n,k}(f,x_{1:n})=\frac{1}{n-2k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-k}f\left(x_{(i)}\right)

where
(\cdot)
is a permutation such that
f\left(x_{(1)}\right)\leq f\left(x_{(2)}\right)\leq\cdots\leq f\left(x_{(n)}\right)
. Following the recent work on high-dimensional Gaussian approximation for the empirical mean
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
, we let
\{G_{P}(f)\}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
be a centered Gaussian process with the same covariance as
\left\{f(X)\right\}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
and look at the Kolmogorov distance between the maximum of the Gaussian process and the maximum of the finite-sample approximation. We show that the trimmed mean satisfies the Gaussian approximation property

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{n}\,\left(T_{n,k}(f,X_{1:n})-Pf\right)\leq\lambda\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{P}f\leq\lambda\right]\right|\to 0

(1.2)

for contaminated heavy-tailed data even in the high-dimensional setting where
|\mathcal{F}|\gg n
. This line of research differs from classical Berry-Esseen results
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
since our main concern is allowing for
|\mathcal{F}|\gg n
, usually paying the price of having a slower vanishing rate and obtaining an approximation bound only over the maximum and not over all convex sets, as in the low-dimensional case.

As discussed in
[
18
,
19
]
, Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for the maximum appear in applications such as constructing simultaneous confidence sets, multiple hypothesis testing, and treatment heterogeneity analysis. High-dimensional settings frequently arise in fields like biology and econometrics, mainly due to sample size limitations or the presence of parameters controlling for heterogeneity. However, as shown by
[
20
]
, the empirical average fails to attain Gaussian approximation properties for heavy-tailed distributions when the dimension is large (see §
1.2.2
for details).

Motivated by this limitation, the main contribution of this paper is establishing (
1.2
) for contaminated heavy-tailed data in the regime
|\mathcal{F}|\gg n
. We explore this result through two applications:

•

Vector mean estimation under arbitrary norms:
We extend our Gaussian approximation results from finite-dimensional settings to VC-subgraph classes. Using this extension in place of classical empirical process tools, we achieve optimal complexity bounds with a Gaussian width term.

•

Uniform confidence intervals:
Building on our bootstrap approximation results, we propose a method for constructing uniform confidence intervals. We provide experimental evidence that these intervals, when obtained via trimmed means, remain valid under heavy tails and adversarial contamination.

This paper is organized as follows. In §
1.1
we introduce the main definitions and notations. In §
1.2
we provide a short literature overview on high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximation. Our Gaussian approximation results are presented in §
2
. In §
3
we apply our results to the problem of vector mean estimation under general norms. We provide bootstrap approximations and their application to uniform confidence intervals in §
4
. The proofs of all results from §
2
are presented in §
5
. All other proofs are in the supplementary material. We observe that our proof techniques can be easily combined with new, improved results for Gaussian approximation of the empirical average, potentially resulting in tighter bounds than those presented here.

1.1
Definitions and notations

Recall that
{\bf X}
is a set,
\mathcal{F}
is a family of functions
f:{\bf X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
P
is a distribution over
{\bf X}
and
X,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables with law

. We denote
\Ex f(X)
by

, use
|\cdot|
to denote the cardinality of a set and let

which is simply the covariance matrix of

or the kernel of

. We let
\{G_{P}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
be a centered Gaussian process such that

\Ex\left[G_{P}(f)G_{P}(g)\right]=\Sigma_{\mathcal{F},P}(f,g)\,\,\forall f,g\in\mathcal{F}.

See
[
24
]
for details on the existence of such processes.

Definition 1.2

(Adversarially contaminated sample)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
,
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}=(X_{1}^{\varepsilon},\dots,X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
is an
\varepsilon
-contaminated version of
X_{1:n}
if

|\{i\in[n]:X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\neq X_{i}\}|\leq\varepsilon n.

In this setup, one has access only to the contaminated sample
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}
, which differs from the clean sample in at most
\varepsilon n
points. This contamination setup is said to be adversarial because no assumption is made on how the contamination procedure works. It has been widely used on the recent literature (see e.g.
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
) and is more adverse than Huber’s classical contamination model
[
30
,
31
]
, where the distribution
P
is assumed to be known and the contaminated points are drawn from an unknown distribution. This setup is closely related to the classical notion of breakdown point
[
31
]
: an estimator that is robust to an
\varepsilon
-contamination has a breakdown point of at least
\varepsilon
. For further discussion and comparisons with other contamination procedures see
[
32
]
.

This model is realistic in a range of modern data-analysis settings where a small fraction of observations may be corrupted by mechanisms that are difficult to model parametrically. In high-throughput biology, sample mislabeling and contamination are well documented
[
33
]
. Similar sparse-corruption settings arise in sensor networks, where a fraction of sensors may be faulty or compromised
[
34
]
, and in crowdsourced or web-scale datasets, where a few percent of labels may be erroneous
[
35
]
. In these settings, the contaminating mechanism is unknown and need not follow a specified outlier distribution, motivating an adversarial rather than a Huber contamination model. The requirement in our results that
\epsilon
vanish with
n
(see §
2
) reflects the fact that distributional (Gaussian) approximation is a strictly harder goal than point estimation, and Lemma
2.2
shows that this requirement is unavoidable.

Given
p>2
we let

\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(P|f-Pf|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\text{ and }\underline{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathcal{F},P}:=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\Sigma_{\mathcal{F},P}(f,f).

We may drop the dependence on
\mathcal{F}
and
P
when it is implicit and simply write
\Sigma
,
\nu_{p}
, and
\underline{\sigma}^{2}
.

Finally, we let
\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f):=T_{n,k}\left(f,X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\right)
and let
\widehat{P}_{n}(f)
denote the empirical mean of
f
. We define the normalized empirical processes corresponding to the empirical mean and the trimmed mean as

\mathbb{G}_{n}(f):=\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{P}_{n}(f)-Pf\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f):=\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)-Pf\right),

respectively. Letting the suprema of these processes over the class
\mathcal{F}
be given by

Z_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,\mathbb{G}_{n}(f),\quad Z_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,\mathbb{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f),\quad\text{ and }\quad Z(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,G_{P}(f),

we define the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distances between the distributions of these suprema and
G_{P}
as

\varrho^{E}:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\quad\text{ and }\quad\varrho:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|.

1.2
Literature overview

1.2.1
Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for the empirical mean

Under strong moment assumptions, the pioneering work of
[
8
]
first bounded
\varrho^{E}\leq C\left(\frac{\ln^{7}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{6}}
.
This bound was then improved by
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
to

\varrho^{E}\leq C\left(\frac{\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}
.
Under additional assumptions, such as
\Sigma_{\mathcal{F},P}
being positive definite,
\varrho^{E}
can be further bounded, as discussed in Remark
2.3
. We say that these are high-dimensional Gaussian approximation bounds because they ensure
\varrho^{E}\to 0
even when the dimension
d
is exponential on a polynomial of
n
. This behavior contrasts with Berry-Esseen-type bounds
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
since they only allow for a polynomial dependence between
d
and
n
. On the other hand, Berry-Esseen-type bounds provide an uniform bound over all convex sets and not only for the maximum. There is also literature on high-dimensional Gaussian approximation for the empirical average in the case
d=\infty
. Assuming the existence of an envelope function and some moment conditions, a line of work started by
[
36
,
9
]
derived bounds depending on the metric entropy of the class
\mathcal{F}
. More recently, dimension and entropy free bounds were obtained by
[
12
]
, although also requiring an envelope function with finite third moment.

All the previously cited works also consider bootstrap approximation results. The most popular bootstrap strategies are the empirical and the multiplier bootstrap. The usual approach is to prove the validity of bootstrap by showing that both the mean and the bootstrap statistics satisfy a Gaussian approximation result with the same limit. Some work has also obtained bootstrap approximations directly:
[
37
]
managed to improve upon the then-current state of the art using a direct bootstrap approximation. Moreover,
[
38
]
obtained a bootstrap approximation rate of
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
independent of the dimension, but under sub-exponential tails and variance decay.

1.2.2
The limitations of the empirical mean

All the previously cited works on high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximations require strong moment assumptions to ensure
\varrho^{E}\to 0
when
d
is exponential on a polynomial of
n
. Considering the case

\nu_{4}^{4}\leq B^{2}\nu_{2}^{2}

1.2.3
Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for robust estimators

Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for robust estimators were studied by
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
for the finite-dimensional (
i.e.
,
|\mathcal{F}|<\infty
). In
[
21
]
, assuming the uncontaminated setting (
\varepsilon=0
), the authors combine a median-of-means approach with a variance-based Winsorization procedure to establish high-dimensional Gaussian and bootstrap approximation guarantees. Their estimator uses a small subset of the sample to construct robust mean and variance estimates,
\widehat{\mu}_{f}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{f}^{2}
, for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. These estimates are then used to construct a Winsorized mean, normalized by
\sqrt{n}\widehat{\sigma}_{f}^{\tau}
for a tuning parameter
\tau\in(0,1)
.

Under an
L^{4+\delta}-L^{2}
moment equivalence condition, bounded densities, and a variance decay assumption, the authors achieve a nearly optimal Gaussian approximation rate of
n^{-1/2+\delta}
. Their result relies heavily on the variance decay condition, which requires the existence of constants
C\geq 1
and
\beta>0
such that

\frac{1}{C}\sigma_{(1)}^{2}j^{-2\beta}\leq\sigma_{(j)}^{2}\leq C\sigma_{(1)}^{2}j^{-2\beta}

for the sorted variances
\sigma_{(1)}^{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{(d)}^{2}
associated with the coordinates
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\in\mathcal{F}
. This variance decay profile is crucial for proving that there exists, with high probability, a low-dimensional subset
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{F}
that contains the index realizing the maximum statistic. Notably, the choice of
\tau\in(0,1)
is of particular importance to their results; if
\tau
were set to
1
, the normalization would place all coordinates on an equal scale, rendering the subset
\mathcal{H}
impossible to construct.

Building on the results from the original manuscript of this paper,
[
22
]
addressed Gaussian and bootstrap approximations for both a quantile-based Winsorized and the trimmed means, which they show to satisfy Gaussian approximation properties as long as
\ln|\mathcal{F}|\ll n^{\frac{1}{5}+O\left(p^{-1}\right)}
. They do not cover the case
|\mathcal{F}|=\infty
nor the problem of vector mean estimation under general norms. Their proof strategy consists in approximating the Winsorized mean given by empirical quantiles by the one given by populational quantiles, controlling the error, and using the Gaussian approximation result from
[
11
]
. Meanwhile, inspired by
[
7
]
, we approximate
\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)
by
\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ f)
, where
\tau_{M}(x)=x\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq M}+M\mathbf{1}_{x>M}-M\mathbf{1}_{x<-M}
and control
M
, without requiring a fine control over the quantiles.

2
High-dimensional Gaussian approximations

Recall the definitions from §
1.1
. In this section we let
|\mathcal{F}|=d<\infty
. Our main result follows:

Theorem 2.1

(High-dimensional Gaussian approximation for trimmed means, proof in §
5.1
)
.

Assume an
\varepsilon
-contaminated sample
X_{i:n}^{\varepsilon}
as in §
1.1
. Let
nd\geq 3
and
\nu_{p}:=\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)<\infty
for some
p\in(2,\infty)
. If
k:=\left\lfloor\varepsilon n\right\rfloor+\left(\left\lfloor\varepsilon n\right\rfloor\vee\left\lceil\ln(nd)\right\rceil\right)<\frac{n}{2}
and the regularity condition

\nu_{p}^{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\leq\frac{\underline{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathcal{F},P}}{8}

(2.1)

holds, then

\varrho\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\nu_{p}}\left(\frac{\ln^{5-\frac{2}{p}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+c_{2}\nu_{p}\left(\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{n\ln(nd)}+\left(\frac{\ln^{3-\frac{2}{p}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{n}

where
c_{1}>0
depends on
\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)
and
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
, and
c_{2}\geq\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}^{-1}
depends only on
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
.

Theorem
2.1
can be compared with the best-known Gaussian approximation bound available for the empirical mean, given by (
1.3
). For a baseline comparison, we first set
\varepsilon=0
, as the standard empirical mean cannot handle adversarial contamination. To ensure
\varrho^{E}\to 0
in (
1.3
), one requires light-tailed distributions and a dimension growth restricted to
\ln(d)\ll n^{\frac{1}{5}}
. In contrast, to obtain
\varrho\to 0
in Theorem
2.1
, it suffices to have
\nu_{p}<\infty
for some
p>2
and
\ln(d)\ll n^{\frac{1}{5}-O\left(p^{-1}\right)}
(indeed, the binding term forces
\ln d\ll n^{(1-2/p)/(5-2/p)}
, whose exponent equals
\frac{1}{5}-\frac{8}{25p}+o(p^{-1})
and increases to
\frac{1}{5}
as
p\to\infty
). While our allowable dimension growth is slightly more restrictive than the
n^{1/5}
rate available for the empirical mean, our result holds under significantly weaker moment assumptions and remains valid under data contamination.

Furthermore, Theorem
2.1
operates within a vanishing contamination framework. The bound on
\varrho
vanishes only if the contamination level
\varepsilon
satisfies

\frac{\nu_{p}}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{n\ln(nd)}\to 0.

(2.2)

This imposes a requirement that
\varepsilon
must decay with both the sample size
n
and the dimension
d
, which contrasts with the fixed breakdown point restrictions (e.g.,
\varepsilon<c
for some
c>0
) typically found in robust sub-Gaussian mean estimation. Lemma

shows that this dependence is not incidental, but actually sheds light on a fundamental limitation of Gaussian approximations under contamination.

Lemma 2.2

(Upper bound on
\varepsilon
; proof in §
6.1
)
.

Assume
p>2
. Let
b_{p}\geq\sigma>0
. Let
\delta>0
, there exist constants
o_{1},d_{1},c_{1}>0
depending on
\delta
and a constant
c_{2}
depending on
\delta
and
\sigma
such that if
d>d_{1}
,
\varepsilon n\geq o_{1}
,

\frac{b_{p}}{\sigma}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\leq 1\wedge\frac{c_{2}}{\ln d}\quad\text{ and }\quad\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\geq\frac{2\delta}{b_{p}}(2n\ln d)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(2.3)

then, one can construct a family
\mathcal{F}
with
|\mathcal{F}|=d
and distributions
P,P^{\prime}
satisfying
b^{p}_{p}=\nu^{p}_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)\leq\nu^{p}_{p}(\mathcal{F},P^{\prime})\leq 2b^{p}_{p}
and
\sigma^{2}=\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}^{2}=\nu^{2}_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)\leq\nu^{2}_{2}(\mathcal{F},P^{\prime})=\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P^{\prime}}^{2}\leq 2\sigma^{2}
, such that
for any given estimator
\widehat{E}_{f}:{\bf X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
it holds

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{E}_{f}\left(X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\right)-Pf\right)\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{P}(f)\leq\lambda\right)\right|\geq c_{1}

or

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{E}_{f}\left(X_{1:n}^{{}^{\prime}\varepsilon}\right)-P^{\prime}f\right)\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{P^{\prime}}(f)\leq\lambda\right)\right|\geq c_{1}.

Observe that the inequalities in (
2.3
) can simultaneously hold only if

is large enough and

n\geq C(\delta,\sigma,b_{p})(\ln d)^{4\frac{p-2}{p-1}-1}

for a given constant

. In particular, as

, this imposes a sample size requirement of at least
\Omega(\ln^{3}d)
. This regime is non-restrictive, as
n\gg\ln^{3}d
is a necessary condition for high-dimensional Gaussian approximations for bounded random variables in the absence of contamination
[
39
, Proposition 2.1]
. Consequently, Lemma
2.2
establishes that a valid Gaussian approximation under adversarial contamination is fundamentally possible only if the contamination level vanishes fast enough to satisfy:

\frac{b_{p}}{\sigma}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{n\ln d}\to 0.

(2.4)

Since the dependence on the contamination level from Theorem
2.1
matches the bound of Lemma
2.2
, it is optimal. The limit established in Lemma
2.2
must be compared with two results from the literature: Lemma 5.4 of
[
3
]
and Theorem 10 of
[
37
]
. First,
[
3
]
demonstrates that the optimal error rate for robust mean estimation under heavy tails and adversarial contamination scales as
b_{p}\varepsilon^{1-1/p}
. Second,
[
37
]
shows that the
\frac{\sqrt{\ln d}}{\sigma}
penalty arising from Nazarov’s inequality is sharp in certain cases. Because establishing a Gaussian approximation requires standardizing the error by a scaling factor of
\sqrt{n}
, it yields a cumulative error term proportional to
\nu_{p}\varepsilon^{1-1/p}\sqrt{n\ln d}
, matching (
2.4
).

Remark 2.3

(Role of the covariance matrix )
.

Theorem
2.1
and all results in this paper require only that
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}>0
, without strictly demanding a positive definite
\Sigma_{\mathcal{F},P}
. This mirrors the setup used by
[
11
]
to derive (
1.3
). By contrast, imposing stronger structural assumptions is known to yield tighter bounds for the empirical mean under light tails:

•

Positive definiteness:
Assuming a positive definite
\Sigma_{\mathcal{F},P}
alongside bounded coordinates for
X_{i}
,
[
39
]
established a bound of order
(\ln n)n^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}(d)
, achieving optimality up to the logarithmic factor. Similar improvements were noted by
[
40
]
.

•

Symmetry and variance decay:
Symmetric distributions
[
11
]
and variance decay
[
38
]
also allow the empirical mean to attain the optimal
n^{-1/2}
rate under light tails.

While extending in these directions is beyond the scope of this article, the proof techniques developed in §
5
provide a natural pathway to adapt these conditions for the trimmed mean, potentially yielding analogous improvements.

2.1
Gaussian approximation for VC classes

Before discussing our results for empirical processes we need a few definitions. Given a measure
Q
and a family
\mathcal{F}
of square-integrable we let
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},d_{Q},\delta)
be the
\delta
-covering number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to the semi-metric
d_{Q}(f,g)=\sqrt{Q(f-g)^{2}}
.

Definition 2.4

(VC-subgraph class)
.

We say that a class
\mathcal{F}
of functions
f:{\bf X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a VC subgraph class with dimension
v=\text{vc}(\mathcal{F})
if
v<\infty
is the VC dimension of the collection of its subgraphs, i.e.,

v:=\text{vc}\left(\left\{\left\{(x,t)\in{\bf X}\times\mathbb{R}:t<f(x)\right\}:f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}\right)<\infty.

Definition 2.5

(VC-type class)
.

We say that a class
\mathcal{F}
of functions
f:{\bf X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a VC-type class with envelope
F
if there are constants
A,v>0
such that

\sup_{Q}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},d_{Q},\delta\|F\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\right)\leq\left(\frac{A}{\delta}\right)^{v}\,\,\forall\delta\in(0,1].

Where the supremum is taken over all probability measures over
{\bf X}
with finite support.

Our result on the Gaussian approximation for empirical processes follows:

Theorem 2.6

(Gaussian approximation for VC classes, proof in §
5.2
)
.

Assume an
\varepsilon
-contaminated sample
X_{i:n}^{\varepsilon}
as in §
1.1
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a VC-subgraph class and let
p\in(2,\infty)
be such that
\nu_{p}:=\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)<\infty
. Define

K_{n}:=K_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\lceil 12(\text{vc}(\mathcal{F})+1)\ln n\rceil\quad\text{and }\quad\Xi(\delta)=\Ex\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g\in\mathcal{F}\\
d_{P}(f,g)<\delta\end{subarray}}G_{P}(f-g)\right].

Suppose that (
2.1
) holds taking
k:=\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+\left(\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\vee K_{n}\right)<\frac{n}{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\varrho\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\nu_{p}}\left(\frac{K_{n}^{5-\frac{2}{p}}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+c_{2}\nu_{p}\left(\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{nK_{n}}+\left(\frac{K_{n}^{3-\frac{2}{p}}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\frac{5\sqrt{K_{n}}}{2\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\,\Xi\left(14\nu_{p}\left(\frac{K_{n}}{n}\vee\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\right)+\frac{2}{n}

where
c_{1}>0
depends on
\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)
and
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
, and
c_{2}\geq\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}^{-1}
depends only on
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The quantity depending on
\Xi
is a continuity modulus of the limiting Gaussian process and vanishes as the net becomes finer. First notice that if
G_{P}
has uniformly
d_{P}
-continuous sample paths, then
\Xi(\delta)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
, so this term is asymptotically negligible. More precisely, non-asymptotic bounds can be derived using entropy integral bounds. For instance, if
\mathcal{F}
is a VC-type class (as in Definition
2.5
) one can easily see that, for
\delta<\frac{A\|F\|_{L^{2}(P)}}{e}
,

\Xi(\delta)\leq C\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{\text{vc}(\mathcal{F})\ln\frac{A\|F\|_{L^{2}(P)}}{s}}\,ds\leq C^{\prime}\delta\sqrt{\text{vc}(\mathcal{F})\ln\frac{A\|F\|_{L^{2}(P)}}{\delta}}

for some absolute constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
. We stress that, plugging this bound with
\delta\asymp\nu_{p}(K_{n}/n\vee\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}
, the resulting
\Xi
-term can be absorbed into the other terms (see the proof of Theorem
3.4
).

Theorem
2.6
establishes the first Gaussian approximation result for a robust estimator in the infinite-dimensional setting. In the uncontaminated setting (
\varepsilon=0
), Theorem
2.6
can be compared with the seminal results of
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
. The convergence rate established by
[
9
]
is of order at least
(K_{n}^{7}/n)^{1/8}
(see §
6.2
for a discussion), whereas our rate improves to
(K_{n}^{5}/n)^{1/4}
in the limit as
p\to\infty
. Furthermore, unlike
[
9
]
, Theorem
2.6
circumvents the need for an envelope, requiring only
\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)<\infty
for some
p\in(2,\infty)
rather than an envelope in
L^{p}(P)
for
p\geq 4
. Even if one were to update the analysis of
[
9
]
using their more recent finite-dimensional approximation bounds (e.g., Lemma
5.1
), which could theoretically improve their polynomial rate to
1/4
, the requirement for an envelope
F\in L^{p}(P)
would still remain. Theorem
2.6
also fares well when compared against
[
12
]
; while their bounds do not require a VC-subgraph class, they yield a worse dependence on
n
and demand an envelope in
L^{3}(P)
.

3
Vector mean estimation under general norms

In this section we apply our Gaussian approximation results to the following problem:

Problem 3.1

(Robust vector mean estimation under a general norm)
.

Let
{\bf X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
P
be a distribution over
{\bf X}
and
\|\cdot\|
be a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
P\|\cdot\|<\infty
. The mean
\mu_{P}
of
P
is characterized by
\langle v,\mu_{P}\rangle=P\langle v,\cdot\rangle
for all
v\in S
, where
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is symmetric and satisfies

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\|x\|=\sup_{v\in S}\langle x,v\rangle.

Let
X_{i:n}^{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
-contaminated sample and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Find an estimator
\hat{\mu}:{\bf X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\mu}\left(X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\right)-\mu_{P}\right\|\leq\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,\varepsilon)\right)\geq 1-\alpha

(3.1)

with
\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,\varepsilon)
as small as possible in terms of
n
,
\alpha
,
\varepsilon
and
P
.

Remark 3.2
.

In Problem
3.1
one can always take
S
to be the dual ball. But some norms can be achieved with much smaller sets, for instance, the infinity norm can be obtained as the supremum over
\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|v\|_{1}=1\}
, but also as the supremum over
\{\pm e_{j}:j\in[d]\}
.

As noticed by Minsker
[
3
]
, the setting of Problem
3.1
instantly suggests a natural family
\mathcal{F}
of functions associated with
\|\cdot\|
:

\mathcal{F}:=\{\langle v,\cdot\rangle:v\in S\}.

(3.2)

The next lemma relates Problem
3.1
to the problem of uniform mean estimation over the class
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\hat{E}_{f}
be any given estimator for
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Then, every function
\hat{\mu}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\forall x_{1:n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\,:\,\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n})\in\argmin_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})-f(\mu)\right|\right)

satisfies

\|\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n})-\mu_{P}\|\leq 2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|.

Moreover, given a mean estimator
\hat{\mu}
for
\mu_{P}
, let
\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})=f(\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n}))
for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, then

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|=\|\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n})-\mu_{P}\|.

Proof.

Just notice that

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n})-\mu_{P}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|f(\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n}))-f(\mu_{P})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|f(\hat{\mu}(x_{1:n}))-\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})\right|+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|f(\mu_{P})-\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})\right|+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|

\displaystyle=2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{E}_{f}(x_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|

where in the second inequality we used the definition of
\hat{\mu}
as a minimizer. The second claim follows by definition.
∎

Let
\Gamma
be the covariance matrix of
P
. Given
p>2
one can check that

\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)=\sup_{v\in S}(P\,|\langle X-\mu_{P},v\rangle|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}=\inf_{v\in S}v\Gamma v^{t}.

In the special case where
\|\cdot\|
is the euclidean norm, it holds that
\nu_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{F},P)=\|\Gamma\|_{\textrm{op}}
. We also define the Gaussian width of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

w(A):=\Ex{\sup_{v\in A}\langle v,W\rangle}

where
W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Notice that
\{G_{P}f:f\in\mathcal{F}\}
has the same distribution as
\left\{\left\langle v,\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}W\right\rangle:v\in S\right\}
, thus

\Ex Z(\mathcal{F})=\Ex\sup_{v\in S}\left\langle v,\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}W\right\rangle=w\left(\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}S\right).

Early work on Problem
3.1
includes
[
41
,
42
]
. In the Hilbert space setting, a breakthrough result by Lugosi and Mendelson
[
4
]
presented a higher-dimensional version of the median of means. A more recent estimator by the same authors
[
6
]
, based on high-dimensional Winsorized means, gives
2
2

2

They only derive explicit bounds for
p=2
, but the more general bounds follow from the same methods.

\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,\varepsilon)=C\,\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\rm tr}(\Gamma)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\|\Gamma\|_{\rm op}}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}}+\inf_{p>1}\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\right)

for some constant
C
. The term depending on
\varepsilon
, here called contamination term, is optimal as it is optimal for single function mean estimation
[
3
]
. The term on
\alpha
, here called fluctuation term, is also known to be optimal if we only assume finite second moments. Finally, the independent term, here called complexity term, is also optimal.

The problem of general norms was studied by Lugosi and Mendelson
[
5
]
and Depersin and Lecué
[
25
]
, both papers present estimators based on the median of means. The best-known bound for Problem
3.1
is given by
[
7
]
using a trimmed mean based estimator and yields

\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,\varepsilon)=C\,\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mu_{P}\right\|+\inf_{q\in[1,2]}\nu_{q}(\mathcal{F},P)\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{3}{\alpha}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}+\inf_{p\geq 1}\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\right)

(3.3)

requiring only
\nu_{1}(\mathcal{F},P)<\infty
.

There are also lower bounds available in the literature. In
[
25
]
a lower bound is obtained for Gaussian distributions under
\varepsilon=0
: let
\Gamma
be positive-definite and let
P=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Gamma)
in the setup of Problem
3.1
, then exists
c>0
such that

\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,0)\geq c\left(\frac{w\left(\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}S\right)}{\sqrt{n}}+\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)

(3.4)

for any choice of
\hat{\mu}
. Moreover, taking
\hat{\mu}
as the empirical mean,
[
25
]
also proved that

\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,0)\leq C\left(\frac{w\left(\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}S\right)}{\sqrt{n}}+\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)

(3.5)

for some constant
C>c
. When
\|\cdot\|
is the euclidean norm and
P
is any distribution with covariance
\Gamma
, all the complexity terms previously discussed are of the same order (see Proposition 2.5.1 of
[
43
]
),
i.e.
,

\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mu_{P}\right\|_{2}\approx\sqrt{\frac{\text{tr}(\Gamma)}{n}}\approx\frac{w\left(\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}{\sqrt{n}}.

However, it is unclear if the expectation of the empirical process is close to the Gaussian width for general norms, which is optimal by (
3.4
). In addition, (
3.4
) also points to the optimality of the fluctuation
\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}}
under finite second moment. Our main result in this section follows:

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume an
\varepsilon
-contaminated sample
X_{i:n}^{\varepsilon}
as in §
1.1
. Recall the setup of Problem
3.1
and the definition of
\Xi
from Theorem
2.6
. Assume
\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)<\infty
for some
p>2
. Let
K_{n}:=K_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\lceil 12(d+3)\ln n\rceil
and suppose that (
2.1
) holds taking
k:=\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+\left(\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\vee K_{n}\right)<\frac{n}{2}
. Exist
c_{1}>0
depending on
\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)
and on
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
and
c_{2}\geq\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}^{-1}
depending only on
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
such that if

c_{1}\sqrt{\nu_{p}}\left(\frac{K_{n}^{5-\frac{2}{p}}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+c_{2}\nu_{p}\left(\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{nK_{n}}+\left(\frac{K_{n}^{3-\frac{2}{p}}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\frac{2}{n}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2},

(3.6)

then
\hat{\mu}_{n,k}(x_{1:n})\in\argmin_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}(f,x_{1:n})-f(\mu)\right|\right)
satisfies (
3.1
) with

\Phi_{P}(n,\alpha,\varepsilon)=\frac{w\left(\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}S\right)}{\sqrt{n}}+\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F})\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\alpha}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
,

\left\|\hat{\mu}_{n,k}(X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n})-\mu_{P}\right\|\leq 2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)-f(\mu_{P})\right|.

(3.7)

We use Theorem
2.6
to bound the RHS of (
3.7
). Notice that the VC-subgraph dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is at most
d+2
. This is because for a given
f=\langle\cdot,v\rangle
its subgraph
sg(f):=\{(x,t):\langle\cdot,v\rangle\leq t\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is a half-space and the VC dimension of all half-spaces is
d+2
, thus
vc(\mathcal{F})\leq d+2
. To apply Theorem
2.6
we need to bound the term depending on
\Xi
. Taking
w=\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}(v-v^{\prime})
we obtain

\Xi(\delta)=\Ex\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\in S,\\
\|\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}(v-v^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\langle\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}(v-v^{\prime}),W\rangle\leq\Ex\sup_{\|w\|_{2}\leq\delta}\langle w,W\rangle=\delta\Ex\|W\|_{2}\leq\delta\sqrt{d}\leq\delta\sqrt{K_{n}}.

Thus, considering the cases
\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\leq K_{n}
and
\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\geq K_{n}
yields

\frac{5\sqrt{K_{n}}}{2\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\,\Xi\left(14\nu_{p}\left(\frac{K_{n}}{n}\vee\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\right)\leq\frac{35\nu_{p}}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}K_{n}\left(\frac{K_{n}}{n}\vee\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\leq\frac{35\nu_{p}}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\left(\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{nK_{n}}+\left(\frac{K_{n}^{3-\frac{2}{p}}}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Given the bound above and the assumptions of Theorem
3.4
, Theorem
2.6
yields

\varrho=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)-f(\mu_{P})\right|\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Now, recall that
Z(\mathcal{F})
has the same distribution as
\sup_{v\in S}\langle\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}v,W\rangle
. The proof follows by taking
\lambda
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\geq 1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
. By Borell-TIS inequality, it suffices to take

\lambda=\frac{w(\Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}S)}{\sqrt{n}}+\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F})\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\alpha}}.

∎

When its assumption on
\alpha
holds, Theorem
3.4
is optimal, as it matches the theoretical lower bound in (
3.4
). Moreover, this is the first result to feature a complexity term governed by the Gaussian width for general norms. Methodologically, Theorem
3.4
departs fundamentally from prior work
[
41
,
42
,
4
,
6
,
5
,
25
,
7
]
. Rather than deriving uniform high-probability bounds for the supremum over
\mathcal{F}
, our analysis tackles the problem by establishing a Gaussian approximation. Unlike previous results that allow a fixed contamination proportion
\varepsilon\in[0,c)
, Theorem
3.4
requires
\varepsilon
to vanish. By Lemma
2.2
, this is a fundamental limitation of the Gaussian approximation approach.

We now discuss the presence of the assumption
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}>0
in our bound. The case
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}=0
corresponds to the existence of a non-trivial subspace
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
X-\mu_{P}\in H^{\perp}
a.s. Let
\mathcal{G}=\{\langle\cdot,v\rangle:v\in S\cap H^{\perp}\}
, on the clean sample we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}(f,X_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}(f,X_{1:n})-f(\mu_{P})\right|

since
\langle X_{i}-\mu_{P},v\rangle=0
for all
v\in H
.
Replacing the previous equality in (
3.7
), Theorem
3.4
follows considering only the subspace
H^{\perp}
. Thus, on the case
\varepsilon=0
, we can replace
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
by the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
\Gamma
. The same also holds when
\varepsilon>0
, but with a more convoluted argument, as discussed in Remark
5.10
. Consequently, since
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
can always be replaced by the smallest non-zero eigenvalue, the assumptions on it will hold trivially for large
n
.

4
High-dimensional bootstrap approximations

Gaussian approximation results are typically accompanied by bootstrap procedures to enable feasible statistical inference. Let
\tilde{X}_{1:n}
be an i.i.d. sample drawn from the empirical measure
\widehat{P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
. Conditionally on a sample
X_{1:n}
, one can define the bootstrapped empirical process for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
as

\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n}(f):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(\tilde{X}_{i})-\widehat{P}_{n}(f)\right).

(4.1)

Setting
\tilde{Z}_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n}(f)
, bounds analogous to those discussed for
\varrho^{E}
are available in the literature for the conditional Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance:

\tilde{\varrho}^{E}:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{Z}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\,\right|X_{1:n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|.

Similarly, we define an empirical bootstrap for trimmed mean as

\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f):=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-2k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-k}\left(f(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{(i)})-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right),

(4.2)

where
(\cdot)
satisfies
f(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{(1)})\leq\cdots\leq f(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{(n)})
. We also let
\tilde{Z}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)
. Our goal is to establish that the conditional distribution of
\tilde{Z}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})
approximates the distribution of
Z(\mathcal{F})
,
i.e.
, we aim to bound

\tilde{\varrho}:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{Z}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\,\right|X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|

with high probability with respect to the contaminated sample
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 4.1

(High-dimensional bootstrap approximation for trimmed means; proof in §
6.3
)
.

Assume an
\varepsilon
-contaminated sample
X_{i:n}^{\varepsilon}
as in §
1.1
. Let
nd\geq 3
and
\nu_{p}:=\nu_{p}(\mathcal{F},P)<\infty
for some
p\in(2,\infty)
. If
k:=22\left(\left\lfloor\varepsilon n\right\rfloor\vee\left\lceil\ln(nd)\right\rceil\right)<\frac{n}{2}
and (
2.1
) holds, then with probability at least

The convergence rate and the dependencies on

5
Main proofs

Our proofs are based on a strategy introduced in
[
7
]
. Start defining, for a given
M>0
, the truncation function

\tau_{M}(x):=\begin{cases}M\text{, if }x>M\\
x\text{, if }-M\leq x\leq M\\
-M\text{, if }x<-M\end{cases}.

The core observation from
[
7
]
— which was inspired by
[
6
,
44
]
— is that the trimmed mean of a contaminated sample is
uniformly
close to the empirical mean of the truncated clean sample, provided that we carefully select the trimming level
k
and the corresponding truncation level
M
. Specifically,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{M}(f(X_{i}))\right|\text{ is small.}

(5.1)

This observation follows from two lemmata that will be introduced shortly. Before proceeding, we emphasize the utility of (
5.1
): it allows us to analyze the trimmed mean exploiting the rich literature on controlling the empirical mean of bounded random variables. In particular, we can apply the best-known Gaussian approximation result for the empirical mean, which is the following:

Theorem 5.1

(Adapted from Theorem 2.4 and Lemma 4.3 of
[
11
]
)
.

Define

\delta_{n,d,B}=\left(\frac{B^{2}\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\text{and }\delta_{n,p,d,B}=\sqrt{\frac{B^{2}\ln^{3-\frac{2}{p}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}}.

Suppose that
\nu_{4}^{4}(\mathcal{F},P)\leq B^{2}\nu_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{F},P)
for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
for some
B>0
and that
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}>0
. If
a,b>0

\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F},P)\geq a
and
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}\geq b
, then exists
C:=C(a,b)>0
such that

1.

if
f(X_{1})-Pf
is sub-exponential with
\psi_{1}

B

f\in\mathcal{F}
, then

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq C\delta_{n,d,B};

2.

if
\Ex{\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(X_{1})-Pf|^{p}}\leq B^{p}
for some

, then

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq C\left(\delta_{n,d,B}\vee\delta_{n,p,d,B}\right).

Of course, the issue remains in accurately bounding the "is small" term in (

\displaystyle V_{M}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\leq

Lemma 5.4

Lemma 5.5

\displaystyle P\left|fg-(\tau_{M}\circ f)(\tau_{M}\circ g)\right|

. Since
\text{Cov}(X^{\prime})-\text{Cov}(X)=-q\sigma^{2}I_{d}+q(1-q)\sigma^{2}M^{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}
and each component of this matrix can be bounded in absolute value by
\varepsilon\sigma^{2}M^{2}
(recall that
M\geq 1
,
q=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
), the last inequality holds for some constant
C(\sigma)>0
depending only on
\sigma
.

Assume that
qM\geq\frac{\delta}{\sigma}(2n\ln d)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, as will be verified below. Since the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance is non-increasing in the size of the shift between its two arguments,

\displaystyle d_{KS}\left(\sup_{f}G_{P}(f),\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)+\sqrt{n}q\sigma M\right)

\displaystyle\geq d_{KS}\left(\sqrt{2\ln d}\sup_{f}G_{P}(f),\sqrt{2\ln d}\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)+\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\leq\sqrt{2\ln d}\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)\leq\lambda+\delta\right).

Recall that, by construction,
\mathcal{F}
is the family of the
d
coordinate projections and the marginals of
P
are i.i.d., so
\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)
is the maximum of
d
i.i.d. standard Gaussians. By the classical extreme-value theorem,
\sqrt{2\ln d}\,\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)-b_{d}
converges in distribution to a standard Gumbel law
G
, where
b_{d}
is the usual centering sequence. Moreover, since convergence in distribution to a continuous distribution on the line is uniform, there is a sequence
\eta_{d}\to 0
such that, uniformly over
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\leq\sqrt{2\ln d}\,\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)\leq\lambda+\delta\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\leq G\leq\lambda+\delta\right)\right|\leq\eta_{d}.

Taking the supremum over
\lambda
gives

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\leq\sqrt{2\ln d}\,\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)\leq\lambda+\delta\right)\geq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\leq G\leq\lambda+\delta\right)-\eta_{d}.

Let
x(\delta):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\leq G\leq\lambda+\delta\right)>0
. Since
\eta_{d}\to 0
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{d\to\infty}\,d_{KS}\left(\sup_{f}G_{P}(f),\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)+\sqrt{n}q\sigma M\right)\geq x(\delta)>0.

We now select
M
so that
\nu_{p}^{p}(\mathcal{F},P)
and
\nu_{p}^{p}(\mathcal{F},P^{\prime})
have the same order. Clearly
\nu_{p}^{p}(\mathcal{F},P)=b_{p}^{p}
. Taking
M=\frac{b_{p}}{\sigma}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{p}}\geq 1
yields

b_{p}^{p}\leq\nu_{p}^{p}(\mathcal{F},P^{\prime})=(1-q)b_{p}^{p}+q(\sigma M)^{p}\leq 2b_{p}^{p}

and

\sigma^{2}\leq\nu_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{F},P^{\prime})=(1-q)\sigma^{2}+q\sigma^{2}M^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}(1+qM^{2})\leq 2\sigma^{2}

where we used that
qM^{2}\leq\varepsilon M^{2}\leq 1
(as required below).

By the definition of
M
,
qM\geq\frac{\delta}{\sigma}(2n\ln d)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
holds as long as
\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\geq\frac{2\delta}{b_{p}}(2n\ln d)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, which is an assumption. For large enough
d
,

\displaystyle d_{KS}\left(\sqrt{n}\sup_{f}\widehat{E}_{f}^{\varepsilon}(X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}),\sup_{f}G_{P}(f)\right)+d_{KS}\left(\sqrt{n}\sup_{f}\left\{\widehat{E}_{f}^{\varepsilon}(X_{1:n}^{{}^{\prime}\varepsilon})-q\sigma M\right\},\sup_{f}G_{P^{\prime}}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x(\delta)-C(\sigma)\ln d\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sigma M-e^{-\frac{\varepsilon n}{6}}.

If
\varepsilon n\geq o_{1}:=6\ln\frac{8}{x(\delta)}
and
\sqrt{\varepsilon}M=\frac{b_{p}}{\sigma}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\leq 1\wedge\frac{x(\delta)}{8\sigma C(\sigma)\ln d}
the previous term can be lower-bounded by
\frac{1}{4}x(\delta)
. We finish observing that for the sum of two distances to be larger than
\frac{1}{4}x(\delta)
at least one distance must be larger than
c_{1}(\delta):=\frac{1}{8}x(\delta)
.
∎

6.2
Order of Theorem 2.1 of
[
9
]

The goal of this section is to show that
[
9
]
yields a bound of order at least
\left(\frac{(K_{n}^{\prime})^{7}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{8}}
. We recall the theorem below for convenience. The result from
[
9
]
was originally stated in terms of a Gaussian coupling, but the authors also provided an anti-concentration lemma to obtain a representation in Kolmogorov distance. Thus, to obtain a comparable result we combined its original form with the anti-concentration lemma the authors provided
[
9
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Theorem 6.1

(Theorem 2.1 of
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions and
P
a probability measure. If

•

\mathcal{F}
is VC-type with envelope
F
and constants
A\geq 2
,
v\geq 1
,

•

there exist constants
b\geq\sigma>0
and
p\in[4,\infty)
such that
\nu_{k}^{k}\leq\sigma^{2}b^{k-2}
for
k=2,3,4
and
\|F\|_{L^{p}(P)}\leq b
,

•

and
K_{n}^{\prime}:=v\left(\ln n\vee\ln\frac{Ab}{\sigma}\right)
satisfies
K_{n}^{\prime}\leq n^{\frac{1}{3}}
,

then, for every
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\varrho\leq\Psi\left(C_{1}\left\{\frac{bK_{n}^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\frac{1}{p}}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}}+\frac{(b\sigma^{2}(K_{n}^{\prime})^{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}}{\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}n^{\frac{1}{6}}}\right\}\right)+C_{2}\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{n}\right)

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are positive constants depending only on
p
and

\Psi(\eta)=\inf_{\delta,r>0}\left\{\frac{2}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\left(\eta+\Xi(\delta)+r\delta\right)\left(\sqrt{2v\ln\frac{Ab}{\delta}}+2\right)+e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}\right\}.

If
\delta\leq n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha<0
, then
\Psi(\eta)\geq C\frac{\sqrt{K_{n}^{\prime}}}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\eta
for some constant
C
depending on
\alpha
. Therefore, the bound given by the previous theorem has order at least

\frac{(K_{n}^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{1}{p}}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}}+\frac{(K_{n}^{\prime})^{\frac{7}{6}}}{\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}n^{\frac{1}{6}}}+\gamma.

Since
p\geq 4
, the previous equation is minimized when the last two terms are equal, yielding at least
\left(\frac{(K_{n}^{\prime})^{7}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{8}}
.

6.3
Proof of the bootstrap approximation result

Before starting the proof of our bootstrap approximation result we recall the following theorem from
[
11
]
, which will be used in the proof:

Theorem 6.2

(Adapted from Lemma 4.5 and Lemma 4.6 of
[
11
]
)
.

Make the same assumptions as in Theorem
5.1
. Let
\tilde{Z}_{n}
be obtained from the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap or the empirical bootstrap. There exists a constant
C
depending only on
\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F})
and
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
such that

1.

if
f(X_{1})-Pf
is sub-exponential with
\psi_{1}
-Orlicz norm bounded by
B
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, then with probability at least
1-C\delta_{n,d,B}
,

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{Z}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\,\right|X_{1:n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq C\delta_{n,d,B};

2.

if
\Ex{\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(X_{1})-Pf|^{p}}\leq B^{p}
for some
p\in(2,\infty)
, then with probability at least

1-C\left(\delta_{n,d,B}\vee\delta_{n,p,d,B}\right)
,

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{Z}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\,\right|X_{1:n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq C\left(\delta_{n,d,B}\vee\delta_{n,p,d,B}\right).

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

As in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we assume, without loss of generality, the class
\mathcal{F}
is centered. Recall that

\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f):=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-2k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-k}f(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{(i)})-\widehat{T}_{n,k}(f,X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n}),\,\,(f\in\mathcal{F}),

where the points
\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{i}
are i.i.d. with law
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}}
. The strategy to prove the empirical bootstrap approximation is to approximate
\tilde{Z}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})
by
\tilde{Z}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F})
and then use bootstrap approximation for the empirical mean. We let

M=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}{11n}\vee\frac{\lceil\ln(nd)\rceil}{11n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p}}\quad\text{ and }\quad t=121n\frac{\nu_{p}^{p}}{M^{p}}=11\left(\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\vee\lceil\ln(nd)\rceil\right).

1.

Counting.
Define

\tilde{V}_{M}(\mathcal{F})=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\left|f(\tilde{X}_{i})\right|>M\right\}}

to be the analogous of
V_{M}(\mathcal{F})
for the empirical bootstrap. By Lemma
5.3
we know that the event
E=\left\{V_{M}(\mathcal{F})<\frac{t}{11}\right\}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(E)\geq 1-de^{-\frac{t}{11}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}
. From now on we will be conditioning on
E
. Lemma
5.3
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{V}_{M}(\mathcal{F})\geq t~\right|~E\right)\leq de^{-t}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

2.

Controlling the contamination.
Let
\tilde{\varepsilon}
be the proportion of contaminated sample points in
\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{1:n}
, i.e.,

\tilde{\varepsilon}=\frac{|\{i:\tilde{X}_{i}^{\varepsilon}\neq\tilde{X}_{i}\}|}{n}.

Notice that
\tilde{\varepsilon}
is independent of
X_{1:n}
. By Chernoff’s bound for the binomial distribution:

\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\varepsilon}n-\varepsilon n\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon n}\right)^{2}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

3.

Bounding.
Now we can use the bounding lemma (Lemma
5.2
) to approximate
\tilde{Z}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{F})
by
\tilde{Z}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F})
with high probability conditioned on
E
. This is done using the lemma twice with
k=\phi n
and
\phi=2\frac{t}{n}
. It yields

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n}(\tau\circ f)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}(f,\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}_{1:n})-\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ f,\tilde{X}_{1:n})\right|

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n}\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}(f,X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n})-\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ f,X_{1:n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 12\phi M\sqrt{n}.

4.

Approximating the empirical approximation.
Lemma
6.2
(case (i)) gives, with probability at least
1-C_{1}\left(\frac{M^{2}\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}
,

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{Z}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\,\right|X_{1:n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{M^{2}\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

for some absolute constant
C_{1}
depending only on
\nu_{2}(\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F})\leq\nu_{2}(\mathcal{F})
and on
\underline{\sigma}_{\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F},P}
. The weak variance
\underline{\sigma}_{\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F},P}
can be chosen such that
\underline{\sigma}_{\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F},P}\geq\frac{1}{2}\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
, as a consequence of our final choice of
M
, of assumption (
2.1
), and of Lemma
5.5
.

5.

Gaussian approximation and anti-concentration
. Lemma
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
yield

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tilde{Z}_{n}(\tau_{M}\circ\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\,\right|X_{1:n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z(\mathcal{F})\leq\lambda\right)\right|\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{M^{2}\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+C_{2}(\ln d)\sqrt{4\nu_{p}^{p}M^{2-p}}

for some
C_{2}
that depends only on
\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}
. After combining with Nazarov’s anti-concentration inequality (Lemma
5.6
) we get, with probability at least
1-C_{1}\left(\frac{M^{2}\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}-\frac{1}{n}
,

\tilde{\varrho}\leq 12\phi M\frac{2+\sqrt{2\ln d}}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\sqrt{n}+C_{1}\left(\frac{M^{2}\ln^{5}(nd)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+C_{2}(\ln d)\sqrt{4\nu_{p}^{p}M^{2-p}}+\frac{2}{n}.

6.

Finish the calculations
. The bound on
\tilde{\varrho}
is the same, up to a constant, as the bound in
\varrho
obtained in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Thus, the same bounds can be used. This finishes the proof.

∎

6.4
Proof of variance approximation

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
M,t,k
be as defined in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Under the same assumptions, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{\varepsilon}_{n,k}(f)-\sigma_{f}\right|\geq\frac{c}{\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}}\left\{\nu_{p\wedge 4}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}}+\nu_{p}^{2}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}.

(6.1)

Proof.

First notice that
\widehat{\sigma}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)
is invariant by translation, so we assume, without loss of generality, that
\mathcal{F}
is centered. From the proof of Theorem
2.1
we know that the event
E:=\{V_{M}(\mathcal{F})\leq t\}
has probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
when taking

M=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}{11n}\vee\frac{\lceil\ln(nd)\rceil}{11n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p}}\quad\text{ and }\quad t=\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\vee\lceil\ln(nd)\rceil.

Recall also that
k=\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+t<\frac{n}{2}
.
If
E
holds,
V_{2M}\left(\left\{f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}\right)<t
, it follows from Lemma
5.2
that

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}\left(\left(f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right)^{2}\right)-\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{2M}\left(f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right)\right)^{2}\right)\right|\leq 24M^{2}\frac{k}{n}.

Now notice that
\tau_{2M}\left(f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right)\neq\tau_{M}(f)-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)
only when
|f|>M
. Given event
E
, the number of sample points for which
|f|>M
is bounded by
t
, thus

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{2M}\left(f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right)\right)^{2}\right)-\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right)^{2}\right)\right|\leq\frac{t}{n}4M^{2}

Using Lemma
5.2
can again be used to yield

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f-\widehat{T}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right)^{2}\right)-\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f-\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)\right)^{2}\right)\right|\leq 24M^{2}\frac{k}{n}+36M^{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{2}\leq 42M^{2}\frac{k}{n}.

Lemma
5.5
let us control the difference between the variance of
\tau_{M}f
and the one of
f
. Combining all these bounds, we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{\varepsilon}_{n,k}(f)^{2}-\sigma_{f}^{2}\right|\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f-\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)\right)^{2}\right)-\sigma_{\tau_{M}f}^{2}\right|+70M^{2}\frac{k}{n}+4\nu_{p}^{p}M^{2-p}

To concentrate the empirical variance appearing above we can decompose

\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f-\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)\right)^{2}\right)-\sigma_{\tau_{M}f}^{2}\right|\leq\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f\right)^{2}\right)-P(\tau_{M}f)^{2}\right|+\left|\left(\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\tau_{M}f\right)\right)^{2}-\left(P\tau_{M}f\right)^{2}\right|

For the first term, Bernstein’s inequality implies that, with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{2nd}
,

\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f\right)^{2}\right)-P(\tau_{M}f)^{2}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2P\left(\tau_{M}f\right)^{4}\ln(4nd)}{n}}+\frac{2M^{2}\ln(4nd)}{3n}.

If
p\in(2,4]
we can use
1\leq M\leq\nu_{p}\left(\frac{\lceil\ln(nd)\rceil}{11n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p}}
and
P(\tau_{M}f)^{4}\leq\nu_{p}^{p}M^{4-p}
to bound, for some absolute constant
c
,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f\right)^{2}\right)-P(\tau_{M}f)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\nu_{p}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}+\frac{2M^{2}\ln(4nd)}{3n}.

Meanwhile, if
p>4
we just bound
P(\tau_{M}f)^{4}\leq\nu_{4}^{4}
. Altogether,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f\right)^{2}\right)-P(\tau_{M}f)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\nu_{p\wedge 4}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}}+\frac{2M^{2}\ln(4nd)}{3n}.

For the second term, we use
\left|a^{2}-b^{2}\right|=\left|a-b\right|\left|a+b\right|\leq\left|a-b\right|\left(\left|a-b\right|+2\left|b\right|\right)
to bound

\left|\left(\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\tau_{M}f\right)\right)^{2}-\left(P\tau_{M}f\right)^{2}\right|\leq\left|\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)-P(\tau_{M}f)\right|\left(\left|\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)-P(\tau_{M}f)\right|+2\left|P(\tau_{M}f)\right|\right).

Since we assumed
f
is centered, we have
\left|P(\tau_{M}f)\right|\leq\sigma_{\tau_{M}f}\leq\sigma_{f}
. Similarly, for some absolute constant
c>0
, Bernstein’s inequality yields, with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{2nd}
,

\left|\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)-P(\tau_{M}f)\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{f}^{2}\ln(4nd)}{n}}+\frac{2M\ln(4nd)}{3n}.

Thus, the first term is the dominating one. We can bound

\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\left(\left(\tau_{M}f-\widehat{P}_{n}(\tau_{M}f)\right)^{2}\right)-\sigma_{\tau_{M}f}^{2}\right|\leq c\nu_{p\wedge 4}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}}+\frac{2M^{2}\ln(4nd)}{3n}

and a union bound yields, with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{\varepsilon}_{n,k}(f)^{2}-\sigma_{f}^{2}\right|\leq c\nu_{p\wedge 4}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}}+\frac{2M^{2}\ln(4nd)}{3n}+70M^{2}\frac{k}{n}+4\nu_{p}^{p}M^{2-p}.

Since
M
has order
\nu_{p}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p}}
we bound, possibly updating the constant,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{\varepsilon}_{n,k}(f)^{2}-\sigma_{f}^{2}\right|\leq c\left\{\nu_{p\wedge 4}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}}+\nu_{p}^{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\right\}.

Considering the case
k
is of order
\varepsilon n
and the case where
k
is of order
\ln(nd)
yields the final bound

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{\varepsilon}_{n,k}(f)^{2}-\sigma_{f}^{2}\right|\leq c\left\{\nu_{p\wedge 4}^{2}\frac{\ln^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}(nd)}{n^{1-\frac{2}{p\wedge 4}}}+\nu_{p}^{2}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\right\}.

The proof follows from the identity
|\widehat{\sigma}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\sigma_{f}|\leq\underline{\sigma}_{\mathcal{F},P}^{-1}|\widehat{\sigma}_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}-\sigma_{f}^{2}|
.

∎
