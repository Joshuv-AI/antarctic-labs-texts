Genetic evolution of Akabane virus isolates and the protective efficacy of inactivated vaccines
Authors: Peng Wang, Mingzhu Zhang, Yanxin qi, Xinyu Cao, Weixin Ji, Xiao Li, Zirui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAkabane virus infection can cause abortion, stillbirth in pregnant ruminants, and congenital malformations in newborns. It is prevalent in most regions of Asia, as well as parts of Africa and Europe, causing severe impacts on the livestock industry. Inactivated vaccines are one of the effective means of disease prevention. In this study, an AKAV strain (CH-JL-01-2022) belonging to genetic group I was isolated, and based on this strain, an inactivated vaccine was developed, with screening conducted for the inactivating agent and adjuvant. Formaldehyde can completely inactivate AKAV, and mixed with the Imject® Alum adjuvant can induce the high differentiation of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells and produce high levels of TNF-α. High titers of neutralizing antibodies can be detected 21 days post-vaccination with inactivated virus and adjuvant mixture The viral load and lesions in some organs after virus challenge can be reduced. It was found that formaldehyde is the optimal Inactivating agent and Imject® Alum is the best adjuvant, laying a foundation for the development of AKAV vaccines.Keywords: Akabane virus, Inactivated vaccines, Immunogenicity evaluation, Adjuvants

Introduction

The Akabane virus (AKAV) is a member of the Peribunyaviridae family and the Simbu serogroup of the genus Orthobunyavirus [1]. It is mainly transmitted through arthropods such as mosquitoes and Culicoides, resulting in abortion, stillbirth and neonatal malformation in pregnant ruminants such as cattle and sheep [2]. Antibodies to AKAV have also been detected in wild animals such as buffalo, camels, deer, wild mustang and wild ass [3].

The AKAV was first found in Japan in 1959 [4], and then detected in South Korea [5], Iraq [6], Iran [7],China [8], and other Asian countries. It has also been reported in Sudan [9], Nigeria [10], Kenya [11], Egypt [1] and Turkey outside Asia [12].

Similar to other viruses within the Bunyaviridae family, the AKAV genome can be divided into L, M, and S segments. The L segment encodes an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, the M segment encodes the viral envelope glycoproteins and non-structural proteins, and fragment exchange is the main way for AKAV gene natural evolution. The S segment encodes the nucleocapsid protein and non-structural proteins [13, 14].

Based on the L and S genes, AKAV is classified into genetic groups Ia, Ib, and II. Segmental exchange of the M gene is the primary mode of natural evolution for AKAV genes, based on the M gene, AKAV can be further divided into four genetic groups: I, II, III, and IV [15].

Inactivated vaccines, nucleic acid vaccines, and viral vector vaccines constitute the various types of vaccines. Inactivated vaccines have been extensively utilized in the prevention of influenza, pabies virus, poliomyelitis, and other diseases [16]. Inactivated vaccine is a kind of vaccine that renders the virus unable to replicate through the application of diverse physical and chemical methodologies. The inactivated vaccine still accounts for a significant proportion in the current global candidate vaccines, even though the technical means are constantly improved [17].

This study assesses the immunogenic effect of whole-virus inactivated vaccines combined with different adjuvants in mice, relying on the screening of inactivating agents.

Materials and methods

Phylogenetic analysis and recombinant analyses

The phylogenetic trees of the coding regions in the S (PQ560880), M (PQ560879), and L (PQ560878) segments were constructed using the maximum likelihood estimation using the Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis program (MEGA11) with bootstrap values based on 1000 replicates. The reference strain was selected based on Xiaohui Zan’s article [14]. RDP4 was used to analyze the recombination events of the AKAV strains (CH-JL-01–2022).

Virus propagation and inactivation conditions screening

AKAV(107 TCID₅₀/100 µL CH-JL-01–2022) was isolated from bovine serum in jilin Provence, China and amplified to the F7 generation. VERO cells were used for 48 h amplified. The Vero cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and cultured at 37 ℃ in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Four concentration gradients (0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5), five time gradients (6, 12, 24, 36, 48), and three types of inactivators (0.25 M Binary ethylenimine, BEI, 0.1 M beta-Propiolactone, BPL, 40% Formaldehyde) was used for screening of inactivation conditions. After inoculating the cells, RNA was extracted for virus load detection to determine the optimal inactivation conditions.

Formulation of the inactivated vaccine

AKAV were amplified on a large scale according to the aforementioned method (Sect. "Virus propagation and inactivation conditions screening"). Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. Formaldehyde at 40% was added to the AKAV at a volume representing of 0.5% of the AKAV volume, and the mixture was continuously shaken at 37℃ for 36 h to prepare the inactivated virus. The volume ratio of the inactive virus to the Montanide ISA 201, Imject® Alum adjuvant, and Mn adjuvant was 1:1. The antigen dose was measured at 107 TCID₅₀/100 µL before inactivation.

Animals and immunization experiments

Forty-five mice (BALB/c, 6-week-old, female) (purchased from Jilin GENET-MED Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changchun, China) were randomly assigned into five groups with nine mice in each group: PBS control group (PBS); AKAV inactivated vaccine group (AKAV), AKAV inactivated vaccine with Imject® Alum adjuvant (AKAV - AL), AKAV inactivated vaccine with Montanide™ ISA 201 adjuvant (AKAV- 201), and AKAV inactivated vaccine with Mn adjuvant (AKAV-Mn).

The mice were immunized with 200 µL vaccine in the leg muscles at day 0, and the immunization was boosted on day 21 (Fig. 2 A). Five mice of each group were randomly selected to be euthanized, and lymphocytes were isolated from the spleen to detect T cells on the day 35. On the 35th day, four remaining mice in each group were challenged with AKAV (10⁶ TCID₅₀/100 µL) by intramuscular injection. Two weeks post-challenge (day 49), the mice were euthanized to collect major organs for viral load detection, and uteruses and brains were harvested for pathological section observation.

RNA extraction and viral load detection

After virus challenge, the RNA of whole blood and major tissues of the mice were extracted according to the instructions of the TIANamp Virus RNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH (BEIJING) CO., LTD). Reinfect the cells using the same method and extract the RNA from the cells. The cDNA was obtained by using the HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd). The virus load was detected by qPCR according to the instructions of AceQ® Universal U+ Probe Master Mix V2(Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd).

Neutralization assay

Serum samples were collected at 0, 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35 days post vaccination. Neutralizing antibody titers in the serum were determined by the virus neutralizing antibody test (VNT), and the antibody titer was assessed by observing the cytopathic effect in Vero cells.

T-cell subset analysis

Mice were euthanized and spleens were removed at 35 day. After 36 h of antigen inactivation stimulation. Lymphocytes were isolated according to the instructions of the mouse lymphocyte isolation kit(Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd). 1 × 106 lymphocytes were incubated with PE/Cyanine7-CD3, PE-CD4, and FITC-CD8 antibodies biolegend, USA in the dark then washed by 1 ×BD Perm/Wash Buffer, and all flow cytometry data were acquired with the Beckman Coulter flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences). Data was further analyzed using FlowJo ™ Software.

Cytokine detection in serum

Serum samples were collected at 35 days post vaccination, and IL − 10, TNF - α, and IFN - α were detected by a Commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (CLOUD-CLONE Corp. wu han).

Necropsy and histopathology

Necropsies were performed two weeks after challenge, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain and uterus were taken for virus load detection. HE staining (hematoxylin and eosin staining) and in situ hybridization of the brain and uterus were performed using commercial kits (Servicebio, China).

Statistics

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). All data are presented as the mean ± SD. Differences in levels between the different groups were determined by one-way repeated measures ANOVA and multiple T test. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05 (* indicates P < 0.05), P < 0.01 (** indicates P < 0.01).

Results

Characteristics and genetic evolution

Vero cells were infected by AKAV and typical CPE was observed 48 h after infection, with some cells shrinking and detaching off (Fig. 1A). The virus was detected at 6 h after infection, and the virus copy number reached 107 Copies/µL at 24 h. However, with the increase in time, there was no significant increase in the virus copy number (Fig. 1B).

CH-JL-01–2022 characteristics and genetic evolution. (A) CPE of CH-JL-01–2022. The cells become round and shrink. (B) Growth curve of CH-JL-01–2022. The plateau is reached 24 h after infected.(C) Evolutionary tree of S segment. (D) Evolutionary tree of M segment. (E) Evolutionary tree of L segment. All evolutionary tree analyses indicated that the isolates belonged to genotype I

The evolutionary tree was constructed based on the M segment, and the newly isolated CH-JL-01–2022 belongs to genetic group I. Based on the S and L segments, CH-JL-01–2022 is further classified into genetic subgroup Ia, clustering with strains such as GXLC02 or GXLC04(Fig. 1C and E). No recombination events were detected using RDP4.

Screening of inactivation conditions

Virions in the inactivated virus solution were detected by RT-qPCR. Binary ethyleneimine (BEI) could only inactivate the virus at concentrations of 0.5%, while beta-propiolactone (BPL) remained effective at lower concentrations, and its inactivation effect became more pronounced over time. The inactivation capabilities of BEI and BPL to AKAV were relatively weak, and virions could still be detected even after 48 h. The inactivation effect of formaldehyde was more pronounced; when the concentration was 0.5%, virions undetectable after 36 h of inactivation. Therefore, the final inactivation conditions were determined to be 0.5% formaldehyde for 36 h (Fig. 2B).

Immune response detection

The percentages of CD4+ T cells in the AKAV, AKAV-AL, and AKAV-201 groups were significantly higher than those in the PBS group. The highest mean, at 24.6%, was observed in the AKAV-AL group (Fig. 3A). The AKAV-AL group also induced high level of CD8+ T cells, with the AKAV-AL group showing significantly higher levels than all other groups except AKAV-Mn. (Fig. 3B)

(B) CD8+ T cells in splenic lymphocytes were detected 35 days after vaccination. (C) The level of IFN-α in serum 35 days after vaccination. (D) The level of TNF-α in serum 35 days after vaccination. (E) The level of IL-10 in serum 35 days after vaccination

All immunized groups exhibited increased levels of IFN-α, and the AKAV-201 group showing a significant increase compared to the PBS group (Fig. 3C). In the AKAV-AL group, there was a significant increase in TNF-α levels post vaccination, while IL-10 levels did not increase but rather decreased to some extent and no significant increase in IL-10 was observed in the other immunized groups (Fig. 3D and E). The TNF-α levels in the AKAV-201 group also increased, but the difference was not significant (Fig. 3D).

Neutralizing antibody levels and viral load

Neutralizing antibodies can be detected 7 days after vaccination, and the antibody titer also increases over time. All adjuvant-added immunized groups exhibited higher antibody levels than the inactivated virus-only group within the first 21 days (Fig. 4A). There was no significant difference between the AKAV-201 and AKAV-AL groups on day 35, with antibody titers approximately at 1:128 (Fig. 4A). The Mn adjuvant showed limited enhancement, with antibody levels during the later stages of immunization only reaching 1:32 − 1:64, which was not significantly different from the AKAV group (Fig. 4A).

(B) Detection of the viral load in the whole blood of mice at 7 and 14 days after challenge. (C) Detection of the viral load in the tissues of mice at 14 days after challenge

There were no significant differences in viremia, and all immunized groups showed a limited degree of decline by day 14 post-infection. (Fig. 4B) Significant reductions in virus loads were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and brain of the AKAV-201 and AKAV-AL immunized groups (Fig. 4C). The AKAV group also showed significant reductions in the heart, spleen, and kidney. The protective effect of the Mn adjuvant was limited, only reducing virus loads in the kidney and brain. Compared with the PBS group, all immunized groups did not exhibit a substantial decrease of viral load in the lung and uterus (Fig. 4C).

Histopathology

A significant number of neuronal degenerations were observed in the PBS group, characterized by shrunken and deeply stained cell bodies. Neuronal deformations were also visible in the other groups, but they were relatively mild (Fig. 5A). A comprehensive evaluation of brain lesions was conducted based on four indicators: neuronal degeneration, tissue edema, necrosis, and hemorrhage. The AKAV and AKAV-AL groups had relatively lower scores, indicating a certain protective effect on the brain (Fig. 5A).

(B) Results of hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and pathological scores of the uterus at 14 days after challenge. (Arrows indicate shedding of epithelial cell layers and exposure of the lamina propria)

The uterine tissue structure in the PBS group partial epithelial cell shedding and exposure of the lamina propria, while no significant epithelial cell shedding was observed in the other groups (Fig. 5B). An evaluation of uterine lesions was conducted based on four indicators: epithelial cell degeneration, inflammatory cell infiltration, decreased gland number, and tissue edema. No lesions were observed in the AKAV-Mn group, and AKAV-AL also exhibited a protective effect on the uterus (Fig. 5B).

In situ hybridization was utilized to detect the distribution of AKAV in tissues. A comparatively larger positive area was observed in brain tissues of the PBS group and the AKAV-Mn group, whereas the positive area in brain tissues of other immune groups was relatively low (Fig. 6A). In the uterus, only the positive area of the AKAV-AL group was lower, and no significant differences were observed among the other groups (Fig. 6B). The average positive area of brain tissue was one order of magnitude higher than that of uterus.

Discussion

Inactivated vaccines have been extensively utilized for the prevention of diseases such as influenza, pabies virus, poliomyelitis, and others [16]. Various physicochemical methods, including beta-propiolactone (BPL), binary ethylene imine (BEI) formaldehyde, and ultraviolet irradiation, are employed for virus inactivation [18].

In 2022, an outbreak of Akabane virus (AKAV) infection emerged in Jilin Province, China, causing abortion, stillbirth, and neonatal paralysis in pregnant livestock [19]. During this epidemic, a strain of AKAV (CH-JL-01–2022) was isolated. Through genetic evolution analysis, it was determined that the strain belonged to group I, the S gene and L gene exhibited a close genetic relationship with GXLC isolates, while the M gene had a close genetic relationship with KSB-2/P/01. RDP4 was used to detect the occurrence of recombination events, but no recombination event was detected.

Consequently, this study developed an inactivated vaccine based on the CH-JL-01–2022 strain isolated from bovine serum in 2022. Through screening of BPL, BEI, and formaldehyde, it was discovered that formaldehyde could completely inactivate AKAV(Fig. 3B). However, our evaluation criteria were relatively restricted, concentrating solely on virus load detection, which merely demonstrate the safety of formaldehyde inactivation.

Inactivated vaccines elicit limited immune responses in the body, often requiring increased immunization doses or frequencies. Adjuvants can enhance the magnitude, breadth, and duration of immune responses [20, 21]. The AL adjuvant primarily stimulates B cell differentiation to enhance antibody production, inducing a TH2-type immune response [22]. Montanide™ ISA201 is a water-in-oil-in-water based mineral oil-based adjuvant, widely used in the development of veterinary vaccines, and can induce both cellular and humoral immunity [23–25]. The Mn adjuvant promotes antigen uptake, presentation, and germinal center formation through cGAS-STING and NLRP3 activation, significantly enhancing immune responses [26]. We immunized mice with with three different adjuvants and inactivated virus. The Imject® Alum adjuvant could induce high levels of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 3A), indicating that the AKAV-AL group could induce a favorable humoral immune response. Simultaneously, high levels of CD8+ T cells were detected in the AKAV-AL group (Fig. 3B), which was previously only observed in human vaccines [22, 27]. High levels of TNF-α, which primarily participates in TH1-type cellular immune responses [28], were also detected in the AKAV-AL immunized group (Fig. 3D). This demonstrated that the AKAV-AL immunized group could simultaneously induce high levels of humoral and cellular immune responses. TNF-α can enhance the activity and killing function of macrophages, boosting their ability to promote immune responses [29].

Compared with the study by Yohsuke OGAWA et al. [30], the vaccine developed in this study induced higher titers of neutralizing antibodies. Compared to the trivalent vaccine developed by Yeon-Hee Kim [31], the neutralizing antibody titer against akav generated in this study is slightly higher and appears earlier, providing some early protection. Compared to the trivalent vaccine developed by Silke Hechinger et al. [3], which includes AKAV, the neutralizing antibody titer induced in this study is marginally lower. However, neutralizing antibodies of this study could be detected in the first week post-vaccination and peaked in the third week, much earlier than the sixth week for the Silke Hechinger developed (Fig. 4A).

Lower viremia was observed in the blood, which may be because mice are not the optimal model for simulating viremia after Akabane virus (AKAV) infection [32]. However, mice are the best model for vaccine research. They have advantages such as controllable quality and low cost, and have been used in the development of various vaccines, including those for AKAV [31], FMDV [33], etc. Therefore, mice were selected as the subject of vaccine evaluation in this study. Compared with the PBS group, the AKAV-AL and AKAV-201 groups significantly reduced virus load in organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, and other organs, which proved that the vaccine had protective effect.

In the HE staining results, the degree of lesion in the AKAV-AL group is milder, including fewer neuronal degenerations in the brain and fewer epithelial cell degenerations in the uterus. In the overall pathological score, the scores of the AL group are lower. In the in situ hybridization results, the virus distribution in the brain and uterus of the AL group is also less, which once again proves that a better protective effect can be obtained after combined with the AL adjuvant. When comparing the positive areas of the brain and uterus, we find that the virus distribution in the brain tissue is higher than that in the uterus, indicating that CH-JL-01–2022 has a higher tissue tropism for the brain, which may also be one of the main reasons for the relatively large proportion of neonatal malformations in this epidemic.

Conclusion

In this manuscript, formaldehyde is shown to completely inactivate the AKAV virus. When combined with AL adjuvant, the inactivated AKAV whole-virus vaccine can elicit high titers of neutralizing antibodies and robust cellular and humoral immune responses, offering a certain degree of protection for the brain and uterus against viral attacks.
