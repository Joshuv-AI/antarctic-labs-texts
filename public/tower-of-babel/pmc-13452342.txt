Protocol for the enrichment of endosteal and periosteal mesenchymal cells from murine bone for single-cell transcriptome analysis
Authors: Alongkorn Kurilung, Visanu Wanchai, Jinhu Xiong, Maria Almeida, Charles A O'Brien, Intawat Nookaew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBone comprises diverse cell populations essential for skeletal development, remodeling, and homeostasis. Current single-cell isolation methods often use bulk digestion that can obscure distinctions between periosteal and endosteal compartments. Here, we present an optimized murine bone single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) workflow using Liberase™ digestion, EDTA decalcification, and sequential depletion of hematopoietic and endothelial cells to improve yield and viability. We also provide a basic Seurat (R) pipeline for data processing and visualization.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Nookaew et al.1Subject areas: Bioinformatics, Single Cell, Genomics

Before you begin

Bone is a complex tissue that contains a wide range of cell types. It retains the potential to repair and regenerate throughout life, relying on skeletal stem cells and progenitor cells capable of differentiating into osteoblasts and producing new matrix. These cells can be found in distinct bone compartments, including the periosteum and endosteum.2 Both these compartments are important sites for bone growth and remodeling. Mesenchymal osteoprogenitor cells account for less than 1% of cells present in the bone microenvironment.3,4 The microenvironment of cells presents at endocortical and periosteal bone surfaces is very different. While cells present in the endosteal surface are indirect contact with the bone marrow, cells in the periosteal surface are in contact with tendons and muscles. In addition, in the endosteal surface, bone is continuously remodeled via a process in which osteoclasts, cells of hematopoietic lineage, resorb bone and osteoblasts produce bone matrix to replace resorbed bone. In contrast, bone formation in the periosteal surfaces occurs independent of bone resorption. The periosteal compartment also contains a layer of fibroblast separating the cells on the bone surface from the attached muscles. At both endosteal and periosteal surfaces, many osteoblasts become entrapped in the matrix and differentiate into osteocytes. Osteocytes are long lived cells that orchestrate bone remodeling by producing factor that modulate bone resorption and formation. All of these cells are under the influence of a variety of biological and mechanical stimuli which maintain or disrupt normal bone homeostasis.5

The presence of diverse cell populations contributes to the functional diversity of bone tissue. However, until recently, our understanding of the cellular composition of bone and the characteristics of individual cells has been limited. In order to comprehend the heterogeneity of mesenchymal cell populations. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers valuable opportunities to examine individual bone cells and the potential impact on gene expression of diseases and disorders. This technique provides the transcriptome profile of individual cells, allowing for the classification and characterization of specific cell types, which has considerably increased our understanding of bone biology and accelerated novel discoveries.6,7,8,9 Nevertheless, capturing sufficient bone mesenchymal cells such as osteoblastic cells, which play essential roles in bone homeostasis, for analysis is challenging due to their extremely low amount relative to other resident cells in the bone microenvironment.

Innovation

Understanding the distinct biology of periosteal and endosteal mesenchymal populations requires isolation methods that can reliably separate these two bone compartments with minimal cross-contamination. However, current bone dissociation workflows often use bulk digestion or whole-surface preparations, limiting spatial resolution and increasing cross-contamination.10,11 In addition, dense bone extracellular matrix and strong cell adhesion make it difficult to obtain viable, high-quality cells for scRNA-seq.10,11,12 Here, we present an optimized and reproducible protocol for isolating periosteal and endosteal mesenchymal cells from murine long bones for scRNA-seq.1,13,14,15,16,17,18 Unlike conventional bulk bone digestion methods, this workflow enables compartment-specific enrichment while minimizing cross-contamination. The protocol combines precise compartment separation, enzymatic digestion, EDTA-mediated decalcification, and MACS-based depletion of non-mesenchymal cells.19 This approach improves recovery of viable skeletal stromal cells, including osteoblasts and osteocytes, suitable for 10× Genomics capture. A key innovation is the use of Wnt16 transcript expression as a molecular quality-control indicator to assess periosteal contamination in endosteal preparations.1 Because Wnt16 is enriched in periosteal cells but minimal to absent from endosteal fractions, it provides a practical validation step for compartment purity. The workflow is flexible and can be adapted to retain endothelial cells or isolate cells from other skeletal regions such as the knee joint.13 Overall, this protocol provides a reproducible method for compartment-resolved bone cell isolation and improves the accuracy of periosteal and endosteal single-cell analysis.

Institutional permissions

All animal procedures must be reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted in accordance with regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. This study was conducted using protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences.

This protocol was used with C57BL/6J wild-type mice and genetically modified lines including Dmp1-Cre, Osx-Cre, Ai9, and humanized Tnfs11 mice.1 The experiments described in this study were performed using 6-months-old mice. Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, with 2-5 mice per cage, provided ad libitum water and an irradiated Purina diet. Mice were maintained on a 12:12 h light-dark cycle under standard temperature- and humidity-controlled conditions. Because bone cell composition can vary with age, sex, and genetic background, researchers may need to optimize the protocol settings depending on the experimental objective.

Workflow for single-cell isolation for periosteum

(A) Carefully dissect all the hindlimbs including femora and tibiae.

(B) Remove all surrounding muscle tissue using a sterile surgical scissor.

(C) Transfer the bones to a new sterile 6-well culture plate and digest with a 2.154 mL HBSS/Ca2+/Mg2+ with Liberase™ (∼2 Wunsch units) and incubate at 37 °C for 20 min with shaking at 200 RPM in an incubated shaker.

(D) Collect the digested solution.

(E) Pellet cells by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C.

(F) Repeat enzymatic digestion and EDTA decalcification following the protocol to obtain four sequential cell fractions.

(G) Combine cell fraction 1-4.

(H) Filter the pooled suspension through a 30-μm cell strainer.

(I) Deplete hematopoietic and endothelial contaminants using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with lineage, CD45, CD117, and CD31 antibody-conjugated magnetic beads.

(J) Collect the enriched periosteal cell fraction.

(K) Assess total cell number and viability using Trypan Blue exclusion assay and a hemocytometer.

(L) Proceed with Gel Bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation using the 10× Genomics Chromium Controller.

Workflow for single-cell isolation for endosteum

(A) Carefully dissect all the hindlimbs including femora and tibiae.

(B) Remove all surrounding muscle tissue using sterile gauze, scrape off the periosteum, and excise the epiphyses with a sterile scalpel.

(C) Open the bones longitudinally and flush the marrow using storage buffer.

(D) Mince the bones into ∼1 to 2 mm sized pieces using new sterile scalpel.

(E) Transfer the bone fragments into a 6-well culture plate, and digest the bone using Liberase™ (∼2 Wunsch units), and incubate at 37 °C for 20 min in a shaking incubator (200 RPM).

(F Collect the digested solution.

(G) Pellet cells by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C.

(H) Repeat enzymatic digestion and EDTA decalcification following the protocol to obtain eight sequential cell fractions.

(I) Combine cell fraction 1–8.

(J) Filter the pooled suspension through a 70-μm cell strainer.

(K) Deplete hematopoietic and endothelial contaminants using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with lineage, CD45, CD117, and CD31 antibody-conjugated magnetic beads.

(L) Collect the enriched endosteal cell fraction.

(M) Assess total cell number and viability using Trypan Blue exclusion assay and a hemocytometer.

(N) Proceed with Gel Bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation using the 10× Genomics Chromium Controller.

Photographs of the items used are provided in the Data S1.

Expected outcomes

Single-cell isolation from murine bone samples (two femurs and two tibiae per animal) typically yielded >20,000 cells per sample. However, cell yield at the completion of the preparation protocol varied among individual samples. Prior to Gel Bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation, microscopic inspection confirmed that cells remained as single, discrete entities with minimal cellular debris present. Figure 3A depicts successful single cells stained with Trypan blue. A minimum cell viability of ≥80% is recommended prior to loading to support accurate cell recovery, minimize background signal, and generate high-quality scRNA-seq data for downstream analysis. This threshold was adopted as a practical guideline consistent with standard 10× Genomics recommendations for optimal GEM generation and library quality. When necessary, samples with 70%–80% viability may still be processed; however, these samples should ideally undergo dead-cell removal or additional cleanup steps, as reduced viability may increase ambient RNA contamination, cell aggregation, and the risk of microfluidic failure. After cDNA synthesis, the yield and size distribution of cDNA products provided preliminary quality assessment of single-cell capture efficiency and served as a checkpoint before library preparation (Figure 3B).

Following sequencing, Cell Ranger generates a series of quality control (QC) metrics that serve as indicators of overall data quality. High-quality scRNA-seq datasets typically exhibit adequate sequencing depth, sufficient numbers of captured cell, high gene and unique molecular identifier (UMI) count per cell, and low levels of technical noise (Figure 3C). In bioinformatic analysis using Seurat R package, we applied a series of quality-control (QC) filters to remove low-quality cells. Cells were retained only if they met the following criteria: the total number of UMI (nUMI ≥500), the total number of genes (nGene ≥1000 and ≤10,000), the number of library complexity (log10GenesPerUMI >0.8), and the percentage of mitochondrial genes (percent.mt ≤10). After pre-processing, comparison of the initial and final cell counts showed that approximately 21% of cells were removed, including low-quality cells, possible doublets, and other outliers.

To assess the purity of endosteal single-cell preparations, we recommend using Wnt16 expression as a practical molecular indicator of periosteal contamination. Because Wnt16 was enriched in periosteal populations in our dataset, it served as a useful indicator of periosteal enrichment; however, it should be interpreted as an indicator rather than an absolute marker of periosteal contamination.1 After generating a Seurat object from the scRNA-seq data, researchers can visualize Wnt16 expression across all clusters using FeaturePlot or VlnPlot. In high-quality endosteal preparations, Wnt16 expression should be minimal to absent, as this gene is specifically enriched in periosteal stromal populations. The presence of distinct Wnt16+ clusters or widespread Wnt16 expression within the endosteal dataset suggests contamination with periosteal-derived cells and may indicate insufficient removal of periosteal tissue during bone sample preparation (Figure 3D).

Based on our scRNA-seq analysis of murine bone, using 29,123 high-quality cells after filtering, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) clustering identified 11 major cell populations in the periosteum and endosteum. Periosteum comprised osteo-X cells (28.07%), pre-osteoblasts (22.74%), tenocytes (12.94%), chondrocytes (9.84%), fibroblast-1 cells (9.71%), osteoblasts (7.96%), articular chondrocytes (2.93%), fibroblast-2 cells (2.77%), osteocytes (2.33%), osteo-Cxcl12-abundant reticular (CAR) cells (0.56%), and adipo-CAR cells (0.13%) (Figure 3E). Endosteum comprised osteoblasts (55.28%), adipo-CAR cells (11.29%), pre-osteoblasts (9.99%), osteo-X cells (9.18%), osteo-CAR cells (8.03%), osteocytes (3.67%), chondrocytes (0.92%), articular chondrocytes (0.91%), fibroblast-2 cells (0.30%), fibroblast-1 cells (0.29%), and tenocytes (0.13%) (Figure 3F).

Additionally, our protocol is designed to be flexible and can be readily adapted to accommodate different experimental objectives. For example, researchers interested in preserving endothelial populations may omit the CD31-based depletion step to retain CD31+ cells for downstream analyses without compromising overall cell viability or dissociation efficiency. The protocol can be also effectively applied to other skeletal sites, including mouse knee joints, where periosteal and endosteal surfaces are anatomically well defined and highly accessible.13

Limitations

A limitation of this protocol is that endosteal cell isolation require a longer and more repetitive digestion workflow than periosteal cell isolation because endosteal cells are tightly associated with the mineralized bone surface. Although repeated alternating Liberase™ digestion and EDTA decalcification steps improve cell release from the bone matrix, the extended processing time may affect cell viability, induce stress-response transcriptional programs, and influence the representativeness of the recovered endosteal cell population.20

An additional limitation is that although the protocol described here is highly optimized for murine bone; however, despite improved overall cell recovery, osteocytes remain difficult to isolate as intact, embedded cells from the mineralized matrix and are therefore likely to be underrepresented in the resulting single-cell suspensions. In addition, while hematopoietic and endothelial depletion substantially increases the purity of bone-derived mesenchymal cells, it may reduce the ability to simultaneously profile immune and vascular components of the bone marrow niche within the same experimental run. Researchers should therefore consider this trade-off carefully when designing studies aimed at capturing the full cellular complexity of the bone microenvironment.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low cell yields after digestion (step 45 for periosteal single cell isolation and step 105 for endosteal single cell isolation).

Potential solution

Make sure that only two femur and two tibiae are used per 2 Wunsch units/mL for periosteum isolation and endosteum isolation. In addition, follow the appropriate incubation times listed in the protocol: Liberase™ at 37 °C for 20 min, and EDTA at 37 °C for 20 min.

Problem 2

Low cell viability after digestion (step 45 for periosteal single cell isolation and step 105 for endosteal single cell isolation).

Potential solution

Make sure to cut the bone into ∼1–2 mm pieces for single cell isolation in endosteum. To avoid damaging cells during pipetting, pipette gently and avoid introducing air bubbles. Follow the recommended incubation time for Liberase™ digestion (20 min at 37 °C) to prevent overdigestion. During filtration, do not scrape the strainer mesh, as this can damage the cells.

Problem 3

MS column is clogged during lineage depletion, and CD45, CD117, and CD31 depletion (steps 32d and 40d for periosteal single cell isolation and steps 93c and 100c for endosteal single cell isolation).

Potential solution

Make sure the cell concentration is not too high when loading the suspension onto the column. If it is too concentrated, split the sample and pass it through two LS columns.

Problem 4

Incomplete cell depletion and lower recovery of the bone cells (step 43 for periosteal single cell isolation and step 103 for endosteal single cell isolation).

Potential solution

Ensure efficient magnetic labeling of mature hematopoietic cells, bone marrow cells, and endothelial cells. Miltenyi Biotec recommends labeling 1×107 total cells with 100 μL of MicroBeads. Using a higher MicroBeads-to-total cell ratio can improve the removal of unwanted cells.
