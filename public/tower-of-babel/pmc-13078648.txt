Resilience among bereaved siblings of homicide and traffic accidents in Japan
Authors: Misato Ota, Kazuma Nonaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Siblings bereaved by homicide or accidents are often referred to as the forgotten bereaved because they face difficulties in accessing adequate support. Globally, research focusing directly on siblings’ perspectives remains scarce, and studies specifically addressing siblings’ resilience are almost absent in Japan. A deeper understanding of siblings’ experiences is required to develop support systems that acknowledge their emotions and strengthen their resilience.Objective: This study aimed to clarify the psychological transformation processes of bereaved siblings in Japan, focusing on interactions between individual and environmental factors of resilience.Method: Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 10 siblings who had lost their brother or sister to homicide or accidents. The data were analysed using a modified grounded theory approach that emphasizes context and social interaction in qualitative research.Results: The analysis generated six categories. The siblings experienced the process of grieving and adapting simultaneously during their adjustment. They engaged in practical coping strategies while struggling with the latent conflicts in their families and social relationships. Importantly, positive interactions from others, such as friends of the deceased greeting them at the family altar or spending time together as before, helped the siblings to construct continuing bonds and supported their resilience.Conclusion: These findings highlight distinctive features of grief and adaptation among Japanese siblings. By highlighting siblings’ perspectives, this study underscores the importance of culturally sensitive community-based support that validates hidden grief and promotes resilience.KEYWORDS: Bereaved siblings, victims of crime, resilience, qualitative research, grief, homicide, traffic accidents, sudden death, traumatic loss

Background

Bereaved family members who have lost loved ones through homicide or traffic accidents are at an elevated risk of developing mental disorders, such as post-traumatic stress disorder and prolonged grief disorder (Jann et al., 2024). Accordingly, high-quality psychological support is needed. However, siblings who lose a brother or sister to a violent death are often described as the forgotten bereaved, as support and research tend to focus on parents while bereaved siblings remain overlooked (Dyregrov & Dyregrov, 2005; Pretorius et al., 2010). Although siblings are also bereaved family members in such contexts, in the majority of cases, parents often take on the central role following the loss, and siblings are less likely to be connected to professional support services (Khang et al., 2020). Furthermore, while Tasker (2021) reported that more than half of the siblings of homicide victims in her study were satisfied with police services, suggesting progress in supporting these siblings, there is still a global scarcity of research focusing specifically on bereaved siblings (D’Alton et al., 2022). In Japan, although the Fourth Basic Plan for Crime Victims explicitly emphasizes the need to enhance sibling support (National Police Agency, 2021), research focusing on the siblings of crime victims remains limited, both in Japan and globally. Therefore, appropriate support for bereaved siblings requires research that deepens our understanding of their psychological experiences.

Sibling relationships generally endure throughout a lifetime, and encompass diverse roles (e.g. playmates or rivals) (Adams et al., 2019). Consequently, the loss of a sibling is a multifaceted form of bereavement (Adams et al., 2019). Simultaneously, grief-stricken parents are unable to provide emotional support to their children after losing a child (Forward & Garlie, 2003). As a result, siblings may lose both their brother or sister and the family’s emotional support. In such circumstances, siblings may attempt to support their grieving parents while being affected by factors such as the spread of misinformation through media coverage regarding their deceased sibling and dissatisfaction with the criminal justice system, which can place them at risk of social isolation (Khang et al., 2020). Thus, siblings often struggle with trauma and grief in invisible ways.

Nevertheless, much of the existing evidence on bereaved siblings is based on parental reports and tends to emphasize the ‘negative’ psychological consequences for siblings. To develop support and interventions that reflect bereaved siblings’ experiences, it is essential to conduct studies that directly include their perspectives. Such research should examine the difficulties that siblings face following victimization and address their capacity for adaptation, namely, their ‘resilience’.

Resilience refers to an individual’s ability to recover and positively adapt to severe stress. Resilience is enabled by individual and environmental resources. However, how to define resilience remains a contested matter. Some scholars conceptualize resilience as an individual trait necessary for recovery (Wagnild & Young, 1993), while others regard it as a dynamic process (Luthar et al., 2000). In this study, resilience is understood comprehensively, drawing on the definition that resilience refers to the process of, capacity for, or outcome of successful adaptation despite challenging or threatening circumstances (Masten et al., 1990, p. 426). Specifically, we conceptualize resilience as a dynamic process emerging from the interaction between individual and environmental protective factors in an individual’s response to a crisis. Using this framework, we examine how siblings undergo psychological transformation through this interaction following the loss of a brother or sister.

Although only a few studies have explicitly defined and examined siblings’ resilience, prior research that included siblings who experienced violent loss identified both individual and environmental factors that contribute to recovery. At the individual level, the flexible use of coping strategies (Cohen & Katz, 2015), emotional expression, maintenance of daily routines, including going to school or work, and engagement in meaningful activities, such as immersing oneself in academic studies, has been identified as a strategy that may alleviate stress-related symptoms (Kasahara-Kiritani et al., 2017; Pretorius et al., 2010). At the environmental level, factors such as having three or more surviving siblings (Rostila et al., 2019) and receiving support from outside one’s family have been shown to contribute to the maintenance of psychological well-being (Dyregrov & Dyregrov, 2005). Nevertheless, little is known about how siblings respond to crises that arise during the recovery process and how psychological transformation occurs from their own perspectives.

Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate, through an interview-based investigation, the psychological transformation process that emerges from the interaction between individual and environmental resilience factors among siblings who have lost a brother or sister to homicide or accidents. By incorporating the Japanese cultural context, this study sought to deepen both the local and global understanding of bereaved siblings, a population that remains largely underrepresented in the literature. This both highlights the importance of cross-cultural perspectives and underscores the need for further international research and comparative work on this topic. The study also aimed to inform psychological support services available to bereaved siblings by detailing their experiences.

Although homicide and traffic accidents differ in intentionality and legal context, prior research has conceptualized such losses within a broader framework of sudden and violent bereavement (e.g. Smith & Ehlers, 2021). As the present study aimed to examine shared psychological processes among bereaved siblings rather than to conduct cause-specific comparisons, these cases were analysed together.

Method

Research design

This study used a qualitative design based on the modified grounded theory approach (M-GTA) (Kinoshita, 2003).

Participants

Ten individuals (three men, seven women; aged 20–65 years) who had lost a brother or sister through homicide or traffic accidents participated (Table 1). Participants were recruited from metropolitan and regional areas in Japan, including regions affected by natural disasters; however, further details cannot be reported for ethical reasons. The inclusion criteria were adults not currently receiving medical or counselling care, more than one year after the loss of the sibling, and adequate social adjustment, defined as not being overwhelmed by trauma-related reactions, being able to maintain daily life (e.g. work or study) without major impairment, and being capable of discussing the incident during the interview.

Participants.

Procedure

Eligible participants were referred by victim support centres and police. Written study information was provided, followed by an online or telephone briefing. Informed consent and health checks were completed on the interview day. Semi-structured interviews (≤ 90 min) were held in private rooms at support centres or participants’ homes; nine were face-to-face and one was online. All interviews were conducted in Japanese by native speakers.

Interview guide

The interviews addressed three phases: (1) immediately after the incident (0–1 month), (2) medium to long term (≥ 1 month to present), and (3) current status. The interview topics included physical and psychological status, coping behaviours and cognitions, supportive or negative responses, feelings towards the deceased sibling, and the impact of their bereavement on their life.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained. Participants were contacted after the interviews to confirm their stability and daily functioning, and the research findings were shared with those who requested feedback. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kagawa University Faculty of Medicine (no. 2021-127).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using M-GTA (Kinoshita, 2003), which emphasizes context rather than fragmenting data, and is suited to process studies. Unlike traditional grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), M-GTA avoids fragmentation of data and instead generates concepts while preserving the narrative context through analysis worksheets (Kambaru, 2018; Kinoshita, 2003). Given that the present study aimed to examine dynamic psychological transformation processes, M-GTA was considered particularly appropriate. The analytical theme was ‘psychological transformation processes of siblings who have lost a brother or sister due to homicide or traffic accidents arising from the interaction of individual and environmental factors’. Transcripts were coded iteratively, relevant segments were extracted, concepts were generated, and contrasting cases were compared using theoretical memos to reduce bias. The concepts were integrated into subcategories and categories to form a storyline and diagram. The analytical process was documented in detail with theoretical memos to ensure reflexivity and an audit trail. A qualitative research expert conducted peer debriefing, including confirming the validity of the concepts, categories, and analytical process, and member checking was performed by providing participants with feedback on the findings, thereby enhancing the credibility and validity of the study.

Although gender and developmental stage influence recovery (e.g. Dyregrov et al., 2015), we focused on adult siblings to examine resilience comprehensively; stratified analyses were not conducted. Participants were labelled A to J to ensure confidentiality.

Results

Participants’ demographic characteristics

Interviews were conducted with 10 siblings aged between their twenties and sixties who had lost a brother or sister to homicide or a traffic accident. Four had lost a sibling to traffic accidents, and six had lost a sibling to homicide. The deceased included three older sisters, four younger sisters, one older brother, and two younger brothers (Table 1).

Generated categories, concepts, and their definitions.

Initial Trauma Responses

Initial Trauma Responses consisted of the concepts Trauma and Grief Responses and Remembering a Deceased Sibling. These represent the psychological and physical reactions that emerged during the initial phases after the incident.

Regarding the second concept of Remembering a Deceased Sibling, one participant explained, ‘My sister was talented, but I wasn’t. I felt sorry for my parents’ (G).

Sibling Emotional Pain Following Family and Social Changes

This category comprises the concepts Family Grief and Changes in Relationships, Misreporting and Social Expectations, and Absence of Support and Negative Interactions. As all these reflected the negative interpersonal changes experienced by siblings after victimization, they were grouped under Sibling Emotional Pain Following Family and Social Changes.

For the concept of Misreporting and Social Expectations, one participant explained: ‘The news covered my sister’s case, but they made her seem like someone she wasn’t. I just wanted them to stop’ (E). For Absence of Support and Negative Interactions, another participant stated, ‘I’d never been hurt by others’ words – only because no one really talked to me at all’ (F).

Latent Conflicts Among Siblings in Interpersonal Relationships

This category consists of two subcategories. The first included Consideration for Family, Feeling Unrecognized as Bereaved Siblings, Difficulty Expressing Feelings, and the Unshareable Presence of the Deceased, which were interpreted as Feelings Latent in Interpersonal Relationships during the adaptation process. The second comprised Awareness of Anger Towards Others’ Interactions and Conflict with Family, reflecting Growing Awareness of Feelings Towards Others while carrying such latent emotions. Together, these subcategories were categorized as Latent Conflicts Among Siblings in Interpersonal Relationships during the adaptation process.

Regarding the concept of Consideration for Family, one participant explained, ‘Seeing my father shaken after the incident, I feared losing him too. So I kept telling everyone, “I’m okay”’ (F). For the concept of Difficulty Expressing Feelings, one participant explained: ‘People’s reactions were often different from what I expected. Talking about it only made me feel worse, and I felt the other person might not want to hear it’ (B). For the concept of Awareness of Anger Towards Others’ Interactions, one participant reflected: ‘At first, it felt like a vague unease. But as I reflected, I could express what bothered me’ (E).

Individual Factors in Siblings’ Resilience

This category consisted of two subcategories. These individual factors were interpreted as individual dimensions of resilience that underpin siblings’ adaptation. The first, Necessary Coping in Crisis, comprises the concepts Maintaining Daily Life and Doing What One Can, observed in the immediate aftermath of an incident. The second, Coping with Emotions, includes Crying Alone, Continuous Verbalization of Feelings, and Sharing Feelings with Family Within Limits. Together, these subcategories represent Individual Factors in Siblings’ Resilience.

Regarding the concept of Doing What One Can, one participant explained, ‘ I felt I had to prove in court that my sister was not at fault. Even though I was sad, I focused on learning about the trial’ (B). Regarding the concept of Continuous Verbalization of Feelings, one participant stated, ‘By putting my feelings into words, I can see my emotions clearly. Writing helps me visualize them’ (E). For the concept of Crying Alone, one participant reflected: ‘I didn’t hold back tears. When I felt overwhelmed after school, I went to my room and cried’ (A).

Environmental Factors in Siblings’ Resilience

This category comprised four concepts: Warm and Respectful Support, Ongoing Appreciation for the Deceased Sibling, Parents Receiving Support, Essential Information and Temporary Emotional Support. Together, these were categorized as Environmental Factors in Siblings' Resilience, which interact with individual resilience factors to support siblings’ adaptation.

For the concept of Parents Receiving Support, one participant explained: ‘When my mother began sharing her grief with others, the pressure I felt from her lessened’ (B). For the concept of Ongoing Appreciation for the Deceased Sibling, one participant reflected: ‘ My sister’s friend greeted her at the altar and took her photo on outings. It made me feel my sister is still here’ (A).

Siblings’ Psychological Transformation Through Adversity

This category consists of two subcategories. The first, comprising Relief from Emotional Pain and Constructing a Continuing Bond with the Deceased Sibling, reflected A Positive Change Perceived in One’s Relationships with Others during the recovery process. The second, composed of Unchanging Physical and Emotional Responses and Perceiving Changes in Beliefs and Psychological and Physical States, represented the Sibling’s Current Emotional State as experienced through these positive relational changes. Together, these subcategories illustrate the category Siblings’ Psychological Transformation Through Adversity, which reflects resilience as a dynamic process emerging from the interaction between individual and environmental factors.

Regarding the concept of Relief from Emotional Pain, one participant stated, ‘Talking with my brother’s friends helped me feel a bit brighter, like I could finally laugh about other things again’ (C). For the concept of Constructing a Continuing Bond with the Deceased Sibling, one participant reflected: ‘I feel my sister is still with me, even though I can’t see her. When I do things I enjoy, it feels like we share those moments’ (A). Regarding the concept of Perceiving Changes in Beliefs and Psychological and Physical States, one participant explained: ‘The incident changed my life goals. By opening up and facing my grief, my life direction may have shifted’ (J).

Resilience of bereaved siblings.

Grieving

After the incident, the siblings experienced various Trauma and Grief Responses, such as sadness, anger towards the offender, and sleeplessness. In the early phases, many were unable to accept the deaths of their brothers or sisters. Living without the deceased led them to recognize the roles and relationships that the sibling had previously fulfilled, such as caring like a parent, through Remembering a Deceased Sibling. This heightened awareness of loss intensified their Initial Trauma Responses.

Simultaneously, siblings encountered Sibling Emotional Pain Following Family and Social Changes. They observed their parents’ profound grief and instability, described as Family Grief and Changes in Relationships, which reinforced the significance of the deceased within the family and further amplified Initial Trauma Responses. They also faced Misreporting and Social Expectations, such as inaccurate portrayals of their siblings in the media or expectations to support grieving parents despite their own loss. Moreover, they experienced Absence of Support and Negative Interactions, as attention was often centred on parents, while siblings’ suffering went unnoticed. Collectively, these negative interpersonal experiences contributed to Latent Conflicts Among Siblings in Interpersonal Relationships.

Specifically, siblings struggled to respond to their grieving parents and often suppressed their own emotions, reflecting Consideration for Family. Misreporting and Social Expectations fostered feelings of Feeling Unrecognized as Bereaved Siblings, while also contributing to Difficulty Expressing Feelings, as siblings withheld their emotions and gradually lost clarity regarding their own psychological state. This inability to verbalize emotions partly functioned as a survival strategy amid the chaotic aftermath, delaying awareness of these latent feelings. In addition, siblings often encounter situations in which others ask about their family. Siblings experience difficulty with this, as, even though the deceased are deeply important, disclosing them and their continued presence in the sibling's life requires an explanation of the circumstances of the crime or accident, leading to ambivalence and the Unshareable Presence of the Deceased.

Adapting

As described above, although siblings suffered in private following their loss and struggled due to difficulties in relationships, they also demonstrated Individual Factors in Siblings’ Resilience that enabled them to cope with post-victimization challenges. Despite ongoing trauma and grief, they managed household responsibilities, continued school or work, and were engaged in Maintaining Daily Life. They also sought the necessary information, were involved in criminal court and other judicial proceedings, and supported their families by Doing What One Can. Collectively, these efforts reflected Necessary Coping in Crisis, which allowed them to survive the immediate chaos.

While facing Sibling Emotional Pain Following Family and Social Changes and carrying latent feelings, siblings also practised Coping with Emotions. They expressed feelings selectively by Sharing Feelings with Family Within Limits, processed difficult emotions through Continuous Verbalization of Feelings, and at times released them by Crying Alone. Such efforts gradually brought Awareness of Anger Towards Others’ Interactions and occasionally led to Conflict with Family, influencing Latent Conflicts Among Siblings in Interpersonal Relationships.

Although they often struggled to receive consistent emotional support from bereaved family members, Environmental Factors in Siblings’ Resilience provided important assistance. For example, Warm and Respectful Support, such as being treated as normal without reference to the incident, helped to sustain daily life. In addition, Parents Receiving Support from professional services, as well as Essential Information and Temporary Emotional Support from judicial procedures or trusted individuals, also contributed to recovery.

Through these positive interactions from others, siblings gradually experienced relief from emotional pain, a dimension of siblings' psychological transformation through adversity. Notably, Ongoing Appreciation for the Deceased Sibling, such as friends speaking to the deceased at the family altar as they had before, fostered Constructing a Continuing Bond with the Deceased Sibling.

As time passed, siblings reached life milestones, such as surpassing their siblings’ age or contemplating their and their parents’ ageing. These experiences reinforced Unchanging Physical and Emotional Responses while also resulting in Perceiving Changes in Beliefs and Psychological and Physical States. In this way, siblings came to hold a Sibling’s Current Emotional State, balancing persistent pain with a renewed desire to live based on the values they had learned from the deceased (see Figure 1).

Discussion

The novelty of this study lies in its presenting four theoretical contributions from the perspective of bereaved siblings, thereby offering internationally relevant insights into a population that has been insufficiently examined across cultural contexts. First, it conceptualizes psychological transformation emerging from the interaction between individual and environmental resilience factors as a dynamic process in which grieving and adaptation occur simultaneously. Secondly, it introduces the concept of latent grief coexisting with overt grief reactions, demonstrating that grief need not be externally expressed or verbalized to be integrated into the adaptation process through internal emotional processing. Thirdly, it identifies an intermediate emotional process that transcends the dichotomy of avoidance and confrontation, characterized by internally confronting emotions without outward disclosure. Fourthly, it demonstrates that continuing bonds are not solely intrapsychic phenomena but are reconstructed through the mediating actions of others.

Characteristics of grieving and adapting in siblings

This study demonstrated that siblings simultaneously experienced both the process of grieving, in which they suffered privately while striving to survive each day, and the process of adapting, in which they developed strategies to endure adversity. This finding aligns with multidimensional grief theory (Kaplow et al., 2013), which posits that maladaptive grief and positive adaptation can co-occur.

Previous studies have emphasized that sibling relationships are unique and encompass roles such as playmates, caregivers, and quasi-parental figures. Consequently, siblings experience multiple forms of loss when bereaved (Adams et al., 2019). Moreover, families are deeply affected by grief (Forward & Garlie, 2003), and society often neglects the support needs of siblings in such contexts (Khang et al., 2020). The present findings show that Japanese siblings experience the same patterns of compounded loss.

Importantly, this study revealed that within the process of grieving, siblings not only exhibited overt Initial Trauma Responses recognizable to themselves and others but also experienced Latent Conflicts Among Siblings in Interpersonal Relationships, which were difficult even for siblings themselves to identify. Thus, siblings endured both explicit trauma and grief, as well as ‘latent grief’ that remained largely unacknowledged. This pattern may reflect the nature of siblings’ grief as disenfranchised grief (Doka, 1999), which is often not publicly acknowledged, openly mourned, or socially supported. Recognizing latent grief appears to be a key factor in recovery and adaptation, and the present study highlights its importance from the siblings’ perspective.

Siblings’ perceptions of family relationships

This study showed that siblings observed their families’ profound grief and responded with a maladaptive process of suppressing their feelings to protect their parents. Simultaneously, they gradually engaged in adaptive processes by sharing their emotions with their families within limited boundaries. Prior studies have emphasized the importance of emotional sharing within families (D’Alton et al., 2022), and the present findings support this perspective.

Beyond confirming the findings of earlier research, this study provides new insights from the perspectives of siblings. Siblings often struggled to determine ‘how much’ emotion they could disclose to their families. For example, one participant stated,

I sometimes cried together with my parents. But at the same time, I felt I should not fully show my feelings. So, although I was crying, I did not feel that I was truly able to share my emotions. (D)

This suggests that siblings’ grief includes both emotions expressed by others and latent grief, which remains difficult to articulate. Support providers should therefore avoid assuming that visible expressions, such as crying or family conversations, mean that siblings are adequately sharing their grief. Instead, siblings must be understood as experiencing both expressed and unexpressed grief.

In addition, siblings reported that external support directed towards their parents functioned as a source of psychological relief. One participant stated the following:

There are feelings I cannot share even within my family. Knowing that my mother could talk to someone else about her loss made me feel relieved. The pressure on me lessened. (B)

Thus, when supporting siblings, it is important to recognize that caring for grieving parents can indirectly provide psychological support to siblings themselves.

Siblings’ resilience

As shown in this study, siblings experienced the process of grieving while simultaneously engaging in the process of adapting. This adaptive process emerged through the dynamic interaction of individual and environmental resilience factors.

In the aftermath of the incident, siblings faced overwhelming demands regarding the funeral, investigations, and court trials. Nevertheless, they practised Necessary Coping in Crisis; for example, by independently gathering legal information and supporting their families. Such actions allowed them to endure their daily lives and represented an important process in regaining agency after the sudden loss of a sibling. These behaviours may also reflect the multiple roles that the deceased previously played within the family. While prolonged continuation of such role-taking may warrant careful attention, when temporary, these actions may contribute to siblings’ restoration of agency.

Siblings also maintained connections with non-trauma-related domains such as household duties, school, and work. Sustaining ordinary routines provides psychological distance from extraordinary situations, offering moments of security and calm (Kasahara-Kiritani et al., 2017; Pretorius et al., 2010).

When siblings struggled to verbalize their emotions within the context of Sibling Emotional Pain Following Family and Social Changes, they turned to Coping with Emotions. Practices such as Crying Alone and Continuous Verbalization of Feelings enabled them to gradually become aware of feelings directed towards others. Prior research has shown the difficulty of emotional expression in siblings (Dyregrov et al., 2015); however, this study clarified how siblings privately engage with their emotions after victimization. While grief research has often emphasized the dichotomy between avoidance and confrontation (Okumura et al., 2024), our findings highlight an intermediate process: surviving by internally confronting emotions without outward disclosure. This tendency may be influenced by siblings experiencing disenfranchised grief (Doka, 1999), which suggests that participants’ internal coping is a response to a lack of social recognition and support for their loss. In addition, cultural factors in Japan, such as an emphasis on relational harmony (Kitayama et al., 1997), may contribute to the difficulty of expressing emotions towards others. Future research should examine siblings in other cultural contexts to deepen this understanding further.

In practice, these findings suggest that psychological care providers should recognize the difficulties that siblings face in articulating emotions and validate their survival strategies. Support may include psychoeducation that normalizes private coping, such as creating a space to cry, allowing oneself to feel multiple emotions, and maintaining daily routines, while encouraging siblings to share their feelings with others at their own pace. Respecting individual timing and readiness is crucial to facilitating adaptation and resilience.

Continuing sibling bonds

‘Continuing bonds’ refer to the sense of maintaining a connection with the deceased and constructing the relationship internally after loss (Klass et al., 1996). Prior research has highlighted the importance of such bonds for siblings’ adaptation (Adams et al., 2019), and the present study demonstrates that siblings engage in this process during the process of adapting.

Previous findings indicate that bereaved siblings preserve connections through practices such as keeping mementos or recalling shared memories (D’Alton et al., 2022). However, little attention has been given to how siblings actually construct continuing bonds. This study revealed that siblings not only expressed feelings towards the deceased themselves but also observed others, such as the deceased’s friends, who continued to honour the sibling in everyday ways, such as speaking to a photograph, carrying the sibling’s portrait, or addressing them at a family altar. These interactions enabled siblings to recognize that the deceased continued to ‘exist’ in the hearts of others. One participant reflected:

When I saw my sister’s friends talking to her at the altar, I felt that although she had died, she still truly existed. (A)

Although previous studies have shown that support from the deceased’s friends can be critical for siblings’ mental health (Dyregrov et al., 2025), this study extends the understanding by clarifying how witnessing others sustains their relationship with the deceased and contributes to siblings’ own continuing bonds.

Cultural context is also relevant. In Japan, where interdependence and relational harmony are highly valued (Kitayama et al., 1997), bereaved individuals may rely more on shared coping strategies and show less variation in grief responses than do Western populations (Okumura et al., 2024). Thus, observing others who maintain the deceased as part of their ongoing relationships may be especially significant for Japanese siblings in reconstructing continuing bonds. Future research should examine whether this process can be observed in other cultural contexts.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides new insights into the resilience of siblings bereaved either through homicide or traffic accidents – a rarely examined population – by identifying shared psychological processes through qualitative interviews. However, siblings’ experiences and support needs may vary depending on their age at the time of the incident, the time elapsed since the loss, family structure, and gender. Future research should incorporate these factors, extend qualitative inquiry, and complement it with large-scale surveys to clarify general trends. In addition, studies that include parents and a broader family system are needed to advance a holistic understanding of bereaved families.

Furthermore, although homicide and traffic accidents were analysed together in this study, differences in loss characteristics, such as the intentionality of the perpetrator, media exposure, and incident-specific factors (e.g. perpetrator suicide), may have influenced siblings’ responses. Future research should examine cause-specific variations in greater detail. In addition, because the present study focused on siblings who demonstrated a certain level of social adjustment to examine resilience, caution is warranted in generalizing these findings to siblings experiencing severe or prolonged psychological difficulties.

Conclusion

This study examined, focusing on resilience, the psychological transformation processes of Japanese siblings who had lost a brother or sister to homicide or a traffic accident. The findings suggest that, although bereaved siblings tend to hesitate to seek professional support, everyday interactions in contexts such as school and the workplace play an important role in alleviating emotional distress. In particular, when significant others, such as teachers, friends of the deceased, or friends of the bereaved family, demonstrated consistent concern while treating siblings as they normally would, they helped siblings to feel that it was acceptable to focus on themselves. Therefore, even when bereaved siblings decline direct support, it is important for professionals to collaborate with parents and others in the social network to maintain indirect support. Establishing systems that facilitate access to professional services when needed is also essential in providing psychological support to bereaved siblings. Furthermore, within the context of supportive relationships, it is beneficial to provide psychoeducation that emphasizes that difficulties in verbalizing emotions are natural responses. Encouraging siblings to first express their feelings in a safe private space may support their psychological well-being. For siblings who experience disenfranchised grief, these findings underscore the importance of community-based support grounded in siblings’ perspectives.
