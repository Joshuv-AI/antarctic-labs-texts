Association between antihypertensive drugs and cancer risk: evidence from a population-based study in the Abruzzo region, Southern Italy
Authors: Annalisa Marotta, Marta Di Nicola, Cristiano Vicenti, Paola Borrelli, Camillo Odio, Patricia Giuliani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, the relationship between the use of antihypertensive medications and the risk of developing cancer has been examined yielding heterogeneous results.Using routinely administrative regional healthcare databases from the Abruzzo Region (in Southern Italy), a retrospective cohort study between the years 2017–2024 was conducted investigating the relationship between the use of antihypertensive drugs and the risk of cancer occurrence, by a descriptive-population based approach.MethodsAll Regional Health Service beneficiaries without a cancer diagnosis in the two preceding years, incident users of at least one antihypertensive drug (ACEIs, ARBs, CCBs, BBs or Loop/Thiazides diuretics) were identified. Participants were followed from the “index date” until September 2024 and classified based on the initial antihypertensive drug prescribed. Associations between antihypertensive drugs and cancer risk were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted for certain tumour Macroclasses, defined in accordance with ICD-9-CM classification.ResultsFive hundred twenty-six thousand, three hundred ninety-four individuals were identified as users of at least one antihypertensive drug. Of these, 87 987 individuals met the inclusion criteria and comprised the final cohort (median age 60 years, IQR: 51–69; 48.3% males). Over a median follow-up of 47.8 months (IQR: 21.0–72.0), 3 577 individuals (4.1%) received a cancer diagnosis and the overall number of cancer cases was 3.6% for ACEIs, 3.5% for ARBs, 3.8% for BBs, 3.6% for CCBs, 3.5% for Thiazides and 5.4% for Loop.Our models show a reduced risk of cancer occurrence among users of ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, CCBs, and thiazides (HR: 0.88; 0.83; 0.90; 0.86; 0.85; respectively), whereas treatment with Loop diuretics was associated with a significantly modest risk increase (HR: 1.47), particularly for the malignant neoplasm of digestive organs and peritoneum; respiratory and intrathoracic organs; bone, connective tissue, skin and breast; other and unspecified sites; lymphatic and hematopoietic tissue tumour Macroclasses; as well as in patients in the same tumour Macroclasses but treated with ACEIs.ConclusionOur observational study from the general population of the Abruzzo analysed the association between antihypertensive treatment and cancer risk providing comforting evidence about the oncological safety for many antihypertensive drug classes, apart from Loop diuretics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15332-0.Keywords: Antihypertensive drugs, Cancer risk, Administrative data, Real-world data, Drug safety

Background

Antihypertensive drugs (AHTs) are commonly prescribed to treat hypertension, a global public health problem affecting almost a third of the world’s adult population and regarded as a crucial risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1, 2]. The most common pharmacological classes of antihypertensive medications used in hypertension are angiotensin- converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers (BBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), and diuretics [3]. Given the high prevalence of hypertension, these medications are widely used, and their use has continued to increase in the last decade [4], contributing to decrease cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [5]. However, several studies have demonstrated that the use of antihypertensive drugs may represent a potential risk for other conditions, such as dementia, diabetes, and cancer.

Cancer is another global public health problem, with approximately 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths (including non-melanoma skin cancers) reported worldwide in 2022 [6].

Both observational studies and clinical trials have investigated the association between the use of antihypertensive drugs and the risk of cancer, providing conflicting results [7]. Observational studies have reported moderate reductions or increase in both the overall cancer risk and site-specific cancers, such as the reported increased risk of skin cancer associated with thiazide use [8, 9]. On the contrary, meta-analyses of randomised clinical trials have shown that the five major classes of antihypertensive drugs are associated with a null or very moderate change in the risk of developing cancer when comparing antihypertensive drug users vs controls [10, 11]. To date, renal cell carcinoma is the only cancer type that has been consistently associated with hypertension [12]. Although associations have also been found for colorectal, endometrial, breast and prostate cancer [13–16], the evidence remains inconsistent.

Moreover, available data about the association between antihypertensive drugs and the risk of cancer are limited by the short duration of the follow-up period compared with the exposure period necessary for the development of the majority of malignancies [17].

Due to the uncertainty regarding the effects of antihypertensive medications on cancer incidence, we carried out a retrospective cohort study to investigate the relationship between their use and the risk of cancer occurrence using administrative regional healthcare databases from the Abruzzo region in Southern Italy, by an intention-to-treat approach. Data were analysed for the main classes of antihypertensive medications, recommended by current hypertension guidelines [3].

Methods

Study design

We carried on a retrospective cohort study using routinely gathered administrative regional healthcare databases from the Abruzzo Region (in Southern Italy), using a descriptive population-based approach. Administrative data, referred to hospital discharges and drug prescription databases [classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) coding system] from 2019 to 2022, were used.

All individuals enrolled in the Regional Health System (RHS) were selected and demographic information (age, gender, date of birth, date of death, vital status and the start and the end dates of RHS coverage) along with healthcare-related information, including diagnoses and surgical procedures [classified through the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) – system] were collected. The lists of ICD-9-CM and ATC codes used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2.

Cohort selection

The initial cohort involved all beneficiaries of the RHS, who were incident users of at least one antihypertensive medication, from January 1, 2019, to September 2024, and had no cancer diagnosis (ICD-9-CM, codes from 140 to 208) during the two years preceding the first drug dispensation and were classified based on their first drug dispensation. Patients were included based on antihypertensive prescriptions, regardless of a specific diagnosis code, since the study aimed to evaluate the association between exposure to these medications and cancer risk independently of the underlying indication. The first recorded dispensation of an antihypertensive medication was defined as the “index date”. To identify new users of antihypertensive agents, individuals with any prescription of one or more blood pressure lowering medications in the two years preceding the “index date” were excluded. Additional criteria of exclusion were individuals: aged ≤ 40 or ≥ 85 years old at the “index date”; not beneficiaries of the RHS for at least two years prior to the “index date”; with an ICD-9-CM diagnosis of cancer, at least one dispensation of anticancer medications, or a co-payment exemption for cancer-related drugs or medical services during the two years preceding the “index date”.

Participants with less than twelve months of follow-up after the “index date” were also excluded. The remaining individuals constituted the final study cohort. Patient characteristics included gender and age at the “index date”.

Intention-to-treat approach and follow-up

Antihypertensive medications were classified into the following categories: ACEIs, ARBs, CCBs, BBs, and Diuretics. Diuretics were further stratified into Thiazides and Loop (Supplementary Table 2). The exposure of the cohort members to each class of antihypertensive drugs was assessed using an ‘‘intention-to-treat’’ approach. According to this method, each cohort participant was assigned to the drug class prescribed at the “index date” regardless of any subsequent treatment discontinuation, addition or switching from one drug class to another, during the follow-up period. Due to the non-mutually exclusive exposure to antihypertensive drugs, patients were analysed independently.

Cancer events and person-time within the first year after “index date” were inherently excluded, which minimizes immortal time bias; the follow-up for each participant was defined from the “index date” + one year and the date of cancer diagnosis, occurring at least one year after the “index date”. The Defined Daily Dose (DDD) was considered a proxy for the standard maintenance dose per day for a drug's primary indication in adults, used in conjunction with ATC classification system [18].

Outcome definition

Primary clinical outcome of the present study was the onset of cancer, identified as the first occurrence of an ICD-9-CM diagnosis code for malignant neoplasm documented during the follow-up period.

Only the occurrence of a cancer after the first year of follow-up was considered. In the principal analysis, ICD-9-CM codes from 140 to 208 we considered. In sensitivity analysis, we classified patients in tumour Macroclasses according to the ICD-9-CM classification, as listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis for both the overall cohort and for the sub cohorts stratified according to exposure to each class of antihypertensive drugs, for at least one month during the follow-up, was carried out using means and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR) for the quantitative variables and absolute frequencies (n) and column percentages (%) for the qualitative ones. Normality distribution for quantitative variables was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk. The association between exposure to the class of drugs received at the “index date” and the risk of cancer occurrence was analysed with the Cox model of proportional hazards (hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI), adjusted for gender and age, and the applicability assumption was evaluated by the Schoenfeld test. Each drug class was compared against all other classes combined as the reference group. Censoring criteria were cancer diagnosis, death, loss of RHS coverage, or end of study period (30 September 2024), whichever occurred first.

Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to ascertain the robustness of primary results. This analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between the use of certain antihypertensive drug classes and the risk of occurrence of certain tumour Macroclasses.

Statistical significance was taken at the < 0.05 level. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2; http://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Cohort selection and characteristics

We identified 526 394 users of antihypertensive drugs between 2019 and 2024. Of these, after excluding subjects with age ≤ 40 and ≥ 85 years (n = 141 774), those with any prescription of antihypertensive drugs and/or an exemption for cancer during the two years preceding the “index date” and those with a follow-up less than twelve months, individuals who met the inclusion criteria and constituted our final study cohort were 87 987, as shown in the flowchart in Fig. 1. In the entire cohort, the median age at the “index date” was 60 years (IQR: 51–69), and 48.3% of patients were males.

Flowchart showing the cohort selected

During follow-up, the number of subjects exposed for at least one month to ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, CCBs, Thiazides and Loop diuretics were 23.1%, 19.0%, 25.1%, 13.7%, 14.3%, 19.9%, respectively (Table 1).

Between the different classes of drugs, the median age varied between 59 and 61 years and the percentage of males varied between 42.0% (patients treated with BBs) and 58.4% (patients treated with CCBs), as shown in Table 1.

Exposure to antihypertensive drugs and risk of cancer occurrence: intention-to-treat approach

During a median follow-up of 47.8 months (IQR: 21.0—72.0), 3 577 (4.1%) individuals had a diagnosis of tumour. In agreement with the first antihypertensive drug dispensation, the overall number of cancer cases was 3.6% for ACEIs, 3.5% for ARBs, 3.8% for BBs, 3.6% for CCBs, 3.5% for Thiazides and 5.4% for Loop diuretics (Fig. 2).

Forest plot illustrating the relationship between various classes of antihypertensive medications and cancer risk. HRs were estimated using all other classes combined as the reference group. Results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on the intention-to-treat methodology

The intention-to-treat method provided evidence of a modest cancer risk reduction among patients who initiate antihypertensive therapy with Thiazides (HR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.77–0.94; p < 0.001, CCBs (HR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.78–0.96; p = 0.005), BBs (HR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83–0.97; p = 0.009), ARBs (HR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.76–0.91; p < 0.001) and ACEIs (HR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.81–0.96; p = 0.002). A significant increase was observed in patients initially treated with Loop (HR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.36–1.58; p < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 2.

Considering the distribution of tumour Macroclasses, we found that the most frequent tumour Macroclass was malignant neoplasm of genitourinary organs (23.7%) while the rarest was malignant neoplasm of lip, oral cavity and pharynx (1.3%).

In patients treated with Loop we observed a modest increase in cancer risk, particularly the malignant neoplasm of digestive organs and peritoneum (Fig. 3B); respiratory and intrathoracic organs (Fig. 3C); bone, connective tissue, skin and breast (Fig. 3D); other and unspecified sites (Fig. 3F); lymphatic and hematopoietic tissue (Fig. 3G) tumour Macroclasses (HR: 1.52; 1.70; 1.28; 1.74 and 2.25, respectively).

Conversely, in patients treated with ACEIs we found a reduction in cancer risk, particularly the malignant neoplasm of digestive organs and peritoneum (Fig. 3B); respiratory and intrathoracic organs (Fig. 3C); bone, connective tissue, skin and breast (Fig. 3D); other and unspecified sites (Fig. 3F); lymphatic and hematopoietic tissue (Fig. 3G) tumour Macroclasses (HR: 0.82; 0.71; 0.78; 0.73 and 0.65, respectively). No association between cancer risk and antihypertensive treatment was observed for malignant neoplasms of the lip, oral cavity, and pharynx (Fig. 3A). The other relationships are shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study based on data from the Abruzzo Region (Southern Italy), we found a reduced risk of cancer occurrence among users of ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, CCBs, and Thiazides, whereas treatment with Loop diuretics was associated with a significantly higher risk.

Hypertension represents a crucial risk factor for CVDs affecting almost a third of the world’s adult population [1, 2]. To treat hypertension, antihypertensive drugs are commonly used, however, different studies have demonstrated that the use of these medications may represent a potential risk for other pathological conditions, such as orthostatic hypotension, dementia, diabetes and cancer.

The present retrospective cohort study is based on routinely collected administrative data from the Abruzzo Region, in Southern Italy, in patients treated with antihypertensive drugs and followed for a median follow-up of 47.8 months.

Using an intention-to-treat approach, we performed a statistical analysis to estimate the relationship between the use of antihypertensive drugs and cancer risk occurrence. As a result, a decreased risk of cancer occurrence was observed for those patients treated with ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, CCBs and Thiazides. This suggests that the use of these drugs was not associated with an increased risk of cancer occurrence.

On the other hand, patients treated with Loop diuretics were exposed to a significantly increased risk of cancer occurrence. This main finding is in line with the work of Franchi and colleagues [19]; however, in their study, the modest increase in cancer risk with Loop diuretics was documented only using the “as treated” approach. To better characterize Loop diuretics, we investigated which active ingredient was mainly used, showing that the most abundant active ingredient used in our cohort was Furosemide (82.4%).

Similar findings were also observed in the work of Cho et al., where the use of antihypertensive drugs, in particular ARBs, was independently associated with a risk reduction for overall cancer in the Korean population, as in our cohort, even if in their work patients were younger and there was a male prevalence respect to our population [20, 21].

In addition, a sensitivity analysis was carried out to address the relationship between antihypertensive drugs and different tumour Macroclasses, defined in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases and Injuries, 9th edition. This analysis showed a modest cancer risk increase in patients treated with Loop that belonged to malignant neoplasm of digestive organs and peritoneum; respiratory and intrathoracic organs; bone, connective tissue, skin and breast; other and unspecified sites; lymphatic and hematopoietic tissue tumour Macroclasses. In addition, a cancer risk reduction was observed for patients belonging to the same tumour Macroclasses but treated with ACEIs. However, no cancer risk association was observed in malignant neoplasm of lip, oral cavity and pharynx, probably due to the small numerosity of this group.

The main strength of this study was that it was based on a large and unselected population, and despite the application of exclusion criteria, the final cohort remained a representative sample size. This was possible thanks to hospital discharges that represent an important source of data and information and can be useful for various purposes, including public health monitoring, quality assessment, research, and policy-making, but also to the Italian healthcare system that includes practically all citizens. Furthermore, the information provided by drug prescriptions was accurate because pharmacists are required to report detailed drug dispensations to obtain reimbursement [22]. Beyond strengths, this study also has some limitations. Firstly, the use of antihypertensive drugs was derived from drug prescriptions, which is a widely used method to evaluate adherence to treatment in population studies [23]. In addition, there is no guarantee that the prescribed dose corresponds to the one taken, because it was not possible to assess the dose recommended for each class of drug based on hospital discharges. This assumption presupposes that the duration of days covered by a prescription directly correlates with the actual days of drug consumption, a relationship that is not universally applicable [24]. Secondly, a further limitation is the relatively short median follow-up of 47.8 months, which may be insufficient to fully capture the long latency period typically required for the development of most cancers. Nevertheless, comparable follow-up durations have been reported in other large real-world studies using administrative and clinical data sources [25–27]. This supports the relevance of our follow-up window, while acknowledging that longer observation would provide a more comprehensive assessment of long-term cancer risk.

Third, unmeasured confounding may be a limitation of the present study. Patients receiving different type of antihypertensive drugs may differ respect to other characteristics. For instance, co-treatment, comorbidities, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, and cancer staging could be associated with both treatment status and outcomes. However, due to the lack of information on these factors in the administrative database, we could not adjust for them in our analyses. As a consequence, this potential variation in the distribution of unmeasured confounders may partly explain some of the differences observed.

Finally, the last limitation was related to the drastic reduction of the number of hospital discharges due to the COVID-19 pandemic. This means that the number of cancer cases that occurred may be underestimated.

Conclusion

This descriptive population-based study from the general population of the Abruzzo Region evaluated the association between antihypertensive treatment and cancer risk. Our findings provide reassuring evidence regarding the oncological safety of most antihypertensive drug classes (ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, CCBs, and Thiazides). In contrast, the increased risk observed with Loop diuretics deserves further investigation, particularly given the widespread use of these drugs.

Nevertheless, in the absence of a direct comparator, our findings should be interpreted with caution and considered as hypothesis-generating rather than causal estimates of risk.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The authors complied with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committee on human experimentation and with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2013.
