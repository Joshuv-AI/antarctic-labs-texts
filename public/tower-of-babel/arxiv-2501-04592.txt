Motion and hydrodynamic resistance of an elastic bead confined in a square microchannel
Charles Paul Moore, Hiba Belkadi, Brouna Safi, Gabriel Amselem, Charles N. Baroud

Abstract
Cells and other soft particles are often forced to flow in confined geometries in both laboratory and natural environments, where the elastic deformation induces an additional drag and pressure drop across the particle. In contrast with other multiphase flows, the physical parameters that determine this additional pressure are still not known. Here we start by measuring the pressure drop across a single spherical hydrogel particle as it flows in a microfluidic comparator. This pressure is found to depend on the amount of confinement, elastic modulus, fluid viscosity and velocity. A model for the force balance on the particle is then proposed, by incorporating the above ingredients and relying on simulations of bead geometry and lubrication flow considerations. The final model collapses the force measurements forces onto a single scaling law spanning several decades, while providing physical insights by recalling elements from classic multiphase flows and contact mechanics.



The motion and resistance of multiphase flows in confined geometries is a classic problem of fluid mechanics that has been studied extensively for the case of an air bubble or a liquid droplet entering into a capillary tube filled with an immiscible liquid
Taylor 1961
. In this flow the equilibria between viscous stresses and surface tension determine the shape of the deformable interface and the thickness of the wetting layer
Bretherton 1961
. This equilibrium in turn determines the mobility of the drop or bubble and the amount of fluid left behind
Schwartz et al. 1986
;
Wong et al. 1995a
. Beyond the classical studies of this subject, interest has been rekindled with the emergence of droplet microfluidics, where the motion of drops in square or rectangular capillaries raised questions of practical importance about the formation and transport of drops and bubbles in microchannels
Baroud et al. 2010
;
Jakiela et al. 2012
. In all of the above studies, the balance between viscosity and capillary forces means that the physics is determined by a single parameter, the capillary number
{Ca}=\mu U/\gamma
, where
\mu
is a typical value of viscosity,
U
a typical value of velocity, and
\gamma
is the interfacial tension
Fairbrother and Stubbs 1935
.

A different multiphase situation has emerged recently in microfluidic applications but has received much less attention, namely the flow of a soft solid bead in a confined geometry. Such particles can represent the passage of cells in microchannels
Shelby et al. 2003
;
Abkarian et al. 2006
, or they can be encountered in situations in which hydrogel beads are co-encapsulated with cells inside droplets
Abate et al. 2011
. In this second case, it was shown that the droplet production could be synchronized with the bead ejection in order to yield a deterministic encapsulation of a single microgel per droplet
Abate et al. 2009
. In contrast with drops and bubbles however, the soft particles do not introduce any surface tension nor do they lead to recirculating patterns inside or outside. Instead, their transport is expected to be dictated by the viscosity of the carrier fluid, the elastic properties of the beads, and the amount of confinement.

Even though the physics of soft particle transport has been studied
Preira et al. 2013
;
Khan et al. 2017
, we are still lacking a scaling law that explains how the physical and confinement parameters combine together to determine the bead velocity. This is done below in order to determine the additional force that is required to flow the bead with the fluid.

To understand the effect that confinement has on the microgel passage, we simulated the geometry of confined microgels using the Abaqus CAE software. The simulations consisted of placing an elastic spherical microgel bead of diameter
d
and Young’s modulus
E^{\star}
in a microchannel with a square cross section of width
w_{0}
, as shown in Fig.
3
a. The walls of the microchannel were assumed to be infinitely rigid. The Poisson ratio of the microgel was chosen to be
\nu=0.35
, a typical value for hydrogels
Chappel 2020
, but it was not found that the exact value of
\nu
played an important role in the following results. The channel dimensions were then reduced by an amount
\Delta w
, while preserving the square cross section, and the deformed shape of the microgel was calculated for different values of
d/w
, as shown in Fig.
3
b-c. For ease of simulation, problem symmetries were invoked to calculate a 1/4 model of the gel and channel.

Two surface areas are of particular interest and are obtained from these simulations. First, we measure the cross-sectional area
\Lambda_{\rm CS}
occupied by the gel within the microchannel, taken in the middle of the microgel in the direction of the channel (see Fig.
3
b). As confinement increases,
\Lambda_{\rm CS}/w^{2}
is also found to increase, as shown in Fig.
3
d. This cross-sectional area is geometrically bounded by
\Lambda_{\rm CS}=\frac{\pi}{4}d^{2}
in the case of a small unconfined microgel, where
d/w=1
and
\Lambda_{\rm CS}\rightarrow w^{2}
for a highly confined microgel, where
d/w=\sqrt{2}
. Then fitting the data with a quadratic relationship between
\Lambda_{\rm CS}
and
d/w
that satisfies the above boundary conditions leads to an analytical approximation

\Lambda_{\rm CS}=\left[-1.25\left(\frac{d}{w}\right)^{2}+3.54\left(\frac{d}{w}\right)-1.50\right]w^{2}.

(1)

This solution is found to be in good agreement with the simulated data in Fig.
3
d (
R^{2}=0.98
).

The second surface of interest concerns the contact surface between the microgel and the channel walls,
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
, as shown in Fig.!
3
. It is known from classical contact mechanics that when an elastic sphere of diameter
d
is pressed upon a flat surface, the area of contact between the two solids scales as
\Lambda_{\rm Hertz}={\pi}dp/2
, where
p
is the penetration of the sphere into the flat surface
Barber 2018
. In the case simulated here, where the bead is squished between four walls,
p=(d-w)/2
. In the current simulations, the contact area between the bead and the wall is also found to depend on
d(d-w)
, though with an additional dependency on the confinement
d/w
, see Fig.
3
e. Comparing this scaling with the numerical results, we find

\Lambda_{\rm CT}=\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{d^{2}}{w}\left(d-w\right).

(2)

This predicted fit is within the 95% confidence of the numerical best fit (
R^{2}=0.99
) and is therefore used here.

The two areas above now allow us to estimate the different forces acting on the bead as it flows. Before calculating the dynamic forces in the direction of flow, it is useful to write down the normal force acting from the wall on the gel. A scaling relation for this force can be obtained by using Hertzian mechanics, which states that the normal force scales as
F_{\rm n}\sim E^{\star}\Lambda_{\rm CT}^{1/2}(d-w)

Barber 2018
.
In the direction of the flow, we recall that the bead quickly reaches a constant velocity during its passage in the comparator (see Supplementary Materials
Moore et al.
). This indicates that the sum of forces acting on it in the streamwise direction is zero, meaning that there is a balance between the force pushing the bead forward and the frictional force slowing it down. The force that pushes the bead forward is due to pressure drop across the gel and can be calculated as
F_{\rm P}=P_{\rm gel}\Lambda_{\rm CS}
, where
P_{\rm gel}
is obtained from the experimental measurements.

Meanwhile, two different domains on the microgel surface experience friction: a gutter domain in the corners of the channel, where the fluid moves past the particle, and a lubricated surface, where the microgel is closest to contacting the microchannel. Assuming the transit of the microgel only minimally deforms the particle beyond the dry deformation calculated in Fig.
3
b-c, the lubricated area can be approximated by
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
. Friction due to flow in the gutters between the microgel and the corner is negligible, since the microgel and fluid velocities are similar in all our experiments, so the magnitude of
u_{\rm gel}-u_{\rm fluid}
is small. The shear force on the surface of the microgel is therefore small, and so the gutter friction is
|F_{\rm f,\;gutter}|<|0.1\;F_{\rm P}|
for 95 % of the data. A quantitative estimate of fluid friction in the gutter region is expanded upon in the Supplementary Materials
Moore et al.
.

In contrast with the gutter friction, a large friction force emerges in the lubrication layers, where the microgel is most nearly in contact with the microchannel wall and entrains the fluid along with it
Hamrock et al. 2004
. The friction force can be calculated as

F_{\rm f}=4\int_{\Lambda}{}_{\rm CT}{\sigma(x,z)}dxdz,

(3)

where
\sigma
is the shear stress acting on
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
. We can approximate the shear stress as
\sigma\approx\mu u_{gel}/t
, letting
t
be the local thickness of the lubricating fluid, as illustrated in Fig.
4
a. To estimate
t
, we turn to the equations of elastohydrodynamics.

When an elastic bead flows above a rigid surface, the thickness of the lubrication layer
t(x,z)
depends on the pressure in the lubricating film
P(x,z)
and on the Young modulus of the bead
Zargari et al. 2007
;
Hamrock and Dowson 1976
;
Hamrock et al. 2004
:

t(x,z)=t_{\rm 0}+2\frac{\pi}{E^{\star}}\int{\frac{P(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}{\sqrt{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}}dx^{\prime}dz^{\prime},

(4)

where
t_{0}
represents the undeformed shape of the microgel, as sketched in Fig.
4
a. Moreover the pressure
P(x,z)
and the height
t(x,z)
of the lubricating film are also related through the Reynolds equation
Guyon et al. 2015
, which describes the pressure field related to the bead motion:

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(t^{3}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(t^{3}\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\right)=6\mu u_{\rm gel}\frac{\partial t}{\partial x}.

(5)

The resulting Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) constitute a coupled set of differential equations for
P
and
t
that can only be solved numerically
Hamrock et al. 2004
. However their solution can be considerably simplified by considering the scaling behavior and then injecting the scaling relation for
P
and
t
into Eq. (
3
). This yields an estimate of the friction force as a function of the physical and geometric parameters:

F_{\rm f}\sim\left(\mu u_{\rm gel}\Lambda_{\rm CT}\right)^{2/3}E^{*1/3}.

(6)

The full derivation of this scaling law can be found in the Supplementary Materials
Moore et al.
.

Finally, writing a force balance between the pressure and friction forces
F_{\rm P}=F_{\rm f}
allows us to explain the physical origin of the force required to move the gel bead, as a function of its velocity and the physical and geometric parameters.

\frac{F_{\rm f}}{4F_{\rm n}}\sim\left[\frac{\mu u_{\rm gel}}{E^{*}(d-w)}\right]^{2/3}\cdot\left[\frac{d^{2}}{wd-w^{2}}\right]^{1/6}=\mathcal{N}^{2/3}\mathcal{A}^{1/6}.

(7)

Here
\mathcal{N}
represents the ratio of shear stress to elastic stress acting on the bead and
\mathcal{A}
is a geometric term that characterizes the confinement of the microgel. The factor 1/4 on the left-hand side corresponds to the 4 sides of microchannel.

Rescaling the data according to Eq. (
7
) yields a very good collapse, as shown in Fig.
4
b. The slope of the collapsed data points agrees well with the theoretical scaling over more than 2 decades. This collapse is especially remarkable given the large range of Reynolds numbers and pressure values, which may lead to channel deformations at the high values of
\mathcal{N}
.

The force ratio proposed in Eq. (
7
) recalls friction coefficients previously described in empirical soft elasto-hydrodynamic studies
Hamrock and Dowson 1976
;
Sadowski and Stupkiewicz 2019
;
Zhao et al. 2019
;
Moghani et al. 2009
;
de Vicente et al. 2005
;
Zakhari and Bonnecaze 2021
;
Peng et al. 2021
, where it is expressed as
\left(\mu u/E^{\star}l\right)^{C}
, with
l
some length scale and
C
a fit coefficient in the range of
C=0.55-0.71
. This places the value
2/3
determined here squarely within this range. The scaling proposed here is also reminiscent of the resistance due to the presence of drops or bubbles moving in square channels
Baroud et al. 2010
, where the pressure drop varies with the capillary number as
\Delta P\sim{\rm Ca}^{2/3}

Wong et al. 1995a
;
Wong et al. 1995b
;
Baudoin et al. 2013
. Although the details are different between the two situations, they both involve balancing a driving pressure that acts on the scale of the channel (
F_{p}
here) with a drag force that is dominated by velocity gradients at within a lubrication layer (
F_{f}
here).

The new physical understanding obtained from the current experiments and theory can be applied in a variety of microfluidic applications. For instance the transport of cells in confined microfluidic channels has been used to measure their mechanical properties, though most of the modeling has been based on complex numerical work
Shelby et al. 2003
;
Preira et al. 2013
;
Luo et al. 2014
;
Xue et al. 2015
. The scaling shown here can be used to relate the physical properties without the need for complex numerics. From a technological point of view, the controlled encapsulation of individual soft particles in microfluidic droplets has emerged as a key enabling aspect in single-cell genetic analysis
Zilionis et al. 2016
. While the presence of the microgels downstream of the droplet production region has been evoked as the source of the deterministic encapsulation
Abate et al. 2009
, the results shown here provide a quantitative physical insight into the limits and opportunities of such downstream interactions.

To measure the resistance of a microgel particle passing through a constriction, particles are sent through a microfluidic comparator

,

The two inlets join, then separate again into two distinct channels, called the reference channel (at the top, with the dyed fluid) and the test channel (at the bottom, with the gel bead), see Fig.
S2

at the channel entrance, see Fig.

The added resistance to flow imposed by the moving microgel is determined from the resistance diagram shown in Fig.

(15)

where the local velocity is approximated by
u(x,y)\approx\left(1-\left(\frac{2y}{h}\right)^{2}\right)\left(1-\left(\frac{2x}{w}\right)^{m}\right)
, and
m=\sqrt{2}(h/w)+0.89(w/h)
.
Empirical testing reveals a proportionality of
x/w=x_{\rm cr}/w_{\rm cr}
, and as such
\Delta Q
is estimated using
x_{\rm cr}
for precision, which is consistent with previous work using microfluidic comparators
Vanapalli et al. 2009
.

Figure S2:

(a) A diagram showing the microfluidic comparator in operation. The deflections of the ink border are highlighted at
x
and
x_{\rm cr}
.
(b) A resistance diagram representing the relationship between pressure and flow in the microfluidic comparator when a microgel is present.

In addition to measuring the added resistance from a microgel, the microfluidic comparator is also used to determine the viscosity of the fluids used here
Gupta et al. 2016
;
Solomon and Vanapalli 2014
. Noting that in a rectangular microchannel,
R_{0}\propto\mu
, we adjust the inlet flow rates such that the dyed ink line lands at exactly
x_{\rm cr}=0
.
The inlet flow rates are then related to the fluid viscosities through

\frac{Q_{2}}{Q_{1}}=\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}f\left(\frac{2w_{cr}}{h},\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\right),

(16)

where the subscripts
1
and
2
represent the two fluids.
f
is a function of the viscosity ratios and channel aspect ratio at the comparator described in Solomon and Vanapalli
Solomon and Vanapalli 2014
, where

f=\frac{\frac{w_{\rm cr}}{48h}-\sum_{n}^{\infty}{\frac{\left(1-(-1)^{n}\right)\left(\tanh{\frac{n\pi w_{\rm cr}}{h}}+\xi\left(1-1/\cosh{\frac{n\pi w_{\rm cr}}{h}}\right)\right)}{n^{5}\pi^{5}}}}{\frac{w_{\rm cr}}{48h}+\sum_{n}^{\infty}{\frac{\left(1-(-1)^{n}\right)\left(-\tanh{\frac{n\pi w_{\rm cr}}{h}}+\xi\left(1-1/\cosh{\frac{n\pi w_{\rm cr}}{h}}\right)\right)}{n^{5}\pi^{5}}}}

(17)

and where
\xi=\frac{\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}-1\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{\cosh{n\pi w_{\rm cr}/h}}\right)}{\left(-1-\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\tanh{n\pi w_{\rm cr}/h}\right)}
.
Using pure water at room temperature as a reference fluid (
\mu_{\rm H2O}=1.00*10^{-3}\;\rm Pa.s
), the viscosity of the water-glycerol solution used here is measured to be
\mu_{\rm glyc}=2.24*10^{-2}\;\rm Pa.s
This methodology is also used to verify that the addition of dye to the fluid has negligible effect on the viscosity of the carrier fluids.

The analysis of lubrication between the microgel and microchannel walls assumes a steady state, where the pressure and friction forces are in equilibrium,
i.e.

F_{\rm P}=F_{\rm f}
.
This can be verified by noting that microgels passing through the constriction of the comparator move at a constant velocity, as seen in the sample microgel trajectories in Fig.
S3
.
While microgels approaching the entry of the comparatory move with relatively unsteady velocities, once they enter into the comparator at
z_{\rm gel}=0
, the microgels have accelerated to within a 95 % confidence interval of their mean value.
This is the case in all samples studied, regardless of fluid, microgel elasticity, flow rate or diameter. It should also be noted that while the total length of the comparator is

, the high speed videos taken of microgel passage comprised only approximately the 1st quarter of this length. Due to the uniformity of the microchannel, there is no reason to believe the microgel velocity changes when it leaves the field of view of the microscope camera.

I.4
Gutter Friction

Figure S4:

(a) A diagram showing the gutter around the microgel, highlighted in blue, around the microgel body (shown as a quarter section along the axes of symmetry). The cross section of gutter, where fluid passes the gel, is highlighted as
\Lambda_{\rm CSO}
, as is the radius of the gel-wall contact,
a
.
(b) The estimated pressure force,
F_{\rm P}
, as well as the estimated gutter friction,
F_{\rm gutter}
. Force acting on the microgel in the direction of flow are marked by open circles, while gutter friction against the direction of flow is represented by x. The lines
y=x
and
y=\frac{1}{10}x
are provided for reference.

Friction acting on a flowing microgel can be separated into two sections, the lubricating region, where the microgel is nearly in contact with the microchannel walls, and the gutters, accounting for all the rest of the surface area around the microgel. The main analysis in the text focuses on the friction occurring in the lubricating region. Here we also account for friction occurring in the gutters between the microgel and microchannel.

To analyze flow through the gutters, we must first define clearly what is meant by this geometry. The area of the gutters,
\Lambda_{\rm CSO}
is shown in Fig.
S4
a.
At its smallest point, this area is the area left between the square microchannel of section
w^{2}
and the gel of cross-section
\Lambda_{\rm CS}
. The cross-sectional area of a gutter is then
\Lambda_{\rm CSO,0}=\frac{1}{4}\left(w^{2}-\Lambda_{\rm CS}\right)
.

We now make several approximations to simplify the analysis. In the absence of flow, the area of contact between the compressed gel and the channel wall is
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
, and has an ellipsoidal shape of major axis
a
and minor axis
b
. Simulation results of
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
show that
|\frac{a-b}{a}|<0.25
, and therefore we approximate
\Lambda_{\rm CT}\approx\pi a^{2}
. We assume that the length of the gutter is
a
. We do not consider changes in the cross-sectional area of the gutter, and scale our analysis with the minimal value of the cross-sectional area, taking
\Lambda_{\rm CSO}=\Lambda_{\rm CSO,0}
. Last, we approximate the profile of the microgel in this gutter area as a quarter circle, and so have
\Lambda_{\rm CSO,0}=\left(w-b\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)
.

Next, we consider the flow passing by the microgel. In the frame of reference of the gel, the channel walls are moving backwards at
u_{\rm wall}^{\prime}=u_{\rm gel}
. From this standpoint, by conservation of volume, the flow rate past the microgel is therefore
Q_{\rm gutter}=(u_{\rm fluid}-u_{\rm gel})w^{2}
.
The mean velocity in the gutters is
u_{\rm gutter}^{\prime}=Q_{\rm gutter}/\Lambda_{\rm CSO}
.

The shear stress acting on the surface of the microgel is defined as

\sigma_{\rm gel}=\mu\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right|_{\Lambda_{\rm gel}},

(18)

where
\sigma_{\rm gel}
and
u
are perpendicular to the surface of the microgel, and
z
is perpendicular, allowing
\Lambda_{\rm gel}
to be the surface of the microgel in the gutter.

The flow in the gutter is due to the pressure difference between the fore and aft of the gel, and to the gel bead entraining fluid with it, leading to a Couette-Poiseuille flow. The full solution of the Couette-Poiseuille flow profile in this geometry is complicated, and we use a scaling law analysis to estimate the shear stress on the bead:

\sigma_{\rm gel}\sim\frac{\mu u_{\rm gutter}}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{\rm CSO}}}.

(19)

To estimate the shear force on the microgel, we take the approximation mentioned above that
\Lambda_{\rm CSO}=\Lambda_{\rm CSO,0}
, for the entire length of the gutter. Taking the curve of the microgel surface to be circular, the total surface area of the gutter (for all 4 gutters combined) is
\Lambda_{\rm surf}\approx 2\pi a(w-2a)
, noting from simulation results that
\Lambda_{\rm CT}\approx\pi a^{2}
for microgels tested. With that in mind, the friction force in the gutters is approximated by

F_{\rm gutter}\sim\pi a(w-2a)\frac{\mu u_{\rm gutter}}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{\rm CSO}}}.

(20)

The gutter friction
F_{\rm gutter}
can be seen compared with the pressure force acting on the microgel,
F_{\rm P}=P_{\rm gel}\Lambda_{\rm CT}
, in Fig.
S4
b. With only a handful of outliers, the magnitude of the gutter friction is less than a tenth that of the pressure force. The gutter friction is also bidirectional, pushing the microgel faster or slowing it down depending on the specific combination of parameters, however most of the microgels are experiencing a weak resistance to flow due to gutter friction. This is simply an effect of whether the mean relative gutter flow is positive or negative. It should also be noted that the estimate here likely considerably overestimates the magnitude of the friction in the gutter due to geometric considerations. By assuming that the microgel side of the gutter is shaped like a quarter cylinder, the shear is kept at its maximum value for the length of the gutter. While this analysis does neglect the end caps of the microgel on either side of the gutter, this friction can be expected to be considerably less than that within the gutter, as
|u_{\rm gutter}|
decreases and therefore so does the shear.

I.5
Scaling Analysis of Friction

As described in the main text, as the microgel passes through the microchannel, a thin film of fluid develops between the two surfaces where the microgel would otherwise be in contact with the microchannel. This lubricating film, acting over the area
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
, is due to the lubrication pressure
P(x,z)
pushing the microgel away from the wall surface a distance of
t(x,z)
. The coupling of the lubrication pressure with the gel elasticity comes to describe the phenomena of elastohydrodynamics
Zargari et al. 2007
;
Hamrock and Dowson 1976
;
Hamrock et al. 2004
. In the case of soft-elastohydrodynamics, the thickness of the lubrication layer
t(x,z)
is described by the Hertz equation, such that:

t(x,z)=t_{\rm 0}+2\frac{\pi}{E^{\star}}\int_{\Lambda_{\rm CT}}{\frac{P(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}{\sqrt{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}}dx^{\prime}dz^{\prime}.

(21)

In this case,
t_{0}
represents the undeformed shape of the microgel. Because the microgel is considerably softer than the PDMS walls, the elasticity
E^{\star}
is that of the microgel. Meanwhile, the pressure in the lubricating film,
P(x,z)
is described by the Reynolds equation
Guyon et al. 2015
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(t^{3}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(t^{3}\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\right)=6\mu u_{\rm gel}\frac{\partial t}{\partial x}.

(22)

The coupled Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
) must be solved numerically, and the precise solution has been shown to depend upon thermal, fluid and solid properties of the elastohydrodyanmic system
Hamrock and Dowson 1976
;
Marx et al. 2016
;
Nijenbanning et al. 1994
;
Hooke 1988
;
Sadowski and Stupkiewicz 2019
;
de Vicente et al. 2005
;
Cartas Ayala 2013
;
Khan et al. 2017
. This analysis can be simplified by considering the scaling of the pressure and thickness in the lubricating film. This simplified scaling analysis is described below.

In order to understand the typical scaling of the lubricating thickness, we define the typical pressure within the lubrication domain,
\bar{P}
, and consider how this affects the typical thickness of the lubricating layer,
\bar{t}
. When considering the Hertzian deformation of the microgel, we must first note that the area of the lubricating domain is approximately that of dry contact between the microgel and microchannel, such that
\Lambda_{\rm CT}\sim a^{2}
, where
a
is the radius of the lubricating film. With this in mind, the scaling of Eq. (
21
) is:

\bar{t}\sim\frac{\bar{P}a^{2}}{E^{\star}a},

(23)

which provides the scaling for the typical pressure in the lubricating film:

\bar{P}\sim E^{\star}\frac{\bar{t}}{a}.

(24)

Likewise, Eq. (
22
) writes as a scaling law:

\frac{\bar{t}^{3}\bar{P}}{a^{2}}\sim\mu u_{\rm gel}\frac{\bar{t}}{a},

(25)

which, combined with Eq. (
24
) leads to the following scaling for the lubricating thickness:

\bar{t}\sim\left(\frac{\mu u_{\rm gel}}{E^{\star}}\right)^{1/3}a^{2/3}.

(26)

The typical order of magnitude for
\bar{t}
is therefore
\bar{t}\sim 1\;\mu\rm m
, using the representative values
E^{\star}=10\;\rm kPa
,
\mu=1\;\rm mPa.s
,
u_{\rm gel}=0.1\;\rm m/s
, and
a=25\;\mu\rm m
.

The thin lubricating layer causes a shear stress,
\sigma
, to act on the surface of the microgel. This shear stress results in a friction acting over the lubricating surface,
\Lambda_{\rm CT}
, such that

F_{\rm f}=\int_{\Lambda_{\rm CT}}\sigma(x,z)dxdz.

(27)

The shear stress scales as

\sigma\sim\frac{\mu u_{\rm gel}}{\bar{t}},

(28)

and therefore the friction on the microgel surface scales as

F_{f}\sim\frac{\mu u_{\rm gel}\Lambda_{\rm CT}}{\bar{t}}.

(29)

Finally, the scaling for the lubricating thickness described in Eq. (
26
) can be expanded, noting that
\Lambda_{\rm CT}=\pi a^{2}
, such that the lubricating friction is expected to scale as

F_{f}\sim(\mu u_{\rm gel}\Lambda_{\rm CT})^{2/3}{E^{\star}}^{1/3}.

(30)
