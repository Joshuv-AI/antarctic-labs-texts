A Commentary on “Feasibility of Minimally Invasive Scoliosis Surgery in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis Exceeding 80° of Curve Magnitude”
Authors: Ibrahim Obeid
Published: 2026

The authors should be congratulated for addressing an important and timely question in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS): can posterior minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS) be safely extended to selected very severe curves exceeding 80°? Their series is technically impressive and demonstrates that, in flexible curves, curve magnitude alone may not necessarily preclude a posterior MISS strategy [1]. This work also adds to the growing literature suggesting that posterior MISS may achieve radiographic results comparable to conventional open scoliosis surgery (COSS) in selected AIS patients [2-4].

However, the key message of this study should be interpreted with caution. Demonstrating feasibility is not equivalent to demonstrating superiority, nor even equivalence, when compared with contemporary conventional posterior spinal fusion. Open AIS surgery is now highly standardized, efficient, reproducible, and reliable in experienced hands. Therefore, any expansion of MISS indications into very severe AIS must be justified by objective, patient-relevant benefits beyond scar length and radiographic correction.

One important missing endpoint is radiation exposure. Posterior MISS may require fluoroscopic localization, level confirmation, and potentially additional image guidance because of the restricted exposure. In adolescents, any increase in intraoperative radiation must be reported precisely, including fluoroscopy time, dose-area product, effective dose when available, and occupational exposure to the surgical team. This issue is not theoretical: radiation exposure varies substantially according to the pedicle screw insertion technique and image-guidance strategy [5,6]. A smaller incision should not be accepted at the cost of undocumented radiation burden, particularly when conventional open freehand pedicle screw insertion can be performed with limited or no intraoperative imaging in experienced deformity centers [5].

The second major question concerns self-image. The most intuitive advantage of MISS in AIS is cosmetic: shorter scars, less visible soft-tissue trauma, and potentially better body image. Yet the present study does not include patient-reported outcome measures. In AIS, self-image is not determined by scar length alone. Rib hump, shoulder balance, waist asymmetry, trunk shift, residual deformity, and the patient’s perception of the back contour may be at least as important as incision length. Future comparative studies should include validated instruments such as the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS)-22 or SRS-30 self-image and mental health domains [7], satisfaction scores, standardized clinical photographs, and ideally surface topography or objective rib-hump measurements. Without these data, it remains uncertain whether posterior MISS truly improves the patient’s lived experience compared with a well-performed conventional posterior approach.

The role of thoracoplasty also deserves particular scrutiny. In the current study, thoracoplasty was performed in most patients, especially in the very severe group, and this group had a significantly higher number of resected ribs [1]. Pleural effusion was frequent, and the very severe group required significantly more invasive management for pleural effusion [1]. This raises a conceptual issue: if the cosmetic success of MISS depends on an associated rib resection procedure, the global invasiveness of the operation must be judged as a combined spinal and thoracic wall intervention. Thoracoplasty may improve rib prominence in selected cases [8], but it may also be associated with pain, pleural complications, pneumothorax, pulmonary consequences, and delayed recovery [8,9]. Therefore, its benefit should be quantified. Does thoracoplasty significantly improve rib hump, selfimage, satisfaction, or quality of life compared with correction alone? Is it systematically necessary, or should it be reserved for selected residual thoracic prominence after spinal correction?

Another important issue is the ability to perform posterior releases and to obtain a reliable fusion bed. In severe AIS, even when curves appear flexible, open posterior surgery allows extensive facetectomies, posterior releases, Ponte osteotomies when needed, wide decortication, and abundant local autograft. These steps may improve flexibility, correction, derotation, and fusion biology. By contrast, MISS relies on limited corridors, more restricted facet preparation, and frequently allograft or bone substitute. The biological quality of the fusion bed may therefore be different. This is particularly relevant in very severe curves, where mechanical stresses are higher and fusion constructs are longer. Fusion rates after MISS using bone graft substitutes have been specifically investigated [10], but longer-term comparative evaluation against COSS remains essential, particularly in curves exceeding 80°.

Operative time is another practical limitation. In the present study, the very severe group required substantially longer procedures [1]. In a young patient, longer anesthesia, technical complexity, implant handling through limited corridors, and additional thoracoplasty must be weighed against any expected benefit. Blood loss and transfusion rates should also be benchmarked against matched open posterior fusion cohorts [3,4]. A technique should not be considered less invasive solely because the incision is shorter; operative duration, blood loss, transfusion, radiation, pain, pulmonary complications, mobilization, length of stay, opioid consumption, return to school or sport, cost, and reoperation are all part of true invasiveness.

The authors are therefore correct to suggest that posterior MISS may be feasible in selected flexible AIS curves exceeding 80° [1]. The emphasis should remain on “selected” and “flexible.” These findings should not be generalized to rigid curves, curves requiring extensive posterior release or osteotomy, severe sagittal plane reconstruction, or cases in which safe correction and fusion would be compromised by limited exposure.

This study is an important step in expanding the technical boundaries of posterior MISS. The next step should be prospective, preferably multicenter, comparative research against conventional open posterior spinal fusion in matched very severe AIS patients. Such studies should include radiographic correction, radiation exposure, operative time, blood loss, transfusion, thoracoplasty-adjusted complications, pulmonary outcomes, fusion quality, implant-related complications, cost-effectiveness, and most importantly patient-reported outcomes. Until then, posterior MISS for curves exceeding 80° should be regarded as a feasible option in expert hands rather than a proven superior alternative to conventional open surgery.
