Quantitative measurement of agonistic behaviors of intra- and interspecifics of Gryllus bimaculatus and Acheta domesticus by using DeepLabCut
Authors: Gilbert Audira, Petrus Siregar, Kevin Adi Kurnia, Tzong-Rong Ger, Chung-Der Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Animal social behavior, such as agonistic behavior, has been extensively studied for a long time since it is one of the key factors that influence the survivability and reproductive success in many animals, including crickets. Gryllus bimaculatus, a field cricket, and Acheta domesticus, a house cricket, are two cricket species whose social behaviors have been extensively studied. However, the interspecific behaviors of these crickets had not been assessed and comprehensively compared to their behaviors when encountering their conspecifics. Moreover, most of the behavior studies in crickets still rely on traditional observational methods, which are labor-intensive and prone to observer bias. Fortunately, due to the advancement in machine learning, a markerless pose estimation method has revolutionized behavioral analysis in animal studies by enabling precise, automated tracking of body parts without the need for physical markers, reducing stress and allowing for naturalistic behavior in animals. Therefore, the study aimed to demonstrate a comprehensive methodology in analyzing the intra- and interspecies social behaviors of G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus by DeepLabCut (DLC), a deep learning-based pose estimation tool. Based on the results, the trained model that was used in the current study showed a relatively high accuracy after undergoing several rounds of training, as indicated by the relatively high value of average likelihood (0.94) and low value of average low likelihood percentage (3.85%) of each detected body part in every tested cricket. Meanwhile, GLMM analysis revealed no significant differences between the proposed method and manual scoring in both calculated aggressive-related behavior endpoints. Moreover, the system achieved high sensitivity with an average of 0.8, with acceptable precision with an average of 0.7 for both behaviors, demonstrating that the automated approach provides accurate and reliable quantification of aggressive interactions in paired crickets. Next, in terms of the cricket’s social behaviors, males of G. bimaculatus displayed aggressive behaviors as indicated by statistically high overt physical combat count, which were followed by high locomotion and movement complexity, while a slightly higher posterior-oriented interaction count was observed in the male × female group of this species. Meanwhile, although overt physical combat behaviors still could be observed in males of A. domesticus, this cricket in other gender combinations did not display noticeable aggressive behaviors compared to G. bimaculatus. On the other hand, aggressive behaviors were still shown by both males and females during interspecific tests, though in a relatively lesser magnitude compared to intraspecific G. bimaculatus. Taken together, the present study highlighted the effectiveness of DLC for markerless pose estimation of crickets, emphasizing the potential of this methodology as an alternative to provide novel quantitative insights into the dynamics of their social interactions, and thus, paving the way for large-scale, reproducible research on animal social interactions that could advance our understanding of animal behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55331-0.

Introduction

In the last quarter-century, fighting behavior has been extensively studied, considering that aggression is one of the most common types of behavior in animals and also one of the key factors that influence the reproductive success in many animal species, including crickets1,2. Besides possessing a simple and segmentally organized nervous system, crickets exhibit a rich and robust behavior repertoire, including aggression, especially in male field crickets (Orthoptera, Gryllidae, Gryllinae), making them suitable model organisms for the study of agonistic behaviors in animals3–5. Crickets live in a dynamic social environment; thus, various factors that can vary considerably within their lifetime, such as population density, may affect their growth and the adaptive value of some suited behavior patterns6. For instance, a previous study suggested that Gryllus bimaculatus possesses different associative processes of social learning in recognizing living and dead conspecifics7. The influences of social interactions were also described in prior studies that found a slower rate in body weight increment in G. bimaculatus in a high population density, compared to the isolated ones, which possessed darker body color than that of crowded ones8. Meanwhile, in terms of aggressiveness, isolated crickets have been demonstrated to be significantly more aggressive than the crowded ones9. These findings highlight the significance of social interactions in influencing various factors in this animal that ultimately affect their biological survival and evolution.

In nature, male field crickets are extremely aggressive towards each other and vigorously defend their burrows from which they call to attract females or over the acquisition of living spaces from intruding males3,10,11. In a battle when neither male retreats, generally the fight will escalate to a series of behaviors, starting with antennal fencing, that might also be used in sex and species recognition, aggressive stridulation, and spreading of the mandibles3. This series of behavior may eventually lead to grappling or wrestling until one of the males surrenders, establishing a behavioral hierarchy between the pair that is commonly indicated by the singing of an aggressive song by the winning cricket12. On the other hand, the loser would display avoidance behavior and avoid additional fights13,14. However, while the intraspecific agonistic behaviors in crickets have been widely addressed, comparative studies involving interspecific aggression in crickets are infrequently reported. A previous study done by Sandford et al. demonstrated the behavioral differences in two different species of crickets. Although no differences were found in the fighting and agonistic interactions between male Gryllus integer and Gryllus alogus, a significant reduction in the level of aggression between heterospecifics was observed in their study15. A study in interspecific aggression in crickets is also intriguing, considering there is a rich literature in eusocial insects, such as defensive responses in honeybees toward threats, such as large predators, and interspecific competition in ants16,17. Nevertheless, this knowledge gap is intriguing to be addressed considering the prediction by ethologists that stated the aggressive or agonistic behavior of any animal species involving conspecifics would be more contested than interactions involving heterospecifics, since interspecific aggression may involve different patterns of behavior response than intraspecific aggression15,18.

Gryllus bimaculatus, a field cricket, is one of the cricket species from Gryllidae that has been studied and described extensively in the ethological literature, especially for their aggressive behaviors towards other conspecific males19. The general form of their agonistic behavior is known to vary depending on their social environment13. For instance, during a decline in population density, G. bimaculatus would prefer to call for females from a stationary burrow rather than actively searching for mates6. In an ideal natural environment, G. bimaculatus males occupy rock crevices or other natural shelters and attract females by emitting a species-specific calling song that could also attract males, leading to conflicts over burrow ownership11,20. Besides, G. bimaculatus, Acheta domesticus, a house cricket, is also one of the cricket species that were studied for examining the influence of their social behaviors, including their agonistic displays, particularly male-male aggression21–23. Similarly, male A. domesticus also compete in aggressive interactions to obtain access to a mate attraction territory, producing a series of long-distance chirps to attract females from a distance3. However, while numerous studies had described the agonistic behaviors of these crickets, they still relied on traditional methods in evaluating their behavioral performance, including manual observation and scoring from the recorded videos4,13,19, or compiled ethograms12,24. Generally, these methods are labor-intensive and prone to observer bias. Therefore, a novel method with implemented automation that could help researchers to conduct a high-throughput, unbiased, and reproducible behavioral analysis in crickets is required as a powerful alternative method to evaluate agonistic behaviors of crickets.

Recent advancements in computer vision and deep learning have led to the development of several programs that are able to accurately, rapidly, and robustly measure animal behavior, overcoming computational data limitations by offering numerous algorithms for processing image data. DeepLabCut (DLC), one of the deep learning tools, has been used by numerous studies to identify complex behavioral changes over time in various animals, including crickets25–28. Thus, the current study aimed to utilize DLC in quantitatively analyzing the agonistic behaviors of intra- and interspecific interactions of two species of crickets, which were G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus. We hypothesized that DLC would be able to automatically label and track several important parts of the crickets’ bodies accurately and assist in the calculation of the behavior endpoints. Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Adult G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus that aged 3–4 weeks after the final molt were obtained from a local supplier in batches of ~ 100 individuals and housed in the laboratory in a 136-litre polypropylene tank (78 cm × 55 cm × 49 cm) in similar mixed-sex and mixed-instar densities. Ad libitum water and food (wheat seeds) that were added every three to four days were provided inside the rearing tank, including ample shelter in the form of staggered egg cartons. While some studies indicated that crickets, especially males, that were kept in isolation would be more aggressive upon encountering conspecifics29,30, a previous report found that males from a commercial facility with abundant resources, such as ad-libitum access to food and shelter, were more aggressive than wild males23. This phenomenon might be due to the increase in population density in captivity, which causes an increase in intraspecific aggression, since, for instance, males sometimes perceive an increase in competition, and will increase their intraspecific aggression to better monopolize females31. The cultures were kept at a maintained room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) on a 14:10 h light: dark cycle. For the subsequent behavior test, only healthy intact crickets possessing all appendages and free from other obvious damages were used, with a total number of 160 crickets for each species that consisting of 80 males and 80 females with an average body length (mouth to abdominal tip) of 2.068 cm with 0.2247 of standard deviation (SD) and 0.01256 of standard error of mean. To confirm that the tested cricket body lengths were sufficiently uniform to avoid confounding effects and considering the potency of body length in increasing the probability of winning aggressive conflicts, as shown in several cricket species, the coefficient of variation (CV) of each group was calculated32. Subsequently, the average CV from every group was calculated as 9.559%, which, in general biological experiments, including animal science, the current CV value (≤ 10%), would be considered a very low variation, indicating tight consistency33.

Behavior test

Here, we recorded the interactions between 20 pairs of males, 20 pairs of females, and 20 pairs of males and females of each cricket species to assess their intraspecific interactions. Meanwhile, for the interspecies interaction evaluation, the behaviors of 20 pairs of male G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus and 20 pairs of female G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus were recorded. The interspecies interaction in different genders of these cricket species was not evaluated, considering the lower intensity of aggressive behaviors that were shown in the interspecific group of the same gender relative to G. bimaculatus groups. This decision was also taken to reduce animal usage and the number of tests that an individual was required to undergo. Later, these videos were divided into two groups, which were training dataset that consisted of the videos of three pairs of cricket from each group and test dataset that consisted of the rest of the recorded videos. Prior to the behavior recording, pairs of crickets according to the combination that was tested were randomly chosen and placed in an acrylic plate (12.7 cm × 8.5 cm × 2.2 cm) that was divided into two compartments, with each pair occupying one of the compartments with a lid. A plastic mesh with a hole diameter of 0.1 cm was installed in each compartment to prevent the crickets from slipping during movements. The plate was put inside ZebraBox (ViewPoint Behavior Technology, Civrieux, France), an observation chamber that was originally designed for fish larval behavior analysis with minimal external disturbances, and the recording process was conducted by Zebralab software version 3.22.3.85 (ViewPoint Behavior Technology, Civrieux, France). Prior to the behavior recording, each pair was allowed to acclimate to the novel environment for 5 min. Afterward, their behaviors were recorded for ~ 10 min with a light source provided below the test plate to enable the crickets to obtain visual information, such as body size, mass, and mandible size, of their potential opponent to help them in making upcoming decisions34. The recorded videos were at 1024 × 768 pixels of resolution with 30 frames per second (fps). After undergoing the behavior test, the tested crickets were put back in the holding tank and allowed to rest for at least 1 week before being used in the subsequent behavior test, if there was any. The recorded videos were stored on a computer for further analysis.

DeepLabCut training and video analysis

In the present study, DeepLabCut version 3.0.0rc8 (https://deeplabcut.github.io/DeepLabCut/README.html (accessed on 15th July 2025)) was used to track the position of each assigned body part of the cricket25. Here, the head (vertex), thorax (pronotum), abdomen (abdominal tip), and front, middle, and jumping (the joint between the tibia and tarsus) legs of each cricket were designated to be tracked by DLC, as shown in Fig. 1. For the model training, three pairs of each group from the recorded videos were used to generate the training datasets. In generating training datasets for the first training, 240 frames were randomly sampled from each video using k-means clustering in DLC, and nine points on the body were manually labelled. Pytorch engine with ResNet-101 network architecture was used during the first network training with a maximum epoch of 1000. Since the model performance of this training was suboptimal, the model was refined several times by manually annotating the body parts of the generated frames from these training dataset videos after being analyzed by DLC. Finally, after four training sessions, the trained model was utilized to analyze all of the videos from the test dataset as the ‘unseen’ videos for the generated model.

Behavior endpoints calculation

In order to quantitatively evaluate the behavior of the tested crickets, several behavior endpoints that represent some general behavior aspects of animals were calculated based on the position of the tracked body parts by DLC. First, the average speed that reflects their overall locomotor activity was calculated by measuring the total travelled distance of the thorax point and dividing it by the duration of the test. Next, fractal dimension and entropy that can provide insights regarding their movement complexity and predictability, which also covers other behavior aspects, such as exploratory behaviors, were also calculated based on the thorax point coordinates over time, according to the previous publication35. In terms of their agonistic behaviors, three behavioral endpoints, which were overt physical combat, posterior-oriented interaction counts, and wandering time percentage, were calculated. Prior to these behavior endpoints calculations by the current method, manual annotations by trained observers were also conducted, covering the number of overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts in every single video, to help in providing essential information during the parameter setting and to evaluate the performance of the current method later. For the manual observation, although fighting crickets are known to exhibit a stereotyped sequence of escalating motor behaviors, the current analysis focused only on direct physical contests for dominance. These were categorized as overt physical combat, including bilateral aggression (face-to-face fighting or mandible locking) and unilateral aggression (grappling or pushing) (Fig. 2A). In addition, posterior-oriented interaction was defined to include behaviors in which one individual directed actions toward the posterior of another. This category encompasses aggressive behaviors, such as chasing or kicking a retreating opponent in same-sex pairs, as well as courtship-related behaviors in male–female pairs, including male pursuit or female approach toward the male’s posterior (Fig. 2B). This classification was adopted due to limitations of the current DLC model, which has difficulty detecting early-stage aggressive behaviors such as antennal fencing3,12,36. Based on these manual observation data, the distance between some specific body parts in every frame was measured to quantify overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts of the tested crickets for the current method. During counting the overt physical combat, the distance between the cricket’s head and the distance between the left front leg of one cricket to the right front leg of another cricket were calculated while for quantifying posterior-oriented interaction, the distance between left and right front leg of one contestant to left and right jumping legs of another contestant and the distance between head on one contestant to abdomen of another contestant were measured. In addition, average speed was also included as one of the parameters in counting the occurrence. To determine the threshold values for each parameter, we utilized manually observed counts of overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interactions from the male-male training videos. We specifically selected male data to establish these baselines because males of both species exhibited the highest frequencies of agonistic behavior, and we subsequently applied these summarized thresholds to compute behaviors across all male groups. Later, several overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts from each video in the females and male × female groups from the training datasets were also summarized and compared to the value of each parameter that was applied for the male groups in their respective species combination. Finally, the applied threshold value of each parameter was applied to calculate the overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts from all of the recorded videos in the test datasets (Supplementary Table 1). However, the threshold values for both distances between body parts were slightly different between each group. These differences in the parameter threshold were applied due to the behavioral differences that were observed between the cricket species. For instance, based on the manual observation, A. domesticus were frequently found to be occluded in the same space with another cricket without any intention of displaying typical aggressive behaviors toward their opponents, and a not-optimal value of these thresholds could generate overestimated results and might produce a misleading conclusion. After identifying the occurrence of overt physical combat or posterior-oriented interactions on a frame-by-frame basis, the results were visualized as distance–time plots (Supplementary Fig. 1) to facilitate quantification and comparison with manual observations. When aggressive interactions occurred in rapid succession, the graphical markers representing these events could overlap, making it difficult to distinguish whether the behavior represented a single continuous sequence or multiple discrete events. In such cases, behavioral endpoints were determined based on the pattern of troughs in the calculated distance between the relevant body parts. Each trough—defined as a local minimum in distance followed by a clear increase to baseline—was interpreted as a single aggressive interaction event. Multiple distinct troughs, therefore, indicated multiple discrete interactions separated by periods in which the crickets were not engaged in aggressive contact. This approach allowed objective separation of closely spaced aggressive events and prevented overestimation of continuous interactions as a single prolonged bout. Finally, in calculating wandering time percentage, the total number of frames when there are no fighting or chasing behaviors with speed ≥ 5 mm/s, considering the average speed of all of the tested crickets was ~ 5 mm/s, to not be misled by freezing behavior, was counted and divided by the total time, and considered as the wandering time percentage.

Statistics and reproducibility

First, the appropriate sample size was determined by the statistical power analysis. By using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n={\left(\frac{\mathrm{Z}{\upsigma\:}}{\mathrm{E}}\right)}^{2}$$\end{document} in all of the calculated behavior endpoints in every tested group, the recommended sample size of ~ 40 was required in order to provide data with a 99% confidence interval (CI) and a margin of error of 2.5 units37. Thus, as mentioned above, a total number of 160 crickets for each species, consisting of 80 males and 80 females, was used with 40 biological replications for each group. Next, to evaluate the behavior differences between crickets due to the effects of gender and species differences, statistical analyses were applied to every calculated behavior endpoint. Prior to the statistical analyses, D’Agostino & Pearson and Anderson-Darling tests were conducted on each group’s data to evaluate their data distribution normality. Since most of the data did not follow a normal distribution pattern, we used the Brunner-Munzel test to compare each behavior endpoint between groups. This non-parametric test is suitable for testing the null hypothesis of stochastic equality between two groups when heteroscedasticity is present or when the distributions are not identical in shape, and this test can be applied to variables without zero-inflation. The test statistic, degrees of freedom, P value, and the estimated probability P(X < Y) + 0.5 * P(X = Y) were extracted for each pairwise comparison. To account for the inflation of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the conservative Holm method to control the family-wise error rate. The data are expressed by using boxplots with individual data points overlaid to provide a clearer and more informative representation of the data distribution, considering that the median of some groups in some behavior endpoints was zero, causing data expression with mean ± SEM may not fully capture the distribution characteristics. The statistical significances (P ≤ 0.05) are signified by different letters between the groups. Meanwhile, to compare the statistical differences in the overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts between manual observation and the current method, a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution and a log link function was used. To account for the non-independence of repeated measurements from the same subject, the random intercept for ID was included in the model. The significance of the fixed effect of the methods was assessed using a likelihood ratio test, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons between group levels were conducted with Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference test, to control the family-wise error rate. When model checks indicated a convergence issue, it was converged successfully using the bobyqa optimizer with increased iterations. Model-estimated marginal means and 95% confidence intervals for each group were calculated and back-transformed from the log scale to the response scale. Afterward, these estimates that overlay the marginal means and confidence intervals on a scatterplot of the raw data are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using R version 4.5.2 (2025-10-31, ucrt) in RStudio version 2025.09.2 or GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software version 8.0.2, La Jolla, CA, USA). Finally, to help evaluate the quality of the current methods, precision (True Positive/True Positive + False Positive) and sensitivity (True Positive/True Positive + False Negative) were calculated, and later, the values of each scoring are shown as mean with standard deviation (SD)38.

Results

DeepLabCut evaluation in tracking cricket’s body parts

Here, the training and refinement processes were repeated four times with manual correction in the labelling predicted by the model that was applied to the extracted frames from the training data sets in each process. To quantitatively measure how accurately the used training model in predicting the body parts in the present study, average likelihood values for every body part of each animal in every frame in the recorded videos were calculated. A likelihood value, which is associated with each body part, provides an estimate of how likely it is that the pose estimates are correct. This value ranges from 0 to 1, with coordinates with values over 0.9 being very likely correct39. From the results, the average likelihood was calculated as 0.941, with the lowest average likelihood found in detecting the left and right front legs (0.884), while the highest average likelihood was observed in detecting the head and thorax with a value of 0.995 (Table 1). In addition, we also calculate the number of frames with a low likelihood value (< 0.5), including the frames when the model could not detect the assigned body part. Based on the results, a value of 3.850% was calculated as the average low likelihood percentage of every body part in each group. Consistent with the results from Table 1, the highest low likelihood percentage was found when detecting the right front leg (8.138%), while the lowest average percentage was shown when detecting the thorax of the cricket (0.140%) (Table 2). Taken together, the current model could already provide a relatively accurate and reliable body part tracking in the tested crickets, although in tracking some specific body parts, such as front legs, a slightly lower likelihood was found compared to the tracking results in other body parts.

Comparative analysis of method accuracy and behavioral detection performance

Next, to evaluate the performance of the current method, two behavior endpoints, which were overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts, that were calculated based on the generated data by DLC, were compared with the results from the manual observation by using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a Poisson error distribution and a log link function. GLMM results indicated no detectable differences between the proposed and the manual observation methods for both behavior endpoints (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables 2&3). The absence of significant effects and the overlapping estimated marginal means suggest that the proposed method provides performance equivalent to the manual observation method under the tested conditions. Afterward, the sensitivity and precision of the proposed method based on the true positives, false positives, and false negatives, in comparison to the ground truth, were calculated (Supplementary Table 4). The precision performance was evaluated to measure the correctly predicted positive classification over the total predicted positive observations for the paired (overt physical combat) or individual (posterior-oriented interaction) behaviors. While overall precision performance during the posterior-oriented interaction count was slightly higher than the overt physical combat count, the proposed method showed the highest precision during counting overt physical combat behaviors in G. bimaculatus groups, with the lowest precision found while analyzing male and female of the A. domesticus group (Table 3). Meanwhile, the sensitivity performance was evaluated to measure the correct positive classification over all the observations per paired or individual behaviors. Higher sensitivity performances were observed in overt physical combat count compared to posterior-oriented interaction count, with the highest sensitivity was shown during the current method’s performance on A. domesticus groups (Table 3). To sum up, the GLMM analysis results indicate that the performance of the new method is statistically comparable to the established method across all evaluated parameters. In addition, the model achieved strong sensitivity for both behaviors with an average value of 0.81 and 0.75, for overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts, respectively, indicating that it correctly identified most aggressive events. Meanwhile, the precision score was also acceptable, with an average value of 0.70 and 0.73 for overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts, respectively, showing that the majority of detections reflected true aggressive actions. Overall, these results indicate that the method provides an accurate and dependable tool for quantifying cricket aggression.

Behavior differences in two species of cricket in different gender combinations

First, to understand the intraspecific behavior differences between male and female crickets, we compared each calculated behavior endpoint between each gender combination group (male × male, female × female, and male × female), as shown in Fig. 3. Based on the results from G. bimaculatus data, slightly higher posterior-oriented interaction count was observed in the male × female group of this species, especially compared to the female × female group although it did not reach a statistical significance while in terms of the overt physical combat count, it is worthy to note that male × male and female × female groups showed a statistically higher value than male × female group (Fig. 3A&B, Supplementary Tables 5&6). In addition, it is also intriguing to note that this male × male group also displayed the highest wandering time percentage, as also shown in its average speed, besides its relatively similar level of movement complexity-behavior endpoints (Fig. 3C-F, Supplementary Fig. 3A-C). Similarly, male × male group of A. domesticus also exhibited the statistically highest overt physical combat count compared to other gender combination groups (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 5). Interestingly, this group also showed the highest posterior-oriented interaction count (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 6). However, regarding their locomotion and movement complexity, no statistical differences were observed between all of the gender combinations of this cricket, as shown in the following behavior endpoints: average speed, wandering time percentage, fractal dimension, and entropy (Fig. 3C-F, Supplementary Fig. 3D-F).

Differences in Interspecific Behaviors between G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus

Next, the differences in the interspecific behaviors between G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus in each gender combination were also comprehensively compared to obtain insights regarding the behavioral performance differences of these species. The results from the male combination signified the more robust aggressive behaviors of G. bimaculatus when they faced their conspecific, considering the higher overt physical combat count of this intraspecific group compared to when they were exposed to the male of another species, despite the statistically similar level of posterior-oriented interaction count between these groups (Fig. 3A&B, Supplementary Tables 5&6). Furthermore, males of G. bimaculatus displayed a higher average speed and wandering time percentage than other male groups, especially males A. domesticus, indicating that they were more active than other male groups, as also supported by their low entropy value, despite no statistical differences observed in the fractal dimension (Fig. 3C-F, Supplementary Fig. 3A, D, G). Similar to their male counterpart, the interspecies aggressive interactions between females of G. bimaculatus were found to be statistically more robust than other female groups, as highlighted by their statistically higher value and lower value of overt physical combat count and entropy, respectively, compared to other female groups (Fig. 3A, F, Supplementary Table 5). Meanwhile, females A. domesticus were found to exhibit the lowest posterior-oriented interaction count among the female groups, with no statistical differences found between these female groups in terms of wandering time percentage, average speed, and fractal dimension (Fig. 3B-D, E, Supplementary Fig. 3B, E, H, Supplementary Table 6). To sum up, G. bimaculatus in all of the gender combinations showed more robust aggressive behaviors compared to A. domesticus during the intraspecific interaction test, which was also relatively lower compared to both interspecific groups.

Discussion

The present study successfully demonstrated the potency of using DeepLabCut to automatically track several essential body parts of two species of cricket, G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus, which greatly aided in the analysis process of their behavioral aspects, including locomotion and aggressive behaviors. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that attempted to utilize DLC in evaluating and comprehensively comparing the agonistic behaviors of these crickets. By the number of training sessions using the training data sets that were applied in the current study, DLC was able to label cricket body parts in the test data sets or ‘unseen’ data with relatively high accuracy labels (average likelihood = 0.941 and average low likelihood percentage = 3.850%). Based on these scorings, it was found that the model performed better in detection when tracking the head and thorax, while slightly lower confidence labels were observed during detecting their front legs. We hypothesized that the relatively greater difficulties for the model in tracking the front legs might be due to the invisibility of those legs when crickets displayed some particular behaviors, such as grooming, considering that grooming in crickets is found to be relatively slow and frequent40. Grooming is a complex behavior that is generally displayed when crickets clean various body parts and maintain their sensory organs, including antennae, cerci, and the subgenual areas. Generally, this behavior is indicated by the usage of their forelegs, which possess a structure designed as an antennal cleaner with the mouthparts41,42. However, during an encounter with novel stimuli, as occurred during the behavior test in the current study, an adequate maintenance of the sensory apparatus becomes more essential since information on the stimuli must be obtained as quickly as possible to aid them in deciding their subsequent moves40. Furthermore, their front legs might also be invisible when they interact with another individual during the test in a form of grappling or mandible engagement since their front legs could be masked by another individual’s body parts during this occurrence, although it might frequently occurred in the males groups considering head-butting and grappling with their mouthparts as a highlight stereotyped series of aggressive behaviors during contests between males while in females, they rarely grapple with their mouthparts even though they can also be aggressive3,13.

Furthermore, the results from the current method were also comprehensively compared with the manual observation method results as the ground truth. First, GLMM analyses revealed no significant differences between methods across both measured aggressive-related behavior endpoints in every gender and species combination, indicating that the current proposed method yields estimates statistically indistinguishable from those produced by traditional manual scoring. This outcome suggests that the proposed method captures the essential aggressive behavioral patterns consistently and without systematic bias. In addition to statistical equivalence, the current method showed strong detection performance. Sensitivity was particularly high for both aggressive-related behavior endpoints, indicating that the algorithm successfully identified the majority of aggressive events that occurred. Although precision values were somewhat lower, they remain within the range commonly accepted for automated behavioral detection in animal studies, especially for rapid or subtle social interactions43,44. In addition, these lower precision values in overt physical combat count were also due to the non-occurrence of this behavior in the majority of the pairs in some groups. As an example, in male × female pairs of the A. domesticus group, 12 out of 17 pairs did not display this behavior, which might be due to their nature of the behavior, as this species rarely fights. Therefore, a larger sample size may be required in the future to provide more accurate precision values of the current method in assessing these aggressive behaviors in this group of A. domesticus. Nevertheless, the combination of high sensitivity and moderate-to-high precision suggests that the method rarely misses aggressive acts and that most detections reflect true events rather than false positives. These performance levels are notable given the complexity of cricket aggression, which often involves rapid, overlapping, and visually subtle actions that can be challenging even for trained observers to score reliably3. The fact that the automated method achieved accuracy comparable to human scoring supports its use as a reliable alternative for behavioral quantification. Moreover, automated detection reduces observer bias, increases throughput, and enables the analysis of large datasets that would be prohibitively time-consuming to score manually.

Next, based on the calculated behavior endpoints, the present study demonstrated the more frequent overt physical combat occurrence between males of G. bimaculatus compared to other groups, especially male × female gender combinations. This result is plausible since males of G. bimaculatus have been well-known to possess intensive aggressive behavior when they encounter another male3,12. These aggressive behaviors might also be related to the slightly higher locomotion as well as movement complexity displayed by this group, since generally, in crickets, besides eliciting grooming behaviors, they may also display an increased locomotion, as it does in many other species during encounters with novel stimuli, which was another cricket in this case40. On the other hand, the slightly less profound posterior-oriented interaction behaviors displayed in the female group found in the current study, especially compared to the male × female group, were also in line with the previous literature that reported females of G. bimaculatus are not likely to engage in intense agonistic behavior; however, their behavior has not been rigorously studied yet45. Meanwhile, male and female G. bimaculatus groups showed the least intensity of overt physical combat behaviors, while their profound posterior-oriented interaction behaviors were more robust than those of other groups. We hypothesized that the observed chasing behavior belongs to courtship behavior rather than aggressive behavior. This courtship behavior does not rely entirely on chemosensory cues, and a previous study had demonstrated that volatile pheromones are not sufficient to induce this behavior46,47. Instead, interaction between G. bimaculatus is mainly affected by behavioral cues from the conspecific, such as threatening posture or a complex mix of visual, olfactory, and tactile cues, even after antennal contact, which is different from other crickets, such as Teleogryllus commodus13,48.

Next, similar results were observed in A. domesticus. In terms of aggressive interaction between males of A. domesticus, besides the statistically lower count of overt physical combat, the tested males displayed relatively comparable posterior-oriented interaction counts to the males of G. bimaculatus. Although the agonistic behaviors of this species have not been addressed as well as G. bimaculatus, a previous study found that paired male A. domesticus may fight several times before clearly determining control of a resource or establishing a consistent dominance relationship that might explain the more robust aggressive behaviors of this group compared to other A. domesticus groups49. On the other hand, females of A. domesticus exhibited a statistically less intense intensity of agonistic behaviors, as also shown in the male × female gender combination. These results indicated that only male A. domesticus displayed robust agonistic behaviors when faced with their conspecifics. Interestingly, contradicting results in terms of how aggressive A. domesticus is related to their sex were found in previous studies. While a prior study showed that females exhibit a robust aggressive behavior50, another finding indicated that there is no difference regarding the aggressiveness level between both genders51. Nevertheless, the aggressive or courtship acts in A. domesticus do not occur until one has established antennal contact with a conspecific, and during an encounter, the male may produce aggressive acts in response to conspecific males and females if the sex discrimination is not established yet47. Although during encounter, A. domesticus may use contact chemoreception for species recognition, this action is insufficient for sex recognition since conspecific male and female contact pheromones in this species share a similar chemical structure52. Thus, the statistically higher intensity of aggressive behaviors in the male × male group observed in the current study might indicate that sex discrimination between the tested crickets had not been established during the behavior test.

Next, besides evaluating the intraspecific interaction of those two species, we also investigate their interspecific interaction and compare it with the intraspecific behaviors data to provide insights regarding the effect of species on their behaviors. From the results of males, when facing each other, these two species of crickets display less profound overt physical combat count than G. bimaculatus groups. Similarly, during an encounter, the females of each species also displayed less robust overt physical combat behaviors compared to the females of the G. bimaculatus group, indicating that interspecific fighting occurred slightly less when G. bimaculatus encountered A. domesticus. While only scarce studies reported interspecific aggression in field crickets and theoretical papers on aggression usually did not mention interspecific fighting, a previous study demonstrated a significant reduction in aggression levels between heterospecifics of G. integer and G. alogus, which was relatively similar to the results from the current study15. As already described in much of the literature, fighting in field crickets mainly occurred in the context of intrasexual competition; thus, when at heterospecific contests, elevated aggression levels would not be expected53. Furthermore, the chemoreceptors on the palpi and mouthparts of the crickets during mandible interlocking might be able to discriminate species-specific stimuli, elucidating why crickets of different species fail to exhibit and engage in sustained combat3. Besides those possibilities, the differences in interspecific behaviors might also result from interspecific discrimination of volatile species-specific pheromones by distance chemoreceptors that are located on several crickets’ body parts, considering the ability of Acheta and Gryllus in discriminating airborne odors of both conspecifics and heterospecifics15,46. Nonetheless, based on the literature, this difference in aggression is related to both overt behavior and physiological responses, indicating the involvement of different central nervous system (CNS) neural pathways54.

Limitations

Next, one has to keep in mind that some limitations were still faced in the current study. First, since not all of the cricket body parts were assigned to be tracked by DLC, such as antennae, some visible stereotyped levels of escalating aggression, including antennal fencing, could not be quantitatively measured. We believed that this issue could be overcome by adding more body parts to be tracked by DLC in the future. Second, another limitation of the present DLC-based behavioral classification is that it cannot distinguish the motivational context underlying similar spatial interaction patterns. Specifically, posterior-oriented interactions may arise from different behavioral motivations, including dominance enforcement (e.g., chasing or kicking) or courtship-related behaviors in male–female pairs. As a result, posterior-oriented interactions were defined based on spatial configuration rather than behavioral intent. Future studies integrating multimodal recordings, such as synchronized audio–video analysis, will be necessary to more precisely distinguish aggression-related behaviors from courtship actions. Third, the current proposed method is unable to automatically distinguish whether aggressive interactions occurring within a short time span represent a single continuous sequence or multiple discrete events, as such behaviors may persist over several frames rather than occur as isolated movements. Consequently, when interactions occurred in rapid succession, manual inspection of the distance–time profiles and identification of distinct troughs were required to accurately determine the number of behavioral endpoints. Next, since crickets may possess unique behaviors that differentiate them from other cricket species, it would be necessary to find the optimal parameter values to accurately quantify the aggressive behaviors for each cricket during initial tests that might be helped by the manual observation assessment while some parameters in a group might also appropriately applied to another group such as when counting posterior-oriented interaction behaviors in both males and females groups of G. bimaculatus. For an example, in the current study, the parameter values in determining the overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts were slightly different between two cricket species as A. domesticus were observed to more frequent to occlude in the same position (Supplementary Fig. 4B), causing difficulties for DLC to track some body parts during these frames, thus, similar parameters setting with G. bimaculatus might cause in overestimated and less accurate data. Besides occlusion between crickets, DLC might also face some difficulties in detecting some body parts that were not visible to the models, which may occur during grappling or rapid movements, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4A. This figure shows an example of a cricket’s rapid movement that reached ~ 33 cm/s. However, due to the rapidity of its movement, the body image in several frames might become blurred, causing DLC to be unable to detect its body parts. This might be the reason of the lower sensitivity of this method when quantifying posterior-oriented interaction count, which included chasing behavior, compared to overt physical combat count since during chasing, generally, the pursuer moves faster than baseline locomotion to keep up with the retreating cricket with a straight and directed movement, in contrast to random exploratory walking (wandering behavior) and it might continue for a noticeable duration (not just a single step or incidental movement)3,13,19. However, we believe that this limitation was mainly related to the recording device instead of the DLC itself. Thus, a more advanced recording device that can record video at a higher fps should be able to overcome this issue or at least minimize this error. Taken together, these limitations highlight that while the current DLC-based framework enables objective and high-throughput quantification of cricket interactions, further methodological refinements—particularly expanded body-part tracking, multimodal recording, species-specific parameter optimization, and higher temporal resolution—will be necessary to improve behavioral specificity and sensitivity in future studies.

Future directions

In crickets, the aggressive behaviors, such as male antennal contact, copulation, and fighting, are known to be influenced by an increase in octopamine levels, an analogue of noradrenaline in invertebrates, in the haemolymph or CNS55,56. Therefore, it is intriguing to evaluate the expression level of this hormone in the tested crickets after their encounter with either their conspecific or heterospecific to comprehend the mechanism of their observed behaviors in the future. Furthermore, it is also interesting to evaluate the toxicity of various compounds in altering the agonistic behaviors of crickets, since although it was rarely studied, a prior study had demonstrated that aggression in crickets could be suppressed by the presence of some chemicals, which was nitric oxide in their case57. A follow-up study that addresses the effect of their housing prior to the encounters may also be worth conducting to evaluate whether isolation housing could result in different agonistic behaviors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study reported the DeepLabCut-based method in automatically and accurately detecting multiple body parts in two cricket species, G. bimaculatus and A. domesticus. By using the output data, we could analyze the intra- and interspecific aggressive behaviors of those crickets during an encounter test as summarized in Fig. 4. The current findings highlight the more robust aggressiveness of G. bimaculatus compared to A. domesticus toward their respective conspecifics in a form of overt physical combat and posterior-oriented interaction counts, together with a high locomotor activity. Furthermore, the present study also indicated less robust fighting behaviors of G. bimaculatus during interspecific behavior tests with A. domesticus of the same gender, although both crickets still displayed noticeable aggressive behaviors during the test. In addition, the performance scoring results support the conclusion that the proposed method is both valid and practical for studying aggressive interactions in crickets. While one has to keep in mind that 100% agreement between the current and manual observation methods was not feasible, unlike humans, the system operates with sustained attention and consistently without human subjectivity. Nevertheless, the output from DLC can still support researchers who require manual annotation. By quantifying the average distance between selected body parts (Supplementary Fig. 1), these data enable investigators to efficiently identify and verify periods of aggressive behavior without the need to review the entire video. However, future refinements—such as additional training data, improved feature extraction, or behavior-specific model tuning—may further increase precision. Nonetheless, in its current form, the method provides a robust platform for automated behavioral analysis and offers a scalable tool for researchers investigating social behaviors in insects. Taken together, this study emphasized the potential of this methodology as an alternative method to provide novel quantitative insights into the dynamics of fighting and mating interactions in crickets, overcoming the limitations of traditional observational methods, highlighted the potency of DLC in broader implications for studying social behavior in other animal species, and could contribute to the fields of ethology and behavioral ecology, advancing our understanding of animal behavior.
