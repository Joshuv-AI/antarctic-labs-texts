Effect of preinitiated glucose-insulin-potassium strategy for patients with undergoing planned percutaneous coronary intervention: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Huiruo Liu, Rugang Liu, Zeyu Yang, Feng Xu, Chuanbao Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesWhether the glucose-insulin-potassium (GIK) should be used as an adjuvant therapy for ischaemic myocardial disease remains controversial nowadays reperfusion era. This meta-analysis aimed to assess the effects of preinitiated GIK for patients undergoing planned percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesPubMed, Web of science, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched through 27 November 2022.Eligibility criteriaOnly randomised controlled trials involving participants preinitiated with GIK or placebo before planned PCI were included.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers used standardised methods to search, screen and code included trials. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane tool. Pooled analysis was conducted using random or effects models according to the heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were carried out for dosage of GIK and if with ongoing myocardial ischaemia.Results13 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) including 3754 participants were evaluated. We found patients preconditioned with GIK before PCI showed a significant increase in Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 3 flow events after angioplasty (OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.46, p=0.04), also revealed improved in-hospital left ventricular ejection fraction (weighed mean difference, WMD 1.62, 95% CI 0.21 to 3.03, p=0.02) and myocardial salvage index (WMD 0.09, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.16, p=0.03). Nevertheless, no benefit was observed in all-cause mortality neither on 30-day (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.59 to 1.11, p=0.18) nor 6 months (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.42 to 2.46, p=0.97). Furthermore, GIK intervention was associated with higher occurrences of complications such as phlebitis (OR 10.13, 95% CI 1.74 to 59.00, p=0.01) and hypoglycaemia (OR 10.43, 95% CI 1.32 to 82.29, p=0.03), but not hyperkalaemia (OR 9.36, 95% CI 0.50 to 175.27, p=0.13), liquid overload (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.25 to 4.13, p=0.98) or in-hospital heart failure (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.06 to 2.96, p=0.39).ConclusionsOur study shows preconditioning GIK exhibits myocardial reperfusion and cardiac function benefits for patients planning to receive PCI intervention, while also some complications such as phlebitis and hypoglycaemia accompany.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022326334.

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has now become widely used and proven procedure, which could effectively reopen the obstructive coronary arteries and salvage the ischaemic tissue. Although the technique of PCI for patients has relatively matured, the morbidity and mortality linked with complications such as no-reflow or slow-reflow still remain high.1 2 Ischaemia-reperfusion injury is known as the key point to the no-reflow or slow-reflow phenomenon, which involves mitochondrial dysfunction, increased toxic lipids and other related mechanisms.3 Thus, a prevention or solution called for to these adverse complications during PCI may be achieved by extra drug therapy strategies influencing cardiac metabolism. Theoretically, glucose-insulin-potassium (GIK) is described as a metabolic strategy for ischaemic myocardium.4

GIK solution has long been advocated as an adjuvant treatment for patients with ischaemic heart disease, which is originally put forward as a strengthening metabolism strategy for myocardium during ischemia.4 GIK is thought to provide cardiac protection through reducing circulating free fatty acids (FFAs) and motivating the fire of glucose as the primary energy substrates for myocardial tissue while myocardial ischaemia evokes several metabolic reactions, such as resultant decrease in insulin secretion and increase in FFAs.5 6 Consistently, in clinical practice, improving composite ischaemic results associated with GIK showed in majority of prior trials in the prereperfusion era.7–9 In parallel, international guidelines once recommended GIK infusion as a cardioprotective strategy to mitigate adverse outcomes in patients with acute myocardial syndrome.10 However, recent findings in patients undergoing PCI have shown that the clinical benefits of GIK was tended to be disappeared,11–13 contradictory to the outcomes as in prereperfusion era. For this reason, the intravenous GIK for ischaemic myocardial disease becomes controversial in today’s reperfusion-therapy era.

As for the GIK solution, the research emphasis has shifted towards adjusting the infusion timing or dose of GIK to achieve the goals that are both effective in reducing adverse events as well as safe in terms of patients treated with PCI. Previous studies have shown that preconditioning with GIK solution obviously improved the haemodynamic parameters during and after coronary artery bypass graft surgery,14 also better preserved systolic and diastolic ventricular function, which motivates us to consider whether an earlier GIK intervention can be extended to patients receiving PCI to get more clinical benefits. While the actual clinical effect remains uncertain.

Here, we performed this systematic evaluation of existing studies to explore the safety and efficacy of preinitiated GIK for patients planning to be treated with PCI, so as to provide a reliable basis supporting their clinical use in nowadays reperfusion era.

Materials and methods

This meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (see online supplemental material 1).15 This study had been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration number: CRD42022326334).

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Web of science, MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane Library databases using the following search terms within titles and abstracts (also in combination with MESH terms and keywords): (Insulin) AND (“Percutaneous Coronary Intervention” OR “Coronary Intervention, Percutaneous” OR “Coronary Interventions, Percutaneous” OR “Intervention, Percutaneous Coronary” OR “Interventions, Percutaneous Coronary” OR “Percutaneous Coronary Interventions” OR “Percutaneous Coronary Revascularization” OR “Coronary Revascularization, Percutaneous” OR “Coronary Revascularizations, Percutaneous” OR “Percutaneous Coronary Revascularizations” OR “Revascularization, Percutaneous Coronary” OR “Revascularizations, Percutaneous Coronary” OR “Angioplasty, Balloon, Coronary” OR “Atherectomy, Coronary”), having limited to articles published prior to November 2022, without language restriction. Articles were also identified from references of relevant original research and reviews to avert possible omissions of eligible research. See online supplemental material 2 for full details of search strategy.

Study selection

Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes and Study (PICOS) framework was used to define the inclusion criteria. Original studies fulfilling the following criteria were included: (a) patients: those with documented or suspected coronary artery disease and now scheduled for PCI as mode of reperfusion, (b) intervention: an early initiation of intravenous GIK ahead of PCI, (c) control: a placebo control group for GIK therapy (absence of GIK infusion), (d) outcomes: the primary outcome included Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) 3 coronary blood flow achievements after PCI. Also, data on Myocardial Salvage Index (MSI), changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), death from any cause, as well as occurrences of phlebitis, hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia, liquid overload and heart failure during the follow-up period were collected and (e) study design: being a randomised placebo-controlled trial. Exclusion criteria were ongoing studies, papers unavailable for full-text and not in English or Chinese. Eligibilities for studies were identified by two investigators independently.

Data extraction

Eligible studies were reviewed and the following parameters were extracted: (a) research characteristics (trial or first author’s name, year of publication and sample sizes in the GIK and control groups); (b) subject characteristics (mean age, gender distribution, symptom to balloon time, stent implant, infarct location, single or more vessel disease, and Killip class at randomisation); (c) treatment characteristics (components of GIK, dosage regimen and treatment duration) and (d) outcome variables during the follow-up.

Quality assessment

The checklists in the Cochrane handbook for systematic reviews of interventions were adopted to examine the risk of bias for RCTs included in this meta-analysis. This tool mainly evaluates possible bias from the following aspects: selection bias (including random sequence generation and allocation concealment), performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias and other bias that is not mentioned above. Each assessment of bias is showed as low, high or unclear risk. Online supplemental figure 1 shows the risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

Data were extracted from each study, and the Review Manager software V.5.4 was used for statistical analysis. Overall effects on dichotomous variables were expressed as pooled OR with 95% CIs while continuous outcomes were expressed as pooled weighed mean difference (WMD) with 95% CI. For the main outcome-TIMI 3 events, we performed subgroup analyses based on the dosage of GIK of low and high and whether with ongoing myocardial ischaemia (ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-STEMI or unstable angina). High-dose GIK was defined as an intravenous infusion of GIK in a dose equal to or higher than used 30% glucose (300 mg/L), 50 IU/L regular insulin and 80 mM potassium chloride at 1.5 mL/kg per hour infusion rate (approximately five units insulin per hour).16

Fixed-effect model or random-effect model was determined by the heterogeneity between studies included. The statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the χ2 statistic and I2 statistical test. Heterogeneity was considered statistically significant when p<0.05 and I2>50%, and the random effect model was applied. Otherwise, the fixed-effect model was applied. The inspection level was set at two tailed, 0.05 for all tests.

Publication bias

The publication bias was assessed graphically by visual inspection using the funnel plots. Symmetrical shape of funnel plots indicated no significant risk of publication bias.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in this design or conduct of this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Results

This meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines.15 All studies included in this study were published data, and all of them have claimed that protocols were approved by ethical committee of corresponding hospital or any other independent ethics committee. All participants from studies included have signed informed consent approved by institutional committee on human research.

Search results

A total of potentially relevant 2853 studies were retrieved from initial research. As the PRISMA selection flow chart shows in figure 1, 68 studies were identified for full-text scanning, after duplicates removing and initial screening by titles and abstracts. After full-text reviewing, 13 eligible studies were included.17–29

Study characteristics

A total of 3754 individuals (average ages 47–65 years and male distribution 55%–100%) were combined enrolled in the 13 eligible RCTs, including 1905 in the GIK arm and 1849 in the placebo arm.17–29 The main characteristics of the study population are listed in online supplemental table 1. These included studies were published between 2002 and 2020. Studies included used different GIK protocols, among 11 trials performed GIK infusions with fixed proportions and rates,17–23 25–28 while another 2 only and independently varied the infusing rates of insulin according to blood glucose.24 29 Symptom to balloon time ranges from 1.3 to 7.5 hours in controls and 1.3 to 7.4 hours in the GIK group. Trials reported stent implantation, anterior infarction, single-vessel disease and class Killip I cardiac function presented 56%–100%, 20%–55%, 48%–100% and 68–94%, respectively. Studies varied from moderate to high quality for unclear reporting of allocation concealment or blinding in some RCTs (online supplemental figure 1).

Effects of GIK on TIMI 3 blood flow achievements after PCI

Pooled analysis of data from nine trials including 1511 participants indicated a significant improvement in TIMI 3 coronary blood flow achievements after PCI,17–19 21 24 25 27–29 following treatment with GIK infusion (OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.46, p=0.04) (figure 2). However, in subgroup analysis, the above-mentioned effect on TIMI 3 was found to be disappeared in patients initiated a GIK infusion at high-dose before PCI (figure 2).17–19 25 Likewise, the similar results were also obtained in other two subsets of trials irrespective of whether the dose of GIK was low-dose (OR 1.96, 95% CI 0.91 to 4.22, p=0.09) or variable dose (OR 1.80, 95% CI 0.98 to 3.32, p=0.06) (figure 2). No significantly different changes in TIMI 3 achievements were observed in trials between low and high-dose GIK. Notably, TIMI 3 improvement remained apparent by preinitiated GIK in subgroup population with ongoing ischaemia (OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.46, p=0.04); whereas, due to data deficiencies, we could not assess whether patients without ongoing ischaemia experienced TIMI benefits from preinitiated GIK (online supplemental figure 2).

Effects of GIK on TIMI 3 coronary flow events. GIK, glucose-insulin-potassium; TIMI, Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction; M-H, Mantel-Haenszel.

Effects of GIK on myocardial function after PCI

Two of the thirteen treatment arms including 292 subjects reported data on MSI.18 20 Meta-analysis did indicate a statistically significant alteration in MSI in participants receiving GIK therapy and starting before PCI (WMD 0.09, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.16, p=0.03) (figure 3A). Similarly, there was also a beneficial effect of GIK infusion reflected in in-hospital LVEF in patients after PCI (WMD 1.62, 95% CI 0.21 to 3.03, p=0.02) (figure 3B).18 19 29 But no apparent difference by preinitiated GIK for the variety of Troponin I during the first 24 hours post-PCI was showed (WMD 0.05, 95% CI −0.84 to 0.93, p=0.92) (figure 3C).

Effects of GIK on in-hospital LVEF, MSI and 24 hours Troponin I post-PCI. GIK, glucose-insulin-potassium; LVEF, left ventricular ejection fraction; MSI, Myocardial Salvage Index; PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention.

Effects of GIK on all-cause mortality

A total count of four RCTs provided data on death from any cause.18 20 22 29 In summary, 30-day all-cause mortality was mentioned in three trials,20 22 29 occurring in 74 of 1556 patients from GIK groups while 89 of 1527 from controls, with no statistically significant change (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.59 to 1.11, p=0.18) (figure 4A). Among, two studies showed data regarding 6-month all-cause mortality and results showed that GIK infusion injected before PCI was not superior in the prevention of all-cause mortality at 6 months (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.42 to 2.46, p=0.97) (figure 4B).18 20

Effects of GIK on all-cause mortality on 30 days and 6 months. GIK, glucose-insulin-potassium; M-H, Mantel-Haenszel.

Effect of GIK on adverse outcomes

Seven trials provided data on the occurrence of follow-up related adverse complications in the form of: (a) phlebitis, (b) hypoglycaemia, (c) hyperkalaemia, (d) liquid overload and (e) in-hospital heart failure.18–20 23 26 27 29 Four of the seven RCTs provided data on the occurrence of phlebitis, and quantitative analyses suggested that GIK treatment resulted in an obvious increase in phlebitis (OR 10.13, 95% CI 1.74 to 59.00, p=0.01) (figure 5A).18–20 29 Negative effect on hypoglycaemia was also observed following GIK infusion (OR 10.43, 95% CI 1.32 to 82.29, p=0.03) (figure 5B). Whereas those other three adverse clinical outcomes, including hyperkalaemia (OR 9.36, 95% CI 0.50 to 175.27, p=0.13) (figure 5C), liquid overload (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.25 to 4.13, p=0.98) (figure 5D) and in-hospital heart failure (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.06 to 2.96, p=0.39) (figure 5E) were not different between the GIK infusion group and controls during the follow-up period.

Effects of GIK on occurring phlebitis, hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia, liquid overload and in-hospital heart failure. GIK, glucose-insulin-potassium; M-H, Mantel-Haenszel.

Publication bias

An apparent asymmetry of the funnel plot for included RCTs was observed via visual inspection (online supplemental figure 3).

Discussion

This is the first meta-analysis to highlight TIMI 3 flow benefits associated with ahead-initiated GIK infusion before PCI among patients who undergo coronary angioplasty, which was might accepted to be linked with the mechanisms of vasodilating effect on inner walls of coronary artery.30 31 No-flow or slow-flow phenomenon during PCI is currently indicated as an obstruction in the microvascular beds, caused by distal microembolisation, microvascular oedema or constriction.32 We considered that GIK pretreatment would improve coronary blood flow mainly because of its components, insulin and potassium, for insulin stimulating endothelial nitric oxide production and external potassium leading to membrane hyperpolarisation that could expand blood vessels by the way of nitric oxide cyclic guanosine monophosphate signals or electrophysiological pathways.33 34 Different from GIK treatment started after PCI, an infusion initiating before PCI may be more likely a prevention treatment that precondition coronary endothelium with insulin and potassium, and therefore, rewarded them vasodilation ability earlier to resist microvascular dysfunction during whole intervention. Patients happening no-reflow or slow-reflow post-PCI have worse cardiovascular outcomes when in comparison to those with successful reperfusion.35 Hence, a deeper understanding of the mechanism of GIK contributing to TIMI 3 flow is pressing and emerging. Notably, high-dose GIK infusion, the concept raised as a strengthening metabolic strategy for ischaemic cardiomyopathy by McDaniel et al to obtain more beneficial clinical effects,9 showed no advantages on TIMI 3 events. A recent meta-analysis reported an improved early outcome after cardiac surgery by perioperative GIK with insulin/glucose ratio >2,36 whereas, in our research, any similar TIMI profits from high insulin/glucose GIK were not appeared in patients planned to receive PCI (online supplemental figure 4).

Findings of our study also show the beneficial cardiovascular effects of GIK pretreatment on increasing in-hospital LVEF and postdischarge MSI, without serious in-hospital heart failure or liquid overload as cost. These results indicated a greater myocardia survival and subsequent a better cardiac function improvement. Patients pretreated with GIK showed increased LVEF and MSI, which may be related to the dilating myocardial microcirculation by insulin and potassium but also plausibly explained by the improved myocardial metabolism via activation of insulin signalling by insulin and glucose.5 6 33 34 Previous laboratory studies demonstrate GIK solution as a considerable cardioprotective intervention,37 38 but clinical trials have revealed disparate results, presumably because of varying formulas and different timing of the GIK treatment. Besides, several past meta-analyses did not explore any potent cardioprotective benefits of GIK when the infusion starting time of included studies was complex.11–13 39 40

The explorations from this meta-analysis showed overall beneficial cardiovascular effects, inconsistent with the results from previous pooled analyses, which was considered on account of an earlier initiation of GIK. Nevertheless, no differential reduction in all-cause mortality was confirmed. This is different from previous reports in 1990s whereby the mortality benefits of GIK were more likely to be present when analysing deaths in prethrombolytic era.40 Several recent meta-analyses examining whether any mortality lowering effects provided by GIK are still be apparent when applied according to the current management of ischaemic heart diseases showed consistently disappointed outcomes,11–13 39 which are in-line with our results. One theory as to ‘ceiling effect’ is that the therapeutic benefits of GIK are concealed by reperfusion treatments, which may account for the larger benefits in prethrombolytic era in response to GIK infusion. Moreover, higher occurrences of complications such as phlebitis and hypoglycaemia following GIK administration are evident. Therefore, an application of GIK may be cautious for high-risk patients with metabolic disturbances or history of uncontrolled diabetes.

There are several limitations in this meta-analysis. First, studies included in our meta-analysis mostly published before 2010, which might lead to a deviation for the difference in medical levels. In addition, we found that there existed potential publication bias which might contribute to an exaggerated protective effect of preinitiated GIK. Second, different types of coronary syndrome may also affect the relationship between pre-PCI started GIK and TIMI 3 flow events. Regrettably, these cannot be ascertained due to the breakdown of the individual type of coronary syndrome events that has not been provided in clinical trials. Third, outcomes included in the evaluation lacked important data, such as myocardial blush grade, ST-segment resolution, major adverse cardiovascular events and so on. Fourth, the duration of follow-up for each trial was short which might hide possible long-term effects of GIK to some extent. Finally, our research is only restricted to literature published in English or Chinese, on account of the language barrier. So, although we did not observe dissymmetry in any funnel plots, it still has the possibility that exist potential publication biases.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that initiating GIK infusion before PCI plays a role in increasing TIMI 3 flow events, also improves myocardial survival and cardiac function. However, no reduction in all-cause mortality is observed associated with GIK pretreatment. Besides, complications such as phlebitis and hypoglycaemia are frequently occurred during the intervention. As an adjuvant therapy for reperfusion treatment, GIK is economical but controversial. To further confirm whether ahead the initiating time of GIK could make patients post-PCI more clinical benefits, relevant large-scaled clinical trials with long-term follow-up are urgently warranted.
