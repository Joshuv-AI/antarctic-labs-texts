Hemifacial spasm caused by multiple vascular attachments due to remote compression effects of a dermoid cyst in the cerebellar hemisphere: illustrative case
Authors: Kento Hirayama, Takafumi Tanei, Takenori Kato, Toshinori Hasegawa, Eiji Ito, Yusuke Nishimura, Ryuta Saito
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDermoid cysts located laterally in the posterior fossa are rare. The authors report the case of a dermoid cyst in the cerebellar hemisphere presenting with hemifacial spasm (HFS) caused by multiple vascular attachments due to remote compression effects.OBSERVATIONSA 48-year-old man presented with left HFS. Computed tomography showed a mass lesion in the left cerebellar hemisphere with calcification and erosion of skull bone. Magnetic resonance imaging showed no contrast enhancement of the lesion and a dural defect. The lesion compressed the brainstem and cerebellopontine cistern, but no vascular attachments to the facial nerve were seen. Tumor removal and microvascular decompression were performed. The lesion was composed of soft tissue containing oil-like liquid and hairs, and the border of the cerebellar arachnoid was clear. There were multiple vascular attachments to the root exit zone, facial nerve, and brainstem. After displacing these arteries, the intraoperative abnormal muscle response disappeared. Histopathological findings showed stratified squamous epithelium, keratin flakes, calcifications, and hairs. The HFS disappeared completely and has remained absent for 27 months.LESSONSThe dermoid cyst originating from occipital bone compressed the cerebellar hemisphere, displacing multiple vessels and leading to HFS. Tumor removal and the removal of all vascular factors can completely resolve HFS.

Dermoid cysts account for approximately 0.3% of intracranial tumors.1,2 The lesions originate from the inclusion of trapped embryonic ectoderm cells, and they contain not only squamous epithelium but also various glands and hair.1,2 Common anatomical locations of dermoid cysts are the cisternal spaces in the midline, such as the prepontine and suprasellar cisterns.1,2 Dermoid cysts located laterally in the posterior fossa are extremely rare.3–8 The lesion is very slow growing and presents with clinical symptoms in early adulthood. Clinical symptoms depend on the location of the lesion. Brain tumors located in the cerebellopontine angle or glioma in the brainstem occasionally induce ipsilateral or contralateral hemifacial spasm (HFS).9,10 It is known that HFS is also produced by brain tumors far from the facial nerve due to remote compression effects.11–18 A case presenting with HFS caused by multiple vascular attachments due to the remote compression effects of a dermoid cyst in the ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere is presented.

Illustrative Case

A 48-year-old man presented with left HFS approximately 1 year earlier. Since several medical therapies had not improved his symptoms, he was referred to our hospital. Computed tomography showed a mass lesion in the left cerebellar hemisphere with calcification and erosion inside the skull bone (Fig. 1A). On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the lesion showed an area of heterogeneous intensity without apparent contrast enhancement on T1-weighted sequences (Fig. 1B and C). There was a defect in the dura mater, and the lesion was spreading to the epidural area. The lesion compressed the brainstem, although findings of brain invasion or edema around the lesion were not evident on T2-weighted MRI (Fig. 1D). On diffusion-weighted MRI, the lesion showed heterogeneous hyperintensity (Fig. 1E). Fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition imaging showed compression of the left cerebellopontine cistern, but no vascular attachments to the facial nerve were seen (Fig. 1F). The lesion was located rostral to the horizontal cerebellar fissure (Fig. 1G and H). Cerebral angiography did not show feeding arteries to the lesion. These imaging findings suggested that the lesion was an epidermoid/dermoid cyst or Lhermitte-Duclos syndrome.

For the purpose of pathological diagnosis and symptom improvement, removal of the lesion and microvascular decompression were performed through a left lateral suboccipital craniotomy. The dura mater already had a defect, and the lesion was exposed at the time of craniotomy (Fig. 2A). The inside of the lesion was composed of soft tissue containing oil-like liquid and hairs (Fig. 2B). The lesion had an outer membrane, and the border of the arachnoid of the cerebellum was clear (Fig. 2C). However, the deep part of the lesion was fragile, and the border of the arachnoid of the cerebellum was unclear. These fragile membranes were difficult to peel off safely and a small amount remained (Fig. 2D). After lesion removal, the cerebellopontine cistern space was expanded. Bilateral vertebral arteries (VAs) were shifted to the left side due to compression of the lesion (Fig. 2E). The junction of the VA and basilar artery was located between the jugular foramen and the internal auditory meatus, and it was also shifted to the left side. The left VA compressed the brainstem, and a postmeatal segment of the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) attached to the root exit zone (REZ) of the facial nerve. First, the left VA and brainstem were detached and a sponge was inserted (Fig. 2F). Next, the postmeatal segment of the AICA was displaced (Fig. 2G). However, the intraoperative abnormal muscle response did not disappear. Then, the contact between a meatal loop of the AICA and the facial nerve was displaced. At that time, the abnormal muscle response disappeared (Fig. 2G). At the end of the surgical field, indentation and discoloration of the facial nerve were confirmed (Fig. 2H).

On histopathological examination, the cyst wall was composed of stratified squamous epithelium (Fig. 3A). The content of the lesion consisted of keratin flakes, calcifications, and hairs (Fig. 3B). The outer membrane of the lesion was attached to the surface of the cerebellum with invasion of inflammatory cells (Fig. 3C and D). The final pathological diagnosis was dermoid cyst. The left HFS disappeared completely immediately after surgery and has remained absent for 27 months. Total removal of the lesion was confirmed by postoperative MRI (Fig. 4A–D). The latest follow-up MRI also showed no evidence of recurrence (Fig. 4E–H).

Discussion

Observations

Dermoid cysts in the lateral posterior fossa are extremely rare, and they are located close to occipital bone at the asterion, intradural space, and cerebellar hemisphere.3–8 These cases are summarized in Table 1. Clinical symptoms of patients with these lesions included headache, ataxia, hearing loss, gait disturbance, dizziness, vertigo, and trigeminal neuralgia. The mechanisms of onset of these symptoms were mainly direct compression or increased intracranial pressure. Even though there is normal cerebellar tissue between the lesion and the cranial nerve, remote effects due to indirect compression of the lesion can induce the cranial nerve symptoms.3 Hemifacial spasm induced by the remote compression effects of a dermoid cyst has not been reported so far, and the present case is the first such report.

Hemifacial spasm is typically caused by mechanical compression at the REZ of the facial nerve by offending arteries, and microvascular decompression is curative surgery. Some brain tumors located at the cerebellopontine angle or glioma in the brainstem can induce ipsilateral or contralateral HFS.9,10 The etiology of HFS induced by such brain tumors is considered direct compression to the facial colliculus or intramedullary facial nerve fibers. On the other hand, some brain tumors far from the facial nerve can very occasionally cause HFS due to the remote compression effect.11–18 Mechanisms of HFS by remote compression are as follows: 1) distortion and/or compression of the facial nerve root, 2) a stretched facial nerve that may be sensitive to vascular stimulation and compression of the REZ, 3) compression of the facial nucleus, and 4) venous outflow obstruction due to the dislocation of anatomical structures.11

In the present case, the following anatomical changes occurred due to the remote compression effect of the dermoid cyst: compression of the brainstem, shift of bilateral VAs and the basilar artery, and narrowing of the cerebellopontine cistern space. In addition, these anatomical changes caused the multiple vascular attachments to the REZ of the facial nerve, the facial nerve itself, and the brainstem, which manifested as HFS. The intraoperative abnormal muscle response disappeared when all of these factors were resolved, and complete disappearance of the HFS was achieved. Hemifacial spasm induced by the remote compression effects of the brain tumor can be caused not only by simple vascular compression but also by many factors. Therefore, to achieve the complete disappearance of HFS, it is necessary to carefully observe around the REZ of the facial nerve, the facial nerve itself, and the brainstem and then to resolve all causes of HFS.

Preoperative imaging findings suggested that the lesion originated from the cerebellar parenchyma in the present case. However, the lesion was located outside the cerebellar parenchyma because the border between the lesion and the arachnoid of the cerebellum was clear. Pathological findings also supported the extra-axial nature of the lesion. It is reasonable that dermoid cysts originating from skull bones appear to show skull bone erosion.4 However, it is surprising that dermoid cysts originating from cerebellar parenchyma demonstrate not only a dural defect but also skull bone erosion.3,7 If a dermoid cyst originates from true cerebellar parenchyma, it should destroy surrounding parenchyma, which is softer than such hard structures. Therefore, in the present case, it is believed that the dermoid cyst originated from the diploic space of the occipital bone, destroyed the dura mater, and compressed the cerebellar parenchyma. The lesion grew so slowly that surrounding edema did not occur, despite the lesion’s large size.

Lessons

A dermoid cyst originating from occipital bone compressed the cerebellar hemisphere and cerebellopontine cistern as an extra-axial mass and resulted in the displacement of multiple vessels, leading to HFS. In order for HFS due to remote compression effects to be resolved completely, the lesion must first be removed, and then all vascular factors around the REZ of the facial nerve, the facial nerve itself, and the brainstem must be addressed.
