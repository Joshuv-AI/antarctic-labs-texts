A quantum analogue of convex optimization
Eunou Lee

Abstract
Convex optimization is the powerhouse behind the theory and practice of optimization. We introduce a quantum analogue of unconstrained convex optimization: computing the minimum eigenvalue of a Schrödinger operator h = − Δ + V h=-\Delta+V with convex potential V : ℝ n → ℝ ≥ 0 V:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} such that V ⁡ ( x ) → ∞ V(x)\rightarrow\infty as ‖ x ‖ → ∞ \|x\|\rightarrow\infty . For this problem, we present an efficient quantum algorithm, called the Fundamental Gap Algorithm (FGA), that computes the minimum eigenvalue of h h up to error ϵ \epsilon in polynomial time in n n , 1 / ϵ 1/\epsilon , and parameters that depend on V V . Adiabatic evolution of the ground state is used as a key subroutine, which we analyze with novel techniques that allow us to focus on the low-energy space. We apply the FGA to give the first known polynomial-time algorithm for finding the lowest frequency of an n n -dimensional convex drum, or mathematically, the minimum eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian on an n n -dimensional region that is defined by m m linear constraints in polynomial time in n n , m m , 1 / ϵ 1/\epsilon and the radius R R of a ball encompassing the region.

The Schrödinger operator

to give another function

such that

\displaystyle(h\psi)(x)=-\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\psi(x)+V(x)\psi(x).

The minimum eigenvalue problem is to compute the
smallest number
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
for which there
exists
\psi\neq 0
such that
h\psi=\lambda_{0}\psi
.
Since the eigenvectors of

form a complete set of basis,
we have an equivalent characterization

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=\min_{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1}\langle\psi|h\psi\rangle,\qquad\text{where}\qquad\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\overline{\phi(x)}\psi(x)\text{d}x.

Physically, the wavefunction

a quantum particle in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.
The total mechanical energy of the particle is

\langle\psi|h\psi\rangle
,
which is the sum of the kinetic energy

-\langle\psi|\Delta\psi\rangle
and the the
potential energy

\langle\psi|V\psi\rangle
.
Our goal is to find the minimum mechanical
energy that a quantum
particle can have under the convex potential

.

Why is this problem a quantum analogue
of unconstrained convex optimization?
Minimizing each of

-\langle\psi|\Delta\psi\rangle

and

\langle\psi|V\psi\rangle

reduces to
classical convex optimization
in the frequency and the position
domain, respectively.
When they are added together, however,
the spectrum becomes discretized (quantized)
and the minimizer is no longer
a classical state;
a quantum problem emerges from two classical objectives.
Alone classical, together quantum.

Another reason is
a physical interpretation of
accelerated gradient descent
[
38
]
, a foundational
algorithm for convex optimization:
the algorithm solves convex optimization by
minimizing the classical mechanical energy of a particle
under a convex potential.
Therefore, finding
the minimum quantum mechanical energy
under convex potential
is
natural quantum analogue of convex optimization.

Previous works
designed
quantum algorithms
for classical optimization problems
by quantizing the classical dynamics
of various gradient descent algorithms

,

Our work departs from these approaches in that we

To clarify what we mean by a quantum objective function,
we adapt the prover-verifier definition of optimization
[
31
]
.
A pair of algorithms
(p,v)

minimizes a function
f
to the value
\alpha

\min f\leq\alpha

The operator

a genuinely quantum objective in

a quantum algorithm (e.g. phase estimation, as we show in the paper),

As far as we are aware,
only few known rigorous optimization algorithms
require quantum verification.
For example,
dissipation-based algorithms for optimizing quantum Hamiltonians
in polynomial time
[

18

and exponential time
[

are recently introduced.
More closely related to our work is a quantum algorithm
computing the ground energy
of a Schrödinger operator on a bounded domain
[0,1]^{n}

with the Dirichlet boundary condition
[
39
]
.
This algorithm prepares the ground state of an initial Hamiltonian,
and then repeatedly measures in the energy basis
of a slowly varying Hamiltonian.
For the correctness of the algorithm,
the Fundamental Gap Theorem
[
3
]
is employed
to assert the spectral gap of the time-dependent Hamiltonian.

We adapt the key ideas from
[
39
]
.
We replace the repeated measurements with
a time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation,
while the Fundamental Gap Theorem
[
3
]

gives the spectral gap

At the same time, we face an additional challenge

\mathbb{R}^{n}

To address this issue,
we develop a set of tools
that allow us to ignore high-energy

We define a low-energy truncation of

which represents the operator up to its low-energy sector

We show that if two Hamiltonians admit similar truncations,

For the analysis, we construct a

As an application of the FGA,

Mathematically, the problem is

Information theoretic arguments

2
Problem formulation and results

2.1
Quantum optimization of quantum objectives

Our objective function
h
is defined on the function space

\displaystyle L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}}):=\left\{\psi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\ |\ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle<\infty\right\}.

An optimization problem is often stated as: find the best element from a given set.
It is unclear
what it means to find
the best element
over the inifinite-dimensional space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
,
when we are bound to
a physical machine hosting only a finite number of qubits.

The infinity of the domain is
already a familiar issue in classical optimization.
For instance, we know
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}x^{2}=0

at
x=0
even though
\mathbb{R}
is infinite.
More generally,
if we are given certificate for
f(x^{*})
,
we know that
\min_{x}f(x)\leq f(x^{*})
.
This idea of a certificate of optimization
can be formalized as follow.

Definition 1

(generalization of
[
31
]
)
.

Given an objective function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a pair of algorithms
(p,v)
minimizes
f
to a value
\alpha
up to error
\epsilon
, if the following conditions hold.

1.

The algorithm
v
correctly verifies
\min_{X}f\leq\alpha
:

(a)

If
\text{min}_{x\in X}f(x)\leq\alpha
:
\exists

|w\rangle
on finitely many (qu)bits such that
v(|w\rangle)=1
w.p.
\geq 2/3
.

(b)

If
\text{min}_{x\in X}f(x)\geq\alpha+\epsilon
:
\forall

|w\rangle
on finitely many (qu)bits, we have
v(|w\rangle)=1
w.p.
\leq 1/3
.

2.

The algorithm
p
, w.p.
\geq 2/3
, outputs a finite length
|w^{\prime}\rangle
such that
v(|w^{\prime}\rangle)=1
.

In this paper, we find the minimum of objective functions in polynomial time in the following sense.

Definition 2
.

Given an objective function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a pair of algorithms
(p,v)
finds the minimum of
f
up to error
\epsilon
in time
T
, if
(p,v)
minimizes
f
to the value
\min_{x\in X}f(x)

\epsilon

and

halt in time

.

Whether

p
,
v
, and
|w\rangle
are classical or quantum is left unspecified by the definition;
all classical/quantum combinations are allowed.
In our case,
p,v
and
|w\rangle
are all quantum.

3
Algorithms

Our algorithm applies an adiabatic evolution under the time‑dependent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{Q}(t)\;:=\;K_{Q}\;+\;V_{Q}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],

(1)

acting on
n
registers of
\log N
qubits each. The kinetic term
K_{Q}
is diagonal in the discrete Fourier basis and mimics the continuous operator
-\Delta
.
The potential term
V_{Q}(t)
is diagonal in the computational basis and mimics a continuous potential operator.
This discretization of kinetic and potential operators follows prior work in quantum simulation
[
48
,
50
,
27
,
16
]
.

The time-dependent
V_{Q}(t)
interpolates between an initial potential
V_{Q}(0)
and a final potential
V_{Q}(T)
.
The initial potential is chosen so that
K_{Q}+V_{Q}(0)
decomposes as a sum of single‑register Hamiltonians
H_{N}
. Consequently, the ground state of
H_{Q}(0)
can be prepared in polynomial time as the product of the ground states of the
H_{N}
.
The final potential is defined so that
\lambda_{0}\big(H_{Q}(T)\big)
is close to
\lambda_{0}(h)
, where
\lambda_{0}(A)

is the minimum eigenvalue of a self-adjoint
A
.

We first describe how we discretize Schrödinger operators so that
they are simulable on a quantum computer, and then
state our two algorithms.

3.1
Discretization of Schrödinger operators

It is more natural to discretize a Schrödinger operator
h_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}=-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}+V_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
on the torus

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}^{n}}:={\mathbb{R}^{n}}/L\mathbb{Z}^{n}

than
h
on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.
Here,
\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}=-\sum_{i\in[n]}\partial^{2}/\partial x_{i}^{2}
is the Laplacian on the flat torus, and
V_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}:{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is smooth.
We discretize
{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
to the grid
\{\,yL/N\;|\;y\in\mathcal{N}^{n}\,\}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\;:=\;\left\{-\frac{N}{2},\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,\frac{N}{2}-1\right\}.

(2)

We assume
N
is a power of 2, and identify the labels for the computational basis states modulo
N
,
so that, for
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,

\displaystyle|y\rangle=|y^{\prime}\rangle\qquad\text{whenever}\qquad y=y^{\prime}\mod N.

We define the discretized Schrödinger, kinetic, and potential operators to be

\displaystyle H_{Q}

\displaystyle:=K_{Q}+V_{Q},

\displaystyle K_{Q}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{N}^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes K_{N}\otimes I_{N}^{\otimes(n-i)},

\displaystyle V_{Q}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}^{n}}|y\rangle\;V_{{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}\!\left(\frac{yL}{N}\right)\!\langle y|,

where
I_{N}
is the
N\times N
identity,

\displaystyle K_{N}\;:=\;\sum_{k_{0}\in\mathcal{N}}U_{N}|k_{0}\rangle\;\frac{4\pi^{2}k_{0}^{2}}{L^{2}}\;\langle k_{0}|\,U_{N}^{\dagger},

and
U_{N}
is the
N
‑dimensional quantum Fourier transform acting on
|k_{0}\rangle
for
k_{0}\in\mathcal{N}
by

\displaystyle U_{N}|k_{0}\rangle\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}}e^{\,i2\pi\,k_{0}y/N}\,|y\rangle.

Equivalently, we can also write

\displaystyle K_{Q}\;:=\;\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{n}}U_{\mathcal{F}}|k\rangle\;\frac{4\pi^{2}\|k\|_{2}^{2}}{L^{2}}\;\langle k|\,U_{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}

where
U_{\mathcal{F}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}U_{N}
is the discrete Fourier transform on
\mathcal{N}^{n}
.

3.2
Algorithm for the Schrödinger problem

We define the time-dependent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{Q}(t)\ :=\ K_{Q}+\frac{T-t}{T}V_{Q}(0)+\frac{t}{T}V_{Q}(T),

where

\displaystyle V_{Q}(0)

\displaystyle:=b\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{N}^{n}}}|y\rangle\ \sum_{i\in[n]}\left(1-\text{Cut}_{\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}}\!\left(\left|\tfrac{y_{i}L}{N}\right|\right)\right)\langle y|,

(3)

\displaystyle V_{Q}(T)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{N}^{n}}}|y\rangle\ \text{Sat}_{c,1}\!\circ\!V\big(\tfrac{yL}{N}\big)\langle y|,

(4)

and
b,c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Here,

\text{Cut}_{\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
smooth cutoff function and
\text{Sat}_{c,1}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is a smooth saturating function
(Definition

)
such that

\displaystyle\text{Cut}_{\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}}(x)

\displaystyle\ \begin{cases}=1\qquad\qquad&x\leq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}},\\
\in[0,1]\qquad\qquad&x\in[\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}],\\
=0\qquad\qquad&x\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\text{Sat}_{c,1}(x)

\displaystyle\ \begin{cases}=x\qquad&x\leq c,\\
:\text{monotone increasing}\qquad&x\in[c,c+1],\\
:\text{some constant in}\ [c,c+1]\qquad&x\geq c+1.\end{cases}

Note that the initial Hamiltonian
H_{Q}(0)=K_{Q}+V_{Q}(0)
is a sum of single-register Hamiltonians:

\displaystyle H_{Q}(0)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{N}^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes H_{N}\otimes I_{N}^{\otimes(n-i)},

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad H_{N}:=

\displaystyle K_{N}\;+\;b\sum_{y_{0}\in\mathcal{N}}|y_{0}\rangle\left(1-\text{Cut}_{\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}}\!\left(\left|\frac{y_{0}L}{N}\right|\right)\right)\!\langle y_{0}|,

(5)

We present our main algorithm that we call the Fundamental Gap Algorithm (FGA).

Algorithm 1

(FGA)
.

Given an efficient circuit that computes
V(yL/N)
for all
y\in\mathcal{N}^{n}
up to a negligible error in

:

1.

Prepare the product ground state
|\Psi_{init}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\Psi_{0}\rangle

with probability

|\Psi_{0}\rangle

i

(a)

Prepare
\Theta(N\log n)

\frac{1}{N}I
on
\log N

(b)

Measure the energy of each copy with respect to
H_{N}
(Equation (
5
)
using phase estimation.

(c)

Move the copy with the lowest measured energy to the
i
‑th register.

2.

Evolve
|\Psi_{init}\rangle
under the time‑dependent Hamiltonian

H_{Q}(t)\;=\;K_{Q}+(1-t/T)\,V_{Q}(0)\;+\;(t/T)\,V_{Q}(T),\qquad t\in[0,T],

via Hamiltonian simulation to obtain the final state

, where

are as in Equations (

3.

Measure the energy of

Since we are only aiming for a polynomial-time algorithm,

For instance, we can use the algorithm from

4.1
Operator theory cheat sheet

In this paper, we are concerned with functions that represent quantum states on
\Omega\in\{{\mathbb{R}^{n}},B,{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\}
, where
B\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is a compact measurable subset.

•

A
Hilbert space
is a complete metric space under the metric induced by an inner product.
For example, the space

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)=\{\ \psi\ |\ \psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\ \int_{\Omega}|\psi(x)|^{2}\text{d}x<\infty\}

is a Hilbert space, equipped with the inner product between
\psi,\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
given by

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\phi\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}\overline{\psi(x)}\phi(x)\ \text{d}x,

where
\overline{a}
is the complex conjugate of
a\in\mathbb{C}.

•

An
operator

A
on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a pair
(A,\mathcal{D}(A))
where
A
is a linear map that is defined by its
operator domain

\mathcal{D}(A)\leq\mathcal{H}
and its action
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}.

For example, the Laplacian operator

\displaystyle\Delta:\psi\rightarrow\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}

is an operator on
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is defined on the domain
\mathcal{D}({\Delta})
.
Note that
\Delta\psi
is
only defined on twice-differentiable functions
as per the definition above, therefore,
\mathcal{D}(\Delta)\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})\cap C^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.
Furthermore, not every twice-differentiable
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})

yields
\Delta\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}}).

In many cases, we want an operator domain on which the operator is self-adjoint (see below).
Finding such a domain is a nontrivial task that can be found in the mathematical physics literature
[
43
]
.

•

An important family of operators is
multiplication operators
. Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, multiplication operator
M_{f}
is defined on
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
so that for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle M_{f}\psi(x):=f(x)\psi(x).

For example, the potential operator
M_{V}
is a multiplication operator.
In this paper, we abuse the notation and denote
V
instead of
M_{V}
,
following the notation in physics.

•

A
unitary operator
is a surjective linear operator
U:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
that preserves the inner product

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle U\phi|U\psi\rangle\qquad\forall\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}.

•

Define
operator norm

\|A\|
of an operator
M
on
\mathcal{H}
to be

\displaystyle\|A\|:=\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)}\frac{\|A\psi\|}{\|\psi\|}=\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{D},\|\psi\|=1}\|A\psi\|.

For a multiplication operator
M_{V}
, we have

\|M_{f}\|=\|f\|_{\infty}.

•

An operator
A
is called
symmetric
if its domain
\mathcal{D}(A)
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, and for all
\psi,\phi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
, we have
\langle A\psi|\phi\rangle=\langle\psi|A\phi\rangle.

•

Given that
\mathcal{D}(A)
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, its
adjoint operator

A^{\dagger}
is defined so that

A^{\dagger}\psi=z
where
z
is the unique vector satisfying
\langle A\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\phi|z\rangle\ \forall\phi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
The domain
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
is the set of
\psi
for which such
z
exists.

•

The operator
A
is
self-adjoint
if
\mathcal{D}(A)=\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
and
A\psi=A^{\dagger}\psi
for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.

•

A vector
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)\setminus\{0\}
is an
eigenvector
of
A
if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
A\psi=\lambda\psi.
The value
\lambda
is called the
eigenvalue
of
\psi.

•

The
spectrum
of
A
is the set

\displaystyle\text{Spec}(A):=\{\lambda\ |A-\lambda I\text{ does not have an inverse with a bounded norm\lx@text@rbrace}.

If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue, then
\lambda\in\text{Spec}(A)
. The inverse of this statement is not necessarily true.

•

A spectrum
\text{Spec}(A)
is
purely discrete
if each
\lambda\in\text{Spec}(A)
is an eigenvalue, the multiplicity of
\lambda
, defined as
\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)
, is finite, and
\text{Spec}(A)
accumulates at no other point than
\infty
.

4.2
Schrödinger operators

A Schrödinger operator
h=-\Delta+V
is a linear operator that is the sum of the negative Laplacian
-\Delta
,
and a multiplication operator
V
.
Its action on
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(h)
is defined by

\displaystyle h\psi(x)=-\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\psi(x)+V(x)\psi(x).

If
\Omega=B\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is a bounded domain,
we always impose the Dirichlet boundary condition, which asserts that an operator acts only on
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\psi(x)=0\qquad\forall x\in\partial B.

We use the superscript
D
to denote that the operator is under the Dirichlet boundary condition.
For example,
\Delta_{B}^{D}

is the Dirichlet Laplacian operator on the domain
B
.

In this paper, we are interested in Schrödinger operators in the following forms.

Definition 4

(Schrödinger operators)
.

We define sets of Schrödinger operators on the real space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, on a compact subset with smooth boundary
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Dirichlet boundary condition,
and on the length
L

\mathbb{T}^{n}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}/L\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle S_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:=

\displaystyle\{h=-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}+V\ |\ V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},V\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ V(x)\rightarrow\infty\text{ as }|x|\rightarrow\infty\}

\displaystyle S_{\Omega}^{D}:=

\displaystyle\{h=-\Delta_{\Omega}^{D}+V\ |\ V:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ V\in C^{\infty}(\Omega\mathcal{)},\ \text{with the Dirichlet boundary condition}\},

\displaystyle S_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}:=

\displaystyle\{h=-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}+V\ |\ V:\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ V\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\},

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
,
\Delta^{D}_{\Omega}
,
\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
denote the Laplacian operators
\sum_{i\in[n]}\partial^{2}/\partial x_{i}^{2}
on the respective domain with the respective boundary condition.
For each

, we assume that

is self-adjoint on its domain
\mathcal{D}(h)
,
and
\mathcal{D}(h)
contains

Remark 1
.

It is a non-trivial but well-known fact that for an operator of the form
h\in S_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\cup S^{D}_{\Omega}\cup S_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
, we can define
\mathcal{D}(h)
so that

is self-adjoint. See Theorem X.28 of
[
43
]
for

, Theorem 3.8 of
[

for a bounded

, and the Sobolev space

forms an operator domain on which

is self-adjoint.

It is important to us that each Schrödinger operator in which we are interested has a purely discrete spectrum.
Eigenfunctions of an operator with a purely discrete spectrum form a complete basis set of the Hilbert space.
Furthermore, an eigenfunction of a Schrödinger operator is smooth as a function.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 3.10 of
[
30
]
)
.

Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator with a purely discrete spectrum on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then, the eigenvectors of

form an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 2

(Spectrum of a Schrödinger operator)
.

Suppose
h\in S_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\cup S^{D}_{\Omega}\cup S_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
. Then,
\text{Spec}(h)
is purely discrete, and the eigenvectors form a complete orthogonal basis.

Proof.

By Lemma

has a purely discrete spectrum.
For

, see

Lemma 3

(Smoothness of eigenfunctions)

Let

An eigenfunction of

5
Proof outline and techniques

The analysis achieves two main goals.
First, we want an inverse-polynomial spectral gap in
H_{Q}(t)
throughout
t\in[0,T]
.
Second, we want the lowest eigenvalue of
h

to be close to that of the final qubit Hamiltonian
H_{Q}(T)
.
Once we have established these points, the correctness of the algorithm follows from the Adiabatic Theorem.

In general, proving a lower bound on the spectral gap can be challenging.
In our case, we leverage the Fundamental Gap Theorem, which ensures an inverse polynomial gap for
a Dirichlet Schr̈odinger operator with a convex potential on a bounded convex domain.

We consider the four pairs of domains and Hamiltonians

({\mathbb{R}^{n}},h),\quad(B,h_{B}^{D}),\quad({\mathbb{T}^{n}},h_{{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}),\quad(\mathcal{N}^{n},H_{Q}),

where

is a Euclidean ball in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Each plays a different role in the analysis:

on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is the objective,

on

provides the spectral gap via the Fundamental Gap Theorem,

h_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
on
{\mathbb{T}^{n}}
plays the role of a “gearbox” that connects other Hamiltonians,
and finally
H_{Q}
on the finite set
\mathcal{N}^{n}
is efficiently simulable on a quantum computer.
We connect them through truncation lemmas.

5.2
Low-energy truncation

A disconnection between Theorem
3
and Theorem
4
is that
we need a bound on
\text{gap}(H_{Q}(t))
,
while we have a bound on
\text{gap}(h^{D}_{B})
.

We relate the properties of each Hamiltonian through Truncation Lemmas.
The key observation is that only low‑energy properties need to be preserved; the rest of the Hilbert space can be ignored, even if infinite‑dimensional.
For the adiabatic simulation, it suffices to approximate the first two eigenvalues.
To formalize this, we introduce
(E,\epsilon)
‑truncated domains, capturing the low‑energy subspace up to an error
\epsilon
.

We then prove the Truncation Lemmas showing that
if

(E,\epsilon)
‑truncated domains of two Hamiltonians
admit an isomorphism between them
that approximately preserves energy,
then their first two eigenvalues must be close to each other.
Our truncation framework is tailored to adiabatic optimization and accommodates unbounded operators.

Definition 6

(Truncation, and Equivalence)
.

For
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle i)

\displaystyle\qquad h[\widetilde{\psi}]\ \leq\ h[\psi]+\epsilon\qquad\qquad\text{(energy)}

\displaystyle ii)

\displaystyle\qquad\|\widetilde{\psi}-\psi\|\ \leq\ \epsilon\qquad\qquad\text{(norm)}.

A subspace

is an

-truncated domain
of

if, for every normalized

.

Self-adjoint operators

that are isomorphic via a unitary

\displaystyle\left|h_{a}[\widetilde{\psi}]-h_{b}[U\widetilde{\psi}]\right|\leq\epsilon\qquad\qquad\forall\ \widetilde{\psi}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{a}.

5.3

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(h)\ =\ \min_{\psi_{0},\dots,\psi_{n-1}}\max\{\ \langle\psi|h\psi\rangle\ |\ \|\psi\|=1,\psi\perp\psi_{i}\ \forall i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}\}.

As a corollary, we have a comparison of each eigenvalue of two Schrödinger operators, if their potentials are comparable
[

, Corollary 4.13]
.

Lemma 8

(Potential comparison)
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}\in S^{D}_{\Omega}
with potentials
V_{1},V_{2}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

V_{1}(x)\geq V_{2}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega
.
Then,
\lambda_{k}(h_{1})\geq\lambda_{k}(h_{2})
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

The following Lemma is a standard result in the spectral theory
[
47
]

that says, for any
k
.
k
-th eigenvalue decreases as the domain increases.
Intuitively, one could view the Dirichlet Schrödinger operators on
\Omega
as
a Schrödinger operator on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with a potential
V
such that

V(x)=\infty
for
x\notin\Omega
.

Lemma 9

(Domain monotonicity)
.

Suppose for bounded
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
,

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle:=-\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}+V\in S_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}},

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle:=-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega_{1}}+V\big|_{\Omega_{1}}\in S^{D}_{\Omega_{1}},

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle:=-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega_{2}}+V\big|_{\Omega_{2}}\in S^{D}_{\Omega_{2}}.

Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(h)\leq\lambda_{k}(h_{1})\leq\lambda_{k}(h_{2}).

If
V=0
, then we have the special case of the domain monotonicity on Dirichlet Laplacian:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega_{1}})\leq\lambda_{k}(-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega_{2}}).

The following lemma is what we want to use in the analysis of the main theorem.

Lemma 10
.

Let
h=th_{1}+(1-t)h_{2}
for
t\in[0,1]
be a convex combination
of two Dirichlet Schrödinger operators

h_{1},h_{2}
on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

with smooth potentials
V_{1},V_{2}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Suppose
V_{2}(x)=0
for all

, where
\Omega^{\prime}
is compact.

Then,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(h)\leq\lambda_{1}(-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}})+\max_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}V_{1}(x).

Proof.

We have
h=-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega}+tV_{1}+(1-t)V_{2}
.
By the domain monotonicity (Lemma
9
), we have
\lambda_{1}(h)\leq\lambda_{1}(h^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}})
,
where
h^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}:=-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}+V_{1}\big|_{\Omega^{\prime}}

is a Dirichlet Schrödinger operator
on
\Omega^{\prime}
with the potential given as the restriction of

.

By Lemma

, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(h^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}})\leq\lambda_{1}(-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}+\max_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}V_{1}(x))=\lambda_{1}(-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}})+\max_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}V_{1}(x),

since

at all

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(h)\leq\lambda_{1}(h^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}})\leq\lambda_{1}(-\Delta^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}})+\max_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}V_{1}(x).

Discussion: Weyl convexity

An interesting notion of noncommutative convexity
arises in
h
.
The functional
|\psi\rangle\rightarrow\langle\psi|h\psi\rangle

is convex under the unitaries of translation
T_{a}

and modulation
M_{b}

T_{a}(\psi)(x):=\psi(x+a),\qquad M_{b}(\psi)(x):=e^{ib\cdot x}\,\psi(x),

and more generally,
under the Weyl operators

\displaystyle W(a,b):=e^{\frac{i}{2}a\cdot b}M_{b}T_{a}

that are noncommuting.
This notion is analogous to the fact that
a classical convex function is convex under translation
of a fixed point.

Geodesic convex optimization
[
42
,
52
,
2
,
9
]

also studies convex
objectives in
noncommutative directions,
but our case
differs in that
the orbit
\{W(a,b)\psi\}_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}

of any
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})

is just a small subset of the feasible set;
the envelope
x\rightarrow|\psi(x)|

is preserved up to translation.
Another notion of noncommutative convex optimization
appears in Noncommutative Polynomial Optimization (NcPO)
[
24
,
25
,
8
,
37
]
.
When the potential
V
is a polynomial,
our formulation can be viewed as an
infinite-dimensional instantiation of NcPO.

We formalize the idea mentioned above that
h[\cdot]
is convex under Weyl operators.

Definition 8

(Weyl convexity)
.

A functional
f:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
Weyl-convex
if
for any fixed
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(f)
, the function

\displaystyle(a,b)\rightarrow f(W_{a,b}\psi)

is convex over
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 11
.

Given a Schrödinger operator
h=-\Delta+V
with convex
V:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
the energy functional
h[\psi]
is Weyl-convex.

Proof.

Let
p:=-i\nabla
, so
-\Delta=p^{2}
. The conjugations are well-known to be

T_{a}^{\dagger}p\,T_{a}=p,\quad T_{a}^{\dagger}V(x)\,T_{a}=V(x-a),\qquad M_{b}^{\dagger}p\,M_{b}=p+b,\quad M_{b}^{\dagger}V(x)\,M_{b}=V(x).

Hence

W_{a,b}^{\dagger}\,p^{2}\,W_{a,b}=(p+b)^{2}.

Since
W_{a,b}
is unitary,
\|W_{a,b}\psi\|=\|\psi\|
, and therefore

h[W_{a,b}\psi]=\frac{\langle\psi|((p+b)^{2}\psi\rangle+\langle T_{a}\psi|V|T_{a}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}.

Set

\bar{p}:=\left(\frac{\langle\psi|p_{j}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\right)_{j\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad K:=-\frac{\langle\psi|\Delta\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}<\infty.

Expanding gives

h[W(a,b)\psi]=\underbrace{\big(\|b\|^{2}+2\,b\cdot\bar{p}+K\big)}_{=:q(b)}\;+\;\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}V(x-a)\,|\psi(x)|^{2}\ \text{d}x}_{=:G(a)}.

The function
q(b)
has Hessian
2I_{n}\succeq 0
, hence is (strictly) convex in
b
.
For each fixed
x
, the map
a\mapsto V(x-a)
is convex;
integrating convex functions preserves convexity, so
G(a)
is convex in
a
.
Therefore
h[W(a,b)\psi]=q(b)+G(a)
is jointly convex in
(a,b)
.
∎

8
Future directions

Complexity theory.

The results on the complexity of the Schrödinger operators are sparse.
As far as we are aware, Zheng et al.
[
53
]
is the only work on the topic.
They prove that the Schrödinger operator is StoqMA-hard for general smooth potential on a bounded Dirichlet domain,
where simulation of Schrödinger operator is BQP-hard.
Is Schrödinger operators with convex potential BQP-complete?
What is the complexity of Schrödinger operators with the fermionic symmetry?
What is the complexity of finding the ground energy of a molecule?
What is the complexity of the convex drum problem?

Optimization of Weyl-convex objectives.

Let us denote
\widehat{x}:=(\widehat{x}_{1},\dots,\widehat{x}_{n})
, where
\widehat{x}_{i}
is the multiplicative operator associated with the coordinate function
x_{i}
in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Similarly, let us denote
\widehat{p}:=(\widehat{p}_{1},\dots,\widehat{p}_{n})
, where
\widehat{p}_{i}:=U_{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}_{i}U_{\mathcal{F}}
for the Fourier transformation unitary
U_{\mathcal{F}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

In this paper, we gave a rudimentary algorithm for computing

\displaystyle\min_{\|\psi\|=1}\langle\psi|\ \|\widehat{p}\|^{2}+C(\widehat{x})\ |\psi\rangle,

for a convex
C
. A natural extension is to find an algorithm for

\displaystyle\min_{\|\psi\|=1}\langle\psi|\ C_{1}(\widehat{p})+C_{2}(\widehat{x})\ |\psi\rangle,

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are convex. A step further is to consider

\displaystyle\min_{\|\psi\|=1}\langle\psi|\ C_{3}(\widehat{p},\widehat{x})\ |\psi\rangle,

for a Weyl-convex
C_{3}
.

A more ambitious goal is to construct a quantum theory that parallels classical mathematical optimization.
For instance, we can aim to solve constraint problems

\displaystyle\min_{\|\psi\|=1}

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\ C(\widehat{p},\widehat{x})\ |\psi\rangle,

subject to

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\ D_{i}(\widehat{p},\widehat{x})\ |\psi\rangle\leq a_{i}\quad\forall i\in[m].

that hopefully are as useful and versatile as classical optimization programming, such as linear and semidefinite programming.

Calculus of variations.

The optimization problems we solve in this paper are
instantiations of the calculus of variations
[
29
]
.
We propose the calculus of variations as a venue for
exponential quantum speedups.

A typical problem is

\displaystyle\text{minimize}\qquad J[u]

\displaystyle:=\int_{x\in\Omega}L(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\ \text{d}x

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\qquad 0

\displaystyle\ =\int_{x\in\Omega}C_{i}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\ \text{d}x\qquad\forall i\in[m],

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle:\ \Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

where
L,C_{i}:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

The problem is a good candidate for an exponential quantum advantage,
since classically solving it, for example via finite element method, requires
exponential time in
n
.
A quantum computer has an advantage in that
the exponentially large data
u
can be efficiently
stored and manipulated in a number of qubits increasing polynomially
as the dimension
n
grows.

Alone classical, together quantum.

The idea refers to the fact that two classically minimizable functionals,
namely the kinetic and momentum terms,
are added to give a quantum objective that
is efficiently optimized by a quantum algorithm.

Does this phenomenon appear in a more general setting?
Can we add more than two classical functionals to
get an optimizable quantum Hamiltonian?
Can we add two optimizable qubit Hamiltonians in the
X
and
Z

basis to get an optimizable Hamiltonian?
Can we add classically approximateable Hamiltonians
to get a quantum approximable Hamiltonian?
Is there a unified framework to solve such problems?

For a concrete example, the Quantum Max Cut
[
21
]
Hamiltonian
\sum_{ij\in E}I-X_{i}X_{j}-Y_{i}Y_{j}-Z_{i}Z_{j}

is the summation of the Max Cut Hamiltonians in the
X,Y,Z
basis,
each of which can be optimally approximated by
the Goemans-Williamson algorithm
[
22
]
.

Dissipation vs. Adiabatic evolution.

We observe that efficient quantum optimization algorithms requiring
a quantum verifier
roughly falls under two categories: dissipation
[
13
,
18
]
,
and adiabatic evolution
[
39
]
.

We conjecture there is a unified framework encompassing both.
Our algorithm solves the Schrödinger problem via adiabatic evolution,
but intuitively, a dissipation-based algorithm makes more sense
for the problem;
it is more natural to imagine a particle in nature
minimizes its kinetic energy via dissipation, rather than by some
adiabatic process.
Furthermore,
[
11
]
devices a time-dependent Hamiltonian
in order to simulate quantum friction, showing that
friction and adiabatic evolution are interchangeable.

Now we prove the energy condition.
Since

on

\displaystyle=\int_{B}2\text{Re}(\overline{\psi^{\downarrow}}\nabla\psi^{\downarrow})\cdot\nabla f^{2}+|\psi^{\downarrow}|^{2}\Delta f^{2}\;\text{d}^{n}x.

Since
f
is 0 outside the ball,

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\ \ \text{Re}(\overline{\psi^{\downarrow}}\nabla\psi^{\downarrow})\cdot\nabla f^{2}\ \text{d}x

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle-\int_{B}\text{Re}(\overline{\psi^{\downarrow}}\nabla\psi^{\downarrow})\cdot\nabla f^{2}\ \text{d}x

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{B}|\psi^{\downarrow}|^{2}\Delta f^{2}\;\text{d}x

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\ \ |\psi^{\downarrow}|^{2}\Delta f^{2}\;\text{d}x\ =\ \langle\psi^{\downarrow}|\Delta f^{2}|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle/2.

(31)

Since

always gives a real number, we have

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \|f\psi^{\downarrow}\|\ h[f\psi^{\downarrow}]

Lemma 17

(Frequency truncation on torus)
.

\displaystyle\ \Omega\left(\frac{L(E+V_{\max})^{3/2})}{\epsilon}\right).

Proof.

Let

be a normalized vector with energy

.
We show that there exists an

-truncation of

in

\displaystyle E\geq\langle\psi|(-\Delta)\psi\rangle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\frac{4\pi^{2}\|k\|_{2}^{2}\widehat{\psi}_{k}}{L^{2}}\langle\psi|\omega_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\frac{4\pi^{2}\|k\|^{2}}{L^{2}}|\widehat{\psi}_{k}|^{2}.

(40)

Given the parameter
K

\displaystyle\psi^{\downarrow}\

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\|k\|\leq K}\widehat{\psi}_{k}\omega_{k}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{T}^{n},f},

\displaystyle\psi^{\uparrow}\

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\|k\|>K}\widehat{\psi}_{k}\omega_{k}=\psi-\psi^{\downarrow}.

\epsilon
-truncation of

For the norm condition:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\psi^{\downarrow}}{\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|}-\psi\right\|

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \left\|\frac{\psi^{\downarrow}}{\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|}-\psi^{\downarrow}\right\|+\|\psi^{\downarrow}-\psi\|

\displaystyle\ =\ \left|\|\psi\|-{\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|}\right|+\|\psi^{\downarrow}-\psi\|

(42)

We choose

.

-truncation, we first bound the Laplacian term.

By linearity and norm considerations, we have

\displaystyle\ =\ |\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|V|{\psi}^{\downarrow}\rangle-(\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|+\langle\psi^{\uparrow}|)V(|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle+|\psi^{\uparrow}\rangle)|

\displaystyle\ =\ |-\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|V|{\psi}^{\uparrow}\rangle-\langle\psi^{\uparrow}|V|{\psi}^{\downarrow}\rangle-\langle\psi^{\uparrow}|V|{\psi}^{\uparrow}\rangle|

\displaystyle\ \leq\ V_{\max}(\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|\|\psi^{\uparrow}\|+\|\psi^{\uparrow}\|\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|+\|\psi^{\uparrow}\|\|\psi^{\uparrow}\|)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ V_{\max}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2}).

(44)

Combining (
43
) and (

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|h|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle-\langle\psi|h|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\ =\ \langle\psi^{\downarrow}|(-\Delta)|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle-\langle\psi|(-\Delta)|\psi\rangle+\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|V|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle-\langle\psi|V|\psi\rangle

By the fact that

\displaystyle h[\psi^{\downarrow}]\ =\ \frac{\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|h|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle}{\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \frac{h[\psi]+V_{\max}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2})}{\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \frac{h[\psi]+V_{\max}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2})}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ =\ h[\psi]+\frac{h[\psi](2\sigma-\sigma^{2})+V_{\max}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2})}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ h[\psi]+\frac{E(2\sigma-\sigma^{2})+V_{\max}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2})}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ \leq\ h[\psi]+\frac{2\sigma+\sigma^{2}}{(1-\sigma)^{2}}(E+V_{\max})

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \epsilon,

where the last line is by our choice

.
By (

),
we have

.
Therefore, we have that

is an
(E,\epsilon)
-truncated domain, when we have

\displaystyle K=\Omega\left(\frac{L(E+V_{\max})^{3/2})}{\epsilon}\right).

Lemma 20
.

Let

Then we have

\displaystyle\log_{\partial}(cf,\mathbb{T}^{n},m)

\displaystyle\log_{\partial}(\sum_{i\in[r]}f_{i},\mathbb{T}^{n},m)

The proof is by manipulating the maximums:

\displaystyle\log_{\partial}(cf,\mathbb{T}^{n},m)=

\displaystyle\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log c|\partial^{l}_{j}f(x)|)^{+}}{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log c+\log|\partial^{l}_{j}f(x)|)^{+}}{l}

\displaystyle(\log c)^{+}+\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log|\partial^{l}_{j}f(x)|)^{+}}{l}=(\log c)^{+}+\log_{\partial}(f,\mathbb{T}^{n},m),

\displaystyle\log_{\partial}(\sum_{k\in[r]}f_{k},\mathbb{T}^{n},m)\ =\

\displaystyle\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log|\sum_{k\in[r]}\partial^{l}_{j}f_{k}(x)|)^{+}}{l}

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log(\sum_{k\in[r]}|\partial^{l}_{j}f_{k}(x)|)^{+}}{l}

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log(r\max_{k\in[r]}|\partial^{l}_{j}f_{k}(x)|))^{+}}{l}

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\max_{l\in[m],j\in[n],x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{(\log(\max_{k\in[r]}|\partial^{l}_{j}f_{k}(x)|))^{+}+\log r}{l}

\displaystyle\ \leq\

∎

Lemma 21
.

Let

Faà di Bruno’s formula gives
\ \forall x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
\forall l\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\partial_{j}^{l}f(g(x))

\displaystyle=\sum{\frac{l!}{a_{1}!\,a_{2}!\,\cdots\,a_{l}!}}\cdot f^{(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{l})}(g(x))\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{l}\left({\frac{\partial_{j}^{t}g(x)}{t!}}\right)^{a_{t}},

such

The second factor is at most

By taking the log of both sides,

\displaystyle\log|\partial^{l}_{j}f(g(x))|

\displaystyle\leq 2l\log l+\log\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n}),\ l^{\prime}\in[l]\end{subarray}}|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|+\sum_{t=1}^{m}\log|\partial_{j}^{t}g(x)|^{a_{t}}

\displaystyle=2l\log l+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n}),\ l^{\prime}\in[l]\end{subarray}}\log|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|+\sum_{t=1}^{m}\log|\partial_{j}^{t}g(x)|^{a_{t}}.

(48)

\displaystyle\ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n}),\ l^{\prime}\in[l]\end{subarray}}\log|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|\ \leq

\displaystyle\ \max_{\ l^{\prime}\in[l]}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n})\end{subarray}}(\log|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|)^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \max_{l^{\prime}\in[l]}\ l^{\prime}\ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n})\end{subarray}}\frac{(\log|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|)^{+}}{l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \left(\max_{l^{\prime\prime}\in[l]}\ l^{\prime\prime}\right)\ \left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n}),\ l^{\prime}\in[l]\end{subarray}}\frac{(\log|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|)^{+}}{l^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ l\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in g(\mathbb{T}^{n}),\ l^{\prime}\in[l]\end{subarray}}\frac{(\log|f^{(l^{\prime})}(y)|)^{+}}{l^{\prime}}

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ l\log_{\partial}(f,g(\mathbb{T}^{n}),l).

(50)

Since

\displaystyle\ \sum_{t=1}^{l}\log|\partial_{j}^{t}g(x)|^{a_{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ l\sum_{t=1}^{l}\frac{ta_{t}}{l}\frac{\log|\partial_{j}^{t}g(x)|}{t}

\displaystyle\ l\max_{t\in[l]}\frac{\log|\partial_{j}^{t}g(x)|}{t}

\displaystyle\ l\max_{t\in[l],y\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\frac{\log|\partial_{j}^{t}g(y)|}{t}\ \leq\ l\log_{\partial}(g,\mathbb{T}^{n},l).

By inserting (

The inequality still holds if we take the maximum over

Lemma 22
.

We have

\displaystyle\log_{\partial}(\text{Bump},\mathbb{R},m)

\displaystyle\leq\log O(m^{4}),

\displaystyle\log_{\partial}(\text{Sat}_{\alpha,\beta}\mathbb{R},m),\log_{\partial}(\text{Cut}_{\alpha,\beta},\mathbb{R},m),\log_{\partial}(\text{Bar}_{\beta},\mathbb{R},m)

The following is a well-known smooth function:

\ 0&x\leq 0.\end{cases}

We use the fact that

j\end{pmatrix}f^{(j)}g^{(m-j)}

j\end{pmatrix}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{0}^{(i)}(x)|\ \max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{0}^{(m-i)}(x)|.

We are left with upper bounding

We prove by induction that there exists a polynomial such that

We showed above the statement for

Suppose the hypothesis is true for

. Then for

\displaystyle f^{(j+1)}_{0}(x)=\frac{dt}{dx}\frac{d}{dt}(p_{j}(t)e^{-t})=-t^{2}\left(p_{j}^{\prime}(t)-p_{j}(t)\right)e^{-t}:=p_{j+1}(t)e^{-t}.

Therefore, the induction hypothesis is true for all

.

The maximum of

(j/e)^{j}
at the maximizer

. Observe that this quantity increases as a function of

for

For an arbitrary polynomial

define sum of the absolute values of its coefficients

\displaystyle\max_{t>0}|p_{j}(t)e^{-t}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\deg p_{j}}|a_{j}|(j/e)^{j}\leq\|p\|_{c}(\deg p/e)^{\deg p}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\max_{t>0}\left|f_{0}^{(j)}(t)\right|\leq\|p_{j}\|\left(\frac{\deg p_{j}}{e}\right)^{\deg p_{j}}.

From the recursive formula

is without a constant term, for all

From the recursive formula, we also have

\displaystyle\|p_{j}\|_{c}

\displaystyle\leq\|t^{2}p_{j}\|_{c}+\|t^{2}p^{\prime}_{j}\|_{c}

\displaystyle\leq\|p_{j-1}\|+2(j-1)\|p_{j-1}\|

\displaystyle=(2j-1)\|p_{j-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq(2j-1)(2j-3)\cdots 3\cdot 1\cdot\|p_{1}\|

Suppose
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
has energy
h[\psi]\leq E
.
We define

\displaystyle\psi^{\downarrow}:=\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}\leq E/\mu^{2}}c_{i}\psi_{i},

where
\psi_{i}
is the
i
-th eigenvectors of
h
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
We define sets

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=V^{-1}([0,E/\mu^{6}]),

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=V^{-1}([0,2E/\mu^{6}]).

Then we have
A\subset B\subset\Omega^{\prime}
, because of (
56
).

The cutoff function
f:=\text{Cut}_{E/\mu^{6},E/\mu^{6}}\circ V

satisfies that

\displaystyle f(x)\begin{cases}=1&x\in A\\
\in[0,1]&x\in B\setminus A\\
=0&x\notin B.\end{cases}

We claim that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}:=\frac{f\psi^{\downarrow}}{\|f\psi^{\downarrow}\|}

is an
\mu
-truncation of
\psi
.
The claim implies the lemma,
since
\widetilde{\psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
.

We first show the norm condition of
\mu
-truncation.
Since
V(x)\geq E/\mu^{6}
for all
x
such that
f(x)=0
, by Markov’s inequality (see the proof of Lemma
15
), we have

\displaystyle\|\psi^{\downarrow}-f\psi^{\downarrow}\|\leq\mu^{3}\|\psi^{\downarrow}\|.

By the same derivation that led to (
30
), we have

\displaystyle\|\psi-\widetilde{\psi}\|\leq O(\mu)\leq O(\epsilon_{0}).

For the rest of the proof, we show the energy condition.
By the same calculation that led to (
33
), we have

\displaystyle\|f\psi^{\downarrow}\|h[f\psi^{\downarrow}\|=\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|\|\ \nabla f\|^{2}|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle+\text{Re}\langle f^{2}\psi^{\downarrow}|h\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle.

We upper bound the two terms similarly to the proof of Lemma
16
.
We have

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\downarrow}|\|\ \nabla f\|^{2}|\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|\psi^{\downarrow}(x)|^{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\ \text{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \max_{x\notin A}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\int_{x\notin A}|\psi^{\downarrow}(x)|^{2}\ \text{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \max_{x\notin A}|\text{Cut}^{\prime}_{E/\mu^{6},E/\mu^{6}}(V(x))|^{2}\ \|\nabla V(x)\|^{2}\mu^{6}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ O\left(\frac{\mu^{12}}{E^{2}}\cdot\frac{m^{2}E^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\mu^{12}}\cdot\mu^{6}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ O\left(\frac{m^{2}n^{4}\mu^{6}}{\epsilon_{0}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ O(\epsilon^{4}/E^{4}).

Also, by the calculation that led to (
39
), we have

\displaystyle\text{Re}\langle f^{2}\psi^{\downarrow}|h\psi^{\downarrow}\rangle\leq h[\psi]+\mu E\leq h[\psi]+\epsilon_{0}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|f\psi^{\downarrow}\|h[f\psi^{\downarrow}\|\leq h[\psi]+O(\epsilon_{0})+O(\epsilon_{0}^{4}E^{4}).

Finally, since

\displaystyle\|f\psi^{\downarrow}\|\ \geq\ \|\psi^{\downarrow}\|-\|(1-f)\psi^{\downarrow}\|\ \geq\ 1-\mu-\mu^{3},

we have

\displaystyle h[\widetilde{\psi}]=h[f\psi^{\downarrow}]\leq\frac{h[\psi]+O(\epsilon_{0})+O(\epsilon_{0}^{4}E^{4})}{1-\mu-\mu^{3}}\leq h[\psi]+O(\epsilon_{0}).
