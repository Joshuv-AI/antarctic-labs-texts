Abstract
. An algebraic interpretation of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials (MVOPs) is provided. 1.
Introduction

The theory of matrix valued orthogonal polynomials (MVOPs for short) was introduced by M.G. Krein in 1949
[
23
]
. Since then, numerous theoretical advancements have been made, closely following the development of scalar-valued orthogonal polynomials. A comprehensive overview of these developments can be found in
[
6
]
. In a manner similar to scalar orthogonal polynomials, there is considerable interest in studying families of MVOPs that also exhibit the additional property of being eigenfunctions of second-order differential operators
[
11
,
10
,
16
,
18
]
, difference operators
[
3
,
9
,
12
]
, or
q
-difference operators
[
1
,
2
]
. One method for constructing such examples is by utilizing the representation theory of compact Lie groups and harmonic analysis on compact symmetric spaces. In this context, scalar-valued polynomials are known to arise as matrix coefficients or spherical functions. The spherical function approach has been extended to matrix analogues and successfully applied in works such as
[
16
,
21
,
22
,
17
]
among others. Despite these advancements, there are still relatively few concrete examples of MVOPs, particularly those that satisfy a difference equation. More specifically, our goal is to identify examples of MVOPs along with their recurrence relations, orthogonality relations, and, when applicable, their corresponding differential or difference equations.

The goal of this article is to provide an algebraic construction for MVOPs as transition coefficients between two specified bases. We adopt the point of view introduced in
[
15
]
and much developed by one of us often in collaboration with Zhedanov, see in particular the reviews
[
14
]
,
[
7
]
. Specifically, an approach for providing an algebraic interpretation of the polynomial families in the Askey scheme involves the algebra generated by two operators: the multiplication by the operator multiplication by the variable and the

(q)
-difference or differential operator. Depending on the case, the resulting algebra can either be a Lie algebra (or its
q
deformation), or a more general quadratic algebra (such as Hahn or Racah) which are specializations of the Askey-Wilson algebra.

In
[
15
,
13
]
, scalar valued orthogonal polynomials are obtained as transition coefficients between two eigenbases of two self-adjoint operators
H
and
P
inside a Lie algebra. If
H
admits a discrete spectrum and
P
acts tridiagonally on the eigenbasis of
H
then the transition coefficients between both eigenbases can be seen to be orthogonal polynomials with the eigenvalues of
P
as variables. This method allows for the uncovering of orthogonality relations, three-term recurrence relations, and differential or difference equations satisfied by the polynomials. This known interpretation for scalar orthogonal polynomials, represents a new and intriguing research direction for MVOPs.

To apply such a construction, a natural framework is
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
modules that admits a matrix valued inner product. More precisely, we look at representations of a Lie (and
q
-deformed) algebra
\mathfrak{g}
into the algebra
\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(M)
of
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-linear maps over a left
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
M
, i.e. Lie algebra morphisms between
\mathfrak{g}
and
\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(M)
. Similarly as in the scalar case, we have two self-adjoint operators
H
and
P
with
P
acting tridiagonally on the eigenbasis of
H
, then MVOPs will be obtained as transition coefficients between both eigenbases.

In the recent preprint
[
20
]
, the authors emphasized that naturally defined MVOP are close to their scalar valued prototypes. In some sense the present work follows the same philosophy since families of MVOP are obtained naturally from the algebra related to families of scalar valued polynomials and their representations. More precisely, since
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is Morita equivalent to
{\mathbb{C}}
there is an equivalence of categories between
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
modules and complex vector spaces. Thus starting from a complex representation of our algebra
\mathfrak{g}
we get a representation in
\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V)
.
Using this equivalence, we can unfold some properties of MVOPs starting from the scalar valued case.

In Section
2
we present the necessary background for the construction, i.e. Hermitian modules and Morita equivalence. Section
3
introduces the general construction for MVOPs seen as transition coefficients. In the last part of the article, we use this algebraic presentation to construct some families of discrete MVOPs together with their orthogonality, recurrence relation and difference equation. This goal is achieved in Section
4
to
6
, using three different algebras:
\mathfrak{su}(2)
,
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
and
\mathfrak{so}_{q}(3)
. This leads to the construction of, respectively, Krawtchouk, Meixner and Discrete Chebyshev matrix analogues.

1.1.
Notations

In all the paper we use
A^{*}
to denote the transpose conjugate of a square matrix
A
. For a symmetric matrix
A
, we write
A\geq 0
for non negative matrices and
A>0
for positive definite ones.

2.
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module

2.1.
Morita equivalence

In this subsection we discuss
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules (thought as left modules) with matrix-valued inner products, that will be called Hermitian modules. First, it is a fact that the algebras
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
{\mathbb{C}}
are Morita equivalent, see
[
24
]
, i.e. their respective module categories
\mathfrak{M}_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
are equivalent. The functors realizing this equivalence are given by

F:\mathfrak{M}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{C})}\to\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{C}},\qquad G:\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathfrak{M}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{C})},

on the objects, the functors are defined by

G(V)=V^{n},\qquad F(M)=e_{11}M,

where
e_{ij}
denote the elementary matrices. Here
V^{n}
is a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module, the action being given, for
A\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, by:

(2.1)

A\cdot(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})=\left(\sum_{k}a_{1k}v_{k},\ldots,\sum_{n}a_{nk}v_{k}\right).

On the morphisms, the functors are defined by

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle:V\to V^{\prime},\quad G(\alpha):G(V)\to G(V^{\prime}),\quad G(\alpha)(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})=(\alpha v_{1},\ldots,\alpha v_{n}),

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle:M\to M^{\prime},\quad F(\beta):F(M)\to F(M^{\prime}),\quad F(\beta)(e_{11}m)=e_{11}\beta(m).

We now introduce introduce Hermitian
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules. This definition is adapted to our purpose from the one in
[
4
, Dfn II.7.1.1]
for Hilbert modules.

Definition 2.1
.

A Hermitian
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
M
is a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module together with a matrix-valued inner product, i.e. a map
(\cdot,\cdot):M\times M\to M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
such that:

(1)

For all
A\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
,
(A\cdot m_{1},m_{2})=A(m_{1},m_{2})
,

(2)

(m_{1},m_{2})=(m_{2},m_{1})^{*}
,

(3)

(m,m)\geq 0
,

(4)

If
(m,m)=0
then
m=0
(we say that the inner product is non degenerate).

In general the non degeneracy condition (4) can be omitted but in our case every inner product will satisfy it. Another remark is that as a direct consequence of the definition we get

(m_{1},A\cdot m_{2})=(m_{1},m_{2})A^{*}.

We will now explicitly extend the Morita equivalence from Hermitian spaces
V
to Hermitian
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules
V^{n}
. Let
(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a Hermitian space and endow the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
G(V)=V^{n}
with the matrix-valued inner product
G(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)=(\cdot,\cdot)_{G}
defined by

(2.2)

\left((v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\right)_{G}=(\langle v_{i},w_{j}\rangle)\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}}).

The first two properties of matrix-valued inner products are routine to check. For the third one, we observe that the matrix
(v,v)
is the Gram matrix of the family
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
which is known to be non-negative. The inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{G}
is non degenerate which is easily seen by looking at the diagonal coefficients.

In the other direction, starting with a Hermitian
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
(V^{n},(\cdot,\cdot))
, we endow
F\circ V^{n}
with the inner product
F((\cdot,\cdot))=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
defined by

\langle v,w\rangle_{F}=((v,0,\ldots,0),(w,0,\ldots,0))_{1,1}.

We have the following

((v,0,\ldots,0),(w,0,\ldots,0))=(e_{11}(v,0,\ldots,0),e_{11}(w,0,\ldots,0))=e_{11}((v,0,\ldots,0),(w,0,\ldots,0))e_{11},

so that the only non zero coefficient in
((v,0,\ldots,0),(w,0,\ldots,0))
is the upper left one. This implies that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
is indeed a non degenerate inner product on
V
.
We have

G\circ F((\cdot,\cdot))=(\cdot,\cdot),\quad F\circ G(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle.

The second equation is direct from the definitions. Regarding the first one, introduce
\sigma_{1j}
to be the permutation matrix associated with the transposition
(1,j)
we have

((v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}))_{ij}=\sum_{k,\ell}\left(\sigma_{1k}((v_{k},0,\ldots,0),(w_{\ell},0,\ldots,0))\sigma_{1\ell}\right)_{ij}=((v_{i},0,\ldots,0),(w_{j},0,\ldots,0))_{11}.

It is then showed to be equal to
G\circ F((\cdot,\cdot))((v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}))_{ij}
.

As a summary of this discussion we showed a “unitary” Morita equivalence (the morphisms of the module categories being the same)

G:(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)\mapsto(V^{n},(\cdot,\cdot)_{G}),\quad F:(V^{n},(\cdot,\cdot))\mapsto(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}).

To conclude this discussion on the Morita equivalence we define the adjunction on
\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V^{n})
in a natural way .

Definition 2.2
.

For an operator
T\in\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V^{n})
, we define the adjoint of
T
with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
by the property

(T\cdot m_{1},m_{2})=(m_{1},T^{\dagger}\cdot m_{2}).

Moreover, we say that
T
is self-adjoint if
T^{\dagger}=T
.

We will abuse notations and use also
†
for the usual adjoint on Hermitian spaces. The existence of the adjoint is proved through the operator
G\circ(F\circ T)^{\dagger}
, and the uniqueness is then a consequence of the Morita equivalence. Using the definitions one proves the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

The functors
G
and
F
preserve adjoints, i.e.

(G\circ T)^{\dagger}=G\circ T^{\dagger},\qquad(F\circ T)^{\dagger}=F\circ T^{\dagger}.

Proof.

This is based on the following for
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
and
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})

(G\circ T\cdot v,w)_{i,j}=(\langle T\cdot v_{i},w_{j}\rangle)=(\langle v_{i},T^{\dagger}w_{j}\rangle)=(v,G\circ T^{\dagger}w)_{i,j}.

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

As a consequence, self-adjoint operators are send to self-adjoint operators. The same is true for unitary operators, defined in a natural way for Hermitian
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules.

2.2.
Free modules

In Section 3 our focus will be on free modules and according to the previous discussion they are of the form
V^{n}
. Free modules admits bases that will be called
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases to avoid confusion with vector spaces bases on
V^{n}
. For the ring
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
all bases have the same cardinal called the rank of
V^{n}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
V^{n}
is a free module of
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
e_{i}=(e_{1}^{i},\ldots,e_{n}^{i})
is a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
then the family
(e_{i}^{j})_{i,j}
is a basis of
V
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of equation (
2.1
). Indeed, any element in
V^{n}
can be written in a unique way as

\sum_{i}A_{i}\cdot e_{i}=\left(\sum_{i,k}a^{i}_{1,k}e^{i}_{k},\ldots,\sum_{i,k}a^{i}_{n,k}e^{i}_{k}\right).

Thus any
v\in V
can be written in a unique way as a linear combination of the
e_{k}^{i}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next corollary gives a characterization of free module of finite rank.

Corollary 2.5
.

A
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
V^{n}
is a free module of finite rank if and only if
n
divides
\dim V
.

Proof.

Assume
V^{n}
is a free
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
module of finite rank. The previous Lemma proves that
n
divides
\dim V
. More precisely,
n\rank(M)=\dim V
. Conversely, if
n
divides
\dim V
. Let
|k\rangle
be a basis of
V
. Then the family

(2.3)

e_{i}=(|ni\rangle,\ldots,|ni+n-1\rangle),

is a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
as seen using equation (
2.1
).
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that if
V^{n}
is not of finite rank but of countable rank then the family (
2.3
) is still a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
. Thus for free module of countable rank there is a correspondence between
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
and basis of
V
. However, it is not one-to-one since two bases of
V
can lead to two different
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases of
V^{n}
for example by permuting the components of the generators
e_{i}
.

We now assume that the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules are all endowed with a matrix-valued inner product. We are then naturally interested in orthogonal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis
(e_{k})_{k}
which satisfies
(e_{k},e_{\ell})=0
for
k\neq\ell
, and in orthonormal basis which moreover satisfies
(e_{k},e_{k})=I_{n}
. It is not true that an orthogonal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis
e_{i}
of
V^{n}
will lead to an orthogonal basis for
V
. It is true if and only if the inner product
(e_{k},e_{k})
is a diagonal matrix. However, orthonormal bases are preserved via Lemma
2.4
.

Corollary 2.7
.

If
V^{n}
is a free Hermitian module of countable rank then
e_{i}=(e_{1}^{i},\ldots,e_{n}^{i})
is an orthonormal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
if and only if the family
(e_{i}^{j})_{i,j}
is an orthonormal basis of
V
.

A last point in this section is to relate eigenbases of operators on
V
to
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbases of morphisms on
V^{n}
. The following definition makes explicit classical notions in the context of
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
V^{n}
be a free
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module and
T\in\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V^{n})
. A family
(e_{i})_{i}
is a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis of
T
if

T\cdot e_{i}=\Lambda_{i}e_{i},

with
\Lambda_{i}\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, and
(e_{i})_{i}
is an
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
. We also say
T
is diagonalisable when
T
admits an eigenbasis.

The next lemma relates diagonalisability on
V
and on
V^{n}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
V^{n}
be a free module of countable rank.
H\in\End_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
is diagonalisable if and only if
G\circ H\in\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V^{n})
is diagonalisable with diagonalisable eigenvalues
\Lambda_{i}
.

Proof.

On one hand, if
H
is diagonalisable with eigenvectors
\mid{i}\,\rangle
then the basis
e_{i}=(\mid{ni}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{ni+n-1}\,\rangle)
is a diagonal eigenbasis for
G\circ H
. Moreover the eigenvalues
\Lambda_{i}
associated to
e_{i}
are diagonal matrices.

On the other hand, if
G\circ H
is diagonalisable with
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis and
\Lambda_{i}
is diagonalized by
P_{i}^{-1}D_{i}P_{i}
then

G\circ H(P_{i}e_{i})=D_{i}\left(P_{i}e_{i}\right).

Since
P_{i}
is invertible the family
(P_{i}e_{i})_{i}
is a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis of
G\circ H
with diagonal eigenvalue
D_{i}
. The coordinates of this
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis form a basis of
V
which is an eigenbasis for
H
.
∎

The following corollary is a generalization of the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
V^{n}
be a free Hermitian module of countable rank. Every self-adjoint operators for
V^{n}
admits an orthonormal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis.

Proof.

If
T
is a self-adjoint operator then so is
F\circ T
, thus it admits an eigenbasis
\mid{k}\,\rangle
of
V
made of orthonormal generators.
Then the family
e_{i}=(\mid{ni}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{ni+n-1}\,\rangle)
is an orthonormal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis.
∎

3.
Algebraic interpretation for MVOPs

In this section we explain how one can recover MVOPs as transition coefficients for representations of algebras over Hermitian
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a (
q
-deformed) Lie algebra, and
(V,\rho)
be a unitary representation of
\mathfrak{g}
. Then we can create a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V^{n})
using the following commutative diagram.

\textstyle{\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(V^{n})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{F}
\textstyle{\End_{\mathbb{C}}(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{G}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\mathfrak{g}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tau}
\scriptstyle{\rho}

Let
H
be an element of
\mathfrak{g}
which admits an orthonormal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis
(e_{k})_{k}
with eigenvalues
\Lambda_{k}\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
:

H\cdot e_{k}=\Lambda_{k}e_{k},

and
P
an operator, which acts tridiagonally on the basis
(e_{k})_{k}
and is self-adjoint. This operator can be described as in the following Proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(e_{k})_{k}
be a orthogonal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
and
P\in\End_{M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}(M)
such that

(3.1)

P\cdot e_{k}=A_{k+1}^{\ast}e_{k+1}+B_{k}e_{k}+A_{k}e_{k-1},

where
B_{k}=B_{k}^{\ast}.
Then
P
is self-adjoint with respect to the matrix valued inner product (
2.2
).
Conversely, if
P
is self-adjoint and acts tridiagonally on the basis
(e_{k})_{k}
then
P
is described by formula (
3.1
).

Proof.

The proof follows verifying that
(P\cdot e_{k},e_{\ell})=(e_{k},P\cdot e_{\ell})
for all
k,\ell.
On one hand, we have

(P\cdot e_{k},e_{\ell})=(A_{k+1}^{\ast}e_{k+1}+B_{k}e_{k}+A_{k}e_{k-1},e_{\ell})=A_{k+1}^{\ast}(e_{k+1},e_{\ell})+B_{k}(e_{k},e_{\ell})+A_{k}(e_{k-1},e_{\ell}).

On the other hand, we have

(e_{k},P\cdot e_{\ell})=(e_{k},A_{\ell+1}^{\ast}e_{\ell+1}+B_{\ell}e_{\ell}+A_{\ell}e_{\ell-1})=(e_{k},e_{\ell+1})A_{\ell+1}+B_{k}^{\ast}(e_{k},e_{\ell})+(e_{k},e_{\ell-1})A_{\ell}^{\ast}

Since
(e_{k})_{k}
is an orthogonal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis of
V^{n}
, and
B_{k}=B_{k}^{\ast}
we get the desired result.

The converse is obtained starting from a tridiagonal action
P\cdot e_{k}=\alpha_{k}e_{k+1}+\beta_{k}e_{k}+\gamma_{k}e_{k-1}
and using that
P
is self-adjoint.
∎

According to Corollary
2.10
, the operator
P
admits an orthonormal
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-eigenbasis
(\phi_{j})_{j}
with eigenvalues
\alpha_{j}\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
:

P\cdot\phi_{j}=\alpha_{j}\phi_{j}.

Now consider the transition coefficients
(e_{k},\phi_{j})
, and define the family
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
where
\theta_{j}=(e_{0},\phi_{j}){\alpha}_{j}^{*}(e_{0},\phi_{j})^{-1}
by

(3.2)

(e_{k},\phi_{j})=\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})(e_{0},\phi_{j}),

which is possible as soon as the matrix
(e_{0},\phi_{j})
is invertible. We will assume this is the case in the rest of the section. The family
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
satisfies the following three terms recurrence relation:

A_{k+1}^{*}\Pi_{k+1}(\theta_{j})+B_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})+A_{k}\Pi_{k-1}(\theta_{j})=\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})\theta_{j},\qquad\Pi_{-1}(\theta_{j})=0,\qquad\Pi_{0}(\theta_{j})=I_{n}.

As long as the matrices
A_{k}
are invertible, there exists only one family of matrix-valued polynomials in
\theta_{j}
with invertible leading coefficient satisfying this recurrence relation.

Moreover, since the basis
e_{k}
and
\phi_{j}
are orthogonal we find that
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
satisfy the following orthogonality relation:

\delta_{k,l}=(e_{k},e_{l})=\sum_{j}(e_{k},\phi_{j})(e_{l},\phi_{j})^{*}=\sum_{j}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})W(j)\Pi_{l}(\theta_{j})^{*},

where
W(j)=(e_{0},\phi_{j})(e_{0},\phi_{j})^{*}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Unlike in the scalar-valued situation, an operator might be diagonalisable with different set of eigenvalues, and thus different eigenbases. This will lead to different matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials that might be equivalent. As an example let us consider diagonal change of eigenbasis. Consider
f_{i}=P_{i}e_{i}
and
\Psi_{i}=Q_{i}\phi_{i}
with
P_{i},\ Q_{i}\in GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Then one has

H\cdot f_{i}=(P_{i}\Lambda P_{i}^{-1})f_{i}=\Lambda^{\prime}_{i}f_{i},\quad P\cdot\Psi_{j}=(Q_{j}\theta_{j}Q_{j}^{-1})\Psi_{j}=\theta^{\prime}_{j}\Psi_{j}.

We then introduce
\Pi^{\prime}_{n}
by

(f_{n},\Psi_{j})=\Pi^{\prime}_{n}(\theta^{\prime}_{j})(f_{0},\Psi_{j}).

The matrix
(f_{0},\Psi_{j})
is invertible if
(e_{0},\phi_{j})
is invertible. This leads to the relation

\Pi^{\prime}_{n}(\theta^{\prime}_{j})=P_{n}\Pi_{n}(\theta_{j})P_{0}^{-1}.

Thus the polynomials
\Pi_{n}
and
\Pi^{\prime}_{n}
gives two equivalent families of matrix valued orthogonal polynomials in the sense that their associated weight are conjugated by an invertible matrix. More precisely one has:

P_{n}P_{m}^{*}\delta_{nm}=\sum_{j}\Pi^{\prime}_{n}(\theta^{\prime}_{j})W^{\prime}(j)\Pi^{\prime}_{m}(\theta^{\prime}_{j})^{*},

with
W^{\prime}(j)=P_{0}W(j)P_{0}^{-1}
.

Back to generalities, we describe a setting that we are going to use to provide several examples in the last three sections. More precisely, using the Morita equivalence we create operators acting tridiagonally on a
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-basis starting from operators acting tridiagonally on a
{\mathbb{C}}
-basis. From now on, to avoid lengthy notations, we will denote by the same letter elements in
\End_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
and their image by the functor
G
.

Let us consider a complex vector space
V
, which admits a discrete basis, together with a self-adjoint operator
H
with eigenbasis
\mid{k}\,\rangle
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
. Consider
P
a self-adjoint operator acting tridiagonally on
\mid{k}\,\rangle

P\mid{k}\,\rangle=a_{k+1}^{*}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle+b_{k}\mid{k}\,\rangle+a_{k}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle,

with non zero
a_{k+1}
. We write
\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle
for the eigenvectors of
P
with eigenvalues
\mu_{j}
.

Now let us consider the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
V^{n}
, and the two
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases

e_{k}=(\mid{nk}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{nk+n-1}\,\rangle),\ \Phi_{j}=(\mid{\phi_{nj}}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{\phi_{nj+n-1}}\,\rangle).

Proposition 3.3
.

The operators
P^{n}
act on the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases
(e_{k}),
and
(\Phi_{j})
by

\displaystyle P^{n}\cdot\Phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j},\qquad\alpha_{j}=\diag(\mu_{nj}^{n},\ldots,\mu_{nj+n-1}^{n}),

\displaystyle P^{n}\cdot e_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{k+1}^{\ast}e_{k+1}+B_{k}e_{k}+A_{k}e_{k-1}.

Moreover,
B_{k}=B_{k}^{\ast}
for
k=0,\ldots,N,
and
A_{k}
is invertible and upper triangular for
k=1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

Since
P
is a self-adjoint operator, so is
P^{n}
. Then Proposition
3.1
tells us that the action of
P^{n}
on
\Phi_{j}
has the expected form if it is tridiagonal. The tridiagonality together with the fact that the
A_{k}
are upper triangular is a consequence of the following formula which is obtained by induction

P^{n}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\sum_{\ell=-n}^{n}\alpha_{\ell,n}\mid{k+\ell}\,\rangle,

with
\alpha_{n,n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{k+i}^{*}
and
\alpha_{-n,n}=\prod_{i+0}^{n-1}a_{k-i}
.
The
j
th (
1\leq j\leq n
) coefficient on the diagonal of
A_{k}
is then

\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{nk+j-1-i}\neq 0.

This proves that
A_{k}
is invertible.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

As seen in the proof, the explicit coefficients in
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
can be computed by induction from the action of
P
on
\mid{k}\,\rangle
. However, the explicit expression for these coefficients quickly become difficult to handle when
n
increase.

For the sake of simplicity we used the operator
P^{n}
, but similar constructions can also be carried out considering
q(P),
where
q
is a polynomial of degree
n
with scalar coefficients. This provides a method for generating additional examples. An even more general situation would be to choose an operator
P
acting
2n+1
diagonally on
\mid{k}\,\rangle
. This is very similar to the ideas developed in
[
8
]
.

To conclude we mention another interesting family of polynomials
R_{k}
related to the family
\Pi_{k}
. It will satisfy a simpler orthogonality relation with diagonal weights but the first polynomial
R_{0}
will not be the identity and might even have non zero degree.

Remark 3.5
.

The case
n=1
of the method simplifies to the scalar case
[
15
,
26
]
, where scalar orthogonal polynomials are recovered as transition coefficients:

(3.3)

p_{k}(\mu_{j})=\langle\,{k}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle w_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\text{and}\quad w_{j}=|\langle\,{0}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle|^{2}.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
L(j)
be the alternant matrix defined by

(3.4)

L(j)_{i,\ell}=p_{i-1}(\mu_{nj+\ell-1})h_{i-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad i,\ell=1,\ldots,n.

The following statements hold true:

(1)

The weight decomposes as
W(j)=L(j)D(j)L(j)^{\ast},
where
D(j)=\diag(w_{nj},\ldots,w_{nj+n-1}).

(2)

The polynomials
R_{k}
defined by

R_{k}(j)_{i,\ell}=p_{nk+i-1}(\mu_{nj+\ell-1}),

i,\ell=1,\ldots,n,

satisfy the orthogonality relation
\sum_{j}R_{k}(j)D(j)R_{m}(j)^{\ast}=\delta_{k,m}
.

(3)

\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=R_{k}(j)L(j)^{-1}.

Proof.

The decomposition of the weight directly follows from the fact that the entries of the matrix
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})
are given by

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})_{i,\ell}=\langle\,{i-1}\mid{\phi_{nj+\ell-1}}\,\rangle=p_{i-1}(\mu_{nj+n-1})w^{\frac{1}{2}}_{nj+\ell-1},

i.e.,
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})=L(j)\diag(w_{nj}^{\frac{1}{2}},\ldots,w_{nj+n-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}).
The orthogonality of the polynomials
(R_{k})_{k}
follows by noticing

\displaystyle(\sum_{j}R_{k}(j)D(j)R_{m}(j)^{\ast})_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{s=1}^{n}p_{nk+i-1}(\mu_{nj+s-1})p_{nm+\ell-1}(\mu_{nj+s-1})w_{nj+s-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}p_{nk+i-1}(\mu_{p})p_{nm+\ell-1}(\mu_{p})w_{p}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nk+i,nm+\ell}.

Since
nk+1\leq nk+i\leq n(k+1),

nm+1\leq nm+\ell\leq n(m+1)
, if
k<m
we have
n(k+1)<nm+1
and then
\delta_{nk+i,nm+\ell}=\delta_{k,m}\delta_{i,\ell}.
Finally, since

(e_{k},\phi_{j})_{i,\ell}=\langle\,{nk+i-1}\mid{\phi_{nj+\ell-1}}\,\rangle=p_{nk+i-1}(\mu_{nj+\ell-1})w_{nj+\ell-1}^{\frac{1}{2}},

we get

\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=(e_{k},\phi_{j})(e_{0},\phi_{j})^{-1}=R_{k}(j)L(j)^{-1}.

This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

4.
Krawtchouk type polynomials

In
[
15
]
, Krawtchouk polynomials are expressed as transition coefficients between eigenbases related to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. Starting from this fact we construct matrix analogues of Krawtchouk polynomials. Generators of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
satisfy the commutation relations

[H,L^{+}]=2L^{+},\qquad[H,L^{-}]=-2L^{-},\qquad[L^{+},L^{-}]=H.

Its
m+1
dimensional representation can be described as follows on the vector space
V
with basis
\mid{k}\,\rangle

\displaystyle H\mid{k}\,\rangle=\lambda_{k}\mid{k}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\qquad\lambda_{k}=2k-m,\qquad k=0,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle L^{+}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\rho^{+}_{k}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\qquad\rho^{+}_{k}=\sqrt{(k+1)(m-k)},

\displaystyle L^{-}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\rho^{-}_{k}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\qquad\rho^{-}_{k}=\sqrt{k(m+1-k)},

observe that
\rho_{k}^{+}=\rho_{k+1}^{-}.
Clearly, the operator
P=\cos(a)H+\sin(a)(L^{+}+L^{-})
acts tridiagonally on
\mid{k}\,\rangle,

P\mid{k}\,\rangle=\sin(a)\rho_{k}^{+}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle+\cos(a)\lambda_{k}\mid{k}\,\rangle+\sin(a)\rho_{k}^{-}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle.

It is known that its spectrum is discrete

(4.1)

P\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle=\mu_{j}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle,\qquad\mu_{j}=2j-m,\qquad j=0,\ldots,m,

and that the operator
H
acts tridiagonally on
\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle

H\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle=\sin(a)\rho^{+}_{j}\mid{\phi_{j+1}}\,\rangle+\cos(a)\lambda_{j}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle+\sin(a)\rho^{-}_{j}\mid{\phi_{j-1}}\,\rangle.

Krawtchouk polynomials are defined by

K_{k}(j;p,m)=\,_{2}F_{1}\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-j,-k}{-m};\frac{1}{p}\right),

and can be recovered as transition coefficients between the two bases
\mid{k}\,\rangle
and
\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle

(4.2)

K_{k}(j;p,m)=\langle\,{k}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle h_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}w_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad j,k=0,\ldots,m,

where

(4.3)

w_{j}=\binom{m}{j}p^{j}(1-p)^{m-j},\qquad h_{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{(-m)_{k}}\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\right)^{k},\qquad\text{and}\qquad p=\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos(a)).

4.1.
A
n\times n
Krawtchouk type

We consider a representation
V
of dimension
n(N+1)
, i.e. we take
m=nN+n-1
. By Corollary
2.5
, the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
V^{n}
is free and we have the following
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-bases for
V^{n}

e_{k}=(\mid{nk}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{nk+n-1}\,\rangle),\qquad\Phi_{j}=(\mid{\phi_{nj}}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{\phi_{nj+n-1}}\,\rangle),\qquad k,j=0,\ldots,N.

The actions of the operators
P^{n}
and
H^{n}
on the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases
(e_{k}),
and
(\Phi_{j})
are described in Proposition
3.3
. As discussed in Section
3
, the construction of MVOPs can proceed provided the matrices
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})
are invertible. We can verify this by examining the entries of the matrix, which are given by

(4.4)

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})_{i,\ell}=\langle\,{i-1}\mid{\phi_{nj+\ell-1}}\,\rangle=K_{i-1}(nj+\ell-1;p,nN+n-1)h_{i-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}w^{\frac{1}{2}}_{nj+\ell-1}\qquad i,\ell=1,\ldots,n.

Additionally, the
n\times n
matrix
L(j)
defined as

(4.5)

L(j)_{i,\ell}=h_{i-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}K_{i-1}(nj+\ell-1;p,nN+n-1),\qquad i,\ell=1,\ldots,n,\quad j=0,\ldots,N,

is an alternant matrix. Each row of
L(j)
is a polynomial of degree
i-1
in
j
. It is known see
[
25
]
that

\det L(j)=p_{n}\det V(nj,\ldots,nj+n-1),

where
p_{n}
is the product of the leading coefficients and the inverse of the norms of the Krawtchouk polynomials, and
V(nj,\ldots,nj+n-1)
represents the Vandermonde matrix. The determinant of
V
is given by

\det V(nj,\ldots,nj+n-1)=(n-1)!(n-2)!\ldots 2!1!.

From equation (
4.4
), we can express

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})=L(j)\diag(w^{\frac{1}{2}}_{nj},\ldots,w^{\frac{1}{2}}_{nj+n-1}),

and since the matrix
L(j)
is invertible, we conclude that
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})
is also invertible.
Since the matrices
A_{k}
constructed from Proposition
3.3
are also invertible, we can proceed as outlined in Section
3
and obtain the family of MVOPs
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
, where
\theta_{j}=(e_{0},\Phi_{j})\alpha_{j}^{*}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})^{-1}.

These polynomials satisfy the recurrence relation

\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})\theta_{j}=A_{k+1}^{*}\Pi_{k+1}(\theta_{j})+B_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})+A_{k}\Pi_{k-1}(\theta_{j}),\qquad\Pi_{-1}(\theta_{j})=0,\qquad\Pi_{0}(\theta_{j})=I_{n},

and the orthogonality relation

(4.6)

\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})W(j)\Pi_{\ell}(\theta_{j})^{\ast}=\delta_{k,\ell},\qquad W(j)=L(j)D(j)L(j)^{\ast},

where the matrix
L(j)
is defined in (
4.5
),
D(j)=\diag(w_{nj},\ldots,w_{nj+n-1}),

w_{j}
as in (
4.3
) for
m=nN+n-1
. Moreover, the polynomials satisfy the difference equation

\Lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j+1})(e_{0},\Phi_{j+1})A_{j+1}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})(e_{0},\Phi_{j})B_{j}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})\\
+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j-1})(e_{0},\Phi_{j-1})A_{j}^{\ast}(e_{0},\Phi_{j}).

We conclude this section by presenting explicit expressions in the
2\times 2
case. The alternant matrix
L(j)
from equation (
4.5
) is

L(j)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}K_{1}(2j;p,2N+1)&h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}K_{1}(2j+1;p,2N+1)\end{pmatrix},

and its inverse is

L(j)^{-1}=-p(2N+1)\begin{pmatrix}K_{1}(2j+1;p,2N+1)&-h_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
-K_{1}(2j;p,2N+1)&h_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

The weight function is
W(j)=L(j)\diag(w_{2j},w_{2j+1})L(j)^{\ast}.
The matrix
A_{k}
on the recurrence relation and difference equation is given by

A_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin(a)^{2}\rho^{-}_{2k}\rho^{-}_{2k-1}&\sin(a)\cos(a)\rho^{-}_{2k}(\lambda_{2k}+\lambda_{2k-1})\\
0&\sin(a)^{2}\rho^{-}_{2k}\rho^{-}_{2k+1}\end{pmatrix},

and the matrix
B_{k}
is given by

B_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin(a)^{2}\rho_{2k}^{-2}+\sin(a)^{2}\rho_{2k+1}^{-2}+\cos(a)^{2}\lambda_{2k}^{2}&\sin(a)\cos(a)(\lambda_{2k}\rho_{2k+1}^{-}+\rho_{2k+1}^{-}\lambda_{2k+1})\\
\sin(a)\cos(a)(\lambda_{2k}\rho_{2k+1}^{-}+\rho_{2k+1}^{-}\lambda_{2k+1})&\sin(a)^{2}\rho_{2k+1}^{-2}+\sin(a)^{2}\rho_{2k+2}^{-2}+\cos(a)^{2}\lambda_{2k+1}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
\lambda_{k}=2k-2N-1
and
\rho^{-}_{k}=\sqrt{k(2N+2-k)}
.
Finally, the MVOPs are given by
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=R_{k}(j)L(j)^{-1},
where

R_{k}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}K_{2k}(2j)h_{2k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&K_{2k}(2j+1)h_{2k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
K_{2k+1}(2j)h_{2k+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&K_{2k+1}(2j+1)h_{2k+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix},

where
h_{k}
is defined in (
4.3
),
m=2N+1
and
\theta_{j}=L(j)\diag(\mu_{2j},\mu_{2j+1})L(j)^{-1},
explicitly

\theta_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\par\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(4p-2)N+2p-1&-2\,p(2N+1)\\
\frac{(4Np-4j+2p)(2Np-2j+p-1)}{(2Np+p)}&(-4p-2)N+8j-2p+1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&h_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\par\end{pmatrix}.

4.2.
Another
2\times 2
Krawtchouk type

In this subsection we exhibit another set of generators which leads to another Krawtchouk
2\times 2
matrix analog. We consider
V
a
2(N+1)
dimensional vector space so that the
M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
-module
V^{2}
is free. Let us consider the
M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases of
V^{2}
given by

f_{k}=(\mid{k}\,\rangle,\mid{k+N+1}\,\rangle),\qquad\Phi_{j}=(\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle,\mid{\phi_{j+N+1}}\,\rangle),\qquad j,k=0,\ldots,N.

The operators
P^{2}
and
H^{2}
act on
\Phi_{j}
and
f_{k}
as described in Proposition
3.3

where the matrices
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
are explicitly given by

A_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{-}_{k}&0\\
0&\rho^{-}_{k+N+1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{k}&0\\
0&\lambda_{k+N+1}\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix
(f_{0},\Phi_{j})
is given by

(f_{0},\Phi_{j})=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
K_{N+1}(j)h_{N+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&K_{N+1}(j+N+1)h_{N+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}w_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&w_{j+N+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix},

where
K_{N+1}(j)=K_{N+1}(j;p;2N+1)
. Using the hypergeometric expansion of Krawtchouk polynomials, one checks that
K_{N+1}(j+N+1)-K_{N+1}(j)
is a non zero polynomial in
1/p
. Thus, except for possibly a finite number of
p
, the alternant matrix

(4.7)

L(j)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
K_{N+1}(j)h_{N+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&K_{N+1}(j+N+1)h_{N+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix},

is invertible, and so is
(f_{0},\Phi_{j})
for all
j=0,\ldots,N
. Notice that numerical computation showed that there exists
0<p<1
such that
K_{N+1}(j+N+1)-K_{N+1}(j)=0
thus we have to assume it is not the case. The inverse of
L(j)
is then given by

(4.8)

L(j)^{-1}=\frac{1}{K_{N+1}(j+N+1)-K_{N+1}(j)}\begin{pmatrix}K_{N+1}(j+N+1)&-h_{N+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
-K_{N+1}(j)&h_{N+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

We can proceed as in Section
3
and obtain the family
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
of MVOPs, where the argument
\theta_{j}=(f_{0},\Phi_{j})\theta_{j}^{\prime}(f_{0},\Phi_{j})^{-1}.
This polynomials satisfy the recurrence relation

\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})\theta_{j}=A_{k+1}^{*}\Pi_{k+1}(\theta_{j})+B_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})+A_{k}\Pi_{k-1}(\theta_{j}),\qquad\Pi_{-1}(\theta_{j})=0,\qquad\Pi_{0}(\theta_{j})=I_{n},

and the difference equation

\Lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j+1})(f_{0},\Phi_{j+1})A_{j+1}(f_{0},\Phi_{j})+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})(f_{0},\Phi_{j})B_{j}(f_{0},\Phi_{j})\\
+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j-1})(f_{0},\Phi_{j-1})A_{j}^{\ast}(f_{0},\Phi_{j}).

Moreover, the following orthogonality relation holds

\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})W(j)\Pi_{\ell}(\theta_{j})^{\ast}=\delta_{k,\ell},\qquad W(j)=L(j)D(j)L(j)^{\ast},

where
L(j)
is defined in (
4.7
),
D(j)=\diag(w_{j},w_{j+N+1})

and
\theta_{j}=L(j)\diag(\mu_{j},\mu_{j+N+1})L(j)^{-1}.

Remark 4.1
.

The Krawtchouk type example given in this section is not equivalent to the one given in Section
4.1
. A proof of this fact can be carried out by showing that if
P
is a constant matrix such that
W_{2}(x)=PW_{1}(x)P^{\ast}
then
P=0.

5.
Meixner type polynomials

In this section we consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
which will give matrix valued analogues of Meixner polynomials. Its generators satisfy the commutation relations

[H,L^{+}]=L^{+},\qquad[H,L^{-}]=-L^{-},\qquad[L^{-},L^{+}]=2H.

We consider a discrete series representation
V
, which is described on the basis
\mid{k}\,\rangle
by

\displaystyle H\mid{k}\,\rangle=\lambda_{k}\mid{k}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\qquad\lambda_{k}=\frac{\beta}{2}+k,\qquad\beta>0,\quad k=0,1,\ldots

\displaystyle L^{+}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\rho^{+}_{k}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\qquad\rho^{+}_{k}=\sqrt{(k+1)(\beta+k)},

\displaystyle L^{-}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\rho^{-}_{k}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\qquad\rho^{-}_{k}=\sqrt{k(\beta+k-1)}.

As in the previous section we have
\rho^{+}_{k}=\rho_{k+1}^{-}.
Given
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the operator
P=-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(a)(L^{+}+L^{-})+\cosh(a)H
which acts tridiagonally on
\mid{k}\,\rangle

P\mid{k}\,\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(a)\rho_{k}^{+}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle+\cosh(a)\lambda_{k}\mid{k}\,\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(a)\rho_{k}^{-}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle.

The spectrum of the operator
P
is

(5.1)

P\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle=\mu_{j}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle,\qquad\mu_{j}=\frac{\beta}{2}+j,\qquad j=0,1\ldots

and the operator
H
acts tridiagonally on
\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle

H\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(a)\rho^{+}_{j}\mid{\phi_{j+1}}\,\rangle+\cosh(a)\lambda_{j}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(a)\rho_{j}^{-}\mid{\phi_{j-1}}\,\rangle.

Meixner polynomials are defined by

M_{k}(j;p,m)=\,_{2}F_{1}\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-j,-k}{\beta};1-\frac{1}{c}\right),

and can be recovered as transition coefficients between this basis

M_{k}(j;\beta,c)=\langle\,{k}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle h_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}w_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad j,k=0,1,\ldots

where

(5.2)

w_{j}=\frac{(\beta)_{j}}{j!}c^{j},\qquad h_{k}=\frac{k!}{(\beta)_{k}(1-c)^{\beta}c^{k}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad c=\left(\frac{\cosh(a)-1}{\sinh(a)}\right)^{2}.

5.1.
A
n\times n
Meixner type

The representation
V
has countable dimension, and we consider the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases of
V^{n}
given by

e_{k}=(\mid{nk}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{nk+n-1}\,\rangle),\qquad\Phi_{j}=(\mid{\phi_{nj}}\,\rangle,\ldots,\mid{\phi_{nj+n-1}}\,\rangle),\qquad k,j=0,1\ldots

The actions of the operators
P^{n}
and
H^{n}
on the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases
(e_{k}),
and
(\Phi_{j})
are described in Proposition
3.3
.
The matrix entries of
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})
are given by

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})_{i,\ell}=\langle\,{i-1}\mid{\phi_{nj+\ell-1}}\,\rangle=M_{i-1}(nj+\ell-1;c,\beta)h_{i-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}w_{nj+\ell-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\qquad i,\ell=1,\ldots,n.\qquad

The invertibility of
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})
is proved as in the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
case using the
n\times n
matrix
L(j)
defined as

(5.3)

L(j)_{i,\ell}=h_{i-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{i-1}(nj+\ell-1;c,\beta),\qquad i,\ell=1,\ldots,n.

from which we can express

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})=L(j)\diag(w_{nj}^{\frac{1}{2}},\ldots,w_{nj+n-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Since the matrices
A_{k}
constructed using Proposition

are invertible, we can proceed as outlined in Section

and obtain the family of MVOPs
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
, where
\theta_{j}=(e_{0},\Phi_{j})\alpha_{j}^{*}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})^{-1}.
These polynomials satisfy the recurrence relation

\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})\theta_{j}=A_{k+1}^{*}\Pi_{k+1}(\theta_{j})+B_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})+A_{k}\Pi_{k-1}(\theta_{j}),\qquad\Pi_{-1}(\theta_{j})=0,\qquad\Pi_{0}(\theta_{j})=I_{n},

and the orthogonality relation

(5.4)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})W(j)\Pi_{\ell}(\theta_{j})^{\ast}=\delta_{k,\ell},\qquad W(j)=L(j)D(j)L(j)^{\ast},

where the matrix
L(j)

Moreover, the polynomials satisfy the difference equation

\Lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j+1})(e_{0},\Phi_{j+1})A_{j+1}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})(e_{0},\Phi_{j})B_{j}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})\\
+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j-1})(e_{0},\Phi_{j-1})A_{j}^{\ast}(e_{0},\Phi_{j}).

We conclude the section by presenting the explicit expressions in the
2\times 2
case. The alternant matrix
L
defined in (
3.4
) is

L(j)=\begin{pmatrix}h_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&h_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{1}(2j;c,\beta)&h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{1}(2j+1;c,\beta)\end{pmatrix},

and its inverse is

L(j)^{-1}=\frac{c\beta}{c-1}\begin{pmatrix}h_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}M_{1}(2j+1;c,\beta)&-h_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
-h_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}M_{1}(2j;c,\beta)&h_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

The weight function is
W(j)=L(j)\diag(w_{2j},w_{2j+1})L(j)^{\ast}.
The matrix
A_{k}
on the recurrence relation and difference equation is given by

A_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sinh(a)^{2}}{4}\rho^{-}_{2k}\rho^{-}_{2k-1}&-\frac{\sinh(a)\cosh(a)}{2}\rho^{-}_{2k}(\lambda_{2k}+\lambda_{2k-1})\\
0&\frac{\sinh(a)^{2}}{4}\rho^{-}_{2k}\rho^{-}_{2k+1}\end{pmatrix},

and the matrix
B_{k}
is given by

B_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sinh(a)^{2}}{4}(\rho_{2k}^{-2}+\rho_{2k+1}^{-2})+\cosh(a)^{2}\lambda_{2k}^{2}&-\frac{\sinh(a)\cosh(a)}{2}(\lambda_{2k}\rho_{2k+1}^{-}+\rho_{2k+1}^{-}\lambda_{2k+1})\\
-\frac{\sinh(a)\cosh(a)}{2}(\lambda_{2k}\rho_{2k+1}^{-}+\rho_{2k+1}^{-}\lambda_{2k+1})&\frac{\sinh(a)^{2}}{4}(\rho_{2k+1}^{-2}+\rho_{2k+2}^{-2})+\cosh(a)^{2}\lambda_{2k+1}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
\lambda_{k}=\frac{\beta}{2}+k
and
\rho^{-}_{k}=\sqrt{k(\beta+k-1)}
.
Finally, the MVOPs are given by
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=R_{k}(j)L(j)^{-1},
where

R_{k}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}M_{2k}(2j;c,\nu)h_{2k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&M_{2k}(2j+1;c,\nu)h_{2k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
M_{2k+1}(2j;c,\nu)h_{2k+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&M_{2k+1}(2j+1;c,\nu)h_{2k+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix},

where
h_{k}
is defined in (
5.2
).

Remark 5.1
.

In both the Krawtchouk and Meixner analogues, extensive symbolic computations indicate that the weight matrix
W
are
irreducible, see
[
27
]
and
[
19
]
. A complete proof of this fact for the
2\times 2
case can be carried out applying
[
27
, Theorem 2.3]
.

6.
Discrete Chebyshev type polynomials

We construct a last example to show that the procedure developed in this article is also suited to create
q
deformed MVOPs. This will be based on the
q
-deformed Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{q}(3)
and the work done in
[
26
]
. We consider
N
a positive integer,
\omega=\frac{\pi}{N}
, and
\mathfrak{so}_{q}(3)
at
q=e^{2i\omega}
a root of unity. The generators of this
q
-deformed algebra satisfy the commutation relations

[K_{0},K_{1}]_{\omega}=K_{2},\quad[K_{1},K_{2}]_{\omega}=-K_{0},\quad[K_{0},K_{2}]_{\omega}=K_{1},

where
[A,B]_{\omega}=e^{\frac{i\omega}{2}}AB-e^{-\frac{i\omega}{2}}BA,
denotes the symmetric
q
-commutator. For
d=1,2,\ldots,N-1
, there is unitary
(d+1)
-dimensional irreducible representation
(V,\rho)
of
\mathfrak{so}_{q}(3)
in which the operator
K_{0}
is diagonal and
K_{1}
two diagonal

\displaystyle K_{0}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\lambda_{k}\mid{k}\,\rangle,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,d,

\displaystyle K_{1}\mid{k}\,\rangle=\rho_{k+1}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle+\rho_{k}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle,

where

\lambda_{k}=\frac{\cos\omega(k+\beta)}{\sin\omega},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\sin\omega k\sin\omega(k+2\beta-1)}{4\sin^{2}\omega\sin\omega(k+\beta)\sin\omega(k+\beta-1)}},

with
2\beta=N-d
.
Given
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the operator
P=K_{1}+b
which acts tridiagonally on
\mid{k}\,\rangle

P\mid{k}\,\rangle=\rho_{k+1}\mid{k+1}\,\rangle+b\mid{k}\,\rangle+\rho_{k}\mid{k-1}\,\rangle.

The operator
K_{1}
has the same spectrum as
K_{0}

[
26
,
5
]
, and therefore
P
is diagonalized as follows

P\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle=\mu_{j}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,d,\quad\mu_{j}=\frac{\cos\omega(j+\beta)}{\sin\omega}+b,

The operator
H=K_{0}
acts tridiagonally on
\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle

H\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle=\rho_{j+1}\mid{\phi_{j+1}}\,\rangle+\rho_{j}\mid{\phi_{j-1}}\,\rangle.

The monic
q
-ultraspherical polynomials at
q
a root of unity can be recovered as the overlaps

P_{k}(x_{j})=\langle\,{k}\mid{\phi_{j}}\,\rangle h_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}w_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad x_{j}=2\cos\omega(j+\beta)

where

(6.1)

w_{j}=\sin\omega(j+\beta)\prod_{\ell=1}^{2\beta-1}\sin\omega(j+\ell),\qquad s=0,\ldots,d.

The formulas for
h_{k}
are more involved and can be found in
[
26
]
(formulas (30), (31)).

It is interesting to realize that the finite Chebyshev are a special case of these

q
-polynomials, taking
\beta=1
and with
\rho_{k+1}^{+}
becoming
\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, the finite Chebyshev polynomials are given by

P_{n}(x_{j})=\frac{\sin\omega(n+1)(j+1)}{\sin\omega(j+1)},\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,d

and the weight function is

w_{j}=\sin^{2}\omega(j+1).

6.1.
A
2\times 2
discrete Chebyshev type

We now construct
2\times 2
discrete Chebyshev type polynomials, which can be generalized to
n\times n
as in Section
4.1
and Section
5.1
. We consider the
(d+1)
-dimensional irreducible representation
(V,\rho)
of
\mathfrak{so}_{q}(3)
, and we assume
2|d+1,
i.e.,
d+1=2(m+1).

We consider the
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
basis of
V^{2}
given by

e_{k}=(\mid{2k}\,\rangle,\mid{2k+1}\,\rangle),\qquad\Phi_{j}=(\mid{\phi_{2j}}\,\rangle,\mid{\phi_{2j+1}}\,\rangle),\qquad k,j=0,\ldots,m.

The action of the operators
P^{2}
and
H^{2}
on the
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-bases
(e_{k}),
and
(\Phi_{j})
is outlined in Proposition
3.3
. Specifically, we have the following:

P^{2}\cdot\Phi_{j}=\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j},\qquad H^{2}\cdot e_{k}=\Lambda_{k}e_{k},

where
\theta^{\prime}_{j}=\diag(\mu_{2j}^{2},\mu_{2j+1}^{2}),

\Lambda_{k}=\diag(\lambda_{2j}^{2},\lambda_{2j+1}^{2})
and

\displaystyle P^{2}\cdot e_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{k+1}^{\ast}e_{k+1}+B_{k}e_{k}+A_{k}e_{k-1},

\displaystyle H^{2}\cdot\Phi_{j}

\displaystyle=A_{k+1}^{\ast}\Phi_{j}+B_{k}\Phi_{j}+A_{k}\Phi_{j-1}.

Here, the matrix
A_{k},
is invertible for
k=1,\ldots,N,

B_{k}=B_{k}^{\ast}.
The matrices
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
are given by

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{2k}\rho_{2k-1}&2b\rho_{2k}\\
0&\rho_{2k}\rho_{2k+1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N,

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{2k}^{2}+\rho_{2k+1}^{2}+b^{2}&2b\rho_{2k+1}\\
2b\rho_{2k+1}&\rho_{2k+1}^{2}+\rho_{2k+2}^{2}+b^{2}\end{pmatrix}\qquad k=0,\ldots,N,

Moreover, in the discrete Chebyshev case
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
are independent of
k
and are given by

A=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}&-b\\
0&\frac{1}{4}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+b^{2}&-b\\
-b&\frac{1}{2}+b^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The construction of MVOPs can be carried out provided the matrices
(e_{0},\Phi_{j})
are invertible. We can verify that this is so by noting that their entries are given by

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})_{i,\ell}=\langle\,{i-1}\mid{\phi_{nj+\ell-1}}\,\rangle=P_{i-1}(x_{nj+\ell-1})h_{i-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}w_{nj+\ell-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\qquad i,\ell=1,2

which implies that

(e_{0},\Phi_{j})=L(j)\diag(w_{2j}^{\frac{1}{2}},w_{2j+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}),

where
L(j)
is the alternant matrix

(6.2)

L(j)=\begin{pmatrix}h_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
P_{1}(x_{2j})&P_{1}(x_{2j+1})\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that since
\omega=\frac{\pi}{N},
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}
the matrix
L(j)
is invertible. Thus
(e_{0},\phi_{j})
is invertible. The inverse of
L(j)
is

L(j)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2(\cos\omega(2j+\beta)-\cos\omega(2j+1+\beta))}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
P_{1}(x_{2j})&P_{1}(x_{2j+1})\end{pmatrix}.\begin{pmatrix}h_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&h_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

We then proceed as in Section
3
and obtain the family of MVOPs
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})
, where
\theta_{j}=(e_{0},\Phi_{j})\alpha_{j}^{*}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})^{-1}.
These polynomials satisfy the recurrence relation

\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})\theta_{j}=A_{k+1}^{*}\Pi_{k+1}(\theta_{j})+B_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})+A_{k}\Pi_{k-1}(\theta_{j}),\qquad\Pi_{-1}(\theta_{j})=0,\qquad\Pi_{0}(\theta_{j})=I_{n},

and the orthogonality relation

\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})W(j)\Pi_{\ell}(\theta_{j})^{\ast}=\delta_{k,\ell},\qquad W(j)=L(j)D(j)L(j)^{\ast},

where the matrix
L(j)
is defined in (
6.2
) and
D(j)=\diag(w_{2j},w_{2j+1}).
These polynomials moreover satisfy the difference equation

\Lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j+1})(e_{0},\Phi_{j+1})A_{j+1}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})(e_{0},\Phi_{j})B_{j}(e_{0},\Phi_{j})\\
+\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j-1})(e_{0},\Phi_{j-1})A_{j}^{\ast}(e_{0},\Phi_{j}).

Finally, the MVOPs are given by
\Pi_{k}(\theta_{j})=R_{k}(j)L(j)^{-1},
where

R_{k}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}h_{2k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}P_{2k}(x_{2j})&h_{2k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}P_{2k}(x_{2j+1})\\
h_{2k+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}P_{2k+1}(x_{2j})&h_{2k+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}P_{2k+1}(x_{2j+1})\end{pmatrix}.

7.
Outlook

In this article we were able to develop a general method to obtain matrix analogs of scalar valued orthogonal polynomials and to apply it successfully on various examples. An interesting feature is that for the same algebra, here
\mathfrak{su}(2)
we were able to produce two non equivalent examples. This raises the question of the classification of MVOPs. In the case of finite families of MVOPs satisfying a difference equation one approach could be a generalization of Leonard pairs in the context of
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-modules as developed in Section
2
. It would also be quite interesting to find applications of these MVOPs in models of interest in physics.
