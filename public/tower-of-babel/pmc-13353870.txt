Cell‐Free Protein Synthesis in Porous Parylene Scaffolds
Authors: Gunoh Lee, So Jeong Lee, Min‐Jung Park, Hsien‐Yeh Chen, Joerg Lahann, Dong‐Myung Kim, Kyung Jin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCell‐free protein synthesis (CFPS) has emerged as a promising platform for point‐of‐care (POC) biosensing due to its programmability, rapid response, and ability to operate without living cells. Integrating CFPS into solid‐state materials is essential for enabling long‐term storage, portability, and spatial multiplexing required for practical POC applications. While previous efforts have demonstrated CFPS integration onto two‐dimensional substrates such as paper, embedding CFPS machinery within a three‐dimensional matrix that preserves its activity remains a significant challenge. Here, we propose a solid‐state CFPS system comprised of a parylene‐based scaffold loaded with CFPS machinery, i.e., CFPS in porous parylene (CinPP). The CinPP is fabricated through ice‐templated chemical vapor deposition (CVD). We further demonstrate protein synthesis that is readily activated upon rehydration. The CinPP platform supports protein expression after prolonged storage and enables spatially localized and programmable expression of distinct fluorescent proteins. Furthermore, selective reconstitution of CFPS machinery with target‐responsive genetic circuits demonstrates its potential as a modular sensing platform. This approach provides a versatile solid‐state CFPS material that can be extended to programmable biosensors, portable diagnostic devices, and on‐demand protein production systems.

Introduction

Rapid point‐of‐care detection (POC) of pathogens and early identification of environmental contaminants is of special interest due to the growing demands for timely decision‐making in public health and environmental management [1, 2]. Biosensors driven by cell‐free protein synthesis (CFPS), which utilize cellular machinery without a living cell, have gained particular interest because of their outstanding selectivity, rapid response time, and high programmability [3]. The naturally evolved biological recognition system in cellular machinery can be harnessed for the detection of specific targets ranging from biological materials such as protein, amino acid, and nutrients to simple chemicals, including organic molecules and metal ions. With rationally designed genetic circuits, CFPS can provide versatile platforms capable of detecting a wide variety of analytes [4]. In addition, by localizing CFPS on a substrate, these platforms can be expanded to multiplexing diagnostic devices such as lab‐on‐a‐chip systems [5, 6].

The concept of immobilizing CFPS machinery on solid substrates has been explored in various formats to enhance stability and spatial control of cell‐free reactions [7, 8]. In our previous studies, transcription–translation systems were successfully entrapped within calcium alginate microbeads, enabling sustained protein synthesis through diffusional exchange of substrates and by‐products [9, 10]. Subsequent incorporation of silica coatings further improved the structural integrity and molecular retention of the alginate beads, thereby prolonging the activity of the encapsulated protein synthesis machinery [11]. In addition, CFPS reactions were localized within agarose gel matrices coated on solid supports, allowing spatially confined protein expression and in situ immobilization for array‐based applications [12, 13]. In parallel, Park et al. demonstrated that a DNA‐crosslinked protein‐producing gel (P‐gel) could retain transcription–translation activity in a solid‐state format, enabling protein synthesis with enhanced robustness and handling stability [14]. Diverse approaches have been also developed to achieve localized CFPS reactions, including lyophilized solid‐state systems, CFPS‐incorporated hydrogels, and paper‐based CFPS platforms [15, 16, 17, 18]. These localized CFPS formats enable spatially confined reactions and facilitate the development of multiplexed POC sensor arrays by allowing independent reactions to occur at predefined positions on a substrate. Moreover, several studies have reported that solidified CFPS systems exhibit significantly enhanced long‐term stability compared to their liquid‐phase counterparts, providing extended shelf life and improved tolerance to environmental stresses before use [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Collectively, these advances underscore the potential of solid‐state CFPS platforms for robust, scalable, and device‐integrated biosensing applications. However, this also highlights the persistent challenges with achieving efficient immobilization and reliable reactivation of CFPS machinery within specific solid substrates. Unlike conventional approaches based on encapsulation or embedding within bulk matrices, the CinPP platform introduces a fabrication‐driven strategy that enables structurally defined organization of CFPS reactions.

Localization of CFPS machinery on polymer matrix represents a promising alternative to dry‐phase CFPS platforms [20, 21, 25]. Most previous reports have relied on hydrogel‐based systems because their hydrophilic networks are advantageous for reactivating CFPS through rehydration [13, 18, 26]. However, hydrogels tend to swell and deform during rehydration, potentially compromising their geometry and leading to cross‐contamination between adjacent reaction sites. The latter is particularly detrimental to CFPS systems requiring spatially separated reactions or independently addressable reaction sites. To address these issues, encapsulation of CFPS systems into hydrophobic and porous polymeric substrates has been explored [25]. The hydrophobic nature of such substrates enables them to maintain their original structures under rehydration conditions, which is advantageous for the preparation of spatially programmable CFPS devices. Nevertheless, efficient encapsulation of CFPS machinery within porous polymeric structures beyond hydrogels, without damaging sensitive biological components, remains extremely challenging.

Recently, Chen et al. reported the fabrication of parylene‐based porous structures by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) using ice template [27, 28, 29]. Under vacuum conditions, the sublimation of ice and deposition of parylene monomer occur simultaneously, forming porous structured parylene [30, 31, 32, 33]. They reported in‐depth studies on porous parylene systems, providing detailed insights into the underlying formation mechanism and related fabrication techniques, and further demonstrated that functional porous parylene scaffolds could be fabricated by employing ice templates containing inorganic nanoparticles, metallic particles, or metal‐organic frameworks [34]. Notably, the parylene polymer is well‐known for its biocompatiblility, making it a suitable matrix for biological applications [35, 36]. Herein, we report a novel CFPS system, which is prepared via ice‐templated CVD polymerization with the goal of encapsulating the entire CFPS machinery, resulting in CFPS‐containing porous parylene scaffolds (CinPP). The utilization of porous parylene as an alternative CFPS medium provides several key benefits: (1) straightforward pore generation within the polymer, (2) inherent biocompatibility, (3) highly tunable 3D structuring, and (4) a robust, hydrophobic matrix exhibiting resistance to aqueous swelling and oxidation [37, 38, 39, 40]. Upon rehydration, the CFPS machinery embedded within the porous parylene scaffold can be re‐activated to support protein synthesis, as the interconnected porous network enables efficient water diffusion and delivery to all encapsulated machinery.

Results

Figure 1a presents a schematic overview of the CFPS system used in this study. The DNA template encoding the target protein is combined with a reaction mixture containing transcription–translation components, energy sources, cofactors, and an Escherichia coli extract that provides the essential molecular machinery for protein synthesis. Because all biochemical components are supplied externally, the composition can be precisely controlled, enabling reproducible and tunable protein expression entirely in vitro. This open and modular nature of the CFPS system allows its integration into diverse physical formats and material platforms.

(a) Overall workflow of the CFPS process, (b) schematic illustration of porous parylene fabrication, and (c) step‐by‐step procedure from fabrication of the CinPP scaffold to rehydration for protein expression.

Parylene is a polymer typically deposited as a conformal thin film via solvent‐free vapour phase processes [35, 36]. However, when ice is used as the substrate (Figure 1b), the parylene CVD process enables the formation of a unique three‐dimensional porous structure that replicates the original morphology of the ice template [41, 42]. Here, these approaches were adopted to fabricate a porous scaffold incorporating CFPS machinery within an ice template. As illustrated in Figure 1c, the CFPS machinery, including amino acids, nucleoside triphosphates (NTPs), salts (K+, Na+), cell extracts, DNA templates, and other necessary constituents, is embedded in the ice template. Typically, an aqueous CFPS solution was simply drop‐casted onto a hydrophobically treated glass substrate and rapidly frozen to produce ice template bearing well‐dispersed CFPS machinery. The frozen CFPS sample was subsequently subjected to parylene CVD, resulting in the replacement of the ice medium with a porous parylene matrix. Protein expression can be initiated by simple rehydration on the CinPP scaffold, which offers enhanced storage stability, improved portability, reduced production cost, and superior processability.

Figure 2a shows the detailed morphological structure of CinPP, in which various CFPS machinery are embedded within the porous parylene network. The porous parylene skeleton retains the CFPS machinery by encapsulating them within pores and around the pore walls, allowing efficient exposure to water upon rehydration for protein expression on demand. The parylene constituting CinPP possesses a polymeric networks through the CVD polymerization without any additional solvents, initiators or additives. As presented in Figure 2a, CinPP with a half‐spherical shape can be obtained by simple drop casting (and immediate freezing) of the CFPS solution onto hydrophobic surfaces; in principle, CinPP of any desired shape can be produced by adopting ice template of corresponding geometries [27]. During deposition (Figure 2b), the parylene dimer is thermally cleaved into two reactive radical monomers, which subsequently polymerize to form the parylene chain structure. Among the parylene derivatives, parylene‐C (PPX‐C; abbreviation of poly(chloro‐p‐xylylene)) was selected for the basic fabrication and fundamental characterization of the CinPP system. PPX‐C is one of the most widely used derivatives and contains a distinctive chlorine atom, which allows for straightforward identification. Theoretically, diverse types of functional groups can be introduced on scaffold by altering the type of dimer [43, 44, 45].

(a) Microscale morphological illustration of CinPP, (b) procedure of polymerizing parylene and information of CFPS machinery, (c) Cross‐sectional SEM images comparing the porous structures of PP (ice‐templated) and (d) CinPP, along with corresponding EDS profiles for (e) porous parylene and (f) CinPP.

Figure 2c,d compares the pore structures of porous parylene prepared from pure ice templates and CinPP through cross‐sectional SEM imaging. While both exhibit a similar overall porous morphology, as the porous architecture is derived from the sublimating ice template, differences in pore size and microstructure were observed, likely attributed to the higher viscosity of the CFPS solution. In general, relatively larger pore sizes were frequently observed in the CinPP system (Figure 2d). Figure 2e,f provide elemental composition information obtained from cross‐sectional EDS analysis. In addition to the Cl signal originating from PPX‐C, various elemental signals such as K and Na were detected in only CinPP, confirming the successful incorporation of CFPS machinery within the porous scaffold. The FT‐IR and XPS spectra (Figure S1) also confirm that the CinPP skeleton is composed of well‐structured PPX‐C polymer.

Upon rehydration of the CinPP structures, protein synthesis was successfully reactivated, as illustrated in Figure 3. As a model system, expression of superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP) was first demonstrated using CinPP containing sfGFP‐encoding DNA (Figure 3a) [46, 47]. For this basic demonstration, CinPP was fabricated in a dome‐shaped geometry, readily formed by drop‐casting the CFPS solution onto a hydrophobic substrate. After rehydration and incubation (60 min at 37°C), strong sfGFP fluorescence signals were clearly detected. One of the key advantages of the CinPP system is its structural tunability via ice‐template engineering for specific applications [27]. As shown in Figure 3b,a PDMS mold with a 3D lettering pattern was employed as to shape the ice template, enabling the fabrication of structured CinPP platforms. The schematic illustration, corresponding fluorescence image, and optical photograph confirm successful protein synthesis while maintaining the original geometry of the CinPP structure. Through the confocal microscopy analysis after expression of the sfGFP (Figure S2), the expressed protein was also detected within the parylene matrix, indicating that the CFPS machinery was dispersed and encapsulated by the porous parylene, as observed in the sequential z‐stack fluorescence images [18].

(b) 3D lettering‐structured CinPP fabricated using a PDMS ice template, showing shape‐preserved protein expression. (c) Fluorescence images of CinPP prepared with varying CFPS concentrations. The amount of expressed protein varied with the composition of the CFPS mixture used during ice‐template preparation. As shown by the gradient fluorescence intensity in Figure 3c, CinPP prepared with diluted CFPS mixtures exhibited correspondingly reduced sfGFP fluorescence upon rehydration, providing a visual representation of the effect of CFPS composition on protein expression. The corresponding fluorescence values obtained from these samples are presented in Figure 3d, reflecting this trend with representative values. In addition, as presented in Figure 3e,f, the CinPP system was further demonstrated for the synthesis of different proteins. Beyond sfGFP, CFPS solutions containing DNA encoding mScarlet and mTurquoise were incorporated into CinPP through the same ice‐templated CVD process. Regardless of the CFPS formulation, CinPP structures were successfully fabricated without noticeable differences in scaffold morphology. Upon rehydration, the corresponding proteins were synthesized, as shown by the representative fluorescence images in Figure 3e, and the expressed proteins were further verified by SDS–PAGE analysis in Figure 3f. To assess the effect of processing steps on CFPS activity, protein expression levels were compared among liquid, freeze–thaw‐treated, and CinPP systems (Figure S3). A partial reduction in fluorescence was observed following freeze–thaw treatment, and the CinPP condition showed a further decrease, but still retained substantial functional activity, suggesting that CFPS activity is largely preserved despite freezing and scaffold fabrication.

One of the intrinsic limitations of conventional aqueous CFPS systems is their poor storage stability, as the biological components are prone to gradual inactivation under hydrated conditions. Although alternative strategies such as lyophilization have been proposed, the CinPP system provides a more stable environment by encapsulating the CFPS machinery within porous parylene [20, 21, 48]. Owing to its hydrophobic nature, porous parylene limits moisture exposure and thereby suppresses the degradation of CFPS machinery. Figure 4 compares the amount of expressed sfGFP from lyophilized CFPS and CinPP after storage over 2 weeks under ambient (near RT) and accelerated conditions (37°C) at low‐vacuum conditions, for each. Figure 4a presents the quantitative analysis of sfGFP expression under ambient conditions. While the lyophilized CFPS retained only 18.9% of its day 1 sfGFP expression on day 13, the CinPP system maintained 29.3% of its initial expression level. Furthermore, under accelerated aging conditions (Figure 4b), the CinPP system preserved 6.5% of its activity, outperforming the lyophilized CFPS, which exhibited only 2.1%. This suggests that the porous parylene matrix exhibits relatively higher CFPS activity compared to the lyophilized CFPS system. These results indicate that the CinPP system may offer a modest improvement in the storage stability of CFPS relative to both conventional systems and established lyophilization methods, highlighting its potential rather than demonstrating definitive superiority.

Extended storage stability of CinPP comparing to lyophilized CFPS for 2 weeks under (a) the ambient (near RT) and (b) accelerated conditions (37°C) for each. Error bars represent standard deviation from independent experiments (n = 2).

The open and modular nature of CFPS allows selective inclusion or exclusion of individual reaction components, enabling flexible reconfiguration of the system depending on the intended application [4, 49]. This intrinsic advantage of CFPS was exploited in the CinPP platform by deliberately omitting specific components during scaffold preparation and restoring them upon rehydration. When a particular component was excluded from the CFPS mixture during deposition, protein expression was strongly suppressed unless the missing component was supplied in the rehydration solution. This on‐demand activation of protein synthesis demonstrates the feasibility of the CinPP platform as a sensing system based on selective component reconstitution.

To evaluate the DNA‐dependent response of the CinPP sensing platform, CinPP structures without a DNA sequence were rehydrated with varying concentrations of plasmid DNA. As shown in Figure 5a, sfGFP fluorescence intensity exhibited an increasing trend with increasing DNA concentration, indicating that protein expression can be triggered in a concentration‐dependent manner. The corresponding quantitative values reflect this trend, demonstrating tunable signal generation across the tested concentration range. The fluorescence‐based results were further supported by SDS–PAGE analysis, which confirmed quantitative expression of sfGFP at the corresponding DNA concentrations (Figure 5b). In addition, fluorescence signals were observed at DNA concentrations down to 0.1 nm under the tested conditions.

The selective reconstitution strategy was further extended to other CFPS machinery. CinPP scaffolds lacking specific components, such as DNA, amino acids, Mg2 +, or ATP, exhibited only weak residual fluorescence when rehydrated with water alone. In contrast, when the corresponding missing component was supplied during rehydration, protein expression was substantially restored (Figure 5c). The corresponding quantitative analysis reflects this component‐dependent activation of protein synthesis (Figure 5d). The weak background signal observed under certain conditions is likely attributable to endogenous small molecules, such as residual amino acids or cofactors, present in the crude cell extract. Similar background expression has been reported in extract‐based CFPS systems [46, 47]. In the current study, background fluorescence levels reached up to approximately 6 × 104 a.u. under certain conditions. As demonstrated in additional experiments using diafiltration‐treated extracts (Figure S4), removal of low‐molecular‐weight components significantly reduced background fluorescence to approximately 5 × 103 a.u., supporting the contribution of endogenous molecules to the observed non‐specific signals. Because fluorescence values were presented without background subtraction or normalization, background‐derived signals may influence the interpretation of low‐intensity fluorescence, particularly under low‐DNA conditions such as the 0.1 nm samples. In most experimental conditions, however, fluorescence signals were substantially higher than the background level, allowing qualitative comparison of expression trends within each experimental set. Furthermore, under reduced‐background conditions, input‐dependent activation of protein expression was demonstrated using an indole‐responsive complementary CFPS system (Figure S4). In this configuration, tryptophan was omitted from the CFPS mixture to suppress protein synthesis, and purified TrpB (15 µg/mL) was incorporated during CinPP fabrication. Upon rehydration, samples were supplemented with either no addition (water only), tryptophan, or indole, with tryptophan and indole added at the same concentration (50 µm). While no addition resulted in minimal fluorescence, both tryptophan and indole restored sfGFP expression, with indole yielding expression levels comparable to those obtained with direct tryptophan supplementation through enzymatic conversion by TrpB. These results suggest that the CinPP platform can support input‐responsive reactions when background signals are minimized. However, these findings represent a proof‐of‐concept demonstration, and further optimization will be required to achieve robust sensing specificity and analytical performance. Furthermore, for a specific application, a practical POC sensor can be readily realized by integrating well‐known technologies: specifically, a UV LED lamp to visualize the expressed proteins as fluorescent signals, coupled with a smartphone‐based detection device to acquire and display the data to the user (Figure S5) [50, 51, 52].

Conclusion

This work established a novel solid‐state CFPS system through ice‐templated CVD polymerization. The resulting porous parylene scaffolds did not interfere with the fundamental operations and functions of the CFPS system. The flexible fabrication process used for CinPP systems establishes a scalable and facile strategy with potential advantages over previously reported solid‐localized CFPS techniques [7, 8, 25]. Importantly, it enables precise control and maintenance of the scaffold geometry. The porous nature of the parylene shell used in CinPP systems provides a stable microenvironment for the CFPS machinery, protecting it from harsh atmospheric conditions and mitigating aging issues. Furthermore, by selectively omitting specific components such as template DNA, amino acids, metal ions, or ATP, the CinPP system has potential for use in biosensing applications that detects target analytes through the conditional reconstitution of CFPS activity. In addition, the CinPP platform enables target protein production in a spatially localized manner, enabling spatially programmable protein expression arrays. Overall, this platform presents a versatile and desirable strategy for realizing solid‐state CFPS and broadening its applicability across diverse fields.

Experimental Section/Methods

Materials

The commercialized dimers used for synthesis and deposition, dichloro[2.2]paracyclophane (PCP‐C) and octafluoro[2.2]paracyclophane (PCP‐VT4), were purchased from NURI TECH. CO. (Republic of Korea). ATP, GTP, UTP, CTP, creatine phosphate, and creatine kinase were obtained from Roche Applied Science (Indianapolis, IN, USA). All other reagents were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Preparation of CinPP System

The CFPS reaction mixture was prepared using an Escherichia coli extract‐based system with the following composition: 57 mm HEPES–KOH (pH 7.5), 1.2 mm ATP, 0.85 mm each of CTP, GTP, and UTP, 2 mm DTT, 90 mm potassium glutamate, 80 mm ammonium acetate, 12 mm magnesium acetate, 67 µg/mL L‐5‐formyl‐5,6,7,8‐tetrahydrofolic acid, 2 mm each of the 20 amino acids, 2% (w/v) PEG 8000, 67 mm creatine phosphate (CP), 3.2 µg/mL creatine kinase (CK), and 26.7% (v/v) E. coli extract. Unless otherwise stated, CFPS reactions were initiated by rehydration of the deposited CinPP structures and incubated at 37°C for 1 h for protein expression.

The parylene polymer constituting the CinPP was deposited using a chemical vapor deposition (CVD) system (ITS, Republic of Korea) [53, 54, 55, 56]. The parylene dimer was sublimated in the gas phase within a temperature range of 90°C–130°C and subsequently transported to the pyrolysis zone by an argon carrier gas (20 sccm), where it was thermally cleaved into radical monomer species. These radical monomers were deposited onto the surface of a sublimating ice template to form the parylene coating. To achieve a well‐defined porous structure, the deposition stage was maintained at −5°C. Throughout the deposition process, the chamber pressure was controlled in the range of 0.1–0.5 Torr.

For experiments involving pre‐encapsulation of genetic templates, plasmid DNA encoding fluorescent reporter proteins (sfGFP, mScarlet, or mTurquoise) was added to the CFPS mixture at a final concentration of 13 µg/mL before deposition. The reaction mixture was dispensed as 50 µL droplets onto hydrophobically coated glass substrates, immediately frozen at −80°C, and subsequently introduced into the deposition system for parylene coating.

For experiments designed to evaluate post‐deposition reconstitution of CFPS machinery, individual CFPS machinery was selectively omitted from the reaction mixture before deposition. Specifically, plasmid DNA, amino acids, salts, or ATP were individually excluded from the CFPS mixture, while all other machinery was maintained at the same concentrations. These incomplete CFPS mixtures were deposited and parylene‐coated following the same procedure described above. After deposition, the porous CinPP structures were rehydrated by adding aqueous solutions containing the omitted component at the original working concentration.

For surface modification, hydrophobic glass substrates were pre‐coated with poly(tetrefluoro‐p‐xylylene) (PPX‐VT4) to induce a high water contact angle against the aqueous CFPS solution. The PPX‐VT4 layer was deposited under CVD conditions distinct from those used for PPX‐C, with a pyrolysis temperature of 690°C–700°C and a deposition holder temperature of 5°C, while the heating chamber was maintained at 80°C.

Material Characterization

The formation of porous parylene in CinPP was characterized by Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR) in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; K‐alpha+, Thermo Fisher). The morphology of CinPP was further analyzed by super resolution confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 880 with Airyscan, Carl Zeiss) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; CLARA, TESCAN), along with the corresponding energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis.

Protein Expression in CinPP System

To induce protein expression, DI water was introduced into the CinPP to rehydrate the incorporated CFPS machinery, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. Protein production was monitored by fluorescence measurement using a microplate reader (CLARIOstar, BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). sfGFP fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 470 nm, while fluorescence signals for other conditions were measured at 365 nm where applicable. Fluorescence intensity values were directly obtained from the instrument without normalization or background subtraction unless otherwise stated. All measurements were performed using identical instrument settings within each experiment. For experiments with replicates, data are presented as mean values with error bars representing standard deviation. For experiments performed as single measurements (n = 1), the data are presented as representative values without statistical analysis. For accelerated aging tests, samples were maintained in a low‐vacuum desiccator at 37°C to evaluate storage stability.
