TRIM3 exacerbates chondrocyte apoptosis through suppression of AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in osteoarthritis
Authors: Huashuang Ou, Baichuan Li, Haibo Liang, Haiquan Deng, Lei Zhang, Mindong Lan, Xiangrong Cui, Shuzhen Li, Jianchao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAs a leading cause of disability worldwide, osteoarthritis (OA) progressively degrades articular cartilage. The incomplete understanding of OA's molecular mechanisms hinders development of disease-modifying treatments.MethodsWe analyzed GEO database knee OA datasets to track TRIM3 expression dynamics throughout disease progression. Western blot and IHC quantified TRIM3 protein differences between OA and normal cartilage. TRIM3-knockdown chondrocytes showed altered Bcl-2/Bax ratios via qRT-PCR/Western blot, with p-AKT/p-mTOR levels indicating AKT/mTOR activation. Therapeutic targeting of TRIM3 may represent a promising strategy to attenuate cartilage degeneration in OA.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), as a highly prevalent chronic degenerative joint disease, represents the leading global cause of chronic pain and functional disability. Its pathological hallmarks include progressive cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and periarticular tissue remodeling (encompassing osteophyte formation and subchondral bone sclerosis) [1–3]. Articular cartilage, a connective tissue composed of chondrocytes and their secreted extracellular matrix (ECM), provides essential lubrication and biomechanical support for joint function. Notably, OA pathophysiology is closely associated with dysregulated chondrocyte autophagy [3], where aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway—a critical regulator of autophagy—can suppress chondrocyte autophagy while promoting apoptosis [4, 5]. Although chondrocytes constitute merely 1% of total cartilage volume, they serve as central regulators of ECM homeostasis. Current evidence indicates that inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to concurrently induce chondrocyte apoptosis and autophagy inhibition [5, 6], a dual effect that accelerates OA progression. Despite advances in symptomatic management, existing therapies remain palliative, failing to effectively decelerate disease progression, control persistent synovitis, or restore dysfunctional chondrocytes. Thus, elucidating its pathogenesis is crucial for identifying molecular targets for disease-modifying interventions.

The TRIM protein family, a group of ubiquitous multifunctional proteins, plays pivotal roles in cellular regulation and disease pathogenesis. Family members typically modulate intracellular signaling pathways to influence cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, and survival [7–9]. TRIM3 (also termed Ro52/TSS-62), a key member of the TRIM (tripartite motif) family, harbors characteristic RING, B-box, and coiled-coil domains that confer functional versatility and interaction capacity [10–12]. Emerging studies demonstrate TRIM3’s involvement in critical biological processes including apoptosis [10], cell cycle progression, migration, and proteasomal degradation. Significantly, other TRIM family members have been implicated in chondrocyte metabolism: for instance, TRIM15 is markedly upregulated in senescent chondrocytes from OA patients and contributes to OA progression by promoting cellular senescence [13], while TRIM8 and TRIM59 regulate ECM metabolism [14, 15]. These findings underscore the TRIM family’s regulatory importance in cartilage homeostasis and OA development. However, research linking TRIM3 to arthritis remains scarce. While TRIM3's role in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) has been preliminarily investigated (showing downregulation in RA synovial tissue compared to controls) [16], its function in osteoarthritis (OA) pathogenesis remains unclear. Specifically, it is unknown whether TRIM3 affects chondrocyte apoptosis and autophagy through AKT/mTOR pathway modulation. [5, 17, 18]. This knowledge gap obscures its mechanistic role in cartilage degeneration.

This study investigates TRIM3’s expression patterns and functional impact on OA-associated chondrocyte apoptosis, while delineating its regulatory relationship with the AKT/mTOR pathway during OA progression. Given that the AKT/mTOR pathway serves as a master regulator of both apoptosis and autophagy [5, 17, 19], its aberrant activation disrupts chondrocyte homeostasis [4, 20]. Our findings not only deepen understanding of programmed cell death mechanisms but also nominate TRIM3 as a potential therapeutic target for OA treatment and drug development.

Methods

GEO transcriptomic analysis of TRIM3 in knee osteoarthritis (KOA)

Transcriptomic and clinical data related to knee osteoarthritis (KOA) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.govgeo). The GSE114007 dataset included 18 normal cartilage samples and 20 KOA cartilage samples. Raw data were processed using R software (v4.3.1) to integrate clinical metadata and generate expression matrices. Perl scripts were employed to filter mRNA expression profiles by removing non-coding RNA data. For external validation, the GEO database was queried using the keyword "knee osteoarthritis" with species restricted to Homo sapiens, yielding the GSE113825 dataset comprising 5 KOA and 5 normal cartilage samples. Transcriptomic and clinical data from GSE113825 were downloaded via R packages (GEOquery), and probe IDs were annotated to standardized gene symbols. Combined with GSE114007 data, raw transcriptomic data underwent preprocessing steps, including background correction (RMA algorithm) and quantile normalization (limma package), to ensure comparability across datasets.

Patients and tissue samples

Normal cartilage tissues (control group, n = 3) were obtained from amputated limbs of three trauma patients. Degenerative cartilage samples (OA group) were collected from four osteoarthritis patients undergoing total joint replacement surgery. One OA sample was excluded due to unsuccessful protein extraction, resulting in a final analyzed OA group sample size of n = 3. (Table 1). Fresh tissues from the included samples were preserved either on ice (for immediate protein extraction) or in formalin (for subsequent immunohistochemical analysis). Patient age and sex information were not systematically recorded for this study. Written informed consent was acquired preoperatively from all participants. Rat chondrocyte isolation and culture

Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were supplied by the Experimental Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University. All experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Ethics Committee of The People's Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (Guangxi Academy of Medical Sciences) (Approval No. KY-KJT-2023-25). Chondrocytes were isolated from rat articular cartilage using enzymatic digestion methods as previously described in the literature [21]. Cell culture and subculturing were performed according to protocols established in our prior studies [22].

SiRNA transfection

siRNA Design and Preparation: TRIM3-specific siRNA duplexes (target sequence: 5′-GCAGCACAAGGCAGCUCUA-3′) or mTOR-specific siRNA duplexes (target sequence: 5′-GCCGCAUAGUCUCCAUCAA-3′) and negative control siRNA (scrambled sequence, non-targeting) were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Stock solutions (20 μM) were prepared in RNase-free water and stored at −80 °C.

Cell Seeding: Rat chondrocytes were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well and cultured in DMEM/F12 (10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin) until reaching 70–80% confluence (24 h after seeding), ensuring optimal transfection efficiency.

Transfection Reagent and Complex Preparation: Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) was used as the transfection reagent, following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor optimization. For each well: 5 μL Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 125 μL Opti-MEM (serum-free), and 5 μL P3000 reagent was mixed with 50 pmol TRIM3 siRNA (or negative control siRNA) in 125 μL Opti-MEM. The two mixtures were combined, incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form lipoplexes.

Transfection Induction: The culture medium of chondrocytes was replaced with 1.75 mL Opti-MEM (serum-free), and the lipoplex mixture (250 μL/well) was added dropwise. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM/F12 (10% FBS) to reduce cytotoxicity.

Knockdown Efficiency Verification: At 48 h post-transfection, total RNA and protein were extracted. TRIM3 or mTOR mRNA expression was detected by qRT-PCR, and protein level by Western blot, confirming > 70% knockdown efficiency in both mRNA and protein levels.

Subsequent Inflammatory Stimulation: After verifying knockdown efficiency (48 h post-transfection), chondrocytes were treated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β (to mimic OA inflammatory microenvironment) for 24 h, followed by detection of apoptosis (Annexin V/PI staining) and ECM-degrading enzymes (by Western blot/qRT-PCR).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cultured chondrocytes using TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was quantified spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and residual genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (RNase-free). DNA-free RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio).

qRT-PCR reactions (10μL total volume) contained 1μL cDNA, 5μL TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio), and 0.5μL each of forward/reverse primers (10 μM). Thermal cycling conditions on a Light Cycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany) included:Initial denaturation: 95 °C for 30 s. 40 cycles of: 95 °C for 10 s (denaturation), 60 °C for 40 s (annealing/extension). GAPDH served as the endogenous control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt)) method, normalized to GAPDH.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from cultured chondrocytes. Briefly, cells were washed thrice with PBS and lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (CWBIO, China) containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 30 min. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit, followed by denaturation with SDS-PAGE loading buffer (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China) at 95 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of proteins (20–50 μg) were resolved on 8–12% SDS–polyacrylamide gels (Beyotime, China) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA).Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST (10 mM Tris [pH 8.0], 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After three TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and quantified by ImageJ (NIH, USA). Protein expression was normalized to GAPDH as the internal control.

Antibodies:Anti-Col2a1, anti-TRIM3, anti-p-mTOR, anti-Bax, anti-Bcl-2, anti-p-AKT (Abcam, UK); Anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Human bone tissue specimens were fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Following deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol series, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) under microwave heating. Sections were then treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by 30 min incubation with 10% normal goat serum to reduce nonspecific binding. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary anti-TRIM3 antibody (1:100 dilution, Abcam, UK). After washing, biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500) and streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) complex were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate was used for chromogenic detection, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Rat chondrocytes transfected with siTRIM3 were treated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β (Peprotech, USA) for 24 h. Cells were then harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and washed twice with PBS. The cell pellet was resuspended in 400μL × Annexin V Binding Buffer (BD Biosciences).

Cells were divided into two aliquots:Experimental group: Stained with 5μL Annexin V-FITC (light-protected, 15 min at room temperature), followed by 10μL propidium iodide (PI). Unstained control: No dye added. Samples were analyzed immediately on a flow cytometer using 488 nm excitation. Apoptotic rates were calculated as the percentage of Annexin V + /PI − (early apoptosis) and Annexin V + /PI + (late apoptosis) cells.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cultured rat chondrocytes were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After three additional PBS washes, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody (anti-Col2a1, 1:100 dilution, Immunoway, Suzhou, China) was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following three PBS washes, cells were incubated with a Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:100, Boshi Biotechnology, China) under light-protected conditions for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (0.1 μg/mL) for 4 min. After final PBS washes, fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus IX73 inverted fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan) with appropriate filter sets.

Data analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, USA). Parametric data (normally distributed) were analyzed by Student’s t -test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, for multi-group comparisons). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Targeted inhibition of the AKT/mTOR signaling axis induces chondrocyte apoptosis

The AKT/mTOR signaling pathway exhibits a context-dependent role in regulating chondrocyte apoptosis. While hyperactivation of this pathway is generally associated with anti-apoptotic effects in various cell types, its specific regulatory mechanisms in chondrocyte apoptosis remain poorly characterized. To address this knowledge gap, we pharmacologically inhibited mTOR and analyzed apoptosis-related markers. Western blotting with densitometric quantification revealed that mTOR inhibition significantly downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (P = 0.006) while upregulating the pro-apoptotic protein Bax (P = 0.0037). Discussion

Osteoarthritis (OA), as the degenerative joint disease with the highest global disability rate, has been confirmed by multiple studies to involve abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in its pathogenesis [3, 20, 23].Our findings align with results showing that Tuina therapy alleviates cartilage degeneration by inhibiting this pathway [20], but contrast with the tumor cell model where TCP1 inhibits apoptosis via AKT/mTOR activation [17], suggesting tissue-specific regulatory patterns of this pathway in chondrocytes. Notably, while TRIM15 is also highly expressed in OA cartilage and promotes cellular senescence [13], TRIM3 exhibits unique pro-survival effects. This functional heterogeneity among TRIM family members may stem from their differential regulation of downstream signaling pathways.

Regarding apoptosis regulation, our TRIM3 knockdown results sharply contrast with reports in breast cancer models where TRIM3 deletion induces apoptosis through p53 accumulation [10], yet partially overlap with mechanisms where SIRT3 overexpression reduces apoptosis by suppressing the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [6, 24]. These regulatory discrepancies may reflect: 1) Cell-type dependency, as seen with TCP1 inhibiting apoptosis via AKT/mTOR activation in leukemia cells [17]; 2) Pathological stimulus specificity, where IL-1β-induced chondrocyte apoptosis is alleviated by 4-octyl itaconate (OI) through inhibition of the same pathway [5], while leptin suppresses autophagy via mTORC1 activation [3].

This suggests TRIM3 may function as an environmentally sensitive signaling node, with its ultimate effects determined by the overall balance of cytokine networks in the microenvironment.

For ECM synthesis findings, our data align with studies showing NT3 maintains cartilage homeostasis through PI3K/Akt signaling [25], but TRIM3 likely operates at a more upstream regulatory level. Reports that MFN2 overexpression causes metabolic disorders by inhibiting PI3K/AKT/mTOR [26] provide new perspectives for understanding the metabolic basis of TRIM3-ECM regulation. Notably, recent studies on BPA regulating mitophagy via the PTEN/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [27] suggest TRIM3 may influence ECM synthesis by modulating mitochondrial quality control—a hypothesis requiring further validation.

This study shares methodological limitations common to most OA mechanism research. For instance, the phenomenon where GDC-0349 still induces apoptosis in Akt1 knockout cells [28] suggests the importance of AKT/mTOR-independent pathways may be underestimated. Future studies could adopt: 1) The bidirectional validation strategy of Sox8 regulating autophagy via PI3K/AKT/mTOR [29]; 2) The multi-pathway interaction analysis framework of ERK1/2MAPK/Akt/mTOR/IRF3 cascades in keratinocytes [30].

These discoveries suggest new targeted strategy ideas for OA treatment: Compared to traditional broad-spectrum AKT/mTOR inhibitors, precise regulation of TRIM3 may achieve better risk–benefit ratios. Particularly for combination therapies, reference could be made to: 1) Curcumin and Probucol's synergistic regulation of apoptosis/autophagy balance via the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway [31]; or 2) The synergistic effects of TBMS1 and TMZ co-inhibiting the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR/NF-κB pathway [32].

Although this study reveals the potential mechanism by which TRIM3 influences chondrocyte apoptosis through the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, several limitations still need to be addressed in future research.

First, the sample size of clinical tissues used in this study was relatively small, which may affect the statistical power of the results. Future studies should expand the sample size or incorporate multicenter clinical data to further validate the role of TRIM3 in osteoarthritis progression. Second, regarding apoptosis analysis, this study lacks ultrastructural evidence such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation of apoptotic bodies and does not comprehensively examine the expression profiles of apoptosis-related genes (e.g., Caspase 3, PARP). Instead, it only assesses apoptosis trends through changes in the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, which may not fully capture the impact of TRIM3 on apoptotic pathways. Furthermore, although this study suggests that TRIM3 may promote chondrocyte apoptosis by suppressing the AKT/mTOR pathway, it only preliminarily explores a few key molecules (e.g., p-AKT, p-mTOR) without in-depth analysis of the upstream and downstream regulatory networks. Therefore, future studies should employ more systematic in vivo and in vitro experiments (e.g., gene knockout animal models, single-cell sequencing) to further elucidate the precise regulatory mechanism of TRIM3 in chondrocyte apoptosis.

In summary, the findings of this study provide new experimental evidence for the role of TRIM3 in osteoarthritis, but its molecular mechanisms still require deeper exploration. Future research should integrate multi-omics analysis and functional experiments to comprehensively decipher the regulatory network of TRIM3 in cartilage degeneration, thereby offering novel insights for targeted therapy of osteoarthritis.

Conclusion

This research reveals the potential mechanism of TRIM3 in cartilage degeneration in osteoarthritis. We found that TRIM3 may promote chondrocyte apoptosis and regulate chondrocyte matrix metabolism by inhibiting the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby promoting cartilage degeneration. These findings provide a new perspective for a deeper understanding of the pathological mechanism of osteoarthritis and suggest that TRIM3 may be a potential therapeutic target for osteoarthritis. Future research can further explore the role of TRIM3 in in vivo cartilage degeneration models and evaluate its feasibility as a therapeutic target.
