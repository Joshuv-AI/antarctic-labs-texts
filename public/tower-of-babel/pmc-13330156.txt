Isotemporal substitution analysis of the effects of in-kindergarten structured and unstructured activity behaviors on sleep problems in preschool children
Authors: Xuewen Hu, Weiquan Deng, Guanggao Zhao, Jinmei Fu, Delong Chen, Tianle Jiang, Yunong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUsing an isotemporal substitution model, this study analyzed the mutual substitution relationships among structured and unstructured physical activities of different intensities and sedentary behavior in kindergarten settings, and explored their associations with sleep problems in young children.MethodsA cross-sectional sample of 336 children aged 3–6 years was recruited. Five behavioral variables—SB, S-LPA, US-LPA, S-MVPA, and US-MVPA (LPA, light physical activity; MVPA, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity)—were measured using video observation, accelerometry, and a standardized assessment tool. Sleep problems were assessed with the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ). Linear regression and isotemporal substitution models were used to analyze the data.ResultsUnivariate model results showed that US-LPA (β=-0.440, 95%CI:-0.829 to -0.050, P = 0.027) and US-MVPA (β=-0.598, 95%CI:-1.092 to -0.104, P = 0.018) were significantly negatively associated with total sleep problems in preschool children. S-MVPA (β=-0.110, 95%CI:-0.212 to -0.008, P = 0.034) and US-MVPA (β=-0.064, 95%CI:-0.122 to -0.007, P = 0.029) were significantly negatively associated with SOD, and US-LPA (β=-0.172, 95%CI:-0.272 to -0.073, P = 0.001) and US-MVPA (β=-0.234, 95%CI:-0.361 to − 0.108, P < 0.001) were significantly negatively associated with SD. In the partition models and isotemporal substitution models, after Bonferroni correction, no other significant associations were observed.ConclusionIncreasing levels of unstructured LPA and unstructured MVPA helps preschool children maintain sufficient sleep duration and alleviate sleep onset delay, thereby improving their overall sleep problems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27669-7.Keywords: Structure, Unstructured, Activities, Sleep problems, Isochronous substitution, Children

Introduction

Sleep problems in early childhood have emerged as a significant public health concern. A national study involving 114,311 preschool children aged 3 ~ 5 years reported a prevalence of 76.78% for sleep disorders as assessed by the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire, with dimension-specific rates of 97.00% for bedtime resistance, 77.68% for daytime sleepiness, and 51.99% for sleep onset delay [1]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study in Shanghai found that 11.67% of kindergarten children aged 3 ~ 6 years had insufficient sleep (< 10 h for ages 3 ~ 5, < 9 h for age 6), and 56.85% experienced bedtime delay (after 21:00) [2]. These sleep disturbances are closely associated with adverse physical, cognitive, and behavioral outcomes in young children [3, 4], highlighting the urgent need for effective interventions. Consequently, identifying strategies to address sleep problems in early childhood has garnered widespread societal attention.

Existing studies have demonstrated significant associations between physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior (SB), and sleep problems in young children [5–7]. However, the direction, magnitude, and consistency of these associations vary across studies: some have reported protective effects of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) on sleep duration [8], while others have found no significant associations for specific sleep dimensions such as night wakings or sleep anxiety [8]. This heterogeneity suggests that it is necessary to differentiate not only by PA intensity but also by activity type. Notably, PA in early childhood encompasses both structured physical activity (S-PA)—defined as planned, goal-oriented activities with explicit rules (e.g., teacher-led physical education)—and unstructured physical activity (US-PA), characterized by spontaneous, child-initiated play without specific objectives [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that S-PA and US-PA may exert differential effects on health outcomes [10]; however, their distinct associations with sleep problems remain underexplored. Furthermore, whether these effects vary by intensity (e.g., light PA [LPA] vs. MVPA) within structured and unstructured contexts has not been systematically investigated.

The isotemporal substitution model (ISM) provides a robust framework for examining the theoretical impact of reallocating time between different movement behaviors on health outcomes [11]. Although compositional data analysis (CoDA) has also been proposed for time-use research [12], ISM offers distinct advantages in physical activity epidemiology: activity guidelines are typically expressed in absolute terms (e.g., minutes per day) rather than relative proportions, making ISM results more intuitive and directly translatable to public health recommendations [11, 13]. ISM also allows for symmetrical interpretation of substitution effects and has been widely applied in pediatric research [14].

Kindergartens serve as crucial settings for promoting healthy behavior development in young children, providing an optimal environment for implementing both structured and unstructured physical activities [15–17]. Recent policy documents including the “Outline for Building a Leading Sports Nation” and “Opinions on Comprehensively Strengthening and Improving School Sports in the New Era” have explicitly advocated for “establishing a physical education curriculum system for young children” and “developing play activities suitable for children’s physical and mental characteristics” [18, 19]. As the primary manifestations of structured physical activity (S-PA) and unstructured physical activity (US-PA) in early childhood, kindergarten physical education curricula and play activities hold significant implications for the prevention and management of sleep problems in young children. Building upon the methodological foundation established in our preliminary research for measuring S-PA and US-PA in kindergarten settings [10], this study investigates the isotemporal substitution effects of structured-unstructured activity behaviors on sleep problems in young children, aiming to enrich and refine the research framework for studying structured and unstructured activities in early childhood.

Study participants and methods

Study participants

This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. [2020] No. 125).

This study employed a cross-sectional design with stratified cluster sampling. All 11 prefecture-level cities in Jiangxi Province were stratified into three tiers (high, middle, and low) based on per capita GDP levels. One city was randomly selected from each tier, resulting in the selection of Yingtan (middle level), Pingxiang (low level), and Ganzhou (high level) as the survey sites. These cities were reasonably representative in terms of population size, geographical distribution, and socioeconomic development, reflecting the characteristics of regions at varying developmental levels across Jiangxi Province. Three kindergartens were randomly selected from each city, yielding a total of nine kindergartens participating in the study. Leveraging the kindergarten research network previously established by our research team, we contacted the kindergartens and introduced the study to the principals and teachers. With their assistance, parent meetings were organized to explain the project details to parents. A total of 372 children were recruited as participants. After excluding those with invalid physical activity data, incomplete questionnaires, or missing information, the final valid sample comprised 336 children (170 boys and 166 girls). Flowchart of sample selection

Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 3–6 years; (2) generally healthy without motor impairments; (3) parents or primary guardians provided written informed consent prior to the survey. Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete physical activity measurement; (2) incomplete Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire or basic information; (3) insufficient in-campus video monitoring data to cover a full day. All participants maintained their usual physical activity and sleep routines during the study period.

Measurement indicators and methods

Measurement of structured and unstructured activity behaviors

Physical activity measurement

Triaxial accelerometers (ActiGraph GT3X-BT, Pensacola, FL, USA) were used to measure physical activity (PA) levels in young children over five consecutive weekdays. To ensure data accuracy, a parent meeting was held prior to the measurement period to explain the procedures and precautions, and parents were instructed to have their children wear the accelerometers continuously (except during bathing or swimming) throughout the entire measurement period. A WeChat group including all participating parents and researchers was established to provide daily reminders and address any questions. Parents assisted with donning and doffing the devices during the measurement period. On weekdays, researchers checked and adjusted the devices twice daily (morning and evening). At the end of the measurement period, the devices were collected, and the data were downloaded and processed using ActiLife (Version 6.13.4).

Parameter settings for the ActiGraph GT3X-BT were determined based on relevant literature [20, 21]. The settings included physical activity intensity cut-points (Butte cut-points) [22], epoch length (15 s), wear time definition (Choi algorithm+) [23], valid day criterion (≥ 480 min of wear time per day), and valid data inclusion criterion (at least two weekdays). The cut-points for different intensities were as follows: moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA): counts ≥ 2120/60s; light physical activity (LPA): 240/60s≤counts ≤ 2119/60s; sedentary behavior (SB): counts ≤ 239/60 s.

Assessment of structured and unstructured activity behaviors

Structured and unstructured activity behavior characteristics in kindergarten settings were recorded using DJI Osmo 4 K sports cameras (produced by DJI, Shenzhen, China). Equipment parameters, placement positions, and observation protocols were established with reference to previous studies [24, 25]. A fixed installation approach was employed for kindergarten observations: one camera per indoor room, and 2–3 cameras per outdoor activity area, resulting in a total of 8–28 cameras per kindergarten. Video monitoring covered all activity spaces utilized by the participating children. Indoor cameras were positioned in corners providing complete visual coverage of the entire room (Fig. 2-left), while outdoor cameras were installed at elevated vantage points offering full panoramic observation (Fig. 2-right).

Schematic diagram of young children’s activity behavior monitoring video (left: indoor; right: outdoor)

The “Structured-Unstructured Physical Activity Assessment Scale for Young Children” was developed based on the Observational System for Recording Physical Activity in Children-Preschool Version (OSRAC-P) [26], with reference to the System for Observing Play and Recreation in Communities (SOPARC), World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, and previous relevant studies [27–29] (See Table 1 for details). The scale comprises 14 activity type indicators: Preacademic, Play, Gross motor, Manipulative, Exercise, Teacher arranged, Sociodramatic, Videos, Large area activities, Team time, Self-care, Time-out, Transition, and Mealtime. The validity of the assessment scale was evaluated by five child health experts, achieving an average score of 95.8 points. After the test, two trained researchers independently reviewed the video recordings and matched them with the assessment criteria in Table 1 to categorize children’s structured and unstructured behaviors. In cases of disagreement, the final determination was made through group discussion.

Sleep problem assessment

Sleep problems in young children were assessed using the Chinese version of the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) developed by Professor Judith from Brown University Pediatrics Department, USA [30, 31]. Following the physical activity testing period, questionnaires were distributed to the participating children’s parents, who were instructed to complete them based on their children’s sleep habits during the PA assessment period. The questionnaire comprises 33 items evaluating eight sleep problem dimensions: Bedtime Resistance, Sleep Onset Delay, Sleep Duration, Sleep Anxiety, Night Waking, Parasomnias, Sleep-Disordered Breathing, and Daytime Sleepiness. Each dimension employs a three-point scoring system: “1” indicating “usually” (5–7 times/week), “2” indicating “sometimes” (2–4 times/week), and “3” indicating “rarely” (0–1 times/week). Scores were calculated according to specific weighting methods for each dimension, with overall sleep problems subsequently evaluated. The internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.7, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The KMO measure of sampling adequacy was 0.799, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity showed significant results (P < 0.001), demonstrating good construct validity for the questionnaire.

Basic information collection

Referring to relevant studies [32, 33], covariates for adjustment models were identified and corresponding information was collected. An online questionnaire was distributed to parents or primary guardians of young children via a WeChat group to collect information on children’s gender, age, urban/rural classification, and parental economic status. Height (cm) and weight (kg) were measured using instruments, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0, and a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive analyses were conducted for participants’ baseline characteristics, accelerometer wear time, physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior (SB), and sleep problems. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), while non‑normally distributed data were described as median (interquartile range). Independent‑samples *t*‑tests and Mann‑Whitney U tests were used to examine sex differences in wear time, PA, SB, overall sleep problems, and subscale scores.

A total of 99 linear regression models were established, including 45 univariate models, 9 partition models, and 45 isotemporal substitution models, to assess the associations between different activity behaviors and sleep problems as well as their substitution effects. Based on previous studies [34–37], 10 min was chosen as the initial substitution unit. Before running the models, the time spent in each activity type (SB, S‑LPA, US‑LPA, S‑MVPA, and US‑MVPA) was divided by the constant 10 to create time variables expressed in 10‑minute units, where a one‑unit increase corresponds to an additional 10 min per day. In addition, a variable representing total accelerometer wear time was created by summing all activity durations and was used in the isotemporal substitution analyses.

To control for type I error inflation due to multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction for the main behavioral variables in the partition models and isotemporal substitution models. Univariate models, which tested only one independent variable each, were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. The overall significance of all models was determined using an F‑test with P < 0.05.

Research results

Basic characteristics of the young children

In terms of anthropometric characteristics, boys had significantly higher height, weight, and BMI than girls (P < 0.05; Cohen’sd = 0.27, 0.31, and 0.24, respectively). Regarding movement behaviors, boys exhibited significantly higher levels of structured LPA (S-LPA) and structured MVPA (S-MVPA) than girls (P < 0.05; d = 0.35 and 0.43, respectively), while no significant sex differences were observed for other behavioral variables (Table 3). Variance inflation factors (VIF) for the five movement behaviors ranged from 1.42 to 8.866. Although the maximum VIF approached conventional thresholds (e.g., VIF > 5 or 10), indicating potential multicollinearity concerns, all VIF values remained below 10. Given the compositional nature of time-use data, subsequent analyses employed partition and isotemporal substitution models to account for the interdependent structure of movement behaviors.

Associations between structured-unstructured activity behaviors and sleep problems in young children

In the univariate models, structured MVPA (β=-0.110, 95%CI:-0.212 to -0.008, P = 0.034) and unstructured MVPA (β=-0.064, 95%CI:-0.122 to -0.007, P = 0.029) were significantly negatively associated with SOD. SB (β=-0.044, 95%CI:-0.069 to -0.020, P < 0.001), unstructured LPA (β=-0.172, 95%CI:-0.272 to -0.073, P = 0.001), and unstructured MVPA (β=-0.234, 95%CI:-0.361 to -0.108, P < 0.001) were significantly negatively associated with SD. Unstructured LPA (β=-0.440, 95%CI:-0.829 to -0.050, P = 0.027) and unstructured MVPA (β=-0.598, 95%CI: -1.092 to -0.104, P = 0.018) showed significant negative associations with total sleep problems.

In the partition models, before correction, SB was significantly negatively associated with SD (β=-0.033, 95%CI: -0.062 to -0.003, rawP = 0.028), and structured MVPA was significantly negatively associated with SOD (β=-0.216, 95%CI: -0.409 to − 0.023, rawP = 0.028). However, after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (corrected significance threshold P < 0.01), neither association remained statistically significant (adjusted P > 0.05) (Appendix 1). No significant associations were observed between the other activity behaviors and any sleep dimension (Table 4).

Changes in young children’s sleep problems following 10-minute isotemporal substitution of structured-unstructured activity behaviors

The results of the isotemporal substitution models (Table 5) showed that when S‑MVPA replaced SB (β=-0.207, 95%CI:-0.400 to -0.014, raw P = 0.036), S‑LPA (β=-0.342, 95%CI:-0.660 to -0.023, raw P = 0.036), or US‑LPA (β=-0.232, 95%CI: -0.416 to -0.048, raw P = 0.014) for 10 min, children’s sleep onset delay (SOD) showed a decreasing trend. However, after applying the Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons (four substitution effects were tested; corrected threshold P < 0.0125), none of the above raw P-values remained below the corrected threshold, and the differences were no longer statistically significant (Appendix 1). No significant associations were observed for the remaining substitution effects.

Discussion

As the first isotemporal substitution study investigating structured-unstructured activity behaviors and sleep problems in young children, and to our knowledge, the inaugural study addressing multidimensional sleep issues through this methodology, this research aims to examine the comprehensive effects of mutually substituting different intensities of structured-unstructured physical activities and sedentary behaviors within the same temporal framework on sleep problems in young children. This approach further expands both the academic depth and practical applicability of the research.

Numerous studies have confirmed that physical activity (PA) can effectively reduce the occurrence of sleep problems in young children [38, 39]. The present study further found that unstructured LPA and unstructured MVPA were significantly negatively associated with overall sleep problems in young children, while structured LPA and structured MVPA showed no significant associations. This suggests that the health benefits of PA on overall sleep problems primarily derive from unstructured physical activity (US-PA), characterized by absence of specific purposes, diverse forms, variable rules, and spontaneous nature. The potential mechanisms underlying US-PA’s influence on sleep problems may include: (1) US-PA, typically conducted through free play and autonomous exploration, better aligns with children’s physical and psychological characteristics [40], facilitating appropriate energy expenditure, increasing sleep pressure, and promoting deeper and longer sleep duration, thereby reducing nighttime awakenings and sleep onset difficulties. (2) US-PA often generates positive emotional experiences, alleviating anxiety and stress, consequently reducing the probability of sleep problems. (3) US-PA naturally integrates into children’s daily routines, helping stabilize sleep-wake patterns and strengthen circadian rhythms, thus positively regulating sleep-wake cycles. This study also found that both S-MVPA and US-MVPA exhibit significant negative effects on children’s sleep onset delay (SOD), while US-LPA and US-MVPA show significant negative effects on sleep duration (SD). These findings indicate that US-LPA and US-MVPA provide health benefits in improving sleep onset delay and sleep duration issues in young children, while S-MVPA demonstrates health benefits specifically for addressing sleep onset delay.

Results from the isotemporal substitution models in this study showed that, at the level of raw P-values, replacing SB, S-LPA, or US-LPA with 10 min of S-MVPA was associated with a decreasing trend in children’s SOD. However, after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, none of the raw P-values remained significant. This suggests that replacing SB, S-LPA, or US-LPA with 10 min of S-MVPA has a small effect on sleep onset delay, but this effect does not reach statistical robustness, and these associations may be due to random fluctuation rather than a true substitution effect.Several factors may explain these findings: 1)The effect sizes observed in this study were small (absolute regression coefficients < 0.35), and the upper limits of the 95% confidence intervals were close to zero (e.g., the upper limit for the SB substitution effect was − 0.014), indicating instability of the point estimates. Even though the raw P-value reached 0.014, it was still insufficient to reject the null hypothesis after strict correction. 2)The sample size of this study was limited and may have been inadequate to detect small effect sizes. Post‑hoc power analysis suggested that to achieve 80% power to detect an effect of β≈-0.2 (α = 0.0125), the required sample size would be much larger than the current one. Future research should conduct large-scale prospective cohort studies or randomised controlled trials to clarify the true effects of substituting different intensities of structured and unstructured physical activities on sleep outcomes in preschool children.

Although this study strictly followed the design protocol for measurements and employed scientific and rigorous statistical analyses and model building, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size may be insufficient, and due to the constraints of large-sample measurement, sleep problems were assessed using subjective questionnaires. Second, the findings are based on cross-sectional data; therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to establish causal relationships between structured/unstructured activity behaviors and sleep problems in preschool children.

Conclusions and prospects

Conclusions

Univariate analysis revealed that unstructured light and moderate-to-vigorous physical activities were negatively associated with total sleep problems, sleep onset delay, and sleep duration in preschool children. However, after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons in the partition models and isotemporal substitution models, none of these associations remained statistically significant. These findings suggest that the potential benefits of unstructured physical activities on sleep should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger sample sizes and rigorous control of multiple comparisons are needed to confirm these results.

Prospects

Conduct large‑scale prospective cohort studies or randomised controlled trials

Although the isotemporal substitution models in this study suggested that replacing other activity behaviours with S‑MVPA had a small effect on sleep onset delay, this effect did not reach statistical robustness. Future research should conduct large‑scale prospective cohort studies or randomised controlled trials to clarify the true effects of substituting different intensities of structured and unstructured physical activities on sleep outcomes in preschool children.

Exploring the multiple isotemporal substitution effects of structured and unstructured activity behaviors in young children

Young children’s activity behaviors exhibit complex interactions. While this study has revealed the isotemporal substitution effects of pairwise substitutions between structured and unstructured activity behaviors on sleep problems, the health effects of multiple isotemporal substitutions among various activity behaviors warrant further in-depth investigation.

Exploring the isotemporal substitution effects of structured and unstructured physical activity on sleep problems in special populations of young children

Compared to healthy young children, special pediatric populations with conditions such as autism spectrum disorder [41], attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [42], and sleep-disordered breathing [43] demonstrate more prominent sleep problems, along with greater complexity in their activity behaviors and other variables. Consequently, investigating the isotemporal substitution effects of structured and unstructured physical activities in these populations carries substantial significance. Therefore, future research should prioritize conducting isotemporal substitution studies focusing on structured and unstructured physical activities in special pediatric populations. Such investigations would provide evidence-based guidance for addressing sleep problem development in these children and contribute to comprehensive sleep health promotion during early childhood.
