Serotype-specific pneumococcal conjugate vaccine effectiveness in Canadian children: an indirect cohort study using Bayesian hierarchical modeling of multisite data
Authors: Zhou Zhou, Geneviève Deceuninck, Nicholas Brousseau, Allison McGeer, Monika Naus, James D Kellner, Hannah Sell, Manish Sadarangani, Philippe De Wals
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) effectiveness (VE) against invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) varies across Streptococcus pneumoniae serotypes (STs). Combining data from multiple sites helps obtain reliable serotype-specific VE estimates but introduces a hierarchical structure. Ignoring this structure can bias results and overstate statistical significance. This study compared Bayesian hierarchical model (BHM) and frequentist fixed-effect model (FFEM) to estimate serotype-specific VE using multisite surveillance data.MethodsThe indirect cohort method was used to estimate serotype-specific VE by immunization status and time since the last dose. IPD cases among children aged 2-59 months were identified according to laboratory and clinical criteria in four Canadian provinces from 2010 to 2019. The FFEM adjusted for province as a fixed effect, whereas the BHM modeled province as a random intercept to account for province-level variability. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) quantified the proportion of variance attributable to province.ResultsA total of 931 IPD cases were included in the analysis. Based on BHM, ICCs indicated that province-level variation accounted for more than 20% of total variance for ST-7F and ST-19F and approximately 5% for ST-3 and ST-19A. Under PCV13 ≥1 dose, VE was 90% (95% credible intervals [CrI] 68 to 97) for ST-19F, 86% (95%CrI: 37 to 97) for ST-7F, 63% (95%CrI: 26 to 81) for ST-19A and 56% (95%CrI: -9 to 82) for ST-3. VE waned over time, particularly for ST-3, decreasing from 80% (95%Crl: 39 to 94) within 12 months after the third PCV13 dose to -44% (95CrI: -272 to 43) thereafter. When compared with FFEM, BHM produced more stable VE estimates in data-sparse strata and yielded more conservative credible intervals. FFEM, by contrast, often failed to converge.ConclusionsPCV vaccine effectiveness varies across serotypes. Introduction

Pneumococcal diseases are caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae (Sp), an encapsulated bacterium that is classified into about 100 different serotypes (STs) according to the phenotypic characteristics of their polysaccharide capsule [1]. Invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPD), including septicemia, meningitis and pneumonia, are serious conditions in children although their incidence has markedly reduced since the introduction of publicly funded immunization programs using pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) worldwide [2]. Since the introduction of the first 7-valent PCV (PCV7), which targets serotypes 4, 6B, 9 V, 14, 18 C, 19 F and 23 F, for children in the early 2000s, other PCVs covering a higher number of serotypes have been mostly authorized on the basis of immunogenicity studies demonstrating non-inferiority to an already authorized vaccine for shared serotypes and meeting predefined antibody thresholds for additional serotypes [3]. Among the authorized formulations used in Canada, 10-valent PCV (PCV10) targets serotypes 1, 5 and 7 F in addition to those included in PCV7, and 13-valent PCV (PCV13) additionally covers serotypes 3, 6 A, and 19 A [4].

Vaccine effectiveness (VE) estimates are thus essentially derived from observational post-marketing studies. One landmark study in the UK showed variation in PCV VE according to Sp serotypes [5]. In a context of low incidence of serotype-specific IPD, combining datasets from multiple sites is a solution to obtain reliable serotype-specific VE estimates [6]. However, multisite datasets inherently exhibit hierarchical data structures due to regional differences in demographics, epidemiological patterns, healthcare practices, and IPD surveillance systems. Failure to appropriately model this hierarchical structure can lead to biased VE estimates, overestimation of statistical significance, and misleading inference [7]. In multicenter studies, clustering is frequently ignored or handled using standard logistic regression models with a fixed center effect, referred to here as frequentist fixed-effects models (FFEM) [6, 8, 9]. When data are sparse, FFEM often experience convergence difficulties and unstable estimates [10]. Frequentist hierarchical (mixed-effects) logistic models (FHM), which account for clustering via random effects, require joint estimation of fixed effects and variance components and are more prone to numerical instability and identifiability issues [11]. In addition, maximum likelihood estimation in hierarchical models can be biased when variance components are large [12]. In contrast, Bayesian hierarchical modeling (BHM) incorporates prior distributions that regularize estimation in sparse-data contexts, yielding coherent uncertainty estimates without overstating precision [13]. Given the widespread use of FFEM in multicenter studies, comparison with BHM therefore has direct practical relevance.

In Canada, publicly funded immunization programs are managed and financed by provinces and territories, resulting in variations in PCV schedules and IPD surveillance systems [14–16]. Geographical variation in IPD incidence rates and serotype distribution is also present [17]. This context provides an opportunity to apply BHM to assess serotype-specific PCV effectiveness using multisite Canadian data.

This study aimed to estimate the serotype-specific VE of PCV10 and PCV13 against ST-3, ST-7 F, ST-19 A, and ST-19 F IPD in children from four Canadian provinces: British Columbia (BC), Alberta (AB), Ontario (ON), and Quebec (QC), to compare the performance of BHM and FFEM in a hierarchical data structure, while also assessing FHM.

Methods

Design and study population

The indirect cohort design (Broome method) was applied to estimate the serotype-specific effectiveness of PCV10 and PCV13 against vaccine-type IPD (VT-IPD) [18]. This case-only approach compares the vaccination status of VT-IPD cases with that of non-vaccine-type IPD (NVT-IPD) cases, defined as infections caused by serotypes that are neither included in PCV13 nor considered PCV13-related (e.g., 6 C). VE was estimated by comparing the odds of vaccination versus no vaccination between VT-IPD and NVT-IPD cases using logistic regression models. IPD cases in children aged 2 to 59 months with disease onset between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019, were identified through population-based surveillance systems in AB, BC, ON, and QC. Province-wide surveillance was conducted in BC and QC, while in AB and ON, surveillance was restricted to major urban regions: the Calgary area (AB) and Toronto-Peel region (ON). Further details on the study sites, population sizes, PCV program implementation timelines, and data sources are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Case definition, immunization data and covariables

IPD was defined as the isolation or detection of Sp from a normally sterile site or fluid by culture or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in a child with a clinical picture compatible with pneumococcal infection. IPD is a notifiable disease in all Canadian provinces and public health authorities are authorized to collect information on demographic variables, the clinical presentation and outcome, IPD risk factors, results of diagnostic tests and the immunization status of the case. Procedures used to assess the immunization status of cases varied by province. In AB and BC, vaccination data were mainly obtained from provincial immunization registries, and medical records were reviewed when the information was incomplete. In ON, parents or legal guardians and children’s health care providers were systematically contacted to assess the immunization status. In QC, immunization registry data were supplemented by the interview of parents or legal guardians for cases with an incomplete number of vaccine doses. The list of variables collected across all centers is provided in Supplementary Table S2. PCV doses received at least 14 days before disease onset were taken into account. High-risk medical status (presence/absence) was defined as the presence of one or more conditions known to be risk factors for IPD, including severe prematurity, immunodeficiency, hemoglobinopathy, asplenia, chronic pulmonary, cardiac, hepatic, neurological or renal disease, diabetes, cochlear implant, cerebrospinal fluid leak, malignancy, and organ transplantation.

Statistical analyses

The primary study outcome was serotype-specific VE against IPD caused by ST-3, ST-7 F, ST-19 A, and ST-19 F. Analyses focused on PCV10 and PCV13, the two vaccines in widespread use in Canada during the study period.

Three logistic regression models were constructed to estimate VE, each based on a different classification of immunization status. For each serotype-specific outcome, three separate models (Models 1–3) were fitted independently. Models 1 and 2 estimated VE irrespective of age at vaccination or time since the last dose. Model 1 defined immunization status based only on vaccine schedule (PCV10, PCV13, PCV7, or mixed), regardless of the number of doses received. Model 2 incorporated both vaccine schedule and number of doses (1, 2, or ≥ 3). Model 3 estimated VE by time since the third dose (< 12 months vs. ≥12 months after dose 3) among children who had received at least three doses by 18 months of age. Model 1 included province, calendar year (ten single-year categories from 2010 to 2019), age group (four categories), and binary indicators for high-risk medical status (presence/absence) and for missing high-risk status. Models 2 and 3 included the same covariates except age group, as age is highly correlated with the number of vaccine doses received. Vaccination schedule categories and covariates are detailed in Supplementary Table S3, and full mathematical model specifications are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

In the FFEM, province and calendar year were included as categorical covariates. The FHM was fitted for each specification using the lme4 package [11], with province modeled as a random intercept to account for clustering. Convergence of the FHM was evaluated using optimizer warnings, Hessian diagnostics, eigenvalue ratios, and singular fit indicators. In the BHM, province was modeled as a random intercept, and calendar year was additionally tested as a random slope to allow for region-specific temporal variation. The BHM was fitted using the Stan probabilistic programming language [19], through the cmdstanr R package, version 0.9.0 [20]. Each model was fitted using four parallel Markov chains of 50,000 iterations each, including a 5,000 iterations warm-up. Weakly informative priors were specified following the prior choice recommendations [21]. Model convergence was assessed using the Gelman–Rubin statistic (R̂), with all parameters close to 1.00 and effective sample sizes (bulk and tail ESS) exceeding recommended thresholds. Model selection within the Bayesian framework, including the decision to model calendar year as a random slope or a fixed effect, was guided by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) and the widely applicable information criterion (WAIC) [22]. In the final BHM, calendar year was retained as a fixed effect. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was estimated to quantify province-level clustering. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1 [23].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Comité d’éthique de la recherche du CHU de Québec - Université Laval, the University of British Columbia Research Ethics Board (REB), the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board, and the Sinai Health Research Ethics Board. Each participating center obtained approval from its institutional Research Ethics Board before data collection. These Research Ethics Boards operate in compliance with national and international ethical guidelines, including the Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement 2 and ICH-GCP, which reflect the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. In AB and ON, informed consent was obtained from all participants or, in the case of minors, from their parents or legal guardians, as approved by the respective Research Ethics Boards. In BC, the study was classified as minimal risk and the use of de-identified surveillance and immunization data collected by the British Columbia Centre for Disease Control and the provincial immunization registry under the public health mandate was approved with a waiver of consent by the University of British Columbia Behavioural Research Ethics Board. In QC, ethics approval authorized access to nominative data on IPD cases held by the Quebec Ministry of Health. Informed consent was obtained from participants who were interviewed or, in the case of minors, from their parents or legal guardians. All case records were de-identified before transmission to the coordinating center under data-sharing agreements compliant with applicable provincial legislation and ethics board requirements.

Results

Of the 931 IPD cases included in the study, 90 (10%) were from BC, 150 (16%) from AB, 180 (19%) from ON, and 511 (55%) from QC. Overall, 72 cases (7.7%) were due to PCV10 serotypes (including 28 ST-7 F and 22 ST-19 F), 295 (31.7%) to additional PCV13 serotypes (including 78 ST-3 and 217 ST-19 A), and 564 (60.6%) to NVT. High-risk status was available for 95% of cases. Details on the distribution of IPD cases according to study variables are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Over the 10-year study period, annual IPD cases decreased from 126 in 2010 to 77 in 2019, driven mainly by the reduction in ST-19 A cases, which declined from 74 to 4 (Fig. 1, Table S5), while ST-3 and ST-19 F remained relatively stable. Children younger than 18 months accounted for 45.4% of cases and had a higher proportion of NVT infections than older children (70.4% vs. 52.4%; Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S5). The breakdown by province is presented in Supplementary Figures S1 and S2.

Under PCV13 ≥ 1 dose, VE was highest for ST-19 F (90%, 95% CrI [68, 97]) and ST-7 F (86%, 95% CrI [37, 97]), and lower for ST-19 A (63%, 95% CrI [26, 81]) and ST-3 (56%, 95% CrI [-9, 82]). For PCV10 ≥ 1 dose, VE estimates against ST-3 and ST-19 A were associated with wide credible and confidence intervals and included the null value. For ST-19 A, VE was estimated at 27% (95% CrI [-82, 71]) in the BHM and 41% (95% CI [-57, 78]) in the FFEM. The FFEM produced similar point estimates to those of the BHM but experienced convergence issues in sparse data strata. Its confidence intervals were less conservative, as province-level variance was not considered. For both vaccines, VE after 2 doses tended to be higher than that after ≥ 3 doses, which may reflect shorter observation periods among 2-dose recipients.

Discussion

This study estimated serotype-specific VE using Bayesian hierarchical and conventional frequentist fixed-effect models based on multisite surveillance data. Although both approaches generally produced consistent VE patterns, BHM performed better under sparse data conditions and allowed the estimation of province-level ICCs to quantify clustering.

Serotype-specific VE estimates

Our findings show that VE varies across serotypes. PCV13 was most effective against ST-7 F (≥ 1 dose = 86%) and ST-19 F (≥ 1 dose = 90%), and less effective against ST-19 A (≥ 1 dose = 63%), and ST-3 (≥ 1 dose = 56%). These figures are congruent with those observed in the multicentric European SpIDnet study: PCV13 VE ≥ 1 dose was 91% for ST-7 F, 84% for ST-19 F, 83% for ST-19 A, and 65% for ST-3 [6]. For many exposure strata, posterior intervals were wide and those obtained for PCV10 and PCV13 overlapped. As a consequence, a comparison of the relative performance of the two vaccines should be interpreted with care. Protection also waned with time since vaccination for all serotypes, a finding consistent with the results of an immunogenicity study showing a decrease in the level of antibody concentration for all PCV13 serotypes in vaccinated children [24], and with the results of a VE study on PCV7 and PCV13 in the United Kingdom [9]. Waning of protection was particularly marked for ST-3, with PCV13 conferring a good level of protection during the first year following the toddler booster dose (80%) but no protection thereafter. This rapid waning, combined with potential serotype replacement, may explain the limited impact of PCV13 introduction on the incidence of ST-3 IPD among children in many countries [25].

In this study covering nearly 10 million person-years of observation, serotype-specific VE for PCV10 and PCV13 could only be estimated for the four most frequent vaccine serotypes (ST-3, ST-7 F, ST-19 A and ST-19 F). Other vaccine serotypes were already rare before PCV introduction or were practically eliminated during the PCV7 era [9]. Together, these results underscore the importance of combining and expanding multisite data to ensure reliable VE estimation.

The indirect cohort method first described by Broome and coworkers [18] was used in our study. This case-only approach has two advantages. First, it can be performed using IPD surveillance data only, provided that the surveillance program collects information on all invasive pneumococcal infections and pneumococcal isolates are available for serotyping irrespective of the immunization status. Second, by restricting the analysis to individuals with IPD, this method naturally controls for underlying risk factors and healthcare-seeking behaviors that may confound VE estimates. The major assumption in the indirect cohort design is that PCVs provide no protection against and do not increase the risk of NVT IPD, otherwise VE would be respectively under- or over-estimated. In a study in the USA, VE estimates of PCV7 against VT IPD in children were very similar using a classic case-control method with healthy children as controls and by the indirect cohort method [26]. This result supports the validity of the indirect cohort method to measure PCV VE.

Bayesian hierarchical versus frequentist fixed-effect models

The BHM generally yielded more conservative VE estimates, reflecting the use of a hierarchical structure that incorporates province-level random effects to account for geographic variability [7, 13]. Through partial pooling across strata, BHM shrinks stratum-specific estimates toward the group mean [27]. This produced more stable parameter estimates and coherent credible intervals under sparse data conditions (e.g., ST-3, ST-19 F). We specified weakly informative priors and did not center prior distributions on overall average VE or on serotype-specific VE estimates derived from other studies. Although such priors may improve precision when data are sparse, they would shrink posterior estimates toward historical expectations, whereas an overall average prior would pull serotype-specific estimates toward the mean and could attenuate true heterogeneity, as observed for ST-3 [28]. In contrast, FFEM often failed to converge and generate valid confidence intervals for rare serotypes, undermining the reliability of inference [10, 29]. Although FHM was fitted, it did not yield reliable estimates due to data sparsity (see Supplementary Table S6). This comparison should be interpreted primarily as a comparison between a Bayesian hierarchical model and a frequentist fixed-effect model, rather than as a pure Bayesian versus frequentist contrast.

In multisite studies, hierarchical modeling is essential for valid and unbiased estimation. As Nezlek succinctly noted, “analysts should use multilevel modeling when they have a multilevel data structure - pure and simple” [30]. Ignoring clustering can underestimate uncertainty and introduce bias particularly when the between-group variance is large [12, 13]. In our study, provinces were modeled as higher-level grouping factors to reflect interprovincial heterogeneity. While conventional fixed-effect models remain a useful analytical starting point, their results should always be validated against hierarchical models to ensure that clustering has not distorted inference. Hierarchical models also explicitly quantify regional variability through the intraclass correlation coefficient, providing insight into the proportion of observed variance attributable to between-province differences. This was especially relevant for ST-7 F and ST-19 F, where ICCs exceeded 20%, highlighting the need to account for clustering.

Convergence failure is a common problem in regression analyses involving low or zero frequencies in many cells [11, 31, 32]. In contrast, the BHM remained numerically stable under sparse data conditions due to partial pooling. This does not necessarily result in narrower intervals but yields robust and valid estimates without inflating Type I errors [13, 27, 33]. Moreover, Bayesian credible intervals, derived from prior distributions and full posterior sampling, remain reliable even when maximum likelihood models fail to converge [34]. This advantage is further supported by the superior convergence and lower prediction error of BHM, particularly for imbalanced data in multilevel structures [27, 32].

Limitations

Surveillance coverage differed across provinces, with population-wide surveillance in British Columbia and Quebec but region-based surveillance in Alberta and Ontario. Although analyses adjusted for province, such heterogeneity may limit the representativeness of the study population relative to the broader Canadian pediatric population. Serotype-specific analyses were constrained by the small number of vaccine-type IPD cases in the post-PCV era. Vaccine effectiveness could be estimated only for the most frequent vaccine serotypes, with estimates remaining imprecise in strata with sparse exposure-outcome counts, limiting the interpretability of comparisons across strata. In addition, the indirect cohort design relies on the assumption that vaccination does not alter the risk of non-vaccine-type IPD. If vaccination influences the distribution of non-vaccine serotypes through serotype replacement or related mechanisms, vaccine effectiveness estimates may be biased. As with all observational surveillance studies, misclassification of immunization status or covariates and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, although FHM was explored, it did not converge due to data sparsity and weak identification, which precluded a direct comparison between Bayesian and frequentist hierarchical models.

Conclusions

The limited effectiveness of PCV13 in preventing IPD caused by ST-3 and ST-19 A remains a challenge, and the impact of new-generation 15-valent and 20-valent PCVs remains to be evaluated. Our study illustrates the advantages of Bayesian hierarchical models over conventional frequentist fixed-effect models for estimating serotype-specific vaccine effectiveness using data from multiple sites with sparse case counts.
