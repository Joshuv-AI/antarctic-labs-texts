Sustainable utilization of biochar and compost using chicken excreta and groundnut shell to enhance soil nutrients and plants growth in Northeast, Nigeria
Authors: Ifeoma Juliet Opara, Eno-obong Sunday Nicholas, Augustine Sunday Ejeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biochar has improved soil fertility and crop productivity in various agricultural and industrial activities. This study assesses the application and characterization of biochar and compost derived from chicken excreta (CEB) and groundnut shells (GSB), evaluating their potential to enhance soil nutrient content and support sustainable agriculture. Collected samples were analyzed for proximate, heavy metals, and physicochemical parameters using FTIR and AAS. Results were analyzed using descriptive and two-way ANOVA statistical analysis for SPSS, and were compared with the WHO and USEPA set standards for soil quality. The obtained results for CEB, GSB and CP gave mean values (%) ranging from 1.13 ± 0.01–3.53 ± 0.01 for moisture content, volatile matter (2.10 ± 0.10–46.01 ± 0.01), ash content (6.18 ± 0.01–11.50 ± 0.05) and fixed carbon (46.77 ± 0.01–85.33 ± 0.00) for proximate analysis, physicochemical parameters gave 8.45 ± 0.01–9.35 ± 0.01 for pH, cation exchange capacity (5.33 ± 0.01–6.33 ± 0.01%) and organic carbon (5.97 ± 0.01–6.26 ± 0.01%), heavy metals gave 0.00±0.00–20.72±0.00 mg/kg for Zn, Cr, Pb and Cd for CP, CEB and GSB, and were significantly different at (p < 0.05). Results obtained were below the set standards by the WHO and USEPA for heavy metal concentration in soil for plant growth. The results demonstrated distinct properties of biochar derived from chicken excreta, groundnut shell, and composted biomass. Biochar exhibited lower moisture and volatile matter, and higher fixed carbon content, indicating its stability and suitability for long-term storage, suggesting its potential for carbon sequestration. In conclusion, essential mineral content (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium, Sodium, Calcium, and Magnesium) found in biochar and compost highlighted their potential to improve soil fertility, thereby providing a sustainable alternative to synthetic fertilizers in agricultural applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53633-x.

Introduction

Biochar provides a wide range of physicochemical and mineral characteristics, which positively impact its various applications, thereby gaining more interest globally due to its versatile use1–6. Biochar made from sewage sludge (biosolids) has more hydrogen and less carbon when compared with the derivation from lignocellulosic biomass7,8,1,9. Generally, Biochar has an alkaline pH, which can be used as an acid-neutralizing soil conditioner, thereby improving the soil’s quality in agriculture10–14. Biochar is used in the treatment of waste from industries in order to terminate organic and inorganic pollutants and different types of dyes and pigments from textiles15,16. It can also be used as a power generation fuel due to its high carbon content17,18. The generation of biochar derived from biomass is a consequence of the technology used and the process involved18–23. Biochars have various physical, chemical, and mechanical characteristics that vary according to the raw material and pyrolysis parameters24–28.

Several findings carried out on the use of biochar in the agricultural sector have increased over the years due to its beneficial actions for sustainable agriculture, as biochar is proven to have a positive effect on soil fertility and crop productivity29–33. More meaningful benefits, such as waste management, carbon sequestration, reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, and climate change mitigation, have been achieved using biochar34–38. The characteristics of biochars are influenced by the chemical composition of the feedstock and pyrolysis conditions. Biomass with higher lignin content, such as eucalyptus, pine bark, and sawdust, has a higher yield of biochar39–42. Typically, these biochars have higher carbon content (C) and aromaticity; also showing alkalinity in pH, however, they have a lower ash content and nutrient content, such as N, P, K, and Ca43,44. On the other hand, biomass with a high content of cellulose and hemicellulose (crop residues) yielded biochar with a high concentration of volatile compounds and nutrients45,46. Biochar is considered one of the best organic fertilizers due to its stability and ability to retain nutrients and reduce leaching47. Biochars with various hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin degrees can have a wide range of physicochemical properties48,49. Lignocellulosic biomass is an excellent bioresource of plant and animal components, including agricultural and wood wastes, energy crops, and municipal solid waste50–52. For example, biochar derived from animal manure has a smaller specific surface area and a relatively richer nutrient level than that derived from wood and plant mass52. The porous structure and negative surface functional group of biochar can decrease density and enhance aeration of the compost pile, as well as provide shelters for micro-organisms53. The porous, and thus, large surface area of biochar influences its sorption capacity54, and the addition of biochar could enhance the water retention capacity of compost and soils, and absorb odours during composting55,56. Feedstock types and pyrolysis temperature mainly determine biochar properties, including surface properties, structures, elemental composition, redox capacity, conductivity, pH, cation exchange capacity (CEC), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs)57,58. Elemental composition and surface functional groups of biochar significantly determine its effects on composting efficiency and agricultural soil qualities59. The objective of this study were to produce biochar from sawdust, chicken excreta and groundnut shell, composting, characterization of biochar produced from chicken excreta and groundnut shell using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy(FTIR), heavy metal assessment in the samples using AAS, proximate and physico-chemical analysis of biochar and compost biomass, and performance test (biological studies) of the biochar produced for the enhancement of soil nutrient content for plant growth and support for sustainable agriculture in Nigeria and in the world at large.

Materials and methods

Materials

All apparatus and sample containers used in this study were decontaminated with 10% HNO3 solution, which was rigorously and properly rinsed with distilled water multiple times immediately before usage, which was in line with the standard method by AOAC60. Reagents used were bought from commercial suppliers (Sigma Aldrich, USA) unless otherwise stated. Standardized approved methods were used to carry out the analyses to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results according to the standard methods by AOAC60 and ASTM61.

Sample preparation

The properly sun-dried chicken excreta and groundnut shells samples were ground using a mortar and pestle, which was later sieved with a 2 mm mesh size and packaged in a clean container for further analysis in this study.

Production of biochar through pyrolysis

Biochar was prepared from groundnut shells and Chicken Excreta by first drying the raw materials to remove moisture. The feedstocks were then crushed or ground to increase surface area and improve pyrolysis efficiency. Pyrolysis was conducted in a closed system with limited oxygen, where the temperature was gradually increased at a heating rate of 5–10 °C per min until it reached 400 °C and a residence time of 1 h as described in the standard method by AOAC60 and ASTM61. The reactor has the capacity of receiving 200 g of feedstock at a time. After pyrolysis, the biochar was allowed to cool inside the reactor to prevent oxidation. Once cooled, it was collected and stored in an airtight container to maintain its quality. The obtained biochars in this study are shown in Plate 2 below.

Preparation of rapid compost biomass

Chicken excreta (1000 g) and groundnut shell (1000 g) were ground to ensure fast decomposition then it was put in compost bin and water were sprinkled on it (50-60%); then it was covered and kept at room temperature for three (3) days without turning it so that microorganism will build and decompose it, on the 4th day the compost was turned to ensure uniform temperature in the compost as the temperature has increased. The turning of the compost at regular intervals was done for three (3) days to facilitate rapid composting. The rapid compositing period lasted for six (6) days. After which, it was concentrated as shown in Plate 3 below.

Characterization

Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The surface chemical properties of the biochar were determined by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer, USA) equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory in which the powder of each sample was inserted in a diamond crystal gate. The FTIR spectra of 64 scans were recorded in the wavenumber range 4000 –450 cm− 1 with 4 cm− 1 resolution.

Proximate analysis

The ASTM International standard method61 was used to determine the content of ash, volatile matter, and moisture content with slight modification. To remove the moisture content, the biochar and compost were dried in an oven at 105 °C until constant weight was achieved. The compost biomass (5 g), chicken excretes biochar (5 g) and groundnut shell biochar (5 g) in a crucible were heated in an oven at 900 °C for 7 min. to determine the volatile content, while the ash content was determined in a furnace at 700 °C for 6 h. The difference was used to determine fixed carbon.

Physicochemical parameters, physical properties and mineral present

Determination of pH

The pH content of the samples was determined using a glass electrode pH meter (Sonkir Soil, Model No: MS02). 2.5 g of sieved samples (biochar and compost) were added to 5 ml distilled water and stirred. The mixture was allowed to stand for 30 min. The electrode of a pH meter was put into the solution, and the pH of the samples was read and recorded.

Determination of minerals content

Three different samples (1.5 g) of Chicken excreta (CEB), groundnut shell (GSB), and compost biomass (CP), respectively, were sieved using a 2 mm mesh size and weighed, then digested using 20 mL of sulphuric acid for total mineral extraction. The flask was heated gradually on a digestion block until initial frothing subsided, after which the temperature was increased (400 °C), and digestion continued until a clear or pale solution was obtained, indicating complete oxidation of organic matter. After that, it was cooled, and the digest was diluted with distilled water. Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS, PerkinElmer 800) was used to measure the emission intensity specific to potassium (K) and sodium (Na) according to the standard method by ASTM61.

The colourimetric method was used to determine Phosphorus (P) content. The molybdenum blue method was used; an aliquot of the digest was reacted with ammonium molybdate and ascorbic acid, and the absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer. The Kjeldahl method was used to determine total nitrogen (N). The digest was titrated with sulphuric acid + catalyst (ammonia).

Determination of effective cation exchangeable capacity (ECEC)

Effective cation exchangeable capacity was determined via ammonium acetate (NH4OAc) extraction at pH 7.0 to evaluate the biochar’s ability to retain and exchange cations (for example, Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and Na+) as described by the standard method by ASTM61.

Procedure for cation exchangeable capacity by ammonium saturation

The samples (10 g) were weighed into a 100 cm3 plastic beaker, then ammonium acetate (40 mL) of 1 N pH 7 was added and stirred rigorously using a glass rod, and was left overnight. The suspension was filtered with light suction using a 55 mm Buchner funnel (Corning size No. 40). The sample was not allowed to dry or crack. The sample was leached in a funnel with 1 N pH 7 of ammonium acetate to a final volume of 250 cm3. The leachate was preserved for the determination of Calcium (Ca), Magnesium (Mg), Potassium (K) and Sodium (Na) using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS) in this study.

The leachate was tested for the absence of calcium by adding a few drops of 1 N NH4 OH and 10% ammonium oxalate. The presence of calcium was indicated by a white precipitate or turbidity upon heating, continued leaching with 1 N pH 7 of ammonium acetate until the leachate is calcium-free. The sample was leached with 1 N NH4Cl for four consecutive times, using 25 mL portions each time, followed by one leaching with 25 mL of 0.25 N pH 7 NH4Cl. The solution was discarded. Excess electrolyte was washed out with 200 mL of isopropyl alcohol or absolute ethanol.

The leachate was tested for chloride with 0.1 N AgNO3 until the leaching showed a negligible reaction (indicating the removal of ammonium chloride). Then the sample was allowed to drain thoroughly.

The sample was leached with acidified sodium chloride (NaCl) to a final volume of 250 mL. Leaching was done gradually. 50 mL of 2% Boric Acid was measured into a 250 mL conical flask, and a few drops of mixed indicator were added. The acidified sodium chloride (NaCl) leachate was poured into a 500 mL Kjeldahl flask and then connected to a distillation apparatus and anti-bumping granules, and 10 mL of 10 N NaOH was added and distilled over the boric acid in the conical flask. 150 mL of the distillate was titrated using the NH3-borate with a standard 0.1 N HCl. The Cation Exchangeable Capacity (CEC) was calculated in Meq per 100 g of sample.

Determination of organic carbon (%O.C)

Organic carbon (O.C) was determined using the Walkley-Black method. Four separate sieved soil samples (0.5 g) were weighed into a flask, namely; blank, Chicken excreta (CEB), groundnut shell (GSB), and compost biomass (CP), respectively. To each sample, 5 ml of potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was added and stirred to mix thoroughly, then 10 ml of concentrated sulphuric acid (H2SO4) was poured in rapidly, and the mixture was allowed to stand for about 15 min. The reaction was then stopped by adding 100 mL of distilled water and allowing the solution to cool. After cooling, five drops of ferrous ammonium sulfate (NH4)2Fe (SO4)2.6H20 indicator were added, and the mixture was titrated until the endpoint was reached. The calculation was done by the difference between the blank titration and the sample titration.

Determination of organic matter (%O.M)

Organic matter (O.M) was determined using the Walkley-Black method. Four (4) separate sieved soil samples (0.5 g) were weighed into a flask (blank, Chicken excreta (CEB), groundnut shell (GSB), and compost biomass (CP) respectively). To each sample, 5 ml of 1 N potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was added and stirred to mix thoroughly, then 10 ml of concentrated sulphuric acid (H2SO4) was poured in rapidly, and the mixture was allowed to stand for about 15 min. The reaction was then stopped by adding 100 ml of distilled water and allowing the solution to cool. After cooling, thirty (30) drops of diphenylamine indicator were added, and the mixture was titrated with 0.5 N ferrous ammonium sulfate (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2.6H20 until the colour changes from blue-violet to green. Organic matter (O.M) was calculated by multiplying the organic carbon content by a factor of 1.724.

Determination of exchangeable acidity

Exchangeable acidity was measured via potassium chloride (KCl) extraction, and then titration was done to assess the presence of exchangeable hydrogen (H+) and (Al3+) ions, which influence soil pH and nutrient availability.

Statistical analysis

Mean values generated in this study were calculated using Microsoft Office Excel 2010 for mean and standard deviation (SD). Results obtained were analyzed using the descriptive and two-way ANOVA statistical analysis of the software Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 20.

Proximate analysis

The result of the proximate analysis (moisture content, volatile matter, ash content, fixed carbon) of the compost biomass, chicken excreta and groundnut shell biochar are shown in Table 2; Fig. 1 above, which are;

Moisture content (%)

The obtained results showed low moisture content, which indicates that they are effectively dried, which makes them easier to handle and less prone to microbial degradation or fungal growth during storage. Volatile matter (%)

The lower values obtained for volatile matter of CEB and GSB when compared to the compost biomass (46.01±0.01) was because the organic matter remains; unlike carbonized biochar, this compost still contains a substantial amount of decomposable organic materials. These obtained results were in line with the earlier findings by Opara et al.1.

Ash content (%)

These values were in accordance with the earlier findings by Opara et al.1

Fixed carbon (%)

And the low fixed carbon of compost biomass (46.77 ± 0.01) leads to the formation of more stable carbon compounds compared to the original feedstock, but less so than biochar produced at high temperature. This obtained result was in support of the earlier findings by Opara et al.1.

Heavy metals concentration in biochar and compost biomass

The mean values of heavy metal concentration in chicken excretes, groundnut shell biochar, and compost biomass, as shown in Table 3 above, indicated lower concentrations of Zn, Pb, Cd, and Cr, which were below the permissible limit for heavy metals concentration in soil by WHO62 and USEPA63. The set standard for heavy metals concentration in soil for plant growth, as recommended by the WHO62, is approximately 0.8 mg/kg for cadmium, 50 mg/kg for zinc, 100 mg/kg for chromium, and 85 mg/kg for lead. The heavy metal (mg/kg) results obtained in this study showed that; Composted chicken excreta and groundnut shell gave a detectable level of Zn (9.81±0.01) and Cr (3.47±0.00), but no detectable levels of lead (Pb) for compost (CP). Chicken excreta biochar (CEB): mean values of (9.81±0.00) and (3.12 ±0.01) were detected for Zn and Cr, respectively, with no detection of Pb as shown in Table 3 above. Groundnut shell biochar (GSB): Exhibited detectable levels of Zn (8.61±0.00), Pb (20.72±0.00), and Cr (3.69 ±0.01). Some insignificant quantities (0.001) of Cadmium (Cd) was observed in all the analyzed samples in this study. The concentration of heavy metals in the analyzed samples was below the permissible limit by WHO62 and USEPA63 and, it is an indication that they can be applied to the soil to enhance plant growth without impacting it negatively. Zinc and Chromium are consistently detected in all samples. Zinc is an essential micronutrient for plants in small amounts, but can be toxic at higher concentrations. The obtained results were in agreement with the earlier findings by Zhang et al.23 and Opara et al.1 who reported a lower concentration of heavy metals in their research works.

Physiochemical parameters of biochar and compost biomass

The obtained results for Physicochemical parameters of biochar and compost biomass as shown in Table 4 above, namely;

Effective cation exchange capacity (ECEC)

The obtained results gave mean values of 6.32±0.01 for CP, CEB (5.49±0.01), GSB (5.33±0.01), and SA (5.18±0.01), as shown in Table 4 above, respectively. Compost (CP) has the highest ECEC (6.32), followed by CEB (5.49) and then GSB (5.33). This measures the soil’s ability to retain and exchange positively charged nutrients. This suggests that compost has a slightly higher capacity to hold onto essential nutrients. This could be attributed to the high organic matter (OM) negative charge and composting process, which retained charged nutrients. Similar findings were reported by Masmoudi et al.15 on biochar.

Percentage organic carbon (% O.C)

The results gave mean values of 6.26±0.01 for CP, CEB (5.98±0.01), GSB (5.97±0.01), and SA (0.48±0.01), as shown in Table 4 above, respectively. All three samples have relatively similar and reasonably high organic carbon (%) content (CP: 6.26, CEB: 5.97, and GSB: 5.97). However, compost was slightly higher, indicating the efficiency of rapid compositing, retaining nutrients without phytotoxicity. Organic carbon is crucial for soil structure, water retention, and nutrient availability. These results were in accordance with the earlier findings by Opara et al. 1 who reported high carbon content in their study.

Percentage organic matter (% O.M)

The obtained values gave mean values of 10.78±0.01 for CP, CEB (10.29±0.01), GSB (10.28±0.01), and SA (0.83±0.01), as shown in Table 4 above, respectively. Several studies have shown that soil fertility and biological activity are improved by organic matter (OM). These values were in line with the report of Lehmann et al. 47 in their research findings.

Exchangeable acidity

The obtained results gave mean values of 1.40±0.02 for CP, CEB (1.25±0.01), GSB (1.15±0.01), and SA (1.05±0.01) respectively. Compost has the highest exchangeable acidity (1.40), followed by CEB (1.25) and GSB (1.15). The results obtained in this study showed higher exchangeable acidity, which increases soil acidity and is beneficial for plant growth. This measures the concentration of acidic cations (like aluminum and hydrogen) that can contribute to soil acidity. These obtained values were in line with the findings of several researchers who reported higher exchangeable acidity in compost biomass.

This is as a result of K+ displacing H+ and Al3+ from the solution, reducing the pH of the biochar samples.

pH in H₂O 1:2:5

This measures the actual pH of the samples in water. The alkaline nature of the biochar and compost is good for neutralizing acidic soil, as reported by Zhang et al. 23. These results were in line with the findings of Dominguez et al.39, who also reported alkalinity in biochar.

Conclusion

In this study, groundnut shells and Chicken excreta were pyrolyzed at 400 °C to produce biochar, and the biomass was also composted to produce organic compost to enhance the soil nutrients for plant growth. The obtained results for proximate analysis indicated high carbon content (%) in the analyzed samples, such as; CEB (79.48±0.01), GSB (85.33±0.00) and CP (46.77±0.01). Minerals such as Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium, Sodium, Calcium and Magnesium were found to be present in higher concentrations in the compost and biochar samples analyzed in this study. This was an indication that biochar produced from groundnut shells, chicken excreta, and compost contains significant potential of minerals content which was observed to be responsible for the improvement of soil fertility and plant growth. By utilizing these locally available resources, farmers can enhance their soil nutrients in order to increase crop yields and equally contribute to sustainable agricultural practices.
