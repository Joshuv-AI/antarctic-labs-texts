Exploration of Parameters in f ( R , T ) Gravity and Comparison with Type Ia Supernovae Data
Vincent R. Siggia, Eric D. Carlson, P. Lee Pryor

Abstract
The expansion of the Universe in f ⁡ ( R , T ) f(R,T) gravity is studied. We consider functions of the form f ⁡ ( R , T ) = R + λ ​ T ϵ f(R,T)=R+\lambda T^{\epsilon} where ϵ < 1 \epsilon<1 . We find that for all models with ϵ < 0 \epsilon<0 , the Universe transitions to exponential growth at late times, just as it does in the standard cosmological model, which corresponds to ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 . It also fits the type Ia supernova data slightly better than the standard cosmological model, without increasing the number of parameters of the theory. In contrast, the fits for ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 rapidly become worse than the standard cosmological model.



I
Introduction

It is generally acknowledged that the Universe’s expansion is accelerating, as first observed using Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia)
et al. 1998
;
et al. 1998
;
et al. 1999
, and later demonstrated using the cosmic microwave background radiation
et al. 2020
and baryon acoustic oscillations
et al. 2017
. The conventional explanation is that this is due to a cosmological constant,
\Lambda
. This, with the indication that most of the nonrelativistic matter in the Universe is cold dark matter (CDM) and the added assumption that the universe is spatially flat, leads to the standard
\Lambda
CDM model of the Universe.

Although the
\Lambda
CDM model fits the data well, there has been enormous interest in alternative theories, including modifications of gravity. For example, in
f(R)
gravity the curvature term,
R
, is replaced by some function of the curvature scalar
Buchdahl 1970
. Another alternative, first proposed by Harko
et al.
, is to consider a contribution of the form
f(R,T)
where
T
is the trace of the stress-energy tensor
Harko et al. 2011
.

In a previous paper
Siggia and Carlson 2025
, we focused on a modification of the standard Lagrangian by replacing the scalar curvature,
R
, by some combination of a function of the curvature and the stress energy tensor such that
R\rightarrow f(R,T)
. We specifically focused on the case where
f
is additively separable, that is,
f(R,T)=f_{1}(R)+f_{2}(T)
, and specialized to

f(R,T)=R+\lambda T^{\epsilon}\;.

(1)

Thus, our action is

S=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\mathcal{L}_{m}-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\big(R+\lambda T^{\epsilon}\big)\right]\;,

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{m}
is the Lagrangian for ordinary and dark matter, and
\kappa^{2}=8\pi G
. The previous paper focused on the value
\epsilon=-1
; in this paper we will explore the full range of
\epsilon<1
.

The goal of the
\lambda T^{\epsilon}
term is to create a universe that behaves in a typical matter- or energy-dominated manner until late times, at which point the new term takes over, possibly explaining the accelerated expansion. To ensure a universe that changes its behavior only at late times, the new term must dominate
\mathcal{L}_{m}
at low energy density. This criterion is satisfied if
\epsilon<1
. Note that
\epsilon=0
corresponds to the standard
\Lambda
CDM Universe with
\lambda=2\Lambda
.

The trace of the stress-energy tensor,
T
, is normally computed beginning with the matter Lagrangian,
\mathcal{L}_{m}
. This is either the standard-model Lagrangian or whatever extension is necessary to include dark matter. Due to the complex or unknown nature of this Lagrangian, it has become standard practice to naïvely assume the stress-energy tensor takes the form appropriate for a perfect fluid. In standard general relativity, the procedure was worked out in
Brown 1993
. However, there are subtleties in applying this formalism in
f(R,T)
gravity that have been often ignored in many articles
Harko et al. 2011
;
Sáez-Gómez et al. 2016
;
Zaregonbadi et al. 2016
;
Velten and Caramês 2017
;
Carvalho et al. 2017
;
Deb et al. 2018
;
Rudra and Giri 2021
, which were later pointed out by

Minazzoli and Harko 2012
and further elaborated by
Fisher and Carlson 2019
. For further discussion on this issue, see
Fisher and Carlson 2019
.

In Sec.
II
, the
f(R,T)
formalism for our model is laid out, resulting in the modified Friedmann equations. Then in Sec.
III
, the scale factor of the Universe as a function of time is derived and compared for multiple values of
\epsilon<1
. Finally in Sec.
IV
, SNe Ia data from the Pantheon dataset
et al. 2018
;
Lu et al. 2022
;
et al. 2019
are compared with our predictions for various
\epsilon<1
. In Sec.
V
we summarize our results.

Throughout, we use conventions where
c=\hbar=1
, the signature of the metric is
(+,-,-,-)
, and the Riemann and Ricci tensors are defined by
{R^{\alpha}}_{\mu\beta\nu}=\nabla_{\beta}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}-\cdots
and
R_{\mu\nu}={R^{\alpha}}_{\mu\alpha\nu}
respectively.

II
General Model

Varying the action Eq. (
2
) with respect to
g^{\mu\nu}
gives the
f(R,T)
Einstein equation for our model as

R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}R\,g_{\mu\nu}=\kappa^{2}T_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda T^{\epsilon}g_{\mu\nu}-\epsilon\lambda T^{\epsilon-1}\frac{\partial\,T}{\partial g^{\mu\nu}}\;.

(3)

Taking the divergence of Eq. (
3
) shows that the stress-energy is not conserved
Siggia and Carlson 2025
;
Barrientos O. and Rubilar 2014
,

\kappa^{2}\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu\nu}=\nabla^{\mu}\left(\epsilon\lambda T^{\epsilon-1}\frac{\partial\,T}{\partial g^{\mu\nu}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\lambda T^{\epsilon-1}\nabla_{\nu}T\;.

(4)

In general, perfect fluids are described in terms of the particle number density
n
and entropy per particle
s
; therefore, the naïve energy density and pressure in a comoving frame can be written as
\rho=\rho(n,s)
and
p=p(n,s)
. Dealing with perfect fluids is difficult because of the necessity of using Lagrange multipliers to enforce conservation laws, as shown by
Brown 1993
and further explored in the context of
f(R,T)
gravity by
Fisher and Carlson 2019
. In this approach, the number density
n
and four velocity
u^{\mu}
are combined into a single unconstrained current density
J^{\mu}=nu^{\mu}
, where now
n
is treated as a dependent quantity given by
n=\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu}J^{\nu}}
. The current density
J^{\mu}
is not conserved; instead, one finds

\nabla_{\mu}\left[\left(1+\frac{2\epsilon\lambda}{\kappa^{2}}T^{\epsilon-1}\right)J^{\mu}\right]=0\;,

(5)

where the expression inside the square brackets can be considered an effective current density
J^{\prime\mu}
.

In standard gravity, because the stress-energy tensor is conserved, it can be shown that the energy density and pressure are related by

n\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial n}=\rho+p.

(6)

In general, this does not necessarily hold. However, this can still be considered a definition of the naïve pressure
p
.

The stress-energy quantities appearing in Eq. (
3
) become

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(\rho+p)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}-g_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}_{m}\;,

(7a)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=(\rho+p)-4\mathcal{L}_{m}\;,

(7b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T}{\partial g^{\mu\nu}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}u_{\mu}u_{\nu}\left(4+n\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\right)(\rho+p)\;.

(7c)

Unfortunately, the matter Lagrangian still contains Lagrange multipliers. All quantities can be calculated “on-shell”; chosen to satisfy the equations of motion in order to eliminate the Lagrange multiplier terms. We denote such quantities by an over-bar. Following the analysis of
Siggia and Carlson 2025
, eliminating these Lagrange multipliers leads to the on-shell matter Lagrangian

\overline{\mathcal{L}_{m}}=p+\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{2\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}+4\epsilon\lambda}n\frac{\partial}{\partial n}(\rho+p)\;.

(8)

Simply replacing
\mathcal{L}_{m}
with
\overline{\mathcal{L}_{m}}
in Eqs. (
7a
)-(
7c
) produces their on-shell equivalents. In particular, by combining Eqs. (
7b
) and (
8
), the on-shell stress-energy trace
{\overline{T}}
becomes an implicit function of
\rho
and
p
as

\rho-3p={\overline{T}}+\frac{2\lambda\epsilon}{\kappa^{2}}{\overline{T}}^{\epsilon}+\frac{2\lambda\epsilon}{\kappa^{2}}{\overline{T}}^{\epsilon-1}\left(4+n\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\right)p\;.

(9)

Assuming a flat Friedmann-Lemaître-Robinson-Walker (FLRW) metric,

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-a^{2}(t)\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\right)\;,

(10)

the equations of motion in terms of
n
and
s
are

\displaystyle 3\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4}\left(3\rho+3p+{\overline{T}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\overline{T}}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{2}{\overline{T}}^{-(1-\epsilon)}\left(4+n\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\right)(\rho+p)\;,

(11a)

\displaystyle-6\left(\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\left(3\rho+3p-{\overline{T}}\right)-\lambda{\overline{T}}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{2}{\overline{T}}^{-(1-\epsilon)}\left(4+n\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\right)(\rho+p)\,.

(11b)

III
The Scale Factor

The initial motivation for considering dark energy was the study of SNe Ia. A plot of the luminosity distance versus redshift
z
seemed to indicate that, unlike a matter- or curvature-dominated universe, the Universe was currently accelerating its expansion. As we demonstrated in our previous paper
Siggia and Carlson 2025
, we can achieve this in the case
\epsilon=-1
. How general is this? We will again assume that the space metric is flat, given by Eq. (
10
). Since the energy density
\rho
and pressure
p
are being derived from the matter Lagrangian, we expect that in the nonrelativistic era, the pressure will still be
p=0
. We expect the naïve energy density
\rho\propto n
because they are both derived from the standard matter Lagrangian,
\mathcal{L}_{m}
.

Using
p=0
, Eq. (
9
) reduces to

\rho={\overline{T}}+\frac{2\epsilon\lambda}{\kappa^{2}}{\overline{T}}^{\epsilon}\;,

(12)

which can be solved for
{\overline{T}}
. Equations (
11a
) and (
11b
) in terms of
{\overline{T}}
become

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a^{2}}=\frac{\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}}{3}\left[1+\frac{(8\epsilon+1)\lambda}{2\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}}+5\bigg(\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}}\bigg)^{2}\right],

(13a)

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}=-\frac{\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}}{6}\left[1+\frac{(11\epsilon-2)\lambda}{2\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}}+5\bigg(\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}}\bigg)^{2}\right].

(13b)

From Eq. (
5
), the effective current density

J^{\prime\mu}=\left(1+\frac{2\epsilon\lambda}{\kappa^{2}}{\overline{T}}^{\epsilon-1}\right)J^{\mu}=\frac{\rho}{{\overline{T}}}\left(n\,u^{\mu}\right)

(14)

must be conserved. In the nonrelativistic present Universe, since
n
and
\rho
are both derived from the matter Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{m}
, we would have
\rho\propto n
, so that
J^{\prime 0}\propto\rho^{2}/{\overline{T}}
will be conserved. This implies that
\rho^{2}a^{3}/{\overline{T}}
is constant, which we write as

\frac{\rho^{2}}{{\overline{T}}}a^{3}=\frac{12N^{3}}{\kappa^{2}}\;,

(15)

where the constant was chosen for later comparison and
N
is an arbitrary normalization factor.
With the help of Eq. (
12
), Eq. (
15
) and its derivative yield the relations

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=N\Bigg[\frac{12\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-2\epsilon}}{\big(\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}+2\epsilon\lambda\big)^{2}}\Bigg]^{1/3}\;,

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{a}}{a}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{{\overline{T}}}\big[\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}-(1-2\epsilon)2\epsilon\lambda\big]}{3{\overline{T}}\big(\kappa^{2}{\overline{T}}^{1-\epsilon}+2\epsilon\lambda\big)}\;.

(17)

It is useful to define a dimensionless time,
\tau
, and a dimensionless stress-energy trace,
x
, by the equations

\displaystyle\tau=t\sqrt{\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\left(\frac{2\lambda}{\kappa^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}}\;,

(18a)

\displaystyle{\overline{T}}(t)=x(\tau)\left(\frac{2\lambda}{\kappa^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\;.

(18b)

Substituting Eq. (
17
) into (
13a
) and rewriting in terms of the rescaled quantities gives the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}=-3

\displaystyle\frac{x^{1-\epsilon}+\epsilon}{x^{1-\epsilon}-(1-2\epsilon)\epsilon}

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{4x^{2(1-\epsilon)}+(8\epsilon+1)x^{1-\epsilon}+5\epsilon^{2}}{2x^{-(1+2\epsilon)}}}\;,

(19)

effectively describing the behavior of the energy density.

III.1
Case:
\epsilon<0

Consider first the case
\epsilon<0
. For the early Universe, the energy density approaches infinity, while for the late Universe, using Eq. (
III
), the energy density approaches a constant, i.e.
x\rightarrow\infty
and
x\rightarrow(-\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}
respectively. Solving Eq. (
III
) in these limits yields

x=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{9\tau^{2}}&\tau\rightarrow 0\;,\\
(-\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}+\alpha_{\epsilon}\exp\left[-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{(-\epsilon)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}\left(\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}\right)}\tau\right]&\tau\rightarrow\infty\;,\end{cases}

(20)

where
\alpha_{\epsilon}
was determined numerically for each
\epsilon
. This leads to a scale factor with asymptotic behavior

a(t)=N\begin{cases}(3t)^{2/3}&H_{\lambda}t\rightarrow 0\;,\\
H_{\lambda}^{-2/3}\beta_{\epsilon}\exp(H_\lambda t)&H_{\lambda}t\rightarrow\infty\;,\end{cases}

(21)

where
\beta_{\epsilon}=\left[\alpha_{\epsilon}^{-2}(1-\epsilon)^{-1}(-\epsilon)^{\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}\right]^{1/3}
and
H_{\lambda}
is defined by Eq. (
13a
) at late times as

H_{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{6}\left[\left(-\frac{2\epsilon}{\kappa^{2}}\right)^{\epsilon}\lambda\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\;.

(22)

In the limit of
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, i.e.
\Lambda
CDM, Eq. (
21
) still holds, where
\beta_{\epsilon}=1
and Eq. (
22
) becomes
H_{\Lambda}^{2}=\lambda/6=\Lambda/3
.

The behavior of the Hubble parameter is shown in Fig.
1
. However, there is no reason to assume the asymptotic Hubble constants
H_{\Lambda}
and
H_{\lambda}
should match, since the goal of these models is not to make the Universe with particular future behavior, but instead to match the observed redshift-luminosity curves.

Assuming the limit of Eq. (
22
) as
\epsilon\rightarrow-\infty
makes sense, the differential Eq. (
III
) can be solved exactly. This yields

x=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{9\tau^{2}}&\tau\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\;,\\
\left\{-\epsilon\left[1+\frac{1}{2}\csch\big(\frac{3\tau}{\sqrt 8}-\frac{1}{2}\big)\right]\right\}^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}&\tau>\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\;,\end{cases}

(23)

where we have delayed completing the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow-\infty
. Using Eq. (
13a
), and defining
H_{\infty}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow-\infty}H_{\lambda}
, the scale factor is determined to be

a=N\begin{cases}(3t)^{2/3}&H_{\infty}t\leq\frac{1}{3}\;,\\
H_{\infty}^{-2/3}\left[1+2\sinh\big(\frac{3}{2}H_\infty t-\frac{1}{2}\big)\right]^{2/3}&H_{\infty}t>\frac{1}{3}\;.\end{cases}

(24)

III.2
Case:
\epsilon\in\left(0,1\right)

For
\epsilon\in\left(0,1\right)
, assuming
\lambda>0
, it can be shown from Eq. (
13b
) that the Universe will have no period of acceleration unless
\epsilon<\frac{1}{41}(22-8\sqrt{5})\approx 0.1003
. However, there is a problem for
\epsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
; namely, there are no real solutions to Eqs. (
12
) and (
15
) combined for sufficiently large values of
a
. This suggests that the theory becomes inconsistent once the number density gets sufficiently low.

We can nonetheless analyze this case, but simply constrain
\tau_{0}
such that
\tau_{0}
occurs before this critical failure point. For quite small values of
\epsilon
, the best fit to the data occurs when we take
\tau_{0}
to be this limiting value. Unfortunately, the fit quickly becomes very poor. The breakdown point occurs where the Hubble parameter takes the value

H_{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1}{12}\left[\frac{2\epsilon^{\epsilon}\lambda}{\kappa^{2\epsilon}(1-2\epsilon)^{1-2\epsilon}}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}(1-\epsilon)(1+16\epsilon-16\epsilon^{2})\;.

(25)

This behavior is illustrated in Fig.
1
for the case
\epsilon=0.03
.

If
\lambda<0
, one might naïvely expect from Eq. (
13b
) that a period of accelerated expansion could occur if
\epsilon>\frac{1}{41}(22+8\sqrt{5})\approx 0.9729
. However, comparison with Eq. (
13a
) shows that the Universe would instead expand to a maximum size followed by an era of collapse without ever attaining accelerating expansion.

In the limit where
\epsilon\rightarrow 1
, Eqs. (
13a
) and (
13b
) are equivalent to the standard flat Friedmann equations without a cosmological constant; the only difference being a rescaling of the constants.

IV
Luminosity Distance and Observation

The relationship between the luminosity distance and redshift can be understood as

d_{L}=a_{0}(1+z)\int_{t}^{t_{0}}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{a(t^{\prime})}\;,

(26)

where
t_{0}
is the time value for the present and
a_{0}=a(t_{0})
. Similarly for the considered
f(R,T)
theory, a relation between the two can be determined starting by combining Eqs. (
17
)-(
III
), followed by evaluating at the present time,

H_{0}\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{4x_{0}^{2(1-\epsilon)}+(1+8\epsilon)x_{0}^{1-\epsilon}+5\epsilon^{2}}{2x_{0}^{1-2\epsilon}}}\;,

(27)

where
x_{0}=x(\tau_{0})
. Then by inserting Eqs. (
16
) in terms of
x(\tau)
and (
27
) into (
26
) yields

\displaystyle H_{0}d_{L}=(1+z)\left[\frac{4x^{2(1-\epsilon)}_{0}+(1+8\epsilon)x^{1-\epsilon}_{0}+5\epsilon^{2}}{2x^{1-2\epsilon}_{0}}\right]^{1/2}\times

\displaystyle\;\left[\frac{x^{1-2\epsilon}_{0}}{(x^{1-\epsilon}_{0}+\epsilon)^{2}}\right]^{1/3}\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\left[\frac{(x(\tau^{\prime})^{1-\epsilon}+\epsilon)^{2}}{x(\tau^{\prime})^{1-2\epsilon}}\right]^{1/3}d\tau^{\prime}\;,

(28)

and

1+z=\left[\frac{x_{0}^{1-2\epsilon}}{(x_{0}^{1-\epsilon}+\epsilon)^{2}}\right]^{1/3}\left[\frac{(x^{1-\epsilon}+\epsilon)^{2}}{x^{1-2\epsilon}}\right]^{1/3}\;.

(29)

From the luminosity distance
d_{L}
, the distance modulus
\mu
can easily be determined using

\mu=5\,\log_{10}\left(\frac{d_{L}}{10\,\text{pc}}\right)\;.

(30)

V
Conclusion

We have studied the expansion of the Universe in
f(R,T)
gravity with
f(R,T)=R+\lambda T^{\epsilon}
and assuming a flat Universe. Similarly to
Siggia and Carlson 2025
, we found an exponential growth transition from the matter-dominated era to present-day cosmology for models where
\epsilon\leq 0
when considering the analysis of
Fisher and Carlson 2019
. We also compared our predictions for luminosity distance versus redshift with SNe Ia data
et al. 2018
. Our numerical results in Table
1
show that all models with
\epsilon<0
fit the data slightly better than the standard
\Lambda
CDM model. Interestingly, the minimum value for
\chi^{2}
occurs very close to
\epsilon=-1
, the value considered in
Siggia and Carlson 2025
. For
\epsilon>0
it was shown that the models diverge from
\Lambda
CDM and quickly become a terrible fit for an accelerating Universe.

It is clear from our analysis that supernovae data indicate an accelerating Universe, but current data cannot necessarily distinguish the cause of such acceleration. Since our
f(R,T)
model fits the data well, as evident in Fig.
2
, the deviation between standard and nonstandard cosmologies is most evident at high redshift so that more supernovae at high
z
are needed to differentiate between these models.

In future work, we hope to understand how our model affects the fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background radiation and possible modifications in the large-scale structure.
