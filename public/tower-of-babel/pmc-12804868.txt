Galantamine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles reduce oxidative stress and inflammation in a rat model of spinal cord injury
Authors: Fernanda Stapenhorst Franca, Laura Elena Sperling, Marcelo Garrido dos Santos, Cristian Euzebio Teixeira, Tiago Franco de Oliveira, Patrick Turck, Adriane Bello Klein, Patricia Pranke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) represents a debilitating condition with no effective treatments currently available. Previous research demonstrated that galantamine improves functional outcomes in SCI models. However, systemic administration results in limited target tissue bioavailability. This study aimed to develop and evaluate the therapeutic potential of galantamine-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles for SCI treatment. Galantamine-containing PLGA particles (PG) were synthesized via electrospraying, yielding nanoparticles with 568 nm average diameter, − 23 mV zeta potential, 12.87% encapsulation efficiency, and drug release over 35 days. In a rat contusion SCI model, animals received direct implantation of galantamine alone, empty PLGA particles, or PG. Locomotor assessment over 6 weeks revealed significant functional improvements only in the PG group. Tissue analysis at 3 days and 6 weeks post-injury showed that PG treatment significantly reduced oxidative stress markers as ROS production and lipid peroxidation, and selectively reduced IL-1β but did not significantly affect IL-6 levels. Flow cytometry analysis revealed reduced GFAP expression in PLGA and PG-treated groups, indicating decreased astrocyte activation, while neuronal markers (neurofilament-M, βIII-tubulin) and oligodendrocyte markers (O4) showed no significant changes between groups. These results demonstrate that galantamine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles provide superior therapeutic outcomes through targeted anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and glial modulation mechanisms.Keywords: Galantamine oxidative stress, Spinal cord injury, Nanoparticles, PLGA, Inflammation spinal cord, ElectrosprayingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Drug discovery, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

The central nervous system (CNS) has a very limited regeneration capacity, thus CNS injuries, such as stroke, traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injury (SCI) lead to debilitating conditions and neurological dysfunctions1. Specifically, the extent of function loss in SCI is dictated by the level of spinal injury, leading to loss of motor, sensory and autonomic nervous system functions at the affected areas2. Despite affecting millions of people worldwide, there are no effective treatments for SCI to date.

In addition to cellular death due to the insult, inflammation and oxidative stress play important roles in the pathophysiology of SCI. Within minutes of the injury, microglia, astrocytes, neurons and oligodendrocytes release proinflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-1b, TNF-a and IL-6, recruiting peripheral immune cells3,4. These inflammatory cells produce reactive species such as superoxide anion, nitric oxide and other free radicals. Due to the high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids and high metabolic activity of neurons and glial cells, these populations are especially prone to oxidative stress5,6. Hence, oxidative stress, particularly lipid peroxidation, is a hallmark in secondary SCI, leading to significant cell death7.

Considering that targeting a single mechanism of injury in a complex condition such as SCI is unlikely to elicit significant regeneration, using a drug that can address multiple pathophysiological pathways seems to be a more promising approach. Galantamine has been shown to have neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, due to its activity as an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, and has been evaluated in pre-clinical studies for several neurological conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, schizophrenia, traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injury8. Specifically, in spinal cord injury, intraperitoneal administration of galantamine led to improved motor recovery and decreased lesion size in rats9.

Traditional drug administration routes require high plasmatic drug concentrations in order to have the proper therapeutic dose at the target tissue, leading to increased side effects, and present a short time of action, calling for repeated drug administration. Although systemic galantamine administration has shown efficacy in SCI, its rapid clearance and broad distribution limit therapeutic targeting and lead to frequent administration with associated risks of peripheral cholinergic side effects10,11.

Nanotechnology based drug delivery systems have been widely studied in SCI in order to increase drug bioavailability at the lesion site12. Nano- and microparticles offer significant advantages in treating spinal cord injuries (SCI), such as targeted drug delivery reducing systemic side effects, enhanced bioavailability, and versatile applications for encapsulating different drugs, both hydrophobic and hydrophilic13,14. They are used to modulate inflammation15, reduce oxidative stress16. However, challenges like toxicity risks, biodegradability issues, and complex manufacturing processes and difficulty for standardization limit their clinical use. Nanoparticles can range from 1 to 1000 nm in diameter and have been successfully used in the treatment of several diseases17. Polymeric nanoparticles are a common type of nanoparticles that have the advantage of being biocompatible and which present a low toxicity for the CNS, as well as presenting good stability and controlled drug release18,19. One of the main polymers used for nanoparticles with sustained drug release is poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), due to its biocompatibility, biodegradability and to the fact that it has been approved by several regulatory agencies for human use. Furthermore, adjusting the PLGA properties and the lactide: glycolide ratio can alter the drug release profile, making it possible to tailor the particle production according to the necessity20. PLGA encapsulation overcomes these limitations by providing sustained release over days, enhanced bioavailability (1.8–3.67-fold increase), and targeted local delivery that maximizes neuroprotective effects while minimizing systemic exposure21. For galantamine specifically, PLGA nanoparticles could protect it from rapid clearance, sustaining the neuroprotective effects, and facilitate targeted local delivery, maintaining therapeutic concentrations to be released over time22.

This approach supports galantamine’s multi-modal neuroprotective mechanisms during the critical secondary injury phase.

In SCI, previous works have shown that PLGA particles containing drugs such as methylprednisolone and flavopiridol administered directly at the lesion site presented improvements in functional recovery of injured rats23,24.

Polymeric particles can be produced by several methods, such as emulsion, nanoprecipitation, coacervation and spray drying, but most of these techniques lead to low encapsulation efficiency and drug inactivation25. Electrospraying, however, is a technique that has a high encapsulation efficiency and which preserves the functionality of the active components26.

In the present work, PLGA nanoparticles containing galantamine were produced and characterized according to their morphology, size, zeta potential and polydispersity index. The galantamine encapsulation efficiency and the drug release profile was assessed. In addition, the particles were implanted in situ in a rat model of SCI and the treatment effects in inflammation and oxidative stress were evaluated 3 and 42 days after the injury. This novel method of galantamine delivery could be beneficial in reducing secondary damage in SCI, leading to better long-term outcomes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

PLGA 75:25 was purchased from Purasorb® (Corbion, Holland) and galantamine hydrobromide was supplied by ChemCruz™(Brazil). Acetonitrile, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) and bovine serum albumin were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Brazil). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were purchased by Boster (California, USA). Dopalen and Anasedan were purchased by Ceva (Brazil). Ultrapure water was obtained by a Milli-Q® system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). All other chemical and analytical reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.

Methods

Electrospraying

The particles were produced using a PLGA solution made with 4% PLGA 75:25 (Purasorb®) dissolved in acetonitrile (Sigma–Aldrich®). For the galantamine-loaded nanoparticles (PG), 20 mg/mL of galantamine hydrobromide (ChemCruz™) were incorporated in the 4% PLGA solution to reach a final concentration of 2.5%. The solutions were electrosprayed using a voltage of + 24/− 2 kV for the PLGA only particles and + 25/− 4 kV for the PG. The solutions were extruded through 0.6 mm needles, with a flow rate of 0.1–0.5 mL/h, and controlled humidity of 30% and temperature of 30 °C. The distance between the needle and the collector plate was 9 cm. Prior to the use of the particles in cell culture or in the animals, the samples were sterilized in UV light for 30 min.

Scanning electron microscopy

Particle morphology and structure were analyzed through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JEOL JSM-6060). The particles were sputter coated with a thin layer of gold before observation. The acceleration voltage was adjusted to 10 kV and magnification was from 2000 to 10,000×.

Particle size, polydispersity index and zeta potential

For both particle formulations (PLGA only and PG), particle diameter, polydispersity index (PdI) and Zeta potential were analyzed by dynamic light scattering using ZetaSizer equipment (Malvern®). The particles were suspended in 1 mM NaCl and analyzed using the refractive index of 1.46 for PLGA 75:25. Each sample was measured three times and 4 different batches of each formulation were evaluated. Results are shown as mean ± standard deviation.

Drug release

The galantamine release from the PG particles was determined after 1, 3, 6, 12, 24 and 72 h, 1 week and weekly up to 4 weeks. The particles were solubilized in PBS and at each time point, 0.5 mL of the supernatant was collected and 0.5 mL of fresh PBS was added to each tube. Aliquots of 1 µL from the collected supernatant were analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). The analyses were conducted on a Nexera UFLC system coupled to an LCMS-8045 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan). Separation was achieved using a Shim-pack XR-ODS II column (75 × 2.0 mm i.d., 2.2 μm; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with mobile phases consisting of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), each containing 0.1% formic acid. A gradient elution was employed as follows: 0.0–2.5 min, 5–100% B; 2.5–3.0 min, 100% B; 3.0–3.5 min, 100–5% B; and 3.5–8.0 min, 5% B, with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min at 40 °C. Mass spectrometric conditions included: positive ion acquisition mode; capillary voltage, 4500 V; desolvation line temperature, 250 °C; heating block temperature, 400 °C; drying gas, 10 L/min; nebulizing gas, 3 L/min; and mass range, m/z, 50–300. Collision-induced dissociation was obtained with 270 kPa argon pressure and with a collision energy of 35 eV. The release profile of galantamine was monitored through transitions at m/z 288.00 → 198.00 and m/z 288.00 → 213.00. At each time point, 0.5 mL of the 2 mL release medium was withdrawn and replaced with fresh PBS. Galantamine concentration in the withdrawn aliquots was quantified (µg/mL) and converted to the total amount of drug present in the tube (Ci × 2 mL). The amount of drug removed at each sampling was calculated as Ci × 0.5 mL. The cumulative amount of galantamine released at each time point was obtained by summing the drug removed in all previous samples plus the amount remaining in the tube at that time, i.e. Qn​ = 2Cn ​+ 0.5∑ni = 1Ci.

Animals

The experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (Porto Alegre, Brazil - CEUA project number 35781). One hundred and eight male adult Wistar rats ranging from 8 to 9 weeks old, weighing from 250 to 300 g, raised at the animal facility at Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul, were used in this study. The animals were housed in groups of two to four rats per cage. Animals were maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with food and water available ad libitum.

All surgical procedures were conducted under general anesthesia, achieved by administering 75 mg/kg of intraperitoneal ketamine combined with 5 mg/kg of xylazine, with a supplemental dose of ketamine (37 mg/kg) provided as needed. For the spinal cord injury, a laminectomy at T9 was performed to expose the spinal cord and, using the NYU MASCIS (New York University, Multicenter Animal Spinal Cord Injury Study) impactor, a 10 g rod was dropped from a height of 12.5 mm onto the spinal cord. Following the contusion, treatments were delivered directly to the spinal cord lesion site using a Hamilton syringe. Afterward, the muscles and skin were sutured, and the animals were maintained at 37 °C with a thermal pad. Postoperative care included intraperitoneal antibiotic administration (Baytril, 10 mg/kg) immediately following surgery and daily thereafter. Analgesic tramadol was given subcutaneously after surgery and at 8- and 16-hours post-surgery. Following the initial 16-hour period of analgosedation, all animals received acetaminophen administered via oral gavage at a dose of 100 mg/kg every 24 h for a further 3 days. Additionally, manual bladder emptying was performed twice daily.

Treatments

The 108 animals were randomly divided in 5 groups: sham, that had a laminectomy procedure, with no injury; spinal cord Injury, that received 10 µl of saline at the injury site; galantamine, that received 10 µl of a solution containing 2.5 µg of galantamine at the injury site; PLGA, that received 10 µl of a solution containing 0.2 mg of PLGA particles at the injury site; and PG, that received 10 µl of a solution of 0.2 mg of PLGA particles containing 2.5 µg of galantamine at the injury site (PG group). The animals were analyzed at two different timepoints, day 3 and day 42 after injury.

Locomotor analysis

Motor behavior was evaluated weekly by two blinded observers using the Basso, Beattie and Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor rating scale27 (n = 6 for the injury group, n = 7 for sham, n = 9 for galantamine treatment, n = 4 for PLGA particles treatment and n = 7 for PG group). Animals were allowed to move freely in an open-field apparatus with a 1 m diameter and assessments focused on criteria such as the extent of joint movements, weight-bearing ability, and stepping/walking behavior of the hind limbs. Functional scores were assigned independently by two observers for each hind limb, ranging from 0 (no observable hind limb movement) to 21 (normal locomotion). The mean score for both hind limbs was calculated. Scoring was conducted over a 4-minute period, following the guidelines of the Spinal Cord Injury Project (WM Keck Center for Collaborative Neuroscience, Rutgers State University of NJ, USA).

Spinal cord tissue preparation

Three and 42 days after the injury, the animals were euthanized with a lethal dose of ketamine and xylazine and 1 cm of the spinal cords centered around the lesion were collected. The spinal cord tissue was weighed and homogenized using 20 mmol/L phenyl methyl sulphonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1.15% w/v KCl in a Potter-Elvehjem homogenizer. The samples were centrifuged at 1000g for 20 min at 4–5 °C and the supernatants were collected. The same tissues were used to evaluate both oxidative stress and inflammatory markers.

Transcardiac perfusion and tissue fixation

Six weeks after implantation, the animals were anesthetized using ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administered via intraperitoneal injection and transcardially perfused with isotonic saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M, pH 7.4 phosphate buffer (0.8 mL of perfusion solution per gram of body weight). The spinal cord was removed, immersed in 30% sucrose for 48 h and processed for histochemistry. A 1.5 cm length of the spinal cord centered at the injury site was separated and embedded in a tissue-embedding medium (Tissue Tek, Sakura Finetek). Cryostat Sect. (20 μm) were cut and mounted onto gelatin-treated slides and stored at − 70 °C.

Hematoxylin and Eosin staining and analysis of lesion size

Longitudinal and transversal cryostat sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and photographed using a Zeiss Imager M2 equipped with an AxioCam MRC. Representative sections passing through the central canal were selected for analysis. Sections with an interval of 400 μm were collected. The cavitation area in each sequential slice was determined and the largest cavitation area, including necrotic and inflammatory tissue of each rat, was measured. The cavitation area was traced using the software Zeiss Zen Blue. 3–5 animals from each group were analyzed.

Immunohistochemistry

Frozen tissue sections were allowed to air-dry at room temperature for approximately 20 min, followed by a 10-min wash in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before antibody labeling. To block nonspecific binding, the slides were incubated in PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature. The primary antibody against neurofilament medium chain (NFM, 1:200; Sigma), was diluted in the same blocking buffer and applied overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000; Molecular Probes). Nuclear staining was performed using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Life Technologies). The spinal cord tissue was imaged by light and fluorescence microscopy using a Nikon EVOS FL Autu2 inverted epifluorescence microscope. The images were processed using ImageJ.

Flow cytometry

Damaged spinal cord segments (1 mm from each side of the lesion site) were dissected, and single-cell suspensions were generated following a rapid dissociation protocol. The resulting cells were permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, then blocked in PBS supplemented with 3% BSA. Cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following primary antibodies: anti-O4 (R&D Systems), anti-GFAP (Abcam), anti-NFM (Sigma), and anti-βIII tubulin (Sigma). After washing, appropriate Alexa Fluor 488 or 568-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher) were applied for 1 h. Excess antibodies were removed by washing with PBS. Samples were analyzed on a FACSAria III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) equipped with a 488 nm argon laser, and data were processed using FACSDiva 6.0 software. Approximately 50 × 103 events were recorded per sample.

Protein concentration

Total proteins were quantified using the Lowry method. Bovine serum albumin was used as the standard and this was also used to express the results.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels quantification

ROS levels in the spinal cord tissue were measured by quantifying the fluorescence of the oxidation of 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) by the ROS present in the sample. The samples were incubated with DCFH-DA for 1 h. A standard curve with known concentrations of 2′,7′- dichlorofluorescein (DCF) was used. The samples were excited at 488 nm; emission was collected at 525 nm and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Lipid peroxidation analysis

The formation of thiobarbituric acid reactive species (TBARS) during an acid-heating reaction was measured and used as an index of lipid peroxidation. Briefly, the samples were incubated with 0.8% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) for 1 h at 100 °C and then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. The amount of malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured at 535 nm. The results are expressed as nmol per milligram of protein.

Sulfhydryls

The sulfhydryl content represents a non-enzymatic antioxidant defense. This assay was performed according to Aksenov and Markesberry28. Briefly, samples were incubated with 0.1 mM of 5,5′ -dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) for 30 min at room temperature. The formation of TNB was measured spectrophotometrically at 412 nm and the results were expressed in nmol/mg protein.

Inflammatory cytokines

The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 in the spinal cord tissue 3 and 42 days after the injury were measured using commercial Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Boster), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism9. Size, zeta potential and PdI differences were compared by Student t-test. For comparison of ROS production, lipid peroxidation levels, antioxidant non-enzymatic defense, IL-1β and IL-6 levels, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons was performed. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to assess the significance of the treatments over the different time points. Data are shown as mean ± standard error. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the experiments were realized in triplicate.

Results

Characterization of the particles

The particle morphology was assessed with scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The PLGA particles presented a round to biconcave shape with a smooth surface (Fig. 1). The addition of galantamine decreased the stability of the process of particle production, where particles of PLGA containing galantamine (PG) showed the presence of fibers, but the round to biconcave shape and smooth surface was maintained.

The PLGA particles presented an average diameter of 3247.58 nm, being considered microparticles, and the addition of galantamine significantly decreased the particle diameter to 568.27 nm (Table 1). The PLGA particles had a zeta potential of -50.05 mV, indicating good stability, and the PG particles presented a significant decrease in potential, to a value of -23.59 mV. The polydispersity index of the PLGA particles was 0.715 ± 0.20, while the PG particles had values of 0.586 ± 0.10.

The galantamine release profile was assessed by HPLC. The encapsulation efficiency was 12.47% ± 8.29. As seen in Fig. 2, the analysis showed a biphasic pattern characteristic of PLGA systems, with a burst release in the first hour, followed by a slower sustained release phase for 35 days. At 35 days, out of 3.11 µg of encapsulated galantamine, approximately 1 µg was released indicating that a significant fraction of galantamine remained surface-localized rather than deeply embedded within the polymer matrix. This release pattern is consistent with the emulsion electrospray method used, where the aqueous galantamine phase may preferentially distribute to the particle surface.

Motor recovery

The therapeutic potential of galantamine and PG was assessed in a contusion spinal cord injury model in rats. Galantamine, PLGA and PG particles to a final concentration of 5 µg/mL were administered at the lesion site in 10 µl after the injury and its effects on the functional recovery was assessed weekly for 6 weeks. All animals showed spontaneous recovery, but after 42 days only the animals treated with PG presented a significant improvement in motor function, with a mean score of 11.5 ± 1.9, compared to the control group score of 7.25 ± 1.2 (p < 0.05, two-way ANOVA, Tuckey’s post-hoc test) (Fig. 3). The variations in animal numbers among the different groups were due to the loss of animals after the surgical procedure or the presence of hindlimb movements in animals 24 h after SCI.

Oxidative stress and inflammation

In addition to the treatment effects on function recovery, oxidative stress markers (Fig. 4) and inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 5) were evaluated 3 and 42 days after the injury. Contrary to expectations, SCI did not significantly elevate ROS compared to sham controls at day 3 (Fig. 4). However, at 3 days post-injury, the PG group showed significantly reduced ROS levels compared to the SCI group (p < 0.05), while free galantamine treatment showed no significant effect (Fig. 4A). At 42 days, both free galantamine and PG groups demonstrated significant reductions in ROS production compared to SCI (p < 0.05), with no significant difference between these two treatment groups (Fig. 4B). PLGA alone showed no significant effect at either time point. Similarly, lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4C, D) and antioxidant enzyme activities (Fig. 4E–H) did not show significant injury-induced changes compared to sham controls. The galantamine and PG treatments were able to reduce lipid peroxidation levels 3 days after the injury. After 42 days, galantamine alone also led to an increase in the non-enzymatic antioxidant defense (Fig. 4F).

When analyzing inflammatory cytokines, PG was the only treatment that led to a decrease in IL-1β levels in both timepoints. At 3 days post-injury, IL-1β levels were elevated in the SCI group compared to sham controls. Treatment with PG significantly reduced IL-1β, whereas free galantamine and PLGA alone showed no effects (Fig. 5A). At 42 days, IL-1β levels in the injury group remained higher than in sham animals, and PG again led to a significant reduction. Neither free galantamine nor PLGA alone altered IL-1β levels at this later time point (Fig. 5B). Overall, PG was the only treatment to consistently reduce IL-1β at both time points, indicating sustained anti-inflammatory activity.

IL-6 results revealed unexpected patterns that differed from both the IL-1β findings and previous literature reports on galantamine. At 3 days post-injury, IL-6 levels were increased in the SCI group compared to sham controls, and the PLGA group showed significant reductions relative to SCI (Fig. 5C). By 42 days, an unexpected pattern appeared in which the sham control group displayed the highest IL-6 levels, compared even to the SCI group (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, animals treated with PLGA particles presented decreased levels of IL-6 both at 3 and 42 days after injury. The treatment groups exhibited IL-6 levels comparable to SCI, and neither galantamine nor PG produced a reduction at this time point.

Neuroregeneration and gliosis

Neurofilament-M (NF-M), a cytoskeletal protein commonly used as a marker of axonal protein, was evaluated to assess neuronal recovery. Its expression was analyzed by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 7) and further quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 8A). Neurofilament-M (NF-M) immunostaining reveals increased axonal protein density in PG-treated animals at 42 days post-injury at the edge of the cystic cavity (Fig. 7). However, this finding was not confirmed by quantitative flow cytometry analysis that revealed no significant differences in NF-M expression between the experimental groups (Fig. 8A). Similarly, the expression of βIII-tubulin (Tuj1), another well-established neuronal marker associated with neurite outgrowth and neuronal differentiation, showed no significant variation among the groups (Fig. 8B), pointing that the treatment did not markedly affect neuronal regeneration.

O4, a surface antigen that serves as a marker for oligodendrocyte progenitor cells and immature oligodendrocytes, was analyzed by flow cytometry. The results showed no statistically significant differences in O4 expression between the experimental groups (Fig. 8C). Expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a well-established marker of astrocyte activation associated with gliosis, was evident in all experimental groups (Fig. 8D). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a reduction in GFAP expression levels in the PLGA-treated and PG-treated group compared to the SCI control group (Fig. 8D).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that PLGA-encapsulated galantamine (PG) shows superior therapeutic outcomes in spinal cord injury compared to systemic administration approaches. The observed functional improvements at 42 days, together with anti-inflammatory, and glial modulating effects, support the hypothesis that galantamine delivery during the critical secondary injury phase enhances neuroprotective efficacy.

Galantamine has been shown to have neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in several neurological diseases8 and has been demonstrated to improve functional recovery and decrease lesion size 42 days after spinal cord injury9. However, galantamine exhibits rapid systemic clearance when administered via IV or IP routes, limiting the sustained neuroprotective effects needed during the critical secondary injury phase of SCI, which extends over days to weeks post-injury. Drug delivery systems based on polymeric particles address this limitation by providing controlled drug release, leading to decreased side effects and increased bioavailability at the target tissue29. Using biodegradable materials, the drug is released as the polymer is naturally degraded by the organism, with degradation by-products metabolized into water and CO230.

The production of PLGA-galantamine particles by emulsion electrospray resulted in morphological changes compared to PLGA particles alone, consistent with findings by Yao and colleagues using water-dissolved BSA25. The addition of galantamine decreased particle size by 6-fold (568 nm), which is advantageous as smaller particles demonstrate higher cellular uptake and blood-brain barrier permeability31. Although galantamine incorporation decreased the zeta potential to -23.59 mV, this value remains within acceptable stability ranges32, demonstrating that the particles were suitable for in vivo administration.

The relatively low encapsulation efficiency (12.87%) resulted in surface-localized drug distribution, explaining the observed burst release profile25,33. This surface localization means that most galantamine is released during an initial burst phase rather than being sustained over the full 35-day period. This aligns with PLGA 75:25 degradation kinetics34 and the temporal evolution of SCI pathophysiology, where inflammation begins within minutes and IL-1β and TNF-α appear at 5 and 15 min post-injury3. The detection of galantamine for up to 35 days demonstrates potential for extended therapeutic effects throughout the critical secondary injury window.

The therapeutic effect of galantamine particles was assessed in vivo. Galantamine, PLGA and PG particles were administered at the lesion site in a contusion model of SCI in rats. This route of administration is widely used for scaffold implants and implantation of particles embedded in hydrogels35,36. Moreover, administration of particles directly at the injury site has proven effective and demonstrated to be retained in the site of administration for up to 5 days after injection37. At 42 days, the animals treated with PG presented a significant increase in motor function. In a previous study, intraperitoneal galantamine administration for 5 days after SCI also led to motor function improvement, corroborating with the hypothesis that the presence of galantamine in the initial phases of the injury leads to functional improvements9.

The investigation of the oxidative stress parameters showed some insights into galantamine’s actions. Our data revealed several unexpected patterns that difficult the interpretations. The lesion group did not show significant changes when compared to sham controls in some of the parameters investigated. This absence of a oxidative response may reflect limitations of bulk tissue homogenate assays, dilution of focal oxidative damage at the epicenter, characteristics of the moderate contusion model, or the possibility that our sampling timepoints missed the transient oxidative peak typically occurring within the first 24–48 h.

Galantamine alone showed delayed antioxidant effects only at the chronic timepoint. PG treatment demonstrated immediate and sustained benefits, including decreased lipid peroxidation levels acutely and maintained ROS reduction chronically. This sustained antioxidant activity likely reflects galantamine’s influence on pathways such as Jak/STAT3 and NF-κB, promoting anti-apoptotic effects and reducing oxidative stress38,39. This temporal pattern is difficult to explain. If sustained release from PLGA were the primary mechanism, we would expect free galantamine to be effective at day 3 when drug is present but not chronically at day 42 after clearance, with the opposite pattern for PG. Instead, we observe the reverse. This suggests that additional mechanisms are involved. The early antioxidant effect of PG may arise from PLGA mediated immune modulation, as phagocytosed PLGA particles can shift macrophages and microglia toward anti-inflammatory phenotypes40,41. Conversely, the delayed effect of free galantamine may reflect indirect cholinergic modulation of inflammatory cytokines, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines as IL-1β that in turn drive oxidative stress. The results indicate that oxidative stress modulation is unlikely to be the primary mechanism that leads to functional improvements observed in PG-treated animals.

Galantamine’s anti-inflammatory activity operates through α7 nAChR allosteric activation and AChE inhibition, activating the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and resulting in decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10)38,42. This mechanism has been demonstrated in other neuroinflammatory models, where galantamine administration significantly reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression39,43. Cytokine levels in the spinal cord have been detected in acute time-points, in which it has been demonstrated high levels of IL-1b and IL-6 4 h post injury44. Further, it has been shown that IL-1b levels remain high up until 7 dpi, and then drop at around 14 dpi45. In blood, high levels of IL-1b, IL-6 and TNF-a were detected in rats 4 weeks after injury46. In humans, IL-1b and TNF-a were also detected in patients’ blood for up to 12 weeks47.

Our results identify IL-1β suppression as the most consistent anti-inflammatory effect of PG treatment. PG reduced IL-1β at both early and late timepoints, whereas neither free galantamine nor PLGA alone produced measurable effects. This IL-1β reduction at both time points represents the most robust treatment effect observed across all outcomes, as this cytokine plays a critical role in perpetuating neuroinflammation and secondary tissue damage as neuronal toxicity, glial activation, disruption of blood–spinal cord barrier3,44. The selective reduction only by PG provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the functional improvements observed supporting a synergistic mechanism involving particle-mediated immune modulation combined with cholinergic signaling.

IL-6 measurements in this study produced results different from our IL-1β data and prior reports of galantamine’s anti-inflammatory effects. At 3 days post-injury, IL-6 levels were elevated in the SCI group as expected, but none of the galantamine-based treatments reduced IL-6 relative to SCI. At 42 days the sham group showed the highest IL-6 levels, a pattern that is biologically implausible for a laminectomy control. Technical factors may have contributed to the anomalous 42 day sham group elevation. Given these inconsistencies, IL-6 does not appear to be a major contributor to the therapeutic actions of PG in this model. Interestingly, PLGA particles alone significantly decreased IL-6 levels at both timepoints, an effect that persisted even without galantamine incorporation. This suggests synergistic anti-inflammatory mechanisms, where negatively charged particles are taken up by inflammatory monocytes through scavenger receptor MARCO binding, decreasing inflammatory cell trafficking40. Previous studies have shown that PLGA particle administration following SCI induces macrophage polarization toward regenerative phenotypes while decreasing proinflammatory markers41, potentially explaining the enhanced anti-inflammatory effects observed with PG treatment.

The absence of significant changes in NF-M and βIII-tubulin expression suggests that the therapeutic benefits observed like functional improvement, reduced oxidative stress and inflammation may be mediated through neuroprotection of existing neurons rather than promotion of axonal regeneration or neuronal differentiation. The lack of significant changes in O4 expression indicates that oligodendrocyte progenitor cell populations were not markedly affected by the treatments at the assessed timepoints, suggesting that remyelination processes may not be the primary mechanism of therapeutic benefit. The significant reduction in GFAP expression in PLGA and PG-treated groups represents an important finding, suggesting that these treatments may reduce astrocyte activation and potentially limit glial scar formation, which could contribute to the observed functional improvements.

The effects observed suggest that galantamine’s neuroprotective mechanisms go beyond acetylcholinesterase inhibition. The modulation of oxidative stress and selective cytokine reduction indicate complex interactions with cellular signaling pathways, including potential effects on microglial activation states, astrocytic responses, and neuronal survival mechanisms. The therapeutic approach appears to work primarily through tissue preservation and inflammation control rather than promoting regeneration, which has important implications for clinical translation and patient expectations. Future studies should investigate these cellular targets directly to optimize formulation parameters and predict clinical efficacy.

This study has some methodological and translational limitations. The in vitro release model does not replicate the in vivo kinetics due to the lack of biological factors such as enzymatic activity, immune cell interactions, and tissue-specific degradation. The direct intraspinal injection and single-dose application limit the clinical relevance, as treatments may require repeated dosing. Behavioral assessment was limited to BBB scoring. The low encapsulation efficiency may affect reproducibility. Long-term safety and repeated dosing of PLGA nanoparticles were not evaluated. Future work should refine treatment delivery and better define therapeutic parameters, including optimal routes, timing, dosing, and compatibility with clinical care. Mechanistic clarity can be improved by adding spatially resolved oxidative stress markers (4-HNE, 3-nitrotyrosine), expanding cytokine profiling (TNF-α, IL-10, IL-4), and incorporating earlier timepoints (6–24 h) to capture peak inflammation. In addition, axonal tracing studies will be important for directly assessing axonal preservation and regeneration.

Conclusions

Galantamine-containing PLGA particles were successfully produced by electrospraying. The present study demonstrated that PLGA galantamine nanoparticles improved motor function in rats 42 days after contusion SCI. This treatment produced consistent IL-1β suppression at both 3 and 42 days after injury, representing the primary anti-inflammatory mechanism underlying therapeutic efficacy. IL-6 levels were not significantly affected by galantamine or PG treatment, indicating cytokine-selective anti-inflammatory effects. While modest effects on ROS production were observed, oxidative stress modulation appears to be a secondary consequence of inflammation reduction rather than a primary therapeutic mechanism. The flow cytometry analysis of cell markers suggest that the therapeutic benefits are primarily mediated through anti-inflammatory and axonal-level protective mechanisms rather than enhanced regeneration, with reduced glial activation representing a potentially important component of the therapeutic response. Future studies must be done in order to establish the optimal dose and therapeutic window of this treatment. Further, modifying the particles’ surface in order to obtain a more sustained release profile might allow us to have a more robust response in future studies.
