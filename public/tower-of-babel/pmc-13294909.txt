Effect of MoS2 Interfacial Engineering across MAPbI3, FAPbI3, and CsPbI3 Perovskite Solar Cells
Authors: Muhammad Noman, Affaq Qamar, Shayan Tariq Jan, Abdul Haseeb Hassan Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perovskite solar cells have achieved rapid efficiency gains; however, interfacial recombination and energy-level mismatch remain major factors limiting performance consistency and stability. This work presents a systematic numerical investigation of interface engineering in perovskite solar cells using SCAPS-1D simulations. Three absorber materials of methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3), formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI3), and cesium lead iodide (CsPbI3) are examined within an identical device configuration employing TiO2 as the electron transport layer and spiro-OMeTAD as the hole transport layer. A thin molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) layer is introduced between the absorber and the hole transport layer to evaluate its influence on the band alignment, electric potential distribution, and photovoltaic performance. The results show that MoS2 modifies the valence band alignment at the back interface, increasing the local electric potential and supporting an improved charge separation. Devices incorporating MoS2 exhibit enhanced short-circuit current density, fill factor, and power conversion efficiency across all absorber compositions. Efficiency improvements from 19.28% to 21.54% for MAPbI3, from 20.10% to 22.25% for FAPbI3, and from 15.32% to 18.25% for CsPbI3 were observed. Parametric analyses further indicate an improved tolerance to variations in absorber thickness, doping concentration, temperature, and bulk defect density in MoS2-integrated structures. These findings demonstrate that MoS2-based interface modification offers a consistent pathway to reduce interfacial losses and improve the photovoltaic performance across hybrid and all-inorganic perovskite solar cells.

Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have emerged as a prominent class of third-generation photovoltaic devices owing to their high power conversion efficiency, solution-processable fabrication routes, and low material cost. Since the first solid-state PSCs were reported with efficiencies below 4%, sustained advances in materials chemistry and device engineering have increased certified efficiencies for small-area devices beyond 26%, placing PSCs among the most efficient single-junction photovoltaic technologies reported to date. The pace of this progress within a relatively short development period reflects the strong optoelectronic suitability of lead halide perovskite absorbers for photovoltaic energy conversion.

Beyond record efficiencies achieved on the laboratory scale, PSCs have demonstrated encouraging performance when extended toward larger device areas. Efficiencies exceeding 20% have been reported for subcentimeter-scale devices, while efficiencies above 10% have been achieved for device areas on the order of several tens of square centimeters. These results indicate measurable progress toward scalability, even as efficiency losses with increasing area remain an active concern. Collectively, these developments position PSCs as promising candidates for next-generation photovoltaic deployment with potential contributions to low-carbon energy generation and reduced manufacturing energy demand relative to conventional silicon-based technologies.

As efficiencies have advanced, increasing attention has focused on factors that limit operational stability and reproducibility at the device level. Environmental sensitivity to temperature, moisture, and prolonged illumination continues to constrain practical deployment. Under operational stress, performance degradation is frequently accompanied by scan-direction-dependent current–voltage characteristics, commonly referred to as hysteresis, which introduce uncertainty in efficiency assessment and reflect nonideal charge transport and accumulation processes within the device stack.

A growing body of experimental and numerical evidence indicates that these limitations arise predominantly from interfacial regions rather than from the bulk perovskite absorber. Junctions formed between the perovskite and charge-transport layers often exhibit a high density of structural and electronic defects, including vacancies, dangling bonds, and trap states. These defects promote nonradiative recombination, reducing the carrier lifetime and limiting photocurrent extraction. In addition, mismatches in energy levels across adjacent layers can introduce unfavorable band offsets that impede selective carrier transport, leading to charge accumulation and enhanced recombination near the contacts. Even absorbers exhibiting long diffusion lengths and strong optical absorption can suffer from limited device performance when the interface quality is insufficient, indicating that interface engineering is a central requirement for translating favorable bulk properties into reliable photovoltaic output.

Perovskite absorber composition further influences photovoltaic performance, thermal tolerance, and long-term stability. Hybrid organic–inorganic absorbers, particularly methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3) and formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI3), have been widely investigated due to their favorable bandgaps, strong optical absorption, and efficient charge transport properties. MAPbI3, with a bandgap near 1.55 eV, has served as a reference absorber in PSC research and has demonstrated high efficiencies under controlled conditions. FAPbI3 exhibits a narrower bandgap of approximately 1.48 eV, providing improved spectral utilization closer to the single-junction optimum and motivating extensive investigation for high-efficiency applications. In parallel, all-inorganic perovskite absorbers have attracted interest as candidates for improved thermal and environmental tolerance. Cesium lead iodide (CsPbI3), with a bandgap of approximately 1.73 eV, exhibits enhanced resistance to thermal stress compared to organic-containing counterparts. Experimental realizations of CsPbI3-based PSCs have generally reported lower efficiencies than hybrid devices, primarily due to phase instability under ambient conditions and pronounced interfacial losses. Drift–diffusion simulations under optimized assumptions suggest that CsPbI3 devices may achieve efficiencies exceeding 20% when material properties and interface conditions are carefully controlled, indicating that performance limitations are strongly influenced by device architecture and junction quality rather than absorber properties alone.

Even with extensive individual studies of MAPbI3-, FAPbI3-, and CsPbI3-based PSCs, systematic comparisons across these absorber classes remain limited, particularly with respect to interface engineering strategies. Many investigations concentrate on a single absorber composition, restricting the ability to determine whether observed performance improvements are absorber-specific or broadly applicable. This gap hinders the development of transferable design guidelines for hybrid and all-inorganic perovskite technologies and motivates a comparative assessment of device configurations.

Two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenides have gained attention as interfacial modification layers in PSCs due to their favorable electronic properties and compatibility with thin-film architectures. Among these materials, molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) has been widely studied as an interfacial layer owing to its tunable bandgap, high carrier mobility, and chemical stability. Depending on thickness, MoS2 exhibits bandgap values in the range of approximately 1.3–1.9 eV, enabling adjustment of energy level alignment between the perovskite absorber and charge transport layers. Previous experimental and numerical studies have reported improved charge extraction and reduced interfacial recombination when MoS2 is introduced at critical junctions, often accompanied by enhanced operational stability under continuous illumination. Nevertheless, existing studies predominantly examine isolated device configurations and focus on single absorber systems. Direct links between interfacial band alignment, internal electric potential distribution, and macroscopic photovoltaic parameters are often not established, limiting generalization across different perovskite compositions. A comparative investigation employing a consistent modeling approach is therefore required to clarify the absorber-independent role of MoS2 in governing charge transport, recombination behavior, and device performance.

The choice of charge transport layers further influences carrier selectivity and interfacial energetics. In this work, titanium dioxide (TiO2) and spiro-OMeTAD are adopted as the electron and hole transport layers, respectively, due to their widespread use in high-efficiency PSC architectures. TiO2 provides favorable conduction band alignment with lead halide perovskites and supports selective electron extraction, while spiro-OMeTAD offers suitable energy level alignment for hole extraction and efficient charge collection. , The use of these standard transport layers allows for the influence of interface layer modification to be evaluated directly without ambiguity arising from variations in the baseline device structure.

Motivated by these considerations, this study examines the influence of MoS2 as an interfacial passivation layer in perovskite solar cells, employing different absorber materials. SCAPS-1D numerical simulations were used to analyze devices based on MAPbI3, FAPbI3, and CsPbI3 within an identical architecture incorporating TiO2 as the electron transport layer and spiro-OMeTAD as the hole transport layer. Device performance is evaluated for reference structures without an interface layer and for modified structures incorporating a thin MoS2 layer between the absorber and hole transport layer. This work provides a comparative assessment of hybrid and all-inorganic perovskite absorbers under consistent conditions, establishes relationships among interfacial band alignment, internal electric potential, and photovoltaic performance metrics, and examines the influence of absorber thickness, doping concentration, temperature, and bulk defect density on device behavior. The results offer quantitative guidance for interface engineering strategies aimed at improving the efficiency and stability in perovskite solar cells.

Device Structure and Simulation Methodology

Several numerical tools are available for modeling the electrical behavior of photovoltaic devices, including SILVACO, COMSOL, SETFOS, ATLAS, and SCAPS. In this work, simulations were carried out using SCAPS-1D (version 3.3.10), selected due to its suitability for one-dimensional drift–diffusion analysis of thin-film solar cells and its established use in perovskite device studies. SCAPS enables simulations under illuminated and dark conditions and supports multilayer heterostructures with user-defined material parameters. The software was developed at the Department of Electronics and Information Systems, Ghent University, Belgium, and evaluates photovoltaic performance by solving the coupled semiconductor transport equations to obtain current–voltage characteristics, band diagrams, and key device metrics, including open-circuit voltage (V OC), short-circuit current density (J SC), fill factor (FF), and power conversion efficiency (PCE).

Carrier transport within the device is described by the continuity equations for electrons and holes, coupled with Poisson’s equation. The electrostatic potential within the device is governed by Poisson’s equation, 3 −∂∂x(ε(x)∂V∂x)=q[p(x)−n(x)+ND+(x)−NA−(x)+pt(x)−nt(x)] where ε­(x) is the position-dependent dielectric permittivity, V is the electrostatic potential, N D + and N A – denote the ionized donor and acceptor concentrations, respectively, and n t and p t represent trapped electron and hole densities, respectively. These coupled equations are solved self-consistently within SCAPS to obtain steady-state device characteristics.

All simulations were performed under standard test conditions, assuming an incident illumination of 1000 W m–2 with the AM 1.5G solar spectrum at a temperature of 300 K. The thermal velocities of electrons and holes were set to 1 × 107 cm s–1. It should be noted that SCAPS-1D considers the device surface area as unity (1 cm2) by default, and therefore all current densities and derived photovoltaic parameters are normalized accordingly. Device performance was first evaluated for the reference structures without the interfacial layer, followed by simulations incorporating the MoS2 layer. Key photovoltaic parameters, including V OC, J SC, FF, and PCE, were extracted for both configurations to assess the influence of the interfacial modification. Additional simulations were conducted by varying absorber thickness and doping concentration to examine trends in device behavior with and without the MoS2 layer.

It should be noted that the study is based on numerical simulations using SCAPS-1D, which inherently involve certain idealized assumptions. Parameters such as defect densities, interface properties, and material uniformity are controlled in the simulation environment and may not fully capture the complexities of real-device fabrication. As a result, the reported efficiencies represent optimized or near-ideal conditions and should be interpreted as indicative of performance trends, rather than absolute experimental outcomes. Nevertheless, the consistency of the observed trends with the reported experimental results supports the validity of the simulation approach and highlights the potential of MoS2 as an effective interfacial modification strategy in perovskite solar cells.

Results and Discussion

Energy Band Alignment

Energy band alignment at the interfaces between the perovskite absorber and the charge transport layers plays a central role in governing charge extraction, recombination behavior, and the overall photovoltaic performance. Efficient electron transport requires close alignment between the conduction band minimum of the perovskite and that of the electron transport layer, enabling electrons to be transferred with a minimal energetic loss. At the same interface, a sufficiently large separation between the valence bands is required to suppress hole transport toward the electron transport layer and limit recombination. At the absorber/hole transport layer junction, effective hole extraction is favored by a small valence band offset, whereas a larger conduction band offset restricts electron transfer toward the hole transport layer.

Modification of the absorber/HTL interface through the introduction of an interfacial layer can alter these offsets and redistribute the internal electric field near the back contact. This redistribution may reduce defect-assisted recombination and improve the charge selectivity. For the MAPbI3/TiO2 interface, the VBO and CBO are calculated as 1.75 eV and −0.10 eV, respectively. The large VBO effectively suppresses hole transport toward the electron transport layer, while the shallow conduction band cliff formed by the negative CBO supports efficient electron extraction. This alignment is favorable for charge separation and limits recombination at the front contact. At the MAPbI3/spiro-OMeTAD interface without an interfacial layer, the VBO and CBO are −0.25 and 1.70 eV, respectively, indicating the presence of a valence band barrier for hole transport alongside strong suppression of electron leakage. Introducing the MoS2 layer redistributes these offsets across two junctions, forming a stepped alignment that facilitates hole transfer. At the MAPbI3/MoS2 interface, a small positive VBO of 0.04 eV introduces a valence band spike, which increases the local built-in potential and can enhance charge separation near the back contact.

At the FAPbI3/TiO2 interface, the VBO and CBO are 1.69 and 0 eV, respectively. This configuration maintains effective hole blocking while providing near-ideal alignment for electron transport. In the absence of an interfacial layer, the FAPbI3/spiro-OMeTAD junction exhibits a VBO of −0.31 eV and a CBO of 1.80 eV. The larger negative VBO relative to MAPbI3 indicates a more pronounced valence band barrier for hole extraction. Incorporation of the MoS2 interfacial layer modifies this junction by forming a stepped valence band profile, where the effective hole barrier is reduced to −0.02 eV at the FAPbI3/MoS2 interface. This reduction lowers the energetic requirement for hole transport across the back contact.

For the CsPbI3/TiO2 interface, the calculated VBO and CBO are 1.55 eV and −0.05 eV, respectively, supporting selective electron extraction and suppression of hole transport. The CsPbI3/spiro-OMeTAD interface without the interfacial layer exhibits the largest negative VBO among the three absorbers (−0.44 eV), together with a large CBO of 1.75 eV. This alignment indicates a substantial barrier for hole transport, which can restrict photocurrent extraction. With the introduction of the MoS2 layer, the valence band offset is reduced to −0.15 eV at the CsPbI3/MoS2 interface, forming a stepped alignment that supports improved hole transfer while preserving strong electron blocking at the back contact.

Electric Potential at Interface

When two dissimilar materials within a perovskite solar cell are brought into contact, a heterojunction is formed, leading to the development of an internal electric potential at the interface. This potential originates from differences in electronic properties, primarily the work function and electron affinity of the adjoining layers, which induce band bending to establish an electrostatic equilibrium. The magnitude and spatial distribution of this electric potential are governed by the relative band alignment at the interface. As discussed in the preceding subsection, valence band spikes are generally associated with an increase in the local electric potential, whereas valence band cliffs tend to reduce it by facilitating charge accumulation and recombination near the junction.

The simulated electric potential profiles at the interface between the perovskite absorber and the hole transport layer, evaluated with and without the MoS2 interfacial layer, are presented in Figure for all three absorber materials. In the absence of the interfacial layer, the MAPbI3-, FAPbI3-, and CsPbI3-based devices exhibit a reduced electric potential at the back contact, consistent with the presence of pronounced valence band cliffs identified in the energy band alignment analysis. These reduced potentials indicate weaker internal fields for separating the photogenerated carriers at the absorber/HTL junction.

Electric potential distribution at the interface between the perovskite absorber and the HTL with and without MoS2: (a) MAPbI3 perovskite, (b) FAPbI3 perovskite, and (c) CsPbI3 perovskite.

Incorporation of the MoS2 interfacial layer leads to a clear increase in the electric potential at the absorber/HTL interface for all three perovskite compositions. This increase is associated with the redistribution of band offsets across the absorber/MoS2 and MoS2/HTL junctions, which reduces the effective valence-band barrier for hole transport and enhances the built-in field near the back contact. The enhanced electric potential strengthens the driving force for charge separation, thereby supporting the more efficient extraction of photogenerated carriers.

Among the simulated absorbers, the MAPbI3-based device exhibits the highest interfacial electric potential after inclusion of the MoS2 layer, reaching values on the order of 1.6 × 107 V cm–1. This behavior is consistent with the formation of a small valence band spike at the MAPbI3/MoS2 interface, which increases the local built-in potential. The CsPbI3-based device shows the lowest interfacial electric potential among the three absorbers, reflecting the comparatively larger residual valence band barrier even after interface modification. The FAPbI3-based device exhibits intermediate behavior, in line with its stepped band alignment and moderate reduction in the hole transport barrier.

These trends indicate that the MoS2 interfacial layer enhances the internal electric field at the absorber/HTL junction across all absorber materials, with the magnitude of improvement being dependent on the specific band alignment of the perovskite. The resulting increase in the electric potential provides a consistent physical explanation for the improvements in charge separation and photovoltaic performance discussed in the subsequent sections.

Quantum Efficiency

Quantum efficiency (QE) describes the ability of a solar cell to convert incident photons into mobile charge carriers and serves as a direct indicator of photocarrier generation and collection within the device. In this study, the quantum efficiency corresponds to the external quantum efficiency (EQE), defined as the ratio of the number of collected charge carriers to incident photons at each wavelength. It is wavelength dependent and reflects the combined influence of optical absorption, carrier transport, and recombination processes. Improvements in EQE are commonly associated with enhanced charge separation and reduced recombination losses, particularly near interfacial regions, where photogenerated carriers are extracted. The calculated EQE spectra for the MAPbI3-, FAPbI3-, and CsPbI3-based perovskite solar cells, evaluated with and without the MoS2 interfacial layer, are presented in Figure .

Quantum efficiency spectra of the PSC structures with and without the incorporation of a MoS2 interfacial layer.

Across all three absorber materials, the introduction of the MoS2 interfacial layer leads to a consistent enhancement in EQE over the measured wavelength range. A relative increase of approximately 2–3% is observed for each device configuration, indicating improved collection of photogenerated carriers. Although this increase appears modest, it is consistent across the entire wavelength range and directly contributes to the enhancement in short-circuit current density. The similarity in spectral shape between the reference and modified devices indicates that the improvement arises primarily from enhanced carrier collection rather than changes in spectral response.

The increase in EQE is because of the higher electric potential established near the back-contact in the presence of the MoS2 layer, which strengthens charge separation and promotes carrier transport toward the respective electrodes. The improved band alignment at the absorber/HTL interface reduces the severity of valence band discontinuities and suppresses interfacial recombination, allowing a larger fraction of photogenerated carriers to contribute to the external current.

At longer wavelengths, slight deviations between the reference and the MoS2-modified devices are observed. These variations are associated with carriers generated deeper within the absorber layer, which are more susceptible to recombination because of their longer transport paths. The presence of the MoS2 interfacial layer reduces recombination losses at the absorber/HTL junction and enhances carrier extraction from these regions, resulting in an improved collection efficiency in the long-wavelength regime.

The similarity of the EQE enhancement across different absorber materials indicates that the MoS2 interfacial layer provides a broadly effective means of improving carrier collection through interface engineering rather than absorber-specific effects. These observations establish a direct link between the interfacial electric potential discussed in Section and the current–voltage characteristics analyzed in Section .

The increase in current density is consistent with the formation of a small valence band spike at the absorber/interlayer junction, which enhances the local built-in potential and strengthens charge separation, as discussed in Sections and 3.2.

A similar trend is observed for FAPbI3-based devices. The reference structure without the interfacial layer exhibits a V OC of 1.17 V, a J SC of 22.8 mA cm–2, a fill factor of 74.37%, and an efficiency of 20.10%. This improvement can be associated with the reduction of the valence-band barrier at the absorber/HTL interface through the stepped band alignment provided by the MoS2 layer, which facilitates hole transport and reduces interfacial recombination.

For the CsPbI3-based devices, the structure without an interfacial layer yields a V OC of 1.10 V, a J SC of 18.3 mA cm–2, a fill factor of 75.56%, and an efficiency of 15.32%. These changes increased the overall efficiency to 18.25%. The comparatively larger improvement in current density reflects the pronounced reduction of the valence band barrier for hole transport in CsPbI3-based devices following interface modification, as identified in energy band alignment analysis.

Across all three absorber materials, the dominant contribution to efficiency enhancement arises from increases in short-circuit current density, accompanied by moderate improvements in the fill factor. Variations in the open-circuit voltage are comparatively small, indicating that the primary effect of the MoS2 interfacial layer is to improve charge extraction and reduce recombination near the absorber/HTL junction rather than altering bulk recombination mechanisms. The trends observed in Figure and Table are consistent with the enhancements in the electric potential and quantum efficiency discussed in the preceding subsections, providing a coherent picture of performance improvement through interfacial engineering.

Temperature Effects

The PV performance of perovskite solar cells is sensitive to operating temperature, as temperature variations influence carrier concentration, recombination processes, and transport properties within the device. As temperature increases, the intrinsic carrier concentration in the perovskite absorber rises, which can increase recombination rates and reduce the separation of quasi-Fermi levels, leading to a decrease in open-circuit voltage. Thermal effects may also influence lattice spacing and electron–phonon interactions, which can affect carrier mobility and the overall charge transport. The improved performance of MoS2-modified devices is because of the enhanced interfacial band alignment and reduced recombination at the absorber/HTL junction, which supports efficient charge extraction even under elevated temperature conditions.

Bulk defects in perovskite absorbers arise from imperfections within the crystal lattice that extend throughout the material volume. These defects commonly include vacancies, interstitials, and antisite defects, all of which introduce localized energy states within the band gap. Such defect states act as recombination centers for photogenerated charge carriers, reducing the carrier lifetime and mobility and thereby limiting photovoltaic performance.

By improving interfacial band alignment and limiting carrier accumulation near the back-contact, the MoS2 layer supports more effective charge separation and extraction, partially compensating for losses introduced by bulk defects. These results indicate that interfacial passivation can alleviate, although not eliminate, the detrimental impact of high defect densities on device performance.

Conclusion

This study employed SCAPS-1D simulations to evaluate the influence of interface engineering on the performance of perovskite solar cells using three absorber compositionsMAPbI3, FAPbI3, and CsPbI3within a consistent device architecture based on TiO2 as the electron transport layer and spiro-OMeTAD as the hole transport layer. A thin MoS2 layer was introduced between the absorber and the hole transport layer to assess its effect on interfacial energetics, charge transport behavior, and photovoltaic performance. The observations reported here are derived from numerical modeling and represent trends associated with interfacial band alignment and carrier transport under controlled simulation conditions.

The results indicate that the incorporation of MoS2 modifies band alignment at the absorber/HTL junction by converting abrupt valence band barriers into stepped profiles. This redistribution of band offsets increases the local electric potential near the back-contact and supports improved separation and extraction of photogenerated carriers. As a result, all three absorber systems exhibit an enhanced short-circuit current density and fill factor, leading to improved power conversion efficiency. The MAPbI3-based device shows an efficiency increase from 19.28% to 21.54%, the FAPbI3-based device from 20.10% to 22.25%, and the CsPbI3-based device from 15.32% to 18.25%, with only minor changes observed in the open-circuit voltage.

Parametric analyses further indicate that the benefits of this interface modification persist across variations in the absorber thickness and doping concentration. Devices incorporating MoS2 maintain a higher current density and efficiency over a broad parameter range, although excessively high doping concentrations lead to reduced performance due to increased recombination. Temperature-dependent simulations reveal a general decline in efficiency with an increase in temperature for all devices, while MoS2-integrated structures maintain higher absolute performance across the examined range. In addition, simulations incorporating bulk and interfacial defect density demonstrate improved tolerance to defect-induced recombination in the presence of MoS2, although the performance remains sensitive to defect levels.

Overall, the results suggest that MoS2 can function as an effective interfacial modification layer by improving band alignment, supporting charge extraction, and reducing recombination losses at the absorber/HTL junction under simulated conditions. However, it should be noted that these findings are based on numerical simulations and a limited number of design parameters. Therefore, the reported performance improvements represent optimized trends, rather than guaranteed experimental outcomes. Further experimental validation is required to assess the practical feasibility of MoS2 integration, particularly with respect to the interface quality, defect control, and fabrication constraints. Despite these limitations, the present study provides useful insights into the role of interfacial engineering in perovskite solar cells and may serve as a guide for future experimental and modeling efforts.
