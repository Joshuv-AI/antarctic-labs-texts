Ascites Bactericidal Capacity in Patients With Decompensated Cirrhosis
Authors: Isanka U. Ratnasekera, Min-Duy Phan, Nhu Nguyen, Eline E. Jessen, Sahar Keshvari, Yvette Ong, Timothy J. Kidd, Steven J. Hancock, Samuel A.S. Morris, David P. Sester, David A. Hume, Elizabeth E. Powell, Matthew J. Sweet, Mark A. Schembri, Katharine M. Irvine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsPatients with decompensated cirrhosis are at high risk of developing bacterial infections. The most common infection is spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in ascites fluid, caused by gut bacterial translocation and facilitated by cirrhosis associated immune dysfunction. Common causative agents of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis are thought to represent pathobionts that increase in abundance in the gut with cirrhosis severity, rather than opportunistic commensals. Despite this there have been few studies describing the characteristics of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis pathogens and immune responses to them.MethodsWe investigated the bactericidal capacity of cell-free ascites fluid and ascites macrophages.ResultsNonpathogenic Escherichia coli K12 was rapidly killed in cell-free ascites fluid, whereas extraintestinal pathogenic E coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains, representative of the most common causes of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, were resistant. A transposon-based genome-wide screen identified a requirement for genes involved in O-antigen biosynthesis for survival of both organisms in cell-free ascites fluid, including the transcription factor RfaH, which controls expression of surface polysaccharides. Although extraintestinal pathogenic E coli were resistant to fluid-mediated killing, ascites macrophages, especially VSIG4Hi cells, phagocytosed and killed extraintestinal pathogenic E coli and nonpathogenic E coli K-12 with similar efficacy. Mice with toxin-induced cirrhosis had higher bacterial burdens in the liver compared with control mice when infected with extraintestinal pathogenic E coli, but rapidly cleared an rfaH mutant attenuated for capsule and O-antigen biosynthesis.ConclusionsThese data highlight the importance of understanding bacterial virulence profiles in addition to taxonomy in cirrhosis dysbiosis and identify critical roles for capsule and O-antigen in host evasion by spontaneous bacterial peritonitis–associated pathogens. Targeting bacterial virulence factors and/or host macrophage functions may reduce the risk of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in cirrhosis.

SummaryThis study demonstrates that spontaneous bactericidal peritonitis–associated Gram-negative pathogens evade killing in cell-free ascites fluid, and identifies critical roles for capsule and O-antigen in resistance to killing. By contrast, ascites macrophages, especially VSIG4Hi macrophages, could rapidly kill pathogenic bacteria. These findings highlight the importance of understanding host pathogen interactions and bacterial virulence profiles to developing novel therapeutic strategies to prevent and treat infections in patients with cirrhosis. What You Need to Know

Background

Patients with decompensated cirrhosis are at high risk of bacterial infections, most commonly spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. We need to better understand host and microbial factors that contribute to infection risk to identify new strategies to improve patient outcomes and minimize antimicrobial resistance.

Impact

This study explored the capacity of cell-free ascites fluid and ascites macrophages to kill pathogenic and nonpathogenic bacteria and identified bacterial defense mechanisms contributing to resistance to fluid-mediated killing. The data suggest that targeting bacterial virulence factors or host macrophage functions may reduce the risk of infection in cirrhosis.

Future Directions

This study highlights the importance of understanding bacterial virulence profiles in addition to taxonomy in cirrhosis dysbiosis and identifies host and microbial factors that could be investigated as therapeutic targets.

Cirrhosis is the result of long-term continuous injury to the liver. In 2019, cirrhosis accounted for over 1.5 million deaths worldwide, representing 2.4% of global deaths and a 10% increment from 2010.1 Clinical status varies between compensated, decompensated, and acute-on-chronic liver failure during the illness, with bacterial infections being 1 of the main reasons for the transition from compensated to acute decompensated cirrhosis and acute-on-chronic liver failure. Bacterial infections increase the risk of mortality 2- to 4-fold, independent of baseline disease severity, and have been suggested to represent a distinct prognostic stage of cirrhosis.2, 3, 4, 5 Frequent hospital visits, intensive care unit stays, surgical procedures, use of proton pump inhibitors, and chronic antibiotic use for intestinal prophylaxis expose these patients to recurrent infections. Cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction, encompassing a spectrum of deficiencies that have been observed in humoral and cellular innate and adaptive immune function, also contributes to the elevated infection risk.6,7

The most common complication in patients with cirrhosis is accumulation of ascites fluid (AF) in the peritoneal cavity. Infection of AF, known as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), is the most common infection in patients with cirrhosis.8 SBP is associated with high morbidity and mortality, with around 20% in-hospital mortality and up to 60% mortality after 1 year, and intensive use of health resources due to long and repeated hospital stays.4,9 A unique combination of host and bacterial factors in cirrhotic patients, including gut dysbiosis with increasing prevalence of pathobionts,10 increased intestinal permeability, and immunodeficiency, is thought to contribute to the high incidence of SBP.6 Patients with ascites due to other causes (eg, malignancy) very seldom develop peritonitis.11 SBP is considered to result from translocation of intestinal bacteria, which reach the peritoneum via the mesenteric lymph nodes and bloodstream, combined with a failure of host defense mechanisms to eliminate translocating organisms.

The most common infectious causes of SBP are extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) among Gram-negative bacteria, and Staphylococcus aureus and nonenterococcal Streptococci among Gram-positives.8,12 Prophylactic antibiotics are used in high-risk patients with decompensated cirrhosis, and empirical antibiotic treatment is frequently administered in patients with SBP, due to the high frequency of culture-negative infections.13, 14, 15 The efficacy of currently recommended empirical antibiotic treatment is reported to be as low as 40%.15,16 During the past decade, there have been multiple reports of increasing multidrug-resistant bacteria in SBP,8,17, 18, 19 although this varies according to geographic region. For example, in a recent longitudinal study, we did not detect an increase in multidrug-resistant infections in either ascites or bloodstream infections over a 10-year period in Australia. However, hospital admissions associated with infections did increase over time, in line with the overall increase in admissions of patients with cirrhosis.12,20,21 Nevertheless, the rise in antibiotic resistance has contributed to an increasing focus on understanding host immune function, with a view to improving innate resistance to infection for better patient outcomes and reduced antibiotic use.6,7

Cirrhosis-associated immunodeficiency affects both humoral and cell-mediated defense mechanisms.6 Liver insufficiency caused by cirrhosis leads to reduced production of circulating immune surveillance and defense factors, such as complement proteins, that are normally produced by the liver. The complement system, along with other factors such as lysozyme and antimicrobial peptides, is important for direct bacterial killing by serum.22 It was reported more than 40 years ago that patients with cirrhosis have reduced serum bactericidal activity.23 As a serum transudate, caused by increased leakage of fluid secondary to portal hypertension, AF may have similar killing capacity and mechanisms to serum, but transudate ascites contain relatively low protein levels. AF from cirrhotic patients was reported to have significantly lower concentrations of total protein, all 3 immunoglobulin classes and complement (C3 and C4), and reduced bactericidal and opsonophagocytic activity compared with noncirrhotic ascites, and complement levels inversely correlated with the development of SBP.11,24, 25, 26, 27, 28

Peritoneal macrophages play a central role in peritoneal immunity, from recognizing, engulfing, and killing invading microorganisms, to immune cell recruitment, antigen presentation, and resolution of inflammation. Ascites macrophage phenotype and function are associated with liver disease severity and may influence clinical outcomes in SBP. We identified 2 distinct ascites macrophage populations; a highly phagocytic population expressing VSIG4 (also known as Complement Receptor for Immunoglobulin, CRIg), which resembled murine resident peritoneal macrophages, and a poorly phagocytic VSIG4Low population, resembling monocytes.29 Others reported elevated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory cytokine production by VSIG4+ compared with VSIG4− macrophages.30 Peritoneal macrophage activation by bacteria or bacterial products led to rapid cleavage of surface VSIG4 and CD206, and accumulation of these markers in AF was a predictor of increased 90-day mortality in patients with SBP.30,31 Inflammatory priming in AF has been associated with chronic translocation of bacteria and/or bacterial products, including bacterial DNA, which is routinely detected in AF from patients with cirrhosis, even in the absence of overt infection.32, 33, 34, 35 To understand host and microbial factors that contribute to the high burden of SBP in patients with cirrhosis, we characterized the bactericidal capacity of cell-free AF and ascites macrophages from patients, as well as subversion mechanisms used by bacterial pathogens. We also validated our findings in a mouse model of cirrhosis-associated bacterial infection, thus revealing potential anti-infective strategies to limit SBP in patients with cirrhosis.

Results

Bactericidal Activity of Cell-Free Ascites Fluid

Healthy serum is bactericidal for many commensal Gram-negative bacteria. The ability to resist the bactericidal capacity of serum facilitates survival in the bloodstream and is an essential virulence trait of ExPEC that cause sepsis and urinary tract infections.36 To investigate the bactericidal activity of cell-free AF, we compared survival of the serum-sensitive nonpathogenic E coli K-12 strain MG1655,37 the ExPEC strain EC958, a serum-resistant strain representative of the globally disseminated sequence type 131 clone36 that causes high rates of SBP and bloodstream infections,38, 39, 40 and an E coli strain isolated from a patient with SBP at our hospital. We also investigated survival of the virulent Kp strain Kp52.14541 (also known as B5055) and a clinical Kp isolate from a liver abscess, representative of the second most common SBP-causing bacteria, and an Enterobacter cloacae SBP isolate. Bacterial strains were incubated for 90 minutes in cell-free AF or heat-inactivated (HI) (ie, complement-inactivated) AF collected from patients with no recent history of antibiotic use, after which bacterial survival was determined by enumeration of colony forming units (CFU). The nonpathogenic MG1655 strain was completely killed following incubation in cell-free AF but not HI AF (control). In contrast, EC958 and both Kp and SBP isolates survived and were recovered in equivalent numbers in cell-free AF and HI AF (Figure 1A). Functional Genomic Profiling Defines the Genetic Basis of Resistance to Ascites Fluid Killing

To investigate mechanisms of bacterial defense against cell-free AF killing, we employed a saturated transposon mutant library of EC95836 and KP52.145 (this study; see Methods) in combination with transposon-directed insertion-site sequencing (TraDIS) to identify genes that contribute to resistance to killing. Approximately 1 million mutants from each library were incubated in cell-free AF from a single donor for 90 minutes, a period that permitted survival of resistant mutants while eliminating mutants sensitive to cell-free AF. Surviving mutants were then enriched by growth in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth for 4 hours, a step used to optimize detection of transposon insertion sites in surviving cells and avoid the impact of genomic DNA from lysed cells in downstream analyses. In parallel, mutant libraries were exposed to HI AF lacking bactericidal activity as a control. Cells from these experiments were then pelleted by centrifugation, and genomic DNA was extracted as previously described.36 In parallel, mutant libraries were exposed to HI AF lacking bactericidal activity as a control. DNA from test and control samples was processed and sequenced (Figure 1B). In EC958, 21 (51.2%) of the identified genes encode proteins directly involved in the regulation or biosynthesis of LPS (including O-antigen and lipid A-core biosynthesis) or enterobacterial common antigen (ECA) (Table 1). Many of the other identified genes likely feed into the production of these cell-surface polysaccharides; for example, by controlling steps in the metabolism of sugars required for polysaccharide biosynthesis (pgm, galE, pgi, fbp), by maintaining outer membrane integrity (lpp, bamB), and by facilitating the correct folding of periplasmic proteins (dsbA). Comparatively fewer genes were identified in Kp. However, consistent with the EC958 screen, most of the identified genes encode factors involved in LPS regulation or biosynthesis (Table 2). The rfaH gene, which encodes a regulator of LPS outer core and O-antigen biosynthesis,43,44 the waaL gene, which encodes an enzyme responsible for O-antigen ligation to LPS core,45 and the arnD gene, which encodes an enzyme responsible for lipid A modification,46 were identified in the TraDIS screens for both organisms (Figure 1E and F). Notably, for both organisms, there was strong overlap with genes identified in previous studies that investigated mechanisms of survival in human serum. Indeed, 35 of 41 genes (85%) identified in EC958 were previously shown to contribute to serum resistance,36,42 and 7 of 9 genes (78%) identified in Kp were also associated with serum resistance42 (Tables 1 and 2). Taken together, this demonstrates a critical role for extracellular cell surface polysaccharides produced by ExPEC and Kp for survival in cell-free AF and strongly suggests that host innate mechanisms of killing in both cell-free AF and serum are similar.

Validation of Ascites Fluid Resistance Genes

To validate our TraDIS data, we mutated several of the identified genes in EC958 and Kp52.145 that were associated with resistance to cell-free AF and tested the survival of these mutants following incubation in cell-free AF sourced from an independent donor. In EC958, mutation of rfaH completely attenuated survival, with mutation of waaL, wzxE, and fis resulting in a moderately reduced survival phenotype (Figure 2A). Given this result, and previous data showing RfaH regulates LPS core, O-antigen, and capsule biosynthesis,43,47 we predicted that, although capsule genes were not identified in our screen, they may also contribute to survival in cell-free AF. Indeed, capsule genes (kpsMT, kpsFEDCS) were just below our stringent TraDIS cutoff criteria (Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, we also constructed an EC958 capsule mutant (by mutating kpsD) and a double O-antigen-capsule mutant (by mutating waaL and kpsD) and tested the survival of both mutants in cell-free AF from the independent donor. In these experiments, the mutation of kpsD significantly reduced survival in cell-free AF, whereas mutation of both capsule and O-antigen completely attenuated survival, mirroring the phenotype of the rfaH mutant (Figure 2A). The fis gene encodes a nucleoid-associated DNA-binding protein which has not previously been linked to the control of LPS biosynthesis in E coli. Thus, we polymerase chain reaction–amplified and cloned the fis gene from EC958 and used the resultant plasmid for complementation. Complementation of the EC958 fis mutant with this plasmid restored survival in AF to wild-type (WT) level, confirming the role of fis in this phenotype (Figure 2A). Targeted mutagenesis of all 9 Kp52.145 genes identified in the TraDIS screen confirmed their role in survival in AF (Figure 2B). The mutants also lost the mucoid colony phenotype of the WT strain, supporting their role in surface polysaccharide expression. Furthermore, complementation of the rfaH mutant with a plasmid encoding the rfaH gene restored survival in AF to WT level. Taken together, based on the analysis of a selection of mutants in EC958 and Kp52.145, we observed strong concordance between the genes identified in the TraDIS screen and their role in survival in AF.

The strong susceptibility of the EC958 kpsD mutant to cell-free AF in the validation experiment suggested substantial variation in killing capacity between donors. This was confirmed by analyzing the survival of selected mutants in cell-free AF from 5 independent donors. Both the EC958 ΔkpsD and ΔwaaL mutants exhibited donor-dependent complete resistance or susceptibility, whereas disruption of capsule and O-antigen determinants by mutation of kpsD/waaL and rfaH were completely susceptible to killing in AF from all donors (Figure 2C). Kp52.145 ΔwaaL and ΔrfaH were similarly attenuated in all donors. Notably, unlike EC958, ΔrfaH mutation did not render Kp52.145 completely sensitive to cell-free AF (Figure 1C).

Mutation of E coli Ascites Fluid Resistance Genes Partially Restores Complement Activation

The survival of the nonpathogenic MG1655 strain in HI AF, combined with the identification of genes associated with LPS core and O-antigen biosynthesis in EC958 and Kp52.145, suggested a role for complement in the bactericidal activity of AF. We therefore assessed the deposition of complement proteins and IgG on MG1655, EC958, EC958ΔrfaH, EC958Δfis, EC958ΔwaaL, and EC958ΔkpsD in AF. We therefore investigated the impact of AF on phagocytosis of E coli bioparticles (BPs, derived from E coli K-12) by ascites macrophages. Incubation in autologous AF significantly increased the percentage of phagocytic VSIG4Low macrophages and their phagocytic capacity (Figure 5B and C). Autologous AF also increased the phagocytic capacity of VSIG4Hi macrophages, but not the percentage of BP-positive cells, which was already >80% (Figure 5B and C). Induction of Chronic Liver Disease in Mice Promotes Extraintestinal Pathogenic E coli Dissemination

To model the increased susceptibility of patients with late-stage chronic liver disease to bacterial infections, male mice were administered the liver toxin thioacetamide (TAA) in drinking water for 12 weeks to induce chronic inflammation and liver fibrosis, then returned to normal drinking water for 2 weeks prior to bacterial infection. Control mice were maintained on normal drinking water throughout. Acute inflammation was largely resolved by 2 weeks post-TAA cessation, but advanced, bridging fibrosis (cirrhosis) remained (Figure 6A and B), as we previously reported.54 Mice were infected with WT EC958 or EC958ΔrfaH by intraperitoneal injection and euthanized 6 hours p.i. Body weight loss in infected mice was similar between groups (Figure 6C). At this time point, there was no significant difference in WT EC958 colonization of the peritoneum of treated and untreated mice (Figure 6D). In contrast, TAA-treated mice infected with WT EC958 had a significantly higher bacterial burden in the liver compared with control mice (Figure 6E), with a similar trend of increased colonization in the spleen (Figure 6F). EC958ΔrfaH failed to colonize both treated and untreated mice, confirming its highly attenuated phenotype (limit of detection = 200 CFU) (Figure 6D–F). WT EC958 infection elicited a significantly greater neutrophil response compared with ΔrfaH in both control and TAA-treated mice, but this was not affected by the presence of liver fibrosis (Figure 6G). Blood lymphocytes were increased in EC958ΔrfaH- compared with WT EC958-infected mice, whereas there was no difference in blood monocytes between groups (Figure 6H and I). Progressive changes in macrophage function are implicated in the breakdown in liver antimicrobial function in severe liver disease, characterized by the loss of resident macrophages and dysregulated pro-and anti-inflammatory cytokine production. Although we did not observe any significant changes in the expression of the resident macrophage marker Timd4 in TAA-treated mice, Trem2, which is expressed on monocyte-derived macrophages recruited in response to injury, was robustly induced (Figure 6J and K), messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of the inflammatory cytokines Il6, Tnf and Il1b, Il12b, Ifng or the cytokine anti-inflammatory Il10 did not differ between naïve and control and TAA-treated mice (Figure 6L–Q). Despite the increased liver bacterial burden, TAA-treated mice infected with WT EC958 exhibited increased expression of Il12p40 compared with control mice (Figure 6P). Although Il12 is critical for Ifng induction and effective antimicrobial responses, there was no difference in expression of Ifng or the other cytokine mRNAs in control and TAA-treated mice (Figure 6L–O). Commensurate with the lack of bacterial colonization in the livers of EC958ΔrfaH-infected mice, we did not observe elevated cytokine mRNA expression (Figure 6L–O). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the bactericidal capacity of AF against ExPEC and Kp, the most common causes of SBP globally.8 We show that cell-free AF has bactericidal activity for nonpathogenic E coli K-12, associated with classical and alternative complement activation. Using a TraDIS genetic screen incorporating saturated transposon mutagenesis combined with genome-scale targeted sequencing, we identified genes in ExPEC and Kp that contribute to resistance to cell-free AF, the majority of which are associated with the production of cell-surface polysaccharides. In particular, LPS O-antigen and lipid A-core biosynthesis were critical for bacterial survival in cell-free AF, at least in part by preventing complement deposition and activation, and we further identified a contribution of capsule to this phenotype. AF opsonization also increased phagocytosis of K-12 E coli bioparticles by ascites macrophages, underscoring the importance of LPS phenotype for bacterial evasion of innate immune killing mechanisms. VSIG4Hi ascites macrophages had greater phagocytic capacity than VSIG4Low ascites macrophages for virulent and attenuated ExPEC strains, but both AF macrophage populations exhibited bactericidal activity. Finally, we demonstrated that ExPEC infection burden is higher in the liver of mice with toxin-induced cirrhosis compared with control mice, and that ExPEC infection is attenuated following mutation of the rfaH gene.

The high risk of infection in patients with cirrhosis, especially SBP, is thought to be a combination of increased probability that intestinal microbes translocate into the vasculature and a decreased ability to clear them from portal or systemic blood. Approaches to modulate cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction frequently focus on cellular immune mechanisms, whereas humoral immunity and modifiable bacterial factors have been little-studied. Changes in the gut microbiota, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses during the development and progression of cirrhosis, presage and likely incite many decompensation events.5,55 Greatly reduced microbial diversity, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and microbiota profiles dominated by Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, Enterococcaceae, and Streptococcaceae have been consistently reported, with relative decreases in potentially beneficial taxa (reviewed in55). This profile, especially over-representation of Proteobacteria, was reflected in the AF microbiome.32,56,57 Although the presence of gut dysbiosis in cirrhosis is clear and clearly associated with poor clinical outcomes, including infections, hepatic encephalopathy, and other complications, the role of bacterial virulence factors (VFs) has been little studied. A recent stool metagenomics-based study reported increased abundance of pathobionts and genes encoding VFs, including siderophores and adhesins, in patients with decompensated cirrhosis compared with controls, along with components of the LPS locus, and highlighted the need to distinguish the presence of pathobionts from those with virulence potential that could be markers of future negative outcomes.58 Lee et al also reported increased accumulation of pathobionts in cirrhosis, and identified VFs belonging to pathways including LPS biosynthesis in decompensated patients.10 The emergence of pathobionts expressing specific VFs in alcohol-related liver disease has also been documented, and specific targeting of pathogens using bacteriophages may be a viable therapeutic approach.59, 60, 61, 62 Several other microbiome-targeting strategies have been investigated for prevention or treatment of cirrhosis decompensation, including antibiotics such as rifaximin, fecal microbiota transplantation, manipulation of bile acid pathways, and enterosorbents that act as a sink for inflammatory microbial products.55 However, a recent randomized placebo-controlled trial reported that the incidence of infection, including SBP, was similar (∼25%) in patients treated with simvastatin alone or simvastatin and rifaximin, a poorly absorbable broad-spectrum antibiotic that has been shown to alter the gut microbiome and reduce the risk of decompensation (specifically hepatic encephalopathy).63

We show that cell-free AF from patients with cirrhosis effectively killed the nonpathogenic E coli strain MG1655, which was associated with deposition of classical and alternative complement factors and assembly of the membrane attack complex. Heat inactivation of AF abrogated complement deposition and permitted bacterial growth, supporting a major role for complement in direct AF killing. AF was completely ineffective against E coli EC958 and Kp Kp52.145, which avoided complement deposition. Our genetic screen identified genes involved in the production of LPS O-antigen and lipid A-core to be critical for bacterial survival in AF. Overall, there was significant overlap between AF- and serum-resistance genes for both EC958 and Kp52.145.36,42 Some of these genes in EC958 (waaU, waaJ, waaO, and waaB) and Kp52.145 (wabM, wabK, and walW) are involved in LPS outer core synthesis, which has also been shown to facilitate anchoring of capsule to the cell in Kp.64 Additionally, some of the cell-free AF resistance genes we identified for Kp overlap with those involved in capsule production.65 The capsule gene KpsM was recently identified as a virulence gene in a gut E coli pathobiont from patients with alcohol-associated hepatitis, which enabled evasion of phagocytosis in the liver, and targeting this gene attenuated alcohol-induced liver disease in mice.60 We identified rfaH as a key transcription factor contributing to AF resistance in both EC958 and Kp52.145, and mutation of this gene in EC958 conferred 100% susceptibility to killing. As RfaH positively regulates both capsule and LPS biosynthesis, we generated a capsule mutant and showed it plays a moderate role in resistance to cell-free AF killing, albeit not significant in the TraDIS analysis. Despite this, a double O-antigen (waaL)-capsule (kpsD) mutant had a similar phenotype to the rfaH mutant and was completely sensitive to killing in cell-free AF. We hypothesize the function of capsule and O-antigen is intricately associated with resistance to soluble and cellular host innate immune factors in AF and contributes to the observed donor variability, along with host factors such as absolute complement levels in AF, which in turn are influenced by liver synthetic capacity, and by AF volume in individual patients. Targeted inhibition of VFs such as RfaH is potentially a viable therapeutic approach to prevent infections.60,61

In addition to cell-free bacterial killing, AF components, including complement, influence phagocytosis and cell-mediated killing. Indeed, a defining feature of resident peritoneal macrophages is expression of Complement Receptor Immunoglobulin (VSIG4).29,30 VSIG4 functions as a receptor for C3-opsonized bacteria and directly enhances uptake of Gram-positive bacteria via binding to lipotechoic acid. VSIG4-deficient mice exhibited impaired clearance of the pathobiont Enterococcus faecalis that translocated from the gut to the liver, and administration of the soluble extracellular domain of VSIG4 reduced hepatic bacterial burden.66 Although VSIG4 itself is functionally important, VSIG4Hi macrophages are enriched for many other genes that likely contribute to infection control (eg, scavenger receptors, cytokine production30). Here, we demonstrated that incubation in autologous AF enhanced macrophage phagocytosis of E coli K12 bioparticles, but reduced VSIG4 surface expression. The rapid (15-minute) release of soluble VSIG4 from ascites macrophages in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns was recently reported.31 Although administration of the extracellular domain of VSIG4 was protective in mice,66 its release from peritoneal macrophages was associated with short-term mortality in patients with SBP.31 Because loss of surface VSIG4 expression would increase the apparent phagocytic capacity of phenotypically VSIG4Low macrophages, we confirmed that preopsonization of E coli BP in AF enhanced phagocytosis in both ascites macrophage populations, although phagocytosis remained higher in VSIG4Hi macrophages, regardless of opsonization. Phagocytosis of EC958 was also significantly higher in VSIG4Hi compared with VSIG4Low macrophages, but both macrophage populations from most donors effectively killed EC958 and MG1655. We tested 2 candidate regulators of resident peritoneal macrophage differentiation and function for their ability to enhance phagocytosis. Retinoic acid is an important mediator of murine peritoneal macrophage differentiation and was also shown to enhance E coli phagocytosis in mice,49,50,67 although its role in human peritoneal macrophages remains to be clarified.29 CSF1R-deficient mice and rats completely lack resident peritoneal macrophages, and administration of CSF1 augments their proliferation in mice.52,53,68 In the current study, 48-hour stimulation with all trans retinoic acid or CSF1 was not sufficient to alter ascites macrophage phagocytic capacity. Taken together, the data demonstrate that soluble factors in AF can eliminate nonpathogenic bacteria and enhance macrophage uptake, but cell-mediated killing mechanisms are required for pathogenic bacteria.

In vivo clearance of bacterial infections reflects the combined contributions of cell and noncell-mediated antimicrobial mechanisms. Previous studies have shown that the liver is an essential barrier for eliminating bacteria, including commensal bacteria, that have escaped from the gut. Increasing severity of liver disease leads to progressive changes and eventually a breakdown in liver antimicrobial function, especially macrophage function, characterized by loss of VSIG4 and gain of the immunosuppressive programmed death ligand 1, and dysregulated pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine production.69, 70, 71, 72 Liver macrophages in mice with carbon tetrachloride–, alcohol-, 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine diet–, or bile duct ligation–induced fibrosis had a reduced capacity to capture intravenously delivered Staphylococcus aureus, E coli K12, or E faecalis.66,69,71 We showed that mice with TAA-induced cirrhosis had a decreased capacity to clear an intraperitoneal ExPEC EC958 infection, resulting in higher bacterial burden in the liver, despite the absence of acute TAA toxicity, as the mice had been returned to normal drinking water 2 weeks prior to infection (modeling, for example, an abstinent patient with alcoholic cirrhosis). A previous report implicated acutely reduced Il12p40 and increased anti-inflammatory Il10 production by liver macrophages in increased susceptibility to a nonpathogenic E coli infection in bile duct–ligated mice.72 On the other hand, Ifng is a strong inducer of Pdl1 on macrophages, which has been associated with impaired bacterial clearance in humans and rodents with cirrhosis.71 Despite the increased bacterial burden in the liver of TAA-treated mice, we detected increased expression of Il12b; however. there was no difference in the expression of Ifng or a range of other inflammatory cytokines. In contrast to WT EC958, EC958ΔrfaH was completely cleared within 6 hours p.i. Impaired liver clearance of an intravenous challenge of a serum-sensitive nonpathogenic E coli K-12 strain in mice with fibrosis has previously been reported; however, these studies administered 107 bacteria intravenously; which is 20× higher than the intraperitoneal dose used here.69,71 The lack of acute TAA toxicity, enabling partial recovery of macrophage function, may also have contributed to the complete clearance of attenuated EC958 in our study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we discovered that ExPEC and Kp isolates representing the 2 most common infectious causes of SBP are resistant to AF microbicidal activity and identified genes contributing to AF resistance. Ascites macrophages, especially VSIG4Hi cells, engulfed and killed both nonpathogenic E coli MG1655 and ExPEC EC958. Together, these studies suggest that, although pathobionts may cause infections in some patients with decompensated cirrhosis,10 many patients may be able to effectively defend against translocating intestinal commensal bacteria. Targeting LPS O-antigen biosynthesis, for example by inhibition of RfaH, or host-directed therapies to improve peritoneal and/or liver macrophage function, for example, by VSIG4 administration, may provide novel avenues to ameliorate the burden of infections in patients with cirrhosis.

Materials and Methods

Ethics

AF was obtained from patients undergoing large volume paracentesis at the Princess Alexandra Hospital, Brisbane, with informed, written consent ethics approval obtained from the Metro South Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/99/QPAH/076 [1999/090]). Bacterial strains were obtained from the Princess Alexandra Hospital microbiology laboratory (HREC/2018/QMS/43677). All samples and strains were deidentified. Animal experiments were approved by a University of Queensland animal ethics committee (AEC2023/259).

Patients and Clinical Samples

AF was centrifuged at 13,000 g for 5 minutes and the ‘cell-free’ supernatant was used for bacterial killing assays. An aliquot of the centrifuged AF was heat-inactivated by incubating at 56 °C for 30 minutes to use as the control. Cells were harvested from AF by centrifugation at 400 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. Red blood cell lysis was performed by resuspending the pellet in sterile red blood cell Lysis Buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.3) for 5 minutes at room temperature on a roller. The remaining cells were washed twice in sterile cell culture media (phenol red- and antibiotic-free, RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% fetal calf serum [FCS]) and used fresh or cryopreserved in a medium containing 90% FCS and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide.

Bacterial Strains

E.coli strains MG165537 and EC958,73 K pneumoniae strain Kp52.145,74 and EC958 mutants36 have been described previously. The liver abscess–derived strain MS9265 and SBP E coli and E cloacae strains were obtained from our hospital microbiology laboratory. Kp52.145 mutants were generated using the λ-Red recombinase gene replacement system as previously described.75,76 Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Bacteria were grown routinely at 37 °C in solid or liquid LB medium unless indicated otherwise. Where appropriate, media was supplemented with 30 μg/mL of chloramphenicol or 20 μg/mL of gentamicin.

Assessment of Ascites Fluid Bactericidal Activity

For assessment of bactericidal activity, AF from patients who were not on antibiotic therapy on or within 5 days prior to day of paracentesis was used. A total of 90 to 180 μL of cell-free AF, HI AF, or PBS control was inoculated with 1 × 108 CFU bacteria in 1/10 volume and incubated at 37 °C for 90 minutes. Five microliters of AF was 10-fold serially diluted in PBS (6 dilutions), then 5 μL of each dilution was plated on LB agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Bacteria were enumerated by calculating CFU from the serial dilutions.

Transposon Mutant Library Screen and Analysis

The EC958 TraDIS library has been described previously.36 This library comprised approximately 1 million mutants generated using an in-house miniTn5 transposon carrying a chloramphenicol resistance gene cassette, with 502,068 independent insertion sites at a density of 1 insertion every ∼10 bp. The Kp52.145 TraDIS library was made using the same protocol.36 This library comprised approximately 1 million mutants generated using an in-house miniTn5 transposon as above, yielding 441,013 independent insertion sites at a density of 1 insertion every ∼13 bp. The transposon mutant library screening assay was performed as previously described.36 Briefly, approximately 2 × 108 viable mutants from the EC958 and Kp52.145 transposon mutant libraries were incubated in cell-free AF or cell-free HI-AF at 37 °C for 1.5 hours. The surviving mutants at the end of the 1.5-hour incubation period were enriched by growing in LB broth for 4 hours. Bacterial cells were pelleted, and genomic DNA was extracted using the Wizard genomic DNA purification kit (Promega). The screening assays were performed in duplicate. Illumina libraries were prepared using the Nextera DNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s instructions with modifications for Tn5-insertion site sequencing, and sequenced with 100 bp single-end reads on the Illumina MiSeq platform at the Australian Centre for Ecogenomics (The University of Queensland) as previously described.36 TraDIS data was analyzed to identify genes required for growth in cell-free AF vs cell-free HI AF as control using an established protocol.36 Briefly, raw sequencing reads were filtered and trimmed to keep 30-bp immediately after the 12-bp Tn5-specific tag (5′-TATAAGAGACAG-3′) at their 5′-ends using FASTX-Toolkit version 0.0.13 (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/index.html). These reads were aligned to the reference genome (EC958 HG941718 and Kp52.145 FO834906) using bowtie with “-M 1 –best” parameters (version 1.0.0). Subsequent analysis steps were carried out as previously described36 to calculate the number of reads (raw read counts) and the number of different insertion sites for every gene. Subsequent analysis steps were carried out in R (version 3.0.3) using the edgeR package (version 3.4.2) to estimate the logFC of read counts and FDR for each gene between test and control samples. To identify genes required for survival in cell-free AF, we used stringent criteria of logFC ≤−1 and FDR ≤0.001. The TraDIS reads have been deposited in the SRA under accession number (PRJNA1369009).

Lipopolysaccharide Analysis

Bacteria were grown overnight (∼16 hours), pelleted by centrifugation, and washed in PBS. Washed cell pellets were standardized to optical density at 600 nm = 1.5, and LPS was extracted using methods described previously.77 LPS extracts were resolved for 1 hour at 150 V by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (12% gel). LPS was visualized using an oxidative silver stain as derived previously.78 Gels were imaged using a white backlight, and the “Enhance Contrast” function of Fiji was applied in postprocessing.79

Complement and IgG Deposition

Forty-five microliters of AF or HI AF was inoculated with 1 × 108 E coli CFU and incubated for 20 minutes at 37 °C. Bacteria were pelleted by centrifugation at 6000 g for 5 minutes, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 50 μL PBS. Anti-human IgG-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC; 056M4822v, Fc specific, Sigma, 1:50), anti-C3c-FITC (Abcam ab4212, 1:100), anti-C1q-FITC (Abcam ab4223, 1:50), or anti-C5b8-C9 (Abcam ab55811, 1:100) were added and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 minutes. C5b-9 samples were pelleted, washed, and incubated with a goat anti-mouse AF647 secondary antibody (Life Technologies) for a further 20 minutes at room temperature. Following staining, all samples were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 5 minutes at room temperature and stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI (Sigma). Samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX Beckman Coulter flow cytometer. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo 10.0 (Treestar).

E coli Bioparticle Assay

A total of 5 × 105 ascites cells were resuspended in autologous cell-free AF, HI AF, or cell culture medium (phenol red- and antibiotic-free, RPMI 1640 with 10% FCS, ThermoFisher Scientific). Cells were incubated 1.5 × 106 E coli bioparticles (pHrodo, Molecular Probes) at 37 °C for 1 hour or left unstimulated. Cells were pelleted then resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS/2% FCS) and stained with anti-VSIG4-PE (SantaCruz Biotechnology) and anti-CD14-BV421 (Biolegend) for 1 hour on ice, washed twice, and resuspended in FACS buffer with viability dye 7AAD to distinguish. Samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) flow cytometer with single color controls for compensation. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo 10.0 (Treestar).

Macrophage Infection Assay

Ascites macrophages or macrophage subpopulations were isolated by flow cytometry sorting or CD14 MACs beads and an AutoMACs system (Milteny, according to the manufacturer’s instructions). For flow cytometry analysis and sorting, ascites cells were stained with CD14-BV421(BioLegend) and VSIG4-PE (SantaCruz Biotechnology) for differentiation of VSIG4Hi and VSIG4Low populations and 7AAD to discriminate live/dead cells. Unstained and single-color controls were performed for compensation to aid in gating of the 2 cell populations. Sorting was performed using a BD FACSAria Fusion by the TRI Flow Cytometry Core Facility, achieving >90% purity.

Sorted ascites macrophages were plated in duplicate in a 96-well tissue culture plate at a density of 5 × 104 or 1 × 105 cells/well in cell culture medium and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 hours, prior to inoculation with E coli MG1655 or EC958 from an overnight culture at an MOI of 5. The exact MOI was calculated by plating 5 μL of 10-fold serial dilutions of the inoculum on LB agar overnight at 37 °C and counting bacterial colonies. Plates were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes after inoculating bacteria and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Thirty minutes postinoculation, cells in wells for uptake determination were washed twice with cell culture medium containing high-dose gentamicin (200 μg/mL) to remove remaining extracellular bacteria and prevent further bacterial uptake by cells, and twice with antibiotic-free cell culture media to remove remaining antibiotic solution in the medium before lysing the cells in 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS solution for 5 minutes. Five microliters of 10-fold serially diluted lysate (6 dilutions) was plated on LB agar in duplicate and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Wells containing cells plated to determine killing of intracellular bacteria at later time points (2 or 4 hours p.i.) were washed twice with high-dose gentamicin medium (200 μg/mL) after the 30 minute timepoint and incubated for 1 hour in high-dose gentamicin medium, followed by incubation in low-dose gentamicin (20 μg/mL) medium before lysis to determine intracellular bacterial load, as previously described, at the appropriate timepoint. Intracellular bacteria were enumerated by calculating the CFU from the serial dilutions, as above. Bacterial uptake was defined as the percentage of bacterial CFU recovered 30 minutes post inoculation relative to inoculum. Bacterial survival was assessed by calculating the percentage of surviving bacteria relative to the uptake.

Animal Experiments

Male C57Bl/6J mice were obtained from Ozgene and housed in a specific pathogen free facility at the Translational Research Institute with 12-hour light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. To induce liver fibrosis, mice were administrated 0.3 g/l TAA in drinking water for 12 weeks and returned to normal drinking water for 2 weeks prior to infection. Mice were infected with 0.3 × 106 CFU/mouse of WT EC958 or EC958ΔrfaH by intraperitoneal injection and euthanized 6 hours post infection via CO2 inhalation. Blood, peritoneal lavage, and tissues were aseptically collected for analysis. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture into MiniCollect EDTA Anti-coagulation tubes (450,532), and cell counts were obtained using an automated hematology analyzer (Mindray BC5000). For determination of bacterial burden, a weighed section of spleen and liver were homogenized in PBS in tubes containing 2 × 1.4 mm Zirconium oxide beads (Precellys) using a FastPrep-24 5G Instrument. Peritoneal lavage was collected by injecting 10 mL PBS into the peritoneal cavity, the abdomen was briefly massaged, and the lavage was collected using an 18G needle. The peritoneal lavage was centrifuged at 400 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. Homogenates and peritoneal lavage were kept on ice, serial diluted, and plated on LB agar incubated at 37 °C. After 24 hours, CFUs were counted. Livers were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours and paraffin-embedded. The 4-μM sections (Leica RM2245 microtome) were stained with Picrosirius red. Whole-slide imaging was done using VS120 Olympus slide scanner, and picrosirius red staining area was quantified using Visiopharm. Total RNA was purified from liver sections collected in TRIzol (Thermofisher) and complementary DNA synthesized using a SensiFAST kit (Meridian Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed using SYBR Green (Thermofisher) and default cycling conditions on a VIA 7 thermocycler. Gene expression was quantified relative to TATA Binding Protein using the 2−ΔCt method. Samples below the limit of detection were assigned values 2 Ct below the lowest detectable Ct to enable visualization. The following primers were used: Il6:_F: TCAGGAAATTTGCCTATTGAAA, R: GGAAATTGGGGTAGGAAGGA; Ifng: F: TCATGAGTATTGCCAAGTTTGAGGT, R: CTGGATTCCGGCAACAGC; Tnf: F: GGTCCCCAAAGGGATGAGAAG, R: TCGAATTTTGAGAAGATGATCTGAGTG; Il10: F: AACTGCACCCACTTCCCAGTC, R: TCCAGCTGGTCCTTTGTTTGAA; Timd4: F: AACTGTTTGCAGAGACACAAGAGG, R: GAAGATCCCGTCTTCATCATCC; Trem2: F: ACCAACTTCAGATCCTCACTGGA, R: TGCAGGCTGGATTGACTCCT; Il12b: F: CTGTATGCCTCCCTGAGGG R: AGTGCTTACCTTGCTCTGGG.
