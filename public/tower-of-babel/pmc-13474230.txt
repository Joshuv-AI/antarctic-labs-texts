Narrative review: Extracorporeal shock wave therapy for pain management in musculoskeletal rehabilitation: Mechanisms, clinical applications, and safety
Authors: Ting Gao, Bingquan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis narrative review synthesizes current evidence on extracorporeal shock wave therapy for pain management in musculoskeletal rehabilitation, emphasizing its analgesic mechanisms, clinical applications, and safety. As a noninvasive physical therapy, extracorporeal shock wave therapy is increasingly used for pain relief in conditions such as chronic low back pain, plantar fasciopathy, knee osteoarthritis, rotator cuff injury, and myofascial pain syndrome. Its analgesic effects arise through multiple pathways, including peripheral neuroregulation characterized by degeneration and subsequent regeneration of sensory nerve fibers, reduction of pro-inflammatory mediators, improved local blood flow, and, according to emerging functional magnetic resonance imaging data, modulation of central pain networks. Evidence from randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses supports the efficacy of extracorporeal shock wave therapy in reducing pain intensity and improving function across these disorders. The safety profile of extracorporeal shock wave therapy is favorable, with most adverse events being mild and transient local reactions. Nonetheless, standardized treatment parameters are lacking, and data on vulnerable populations remain sparse. This review aims to serve as a scientific reference for extracorporeal shock wave therapy in musculoskeletal pain management and to identify priorities for future research.Keywords: Extracorporeal shock wave therapy, musculoskeletal rehabilitation, pain management, rotator cuff injury, analgesic mechanisms, functional magnetic resonance imaging, safety

Introduction

Pain is the most common and disabling symptom of musculoskeletal disorders, the primary reason patients seek care, and the principal target of rehabilitation. Despite advances in pharmacologic and surgical treatments, many patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain continue to experience inadequate relief or significant adverse effects, highlighting the need for safe, effective, noninvasive alternatives. 1 Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) has emerged as a promising noninvasive physical modality that helps address this clinical need, and a growing body of evidence supports its analgesic efficacy across a range of musculoskeletal conditions.

ESWT applies high-intensity mechanical shock waves to targeted tissues, initiating biological responses that produce pain relief and functional improvement. The analgesic effects of ESWT are multifactorial and occur at multiple levels of the nervous system. At the peripheral level, ESWT causes selective degeneration of unmyelinated sensory nerve fibers, thereby reducing nociceptive input. 2 Concurrently, it promotes angiogenesis through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) pathways, improving local blood circulation and reducing ischemia-related pain. 3 ESWT also modulates the local inflammatory milieu by reducing pro-inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β).4,5 Notably, emerging evidence from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies indicates that ESWT produces centrally mediated analgesia by modulating activity within pain-related brain networks, including the somatosensory cortex, thalamus, and anterior cingulate cortex.6,7 This multilevel mechanism—from peripheral nociceptor modulation to central pain network regulation—distinguishes ESWT from many conventional analgesic interventions.

ESWT has been evaluated for pain management across a variety of musculoskeletal disorders. In rotator cuff injury, ESWT significantly reduces pain scores and improves shoulder function. 8 For knee osteoarthritis, systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that ESWT effectively relieves pain and improves joint function.9,10 Similarly, ESWT has demonstrated significant analgesic effects in chronic low back pain, 11 plantar fasciopathy, 12 and myofascial pain syndrome.13,14 In neuropathic pain, ESWT has been effective for carpal tunnel syndrome15,16 and postherpetic neuralgia, 17 with electrophysiological studies demonstrating improved nerve conduction velocity.15,18 ESWT has also shown promise in managing pain associated with muscle injuries and in reducing spasticity following upper motor neuron injury.19,20

Despite these encouraging findings, several challenges remain. Treatment parameters—including energy flux density, pulse number, frequency, and the number of treatment sessions—vary considerably across studies, and consensus regarding optimal protocols for specific pain conditions has not yet been established.21–23 The safety profile of ESWT is generally favorable, with mild, transient local reactions being the most commonly reported adverse events;24,25 however, evidence regarding safety in vulnerable populations, including children, older adults, and immunocompromised patients, remains limited.26–29 Finally, although preliminary fMRI data support the central analgesic mechanisms of ESWT, these mechanisms require further investigation in larger, well-controlled studies.

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on ESWT for pain management in musculoskeletal rehabilitation, focusing on three areas: (a) peripheral and central analgesic mechanisms, (b) clinical efficacy across major musculoskeletal pain conditions, and (c) the safety profile and unresolved challenges. It draws primarily on studies published during the past decade while also incorporating seminal mechanistic studies from earlier periods. By integrating mechanistic findings with clinical evidence, this review aims to guide clinical decision-making and identify priority directions for future research.

Basic principles and mechanism of action of ESWT

Physical properties of shock waves and their conduction in tissues

A shock wave (SW) is a sound wave that travels through a medium with a very short rise time and high peak pressure, enabling the rapid release of mechanical impact energy. 30 In musculoskeletal tissues, SW conduction produces brief mechanical stress and strain that mechanically stimulate cell membranes, improve microcirculation, and modulate cellular activity, thereby promoting repair and regeneration. 31 In soft tissues, SW propagation depends on the tissue’s physical properties; energy is transmitted as acoustic waves and induces cavitation, which amplifies the mechanical stimulus. 32 Moreover, SW energy in soft tissue attenuates exponentially, so peak pressure and energy density decrease with propagation depth. 33 This attenuation governs the effective depth and safety profile of shock wave therapy and is therefore crucial for clinical application. In bone, propagation reflects its high density and rigidity; studies indicate that SWs can penetrate bone and impose localized mechanical loads that activate bone cells and stimulate remodeling. 34

SW therapy is divided into focused extracorporeal shock wave therapy (f-ESWT) and radial extracorporeal shock wave therapy (r-ESWT). f-ESWT is indicated for deep-tissue conditions, whereas r-ESWT is better suited for superficial soft-tissue pathologies. 35 The two modalities differ in indications and therapeutic effects; therefore, selection should be guided by lesion depth, tissue properties, and treatment goals to optimize efficacy and safety. These physical distinctions explain the clinical use of ESWT for deep vs. superficial lesions and underpin its application in pain management across diverse musculoskeletal conditions, as detailed below.

Biological effects at the cellular and molecular levels

ESWT activates multiple signaling pathways through mechanical energy, thereby promoting angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and tissue repair while modulating inflammation and reducing apoptosis. Studies have shown that ESWT markedly increases the expression of VEGF and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), thereby promoting neovascularization and improving blood supply to injured tissues. 3 ESWT also stimulates fibroblast proliferation and metabolism and induces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines that provide a cellular basis for repair while concurrently enhancing collagen synthesis to strengthen tissue structure. 36 Moreover, ESWT modulates local inflammatory responses, reduces levels of inflammatory mediators, and activates antiapoptotic signaling pathways to improve cell survival and facilitate functional recovery. 4 These mechanisms act synergistically to promote the repair and regeneration of musculoskeletal tissues, providing a robust biological rationale for the broad clinical application of ESWT.

Neuroregulation and analgesia mechanisms

In recent years, the analgesic mechanisms of ESWT in musculoskeletal rehabilitation—particularly its modulatory effects on the nervous system—have become a research focus. As a nonpharmacologic physical therapy, ESWT improves local blood flow and promotes muscle relaxation, and these changes may modulate neural excitability. Studies indicate that ESWT exerts excitatory and reparative effects on peripheral nerves, significantly increases sensory nerve conduction velocity, reduces distal nerve latency, and outperforms local corticosteroid injection.15,18 ESWT has also been shown to reduce nerve swelling and relieve pain in compressive neuropathies such as carpal tunnel syndrome.16,37 From a molecular perspective, ESWT can damage sensory unmyelinated nerve fibers, thereby reducing pain transmission. It can also promote nerve regeneration and improve the local microenvironment. ESWT has shown promising clinical potential in patients with chronic myofascial pain syndrome (MPS) and patients with postburn nerve injury.2,38,39 However, direct evidence in humans remains limited, and further neuroimaging studies are needed. Although direct evidence for the central effects of ESWT is currently sparse, it is plausible that ESWT alters peripheral afferent signaling and thereby modulates central pain networks, improving pain perception and related symptoms. For example, low-dose ESWT significantly reduces pain and pruritus in patients with postherpetic neuralgia. 17

Moreover, ESWT outperforms other physical therapies in treating chronic shoulder and neck pain, further supporting its potential for neuromodulation.40,41 ESWT produces effective analgesia through multilevel neuromodulatory mechanisms. Future studies should investigate the effects of ESWT on the central nervous system in greater depth and integrate molecular biology and neuroimaging approaches to elucidate its analgesic mechanisms more comprehensively, thereby providing a stronger theoretical basis for its clinical application.

Study design

This narrative review synthesizes the mechanisms, clinical applications, and safety of ESWT for pain management in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. We performed a comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for English-language articles published through 2025. The search combined the following terms: “extracorporeal shock wave therapy,” “ESWT,” “musculoskeletal,” “rehabilitation,” “pain management,” “rotator cuff injury,” “muscle injury,” and “safety.”

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (a) randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, or narrative reviews addressing ESWT for musculoskeletal conditions; (b) reports of mechanisms of action, clinical outcomes (e.g. pain intensity and functional recovery), or safety data; and (c) preference was given to publications from the past decade to reflect contemporary evidence, although seminal mechanistic studies published earlier were included when directly relevant. Studies were excluded if they were: (a) case reports, editorials, letters, or conference abstracts; (b) non-English publications; (c) not directly related to musculoskeletal rehabilitation; or (d) lacking sufficient outcome data. Duplicate records were removed. Research progress of ESWT in muscle injury and spasm

Pain management in muscle injuries

ESWT has shown substantial promise for treating pain associated with acute and chronic muscle injuries. Among athletes, acute muscle trauma is common, particularly Type 3b hamstring injuries, which are characterized by prolonged recovery and a high risk of reinjury. 53 Conventional conservative rehabilitation can be partially effective but remains limited by prolonged recovery times and incomplete pain resolution. Research indicates that radial ESWT (r-ESWT) offers notable therapeutic advantages by markedly shortening athletes’ recovery periods and accelerating both pain relief and functional restoration. 53

Animal studies have clarified both the mechanisms underlying the analgesic effects of r-ESWT and the influence of treatment frequency on therapeutic outcomes. The 10 Hz group showed the greatest reductions in inflammatory mediators, such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), increased expression of the muscle regeneration marker myogenic differentiation antigen (MyoD), and the most favorable tissue morphology. These findings indicate that r-ESWT promotes analgesia and tissue repair primarily by modulating inflammatory responses and promoting myogenesis. 5 Clinically, r-ESWT incorporated into multimodal regimens has also accelerated recovery in professional football players. In a retrospective study, a multimodal strategy centered on r-ESWT shortened recovery time after muscle injury by more than 50% compared with conventional treatments reported in the literature and produced marked pain relief without adverse reactions. 43

ESWT also shows promise for the management of chronic muscle pain. MPS is a common chronic skeletal muscle pain disorder characterized by myofascial trigger points as its primary pathological feature. Currently, no single treatment is preferred for patients with MPS. Studies have reported that combining loxoprofen sodium ointment with ESWT provides therapeutic benefits for patients with MPS. The combined treatment group showed significantly greater pain relief and improved muscle function than either the medication-only group or the physical-therapy-only group. Tolerance of the combined treatment was good, and no obvious adverse events were observed. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness and safety of ESWT for alleviating myofascial pain and improving muscle function. 13 In summary, ESWT can reduce muscle pain through multiple mechanisms, improve muscle function, and lower the risk of reinjury.

Pain management in muscle spasms and spasticity

ESWT has also demonstrated significant pain relief in muscle spasms and spasticity. In patients with lumbar degenerative disease, ESWT markedly reduced the frequency and duration of lower-limb muscle cramps, improved pain scores and motor function, and maintained these benefits for up to 3 months after treatment, indicating sustained effects on pain and dysfunction associated with muscle cramps. Similarly, in poststroke patients with calf muscle spasticity, combining ESWT with isokinetic strength training significantly reduced muscle tone scores and electromyography (EMG) activity, demonstrating a synergistic analgesic effect on spasticity and muscle function. This combined approach was also shown to be safe. 19

Narrative reviews and meta-analyses evaluating the efficacy and safety of ESWT for spasticity following upper motor neuron injury have sought to provide evidence-based guidance for clinical practice. Preliminary data indicate that ESWT can reduce spasticity-related pain and improve motor function while maintaining a favorable safety profile. 20 ESWT is therefore anticipated to emerge as a noninvasive treatment option for spasticity following upper motor neuron injury.19,20

ESWT provides effective analgesia for both muscle cramps and spasticity while yielding concomitant functional improvements. Its indications encompass neurogenic spasticity and region-specific muscle cramps, and combining ESWT with physical therapy or rehabilitation training may further enhance therapeutic outcomes. Nevertheless, additional high-quality randomized controlled trials and mechanistic studies are required to define optimal treatment parameters and establish long-term efficacy before ESWT can be widely adopted as a primary modality for managing pain associated with muscle cramps and spasticity.

Pain management in fracture rehabilitation

ESWT has also been used in fracture rehabilitation, where pain control is central to recovery. ESWT delivers mechanical energy to bone and has been shown to accelerate fracture repair.44,45 Mechanistically, ESWT improves bone microcirculation and increases local capillary blood flow, thereby alleviating ischemia-related pain associated with fractures and promoting tissue healing. At the molecular level, ESWT stimulates the release of growth factors such as VEGF, which supports angiogenesis and pain relief. 3

Although ESWT in fracture management has primarily focused on bone healing, its concurrent analgesic effects offer a clinically meaningful advantage for patients with fractures, especially those with delayed recovery. The analgesia provided by ESWT may enable earlier mobilization and greater participation in physical therapy, both of which are crucial for achieving optimal functional outcomes after fracture. Additional studies should evaluate pain outcomes as primary endpoints in ESWT trials focused on fracture rehabilitation.

Pain management: Clinical applications, neural mechanisms, and parameter optimization

Pain management is the most extensively studied and clinically important application of ESWT in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. The following subsections synthesize current evidence on ESWT for chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions, summarize the central and peripheral neural mechanisms underlying its analgesic effects, and outline key considerations for optimizing treatment parameters.

Application for chronic pain management

The use of ESWT for chronic pain, particularly chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS), has garnered increasing interest. Four weeks of ESWT significantly improved Visual Analog Scale scores in patients with CPPS, with benefits persisting for 12 weeks. 46 Meta-analyses have also shown that a higher total number of pulses produced better treatment outcomes, 47 whereas low-energy regimens improved pain and quality-of-life measures.48,54

The analgesic effects of ESWT arise from reductions in inflammatory mediators, enhanced angiogenesis, and improved local blood flow, 55 as well as modulation of nervous system excitability to relieve neuropathic pain. 56 Although large-scale randomized controlled trials are still lacking for idiopathic conditions such as vulvodynia and primary dysmenorrhea, the efficacy of ESWT in chronic pelvic pain and other persistent pain syndromes supports its potential as a noninvasive treatment option.

In summary, the medium- and long-term efficacy and reproducible safety of ESWT in CPPS provide a foundation for its broader application in chronic pain. Controlled trials and neuroimaging studies involving primary dysmenorrhea, vulvodynia, and related conditions will help clarify its indications and establish standardized treatment parameters.

Neural mechanisms of pain relief

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a key tool for investigating the effects of ESWT on the central nervous system. 57 ESWT delivers mechanical waves to surrounding tissues, activates local nerve endings, and modulates central neural activity to produce analgesia. 7 Studies indicate that ESWT not only improves peripheral nerve function but also alters activation patterns in brain regions involved in pain perception, thereby influencing the central experience of pain. 7 Recent human fMRI studies have provided preliminary evidence supporting these central analgesic mechanisms. Liu et al. 6 reported ESWT-related changes in activity in the left middle temporal gyrus in patients with primary dysmenorrhea. Taken together with the significant improvements in pain and cervical spine function observed during the study, 58 these results suggest that ESWT may exert central analgesic effects by modulating neural activity within core pain-related networks, such as the somatosensory cortex, thalamus, and anterior cingulate cortex, thereby influencing pain perception, emotional processing, and endogenous descending pain modulation.

In patients with carpal tunnel syndrome, ESWT not only improves symptoms and reduces pain scores but also enhances median nerve conduction velocity, shortens nerve latency, and alleviates nerve compression-related pain.59,60 MRI studies further indicate that ESWT may modulate functional connectivity among pain-related brain regions, thereby altering pain perception. 7

Mechanistically, ESWT analgesia involves regulation of neural networks. It induces selective degeneration of sensory nerve endings, thereby reducing pain transmission. This process is followed by nerve fiber regeneration, which modulates both nerve structure and function.2,49 ESWT also reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators and attenuates neuroinflammation through mechanotransduction, thereby contributing to pain relief.1 At the neural network level, ESWT modulates central pain circuits, reducing pain sensitivity and the formation of pain memory in chronic pain states. Additionally, it promotes restoration of normal nociceptive processing in the spinal dorsal horn and brainstem. 17 Its pronounced analgesic and antipruritic effects in chronic neuropathic pain further support its role in modulating central nervous system function.

In summary, fMRI and related technologies indicate that ESWT produces multilevel analgesia by modulating central neural networks and acting on peripheral nerve structures, including receptor degeneration and regeneration, suppression of neuroinflammation, and regulation of central nervous system plasticity. These mechanisms promote pain relief and support the clinical use in musculoskeletal pain management. Future studies combining fMRI with electrophysiological methods will deepen our understanding of the neural mechanisms by which ESWT relieves pain and help refine and optimize its clinical application.

Parameter optimization in pain treatment

In ESWT, optimizing treatment parameters is essential for improving efficacy and safety. SW dose strongly influences outcomes; studies in chronic pelvic pain have shown that regimens with ≥2000 pulses are superior. 47 Treatment frequency and pressure have also received increasing attention, and a systematic evaluation found that optimal effects occur at frequencies below 8 Hz and pressures below 2 bar, which reduce the risk of tissue damage while enhancing biological responses. 50 In plantar fasciitis, appropriate adjustment of parameters directly affects pain relief and patient adherence. 12 Treatment-site selection should be guided by the anatomical location of the lesion. f-ESWT is appropriate for deep muscles, whereas r-ESWT is better suited for superficial tissues. 35 Focused ESWT outperforms other therapies for cervical–shoulder myofascial pain. 14 For musculoskeletal conditions such as calcaneal spurs and carpal tunnel syndrome, precise targeting of the treatment site is a crucial prerequisite for achieving the analgesic effects of SW therapy.1,16

Despite numerous studies, unified standards and individualized protocols remain lacking. Because patients differ in pathological mechanisms and pain sensitivity, clinicians should tailor treatment strategies according to the clinical presentation to maximize efficacy and minimize adverse effects. Optimization of ESWT parameters should therefore consider dose, frequency, pressure, and treatment site. Future research should prioritize individualized treatment strategies to further enhance the effectiveness of ESWT in pain management.

Safety assessment of ESWT

The safety profile of ESWT is crucial for its clinical use in musculoskeletal pain management. This section summarizes the overall safety evidence, details contraindications with emphasis on anticoagulant therapy, and examines safety issues in vulnerable groups, including oncologic patients.

General safety profile

ESWT is widely regarded as a safe, well-tolerated, noninvasive treatment for musculoskeletal pain. The Italian Physical Therapy Society corroborates its safety in adults while advising caution in pediatric and immunocompromised patients. 25 A scoping review of 117 narrative reviews found that ESWT is associated with a higher incidence of minor adverse events but a low frequency of serious complications. 24

Minor adverse reactions to ESWT commonly present as local redness, swelling, and pain, which are typically mild and self-limiting. 24 For example, when treating rotator cuff injuries, plantar fasciitis, or spasticity after upper motor neuron injury, the discomfort resolves spontaneously within a short period, and conservative management is generally sufficient. Patients should be informed about these transient reactions and their management to improve adherence and satisfaction.12,24,61 Severe adverse events are exceedingly rare, and no serious tissue damage or lasting dysfunction has been reported. 25

Contraindications

Safe application of ESWT requires careful consideration of contraindications, which are classified as absolute or relative according to current evidence and consensus guidelines.21,25

Absolute contraindications—conditions in which ESWT must not be applied—include malignant tumors in the treatment area, local infection, pregnancy, and the presence of a pacemaker or other implanted electronic device near the target region.21,25 Current best-practice guidelines further specify active infection (osteomyelitis), malignant tumors (for focused shockwave), and pregnancy as absolute contraindications for all energy treatments. 52

Relative contraindications include conditions in which ESWT may be used only after careful evaluation, such as severe coagulopathy (for high-energy treatments), advanced age with multiple comorbidities, and local soft tissue inflammation.21,24,52 Current anticoagulation therapy, recent corticosteroid injections, and the presence of cardiac pacemakers or other implantable devices also require careful clinical assessment. 52

A key consideration in musculoskeletal pain management is the use of anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications. Historically, anticoagulant therapy was considered an absolute contraindication to ESWT because of the theoretical risk of hematoma formation. Current International Society for Medical Shockwave Treatment (ISMST)-based best-practice guidelines, however, list severe coagulopathy as a relative contraindication to high-energy ESWT, and they consider ongoing anticoagulation an important clinical factor that warrants careful evaluation rather than an absolute contraindication to treatment. 52 Emerging clinical experience indicates that low-energy ESWT can be used cautiously in patients on stable anticoagulation after an individualized risk–benefit assessment. 52 Because high-quality evidence remains limited, anticoagulant use should be regarded as a relative contraindication requiring careful clinical judgment, with treatment decisions made on a case-by-case basis under close supervision.

Before initiating ESWT, comprehensively evaluate the patient's health status, obtain a detailed medical history, and formulate an individualized treatment plan. Closely monitor for adverse reactions during therapy and promptly adjust parameters to ensure safety. 51

Safety and efficacy in vulnerable populations

The safety and efficacy of ESWT in vulnerable populations—including children, older adults, immunocompromised patients, and patients with cancer—demand specific attention. In pediatric populations, several small observational studies and case reports have shown ESWT to be safe and well tolerated for musculoskeletal disorders and cerebral palsy-related spasticity, with no severe adverse events reported, although standardized dosing regimens remain undefined.26–28 In older adults, small clinical trials indicate that low-energy ESWT is acceptably safe for chronic wounds and musculoskeletal conditions despite age-related impairment in tissue healing and a greater burden of comorbidities. 29 For patients with hematologic disorders or coagulation abnormalities, ESWT is generally considered relatively contraindicated; international consensus guidelines explicitly list severe coagulopathy as a relative contraindication to high-energy ESWT. 52 Finally, a recent Delphi consensus emphasizes the need for research on the safety of physical agent modalities in frail populations, including immunocompromised patients. 25

Historically, patients with malignant neoplasms were categorically excluded from ESWT; however, current international guidelines from ISMST have relaxed this restriction. ESWT is contraindicated only when malignant lesions occupy the intended treatment zone, rather than in all individuals with cancer. For cancer survivors and patients receiving chemotherapy who have impaired tissue repair, both low- and high-energy radial or focused shockwave therapy can safely alleviate cancer-associated musculoskeletal conditions—such as plantar fasciitis, calcific tendinopathy, chronic wounds, and chemotherapy-induced polyneuropathy—provided no tumor lesions are present at the intervention site. ISMST recognizes these applications as evidence-based expert indications, supporting the view that standardized pretreatment radiological screening makes ESWT a safe adjunctive rehabilitation modality for supportive cancer care. 51

In summary, although current evidence is limited to case reports and small-sample studies, children, older adults, immunocompromised patients, and patients with cancer remain key populations for which additional safety data are needed to establish reliable dosing guidelines and clear indications. 51

Future development trends and research directions

Growing evidence supports ESWT for pain management in musculoskeletal rehabilitation, but several priority areas require further investigation.

Standardization and individualization of treatment protocols

As a noninvasive modality, ESWT has shown analgesic effects across diverse musculoskeletal conditions, including rotator cuff injury, plantar fasciopathy, knee osteoarthritis, chronic low back pain, and MPS. Current treatment protocols, however, remain heterogeneous, and no unified standards exist for key parameters such as energy flux density, pulse number, frequency, and number of sessions, which impede cross-study comparisons and the development of evidence-based recommendations. For example, although moderate evidence supports ESWT for rotator cuff injury, the optimal energy and frequency remain undetermined because treatment protocols vary widely. 23 A dose-escalation strategy for plantar fasciitis appears to improve analgesic outcomes, indicating that systematic dose adjustment is a promising approach for optimizing treatment. 62

Future large-sample, multicenter randomized controlled trials should standardize treatment parameters for specific pain conditions, enroll patients across different disease stages, and include multidimensional assessments that combine subjective clinical outcomes with objective biomarkers. Long-term follow-up is necessary to determine the durability of analgesic effects and functional improvements. Developing a core set of outcome measures for ESWT trials in pain management would enable more consistent integration and comparison of study results. Standardization, combined with individualized adjustments based on patient characteristics and pain phenotypes, is essential to enhance the effectiveness of ESWT in musculoskeletal pain management.

Integration of novel technologies and multimodal treatment

With advances in musculoskeletal pain management, ESWT is increasingly combined with other modalities to form comprehensive multimodal treatment strategies that aim to enhance analgesic efficacy and improve long-term outcomes. Emerging directions include synergistic applications of ESWT with biomaterials, regenerative medicine techniques, and pharmacotherapy. For example, combining ESWT with platelet-rich plasma injections has shown preliminary promise for enhancing tissue healing and reducing pain, although robust randomized clinical trials directly comparing ESWT alone with ESWT plus other modalities remain lacking. 44

In neuromodulation, combining ESWT with pharmacologic analgesics or nerve blocks may provide additive benefits for patients with refractory neuropathic pain. ESWT can also augment rehabilitation programs by reducing pain intensity and facilitating earlier mobilization. Well-designed comparative-effectiveness studies are needed to identify which combination strategies provide clinically meaningful advantages over ESWT alone. Integrating ESWT with digital health technologies and telerehabilitation platforms could further broaden access to this noninvasive analgesic modality.

Advancing mechanistic understanding

Although substantial progress has clarified the peripheral analgesic actions of ESWT, its central mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Future investigations fMRI, electrophysiology, and molecular biology techniques are required to comprehensively characterize the neural circuits and signaling pathways through which ESWT modulates pain processing. 57 Longitudinal neuroimaging studies could determine whether repeated ESWT sessions induce neuroplastic changes in pain-related brain networks and whether these changes correlate with sustained clinical improvement. Biomarker studies that identify patients with the greatest likelihood of benefiting from ESWT would facilitate more precise pain management.

Strengths and limitations

This narrative review offers several strengths. It presents a focused and comprehensive synthesis of ESWT for pain management in musculoskeletal rehabilitation, thereby addressing a clinically important gap in the literature. The review integrates evidence on multilevel analgesic mechanisms, spanning peripheral nociceptor modulation to regulation within central pain networks, and supports these mechanisms with emerging fMRI data. It also provides an updated, detailed discussion of contraindications, including the evolving status of anticoagulant therapy. Finally, the inclusion of evidence-stratification tables (Tables 1 and 2) enhances transparency and enables readers to appraise the strength of the evidence.

This review has several limitations. First, its narrative design may have missed relevant studies, and the exclusion of non-English publications could have introduced language bias. Second, heterogeneity among the included studies regarding study design, ESWT parameters, and outcome measures prevents firm conclusions and precludes quantitative synthesis. Third, evidence remains limited in certain areas, notably fracture rehabilitation and vulnerable populations; therefore, conclusions in these domains should be regarded as preliminary. Well-designed randomized controlled trials using standardized protocols are required to address these gaps.

Conclusion

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on ESWT for musculoskeletal pain management. Several key conclusions emerge from the analysis.

First, ESWT is an effective and well-tolerated noninvasive therapy for diverse musculoskeletal pain conditions, with the strongest support for chronic low back pain, plantar fasciopathy, knee osteoarthritis, rotator cuff injury, and MPS. Emerging data also indicate benefits in neuropathic pain syndromes, such as carpal tunnel syndrome and postherpetic neuralgia. The analgesic effects appear to reflect multiple mechanisms acting at peripheral and central levels of the nervous system. Peripherally, ESWT induces selective degeneration of sensory nerve fibers, reduces pro-inflammatory mediators, and enhances local blood flow. Centrally, preliminary fMRI studies suggest that ESWT modulates activity within core pain-related networks, including the somatosensory cortex, thalamus, and anterior cingulate cortex.

Second, the safety profile of ESWT is generally favorable, with mild, transient local adverse events being the most common. Severe adverse events are extremely rare. Anticoagulant therapy warrants particular attention and should be considered a relative contraindication requiring an individualized risk–benefit assessment. Evidence regarding safety in vulnerable populations—including children, older adults, immunocompromised patients, and patients with cancer—remains limited and represents a priority for future research.

Third, several challenges must be addressed to advance the field. Standardized treatment parameters—including energy flux density, pulse number, frequency, and number of sessions—are urgently needed to enable reliable comparisons across studies and to establish evidence-based clinical guidelines. Individualized treatment protocols should be based on the pain condition, disease severity, and patient characteristics. Long-term follow-up studies are required to assess the durability of analgesic effects. Future research integrating fMRI, electrophysiology, and molecular techniques will clarify the central analgesic mechanisms of ESWT and may facilitate more precise, personalized treatment strategies.

In conclusion, ESWT is a valuable noninvasive analgesic option in the musculoskeletal rehabilitation armamentarium. Current evidence supports its use for a range of pain conditions, but high-quality, multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols and long-term follow-up are essential to clarify its therapeutic potential and define its role within multimodal pain management strategies.
