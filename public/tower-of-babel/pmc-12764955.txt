Early prediction of very and extreme preterm births using a one-class classification framework on electronic health records in UAE
Authors: Amir Ahmad, Wasif Khan, Md Mozakkir Ansari, Mohammad M Masud, Nadirah Ghenimi, Luai A Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVery Preterm birth (vPTB) and extreme Preterm birth (xPTB) are the major concerns in maternal and child healthcare and are associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Machine learning methods have traditionally been used to predict preterm births (vPTB and xPTB). However, most medical datasets, including preterm births, are imbalanced in class distribution. Although data-balancing techniques can be employed, complications due to the limited sample size of the minority class are frequently encountered, leading to inconsistent results. This study adopted a novel approach by employing one-class classification (OCC) in conjunction with several strategies to predict instances of vPTB and xPTB within an Emirati pregnant population. We used a well-curated dataset acquired during the first trimester of pregnancy. We employed multiple OCC algorithms and their ensembles involving multiple aggregation strategies to predict vPTB and xPTB in both parous and nulliparous populations. Our approach effectively incorporated only majority class information during training. Our detailed experimental setup demonstrated that the proposed methodology achieved promising performance with a maximum AUC-ROC of 0.823 for the parous population without any explicit modeling of the minority class. Our approach demonstrated robustness and efficacy in identifying at-risk pregnancies within the Emirati population. Our results suggest that one-class classification framework which requires only normal data points for training can be used for early prediction of very preterm and extreme preterm births with reasonable accuracy. In this paper, we applied one-class classification framework only on the Emirati population. Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB), which refers to the birth of a baby before completing 37 weeks of gestation, continues to be a serious global issue that contributes to elevated rates of infant mortality worldwide1–3. PTB can be categorized into very preterm birth (vPTB), where the infant is born before 32 weeks of gestation, and extreme preterm birth (xPTB), where the infant is born before 28 weeks of gestation4,5. This phenomenon affects a large number of newborns annually, with approximately 15 million babies being born prematurely, constituting ~ 10% of total global births1. Unfortunately, approximately 1 million premature infants do not survive, highlighting the significance of the problem6.

vPTB and xPTB impose substantial emotional and financial burdens on families6. Effective strategies and interventions are urgently needed to determine the complex factors contributing to these adverse pregnancy outcomes. Detecting vPTB and xPTB at an early stage, especially during the first trimester, and implementing preventive measures can help mitigate the occurrence of such adverse outcomes.

Machine learning (ML)-based algorithms have shown promising performance in predicting vPTB and xPTB delivery at an early stage. Different types of physiological measurements are used to predict vPTB and xPTB, including ultrasound, electrohysterography (EHG)6, tocodynamometry2, internal uterine pressure3, and fetal fibronectin level7. However, these measurements are either subjective or invasive8. In contrast, an analysis of electronic health records (EHR), which provides a comprehensive overview of a patient’s health, may be a reliable and noninvasive approach for predicting PTB (vPTB and xPTB)9–11.

Despite considerable research into predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes such as vPTB and xPTB, data imbalance remains a problem due to the rarity of case-patients. Various data-balancing techniques have been employed to mitigate this challenge, such as ADASYN6,12–14, SMOTE14–21, bootstrapping, applying a steeper penalty on the model, and stratified k-fold cross-validation. However, these traditional techniques can introduce bias and may not yield accurate results20. Furthermore, these traditional oversampling approaches can be ineffective when dealing with mixed data types22,23. Moreover, obtaining enough PTB instances for accurate prediction is challenging and costly24.

Conventionally, individual algorithms are used for vPTB and xPTB prediction, such as logistic regression (LR)25, support vector machines (SVM)26,27, random forest (RF)28, and neural networks29. However, combining the prediction of multiple models will lead to forming a collective decision-making mechanism that surpasses the capabilities of a single model. The combined prediction of such algorithms is termed ensemble modeling30. However, the dependence of ensemble models on individual algorithms considerably limits their efficacy.

Despite their potential to provide a novel and effective framework, one-class classification (OCC) techniques are rarely used for vPTB and xPTB prediction. OCC modeling identifies instances that deviate significantly from the standard, making it an ideal candidate for handling the skewed distribution of vPTB and xPTB9. OCC can overcome the challenges of class imbalance, mitigate bias, and achieve accurate evaluation and prediction of vPTB and xPTB. Therefore, this study explored the potential of OCC methods for predicting vPTB and xPTB as an alternative to traditional ML algorithms.

Early prediction of vPTB and xPTB is crucial; therefore, this study focuses on first-trimester data. Obtaining imaging modalities like ultrasounds is difficult, especially in remote areas; therefore, this study is focused on easy-to-collect tabular medical features (binary, numeric, and categorical). vPTB and xPTB data points are rare or may not be representative of the class during training. Therefore, we utilized OCC methods to train ML models with only one class (term patients) and predict the instances of vPTB and xPTB. The contribution of this study is to provide a comprehensive OCC framework that encompasses various OCC algorithms for predicting vPTB and xPTB in both parous (patients with a history of pregnancies) and nulliparous (first pregnancy) populations. Furthermore, we incorporated an ensemble of multiple OCC models using different aggregation and normalization strategies based on a novel dataset obtained during the first trimester of an Emirati pregnant population in the United Arab Emirates.

Literature review

Adverse pregnancy outcomes such as vPTB and xPTB are serious issues, and many studies have explored how to minimize the associated risks. Analyses on vPTB and xPTB from the medical perspective are abundant; however, few studies have attempted to predict PTB using ML algorithms. For instance, Mercer et al.31 performed LR analysis of PTB using data on 2,929 women to identify the risk factors and odds ratios for PTB. Lee et al.32 proposed ML methods to predict PTB and its significant risk factors using a dataset from 596 patients. The authors used six different classifiers (ANN, LR, DT, NB, RF, and SVM) for PTB classification and achieved accuracies of 0.911, 0.918, 0.832, 0.111, 0.891, and 0.914, respectively. The authors also identified BMI as the most significant risk factor followed by hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior cone biopsy, and prior placenta previa for PTB prediction. Tran et al.33 proposed stabilized sparse LR for PTB prediction to identify important risk factors using a dataset from 15,814 women that achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.85 to predict PTB at 34 weeks’ of gestation and 0.79 at 37 weeks.

Taha et al.34 identified the factors associated with low birth weight (LBW) and PTB using a dataset of 1,610 mothers in the United Arab Emirates. They revealed that the PTB and LBW ratios based on the sample size were 6.3% and 9.4%, respectively. Moreover, they reported that Arab women had a 2-fold higher risk of having PTB than non-Arab women.

Sun et al.9 evaluated the performance of six ML algorithms (RF, SVM, ANN, K-Mean, and Naïve Bayesian) for PTB prediction. They used data from 9,550 pregnant women (PTB: 4775; control: 4775) and constructed an ML model to predict PTB at 20, 22, 24, and 26 weeks of gestation. They revealed that RF exhibited the best performance with an AUC, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.89, 0.75, and 0.88, respectively, at 26 weeks of gestation.

Koivu et al.35 evaluated the performance of multiple classifiers such as LR, ANN, and lightgbm for PTB prediction. They used CDC and NYC for external validation, with 10% of the data used for testing. ANN and lightgbm models showed an AUC-ROC value of 0.67 with the CDC dataset and a maximum AUC-ROC value of 0.64 with the NYC dataset.

Raja et al.36 evaluated the performance of ML classifiers SVM, RF, DT, and LR for PTB classification on a dataset of 1,300 samples with 35 different features. The authors found that SVM achieved the best performance when the dataset was balanced using the SMOTE technique with an accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.90, 0.89, and 0.78, respectively.

Belaghi et al.24 used multiple ML models (RF, LR, DT, and ANN) to predict PTB in nulliparous women during the first and second trimesters. The authors created a feature set based on a dataset obtained from 112,963 nulliparous women. The sensitivity and specificity of LR in the first trimester were 0.50 and 0.64, respectively, whereas those of RF were 0.29 and 0.84, respectively. For the second trimester, the sensitivity and specificity obtained for RF and ANN were 0.45, 0.94 and 0.62, 0.84, respectively. Belaghi et al.24 performed multivariate statistical analysis for PTB and spontaneous PTB in multiparous and nulliparous women using data from 267,226 births. The AUCs obtained by LR analysis for the first trimester were 0.68 and 0.73 for nulliparous and multiparous women, respectively, whereas those for the second trimester were 0.72 and 0.78, respectively.

Another study12 on PTB used EHR data from 25,689 deliveries and trained recurrent neural network (RNN) ensemble models to predict xPTB. The study also identified risk factors associated with xPTB, such as twin pregnancy, short cervical length, hypertensive disorder, systemic lupus erythematosus, and hydroxychloroquine sulfate level. Similarly, Ren et al.14 proposed a PTB screening method that adopted a long short-term memory model based on a time-series analysis of obstetric examination records. Electronic medical record (EMR) data from 5,187 Chinese pregnant women were collected, encompassing over 25,000 obstetric EMRs from the early trimester to 28 weeks of gestation. The proposed method achieved an AUC-ROC value of 0.65, and the important risk factors identified were blood pressure, blood glucose, lipids, uric acid, and other metabolic factors. Similarly, another study done by16 evaluated a PTB prediction model trained on data from 4,332 pregnancies in 3,611 patients. They identified gestational age, dilation, rupture of membranes, time since admission, and heart rate as important risk factors associated with PTB delivery16. Although extensive research has been conducted to predict vPTB and xPTB, no studies have focused on OCC algorithms. OCC algorithms could provide valuable tools for improving prenatal care and intervention strategies.

Methods

This study evaluated the effectiveness of the OCC and ensemble models in accurately identifying vPTB and xPTB outcomes as outliers. Incorporating OCC algorithms only requires normal data points during the training phase; hence, no minority samples are needed. During the testing phase, the maximum number of positive (minority) data points is used, which helps obtain more reliable results reflective of real-world scenarios. A flowchart of the proposed methodology is presented in Fig. 1. Furthermore, Algorithm 1 details the process for predicting vPTB and xPTB using outlier detection and ensemble methods. Our method consists of several modules. The first module involves obtaining a well-curated dataset of vPTB and xPTB cases, wherein the selection of risk factors was carefully based on medical recommendations. All risk factors considered in this study were observed in the first trimester. In the second module, we trained multiple outlier detection algorithms using different training–testing strategies, including ensemble techniques. We aimed to enhance the accuracy and reliability of outlier detection by using multiple algorithms and combining their results through multiple ensemble strategies. The third module involves an extensive assessment using various evaluation metrics to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology. Through rigorous evaluation, we may gain insights into the strengths and limitations of the methodology, enabling us to further refine and enhance its performance.

Ensemble strategies

We implemented three different ensemble strategies, which are described in the subsequent sections.

Min–Max and Z-score normalization-based ensemble models for outlier detection

An OCC method generates an outlier score (OS) for a data point which represents how anomalous the point is, for a normal point OS score is low whereas for an outlier point OS is large. These outlier scores (OSs) are in different scales for different OCC models. In our ensemble-based outlier detection approach, we applied two different normalization techniques: min–max normalization-based and Z-score–based ensembles. In the normalization-based ensemble, we aimed to standardize and combine the outlier scores (OSs) from multiple models in the ensemble using the OS obtained from the training data. We employed three strategies: minimum, maximum, and median. For each strategy, we computed the corresponding OS (minimum, maximum, or median) among the ensemble models for each instance in the testing set.

To normalize the OSs, we used the minimum and maximum values obtained from the training set. For each instance in the testing set, we computed the normalized OS by subtracting the minimum OS and dividing it by the difference between the maximum and minimum OSs from the training set. We repeated this process for the maximum and median strategies to obtain three sets of normalized OSs.

In the Z-score–based ensemble, we employed the Z-score normalization technique. We calculated the mean and standard deviation of the OSs from the training set. For each instance in the testing set, we computed the Z-score by subtracting the mean of the training OSs and dividing it by the standard deviation of the training OSs.

By incorporating these normalized and standardized OSs into our ensemble-based outlier detection approach, we achieved a common scale and consistent interpretation of the OSs across multiple models.

Ensemble with sorted median aggregation

This model first sorts the OS obtained from multiple models in ascending order and then calculates the median of the sorted scores to obtain the aggregated OS for a given sample . We explored two distinct strategies for performing median aggregation: computing a single median value, referred to as “median (1),” and determining three median values or “median (3).” For median (1), the median value of the OS was directly calculated. In contrast, for median (3), we determined the median value of the OS along with the two nearest values (one on each side) and computed their average.

Ensemble with max aggregation

We explored two strategies for maximum aggregation: computing the maximum value or “max (1)” and determining the three maximum values or “max (3).” For max (1), the largest OS value was directly calculated. Conversely, for “max (3),” we identified the three largest OS values and calculated their average, offering a more balanced and optimistic outcome.

Ensemble with min aggregation

In this model, the minimum OS was selected from the OSs obtained from different models. We explored two strategies for minimum aggregation: calculating the minimum value or “min (1)” and determining the three minimum values “min (3).” For “min (1),” the smallest OS value was directly computed. Conversely, for “min (3),” we identified the three smallest OS values and calculated their average, which provided a more balanced outcome and reduced uncertainty.

The ensemble predictions obtained through these aggregation strategies allow for a more robust and comprehensive data analysis, potentially improving the accuracy and reliability of the final prediction for PTB51.

Locally selective combination of parallel outlier ensembles

We also used the locally selective combination of parallel outlier ensembles (LSCP) method from PyOD53 for ensemble-based outlier detection. LSCP is a powerful algorithm that combines the OSs obtained from multiple base models by selectively considering the local characteristics of the data. We trained various base models using algorithms such as GMM, KNN, IF, AE, SOS, KDE, LUNAR, OCSVM, KPCA, DeepSVDD, and VAE and obtained their OSs for the testing instances. The LSCP algorithm then selectively combines these OSs and assigns weights based on performance in local regions to compute the ensemble OS for each instance. This adaptive approach allowed us to effectively handle variations in the data and achieve improved outlier detection performance. By leveraging the diversity of base models and considering local patterns, the LSCP ensemble method enhanced the accuracy and robustness of outlier detection.

Stacked ensemble

In the stacked ensemble model52 of OCC models, we use two layers of OCC models. One OCC model is used in the second layer and the rest in the first layer. The training data is fed to each OCC model of the first layer of the stacked ensemble. We train OCC models in the first layer. These trained OCC models are used to generate outlier scores of each data point of the training data. The outputs of the trained OCC models in the first layer are used as the input to train the OCC model in the second layer of the stacked ensemble. If there are m OCC models, the first layer has m-1 trained OCC models. For each data point in the training data, (m-1) outliers scores are created by m-1 trained OCC models. These scores are the new features for that data point. If we have n data points in a training dataset, the first layer generates a dataset of size nx(m-1). This dataset is used to train OCC model in the second layer. When a testing point arrives, it will be fed to m-1 trained OCC models of the first layer, resulting in m-1 dimension feature vector for this testing point. These new features will be fed to the trained OCC model of the second layer to get the final outlier score of the testing point.

Performance metrics

The performance of the methods was evaluated using AUC-ROC (Area Under Curve for the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) and AUC-PRC (Area Under Curve for the Precision Recall Curve)54. These metrics provide a focused and effective assessment, considering the class distribution of our dataset. The AUC-ROC quantifies the ability of the model to distinguish between positive and negative instances, whereas the AUC-PRC value emphasizes precision and recall trade-offs. The value of AUC-ROC by a random model is 0.50, whereas for AUC-PRC value, for a random model is the ratio of minority samples to total number of samples and minority samples. The AUC-PR values for vPTB prediction are shown in Table 20. All models achieved better AUC-PRC values than random guessing. Furthermore, none of the ensemble models, whether stacked ensembles or ensembles with combinations, could manage to surpass the AUC-PRC values achieved by the best individual model in any of the datasets.

For xPTB prediction, the AUC-ROC value of all models (single, stacked ensembles, and ensembles with combination) shown in Table 21 performed better than the random guess (AUC = 0.50), demonstrating that the OCC framework has superior anomaly detection performance. Furthermore, no single OCC algorithm obtained superior results for all datasets. Finally, the ensemble models did not perform better than any single OCC algorithm. The results of AUC-PRC presented in Table 22 indicate that single models consistently outperform random guessing, with AUC-PRC values ranging from 0.0074 to 0.0149, whereas the best performing OCC models obtained varying results depending on the dataset characteristics. Furthermore, single models consistently performed better than ensemble models.

In summary, the findings of this study provide a comprehensive exploration of the efficacy of OCC methods in predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes and addressed the research questions we sought to elucidate. The results consistently demonstrate that OCC models outperformed random guessing thresholds, underscoring their value in addressing the challenges posed by class imbalance and anomaly detection. However, the best OCC model varies across datasets, reflecting the importance of tailoring models to specific contexts. Interestingly, while ensemble methods exhibited potential in enhancing OCC model performance, single models consistently maintained a strong predictive performance, both in terms of AUC-ROC and AUC-PRC value. These results emphasize the robustness and versatility of single models across different datasets. Furthermore, the research underscores the significance of appropriate normalization methods and combination methods in enhancing model performance.

As discussed in the Introduction section, it is very challenging to obtain high prediction accuracy for early prediction in this domain. We are getting reasonable accuracy which is better than random prediction. In other words, the models learned the problem. The models can be helpful to the medical practitioners to take better decisions. Overall, this study lays a sound foundation for the development of models for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes for highly imbalanced data.

Conclusion

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of models for predicting vPTB and xPTB in an Emirati pregnant population with nulliparous and parous women. The analyses exclusively used EHR data obtained during the first trimester of pregnancy that included vital information on maternal health, lifestyle, medical history, and socioeconomic factors. Multiple experimental setups were explored, and different combinations of risk factors were used to assess their impact on prediction performance in both parous and nulliparous populations. We employed several OCC algorithms and ensemble techniques to combine the strengths of individual algorithms. Our analysis highlighted the significance of appropriate normalization methods and feature selection in enhancing the predictive capabilities of the models. To address the limitations of predicting vPTB and xPTB, we demonstrated that OCC algorithms and ensembles can be employed using majority samples alone (without the need of anomaly samples during training).

This study utilizes a dataset collected from the United Arab Emirates (UAE), which may limit the generalizability of its findings to other countries. Variations in genetics, demographic profiles, socio-economic factors, healthcare infrastructure, and access to healthcare can significantly influence health outcome. These factors must be considered when applying the results beyond the UAE. OCC algorithms have shown better results across diverse domains and dataset types56,57. Therefore, the proposed OCC framework is expected to be applicable to similar datasets from other regions. We also aim to improve our pregnancy outcome prediction methods by exploring different algorithms. To provide a timely predictive model, this paper addresses the problem of early prediction of very preterm and extreme preterm births; hence, only first-trimester data were used. However, the risk factors may change over time (for instance, during the second trimester); therefore, such changes should be incorporated into future prediction models.

Furthermore, we aim to perform extensive hyperparameter optimizations. Traditionally, the hyperparameter optimization is done using validation data. However, in OCC problems, training data has only normal data points. In the absence of outlier points, we cannot create validation data (consisting of normal and outlier points)55. Therefore, we aim to generate artificial outlier points to create validation data that can be used for hyperparameter optimization. Various methods have been proposed to create artificial outlier points from one class dataset57–59. Desir et al.58 propose an artificial outlier generation process using the distribution of the training one class data. Khan et al.59 suggest that ‘outliers’ from the normal class can serve as proxies for the outlier points. In future, we aim to use these methods to create validation data.
