Spatial transcriptomics of en bloc transurethral resection of bladder tumor specimens defines an invasive-front gene expression signature
Authors: Kentaro Yoshihara, Fumihiko Urabe, Miyaka Umemori, Hironori Suzuki, Kagenori Ito, Takafumi Yanagisawa, Shun Sato, Keisuke Sekine, Takahiro Kimura, Yusuke Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder cancer is classified by depth of invasion into muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). Because some NMIBC cases progress to MIBC, it is important to elucidate the mechanisms of invasion. Spatial transcriptomics is a powerful tool for characterizing the tumor microenvironment. We applied Visium to seven high-grade NMIBC tumors using en bloc transurethral resection of bladder tumor (ERBT) specimens that preserve three-dimensional tumor architecture. Malignant regions were divided into the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions, and seven cases were further stratified by pathological subgroup based on the muscularis mucosae. We identified 15 genes with boundary-enriched expression in the higher-risk subgroup. These genes were enriched for epithelial-mesenchymal transition and myogenesis features. To assess the generalizability of this finding, we analyzed public bulk RNA-seq datasets and found that higher expression of the 15-gene signature was associated with worse overall survival, suggesting potential utility for prognostic stratification and therapeutic decision-making.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology, Urology

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the ninth most common cancer worldwide1. The majority of bladder cancer cases arise from urothelial carcinoma2. Clinically, bladder cancer is classified by depth of invasion into muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), and approximately 75% of newly diagnosed cases present as NMIBC3. Notably, NMIBC is characterized by high rates of recurrence and progression to MIBC, occurring in about 30–50% of patients. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of invasion at the NMIBC stage is essential.

Transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) is the standard for diagnosis and initial treatment of bladder cancer. However, specimen fragmentation and thermal artifacts disrupt spatial orientation, degrade specimen quality and impair the accurate assessment of pathologic stage and histologic grade4,5. Recently, en bloc transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (ERBT) has gained attention. ERBT is designed to excise the tumor in a single piece along with the detrusor muscle. This technique allows ERBT specimens to preserve the tumor’s three-dimensional architecture. Compared with TURBT, ERBT is associated with more accurate pathologic staging and higher rates of detrusor muscle presence and surgical margin evaluation6,7.

With advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS), studies using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) have been increasing8,9. In various cancers, these techniques elucidated the tumor heterogeneity at the gene expression level10,11, which enables direct observation of cellular diversity within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and characterization of intercellular communication. The TME is composed of immune cells, stromal cells, and endothelial cells, as well as non-cellular components like the extracellular matrix and signaling molecules12,13. The interactions among these factors influence tumor growth and invasion. By preserving tissue architecture, ST enables analysis of cellular states and functions14,15. This makes the TME and underlying molecular mechanisms visible, helping us uncover the invasion pathway and identify new therapeutic targets.

Here, we performed ST and analyzed seven cases of pT1 high-grade NMIBC using Visium Cyt Assist. In other cancer types, the tumor-boundary region has been reported to exhibit molecular profiles distinct from those of the tumor-upper region and to display features associated with invasion and metastasis. Therefore, we focused on the tumor-boundary region to elucidate the invasive area of the tumor. We used ERBT, preserving the layered architecture and improving our understanding of the tissue planes. We first annotated spots using two independent deconvolution tools and compared the results. Malignant regions were divided into the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions. Finally, we defined pathology-based subgroups and performed a comprehensive assessment of gene expression within the invasive region of NMIBC. We identified a boundary-enriched gene set, created a signature score, and demonstrated that this score correlates with tumor aggressiveness.

Results

Spatial transcriptome profiling and deconvolution of seven bladder cancer samples

We performed ERBT in patients with suspected primary bladder cancer and analyzed seven formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples diagnosed as T1 high grade using Visium Cyt Assist (Fig.1A). Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The number of Visium spots ranged from 2078 to 4096 per sample, and the median number of genes detected per sample ranged from 2403 to 7532 (Supplementary Data S1). Using Seurat, we performed gene expression clustering for each sample and identified 16–19 clusters per sample. We assigned clusters to cell classes based on canonical markers16: Epithelial (KRT5, KRT19, EPCAM), CAF (VIM, COL1A1, COL3A1), Endothelial (SELE, PECAM1), Myeloid cells (LYZ, MS4A7, CD14), Lymphoid cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD79A), and Smooth muscle (ACTA2, TAGLN). In molecular subtyping of bladder cancer with bulk RNA-sequencing, UROMOL cohort has been reported for NMIBC17. It defines three classes, namely Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3. High-risk features such as T1 tumors, high-grade, and CIS are enriched in Class 2. In our cohort, all seven samples were classified as Class 2 (Table 1).

The Visium spot is 55 μm, so each spot holds approximately 10 cells. We used two deconvolution tools to estimate cell type composition (Fig.1B). First, SpaCET (v1.2.0) passed quality checks (Supplementary Fig. 1). We then distinguished malignant spots from nonmalignant spots using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Copy Number Alteration Dictionary with cancer type “BLCA”18. Next, we inferred immune and stromal profiles using hierarchical references built from multi-cancer scRNA-seq datasets (Supplementary Fig. 2). For each spot, we assigned the cell type with the highest estimated proportion, and delineated the tumor-stroma interface using SpaCET package (Supplementary Fig. 2). Because SpaCET covers a limited set of cell types, we added STdeconvolve, which infers cell mixtures without a single-cell reference19 (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 4). Using the tissue images for guidance, we used STdeconvolve to fill in cell types that SpaCET did not cover, then finalized annotations for each spot (Fig.1C). To validate our reference-free deconvolution methods with SpaCET and STdeconvolve, we also applied a reference-based approach with Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) (Supplementary Fig. 5). This approach also yielded results largely consistent with the reference-free methods. We applied the same procedure to all seven samples, and the results are shown in Fig.1D. We also confirmed the spatial distribution of canonical marker genes in each sample (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Integration of seven samples and classification of tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions with identification of Differential expression genes (DEGs)

We extracted malignant spots and integrated the data from the seven samples. The number of malignant spots per sample ranged from 644 to 2952, for a total of 15159 spots. To reduce batch effects, we applied the Harmony algorithm20. We then performed clustering with the “Findclusters” function and obtained 17 clusters (Fig.2A). Using “PlotClusterTree” function, we generated a clustering tree for the integrated object (Fig.2B). With reference to the tissue images, we assigned clusters to the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions, and the two regions showed clear separation on UMAP (Fig.2C). The tumor-boundary region in this study was confirmed by a board-certified genitourinary pathologist (S.S.), and defined as the invasive front of the tumor. We mapped these regions onto the tissue images and confirmed separation into the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary (Fig.2D).

It has been reported that molecular profiles differ between the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary, and that the tumor-boundary exhibits invasive and metastatic characteristics in oral squamous cell carcinoma21. Because we considered that tumor cells at the tumor-boundary are enriched for progression phenotypes in bladder cancer, we performed patient-level paired pseudo-bulk analysis to compare transcriptomic profiles between the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions and listed highly expressed genes in each group (Supplementary Data S2). We identified DEGs using a cutoff of |log2 fold change (log2FC)| greater than 1 and visualized the results with a volcano plot (Fig.2E). The tumor-boundary region showed higher expression of collagen-rich extracellular matrix genes (ECM) such as COL1A1 and COL3A1, remodeling genes such as MMP2, and immune-related genes such as IGHG1 and IGHG322,23. Pathway analysis of DEGs using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) showed upregulation of Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and Focal adhesion in the tumor-boundary region (Fig.2F). This suggests increased cell motility and tissue contractility at the tumor-boundary. We also performed GSEA on the DEGs from both groups. The tumor-upper region showed enrichment of HALLMARK_MYC_TARGETS_V1, HALLMARK_OXIDATIVE_PHOSPHORYLATION, and HALLMARK_E2F_TARGETS. The tumor-boundary region showed enrichment of HALLMARK_EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION, HALLMARK_ALLOGRAFT_REJECTION, and HALLMARK_INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE (Supplementary Fig. 7). These results indicate more active cell proliferation in the tumor-upper region and enhanced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated invasion at the tumor-boundary region.

We also examined gene expression related to immune cell infiltration. CD4 T cell and B cell marker genes were highly expressed, and immunohistochemistry confirmed their presence around the tumor boundary (Supplementary Fig. 8A). We investigated intercellular communication between these immune cells and tumor cells using CellChat. Specifically, we analyzed signaling interactions among tumor-boundary spots, CAF spots, B cell spots, and CD4 T cell spots. LIGHT signaling was increased from B cells to malignant spots and from CD4 T cell spots to malignant spots (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C). The most frequent ligand-receptor pairs were “TNFSF14 to TNFRSF14” and “TNFSF14 to LTBR” (Supplementary Fig. 8D). Spatial mapping of ligand and receptor gene expression showed that TNFSF14 was enriched in regions with high immune cell infiltration, whereas LTBR was enriched at the tumor-boundary (Supplementary Fig. 8E). These findings suggest that immune cells are present around the tumor-boundary region and that intercellular communication may contribute to tumor progression.

Identification of genes upregulated in recurrent group and pathway analysis

NMIBC is characterized by high rates of recurrence, occurring in approximately 30-50% of patients. Recurrence was observed in three cases in our cohort. After excluding one case with disease progression, patients were classified into recurrent and non-recurrent groups. We identified genes highly expressed in the recurrent group. We then performed patient-level paired pseudo-bulk analysis focusing on malignant spots and generated a volcano plot (Supplementary Data S3). Using a cutoff of |log2FC| greater than 1, we identified 107 upregulated genes and 18 downregulated genes in the recurrent group (Supplementary Fig. 9A). In recurrent cases, genes associated with cellular differentiation and therapy resistance were upregulated24,25. Gene Ontology pathway analysis and GSEA showed that pathways related to epithelial cell proliferation and cytokine-associated processes were enriched in the recurrent group, suggesting features associated with chronic inflammation and tumor growth (Supplementary Fig. 9B, C)

pT1 subgroups show region specific gene profiles with proliferation in the tumor-upper and ECM at the tumor-boundary

Several reports have been proposed to subgroup pT1 bladder cancer26. The most widely used approach treats the muscularis mucosae as a diagnostic landmark. Tumors with invasion superficial to the muscularis mucosae are classified as pT1a, and tumors with invasion beyond the muscularis mucosae are classified as pT1b (Fig.3A). Previous reports showed that tumors beyond the muscularis mucosae were associated with worse progression-free survival (PFS)27. Therefore, by comparing the tumor compartments of pT1a and pT1b, we expected to identify genes associated with progression. In our study, a genitourinary pathologist (S.S.) classified the seven cases as pT1a (n = 2) and pT1b (n = 5) (Fig.3A). Using “FindMarkers” function, we compared malignant spots labeled as tumor-upper region between pT1a and pT1b, identified higher-expressed genes, and examined overall transcriptional differences (Supplementary Data S4). Because tumor-upper spots contain fewer stromal and immune cells, this comparison focuses on cancer cell profiles. With a cutoff of |log2FC| greater than 1, the tumor-upper analysis identified 2910 genes upregulated in pT1b and 1298 genes downregulated (Fig.3B). Genes related to cell proliferation and cell adhesion were increased in pT1b. We also observed increases in genes linked to carbohydrate and lipid synthesis. We performed the same analysis for the tumor-boundary region and generated a volcano plot of DEGs (Supplementary Data S5). With the same cutoff, the tumor-boundary analysis identified 2613 genes upregulated in pT1b and 437 genes downregulated (Fig.3C). Genes related to ECM, cell adhesion, and stress responses were increased in pT1b. KEGG pathway analysis of the tumor-upper DEGs showed enrichment of Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, and Cornified envelop formation (Fig.3D). These findings suggest the tumor-upper region in higher-risk lesions appears to have increased cell motility and proliferation. KEGG pathway analysis of tumor-boundary DEGs showed enrichment of PI3K-Akt signaling, Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, Rap1 signaling pathway, Proteoglycans in cancer and Axon guidance (Fig.3E). These results indicate that the tumor-boundary region in higher-risk lesions appears to have increased cell proliferation, ECM composition, and cell adhesion. In addition, CellChat analysis showed that MK (midkine) and PTN (pleiotrophin) signaling from CAF to tumor-boundary region tended to be stronger in pT1b samples (Supplementary Fig. 10). These pathways have been reported to promote ECM remodeling through downstream signaling28, which is consistent with our KEGG findings.

Identification of genes upregulated in pT1b and confirmation of spatial expression

We compared pT1a and pT1b cases. In the tumor-upper region, we found 2910 genes upregulated in pT1b. In the tumor-boundary region, we found 2613 genes upregulated in pT1b. Because boundary spots are more affected by mixed cell composition, whereas tumor-upper spots are less affected, we extracted 1950 genes that were upregulated in pT1b in both regions as tumor-intrinsic upregulated genes (Fig.3F). Next, we compared the tumor-boundary region with the tumor-upper region using pseudo-bulk analysis and listed the higher-expressed genes within pT1b. With a cutoff of |log2FC| greater than 1, we extracted 106 genes with higher expression at the tumor-boundary (Supplementary Data S6). A patient-level comparison based on pT1a (n = 2) and pT1b (n = 5) also yielded similar results to the spot-level analysis (Supplementary Data S7, S8). We identified 15 genes from the intersection of the two sets and interpreted them as genes intrinsically upregulated in pT1b, with particularly high expression at the tumor-boundary region (Fig.3F). We confirmed the expression of these 15 genes across all samples. A heat map showed higher expression in pT1b than in pT1a for each gene (Fig.3G). We visualized the spatial expression of each gene in pT1b tumors and confirmed higher expression at the tumor-boundary region (Fig.3H). Functionally, this gene set, which includes TIMP2 and CDH11, is associated with ECM remodeling and EMT-like changes, suggesting a role in promoting invasion at the tumor-boundary region.

The 15-gene signature score is enriched at the tumor-boundary and increases along pT1 subgroups

We computed a signature score from the 15-gene set using Seurat’s “AddModuleScore” function. For each spot, we calculated the average expression of the 15 genes and subtracted the average expression of matched control gene sets. We then combined per-spot scores across samples and visualized higher scores at the tumor-boundary region(Fig.4A). For each sample, the mean signature score was measured in tumor-upper and tumor-boundary spots. The tumor-boundary region showed a significantly higher score than the tumor-upper region (p = 0.019) (Fig.4B). Within the tumor-boundary region, signature scores tended to be higher in pT1b than pT1a, although the difference was not significant given the small sample size (p = 0.087) (Fig.4C). These findings suggest that the signature score captures the tumor-boundary and may follow a gradient consistent with the pathology-based subgroups. Because the tumor-boundary region may be affected by compositional effects, we repeated the analysis using a malignant-fraction threshold of 0.7. We identified 18 genes, 12 of which overlapped with the 15-gene set identified in the primary analysis. Furthermore, when we visualized the 15-gene signature score in spots with a malignant fraction > 0.7, the score remained higher at the tumor-boundary region than in the tumor-upper region (Supplementary Fig. 11) (Supplementary Data S9, S10). These findings indicate that boundary-enriched expression was preserved even after accounting for compositional effects.

Higher 15-gene signature scores indicate worse survival and increase with pathologic stage in a public cohort

The area that Visium can analyze is limited to 6.5 mm square, allowing examination of only a small portion of each specimen. We validated the association between 15-gene expression levels and patient outcomes using public RNA-seq data. To ensure our findings at the whole-tumor level, we downloaded bulk RNA-seq data from the UROMOL cohort. We also downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal and analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from 431 bladder cancer cases with clinical information29. For each case, we computed a 15-gene signature score as the mean expression of the 15 genes (Fig.4D). Using the 15-gene signature score, we used the “survminer” package to determine the optimal risk-score cutoff, then generated Kaplan-Meier curves and performed log-rank tests to evaluate the association with overall survival (OS) and PFS. In the UROMOL cohort, a higher signature score was associated with worse PFS (HR 2.24, 95% CI 1.23–4.10, p = 0.007) (Fig.4E). We also calculated the 15-gene signature score in the TCGA-BLCA cohort. A higher signature score was associated with worse OS (HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.20–2.16, p = 0.001) (Fig.4F). We also evaluated each of the 15 genes individually. Patients were split into high and low groups based on gene expression, and OS was compared. Five genes showed a significant association with worse OS (Supplementary Fig. 12). PFS was analyzed in the same manner. A higher signature score was associated with worse PFS (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.06–2.04, p = 0.02) (Fig. 4G). We obtained the T stage and N stage from the clinical data and then compared the signature scores by stage. The score increased with advancing T stage (T1 vs. T2 p = 0.2, T1 vs. T3 p = 0.01, T1 vs. T4 p = 0.003) (Fig.4H). For N stage, with lymph node metastasis had significantly higher signature scores (p = 0.01) (Fig.4I). For M stage, the number of metastatic cases was limited, so we did not observe a clear difference in the signature score (Supplementary Fig. 13). We performed immunohistochemistry on the seven bladder cancer specimens to confirm intratumoral protein expression. From the public bulk RNA-seq analysis, we selected five genes that showed a significant association individually with worse OS. Positive staining was observed in tumor regions, but staining intensity did not differ between pT1a and pT1b cases (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results indicate that protein level differences were not detectable, whereas RNA-level differences were evident in the bladder cancer samples.

Discussion

We analyzed ERBT specimens and compared the tumor-upper with the tumor-boundary region. ERBT specimens allow us to examine regions that are hard to assess after conventional TURBT, where sections become fragmented and thermally damaged. For MIBC, current guidelines recommend neoadjuvant chemotherapy before cystectomy3. Therefore, when cystectomy specimens are used, the tissue assessment reflects modifications introduced by the initial TURBT and any neoadjuvant chemotherapy. A key feature of our cohort is the use of treatment-naive specimens. Studies on ST in bladder cancer have been reported before, but most have focused on MIBC30,31. Only one study of ST in NMIBC has been reported in BCG-resistant tumors32. Therefore, to our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of NMIBC using treatment-naive ERBT specimens. Because some NMIBC cases progress to MIBC, our study is novel in that it analyzes invasive mechanisms at the NMIBC stage while preserving the tumor architecture.

Additionally, in the present study, we analyzed the data using detailed pT1 substaging made possible by the histologic advantages of ERBT specimens. Evidence on the prognostic value of the muscularis mucosae-based method in ERBT samples remains limited. Prior studies have proposed several pT1 subgroup schemes, including the use of the muscularis mucosae, depth from the epithelial basement membrane, and the papillary stalk landmark26,33. Because TURBT specimens are often inadequate, subgroup schemes have not been standardized in clinical practice. In conventional TURBT specimens, pT1 substaging is feasible in 70% of cases. In contrast, ERBT specimens increase evaluability to nearly 100%34, and pT1a cases have shown favorable PFS35. Therefore, ERBT specimens enable detailed subgroup analyses and allow for the assessment of invasion-related differences. Focusing on the DEG between pT1a and pT1b samples will deepen our understanding and support more accurate prognostication and more appropriate postoperative planning.

Comparing the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions, the tumor-boundary region showed enrichment of EMT-related and cell cycle genes, and immune-related genes such as IGHG1 and IGHG3 were also detected. Increased EMT and cell cycle activity in the tumor-boundary region may reflect enhanced tumor cell invasion and proliferation. By contrast, elevated immune-related genes may reflect immune infiltration. Although several studies have reported that associations between immune cell density and prognosis, the impact of immune cells on tumor progression cannot be determined from our data and requires further investigation36. Next, we grouped the seven samples by pathology subgroup based on the musclularis mucosae. The seven samples were classified as pT1a (n = 2) and pT1b (n = 5). Using this analysis, we identified a set of 15 genes that are strongly expressed at the tumor-boundary region in higher-risk cases. These genes are related to HALLMARK_EMT and HALLMARK_Myogenesis. Indicating EMT changes and enhanced contractile programs at the invasive boundary.

Among EMT-related genes, TIMP237, POSTN38, COMP39, FBLN540, LUM41, and CDH1142 were upregulated at the tumor-boundary region in higher-risk cases. In the TME, increased expression of these genes drives excessive ECM deposition and crosslinking of ECM components. Elevated ECM stiffness has been reported to enhance cell proliferation and motility and to promote invasion43, consistent with our findings. Among contraction-related genes, DES44, ACTG245, and MYH1146 were upregulated at the tumor-boundary region in higher-risk cases. As part of the cell-motility program driven by these genes, they promote focal-adhesion assembly and actin filaments polymerization47. The collective contractility increases mechanical tension, which can help maintain tissue architecture and thereby facilitate tumor-cell migration and invasion. Taken together, these data suggest that EMT-linked remodeling induces stiffening of the ECM at the tumor-boundary region. Adhesion to the stiffened matrix, coupled with actomyosin contractility, promotes tumor-cell proliferation and progression. To validate the association of this gene set with tumor aggressiveness at the whole tumor level, we analyzed public bulk RNA-seq data. The 15 gene signature was associated with worse OS and PFS.

This study has limitations. First, the resolution of Visium is a limitation. Each spot is 55 μm in diameter and contains approximately 10 cells. We therefore applied deconvolution to estimate the cell composition of each spot before analysis. Future higher-resolution platforms will likely enable more detailed studies. Second, the cost is high, so the number of samples we can analyze is limited. Third, because our study was based on a single-center cohort that included only pT1 high-grade cases, the findings may not be fully generalizable. Future studies should include additional stages to validate our results. The findings from Visium may have clinical applications, including the prediction of treatment response and the identification of new biomarkers for personalized care. Immunohistochemistry revealed positive staining in tumor regions, with no difference in intensity observed between pT1a and pT1b. This suggests that evaluation at the transcriptome level is needed for clinical application. Insights from this exploratory result can be simplified into practical assays, such as qPCR panels, which would reduce the need to run spatial profiling on every specimen. Sampling strategy is an important consideration. ERBT specimens are well-suited for boundary-focused analyses. If the ERBT sample is not available, targeted sampling of the boundary during TURBT or during a second TUR may be useful.

Methods

Sample collection

We enrolled seven patients who underwent ERBT at The Jikei University Hospital and Jikei Kashiwa Hospital. All cases were diagnosed pathologically as T1 high-grade urothelial carcinoma, and resected tissues were preserved as FFPE blocks. The details of the samples and patient information are summarized in Table 1. These samples were used with the approval of the Ethics Review Committee of the Jikei University School of Medicine (33-004). This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients received a written explanation of the study and provided written informed consent before participation.

En bloc transurethral resection of bladder tumor procedure

ERBT was performed using a bipolar TURis system (Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) with either a needle-type or loop-type electrode. A circumferential margin of 5 mm from the visible edge of the tumor was marked. We cut through the lamina propria to the muscularis propria and dissected around the entire circumference of the tumor. A shallow plane within the muscle layer was then incised, and the tumor was excised en bloc, including the muscle propria. Biopsy forceps were used to extract the specimen (Fig. 1A).

Spatial transcriptomics: tissue preparation, library construction, and preprocessing

ST was performed on FFPE sections from pT1 high-grade bladder cancer. We used the Visium Spatial Gene Expression for FFPE platform (10x Genomics, USA) to capture RNA transcripts from each spot on the slide-mounted FFPE tissue sections. Sections of 5 µm thickness were prepared using a microtome and placed on Visium Spatial Slides. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, imaged using a BZ-X700 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). Then the coverslips were removed, followed by destaining and decrosslinking according to the Visium FFPE workflow. Probe hybridization and library construction were performed. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus system (Illumina, USA). The sequencing data were processed with Space Ranger (v 2.0.1, 10x Genomics). Downstream analyses were performed in R using the Seurat package (v 5.1.0). Data were normalized with “SCTransform” function. Quality control, dimensionality reduction, and spot clustering were conducted following the Seurat vignette.

Deconvolution and annotation with SpaCET

We analyzed 10x Visium data using the SpaCET package (v1.2.0). Visium data were processed using “create.SpaCET.object.10X” function. Quality control metrics were processed using “SpaCET.quality.control” function. Spots were deconvolved into various cell types with “SpaCET.deconvolution” function (malignant component modeled with cancerType = “BLCA”). From the resulting cell-type proportion matrix, we assigned to each spot the cell type with the highest proportion as its primary annotation.

Deconvolution and annotation with STdeconvolve

For complementary reference-free deconvolution, we applied the STdeconvolve package (v 1.9.0). Low-quality spots were removed using “cleanCounts” function, excluding spots with a library size of less than 100 and genes detected in fewer than 10 reads. Feature selection was performed with “restrictCorpus” function to remove genes detected in 100% of spots and those detected in less than 5% of spots.

We then fitted multiple latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) models with varying K values using “fitLDA” function and selected the model with the lowest perplexity. To encourage sparse spot compositions, the symmetric Dirichlet hyperparameter α was less than 1. Topic–gene profiles (β matrix) and spot–topic proportions (θ matrix) were obtained with “getBetaTheta” function. Cell types contributing less than 5% to any given spot were filtered with perc.filt = 0.05.

Spatial visualization was performed using “vizAllTopics” function. We further assessed concordance with prior marker-based genes by computing correlation matrices with “getCorrMtx” function and visualizing them using “correlationPlot” function. Marker sets included Epithelial (KRT5, KRT19, EPCAM), CAF (VIM, COL1A1), Endothelial (SELE, PECAM1), Myeloid cells (LYZ, MS4A7), Lymphoid cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD79A), and Smooth muscle (ACTA2, TAGLN). We used these heat maps to evaluate concordance between the deconvolved latent cell types and reference expectations. We then assigned each topic the reference label showing the most concordant reference when the maximum concordance score was > 0.3 and the difference from the second-highest score was > 0.15. When these criteria did not allow a unique assignment, we calculated marker gene-based scores for each cell type and annotated the topic with the cell type showing the highest score. Finally, we confirmed the validity of the annotations using heatmaps of marker gene expression.

Deconvolution and annotation with the RCTD

Cell type deconvolution was conducted using “spacexr” R package. As a reference, we used a publicly available NMIBC scRNA-seq to construct cell type expression profiles. RCTD algorithm was applied to Visium datasets to estimate cell type contributions for each spot. Based on these estimates, the cell type with the highest contribution in each spot was defined as first_type and displayed on the spatial map.

Integration of seven samples

We analyzed seven pT1 high-grade bladder cancer samples. Gene expression matrices were read with “Read10X” function, and a Seurat object was created for each sample using “CreateSeuratObject” function. Spots annotated as “Malignant” by deconvolution were extracted to build malignant-only Seurat objects. Quality control and dimensionality reduction were performed with Seurat, using “SCTransform” function for data normalization. We applied the Harmony algorithm to correct batch effects across samples. Clustering was performed with “FindNeighbors” function and “FindClusters” function (resolution = 0.4), and visualization using “RunUMAP” function.

DEGs between tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions in malignant spots

We integrated malignant spots into a Seurat object and classified clusters using “PlotClusterTree” function. After reviewing the tissue slide images, spots were annotated as the tumor-upper or tumor-boundary regions. To compare transcriptomic profiles between these regions, we conducted a pseudo-bulk analysis. The expression data were aggregated with “AggregateExpression” function, and DEGs between the tumor-upper and tumor-boundary regions were assessed using DESeq2 method. DEGs were defined as those with a log2FC greater than 1.0 and an adjusted p value less than 0.05. Volcano plots were generated with ggplot2 package (v3.5.2).

Additionally, to compare transcriptomic profiles between pT1a and pT1b cases, we used Seurat. Counts were normalized with “SCTransform” function, followed by model adjustment with the “PrepSCTFindMarkers” function. DEGs were then tested using “FindMarkers (test.use = “wilcox”)” function. Volcano plots were generated with ggplot2 package.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler package (v 4.10.1) with an over-representation analysis (ORA) framework. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using “enrichKEGG” function with organism = “hsa” and pvalueCutoff = 0.05. Statistical significance was defined as FDR less than 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea package (v 1.28.0), ranking all genes by log2FC and employing the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB).

Calculation of the 15-gene signature score

We defined the 15-gene signature score comprising “TIMP2”, “INHBA”, “SPON1”, “SLURP2”, “HHIP”, “DES”, “POSTN”, “COMP”, “FBLN5”, “ACTG2”, “LUM”, “ISLR”, “CDH11”, “MYH11”, and “SPARCL1”. Signature scores were assigned to each spot using “AddModuleScore” function.

Public RNA-seq data collection, processing, and survival analysis

For external validation, we used bulk RNA-seq data from the UROMOL cohort annotated with NMIBC molecular subtypes (UROMOL classes), and included 530 cases with available PFS data. In addition, bulk RNA-seq datasets were obtained from the GDC Data Portal. A total of 431 cases with gene expression files were downloaded, along with their corresponding clinical information. A total of 417 cases were retained for OS analysis. Samples were divided into High and Low score groups by selecting the optimal cutoff of the signature score that yielded the minimum log-rank p-value, and Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the “survival” and “survminer” packages.

Immunostaining

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed in ImmunoSaver (10% sodium citraconate solution; FUJIFILM Wako) diluted 1:200 in distilled water using an electric pot at 95–100 °C for 45 min, followed by bench cooling for 30 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Sections were incubated at room temperature for 60 min with the following primary antibodies: TIMP2 (#ab1828, Abcam, 1:200), SPON1 (#CSB-PA974733, Cusabio Technology, 1:50), COMP (#ab231977, Abcam, 1:200), CDH11 (#71-7600, Invitrogen, 1:100), SPARCL1 (#13517-1-AP, proteintech, 1:50), CD4 (#413181, Nichirei Bioscience, pre-diluted), and CD19 (#ab134114, Abcam, 1:250). After washes in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS-T), sections were incubated for 30 min with Histofine® Simple Stain™ MAX PO (MULTI) (Nichirei Biosciences; code H2402), and followed by Mayer’s hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and permanent mounting.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio and R software (v 4.3.3). Two-group comparisons of continuous variables used Welch’s t-test, and a p-value (less than 0.05) was considered statistically significant. For stage comparisons, we conducted pairwise two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Holm correction for multiple testing.
