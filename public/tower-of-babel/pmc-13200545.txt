KSDiffusion: conditional diffusion for kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction under data-limited and imbalanced regimes
Authors: Shi Qiu, Chunguo Wu, Yuxiang Ma, Songye Gao, Limin Wang, Yanchun Liang, Xiaohu Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein phosphorylation governs cellular signaling, making accurate identification of kinase-specific sites essential for understanding regulatory and disease mechanisms. Although computational approaches have shown promise in inferring kinase specificity, most existing methods primarily rely on local sequence patterns and remain limited in their ability to capture broader contextual information. More critically, experimentally validated kinase–substrate data are inherently scarce and highly imbalanced across kinase groups, substantially restricting the generalization performance of purely discriminative models, especially for underrepresented kinases. To address these challenges, we propose KSDiffusion, a unified framework for kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction explicitly designed for data-limited and imbalanced regimes. KSDiffusion integrates a protein language model with task-aware conditional diffusion-based generative modeling. Specifically, an ESM-2-based encoder is employed to extract context-aware peptide representations enriched with evolutionary and structural information, while supervised contrastive learning further enhances kinase-specific discriminability in the embedding space. To alleviate data scarcity for rare kinase groups, we introduce a conditional diffusion model, termed KS-DiT, which generates biologically plausible and kinase-consistent synthetic representations that directly support downstream prediction. Comprehensive experiments across kinase groups spanning low-, medium-, and large-data regimes demonstrate that KSDiffusion consistently outperforms representative baseline methods. In particular, substantial improvements are achieved for data-scarce kinase groups, with AUC gains of up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim $\end{document}15%, while maintaining competitive performance when sufficient training data are available. These results underscore the regime-dependent effectiveness of conditional diffusion-based augmentation and highlight the value of integrating protein language models with task-aware generative modeling for robust kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction under realistic data constraints.

Introduction

Protein phosphorylation, catalyzed by protein kinases, is one of the most fundamental and widespread posttranslational modifications (PTMs) in eukaryotic cells [1–3]. This process transfers a phosphate group from adenosine triphosphate to specific amino acid residues of substrate proteins under precisely regulated temporal and spatial contexts [4–6]. In eukaryotes, phosphorylation predominantly occurs on serine (S), threonine (T), and tyrosine (Y) residues. It is tightly controlled by kinase-mediated signaling networks to maintain cellular homeostasis [7]. Dysregulation of phosphorylation dynamics has been implicated in a wide range of diseases, including cancer, diabetes, and developmental disorders [8, 9]. Owing to their central regulatory roles, protein kinases have become one of the most important classes of therapeutic targets. Consequently, systematic characterization of kinase-mediated phosphorylation events is critical for both mechanistic studies and drug discovery [10]. In this context, the accurate identification of kinase-specific phosphorylation sites is essential for elucidating phosphorylation-dependent signaling mechanisms and advancing biomedical research [11].

The rapid development of high-throughput, mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomics has enabled the large-scale identification of hundreds of thousands of phosphorylation sites across the human proteome [12]. However, these technologies struggle to determine the upstream kinases responsible for specific phosphorylation events. They remain particularly insufficient for resolving kinase-specific sites [13, 14]. Experimental identification of kinase–substrate relationships still relies largely on low-throughput, labor-intensive, and costly biochemical techniques. Examples include in vitro kinase assays, phosphorylation-specific antibody enrichment, and site-directed mutagenesis [15, 16]. As a result, only a small fraction of experimentally identified phosphosites have been confidently annotated with their cognate kinases, leaving substantial gaps in current annotations [17]. This incompleteness severely constrains system-level analyses of cellular signaling networks and limits our understanding of kinase-regulated biological processes [18, 19].

Consequently, computational approaches have become indispensable tools. They scalably predict kinase-specific sites by learning discriminative patterns from established kinase–substrate relationships. Early computational methods primarily relied on manually engineered sequence features combined with simple statistical or machine learning models [20]. For instance, Scansite employed position-specific scoring matrices to scan protein sequences for motifs matching known kinase substrate preferences [21]. NetPhos introduced one-hot encoding of peptide sequences and applied artificial neural networks for prediction, laying the foundation for subsequent predictors [22, 23]. GPS 1.0 further proposed a group-based strategy by constructing hierarchical models according to the evolutionary relationships of kinases at the group, family, and subfamily levels [16, 24]. Although these approaches provided early insights into kinase specificity, their reliance on handcrafted features and shallow model architectures fundamentally limited predictive performance.

To improve accuracy, subsequent studies incorporated richer feature representations and more advanced machine learning algorithms [25]. The KinasePhos series exemplifies this methodological evolution. KinasePhos 1.0 encoded sequences using position-specific scoring matrices and physicochemical properties, employing Hidden Markov Models for prediction [26]. KinasePhos 2.0 introduced the maximum relevance minimum redundancy feature selection strategy and adopted Support Vector Machines to enhance discriminative capability [27]. More recently, KinasePhos 3.0 represented protein sequences as consecutive tripeptide “words” and integrated a pretrained word2vec model with convolutional neural networks to learn high-level sequence representations [28]. With the emergence of transfer learning, GPS 6.0 further expanded its scope by pretraining a universal phosphorylation predictor on nearly 500 000 sites and transferring the learned knowledge to construct 577 kinase-specific models across 185 species [29]. Phosformer proposed an end-to-end deep learning framework that abandons manual feature engineering and explicit kinase category encoding. By feeding kinase domain sequences and substrate peptides directly into a Transformer-based architecture, it captures evolutionary and structural dependencies to achieve state-of-the-art performance [30, 31].

Despite these advances, kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction continues to face two fundamental challenges. First, existing models rely predominantly on local substrate sequences. They often neglect broader biological contexts, including tertiary structures, evolutionary constraints, and dynamic interactions. Consequently, their predictions frequently lack essential biophysical and physiological constraints [31]. Second, experimentally validated kinase–substrate relationships are inherently data-limited and highly imbalanced. While a small number of well-studied kinases are associated with abundant phosphorylation annotations, many kinase groups possess only a handful of confirmed substrates [30]. Under such conditions, purely discriminative models tend to overfit dominant kinase groups and exhibit poor generalization to data-scarce kinases, substantially limiting their practical applicability.

To address these challenges, we propose KSDiffusion, a unified computational framework explicitly designed for data-limited and highly imbalanced regimes. KSDiffusion integrates protein language model-based representation learning with task-aware conditional diffusion modeling. Specifically, we construct a context-rich encoding module based on ESM-2, a large-scale protein language model capable of capturing evolutionary and structural information directly from raw sequences. To further enhance representation quality, we introduce a supervised contrastive learning (SCL) strategy that encourages discrimination among kinase-specific patterns while preserving biologically meaningful similarities. To mitigate the scarcity of annotations in underrepresented kinase groups, we developed a conditional diffusion model, termed KS-DiT. This model generates biologically plausible synthetic peptide embeddings conditioned on kinase-specific information. Rather than serving as a standalone augmentation technique, this diffusion model is tightly coupled with the downstream prediction task. This integration enables the principled supplementation of training data where severe scarcity hinders discriminative learning.

Importantly, KSDiffusion targets realistic data regimes where data availability gradually transitions from sparse to sufficient, rather than extreme few-shot scenarios. Through systematic experiments across varying data scales, we demonstrate that KSDiffusion effectively improves prediction performance for data-scarce and imbalanced kinase groups while maintaining competitive accuracy when sufficient training data are available. These results highlight the regime-dependent effectiveness of diffusion-based augmentation and provide insights into when and why generative modeling can enhance phosphorylation site prediction. Overall, KSDiffusion offers a biologically informed and robust solution to the longstanding challenges of limited context integration and severe data imbalance. Ultimately, it facilitates a more reliable exploration of kinase-regulated signaling networks.

Materials and methods

Dataset construction and problem setting

To support kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction under data-limited and highly imbalanced regimes, we construct two complementary datasets: (i) a general phosphorylation site dataset, used for representation adaptation and diffusion pretraining; and (ii) a kinase-specific phosphorylation site dataset, used for conditional generation and downstream evaluation. This dual-dataset design enables the model to capture global phosphoproteomic patterns required for learning general phosphorylation-related representations, while simultaneously preserving kinase-specific regulatory characteristics essential for conditional modeling and prediction.

General phosphorylation site dataset

The general phosphorylation site dataset was derived from the PhosphoSitePlus database [3]. All serine (S), threonine (T), and tyrosine (Y) residues across annotated proteins were treated as candidate sites. Sequence boundaries were padded with the placeholder symbol “X” when necessary. This design aims to capture richer contextual information surrounding phosphorylation events, including longer-range sequence dependencies and potential structural signals [32].

Experimentally validated phosphorylation sites were treated as positive samples, while unannotated S/T/Y residues were used as negative candidate sites. To reduce redundancy and mitigate bias introduced by homologous sequences, CD-HIT clustering was applied with a sequence identity threshold of 90% [33]. Problem formulation and framework overview

In kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction, the task is formulated as a binary classification problem that distinguishes true kinase–substrate relationships from non-substrate candidate sites. This formulation preserves kinase specificity and avoids collapsing heterogeneous phosphorylation patterns across different kinase families.

A key characteristic of this problem is the highly uneven distribution of available data across kinase groups. While some kinases are well characterized with abundant experimentally validated substrates, many others remain severely underrepresented. This naturally results in a heterogeneous and highly imbalanced learning setting, where the number of positive samples varies dramatically across kinase groups.

Such data conditions impose significant challenges for standard discriminative models, particularly in low-data regimes where limited positive samples restrict the ability to learn robust and generalizable decision boundaries. These challenges motivate the need for learning strategies that can adapt to varying levels of data availability and effectively capture the underlying distribution of kinase–substrate relationships.

The objective of this study is to predict kinase-specific phosphorylation sites by determining whether a given residue within a substrate protein can be phosphorylated by a specific kinase.

Context-aware representation learning

To leverage shared structural patterns, the ESM-2 encoder is jointly fine-tuned on an aggregated training set containing samples from all kinase groups using masked language modeling (MLM) and SCL. By optimizing relative similarities rather than absolute class frequencies, the SCL objective ensures that even data-scarce kinases form compact, well-separated clusters in the embedding space, thereby significantly enhancing representation quality and downstream learning under severe class imbalance.

ESM-2-based sequence encoding

We employ ESM-2 (650 M parameters) as the backbone encoder for substrate peptide sequences. ESM-2 is a pretrained protein language model trained on millions of protein sequences using self-supervised objectives, enabling it to capture rich contextual information related to protein evolution and structure [37, 38].

Given an input peptide sequence centered at a candidate phosphorylation site, the sequence is first tokenized using the official ESM tokenizer and augmented with special tokens at the sequence boundaries. The tokenized sequence is then processed by the ESM-2 Transformer encoder to produce contextualized embeddings for each residue, along with a pooled sequence-level representation. In contrast to approaches that treat pretrained language models as frozen feature extractors, we fine-tune all ESM-2 parameters to adapt the representations to phosphorylation-related sequence patterns.

To preserve the generalization capability of the pretrained model while enhancing task relevance, we adopt a controlled fine-tuning strategy based on MLM [39, 40]. Supervised contrastive fine-tuning

While MLM enhances contextual understanding at the sequence level, it does not explicitly enforce discrimination between kinase-specific phosphorylation patterns. To address this limitation, we further introduce SCL to fine-tune the sequence-level embeddings [41].

This contrastive objective explicitly structures the embedding space according to kinase specificity, promoting intra-kinase compactness while significantly increasing inter-kinase separability.

Crucially, the ESM-2 encoder is not fine-tuned in isolation for each individual kinase group. Instead, the fine-tuning process (incorporating both MLM and SCL) is performed jointly across the entire aggregated training set comprising all available kinase groups. This joint training strategy is particularly vital for handling sequence differences under data-scarce conditions. By sharing encoder parameters across diverse kinase groups, underrepresented kinases can leverage the universal structural and physicochemical “grammar” of phosphorylation learned from data-rich groups, thereby preventing severe overfitting. Simultaneously, the SCL objective explicitly forces the model to capture the subtle sequence differences between various groups by pushing embeddings of different kinases apart. This ensures that the shared encoder does not collapse into a generic representation but strictly preserves inter-kinase separability, enabling the downstream diffusion model to capture distinct kinase-specific manifolds even when empirical samples are extremely limited.

Furthermore, this contrastive geometric regularization directly addresses the challenge of extreme data scarcity [41]. In standard cross-entropy-based training, rare kinase classes are frequently overwhelmed by majority classes, leading to collapsed or heavily skewed decision boundaries. SCL mitigates this fundamental issue by optimizing the representation space independently of absolute class frequencies. By forcing even a highly limited number of positive samples to form a tightly bound, well-separated cluster, SCL ensures that underrepresented kinases carve out a distinct and robust manifold in the high-dimensional space. This well-defined localized structure serves as a critical prerequisite for the subsequent generative stage: it provides the conditional diffusion model (KS-DiT) with a clear, localized target distribution to learn from. Consequently, the diffusion model can accurately sample from this structured latent space to generate biologically coherent synthetic samples, rather than producing out-of-distribution artifacts that would otherwise occur in a poorly clustered embedding space.

Conditional diffusion for task-aware data augmentation

Learning kinase-specific phosphorylation patterns is fundamentally constrained by the severe scarcity and imbalance of experimentally validated kinase–substrate relationships. For kinase groups with limited annotations, purely discriminative models are prone to overfitting dominant patterns and often fail to generalize, while heuristic data augmentation strategies based on random perturbations or oversampling may introduce biologically implausible samples. To address this challenge in a principled manner, we introduce a conditional diffusion-based generative module that performs task-aware data augmentation in representation space.

Rather than generating peptide sequences directly, the proposed module operates on context-aware embeddings learned by the upstream encoder, enabling stable training and direct alignment with the downstream prediction task. By explicitly conditioning generation on kinase-specific information, the model captures group-dependent substrate preferences and produces synthetic samples that are both diverse and biologically consistent.

Task-aware data augmentation strategy

The conditional diffusion model is employed to generate synthetic peptide embeddings for kinase groups with limited positive samples. Generated representations are combined with original training data to form an augmented dataset for the downstream kinase-specific predictor. Unlike conventional augmentation methods that apply uniform perturbations, KSDiffusion leverages conditional generation to ensure that augmented samples remain consistent with kinase-specific phosphorylation patterns.

Integration with kinase-specific prediction

The primary objective of KSDiffusion is to improve kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction under data-limited and highly imbalanced conditions. To achieve this goal, the proposed framework tightly integrates context-aware representation learning, conditional diffusion-based generation, and a downstream discriminative predictor into a unified learning pipeline.

For each substrate peptide–kinase pair, biologically meaningful embeddings are first produced by the ESM-2–based context-aware encoder. This shared embedding space serves as the common interface between the generative and discriminative components. The downstream predictor operates directly on these embeddings to estimate the probability that a given residue is phosphorylated by a specific kinase, ensuring semantic consistency between generation and prediction.

For kinase groups with limited training data, the conditional diffusion model is employed to generate additional synthetic embeddings conditioned on kinase-specific information. These generated samples are combined with the original training data to form an augmented dataset. Importantly, diffusion-generated samples are not treated as independent perturbations or noise-injected observations. Instead, they are explicitly constrained to follow the embedding distribution learned from real kinase–substrate relationships, thereby preserving task-relevant biological characteristics.

By jointly leveraging empirical and generated samples, the kinase-specific predictor is able to learn more robust decision boundaries for underrepresented kinase groups while maintaining discriminative performance for well-represented kinases. As data availability increases, the contribution of generated samples naturally diminishes, allowing the predictor to rely predominantly on real observations. This integrated design enables KSDiffusion to adapt seamlessly to varying data regimes without requiring separate models or heuristic switching strategies, effectively balancing generative support and discriminative learning.

Training strategy overview

KSDiffusion is trained using a staged strategy that coordinates representation learning, conditional generation, and downstream prediction.

First, the ESM-2 encoder is adapted using the General Phosphorylation Site Dataset (Table 1) to produce context-aware peptide embeddings. The diffusion backbone is pretrained on the same dataset to capture global phosphoproteomic distributions.

Second, the pretrained diffusion model is fine-tuned in a kinase-conditioned manner (KS-DiT) using the Kinase-specific Phosphorylation Site Dataset (Table 2), enabling it to model kinase-dependent substrate distributions.

Finally, a kinase-specific predictor is trained using both original samples from the Kinase-specific Phosphorylation Site Dataset and diffusion-generated synthetic samples to learn discriminative decision boundaries.

These stages are tightly coupled: the encoder provides structured representations for diffusion modeling, the diffusion module expands the effective support of kinase-specific substrates, and the classifier leverages both to improve generalization under varying data regimes. A detailed description of data usage, training procedures, and implementation settings is provided in the Experimental setup section.

Two datasets are used in this study with distinct roles across different stages: a General Phosphorylation Site Dataset (Table 1) and a Kinase-specific Phosphorylation Site Dataset (Table 2), as described in the Dataset construction and problem setting section.

The General Phosphorylation Site Dataset is used for encoder adaptation and diffusion pretraining. Importantly, it is constructed by explicitly excluding all protein families associated with the kinase-specific dataset, ensuring strict separation between pretraining data and downstream evaluation.

Impact of core components in low-data regimes

In low-data regimes, where both data scarcity and class imbalance are most severe, traditional non-generative strategies show limited effectiveness (Table 4). Compared with the class-weighted BCE baseline (AUC = 0.6996), applying Focal Loss and SMOTE directly to the ESM-2 embedding space leads to severe performance degradation, yielding AUC scores of 0.6108 and 0.6376, respectively.

This suggests that, under extreme data scarcity, cost-sensitive reweighting may lead to overfitting on a small number of positive samples, while linear interpolation in SMOTE may not adequately capture the complex structure of the high-dimensional ESM-2 embedding space. In contrast, the conditional diffusion module provides a more flexible way to enrich the support of the positive class distribution, leading to improved performance.

Crucially, removing adversarial fine-tuning (w/o Adversarial) yields the most substantial drop among the internal modules (AUC decreases to 0.7234). This validates our hypothesis that unconstrained diffusion may generate mathematically valid but biologically implausible sequences; adversarial guidance is indispensable for enforcing physical constraints and biological fidelity. Finally, ablating the dynamic screening mechanism (w/o Sample selection) degrades the AUC to 0.7419, indicating that without explicit quality and diversity filters, the inclusion of noisy or redundant synthetic samples can easily corrupt the delicate decision boundaries in low-data regimes.

Component contributions in medium-data regimes

In the medium-data regime, the dynamics of component contributions undergo a discernible shift (Table 4). Interestingly, the standard class-weighted BCE baseline achieves a highly competitive AUC of 0.8994, indicating that a moderate number of empirical samples provides a reasonable foundation for discrimination. However, forcefully applying Focal Loss or SMOTE to this space leads to drastic performance deterioration, plunging the AUC to 0.7698 and 0.7444.

This observation indicates that, even when data availability improves, simple reweighting or linear interpolation strategies may still be insufficient to model the underlying data distribution. Instead, maintaining the quality and consistency of representations—through adversarial fine-tuning and dynamic sample selection—becomes increasingly important.

Consequently, as empirical data availability improves, the primary role of the internal architectural modules shifts from basic distribution support to strict generative quality assurance. As demonstrated in Table 4, removing adversarial fine-tuning (w/o Adversarial) or dynamic screening (w/o Sample selection) causes the AUC to regress to 0.8577 and 0.8624, respectively—dropping significantly below the non-generative BCE baseline. This emphatically proves that without rigorous adversarial physical constraints and diversity-aware filtering, unconstrained generative augmentation risks injecting out-of-distribution noise that corrupts the already established decision boundaries. Thus, these internal modules are paramount for ensuring that synthetic samples remain strictly beneficial and biologically coherent.

Ablation results in large-data regimes

In the large-data regime, where empirical training samples are relatively abundant, the standard class-weighted BCE baseline naturally establishes a robust discriminative performance (AUC = 0.8527, Table 4). Replacing the diffusion module with Focal Loss or SMOTE results in lower AUC values (0.8287 and 0.8294) compared with the full model (0.8757).

This suggests that even in data-rich scenarios, naive reweighting or interpolation may introduce biases that are not well aligned with the complex structure of protein representations. In contrast, the diffusion-based approach remains stable and continues to provide performance gains without degrading model reliability.

Crucially, the ablation of internal modules further emphasizes that generative augmentation in large-data scenarios must be strictly regularized. The Frozen Encoder variant suffers a severe performance drop (AUC = 0.8115), reiterating that generic protein language representations must be fine-tuned for kinase-specific tasks regardless of the available data volume. Furthermore, removing adversarial fine-tuning (w/o Adversarial, AUC = 0.8221) or dynamic screening (w/o Sample selection, AUC = 0.8356) causes the performance to fall considerably below the non-generative BCE baseline. This highlights a profound conclusion: when original data are abundant, injecting unconstrained or noisy synthetic samples actively dilutes the high-quality empirical data. Only through rigorous biological constraints and diversity screening can generative augmentation safely surpass a strong large-data baseline. Interestingly, the removal of meta-learning (w/o Meta-learning, AUC = 0.8515) results in a noticeably milder performance drop compared with its impact in the low-data regime. This observation perfectly aligns with the theoretical foundation of meta-learning, which acts as a few-shot initialization technique and naturally exhibits diminishing returns as empirical data becomes sufficiently abundant.

Summary of ablation findings

Across all evaluated data regimes, replacing the conditional diffusion module with traditional non-generative imbalance-handling strategies (Focal Loss or SMOTE) consistently results in inferior performance (Table 4). These extensive comparisons conclusively demonstrate that in the highly non-linear, high-dimensional ESM-2 embedding space, naive cost-sensitive reweighting or linear interpolation fails to capture the true topological distribution of protein substrates. In contrast, diffusion-based augmentation provides a mathematically principled and flexible mechanism for modeling complex structural manifolds, successfully enriching the minority class support without distorting the established decision boundaries.

Crucially, the ablation study further reveals that generative augmentation alone is not a universal panacea; its success is strictly predicated on the rigorous internal architectural safeguards embedded within KSDiffusion. The consistent performance degradation observed upon removing task-aware encoder fine-tuning, meta-learning initialization, adversarial fine-tuning, or dynamic sample selection proves that these components are highly indispensable. Together, they form a robust quality assurance pipeline: meta-learning enables rapid adaptation under extreme scarcity, while adversarial guidance and explicit diversity screening prevent the injection of biologically implausible or topologically destructive noise. Ultimately, it is this precise synergy between manifold-aware generative augmentation and strict biological regularization that empowers KSDiffusion to achieve robust and state-of-the-art performance across diverse data environments.

These experiments aim to provide a more fine-grained empirical understanding of when and how diffusion-based augmentation contributes to kinase–substrate relationship prediction.

Discussion

As demonstrated in section Experiments and result, the KSDiffusion framework consistently improves kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction, especially under data-limited conditions. Beyond merely reporting performance gains, we must explore the underlying reasons for these behaviors. Furthermore, it is crucial to identify when diffusion-based augmentation is most effective and to consider the broader implications of applying generative models in biological sequence analysis. Therefore, this section synthesizes our empirical findings to discuss these aspects, rather than introducing new experimental evidence.

Representation-level interpretation and structural validation

Representation-level interpretation

KSDiffusion operates entirely in the continuous ESM-2 embedding space, where synthetic samples are generated as latent representations rather than discrete sequences. From a biological and modeling perspective, a diffusion-generated “positive sample” does not correspond to a single realizable peptide sequence. Instead, it represents a distribution-level embedding that captures shared biochemical and contextual characteristics of kinase-specific substrates. Because ESM-2 embeddings encode high-order dependencies, including evolutionary constraints and physicochemical properties, each generated embedding can be viewed as a plausible point within the latent substrate space associated with a given kinase. In this sense, the diffusion model approximates a functional region of substrate representations, rather than generating discrete molecular instances.

This design reflects the primary objective of KSDiffusion: to enhance discriminative learning under data scarcity by enriching the representation space with kinase-consistent samples. Mapping these embeddings back to explicit peptide sequences is non-trivial and is not required for the downstream prediction task, representing a trade-off between representation flexibility and sequence-level interpretability.

Limitations and scope

Operating purely in embedding space imposes important limitations. In particular, the model does not directly generate peptide sequences and therefore cannot support sequence-level validation or experimental design. Consequently, the utility of KSDiffusion is primarily confined to improving predictive inference, rather than enabling de novo substrate design. Future work may explore hybrid approaches that integrate embedding-level generation with sequence reconstruction to enhance biological interpretability and practical applicability. In addition, while the current study focuses on kinase-specific phosphorylation, extending this embedding-level generative framework to other PTMs remains an important direction for future investigation.

Why diffusion-based augmentation is most effective under data scarcity

Our empirical results reveal a clear trend: diffusion-based augmentation substantially improves performance in low- and medium-data regimes. However, this marginal benefit naturally decreases as real data becomes abundant. This behavior stems from the interaction between data diversity, distributional coverage, and model capacity.

In low-data settings, kinase-specific phosphorylation patterns are sparsely sampled. This sparsity leads to incomplete coverage of the underlying sequence-function space. The conditional diffusion model alleviates this limitation by generating synthetic samples that interpolate between observed phosphorylation sites. Crucially, these samples remain constrained by kinase-specific conditioning. As a result, the classifier learns a smoother, more continuous decision boundary, which significantly improves generalization. However, if the proportion of generated samples becomes too large, the limited real data can no longer sufficiently anchor the training distribution. This destabilization explains the performance degradation observed at higher augmentation ratios.

In medium-data regimes, abundant real samples provide stronger distributional constraints. Here, diffusion-generated samples act primarily as diversity enhancers rather than distributional substitutes. Under these conditions, moderate augmentation ratios enrich the training set with biologically plausible variations. They preserve the core characteristics of the original data, leading to stable and consistent improvements across evaluation metrics.

By contrast, in large-data regimes, the original dataset already offers broad and representative coverage of kinase-specific phosphorylation patterns. Consequently, the additional information provided by synthetic samples becomes redundant. The role of diffusion-based augmentation thereby shifts from performance enhancement to regularization. This shift explains why performance gains become marginal yet remain stable, avoiding overfitting or degradation.

Architectural independence and robustness of the conditional diffusion module

Our experiments also reveal the strong architectural independence of the proposed conditional diffusion module. As demonstrated in section Cross-model generalization of the conditional diffusion module, diffusion-generated samples consistently improve or preserve predictive performance, even when integrated into alternative backbone architectures.

This behavior suggests that the diffusion module primarily captures data-level regularities rather than model-specific inductive biases. Operating on protein sequence embeddings and conditioning on kinase identity allows the generator to learn transferable representations. Various downstream classifiers can then leverage these captured kinase–substrate relationships. Consequently, the benefits of diffusion-based augmentation extend beyond the KS-DiT architecture to other sequence modeling frameworks.

Furthermore, the absence of performance degradation in data-rich regimes highlights the approach’s robustness. Even when synthetic samples contribute marginally, the diffusion module introduces no instability. This indicates it can serve safely as a plug-and-play augmentation component without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the conditional diffusion module serves as an effective augmentation mechanism for tasks characterized by heterogeneous data availability. Its architectural independence allows it to be readily integrated into different prediction pipelines, particularly in scenarios where data scarcity varies across functional groups.

Implications and practical guidelines

Our analyses yield several practical guidelines. First, diffusion-based augmentation is most beneficial under data scarcity, where limited training samples constrain model generalization. In these scenarios, moderate augmentation ratios maximize performance gains while avoiding distributional bias.

Second, the conditional diffusion module readily integrates into existing kinase–substrate prediction pipelines without architectural modifications. Serving as a flexible augmentation component rather than a task-specific redesign, this flexibility lowers the barrier to adoption and facilitates application across diverse predictive frameworks.

Finally, our findings highlight that generative augmentation is a data-regime-aware strategy, not a universally optimal solution. Although diffusion models powerfully enrich sparse biological datasets, their contribution remains inherently bounded by the quality and quantity of available empirical data. Recognizing these boundaries is essential for the responsible and effective use of generative models in computational biology.

Conclusion

This study addresses the persistent challenge of data scarcity in kinase-specific phosphorylation site prediction by proposing KSDiffusion, a unified framework that integrates protein language representations with conditional diffusion-based data augmentation. By leveraging contextual sequence embeddings from ESM-2 and a conditional generative mechanism, the proposed approach effectively enhances model learning in regimes where kinase-specific annotations are limited and highly imbalanced.

Comprehensive experiments across kinase groups with varying data scales demonstrate that KSDiffusion consistently achieves competitive or superior performance compared with representative baseline methods. The performance gains are most pronounced in low-data scenarios, where diffusion-based augmentation substantially improves generalization, while remaining stable and non-degradative in data-rich regimes. These results highlight a clear data-scale-dependent behavior, indicating that conditional diffusion serves as a targeted and effective strategy for alleviating sample scarcity rather than a uniform substitute for large empirical datasets.

While the current framework is developed for kinase–substrate relationship prediction, the architectural independence of the generative module enables it to be naturally extended as a plug-and-play augmentation component for other data-scarce biological prediction tasks. Future work will focus on exploring its application to diverse PTMs, such as ubiquitination and methylation, as well as incorporating structural and dynamic interaction information to further enhance biological fidelity.
