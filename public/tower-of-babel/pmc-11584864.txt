Attentional capacity matters for visuomotor adaptation to a virtual reality driving simulator
Authors: Régis Lobjois, Sami Mecheri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies have shown that adaptation to a virtual reality driving simulator takes time and that individuals differ widely in the time they need to adapt. The present study examined the relationship between attentional capacity and driving-simulator adaptation, with the hypothesis that individuals with better attentional capacity would exhibit more efficient adaptation to novel virtual driving circumstances. To this end, participants were asked to steer in a driving simulator through a series of 100 bends while keeping within a central demarcated zone. Adaptation was assessed from changes in steering behavior (steering performance: time spent within the zone, steering stability, steering reversal rate) over the course of the bends. Attentional capacity was assessed with two dynamic visual attention tasks (Multiple Object Tracking, MOT; Multiple Object Avoidance, MOA). Results showed effective adaptation to the simulator with repetition, as all steering-behavior variables improved. Both MOT and MOA scores significantly predicted adaptation, with MOT being a stronger predictor. Further analyses revealed that higher-capacity participants, but not their lower-capacity counterparts, produced more low-amplitude steering-wheel corrections early in the task, resulting in finer vehicle control and better performance later on. These findings provide new insights into adaptation to virtual reality simulators through the lens of attentional capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79392-1.Keywords: Adaptation, Steering task, Driving simulator, Mental effort, Attention, Multiple object tracking and avoidance taskSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour, Attention, Perception

Introduction

The human perceptual-motor system must adapt during interactions with novel environmental conditions by adjusting existing skills to new circumstances. This adaptation is necessary to maintain performance, and it involves reshaping acquired correspondences between environmental information and motor actions1. In high-speed locomotion, even minor changes in task conditions, such as driving a new vehicle, can lead to a significant discrepancy between actual and desired performance2. This discrepancy may be particularly pronounced when driving a simulator for the first time, where the sensory information differs from that available in the real world3, and where cognitive resources might be needed for visuomotor adaptation4. Notably, a comparison between adaptation to a new real vehicle and a simulator revealed that perceived ease of driving and perceived driving quality were lower in the simulator, along with higher mental workload5. Additionally, simulator participants did not feel they were reaching their usual driving standards, unlike those using a real vehicle5. This highlights the challenging and effortful nature of adapting to novel virtual driving circumstances. To gain insight into this issue, the present study focused on the unexplored and potentially beneficial role of attentional capacity during adaptation to a driving simulator.

Driving-simulator adaptation has been defined as the period of time during which drivers transfer their driving skills to the simulator in order to control it effectively6. Differences have been found in the time needed to adapt, between both studies and individuals. For example, a minimum of 240 s was shown to adapt steering behavior in2. Another study found a mean time of 457 s, with three participants adapting in less than 300 s and four requiring more than 600 s7. Recently, a mean time of 211 s was found, with a range between 5 and 963 s6. Several factors may explain the between-studies differences. First, the driving environment was shown to influence adaptation time, with more time needed to adapt in demanding environments such as winding roads8. The indicators used to assess adaptation are also critical, as some are more sensitive to adaptation than others, such as steering-control measures2,8. The experimental scenario itself also influences adaptation, as participants with previous simulator experience have significantly shorter adaptation times than those without7,9. However, explaining the inter-individual differences in adaptation time is harder. No significant relationship was found between age and adaptation time2,10, although older drivers remained more variable than younger ones2. A moderate correlation between age and adaptation time has nevertheless been shown, with younger participants adapting more quickly9. Regarding driving experience, the same pattern of results was found, with less experienced participants adapting more quickly9. In another study, on the contrary, driving experience did not explain adaptation time6. Last, it was shown that gender has no influence on adaptation time9. Taken together, these results do not provide conclusive explanations for the observed individual differences in adaptation time. However, considering the emphasis in the aforementioned studies that adaptation requires mental effort on the part of the driver2,7–9, another avenue worth investigating is the role of attentional resource capacities in accounting for these differences.

While “just driving” on real roads remains a demanding task11, several studies have demonstrated that driving a simulator demands more mental effort than real-world driving [5,12,13,14 but see 15,16]. This can be attributed, at least in part, to the cognitive resources allocated toward transferring the previously acquired driving skills to the simulator. Indeed, when physiological strain was monitored as a measure of mental workload during adaptation, heart rate variability increased8. This aligns with the idea that participants become more at ease and experience a decrease in mental workload with driving time. Similarly, a decrease in mental workload17 or steering effort18 was found with time-on-task. Collectively, these studies indicate that mental workload is high during early exposure to a driving simulator but decreases with adaptation, a finding already shown in (non-driving) immersive virtual environments4. With experience, vehicle control, which belongs to the operational level of the driving task19, becomes automatic and less attentionally demanding. Consequently, when adapting to a driving simulator, attentional resources probably need to be reallocated to the operational control of the vehicle that is usually automated. Building upon this assumption—and given that individuals vary in their ability to adapt—attentional capacity might facilitate the adaptation process. Individuals with better attentional capacities may thus better handle the increased demands induced by simulated driving, leading to more efficient adaptation. To our knowledge, only one study has examined the role of cognitive functions in adaptation, showing a moderate but significant relationship between adaptation time and mental abilities10.

A large body of literature deals with the prediction of driver behavior on the basis of cognitive or perceptual-cognitive tests, the Useful Field of View (UFOV) being widely used20,21. More recently, the Multiple Object Tracking (MOT) paradigm has been employed to assess attentional capacities as a predictor of driver behavior22. The MOT task involves tracking and attending to multiple moving objects among distractors. MOT performance, which relies on several dimensions of attention (selective, sustained and divided23) directed toward dynamic stimuli, has been assumed to reflect driving demands more accurately than UFOV24,25. More broadly, the MOT task is also used to measure attentional capacity26,27. Several studies have demonstrated that MOT scores are predictive of individual differences in driving behavior24,25,28. A significant correlation was found between MOT scores and driving metrics in a moderate mental workload scenario, with participants having lower MOT scores exhibiting higher crash rates, slower speeds, and more lateral position variability25. Likewise, individuals with higher MOT scores had higher driving performance, as assessed by driving errors24. This study additionally investigated whether a Multiple Object Avoidance (MOA) task can predict driving errors. The MOA task involves focusing attention on several surrounding moving objects to avoid a collision between a manually controlled object and the surrounding objects, thereby sharing a visuomotor component with the driving task. MOA scores showed significant correlations with driving performance, similar to MOT scores. MOA scores also correlated with measures of eye movements (wider horizontal spread of visual attention, faster and larger saccades), indicating more effective attentional strategies within the two more complex driving environments, in contrast to MOT scores. These findings thus suggest that individuals with better MOA scores had more attentional resources available to visually scan the road as driving demands increased24. Overall, these studies highlight the usefulness of MOT and MOA scores for both predicting driving performance and distinguishing individuals based on their attentional capacity for coping with increased driving demands.

The present study investigated the role of attentional capacity in adapting to a virtual reality driving simulator, with the hypothesis that individuals with better attentional capacity would exhibit more efficient adaptation to novel virtual driving circumstances. To this end, participants’ driving behavior was examined in a steering task that emphasizes selecting and correcting lateral position, the most prominent challenge for drivers using a simulator for the first time2. Participants were required to drive through 100 right-hand bends presented as a sequence of individual trials bends while keeping within a central zone 0.25 m wide (Fig. 1). The first 50 m of each trial consisted of a straight road followed by a 200-m bend. Steering-behavior variables were computed for each bend segment (the straight-line approach was excluded) and averaged over blocks of 10 trials. Driving-simulator adaptation was assessed by the difference in steering performance (time spent within the central zone along the bend) between initial and final blocks of trials. Building upon previous studies24,25, individual attentional capacities were assessed using MOT and MOA tasks (Fig. 1).

Experimental set-up and tasks. The top view illustrates the road situation as seen by the participant. The participant had to control the trajectory of his/her vehicle in a 200 m long bend, with the instruction to keep their midline within a 0.25 m wide demarcated zone as much as possible. The driving speed was held constant (90 km/hr) by the simulation software so that speed reduction could not be used to alleviate the difficulty of the steering task7. Participants drove on the right side of the road. Steering behavior was analyzed through Steering performance (log-transformed percentage of time spent within the zone), Steering stability (standard deviation of lateral position in cm) and Steering reversal rate (number of steering-wheel direction changes per minute; SRR) along the bend. Each of these variables was averaged in blocks of 10 trials. The bottom view shows a MOT trial (left) and a MOA trial (right) from the participant’s point of view. The MOT task consisted of visually tracking four targets among a set of eight distractors. Once the objects stopped (after 10 s of tracking while all objects were the same color), participants had to indicate which objects were the four targets. Performance was measured as the percentage (log-transformed) of correct detections (20 trials). The MOA task consisted of controlling an object (blue) with the mouse in such a way as to avoid colliding with other objects (red) whose number increased progressively every 14 s. Performance was measured as the time elapsed (in seconds) until collision (10 trials).

The first hypothesis predicted that participants’ steering behavior would improve with adaptation, while their mental effort (assessed with the Rating Scale Mental Effort: RSME) would decrease. Second, it was hypothesized that MOT and MOA scores would predict simulator adaptation, as individuals with higher scores were expected to demonstrate better attentional capacities24,25, thus facilitating adaptation processes. Following previous results24, it was also expected that MOA scores would be a better predictor of adaptation given that both the MOA task and the steering task involve deploying one’s visual attention in order to control actions. The third hypothesis concerned the relationship between steering performance and attentional capacity over the course of adaptation. Given that discrepancy between actual and desired performance should be greater initially, it was hypothesized that this relationship would be more pronounced in the early stage of adaptation.

Steering behavior

The repeated-measures ANOVA on the mean log-transformed steering performance revealed a significant main effect of block number (F5.26,273.71 = 20.3, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.28). Steering performance was lower in Blocks 1, 2 and 3 compared to Blocks 2 to 10, 4 to 10 and 6 to 10, respectively. It was also significantly lower in Blocks 4 to 7 than in the last block. Block number also had a main effect on steering stability (F3.13,162.62 = 63.4, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.55), which improved between Block 1 and Blocks 2 to 10, between Blocks 2 and 3 and Blocks 4 to 10, and between Blocks 4 and 5 and Blocks 8 to 10. Steering in the last block was also more stable than in Blocks 6 and 7. A significant effect of block number was also found on SRR (F4.12,240.14 = 12.0, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.19), which was significantly higher in Block 1 than in all other blocks and in Block 2 compared to Blocks 3 to 10.

Mental effort

The repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of block number on RSME scores (F3.56,185.30 = 5.49, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.10), with higher mental effort in Block 1 than in all other blocks.

Relationship between driving-simulator adaptation and attentional capacity

Simple linear regressions revealed that both MOT and MOA scores significantly predicted simulator adaptation (log-transformed percentage of the difference between initial and final blocks of trials). The higher the MOT (F1,51 = 11.1, p < .001, r = .42) and MOA (F1,51 = 7.33, p < .01, r = .35) scores, the higher the adaptation (Fig. 3). A multiple linear regression was also conducted to determine which task best predicted adaptation. While the overall model was significant (F2,50 = 6.85, p = .002; R2 = 0.22), MOT scores were significantly correlated with adaptation (b = 0.93, β = 0.33, t(52) = 2.39, p = .02), whereas MOA scores were not (b = 0.02, β = 0.21, t(52) = 1.52, p = .13).

As a control step, we also examined whether adaptation was related to age, sex, driving license seniority, and driving experience (number of kilometers). The overall model of the multiple linear regression was not significant (F4,48 = 2.40, p > .06; R2 = 0.14; Age: β = 0.16, t = 0.51 p = .61; Sex: β = 0.67, t = 1.14 p = .26; License seniority: β = 0.17, t = 0.43, p = .67; Driving experience: β = 0.01, t = 0.29, p = .77).

As shown in Table 2, the interaction between MOT scores and block number significantly predicted steering performance, steering stability and SRR, indicating that the relationship between each steering-behavior variable and MOT changed longitudinally. Post-hoc power values (1-β error probability) for these three analyses were 0.96, 0.99, and 0.34, respectively. For steering performance, the results revealed that a 1-unit increase in MOT score in the first block decreased steering performance by − 0.12 units, while in the last block, a 1-unit increase in MOT score increased steering performance by 1.06 units. This made a 1.18-unit difference in the MOT effect between the last and first block (Table 3). Estimations revealed that all slopes increased but only those in Blocks 4 and 6 to 10 were significantly higher than that in the first block. This shows that steering performance was not associated with MOT score in the early blocks, but its effect on steering performance was stronger from Block 4. Also, this analysis revealed that the intercepts did not differ from one another depending on the block. This suggests that the performance of participants with the lowest MOT scores did not change significantly with block number (Fig. 4). For steering stability, the results revealed that a 1-unit increase in MOT score predicted a significant change of -1.07 in Block 1, indicating that participants with higher MOT scores swerved less from the very beginning of the task. This relation was the same for all blocks since no estimated difference in slopes was significant. However, estimated differences in intercepts were all significant, indicating that all participants, regardless of their MOT score, were increasingly stable with every new block (Fig. 4). For SRR, the results revealed that a 1-unit increase in MOT score predicted a significant change of 8.03 units in Block 1, showing that participants with higher MOT scores made more steering-wheel corrections from the very beginning of the task. Although slopes decreased with the block, the effect of having a higher MOT score on SRR was statistically the same longitudinally. Estimated differences in intercepts were not significant, indicating that participants with the lowest MOT scores produced similar SRR across all blocks.

Regarding MOA, overall models revealed that the interaction between MOA scores and block number significantly predicted changes in steering performance, steering stability and SRR, indicating that the relationship between each steering-behavior variable and MOA changed longitudinally (Table 2). Post-hoc power values (1-β error probability) for these three analyses were 0.85, 0.99, and 0.65, respectively. For steering performance, MOA did not significantly predict performance in Block 1, and this relationship remained similar until Block 10, where the effect of MOA on steering performance was significant. No estimated difference in intercepts was significant, suggesting that participants with the lowest MOA scores had similar steering performance across all blocks (Fig. 4). For steering stability, MOA scores did not significantly predict stability in Block 1, and this relationship remained the same across all blocks. In contrast, estimated differences in intercepts were significant for Blocks 4 to 10, suggesting that all participants became increasingly more stable from Block 4, regardless of their MOA score. For SRR, participants with higher MOA scores made more steering-wheel corrections from Block 1, and this relationship was the same regardless of block number. Estimated differences in intercepts were not significant since participants with the lowest MOA scores had similar SRR regardless of block number.

Discussion

The present study investigated the potential role of attentional capacity in driving-simulator adaptation. The results showed effective adaptation to the simulator, as steering performance (i.e., time spent within the central zone) improved over the task, along with improvements in steering stability and SRR. Both MOT and MOA scores significantly predicted individual differences in simulator adaptation, with MOT being a stronger predictor. Analysis of the block-by-block changes in the relationship between attentional capacity and steering behavior revealed that performance was increasingly associated with MOT scores over the course of adaptation and that MOA scores significantly predicted steering performance in the final block. Notably, participants with low attentional capacities (assessed by both MOT and MOA tasks) had similar steering performances across blocks. These findings are discussed below.

As expected, and consistent with previous studies6,10, results revealed that participants adapted to the novel and virtual circumstances associated with driving a simulator for the first time. Steering performance and steering stability improved throughout the task, reaching a peak in the final block. In addition, participants made more steering-wheel corrections in the first two blocks, highlighting a bang-bang steering control early in the task before maintaining a lower rate of corrections thereafter. Since mental effort results closely paralleled SRR results (RSME was higher in Block 1 compared to all subsequent blocks), one can argue that the participants drew on more attentional resources while they sought to control their steering. Beyond this point, and although steering performance and stability continued to improve, they reported a low and consistent level of mental effort (a RSME score of 30 indicates little to some effort). Overall, these results suggest a two-stage mechanism in adapting to a driving simulator, where participants first made a mental effort to better control their steering actions, before seeking to be more precise and stable. These results align with earlier work examining adaptation in perceptual-motor tasks29–31. More recently, a number of studies have examined adaptation to visuomotor latency in a virtual steering task32 and visuomotor adaptation to spatial offset in a virtual pointing task4,33. These studies proposed that both a cognitive strategy and a perceptual-motor realignment are involved in users’ adaptation to new steering conditions32 and that error reduction in the early stages of adaptation is supported by cognitively demanding strategies4,33.

In line with our hypothesis, participants’ attentional capacity, as assessed by MOT and MOA scores, was a significant predictor of individual differences in simulator adaptation (MOT: r(51) = 0.42, p < .001; MOA: r(51) = 0.35, p < .01). This supports the notion that the operational control of the vehicle, which is usually automated19, requires additional resources during adaptation, thereby enabling individuals with better attentional capacity to more efficiently cope with increased demands. To our knowledge, only one study has investigated the relationship between attentional functions and driving-simulator adaptation; using pencil-and-paper tests involving selective attention (attention and concentration, cognitive flexibility, inferential thinking), the study showed that attention scores were weakly correlated (r = .22) with adaptation time10. Our findings, indicating a stronger association, support the idea that dynamic visual attention tests involving multiple dimensions of attention (selective, sustained and divided) are superior predictors of driving performance compared to tests using static stimuli24,25,34 or targeting specific dimensions of attention10. Furthermore, our results contribute to the growing evidence that MOT scores can effectively predict performance in complex and dynamic environments, as shown recently in other performance domains35–37.

Following24, it was also expected that MOA would be better than MOT at predicting adaptation, as it shares a visuomotor component with driving. This was not confirmed, as MOT scores were a better predictor of adaptation than MOA scores. One possible explanation for this result may lie in task requirements. In24, the driving demands were manipulated by various driving conditions that required constant monitoring of potentially hazardous interactions to avoid collisions, similar to MOA. In our steering task, although participants had to monitor their instantaneous lateral position to initiate corrective actions, this was not motivated by the avoidance of impending collision with surrounding moving objects24. also reported that MOA scores significantly predicted horizontal dispersion of fixations and saccade velocity during driving, synonymous with more efficient scanning behavior. Although eye movements were not studied in an MOA task, the correlation between MOA and behavioral indicators was explained by common requirements between the two tasks in terms of goal-directed deployment of visual attention. Contrastingly, several studies examining gaze behavior in a MOT task reported that the most effective visual strategy is the center-looking strategy rather than fixating each target individually38–40. Center-looking strategy involves looking at the centroid of a virtual polygon formed by the individual targets while maintaining multi-focal attention on each of them38. This strategy makes sense in the context of our steering task since the visual control of steering in bends relies on two distinct visual processes41,42, a compensatory control that relies on peripheral vision encompassing the edge lines relatively close to the vehicle43 and an anticipatory control fed by distant gaze fixations on the trajectory ahead of the vehicle44,45. The finding that MOT scores predict adaptation better than MOA scores in our study may thus suggest that MOT and steering rely on similar visuo-attentional processes: fixating to the centroid and using peripheral vision to track targets (in MOT), and fixating to a far point and using peripheral vision to monitor in-lane position (in driving).

Multiple linear regression analyses revealed that attentional capacity did not predict steering performance across the first three blocks of trials, regardless of whether MOT or MOA was used as predictor. However, significant relationships were found between attentional capacity and steering stability (with MOT as a predictor) and SRR (with both MOT and MOA as predictors) from Block 1. These relationships highlighted that lower-capacity participants performed corrective actions of high amplitude since they made fewer reversals for more lateral position variability. This pattern remained unchanged over the entire task, as did their steering performance. Participants with higher MOT/MOA scores, on the other hand, initially performed corrective actions of lesser amplitude as they made more reversals for less variability than their lower-capacity counterparts, before gaining in steering precision and stability. The lack of association between attentional capacity and steering performance early in the task could thus be explained by higher-capacity participants seeking primarily to refine their steering-wheel actions during this period.

The finding that higher-capacity participants made more small amplitude steering-wheel corrections from Block 1 may reflect that these participants were exploring how the steering-wheel force feedback was set up in order to establish new relationships between available sensory cues and motor actions1,29. Arguably, the higher production of reversals early in the task aimed to better specify the state of the relationship between a particular steering input and its output, enhancing the subsequent transfer of driving skills to the simulator in these participants46, as suggested by the increase in the effect of MOT on steering performance from Block 4. Therefore, one can contend that participants with better attentional capacities invested their greater resources into more exploratory activity or, at least, into a greater production of steering-wheel corrections that resulted in greater exploration as a by-product. As outlined above, these results support the view that adaptation involved transitioning from a bang-bang style to finer vehicle control. This transition was probably facilitated by an initial exploration period that only participants with better attentional capacity were capable of.

Beyond this initial period, the results also revealed that MOT scores predicted steering performance from Block 4. One possible explanation for this result may lie in the visual control of steering. When driving in bend, it is well established that not only does trajectory follow gaze directions47–49, but also that the way gaze is distributed is influenced by the demands placed on steering control. In particular, it has been shown that an increase in mental workload50, driving inexperience51, or even the simple fact of actively controlling steering52 modifies the way drivers look at the road ahead to plan their trajectory. After exploring the steering-wheel force feedback to tune their steering responses, higher-capacity participants may change the way they gather visual information to improve steering precision and performance. This refined search for more relevant information would take place gradually (since steering performance was increasingly associated with MOT scores from block 4) and may reflect processes underlying perceptual-motor realignment32.

To sum up, the present study is the first to examine visuomotor adaptation to a virtual reality driving simulator using a longitudinal design and a strong experimental control over the steering task. While the cognitive cost of visuomotor adaptation in virtual reality has already been demonstrated4, this study is also among the first to examine the role of attentional resource capacities in adapting to a virtual reality device (see10). In line with previous studies showing a two-stage mechanism29,43, the main results revealed that adaptation occurred after an initial exploration period—which only higher-capacity participants were capable of—consisting of a higher rate of steering-wheel corrections, probably to better specify the state of the relationship between steering inputs and their visual consequences. This may have enhanced the subsequent perceptual-motor realignment that permitted better steering performance and stability later in the task. There are, however, a number of limitations that warrant consideration. While MOT/MOA tasks have previously been shown to predict performance in dynamic and complex environments24,25,34–37, the present study revealed significant but weak correlations between MOT/MOA and adaptation. This weakness probably reflects the challenging issue of predicting performance in a multi-component activity such as driving from a single indicator integrating different dimensions of attention. A step forward would be to explore the link between underlying processes such as eye movements and MOT/MOA scores, especially as gaze behavior is a key determinant of steering control42,45. Additionally, post hoc power analysis unveiled that the attained statistical power for assessing the relationship between attentional capacity and steering reversal rate along adaptation was insufficient, limiting the statistical rigor of the findings. Future studies should include a larger sample to enable more confident statistical analysis of the relationship between attentional capacity and steering behavior over time in participants with no prior driving simulator experience. Furthermore, we used the number of targets that can be tracked to measure MOT performance, although other approaches based on speed limits as part of adaptive procedures (i.e., correctly tracking all objects, speed increased; errors made, speed decreased) can be used23. As adaptive MOT may better account for inter-individual differences in tracking ability53, it would be worthwhile to test the predictive value of MOT for driving-simulator adaptation based on speed thresholds to consolidate the present results. Another area of concern is the consequences of maintaining a constant speed, since it has been shown that reducing speed is a strategy for making the steering task less difficult7,54 and that low speeds may accelerate adaptation54. Nevertheless, participants were found to adapt in the same way whether the speed was free or imposed32. Lastly, while these results highlight adaptation at the visuomotor level, adaptation may also operate at (non-visual) perceptual and sensory levels. Although speed was constant, adjusting to the sensation of speed can be a factor, given that speed estimation or production is challenging55 and that the effects on vehicle heading of any steering action are speed-dependent. Adaptation may also have occurred at the sensory level due to the absence of vestibular information and the need to reweight it against other sources of information to better control action4. To conclude, future research should therefore consider inter-individual differences in visuomotor adaptation to driving simulators, and more broadly to virtual reality simulators, as well as how individual attentional capacities interact with the perceptual-motor realignment underlying adaptation. This is critical, since it may influence the reliability of the data collected in virtual environments, which are increasingly analyzed as a result of the growing use of virtual reality simulators in behavioral research and training.

Method

Participants

To be included in the study, participants had to be under 31 years old, have held a driving license for at least 3 years, have driven at least 10,000 km, and have never driven a simulator. This allowed for controlling potential effects of age and driving experience on driving-simulator adaptation. Fifty-three drivers (17 females), with normal or corrected-to-normal vision, took part in the study. They had a mean age of 22.8 years (SD = 2.3) and had been licensed for an average of 4.2 years (SD = 1.8) and 29,600 km (SD = 36,000). The sample size was determined by the study period, as all participants who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were eligible to participate and included in the data analysis. To address this, post hoc power analysis was conducted relative to the primary aim of the study, which was the relationship between steering-behavior variables and attentional capacity along adaptation. Post hoc power values for each multiple linear regression were reported in the results section, assuming that the effect size estimated from our data is the true effect size. All participants gave written informed consent and were unaware of the hypotheses under investigation. They were informed that they were free to withdraw at any time, for any reason. None of the participants reported any discomfort caused by the simulator. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Université Gustave Eiffel. Ethical considerations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the French regulation for data protection (RGPD) with regard to experimentation were respected.

Experimental set-up

The steering task was conducted using a fixed-base driving simulator composed of a force feedback steering wheel, foot pedals, and a gearbox (Logitech G25), mounted in line with a driving seat. The simulated scene was displayed on three screens 1.22 m wide and 0.70 m high (refresh rate of 60 Hz, with a resolution of 3,840 × 2,168 pixels), making a visual angle of 180° horizontally and 37° vertically. The simulated viewing angle was aimed at the vanishing point of the visual scene. Engine sounds and environmental noises were generated by three speakers. The driving environment consisted of a single roadway with standard lane markings in a traffic-free environment. The simulated route was a two-lane rural road (lane width = 3.50 m) and included curves with a radius of curvature and length of 200 m. The road was lined with trees and surrounded by a flat rural expanse. Data from the simulator were collected at a frequency of 60 Hz.

MOT and MOA tasks were displayed at eye level on a computer screen 1.06 m wide and 0.60 m high (50 Hz, 1,920 × 1,080 pixels). Participants were seated on a chair with their head positioned at a distance of 0.95 m from the screen. Both MOT and MOA stimuli subtended a visual angle of 30° horizontally and vertically in the participants’ visual field.

Experimental tasks

In the steering task, participants had to drive through 100 right-hand bends presented as a sequence of individual trials. They started each trial on a straight part of the road (50 m before curve entry), positioned at the center of the lane. They were asked to drive as much as possible within a central zone (width = 0.25 m) delineated by two lines throughout the lane (Fig. 1). The participants were explicitly told that “keeping in the zone” meant keeping their midline within the zone. Given that corner-cutting is a well-established strategy in driving49,56, this task precluded the use of distinct corner-cutting strategies, which could have resulted in varying control demands across trials and participants. In addition, the driving speed was held constant at 90 km/hr throughout each trial by the simulation software so that speed reduction could not be used as a means to compensate for the difficulty of the steering task7. With this set speed, the cumulative driving time amounted to 16.5 min (10 s per trial). At the end of each trial, participants were presented with the RSME and prompted to rate the mental effort they had invested in negotiating each bend. Participants had to respond to the following instruction: “Please indicate, by marking the vertical axis below, how much mental effort it took for you to negotiate the bend you have just finished.”

In the MOT task, participants had to visually track four targets among a set of eight distractors, making a total of 12 objects (all had an angular size of 0.9°). On the first frame, all objects were stationary with targets and distractors distinguished by red and gray colors, respectively. When a trial began, all objects moved randomly for 2 s after which the targets turned gray. From that point, all objects had the same color and continued to move at a speed between 4.7 and 9.5°/s for 10 s. After that, all objects remained stationary and participants had to indicate the four (red) targets with a mouse click. Immediate feedback was provided, displaying how many targets had been correctly selected. Object trajectories were free of collision and occlusion.

In the MOA task, participants had to control a blue object (using a mouse) to avoid collision with randomly moving red objects (all having an angular size of 1.6°) as long as possible. A trial began with the presence of three red objects. After 15 s, their number increased by one and time slots lasted longer with each new item added (20 s with four objects, 30 s with five objects, etc.). A trial ended when a collision occurred with any of the randomly moving objects. The participants received immediate feedback corresponding to the time elapsed before a collision occurred. The MOA task was the same as the one used in24.

Procedure

The study was conducted as a single experimental session divided into two parts (steering task and MOT/MOA tasks) counterbalanced in order.

In the steering part, participants were told that they would have to steer through 100 right-hand bends, presented as a series of individual bend trials, while keeping their midline in a central zone as much as possible. They were also informed that their speed would be kept constant by the simulation. They were told that each trial would begin by a black screen before it disappeared. At that time, they would have to steer until they reached the end of the curve. Once the bend was completed, a black screen would pop up and they would have to rate the mental effort they had invested in negotiating the just-completed bend. Participants did not receive familiarization trials but had the opportunity to observe a test run during which they did not drive. Participants had short breaks every 10 trials. No feedback was provided.

In the MOT/MOA part, participants received instructions prior to each task to be performed (both tasks were counterbalanced). For the MOT task, participants were given three training trials before they performed 20 experimental trials. For the MOA task, participants were given three practice trials before they performed 10 experimental trials.

Data analysis

Steering behavior was analyzed by computing three variables for the curved section of the road (the straight-line approach was excluded): steering performance, steering stability, and steering reversal rate (SRR). All these variables were averaged over blocks of 10 trials each. Steering performance corresponded to the proportion of time spent within the central zone relative to the total time taken to negotiate the bend. This was expressed in percentage and given the truncated range phenomenon associated with proportional data, percent scores were log-transformed for statistical analyses. Steering stability was determined by computing the standard deviation of participants’ lateral position along each bend. Lateral position was defined as the distance in centimeters between the center of the lane (which coincided with the center of the zone) and the participants’ midline (determined as the midpoint of their driving seat). SRR corresponded to the number of steering-wheel direction changes per minute. A conventional threshold of 1° was used to detect reversals15,57, thus capturing steering-wheel movements dedicated to correcting lateral deviations from the desired path41.

Mental effort was measured with the RSME, which is a unidimensional continuous visual scale58. The scale was a computerized 150 mm line with nine anchor points, each accompanied by a descriptive label ranging from 0 = absolutely no effort to 130 = extreme effort. Participants rated by moving a slider with up and down keys on a keyboard positioned close to the gearbox. This variable was also averaged over 10 blocks.

Driving-simulator adaptation was assessed by the difference in steering performance between the first and final blocks of trials. This variable was log-transformed for statistical analyses.

MOT performance corresponded to the number of target objects successfully identified at the end of the trial. MOT scores were calculated as the average number of targets correctly identified over the 20 experimental trials, expressed as a percentage and log-transformed for statistical analyses. MOA performance corresponded to the total time during which participants prevented a collision between the target object and any of the randomly moving objects (a longer time indicated better performance). MOA scores corresponded to the average total time (in seconds) across the 10 experimental trials.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in three steps and carried out using Jamovi (version 2.3.21) with alpha set at 0.05. As steering behavior was expected to change during adaptation, the first step examined whether steering improved throughout trials. To this end, repeated-measures ANOVAs were run on (log-transformed) steering performance, steering stability, SRR and mental effort, with block number as a within-participant factor. Sphericity violations were explored with Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse–Geisser correction was performed in cases of violation. For each significant effect, partial eta-squared (ηp2) was calculated to determine the proportion of explained variance. Post-hoc analyses were conducted with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons. The second step examined whether MOT and MOA scores predicted driving simulator adaptation. To this end, simple linear regressions were conducted between individual simulator adaptation and MOT scores, as well as between individual simulator adaptation and MOA scores. The third step was to examine the relationship between steering behavior and attentional capacity over the course of adaptation. Two series of three multiple linear regressions were conducted (one for MOT, one for MOA) to predict each steering-behavior variable, with MOT/MOA as a continuous predictor and block number as a categorial factor (with Block 1 as the reference level). This enabled the capture of significant block-by-block changes, relative to Block 1, in the relation between attentional capacity and steering behavior.
