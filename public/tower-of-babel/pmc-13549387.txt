Integrative Transcriptomic Profiling Delineates LTBP4‐Driven Fibroblast Reprogramming Across the Ossification Continuum of the Ligamentum Flavum
Authors: Xiao Zhang, Tengyao Niu, Yulong Zhao, Zhenguo Liu, Wenbo Gu, Donghui Cao, Yanrong Tian, Haifeng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo delineate the dynamic transcriptomic landscape of ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) across normal, immature, and mature stages, identify core genes, and screen stage‐specific drug candidates. Normal, immature, and mature OLF tissues (nine samples) from three patients with multilevel OLF were collected for RNA‐seq. Differential expression, functional enrichment, and dynamic trend analyses were performed. Key genes were validated using an external GEO dataset. Single‐cell and spatial transcriptomics were integrated to determine cellular origin and spatial localization. AI‐based drug prediction and molecular docking were conducted. Between normal versus immature, 1026 DEGs were identified; between immature versus mature, 713 DEGs; and between normal versus mature, 1285 DEGs. Upregulated genes were enriched in RNA processing and ribosome biogenesis, whereas downregulated genes were enriched in ECM‐receptor interaction. LTBP4 showed progressive downregulation during ossification (AUC = 0.88). Single‐cell transcriptomics and spatial transcriptomic analysis of a public multi‐omics dataset (GSE255942) revealed that LTBP4 is expressed in fibroblasts, with lower expression in ossified than normal tissues. Virtual knockout of Ltbp4 in fibroblasts recapitulated the OLF transcriptomic signature. AI‐based drug prediction identified stage‐specific candidates; BRD‐K59831625 exhibited the lowest binding energy with LTBP4 (−8.9 kcal/mol). Sustained LTBP4 downregulation drives OLF progression, with fibroblasts as core effector cells. Stage‐specific drug candidates were identified. These findings provide new targets for molecular diagnosis and precision treatment of OLF.

Introduction

Ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) is a major cause of thoracic spinal stenosis and spinal cord compression, with a significantly higher prevalence in East Asian populations than in Western countries [1, 2]. This disease frequently involves multiple segments and is often accompanied by ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament or other spinal ligament ossifications, resulting in complex clinical manifestations and a high rate of misdiagnosis [3, 4]. Currently, posterior laminectomy and decompression remain the primary surgical treatment for thoracic OLF; however, surgical outcomes are suboptimal, with high incidences of postoperative complications such as spinal cord injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, symptom recurrence, or progression of ossification at adjacent segments [5, 6, 7]. The key factor underlying this dilemma is the difficulty in accurately preoperatively assessing the maturity and progression tendency of ossified lesions at different segments, leading to insufficient or excessive decompression.

Previous studies have classified ligamentum flavum ossification into mature and immature types based on histopathological features. Mature ossification is predominantly composed of lamellar bone with sparse chondrocytes and tends to be quiescent, whereas immature ossification is characterized by woven bone and abundant proliferating chondrocytes, indicating a potential for continued progression [8, 9]. Zhou and Dang [10] conducted a matched control study between imaging and pathology, demonstrating a high consistency between CT homogeneity and MRI T2WI signal intensity and pathological classification: homogeneous lesions with signal void on MRI corresponded to mature ossification, while heterogeneous lesions with hypointensity on MRI corresponded to immature ossification. These findings provide an important basis for clinically determining the progression tendency of ossification and lay the imaging foundation for precisely harvesting ossified tissue at different stages during surgery. Furthermore, although some scholars have classified OLF into degenerative and metabolic types based on clinical and imaging features [11], the underlying molecular continuum of the ossification process remains poorly understood.

To date, research on the molecular mechanisms of ligamentum flavum ossification remains limited. Although epidemiological investigations have identified age, obesity, and metabolic syndrome as important risk factors for OLF [12, 13, 14, 15], the exact pathogenesis of OLF has not been fully elucidated. Our previous study performed a preliminary analysis of BMP genes associated with OLF [16]. However, overall, prior work has largely focused on a few candidate genes or classical signaling pathways, such as TGF‐β/BMPs, Wnt/β‐catenin, and MAPK signaling pathways [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. In recent years, with the advancement of omics technologies, several studies have begun to use transcriptomic or proteomic approaches to screen differentially expressed genes in OLF [22, 23], revealing the potential roles of inflammatory immune responses, extracellular matrix remodeling, and noncoding RNA regulatory networks in this process [24, 25]. Nevertheless, current studies still lack a systematic depiction of the global dynamic changes in the transcriptome during the ossification process.

To address these issues, the present study utilized normal ligamentum flavum, immature ossified ligamentum flavum, and mature ossified ligamentum flavum tissues collected intraoperatively from the same patients. Based on imaging–pathological classification [10], we systematically analyzed transcriptomic differences across the three pathological stages. Through differential expression gene screening, functional enrichment analysis, dynamic trend analysis, and external dataset validation, we aimed to delineate the molecular evolution map of ligamentum flavum ossification from initiation to maturation and to identify core genes closely associated with the ossification process. Furthermore, we integrated single‐cell transcriptomic data and spatial transcriptomic data from public databases to investigate the cellular origin and spatial expression characteristics of hub genes. Finally, based on the transcriptomic profiles of different disease stages, we employed AI‐based drug repositioning techniques to screen candidate drugs with stage‐specific intervention potential and evaluated their binding affinity to the target protein via molecular docking simulations [26]. Through a research paradigm of “clinical imaging classification—transcriptome sequencing—multi‐omics validation—drug prediction,” this study aims to provide new theoretical bases and candidate therapeutic agents for the early molecular diagnosis, progression prediction, and precision treatment of ligamentum flavum ossification.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (approval no. KYLL‐2021‐1028). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgery. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments [27].

Sample Collection and Grouping

Inclusion criteria: Patients diagnosed with multilevel thoracic OLF and undergoing posterior total laminectomy and decompression in the Department of Spinal Orthopedics, General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. Exclusion criteria: Patients with other spinal degenerative diseases, metabolic bone diseases, infection, tumors, or a history of previous spinal surgery. A total of three patients (2 males and 1 female) were enrolled. Intraoperatively, two senior surgeons localized the target regions based on CT values with assistance from MRI (one patient did not undergo MRI due to claustrophobia) [10]. Three types of tissue samples were collected from the same surgical field for each patient: (1) Normal ligamentum flavum group (Group N): tissues harvested from normal non‐ossified regions; (2) Immature OLF group (Group I): tissues from regions with heterogeneous ossification foci showing low‐density areas and ill‐defined borders on CT, and hypo‐ or isointense signals on T2WI; (3) Mature OLF group (Group M): tissues from regions with homogeneous ossification foci, clear margins, and uniform internal structure on CT, and no signal on T2WI. Each patient provided samples from N, I, and M groups, yielding a total of nine samples. Samples were immediately snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

RNA Extraction, Library Construction and RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from nine ligamentum flavum samples (N, I, M groups, n = 3 each) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop ND‐1000; all samples met the QC criteria (A260/A280 1.8–2.0, RIN ≥ 7.0). mRNA was enriched with the NEBNext Poly(A) Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, USA), and strand‐specific libraries were constructed using the KAPA Stranded RNA‐Seq Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). Libraries were quantified and pooled, then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired‐end). Raw reads were processed with FastQC and Cutadapt (v1.17) for adapter trimming and quality filtering (Q30, minimum length 20 bp). Clean reads were aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome (GenCode) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [28].

Differential Expression Analysis

Transcripts were assembled and FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million mapped fragments) values were calculated using StringTie (v1.3.3) [29]. Differential expression analysis between groups was performed using Ballgown (v2.10.0) [30]. Genes with |log2FC| ≥ 0.585 (fold change ≥ 1.5), raw p < 0.05, and average within‐group FPKM ≥ 0.5 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To address multiple testing, the false discovery rate (FDR) was estimated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and adjusted p values (q values) were calculated for all genes; both raw and adjusted p values are provided in Table S1 for full transparency.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed for upregulated and downregulated DEGs separately. The R package clusterProfiler (v4.0.5) was used for enrichment analysis [31]. The p value was calculated by Fisher's exact test and adjusted to the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. A threshold of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The top 10 significantly enriched terms were visualized as bar charts.

Screening of Hub Genes and External Dataset Validation

To identify core genes involved in the progression from normal ligamentum flavum to mature OLF, genes with consistent expression trends (continuous upregulation or downregulation) from N to I and then to M were screened. Core genes with persistent expression changes across N, I, and M groups were identified using Venn diagram and heatmap trend analysis. Among the top 10% most significantly DEGs and combined with previous literature, LTBP4 was identified as a candidate key gene. The key regulatory network of LTBP4 was visualized using the GeneMANIA database [32]. The expression difference of LTBP4 was validated using an external dataset GSE181716 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, which included normal and OLF samples [23]. Expression differences were displayed using raincloud plots, and predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq Data Analysis

The single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset GSE126060 (mouse heterotopic ossification model, time points: 3, 7, and 21 days post‐injury) was downloaded from the GEO database [33]. Data integration, dimensionality reduction (t‐SNE), and cell clustering were performed using the Seurat package (v4.0) [34]. Fibroblast subsets and other cell types were identified based on canonical marker genes. Cell–cell communication strength between fibroblasts and other subsets at different time points was analyzed using CellChat (v1.1.0) [35]. t‐SNE plots were generated to show cell distribution and the expression pattern of Ltbp4 (mouse homolog of human LTBP4).

Spatial Transcriptomics Data Analysis

To further investigate the cellular origin and spatial expression pattern of LTBP4, the publicly available dataset GSE255942 (mouse heterotopic ossification model), which contains both spatial transcriptomic and single‐cell RNA‐seq components, was downloaded from the GEO database [36]. For the spatial transcriptomic component, data processing was performed using the Seurat and SpatialExperiment (v1.8) packages [37]; spatial transcript density, cell subpopulation distribution, and fibroblast localization were assessed, and the spatial expression pattern of Ltbp4 was compared between normal and ossification groups. For the single‐cell RNA‐seq component, data integration, UMAP dimensionality reduction, and cell clustering were performed using the Seurat package (v4.0) [34]. Fibroblast subsets and other cell types were identified based on canonical marker genes, and Ltbp4 expression was examined across different cell clusters between normal and ossification groups.

Single‐Cell Transcriptome Virtual Knockout Analysis

To further explore the functional impact of Ltbp4 deficiency in ligament fibroblasts, we performed a virtual knockout analysis on single‐cell RNA‐seq data from the GSE126060 dataset using the scTenifoldKnk pipeline [38]. Specifically, cells from the normal group were selected. A gene regulatory network was constructed based on the co‐expression relationships among highly variable genes. Subsequently, the Ltbp4 gene was computationally “knocked out” by removing its edges from the network, and the network was rebalanced to identify genes whose regulation was most significantly affected. Differentially regulated genes were identified using a Z‐score threshold (|Z| > 2) and an adjusted p value < 0.05.

Transcriptome‐Based Drug Repositioning

DEGs from three pairwise comparisons were separately uploaded to the CMap database to screen for candidate drugs capable of reversing the expression patterns of these DEGs. Volcano plots were constructed with LogitScore on the x‐axis and Probability on the y‐axis. The top 3 highest‐scoring drugs from each comparison were selected for further analysis.

Molecular Docking Simulation

The 3D structures of candidate small‐molecule drugs were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the crystal structure of LTBP4 was retrieved from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Tools (v1.5.6) [39]. The docking box was set to cover the active site. Binding free energy (ΔG) was used as an indicator of binding affinity, and the conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected. Docking results were three‐dimensionally visualized using PyMOL (v2.5) and Discovery Studio.

Results

Patient Demographics and Sample Overview

Three patients with multilevel thoracic ossification of the ligamentum flavum (TOLF) were enrolled in this study (two males, one female). All were confirmed to have TOLF by preoperative imaging and intraoperative pathology. Based on preoperative CT and MRI features together with intraoperative findings, the ossified segments were precisely localized and classified. According to the ossification process, ligamentum flavum tissues were divided into three groups: normal (Group N), transitional/progressive ossification (Group I), and mature ossification (Group M). Three samples per group were collected from each patient, yielding a total of nine specimens (Figure 1A–O). One patient did not undergo MRI because of claustrophobia; for this patient, group assignment relied on CT imaging and direct intraoperative observation. Differentially Expressed Genes

Based on the transcriptome sequencing data, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria |log2FC| ≥ 0.585 (≥ 1.5‐fold change), p < 0.05, and a mean intragroup FPKM ≥ 0.5. The results are presented in Figure 2. Figure 2A–C shows clustering heatmaps of DEG expression patterns (red: upregulation; green: downregulation), illustrating the overall distribution of DEGs in each comparison. Figure 2D–F shows the chromosomal distribution of DEGs. Figure 2G–I shows volcano plots (red: significantly upregulated; green: significantly downregulated; black: nonsignificant). In the N versus I comparison, 1026 DEGs were identified (408 upregulated, 618 downregulated). In the I versus M comparison, 713 DEGs were identified (423 upregulated, 290 downregulated). These results indicate that many genes are substantially altered during disease progression, and the number and pattern of DEGs differ between the two transitional stages.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed separately for upregulated and downregulated genes at the three stages. GO analysis (Figure 3A–C) showed that upregulated genes were mainly enriched in biological processes such as cellular response to DNA damage, stress response, regulation of cell transformation, RNA processing, and translation; in cellular components such as ribosome, nucleus, and transcription complexes; and in molecular functions including nucleic acid binding, ribosomal structural constituents, and translation elongation factor activity. These results highlight the involvement of upregulated genes in cell proliferation, stress responses, and genetic information transfer. Downregulated genes were also enriched in three GO categories (Figure 3D–F). They were primarily associated with biological processes such as extracellular matrix organization, development, regulation of cell adhesion, and signal transduction; cellular components such as extracellular matrix, focal adhesion, and membrane receptor complexes; and molecular functions including extracellular matrix structural constituent, adhesion molecule binding, and receptor ligand activity. Thus, downregulated genes mainly participate in the construction of the cellular microenvironment and cell–cell interactions.

KEGG pathway analysis (Figure 4) revealed that upregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to cell proliferation, stress responses, and genetic information processing, including ribosome, RNA transport, DNA replication, and protein processing, as well as cell cycle‐related pathways (Figure 4A–C). Downregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways involved in cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and signal transduction (Figure 4D–F).

Screening and Validation of the Key Gene LTBP4

To identify core genes that show consistent expression changes throughout the normal‐to‐mature ossification process, we screened genes with a concordant trend across the three stages. Venn diagrams showed 16 persistently upregulated genes and 34 persistently downregulated genes (Figure 5A,B). Heatmaps demonstrated that these persistently changed genes clearly distinguished the N, I, and M groups (Figure 5C–E). Among the top 10% most significantly changed genes, LTBP4 showed the most pronounced downregulation at all three stages. Based on this and literature evidence, LTBP4 was selected as a candidate key gene. The LTBP4 regulatory network constructed with GeneMANIA indicated close interactions between LTBP4 and TGF‐β signaling pathway genes and extracellular matrix‐related genes (e.g., FBN1, FBN2, LTBP1) (Figure 5F). In the external dataset GSE181716, LTBP4 expression was significantly lower in ossified ligamentum flavum than in normal tissue (Figure 5G). The ROC curve gave an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 for discriminating ossified from normal tissues (Figure 5H).

Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Features

To investigate the cellular dynamics and key molecular features during ligamentum flavum ossification, we analyzed the mouse heterotopic ossification model GSE126060 (Days 3, 7, and 21 after injury) to simulate the temporal progression of ossification. t‐SNE clustering showed that the distribution of cell subpopulations in the microenvironment underwent substantial dynamic remodeling over time, with marked differences in cellular composition at the three time points (Figure 6A–C, Figure S1A–F). Because fibroblasts are the major cell type in ligamentum flavum, we examined fibroblast‐mediated cell–cell communication. The interaction strength between fibroblasts and other cell types (e.g., macrophages, endothelial cells) increased initially and then decreased, peaking on Day 7 and dropping markedly by Day 21 (Figure 6D–F, Figure S1D–F). We also analyzed the expression of Ltbp4 (the mouse homolog of LTBP4) in all clusters. t‐SNE plots revealed clear Ltbp4 expression in fibroblasts, with a progressive decline from Day 3 to Day 7 and the lowest expression on Day 21.

Differentially Expressed Genes After Ltbp4 Virtual Knockout

For functional validation of the role of Ltbp4 in ligament fibroblasts, we performed a virtual knockout of Ltbp4 on single‐cell RNA‐seq data from the normal group using the scTenifoldKnk pipeline. After Ltbp4 knockout, a total of 46 genes were significantly differentially expressed (|Z| > 2, p.adj < 0.05), all of which were upregulated (Figure 8A–C, Table S2). The top 20 upregulated genes are shown as a bar plot (Figure 8A). Among them, Timp2 showed the most significant upregulation (FC = 590.9, Z = 3.06), followed by Fcer1g (FC = 99.5, Z = 2.54), Ctss (FC = 53.0, Z = 2.37), and Laptm5 (FC = 50.8, Z = 2.36). Notably, multiple genes involved in extracellular matrix remodeling (Col33a1, Col5a1, Col5a2, Col6a1, Col6a2, Dcn, Nid1), immune/inflammation‐related genes (C1qa/b/c, Cd68, Adgre1/Aif1, Csf1r, Lyz2), and lysosomal protease‐related genes (Ctss, Ctsc, Lgmn) were significantly upregulated (Figure 8B, volcano plot). A pie chart shows that all differentially regulated genes were significant (p.adj < 0.05) (Figure 8C). These results indicate that loss of Ltbp4 in fibroblasts leads to widespread activation of extracellular matrix‐related and immune‐related genes, similar to the transcriptomic changes observed in human OLF tissues.

Drug Prediction and Molecular Docking

Drug prediction was performed using the Drug Repurposing Hub platform based on the three sets of DEGs, and the top three scoring candidate drugs were selected for each comparison (Table S1). Volcano plots displayed the LogitScore versus Probability distribution of the candidate drugs in each comparison (Figure 8D–F). Molecular docking using LTBP4 as the target protein was performed. For the early disease stage (N vs. I): BRD‐A41450521 (−7.5 kcal/mol) and BRD‐K93023739 (−7.5 kcal/mol) exhibited strong affinity for LTBP4, whereas BRD‐K18742343 (+5.8 kcal/mol) showed no binding activity. For the progressive stage (I vs. M): BRD‐K14880289 (−7.7 kcal/mol), BRD‐K06234293 (−7.0 kcal/mol), and BRD‐K82091397 (−6.8 kcal/mol) all showed binding activity, with BRD‐K14880289 having the strongest affinity. For the terminal stage (N vs. M): BRD‐K59831625 (−8.9 kcal/mol), BRD‐A81866333 (−7.6 kcal/mol), and BRD‐K53972329 (−6.4 kcal/mol) were active, with BRD‐K59831625 showing the strongest affinity. The docking conformations were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio (Figure 9A–I).

Discussion

In this study, we used RNA‐seq technology to systematically delineate, at the transcriptome level, the dynamic molecular landscape of OLF across normal, immature, and mature stages. Our work not only confirms several classic concepts regarding OLF pathogenesis but also reveals novel molecular events, most notably the progressive downregulation of LTBP4. By integrating single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses, we further established fibroblasts as the primary source of LTBP4 and documented its altered spatial distribution in ossified tissues. Finally, based on the transcriptomic profiles of different pathological stages, we employed AI‐based drug repositioning to identify candidate drugs with stage‐specific intervention potential and validated their binding to LTBP4 through molecular docking simulations. These findings provide new theoretical insights into the pathogenesis of OLF and its precision treatment.

Our grouping strategy strictly adhered to preoperative CT and MRI features, classifying ossified lesions into normal (Group N), immature (Group I), and mature (Group M) types. Zhou and Dang [10] conducted a matched imaging‐pathology study on 73 ossified ligamentum flavum segments and demonstrated high consistency between CT homogeneity and pathological type: all 18 homogeneous lesions were mature ossification (100% agreement), and 51 of 55 heterogeneous lesions were immature ossification (94.5% agreement). On MRI, 22 lesions with signal void were all mature ossification, and 50 lesions with hypointensity were all immature ossification (100% agreement). This classic study provides a solid imaging basis for our grouping, ensuring that each group carries distinct pathological meaning and laying a foundation for reliable interpretation of subsequent transcriptomic differences.

We identified a large number of differentially expressed genes in the three comparisons. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that upregulated genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to cell proliferation and protein synthesis, such as RNA processing, ribosome biogenesis, DNA replication, and cell cycle regulation. In contrast, downregulated genes were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with microenvironmental remodeling, including extracellular matrix organization, focal adhesion, and ECM‐receptor interaction. Notably, the strong enrichment of RNA metabolism pathways (spliceosome, RNA transport) is a novel finding. Previous studies have largely focused on classical osteogenic pathways such as BMP and TGF‐β signaling [40, 41, 42]. The global activation of RNA processing pathways observed here suggests that extensive posttranscriptional regulatory reprogramming may occur during OLF progression. This is consistent with the differential alternative splicing events we detected, together indicating that posttranscriptional regulation is an underappreciated layer in OLF pathogenesis.

LTBP4 (latent transforming growth factor beta binding protein 4) exhibited the most consistent and significant changes in this study, showing a progressive downregulation from the normal stage to the immature stage and then to the mature stage. The physiological function of LTBP4 is to bind TGF‐β1 and store it in its inactive form within the extracellular matrix, thereby enabling precise regulation of TGF‐β signaling [43]. The sustained downregulation of LTBP4 observed in this study may suggest impairment of the negative regulatory mechanism of TGF‐β signaling, leading to abnormally elevated local TGF‐β activity. In a study of 43 patients with thoracic OLF, Yayama et al. found using immunohistochemistry that TGF‐β was significantly expressed in chondrocytes within ossified lesions, most notably at the calcification front. This suggests that TGF‐β plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of ligamentum flavum ossification. In the broader field of heterotopic ossification, Wang et al. [44] demonstrated using multiple mouse models that elevated active TGF‐β levels are key drivers of the induction and promotion of ectopic bone formation. Moreover, systemic administration of a TGF‐β neutralizing antibody effectively inhibited heterotopic bone formation, indicating that TGF‐β is an important therapeutic target for heterotopic ossification. Therefore, the sustained downregulation of LTBP4 in this study may lead to excessive activation of local TGF‐β signaling by relieving the negative regulation of TGF‐β, thereby driving transdifferentiation of ligament fibroblasts into osteoblast‐like cells. However, a mass spectrometry analysis of ligamentum flavum tissue from patients with lumbar spinal stenosis and comorbid diabetes by Sidon et al. [45] revealed a significant increase in LTBP4 expression, which contrasts with the findings of this study. Possible reasons for this discrepancy include the fact that lumbar spinal stenosis is primarily characterized by ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and fibrosis, whereas OLF is characterized by ectopic bone formation, representing fundamentally different pathological processes. Additionally, a high proportion of patients in their study had diabetes, which is a known risk factor for spinal ligament pathologies. Hyperglycemia can promote the expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TGF‐β1 by activating the aldose reductase pathway [46]. Therefore, the expression changes of LTBP4 in spinal ligament pathologies may be disease‐specific and stage‐specific, and its biological significance must be interpreted in the context of the specific pathological background.

Single‐cell transcriptome analysis showed that in the GSE126060 mouse model of heterotopic ossification, the communication intensity between fibroblasts and macrophages/endothelial cells peaked on Day 7 after injury and then significantly decreased by Day 21. This dynamic change suggests that fibroblasts play a central role in the initiation and progression of heterotopic ossification. In this model, Ltbp4 showed clear expression signals in fibroblasts and exhibited a sustained downregulation over time after injury, which is highly consistent with observations in human OLF specimens. Spatial transcriptomic analysis from the multi‐omics dataset GSE255942 further validated this conclusion: fibroblasts were scattered in normal tissues, whereas in ossified tissues, fibroblasts aggregated around the ossification foci; the spatial expression pattern of LTBP4 highly overlapped with fibroblast localization, and its expression intensity was lower in the ossification group than in the normal group. The accompanying single‐cell RNA‐seq component of the same dataset further corroborated these spatial observations at the cellular level. The central role of fibroblasts in ligamentum flavum pathologies has also been supported by other studies. Ham et al. [47] performed single‐cell RNA sequencing on surgical specimens from patients with lumbar spinal stenosis and ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and found that fibroblasts accounted for more than 75% of all cells and identified a population of myofibroblasts formed through transdifferentiation. Moreover, their study revealed that signaling pathways such as TGF‐β, PDGF, CXCL, ANGPTL, GAS, and GRN are involved in the interactions between fibroblasts and immune cells. Although the pathological background of that study is ligamentum flavum hypertrophy (characterized by fibrosis and myofibroblast conversion) rather than ossification of the ligamentum flavum, the common finding with our study is that fibroblasts are the predominant cell type in ligamentum flavum pathologies, and their phenotypic conversion and altered cell–cell communication are key drivers of disease progression.

We further validated the causal role of LTBP4 downregulation in OLF by performing a virtual knockout of Ltbp4 in mouse fibroblast single‐cell data. Notably, all 46 significantly regulated genes were upregulated, mirroring the human OLF transcriptome where LTBP4 loss is associated with increased expression of collagens and immune‐related genes. Among these, Timp2—the most upregulated gene—regulates ECM turnover and has been linked to heterotopic ossification [48, 49]. The coordinated upregulation of multiple collagens (Col3a1, Col5a1, Col6a1) and matrix proteoglycans (Dcn, Nid1) suggests that Ltbp4 deficiency directly promotes ECM synthesis and remodeling, a prerequisite for ectopic bone formation [50, 51]. Concurrent activation of macrophage‐associated genes (C1qa/b/c, Cd68, Adgre1, Csf1r) and lysosomal proteases (Ctss, Ctsc, Lgmn) points to a pro‐inflammatory and tissue‐resorptive microenvironment that may favor osteogenic differentiation [52]. Collectively, these virtual knockout results provide computational evidence that LTBP4 acts as a master negative regulator of both ECM production and immune activation in the ligamentum flavum, and that its downregulation is sufficient to drive the transcriptomic hallmarks of OLF. Future experiments using conditional knockout mice or CRISPR‐based knockdown in primary fibroblasts will be needed to confirm these findings.

Through drug prediction and molecular docking, this study found that compound BRD‐K59831625 had the strongest binding affinity with LTBP4 (−8.9 kcal/mol), suggesting that it may intervene in the terminal stage of ossification of the ligamentum flavum by regulating LTBP4‐related pathways. Structural analogues of BRD‐K59831625 (e.g., LDN‐212854, ML347) belong to the pyrazolo[1,5‐a]pyrimidine class of compounds and have been proven to be potent and selective inhibitors of ALK2 (BMP type I receptor). Among them, LDN‐212854 effectively inhibited heterotopic ossification in mouse models [53]. And ML347 exhibited > 300‐fold selectivity for ALK2 over ALK3, with crystal structure analysis revealing the molecular basis for its high selectivity [54]. Since overactivation of BMP/TGF‐β signaling is a key driver of heterotopic ossification [55, 56], the sustained downregulation of LTBP4 observed in this study may lead to dysregulation of this pathway. Therefore, BRD‐K59831625 may exert its anti‐ossification effects by inhibiting ALK2 activity or indirectly affecting LTBP4 function. In summary, this compound provides a new candidate molecule for stage‐specific pharmacotherapy of OLF, but its efficacy and safety still require experimental validation.

This study has the following limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the statistical power of some differentially expressed genes. Future studies are needed to validate the core findings in independent large‐scale cohorts. Second, the single‐cell and spatial transcriptome data were obtained from public databases. The mouse model used is an acute injury model, which differs from the pathophysiological process of chronic human OLF. Moreover, the spatial transcriptome data were derived from heterotopic ossification tissues, and the homology with ligamentum flavum ossification requires further confirmation. Finally, the core findings of this study (e.g., functional validation of LTBP4, evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of candidate drugs) need to be verified through subsequent functional experiments.

Conclusion

This study is the first to systematically delineate the dynamic transcriptomic landscape of ligamentum flavum ossification from the normal to the mature stage, revealing a molecular mechanism characterized by sustained downregulation of LTBP4 and activation of RNA metabolism pathways. It identifies fibroblasts as the core effector cells in the ossification process and, through virtual knockout experiments, demonstrates that Ltbp4 loss directly induces ECM and immune gene activation, mimicking the human OLF transcriptome. Additionally, it predicts candidate drugs with stage‐specific intervention potential based on different pathological stages. These findings provide new theoretical bases for molecular diagnosis, progression prediction, and precision treatment of OLF. Future studies should validate the expression changes of core genes in larger cohorts and elucidate the specific role of LTBP4 in OLF through in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, thereby laying the foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.
