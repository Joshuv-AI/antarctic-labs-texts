Delays in Malignant Neck Mass Diagnosis: Missed Opportunities in Clinical Decision-Making
Authors: Jacob Edward Hoerter, Peter Debbaneh, Abhishek D Doshi, Bruce F Folck, Miranda L Ritterman Weintraub, Kevin H Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeck masses are a common presenting symptom for head and neck cancers and lymphomas. However, the diagnostic pathway for these malignancies remains underexplored.MethodsThe authors reviewed a subset of adult patients with neck masses ultimately diagnosed as malignant. Clinical decisions by primary care physicians (PCPs) and otolaryngologists were assessed. The authors evaluated diagnostic time intervals and biopsy diagnostic rates.ResultsAmong 192 patients, the median time to diagnosis was 21 days. Antibiotics prescribed by PCPs were associated with a 20-day delay (P < .001), while PCP-ordered imaging led to a 4-day delay (P < .001). Initial biopsy methods included fine needle aspiration ( 70%), core needle biopsy (28%), and excision (2%), with diagnostic success rates of 56%, 74%, and 100%, respectively (P = .017). Eighteen percent of patients followed the most streamlined diagnostic pathway and experienced a median total diagnostic interval of 6 days.ConclusionsMost patients did not undergo the most optimal diagnostic process. Antibiotic use, PCP-ordered imaging, and delayed or unsuccessful biopsies were common contributors to delay.Keywords: neck mass, diagnosis, delay, biopsy, antibiotics

Introduction

Neck masses are a frequent presenting complaint in clinical practice, encompassing a broad differential diagnosis. The most urgent and clinically substantial task is to distinguish malignant from benign etiologies, which is often challenging. Despite the high incidence of neck masses and the well-established link between early treatment and improved survival in head and neck cancers, 1–3 few studies have evaluated the diagnostic process itself.

Most prior investigations into increased diagnostic intervals in head and neck cancer have focused on patient-related factors such as demographics, socioeconomic status, or health-seeking behaviors. 4–9 However, clinical decisions, including the use of empiric antibiotics, timing and type of imaging, and biopsy method, also play a critical role in shaping the diagnostic trajectory.

This study examines the clinical decision-making patterns within a large cohort of patients ultimately diagnosed with malignant neck masses. By identifying factors that contribute to increased diagnostic intervals, the authors aim to inform more efficient and effective evaluation pathways for these high-risk patients.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Kaiser Foundation Research Institute Institutional Review Board (IRB #00001045) with waiver of informed consent. The authors reviewed electronic consult records for all adults aged 18–89 referred to otolaryngology–head and neck surgery for evaluation of neck or salivary gland masses between January 1 and December 31, 2017, within a large integrated health care system in Northern California. This cohort has been previously studied. 10 For the current analysis, the authors cross-referenced the cohort with the institutional cancer registry to identify cases representing a new malignancy. The integrated health care system includes shared electronic medical records across primary care, specialty clinics, imaging, pharmacy, and pathology. Primary care physician (PCP) visits, prescriptions, and imaging orders were therefore captured alongside specialist documentation. However, patient encounters occurring outside the system prior to presentation could not be assessed.

Patients with a prior cancer diagnosis or with less than 12 months of follow-up were excluded. Detailed chart review was performed to extract the following: dates and types of imaging studies, appointment dates with PCPs and otolaryngologists (OHNSs), antibiotic prescriptions, type and number of biopsies, biopsy-performing clinicians, and final diagnosis. Antibiotic prescriptions were specifically examined because empiric antibiotics represent the most common initial treatment alternative to immediate referral or biopsy for unexplained cervical lymphadenopathy in adults. A biopsy was considered diagnostic if no further tissue sampling was required for treatment initiation. A biopsy was classified as nondiagnostic if pathology failed to provide sufficient information to initiate definitive oncologic treatment. Reasons included inadequate cellularity, necrotic or acellular samples, insufficient tissue architecture for lymphoma subtyping, or indeterminate cytology requiring repeat tissue sampling.

Four diagnostic time intervals were defined: 1) PCP visit to initial OHNS visit (PCP–OHNS); 2) OHNS visit to diagnostic biopsy (OHNS–DB); 3) diagnostic biopsy to pathology report (PTAT); and 4) total diagnostic interval from PCP visit to pathology report (PCP–PR). Any independent variable that led to a statistically significant increased diagnostic interval was considered a contributor to prolonged time to diagnosis. The terms “PCP visit” and “initial OHNS visit” refer to the first encounter with each clinician for a chief complaint of “neck mass.”

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For all time intervals, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were calculated. Biopsy types were compared using χ2 and 2-tailed 2-sample t tests. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare median diagnostic intervals between patients with and without specific clinical exposures (eg, antibiotic use, delayed biopsy). Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than .05. Independent variables included antibiotic use, PCP-ordered imaging, biopsy timing, biopsy modality (fine needle aspiration [FNA], core needle biopsy [CNB], excision), biopsy guidance method, and need for repeat biopsy. Variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature. This study was approved by the Kaiser Foundation Research Institute’s Internal Review Board with waiver of patient consent.

Results

Among 4103 patients referred to OHNSs for neck masses, 205 (5.0%) were diagnosed as malignancy. Thirteen underwent biopsy of a separate anatomic site; the remaining 192 cases formed the primary study cohort. The most common diagnoses were lymphoma (n = 76, 37.1%) oropharyngeal carcinoma (n = 64, 31.2%), and thyroid carcinoma (n = 18, 8.8%; Table 1). The malignant neck mass cohort was predominantly male (n = 124, 60.5%).

Antibiotics were prescribed by PCPs in 16.0% of cases and associated with a median diagnostic increased diagnostic interval of 21 days (36 vs 16 days, P = .001). Imaging ordered by PCPs was observed in 54% of cases and associated with longer PCP–OHNS intervals (11 vs 7 days; P < .001; Table 2).

Biopsies were performed at the initial OHNS visit in 58.3% of patients, yielding a diagnosis in 33.1% (Figure 2). Delayed biopsy (after the first visit) was associated with a 9-day increase in the OHNS–DB interval (P = .010). Multiple biopsies were required in 81 patients (42.2%) and were linked to a 16-day increase in median total diagnostic interval (PCP–PR) (P = .025). In total, 18.2% of patients (n = 35) underwent an optimal diagnostic pathway, defined as immediate specialist referral with diagnostic biopsy performed at the initial OHNS visit, without empiric antibiotics or PCP-ordered imaging, with a median total interval of 6 days (IQR 1–13). Among nondiagnostic biopsies, the most common limitations were inadequate tissue architecture for lymphoma diagnosis and insufficient cellularity, rather than sampling error alone.

Among 283 total neck mass biopsies, 72.1% were performed by OHNS, 19.8% by interventional radiology (IR), and 3.9% by endocrinology (Table 3). First biopsy techniques included fine needle aspiration (FNA, 69.7%), core needle biopsy (CNB, 28.3%), and excisional biopsy (EB, 2.0%). Diagnostic yields were 56.3% for FNA, 74.2% for CNB, and 100.0% for EB (P = .017; Figure 2). IR clinicians, who predominantly performed CNB, had higher overall diagnostic success (80.4%) compared to OHNSs (61.8%), who primarily used FNA (P < .011). However, CNB diagnostic success was comparable between specialties (77.3% vs 77.8%). EB was performed in 34 cases, 85.3% followed a nondiagnostic needle biopsy and 88.2% were for lymphoma. In the 5 patients whose first biopsy was an EB, all were diagnosed with lymphoma.

Discussion

This is the first US-based study to systematically examine the diagnostic workup of malignant neck masses. The median total time to diagnosis was 21 days, comparable to timelines reported in the United Kingdom (22–24 days). However, increased diagnostic intervals were substantial for a significant portion; 25% of patients waited at least 38 days. Although the literature shows mixed evidence on the effect of increased diagnostic intervals on survival, delays in treatment have a consistent association with worse outcomes. 2,3,6,11–14 For example, one study of 38,000 patients found a 4.6% increased mortality risk for every 30-day delay in treatment initiation. 12 As diagnosis is a prerequisite to treatment, minimizing delays in this time interval may have downstream survival benefits.

Several modifiable factors contributed to increased diagnostic intervals, including the use of empiric antibiotics, primary care-initiated imaging, and delayed or inconclusive biopsy procedures.

ANTIBIOTICS BY PRIMARY CARE

Although prescribed in only 16% of cases, antibiotics were associated with a median 21-day diagnostic delay. This practice likely stems from the difficulty in distinguishing benign from malignant lymphadenopathy in primary care settings, as established risk factors are nonspecific (age > 40) or uncommon (fixed or ulcerated masses). The authors’ prior study identified additional malignancy predictors, including White race, older age (as a continuous variable), short-axis lymph node dimension > 1 cm 15,16 lateral neck location, and multiplicity of masses. These findings could inform better risk stratification. Guidelines are inconsistent; although some support empiric antibiotics for cervical lymphadenopathy, others, such as the AAO–HNS Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG), advise against their use for unexplained neck masses. 4,14,17 The findings support more cautious use of empiric antibiotics in ambiguous cases, as treatment delays can impact survival, particularly in aggressive malignancies. 16,18

IMAGING BY PRIMARY CARE

Over half of the cohort underwent imaging initiated by their PCP, which was associated with a 50% increase in time to specialist evaluation (PCP–OHNS interval). The role of PCP in ordering imaging is unclear. The AAO–HNS CPG recommends starting with a thorough head and neck examination, including laryngoscopy, 4 which is generally not performed in primary care settings. Furthermore, 31% of initial imaging studies in the authors’ prior research revealed no abnormalities, exposing patients to unnecessary radiation, cost, and incidental findings. 19–22 The study period was 2017, when the CPG was released recommending specialist evaluation first. Subsequently, in 2019, the authors’ institution internally began recommending OHNS evaluation before imaging, but adherence to this policy is variable. During the study period, there was no standardized educational initiative to ensure PCP awareness of an “optimal” diagnostic pathway for adult neck masses. Thus, variation in PCP practices likely reflects individual clinician judgment rather than systematic adherence to institutional protocols.

Some OHNS clinicians still prefer formal imaging first, possibly due to limitations in neck palpation accuracy or infrequent use of POCUS, which was used in only 18% of cases. POCUS enables precise measurement, risk stratification, and immediate biopsy of suspicious masses. It also obviates the need for formal imaging for normal anatomic structures or clearly benign lymph nodes. Wider spread adoption of POCUS in OHNS practice has substantial potential to expedite neck mass assessment, as it has for many other medical conditions.

BIOPSY PRACTICES

Biopsy timing and modality significantly influenced diagnostic efficiency. Biopsy was performed at the initial OHNS visit in only 58.3% of cases; when delayed, diagnosis was postponed by a median of 9 days. Increased diagnostic interval commonly results from subsequent OHNS visits or referral to IR.

FNA was the most commonly used technique (70%), but it had the lowest diagnostic yield (56%). This finding is consistent with other studies reporting high failure rates of FNA in diagnosing lymphoma (32%–37%) 23,24 and HPV-related oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (30%–54%). 25,26 FNA has been recommended as the first-line technique for neck mass biopsy 4,27 because of reported high sensitivity and specificity. 24,26 Reported high diagnostic accuracy of FNA may be inflated by excluding nondiagnostic samples in their calculations 24,26,28,29 or by the use of rapid onsite evaluation by cytopathology, which is frequently unavailable.

CNB had a significantly higher diagnostic yield (74%), matching multiple other large studies in the literature. 23,30–33 The reason for the choice of biopsy method was unclear. However, the predominant use of FNA may be due to clinician preference given its longer history as a biopsy technique. CNB is a relatively newer technology with which some clinicians may be less experienced or have safety concerns. The literature has shown CNB to have a safety profile very similar to FNA, with minimal chance of bleeding or nerve injury. 32,34,35 Systematic reviews across organ systems, 36 including salivary gland, 37 thyroid, 38 and breast, 39 support CNB as the first-line modality. Wider adoption of CNB could reduce repeat procedures and expedite diagnosis. The high rate of repeat biopsies observed in this cohort mirrors prior reports in lymphoma and HPV-associated oropharyngeal carcinoma, where cytologic sampling alone is often insufficient. Limitations in tissue architecture, tumor necrosis, and need for immunophenotyping likely contribute to repeat procedures. These findings support earlier consideration of CNB or EB in selected patients. 30–33

IR clinicians, who primarily performed CNB, achieved higher diagnostic overall biopsy success rates than OHNSs, who primarily used FNA. When comparing only CNB procedures, diagnostic yields were similar between specialties. POCUS-guided biopsies outperformed palpation-guided methods (73% vs 61%) but were underutilized. Although this study was not powered to detect statistical significance for this variable and may be confounded by needle-type and cancer histology, existing literature shows a higher diagnostic rate for POCUS-guided needle biopsy over a palpation-guided technique. 23,30,40–44

At the first OHNS visit, biopsy was performed in just over half of cases; when biopsy was performed separately, this delayed the diagnosis by a median of 9 days. Delayed biopsy was due to IR referral or biopsy at a subsequent OHNS visit. Biopsy timing was not explicitly addressed in the AAO–HNS CPG, but if feasible, a same-day procedure is optimal for both oncologic and psychological reasons. Recent literature has shown that a significant portion of cancer patients have emotional distress, 45 and rapid same-day biopsy has been shown to decrease anxiety for patients with breast nodules. 46 One-stop neck lump clinics that perform same-day POCUS and biopsy have been well described for 2 decades 43,47–49 and have become standard in the UK’s National Health System. A similar expedited process was performed in the study’s cohort in 18% of cases, which led to an efficient diagnosis in less than 1 week. Implementation of a standardized, efficient diagnostic pathway for malignant neck masses merits further effort.

STRENGTHS

This study is among the first to focus on the diagnostic timeline of malignant neck masses in a US health care system. By identifying cases based on presenting symptoms rather than histology, the authors provide insight into real-world diagnostic challenges. The evaluation of clinical decision-making, rather than patient demographics or tumor biology, offers a fresh perspective on actionable quality improvement targets. Moreover, the emphasis on diagnostic intervals highlights an underrecognized but essential component of the cancer care continuum. Such data are unavailable in national cancer databases and other health care systems. As time to treatment initiation influences survival and the time interval prior to diagnosis is often of greater magnitude, improving the diagnostic workup has potential to improve patient survival.

LIMITATIONS

This retrospective study was conducted within an integrated health care system, which may limit generalizability to other practice settings with different referral structures. Some clinician decisions, such as biopsy method and use of POCUS, may reflect individual practitioner preferences and were not standardized. Additionally, the analysis does not account for patient-driven increased diagnostic intervals before seeking medical care, nor does it evaluate survival outcomes, both of which warrant future investigation.

This study did not include a comparator cohort of patients with benign neck masses. Diagnostic timelines and biopsy utilization in nonmalignant cases may differ substantially, and future work sampling benign presentations could clarify whether delays observed here are unique to malignant disease.

The study cohort reflects practice patterns from 2017. Since that time, changes in clinical guidelines, imaging availability, antibiotic stewardship efforts, and biopsy techniques may have influenced contemporary diagnostic pathways. Therefore, absolute timelines may differ in current practice, although the relative impact of clinical decision-making on diagnostic intervals is likely preserved.

Finally, given the number of comparisons performed, there is an increased chance of type I error, and findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

Increased diagnostic interval in diagnosing malignant neck masses is common and associated with specific, potentially modifiable clinical practices; namely, empiric antibiotics, PCP-ordered imaging, increased interval to performing biopsy, and nondiagnostic biopsies. Nearly half of patients required multiple biopsies to establish a diagnosis. Only a small proportion of patients underwent the optimal pathway, involving immediate specialist referral and definitive biopsy at the initial OHNS visit. These findings establish a baseline for performance improvement and support the development of diagnostic quality metrics. Further research should explore whether expedited diagnostic pathways can improve patient satisfaction and survival.
