Introduction

Surgical decision-making for anterior cervical pathology, particularly concerning anterior cervical osteotomy (ACO) techniques, has traditionally been framed along a gradient of decompression extent, 1 resting on the assumption that greater ventral resection yields proportionally improved neurological decompression and sagittal correction. Within this paradigm, anterior cervical osteotomy has often been viewed as an extension of resection-based thinking rather than as a distinct biomechanical category. This “resection-centric” logic has contributed to the escalation from discectomy to multilevel corpectomy in complex cervical myelopathy, 2 particularly in rigid kyphosis and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL). However, increasing clinical experience and biomechanical evidence suggest that this assumption is increasingly fundamentally flawed. 3 The relationship between decompression strategy, alignment restoration, and construct stability is highly nonlinear, 4 and expanding the extent of anterior resection frequently fails to produce meaningful sagittal correction while disproportionately increasing mechanical instability and dural risk.1,4 Consequently, anterior cervical interventions, particularly those utilizing osteotomy, are often chosen based on anatomical exposure instead of the specific mechanism used to achieve correction and decompression. 5 This approach results in predictable ceilings in alignment restoration and avoidable mechanical and dural complications.

These limitations are most evident in rigid cervical deformity and adhesive OPLL, 5 where traditional corpectomy-based strategies provide direct ventral access yet remain constrained by long-span strut biomechanics, loss of anterior column load sharing, and elevated risk of dural injury.6,7 In contrast, anterior cervical osteotomy techniques represent a conceptually distinct class of anterior interventions, introducing fundamentally different corrective mechanisms that shift the focus from extent of resection to biomechanical intent. 8 By enabling realignment and decompression through controlled segmental release or vertebral-body translation rather than progressive bony removal, osteotomy-based strategies challenge the implicit equivalence between decompression extent and corrective capacity.9,10 Despite these advances, current literature lacks a unifying framework that explains why specific anterior cervical osteotomy strategies succeed or fail across different pathological contexts, and how alignment correction, structural stability, and procedural safety should be prioritized during preoperative planning.

Accordingly, this review reframes anterior cervical osteotomy as a biomechanical continuum and introduces a mechanism-based, tiered decision framework that integrates alignment restoration, construct stability, and procedural safety to guide rational surgical decision-making in complex cervical disease.

Methods

Literature Search and Study Selection

We conducted a comprehensive narrative review to examine anterior cervical osteotomy techniques. Electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science) were queried to capture the evolution of anterior cervical decompression concepts. Search algorithms combined disease entities (e.g., cervical kyphosis, cervical myelopathy, OPLL) with procedural strategies (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF), anterior cervical osteotomy, trans-discal osteotomy, partial corpectomy) and translation-based techniques (vertebral body sliding osteotomy (VBSO), anterior controllable antedisplacement and fusion (ACAF)).

Rather than a quantitative meta-analysis, this review adopted a framework-guided approach. Studies were selected based on their relevance to the biomechanical mechanisms of correction, specifically those providing data on: (i) radiographic alignment parameters (e.g., segmental lordosis, K-line), or (ii) stability and safety outcomes (e.g., subsidence, dural complications). The gathered evidence was synthesized conceptually to construct the “ACO Triad” framework.

Analytical Framework: The ACO Triad

To translate heterogeneous procedural descriptions into a coherent decision framework, each technique was analyzed using a predefined biomechanical construct, the ACO Triad, comprising alignment, stability, and safety:

• Alignment (Correction Mechanism): the primary means by which sagittal correction was achieved, including segmental release and hinging versus long-span strut behavior or vertebral-body translation, as well as the capacity to restore lordosis and facilitate K-line 11 conversion in kyphotic OPLL.

• Alignment (Correction Mechanism): the primary means by which sagittal correction was achieved, including segmental release and hinging versus long-span strut behavior or vertebral-body translation, as well as the capacity to restore lordosis and facilitate K-line 11 conversion in kyphotic OPLL.

• Stability (Load Sharing and Failure Modes): preservation of endplate integrity and anterior column continuity, degree of physiological load sharing, and susceptibility to subsidence or reconstruction failure with increasing decompression span. 12

Tier I: Disc-Level Osteotomy

Tier I encompasses disc-level anterior osteotomy techniques in which comprehensive bilateral uncovertebral release restores a segmental hinging fulcrum. 14 Biomechanically, this approach maximizes angular efficiency by removing lateral constraints and enabling controlled segmental lordosis. Clinically, Tier I functions as the most effective corrective lever when deformity is driven primarily by segmental stiffness rather than by extensive retro-vertebral pathology.

Tier II: Partial Corpectomy

Tier II includes targeted strategies (e.g., trumpet-shape decompression, trapezoidal corpectomy, oblique corpectomy) designed to access focal posterior pathology while maintaining vertebral body integrity.15-17 The biomechanical intent is to balance targeted decompression with anterior column load sharing, accepting selective rather than maximal sagittal correction in exchange for minimized subsidence risk.

Tier III: Complete Corpectomy

Tier III represents complete vertebral body resection with strut reconstruction. Although this approach provides the most direct ventral decompression corridor, its corrective capacity in rigid deformity is constrained by long-span strut biomechanics. 18 Alignment change is mediated primarily through construct positioning rather than segmental release, resulting in a predictable correction ceiling. The associated structural cost “loss of anterior column continuity” increases susceptibility to reconstruction failure as the decompression span lengthens.19,20

Tier IV: Vertebral-Body Translation Techniques

Tier IV comprises translation-based anterior osteotomy techniques in which the vertebral body–pathology complex is mobilized and translated en bloc (e.g., VBSO, ACAF).21,22 The defining biomechanical advantage of this strategy is multifaceted. First, it prioritizes procedural safety by achieving decompression without direct manipulation at the adhesive dura-OPLL interface. Second, unlike resection-based strategies, it preserves anterior column continuity, maintaining a robust biological load-bearing axis rather than relying on a long-span strut. Crucially, this approach integrates the corrective advantages of anterior discectomy: by utilizing multiple adjustable interfaces-comprising both the osteotomy sites and the adjacent disc spaces-it creates a multi-level lever arm that facilitates superior segmental lordosis correction and K-line conversion compared to static corpectomy constructs. 23

Evidence Synthesis Along the ACO Triad

Alignment: Why Resection Extent Fails to Predict Sagittal Correction

Across the reviewed literature, sagittal alignment correction is more consistently explained by the mechanism of correction than by the extent of ventral bony resection, directly supporting the Alignment domain of the ACO Triad. In multilevel disease, corpectomy-based constructs frequently demonstrate a correction ceiling, particularly in rigid deformity, despite aggressive decompression.18,23 Mechanistically, this limitation reflects a fundamental geometric conflict: attempting to reconstruct a curved lordotic alignment with a straight strut graft results in a chord-spanning effect, whereby the construct bridges the deformity apex without restoring its curvature. 19 This phenomenon creates a correction ceiling, as clearly demonstrated in Figure 2.

Translation-based techniques (Tier IV) appear to inherit this advantage by enabling en bloc anterior displacement of the pathological segment. 24 Because fixation in these strategies relies on anterior column lengthening rather than static strut positioning, translation synergistically amplifies sagittal correction. Collectively, these observations underscore a core principle: effective sagittal realignment in rigid cervical deformity depends on restoring apical control through segmental hinging or achieving en bloc translation, rather than simply enlarging the decompression corridor. Thus, alignment outcomes are governed by how correction is mechanically generated, not by how much bone is removed. Stability: Load Sharing Versus Load Bearing as the Determinant of Construct Durability

From a biomechanical perspective, construct stability in anterior cervical osteotomy is governed less by the extent of decompression than by how physiological load transmission is preserved or disrupted. Specifically, stability reflects whether axial and shear forces are shared across intact endplates and residual vertebral bodies or forcibly borne by a long-span reconstructive strut, representing a transition from load sharing to load bearing.19,26,27

In strategies that preserve segmental endplates and anterior column continuity (Tier I and Tier II), physiological load sharing is largely maintained, allowing compressive forces to be distributed across multiple interfaces. In contrast, complete corpectomy constructs (Tier III) inherently eliminate anterior column continuity, obligating the reconstruction to function as a load-bearing strut spanning multiple levels. As reconstruction span increases, this forced load-bearing behavior amplifies endplate stress, shear forces, and micromotion, predisposing to subsidence and structural failure.

Importantly, reported modifiers such as oblique graft placement further exacerbate this transition by increasing shear components and reducing effective endplate contact area, underscoring that failure is mechanistically driven rather than stochastic. 28 Translation-based strategies (Tier IV) occupy a distinct position within this continuum by redistributing the load path without interrupting vertebral body continuity, thereby mitigating the load-bearing penalties associated with long-span resection constructs.22,29,30

Collectively, these observations indicate that construct durability is determined by the preservation of physiological load-sharing pathways rather than by decompression extent alone, reinforcing Stability as a mechanistic pillar of the ACO Triad.

Safety: Mechanism-specific and Collateral Anatomical Risks in Anterior Cervical Osteotomy

The Safety domain of the ACO Triad is most clearly illustrated in adhesive OPLL, where operative risk is qualitatively different rather than merely increased. In DLS-positive disease, the primary hazard is loss of a safe dissection plane at the dura-OPLL interface, and anterior direct extirpation is associated with substantially increased rates of dural tear and cerebrospinal fluid leakage. 13 Figure 4 exemplifies this mechanism-specific risk, where direct extirpation (Tier III) of a massive ossified mass resulted in a dural defect and persistent cerebrospinal fluid leakage. As shown in Figure 5, by mobilizing the vertebral body-OPLL complex ventrally en bloc, the technique achieves indirect decompression and bypasses the high-risk dural interface, thereby preventing dural complications despite massive ossification.

Beyond the dura-OPLL interface, anterior cervical osteotomy introduces collateral anatomical risks that are tier- and boundary-dependent. First, lateral boundary violation during uncinate resection and aggressive uncovertebral release (Tier I) can place the vertebral artery at risk, with CT-based analyses highlighting segment-specific vulnerability (notably C5-6) during uncinectomy. 31

Second, effective decompression may paradoxically precipitate postoperative C5 palsy through nerve-root tethering mechanisms. Unlike posterior surgery where the cord drifts backward, in anterior osteotomy-based strategies, the mechanism involves the anterior expansion of the dural sac into the decompressed space and iatrogenic foraminal narrowing secondary to the restoration of lordosis. This complication profile is strongly influenced by the reconstructive strategy. Recent large-scale evidence, including a network meta-analysis by Takase et al. 32 (2024) and a systematic review by Muthu et al. 33 demonstrates a clear risk hierarchy: multilevel anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF, Tier III) carries a significantly higher incidence of C5 palsy compared to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion.24,32-34

Mechanistically, substantial changes in canal geometry induced by multilevel corpectomy (Tier III) or extensive translation (Tier IV) increase the potential for anterior tethering on the C5 nerve roots. While Tier IV strategies (VBSO) prioritize dural safety by avoiding direct manipulation, their profound indirect decompression effect, accompanied by substantial lordosis restoration, inherently facilitates maximal anterior spinal cord shift. Consequently, the mechanism-specific susceptibility to C5 palsy remains comparable to, or potentially higher than, that of long-segment corpectomy. This risk profile is corroborated by clinical data, with C5 palsy incidence reported at 4.6% in Korean VBSO cohorts 24 and ranging from 0% to 3.1% in Chinese ACAF studies.35,36 Therefore, as the tier of osteotomy escalates to achieve greater decompression and lordosis, vigilance regarding prophylactic foraminotomy and intraoperative monitoring becomes increasingly critical.

Finally, anterior exposure and geometric translation can impose clinically meaningful visceral and soft-tissue risks. Dysphagia is notably prevalent following VBSO-based strategies (Tier IV), underscoring a mechanism-specific hazard where anterior protrusion of the translated vertebral body encroaches upon the retro-pharyngeal space—a geometric constraint distinct from resection-based techniquesv.24,29 While recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and esophageal perforation are recognized risks of any anterior approach, the extensive lateral dissection required for aggressive uncovertebral release (Tier I) or lateral osteotomy troughs (Tier IV) places the sympathetic chain at higher risk. 37 Consequently, Horner’s syndrome represents a tier-relevant hazard linked to the lateral extent of sub-longus colli dissection, 38 rather than merely a generic exposure complication.

Alignment: Mechanism of Sagittal Correction

The Alignment domain addresses the fundamental question: Is sagittal realignment required, and can the deformity apex be mechanically controlled? From a biomechanical perspective, effective correction depends on the ability to restore controlled apical hinging or to achieve en bloc translation of the pathological segment, rather than on the extent of bony resection alone. Accordingly, when angular correction is the primary objective, Tier I strategies are favored in the presence of residual segmental flexibility, whereas Tier IV translation-based mechanisms are prioritized in rigid deformities where hinging capacity is exhausted. Resection-based constructs (Tier III) occupy a limited role within this domain, as their contribution to alignment correction is constrained by a reproducible correction ceiling.

Stability: Preservation of Physiological Load Sharing

Within the Stability domain, the guiding question is: Can anterior column integrity and physiological load sharing be preserved across the planned reconstruction span? The critical biomechanical consideration is that disruption of vertebral body continuity progressively shifts constructs toward forced load-bearing behavior, thereby increasing the risks of subsidence and structural failure with increasing span. Consequently, the framework favors mechanisms that preserve or restore load-sharing pathways, including Tier II strategies with partial structural preservation and Tier IV approaches that maintain vertebral continuity through translation. In contrast, Tier III corpectomy-based reconstruction is carefully scrutinized, particularly in multilevel applications, due to its inherent compromise of this stability principle.

Safety: Management of Anatomical Risk Boundaries

The Safety domain interrogates the anatomical feasibility of direct decompression by asking: Is there a safe dissection plane at the neuro-dural interface? In the setting of adhesive pathology, such as OPLL with radiographic evidence of dural involvement, direct extirpation becomes mechanistically hazardous irrespective of technical execution. Under these conditions, the framework mandates a shift toward indirect decompression mechanisms—most notably Tier IV translation-based strategies—which achieve canal expansion without violating the dura–OPLL interface. Direct decompression techniques (Tier II or Tier III) are reserved for scenarios in which dural risk is judged acceptable and a reliable anatomical boundary is preserved.

By systematically interrogating these three biomechanical domains, the ACO Triad ensures that osteotomy tier selection is driven not merely by technical feasibility or decompression extent, but by mechanistic optimization across the competing constraints of alignment correction, construct stability, and procedural safety.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This review reframes anterior cervical osteotomy not as a spectrum of increasing resection, but as a mechanism-driven continuum of correction strategies governed by three interdependent constraints: alignment restoration, construct stability, and procedural safety (the ACO Triad). Our synthesis demonstrates that escalation of bony resection, such as multilevel corpectomy, does not linearly translate into greater sagittal correction, especially in rigid cervical kyphosis. Instead, sagittal realignment is predominantly determined by the mechanism of release or translation, rather than the volume of decompression alone.

Within this framework, disc-level osteotomy (Tier I) and vertebral-body translation techniques (Tier IV) emerge as biomechanically distinct from corpectomy-based strategies. Tier I maximizes segmental hinging and angular gain, whereas Tier IV achieves decompression and alignment indirectly by en bloc anterior translation of the pathological vertebral segment. In contrast, Tier III corpectomy, while providing direct ventral access, demonstrates a reproducible correction ceiling and an unfavorable escalation of structural failure as reconstruction span increases.

Collectively, these findings challenge the long-standing assumption that “more extensive anterior resection yields superior correction,” and instead support a paradigm in which mechanism-specific selection is central to optimizing outcomes in complex cervical deformity and OPLL.

Mechanistic Integration: The ACO Triad as a Biomechanical Triangle

Collectively, the Alignment, Stability, and Safety domains should not be interpreted as independent considerations, but as interdependent axes that define a biomechanical decision space for anterior cervical osteotomy. Alignment addresses how sagittal curvature is generated and controlled via apical hinging or en bloc translation, whereas Stability describes how physiological loads are transmitted or disrupted, distinguishing load-sharing constructs from forced load-bearing struts. Safety, in turn, reflects which anatomical boundaries are engaged by a given corrective mechanism, including the dura-OPLL interface, lateral neurovascular structures, and anterior visceral envelope.

These three domains form a biomechanical triangle in which optimization along one axis necessarily influences the others. Strategies that maximize angular correction at the deformity apex may alter load transmission or increase engagement of lateral or anterior anatomical boundaries, whereas approaches that prioritize safety by avoiding direct dural manipulation may redistribute loads or shift correction from angular deformation to translation. Within this framework, anterior cervical osteotomy is best understood not as a hierarchy of increasing resection, but as a set of mechanistically distinct solutions that occupy different regions of the Alignment–Stability–Safety decision space.

Framing surgical selection within this biomechanical triangle clarifies why no single anterior strategy is universally optimal, and why escalation of resection alone cannot resolve the competing demands of deformity correction, construct durability, and procedural safety. Instead, effective decision-making requires explicit recognition of the dominant limiting constraint in each case and selection of the osteotomy mechanism that best balances the three axes of the ACO Triad.

Biomechanical Interpretation: Why Resection Fails Where Release or Translation Succeeds

From a biomechanical standpoint, the Alignment domain of the ACO Triad is governed by two fundamental mechanisms: (1) restoration of controlled motion segments capable of angular deformation or (2) displacement of the pathological mass away from the neural axis. Corpectomy-based constructs primarily rely on long-span strut behavior, which resists bending and tends to preserve preoperative alignment, particularly in rigid deformities.18,19 As the number of resected vertebrae increases, the reconstruction increasingly behaves as a straight load-bearing column, limiting lordotic gain despite aggressive decompression.

Disc-level osteotomy, by contrast, restores a segmental fulcrum through bilateral uncovertebral release and posterior longitudinal ligament relaxation. This enables cumulative angular correction across multiple levels when residual flexibility exists. 14 However, its efficacy diminishes in rigid kyphosis where segmental mobility is exhausted.

Translation-based techniques represent a fundamentally different biomechanical solution. Rather than relying on angular deformation, vertebral-body translation repositions the entire vertebral–OPLL complex anteriorly, increasing canal dimensions and facilitating spinal cord drift without requiring dural separation.10,22 This strategy effectively decouples decompression from resection and explains why Tier IV approaches can simultaneously address alignment, canal compromise, and safety in scenarios where traditional release or resection strategies fail.

Decision Logic, Boundary Conditions, and Anticipated Critiques

A potential concern with any tiered framework is the perceived overlap in clinical indications, as well as the influence of case selection and technical expertise on reported outcomes. These issues warrant clarification, not as limitations of the model, but as intrinsic features of complex cervical pathology.

The proposed classification is intentionally mechanism-based rather than outcome-driven. It does not presume equivalence between tiers, nor does it suggest that techniques should be directly compared across heterogeneous patient populations. The framework guides preoperative strategy by identifying the dominant limiting factor within each domain: insufficient angular mobility in Alignment, compromised load sharing in Stability, or unacceptable dural risk in Safety.

Within this context, selection bias and learning-curve effects 39 are not confounders to be eliminated, but variables that are explicitly incorporated into decision-making. Overlap in clinical presentation reflects the reality that multiple biomechanical solutions may coexist for a given pathology, each associated with distinct trade-offs along the Alignment–Stability–Safety triad. The value of the framework lies not in enforcing rigid boundaries, but in structuring this complexity into a transparent and reproducible decision space.

Clinical Implications: A Mechanism-Based Selection Strategy

When interpreted through the ACO Triad, these considerations reflect dominant biomechanical priorities rather than prescriptive recommendations. The tiered classification proposed here is not intended to promote technical novelty, but to facilitate mechanism based surgical selection. In practical terms, disc level osteotomy (Tier I) should be prioritized for its superior angular efficiency whenever residual flexibility exists. For cases where retro vertebral pathology dominates but alignment is preserved, limited corpectomy (Tier II) serves to balance access with stability. In the presence of rigid deformity where adhesion risk is acceptable, corpectomy (Tier III) remains a viable option, provided there is clear recognition of its correction ceiling and structural penalties. Crucially, when rigidity and adhesive OPLL coexist, translation based strategies (Tier IV) should be considered early rather than as salvage procedures. This approach shifts decision making away from procedural hierarchy and toward biomechanical intent, aligning surgical strategy with the dominant limiting constraint in each case.

The Influence of Posterior Column Rigidity

While the ACO Triad primarily focuses on anterior column reconstruction, the preoperative state of the posterior elements, specifically facet arthrosis, may influence the overall surgical plan under certain conditions. In our routine practice, we utilize preoperative dynamic radiographs and computed tomography (CT) to carefully evaluate the facets for severe ankylosis or complete fusion. If a CT scan reveals completely fused facets driving rigid kyphosis, the posterior tension band will restrict anterior mobilization. Under such circumstances, the surgical sequence may need to be altered to a posterior-anterior-posterior (P-A-P) approach, beginning with an initial posterior facet release to unlock the spine prior to the anterior osteotomy. However, in the illustrative cases presented in this review, none exhibited completely ankylosed or fused facets; thus, an anterior-then-posterior (A-P) strategy was sufficient. During the subsequent posterior stage of our A-P approach, we routinely perform posterior facet capsular releases. It is important to note, however, that the majority of the cervical lordosis is already established and secured by the anterior plate during the anterior procedure. Consequently, the ability to further increase lordosis substantially through posterior instrumentation alone is limited once the anterior construct is fixed.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This article presents a conceptual framework derived from current literature. A limitation of this narrative review is that many included studies are retrospective case series (Level IV evidence). Consequently, the ACO Triad should be viewed as a decision-support tool to guide surgical logic, rather than a rigid algorithm derived from high-level randomized data. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the predictive value of the ACO Triad. Further research should aim to substantiate the biomechanical superiority of translation mechanisms over volumetric resection, particularly regarding their impact on construct stability and procedural safety in complex deformities.

Conclusions

Anterior cervical osteotomy should be understood as a comprehensive biomechanical framework rather than a ladder of increasing resection. Effective correction of complex cervical deformity depends less on how much bone is removed than on how alignment, stability, and safety are mechanistically addressed within the ACO Triad. By reframing anterior strategies through the ACO Triad and a tiered classification, this review provides a rational, mechanism-driven foundation for surgical decision-making in complex cervical disease.
