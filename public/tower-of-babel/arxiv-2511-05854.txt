Can a Small Model Learn to Look Before It Leaps? Dynamic Learning and Proactive Correction for Hallucination Detection
Zepeng Bao, Shen Zhou, Qiankun Pi, Jianhao Chen, Mayi Xu, Ming Zhong, Yuanyuan Zhu, Tieyun Qian

Abstract
Hallucination in large language models (LLMs) remains a critical barrier to their safe deployment. Existing tool-augmented hallucination detection methods require pre-defined fixed verification strategies, which are crucial to the quality of tool calls. Some methods directly employ powerful closed-source LLMs such as GPT-4 as detectors, which are effective but too costly. To mitigate the cost issue, some methods adopt the teacher-student architecture and finetune open-source small models as detectors via agent tuning. However, these methods are limited by fixed strategies. When faced with a dynamically changing execution environment, they may lack adaptability and inappropriately call tools, ultimately leading to detection failure. To address the problem of insufficient strategy adaptability, we propose the innovative “Learning to Evaluate and Adaptively Plan” (LEAP) framework, which endows an efficient student model with the dynamic learning and proactive correction capabilities of the teacher model. Specifically, our method formulates the hallucination detection problem as a dynamic strategy learning problem. We first employ a teacher model to generate trajectories within the dynamic learning loop and dynamically adjust the strategy based on execution failures. We then distill this dynamic planning capability into an efficient student model via agent tuning. Finally, during strategy execution, the student model adopts a proactive correction mechanism, enabling it to propose, review, and optimize its own verification strategies before execution. We demonstrate through experiments on three challenging benchmarks that our LEAP-tuned model outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are profoundly reshaping numerous fields with their unprecedented capabilities
(
Zhao et al. 2023
)
, yet their widespread adoption is shadowed by a critical and inherent challenge:
hallucination
. This tendency to generate content that is factually incorrect, logically inconsistent with the provided context, or entirely fabricated, poses significant risks, particularly in high-stake domains such as medicine, law, and finance
(
Ji et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Consequently, the development of a truly trustworthy LLM necessitates not only fluency and instruction-following but also the ability to discern the veracity of its own outputs. This positions
hallucination detection
—the task of accurately assessing whether generated content is faithful to factual reality—as a critical hurdle on the path toward safe and reliable LLM deployment
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
.

Prevailing research on hallucination detection falls into two paradigms:
intrinsic self-check
and
tool-augmented verification
. The former leverages a model’s internal signals for hallucination flagging, such as token probabilities, self-consistency checks
(
Manakul, Liusie, and Gales 2023
)
, or patterns in activation states
(
Du, Xiao, and Li 2024
;
Bazarova et al. 2025
)
. However, these methods are fundamentally capped by a model’s own knowledge boundaries and may fail when the model is confidently wrong.

Figure 1:
Fixed strategies and dynamic strategies for hallucination detection on diverse claims.

This limitation has spurred a paradigm rooted in tool-augmented verification.
The core of this paradigm is a predefined and fixed verification strategy that remains constant regardless of the claim.
Following this strategy, a model engages in multi-turn interactions with external tools such as search engine to make a final judgment based on the returned information.
Some methods
(
Chern et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2024
)
employ powerful closed-source LLMs such as GPT-4 as direct detector, which is effective but expensive
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
. To mitigate this cost, some methods
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
use closed-source LLMs as teacher models, collecting trajectories to finetune small and efficient student models via agent tuning.
However, both approaches are fundamentally constrained by the fixed verification strategy.
This fixed approach lacks the adaptability needed for dynamically changing execution environments, often resulting in inappropriate tool calls and failure detection.
As illustrated in Figure
1
, a fixed strategy applies the same verification approach to every claim.
Although this offers some generality, it generates inaccurate tool input because it overlooks the causal mechanism in the
coffee
claim and the discriminating descriptor “hopping” in the
kangaroo
claim leading to detection failure in both scenarios.
The failure lies not in the tool, but in the strategy’s inability to ask the right question.

Our work aims to bridge this gap by introducing a novel
L
earning to
E
valuate and
A
daptively
P
lan (
LEAP
) framework, which leverages an efficient teacher-student architecture equipped with a dynamic verification strategy for hallucination detection. It consists of three parts: a teacher model first generates diverse strategies and trajectories, agent tuning then distills these capabilities into a student model, and finally, the student model adaptively selects the appropriate strategy.
However, the complexity of hallucinations makes it impractical to predefine an optimal strategy for every claim. Furthermore, external tools may return noisy information, challenging the formulation of a strategy that can consistently find effective evidence. Therefore, the first challenge is how to generate a diverse set of high quality strategies.
In addition, different claims require different strategies, which requires a deep understanding of both the claim structure and how strategies utilize tools before execution. This leads to the second challenge: how to ensure the adaptive selection of the appropriate strategy.

LEAP is engineered to systematically address these issues.
To generate diverse strategies, we employ a powerful teacher model within a dynamic learning loop, allowing it to learn from execution failures and dynamically create a diverse and evolving set of strategies.
To achieve adaptive selection, we propose a novel proactive correction mechanism
which adaptively evaluates and refines strategies for each specific claim before execution. This ensures that the model can always utilize an appropriate verification strategy.
Figure
1
shows LEAP generates the dynamic strategy for each claim. For the
kangaroo
claim, it forms a multistep strategy that first uses “hopping” to identify the subject before verifying its family. For the
coffee
claim, a causal verification strategy is devised to specifically query the scientific mechanism.
This adaptive approach enables the gathering of more effective evidence, leading to robust detection.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose LEAP, a new framework to transfer a powerful model’s capabilities into an efficient one via learning, distilling, and adaptively applying verification strategies.

•

We propose a novel proactive correction mechanism, which a learned critic evaluates and triggers the refinement of verification strategies before execution, enhancing the robustness of strategy execution.

•

Experiments on three datasets validate the superiority of our framework over baselines based on the fixed strategy in hallucination detection scenarios.

Related Work

2.1
Hallucination Detection

Hallucination detection aims to assess the veracity of content from LLMs, a critical step towards ensuring their reliability
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
. Existing methods fall into two main paradigms: intrinsic self-check and tool-augmented verification.
Intrinsic methods operate without external knowledge, leveraging signals like token probabilities for uncertainty estimation
(
Varshney et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2023a
;
Luo, Xiao, and Ma 2024
)
, self-consistency
(
Manakul, Liusie, and Gales 2023
;
Cheng et al. 2025
)
, or patterns in the model’s internal activations
(
Du, Xiao, and Li 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Bazarova et al. 2025
)
.
Although insightful, these methods are limited by the model’s knowledge boundaries, failing to spot highly confident but incorrect beliefs.
Tool-augmented methods address this limitation by external tools. Some methods
(
Min et al. 2023
;
Chern et al. 2023
;
Dhuliawala et al. 2024
;
Wei et al. 2024
;
Xie et al. 2025
)
leverage closed-source models to execute verification strategies.
Some methods
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
use smaller models for efficiency.
Despite scale differences,
they all adhere to a predefined and fixed verification strategy. This makes them brittle when faced with complex claims or noisy evidence, as they lack the ability to adapt dynamically.
Thus, the core challenge of learning adaptive strategies remains unsolved.

2.2
Agent Tuning

Agent tuning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for allowing smaller models to learn sophisticated behaviors by finetuning them on high quality decision trajectories
(
Zeng et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Lai et al. 2025
)
.
Although prior work has successfully distilled strategies for general purpose reasoning and planning
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Yao et al. 2023b
;
Shinn et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Bo et al. 2024
)
, its application to hallucination detection
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
reveals a critical limitation.
The student model’s capability is fundamentally capped by the nature of the distilled trajectories, which distills from a teacher executing a fixed verification strategy. The core limitation is that the student learns to follow a fixed strategy but not how to adapt and correct when the strategy itself is flawed.
This highlights the need to distill a strategy that is inherently dynamic and adaptive.

3
Method

Figure 2:
The LEAP framework with its (a) workflow and (b) core components, including three main steps:
1. Dynamic Strategy Learning and Trajectory Generation Using Teacher Model: A teacher model uses the dynamic learning loop to learn from failure and generate trajectories.
2. Capability Distillation via Agent Tuning: The trajectories are distilled into an efficient student model.
3. Detection with Proactive Correction Using Student Model: The student model adaptively refines its plan before execution to ensure appropriate strategies.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Given a claim consisting of a user query
Q
and a response
R
generated by the model, our ultimate goal is to predict a binary label
Y\in\{\text{Hallucination},\text{Not Hallucination}\}
.
Rather than directly mapping the claim
(Q,R)
to
Y
, our approach focuses on optimizing the evidence gathering process that informs the final decision.
A high quality process is guided by an appropriate strategy for selecting and using information gathering tools. We propose that the final verdict is determined by the quality of this evidence gathering process.

Given our premise that strategy is critical for effective information gathering, we reframe hallucination detection as a learning problem of dynamic strategies and formalize this process through a verification strategy
\pi_{strat}
,
which orchestrates the verification process, as illustrated in Figure
1
.
Our goal is to learn an optimal strategy
\pi_{strat}^{*}
that is both effective and dynamically adaptable to the specific claim.

Strategy execution relies on the collaboration of multiple specialized agents, as hallucinations are complex and diverse, requiring them to jointly operate the detection framework. Their interactions are captured in a verification trajectory
\tau
similar to ReAct
(
Yao et al. 2023c
)
. A trajectory is a sequence of states and the transitions between them, composed of interleaved thoughts, actions, and observations:

\tau=(s_{0},t_{1},a_{1},o_{1},s_{1},t_{2},a_{2},o_{2},s_{2},\dots,s_{N}),

where
s_{n}
represents the state at step
n
, which includes the initial claim and the history of all prior steps
(t_{i},a_{i},o_{i})_{i=1}^{n-1}
. Each state consists of:

•

Thought (
t_{i}
)
: The explicit reasoning guided by the overarching strategy
\pi_{strat}
, analyzing the current state to decide the next action.

•

Action (
a_{i}
)
: A concrete operation, typically an external tool call such as Search(query).

•

Observation (
o_{i}
)
: The output returned from the tool after executing action
a_{i}
.

The final verdict
Y
is determined by the terminal state
s_{N}
. Since the initial strategy
\pi_{strat}
orchestrates the interactions generating this trajectory, the final verdict is a direct result of the initial plan. Therefore, the core challenge shifts from learning a simple classifier to discovering an optimal verification strategy
\pi_{strat}^{*}
, that consistently guides the agent interaction to a correct and robust verdict.

3.2
Overview

To overcome the insufficient adaptability of the fixed verification strategy, we propose LEAP, a framework designed to learn, distill and adaptively apply verification strategies.
As illustrated in Figure
2
, our framework consists of three stages.
First, to gather diverse strategies, we employ dynamic strategy learning, using a powerful teacher model within a dynamic learning loop that systematically learns from execution failures to continually generate superior strategies.
Second, to transfer dynamic learning capabilities into an efficient small model, we utilize agent tuning to distill the dynamic learning capabilities from the teacher model’s trajectories into the efficient student model.
Finally, to ensure that the appropriate strategy is adaptive in dynamical execution environments, we introduce a proactive correction mechanism, which can adaptively evaluate and refine its own verification strategies for each specific claim before execution, thus ensuring robust performance.

3.3
Dynamic Strategy Learning

The first phase is dynamic strategy learning designed to generate the diverse verification strategies absent in fixed strategy methods.
We achieve this through a closed loop where four agents collaborate to systematically learn from execution failures: the planner designs a strategy, the actor executes it to produce a trajectory, the critic evaluates the outcome, and for any failures, the reflector generates corrective feedback that is fed back to the planner.
This iterative process not only drives the continuous evolution of strategies, but also yields the high quality trajectories required for the subsequent agent tuning phase.

Planner: Strategy Design

The planner is an agent responsible for creating a high-level customized verification strategy
\pi_{strat}
for an input claim, using past experiences to go beyond fixed strategies. It generates the strategy as follows:

\pi_{strat}=\text{Planner}(s_{0},\mathcal{P}_{p},\mathcal{R}_{\text{retrieved}}),

(1)

where
s_{0}
is the initial state containing the claim,
\mathcal{P}_{p}
is a static prompt with task instructions, and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{retrieved}}
is a set of relevant reflections retrieved from memory. A complete strategy
\pi_{strat}
usually specifies the type of problem, a high-level verification strategy, and a concrete verification plan.

To inform its planning, the planner performs reflection retrieval, querying a memory
\mathcal{M}_{P}
to find the
K
most relevant past reflections.

\mathcal{R}_{\text{retrieved}}=\{r\mid\text{rank}(d(r,s_{0}))<K,r\in\mathcal{M}_{P}\},

(2)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Euclidean distance between text embeddings, implemented with FAISS
(
Johnson, Douze, and Jégou 2019
)
for efficient retrieval. The retrieved reflections provide relevant experiences for the planner to synthesize a new strategy. When the reflector generates a new reflection
r_{\text{new}}
from a failure, it is integrated into the memory:

\mathcal{M}_{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{P}\cup\{r_{\text{new}}\}.

(3)

Actor: Strategy Execution

The actor executes the verification strategy
\pi_{strat}
by generating the verification trajectory
\tau
. To do so, it performs a series of actions, primarily by invoking external tools. We equip the actor with a versatile toolbox inspired by previous work
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
, including search engine, calculator, code interpreter, etc.
Details are available in the Appendix.

At each step
n
, the action
a_{n}
is determined based on the strategy
\pi_{strat}
, the current state
s_{n}
, and a dynamically retrieved set of relevant examples
\Psi^{n}
:

a_{n}=\text{Actor}(s_{n},\pi_{strat},\mathcal{P}_{A},\Psi^{n}),

(4)

where
\Psi^{n}
is retrieved based on similarity to the current claim from the actor’s memory
\mathcal{M}_{A}
, which stores tuples of (claim, strategy, advantage value).
Based on the stored advantage value
A
, it’s composed of samples from both successful precedents
\Psi_{\text{pos}}^{n}
for
A>0
and cautionary tales
\Psi_{\text{neg}}^{n}
for
A\leq 0
.
Upon completing the trajectory
\tau
, the actor passes it to the critic for evaluation. The actor’s memory is updated with the strategy and its evaluated advantage:

\mathcal{M}_{A}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{A}\cup\{(s_{0},\pi_{strat},A(\pi_{strat},\tau))\},

(5)

where
A(\pi_{strat},\tau)
is the advantage value calculated by the critic.

Critic: Strategy Evaluation

The critic is an evaluator agent that provides a quantitative feedback signal by calculating the advantage value
A(\pi_{strat},\tau)
for a completed trajectory
\tau
. This value measures the quality of the strategy by comparing its actual result with a baseline learned.

To compute the advantage, the critic first estimates a state-value function
V(s_{n})
, which represents the expected future outcome of being in state
s_{n}
. The critic models this function directly, using its memory
\mathcal{M}_{C}
to store past (state, value) pairs. This memory helps the critic learn to fit the value function via in-context learning:

V(s_{n})=\text{Critic}(s_{n},\mathcal{P}_{C},\mathcal{M}_{C}).

(6)

After each trajectory, the newly computed state values are used to update the memory:

\mathcal{M}_{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{C}\cup\{(s_{n},V(s_{n}))\mid s_{n}\in\tau\}.

(7)

With the learned value function, we compute the advantage. We adapt the commonly used advantage function to our task. The final result is represented by the terminal reward
R_{T}
.
The advantage value is:

A(\pi_{strat},\tau)=R_{T}+\gamma*V(s_{n+1})-V(s_{n})-\lambda*N_{\text{tools}},

(8)

where
V(s_{n})
denotes the state reward at step
n
,
\lambda
is a penalty coefficient, and
N_{\text{tools}}
is the number of tool calls. This advantage value provides a fine-grained signal indicating the strategy’s performance beyond simple correctness.

Reflector: Strategy Evolution

The reflector is the engine agent for strategy evolution, operating specifically on failures. When a trajectory is assigned a negative value by the critic, the reflector generates corrective feedback:

r_{\text{new}}=\text{Reflector}(\tau_{\text{fail}},\mathcal{P}_{R}),

(9)

using a specialized prompt
\mathcal{P}_{R}
, the reflector analyzes the failed trajectory
\tau_{\text{fail}}
and generates a structured reflection
r_{\text{new}}
, which contains diagnosis of failures, generalizable high-level principles to prevent similar errors, and a revised verification strategy. The new reflection
r_{\text{new}}
is then added to the planner’s memory
\mathcal{M}_{P}
, directly closing the learning loop and systematically converting failures into improved future strategies.

3.4
Agent Tuning

The second phase is agent tuning, where we finetune efficient small models using the trajectories collected in the first phase. The trajectories provide the entire reasoning process, allowing the student model to learn how to plan, not just what the result is.
This process distills dynamic learning capabilities, resulting in a small but powerful detector capable of adaptive learning.

Trajectory Collection

First, we employ the LEAP framework that uses a powerful model to process verification tasks from commonly used benchmarks such as HaluEval
(
Li et al. 2023
)
and MMLU-Pro
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
To ensure the quality of training data, we perform a strict curation process. We retain only the trajectories, those that not only reach the correct final verdict but also exhibit high efficiency. This quality is quantified by the advantage value computed by the critic; we filter for trajectories where
A(\pi_{strat},\tau)
is positive. This process yields high quality trajectories
\mathcal{D}_{\text{expert}}
.

Trajectory Finetuning

We use these trajectories to finetune small models with LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
. This process teaches the small model to mimic the reasoning process of the framework. For each trajectory
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{expert}}
, the input is the initial state
s_{0}
, which contains the claim
(Q,R)
. The target output
\tau^{\prime}
is the linearized sequence of all pairs of thought and action
(t_{1},a_{1},t_{2},a_{2},\dots,t_{T},a_{T})
. The model is trained to maximize the likelihood of generating this target reasoning trace given the initial claim, using a standard causal language modeling loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)=-\sum_{(s_{0},\tau^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{expert}}}\sum_{j=1}^{|\tau^{\prime}|}\log P_{\theta}(\tau^{\prime}_{j}\mid s_{0},\tau^{\prime}_{<j}),

(10)

where
\theta
represents the trainable LoRA parameters and
\tau^{\prime}_{j}
is the
j
-th token in the target sequence
\tau^{\prime}
.

3.5
Proactive Correction

The final phase is the proactive correction mechanism designed to ensure that the tuned small model adaptively selects the appropriate strategy in dynamical execution environments.

Given a claim to verify, represented by the initial state
s_{0}
, the detection begins with finetuned planner. Following Equation
1
, it generates an initial verification strategy
\pi_{strat}
. Instead of passing on this strategy directly to the actor, we introduce a critical preemptive evaluation step. The strategy
\pi_{strat}
is first sent to finetuned critic, which assesses its potential quality before the strategy execution. This is different from the conventional reflection based on the results. The critic produces an estimated advantage score:

\hat{A}(\pi_{strat})=\text{Critic}_{\text{tuned}}(s_{0},\pi_{strat},\mathcal{P}_{C},\mathcal{M}_{C}),

(11)

where
\hat{A}(\pi_{strat})
predicts the likely success and efficiency of the proposed strategy.

The predicted advantage is then compared to a confidence threshold
\theta_{\text{corr}}
. If the strategy is deemed sufficiently robust (i.e.
\hat{A}(\pi_{strat})\geq\theta_{\text{corr}}
), it is approved and passed to the actor. If the score falls below the threshold, the proactive correction loop is triggered. The flawed strategy is sent to the reflector, which generates corrective feedback
r_{\text{corr}}
. This feedback, which contains insights on potential failure points, is then routed back to the planner. The planner incorporates this feedback to generate a revised superior strategy
\pi^{\prime}_{strat}
. This closed-loop process of evaluation and revision ensures that only high quality strategies guide the verification. Once a strategy is approved, the finetuned actor takes over. It executes the strategy, generating the thought-action trace
\tau^{\prime}
by interacting with the necessary tools. This execution ultimately leads to the final verdict on hallucination detection.

4.2
Main Results and Analysis

Table
1
demonstrates the consistent superiority of LEAP. Across all evaluated models, LEAP always achieves the best performance. For Qwen2.5-7B model, LEAP achieves an average accuracy of 69.89% and a F1 score of 72.88%, outperforming the best baseline by a margin of 7.31% in accuracy and 6.75% in F1 score.
We argue that accuracy is the most critical metric for this task, as it directly measures the correctness of the final judgment—the ultimate goal of a practical detection system. This consistent lead in accuracy stems directly from LEAP’s core methodological advantage: its ability to learn and deploy a dynamic verification strategy, overcoming the limitations of the fixed strategies.

Intrinsic methods like Self-CheckGPT are inherently limited by their lack of external knowledge, resulting in poor performance.
For tool-augmented methods that rely on closed-source models, we replace them with open-source small models for fair comparison. As results show, without the powerful reasoning capabilities of closed-source models, their performance is fundamentally limited by their fixed strategies.
Factool performs poorly on XTRUST with 47.42% accuracy on Qwen2.5-7B, which indicates that its predefined decomposition verification pipeline is easily broken by hard examples.
FIRE performs well on MMLU-Pro with Llama3.1-8B because its dynamic decision making based on verification confidence to some extent avoids the noise from excessive irrelevant evidence. However, it is similarly constrained by a fixed strategy, lacking adaptability.
SAFE achieves the best F1 score on MMLU-Pro with Mistral-8B and XTRUST with Llama3.1-8B, which stems from its atomization and repeated use of web search for verification to maximize recall.
However, this design limits its accuracy on complex relational hallucinations, as these errors do not stem from incorrect individual facts, but rather from the flawed logical and semantic connections that yield a false conclusion.
This explains why its accuracy can be poor despite a high F1 score, e.g. the poor accuracy of 55.81% on MMLU-Pro with Qwen2.5-7B.
LEAP’s focus on strategic over exhaustive atomization proves decisive for achieving superior overall accuracy, the ultimate measure of correct judgment.

Regarding agent-tuning methods such as HaluAgent, the performance gap between HaluAgent and our method stems from a fundamental difference in the distilled knowledge. HaluAgent distills trajectories from a fixed strategy, causing its student model to inherit the same fixed. In contrast, LEAP distills dynamic learning and correction capabilities. This distinction explains LEAP’s consistent outperformance, as our student model learns not merely a fixed procedure, but an adaptive strategy for learning and self-correction.

4.4
Analysis on Trajectory Distillation

A key goal of our work is to create an efficient and deployable detector. We distill the LEAP framework’s complex reasoning into a compact student model. To evaluate the success of this distillation, we compare the performance of the Qwen2.5-7B model directly with its GPT-4o mini teacher.
As shown in Table
3
, the student model performs comparably to its teacher, and even surpasses it in accuracy on both HaluEval at 74.19% and MMLU-Pro at 69.81%.
This success stems from our distillation of high quality trajectories, which enables the student model to learn the entire complex reasoning path.
It demonstrates that LEAP successfully transfers the dynamic planning and reasoning capabilities of a powerful model into a small and efficient model.

4.5
Analysis on Strategy Set Size

To investigate the impact of the scale of strategies on performance, we varied the number of strategies available to agents. Figure
4
reveals a distinct nonmonotonic relationship. Initially, performance increases as a larger strategy pool provides greater diversity and adaptability.
Performance peaks at approximately 1,400 strategies, representing an optimal balance. Beyond this point, we observe a slight but consistent degradation. We attribute this to our retrieval mechanism. As the total number of strategies in the memory grows, the likelihood increases that similarity-based retrieval which considers only a few top-k examples may fetch less relevant or lower quality strategies into the context. This introduces noise into the planning process, potentially degrading the quality of the final generated strategy. But LEAP performance remains significantly above the baselines in Table
1
, underscoring the overall robustness of our framework.

4.6
Case Study

Figure
3
presents a complex legal case where a claim contains multiple nuanced legal concepts: solicitation, conspiracy, and attempted murder. The claim subtly misapplies the concept of “attempted murder” to accidental death, creating a challenging hallucination. We compare the fixed verification process of HaluAgent with the adaptive approach of LEAP.
HaluAgent hindered by its fixed strategy adopts a naive verification plan. It attempts to validate the entire claim at once, without scrutinizing its individual components.
As a result, it retrieves only general information and misses the subtle factual error regarding “attempted murder” thus incorrectly labeling the statement as non-hallucination.

In contrast, LEAP demonstrates a multistage adaptive process.
The planner first designs an initial strategy to analyze the legal definitions of the three crimes mentioned.
Critically, before execution, the proactive correction mechanism assesses this initial strategy. It identifies the strategy as suboptimal and leverages its memory from past experiences to refine it into a more precise strategy focused on verifying the core legal elements of each crime.
This optimized strategy enables a focused execution—verifying ”attempted murder” as a distinct sub question—that successfully isolates and identifies the hallucination.
This case study highlights how LEAP’s ability to plan, critique, and revise its own strategy leads to a more robust detection process.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced LEAP, a framework designed to overcome the fixed strategy of existing hallucination detectors through three stage process. First, a powerful teacher model learns from execution failures to generate diverse verification strategies. This dynamic planning capability is then distilled into an efficient student model via agent tuning. Crucially, the student employs a proactive correction mechanism to review and adaptively refine the strategy for each specific claim before execution.
Experiments on three datasets validate the superiority of our framework over baselines based on the fixed strategy in hallucination detection scenarios.
