Post-Training Large Language Models via Reinforcement Learning from Self-Feedback
Carel van Niekerk, Renato Vukovic, Benjamin Matthias Ruppik

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) often produce plausible but poorly-calibrated answers, limiting their reliability on reasoning-intensive tasks. We present Reinforcement Learning from Self-Feedback (RLSF), a post-training stage that uses the model’s own confidence as an intrinsic reward, mimicking how humans learn in the absence of external feedback. After a frozen LLM generates several chain-of-thought solutions, we define and compute the confidence of each final answer span and rank the traces accordingly. These synthetic preferences are then used to fine-tune the policy with standard preference optimization, similar to RLHF yet requiring no human labels, gold answers, or externally curated rewards. RLSF simultaneously (i) refines the model’s probability estimates – restoring well-behaved calibration – and (ii) strengthens step-by-step reasoning, yielding improved performance on arithmetic reasoning and multiple-choice question answering. By turning a model’s own uncertainty into useful self-feedback, RLSF affirms reinforcement learning on intrinsic model behaviour as a principled and data-efficient component of the LLM post-training pipeline and warrents further research in intrinsic rewards for LLM post-training.



1
Introduction

Figure 1:

An overview of the RLSF pipeline.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have led to impressive capabilities in text generation and comprehension
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
.
Nevertheless, performance often degrades on tasks that demand logical reasoning, a critical limitation when LLMs are deployed in domains such as legal analysis, scientific computation, and decision support
(
Kambhampati 2024
)
.
While contextually appropriate text can be produced with ease, consistency, and accuracy across extended chains of reasoning is frequently not maintained.

It has also been observed that the output of an LLM is largely uncalibrated – its confidence is not predictive of its accuracy, particularly after reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is applied
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
.
Such miscalibration results in overconfidence during complex reasoning tasks
(
Tian et al. 2023
;
OpenAI et al. 2024a
)
.
It has been observed, however, that confidence plays a critical role in human learning.
Humans use confidence as an intrinsic reward in the absence of external feedback
Ptasczynski et al. 2022
.

Calibration and Reasoning

To address the challenges of calibration and reasoning, various techniques have been explored to enhance the logical reasoning capabilities of LLMs:
In Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, prompting encourages models to generate and articulate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final conclusion.
For example, prompts such as “Let us think step by step” or “Let us break it down” are used
(
Kojima et al. 2022
)
.
By making the reasoning process explicit, CoT reasoning improves the model’s ability to handle complex tasks that require logical consistency and deep understanding.
This approach, however, heavily relies on the prompt design, leading to inconsistent performance and user experience.
Going beyond prompting, there has been an explosion of models specifically trained to produce reasoning traces before answering, such as the OpenAI o-series of models
(
OpenAI et al. 2024b
;
OpenAI 2025
)
and DeepSeek-R1
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025a
)
.
But it should be noted that these methods do not address the mismatch between the model’s internal confidence and the observable accuracy.

Confidence as Reward

Our approach is based on a simple observation:
In a well-calibrated model, answer confidence is correlated with the presence of reasoning, which in turn leads to better quality answers.
We build upon this observation and use confidence as an intrinsic reward signal in reinforcement learning.

To construct our preference dataset, we apply Chain-of-Thought decoding to make the LLM generate a collection of candidate answers.
The generated beams can be ranked by the model’s answer confidence to produce a preference dataset, which can then be used to train a reward model that assesses answer quality, as in
Figure

1
.
Then this reward model is used to fine-tune the original LLM via reinforcement learning.

Practically, we implement this in a similar spirit to RLHF, and we call this post-training step
Reinforcement Learning from Self-Feedback (RLSF)
.
In particular, our proposed method can be inserted as an
additional
step in the customary model post-training pipeline
(
Kumar et al. 2025
)
, augmenting typical techniques such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Preference Optimization (PO), and task-specific reward modelling like RLVR
(
Lambert et al. 2025
)
.

Contributions

For our test bed, we focus on logical reasoning and multiple-choice questions.
This task selection is particularly relevant, because these are long-horizon problems, i.e. the system needs to make several steps before arriving at the answer.
Second, it has been observed that these are the tasks where LLMs typically struggle both in terms of accuracy and calibration.

Our main contributions are confirmed by the experiments in
Sections

4
and
5
:

1.

We provide empirical evidence that RLSF improves the performance on all examined tasks in terms of calibration, without harming accuracy.
We observe improvements even if the original model is
not
well-calibrated.

2.

A byproduct of our method is that it can infer a meaningful reward model from an LLM solely from a collection of prompts, without requiring labels or human oversight.
RLSF provides competitive performance on the considered reward benchmark by using only the prompts of the benchmark.

3.

We show that for the reasoning tasks under consideration, our self-feedback mechanism does not amplify biases of the base model.

2
Related Work

2.1
Confidence Estimation in LLMs

Well-calibrated confidence estimates improve both the trustworthiness and the performance of language models
(
Wang and Zhou 2024
)
. Recent studies have analysed calibration behaviour across pre-trained and fine-tuned LLMs.
Supervised fine-tuning on diverse datasets has been shown to preserve or even improve token-level calibration
(
Kuhn et al. 2022
;
Xiao et al. 2022
)
.
However, instruction-tuned or aligned models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) often exhibit degraded calibration
(
Tian et al. 2023
;
OpenAI et al. 2024a
)
.
This is attributed to the reward signals in RLHF optimizing for human preference and fluency, rather than correctness or calibrated uncertainty.

Traditional confidence metrics focus on the probability assigned to the final token of a generated answer.
However, this can yield high confidence for incorrect completions.
As a result, recent methods shift toward span-level confidence, computing aggregate certainty over full answers or reasoning chains, which has been shown to better reflect answer correctness
(
Kojima et al. 2022
;
Wang and Zhou 2024
)
.

To improve calibration, existing approaches include prompting LLMs to verbalize their confidence
(
Tian et al. 2023
)
, training auxiliary heads to predict correctness likelihood
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
, or fine-tuning models with explicit calibration-aware objectives
(
Kapoor et al. 2024
;
Stangel et al. 2025
)
.
While such methods improve calibration, they require labelled data.

2.2
Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

The performance of large language models (LLMs) on reasoning tasks improves when the model generates a chain of thought (CoT).
To elicit CoT reasoning,
Wei et al. 2022
include examples of multistep reasoning in the prompt, while
Kojima et al. 2022
prompt the model in a zero-shot manner to
‘‘think step by step’’
.
Reasoning capabilities can be further enhanced through specific training on CoT data
(
Chung et al. 2024
)
, or by teaching the model how to reason
(
Zelikman et al. 2022
)
.

CoT-decoding
(
Wang and Zhou 2024
)
improves logical reasoning of an LLM by making it generate multiple hypotheses and choosing the one where the answer tokens have the highest confidence. It does not necessitate specific prompting or supervised fine-tuning, but increases inference time by an order of magnitude.

2.3
Preference Learning

Alignment of large language models (LLMs) involves reinforcement learning from human feedback
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
, RLHF;)
, where human-labeled preferences are used to train a reward model, commonly based on the Bradley-Terry model
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)
, followed by policy optimization via proximal policy optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
.

Recent approaches have explored the use of more powerful LLMs to generate preference data, reducing the reliance on human annotators.
For instance, AutoPM
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
employs LLMs to automatically generate preference data by eliciting pairwise comparisons based on helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness (HHH) criteria.
This method enables the training of preference models without extensive human supervision.

Similarly, Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback
(
Lee et al. 2023
, RLAIF;)
introduces a framework where AI-generated feedback replaces human annotations.
In this approach, an off-the-shelf LLM serves as the feedback model, evaluating responses based on a predefined set of principles.
RLAIF has demonstrated performance comparable to traditional RLHF methods across tasks such as summarization and dialogue generation, offering a scalable and efficient alternative for aligning LLMs with human values.

These advancements demonstrate the potential of utilizing LLMs themselves to facilitate preference learning.

Instead of first learning the reward model and then applying reinforcement learning,
Rafailov et al. 2023
propose to optimise LLMs using the preference dataset in a purely supervised fashion.

Inverse reinforcement learning
Adams et al. 2022
is a long-standing framework that allows the extraction of a reward function from the corpus of interactions.
It has been applied to LLMs in an approximate form to infer the underlying reward function from the demonstration data in the process called Alignment from Demonstration
(
Sun and van der Schaar 2025
, AfD;)
.
An LLM is first fine-tuned on the demonstrations.
For a prompt from the demonstration dataset, the fine-tuned LLM produces a response, which is considered the negative example, while the positive example is the response from the data itself. This preference dataset is used to optimise the final LLM with DPO. This LLM serves as the reward model.
This removes the need for explicit rankings or reward labels, but still relies on the demonstrations.

2.4
Intrinsic motivation

Reinforcement learning offers a rich framework whereby decision-making can be optimised to maximise the long-term reward.
However, there are cases where the environment does not provide such an
extrinsic
reward or where such a reward is sparse.
A variety of methods based on intrinsic motivation propose the use of
intrinsic
rewards
Chentanez et al. 2004
;
Oudeyer et al. 2007
;
Barto 2013
;
Machado and Bowling 2016
.
One such work is
Klissarov et al. 2024
, where the preference dataset is created by querying the LLM and then used to train an intrinsic reward model for the same LLM.
However, instead of trying to predict preferences, we utilise the model’s internal confidence, similar to how humans learn in the absence of external feedback
Ptasczynski et al. 2022
.

3
Methodology

Our method is based on the observation reported in
(
Wang and Zhou 2024
)
that if the model is well calibrated, the confidence of the answer correlates with the presence of reasoning and hence with the accuracy of this answer.
Therefore, a sequential decision-making process is needed that chooses the tokens of the generated text in such a way that the confidence of the answer tokens is maximal.
This can be achieved via reinforcement learning.

Notation

An (auto-regressive) large language model can be viewed as a policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
in the reinforcement learning framework, and its parameters
\bm{\theta}
are optimised in the standard way, for instance via proximal policy optimisation (PPO).

At the initial time step, the state
s_{0}
is the initial input (prompt or a question)
q
.
The next action
a_{0}
is a token from the vocabulary
w\in\mathcal{V}
selected by the large language model
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
, which for a given context (state) provides a distribution of the next token (action)
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}\left(a\mid s\right)
.
The next state
s_{1}
is then
\texttt{q}\odot a_{0}
, where
\odot
is the concatenation operation, and the next reward is
r_{1}
.

3.1
Chain-of-Thought Decoding

The goal of
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) decoding
is to evoke the inherent reasoning ability of a large language model by generating multiple hypotheses for a given input
q
.

For a given input
q
,
CoT decoding

(
Wang and Zhou 2024
)
samples the
K
-top probability tokens
w^{k}
for
k=1,\dots,K
at the very first decoding step from
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(w|\texttt{q})
.
From here, alternative hypotheses
\texttt{h}^{k}
for
k=1,\dots,K
are generated using greedy auto-regressive decoding:
Initially, the hypothesis
\texttt{h}^{k}
is just
w^{k}
, and it becomes
\texttt{h}^{k}\odot\argmax\left(\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(w|\texttt{h}^{k})\right)
iteratively until the end-of-sequence token is generated.

The answer spans
\texttt{g}^{k}
to the input,
q
, are identified in each hypothesis
\texttt{h}^{k}
in the following way.
The text
‘‘So the answer is’’
is added to each hypothesis
\texttt{h}^{k}
to prompt the model to continue the decoding process and generate the answer tokens
\texttt{g}^{\prime}_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,K
. Then, the answer span
\texttt{g}^{k}
is located in the original hypothesis
\texttt{h}^{k}
using string matching with
\texttt{g}^{\prime}_{k}
.

Given a hypothesis
h
and an answer span
g
for a sequence at the position
m
in that sequence and consisting of
M
tokens, the disparity
c
of the answer span is calculated as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}c=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}&\Big[\max_{w}{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(w|\texttt{q}\odot\texttt{h}_{m+i})}\\
&-\max_{w\neq\argmax{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}}{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(w|\texttt{q}\odot\texttt{h}_{m+i})}\Big],\end{split}

(1)

Probability disparity tends to be a more reliable indicator of the model’s confidence than the probability of the token itself.
This is because it also considers the probability of the second most likely token, which helps capture the spread of probability mass across the vocabulary.
Higher disparity corresponds to the model having a high certainty in sampling of the answer tokens, since an alternative continuation is significantly less likely to be sampled.

3.2
Reinforcement learning from self-feedback

For an input query
q
, CoT decoding provides hypotheses
\texttt{h}^{k}
for
k=1,\dots,K
, each scored with a confidence measure
c_{k}
.
The sequences can then be ordered according to the confidences to form the preference dataset
\mathcal{D}
.
We propose to use this dataset to derive the reward function
R_{\phi}
and optimise the policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
with PPO in a similar fashion as RLHF, as outlined in
Appendices

A
and
A.1
.
We refer to this method as reinforcement learning from self-feedback RLSF, and more specifically, RLSF with PPO.
See Figure
1
for the overview of the procedure.

Alternatively, the dataset
\mathcal{D}
can be used to directly fine-tune the language model with DPO (Section
A.2
) in a self-supervised manner.
We refer to this as RLSF with DPO, although strictly speaking, this does not involve any reinforcement learning.

In terms of computational complexity, RLSF needs access to preference data for which CoT decoding is deployed, which depends on the number of alternative sequences
K
that are generated for each prompt.
However, the inference time of an RLSF-trained LLM is unaffected.

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Datasets

For mathematical reasoning, we conduct experiments on the Multi-Arith and GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
datasets, both of which contain arithmetic word problems requiring multistep computation and reasoning.
For this task, our prompt only contains the question.

For multiple-choice question answering, we use the CommonsenseQA
(
Talmor et al. 2019
)
and ARC Easy
(
Clark et al. 2018
)
datasets.
These benchmarks test the model’s ability to perform common-sense and elementary-level reasoning in a constrained answer format, providing a complementary evaluation to the open-ended arithmetic tasks.
For this task, our prompt contains the question as well as a list of possible answers.

We also evaluated our reward models on the RewardBench
(
Lambert et al. 2024
)
benchmarking dataset.
This dataset consists of questions ranging from general multiple-choice and mathematical reasoning to code generation questions.
For each question, the dataset contains two AI-generated responses, which are rated as preferred and rejected by a human annotator.
We use only the relevant subset of the dataset for evaluation.
For example, reward models trained on mathematical reasoning are evaluated on the mathematical reasoning subset of RewardBench.

4.2
Models

We evaluate RLSF across a diverse set of large language models to assess its robustness and general applicability.
For the multiple-choice question answering experiments, we use Phi-2
(
Hughes 2023
)
and Gemma 2
(
Riviere et al. 2024
, 2 billion parameter instruction tuned variant;)
.
For the mathematical reasoning tasks, we use Gemma 2
(
Riviere et al. 2024
, 2 billion parameter instruction tuned variant;)
and the DeepSeek R1 distilled variant via QWEN 2.5
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025b
, 7 billion parameter variant;)
.

4.3
Metrics

We report three evaluation metrics to quantify model and reward model performance:

Answer Accuracy

measures the percentage of model responses that exactly match the correct answers in the dataset.
This metric applies to both arithmetic (via numeric equivalence) and multiple-choice questions.

Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

assesses the alignment between token-level confidence estimates and actual correctness, indicating the degree of over- or under-confidence in the model’s predictions.
Note that this is calculated on the answer tokens for the arithmetic task and the choice token in the multiple-choice questions.

Reward Model Accuracy

is evaluated using the RewardBench mathematical reasoning subset.
Each instance contains two model responses to the same question and a human-provided preference label.
The reward model produces a scalar score for each response, and a prediction is considered correct if it assigns a higher score to the preferred response.
Overall accuracy reflects the proportion of correctly ranked response pairs.

4.4
Reward Model

Each reward model is initialized from the same base language model that is subsequently optimized using RLSF.
A scalar regression head is attached to the base model and fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).
All training is performed using the TRL (Transformer Reinforcement Learning) library
(
von Werra et al. 2020
)
.
The implementational details and hyperparameters are provided in
Appendix

B
.

To construct the preference dataset, we sample questions from the relevant datasets.
For each input, we decode
K=10
candidates via chain-of-thought decoding as in
Section

3.1
.
To promote stable learning dynamics, raw reward values are linearly rescaled to lie in the
[-1,1]
interval.

5.2
RLSF improves Calibration and Performance

5.2.1
Mathematical Reasoning

We now move to examining the full RLSF method, which includes the policy optimisation step.
To that end, we first examine the performance on mathematical reasoning tasks, namely MultiArith and GSM8K.
We investigate two base models, QWEN 2.5 7B finetuned using the DeepSeek R1 distillation reward model and the instruction-tuned variant of Gemma 2 2B.
We apply CoT prompting as well as CoT decoding (
K=10
) to the base models to provide additional baselines.
We additionally train the base models using the URM reward model as the best-performing reward model in
Section

4.4
.
We compare both the PPO and DPO variants of the RLSF.
The results are given in
Table

2
.

On QWEN 2.5 7B, our RLSF-trained model achieves the highest GSM8K accuracy overall, marginally outperforming the CoT decoding baseline.
It also achieves the lowest ECE on MultiArith.
While CoT decoding helps the DeepSeek baseline on GSM8K, it does not lead to improvements on MultiArith and slightly worsens calibration.
CoT prompting yields the worst performance, confirming the observation of
Saparov and He 2022
that applying a CoT prompt only at inference time is ineffective.
URM, despite being a strong publicly available reward model, underperforms in comparison to RLSF both in terms of accuracy and calibration.
RLSF with DPO improves calibration modestly on GSM8K but overall fails to match the PPO variant of RLSF.

On Gemma 2 2B, our method ranks among the top-performing configurations.
While CoT decoding with the proprietary Gemma reward model achieves the highest accuracy on both benchmarks, our method comes second overall but with inference time reduced by an order of magnitude.
Crucially, RLSF with PPO achieves better calibration than most other methods except for CoT decoding, offering a trade-off between performance, trustworthiness, and inference time.
Compared to the URM, RLSF shows consistent gains across both datasets. Notably, our method also outperforms CoT prompting.

The reward model
R_{\phi}(h)
gives a value in
[-1,1]
for a sequence
h
.
We can use it to visualise the reward for each token calculated as the reward model for the partial sequence leading up to that token.
Examples are given in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:

Example responses from the RLSF fine-tuned Gemma 2 model. The response words are colored based on the rewards obtained from the RLSF reward model.

In Examples 1 and 5, the RLSF reward model assigns token-level rewards in a manner that reflects general preferences for correctness, interpretability, and concise reasoning.
Correct final answers receive high rewards, with intermediate reasoning steps also being rewarded.
Example 3 shows a case of a verbose explanation that receives lower scores, indicating a learned preference for efficiency.
In Example 4, an answer is provided without reasoning, showing that RLHF is scoring it positively but not particularly high, suggesting that the model distinguishes between bare correctness and informative justification.
We also observe that the model’s reasoning can be imperfect, see Example 2, where the model justifies answers post hoc and produces irrelevant explanations.

6
Discussion

RLSF is a purely self-supervised fine-tuning step.
Its role is not to replace RLHF on human-generated preference data, but rather, we show how RLSF can be added to the model post-training pipeline.
Note that RLSF can only improve the behaviour observed in the data that the LLM came in contact with in the optimisation steps prior to RLSF.
Nevertheless, we also show that RLSF can improve even a poorly calibrated model.
Furthermore, when applied to specific target tasks such as mathematical reasoning, RLSF will not enhance safety related biases.

The use of CoT decoding to generate data for RLSF and the subsequent use of reinforcement learning is costly.
However, while CoT decoding increases the inference cost by
K
-fold, the inference cost of an RLSF-trained language model remains unaffected.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we investigate intrinsically motivated reinforcement learning for logical reasoning and multiple choice questions and propose reinforcement learning from self-feedback (RLSF).
RLSF uses internal confidence of an answer span as an intrinsic reward.
We test our method on mathematical reasoning tasks and multiple-choice tasks, achieving improvements both in terms of accuracy and calibration.
We also show that RLSF combined with PPO is superior to DPO, showing that RL is particularly well-suited for incorporating intrinsic motivation.
This method can also be used to derive the underlying reward model of the LLM using solely relevant prompts, without demonstrations or preferences.

RLSF, similarly to RLHF, performs single-step reasoning.
Extending it to handle tasks requiring long-term planning goes beyond the scope of this work.
It is, however, important to note that there is a lot of activity in this area, see
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
for example, and RLSF would be applicable to any such solution since an intrinsic reward can always replace or be added to the extrinsic reward, as is done in goal-oriented dialogue modelling
Wesselmann et al. 2018
.

8
Limitations

The reliance on the identification of the answer span can be considered a limitation of this work.
However, it has to be noted that span prediction is a standard task in NLP and widely used in dialogue state tracking
(
Chao and Lane 2019
;
Heck et al. 2020
)
, named entity recognition
(
Fu et al. 2021
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
, and retrieval augmented generation
(
Kojima et al. 2022
)
.

For evaluating the calibration of a language model via the ECE on a labelled dataset, it is necessary to identify an answer span.
In order to do this, we rely on the predictive abilities of the LLM to find the answer span in its response.

Moreover, the application of RLSF does
not guarantee
that any reasoning takes place; it simply
fosters
this behaviour by the means of intrinsic motivation.
For this bootstrapping approach to work, some basic level of reasoning needs to be observed in the data used in prior optimisation steps.

Finally, RLSF, like any other RL-based fine-tuning approach, has the potential to amplify certain biases present in the base model.
We demonstrate that fine-tuning on the XSTest dataset increases the overcautious safety bias of the Gemma 2 model.
Conversely, we suspect that fine-tuning a model with pre-existing biases could exacerbate safety issues.
Designing such a test is beyond the scope of the current work, since, without major modification, the current bias datasets are not amenable to the answer span identification that would be necessary for our RLSF setup.

9
Ethical Considerations

Although the presented approach improves performance and encourages reasoning in LLMs, it does not include measures to prevent harmful output.
If the underlying data used for LLM alignment prior to RLSF contains harmful behaviour, and if the prompts used for RLSF elicit such behaviour, it might be possible that during the RLSF, such behaviour is reinforced.
This is in line with the longstanding observation in NLP, that the methods are only as good as the data they are trained on
(
Eisenstein 2019
, 14.6.3)
.

Appendix A
Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

The goal in RL-optimization is to find the parameters
\theta
that maximise the expected discounted return starting from the initial state under the policy
\pi_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}r_{t},

(2)

where
T
signifies a point where the chosen token is the end-of-sequence token.
The policy gradient theorem
Sutton et al. 1999
provides the formulation of gradient for
\pi_{\theta}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})A_{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{t},a_{t})}

(3)

where
A_{\pi}\left(s,a\right)
is the advantage function for policy
\pi
.
Instead of directly working with the above gradient, PPO uses a gradient of the surrogate loss, which approximates the direction of the gradient above.
The loss applies clipping and a KL penalty to prevent the small changes in the parameters
\theta
from having unexpected changes on the policy
\pi_{\theta}
.
For full details, please refer to
Schulman et al. 2017
and Appendix
A.3
.

A.1
Bradley-Terry reward model

When the policy choses the end of sequence token the system receives the reward from the reward model
r_{T}=R_{\phi}(s_{T-1}\odot a_{T-1})
, where
s_{T-1}
is the current context and
a_{T-1}
is the end of sequence token.
R_{\phi}(s)
is another language model optimised with the Bradley-Terry
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)
loss

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}\left(h^{1},h^{2};\phi\right)=-\mathbb{E}_{\{\texttt{q},h^{1},h^{2}\}}\log(&\sigma(R_{\phi}(\texttt{q}\odot h^{1})\\
&-R_{\phi}(\texttt{q}\odot h^{2}))),\end{split}

(4)

where hypothesis
h^{1}
is preferred over hypothesis
h^{2}
for query
q
. Since the reward model is utilised only in the ultimate step, for its estimation, only the complete sequences are used.

A.2
Direct Preference Optimisation

Given a preference dataset and assuming the Bradley-Terry preference model, the policy can be directly optimised with the
direct preference optimisation
(DPO) loss given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}\left(h^{1},h^{2};\theta\right)=&-\mathbb{E}_{\{\texttt{q},h^{1},h^{2}\}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(h^{1}|\texttt{q})}{\pi_{\mbox{ref}}(h^{1}|\texttt{q})}\\
&-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(h^{2}|\texttt{q})}{\pi_{\mbox{ref}}(h^{2}|\texttt{q})}\Big)\Big]\end{split}

(5)

where for a query,
q
hypothesis
h^{1}
is preferred over hypothesis
h^{2}
,
\pi(h|\texttt{q})\approx\pi(h_{T}|\texttt{q}\odot h_{0:T-1})
and
\pi_{\mbox{ref}}
is the reference LLM before DPO is applied.
DPO bypasses the need to first learn the reward model and then perform reinforcement learning.
Note, however, that discounting of the reward does not feature in DPO.

Direct preference optimisation bypasses the need for reinforcement learning by performing supervised learning with the objective given in Eq
5
.
Practically, it is still beneficial to perform some label smoothing to have better-calibrated responses.

A.3
Proximal policy optimisation

The advantage function
A_{\pi}(s,a)
denotes the difference between the
Q
-function
Q_{\pi}(s,a)
, which is the expected return when taking action
a
in state
s
and from then on following the policy
\pi
and the value function
V_{\pi}(s)
, which is the expected return of state
s
for policy
\pi
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{\pi}(s,a)&=Q_{\pi}(s,a)-V_{\pi}(s)\\
Q_{\pi}(s,a)&=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\sum^{T-1}_{t=0}\gamma^{l}r_{t+1}|s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a\\
V_{\pi}(s)&=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\sum^{T-t}_{l=0}\gamma^{l}r_{t}|s_{t}=s\\
\end{split}

(6)

The surrogate loss, consisting of the clipping term and the KL-penalty term, is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP+KL}}(\theta)&=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\min\Big(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}\hat{A}_{t},\\
&\text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})},1-\epsilon,\ 1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_{t}\Big)\\
&-\beta\cdot\sum_{a}\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s_{t})\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s_{t})}\right)\Big],\end{split}

(7)

where
\text{clip}(x,a,b)
ensures that
x
is within the interval
[a,b]
.
The gradient of the surrogate loss (Eq
7
) approximates the direction of the policy gradient (Eq
3
).

To estimate the advantage function
\hat{A}_{t}
needed for the surrogate loss, generalised advantage estimation is used:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{A}_{t}&=\sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}\,\delta_{t+l}\quad\text{where}\\
\quad\delta_{t}&=r_{t}+\gamma V_{\pi}(s_{t+1})-V_{\pi}(s_{t})\end{split}

(8)

Parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
trades off bias (lower
\lambda
) and variance (higher
\lambda
).

V_{\pi}
is initialised with the reward model
R_{\phi}
for complete sequences, and parameters
\phi
are updated in such a way so that
V_{\pi}
estimates observed returns for partial sequences.
In this way, the critic parameterised with
\phi
influences the actor parameterised with
\theta
.

PPO is an online learning algorithm, which means it needs to adopt exploration during the process of optimisation.
That is achieved by applying a temperature parameter to the softmax layer of policy
\pi_{\theta}
to induce more varied responses.
