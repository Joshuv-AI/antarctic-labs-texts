Advances in DNA nanostructures for pathogenic microorganisms
Authors: Xiu Han, Jianfeng Zhang, Shuyun Pang, Gen Wei, Jie Niu, Yan Lu, Juqun Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA, the genetic material of life, encodes genetic information for most organisms, including humans. Its unique double-helix structure and base-pairing rules not only provide the foundation for the transmission of genetic information, but also offer a natural molecular tool for nanomaterial construction. DNA nanostructures, composed of DNA, possess advantages such as controllable structures, simple assembly, easily modified, and excellent biocompatibility. These features make them highly promising for applications in tissue engineering and biomedicine, such as providing new strategies to combat infectious diseases caused by pathogenic microorganisms. Their tunable sizes and shapes enhance cellular uptake and tissue penetration of antimicrobial agents, improving cell-DNA nanostructures interactions and therapeutic efficacy against pathogenic microorganisms. Meanwhile, their programmable and unique spatial configuration allows precise arrangement of probes, and their compatibility with other detection strategies improves the detection efficiency and sensitivity of pathogenic microorganisms and can block the pathways of pathogenic microorganisms into host cells. Furthermore, high drug-loading capacity and functional modification enable them to serve as carriers for various types of antimicrobial agents. These features position DNA nanostructures as key contributors to the detection, treatment, and prevention of pathogenic microorganisms, thereby supporting global public health security. Although numerous studies have demonstrated diverse successful applications of DNA nanostructures both in vitro and in vivo, substantial barriers still impede their translation into clinical use, particularly with regard to their employment in the treatment and prevention of pathogenic microorganisms. This review comprehensively introduces DNA nanotechnology, the advantages and limitations of DNA nanostructures, summarizes the progress and challenges of DNA nanostructures in the applications of pathogenic microorganisms, including detection, therapy, and prevention. Beside these, future perspectives are also discussed.Keywords: DNA nanostructures, Pathogenic microorganism, Detection, Pathogenic therapy, Pathogenic prevention

Introduction

DNA, guided by Watson-Crick base pairing, has been engineered into two-dimensional (2D) or three-dimensional (3D) nanostructures since Seeman's pioneering work in the 1980s [1,2]. These programmable, biocompatible nanocarriers exhibit high drug-loading capacity and are widely applied in tissue engineering and biomedicine [[3], [4], [5]].

Current challenges in the diagnosis and treatment of pathogenic microorganisms include high costs and low sensitivity of traditional detection methods, which fail to meet the demands of rapid diagnosis [6]. Additionally, the serious problem of pathogenetic drug resistance poses significant treatment challenges [7,8]. DNA nanostructures, with their high efficiency in drug delivery, unique spatial conformation and ease of functionalization, offer promising solutions to these challenges [9,10]. Although DNA nanostructures have been increasingly applied in pathogenic microorganisms, few reviews systematically summarize their current advances and challenges. This review systematically discusses the advantages, limitations and the current progress of DNA nanostructures in pathogenic microorganisms, as well as the challenges and future directions.

DNA nanotechnology and DNA nanostructures

DNA nanotechnology

In the 1980s, Seeman et al. proposed using cross-shaped Holliday junctions as building blocks to synthesize larger, more complex 2D structures [1]. They demonstrated that under conditions ensuring base pairing of the sequences and reduced sequence symmetry, the sequences could form fixed nucleic acid junctions. Based on this discovery, more complex 2D or 3D structures were constructed by modifying the sticky ends of the Holliday junctions' four arms. This pioneering work laid the foundation for DNA nanotechnology. In 1983, Seeman designed the crossed structure successfully known as the "four-arm junction" or "tile" [11,12]. Multiple tiles can be connected via sticky ends to form complex 2D structures. Subsequently, various types of DNA nanostructures were developed by tile-based assembly, including six-arm junctions, eight-arm junctions, and double-crossover (DX) tiles [[13], [14], [15]]. In 1998, Winfree et al. firstly synthesized 2D DNA crystals using DX tiles [16]. In 2006, Rothemund reported DNA origami which is based on the assembly of DNA molecules [17]. During the process of forming DNA origami, the long DNA single-strand (M13mp18) from bacteriophages, which was used as a scaffold folded into ∼100 nm nanostructures under the guidance of short single-stranded DNA (staple strands). This method enabled the generation of pre-designed patterns ranging from simple geometric shapes such as triangles and rectangles to complex designs like smiling faces and the map of China. This method overcomes the problems of traditional self-assembly which can be only used to synthesize simple structures with low yields. It is another milestone in the history of DNA nanotechnology. Yin et al. proposed the “DNA bricks” in 2012. This method utilized short DNA fragments called "DNA bricks" to self-assemble into stable 3D nanostructures, including rectangular shapes as well as complex structures with internal cavities, etc [18]. This method no longer relies on long single-stranded scaffold DNA, and each DNA brick can be freely added or removed as a separate module. It has demonstrated great potential in the synthesis of intelligent drug delivery particles and other aspects. Other synthetic approaches for DNA nanostructures, such as enzymatic DNA assembly reactions (EDARs) and nucleic acid enzyme-free DNA assembly reactions (NEDAR). Among them, EDAR with high yield mainly consist of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and isothermal amplification techniques such as rolling circle amplification (RCA) and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), but all of which are polymerase-driven [28]. EDAR is frequently utilized for the fabrication of DNA nanoflowers, DNA origamis, three-dimensional networks.

In contrast, NEDAR including hybridization chain reaction (HCR), catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) and entropy-driven DNA reaction (EDR), rely on DNA strand displacement reactions, which triggered the cross-opening of two DNA hairpin molecules by initiator strands under enzyme-free isothermal conditions. These methods generate long nicked or branched DNA polymers through alternating opening and hybridization of hairpin substrates. Their core feature lies in the simultaneous implementation of signal amplification and structural construction. Operating without enzymes, they feature mild reaction conditions, simple operation procedures, outstanding amplification efficiency and high controllability. Such strategies are suitable for fabricating linear DNA polymers, DNA nanowires, network hydrogels and branched nanostructures, and have been widely applied for in situ assembly in biosensing signal amplification, cellular imaging, and drug delivery carriers [2,[28], [29], [30]].

Besides, enzymatic ligation and chemical crosslinking (e.g., click chemistry) can also be adopted for the fabrication of DNA-based supramolecular nanostructures, which strengthens structural stability and improves mechanical strength. For instance, DNA hydrogel networks and DNA branched architectures prepared by these methods exhibit enhanced resistance to exonuclease degradation and superior thermal tolerance under elevated temperatures. Nevertheless, enzymatic ligation requires extra time and higher experimental costs. Meanwhile, chemical crosslinking may introduce potential cytotoxicity arising from the incorporated catalysts [31].

DNA nanostructures

Based on the development of DNA nanotechnologies, various 2D and 3D DNA nanostructures have been developed (Fig. 2), including tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDN), polyhedral DNA nanostructures, DNA cubes, and DNA walkers, DNA hydrogels, DNA networks [32]. Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) represent a special class of DNA nanostructures, including DNA-functionalized inorganic nanoparticles, polymersomes, micelles, and liposomes, which consist of a nanoparticle core surrounded by dense and highly oriented oligonucleotides. Unlike above-mentioned DNA nanostructures, the unique spherical, high-density architecture of SNAs endows them with numerous distinctive physicochemical and biological properties, enabling their promising applications across diverse fields such as material synthesis, catalysis, diagnosis, and therapeutics [33]. A well-established synthetic route for SNAs is the classic thiol-gold (Thiol-Au) conjugation method. Overall, DNA nanotechnology has simplified the synthesis of DNA nanostructures, reduced costs, and provided powerful tools for constructing complex DNA nanostructures, thereby advancing disease diagnosis and treatment.

Design principles, assembly and characterization of DNA nanostructures

The preparation of DNA nanostructures involves several key steps, including sequence designing and synthesizing DNA sequences, mixing the DNA sequences, self-assembly under controlled conditions, adjustment and modification of DNA nanostructures, characterization and verification of DNA nanostructures [34,35].

Design principles of DNA nanostructures

The design of DNA nanostructures mainly follows two core principles: thermodynamically driven minimum free energy and modular construction for high-order structures [36].

Regardless of structural complexity, the formation of all DNA nanoassemblies is driven by the reduction of thermodynamic free energy of the entire self-assembly system, which enables spontaneous structural formation. High-order architectures are primarily assembled from modular building blocks (e.g., Holliday junctions, DX tiles, triple-crossover (TX) tiles, single-strand tiles (SSTs)) via hybridization of complementary sticky ends. This "building-block" strategy enables the rational design of constructs ranging from discrete polyhedra to DNA lattices [36].

However, in conventional assembly by SSTs or DNA bricks, short DNA strands tend to dissociate under environmental fluctuations, leading to poor structural stability and prolonged annealing requirements. Liu and co-workers proposed the kinetically interlocking multiple-unit (KIMU) strategy, in which multiple noncovalent interaction sites are tethered via flexible covalent linkages. This approach drastically enhances the overall stability of the entire molecular assembly, while accelerating the annealing (assembly) process and boosting yields. The KIMU strategy is widely adopted for fabricating supramolecular polymers, such as DNA hydrogels [37].

Assembly of DNA nanostructures

One-pot reaction

The simplest and most effective method currently used for preparing DNA nanostructures is single-strand annealing by one-pot reaction. The single strands, which have been specially designed and are complementary to each other, can quickly combine through annealing, thereby forming a DNA nanostructure [38]. Considering the simplicity and the speed, this method holds a dominant position in the construction and application of DNA nanostructures. The yield of a DNA nanostructure prepared by one-pot reaction is approximately 90% [[39], [40], [41]]. The GC content, the maximum length of the repetitive sequence, and the number of repetitive sequences all have significant impacts on the yield. The straightforward, high-efficiency strategy is ideal for regular nanostructures with fewer components, such as DNA origami, DNA bricks and TDNs. TDNs have been almost all prepared by this method. However, this pathway-independent assembly requires custom-designed sequences, and interactions between single strands occur randomly. Under one-pot reactions, assembly intermediates can always follow a pathway toward the target product. Provided the stoichiometric ratios of mixed strands are accurately calibrated, assembly yields can approach 100%. Nevertheless, this approach faces substantial challenges when assembling complex 3D architectures.

Stepwise assembly

Beyond the one-pot reactions, another route for DNA nanostructures is stepwise assembly, also referred to as pathway-dependent assembly. Unlike the one-pot reaction, in which all DNA strands are mixed and assembled via a single annealing process, stepwise assembly follows a strict pathway-dependent principle: complementary domains must hybridize in a predefined sequential order to successfully form the desired architecture. This technique is defined by its controllability and hierarchical assembly; researchers can assemble partial DNA strands into substructural units (intermediate constructs or preassembled monomers) akin to building blocks. After purification, these subunits are iteratively linked through subsequent hybridization steps to yield more sophisticated final nanostructures [42].

Compared with one-pot reaction, stepwise assembly offers key advantages: for highly intricate DNA nanostructures with numerous components or stringent stoichiometric requirements, this strategy drastically suppresses byproduct formation. It also enables purification and characterization of intermediates at each assembly stage, improving the homogeneity and addressability of the final constructs. The tradeoff, however, is a far more laborious and time-consuming fabrication workflow. This method is generally suited for constructing DNA lattices, complex networks requiring precise order, linear block copolymer analogues, and 2D or 3D superstructures assembled from multiple preformed DNA origamis [43,44].

Characterization of DNA nanostructures

To verify the successful fabrication of DNA nanostructures, multiple analytical techniques are commonly employed for characterization. The molecular weight of DNA nanostructures is generally determined via electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) based on polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) or agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE). Furthermore, the morphology of DNA nanostructures can be characterized using cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) [45,46], atomic force microscopy (AFM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In addition, dynamic light scattering (DLS) is widely used to analyze the particle size and zeta potential of DNA nanostructures [47]. Size assessment can also be evaluated by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy can be used to observe changes in the DNA's secondary structure. Collectively, these techniques provide valuable information regarding the structures and overall physicochemical properties of DNA nanostructures [48].

Advantages and challenges of DNA nanostructures

Advantages of DNA nanostructures

DNA nanostructures offer distinct advantages over traditional materials in biomedicine, including unique spatial conformations, efficient cellular uptake, tissue penetration, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, biocompatibility, drug loading, and favorable biodistribution (Fig. 3).

The advantages of DNA nanostructures. These include unique spatial conformations, efficient cellular uptake and tissue penetration, ROS scavenging, excellent biocompatibility, high drug-loading capacity for both small-molecule and macromolecular drugs, and favorable biodistribution, collectively highlighting their superiority over traditional materials in biomedical applications.

Unique spatial conformations

The advantages of spatial conformation endow DNA nanostructures with high-precision biosensing, excellent cellular uptake capacity and tissue penetration ability (Fig. 4). The distinctive spatial conformations of DNA nanostructures originate from their unique geometric parameters and structural characteristics [49]. 2D structures (nanosheets, DNA origami plates) provide ultrahigh surface-area-to-volume ratios for constructing high-density sensors, as well as investigating cell adhesion and migration [50]. 3D nanostructures (tetrahedrons, icosahedrons and origami cages) can resist nuclease degradation and serve as rigid scaffolds for precise molecular arrangement [51,52]. Geometry critically affects internalization [53]. For example, TDN mimicking coronavirus spikes increase siRNA delivery efficiency from 20% to 95% [54].

Excellent endocytosis and tissue penetration capabilities

Despite negative charges, DNA nanostructures enter cells via caveolin-dependent and macropinocytosis pathways in a time-, concentration-, and cell-type-dependent manner [[56], [57], [58], [59], [60], [61]], which is a foundation for their application in delivering antipathogenic agents and detecting pathogenic microorganisms in living cells. But their cellular uptake efficiency is affected by structures, particle size and rigidity [[62], [63], [64]]. These factors collectively determine cellular uptake efficiency by modulating the interactions between DNA nanostructures and the cell membrane, as well as the endocytic pathways. Size is the primary factor; medium-sized DNA nanostructures typically trigger efficient endocytosis, and larger, more compact particles are more readily internalized due to their greater contact area with the cell membrane. However, the formation of a protein corona can alter this size dependence. In terms of shape, 3D structures (e.g., tetrahedra) outperform 2D ones, and compact shapes are superior to elongated ones, while sharp edges or vertices may enhance membrane deformation and endocytosis by inducing local high curvature. Rigidity influences the structural deformability; although flexible structures exhibit higher stability, more rigid structures can more effectively transmit mechanical forces, thereby triggering active endocytosis driven by cytoskeletal rearrangement. These physical properties do not act in isolation, and their effects are cell-type dependent. Therefore, when designing DNA nanocarriers, it is essential to consider the specific characteristics of the target cells for optimal performance. In addition to enhanced endocytosis, DNA nanostructures can penetrate skin (DNA nanostructure with a size of ≤75 nm reaches dermis; TDN even penetrates up to 450 μm), oral mucosa, cornea, and even brain after intracerebroventricular in situ injection [[65], [66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73]]. They also diffuse into bacterial biofilms, offering new anti-biofilm strategies [74].

Efficient ROS scavenging

The ROS-scavenging capacity can be attributed to multiple factors. Firstly, DNA molecules neutralize ROS intrinsically [[75], [76], [77], [78], [79]]. DNA origami scavenges singlet oxygen ∼50,000× more efficiently than double-stranded DNA. However, the ROS-scavenging capacity is not consistent across all DNA nanostructures. Differences in the ROS-scavenging capacity of DNA nanostructures mainly originate from disparities in their 3D conformations and mechanical properties. Folded architectures (such as DNA origami) enable dense enrichment of guanine active sites, greatly increasing the collision frequency with ROS. Meanwhile, DNA nanostructures with distinct shapes exert different effects on the secondary structure of copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu, Zn-SOD) which serves as the first-line antioxidant enzyme, and exhibit varying affinities toward SOD, which further modulate their ROS-scavenging capacities. Among them, TDNs show the strongest SOD binding affinity (Kd = 7.59 × 10−12 for Cu, Zn-SOD binding) [80]. Secondly, they can eliminate ROS by modulating Nrf2/HO-1, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways, protecting against oxidative stress in various disease models [66,67,[81], [82], [83], [84], [85]].

Biocompatibility of DNA nanostructures

As a natural genetic material, DNA nanostructures exhibit low cytotoxicity (<250 nM) [61,[86], [87], [88], [89]] and excellent blood compatibility [90], with negligible effects on platelets and red blood cells [91,92]. Although DNA is natural and its degradation products are nucleotides, the biocompatibility and immunogenicity of DNA nanostructures depend on their structural design, sequence composition, and chemical modifications.

Drug loading

Their drug-loading capacity is reflected not only in the ability to encapsulate various types of therapeutic molecules, but also in the unique advantages of precisely controlling loading sites, quantity, and release kinetics [93,94]. Drugs are loaded via intercalation (e.g., DOX) [39,[95], [96], [97]], groove binding (paclitaxel) [39,98], electrostatic interactions (cationic peptides, IR780) [10,99,100], or complementary sequence hybridization and covalent modification (nucleic acid therapeutics) [96]. Loading efficiency depends on topology, pH, ion concentration, temperature and the binding affinity between drugs and DNA.

In vivo biodistribution

The distribution of DNA nanostructures in different tissues exhibits obvious selectivity. Intravenously injected DNA nanostructures accumulate in kidneys, liver, and gallbladder, with rapid renal clearance when the size is small (∼10 nm) [101,102]. Such relatively rapid clearance is of great significance for reducing long-term in vivo accumulation and potential chronic toxicity [72].

These combined properties make DNA nanostructures promising for precise drug delivery, biosensing in pathogenic microorganisms.

Challenges of DNA nanostructures

Although DNA nanostructures possess a variety of merits, they still confront several critical challenges: structural stability, manufacturing cost and scalable production. These bottlenecks serve as major barriers hindering their translation to clinical applications. First, they exhibit high sensitivity to ionic surroundings [38]. Since DNA carries negative charges, the formation and structural integrity of DNA nanostructures generally rely on cations (e.g., Mg2+) at specific concentrations to offset electrostatic repulsion. Upon exposure to physiological media such as blood, shifts in ionic composition readily trigger structural disassembly. Second, DNA nanostructures are susceptible to nuclease degradation [103]. Third, high raw material costs present another limitation. Synthetic single-stranded DNAs, particularly the hundreds of custom short oligonucleotides required for DNA origami, come with substantial expenses. Furthermore, difficult to achieve large-scale production also hinders their applications. Their laboratory-scale fabrication cannot be readily scaled up to stable, cost-effective industrial manufacturing, which cannot meet the requirement of good manufacturing practice (GMP).

In addition, DNA nanostructures face multiple obstacles in clinical translation. First comes the regulatory pathways. As novel nanomaterials, the ambiguous approval routes, coupled with the absence of standardized methodologies for evaluating their quality, delay the path to market launch. Besides, consistent critical attributes including particle size and purity across batches are hard to guarantee. For example, complex assembly procedures are prone to DNA strand mismatches, combined with purification workflows, which will drastically lower the final yield. These interrelated challenges collectively form a systematic bottleneck hindering the transition of DNA nanostructures from laboratory research to clinical applications.

Beyond these general barriers, the clinical translation of DNA nanostructures for infectious diseases faces pathogenic microorganism-specific challenges that demand distinct considerations. For bacterial infections, the dense extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix of biofilms which are composed of environmental DNA, proteins, and polysaccharides constitutes a formidable physical barrier that impedes antibiotic penetration [104]. While TDNs have demonstrated superior biofilm penetration, exhibiting 44-fold and 11-fold stronger fluorescence intensity at 20 μm biofilm depth compared to linear and triangular counterparts, respectively. The hydrophobic character of DNA origami in their free form remains an obstacle for effective permeation through the hydrophilic network of EPS [74]. Moreover, the interactions between DNA nanoparticles and biofilm matrices, including diffusion, penetration, and retention behaviors remain insufficiently characterized. For viruses, the high mutation rates of RNA viruses facilitate rapid escape from existing therapeutics. DNA nanotechnology offers a unique advantage here through multivalent, multi-epitope recognition strategies: spatially patterned DNA frameworks can engage multiple distinct epitopes simultaneously, conferring robustness against single-point mutations and reducing the risk of viral escape [[105], [106], [107]]. However, the design principles for achieving broad-spectrum activity against constantly evolving viral quasi-species remain to be fully established. Fungi present yet another challenges, as their cell walls create physical barriers that limit drug access. Although TDNs have shown promise in improving the transport efficiency of antifungal peptides such as Histatin 5 against Candida albicans (C. albicans), the intrinsic structural complexity and variability of fungal cell walls across different species and morphotypes pose additional challenges for the rational design of DNA-based antifungal nanocarriers [108].

In summary, as an emerging nanomaterial, DNA nanostructures possess unique advantages, yet numerous challenges remain to be addressed to facilitate their practical applications. Applications of DNA nanostructures in pathogenic microorganisms

With the development of DNA nanotechnology, various functional DNA nanostructures have been fabricated. And DNA nanostructures possess unique advantages that endow them with vital applications in the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of pathogenic microorganisms (Fig. 5). Their programmable and unique geometric configurations enable highly specific recognition of pathogenic nucleic acids or proteins. Meanwhile, aptamers and other functional moieties can be arranged in an ordered manner on DNA nanostructures to prevent probe entanglement, effectively boosting the efficiency of pathogenic microorganisms’ identification and signal amplification, thereby achieving ultrasensitive and rapid detection of low-abundance targets. Beyond that, featuring high drug loading capacity, efficient cellular internalization, outstanding biocompatibility and favorable tissue penetration, these structures can safely, precisely and efficiently deliver antibacterial or antiviral agents to deep infected tissues. Their ROS-scavenging capability further alleviates inflammatory damage. Furthermore, their favorable biocompatibility, high loading efficiency and tissue penetrability render them promising vaccine carriers. They can stably present antigens to elicit long-lasting immune responses.

Overview of the diverse applications of DNA nanostructures in combating pathogenic microorganisms. DNA nanomaterials have emerged as versatile platforms for addressing infectious diseases through three major avenues: detection, therapy, and prevention.

Detection of pathogenic microorganisms

Bacterial detection

Rapid and ultrasensitive detection of pathogenic bacteria is critical for clinical diagnosis and infection control. DNA walkers, a class of dynamic DNA nanostructures capable of programmed movement along tracks, possess inherent signal amplification capabilities and are widely used in biosensors. By combining DNA walkers with electrochemical, optical, or mass spectrometry techniques, highly sensitive and selective sensors have been developed, with limits of detection (LOD) as low as sub-micromolar levels. Zhou et al. developed an electrochemical biosensor based on a DNA walker and DNA nanoflowers for highly sensitive detection of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). Upon binding with target pathogenic bacteria, this electrochemical sensor will trigger the periodic movement of a DNA walker on the electrode surface, which induces the self-assembly of DNA nanoflowers and signal amplification, achieving an LOD of 9 CFU/mL [109].

Beyond serving as signal amplifiers, DNA nanostructures can also act as probes or probe carriers, integrating signal amplification technologies for ultrasensitive and specific bacterial detection. Aptamers, short single-stranded oligonucleotides that bind specifically to targets, are often combined with DNA nanostructures to achieve efficient and specific detection. Compared to antibodies, aptamers offer advantages such as non-immunogenicity, and easy modification [110]. Because their nature is nucleic acids, aptamers can be easily attached to DNA nanostructures via sequence design. Zhang et al. constructed a multi-branched DNA nanostructure (DNA Pom-Pom nanostructure, PP-N) using trigger and hairpin probes [111]. This PP-N enabled specific recognition of different bacteria based on higher affinity between aptamers and targets, triggering a cascade reaction that amplified fluorescence signals for ultrasensitive detection of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and S. aureus.

In addition, DNA nanostructures can also combine with other signal molecules, such as hemin [112,113] and methylene blue [114], to realize the highly sensitive detection of various pathogenic bacteria. Methylene blue, for instance, possesses excellent solubility and electrochemical activity, and is commonly used as a redox indicator. Feng et al. fabricated a sensor using the combination of target bacteria and aptamers to form dense DNA nanostructures by dumbbell hybridization chain reaction. This dense DNA nanostructure can bind more polymethylene blue nanoparticles (pMB NPs) to realize the highly sensitive detection of S. aureus in human serum and food (LOD:1 CFU/mL) [114]. This method avoids the drawbacks of traditional detection methods such as PCR and ELISA, including cumbersome extraction procedures, expensive required instruments, long detection time and low sensitivity.

Surface-enhanced raman spectroscopy (SERS) which can achieve a raman signal enhancement factor of 104-1011 enables detection of low-concentration substances. Zhu et al. integrated a DNA walker signal amplification strategy into a dual-mode biosensor based on photoelectrochemical (PEC)-SERS for rapid, highly sensitive, and highly accurate detection of S. aureus [115]. It is worth noting that the PEC and SERS modes in this dual-mode biosensor exhibit complementary characteristics in practical applications, rather than being functionally redundant. As summarized in Table S1, the PEC mode offers a lower LOD (0.70 CFU/mL) and a wider linear range (5-108 CFU/mL), making it more suitable for initial screening of trace-level contamination in large-scale food samples, where high sensitivity and rapid photocurrent response are prioritized. In contrast, the SERS mode (LOD: 1.35 CFU/mL; linear range: 10-108 CFU/mL) can distinguish S. aureus from complex matrices, especially when false-positive signals from PEC interferents are of concern. Moreover, SERS offers enhanced robustness in optically turbid or colored samples such as fruit juices. Therefore, in real food samples, the PEC mode can be employed as a high-throughput screening tool, while the SERS mode serves as a validation channel to confirm positive results, thereby fully leveraging the mutual verification capability inherent to the dual-mode design. This hierarchical strategy not only improves detection reliability but also provides operational flexibility according to sample characteristics and testing throughput requirements. Gu et al. used cyclic DNA nanostructure@AuNP tags (CDNA tags) combined with cascade primer exchange reaction to product highly stable and amplified SERS signals for detecting E. coli O157:H7. This method can achieve an LOD of 1.91 CFU/mL and recovery rates of 87.86 - 107.97% in complex samples (Fig. 6) [116].

The CRISPR/Cas system has also shown great potential in biomedical diagnostics. The combination of DNA nanostructures and CRISPR/Cas systems not only enhances the efficiency and precision of gene editing, but also underpins the implementation of more complex functionalities in future applications [117]. Xiong et al. used TDNs as aptamer carriers, increasing the affinity of aptamers for Salmonella by 3.5-fold, and used CRISPR/Cas12a for secondary amplification to achieve a 2.7-fold signal [118]. Furthermore, since the aptamers target intact Salmonella, nucleic acid extraction is unnecessary, enabling simpler operation and suitable for rapid detection. Nevertheless, probes immobilized on the electrodes suffer from entanglement and local aggregation, which will reduce the efficiency of Cas enzymes. Qin et al. improved by using TDNs anchored to gold electrodes via thiol-gold bonds to render ssDNA probes in an orderly and dispersed state, enhancing the recognition and cleavage efficiency of Cas12a, enabling femtomolar-level detection of Fusobacterium nucleatum-specific gene fadA without amplification [119].

In addition to direct bacterial detection, DNA nanostructures have also been used to detect bacteria indirectly. For instance, Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) contains abundant urease, which can specifically decompose urea and induce changes in the pH value of the surrounding environment. Based on this property, Wang et al. utilized the pH responsiveness of polyadenine DNA sequences and the excellent optical properties of gold nanoparticles to realize early visual detection of H. pylori, providing a simple, portable and household detection strategy. This strategy is also suitable for rapid, sensitive and real-time detection of food to evaluate food freshness and spoilage status [120]. Because, during food spoilage, alkaline compounds will be produced when proteins of food being decomposed by bacteria, while acetic acid and other organic acids generated by glucose fermentation. These alkaline or acidic compounds will change the pH of food, which can be detected by this sensor.

DNA nanostructures drastically boost the sensitivity of bacterial detection. Meanwhile, serving as probe scaffolds, they effectively reduce steric hindrance and improve enzymatic cleavage efficiency. Despite their ultrahigh sensitivity and programmable design flexibility in bacterial detection, several critical bottlenecks still hinder their practical deployment. The primary existing limitations are summarized as follows: (1) in complex samples such as serum and food homogenates, DNA nanostructures suffer from insufficient stability and poor resistance to non-specific adsorption, readily leading to aggregation or probe detachment. (2) DNA walkers immobilized on electrode surfaces are frequently impaired by steric hindrance and passivation layers. Their walking efficiency and enzymatic activity degrade with repeated use, and their long-term reproducibility has not been fully validated. (3) In addition, multi-step self-assembly and signal amplification workflows raise overall complexity, imposing stringent requirements on operators and testing equipment. (4) Moreover, the absence of standardized fabrication and quality control systems impedes the translational transition from laboratory research to clinical applications. Future research priorities can focus on enhancing robustness of DNA nanostructures against complex samples; developing microfluidic chips to enable parallel multiplex target detection in a single reactor, with built-in machine learning algorithms to eliminate environmental interferences; exploring lyophilized or paper-based DNA nanostructures that allow ambient-temperature transportation and storage; conducting large-scale double-blind validation using authentic clinical samples (rather than spiked specimens).

Fungal detection

Although DNA nanostructures can improve detection sensitivity, their applications in fungal detection remain extremely limited. This is mainly attributed to the intricate structures of fungal cell walls, and the extracted fungal DNA often suffers from unsatisfactory purity and concentration, creating an insurmountable barrier at the sample preprocessing stage. Meanwhile, fungal genomes are large and structurally complex, frequently folding into complicated conformations such as hairpins and G-quadruplexes, which raises the difficulty of probe design. Furthermore, far fewer studies have reported the screening of high-affinity aptamers for fungi compared with those for bacteria. For these reasons, mycotoxins generally serve as an alternative target for indirect fungal detection.

Mycotoxins, toxic secondary metabolites produced by fungi in food or the environment. Due to their high mutagenicity, carcinogenicity and teratogenicity, they pose a significant potential threat to human and animals through the food chain. Therefore, the detection of mycotoxins is important for food and environmental safety. Ochratoxin A (OTA) and aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) are the most common and dangerous mycotoxins. Wei et al. developed a dual cross DNA nanostructure using aptamers to trigger fluorescence for simultaneous detection of OTA and AFB1 [121]. Yang et al. constructed an aptamer-triggered and Y-shaped DNA nanostructure for simultaneously quantitative detection of OTA and AFB1, which can be adapted for other mycotoxins’ detection by simply replacing the aptamer sequence [122]. He et al. built an electrochemical aptasensor using TDNs to avoid the entanglement of aptamers, thereby improving the affinity between aptamers and targets. In addition, magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles, which can amplify the electrochemical signal effectively and reduced graphene oxide with high electrical conductivity, ultra-high specific surface area and dispersed surface functional groups, which can effectively alleviate the aggregation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles were used in this strategy to achieve detection in an extremely low concentration of patulin (LOD: 30.4 fg/mL) [123]. Although DNA nanostructures can effectively prevent aptamer tangling, enhance binding affinity and recognition efficiency, and improve detection sensitivity, current detection strategies still rely on aptamers. The cross-reactivity of aptamers against mycotoxin analogues has not been fully resolved, which compromises detection specificity.

In the future, DNA nanostructures can be used to organize multiple aptamers precisely, potentially overcoming the low binding affinity of individual monovalent probes. Furthermore, DNA nanostructures can be functionalized with cell-wall-penetrating peptides or chitin-binding domains to facilitate targeted delivery and proximity labeling, thereby enhancing recognition efficiency at the cell surface. Additionally, DNA nanostructures can be integrated with in situ lysis reagents, bypassing the need for high-quality extracted genomic DNA by directly detecting fungal nucleic acids.

Parasite detection

Malaria is one of the major parasitic diseases threatening human life and health. At present, the global situation of malaria prevention and control remains severe, among which infection with Plasmodium falciparum (P. falciparum) exhibits the highest mortality [124,125]. P. falciparum lactate dehydrogenase (PfLDH) is an enzyme involved in the glycolytic pathway of malaria parasites, which catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactic acid [126]. During infection, PfLDH is produced by P. falciparum during its asexual reproduction in erythrocytes and released into the host bloodstream. Accordingly, PfLDH can serve as a universal biomarker for malaria diagnosis. Tanner et al. pioneered the specific recognition of PfLDH using DNA aptamers, laying a foundation for subsequent malaria detection methods based on DNA nanostructures [127]. Later, they integrated PfLDH-targeting aptamers into DNA origami. This enabled the dynamic observation of the binding process between PfLDH protein and aptamers under AFM, laying a foundation for the subsequent clinical diagnosis of malaria using this aptamer [128]. Subsequently, this team further optimized the system and integrated aptamers into DNA nanoboxes. Aptamers act as fluorescent switches of DNA nanoboxes, enabling precise detection of PfLDH [129]. This system possesses excellent universality, which can be applied not only to the diagnosis and detection of various biomarkers, but also serve as a targeted drug delivery system. In addition to detecting malaria biomarkers, researchers have successfully applied DNA nanostructures to the on-site, multiplex and highly sensitive detection of antimalarial drugs piperaquine and mefloquine, which is crucial for evaluating the spread of drug resistance in P. falciparum [130].

However, the applications of DNA nanostructures in parasite detection remain limited. Parasites possess a multistage life cycle consisting of ova, larvae and adults, during which their surface antigens undergo constant variation, making it difficult to identify universal and stable recognition targets. Clinical samples such as blood and feces feature highly complex matrices abundant in proteases, bile salts and polysaccharides. These substances readily trigger non-specific aggregation and degradation of DNA nanostructures, severely impairing the repeatability and reliability of detection signals. In addition, the absence of standardized large-sample clinical validation, coupled with elevated system complexity arising from multi-step assembly and signal amplification procedures, restricts such technologies to laboratory spiking assays and hinders their translation to practical diagnostic scenarios. In the future, the multiple advantages of DNA nanostructures should be exploited, and other complementary strategies should be integrated to overcome their shortcomings, thereby addressing the aforementioned challenges of parasite detection. First, the highly programmable and addressable nature of DNA nanostructures allows precise spatial organization of multiple aptamers or recognition elements on a single scaffold. This multivalent display can simultaneously target different surface antigens or conserved epitopes across various life-cycle stages, thereby mitigating the issue of antigenic variation and enhancing overall binding affinity. Moreover, DNA nanostructures can be functionalized with protective coatings (e.g., PEGylation, lipid bilayers, or protein corona shielding) to resist nuclease degradation and non-specific protein adsorption in complex clinical matrices, thus improving signal stability and reproducibility. And, the integration of DNA nanostructures with microfluidic chips enables online sample pretreatment, separation, and detection in a closed system, which minimizes manual operation steps.

Viral detection

Viral infectious diseases have always been a major challenge to global public health. From historical smallpox and influenza pandemics to Ebola virus and Zika virus outbreaking in recent years, viral transmission poses not only a direct threat to human health, but also profound impacts on the global economy, social order and public health systems. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has further highlighted the importance of rapid and highly sensitive viral detection, which is critical for eliminating infection sources and blocking disease transmission [131].

TDNs can serve as carriers for recognition probes, and their rigid structure effectively prevents probe entanglement. Combined with various detection technologies, they enable accurate detection of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid sequences. Wei et al. employed TDNs as supporting materials for electrically enrichable field-effect transistors to realize the pre-enrichment of low-abundant analytes. This strategy enables direct detection of SARS-CoV-2 which feature high integration, rapid response, readout by one-step and efficiently signal transduction. The average time of this biosensor is approximately 80 s. It requires no extraction or amplification, with LOD as low as 1-2 copies per 100 μL clinical sample. This strategy addresses the dilemma that diagnostic accuracy and speed are difficult to balance in large-scale COVID-19 testing [132]. Encarnación Lorenzo et al. conjugated TDNs with capture probes for SARS-CoV-2 detection. TDNs enable the oriented arrangement of capture probes, thereby improving detection efficiency. This method allows direct detection of viruses in nasopharyngeal swabs without any amplification steps [133]. Fan et al. immobilized TDN-coupled capture probes on electrode surfaces for intelligent online monitoring of SARS-CoV-2 [134]. Wang et al. designed a lateral flow assay strip based on a net-like DNA nanostructure (DNA net) coupled with aptamers to detect the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, overcoming the low specificity and sensitivity of antigen detection [135]. Brian T. Cunningham et al. combined aptamer-coupled DNA nets with a fluorimeter for rapid, sensitive, low-cost and on-site detection of SARS-CoV-2 in saliva [136]. Ulrich F. Keyser et al. employed DNA nanostructures as substrates for probes. In the presence of target sequences in solution, probes and targets form G-quadruplexes, enhancing signals which can be detected by nanopore for facile and multiplex detection. This strategy enables discrimination of single-base mismatches and low crosstalk when detecting various virials [137]. Subsequently, the team developed a DNA nanobait on this basis, which enables direct detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA in oropharyngeal swab samples (Fig. 7a). This DNA Nanobait requires no reverse transcription, pre-amplification or purification, and can simultaneously realize single-molecule detection of multiple respiratory viruses [138]. Miao et al. constructed a DNA bipedal walker with a dumbbell-wheel transition for SARS-CoV-2 RNA detection (Fig. 7b) [139]. This method achieves further amplification through strand displacement amplification (SDA), generating abundant target sequences. These targets trigger the structural transformation of DNA dumbbell-wheel to drive the walking of DNA walkers, thereby cleaving the tracks immobilized on the electrode surface. In addition, the regeneration of the electrochemical biosensor was achieved through a pH-dependent and separable intermolecular DNA triplex design, reducing the cost. Since the DNA walker consists of two legs, the cutting reaction can be completed within 15 min, which is one quarter of a single-legged walking. Through dual amplification using continuous SDA and DNA walkers, ultra-high sensitivity is ensured. Besides, this method possesses the high single-base resolution, and LOD is as low as 2.2 aM.

In addition to the research on detection on SARS-CoV-2, the researchers also focused on the potential of DNA nanostructures in the detection of other viruses. Considering the 100% lethality of Rabies Virus (RABV), it is urgent for a rapid and sensitive detection for RABV. Han et al. developed a dual-mode amplified strategy using Y-shaped DNA nanostructures for RABV detection [140]. Wang et al. constructed a star-shaped DNA nanostructure loaded with aptamers. This structure enables not only highly sensitive detection of the envelope protein domain 3 (ED3) of Dengue Virus (DENV), but also efficient inhibition of DENV infection in host cells [49]. Song et al. developed a TDN-based electrochemical biosensor for detecting the hemagglutinin gene of influenza A virus [141]. Tinnefeld et al. used DNA origami to create columnar structures with fluorescence-quenching hairpins, when targets occur fluorescence will be captured and enhanced by the plasmonic effects. This method can be used for single-molecule detection of Zika virus [142].

DNA nanostructures exhibit remarkable advantages in viral detection, including drastically enhanced detection sensitivity. Meanwhile, DNA origami and DNA nets enable direct capture of intact viral particles via multivalent recognition. Nevertheless, DNA nanostructures are vulnerable to nuclease degradation in complex clinical samples such as serum and saliva. In addition, multi-step self-assembly and signal amplification workflows increase complexity of large-scale manufacturing, and batch-to-batch reproducibility stands as core bottlenecks restricting their clinical translation. In the future, integrating DNA nanostructures with automated microfluidics, CRISPR-Cas diagnostics, artificial intelligence and other technologies is expected to facilitate simple, point-of-care testing without nucleic acid amplification.

Limitations in detecting other pathogenic microorganisms

Later, numerous DNA nanostructures have been designed, such as DNA origami, DNA hydrogels, DNA nanomachines and Y-shaped DNA nanostructures, to achieve simple, highly sensitive and multiplexed detection of bacteria [[143], [144], [145], [146], [147], [148], [149], [150], [151], [152], [153], [154], [155]] and viruses [[156], [157], [158], [159], [160], [161], [162]]. For example, dual-modal fluorescence/colorimetric sensors are fabricated by integrating DNA nanostructures with aptamers and CRISPR/Cas [148,152]. The fluorescence mode is suitable for high-sensitivity screening, whereas the colorimetric mode provides naked-eye readout, making it ideal for on-site testing in resource-limited settings. Overall, the emerging of DNA nanostructures improves the sensitivity and specificity of pathogenic microorganisms’ detection, making early diagnosis possible. In addition, DNA nanostructures simply the detection steps including no need for extraction procedures and amplification-free. “Amplification-free” herein refers to the absence of target nucleic acid amplification (e.g., PCR, LAMP), while signal amplification strategies (e.g., CHA, HCR, DNA walker) are employed to enhance the readout signal. And anti-interference capability, good universality, broad linear range, enhanced Cas12a recognition and cleavage efficiency have been achieved, complex probe design has also been simplified. To benchmark against conventional methods, DNA nanostructure-based biosensors have achieved LODs comparable to or better than PCR with comparable specificity via programmable probes, often outperforming ELISA in cross-reactivity control. Operationally, they require far fewer steps than ELISA and shorter assay times, although pre-assembly remains laboratory-intensive.

However, DNA nanostructure-based methods still have some problems. In detail, the cost varies with the design of DNA nanostructures. Generally, the more sophisticated the structure, the longer the required DNA oligonucleotides, the more laborious the fabrication process, and the higher the production cost. Complicated and time-consuming preparation of DNA nanostructure-based biosensors, the requirement of professional readout equipment, signal stability vulnerable to complex matrices, lack of validation on large-scale clinical samples, and unvalidated long-term storage stability also make them difficult to be applied in clinical use, especially in other pathogenic microorganisms such as Mycoplasma and Chlamydia.

Therapy for pathogenetic infections

Antibacterial therapy

Due to their excellent biocompatibility, DNA nanostructures are primarily used as drug delivery vehicles for antibacterial agents, including antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, and lysozymes [163]. Lin et al. firstly used TDN to deliver the antimicrobial peptide GL13K, enhancing its antibacterial effect against E. coli and partially restoring its activity against Porphyromonas gingivalis [10]. On this basis, Cai et al. reported a TDN based injectable thermosensitive system for the co-delivery of C/EBPα-saRNA and GL13K to treat periodontitis which caused by pathogenic bacterial infections and dysregulated inflammatory responses (Fig. 8) [99]. Subsequently, they combined TDNs with clindamycin (CLI) via electrostatic adsorption, forming a stable TDN-CLI complex with higher loading efficiency, cell penetration, and antibacterial activity. The minimum inhibitory concentration of TDN-CLI was eight times lower than that of CLI alone, reducing the resistance of methicillin-resistant S. aureus and effectively resolving antibiotic resistance as well as insufficient bone regeneration for infected bone defect treatment [164]. Pui Lai Rachel Ee et al. used DNA nanogels as delivery carriers for the antimicrobial peptide L12 without loss of biological activity, which was applied for the treatment of bacterial keratitis caused by S.aureus [165]. Kaminski et al. developed a DNA origami-based targeting platform combined with aptamers to deliver lysozyme, inhibiting Bacillus subtilis and E. coli (Fig. 9a) [163]. Ding et al. constructed a DNA origami nanostructure co-delivering G4/hemin DNAzyme (producing reactive oxygen species to damage bacterial membranes) and levofloxacin. After the bacterial membrane is disrupted by G4/hemin DNAzyme, levofloxacin can easily penetrate into bacteria, achieving high-efficiently antibacterial therapy against gram-positive bacteria, gram-negative bacteria and levofloxacin-resistant strains (Fig. 9b) [166]. Meanwhile, it can promote the healing and repair of infected wounds.

In addition to traditional antimicrobial drugs, DNA nanostructures can deliver antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to silence key genes of pathogenic microorganisms. Lin et al. developed a TDN to deliver ASO inhibiting the biofilm formation of Streptococcus mutans (Fig. 10) [167]. Li et al. constructed a DNA six-helix bundle (6HB) co-delivering ASO and Ag+, showing synergistic antibacterial effects against E. coli and S. aureus [168].

Schematic illustration and key results of TDN delivering multi-targeted ASOs for the inhibition of biofilm formation [167]. The ASOs-TDNs simultaneously suppress gtfBCD, gbpB, ftf expression, reducing extracellular polysaccharides synthesis and biofilm formation. Copyright 2020 by The Author(s).

In addition, they have also been reported to inhibit biofilm formation, eradicate mature biofilms and mitigate inflammatory responses by potentiating the efficacy of aptamers and antimicrobial peptides [[169], [170], [171]].

Antifungal therapy

C. albicans is a major pathogenic fungus. Cai et al. pioneered the delivery by TDNs to pathogenic fungus. The antimicrobial peptide His-5 combined with TDN by electrostatic adsorption. This complex exhibited excellent serum stability, cellular uptake of C. albicans, as well as antifungal activity by increasing intracellular ROS and potassium efflux [108]. Although DNA nanostructures can serve as effective carriers for delivering drugs to inhibit fungi, the mechanisms by which they penetrate fungal biofilms and cell walls remain unclear. It is hypothesized that the size, surface charge of DNA nanostructures, as well as their interactions with specific biofilm components, play pivotal roles, which need verification through further research. Furthermore, researches and applications of DNA nanostructures in antifungal therapy, especially in treating drug-resistant fungi remain scarce. This can be mainly attributed to two factors. First, fungi possess intricate cellular architectures and physiological mechanisms; second, DNA nanostructures are subject to inherent limitations, such as insufficient in vivo stability and low bioavailability. Nevertheless, their structural programmability, excellent biocompatibility and high targeting capacity provide potential strategies to break through the bottlenecks of traditional antifungal technologies.

Antiviral therapy

Based on the infectious mechanism of viruses, blocking the entry of the viruses into cells is one of the approaches in antiviral therapy. Song et al. exploited icosahedral DNA nanostructures (IDN) to realize the ordered arrangement of neutralizing aptamers in terms of spatial position and distance, efficiently blocking the interaction between the spike protein and angiotensin converting enzyme2. IDN with aptamers showed broad-spectrum neutralization, including Omicron infection. This can overcome the limitations of traditionally monoclonal antibodies, including susceptibility to variant escape and low neutralization efficiency of aptamers (Fig. 11) [105]. Multiple studies have also verified that DNA origami-based DNA nanostructures can potentiate the neutralization of peptides to achieve broad-spectrum activity against viruses including HIV, influenza virus, and SARS-CoV-2 [106,107]. Aptamers are “chemical antibodies” that can specifically bind to target molecules. Nevertheless, aptamers exhibit unsatisfactory performance in blocking coronavirus invasion. Inspired by spatial matching from IDN, Yang et al. employed rolling circle amplification to construct nanostructures based on multivalent aptamers that perfectly matches the antigen of the coronavirus in spatial, aiming to block infection and alleviate pulmonary inflammation [172]. Dietz et al. developed a cone-shaped DNA origami nanostructure that captures and engulfs pleomorphic virus particles (>100 nm), including influenza A, SARS-CoV-2, Zika, and chikungunya viruses [173]. Wang et al. proposed designer DNA nanostructures (DDNs) for inhibiting the infection of dengue viruses. This work elaborates in detail how to utilize available structural data to design DDNs for recognizing the uniquely spatial patterns of antigens on viral surfaces. As these antigens arrange in a specifically geometric architecture, and such structural characteristics are unique to each virus, the binding between DDNs and viruses exhibits high specificity [49,174].

Due to their high drug-loading capacity, DNA nanostructures are also used as drug delivery vehicles [175]. Zhao et al. used TDN to deliver siRNA against classical swine fever virus, effectively inhibiting viral replication in porcine cells [176].

Overall, DNA nanostructures can enhance the stability and cellular uptake of drugs, penetrate biofilms, thereby improving in vivo therapeutic efficacy and reducing the toxicity. And they can improve the interaction between viruses and host cells, opening up new strategies to tackle antibiotic resistance and viral mutation. However, long-term stability, potential immunogenicity, the effect of high-temperature preparation (95 °C) on drugs’ properties and the therapeutic efficiency in vivo still need to be validated. The complex design and preparation of DNA nanostructures, as well as the high cost of large-scale production remain major bottlenecks. In addition to detection and therapy, DNA nanostructures also play an important role in prevention of pathogenic microorganisms. But research on DNA nanostructures for prevention of pathogenic microorganisms is still limited. Their complicated fabrication and high costs make them unfit for large-population applications. Meanwhile, they encounter far more severe challenges regarding in vivo stability, long-term biosafety and regulatory approval hurdles than in vitro detection technologies, which restricts their translational development toward preventive fields. But they still have shown great potential as vaccine platforms. Chang et al. used highly programmable DNA nanostructures to precisely control the spatial arrangement of model antigens and CpG adjuvants, inducing strong and durable immune responses without serious adverse effects [177]. Kostiainen et al. used programmable DNA origami nanostructures to control the shape, size and topological structure of viral capsid, offering a new strategy for customized vaccines [178]. Halvorsen et al. firstly incorporated RNA into the preparation of DNA origami. This DNA-RNA hybrid nanostructures can not only serve as a drug delivery platform, but also be used as an mRNA vaccine directly [179]. With prominent resistance to ribonuclease, this hybrid holds promising prospects for applications in drug delivery and vaccine development (such as COVID-19 mRNA vaccines). Kostiainen et al. further investigated the impact of this hybrid on mRNA translation and the viral capsid was utilized to deliver this mRNA vaccine, making the technology closer to practical application [180]. Bathe et al. reported reports a scalable and efficient strategy for the covalent functionalization of DNA origami [181]. By click chemistry, the HIV antigen (eOD-GT8) was covalently modified onto DNA origami, thereby constructing vaccines. To date, the vaccine has only been validated at laboratory to possess both structural stability and immune-activating capability. In addition, its performance in B cell activation is comparable to that of previously reported constructs, while it has not yet progressed to practical application. Beyond viral prevention, Dai et al. were the first to prepare inhalable DNA nanostructures as adjuvants for pneumonic plague caused by Yersinia pestis. And, this inhalable formulation has been proven to activate memory T and B cell immunity in mice [182]. All these references are summarized in Table S3.

Although this review summarizes the applications of DNA nanostructures in pathogenic microorganisms from the perspectives of diagnosis, treatment, and prevention so as to facilitate an intuitive understanding of their main application directions, this does not imply that these three aspects are necessarily independent or incapable of synergy. In fact, studies have already utilized DNA nanostructures to achieve both viral and bacterial detection and therapy [55,145]. Owing to their unique programmability and addressability, DNA nanostructures hold great potential to integrate diagnosis, treatment, and prevention into a single platform.

Summary and outlook

DNA nanostructures offer unique advantages, including precise molecular recognition, high programmability, biocompatibility, degradability, and dynamic tunability. Owing to these properties, they have achieved significant breakthroughs in the detection and treatment of pathogenic microorganisms. Firstly, they exhibit extremely high flexibility, being able to bend and deform without breaking or fracturing under external pressure [183,184]. When the external force is removed, they can return to their original state, maintaining the integrity and stability of their structure. This flexibility enables their application in the treatment of bacterial infection in diabetic foot. Furthermore, their controllable assembly enables the high-density and oriented arrangement of pathogenic molecules, thereby greatly improving detection sensitivity and specificity. Besides, their structural rigidity and dynamic responsiveness, such as switch-like or cage-like designs, provide an ideal platform for the capture of multi-pathogenic microorganisms [185,186]. In addition, they can penetrate cell membranes without the need for transfection reagents and possess favorable tissue permeability, which makes them ideal candidates for delivering antipathogenic agents to control pathogenic infections of the skin, cornea and other tissues. Meanwhile, their extensive accumulation in the liver enables them to serve as an optimal drug delivery platform for managing malaria infection in hepatic stage, while exerting minimal adverse effects on other organs [187,188]. And, their high drug-loading capacity and multiple functionalization sites, which can be flexibly modified by aptamers, peptides and antibodies, make them widely used in delivery of antipathogenic agents and prevention of pathogenic microorganisms. Additionally, due to their unique spatial conformation and signal amplification capability (e.g., DNA walkers), they are also extensively applied in disease detection. Overall, these structures have improved diagnostic efficiency and provided new therapeutic strategies for drug resistance in pathogenic microorganisms. To facilitate readers in selecting suitable DNA nanostructures according to their specific needs, we provide a decision tree (Fig. S1).

Despite the considerable advantages of DNA nanostructures, their clinical translation remains hampered by several challenges; addressing these barriers will define the key breakthrough directions in the next five years. First and foremost, the stability of DNA nanostructures needs to be improved. When DNA nanostructures that maintain over 80% structural integrity after 24 h incubation at 37 °C in 100% human serum qualify for clinical translation trials. Their instability is not governed by a single factor but stems from the combined effects. Composed of nucleic acids, these structures are susceptible to nucleases. Meanwhile, the design of DNA nanostructures (e.g., sequence and topological architecture) as well as external chemical conditions (such as ion species, ionic strength, and pH) all exert impacts on their stability. Additionally, their cellular uptake efficiency is affected by structures and particle size. Furthermore, rapid clearance by the liver and kidneys in vivo also hinders its clinical translation. Future research should focus on structural design and chemical modification to enhance their stability and achieve robust in vivo retention time, universal cellular uptake, thereby facilitating their broader in vivo applications. For instance, the introduction of xenonucleic acid backbone replacement, incorporating artificial intelligence for assisted sequence and geometric design of DNA nanostructures, disulfide covalent crosslinking, or oligolysine-PEG polymer coatings. Second and equally importantly, most DNA nanostructures currently applied in the detection of pathogenic microorganisms lack validation in clinical samples. And, as an emerging technology, DNA nanostructures-based sensors require professional operation, which leads to great difficulties in popularization and limits their practical application. In the future, double-blind validation with a minimum of 500 clinical samples is required before clinical diagnosis, including no fewer than 200 positive samples and 300 negative samples. And DNA nanostructures can be combined with microfluidics and test strips to simplify operational requirements, thereby enabling at-home use by non-professionals. Meanwhile, combining bioengineering and other technologies can comprehensively cut down the costs throughout the entire workflow, from structural design to the synthesis of DNA strands. Thirdly, the inevitable byproducts generated during assembly. The byproducts in DNA nanostructure synthesis mainly originate from kinetic traps and thermodynamic competition [[189], [190], [191]]. Kinetic traps refer to the formation of non-dissociable intermediates during annealing due to erroneous hybridization pathways; thermodynamic competition leads to the formation of metastable structures that are less stable than the target product. In addition, misfolding occurs in complex systems such as DNA origami, along with disordered aggregation induced by non-specific interactions like base stacking, and the structures resulting from unassembled strands, all of which collectively contribute to byproduct generation. The key to addressing these issues lies in rational design and process control: guiding correct folding pathways through sequence optimization (e.g., minimizing erroneous hybridization sites); and, in terms of processing, precisely adjusting annealing protocols (e.g., slow cooling or gradient annealing) to bypass kinetic traps, while optimizing reactant ratios and ionic strength to suppress non-specific aggregation, thereby steering the assembly toward the target product. Fourthly, some small-molecule drugs can affect the conformation of DNA nanostructures. For instance, DOX intercalates into DNA strands (the number of DOX bound per DNA molecule was approximately 1.2, with a binding site size of about 3 base pairs), which induces partially conformational transitions of DNA from B-form to A-form. And the intercalation decreases the rigidity of DNAs, that results in greater susceptibility of dsDNA to break and faster release. It is necessary to develop drug loading approaches that exert minimal impact on DNA structures, so as to achieve high drug loading efficiency while preserving the delivery performance of DNA nanostructures. Alternatively, drug analogues can be developed. For instance, N-(trifluoroacetyl)doxorubicin (FDOX), an analogue of DOX, retains intercalation capability but does not induce DNA conformational changes. Fifthly, to tackle the specific challenges of applying them to pathogenic microorganisms, promising solutions include surface modification of DNA nanostructures (e.g., hydrophobic conjugation or chitosan coating) to overcome biofilm barriers, and geometry-matched multivalent scaffolds to combat viral mutational escape through cooperative epitope engagement. For fungal pathogenic microorganisms, electrostatic interaction-mediated targeting of cell wall components offers a viable route to enhance antifungal delivery. Furthermore, DNA nanostructures co-delivering hydrolytic enzymes can be designed to disrupt the biofilms, followed by surface charge modulation to increase retention time within the biofilms.

In summary, the multiple superior properties of DNA nanostructures have driven significant advances in the detection, treatment, and prevention of pathogenic infections. DNA nanostructures can be customized for targeting specific pathogenic microorganisms, while exhibiting low cytotoxicity and immunogenicity. DNA nanostructures have found growing applications in pathogenic microorganisms, however, current reviews on DNA nanostructures mainly focus on their construction principles, design strategies, and biomedical applications as delivery systems and biosensors. Few systematic reviews have comprehensively addressed their roles in the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of pathogenic microorganisms. This review systematically discusses the distinct characteristics of DNA nanostructures and their inherent advantages in applications related to pathogenic microorganisms. In addition, it addresses the limitations of DNA nanostructures and summarizes the challenges encountered in preclinical studies, aiming to provide insights into their future applications in the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of pathogenic microorganisms.
