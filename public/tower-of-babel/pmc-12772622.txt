Assessment of blended learning palliative care educational programme in cancer treatment centres in India
Authors: Sushma Bhatnagar, Apoorva Mittal, Shirlynn Ho, Giam Cheong Leong, Raghav Gupta, Naveen Salins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study evaluates the fifth cohort of the palliative care cancer treatment centres (CTC) educational programme in India with the aim of refining the course on the basis of participant feedback thereby improving palliative care services at cancer centres.MethodsThe intervention included participation in the CTC 5 teaching and training programme, which encompassed foundation course, refresher course, access to palliative care training modules, clinical training and mentorship under palliative care experts. The feedback was taken from all 57 participants (29 doctors and 28 nurses) of CTC 5 programme representing 14 hospitals across nine regions in India. The assessment tools included Likert scale ratings and free-text comments. 3-point and 5-point Likert scale ratings were used to gauge the usefulness of course topics, quality of content and delivery. A mixed methods approach was used for a comprehensive evaluation by taking both quantitative measures of participant inputs and qualitative insights into their experiences and suggestions. The knowledge gained by the participants was analysed using pre-training and post-training assessments.ResultsThe feedback obtained from the participants highlighted both strengths and scope for improvement. The foundation course received positive feedback for its sessions, especially on communication skills and pain management. The refresher course received positive feedback for team presentations but lacked sufficient case discussions. The interactive nature of sessions was well-received. Following the CTC 5 programme, participants showed a 30.38% knowledge gain. Overall, the programme exceeded expectations for 62% of participants, highlighting its impact on personal development and clinical practices in palliative care.ConclusionThe study provides useful insights for designing future palliative care educational courses to address the needs of health professionals. The CTC programme has promising potential to bring about positive changes in both the personal and professional development of participants ultimately improving palliative care services across India.

Background

According to the Lancet Commission Report on Global Access to Palliative Care and Pain Relief, low-income and middle-income nations account for 80% of global serious health-related suffering, with India alone having 7.2 million patients experiencing significant distress.1 This growing need is exacerbated by increasing cancer incidence, as India accounts for over 6% of global cancer-related deaths with a mortality rate of 97 per 100,000, comparable to figures seen in high-income countries.2 By the end of this decade, it is projected that over 900,000 deaths in India will be due to cancer, highlighting an urgent and unaddressed need for comprehensive palliative care services.35 However, India’s capacity to deliver effective palliative care faces significant challenges including restrictive opioid access, especially in rural areas, and a lack of awareness and training.610 Despite several international palliative care education programmes designed to train healthcare professionals with essential skills for palliative care, India struggles to adapt these models due to its sociocultural diversity.1116 While initiatives like online education programmes for palliative care clinicians have demonstrated some success in overcoming geographical and logistical barriers, issues such as technological constraints and limited opportunities for human interaction remain substantial obstacles.17 18 Moreover, conventional palliative care training programmes in India often lack a structured approach that effectively combines theoretical knowledge with clinical training.19 To address these gaps, the palliative care cancer treatment centres (PC-CTC) programme was launched in 2016, representing a novel mixed-learning approach to palliative care education in India.20 The CTC programme offers a blended model that integrates in-person education with continuous dedicated mentorship, providing both theoretical training and practical skill development. The programme aims to strengthen palliative care services across the country by offering education and training to doctors and nurses working in cancer hospitals nationwide. It engages with national and international palliative care organisations to create a sustainable model for capacity building in India.21 The main objective of the PC-CTC educational programme is to enhance the palliative care skills and knowledge of healthcare practitioners, ensuring that they can effectively address the needs of patients with advanced illnesses. It strives to build local leadership in palliative care, empowering participants to take on advocacy roles and drive change at their institutions. The programme seeks to promote awareness and acceptance of palliative care at both grassroots and institutional levels, ensuring that the principles of palliative care become ingrained in healthcare practice throughout India. The innovative approach to education makes PC-CTC programme a novel teaching and training model. By offering in-person workshops, interactive learning sessions, group discussions and dedicated mentorship, the programme seeks to foster long-term professional growth and leadership in palliative care. The CTC 5 cohort comprised 57 participants from 14 hospitals across nine regions, providing them with access to a diverse pool of 18 faculty members, including both national and international experts in palliative care. This study aims to assess the CTC 5 palliative care educational programme for refining this course further based on participants’ feedback and enhancing palliative care services at participating centres.

Methodology

Study design

This study is an evaluation of the CTC 5 palliative care teaching and training programme, spanning from August 2022 to August 2023. It is designed in accordance with the STROBE guidelines in which the participant feedback is the variable of interest. The feedback from the participants was obtained through paper-based evaluation forms completed by participants at the end of each day of the course. The assessment tools included Likert scale ratings and free-text comments. 3-point and 5-point Likert scale ratings were used to gauge the usefulness of course topics, relevance in daily clinical work, quality of content, presentation and organisational aspects. Additionally, qualitative feedback was taken through open-ended comments from the participants. A mixed methods approach was used for a comprehensive evaluation by taking both quantitative measures of participant inputs and qualitative insights into their experiences and opinions. The uniformity in assessment methods across participants and regions ensured the comparability of feedback data. A comparative assessment was administered on the first and final days of the course to evaluate the learning gains of all the participants. For analysing the quantitative variable, that is, participants’ knowledge gain, pretest and post-test data were collected and the change in percentage score was computed. For the quantitative analysis, all the compiled data were entered and analysed in Microsoft Excel. Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the institute ethics committee (reference number AIIMSA1347/03.05.2024, RP-41/2024).

Sample

The study included participants from the CTC 5 teaching and training programme. The participant pool consisted of 57 individuals, comprising 29 doctors and 28 nurses from 14 hospitals across the country. To ensure diverse representation, participants were included from different regions of India, covering eight states: Rajasthan, Jharkhand, Bihar, Karnataka, West Bengal, Goa, Telangana and Madhya Pradesh. All 57 individuals participated in the study with no drop outs.

Intervention and course summary: CTC 5 teaching and training programme

The primary exposure included participation in the CTC 5 teaching programme and access to palliative care training modules and clinical attachments. The course comprised a 5-day foundation programme, a 5-day clinical attachment, year-long mentoring and 3-day refresher course. The curriculum included topics such as pain and symptom control, effective communication techniques to provide psychosocial support to both patients and their families, ethical considerations surrounding end-of-life care, strategies for managing palliative care services and monitoring standards and auditing treatment outcomes. All the teaching and training components were conducted in-person. The programme was funded by the Lein Foundation project under the Asia Pacific Hospice Palliative Care Network, Singapore.

Foundation Course

The 5-day foundation course was conducted at the Lemon Tree Hotel, New Delhi from 15 to 19 August 2022. It covered the components of palliative care comprehensively, from basics of palliative care to specialised topics (online supplemental appendix table 1). It focused on pain management, including assessment, analgesic use and oncology emergencies, along with non-opioid and opioid strategies. The total teaching hours for the foundation course were 40 hours (8 hours/day for 5 days).

Clinical training

The foundation course was followed by clinical attachment at All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, India and Manipal Comprehensive Cancer Care Centre, Manipal, India in the month of October 2022. The patients observed the palliative care outpatient department (OPD), ward rounds, various procedures like wound care, morphine titration, interventional pain management, stoma care, prescription of medicines, pleural tapping, ascitic tapping, etc. The 3-day follow-on clinical training at the respective mentor’s centres took place in the month of December 2022.

Refresher course

The 3-day refresher course was conducted in the month of February 2023 at the Pride Plaza Hotel, New Delhi. The refresher course included centre-specific progress reports and case scenario presentations (online supplemental appendix table 2). Each centre presented its progress report discussing the status of outpatient and inpatient care, ongoing advocacy programmes and the availability of opioids at their centre. They also discussed the challenges being faced in strengthening palliative care services at their centre; the mentors and participants offered possible suggestions for overcoming those obstacles. A case study was assigned to each centre to present a lecture, which was moderated by the faculty. Such exercise not only helped in assessing the understanding of each team but also helped them learn teaching skills, which could enable them to dissipate the knowledge of palliative care in future to their staff and peers. The total teaching hours for the refresher course were 20 hours.

Teaching technique

The teaching techniques employed in CTC 5 were designed to promote active engagement among participants through an interactive approach. Instead of focusing on traditional theoretical modules, the programme used more dynamic methods such as group discussions, role plays, case-based learning, the fishbowl technique and group activities. These teaching strategies allowed participants to learn not only from the faculty but also from one another. Each session included a mixed team of four participants: two doctors, two nurses accompanied by an expert, all seated at a round table to facilitate smooth discussions and collaborative learning.

Mentorship

Each participating centre was assigned a dedicated mentor to support its ongoing development. The mentors’ role went beyond formal teaching as they actively engaged with participants to gain a deeper understanding of the challenges faced by them in practising palliative care. Mentors also assisted participants in addressing institutional challenges, particularly in availing opioids at their centres. After the foundation course, mentors made physical visits to inspect palliative care OPDs and IPDs, assess opioid management and provide support for any difficulties with hospital administration.

Feedback from the participants

The participants were given paper-based feedback forms to collect feedback at end of each day. Structured assessment tools like Likert scales and open-ended questions were used for data collection to reduce response bias. The participants were asked to rate the contents of the sessions on the basis of their usefulness for their daily work and the delivery style by the faculty. Various strategies were implemented to ensure the validity and reliability of the findings, while mitigating potential biases. Participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality regarding their feedback to encourage candid responses and to minimise social desirability bias. Additionally, data analysis was performed independently by researchers, safeguarding against bias from vested interests. These measures collectively enhanced the credibility of the evaluation, strengthening the validity and reliability of the study.

Results

Demographical data

The demographic breakdown of the 57 participants represented that 45% of doctors were in the age group of 31–40 years (figure 1), with a gender distribution of 45% women and 55% men. The eastern zones (West Bengal, Jharkhand, Bihar) and western zones (Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh) had the largest representation of doctors and nurses at 31% while the north zone (Delhi) and south zone (Goa, Karnataka, Telangana) had representations at 17% and 21%, respectively. Anaesthesia specialists made up the majority with a proportion of 59%, followed by general medicine at 25% and radiation oncology at 7%. Among the 30 nurses, 39% were below 30 years old, 21% were between 31 and 40 and 36% were aged 41–50.

Participant feedback

The participants provided feedback on various topics covered in the foundation course on 0–5 Likert scale. Figure 2 represents the mean of participant feedback on the basis of the content and presentation of various topics. The participants were asked to rate the contents of the sessions relevant to their daily clinical work and the delivery style of the presentations. It was found that the content and presentation of the 5-day sessions on a scale of usefulness were rated from 4.4 to 4.8, indicating that most sessions were perceived as useful to very useful. The introductory sessions received positive scores, indicating an understanding of the importance of palliative care in cancer centres. The initial sessions on pain management were highly rated, however, as the course progressed, more specialised sessions on pain saw a decline in scores (mean 4.56). Communication skill sessions were consistently scored well (mean 4.84), suggesting an acknowledgement of the importance of good interaction skills in palliative care. More advanced pain management topics and sessions on pain physiology, types of pain (pain 2) and interventional pain management (pain 12) received lower ratings, indicating a potential requirement for teaching these topics more effectively. The concluding sessions on end-of-life care and the practical aspects of setting up palliative care services received mixed feedback. Overall, the feedback indicated that while foundational topics received positive responses, more complex topics struggled to hold participants’ interest. This suggests a requirement for a more effective approach to teaching detailed topics, ensuring consistent engagement and understanding.

The feedback provided across the 5 days of the course highlights various positive aspects and the scope of improvement (online supplemental appendix table 3). Participants appreciated the interactive sessions, faculty engagement and specific topics like self-care and managing bed-bound patients. However, they recommended more practical elements, such as sessions on lymphedema management, interventional procedures and prescription writing, along with a longer course duration for comprehensive learning. The future course considerations included sessions on nursing education, home-based end-of-life care and non-pharmacological pain management. The refresher course also received positive feedback for team progress report presentations, which were valued for motivating participants and fostering professional growth (online supplemental appendix table 4). Participants requested more sessions on integrating palliative care in community and home settings, circulating case histories via WhatsApp groups before sessions and incorporating quizzes and role-play scenarios. In the preliminary survey assessing the impact of the CTC 5 programme, participants reported applying their training to improve palliative care through nurse training, wound care management and improved communication skills. The programme also resulted in key advancements, such as the initiation of home care services, systematic pain management and opioid procurement at participating centres (online supplemental appendix table 5)

The study also included participant feedback regarding the organisational aspects of the programme (figure 3). A positive rating was received for the dates, duration, timing and programme notice (4.84 to 4.91). Seminar rooms received a low rating of 3.93, mainly due to obstructed views caused by pillars in the room. The overall feedback data indicated a positive response from participants, with 62% stating that the programme exceeded their expectations (figure 4). 27% of the participants mentioned that the programme completely met their expectations, while 11% felt that it mostly met their expectations. Notably, none of the participants reported that the programme only somewhat met their expectations or did not meet them at all. This data underscore the success of the programme in surpassing participant expectations, emphasising its value and impact on the participants’ learning experience.

Discussion

The CTC 5 programme effectively addressed regional disparities in healthcare access across India by involving hospitals from diverse regions, emphasising the importance of palliative care training in both well-established and developing hospitals.22 The participation of non-cancer specialists like general medicine further underscored the programme’s relevance in addressing palliative care needs beyond oncology.23 24 This interdisciplinary approach aligns with global practices where integrating palliative care with primary care ensures accessible support for patients with chronic illnesses.25 26 Such integration is crucial in India to reduce the burden on specialised oncology departments while meeting palliative care needs in community-based and resource-limited settings. The year-long dedicated mentorship added a novel dimension to the CTC 5 programme, stimulating a strong mentor–mentee relationship that extended beyond the course duration. This approach cultivated a national ‘CTC family’, enabling participants to seek guidance on challenging cases and creating a continuous support network. In a culturally diverse, resource-limited setting like India, this long-term engagement offered a more effective model compared with short-term training programmes.2729 As palliative care is often considered secondary in many Indian hospitals, securing administrative support from hospital directors was crucial to ensure active participation.30 The participants’ feedback on various sessions revealed that the foundational topics were well-received, though more advanced topics saw lower engagement highlighting the need for more interactive teaching methods like case studies. The positive feedback on communication skills highlights the acknowledgement of cultural sensitivity required when discussing terminal illness in India, where open conversations are often avoided and health professionals struggle to deliver bad news.3133 Given India’s diverse patient backgrounds, their end-of-life needs vary significantly based on beliefs about life and death.34 Thus, palliative care doctors and nurses need specialised training to communicate effectively, considering patients’ unique perspectives and needs. The programme’s clinical attachment component further reinforced practical knowledge application, aligning with literature that supports combining theoretical and clinical training.35 36 Overall, the demographic diversity, mentorship and knowledge gains underline the CTC 5 programme’s significance, though further research is required to assess its long-term impact on palliative care expertise in India.

Limitations and scope of future work

The study acknowledges some limitations that might influence the interpretation of its findings. Although efforts were made to minimise social desirability bias, its potential impact on participant feedback and study conclusions cannot be completely ruled out. A more structured approach to the evaluation of participants’ knowledge would have enabled the establishment of correlations in the findings. In future work, the long-term impact of CTC 5 programme can be studied.

Conclusion

The feedback from the palliative care CTC 5 programme participants highlighted both its strengths and areas that require improvement. Although the content and presentation aspects of the modules were generally found useful, more complex subjects struggled to maintain engagement, indicating a need for improved teaching methods. The interactive nature of the sessions and the openness of the faculty were appreciated. The organisational aspects of the programme were highly praised for effective planning and execution. The programme’s success was underscored by participant satisfaction (62% exceeding expectations, 27% completely satisfied). Participants acknowledged the acquired knowledge of holistic care, teamwork and communication skills from this educational intervention, thereby improving the palliative care services at their hospitals.
