Upcoming Terminology in the World of Function Spaces
Hans G. Feichtinger

Abstract
It is the purpose of this article to compare various concepts of “function spaces”. In particular we compare notions of the concept of Banach Function Spaces (in the spirit of Luxemburg-Zaanen) to the setting of solid BF-spaces as it is widely used in a number of papers in Time-Frequency Analysis or Coorbit Theory. More recently ball quasi-Banach function spaces come into the picture. In a way this note is a companion to the authors’ article entitled “Choosing Function Spaces in Harmonic Analysis” (from 2015, February Fourier Talks at the Norbert Wiener Center, Vol.4) and the recent article by E. Lorist and Z.Nieraeth, entitled “Banach Function Spaces done right” (Indag. Math., 2023). We restrict our attention to the Banach space case, avoiding the complications arising in the quasi-Banach case.

BK-spaces

Obviously it is easier to deal with function spaces over discrete domains,
which are usually just called
sequence spaces
.
The corresponding theory of BK-spaces, i.e. (partially German terminology!)
of “Banach-Koordinaten Räume” goes probably back to G. Köthe. Thus they are also called “Köthe sequence spaces”. These spaces have been studied
in the 60th and 70th and are Banach spaces of sequences with the (natural,
but non-trivial) assumption that coordinate mappings are continuous). In other
words, a Banach space
({\boldsymbol{B}},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\boldsymbol{B}})
of sequences over a (discrete) domain
I

can be described as families
X=(x_{i})_{i\in I}
. Thus we assume that
for every
i\in I
there exists
C_{i}<\infty
such that

|x_{i}|\leq C_{i}{\|(x_{i})_{i\in I}\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}},\quad\forall X\in{\boldsymbol{B}}.

There is an abundance of such BK-spaces. Many of them are
solid
,
i.e satisfy the property that for any
X\in{\boldsymbol{B}}
and any family

Y=(y_{i})_{i\in I}
one has:

|y_{i}|\leq|x_{i}|\,\,\,\forall i\in I\,\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\,Y\in{\boldsymbol{B}}{\quad\mbox{and}\quad}{\|Y\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}}\leq{\|X\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}}.

Clearly weighted
{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{p}}
-spaces belong to the class of solid spaces,
while the Banach spaces
{\left({{\mathcal{F}}\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}},\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{{{\mathcal{F}}\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}}}\right)}
of all Fourier coefficients of functions in

\big({{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}(\mathbb{T})},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{p}\big)
, with
p\neq 2
, with the norm

{\|{{\widehat{f}}}\|_{{{\mathcal{F}}\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}}}\,}:={\|{f}\|_{{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}(\mathbb{T})}}\,}

are good examples of BK-spaces which are
not solid
. As V. Temlyakov has shown in
[
48
]
this has serious consequences for greedy algorithms (the attempt to recover
f
from the big Fourier coefficients via hard thresholding).

A good example of usefulness of abstract functional analytic arguments
being useful in such a context is the following simple consequence
of the Closed Graph Theorem: Assume that we have two BK-spaces

{({{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1}},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|^{(1)})}
and
({\boldsymbol{B}}^{2},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|^{(2)})
, such that
{{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1}}\subseteq{{\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}}
. Then
the embedding is automatically continuous, i.e. there exists

C>0
such that
{\|{f}\|_{{{\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}}}\,}\leq C{\|{f}\|_{{{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1}}}\,}\,,\,\,f\in{{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1}}.

Function Spaces in Harmonic Analysis

Traditionally Fourier Analysis has been the source for many developments

5
Banach Function Spaces

Moving from the discrete setting of BK-spaces to the continuous setting we observe at the outset that - despite some obvious vague ideas -
the concept of what it means to deal with a
Banach space of functions

is by no means mathematically well defined and depends on the context and the authors dealing with questions in this area.

While it is clear that point evaluations
\delta_{x}:f\mapsto f(x)
are
meaningful on spaces of continuous functions they are not anymore meaningful
for standard examples such as
{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}(\mathbb{T})}
or even for
{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{2}}({\mathbb{R}})}
, except for subspaces
of band-limited functions, which form a RKH (reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

The theory of what has been called
Banach Function Spaces
seems to
fit here. It has been
developed in great detail by Luxemburg and Zaanen, in a series of more
than 17 papers, starting in 1963. It was based on the PhD thesis of
Luxemburg
[
37
]
. The book
[
55
]
, Chap.15 is a standard
reference here. There are even at least two more books on the subject:

[
38
]
and

[
56
]
, dealing with so-called
Riesz spaces
.

The goal of this theory of Banach Function Spaces was to have common setting to deal with
{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}
-spaces and their generalizations, such as Orlicz and Lorentz
spaces (see
[
2
]
) of measurable functions over general measure spaces.
Thus it is natural to include in the definition also the solidity
condition, and also that the indicator function of any set of finite
measure should belong to
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. In fact, for rearrangement
invariant spaces this is no problem, but weighted function space
do not contain indicator functions of sets of finite measure, if they
are spread out too much.

Banach function spaces are slightly different from solid BF-spaces.
On the one hand, Banach function spaces are usually assumed to contain
the indicator function of any measurable set of finite measure. Probably
this property is inspired by the fact that one was mostly concentrating
on so-called rearrangement invariant function spaces in the early period.
However, in the context of Banach spaces of measurable functions on locally
compact groups it is more natural to assume on the one hand local integrability
of the functions under consideration (with respect to the Haar measure, left or
right) and the fact that the indicator functions of arbitrary compact subsets
should belong to such a space. This mostly excludes (large) sets of the
underlying group where all the functions from a given Banach space
({\boldsymbol{B}},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\boldsymbol{B}})

vanish. In a series of recent papers this condition is prominently appearing
under the name of
ball Banach function spaces
(BBFS).

Unfortunately the concept of the BBFS concept requires (for convenience) the
validity of Fatou’s Lemma, which appears to be somehow related to the admissibility condition of Triebel. Anyway, given any Banach function
space its
\alpha
-dual, also called Koethe-dual, namely

{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\alpha}:=\{h\in{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}_{\negthinspace\it{loc}}}{\,|\,}hf\in{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}\negthinspace({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\forall f\in{\boldsymbol{B}}\}.

Any
\alpha
-dual space satisfies the Fatou property (and conversely), but
some important Banach function spaces (in the original sense) do not satisfy
this property. In fact, a few non-reflexive Banach spaces may be excluded in this case. In the discrete setting
\big({{\boldsymbol{c}}_{0}},\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\infty}\big)
would provide an example. It is
a closed subspace of
\big({{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\infty}},\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\infty}\big)
, which obviously does not satisfy the Fatou property. The authors of
[
36
]
share this opinion.

BF-spaces in Harmonic Analysis

For applications in Harmonic Analysis, especially in the study of
translation invariant Banach spaces of functions it was natural
to assume that one has local integrability (with respect to the
Lebesgue measure, which is the Haar measure on the LCA group
{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}
).
We thus came up (already in
[
13
]
) with the concept of BF-spaces,
thinking of “
Banach Function
” spaces (instead of Banach Koordinaten
spaces). This setting is meanwhile widely used in papers in the field
of time-frequency analysis. It would go beyond the scope of this note
to list only a representative list of papers using this notion.

Definition 1
.

A BF-space is a Banach space of locally integrable functions over a locally compact group. More precisely,

({\boldsymbol{B}},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\boldsymbol{B}})
is a Banach space, continuously embedded into
{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}_{\negthinspace\it{loc}}}({{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}})
, which means that for every compact
subset
K\subseteq{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}
there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
such that

\int_{K}|f(x)|dx\leq C_{K}\|f\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}\quad\forall f\in{\boldsymbol{B}}.

(4)

For discrete groups it is sufficient to have the estimate for single points, because compact sets are then
just finite (unions of one point) sets. The corresponding (older) terminology is thus a special case of
the concept of BF-space for discrete groups:

Definition 2
.

A BF-
space
is called
solid
if the following is true:

Given
g\in{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
f\in{\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
with
|f(x)|\leq|g(x)|
a.e.
implies

f\in{\boldsymbol{B}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|f\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}\leq\|g\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}.

(5)

An important subclass of solid BF-spaces are the translation invariant ones.

Ball Banach Function Spaces

Next, we recall the concept of
ball quasi-Banach function spaces

[
45
, Definition 2.2]
, where the motivation for
the change of assumptions compared to the classical Banach
function spaces is given.
They have been used extensively in a series of papers, such as

[
57
,
7
,
47
,
30
,
45
]
.
It is argued that especially
the assumption of local integrability may be too restrictive,
e.g. when one wants to include the spaces
{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
with
0<p<1

in the consideration (family of quasi-Banach spaces). Again, for
simplification we adapt the definition to the Banach space case:

Definition 3
.

A normed linear space
{\boldsymbol{X}}\subset\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
which makes sense
for all functions in
\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is called
a
ball Banach function space

(for short, BBFS) if
it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

For any
f\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}=0
if and only if
f(x)=0
almost everywhere.

(ii)

For any
g\in{\boldsymbol{X}}
one has:
Given
f\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
|f(x)|\leq|g(x)|
almost everywhere, one
has
g\in{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
\|g\|_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\leq\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
(
solidity
).

(iii)

For any sequence

\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
{\boldsymbol{X}}
with
f_{n}(x)\uparrow f(x)
a.e.
for some
f\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
one has
f\in{\boldsymbol{X}}
and

\|f_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\uparrow\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}

as
n\to\infty
(
Fatou property
);

(iv)

For any ball
B\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}},\,{\bf{1}}_{B}\in{\boldsymbol{X}}
.

We believe, that the assumption (
4
) is not really restrictive
for applications in Harmonic Analysis, among others. In fact, the
space
{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
with
0<p<1
cannot even be interpreted as (quasi)-
Banach spaces of tempered distributions, since a singularity of

h\in{{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{p}}({\mathbb{R}})}
may prohibit the interpretation as a regular distributions via

\sigma_{h}(k):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)\,h(x)dx
,
just choose a non-negative bump function
h\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Double Modules

A class of function spaces (of whatever family you may start) which is gaining relevance in the last years is the collection of Banach spaces having a double module structure, namely with respect to both convolution and pointwise multiplication. A systematic study of such spaces was already undertaken
in
[
4
]
. Note that the term “
double module
” has to be distinguished from the term “bi-module”, where one typically has a left
and a right action of two different Banach algebras, and these two actions
commute, which in such a case typically corresponds to some kind of
associativity law
.

For this author the first encounter with such structures was the study
of
strongly character invariant Segal algebra
. This are Segal
algebras in Reiter’s sense (hence homogeneous Banach spaces and thus
furthermore Banach modules over
\big({{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}\negthinspace({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{1}\big)
with respect to convolution,
in fact Banach ideals) with the extra property that multiplication
with characters is isometric, i.e. ,
{\|\chi_{s}f\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}}={\|f\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}}

for all
s\in{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}
. In this situation one shows that
s\mapsto\chi_{s}f

is a continuous mapping and via integration (see
[
20
]
for a modern
approach) one obtains that
{{\mathcal{F}}{\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}}({{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}})}\cdot{\boldsymbol{B}}={\boldsymbol{B}}
defines an additional
Banach module structure.

It was one of the key results of
[
14
]
to demonstrate that there
is a minimal space in this family of Banach spaces (it is no problem to
consider only subspaces of
\big({{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{2}\big)
, the most important space in this family)
which can be characterized as the Wiener amalgam space
{({{\boldsymbol{W}}({\mathcal{F}}{\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}}\negthinspace,{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{1}})({{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}})},\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{{{\boldsymbol{W}}({\mathcal{F}}{\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}}{\negthinspace\>\negthinspace},{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{1}})}})}
,
denoted by
\big({{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\negthinspace 0}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\negthinspace 0}}\big)
in this paper. Any solid BF-space is a pointwise
Banach module over
\big({\boldsymbol{L}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\infty}\big)
, hence also over
{\big({{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\negthinspace 0}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{\infty}\big)}
and in particular
over
\big({{\mathcal{F}}{\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}}({{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}})},\,\|\mbox{$\,\cdot\,$}\|_{{{\mathcal{F}}\negthinspace{{\boldsymbol{L}}^{1}}}}\big)
. In fact, in most cases such a double module structure
arises via the
integrated group representation
