Visual Outcomes and Patient-Reported Dysphotopsia After PanOptix and PanOptix Toric Implantation Stratified by Preoperative Chord Mu: A Prospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Xia Zhang, Lu Sun, Yongxiang Tang, Wenda Sui, Lulu Chen, Ying Xie, Ailing Bian, Yong Zhong, Zaowen Wang, Shunhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aimed to evaluate the tolerance of preoperative chord mu (angle kappa) after cataract or clear lens exchange surgery and implantation of AcrySof IQ PanOptix and PanOptix Toric in a Chinese cohort.MethodsThis prospective observational study consecutively enrolled patients undergoing PanOptix/PanOptix Toric implantation at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Eyes were analyzed by chord mu (< 0.3 mm vs. ≥ 0.3 mm), lens opacity (clear lens or cataract), and intraocular lens (PanOptix or PanOptix Toric). Visual acuity was assessed at 1 and 3 months. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed at 3 months using the IOLSAT and the QUVID.ResultsA total of 122 eyes from 62 patients were analyzed. Seventy-nine eyes received PanOptix Toric lenses, and 43 eyes received non-toric PanOptix lenses. The κ ≥ 0.3 mm group had less myopic spherical equivalent (P = 0.004) than the κ < 0.3 mm group. At 1 and 3 months, the uncorrected and corrected distance, intermediate, and near visual acuity outcomes were excellent and did not differ significantly between κ groups in the overall cohort (all P > 0.05). In the toric subgroup,1-m UNVA was better in eyes with κ < 0.3 mm (P < 0.001, adj. P = 0.024) than in those with κ ≥ 0.3 mm. Overall spectacle independence was 90% (45/50), with 100% for distance and intermediate vision in both κ groups; near spectacle independence was similar between groups (P = 0.797). The most frequent visual disturbances were halos (78%, 39/50), starbursts (60%, 30/50), and glare (42%, 21/50), with no significant differences between the κ groups (all P > 0.05). Patient-reported outcomes and disturbance frequencies were similar between PanOptix and PanOptix Toric (all P > 0.05). Compared with cataract surgery, clear lens exchange resulted in increased rates of halos (92.3% vs. 62.5%, P = 0.016).ConclusionsIn this carefully selected, small sample Chinese cohort implanted with PanOptix/PanOptix Toric lense and restricted to eyes with chord mu ≤ 0.5 mm, a κ ≥ 0.3 mm was not associated with worse overall visual acuity, spectacle independence, or the likelihood/severity of visual disturbances. The PanOptix Toric lens demonstrated patient-reported outcomes comparable to those of the non-toric PanOptix. Patients who underwent clear lens exchange reported more visual disturbances than those who underwent cataract surgery, suggesting a need for careful candidate selection and preoperative counseling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01438-6.Keywords: Chord mu, Angle kappa, Trifocal intraocular lens, PanOptix, Toric, Patient-reported outcomes, Dysphotopsia, QUVID, IOLSAT

Key Summary Points

In this prospective Chinese cohort, eyes with κ ≥ 0.3 mm achieved postoperative visual acuity, spectacle independence, and visual disturbance profiles comparable to those of eyes with κ < 0.3 mm after PanOptix or PanOptix Toric implantation.In the toric subgroup, 1-month uncorrected near visual acuity was slightly better in eyes with κ < 0.3 mm with small effect size.Overall patient-reported outcomes remained similar between the two κ groups in the toric subgroup although subgroup questionnaire analyses should be interpreted as exploratory because of the limited sample size.Patients with clear lens exchange reported more halos than cataract surgery patients, highlighting the importance of careful preoperative counseling when trifocal optics are offered to clear-lens candidates.

Introduction

In the past 10 years, refractive lens exchange with multifocal intraocular lens (MIOL) implantation has become widely used to restore functional near, intermediate, and distance vision and to reduce spectacle dependence. Previous studies have revealed that the misalignment of the visual axial, pupil center, and optical centers of the MIOL can result in an increase in intraocular higher-order aberrations and visual disturbances. Angle kappa (chord mu), therefore, is an important factor for predicting the postoperative position of MIOLs. The angle kappa is determined as the angle spanning between the visual axis and the pupillary axis. Since the angle kappa can be measured directly, there is a convention to express these angles in terms of the distance of its piercing point on the cornea, known as the chord mu [1]. Clinical guidelines often support implantation of MIOLs when the preoperative angle kappa (or chord mu) is ≤ 0.5 mm. Nevertheless, many surgeons remain cautious if angle kappa exceeds 0.3 mm, driven by concerns about increased visual disturbances such as halos, glare, and starbursts. For example, in a recent narrative review, Kohnen et al. reported that although angle kappa did not reliably influence refractive or acuity outcomes, the associations with subjective visual quality remained inconsistent [2].

Several recent studies have specifically explored chord mu (angle kappa) in relation to visual outcomes after trifocal IOL implantation. Sandoval et al. [3] reported on 56 eyes of patients implanted bilaterally with PanOptix IOLs; there was no significant correlation between preoperative angle kappa and postoperative uncorrected acuity, patient-reported satisfaction, or spectacle dependence. More recently, Ang et al. [4] evaluated 122 eyes implanted with three types of multifocal IOLs, including PanOptix, and reported that neither preoperative nor postoperative magnitude nor the change in angle kappa or angle alpha was significantly correlated with uncorrected distance, intermediate, or near visual acuity or with visual disturbances, such as halos and starbursts. However, to date no published study has specifically evaluated the predictive value of angle kappa or chord mu in patients implanted with trifocal toric IOLs, leaving a gap in the evidence for the selection of toric multifocal lens implantation candidates.

Epidemiological data suggest that approximately 58% of Chinese patients with cataract present with angle kappa < 0.3 mm, and approximately 29% are within the 0.3–0.5 mm range [5]—considered a borderline group for MIOL implantation decisions. However, a recent meta-analysis found overall pooled incidences of 30.4% for starbursts, 43.9% for halos, and 33.6% for glare after bilateral PanOptix implantation, with only 3% of patients reporting “very bothersome” symptoms [6]. These data emphasize that visual disturbances remain an important endpoint of MIOL performance evaluation. Given the high prevalence of borderline kappa values and the lack of data specifically on toric lenses, our study aimed to evaluate visual acuity and patient-reported visual disturbance outcomes stratified by angle kappa < 0.3 mm vs. ≥ 0.3 mm in a Chinese cohort implanted with PanOptix or PanOptix Toric lenses. However, as an exploratory, observational analysis in carefully selected patients within the guideline-recommended range (angle kappa ≤ 0.5 mm), our intent was not to define a universal “safe” cutoff but to provide real-world evidence on whether the commonly used 0.3-mm threshold meaningfully differentiates outcomes in this setting. The results may refine patient selection criteria and inform real-world clinical practice regarding tolerances for angle kappa in refractive lens exchange and trifocal IOL implantation within the studied range and population.

Methods

Subjects

This prospective, observational study was conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Beijing, China. Consecutive patients with age-related cataracts or clear lens exchange who were scheduled for implantation of a trifocal intraocular lens (PanOptix or PanOptix Toric, Alcon Laboratories, Inc.) between September 2022 and April 2025 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age 45–80 years, preoperative angle kappa ≤ 0.5 mm (measured using iTrace aberrometry), and willingness and ability to complete a 3-month follow-up. All the enrolled eyes were considered suitable candidates for multifocal IOL implantation after standardized preoperative evaluation and counseling according to published recommendations on IOL selection and patient eligibility [7]. Eyes were excluded if (1) they had undergone intraocular surgeries or corneal laser refractive surgeries (laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis, laser-assisted subepithelial keratectomy, small-incision lenticule extraction, or photorefractive keratectomy) before cataract surgery or during the follow-up period or had clinically significant corneal irregularity that could affect optical quality; (2) they had coexisting corneal or retinal disease, uveitis, any type of glaucoma, or non-glaucomatous optic neuropathy before or during the observation period; (3) they had severe ocular surface disease, defined as a preoperative Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) score > 33, that could affect measurements and/or visual quality; (4) they had amblyopia (defined as pre-cataract best-corrected distance visual acuity worse than 0.3 logMAR); or (5) the patient was unable to complete examinations or follow-up.

Study Endpoint

The primary endpoint was binocular uncorrected distance visual acuity (UCDVA, logMAR) at 3 months postoperatively. Secondary endpoints included monocular and binocular UCDVA and best-corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) at 1 and 3 months, uncorrected and corrected intermediate and near visual acuities (UIVA, UNVA, CIVA, and CNVA) at 3 months. Key secondary patient reported endpoints assessed at 3 months include QUVID halo rated “quite a bit/very much” and spectacle independence and satisfaction assessed via the IOLSAT. All other QUVID questionnaire derived patients reported endpoints including glare, starbursts, blurred vision, hazy vision, double vision, and dark areas and severity distribution were considered exploratory.

Statistical Power and Sample Size

This study was designed as an exploratory, consecutively enrolled observational cohort. The sample size was determined by consecutive recruitment during the study period. Power considerations are provided for the patient-reported outcome analyses. We therefore provide power context using published QUVID data to describe the detectable effect size for severe or bothersome halo. Specifically, we used QUVID “halo bothered quite a bit/very much” as a clinically meaningful endpoint, for which published trifocal IOL cohorts generally report low rates. Under a two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, detecting a large absolute difference of 20% (e.g., 5% vs. 25%) would require approximately 45 patients per group with complete questionnaires. Accordingly, the questionnaire analyses in the present study (n = 50 respondents) are exploratory, and modest between-group differences in severe/bothersome halo may not be detectable.

Intraocular Lenses

The AcrySof® IQ PanOptix® and PanOptix® Toric (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX, USA) are single-piece, UV- and blue-light-filtering, hydrophobic acrylic trifocal intraocular lenses with an overall diameter of 13.0 mm and a 6.0-mm optical zone. They incorporate a diffractive optical design to provide vision in the distance (0 D), intermediate (2.17 D at the IOL plane, 60 cm), and near (3.25 D at the IOL plane, 40 cm) ranges. The toric version additionally corrects preoperative corneal astigmatism. All lenses were targeted for emmetropia using the Barrett Universal II and Barrett Toric formulas.

Surgical Procedure

All surgeries were performed consecutively by one experienced cataract surgeon (S.Z.) at Peking Union Medical College Hospital under topical anesthesia (0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride ophthalmic drops; Santen Pharmaceutical, Japan). A 2.4-mm main clear corneal incision was made on the steepest meridian per topography (TMS-5; Tomey Corp., Japan), along with a 1.0-mm side-port incision approximately 90° away. A manual capsulorhexis of 5.5 mm was performed, followed by standard phacoemulsification (Centurion Vision System, Alcon). The IOL was implanted in the capsular bag with in-the-bag centration. The postoperative regimen included topical steroids and antibiotics for 2 weeks.

Preoperative and Postoperative Assessment

All patients underwent comprehensive ophthalmologic examinations preoperatively and postoperatively. Preoperative assessments included monocular and binocular UCDVA, CDVA, refraction, keratometry, slit-lamp examination, intraocular pressure, macular OCT, and wide-field fundus imaging. Two cataract specialists (X.Z. and L.C.) independently examined patients with a slit lamp after dilation and rated lens opacity. Patients with clear lenses who received IOLs were defined as having undergone clear lens exchange.

Biometry was performed using an IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG), and the IOL power was calculated with the Barrett Universal II and Barrett Toric formulas. Emmetropic target refraction was selected in all cases.

Angle Kappa Measurement

Angle kappa was assessed preoperatively using an iTrace ray-tracing aberrometer (Tracey Technologies, Houston, TX, USA) and was recorded as chord μ, defined as the linear distance (mm) between the pupil center and the corneal light reflex (a practical proxy for angle kappa on the corneal plane). During each visit, an experienced technician (L.S.) obtained three consecutive measurements under standardized lighting conditions. To minimize the influence of fixation instability, pupil size variability, tear film fluctuations, and device repeatability, the single acquisition with the best built-in quality metrics and stable fixation was selected for analysis. Acquisitions were excluded if (1) the eyelid blocked the cornea; (2) rejection points ≥ 10; (3) the transmission of the retinal tracking image was not located in the macula fovea; and (4) no energy spike was detected in the energy map. were excluded. A value of 0.3 mm was selected as the cutoff, and patients were divided into two groups, defined by angle kappa < 0.3 mm and ≥ 0.3 mm.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

At 3 months postoperatively, patient-reported outcomes were collected from patients who completed the Quality of Vision/Visual Disturbance questionnaire (QUVID) and IOL Satisfaction questionnaire (IOLSAT). The QUVID evaluates seven forms of visual disturbance (halos, glare, starbursts, hazy vision, blurred vision, double vision, and dark areas) in terms of frequency, severity, and bother. The IOLSAT assesses spectacle independence and overall satisfaction. All survey administration and response collection were performed under the supervision of trained technicians (W.S. and Z.Y.).

Statistical Analysis

Data that followed a normal distribution are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, whereas data that did not follow a normal distribution are expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR], Q1-Q3). Categorical variables are summarized as percentages. For the evaluation of postoperative visual acuity, eyes were grouped by angle kappa (< 0.3 mm vs. ≥ 0.3 mm). For the evaluation of postoperative visual disturbance, patients were grouped according to the eye with a larger angle kappa (< 0.3 mm vs. ≥ 0.3 mm). Residual cylinder is summarized at the patient level as the mean absolute postoperative cylinder across eyes. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared between groups using the independent-samples t test or Welch’s t test, whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test. P values for eye-level variables were derived from generalized estimating equation models to account for inter-eye correlation. To address potential confounding by baseline differences between chord mu groups, additional covariate-adjusted analyses were performed. For eye-level outcomes, multivariable GEE models [8,9] were fitted with patient identification as the clustering variable and an exchangeable working correlation structure. Age, spherical equivalent, anterior chamber depth, and axial length were included as covariates. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using linear GEE models, whereas binary outcomes were analyzed using logistic GEE models. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and R software version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (approval no. K25N0454). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This study was prospectively registered in the Medical Research Registration Information System of the China National Healthcare Information Platform. The registration number is MR-11-25-048434.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Between September 2022 and April 2025, a total of 135 eyes of 69 individuals were eligible for trifocal IOL implantation and underwent lens extraction with implantation of AcrySof IQ PanOptix or PanOptix Toric lenses at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Following the application of exclusion criteria, 2 individuals with a history of uveitis (4 eyes) and 2 individuals with amblyopia (3 eyes) were excluded from the study. After the exclusion of patients who did not complete postoperative assessments, the final analysis included 122 eyes from 62 patients (Fig. 1). The cohort comprised 38 female patients and 24 male patients, with a mean age of 53.00 ± 8.18 years (Table 1). In total, 79 eyes received PanOptix Toric lenses, and 43 eyes received non-toric PanOptix lenses. The mean preoperative spherical equivalent (SE) was − 4.66 ± 5.81 D, reflecting moderate to high myopia. The median preoperative CDVA was 0.10 logMAR, and the median UCDVA was 1.30 logMAR, indicating significant preoperative refractive error. Notably, 48.3% of eyes (59/122) had a CDVA of 0.0 logMAR or better, while the poorest baseline CDVA observed was 0.9 logMAR (Table 1). Consequently, 53.3% (65/122) of eyes underwent clear lens exchange, whereas 46.7% (57/122) eyes underwent cataract surgery.

Among the 122 included eyes, 41 had κ ≥ 0.3 mm, and 81 had κ < 0.3 mm. The κ ≥ 0.3 mm group had a less myopic spherical equivalent (− 1.09 ± 5.07 vs. − 6.45 ± 5.32; P = 0.004) than the κ < 0.3 mm group. Age (55.92 ± 6.01 vs. 51.50 ± 8.73; P = 0.076) and axial length (24.51 ± 2.32 vs. 25.52 ± 1.90; P = 0.116) showed numerical differences but were not statistically significant after GEE analysis. Anterior chamber depth, white-to-white distance, and pupil diameter did not significantly differ between the groups (all P > 0.05). Residual cylinder was similar between the κ groups (0.360 ± 0.321 D for κ ≥ 0.3 vs. 0.373 ± 0.266 D for κ < 0.3; P = 0.514). The proportion with residual cylinder ≤ 0.50 D was also comparable (85.3% [35/41] vs. 83.9% [68/81]; P = 0.822). The median distance of chord mu was 0.377 (0.342, 0.431) mm in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm group and 0.19 (0.140, 0.247) mm in the κ < 0.3 mm group (P < 0.001; all shown in Table 1).

Visual Acuity Outcomes

At 1 month postoperatively, monocular distance, intermediate, and near visual acuity outcomes, including 1-month UCDVA (P = 0.432), UIVA (P = 0.176), and UNVA (P = 0.074, adj P = 0.527, adjusted β, 0.010 logMAR; 95% CI − 0.021, 0.042; Table 1), were comparable between the two groups. At 3 months, visual acuity outcomes remained excellent and did not significantly differ between groups: the 3-month UCDVA was 0.0 (0.0, 0.0) logMAR in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm group and 0.0 (− 0.1, 0.075) logMAR in the κ < 0.3 mm group (P = 0.895); the 3-month UIVA was 0.0 (0.0, 0.1) vs. 0.0 (0.0, 0.1) logMAR (P = 0.911); and the 3-month UNVA was 0.0 (0.0, 0.1) vs. 0.1 (0.0, 0.1) logMAR (P = 0.818). Corrected outcomes at 3 months were similar, including CDVA (P = 0.517), CIVA (P = 0.459), and CNVA (P = 0.558; Table 1).

In the PanOptix Toric subgroup, GEE and covariate-adjusted GEE analyses were performed to account for inter-eye correlation and baseline differences in age, spherical equivalent, axial length, and anterior chamber depth. At 1 month postoperatively, UCDVA did not differ significantly between the κ groups after adjustment (adjusted β, − 0.002 logMAR; 95% CI − 0.049 to 0.045; adjusted P = 0.925, Table 2). Similarly, UIVA showed no statistically significant adjusted difference (adjusted β, − 0.027 logMAR; 95% CI − 0.062 to 0.008; adjusted P = 0.131). Although 1-month UNVA was statistically worse in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm group after adjustment (adjusted β, 0.044 logMAR; 95% CI 0.006–0.082; adjusted P = 0.024), the magnitude of this difference was small and below one line of visual acuity.

Patient-Reported Visual Outcomes and Spectacle Independence

Patient-reported outcomes were available for the subsets of patients who completed each questionnaire (n = 50). Baseline characteristics between questionnaire completers and non-completers are presented in Table S1. All patient-reported outcomes were analyzed on a per-patient basis. Patients were grouped by chord mu: κ ≥ 0.3 mm was defined as chord mu ≥ 0.3 mm in either eye, whereas κ < 0.3 mm required chord mu < 0.3 mm in both eyes. Tolerance of Angle Kappa Size for PanOptix Toric Implantation

Subgroup studies were performed in patients implanted with PanOptix Toric lenses and in patients who underwent clear lens exchange to further evaluate the tolerance of large chord mu in patients implanted with toric trifocal lenses and in patients with superior preoperative visual acuity.

The PanOptix Toric cohort encompassed 79 eyes with κ ≥ 0.3 mm in 24 eyes and κ < 0.3 mm in 55 eyes. Patient-reported outcomes were available for 30 patients, comprising 13 patients with κ ≥ 0.3 mm and 17 patients with κ < 0.3 mm (Table S2). The proportion of patients reporting any degree of postoperative spectacle dependence was 13% (4/30) overall and did not differ between the κ groups (7.6% vs. 18%, P = 0.613; Table S2). The mean IOLSAT satisfaction score was significantly higher in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm group than in the κ < 0.3 mm group (24.61 ± 3.79 vs. 20.76 ± 4.86, P = 0.036). However, this exploratory finding should be interpreted cautiously because only 30 patients contributed questionnaire data. Visual disturbance frequencies were similar between groups, with starbursts occurring in 46% of the patients in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm group vs. 76% in the κ < 0.3 mm group (P = 0.132), halos in 84% vs. 76% (P = 0.672), glare in 46% vs. 35% (P = 0.711), hazy vision in 23% vs. 29% (P > 0.999), blurred vision in 23% vs. 24% (P = 0.672), double vision in 15% vs. 24% (P = 0.469), and dark areas in 0% vs. 12% (P = 0.492, all shown in Table S2).

Tolerance of Angle Kappa for Clear Lens Exchange

Sixty-five eyes, including 22 eyes with κ ≥ 0.3 mm and 43 eyes with κ < 0.3 mm, underwent clear lens exchange. Satisfaction outcomes were available for 26 individuals (13 per group, Table S3). The proportion of patients reporting any postoperative spectacle dependence was 15% (4/26) overall and did not differ between the κ ≥ 0.3 mm and κ < 0.3 mm groups (7.6% vs. 23%, P = 0.593; Table S3). The mean IOLSAT satisfaction score was also similar between the groups (22.38 ± 4.49 vs. 20.84 ± 5.17, P = 0.426). The frequency of visual disturbances, including starbursts (P = 0.680), halos (P > 0.999), glare (P = 0.434), hazy vision (P > 0.999), blurred vision (P > 0.999 ), double vision (P > 0.999), and dark areas (P > 0.999, all shown in Table S3), did not significantly differ between the κ ≥ 0.3 mm and κ < 0.3 mm groups.

Discussion

Over the past few years, the clinical relevance of preoperative angle kappa (often reported as chord μ) for trifocal IOL implantation has been repeatedly discussed, mainly because of the theoretical concern that a larger offset between the visual axis and the pupil center may increase photic phenomena and reduce the quality of vision [2]. However, the overall evidence remains mixed. Recent work has generally suggested that angle kappa is not a consistent predictor of postoperative visual acuity or quality of vision after trifocal implantation, although individual studies differ in design, IOL platform, and patient-reported outcome tools [1–3]. In addition, our recent prospective work in an East Asian cohort suggested that neither preoperative chord μ nor chord α showed a robust relationship with postoperative vision or patient-reported outcomes after trifocal IOL implantation [10]. Nevertheless, evidence specifically addressing trifocal toric lenses remains scarce. This gap is clinically relevant because toric trifocal implantation introduces additional alignment-related considerations [11], and surgeons often hesitate when angle kappa is borderline. Therefore, we prospectively enrolled 62 patients (122 eyes), including 79 eyes receiving PanOptix Toric lenses, to evaluate visual outcomes and patient-reported visual quality stratified by angle kappa < 0.3 mm versus angle kappa ≥ 0.3 mm.

In our cohort, eyes with larger angle kappa were often associated with older age, shorter axial length, and hyperopic refraction. This pattern is in line with prior population-based observations [5, 12]. Despite these baseline differences, we found that visual performance after PanOptix/PanOptix Toric implantation was comparable and excellent in both groups. At both 1 month and 3 months, uncorrected distance/intermediate/near acuity did not differ significantly between the κ groups. These findings align with those of Sandoval et al. [3], who found no significant correlation between preoperative angle kappa and postoperative visual acuity or patient satisfaction in 56 eyes implanted with the PanOptix IOL. These results support that angle kappa may not serve as a key predictor of postoperative vision in trifocal IOL implantation [13]. This interpretation is also consistent with the review by Fernández et al. [14]., who emphasized that chord mu, chord alpha, and related ocular-axis measurements should prompt careful evaluation of atypical cases but should not be used as isolated exclusion criteria for IOL implantation; they also highlighted postoperative IOL centration as an important determinant of visual outcomes.

In the PanOptix Toric subgroup, most early postoperative visual acuity outcomes remained comparable between κ groups after adjustment for age, spherical equivalent, axial length, and anterior chamber depth. Although 1-month UNVA showed a statistically significant adjusted difference favoring the κ < 0.3 mm group, the adjusted effect size was only 0.044 logMAR, with the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval below 0.1 logMAR. Therefore, this difference was smaller than one line of visual acuity and is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. These findings support the interpretation that, within the studied chord mu range, a κ value around the 0.3-mm threshold may be associated with minor numerical differences in selected acuity measures but does not appear to substantially compromise postoperative visual performance. Previous studies have shown that trifocal toric lens implantation provides satisfactory surgical outcomes as well as patient-reported visual outcomes [15,16], which is consistent with our observations.

Regarding postoperative visual disturbances, halos, starbursts, and glare were the most frequently reported symptoms in our cohort, whereas hazy vision, blurred vision, double vision, and dark areas were less common. The rates of these symptoms did not differ significantly between the different angle kappa groups. This overall pattern is consistent with the concept that photic phenomena remain relatively common after diffractive trifocal optics but are often mild and acceptable for most patients [17,18]. Previous studies have shown that angle kappa is not significantly correlated with postoperative visual disturbances in recipients of enhanced-depth IOLs [19] or multifocal IOLs [3]. When we further examined the symptom severity distribution, severe disturbances were uncommon in both angle kappa groups, and the “very much” category generally accounted for only a small fraction of the responses across the whole cohort. Taken together, these findings suggest a small directionally consistent trend toward slightly better distance acuity with smaller angle kappa in toric eyes, but the magnitude was limited and did not translate to worse patient-reported satisfaction in our dataset, supporting the concept that visual quality following toric trifocal IOL implantation is multifactorial. In addition to residual refractive error and accuracy of astigmatism correction, postoperative visual quality may also be influenced by mesopic pupil size, angle alpha, IOL centration [20], posterior corneal astigmatism, ocular surface quality, and neuroadaptation, rather than by a single preoperative geometric metric [21].

From the perspective of spectacle independence, both angle kappa groups achieved high overall freedom from spectacles, with complete distance and intermediate spectacle independence in both groups and near spectacle independence in approximately 90% of participants. These results are clinically reassuring and align with those of previous studies on trifocal IOLs, where functional range is typically achieved for most patients, while near tasks may still require occasional correction in a minority of patients [22,23]. Importantly, overall satisfaction (IOLSAT Part II) did not differ significantly between angle kappa groups in the whole cohort, supporting the fact that angle kappa around the 0.3-mm threshold is not, by itself, a strong discriminator of patient-perceived success after trifocal implantation.

We further compared visual disturbances after PanOptix and PanOptix Toric implantation directly. In our data, toric implantation did not result in reduced satisfaction or increased rates of visual disturbances. This finding is clinically useful because it supports the use of toric trifocal IOLs in patients with corneal astigmatism without assuming an inevitable penalty in subjective visual quality, provided that standard surgical planning and alignment principles are followed. The apparently higher IOLSAT satisfaction score in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm toric subgroup should be interpreted as exploratory and likely influenced by the small questionnaire sample size, rather than as evidence that larger chord mu improves satisfaction.

A critical finding of our study was that patients with clear lens exchange (CLE) reported higher rates of halos (88%) than those with preoperative cataracts. The higher rate of halos among patients with CLE should be interpreted as a real-world association rather than an independent effect of CLE itself, as these patients may differ from patients with cataract in baseline lens clarity, refractive status, visual expectations [24], and sensitivity to multifocal optical phenomena [25].

To further clarify the tolerance of larger angle κ in “higher-demand” subgroups, we performed subgroup analyses in toric eyes and in patients with CLE. In the toric subgroup, spectacle dependence and disturbance rates remained similar across the κ strata, whereas the satisfaction score was paradoxically higher in the κ ≥ 0.3 mm group. At the same time, the results of the toric acuity analysis suggested slightly better UCDVA in the κ < 0.3 mm group. Clinically, this combination may reflect that satisfaction is influenced by multiple dimensions beyond single-timepoint monocular acuity and that the benefit of correcting corneal astigmatism outweighs the potential minor optical degradation caused by a larger angle kappa. Moreover, it highlights the likelihood of sampling effects in subgroup analyses with limited size. In the CLE subgroup, κ did not materially affect spectacle dependence, satisfaction, or disturbance rates, suggesting that within the studied κ range, angle κ alone is not sufficient to identify CLE candidates at high risk for dissatisfaction. These results supported the use of toric trifocal IOLs or clear lens exchange, even in patients with borderline angle kappa, to provide satisfactory postoperative visual quality.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, although the overall cohort included more than 120 eyes, the patient-reported outcome analyses were limited to the subset completing questionnaires (50 of 69 enrolled patients), and subgroup analyses further reduced statistical power. The study was exploratory and based on consecutive enrollment, without a formal sample-size calculation; therefore, modest between-group differences in visual disturbance or satisfaction cannot be excluded. Consistent with published QUVID data in trifocal IOL recipients, larger samples would be required to detect modest between-group differences in severe or bothersome dysphotopsia. Therefore, our questionnaire analyses should be interpreted as exploratory, and smaller effects cannot be excluded. Eye-level analyses were reanalyzed using GEE models to account for inter-eye correlation; nevertheless, residual confounding may remain because of the observational design. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short; longer-term observation may better capture neuroadaptation and gauge the stability of subjective symptoms. Third, contrast sensitivity and objective straylight testing were not performed, limiting objective assessment of visual quality. Fourth, postoperative IOL centration, tilt, and toric rotation were not systematically assessed. This is relevant because recent toric IOL data, including the study by Shetty et al. [26], suggest that postoperative IOL alignment and rotation may influence refractive efficacy. Fifth, while surgery was standardized, the study was conducted at a single center and may not represent all practice patterns. In the analysis of patient-reported visual outcomes and spectacle independence, a dominant-eye-based analysis was not performed because ocular dominance was not prospectively recorded. Potential response bias should be considered, as questionnaire completers and non-completers differed in IOL type, spherical equivalent, and axial length. Although QUVID and IOLSAT were selected a priori to assess trifocal IOL–related photic phenomena, spectacle independence, and satisfaction, future studies using the QoV [27] questionnaire may provide more granular assessment of visual quality.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort implanted with PanOptix and PanOptix Toric IOLs, κ ≥ 0.3 mm versus κ < 0.3 mm was not associated with clinically meaningful differences in uncorrected or corrected visual outcomes in the overall cohort at 1 and 3 months. Angle κ was also not strongly related to the frequency or severity distribution of postoperative visual disturbances, and this pattern was consistent in the overall analysis and in the toric and CLE subgroups. PanOptix and PanOptix Toric IOLs showed comparable patient-reported outcomes and disturbance profiles. By contrast, patients with CLE tended to report more disturbances than patients with cataract, with significant differences in the frequency of halos, emphasizing the need for careful patient selection and expectation management when trifocal optics are offered to clear-lens candidates.
