Applications of the Early Warning Score as an outcome measure: a systematic review of methodologies
Authors: Katherine Griffin, Jessica M Ryan, Jan Sorensen, Laura Labbe, Qistina Emily Mohammad Feisal, Dara O Kavanagh, Walter Eppich, Deborah A McNamara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate how Early Warning Scores (EWS) are used and analysed as research outcome measures in interventional and comparative studies and to assess the consistency and methodological rigour of these approaches.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (INPLASY 202540054), searching OVID Medline (MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL) (1946 to 6 January 2026) for interventional or comparative studies using an EWS as a research outcome measure. Three reviewers independently screened articles, extracted data and performed a narrative synthesis of methodological approaches regarding the use and analysis of EWS as a research outcome.ResultsEWS were most commonly derived from intermittent ward observations (92/127, 72.0%) rather than continuous monitoring (2/127, 1.6%). Outcomes were typically defined as clinically interpretable summary measures, including index-time values, maximum scores within defined windows or threshold-based categories. Temporal analysis was limited, with most studies not incorporating time explicitly (91/127, 71.7%). Where time was considered (32/127, 25.2%), approaches focused on short-horizon prediction or fixed time point comparisons rather than modelling longitudinal trajectories; explicit assessment of change in EWS was uncommon (14/127, 11.0%). Reporting prioritised discrimination (98/127, 77.2%) over calibration (38/127, 29.9%), with variable consideration of repeated measures, missing data and validation.ConclusionsEWS are predominantly used as simple, clinically interpretable severity endpoints rather than as longitudinal measures of physiological change. Inconsistent handling of temporality and limited methodological standardisation restrict comparability and interpretation despite the availability of repeated time-stamped data. Development of reporting and analytic guidance is needed to support more robust use of EWS as research outcomes.Keywords: Outcome and Process Assessment, Health Care; Outcome Assessment, Health Care; Critical Care Outcomes; Electronic Health Records; Access to Information

Introduction

Early Warning Scores (EWS) are increasingly used as outcome measures in interventional and comparative research evaluating therapeutic interventions, clinical pathways and service-level innovations.1 2 EWS are composite clinical tools that combine routinely measured physiological observations (including respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, temperature and level of consciousness) into a single score representing the severity of acute illness.3 Increasing scores correspond to increasing risk of clinical deterioration and trigger predefined escalation pathways, such as increased observation frequency, rapid response team review or critical care referral. Numerous EWS have been developed for different patient populations and healthcare settings and those included in this review are detailed in table 1 and table 2. Although these systems differ in physiological thresholds, weighting and intended populations, they share the common principle of repeated bedside assessment, generating longitudinal physiological data that can be operationalised in multiple ways as research outcomes.2 Their appeal as research outcomes lies in their widespread availability, standardised structure and ability to summarise routinely collected physiological data that inform clinical escalation, such as increased monitoring or rapid response activation.3 Derived from repeatedly measured vital signs, EWS generate correlated observations with complex temporal patterns of deterioration, stability and recovery.4 5 Unlike retrospective systems such as the Clavien-Dindo classification,6 EWS provide a prospective, real-time, physiology-based measure of patient status. Their relevance as research outcomes is likely to grow with increasing adoption of continuous monitoring and expanded capacity to manage large-scale clinical data.7–9

Beyond bedside escalation, EWS can be operationalised in multiple ways in research, including as threshold-based binary outcomes,10 summary measures within defined time windows,11 or indicators of change over time.1 However, methodological and reporting standards remain poorly defined. Missing physiological data are common, yet there is no consensus on handling approaches.12 More broadly, existing studies tend to prioritise discrimination over calibration, limiting assessment of how well predicted risks align with observed outcomes and constraining interpretability and comparability across settings.

This review synthesises how EWS have been used as research outcome measures, with particular focus on their operationalisation and the incorporation of time and change, to inform more consistent and methodologically robust future use.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.8 A review protocol was developed a priori and prospectively registered on INPLASY (INPLASY202540054). The review question, eligibility criteria and outcomes of interest were defined using a PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome), detailed in online supplemental figure 1.

Search strategy and study selection

In collaboration with an information specialist, we searched OVID Medline (1946 to 6 January 2026) using a combination of subject headings and keywords for Early Warning Scores (including “early warning score”, “national early warning score”, “paediatric/pediatric early warning score”, “maternal/maternity early warning score”, and abbreviations such as EWS, PEWS and MEWS), combined with terms for health outcomes (including outcome assessment, treatment outcome, patient outcome and fatal outcome), with results limited to human studies.

Three reviewers independently screened title and abstract for all retrieved citations, with full-text review for potentially eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Studies in which conventional EWS were used as comparator outcomes, methodological reference standards or benchmark clinical scores within algorithmic early warning system development were retained where they informed methodological approaches to EWS operationalisation. The study selection process is summarised in a PRISMA flow diagram provided in online supplemental figure 2.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted using a standardised form, including study design, setting, population, EWS type, data source, outcomes and statistical methods, along with frequency of observations, handling of repeated measures and missing data, and reporting of discrimination, calibration, validation and workload.

Physiological data capture was classified as continuous or intermittent. Continuous monitoring was defined as automated recording at ≥1 observation per minute without clinician initiation, distinguishing time-series monitoring from routine observations. Intermittent monitoring referred to clinician-initiated measurements at discrete time points, typically every 4–12 hours or more frequently if clinically indicated.

Missing data were defined as absence of one or more variables required to calculate an EWS at a given time point, including incomplete observation sets or gaps between expected measurements, and were assumed to reflect routine clinical practice where reported. Owing to heterogeneity in study design and analysis, results were synthesised descriptively without meta-analysis.

Methodological assessment

The research team undertook a narrative assessment of methodological practices for the use of EWS as a research outcome. This included evaluation of how studies assessed discrimination, calibration, validation and how they applied threshold-based analyses. Authors reviewed how studies handled repeated measures, missing data and time-dependent observations and how they compared performance across alternative EWS. Where studies reported dynamic modelling, authors evaluated the techniques used, along with subgroup analyses and context-specific results across clinical settings.

Risk-of-bias assessment

A formal study-level risk-of-bias assessment was not undertaken because the objective of this review was to characterise methodological approaches to the operationalisation and analysis of EWS as research outcome measures, rather than to estimate intervention effects, diagnostic accuracy or prognostic performance. Additionally, the included studies comprised heterogeneous designs, for which no single validated risk-of-bias instrument is applicable. Instead, methodological features directly relevant to the review objective were systematically extracted and synthesised.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Results

Search results

Of 898 records identified and screened, 261 underwent full-text review and 127 studies met the predefined eligibility criteria.

How EWS were operationalised as research outcomes

Across the dataset, EWS were most often operationalised as outcome measures that compress routinely collected physiology into interpretable summaries aligned with escalation logic, including a single EWS value at a defined index time (eg, admission/referral),13 14 a maximum or ‘worst’ EWS within a time window,11 15 threshold exceedance (eg, National Early Warning Score (NEWS) ≥5 or ≥7), or risk-band categorisations used to define deterioration,10 16 escalation,17 18 Intensive Care Unit (ICU) transfer19 20 or mortality risk.10 17 19 21 This framing supported standardised endpoint definition across heterogeneous datasets, but it also meant that EWS were frequently treated as episodic severity markers rather than longitudinal outcomes intended to quantify within-patient physiological change.22 23

Data sources and measurement provenance

Where reported, nurse-recorded intermittent observations, recorded manually or electronically, were the dominant data source for EWS outcomes; electronic health records (EHRs) and electronic observation systems often acted as the source systems for time-stamped vital signs24 25 (online supplemental table 1). Studies explicitly using EHR-derived data to generate EWS outcomes were common (n=48, 37.8%) with a smaller subset reporting EWS outcomes from electronic observation systems (n=11, 8.7%) (online supplemental table 1). Manual extraction from paper charts or nursing documentation was less frequently specified (n=12, 9.4%).10 13 14 16 26–29 Continuous physiological monitoring was rare (n=2, 1.6%),30 31 with most studies relying on intermittent, non-continuous ward observations (n=92, 72.4%) (online supplemental table 1).

Temporal handling and change-based outcomes

Most studies did not incorporate temporal analysis (n=91, 71.7%), with fewer undertaking time-based approaches (n=32, 25.2%) (online supplemental table 4). Even within these, time was typically used for short-horizon prediction rather than modelling EWS trajectories, reflecting a preference for operationally interpretable endpoints.1 4 13 17 25 32 33

Recent studies have operationalised change in EWS as an outcome in two distinct ways. Ryan et al1 measured EWS at fixed time points following an intervention (baseline, 6, 12, 24 hours) and analysed differences between groups at each time point, treating EWS as a time-indexed physiological measure.1 In contrast, Ehmann et al33 derived trend features from serial NEWS observations by estimating slope and intercept parameters using linear regression across up to five consecutive measurements.33 These trajectory-based features were incorporated into a logistic regression model, with performance evaluated using calibration (integrated calibration index), efficiency and decision curve analysis. These approaches represent two methods of analysing EWS change: fixed time point comparisons following an intervention and trajectory-based modelling of serial observations. Other studies more commonly reported EWS change descriptively or as a secondary measure.

Analytic evaluation of EWS outcomes

Discrimination reflects how well an EWS separates patients who deteriorate from those who do not.34 Most studies reported discrimination using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUROC) with 95% CIs (n=98, 77.2%) (table 3), reflecting emphasis on distinguishing outcomes within defined time horizons. AUROC represents the probability that a deteriorating patient has a higher score than a non-deteriorating patient.35–37 Comparative analyses commonly used DeLong’s test.38 Some studies used time-dependent ROC methods24 39 or survival analysis (eg, Kaplan-Meier, Cox models) to account for changing risk over time (table 3).

Calibration, describing agreement between predicted and observed risk, was less frequently assessed (n=38, 29.59%). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was most common (n=11, 8.7%),40 with few studies reporting calibration slope and intercept (n=3, 2.4%) or calibration plots (n=3, 2.4%) (table 3). Overall, discrimination was reported more consistently than calibration, limiting interpretation of absolute risk even when EWS were used as outcomes.34 41

Subgroup and context-specific findings

Context-specific and pathway-specific analyses were common, with studies applying similar EWS-derived outcomes (eg, worst score within a time window, threshold exceedance, short-horizon deterioration) across clinical pathways rather than modelling longitudinal trajectories. This was particularly evident in critical care contexts, including ICU admission, post-ICU care and step-down settings21 24 42 (online supplemental table 1).

Similar approaches were used in respiratory, sepsis and COVID-19 cohorts43 44 (online supplemental table 2), where EWS defined severity strata or adverse outcomes from routine observations. Applications also extended to oncology/haematology and immunocompromised populations23 45 (online supplemental table 2).

Paediatric studies used population-specific scores to define deterioration, escalation or treatment response26 31 (online supplemental table 2).

Bias, limitations and reporting transparency

Across studies, the most common limitations related to study design and generalisability. Many were single-centre or context-specific, with limited transferability across organisations, case-mix or escalation systems, and a need for external validation or multisite replication (online supplemental table 3). Interventional studies were further constrained by small sample sizes, low event rates, or open-label designs, limiting inference for EWS-defined outcomes.46 47

Limitations also related to data completeness and measurement quality. Incomplete capture or variable recording of key components (eg, consciousness, oxygen use) may introduce misclassification and reduce comparability1 3 (online supplemental table 3). Missing data were often handled by excluding incomplete observations or records, risking bias when missingness is informative33 48 (online supplemental table 3). Simple imputation approaches—such as last observation carried forward,32 49 nearest value selection27 or assuming normality50 51—were also used, which may attenuate or distort signals during physiological change. Missing-data handling was sometimes not reported, limiting transparency13 30 (online supplemental table 3).

Analytic limitations were also common. Small sample sizes and low event rates reduced precision and stability of performance estimates, particularly for high-threshold or rare outcomes52–54 (online supplemental table 3). Few studies accounted for within-patient correlation from repeated measures, risking underestimated uncertainty.55–57 Additional issues included irregular measurement timing and timestamp accuracy11 19 22 (online supplemental table 3), which may affect time-based analyses. While some studies used validation, sensitivity analyses or explicit data-quality rules,3–5 13 32 58 59 others reported no validation,42 60 61 limiting generalisability.

Machine-learning and algorithmic early warning systems

A subset of studies operated at the interface between EWS-as-outcome research and machine-learning (ML) or algorithmic early warning systems (n=13, 10.2%) (see table 4). In these studies, the main analytic product was a continuously updated risk estimate or alert rather than a fixed, guideline-based score. Conventional EWS (eg, NEWS/Modified Early Warning Score/Paediatric Early Warning Score) were commonly used as comparators or baseline features while ML models (including random forest/gradient boosting, deep learning or penalised regression approaches) generated alternative risk scores or early warning systems from routine EHR observations. Despite time-updated modelling, outcomes were usually defined as discrete deterioration events within a fixed horizon (eg, ICU transfer, cardiac arrest, death within 24–48 hours), rather than as longitudinal measures of within-patient physiological change. When time was used more explicitly, studies summarised recent observations (eg, the highest recent value or a short-term trend)49 62 or calculated change scores, but this was uncommon across the wider literature.49

Discussion

Summary of results

Across the included studies, EWS were most often operationalised as clinically interpretable summary endpoints rather than longitudinal measures of physiological change.21 22 63–65 Although this reflects current clinical practice and supports pragmatic use of EWS as research outcomes, considerable variation in temporal handling and analytical approaches limits comparability across studies.

Availability of data

A consistent theme across the literature is that the inputs for EWS-outcome research have become richer: many studies drew on time-stamped EHR data and/or electronic observation platforms that can support repeated-measures analyses at scale.3 4 17 24 25 32 65 Where electronic data capture was available, it primarily reflected improved digitisation and timestamping rather than continuous physiological monitoring.13 22 26 27 59 66 This distinction is important because it frames the kind of questions that can be answered reliably and where automation may reduce burden without changing the underlying measurement modality.17 24 67 As analytic capacity and the ability to manage large datasets expands, there is opportunity to improve the methodological quality of EWS-outcome research through more consistent handling of repeated observations, missing data, data validation and standardised outcome derivation. As electronic health systems continue to mature, establishing clearer methodological standards for managing these routinely collected data will be essential to improve the reproducibility, comparability and scalability of EWS-derived outcomes across healthcare settings.

Underutilisation of available data

Despite the prevalence of repeated observations in electronic datasets, many studies summarised serial measures using clinically meaningful endpoints such as admission scores, maximum values or threshold exceedance.14 17 19 21 64 These approaches are appropriate for some comparative and service-evaluation studies; however, they may be less informative when the objective is to evaluate physiological recovery or treatment response over time.3 22 63 65 As digital health infrastructure and analytical capacity have advanced, the computational and practical constraints that once favoured simple summary outcomes have diminished, creating opportunities to make fuller use of routinely collected physiological data while selecting outcome definitions that best align with the research question. This evolution also underscores the need for greater methodological standardisation to ensure that increasingly sophisticated analytical approaches remain transparent, reproducible and comparable across studies.

Limited analysis of trends or temporal change

A key finding of this review is the relative scarcity of analyses that treat EWS as explicitly time-indexed outcomes. Most studies operationalised EWS using clinically interpretable summary endpoints. Where temporal analyses were undertaken, two broad approaches emerged: repeated comparisons at predefined time points and trajectory-based modelling of serial observations. Although both demonstrate that longitudinal analysis of EWS is feasible, these approaches remained uncommon relative to analyses centred on summary endpoints.1 33 Across the dataset, ‘dynamic’ methods most often appeared in the context of near-term risk prediction (or time-updated modelling for deterioration within a horizon), rather than as longitudinal outcome modelling of EWS trajectories.4 25 32 49 This helps to explain why temporal information was frequently used to improve prediction performance, while outcome operationalisation remained anchored to clinically interpretable summary outcomes.17 22 64 65 The practical implication is that EWS are commonly treated as episodic severity markers in research while their potential role as outcomes capturing direction and rate of change remains underdeveloped.1 33 Advances in this use of EWS as outcome measures are compelling, given the greater computing capacity to manage complex datasets.

Inconsistent reporting of key methodological domains

Methodological reporting suggests a generally consistent emphasis on discrimination-based evaluation, with comparatively variable attention to calibration, missing-data handling, repeated-measures dependence, workload/alert burden and generalisability.3 17 24 25 32 This may reflect the historical emphasis within EWS validation studies on ranking patients according to deterioration risk, whereas assessment of absolute risk calibration has received comparatively less attention, particularly in smaller observational studies. Improved calibration reporting would facilitate assessment of transportability across healthcare systems and increase confidence in applying EWS-derived outcomes beyond the populations in which they were developed. Where studies reported internal validation, sensitivity analyses or explicit data-quality rules, interpretability was strengthened by clarifying how EWS outcomes were derived and how robust findings were to analytic choices.17 33 65 Conversely, studies that did not report validation or provided limited detail on missingness and repeated-measures handling constrain confidence in generalisability across settings, particularly when EWS outcomes depend on local documentation practices and escalation systems.16 68 69

Challenges in threshold evaluation, workload assessment and model validation

Because EWS outcomes are often anchored to escalation thresholds, the evidentiary value of many analyses depends on how threshold choice affects both discrimination and operational burden.17 22 64 65 Yet workload quantification and decision-analytic evaluation were less prominent than conventional diagnostic-performance reporting, limiting translation from performance to service impact in some settings.17 22 24 70 Similarly, limited external validation in many studies reduces confidence that EWS-outcome definitions (and any derived effects) will hold when escalation pathways, observation frequency and case-mix differ.5 16 17

Contextual considerations: population diversity and data capture

The evidence base remains concentrated in adult inpatient care, while paediatric applications illustrate that outcome definitions and thresholds are inherently population-dependent and context-dependent (see online supplemental table 2). The predominance of adult inpatient studies should be recognised when interpreting these findings. This is particularly relevant because paediatric and obstetric EWS differ substantially in physiological thresholds, score construction, escalation criteria and intended clinical application. Consequently, methodological recommendations derived predominantly from adult studies should not be assumed to transfer directly to these populations without further validation. Most included studies used intermittent ward observations, so ‘time’ is usually a series of separate measurements rather than continuous monitoring. This makes it straightforward to analyse scores within time windows or to summarise short-term trends, but more difficult to describe true trajectories unless studies account for irregular observation timing and repeated measurements within the same patient.4 17 25 33 As continuous monitoring through wearable technologies or intensive care systems becomes more widespread, analytical approaches developed for intermittent ward observations may not be directly transferable. Separate methodological guidance may therefore be required to address the differing characteristics of continuous and intermittent physiological data.

Machine-learning and algorithmic early warning systems

A subset of studies examined ML or algorithmic warning systems that produced continuously updated risk estimates from routinely collected observations, with conventional EWS primarily serving as comparator scores or benchmark clinical standards.3 4 33 49 Interestingly, advances in ML methodology have not yet translated into widespread adoption of longitudinal EWS outcome definitions. Despite increasingly sophisticated modelling approaches, studies continued to define outcomes using conventional deterioration events within fixed prediction horizons, reflecting a continued emphasis on improving prediction accuracy rather than reconsidering how EWS themselves are operationalised as research outcomes.4 49 This finding reinforces the broader pattern identified throughout this review, namely that methodological innovation has largely focused on generating better predictors of deterioration rather than exploiting the longitudinal information contained within serial EWS observations. Nevertheless, studies that incorporated engineered trend features or quantified changes in EWS demonstrate that serial observations can be analysed in clinically interpretable ways while retaining the temporal information inherent in repeated physiological measurements. Although these approaches remain uncommon, they illustrate practical methodological strategies that future evaluative research could adopt when physiological recovery or deterioration over time is of primary interest.1 3 33 65

Limitations

This review is constrained by the completeness and clarity of reporting in included studies. The heterogeneity of populations, settings and outcome operationalisations limits direct comparability, particularly where thresholds and window definitions differ across escalation systems.17 64 65 Furthermore, the evidence base was dominated by adult inpatient studies, limiting the extent to which the methodological findings can be generalised to paediatric, obstetric and other clinical populations where EWS differ substantially in their design and intended application. ML studies were not the primary focus, although the ML-adjacent subset still provides relevant examples of time-aware feature construction.4 33 49 Publication bias and selective reporting cannot be excluded because only published English-language studies were included due to feasibility constraints. As no formal study-level risk-of-bias assessment was performed, the review cannot comment on the internal validity of individual studies. However, this reflects the methodological rather than effectiveness-focused objective of the review.

Conclusions

Taken together, the literature shows that EWS have considerable value as research outcomes, providing pragmatic, standardised summaries of routinely collected physiological data embedded within electronic health systems and aligned with escalation pathways.10 22 64 However, despite increasing availability of high-resolution, time-stamped data, these measurements are typically reduced to static summaries, with limited use of approaches that explicitly model time and change. This underuse of longitudinal and delta-based representations represents a missed opportunity to better capture patient trajectories, including both deterioration and recovery, within evaluative research.1 17 24 33 Future studies should explicitly report how EWS outcomes are operationalised, including the rationale for selected thresholds or summary measures, handling of repeated observations and missing data, consideration of calibration alongside discrimination, and whether temporal change forms part of the outcome definition. From a health informatics perspective, clearer methodological and reporting standards are needed to support consistent handling of temporality, repeated measures, and data quality, thereby improving the reproducibility, comparability, and downstream utility of EWS-derived outcomes across digital health systems.
