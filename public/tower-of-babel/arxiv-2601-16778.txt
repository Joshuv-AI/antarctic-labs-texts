Abstract
. People’s transportation choices reflect complex trade-offs shaped by personal preferences, social norms, and technology acceptance. Predicting such behavior at scale is a critical challenge with major implications for urban planning and sustainable transport. Traditional methods use handcrafted assumptions and costly data collection, making them impractical for early-stage evaluations of new technologies or policies. We introduce Generative Traffic Agents (GTA) for simulating large-scale, context-sensitive transportation choices using LLM-powered, persona-based agents. GTA generates artificial populations from census-based sociodemographic data. It simulates activity schedules and mode choices, enabling scalable, human-like simulations without handcrafted rules. We evaluate GTA in Berlin-scale experiments, comparing simulation results against empirical data. While agents replicate patterns, such as modal split by socioeconomic status, they show systematic biases in trip length and mode preference. GTA offers new opportunities for modeling how future innovations, from bike lanes to transit apps, shape mobility decisions.

1.
Introduction

Today’s society is more mobile than ever. Whether on foot, by bicycle, car, bus, or train, mobility shapes our lives. With 55% of the global population living in cities
(
62
)
, urban mobility faces critical challenges like congestion and emissions
(
67
;
42
)
. To address these, researchers and policymakers are exploring technology- and policy-driven innovations
(
7
)
.
Technological approaches include automated vehicles, traffic flow optimization, or new modes of transportation such as e-scooters or urban air mobility
(
7
)
.
Policy is another driver of more sustainable, accessible, and equitable urban mobility. Examples include fare-free public transport
(
6
)
, or serious investments in cycling infrastructure like recently decided on in Paris
(
25
)
.
A fundamental challenge lies in predicting which innovations will succeed.
This is particularly complex for individual mobility choices, where human factors are crucial
(
53
)
. Decisions are shaped by various factors, including needs and preferences, as well as sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors. This makes it challenging to predict personal mobility choices.
For example, ride-hailing was expected to reduce private car ownership and ease traffic congestion by turning idle vehicles into shared resources. Empirical studies, however, report little change in car ownership, but instead increases in congestion and delays, and significantly decreased public transportation usage
(
14
)
.

To better anticipate the consequences of new mobility innovations, decision makers often rely on empirical studies and traffic simulations before any real-world deployment. These methods can be effective, but they are typically applied only after the design space has already been narrowed to a few concrete alternatives.
Today, upfront user studies and subsequent traffic simulations remain the standard to understand personal mobility decisions and overall traffic behavior
(
3
;
33
)
.
The simulations are built on complex rule sets tailored to specific scenarios and assumptions derived from the user studies.
When carefully designed, these studies provide valuable prior knowledge of individual mobility choices and offer a solid foundation for simulation models.
However, this workflow is poorly suited for the
early stages
of the design and decision-making process, where potentially disruptive ideas need to be explored.
We use the term
early-stage
to describe a phase in which researchers aim to obtain predictions for emerging concepts as precise as possible, but generated quickly and with limited resources.
In this phase, designers and decision makers need rapid, low-cost feedback to screen ideas, identify promising directions, and refine hypotheses before committing to extensive data collection or costly full-scale modeling.
Current methods do not support this. User studies require considerable recruitment, coordination, and analysis effort, often taking months to complete
(
18
)
. Consequently, high costs leave many promising innovations untested.
Furthermore, current simulation approaches
(
57
;
8
)
impose structural constraints: they rely on predefined behavioral rules, fixed activity schedules, and probabilistic choice models that restrict how agents can act.
As a result, these models struggle to capture the flexibility, contextual reasoning, and interpretability needed to understand how and why people make mobility decisions.

LLM
(
LLM
)-based generative agents
(
45
;
46
;
38
)
have emerged as a method for simulating believable human behavior, producing plausible natural-language reasoning for their decisions.
Motivated by this work, early transport-oriented studies have begun adopting such agents to support simulation setup or route choice modeling.
However, these approaches still evaluate agents mainly on narrative plausibility; they are rarely tested on whether they can reproduce empirically validated, large-scale human behavior.
As a result, existing traffic-related systems lack sociodemographic grounding and seldom embed agents in environments where behavior must respect real-world constraints, leading to mobility patterns that are not validated against census data or large-scale mobility surveys (see
Table 1
).

To address these challenges, we take a step toward plausible
and
empirically grounded mobility simulations using
LLM
-based agents.
With
GTA
(
GTA
), we show that
LLM agents can reproduce real-world mobility decisions and traffic patterns when grounded in population data and embedded in a realistic urban environment
.
We position this as a foundational contribution for HCI: reliable, interpretable simulations of human behavior are a prerequisite for using generative agents to support early-stage design work, explore novel mobility innovations and policies, or reason about future mobility-related user experiences.

To achieve this goal and unlike traditional agent-based traffic simulations that rely on fixed data distributions, survey-derived activity patterns, and probabilistic choice models,
GTA
builds agent personas from census-aligned microdata and positions them into a simulation environment in which mobility behavior can only emerge under real-world constraints (i.e., the topology of the city or the availability of modalities and routes as defined by the traffic network and transit schedules).
Personas are sampled from
census-aligned microdata
. Integrated with the traffic simulation
SUMO
(
SUMO
),
GTA
produces realistic traffic flows with minimal manual tuning. We ground and evaluate the system with real data, aligning personas to the "Mobility in Germany 2017"
(
18
)
distributions, and benchmarking outputs against travel survey indicators and official traffic counts. This grounding moves
GTA
beyond narrative plausibility toward empirically validated behavioral realism, a prerequisite for any future use of generative agents in evaluating new mobility technologies or HCI concepts.

Our evaluation shows that agents plan believable daily activity schedules.
When integrated with the traffic simulation
SUMO
, they make realistic mode choices for their trips, and traffic simulations generated with
GTA
resemble real traffic. We further show that generative traffic agents are well-suited to capture the impact of sociodemographic factors on individual mobility behavior.

GTA
’s simulated mobility behavior aligns with patterns reported in large-scale, survey-based mobility studies
(
18
)
. Despite promising results, our evaluations also reveal systematic deviations.

2.1.
Traffic Simulations and Scenarios

Traffic simulations are commonly categorized by their resolution. Macroscopic models describe flows, densities, and speeds on network links, while microscopic simulators track individual vehicles and interactions (e.g., lane-changing
(
1
)
). Mesoscopic approaches occupy the middle ground. Agent-based models represent individual travelers with activity schedules and mode choices.
For Berlin,
69
used the mesoscopic simulator MATSim
(
27
)
to build a synthetic population and activity-travel patterns from census and survey data.
57
transferred this scenario to
SUMO
, yielding a microscopic network with 2.2 million detailed trips. Such scenarios are powerful but difficult to modify: most behavioral variation is encoded via fixed utility functions and distributions. Adding new technologies or policies often require new estimation runs, new surveys, or substantial engineering effort
(
43
)
. Interdisciplinary researchers wanting to explore “what-if” scenarios face steep learning curves and limited access to calibration workflows.
Recent work combines traffic simulation with
LLM
-enabled systems to lower these barriers. ChatSUMO integrates an
LLM
with
SUMO
to generate traffic scenarios from natural language, import road networks from OpenStreetMap, and run simulations without requiring expert knowledge of configuration files
(
34
)
. Other work enriches multi-agent traffic models with cognitive,
LLM
-driven agents that exhibit memory and adaptation under transport disruptions
(
5
)
. These approaches reduce technical friction and illustrate how generative tools can accelerate simulation setup, but still rely on conventional demand models and do not systematically validate agent behavior against city-specific mobility data.
GTA
builds directly on existing Berlin scenarios, yet replaces fixed decision rules with
LLM
personas whose planning and mode choice behavior are calibrated against real-world mobility statistics.

2.2.
LLM-Based Generative Agents

LLM
show strong performance in text generation and in-context reasoning across a wide range of tasks
(
44
;
16
;
68
;
64
)
. Early work on “generative agents” demonstrated that LLM-driven characters can form memories, pursue goals, and interact credibly in simulated social environments
(
46
;
45
)
. Surveys on
LLM
-based agents and agent-based simulation argue that LLM agents can address classic challenges of agent-based modeling, including perception, heterogeneity, and adaptive decision-making
(
19
)
. These works establish that
LLM
agents can support open-ended scenario exploration but provide limited evidence on how well they reproduce measured human behavior.

In transport research, several works examine LLMs as models of travel behavior. Liu et al. investigate whether LLMs can reproduce observed mode choice patterns and show that raw, zero-shot
LLM
responses misalign with human data but that few-shot calibration and persona prompts can improve alignment
(
36
)
. Qin et al. propose LingoTrip, a spatiotemporal prompting scheme for predicting individual next trips with an
LLM
and report advantages over state-of-the-art prediction models in terms of accuracy and data efficiency
(
49
)
. More recently, Wang et al. introduce an “agentic”
LLM
framework for day-to-day route choice modeling, where
LLM
agents iteratively adjust their choices in a dynamic network setting
(
65
)
. Together, these studies treat
LLM
as behavioral models for specific choice problems, but they do not embed agents into a full city-scale traffic simulation that is directly comparable to a baseline scenario.

HCI has also developed
LLM
-based synthetic participants and personas. Hämäläinen et al. show that
LLM
can generate synthetic HCI study data that resemble human responses, but warn that such data are better suited for early-stage ideation than for replacing participants
(
23
)
. Prpa et al. discuss opportunities and risks of
LLM
-based synthetic personae and data in HCI, including issues of bias, validity, and over-reliance on synthetic samples
(
48
)
. Hu and Collier analyze the “persona effect” and quantify how persona prompts change
LLM
outputs, but also demonstrate that these shifts only partially explain human variation
(
28
)
. Across this literature, synthetic agents are framed as complements to, not substitutes for, human data. Unlike prior generative-agent transport systems such as GATSim
(
35
)
, which focus on cognitively rich agents in stylized networks, GTA targets city-scale scenarios and explicitly calibrates LLM-driven personas against census and survey data in Berlin.

GTA
connects these strands. Conceptually, it follows generative-agent work by using
LLM
-driven personas with goals, memories, and constraints. Methodologically, it is closer to transport studies that calibrate
LLM
behavior against measured mode choice and trip patterns. Unlike prior HCI work on synthetic participants,
GTA
agents are not only plausible but also explicitly anchored in census statistics and a Berlin mobility survey; their daily schedules and mode choices are validated against empirical distributions. Unlike prior
LLM
–traffic integrations, our agents operate inside a large-scale
SUMO
scenario and can be evaluated at both the individual (persona) and aggregate (traffic) levels. This dual focus enables us to examine when persona-based generative agents are useful for mobility research and where they deviate in systematic, interpretable ways.

3.
Introducing GTA: Generative Traffic Agents

Figure 2
.
GTA’s modularized architecture.
A three-part diagram showing the modular structure of generative agents, divided into Profile, Planning, and Action components.

The design of
GTA
is guided by three goals that enable rapid, early-stage, yet empirically grounded mobility simulation.
First,
population-grounded diversity
: agents should reflect sociodemographic variation without relying on handcrafted rules derived from costly user studies. We therefore combine census-aligned microdata with persona-driven
LLM
to generate heterogeneous preferences and day plans.
Second,
contextual decision-making under real-world constraints
: agents must reason about
actual distances, travel times, and available modes. Integrating with
SUMO
(and
OTP
(
OTP
)) enables
mode choice and routing based on concrete, network-informed options rather than abstract prompts.
Third,
interoperability
: to support HCI and mobility
researchers,
GTA
is designed as a modular pipeline that operates on
OSM
(
OSM
)/
GTFS
(
GTFS
) data and
produces outputs comparable to standard validation datasets (e.g., modal split, trip lengths,
traffic counts). We decided to use
SUMO
as it is frequently used in HCI
(
10
;
9
)
and allows GTA to be extended easily.

These goals motivate
GTA
’s three core components (see
Figure 2
). The
profile module
samples
socio-demographic attributes from census-calibrated microdata and generates matching textual
persona using an
LLM
. The
planning module
produces full-day activity schedules,
assigns concrete destinations based on
OSM
categories, and selects transport modes using
persona information and network-informed route options. The
action module
instantiates
these plans by computing multimodal routes, refining them through the dynamic user equilibrium in

SUMO
, and executing the final traffic simulation on
OSM
/
GTFS
-derived networks.
Figure 3
shows how the model components are connected.

3.1.
Profile Module: Population Sampling and Agent Generation

Each agent receives a profile consisting of socio-demographic attributes and a natural-language persona description. Attributes are first sampled from population statistics, after which an
LLM
generates a matching persona description.

Population Sampling

The synthetic population is based on anonymized nationwide census data
(
18
)
. Each survey record includes a calibrated weight reflecting its representation in the population, adjusted through design weights, non-response correction, and post-stratification to known margins
(
30
;
31
)
.

To generate a synthetic population of size
N
, we scale the weights
w_{i}
such that
\sum_{i}w_{i}^{*}=N
, and apply
TRS
(
TRS
)
(
40
)
to convert the fractional weights into integer replication counts. TRS handles the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost.
While we focus on the "Mobility in Germany 2017" dataset due to its regional relevance and detail, our approach generalizes to any census data. If only marginal distributions are available, joint distributions can be inferred using statistical techniques like
IPF
(
IPF
)
(
13
)
.

Description Generation

Each synthetic agent’s attributes (e.g., household income) are provided in the
LLM
prompt generating the persona description (see Listing
1
). These personas enrich the simulation with personality cues while preserving demographic realism.

3.2.
Planning Module: Activity Scheduling and Trip Planning

To generate realistic travel demand, agents construct individual full-day activity plans prior to traffic simulation. This includes a sequence of activities with assigned times and locations, plus transport modality for each trip. These activities are generated based on the persona description and sociodemographic attributes. Pre-planning reduces runtime and
LLM
queries, and enables diverse, yet tractable, behavioral modeling.
While trip execution is handled by SUMO, activity planning depends on mobility constraints such as location availability and travel durations. Consequently, the boundary between planning and action is not as strict as
Figure 2
suggests.

Activity Scheduling

Each agent’s schedule specifies the activity type, timing, and associated location. To ensure spatial plausibility, location candidates are extracted from
OSM
, using attributes such as
building
,
amenity
, and
shop
. These are grouped into predefined location categories appropriate for different activity types. Activities are only linked to locations within these categories, selected according to the sampling strategy described in
subsection 3.3
.

Temporal and contextual variation is captured by embedding structured prompts with factors like weekday/weekend distinctions
(
50
;
15
;
55
)
, policy interventions
(
6
)
, or major events
(
20
)
. Based on the agent’s persona, date, and valid
OSM
location categories, the
LLM
returns a structured activity schedule. Agents are instructed to avoid mentioning transportation or commuting behaviors in the plan to maintain a clean separation between abstract planning and concrete mobility execution.

Trip Planning

After generating daily schedules, we resolve activities requiring travel by mapping their types to predefined building categories and sampling concrete locations (see
subsection 3.3
).

SUMO
estimates travel times and distances for all modes of transportation to compute feasible travel options. We provide the
LLM
with the agent’s persona, identified trip segments, and candidate routes. Using few-shot
CoT
(
CoT
), the model is given domain knowledge and example reasoning steps, enabling it to evaluate the available route options and select the mode and route that best match the agent’s persona and the temporal and spatial characteristics of each candidate. See the prompt in
4
in
Appendix A
.

3.3.
Action Module: Traffic Simulation and Dynamic Routing

The action module supports planning by (1) defining building categories for each activity type, (2) selecting specific destination buildings based on agent context, and (3) computing multi-modal routes between them. It also executes the simulation and performs dynamic user equilibrium routing
(
39
)
, ensuring agents adapt their routes to congestion rather than following static shortest paths.

Location Choice

Activity destinations (e.g., for work or shopping) are selected using a proximity-based rule: agents choose the nearest building of a suitable type. While simplistic, this approach best matched empirical trip length distributions in early experiments. More complex methods (e.g., distance-decay weighting) shifted the distribution toward longer trips, worsening alignment with real-world data. Although trips
<
1\text{\,}\mathrm{km}
are still underrepresented, the nearest-only rule offers a good trade-off between computational efficiency and realistic destination choice.
Relevant building types are defined via
OSM
tags—including
building
,
amenity
,
office
,
shop
, and
craft
—and mapped to categories like workplaces or leisure venues (see
3
). During trip plan generation, the
LLM
selects activity types based on these categories.
Home locations are sampled using an area-weighted heuristic: each residential building receives a probability
p_{i}
proportional to its floor area, assuming larger buildings house more people:
p_{i}=\frac{\text{area}_{i}}{\sum_{j}\text{area}_{j}}
.

Initial Routing

To inform mode choice, initial route options are computed per trip and modality. For car, bike, and walk trips,
SUMO
estimates shortest paths. For public transport,
OTP
computes multimodal, time-dependent transit routes from
OSM
and
GTFS
data. Both methods ignore traffic congestion and serve as approximations; final routing is refined later through dynamic equilibrium.

Scaling

Full-scale simulation of Berlin’s population (
\approx 3.9
million inhabitants) is computationally very expensive. With 8 CPUs and 8 Nvidia A30 GPUs, simulating 10% of the population takes
43\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
, while equilibrium computation can take weeks
(
57
)
. We thus simulate 10% of the population and scale agents by weight, resampling locations within a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{km}
radius to preserve spatial diversity.

Dynamic User Equilibrium and Final Simulation

Final route assignments are computed by iteratively adjusting paths to approximate dynamic user equilibrium
(
39
)
to capture network effects and congestion, yielding more realistic travel patterns in the final simulation.

3.4.
Simulation Setup

To enable agents to reason about the traffic environment, such as the durations and lengths of potential routes, and to simulate the resulting traffic flow,
GTA
integrates external traffic simulators and trip planners.

SUMO

(
39
)
supports various modes of transportation, including walking, cycling, driving, and public transit, and offers high-resolution, vehicle-level simulation. This granularity is particularly valuable for analyzing the impact of disruptions, policy interventions, and mobility innovations, as it captures detailed effects such as speed adjustments, rerouting, and congestion dynamics.
GTA’s modular architecture also allows alternative services for route planning (e.g.,
OTP

(
12
)
).

3.5.
Urban Mobility Behavior - Case Study Berlin

To evaluate the impact of mobility innovations, simulations must capture how people move within cities, which transport systems they use, and how sociodemographic factors shape their choices. We use Berlin as case study because it shares key challenges with other global cities, such as socioeconomic pressures and inner-city gentrification
(
26
;
29
)
, while offering rich census and mobility data for validation
(
18
)
. Like Shanghai
(
41
)
or London
(
51
)
, Berlin faces increasing demand for sustainable and equitable mobility solutions.

86% of Berlin’s 3.9 million residents are mobile daily, making an average of 3.1 trips that cover 39 kilometers and take 97 minutes
(
18
)
. The modal split reflects a diverse mobility landscape: 34% car, 27% walking, 25% public transport, and 15% cycling. These patterns are strongly shaped by socioeconomic status. Higher-income groups rely more on cars and less on public transport; students (40%) and unemployed individuals (37%) are the heaviest public transport users. Older adults cycle less and shift away from transit. These trends highlight the heterogeneity of mobility choices, underscoring the need for simulations that account for demographic diversity.

4.
Evaluating GTA

We evaluate
GTA
by comparing simulation outputs against traffic counts and survey data
(
18
)
.

4.1.
Experimental Setup

To evaluate
GTA
, we ran experiments that vary in scope, model components, and simulation configurations. We compare the results against empirical data to assess plausibility.

Simulation Scope and Population Sampling

For efficiency, we simulate a 1% sample of Berlin’s population (35,769 agents) without scaling. While we generate personas, activity schedules, trip plans, and simulate mode choices for this sample, we do not run the final traffic simulation for Berlin due to the substantial manual corrections required to prepare the
SUMO
network
(
57
)
.
Instead, we run the full simulation in the Wedding district using a 10% sample (8,680 agents), where the smaller network allowed the required manual corrections. For realistic traffic counts, we scale agents to represent the full 2024 population of Wedding (86,796 residents). We evaluate simulation realism by comparing modeled traffic volumes with official traffic counts, sampling at the same monitoring locations.

Model Variants and Ablation Studies

By default, agents use the Llama 3.1 8B language model
(
21
)

4.2.
Evaluation Methodology

To assess the quality of our simulation outputs, we compare key indicators produced by
GTA
against real-world network-level counts and individual travel behavior patterns.
For the network-level evaluation, we use traffic counts available in the Berlin Open Data Portal
(
2
)
. To analyze travel behavior, we compare the following key indicators from the "Mobility in Germany 2017" survey
(
18
)
with our results:
modal split, modal split by economic status, trip length by mode, trip duration by mode.

We compute
RMSE
(
RMSE
) and an aggregate average
RMSE
over modal split, trip length, and trip duration.

Interregional Comparison

Lacking a direct baseline, we benchmark
GTA
against mobility data from other German states. If
GTA
’s error is smaller than natural interregional differences, it suggests the model captures city-specific mobility with realistic fidelity.

Traffic Counts

We analyze traffic flow plausibility via hourly vehicle counts at four representative traffic count stations (see
Figure 8
) in Wedding. We normalize traffic counts to compare the shape of the daily profiles.

6.
Discussion

This short paper introduces
population-grounded generative agents integrated with traffic
simulations
as a methodological bridge between survey-based modeling and early-stage innovation evaluation in mobility research.
By validating emergent behaviors against empirical data, we propose a scalable framework for designing realistic mobility behaviors. Unlike many recent agent-based approaches
(
45
;
46
)
that focus on generating plausible individual behavior,
GTA
goes further by producing behavior that is not only plausible at the individual level but also consistent with population-scale mobility patterns observed in empirical data. We demonstrate that
GTA
captures the overall distribution of mode choices and models real-world trends in how economic status and occupation influence mobility choices.

6.1.
GTA as a Method to Simulate Situated Mobility at Scale

GTA
bridges traditional agent-based models and persona-driven generative agents by combining census-aligned agent generation with
LLM
-based contextual reasoning. Rather than relying on fixed utility functions, agents generate interpretable rationales for their mobility choices while remaining constrained by real-world networks.
A key contribution of
GTA
is that this generative reasoning yields behavior consistent with empirical mobility patterns and observed traffic volumes. By evaluating GTA against mobility studies and traffic counts,
GTA
moves beyond narrative plausibility toward behavioral realism.
Demonstrating this alignment is essential because it validates natural-language reasoning as a reliable foundation for HCI simulations.
We consider this a necessary foundation before generative agents can reliably support HCI applications.
For
HCI
,
GTA
provides a fast, low-cost method for prototyping mobility innovations.
GTA
captures the situated interplay between socio-demographics, temporal context, and network structure while retaining natural-language interpretability, making it a practical basis for exploring mobility-related user experiences long before costly field studies are feasible.

6.2.
Behavioral Biases Toward “Role Model Behavior”

Our agents, regardless of socioeconomic profile, tend to exhibit behavior aligned with urban ideals: frequent biking, healthy life styles, and low nighttime activity. This homogeneity likely reflects the biases of both training data and model objectives, which prioritize plausibility over variability. LLMs often overrepresent socially desirable behaviors
(
52
)
, which might be due to their reliance on text
58
. This “role model paradox” limits the diversity of agent behaviors.
A key challenge is introducing heterogeneity. This might not be achievable by varying personas alone, but by explicitly modeling behavioral priors, uncertainty, and human constraints in decision-making. Doing so could allow
HCI
application to simulate diverse populations with greater fidelity.

6.3.
Trade-offs Between Rapid Prototyping and Empirical Validity

Using
LLM
-based agents as scalable proxies for human decision-making and cognition
(
4
)
has been criticized for introducing biases from textual training data, underrepresenting the variability of human behavior, and remaining fundamentally distinct from human minds
(
58
)
. Our ablation experiments also reveal that the empirical outcome validity
GTA
is highly sensitive to both prompting strategies and model choice (see
Table 3
). Small changes, such as omitting CoT scaffolding or switching from Llama 3.1 to Qwen2.5, can significantly alter trip characteristics and distort mode choice distributions, even under identical conditions. A further challenge is ensuring behavioral consistency (e.g., an agent leaving work by car only if it arrived by car).

Nonetheless,
GTA
is a valid method for rapid prototyping of mobility innovations.
We view
LLM
-based simulations as tools for ideation, not replacements for empirical studies
(
24
)
. While validated here for Berlin, re-evaluation in other regions is necessary. Given its low cost compared to surveys,
GTA
offers a practical means to augment current
HCI
mobility methods
(
56
)
.

6.4.
Future Work on GTA

Several extensions can increase
GTA
utility.
First, traffic simulation remains a bottleneck:
SUMO
’s equilibrium calculations are slow, and the traffic network requires manual improvements. Integrating the
LLM
-driven action module with simulators like MATSim
(
27
)
could reduce manual preprocessing and support multi-day scenarios.
Second, individual mobility behavior is partially influenced by
others
(
32
;
47
)
.
GTA
could model simple social networks
(
45
;
46
)
, allowing agents to
adapt activities or mode choices based on routines or invitations of connected agents.
While outside the scope of this short paper, a dedicated interactive front-end would make
GTA
more accessible.
Finally, to improve simulation accuracy, agents could incorporate multi-day memory and adaptation.
Persistent memory would allow capturing long-term behavioral shifts and policy impacts
(
37
)
and could increase travel plan consistency.
