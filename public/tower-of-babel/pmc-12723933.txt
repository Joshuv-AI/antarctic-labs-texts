Silver nanoparticles fabricated using phycocyanin from Arthrospira platensis: green synthesis, physicochemical characterization, and biological activities
Authors: Ani Harutyunyan, Astghik Hambardzumyan, Liana Gabrielyan, Anush Aghajanyan, Meri Kocharyan, Nikolay Avtandilyan, Alina Zurnachyan, Inga Bazukyan, Wojciech Kujawski, Lilit Gabrielyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCyanobacteria and their bioactive components represent a promising potential for the green synthesis of nanoparticles. This work investigates the physicochemical characteristics and biological activities of silver nanoparticles (PC-AgNPs) synthesized using phycocyanin, a photosynthetic pigment extracted from the cyanobacterium Arthrospira platensis Pc-005.ResultsThe phycocyanin-derived AgNPs were characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDX), and Dynamic light scattering (DLS). Physicochemical analysis confirmed the formation of PC-AgNPs, and revealed their nanoscale size, crystalline nature and purity. PC-AgNPs demonstrated a UV-Vis absorption peak at 410 nm. TEM analysis revealed that phycocyanin-derived AgNPs were spherical in shape with an average diameter of 20.0 ± 4.2 nm. XRD and EDX analyses confirmed the crystallinity and purity of PC-AgNPs. FTIR analysis revealed the involvement of phycocyanin functional groups in AgNPs formation. The results demonstrated that PC-AgNPs exhibited strong antimicrobial activity against opportunistic pathogenic bacteria Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhimurium, Staphylococcus aureus, as well as fungi Aspergillus flavus, Penicillium aurantioviolaceum, and Trichoderma viride. The findings indicated that significantly higher concentration of PC-AgNPs was needed to achieve antifungal activity compared to antibacterial activity. The PC-AgNPs also affected the activity of crucial antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase and catalase, which play key role in the bacterial defense against oxidative stress induced by AgNPs. Moreover, PC-AgNPs demonstrated significant concentration-dependent anticancer activity against A549 adenocarcinoma cells. They also inhibited key angiogenic factors, including VEGF-A and MMP-2, as well as inflammation-related markers such as TNF-α and COX-2.ConclusionThus, this study provides a comprehensive physicochemical and biological characterization of silver nanoparticles derived from the phycocyanin of A. platensis. The PC-AgNPs exhibit a broad spectrum of biological activities, underscoring their promising potential for innovative applications in biomedical and biotechnological fields.Graphical Abstract

Background

The escalating incidence of bacterial infections, coupled with the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance, represents one of the most critical challenges in modern biomedicine. Antimicrobial resistance plays a role in about 5 million of the nearly 8 million global deaths caused by bacterial infections each year, according to the World Health Organization [1]. Nanoparticles (NPs) have emerged as a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics, demonstrating significant potential in addressing antibiotic resistance and specifically targeting drug-resistant bacterial strains. The development of NPs with antimicrobial properties has become a key focus of modern nanotechnology for the creation of novel medical agents. Nanoparticles, due to their unique physicochemical properties, have widespread applications in biomedicine, pharmacy, biotechnology, and agriculture [2–5]. From this perspective, silver NPs (AgNPs) have been extensively investigated for their antibacterial and therapeutic potential [3–6]. It has been reported that AgNPs effectively suppressed the growth of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [6–9]. Their significant antimicrobial properties have been widely studied and offer promising results as alternative antibacterial remedies [6–9]. Furthermore, NPs have exhibited significant anticancer and antioxidant potential [8–13].

Currently, the synthesis of various NPs is accomplished using a range of physical and chemical methods. However, these techniques are often linked to high production costs and potential environmental risks. The biological or green synthesis of NPs presents a promising alternative by using natural materials, thereby potentially lowering the cost and environmental impact of the technology [14–16]. Biological synthesis of NPs offers distinct advantages over conventional physicochemical methods, including cost-effectiveness, environmental sustainability, ease of production, and non-toxicity. This approach uses natural resources (microalgae, plants, and bacteria) as reducing and stabilizing agents [6–9, 13, 17, 18]. Moreover, the use of “green” materials simplifies the technology, making it more suitable for large-scale production [14–16].

For an extensive period, cyanobacteria have served as significant contributors in biotechnology and biomedicine, acting as producers of a diverse array of valuable substances [19–22]. Cyanobacteria are also promising candidates for the green synthesis of NPs [17, 23–27]. AgNPs generated from the cyanobacteria Oscillatoria sp. extract have demonstrated significant bactericidal and antibiofilm properties, along with cytotoxic effect against certain human cancer cell lines [24–26]. In our previous research, we showed that AgNPs derived from Spirulina platensis demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against conditionally pathogenic Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhimurium, Enterococcus hirae and Staphylococcus aureus [7]. Ahmed and Othman [27] reported that zinc oxide NPs synthesized using the extract of S. platensis inhibited biofilm formation by S. aureus and Klebsiella pneumonia.

The cyanobacterial metabolites are natural alternatives to antibiotics and other medications to treat various diseases, and have great potential for biomedical applications [19, 22, 28, 29]. The biological activity of cyanobacteria is due to the high content of proteinaceous pigment, phycocyanin (phycobiliprotein family’s light-harvesting pigment-protein complex). Phycocyanin exhibits antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective potential, and is widely used in biotechnology, biomedicine, pharmacy, and food processing [29–32]. Additionally, phycocyanin can serve as a potential platform for NPs synthesis [33–36]. El-Naggar with co-workers [33] reported that AgNPs synthesized using phycocyanin from the cyanobacterium Nostoc linckia demonstrated anticancer activity. Ramasamy and Nooruddin [34] reported that AgNPs derived from phycocyanin of Oscillatoria pseudogeminata showed antibacterial activity against pathogens S. aureus and P. aeruginosa. Saad with co-workers [35] demonstrated that selenium NPs synthesized from phycocyanin of cyanobacterium Leptolyngbya sp. SSI24 exhibited strong antibacterial and anticancer activities.

This study presents, for the first time, the biosynthesis of AgNPs using phycocyanin extracted from the cyanobacterium Arthrospira platensis Pc-005. The physicochemical properties of the synthesized PC-AgNPs were characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDX), and Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). In addition, the antimicrobial activity of PC-AgNPs against various conditionally pathogenic bacteria and fungi was evaluated, along with their hemolytic and anticancer potential. The effect of PC-AgNPs on the activity of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase and catalase) was also investigated.

Materials and methods

Arthrospira platensis cultivation and growth properties

A. platensis Pc-005 (Microbial Depository Center, NAS, Yerevan, Armenia, WDCM803) was cultivated in 1000 mL Erlenmeyer flasks with 500 mL of standard Zarrouk medium under aerobic conditions at 27 ± 2 °C and pH 9.0 ± 0.02 and continuous illumination in a Climate chamber (ICH110L, Memmert, USA), as described [7]. The growth of A. platensis was monitored by measuring the optical density at 680 nm (OD680). The absorption spectrum of A. platensis was measured in the wavelength range of 400–750 nm using a UV-Vis Auto spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 S UV–VIS-Thermo Fisher Scientific and UV 2700, Shimadzu) [7].

Phycocyanin extraction

Biomass of A. platensis (OD680 ~ 2.0) was collected during the stationary growth phase via centrifugation (ROTINA 420 R, Hettlich Zentrifugen) as described [7]. Then, the biomass was used for the extraction of phycocyanin (PC).

Phycocyanin was extracted from A. platensis biomass according to Boussiba and Richmond [37] using 0.1 M Na-phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) followed by freeze-thaw cycles [38]. The suspension was subjected to freezing at − 18 °C, after which it was thawed at room temperature (25 °C). This cycle was repeated three times. Then, phycocyanin was extracted by centrifugation at 17,000×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for quantitative determination.

The absorption spectrum of extracted phycocyanin was recorded over the wavelength range of 500 and 700 nm using a UV-Vis Auto spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 S UV–VIS-Thermo Fisher Scientific and UV 2700, Shimadzu). Absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 620 and 652 nm [35, 39]. Phycocyanin purity was calculated using the equation: PCpurity = OD620/OD280, where the absorbance at 620 nm corresponds to phycocyanin and the absorbance at 280 nm represents total protein [32, 35].

Phycocyanin-mediated synthesis of AgNPs

For the synthesis of PC-AgNPs, 5 mL of phycocyanin extract was mixed with 45 mL of 1 mM silver nitrate (AgNO3) aqueous solution in a 1:9 volume ratio. The reaction mixture, maintained at pH 9.0, was incubated at 30 °C for 2–3 h under continuous illumination in a Climate chamber with light (ICH110L, Memmert, USA). The biosynthesized PC-AgNPs were purified by double filtration using sterile Rotilabo-syringe filters (PVDF, Carl Roth GmbH). The filtrate containing the PC-AgNPs was then collected for further analysis. Phycocyanin extract without AgNO3 solution and AgNO3 solution alone served as the positive and negative controls, respectively.

Physicochemical characterization of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs

UV-Vis spectroscopy

The absorption spectrum of PC-AgNPs was measured in the wavelength range of 300–700 nm using a UV-Vis Auto spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 S UV–VIS-Thermo Fisher Scientific and UV 2700, Shimadzu) as described [7]. The optical absorption characteristics, including the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak of PC-AgNPs, were measured with one nm spectral resolution.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-Ray spectroscopy (EDX)

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) was used to analyze the morphology of the PC-AgNPs samples in detail. Sample preparation and data acquisition were carried out using a Tecnai G2 F20 X-Twin apparatus (FEI Europe, B.V., Eindhoven, The Netherlands).

The elemental composition and purity of the PC-AgNPs were analyzed using the SEM with Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX) Spectroscopy module—Phenom, Generation 5 (Phenom-Word B.V., Eindhoven, The Netherlands).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was applied to reveal the contribution of functional groups of phycocyanin in the interaction with Ag+ [7, 8]. The FTIR spectra of PC-AgNPs were measured in transmittance mode over the range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 parallel scans, using a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer. Dried samples of phycocyanin and PC-AgNPs were separately mixed with KBr powder in an agate mortar, and the resulting mixtures were pressed into transparent KBr pellets for analysis.

X-Ray diffraction (XRD)

The phase composition of the PC-AgNPs was determined by X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis by MiniFlex 600 Rigaku Smart Lab SE diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan, D/teX Ultra 250 1D detector, CuKα radiation, λ = 0.1542 nm, step size of 0.01o, 2θ = 10–90o, 40 kV, 15 mA) and a PDF-2 database, as previously described [8]. The XRD pattern demonstrated characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to face-centered cubic (FCC) Ag (PDF#65-2871) and AgCl (PDF#31-1238). The relative proportions of the phases present, specifically silver and silver chloride, were quantified using the Rietveld refinement method (S = 2.87, χ2 = 8.25).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was used to analyze the hydrodynamic size of PC-AgNPs, following the previously reported method [7]. Measurements were collected by applying a 20-fold diluted PC-AgNP suspension (Spectrosize 300, XtalConcepts GmbH, Germany). Furthermore, the obtained autocorrelation functions’ analysis was performed by using the CONTIN algorithm [40].

Biological activities of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs

Antibacterial activity of PC-AgNPs

The antibacterial properties of PC-AgNPs were assessed against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa Gar3, Salmonella typhimurium MDC1759, and Staphylococcus aureus MDC5233 (Microbial Depository Center, NAS, Yerevan, Armenia; WDCM803). Bacterial cultures were grown in Nutrient Broth (NB) medium at pH 7.5 and 37 °C, except for P. aeruginosa, which was incubated at 32 °C, as previously described [7]. Cultures grown without PC-AgNPs served as negative controls, while the phycocyanin extract was used as a positive control. The growth of bacteria was measured using a densitometer (DEN-1B McFarland, Biosan, Latvia) following the addition of PC-AgNPs to the culture media at concentrations of 5, 10, and 20 µg mL−1 [7]. where µ represents the specific growth rate, ODt and OD0 represent the final and initial optical densities of the cells, respectively; ∆t corresponds to the duration of the exponential growth phase [8].

Bacterial susceptibility to PC-AgNPs

Bacterial susceptibility to PC-AgNPs was evaluated using the spread plate method. Sterile Petri dishes were prepared with Nutrient Agar (NA) medium (30 mL). A bacterial suspension containing 106 colony-forming units (CFU) per mL was treated with PC-AgNPs at concentrations of 5 and 10 µg mL−1. After treatment, 100 µL of each sample was plated onto the agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Viable colonies were counted to determine CFU, following the procedure described previously [7]. The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was determined as the lowest concentration of PC-AgNPs that resulted in complete inhibition of visible bacterial growth on agar plates.

Bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics was evaluated using the disk-diffusion method. Commercial antibiotic-impregnated paper discs (Research Center for Pharmacotherapy, St. Petersburg, Russia) were applied. These discs contain penicillin, ampicillin and streptomycin at 10 µg per disc; amoxicillin at 20 µg per disc; and kanamycin, tetracycline, cefepime and cefotaxime at 30 µg per disc. The discs were placed on the surface of agar plates (Petri dishes), inoculated with the bacterial strains, and the plates incubate were overnight. The bacterial susceptibility was determined by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zone around each disc.

Antifungal activity of PC-AgNPs

Fungi Aspergillus flavus, Penicillium aurantioviolaceum, and Trichoderma viride (isolated from spoiled food, Collection of fungi, Yerevan State University) were used to reveal the antifungal properties of PC-AgNPs. The antifungal activity of PC-AgNPs was determined by slightly modifying the full-diffusion assay, carried out using Beer Wort as the cultivation medium with 0.9% w v−1 of agar according to Matevosyan and co-workers [41]. This medium was chosen because it is widely used and suitable for the growth of various fungi. PC-AgNPs at concentrations from 100 to 300 µg mL−1 were added to small (3 mL) Petri dishes. A drop of fungal spore suspension was added to the surface of the media containing PC-AgNPs. The final spore concentration per Petri dish was 104 spores mL−1. Antifungal activity was assessed qualitatively by the absence of fungal growth on the surface of the medium after 10 days. The spore suspension was prepared by growing the fungi on the surface of the same medium until complete sporulation, after which only spores were collected in a 0.9% NaCl solution. The number of spores in 1 mL of suspension was determined using the Fuchs-Rosenthal counting chamber.

Antioxidant activity of PC-AgNPs

The antioxidant activity of PC-AgNPs was evaluated using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay. DPPH free radical scavenging activity was measured following the method of Liu and co-workers [42] with slight modifications. The percentage of inhibition was calculated relative to the blank control. Catechin was used as a positive control, and the results were presented as IC50 values (µg mL−1), indicating the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the DPPH free radicals. The activities of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), were measured in the presence of PC-AgNPs. SOD activity was measured by its capacity to inhibit the auto-oxidation of adrenaline to adrenochrome, following the method described by [43]. SOD activity was calculated from the change in absorbance over time, accounting for protein content, and expressed as U mg protein−1. One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that decomposes 1 µmol of H2O2 (min×mg protein)−1 [44].

Triton X-100 (0.1%) and Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) served as the positive and negative controls, respectively. Anticancer activity of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs

Cell culture

The cytotoxicity properties of the PC-AgNPs were tested on lung adenocarcinoma (A549, CCL-185) cells. A549 cells were obtained from the Orbeli Institute of Physiology (NAS, Yerevan, Armenia), which had originally purchased them from the ATCC cell repository (CCL-185™). Passages 10 to 20 were used for all experiments. Dulbecco’s Modified Essential Medium (DMEM) containing 10% bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin was used for cell culture. Tissue culture multiwell plates were used for the cell cultivation, which were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 [46].

Cytotoxicity assessment using the MTT assay

The cytotoxicity of the tested substances was evaluated using the MTT (3[(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) colorimetric method [47]. This assay relies on mitochondrial dehydrogenases, which transform MTT into insoluble formazan crystals in viable cells. PC-AgNPs at concentrations up to 10 µg mL−1 were applied to cultured cells for 4, 24, and 72 h, while sterile distilled water, used as the NPs solvent, served as a negative control. The cells were incubated in fresh medium containing 10% MTT at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, the medium was discarded, and formazan crystals were dissolved in pure dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Optical density was determined at 570 nm using a SPECTROstar® Nano [8, 48].

ELISA of TNF-α, VEGF-A, COX-2, MMP-2, and Akt

A549 cells (2 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Afterward, 630 µL of medium was replaced, and cells were exposed to PBS with 1% ethanol (control, A549) or 2.5 µg mL−1 PC-AgNPs for 24 h. After treatment, the culture supernatant was collected for analysis [49]. Levels of Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor-A (VEGF-A), and Matrix Metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) in the supernatant were quantified according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells from each group were collected (trypsinized, neutralized, centrifuged), lysed on ice using Ripa lysis buffer. The lysates were collected into centrifuge tubes, and further lysed for 10 min. Following centrifugation at 13,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. Changes in Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and Akt levels were measured using ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentration in both the cell culture medium and lysates was determined using the Bradford method [49]. Each test sample (70 µL) was analyzed in triplicate across three different passages.

Phospho-PI 3 kinase p85 + Total In-Cell ELISA assay

A549 cells (1.5 × 104 per well) were seeded in 96-well tissue culture-treated plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 180 µL of fresh medium was added, and the cells were treated with 20 µL of either the control (distilled water) or PC-AgNPs at a concentration of 2.5 µg mL-1. Calculations were made during the cells’ seeding to achieve approximately 80% confluence at the time of fixation. The culture medium was removed after 24 h of exposure, and the cells were fixed with 100 µL of 4% formaldehyde in PBS. Crystal Violet staining was performed to stain the cells, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured to quantify both total and phospho-PI 3 kinase p85 levels. The OD450 measurement for each well was corrected for cell number by dividing each OD450 reading by the corresponding OD595 value from the same well. This relative cell number was subsequently used to normalize the readings. Both total and phospho-PI 3 kinase p85 were quantified in triplicate using the specific antibodies included in the PI 3 Kinase Kit. Measurements were conducted using an In-Cell ELISA kit (ab207484), following the manufacturer’s instructions [49].

Chemicals and reagents

All reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), Condalab (Spain), Carl Roth GmbH (Germany), and Abcam (UK). d-glucose (≥ 98%, anhydrous), DMSO (≥ 99.9%) and AgNO3 (≥ 99.9%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA); components of Zarrouk medium were supplied from Carl Roth GmbH (Germany); and NB media were purchased from Condalab (Spain). Antibodies against TNF-α (ab46087), VEGF-A (ab193555), MMP-2 (ab92536), COX-2 (ab38898), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and phosphorylated (p)-PI3K 154 (ab191606), as well as ELISA kits for Akt and p-Akt (ab179463) were purchased from Abcam.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA), with a significance level of p < 0.05. Data were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test.

Results and discussion

Biosynthesis of AgNPs using phycocyanin extracted from arthrospira platensis

In this study, phycocyanin extracted from cyanobacterium A. platensis Pc-005 was used for the biosynthesis of AgNPs (Fig. 1). The absorption spectra of both bulk and extracted phycocyanin from A. platensis were recorded, revealing absorption peaks characteristic of cyanobacteria, which indicate the presence of chlorophyll a, carotenoids, and phycocyanin (Fig. 1A, B). Phycocyanin extracted from A. platensis demonstrated absorbance (one prominent peak) at 620 nm, as shown in Fig. 1B. The phycocyanin concentration in the sample was 0.25 ± 0.01 mg mL−1. The phycocyanin purity index based on the ratio OD620 (absorbance of phycocyanin)/OD280 (absorbance of total proteins) was 1.26. If 0.7 ≤ OD620/OD280 ≤ 3.9, phycocyanin is classified as reagent grade [31].

Fig. 1Culture and absorption spectrum of A. platensis Pc-005 (A); extract of phycocyanin (PC) of A. platensis and its absorption spectrum (B); PC-AgNPs obtained from PC and absorption spectrum of biosynthesized PC-AgNPs (C)

Culture and absorption spectrum of A. platensis Pc-005 (A); extract of phycocyanin (PC) of A. platensis and its absorption spectrum (B); PC-AgNPs obtained from PC and absorption spectrum of biosynthesized PC-AgNPs (C)

The green synthesis of PC-AgNPs was conducted at 30 °C under continuous illumination. Over time, the conversion of blue-green colored reaction mixture to dark brown color was observed indicating the bio-reduction of Ag+ to Ag0 and synthesis of PC-AgNPs (Fig. 1C). In contrast, aqueous AgNO3 solutions incubated without phycocyanin showed no color change. The color change is associated with the excitation of surface plasmon vibrations in AgNPs. Light-dependent synthesis of PC-AgNPs involves the reduction of Ag+ to Ag0 through direct or photosensitized photolysis. Upon illumination, the chromophores in phycocyanin absorb light energy, causing electrons to transition from the ground state to a higher energy level. The excited electrons then reduce AgNO3, leading to the formation of AgNPs [7, 33].

The physicochemical characterization of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs

The color changes of the reaction mixture, along with the absorption spectrum with a characteristic peak at 410 nm, confirm the biosynthesis of PC-AgNPs (Fig. 1C). Numerous studies have reported that absorbance within the 400–500 nm range indicates the silver nanoparticles formation [7, 33, 34].

TEM was applied to provide detailed information about the size and morphology of the PC-AgNPs [7, 8]. TEM analysis revealed that the PC-AgNPs were spherical in shape, with particle sizes ranging from 10 to 60 nm and an average diameter of 20.0 ± 4.2 nm (Fig. 2A). TEM images demonstrated that the PC-AgNPs possessed smooth and uniform surfaces, free from visible irregularities, which suggests a well-defined and consistent structure (Fig. 2A).

Fig. 2TEM image of PC-AgNPs (A); EDX spectrum and elemental profile of PC-AgNPs (B)

TEM image of PC-AgNPs (A); EDX spectrum and elemental profile of PC-AgNPs (B)

EDX analysis was performed to verify the purity and elemental composition of the phycocyanin-derived AgNPs. EDX spectrum of the PC-AgNPs exhibited a prominent peak at 3 keV, confirming that silver predominates in the sample (65.95%) and indicating the high purity of the PC-AgNPs (Fig. 2B).

DLS was applied for the determination of the hydrodynamic radius of green-synthesized AgNPs [7]. Measurements were collected by applying a 20-fold diluted PC-AgNPs suspension (Spectrosize 300, XtalConcepts GmbH, Germany). Further, the obtained autocorrelation functions’ analysis was performed by using the CONTIN algorithm [40]. DLS results revealed that PC-AgNPs have a hydrodynamic radius size of 38.4 ± 6.0 nm and a polydispersity index of 26.5%. The latter is considered moderate polydispersity, indicating minor size variations (Fig. 3A).

Fig. 3Hydrodynamic radius evolution of PC-AgNPs (A), and XRD pattern of PC-AgNPs (B)

Hydrodynamic radius evolution of PC-AgNPs (A), and XRD pattern of PC-AgNPs (B)

A comprehensive analysis of XRD data for AgNPs obtained using phycocyanin of A. platensis was aimed to determine the structural characteristics, including phase identification, Rietveld analysis, crystallite size, and lattice parameters [8]. XRD analysis confirmed the crystalline nature of PC-AgNPs with a face-centered cubic structure (Fig. 3B). The crystallinity Index (CI) for Ag, calculated from the XRD data, was 69.3%, indicating that the sample possesses a moderately high degree of crystallinity. The XRD pattern revealed the presence of 76.4wt.% Ag from the phycocyanin-derived samples (Fig. 3B). The lattice parameter (a) calculated from the pattern using Bragg’s law and the formula for cubic structures (a = b = c = 4.085 Å) aligns well with literature values for bulk silver (4.086 Å). The identified Bragg reflection planes (hkl indices) for PC-AgNPs include: (111) at 38.126°, (200) at 44.313°, (220) at 64.464°, and (311) at 77.424°. The average crystallite size of the PC-AgNPs was determined to be 93 Å (9.3 nm) according to Rietveld refinement. The lattice strain, calculated using Williamson-Hall analysis, was negligible (0.37%), indicating minimal strain-induced broadening. The PC-AgNPs crystallite size of ~ 10 nm is within the typical range reported for green synthesized AgNPs [15, 17, 33].

Figure 4A shows one of the stable conformers of phycocyanin, the structure of which was optimized using the B3LYP/6-31G(d) density functional theory method (Gaussian 16 program package [50]). The absence of imaginary frequency in the calculated infrared spectrum of the phycocyanin molecule confirms that the obtained structure represents a minimum on its potential energy surface.

Fig. 4Optimized structure of the phycocyanin by B3LYP/6-31G(d) method (A). FTIR spectra of phycocyanin and PC-AgNPs (KBr powder, 32 scan, 4 cm-1 resolution) (B)

Optimized structure of the phycocyanin by B3LYP/6-31G(d) method (A). FTIR spectra of phycocyanin and PC-AgNPs (KBr powder, 32 scan, 4 cm-1 resolution) (B)

FTIR analysis was conducted to identify the key functional groups involved in stabilizing the PC-AgNPs [7, 8]. Figure 4B illustrates the FTIR spectra of phycocyanin and phycocyanin-derived AgNPs, measured in the transmittance mode. The main bands in the FTIR spectrum of phycocyanin, observed at 3447, 3381, 3130, 2923, 2450, 1654, 1274, 1138, 1073, 955, 863, and 541 cm−1, characterize the fundamental vibrational modes of different functional groups present in this molecule. The peaks at 3447 and 3381 cm−1 are attributed to the NH and OH stretching vibrations in phycocyanin. The band at 3130 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of the CH bond in aromatic groups, and 2923 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of CH bonds in the methylene group. The wide band at 1654 cm−1 could be overlapped by different vibrations such as the C=O stretching vibrations in the amide group, as well as C=C and C=N stretching vibrations in the pyrrole ring. The band at 1274 cm−1 could be assigned to the stretching vibration of the C–O bond in carboxylic acid groups. Pronounced peaks at 1138 and 1073 cm−1 are also associated with C–O stretching vibrations. The peaks at 863 cm−1 could be attributed to the bending vibrations of the C–H group, and the band at 541 cm−1 is associated with bending and wagging vibrations of the NH group in the pyrrole ring or the C–H group out-of-plane bending. Compared with the infrared spectra of phycocyanin, the above mentioned peaks in the FTIR spectrum of PC-AgNPs have changed in both wavenumbers and intensity; some of the peaks were broadened, including stretching vibrations of the NH and OH bonds in phycocyanin, stretching of the C=O bond in the amide group, as well as the C–O stretching vibrations.

These findings indicate the participation of various functional groups of phycocyanin, such as carboxylic, hydroxyl, carbonyl and others, in the stabilization of AgNPs.

Antibacterial and antifungal activities of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs

The antibacterial activity of PC-AgNPs was assessed against the opportunistic pathogenic Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus MDC5233, as well as the Gram-negative bacteria S. typhimurium MDC1759 and P. aeruginosa Gar 3. Pathogenic forms of the genus Salmonella are responsible for various infectious diseases in humans [51]. Meanwhile, the opportunistic pathogens P. aeruginosa and S. aureus are linked to nosocomial infections and are known for their multidrug resistance [11, 52, 53].

All tested bacteria exhibited multidrug resistance: S. aureus MDC5233 demonstrated resistance to streptomycin, penicillin, kanamycin, and cefepime; P. aeruginosa Gar 3 was resistant to penicillin, tetracycline, ampicillin, cefepime, and cefotaxime; and S. typhimurium MDC1759 showed resistance to penicillin, amoxicillin, streptomycin, and cefepime. Antibiotics susceptibility was assessed using the disk-diffusion method with commercial antibiotic-impregnated paper discs, which contained the following concentrations of antibiotics: penicillin, ampicillin and streptomycin at 10 µg per disc; amoxicillin at 20 µg per disc; and kanamycin, tetracycline, cefepime and cefotaxime at 30 µg per disc.

Phycocyanin-derived AgNPs were introduced into the bacterial growth medium at concentrations of 5, 10, and 20 µg mL−1. The phycocyanin extract and AgNO3 solution were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. The specific growth rates of bacteria were calculated during the exponential growth phase, when bacteria divided at a constant rate. PC-AgNPs demonstrated concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on the bacteria tested (Fig. 5). The addition of PC-AgNPs significantly inhibited the growth rate of the Gram-negative bacteria P. aeruginosa and S. typhimurium compared to the Gram-positive S. aureus (Fig. 5). PC-AgNPs at a concentration of 5 µg mL−1 suppressed the growth rates of P. aeruginosa and S. typhimurium by 67% and 72%, respectively, while the growth of S. aureus was decreased by 52%. By addition of 10 µg mL−1 of PC-AgNPs the growth rates of P. aeruginosa, S. typhimurium and S. aureus were inhibited by 88%, 90% and 75%, respectively. 20 µg mL−1 of PC-AgNPs inhibited the specific growth rates of Gram-negative bacteria by ~ 97%, and Gram-positive S. aureus by ~ 90% (Fig. 5).

Figure 6 demonstrated the effect of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs on the CFU of Gram-negative bacteria P. aeruginosa and S. typhimurium, as well as Gram-positive S. aureus. At a concentration of 5 µg mL−1, PC-AgNPs reduced the viable colony count of P. aeruginosa and S. typhimurium by 86% and 82%, respectively (Fig. 6A, B, D). Moreover, at 10 µg mL−1, PC-AgNPs completely inhibited the colonization of Gram-negative bacteria (Fig. 6D). The number of CFU of S. aureus was reduced by 66% and 84% with 5 and 10 µg mL−1 of PC-AgNPs, respectively (Fig. 6C, D). At a concentration of 20 µg mL−1, PC-AgNPs completely inhibited the visible growth of S. aureus. The obtained data confirm the bactericidal effect of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs. These results indicate that the MBC of PC-AgNPs is 10 µg mL−1 for Gram-negative bacteria, while for the Gram-positive S. aureus, the MBC is 20 µg mL−1. The phycocyanin extract and AgNO3 solution did not exhibit any antibacterial effect at the same concentrations.

Gram-negative bacteria exhibited greater susceptibility to PC-AgNPs than Gram-positive bacteria, a difference attributed to the structure of their cell walls [7, 8]. The thick peptidoglycan layer in Gram-positive bacteria’s cell walls makes it more difficult for PC-AgNPs to penetrate the cytoplasm, necessitating higher concentrations of NPs to achieve antibacterial effects (Fig. 7). The chemically complex peptidoglycan of Gram-positive bacteria acts as a barrier, limiting the entry of PC-AgNPs. In contrast, Gram-negative bacteria are more sensitive to PC-AgNPs due to their outer membrane high permeability and thin peptidoglycan layer, which facilitates easier NPs penetration (Fig. 7).

The antibacterial mechanism of PC-AgNPs is primarily driven by the release of free, positively charged Ag+ ions, which bind to the negatively charged bacterial membrane surface (Fig. 7). These ions interact with membrane-bound proteins, causing their inactivation, and subsequently penetrate the bacterial cell. This disrupts membrane integrity and impairs ion transport across it. Additionally, the membrane structural damage caused by PC-AgNPs, along with the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) from Ag+ release, interferes with key bacterial metabolic pathways, such as the respiratory chain, inhibiting bacterial growth and leading to cell death.

PC-AgNPs also demonstrated antifungal activity against T. viride, A. flavus and P. aurantioviolaceum. As shown in Fig. 8, complete inhibition of mold growth was observed at a PC-AgNPs concentration of 300 µg mL−1. However, the inhibitory concentration was found to be species-dependent. For example, the growth of A. flavus was inhibited at just 200 µg mL−1, while T. viride was inhibited even at 100 µg mL−1 (Fig. 8). The most notable and significant finding is the inhibition of mold sporulation at lower concentrations. This is particularly important from an economic standpoint, as PC-AgNPs can be used in food preservation, where preventing sporulation helps to limit the spread of molds.

Fig. 8Effect of PC-AgNPs on the fungi T. viride, A. flavus and P. aurantioviolaceum. The control group consisted of fungi cultured in the absence of PC-AgNPs

Effect of PC-AgNPs on the fungi T. viride, A. flavus and P. aurantioviolaceum. The control group consisted of fungi cultured in the absence of PC-AgNPs

The results indicate that the inhibitory concentration of PC-AgNPs required for antifungal activity is significantly higher than that for antibacterial activity. This difference is mainly due to various factors, especially the structural and compositional differences between fungal and bacterial cell walls. It is well known that the bacterial cell wall, particularly in Gram-negative bacteria, contains a thin peptidoglycan that is more permeable to NPs. In contrast, the chitin, β-glucans and mannoproteins in fungal cells provide extreme protection [54, 55]. Additionally, fungal spores or cells are generally larger than those of bacteria. Smaller organisms such as bacteria possess a higher surface area-to-volume ratio, making them more susceptible to membrane damage caused by NPs [56]. Many fungi also possess strong detoxification systems and efficient efflux pumps [57]. Furthermore, as multicellular organisms, fungi are more prone to biofilm formation, which requires a higher concentration of the active agent to effectively disrupt [58]. Fungi produce highly active enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase or catalase, which help neutralize toxic oxidative radicals released during cell death [59]. Finally, the larger size of fungal cells allows them to adsorb NPs or trap them in the extracellular space, reducing intracellular uptake [60].

Phycocyanin-derived AgNPs have demonstrated increased antimicrobial activity against both bacterial and fungal strains. Therefore, using phycocyanin for the biosynthesis of NPs offers significant advantages. Furthermore, NPs synthesized using phycocyanin from A. platensis exhibited antibacterial activity at lower concentrations compared to other phycocyanin-derived AgNPs [33, 34].

The results show that phycocyanin-derived AgNPs exhibited strong antibacterial activity against the tested bacteria, compared to standard antibiotics. The multi-target action of AgNPs helps overcome the resistance mechanisms that limit the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics. As a result, biosynthesized AgNPs offer significant advantages in antimicrobial activity, efficacy against resistant strains, and potential applications in various biomedical and biotechnological fields. While conventional antibiotics remain essential in clinical practice, PC-AgNPs represent a promising alternative, especially in the context of increasing antimicrobial resistance.

Antioxidant activity of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a mechanism behind the toxic effects of AgNPs on living organisms [12, 61–63]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), including free radicals such as superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radical, play a major role in inducing oxidative stress and causing cellular damage. Excessive accumulation of ROS leads to oxidative stress, which can severely impair essential biomolecules and subcellular structures, such as DNA, proteins, lipids, and cell membranes (Fig. 7). The excessive generation of ROS in cells, often triggered by direct interaction with nanoparticles, is currently recognized as a primary mechanism of NPs-induced cytotoxicity.

Antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), catalyze a series of complex reactions that stabilize ROS and free radicals [11, 59]. ROS can also modulate the activity of these enzymes. SOD converts superoxide radicals into H2O2, which catalase further decomposes into H2O and O2. Thus, SOD and CAT are key antioxidant enzymes responsible for neutralizing ROS and are recognized as key regulators of oxidative stress. Moreover, superoxide dismutase and catalase are commonly used as bioindicators of ROS production. The activity of these antioxidant enzymes is essential for maintaining redox homeostasis and controlling intracellular ROS levels, thereby affecting overall cell function. While ROS play important roles in cellular signaling and information processing, their excessive accumulation can overwhelm the antioxidant defense system, resulting in DNA and proteins damage. Through their antioxidant action, SOD and CAT help protect cells by supporting DNA repair mechanisms. Therefore, investigating the effect of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs on the function of antioxidant enzymes is essential for a comprehensive assessment of their toxicity.

To assess the role of antioxidant systems in bacterial response to oxidative stress induced by phycocyanin-derived AgNPs, the activities of SOD and CAT were determined in the tested bacteria exposed to PC-AgNPs. Our findings indicate that PC-AgNPs demonstrated significant antioxidant activity against the bacterial strains tested (Fig. 9A, B).

The activities of antioxidant enzyme: (A) superoxide dismutase (SOD), and (B) catalase (CAT) in PC-AgNPs-treated P. aeruginosa Gar 3, S. typhimurium MDC1759, and S. aureus MDC5233 (n = 3, p < 0.05)

In this study, SOD activity was slightly increased in all bacterial strains treated with PC-AgNPs compared to their respective controls (Fig. 9A). SOD activities in P. aeruginosa, S. typhimurium, and S. aureus treated with PC-AgNPs were 1.94, 1.88, and 1.32 U mg protein-1, respectively, significantly higher than the control values of 1.50, 1.58, and 1.08 U mg protein-1 (Fig. 9A). A similar result was reported by Liao with co-workers [11] for multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa in the presence of AgNPs. Increased SOD activity serves as a biomarker of oxidative stress, indicating that bacterial cells are undergoing redox imbalance and are actively attempting to detoxify harmful superoxide radicals. Therefore, the increase in SOD activity suggests that PC-AgNPs effectively induce oxidative stress in bacterial cells, disrupting their redox homeostasis. This increase may represent a detoxification response to oxidative stress induced by the PC-AgNPs toxicity.

The addition of PC-AgNPs significantly suppressed CAT activity in all tested bacteria (Fig. 9B). CAT activity was reduced by 33% in P. aeruginosa, 47% in S. typhimurium, and 38.5% in S. aureus (Fig. 9B). AgNPs or released Ag+ ions can bind to CAT, causing conformational changes or denaturation that reduces its ability to decompose H2O2.

Increased SOD activity leads to greater production of H2O2, which may overwhelm CAT, particularly if PC-AgNPs impair CAT function or expression. This can result in a measurable decrease in CAT activity. CAT is often more sensitive to AgNP-induced damage than SOD, possibly due to differences in protein structure or cellular localization, leading to stronger inhibition [12, 64]. It is hypothesized that the excessive production of superoxide radicals following NPs exposure contributes to the reduction in catalase activity. One proposed mechanism of AgNPs-induced cytotoxicity in bacteria involves NPs binding to proteins or carbohydrate components on the cell surface, which disrupts the cell membrane [65]. This membrane disruption can cause downregulation or denaturation of CAT, impair the electron transport chain, and ultimately lead to cell death (Fig. 7). At the same time, elevated SOD levels indicate an increased oxidative stress response, likely representing a compensatory mechanism to counteract the excessive ROS generated by AgNP exposure. PC-AgNPs-induced toxicity results in ROS accumulation, which increases oxidative stress and may activate antioxidant enzymes as a protective response. Moreover, Gram-negative bacteria may preferentially upregulate SOD due to their heightened sensitivity to superoxide radicals [61].

The observed increase in SOD activity alongside decreased CAT activity suggests that PC-AgNPs disrupt the bacterial antioxidant defense system, leading to H2O2 accumulation and enhanced oxidative damage. These findings support the potential of PC-AgNPs as effective antimicrobial agents.

Our results show that PC-AgNPs do not possess notable (exhibit minimal) DPPH radical scavenging activity, even at high concentrations. The scavenging activity was only 2.7%, 5.0%, 6.5%, and 12.0% at PC-AgNPs concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 30 µg mL−1, respectively. In comparison, catechin (IC50 = 12.62 ± 0.8 µg mL−1) showed significantly stronger radical scavenging capacity, highlighting the negligible antiradical properties of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs. In contrast, phycocyanin (30 µg mL−1) demonstrated higher DPPH scavenging activity of 30%, indicating superior antioxidant potential. Although the PC-AgNPs exhibited relatively low antiradical activity, this does not diminish their potential antibacterial effectiveness. The antibacterial mechanisms of AgNPs, such as disruption of bacterial membranes, generation of ROS, and interaction with cellular proteins, can operate independently of free radical scavenging. Our findings suggest that the low antiradical activity of PC-AgNPs indicates a limited ability to neutralize ROS. Instead, they may contribute to ROS generation, thereby enhancing their antibacterial effects.

Hemolytic and anticancer activities of PC-AgNPs

Currently, AgNPs have widespread biomedical applications; however, their cytotoxicity may limit their broader diagnostic and therapeutic use. The hemolytic activity of nanomaterials is considered an indicator of their compatibility with blood cells [18, 45].

The hemolytic potential of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs was evaluated to determine their compatibility with healthy human red blood cells (Fig. 10A). Triton X-100 and PBS served as the positive and negative controls, respectively [45]. The results showed that PC-AgNPs did not demonstrate hemolytic activity at low concentrations (5 and 10 µg mL−1), suggesting their potential for biomedical applications. However, at higher concentrations of 20, 30 and 40 µg mL−1, PC-AgNPs caused hemolytic activity of 25, 56 and 78%, respectively (Fig. 10B). Triton-X demonstrated 100% hemolysis (Fig. 10B).

According to the literature, AgNPs exhibit anticancer effects against a variety of cancer cell lines [8–10, 13]. El-Naggar and co-workers [33] revealed that AgNPs, fabricated using phycocyanin from Nostoc linckiai, exhibited a strong cytotoxic activity against the MCF-7 breast cancer cell line.

This study is the first to evaluate the growth-inhibitory effects of phycocyanin-derived AgNPs on the A549 cancer cell line over different exposure times. Cancer cells were cultured with varying concentrations of PC-AgNPs (0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, and 10 µg mL−1) for 4, 24, and 72 h. The results demonstrated that PC-AgNPs significantly inhibited the growth of A549 cancer cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 11), with the cell viability decreasing as the concentration of PC-AgNPs increased. Thus, the highest concentration of 10 µg mL−1 reduced cell viability by 90–92% consistently across all time points (4, 24, and 72 h) (Fig. 11). At PC-AgNPs concentration of 5 µg mL−1, cell viability decreased to 50% after 4 h, while at 24 and 72 h, a stronger inhibitory effect was observed, with cell viability reduced by 80–85%, as reflected in the calculated IC50 values. Similar inhibitory effects, ranging from 40 to 60%, were observed at concentrations of 2.5 and 1.25 µg mL−1 after 24 and 72 h.

The mechanisms of PC-AgNPs-induced cytotoxicity in cancer cells were also investigated. The results showed a notable increase in TNF-α levels in A549 cells treated with 2.5 µg mL−1 PC-AgNPs compared to the control group, indicating an activation of apoptotic pathways (Fig. 12A). When A549 cells were exposed to PC-AgNPs for 24 h, there was a significant decrease in VEGF-A levels (Fig. 12B), dropping from 42 pg mg protein−1 in the control group to about 16 pg mg protein−1, a reduction of approximately 2.6 times. Likewise, the COX-2 level in the experimental group decreased compared to the control group, from 541 pg mg protein−1 to 233 pg mg protein−1 (Fig. 12C). Such decrease suggests a suppression of inflammation-driven tumor progression. Further analysis revealed alterations in key signaling molecules involved in cancer cell survival and metastasis. Experiments also revealed changes in the levels of PI3K, Akt, and MMP-2. Specifically, incubation of A549 cells with 2.5 µg mL−1 PC-AgNPs for 24 h resulted in a decrease in MMP-2 levels to 439 pg mg protein−1, compared to 570 pg mg protein−1 in the control group, indicating a potential impairment of extracellular matrix degradation and tumor invasion (Fig. 12D). Furthermore, PC-AgNPs suppressed the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, a crucial regulator of cancer cell survival and proliferation. As shown in Fig. 12E, a 1.5-fold decrease in total phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) levels was observed in the PC-AgNPs-treated group in comparison with the control. Moreover, total Akt levels decreased fourfold after 24 h of PC-AgNPs treatment (Fig. 12G), while phospho-Akt (Ser473) levels decreased threefold (Fig. 12H), indicating a decrease in both Akt and its activated form.

Treatment of A549 cells with PC-AgNPs resulted in a significant decrease in PI3K/Akt activity, which may inhibit protein synthesis and cell proliferation. Studies have indicated that VEGF-A activates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which helps prevent apoptosis, stimulates DNA synthesis, and promotes the transition from G1 to S phase in endothelial cells. However, our experiments demonstrated that PC-AgNPs also reduced VEGF levels, thus suppressing PI3K/Akt activity. On the other hand, VEGF is crucial in angiogenesis and invasion processes, and studies have linked vascular mimicry to lung cancer pathogenesis. The data revealed that PC-AgNPs significantly reduced MMP-2 expression, a key factor linked to angiogenesis, invasiveness, and metastasis [66, 67]. In addition to PI3K/Akt, VEGF, and MMP-2, COX-2 production was also examined, as it plays a vital role in the development of various inflammatory diseases and cancer [68]. While COX-2 is generally expressed at low levels, it can be induced by specific cytokines, including TNF-α. The role of TNF-α is particularly significant; it is now recognized for mediating two signaling pathways. One pathway involves apoptosis, where TNF-R1 recruits TRADD and FADD to activate the apoptotic machinery. In the other pathway, TRAF2 activates multiple signaling cascades that lead to the activation of NF-κB, which prevents TNF-induced cell death, highlighting NF-κB role as a negative regulator. Throughout the study, TNF-α levels were observed to increase, and the MTT assay revealed the cytotoxic effect of PC-AgNPs, suggesting activation of the TNF-R1 pathway and induction of apoptosis in the cells (Fig. 12I).

Thus, PC-AgNPs function as anticancer agents by suppressing key angiogenic factors, including VEGF-A and MMP-2, as well as inflammation-related markers such as TNF-α and COX-2. This inhibition effectively contributes to the prevention of angiogenesis and metastasis (Fig. 12I).

Conclusions

A novel approach was employed to fabricate silver nanoparticles using phycocyanin, a key photosynthetic pigment extracted from the cyanobacterium Arthrospira platensis Pc-005. The physicochemical properties of the PC-AgNPs were characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy, FTIR spectroscopy, XRD, TEM, EDX, and DLS. Phycocyanin acted as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent, facilitating the AgNPs biosynthesis. The biosynthesized PC-AgNPs exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against various opportunistic pathogenic bacteria and fungi in a concentration-dependent manner. In addition, PC-AgNPs induced oxidative stress in bacteria, leading to an increase in superoxide dismutase activity. However, these NPs also inhibited catalase activity, thereby preventing the timely elimination of excessive ROS, such as H2O2 and other peroxides, which led to oxidative damage to proteins and DNA, causing cell death. Furthermore, our research revealed the anticancer potential of PC-AgNPs. These NPs downregulated key angiogenesis-related factors (VEGF-A and MMP-2) and inflammation-related markers (TNF-α and COX-2), effectively inhibiting both angiogenesis and metastasis.

This study underscores the potential of cyanobacterial phycocyanin as a sustainable and environmentally friendly agent for the biosynthesis of nanoparticles and the effective control of microbial pathogens. Therefore, phycocyanin-derived AgNPs hold great promise as anticancer and antimicrobial agents, with prospective applications in biotechnology, biomedicine, and pharmaceutical development.
