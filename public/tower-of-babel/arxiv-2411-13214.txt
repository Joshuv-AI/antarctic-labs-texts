Existence and Nonexistence of Invariant Curves of Coin Billiards
Santiago Barbieri, Andrew Clarke

Abstract
In this paper we consider the coin billiard introduced by M. Bialy. It is a modification of the classical billiard, obtained as the return map of a nonsmooth geodesic flow on a cylinder that has homeomorphic copies of a classical billiard on the top and on the bottom (a coin ). The return dynamics is described by a map T T of the annulus 𝔸 = 𝕋 × ( 0 , π ) \mathbb{A}=\mathbb{T}\times(0,\pi) . We prove the following three main theorems: in two different scenarios (when the height of the coin is small, or when the coin is near-circular) there is a family of KAM curves close to, but not accumulating on, the boundary ∂ 𝔸 \partial\mathbb{A} ; for any noncircular coin, if the height of the coin is sufficiently large, there is a neighbourhood of ∂ 𝔸 \partial\mathbb{A} through which there passes no invariant essential curve; and the only coin billiard for which the phase space 𝔸 \mathbb{A} is foliated by essential invariant curves is the circular one. These results provide partial answers to questions of Bialy. Finally, we describe the results of some numerical experiments on the elliptical coin billiard.

Introduction

At a conference at CIRM in Luminy in 2021 the following modification of the classical billiard was suggested by Bialy
[

\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)

\Gamma

\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)

The motion can be described as follows. We introduce an arclength parametrisation
\gamma=\gamma(\varphi)

\partial\Gamma

\mathbb{A}

(\varphi,\theta)

\theta\in(0,\pi)

\gamma(\varphi)

\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)

\theta_{1}
at
\gamma(\varphi_{1})

\theta_{1}

\theta_{1}

This geodesic motion can be described as a map

T:(\varphi,\theta)\mapsto(\varphi_{1}+\ell\,\cot\theta_{1},\theta_{1})

which means that

is the composition
T=T_{2}\circ T_{1}

What are the shapes of

Question 3

Can

We refer to the configuration cylinder

(I)

(II)

The coin billiard is a special case of the so-called

Main Results

In this paper, we provide partial answers to Questions
1
-
3
. In what follows we give qualitative statements of our main results. The more detailed statements can be found in the relevant sections later in the paper.

With regards to Question
1
, we have found KAM curves in two different perturbative settings: where the height of the coin is small; and when the domain is near-circular.

Theorem A
.

Consider the coin mapping
T
on the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
, where
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded and strictly convex domain with real-analytic boundary, and
\ell>0
. Suppose either:

(a)

The height of the coin is perturbative:
\ell\ll 1
; or

(b)

The curvature is close to constant:
\partial\Gamma
is close to a circle.

Then there is a strip in
\mathbb{A}
close to (but not accumulating on)
\partial\mathbb{A}
that contains a set of positive Lebesgue measure of
T
-invariant curves with Diophantine rotation numbers.

The formal statements of these results can be found in Theorems
2
and
3
in Section
4
below. The idea of the proof is very simple: we prove that, in each of these perturbative settings, we can make a coordinate transformation in a strip so that the map takes the form
(x,y)\to(x+y,y)+O(\varepsilon)
. We then apply Moser’s version of the KAM Theorem. Although Theorem
A
requires the boundary to be real-analytic, it is likely true for much less regular boundaries. The reason we state real-analyticity as the required regularity is to allow us to apply a straightforward version of the KAM theorem (Moser’s original theorem, described in the appendix) in order to simplify the exposition. A version of the KAM theorem with lower regularity requirements likely could be applied, but there may be other more cumbersome conditions to check, which would detract from the simple nature of the proof.

Theorem
A
is about
existence
of invariant curves. The following theorem is about
nonexistence
of invariant curves in a neighbourhood of the boundary of the phase space
\mathbb{A}
.

Theorem B
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded and strictly convex domain with
C^{5}
boundary. Suppose moreover that
\Gamma
is not a disc. Then there exists an
\ell_{0}=\ell_{0}(\Gamma)>0
such that for any
\ell>\ell_{0}
, there is
\delta>0
for which no invariant essential curve of the coin mapping
T
on the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
passes through the neighbourhood
\left(\mathbb{T}\times(0,\delta)\right)\cup\left(\mathbb{T}\times(\pi-\delta,\pi)\right)
of
\partial\mathbb{A}
.

We give two proofs of Theorem
B
. The first one is variational, and makes use of a particular coordinate transformation in strips accumulating on
\partial\mathbb{A}
. In these coordinates, the coin mapping
T
is a small perturbation of a generalised standard map. We write a generating function for this map, and combine this with a technique introduced by Mather to prove the absence of invariant curves
[
17
]
. In the second proof it is shown that, when
\ell>\ell_{0}
, if essential invariant curves were to exist near the boundary, their Lipschitz constants would not respect the bounds found by Herman in
[
10
]
. A future goal is to establish similar results in the case of more general pensive billiards (i.e. with different delay functions
[
8
]
), which is why we have been considering different approaches to the problem.

Naturally, we would like to provide a complement to Theorem
B
for the case where the height of the coin is small. As we will discuss in more detail later (see the discussion on Bialy’s second question below) this is quite difficult, as it is closely related to proving nonintegrability of a generalised standard map for small values of the parameter, i.e. a notoriously difficult problem that is still open in its full generality.

Upon consideration of Theorems
A
and
B
, one may wonder if the coin map on
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
admits any essential invariant curve at all when the table
\Gamma
is far from circular and
\ell
is sufficiently large. Indeed, for certain values of
\theta_{*}\in(0,2\pi)
, Gutkin constructed in
[
9
]
noncircular analytic boundaries
\Gamma_{*}
for which the corresponding billiard map
T_{1}
has an invariant curve
C_{*}=\{(\varphi,\theta):\theta=\theta_{*}\}
(i.e. a circular caustic). Since the map
T_{2}
does not alter
\theta
, the invariant curve
C_{*}
remains invariant for the coin map
T
on
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma_{*},\ell\right)
for
any
value of
\ell>0
.

Finally, the third main result of this work is the following.

Theorem C
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be any bounded strictly convex domain with
C^{5}
boundary. For any
\ell>0
, if the map
T
associated to the coin billiard
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
admits a family of essential invariant curves whose union is
the annulus
\mathbb{A}
, then
\Gamma
is a disc.

This result is an extension to coin billiards of a Theorem of Bialy about classical billiards. See the original paper
[
4
]
for a variational proof,
[
21
]
for a geometric approach, and
[
16
]
for its relation to the Birkhoff conjecture and to questions related to entropy. The proof of Theorem
C
is essentially different from those that are found in the context of classical billiards, which rely either on variational or on geometric methods. As we will see in Section
7
we use in an essential way the strong twist property of
T
near the boundary, and the bounds on the Lipschitz constants that any hypothetical essential invariant curve of
T
should satisfy, according to Herman
[
10
]
. In particular, it is shown that, for noncircular tables, Herman’s bounds are incompatible with the existence of a foliation of the phase space near the boundary by essential invariant curves, due to the strong twist property of the coin map in that region. Incidentally, the same kind of arguments are used in the second proof of Theorem
B
. We also observe that another reason why Theorem
C
is interesting is that it holds for any height of the coin billiard. As we will discuss later in the paper, we conjecture that some aspects of its proof might be used in order to prove nonintegrability for small heights of the coin.

The proof of Theorem
C
presents the following trichotomy: either
\Gamma
is a disc; or the coin map
T
in
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
is not integrable; or else
\Gamma
is not a disc, but the coin map
T
in
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
is integrable, and there are chains of (likely elliptic) islands, surrounded by separatrices of integer rotation number, that accumulate on
\partial\mathbb{A}
. We expect the latter configuration to be very fragile, as a generic perturbation should break the separatrices of integer rotation number (see e.g.
[
19
]
). This could be used in future works to prove nonintegrability of the coin billiard when the height is small.

In Theorems
B
and
C
we require that the boundary be
C^{5}
, for the following reason. In the proofs of these theorems, we first perform an expansion of the coin map
T(\varphi,\theta)=(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})
in powers of
\theta
(see equation (
2
) below). The term of order
\theta^{2}
in the expansion of
\bar{\theta}
depends on the first derivative
\rho^{\prime}
of the radius of curvature of
\partial\Gamma
with respect to
\varphi
(i.e. the third derivative of
\partial\Gamma
), and similarly the term of order
\theta^{3}
depends on
\rho^{\prime}
and on
\rho^{\prime\prime}
. We then need to work with the
\varphi
derivative of
\bar{\theta}
; in particular, we work with its Taylor polynomial up to terms of order
\theta^{2}
, and we need that the higher order terms are well-behaved in the limit as
\theta\to 0
. This holds when
\partial\Gamma
is
C^{5}
.

In what follows we describe some important consequences of Theorems
B
and
C

Corollary D

Under the hypotheses of Theorem

, there exists an orbit whose

\mathbb{A}
, and whose

-limit set is contained in

Moreover, other classical results about twist maps of the annulus due to Angenent, Boyland and Hall, and Katok ensure the following

.

Corollary E
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem

As a direct consequence of Corollaries

Corollary F
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded and strictly convex domain with
C^{5}
boundary. Suppose
\partial\Gamma
is noncircular. Then for
\ell>\ell_{0}
, with
\ell_{0}
as in Theorem

T

In addition to these analytical theorems, we have performed some numerical experiments with elliptical coin billiards. These simulations are described in Section

Responses to Bialy’s Questions and Comparison with Classical Billiards

We now describe the significance of Theorems
A
,
B
, and
C
in the context of Questions
1
,
2
, and
3
. Moreover, we compare the dynamical behaviour of the map on the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
with the case when
\ell=0
: the classical billiard.

1.

Invariant Curves:
The main results of this paper regard existence and nonexistence of invariant curves of
T
. In particular, we have established the existence of some invariant curves in two perturbative settings, as well as the nonexistence of invariant curves for any noncircular coin billiard, when the height
\ell
of the coin is sufficiently large. This provides a partial answer to Bialy’s first question. As mentioned previously, in addition to the KAM curves we construct, there are also the special cases of Gutkin tables in which
T_{1}
-invariant curves of constant height correspond to
T
–invariant curves of constant height
[
9
]
.

The nonexistence of invariant curves near the boundary for
\ell\gg 0
in the noncircular case is in contrast with the situation in classical billiards. Indeed, a celebrated result of Lazutkin implies that classical billiards (sufficiently smooth, planar, strictly convex)
always
have a family of invariant curves accumulating on the boundary
[
14
]
. In fact, in case (
a
) of Theorem
A
, it can be seen that the strip of the phase space containing
T
-invariant curves tends towards
\partial\mathbb{A}
as
\ell\to 0
(see Theorem
3
). Therefore in this sense, we see that the limiting behaviour of the classical billiard can be observed in the coin billiard as we flatten the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
.

2.

Integrability:
We point out that if
\mathbb{D}
is a disc then the coin billiard on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D},\ell)
is integrable. Indeed, the angle of reflection
\theta
is a constant of motion. On one hand, Theorem
B
implies that if
\Gamma
is
any
noncircular billiard, and
\ell>\ell_{0}(\Gamma)
then the coin billiard on
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
is not integrable. On the other hand, Theorem
C
states that for any height of the coin the
only
billiard whose phase space is completely foliated by essential invariant curves is
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D},\ell)
. This provides an almost complete classification of the integrable coin billiards. However
\ell_{0}(\Gamma)
is strictly positive whenever
\Gamma
is not a disc, and while it seems very unlikely that there could be any
\ell>0
for which
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
is integrable with
\Gamma
different from a disc, we have not yet been able to obtain a proof of this. Indeed, close to the boundary
\partial\mathbb{A}
, the map can be reduced to a generalised standard map, with parameter
\ell
. The question of nonintegrability of generalised standard maps for small values of the parameter is a notorious open problem plagued by the exponentially small splitting of separatrices, and was solved in the special case of the Chirikov standard map by Lazutkin and his school only after a tremendous effort over many years
[
13
]
. However, as we shall see in Section
7
, in the proof of Theorem
C
it is demonstrated that, for any
\ell>0
, if
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
is integrable, then there exist separatrices of integer rotation number that bound (likely elliptic) islands accumulating on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{A}
. This kind of configuration appears to be very fragile, and we expect that the separatrices should generically split, thus giving rise to chaos. We point out that, although the coin map is closely related to the standard map, this is only after performing a local change of variables that effectively hides the strong twist condition. It is possible that the strong twist condition could itself be used to prove nonintegrability via different methods, thus avoiding problems of exponential smallness.

By contrast, in the classical setting, the only known integrable billiard is in an ellipse (with the circle being a special case). Indeed, this is famously conjectured to be the only integrable classical billiard, a problem which remains open in its full generality, and is called the
Birkhoff conjecture
.

3.

Ergodicity:
Clearly, in the case of homeomorphisms of a two-dimensional annulus, the presence of invariant essential curves implies that the system is not ergodic, as such curves necessarily divide the phase space into invariant regions of strictly positive, but not full, Liouville measure. As a result, our Theorem
A
implies that if
\ell
is small or if
\Gamma
is close to a disc, then the coin mapping on
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
is not ergodic.

As a comparison with classical billiards, a famous result of Lazutkin implies the existence of invariant curves near the boundary in
any
strictly convex and sufficiently smooth billiard
[
14
]
, so the classical billiard is not ergodic. Therefore, with respect to ergodicity, the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
for small values of
\ell
and the limiting billiard
\Gamma
agree.

Essential invariant curves are not, however the only obstacle to ergodicity. Elliptic islands around elliptic periodic orbits, whenever they exist, are nontrivial invariant sets of positive measure. As an example of when they can exist, we can consider the case when
\Gamma
is an ellipse (see Section
8
for details). In general, it is not easy to rule out the existence of elliptic periodic orbits for a concrete model, but if this could be done, then for sufficiently large values of
\ell
, it may be possible to observe ergodicity.

2
The Coin Mapping

In this section we define the classical billiard map, and subsequently the coin map. We describe several salient features of the latter.

2.1
The Classical Billiard

Denote by
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
a bounded strictly convex domain with
C^{r}
(
r=2,3,\ldots,\infty,\omega
) boundary. Rescaling
\mathbb{R}^{2}
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that the length of
\partial\Gamma
is
2\pi
, and introduce an arclength parametrisation
\gamma:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. We assume that
\gamma
is oriented counterclockwise. Denote by
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
the arclength parameter, and let
\mathbb{A}:=\mathbb{T}\times(0,\pi)
. The billiard map
T_{1}:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
is defined by
T_{1}(\varphi,\theta)=(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})
in the following way. A phase point
(\varphi,\theta)
uniquely determines a point
x\in\partial\Gamma
and an inward-pointing unit velocity vector
v\in T_{x}^{1}\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
x=\gamma(\varphi)
and
v=\mathcal{R}(\theta)\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)
, where
\mathcal{R}(\theta)
is the usual counterclockwise rotation matrix by an angle
\theta
. The billiard dynamics follows the straight line in the direction of
v
until the next point of collision with the boundary at
\gamma(\bar{\varphi})
, where the velocity vector
v
is reflected according to the optical law: the angles of incidence and reflection coincide. Then
\bar{\theta}
is this new incidence/reflection angle.

It is well-known that the billiard map
T_{1}
is a
C^{r-1}
twist diffeomorphism. Indeed, we lose one degree of differentiability in the finite regularity case because the definition of the map requires
\gamma^{\prime}
. Moreover invertibility, as well as the regularity of the inverse follows from the easily verifiable fact that
\mathcal{I}\circ T_{1}\circ\mathcal{I}=T_{1}^{-1}
, where
\mathcal{I}(\varphi,\theta)=(\varphi,\pi-\theta)
. The latter property is called
reversibility
. The twist property
\partial\bar{\varphi}/\partial\theta>0
too is easily verifiable by elementary geometrical considerations: if we fix a phase point
(\varphi,\theta)\in\mathbb{A}
, keep
\varphi
unchanged, and consider
\theta^{\prime}>\theta
, then the point
\gamma\circ\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T_{1}(\varphi,\theta^{\prime})
lies strictly in the positive counterclockwise direction from
\gamma\circ\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T_{1}(\varphi,\theta)
.

Although the billiard map admits a pleasant geometrical definition, there is in general no closed formula for
T_{1}
. However, near the boundary
\partial\mathbb{A}
we can compute its Taylor series expansion. Indeed, for values of
\theta
close to zero the expansion

\bar{\varphi}=\varphi+2\,\rho(\varphi)\,\theta+O\left(\theta^{2}\right),\quad\bar{\theta}=\theta-\frac{2}{3}\,\rho^{\prime}(\varphi)\,\theta^{2}+O\left(\theta^{3}\right)

(1)

with
(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})=T_{1}(\varphi,\theta)
was computed in
[
14
]
, where
\rho(\varphi)
is the radius of curvature of
\partial\Gamma
at
\gamma(\varphi)
. Obviously an analogous expansion holds near the other part of the boundary, for values of
\theta
close to
\pi
, by the reversibility property. In the paper
[
14
]
the author makes a further transformation to a remarkable system of coordinates (now commonly called
Lazutkin coordinates
) in which the map
T_{1}
admits an even simpler expansion. We note that Lazutkin coordinates are not helpful in the present paper because in those coordinates when we consider, as in the next section, the composition
T_{2}\circ T_{1}
, we lose track of the near-integrable nature of the problem.

2.2
The Coin Billiard

Maintaining the notation of the previous section, and with
\ell>0
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
the cylinder of height
\ell
with homeomorphic copies of
\Gamma
on the top and on the bottom, and call it the
coin of height
\ell
. We consider the motion of a particle on the surface of
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
. On the top of the coin, the motion of the particle is equivalent to the billiard on
\Gamma
. It starts at a point
\gamma(\varphi)
with its unit velocity vector
v
making an angle
\theta
with the boundary
\partial\Gamma
. It moves in a straight line in the direction of
v
until it reaches
\partial\Gamma
again. When the particle reaches the boundary
\partial\Gamma
with angle of incidence
\bar{\theta}
at
\gamma(\bar{\varphi})
(as given by
T_{1}
), rather than reflect back inside, the velocity vector reflects over the edge of the cylinder so that it makes an angle
\bar{\theta}
with the upper boundary of the cylinder. The particle then follows the geodesic on the surface of the cylinder until it reaches the copy of
\Gamma
at the bottom of the coin. Here the motion is reflected back inside
\Gamma
with an equal angle
\bar{\theta}
(see Figure
1
). In order to calculate the augmentation of the
\varphi
coordinate caused by following the geodesic on the cylinder
\mathbb{T}\times[0,\ell]
, we consider the universal cover
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\ell]
of the cylinder. On this strip, the geodesic becomes a straight line, making an angle
\bar{\theta}
with the upper boundary. Its projection onto the real line thus has length
\Delta
which can be calculated from
\tan\bar{\theta}=\frac{\ell}{\Delta}
.

Figure 1
:
A schematic picture of the motion of the particle on the configuration coin.

The map
T
describing this motion is therefore given by
T=T_{2}\circ T_{1}
where
T_{1}
is the billiard map from the previous section, and
T_{2}(\varphi,\theta)=(\varphi+\ell\cot\theta,\theta)
.

Example
.

As an example, consider the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\mathbb{D},\ell\right)
where
\mathbb{D}=\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\|q\|=1\}
is the unit disc in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\ell>0
. It is well-known (and can be proved by basic trigonometry) that the billiard map in
\mathbb{D}
is given by
T_{1}(\varphi,\theta)=(\varphi+2\theta,\theta)
. Therefore the coin map in
\mathcal{C}\left(\mathbb{D},\ell\right)
is given by
T(\varphi,\theta)=(\varphi+2\theta+\ell\cot\theta,\theta)
. It follows that every curve corresponding to constant values of
\theta
is invariant, so the map is completely integrable.

Notice moreover that, writing
(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})=T(\varphi,\theta)
, we have
\frac{\partial\bar{\varphi}}{\partial\theta}=2-\frac{\ell}{\sin^{2}\theta}
. The latter has two zeros
\theta_{\pm}(\ell)\in(0,\pi)
whenever
\ell\in(0,2)
. It follows that there are two essential curves
\{\theta=\theta_{\pm}(\ell)\}
in
\mathbb{A}
where the twist condition fails whenever
\ell\in(0,2)
, one such curve
\{\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\}
when
\ell=2
, and whenever
\ell>2
, the coin map in
\mathcal{C}\left(\mathbb{D},\ell\right)
is a twist map.

More generally, for any billiard
\Gamma
if
\ell>0
is small then there are curves where the twist condition fails, but if
\ell
is sufficiently large then
T
is a twist map. However for any
\ell
, the map
T
is a twist map in a neighbourhood of the boundary
\partial\mathbb{A}
of the phase space, for the following reason. Although we have no closed formula for
T
in general (since we have none for
T_{1}
), we can combine our formula for
T_{2}
with the expansion (
1
) of
T_{1}
to obtain the expansion

\bar{\varphi}=\varphi+\frac{\ell}{\bar{\theta}}+O\left(\theta\right),\quad\bar{\theta}=\theta-\frac{2}{3}\,\rho^{\prime}(\varphi)\,\theta^{2}+O\left(\theta^{3}\right)\ ,

(2)

with
(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})=T(\varphi,\theta)
. It is thus clear that
\frac{\partial\bar{\varphi}}{\partial\theta}<0
for all sufficiently small values of
\theta
. Analogous formulas hold for values of
\theta
close to
\pi
.

3
Generating Function

In this section we construct a generating function for the coin map. Denote by
\lambda=\cos\theta\,d\varphi
the Liouville 1-form, so that the symplectic form is
\Omega=d\lambda=\sin\theta\,d\varphi\wedge d\theta
. Recall that a map
F:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
is exact symplectic if and only if
\oint_{C}\lambda=\oint_{F(C)}\lambda
for any closed path
C\subset\mathbb{A}
, or equivalently, if there is a function
h:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F^{*}\lambda-\lambda=dh
. Sometimes, however, it is more convenient when
h
is not a function of the phase space
\mathbb{A}
, but rather of the second power
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the universal cover
\mathbb{R}
of the configuration space
\mathbb{T}
. We say that
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
generating function
for (the lift to the universal cover of)
F
if
F(\varphi,\theta)=(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})
if and only if
\partial_{1}h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=\cos\theta
and
\partial_{2}h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\cos\bar{\theta}
.

For the argument in Section
6
we require the existence of a generating function for the coin map
T
. In the following lemma, we construct generating functions for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. This implies immediately, of course, that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are exact symplectic, and in turn the coin map
T=T_{2}\circ T_{1}
is exact symplectic. However this
does not imply
the existence of a global generating function for
T
, because the coin map
T
is not necessarily a twist map, as pointed out in Section
2
. Since the coin map does satisfy a twist property in a neighbourhood of
\partial\mathbb{A}
a generating function can be shown to exist in those regions. For completeness, we include a proof of this fact.

Lemma 1
.

The maps
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
,
T
satisfy the following properties.

1.

The billiard map
T_{1}
is exact symplectic with respect to the 1-form
\lambda
. Moreover the function
h_{1}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\|\gamma(\varphi)-\gamma(\bar{\varphi})\|
generates
T_{1}
in the sense that
T_{1}(\varphi,\theta)=(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})
if and only if
\partial_{1}h_{1}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=\cos\theta
and
\partial_{2}h_{1}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\cos\bar{\theta}
.

2.

The map
T_{2}
is exact symplectic with respect to the 1-form
\lambda
. Moreover the function
h_{2}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\ell\sqrt{1+\ell^{-2}\left(\bar{\varphi}-\varphi\right)^{2}}
generates
T_{2}
in the sense that
T_{2}(\varphi,\theta)=(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})
if and only if
\partial_{1}h_{2}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=\cos\theta
and
\partial_{2}h_{2}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\cos\bar{\theta}
.

3.

The coin map
T
is exact symplectic with respect to the 1-form
\lambda
. Moreover there is a
\delta>0
such that, defining the set
D=\left\{(\varphi,\theta):\theta\in(0,\delta)\right\}
, there is a function
h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})
such that for
(\varphi,\theta)\in D
,
T(\varphi,\theta)=(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})
if and only if
\partial_{1}h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=\cos\theta
and
\partial_{2}h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\cos\bar{\theta}
.

Proof.

Part
1
of the lemma is well-known, and can easily be verified by direct differentiation of the function
h_{1}
. As for part
2
, observe that the equation
\bar{\varphi}=\varphi+\ell\cot\theta
for the
\varphi
component of
T_{2}
can be rewritten to read

\ell^{-1}(\bar{\varphi}-\varphi)=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\theta}}

(3)

so we obtain

\cos\theta=\frac{\ell^{-1}(\bar{\varphi}-\varphi)}{\sqrt{1+\ell^{-2}(\bar{\varphi}-\varphi)^{2}}}.

Therefore, differentiation of the function
h_{2}(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=-\ell\sqrt{1+\ell^{-2}\left(\bar{\varphi}-\varphi\right)^{2}}
implies that
\partial_{1}h_{2}\left(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}\right)=\cos\theta
and
\partial_{2}h_{2}\left(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}\right)=-\cos\theta=-\cos\bar{\theta}
.

We now address part
3
of the lemma. The fact that
T=T_{2}\circ T_{1}
is exact symplectic follows immediately from the exact symplecticity of
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
. We fix
\delta>0
small enough so that the expansion (
2
) makes sense, and
T
satisfies the twist condition
\frac{\partial\bar{\varphi}}{\partial\theta}<0
in
D
. Define the set

\Delta=\left\{(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\exists\,\theta_{0}\in(0,\delta)\textrm{ with }\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T(\varphi_{0},\theta_{0})=\varphi_{1}\right\}.

Let
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})\in\Delta
. Then there is
\theta_{0}\in(0,\delta)
with
T(\varphi_{0},\theta_{0})=\varphi_{1}
. Observe that this
\theta_{0}
is unique, as a result of the twist property of
T
in
D
. Indeed, if
\theta_{0}^{\prime}<\theta_{0}
then
\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T(\varphi_{0},\theta_{0}^{\prime})>\varphi_{1}
, and we get the opposite inequality in the case where
\theta_{0}^{\prime}>\theta_{0}
. It follows that there is a well-defined function
\Phi:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\Phi(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})=\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T_{1}(\varphi_{0},\theta_{0})
.

The map
\Phi
is
C^{r-1}
whenever the boundary is
C^{r}
. Indeed, this can be seen by direct computation of the derivatives, differentiating the equations

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}={}

\displaystyle\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T_{1}^{-1}\left(\Phi(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}),\arccos\partial_{1}h_{2}\left(\Phi(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}),\varphi_{1}\right)\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}={}

\displaystyle\Pi_{\varphi}\circ T_{2}\left(\Phi(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}),\arccos\left(-\partial_{2}h_{1}\left(\varphi_{1},\Phi(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})\right)\right)\right).

(5)

We do not include the computations as they are lengthy, and as we do not require formulas for the derivatives of
\Phi
in this paper. However it can be seen from these equations that the first derivatives of
\Phi
depend on the first derivatives of
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and the second derivatives of
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
, so they are
C^{r-2}
, and so
\Phi
is
C^{r-1}
.

We then define the function
h:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=h_{1}\left(\varphi,\Phi\left(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}\right)\right)+h_{2}\left(\Phi\left(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}\right),\bar{\varphi}\right).

Suppose
(\varphi,\theta)\in D
and write
(\bar{\varphi},\bar{\theta})=T(\varphi,\theta)
. Then, by the construction of
\Phi
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{1}h_{1}(\varphi,\Phi(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}))=\cos\theta,\quad\partial_{2}h_{1}(\varphi,\Phi(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}))=-\cos\bar{\theta}

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{1}h_{2}(\Phi(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}),\bar{\varphi})=\cos\bar{\theta},\quad\partial_{2}h_{2}(\Phi(\varphi,\bar{\varphi}),\bar{\varphi})=-\cos\bar{\theta}.

(7)

Writing
\Phi=\Phi(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})
, it follows that

\partial_{1}h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=\partial_{1}h_{1}\left(\varphi,\Phi\right)+\left(\partial_{2}h_{1}\left(\varphi,\Phi\right)+\partial_{1}h_{2}\left(\Phi,\bar{\varphi}\right)\right)\partial_{1}\Phi=\cos\theta

and

\partial_{2}h(\varphi,\bar{\varphi})=\partial_{2}h_{2}\left(\Phi,\bar{\varphi}\right)+\left(\partial_{2}h_{1}\left(\varphi,\Phi\right)+\partial_{1}h_{2}\left(\Phi,\bar{\varphi}\right)\right)\partial_{2}\Phi=-\cos\bar{\theta},

so
h
is the function we are looking for.
∎

4
Existence of KAM Curves

In this section we prove, in two different scenarios, the existence of some KAM curves. These are homotopically nontrivial invariant curves
C
of the map
T
in
\mathbb{A}
such that
T|_{C}
is conjugate to a rotation by a Diophantine frequency. The two scenarios we consider are: when the corresponding billiard table
\Gamma
is close to a disc; and when the height
\ell
of the coin is small. In each of these situations we make a coordinate transformation in a band in the phase space that is close to, but not accumulating on, the boundary. We then apply an existing KAM theorem (see Theorem
13
in the appendix) to establish the existence of the invariant curves. For simplicity of exposition, we only prove the existence of the invariant curves in the analytic category, but we point out that they their existence in less regular topologies would follow from existing KAM theorems that deal with those topologies.

4.1
The Near-Circular Case

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a real-analytic family of compact and strictly convex billiard domains, such that
\partial\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is a closed
C^{\omega}
plane curve of length
2\pi
, and such that
\Gamma_{0}
is a disc. Fix a constant
c_{1}>0
. Let
\ell>0
, and denote by
T
the coin mapping with respect to
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\ell\right)
. Then there is an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
the subset
\mathbb{T}\times[\varepsilon-c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{2},\varepsilon+c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{2}]
of
\mathbb{A}
contains
T
-invariant essential curves with Diophantine rotation numbers, and the union of these curves is a set of positive measure.

Proof.

In order to prove Theorem
2
we use the assumptions to put the map
T
in a form in which Theorem
13
can be applied. In a neighbourhood of the boundary, the coin map admits the expansion (
2
). Fix some
c_{1}>0
, as in the statement of Theorem
2
. In the set
\mathbb{T}\times[\varepsilon-c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{2},\varepsilon+c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{2}]
we make the coordinate transformation
:(x,y)\mapsto(\varphi,\theta)
where

\varphi=x,\quad\theta=\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}y

so that
x\in\mathbb{T}
and
y\in[-c_{1},c_{1}]
. From (
2
) the map takes the form
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T(x,y)
with

\bar{x}=x+\omega+\ell\bar{y}+O(\varepsilon),\quad\bar{y}=y+\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime}(x)+O(\varepsilon)

where we have defined
\omega=\ell/\varepsilon\mod 2\pi
.

Now, since
\partial\Gamma_{0}
is a circle of radius
1
, we can expand the radius of curvature of
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
in powers of
\varepsilon
to find that
\rho=1+\varepsilon\,\bar{\rho}+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
. It follows that
\rho^{\prime}=O(\varepsilon)
. Therefore the map
T
becomes

T:\begin{cases}\bar{x}=&x+\omega+\ell\,y+O(\varepsilon)\\
\bar{y}=&y+O(\varepsilon).\end{cases}

This map is real-analytic because, after making an analytic coordinate transformation, it is the composition of two analytic maps:
T_{2}
is always analytic, independently of parameters, and
T_{1}
is analytic because
\partial\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is. Obviously it is a twist map since
\frac{\partial\bar{x}}{\partial y}=\ell+O(\varepsilon)>0
for all sufficiently small values of
\varepsilon
. Finally, part
3
of Lemma
1
tells us that
T
is exact symplectic (with respect to some Liouville 1-form) which in turn implies that
T
satisfies the intersection property. Therefore Theorem
13
can be applied to complete the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

4.2
The Case when
\ell\ll 1

In this section we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact and strictly convex billiard domain, such that
\partial\Gamma
is a real-analytic closed plane curve of length
2\pi
. Fix any
b_{0}>a_{0}>0
. Denote by
T
the coin mapping with respect to
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
. Then there is an
\ell_{0}>0
such that for any
\ell\in(0,\ell_{0})
the subset
\mathbb{T}\times\left[\frac{\ell}{b_{0}},\frac{\ell}{a_{0}}\right]
of
\mathbb{A}
contains
T
-invariant essential curves with Diophantine rotation numbers, and the union of these curves is a set of positive measure.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
2
, we look for a suitable coordinate transformation that puts the map in a form where we can apply Theorem
13
. Fix
b_{0}>a_{0}>0
as in the statement of the theorem, and choose real numbers
a,b
such that
b>b_{0}>a_{0}>a>0
. In the set
\mathbb{T}\times\left[\frac{\ell}{b},\frac{\ell}{a}\right]
we make the coordinate transformation
:(\varphi,\theta)\mapsto(\xi,\eta)
where

\xi=\varphi,\quad\eta=\frac{\ell}{\theta}

so that
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
and
\eta\in[a,b]
. Then from (
2
) we see that the map takes the form
T(\xi,\eta)=(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})
where

T:\begin{cases}\bar{\xi}=&\xi+\eta+O(\ell)\\
\bar{\eta}=&\eta+O(\ell).\end{cases}

Then obviously the map is real-analytic, satisfies a twist condition for all sufficiently small
\ell>0
since
\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial\eta}=1+O(\ell)
, and has the intersection property by part
3
of Lemma
1
. Therefore the rescaled map satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
13
, and so Moser’s theorem completes the proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

5
Construction of Vertically-Mapped Graphs

In this section we prove some statements that will be used later in the paper. In particular, we show that, due to its unbounded twist near the boundary, the coin map admits a sequence of smooth graphs accumulating on the boundary whose points are mapped onto the same abscissa after one iteration (for this reason, we sometimes refer to them as
vertically-mapped graphs
). This has important consequences because it determines a priori the regions where possible invariant curves may lie close to the boundary. The reasonings that we make here are loosely related to those made by Poincaré when he showed that, under a small perturbation, a curve with integer rotation number generically does not persist but nevertheless gives rise to points with integer rotation number
[
3
, Section 20]
. Throughout this section, we will indicate by
\widetilde{T}
the lift of
T
to the universal cover
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi)
. The main result of this section is the following.

Lemma 4
.

There exists
m_{0}=m_{0}(\Gamma,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}
, and a sequence of disjoint well-ordered graphs
\{\mathscr{G}_{m}\}_{m\geq m_{0}}
of
2\pi
-periodic functions
g_{m}\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfy the following properties:

1.

The graphs
\{\mathscr{G}_{m}\}_{m\geq m_{0}}
accumulate uniformly on the boundary;

2.

For any
(\varphi,\theta)\in\mathscr{G}_{m}
one has
\Pi_{\varphi}\widetilde{T}(\varphi,\theta)=\varphi+2m\pi
;

3.

|g_{m}^{\prime}(\varphi)|\leq\displaystyle\frac{8}{3}\rho^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)|g_{m}(\varphi)|^{2}
for any
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the notation

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{m}(\varphi,\theta):=&\Pi_{\varphi}(\widetilde{T}(\varphi,\theta))-\varphi-2m\pi=\frac{\ell}{\theta}+\frac{2}{3}\ell\rho^{\prime}(\varphi)+R(\varphi,\theta)-2m\pi\ ,\end{split}

(8)

where we have used the expansion (
2
) of
\widetilde{T}
for small values of
\theta
, and where
R(\varphi,\theta)=O(\theta)
.
Then
F_{m}\in C^{4}
, as the billiard table is
C^{5}
by hypothesis. Due to expression (
8
), the twist property, and the continuity of
F_{m}
, for any fixed
\varphi_{\star}\in\mathbb{T}
there exists
m_{1}=m_{1}(\Gamma,\ell,\varphi_{\star})\in\mathbb{N}
, and a strictly decreasing sequence
\{\theta_{m}=\theta_{m}(\varphi_{\star})\}_{m\geq m_{1}}
such that one has
\theta_{m}\rightarrow 0
, and
F_{m}(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{m})=0
.

Moreover, as
\theta_{m}\rightarrow 0
, and
\partial_{\theta}F_{m}(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{m})=-\ell/\theta_{m}^{2}+O(1)
, for some positive integer
m_{2}=m_{2}(\Gamma,\ell,\varphi_{\star})\geq m_{1}
one has
\partial_{\theta}F_{m}(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{m})\neq 0
for any
m\geq m_{2}
. Then, for any given
m\geq m_{2}
one can apply the Implicit Function Theorem, so that
F_{m}(\varphi,g_{m}(\varphi))=0
in a neighborhood of
\varphi=\varphi_{\star}
, for an implicit function
g_{m}
of class
C^{4}
verifying
g_{m}(\varphi_{\star})=\theta_{m}
.

The same construction can be repeated around any other point
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
, so that by the compactness of the domain, and by the uniqueness of the implicit function, there exists
m_{0}(\Gamma,\ell)
such that for any given
m\geq m_{0}
the function
g_{m}
can be extended to the whole torus
\mathbb{T}
. Then, due to the
2\pi
-periodicity of
F_{m}(\varphi,\theta)
with respect to
\varphi
, one has that
g_{m}
can be extended to the whole real line and has period
2\pi
. Moreover
g_{m+1}(\varphi)<g_{m}(\varphi)
for any
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\geq m_{0}
, due to the negative twist property near the boundary.

Then, as
g_{m}(\varphi)\rightarrow 0
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
, by Dini’s Theorem and by the periodicity of
g_{m}
one has the uniform convergence
\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|g_{m}(x)|=\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}}|g_{m}(x)|=0
, and the graphs
\mathscr{G}_{m}:=\text{graph}(g_{m})
accumulate uniformly on the horizontal axis.

It remains to prove the estimate on the first derivative of
g_{m}
, which is a simple consequence of the Implicit Function Theorem. In fact, for
m
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}|g_{m}^{\prime}(\varphi)|=\left|\frac{\partial_{\varphi}F_{m}(\varphi,g_{m}(\varphi))}{\partial_{\theta}F_{m}(\varphi,g_{m}(\varphi))}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Fm}}}{{=}}\left|\frac{2/3\,\ell\rho^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)+\partial_{\varphi}R(\varphi,\theta)}{\ell/(g_{m}(\varphi))^{2}+O(1)}\right|\leq\frac{8}{3}\rho^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)(g_{m}(\varphi))^{2}\ .\end{split}

(9)

This concludes the proof.

∎

Lemma
4
has three important corollaries, that we state and prove below.

Corollary 5
.

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, for any
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max\{|g_{m-1}(\varphi)-g_{m}(\varphi))|,|g_{m}(\varphi)-g_{m+1}(\varphi))|\}\leq\frac{3\pi}{\ell}(g_{m}(\varphi))^{2}\ .\end{split}

(10)

Proof.

Fix
m\geq m_{0}
,
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
, and write
\theta_{m}=g_{m}(\varphi)
. With the notations of Lemma
4
, we have
F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m})=0
. By the constructions of Lemma
4
, and by the monotonicity of
F_{m}
with respect to the second variable close to the boundary, it is sufficient to determine a real number
\alpha>0
such that

\min\{|F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m}+\alpha)|,|F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m}-\alpha)|\}\geq 2\pi\ .

More precisely, for
\alpha<\theta_{m}
, by (
8
), we can write

\displaystyle F_{m}

\displaystyle(\varphi,\theta_{m}\pm\alpha)=\frac{\ell}{\theta_{m}\pm\alpha}+\frac{2}{3}\ell\rho^{\prime}(\varphi)+R(\varphi,\theta_{m}\pm\alpha)-2m\pi

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{\theta_{m}}\mp\frac{\ell\alpha}{\theta_{m}^{2}}\pm O\left(\ell\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\theta_{m}^{3}}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\ell\rho^{\prime}(\varphi)+R(\varphi,\theta_{m})\pm\partial_{\theta}R(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{m})\alpha-2m\pi

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m})\mp\frac{\ell\alpha}{\theta_{m}^{2}}\pm O\left(\ell\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\theta_{m}^{3}}\right)\pm\partial_{\theta}R(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{m})\alpha

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mp\frac{\ell\alpha}{\theta_{m}^{2}}\pm O\left(\ell\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\theta_{m}^{3}}\right)\pm\partial_{\theta}R(\varphi,\widetilde{\theta}_{m})\alpha\ ,

(14)

where
\widetilde{\theta}_{m}
is a point on the line segment joining
\theta
to
\theta\pm\alpha
, and where we have taken into account the fact that
F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m})=0
by construction. It is clear from (
14
) that, for
m
sufficiently large, that is for sufficiently small
\theta_{m}
, if one chooses
\alpha=\displaystyle\frac{3\pi}{\ell}\theta_{m}^{2}
, then
\min\{|F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m}+\alpha)|,|F_{m}(\varphi,\theta_{m}-\alpha)|\}\geq 2\pi
, as required.
∎

Corollary 6
.

Let
C
be an essential invariant graph of the map
\widetilde{T}
having at least one point lying below the graph
\mathscr{G}_{2m_{0}}
. Then, one has
\mathscr{G}_{m}\cap C\neq\varnothing
for at most one value of
m\geq 2m_{0}
. In particular, there exists
m^{\star}=m^{\star}(C)\geq 2m_{0}
such that
C
is contained in the band having
\mathscr{G}_{m^{\star}-1}
and
\mathscr{G}_{m^{\star}+1}
as boundaries.

Proof.

For any
m\geq 2m_{0}
, we define the sets

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{D}_{m}:=&\{(\varphi,\theta)\in\mathscr{G}_{m}\ |\ \ \Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi,\theta)\neq\theta\}\\
\mathscr{E}_{m}:=&\{(\varphi,\theta)\in\mathscr{G}_{m}\ |\ \Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi,\theta)=\theta\}=\mathscr{G}_{m}\setminus\mathscr{D}_{m}\ .\end{split}

(15)

We firstly claim that
\mathscr{D}_{m}\cap C=\varnothing
for any value of
m\geq 2m_{0}
. In fact, given
m\geq 2m_{0}
, if
\mathscr{D}_{m}=\varnothing
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, if
\mathscr{D}_{m}\neq\varnothing
and
\mathscr{D}_{m}\cap C\neq\varnothing
then the image
T(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})
of any point
(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\in\mathscr{D}_{m}\cap C
would satisfy
\Pi_{\theta}T(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\neq\theta_{\star}
by definition of
\mathscr{D}_{m}
, and
T(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\in C
by invariance of
C
, but also
\Pi_{\varphi}T(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})=\varphi_{\star}
by the second point of Lemma
4
, in contradiction with the hypothesis that
C
is the graph of a function.

By the previous claim, for any
m\geq 2m_{0}
one has
\mathscr{G}_{m}\cap C=\mathscr{E}_{m}\cap C
. Then, if
\mathscr{E}_{m}\cap C\neq\varnothing
for at most one value of
m\geq 2m_{0}
, the proof ends here by taking into account the fact that the vertically-mapped graphs of Lemma
4
are well-ordered. Now, assume that there exist two different values
m_{3},m_{4}\geq 2m_{0}
, such that
\mathscr{E}_{m_{3}}\cap C\neq\varnothing
and
\mathscr{E}_{m_{4}}\cap C\neq\varnothing
. By definition (
62
), for any
m\geq m_{0}
points in
\mathscr{E}_{m}
have rotation number
m
under
\widetilde{T}
, so that points in
\mathscr{E}_{m_{3}}\cap C
and in
\mathscr{E}_{m_{4}}\cap C
have rotation number
m_{3}
and
m_{4}\neq m_{3}
, respectively. However,
C
is invariant under
\widetilde{T}
, so that its points must have a unique rotation number due to Poincaré’s theorem, which leads to a contradiction. This concludes the proof.

∎

Corollary 7
.

Given an integer
m\geq 2m_{0}
, and a point
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
, consider any pair of points

and

in the band whose boundary is given by

and
\mathscr{G}_{m+1}
. Then one has

(16)

Proof.

It follows immediately from the constructions of Lemma
4
: by the twist condition the abscissa of all points below
\mathscr{G}_{m-1}
(respectively, above
\mathscr{G}_{m+1}
) increases by a quantity larger than

(respectively, smaller than

) under one iteration of
\widetilde{T}
.
∎

A direct consequence of the previous corollaries is the following

There is a constant

6
Drifting Trajectories when
\ell\gg 0

The following theorem is the main result of this section. It is the precise statement of the result described by Theorem
B
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact and strictly convex billiard domain, such that
\partial\Gamma
is a closed
C^{5}
plane curve of length
2\pi
. Suppose that
\partial\Gamma
is not a circle. Define

\ell_{0}=-\frac{3}{\min_{\varphi\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)}>0.

(24)

Then for any
\ell>\ell_{0}
and
\delta\ll\ell-\ell_{0}
the coin mapping
T
on the coin
\mathcal{C}\left(\Gamma,\ell\right)
has no essential invariant curves in the region
\mathbb{T}\times(0,\delta)
of the phase space
\mathbb{A}
.

The rest of this section is dedicated to the proof of this theorem. In fact, we give two proofs of the theorem, using different methods. The first proof is an adaptation of an argument of Mather using generating functions
[
17
]
, while the second analyses the Lipschitz bounds of any hypothetical invariant essential curves. For simplicity, by an abuse of notation we will often denote by
T
the lift of
T
to the universal cover
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi)
of
\mathbb{A}
; the distinction between the two will be made clear from the context.

First proof of Theorem
9
.

Suppose now that
T
has an essential invariant curve
C
near the boundary. The form (
2
) of the map implies that
T|_{C}
is orientation-preserving.

Let
c_{0}>0
be the constant from Lemma
8
, and let
\varepsilon=(\theta_{+}+\theta_{-})/2
where
\theta_{\pm}
are the maximal and minimal values of
\theta
over all
(\varphi,\theta)\in C
. In the strip
\mathbb{T}\times\left[\varepsilon-c_{0}\varepsilon^{2},\varepsilon+c_{0}\varepsilon^{2}\right]\subset\mathbb{A}
we make the coordinate transformation
:(x,y)\mapsto(\varphi,\theta)
where

\varphi=x,\quad\theta=\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}y

so that
x\in\mathbb{T}
and
y\in[-c_{0},c_{0}]
. From (
2
) the map takes the form
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T(x,y)
where

\bar{x}=x+\omega+\ell\bar{y}+O(\varepsilon),\quad\bar{y}=y+\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime}(x)+O(\varepsilon)

where we have defined
\omega=\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{\varepsilon}\mod 2\pi
. If we remove the terms of order
\varepsilon
we obtain the map

T_{0}:\begin{cases}\bar{x}=x+\omega+\ell\bar{y}\\
\bar{y}=y+\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime}(x).\end{cases}

Rearranging these equations, we obtain
\bar{y}=\ell^{-1}(\bar{x}-x-\omega)
and
y=\bar{y}-\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime}(x)
. Therefore the function

h_{0}(x,\bar{x})=\ell^{-1}\left(x\bar{x}-\frac{\bar{x}^{2}}{2}-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\omega(\bar{x}-x)\right)-\frac{2}{3}\rho(x)

which is
C^{3}
because
\partial\Gamma
is
C^{5}
, is such that
T_{0}(x,y)=(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if and only if

\partial_{1}h_{0}(x,\bar{x})=y,\quad\partial_{2}h_{0}(x,\bar{x})=-\bar{y}.

(25)

The second order partial derivatives of
h_{0}
are

\partial_{1}^{2}h_{0}(x,\bar{x})=-\ell^{-1}-\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x),\quad\partial_{2}^{2}h_{0}(x,\bar{x})=-\ell^{-1},\quad\partial_{2}\partial_{1}h_{0}(x,\bar{x})=\ell^{-1},

(26)

which we will use below.

In what follows we want to use the function
h_{0}
to describe trajectories of
T
at first order, but
T
is not exact symplectic with respect to the 1-form
y\,dx
, and so does not admit a generating function of this type. Lemma
1
, however, implies that there is a function
h(x,\bar{x})
such that
T(x,y)=(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if and only if

\partial_{1}h(x,\bar{x})=\cos\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}y\right),\quad\partial_{2}h(x,\bar{x})=-\cos\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}\bar{y}\right).

(27)

Since
\cos\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}y\right)=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)+\varepsilon^{3}y+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
, and
y=\partial_{1}h_{0}(x,\bar{x})
at first order in
\varepsilon
due to (
25
), it can be seen that the generating function
h
, up to additive constants, takes the form

h(x,\bar{x})=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)(x-\bar{x})+\varepsilon^{3}\,h_{0}(x,\bar{x})+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right).

Now, we define the function

H(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=\partial_{2}h(x_{0},x_{1})+\partial_{1}h(x_{1},x_{2}).

Using (
26
) we can compute the first derivatives of
H
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{0}}={}

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\partial_{2}h(x_{0},x_{1})=\varepsilon^{3}\partial_{1}\partial_{2}h_{0}(x_{0},x_{1})+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)=\varepsilon^{3}\ell^{-1}+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{1}}={}

\displaystyle\partial_{2}^{2}h(x_{0},x_{1})+\partial_{1}^{2}h(x_{1},x_{2})=\varepsilon^{3}\left[\partial_{2}^{2}h_{0}(x_{0},x_{1})+\partial_{1}^{2}h_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\right]+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{3}\left[-2\ell^{-1}-\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\right]+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{2}}={}

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\partial_{1}h(x_{1},x_{2})=\varepsilon^{3}\partial_{2}\partial_{1}h_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)=\varepsilon^{3}\ell^{-1}+O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)\ .

(31)

Recall we have assumed that there is an invariant essential curve
C
of
T
, such that
C\subset\mathbb{T}\times[-c_{0},c_{0}]
in
(x,y)
coordinates, and we have pointed out that
T|_{C}
is necessarily orientation-preserving. Equation (
27
) implies that the short sequence
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
represents a segment of a trajectory of
T
if and only if
H(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=0
. Since
\Gamma
is not a circle, there are both points on
\mathbb{T}
for which
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
and points for which
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
. Choose
x_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
so that
x_{1}
minimises
\rho^{\prime\prime}
. In particular,
\rho^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})<0
. Since
C
is an essential curve there exists
y_{1}\in(-c_{0},c_{0})
such that
(x_{1},y_{1})\in C
. Denote by
x_{0}
the
x
-component of
T^{-1}(x_{1},y_{1})
, and by
x_{2}
the
x
-component of
T(x_{1},y_{1})
. Then
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
represents a segment of a trajectory of
T
, so
H(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=0
. Moreover, since
\ell>-\frac{3}{\rho^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})}
(indeed, see (
24
)) and since
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, we have
\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})<0
. Therefore, by the implicit function theorem, there is a
C^{1}
function
\nu(x_{0}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})
such that for all
(x_{0}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})
in a neighbourhood of
(x_{0},x_{2})
we have
H(x_{0}^{\prime},\nu(x_{0}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}),x_{2}^{\prime})=0
. Moreover the derivatives of
\nu
are given by

\frac{\partial\nu}{\partial x_{0}^{\prime}}(x_{0},x_{2})=-\frac{\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{0}}}{\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{1}}}<0,\quad\frac{\partial\nu}{\partial x_{2}^{\prime}}(x_{0},x_{2})=-\frac{\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{2}}}{\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{1}}}<0.

It follows that
\nu
is a decreasing function of
x_{0},x_{2}
, and so
T|_{C}
is orientation-reversing. This contradiction implies that there can be no essential invariant curves of the mapping in a neighbourhood of the boundary, as required.
∎

We will now give a second proof of Theorem
9
.
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, hereafter we set
\|x\|_{\mathbb{T}}:=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}|x+2p\pi|
. The first intermediate result that we use is the following Lemma, which is inspired by Herman’s ideas
[
10

.

where
\theta_{\pm}

\theta

\displaystyle L\end{matrix}\right).

Second proof of Theorem
9
.

Since by hypothesis we have

, for any given height

8

We observe that such an interval
[\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}]
exists because
\rho^{\prime\prime}
is continuous (
\partial\Gamma
is

), and that

is not a singleton because the table

Then, for

, we use the notations

(47)

and
we set

. One has

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{\prime}(t)=&1+\frac{2}{3}\ell\rho^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{t})+O(\theta_{t})-\frac{L\ell}{\theta_{t}^{2}}(1+O(\theta_{t}^{2}))\\

where

so that, due to (

\text{ with }\quad R^{\star}:=\frac{\theta_{0}^{2}}{\ell}\times\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in[\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}]\\

is true, then

for all

.
We now suppose that condition (

, and one has

H(1)-H(0):=\frac{\Pi_{\varphi}\left[\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{1},\xi(\varphi_{1}))-\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{0},\xi(\varphi_{0}))\right]}{\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{0}}<0.

(51)

Expression (
51
) indicates that
\widetilde{T}
reverses the orientation of the points
(\varphi_{1},\xi(\varphi_{1}))
and
(\varphi_{0},\xi(\varphi_{0}))
, in contradiction with the fact that, as we have already pointed out previously in the paper,
\widetilde{T}|_{\xi}
preserves orientation. Therefore, condition (
50
) must be false, and one must have

L\leq\left(\frac{1}{\ell}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)}{K}\right)\theta_{0}^{2}+R^{\star}.

(52)

Then, due to formulas (
37
) and (
39
), and to Lemma
8
, the inverse dynamics yields

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{T}^{-1}(\varphi_{1},\xi(\varphi_{1}))-\widetilde{T}^{-1}(\varphi_{0},\xi(\varphi_{0}))}{\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{0}}=(1+O(\widetilde{\theta}))+\frac{\ell L}{\widetilde{\theta}^{2}}(1+O(\widetilde{\theta}))

(53)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{maggiorante L}}}{{\leq}}(1+O(\widetilde{\theta}))+\frac{\ell}{\widetilde{\theta}^{2}}(1+O(\widetilde{\theta}))\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\ell}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)}{K}\right)\theta_{0}^{2}+R^{\star}\right\}

(54)

\displaystyle=(1+O(\theta_{0}))+\frac{\ell}{\theta_{0}^{2}}(1+O(\theta_{0}))\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\ell}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)}{K}\right)\theta_{0}^{2}+R^{\star}\right\}

(55)

\displaystyle=2+\frac{2}{3}\ell\frac{\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)}{K}+\frac{\ell R^{\star}}{\theta_{0}^{2}}+O(\theta_{0}).

(56)

Now, we have that

2+\frac{2}{3}\ell\frac{\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)}{K}<0,\qquad\frac{\ell R^{\star}}{\theta_{0}^{2}}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in[\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}]\\
\theta\in[\theta_{0}-c_{0}\theta_{0}^{2},\theta_{0}+c_{0}\theta_{0}^{2}]\end{subarray}}R(\varphi,\theta)=O(\theta_{0})

(57)

due, respectively, to condition (
45
), and to the previous constructions. Moreover, as
K
depends only on
\ell
and
\ell_{0}
, the value of
2+\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\ell\frac{\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)}{K}
is a fixed quantity when
\theta_{0}\rightarrow 0
. Hence, by expression (
56
) we have that
\widetilde{T}^{-1}
is orientation reversing for the points
(\varphi_{1},\xi(\varphi_{1}))
and
(\varphi_{0},\xi(\varphi_{0}))
when the graph of the curve
\xi
lies too close to the boundary, which is once again impossible.

We have derived a contradiction when either (
50
) or (
52
) is satisfied, hence there exists no essential invariant curve of
\widetilde{T}
close to the boundary.

∎

7
Extension of a Theorem of Bialy

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
C
. As mentioned in the introduction, we prove that if the billiard table is noncircular then there exists a sequence of homotopically trivial regions (elliptic islands or chaotic regions) through which there passes no essential invariant curve of the coin map. In addition, we provide estimates on the Lebesgue measure of these regions, which is of order
O\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right)
, where
m
is the index of the closest vertically-mapped graph (see Section
5
). The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact and strictly convex billiard domain, with
\partial\Gamma
a closed
C^{5}
plane curve of length
2\pi
. Suppose, moreover, that
\Gamma
is not a disc. Then

1.

For any
\ell>0
there exists
M=M(\Gamma,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}
, and a sequence
\{\mathscr{I}_{m}\}_{m\geq M}
of open, bounded, connected, homotopically-trivial regions accumulating on
\partial\mathbb{A}
, and intersecting no essential invariant curve of the coin map
T
on
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\ell)
;

2.

The Lebesgue measure of these regions is

\text{meas}(\mathscr{I}_{m})\geq\frac{\ell^{2}\,\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime}(x)}{8(3+2\ell\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime\prime}(x))}\times\frac{\text{meas}(J_{\Gamma})}{\left(2m\pi+1-\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\ell\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}},

(58)

where
J_{\Gamma}
is the largest connected component of the set

\left\{\varphi\in\mathbb{T}\ |\ \rho^{\prime}(\varphi)\in[\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime}(x)/2,\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\rho^{\prime}(x)]\right\};

3.

If
T
is integrable, for each
m\geq M
the boundary of
\mathscr{I}_{m}
is given by pieces of essential
T
-invariant graphs of rotation number
m
.

We now consider the setting and the notations of Section
5
. The following simple result also turns out to be fundamental in order to prove Theorem
11
.

Lemma 12
.

Given
m\geq 2m_{0}
, with
m_{0}
as in Lemma
4
, choose, if it exists, a point
(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\in\mathscr{G}_{m}
such that
\Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\neq\theta_{\star}
. Then there is a neighborhood of
(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})
that intersects no essential invariant curve of
\widetilde{T}
.

Proof.

In the proof of Corollary
6
we had defined the set

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{D}_{m}:=&\{(\varphi,\theta)\in\mathscr{G}_{m}\ |\ \ \Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi,\theta)\neq\theta\}\ \end{split}

(59)

and we showed that, for any
m\geq m_{0}
, any possible invariant graph
C
of
\widetilde{T}
satisfies
C\cap\mathscr{D}_{m}=\varnothing
.

Now, given
m\geq 2m_{0}
and
(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\in\mathscr{D}_{m}
, suppose for contradiction that there exists a sequence of essential invariant curves
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
whose graphs
\{C_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
approach
(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})
, that is

\inf_{(\varphi,\theta)\in C_{k}}|(\varphi,\theta)-(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})|\rightarrow 0
. Then, as the graphs have uniformly bounded Lipschitz constants by Lemma
10
, one must have
(\varphi_{\star},\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star}))\rightarrow(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})
. For any given
k
, the Lipschitz constant
\mathscr{L}_{C_{k}}
of the graph

must satisfy

\mathscr{L}_{C_{k}}\geq\frac{|\Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star}))-\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star})|}{\|\Pi_{\varphi}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star}))-\varphi_{\star}\|_{\mathbb{T}}}.

(60)

However, we have that
|\Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star}))-\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star})|\rightarrow|\Pi_{\theta}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})-\theta_{\star}|\neq 0
by hypothesis, and that
\|\Pi_{\varphi}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\xi_{k}(\varphi_{\star}))-\varphi_{\star}\|_{\mathbb{T}}\rightarrow\|\Pi_{\varphi}\widetilde{T}(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})-\varphi_{\star}\|_{\mathbb{T}}=0
by Lemma

as
(\varphi_{\star},\theta_{\star})\in\mathscr{G}_{m}
, so that the
\mathscr{L}_{C_{k}}
are unbounded, in contradiction with Lemma

.
∎

We are now able to prove Theorem
11
.

are Lipschitz over

Appendix A
Moser’s Theorem

In this appendix, we give the necessary definitions, and the statement of the version of Moser’s theorem on the existence of essential invariant curves for twist maps that we use in this paper. As mentioned earlier in the paper, we do not choose the strongest possible version of this result, but rather the simplest for our exposition. The version of the theorem presented here is from Sections 32 and 33 of
[
20
]
.

Consider a map
F:\mathbb{T}\times[a,b]\to\mathbb{T}\times(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
defined by

F:\begin{cases}\bar{x}={}&x+\alpha(y)+f(x,y)\\
\bar{y}={}&y+g(x,y)\end{cases}

(78)

where
x\in\mathbb{T}
,
y\in[a,b]
, and
a^{\prime}<a<b<b^{\prime}
. We make the following assumptions regarding
F
:

(i)

F
is real-analytic;

(ii)

F
satisfies the twist property
\alpha^{\prime}(y)\neq 0
for all
y\in[a,b]
; and

(iii)

F satisfies the
intersection property
: for any essential curve of the form
C=\{(x,\zeta(x)):x\in\mathbb{T}\}
where
\zeta\in C^{0}(\mathbb{T},[a,b])
we have
F(C)\cap C\neq 0
.

Since
f
,
g
are real-analytic, they admit holomorphic extensions to a complex neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathbb{T}\times[a,b]
. We denote by
\|.\|
the supremum norm on the space of holomorphic functions on
\mathcal{U}
. A condition of the theorem is that
\|f\|
and
\|g\|
are sufficiently small. Observe that when
\|f\|,\|g\|=0
then for each
y_{0}\in[a,b]
, the curve
\{y=y_{0}\}
is an essential
F
-invariant curve with rotation number
\omega=\alpha(y_{0})
. It has been known since the works of Poincaré that invariant curves with a rotation number of the form
\omega=2\pi\,p/q
where
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
can be destroyed by arbitrarily small perturbations satisfying conditions (
i
)-(
iii
). Instead, we consider frequencies which are not only irrational multiples of
2\pi
, but are poorly approximated by rational multiples of
2\pi
: we say that
\omega
is
Diophantine
and write
\omega\in\mathcal{D}
if there are
k>0
and
\mu\geq 2
such that

\left|\frac{\omega}{2\pi}-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{k}{|q|^{\mu}}

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
with
q\neq 0
. Then Moser’s theorem tells us that any curve
\{y=y_{0}\}
that is not too close to the boundary of
