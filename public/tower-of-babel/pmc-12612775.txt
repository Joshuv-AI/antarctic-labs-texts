Effect of extracellular vesicles in remodeling the tumor microenvironment by DNMT1 downregulation for enhanced cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Salvatore Russo, Yvonne Giannoula, Sara Feola, Justin Cerioni, Firas Hamdan, Jacopo Chiaro, Anja Thu Ha Dang, Paolo Bottega, Michaela Feodoroff, Manlio Fusciello, Virpi Stigzelius, Carmine D’Amico, Carmen Caiazza, Gabriella Antignani, Federica D’Alessio, Milda Sakalauskaite, Julia Petryk, John Bell, Carolina Ilkow, Mikaela Grönholm, Vincenzo Cerullo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy of immunotherapy is often hindered by the suppression of immune responses via the tumor microenvironment (TME). The presence of cancer cells forces other proximal non-cancerous cells to support tumor growth and persistence. A clear example of this cancerous-to-non-cancerous communication is represented by the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) within the TME. Several studies have convergently shown that the overexpression of DNA-methyl-transferase-1 (DNMT1) in these cells results in protection from necroptosis and enhanced accumulation in vivo. Conversely, targeting DNMT1 through hypo-methylating agents has shown promising therapeutic potential by not only reducing the levels of MDSCs but also enhancing cancer immunogenicity and the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).MethodsMurine 4T1 (triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)) and CT26 (colon carcinoma) cell lines were cultured under standard conditions and used to generate tumor models in BALB/c mice. An oncolytic adenovirus expressing a DNMT1-targeting short hairpin RNA (OAd.shDNMT1) was engineered and validated for DNMT1 knockdown and genome-wide methylation reduction. Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) were isolated from virus-infected cancer cells and characterized for RNA content and uptake by MDSCs. MDSC differentiation and suppressive function were assessed in vitro using flow cytometry and co-culture assays with murine splenocytes. Furthermore, the co-administration with ICI resulted in a significant tumor growth reduction in mice bearing poorly immunogenic TNBC 4T1 cells. Also, our treatment promoted antitumor immunity, prolonged survival, and complete tumor eradication in modestly immunogenic colon CT26 cancer cells.ConclusionThis multifaceted strategy, based on OV-mediated immune stimulation and reduction of MDSC levels via sEVs, may improve clinical outcomes and the success of immuno-based regimens for patients facing MDSC-rich and highly aggressive cancer subtypes.

Background

Immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment for various cancers; however, the efficacy of such therapies is often hindered by the suppression of immune responses via the tumor microenvironment (TME).1 The TME is characterized by a complex relationship between cancer and its proximal non-cancerous cells and is heavily based on cell–cell communication via direct interaction, secretion of biomolecules, and cancer-derived extracellular vesicles.2 3 In this way, early-stage cancerous or pre-cancerous cells alter both the metabolic and gene expression profile of the surrounding fibroblasts, endothelial, and myeloid cells. Furthermore, besides directly facilitating the cancer progression via structural support, cytokines and growth factors release, non-cancer cells can limit the anticancer immune responses within the TME. A clear example of this cancerous-to-non-cancerous communication is represented by the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs).4 MDSCs are involved in the suppression of autoimmune reactions in physiological conditions. In the presence of cancerous cells, MDSCs heavily accumulate in the bone marrow, spleen, and peripheral tissues, where they inhibit antitumor T and natural killer cells via the expression of Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1), the secretion of reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide and immunosuppressive cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-10 and Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β).5 In clinical settings, the intratumoral presence of MDSCs is associated with an impaired response to chemotherapies and immunotherapies in glioblastoma, breast cancer, colon cancer, and lung cancer.69 The cancer-mediated secretion of myeloid growth factors like monocyte-granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (GM-CSF), monocyte colony-stimulating factors, and other cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β) promote the viability and suppressive function of MDSCs.4 Furthermore, several studies have convergently shown that the downstream overexpression of DNA-methyl-transferase-1 (DNMT1) on IL-6 exposure results in MDSCs protection from necroptosis and enhanced tissue infiltration in vivo.10 11 At the same time, high levels of DNMT1 have been associated with less inflamed tumors and a worse prognosis in patients with cancer.10 12 Conversely, targeting DNMT1 through hypo-methylating agents (HMAs) has shown promising therapeutic potential by not only reducing the accumulation and suppressive activities of MDSCs12 but also enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).13

In these regards, we hypothesized that counteracting DNMT1 activity in MDSC-rich tumors has potential benefits to cancer immunotherapy. To achieve this, we generated an oncolytic adenovirus expressing a DNMT1-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) named OAd.shDNMT1 to reduce MDSC levels within the TME. Previous studies have reported that when delivered to cancer cells, oncolytic viruses (OVs) expressing therapeutic small RNAs extend their action range to the surrounding cells of the TME via cancer-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs).14

sEVs are small extracellular vesicles with a phospholipid bilayer membrane that facilitate intercellular communication by transporting proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, like messenger RNAs, miRNAs, and long non-coding RNAs. In the cancer context, sEVs arising from tumor membranes play a crucial role in the TME by facilitating tumor progression and metastasis. Furthermore, several studies have shown that cancer-derived sEVs increase the expansion and suppressive abilities of immune cells, such as MDSCs and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs),15 16 enhancing the resistance to anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 therapies.17 On the other hand, extracellular vesicles can be engineered to deliver therapeutic molecules with higher stability, improved safety, and effectiveness than liposomes and nanoparticles.18 Indeed, cancer sEVs containing therapeutic small RNAs have shown efficient intratumoral gene downregulation in vivo.19 However, the studies supporting their applicability in immunotherapeutic settings are limited. Additionally, the intrinsic capacity of OVs to elicit an inflammatory response is beneficial in immune responses against tumors. Indeed, several studies have illustrated that oncolytic virotherapy can synergistically improve ICI therapeutic efficacy.20

For this study, we evaluated our approach in the poorly immunogenic 4T1 (triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)) and the modestly immunogenic CT26 murine cell lines, which are known to induce MDSC accumulation in tumor-bearing mice.21 Collectively, in this study, we report that, by using virally infected TNBC murine cells as a source for shDNMT1-loaded sEV production, OAd.shDNMT1 successfully reduced MDSC levels in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, the co-administration with ICI resulted in a significant tumor growth reduction in mice bearing poorly immunogenic TNBC 4T1 cells, and, in addition, promoted antitumor immunity, prolonged survival, and tumor eradication in modestly immunogenic colon CT26 cancer cells. This multifaceted strategy, based on OV-mediated immune stimulation and reduction of MDSC levels via sEVs, may improve clinical outcomes and the success of immuno-based regimens for patients facing MDSC-rich and highly aggressive cancer subtypes.

Methods

Cell lines

Human lung cancer A549 (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) catalog no. CCL-185, Research Resource Identifier (RRID): CVCL_0023) and human TNBC MDA-MB-436 (ATCC catalog no. HTB-130, RRID: CVCL_0623) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% glutamine (Gibco), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Life Technologies). Murine TNBC cell line 4T1 (ATCC catalog no. CRL-2539, RRID: CVCL_0125) and CT26 (ATCC catalog no. CRL-2638, RRID: CVCL_7254) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) high glucose with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% glutamine (Gibco), 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Life Technologies). The 4T1 Rab27a KO cell line was cultured as previously published.14 The cells were cultivated at 37°C, 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere, and regularly tested for Mycoplasma contamination.

Oncolytic adenovirus

The viral particle concentrations were measured by calculating the genome light absorbance at 260 nm. At the same time, the infection titer (infective units, IFU) was determined by immunocytochemistry staining the adenoviral hexon protein with mouse anti-Hexon (Novus, catalog no. NB600-413) and secondary Biotin-SP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (Jackson ImmunoResearch, catalog no. 115-065-062) antibodies on adenoviral-infected A549 cells.

Briefly, MISSION Lentiviral shRNA plasmids containing the U6 promoter-shRNA sequences were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Mission pLKO.1-puro eGFP shRNA Control Plasmid DNA, catalog no. Shc005; DNMT1 MISSION shRNA Bacterial Glycerol Stock DNA (cytosine-5-)-methyltransferase 1—TRCN0000225698, catalog no. SHCLNG-NM_010066). A PCR followed by agarose gel fragment purification was used to amplify and isolate the U6-shRNA cassette. The cassette was then cloned within an AD5/3 D24 backbone plasmid in the gp19k+7.1 region through Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs, catalog no. E2611S) as previously described.22

Cell viability assay

A total of 10,000 cells were plated in a 96-well plate overnight and subsequently infected with 10-fold increasing Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) dilutions of OVs. MTS (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)−2-(4-sulfophenyl)−2H-tetrazolium) determined cell viability according to the manufacturer’s protocol (CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay, Promega, catalog no. G3580). Spectrophotometric data were acquired with Varioskan LUX Multimode Reader (Thermo Scientific) operated by SkanIt software.

Animal experiments

All the procedures were carried out at the F-Talo facility, University of Helsinki, Helsinki, Finland. All the mice followed a 7-day acclimatization period on delivery to the facility. For experiments involving tumor-bearing mice, 200,000 4T1/4T1 Rab27a KO or 500,000 CT26 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flank in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Every treatment group included five to seven mice for statistical group comparison. For all the experiments used in this study, OAd.shDNMT1 or OAd.shGFP (5×109 IFU) were given intratumorally (four times, on days 0, 2, 4, 6), 0.5 mg/kg decitabine (DAC) (Selleck, catalog no. NSC 127716) was given intraperitoneally (four times, on days 0, 2, 4, 6) and PBS was given intratumorally (four times, on days 0, 2, 4, 6) according to the treatment schedule indicated in the relative figure legend. Checkpoint inhibitor anti-PD-1 was given intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 µg/mouse (Bio X Cell, catalog no. BE0146) (four times, on days 1, 3, 5, 7). In the sEV rescue experiment, 4T1 Rab27a KO tumor-bearing mice received four intratumoral injections of 1 × 10⁹ sEV particles (quantified by nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA) on alternating days. Every other day, mice received four anti-PD-1 antibody treatments. Tumor growth was measured every second day with a caliper and calculated using the following formula: (long side)×(short side)2/2. All injections and tumor measurements were performed under isoflurane anesthesia. Tumors, spleens, and bone marrow were processed into a single-cell suspension and stained for flow cytometry analysis. For tumor re-injection on the left flank, 200,000 CT26 cancer cells were subcutaneously injected on the left flank at day 18 post-treatment (PT). A tumor volume of 30 mm3 was set as a threshold for tumor detection at day 28 PT. Bone marrows and spleens from tumor-free mice were used as controls to check MDSC basal infiltration or for MDSC derivation in vitro. More detailed information about the treatment schedule can be found in the figure legends. Animals that did not develop a tumor on cell implantation were not included in the study. For all the experiments, complete randomization was performed on the day of the treatment, while mice were sacrificed when the human ethical endpoint was met (animal sickness, tumor length exceeding 15 mm size).

MDSCs differentiation

MDSCs were derived from murine bone marrow using the “MDSCs STAR-Protocol” (Choi et al, 2022). 1×106 cells isolated from murine bone marrow were plated in each well of a 24-well plate in 2 mL of MDSC-Derivation Medium, which consists of RPMI high glucose with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% glutamine (Gibco), 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Life Technologies), 100 U/mL penicillin (Life Technologies), 10 ng/mL IL-6 (Merk, cat no. 19646–5 UG), and 20 ng/mL GM-CSF (BioLegend, cat no. 71374). Different concentrations of siRNAs, DAC 0.1 or 1 µM (Selleck, catalog no. NSC 127716), OAd.shDNMT1, OAd.shGFP, or 108 sEVs were added to the MDSC-Derivation Medium across different experiments as indicated in the figure legends. For co-culture experiments (figure 3), 4T1 or 4T1 Rab27a KO cells were infected with 100 MOI of each virus and co-cultured after 24 hours with mouse splenocytes at 1:20 ratio.

Flow cytometry

All flow cytometry samples were run with BD Accuri C6 (BD Bioscience) or BD LSRFortessa Flow Cytometer (BD Bioscience). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software V.10. Animal tissues were passed through a 70 µm cell strainer (Falcon) to obtain a single-cell suspension. 1 million cells were then plated in a 96 V bottom well plate (Sarstedt) and stained with the following antibodies: FITC-CD11b (BioLegend, catalog no. 101205), APC-Ly-6C (BioLegend, catalog no. 128015), PE-Ly-6G (BioLegend, catalog no. 127607), BV711-CD3 (BD, catalog no. 563123), BV510-CD8 (BD, catalog no. 563068).

Extracellular vesicles extraction

sEVs were collected using differential centrifugation of conditioned media. 4T1 cells were treated with either PBS, OAd.shDNMT1 (100 MOI), or OAd.shGFP (100 MOI) and incubated in exosome-depleted media for 24 hours at 37°C. Samples were subjected to 2,000×g (25 min) and 12,000×g (10 min). The sEVs were then pelleted using ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 3 hours and collected in 1×PBS.

Extracellular vesicles labeling and quantification

The sEVs derived from 4T1 cells uninfected or infected with OAd.shDNMT1, OAd.shGFP were stained following the protocol published in Wedge et al.14 Briefly, 108 sEVs from each condition were stained with Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Invitrogen) at a final concentration of 15 µM for 10 min at 37 °C. The sEVs were used to treat differentiated bone marrow cells (BMCs) grown on coverslips and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. Cells were then washed three times with PBS, fixed using 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 5 min, washed with PBS, stained using Hoechst 33342 nucleic acid stain (Invitrogen) for 10 min, and washed with PBS. Coverslips were then mounted on slides and imaged on the EVOS M7000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Unlabeled sEVs were used as controls. The amount of internalized green sEVs by murine BMCs was calculated using the EV analyzer plug-in on FiJi.

RNA extraction

RNA was extracted from total cells or sEVs using RNAzol RT (Sigma, cat no. R4533-50ML) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before RNA extraction, sEVs were treated with RNAse to remove non-sEV-derived RNA.

shRNA detection by PCR

RNA from cells or sEVs was elongated with the Poly(a) Tailing Kit (Thermo Scientific, catalog no. AM1350M). Poly(a) elongated RNA was converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Invitrogen SuperScrip IV First-Strand Synthesis (Thermo Scientific, catalog no. 18091050) using a single strand poly(dT) containing a custom unique sequence (CUS) to obtain an shRNA-poly(dT)-CUS cDNA single strand. PCR products were obtained by using primers annealing to the shRNA and a CUS.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

Particle concentration and size were measured with the ZetaView PMX-120 NTA instrument (Particle Metrix GmbH, Ammersee, Germany) equipped with a Z NTA cell assembly, a blue (488 nm, 40 mW) laser, and a CMOS camera with 640×480 pixel resolution. Samples were diluted in a total volume of 1 mL of particle-free ultra-pure Milli-Q water to obtain 50–200 particles per frame. Videos in NTA mode were recorded at 11 positions across the measurement chamber in 2 s increments at 30 frames per second framerate with camera shutter speed at 100 s-1 and sensitivity at 85. The temperature was controlled at 22°C for NTA. Videos were processed, and outliers (>10% coefficient of variation) were removed using the Grubbs method with the built-in ZetaView software (V.8.05.12 SP2). Particles between 10 and 1,000 nm in diameter with a minimum trace length of 15 frames and a minimum brightness of 20 were included in the analysis. The obtained particle concentrations of the preparations were used for the experimental dosing of the sEVs.

DNA methylation quantification

DNA was extracted from cells using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, catalog no. 69504). The whole DNA methylation (5-methylcytosine (5-mC)) was measured through the Abcam Methylated DNA Quantification Kit (cat no. AB 117128). Absorbance data was acquired with Varioskan LUX Multimode Reader (Thermo Scientific) operated by SkanIt software.

DNMT1 levels

Nuclear proteins from cells were extracted using the Nuclear Extraction Kit (ab113474). Levels of DNMT1 were assessed through the murine DNMT1 Assay Kit (ab113469). Absorbance data was acquired with Varioskan LUX Multimode Reader (Thermo Scientific) operated by SkanIt software.

IFN-γ ELISpot

Interferon (IFN)-γ Enzyme-Linked immunospot (Elispot) assays were performed using commercially available mouse ELISpot reagent sets (ImmunoSpot), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Spleens, collected at the endpoint of the experiment, were reduced to a single-cell solution by passing them through a 70 µm strainer using the back of a syringe plunger. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, red blood cells were lysed using ACK buffer (Gibco, catalog no. A1049201). Splenocytes were then resuspended in Cellular Technology Limited (CTL) test medium (ImmunoSpot) and counted. For each well, 3×105 splenocytes were seeded and were stimulated with 20 ng/µL (2 µg/well in total) of each peptide at 37°C for 72 hours. After 3 days of stimulation, plates were developed, and the spot count was obtained using the CTL ImmunoSpot ELISpot plate reader system (ImmunoSpot). The AH1 peptide (SPSYAYHQ) was purchased from Chempeptide. Whole cell lysate was obtained after three freeze-thaw cycles of CT26 cells. 100 µL of crude lysate was used for each well (1:1, CT26: splenocytes).

IFN-γ ELISA

MDSCs (CD11b+Gr1+) were treated with sEVs or siRNA and magnetically isolated as indicated in figure 3D. Sorted MDSCs were co-cultured with fresh mice splenocytes (0.5×105 MDSCs with 1×105 splenocytes) in RPMI without serum.23 Poly: IC (InvivoGen, catalog vac-pic)-stimulated and unstimulated splenocytes were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. All the groups were cultured in the presence of Dynabeads Mouse T-Activator CD3/CD28 for T-Cell Expansion and Activation (Thermo Scientific, catalog no. 11 456D) to induce basal activation. After 72 hours, IFN-γ levels from the culture medium were detected by ELISA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 88-7314-22).

Magnetic isolation of MDSCs

Murine MDSCs were magnetically isolated through the EasySep Mouse MDSC (CD11b+Gr1+) Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, catalog no. 19867) and immediately used for nuclear protein isolation or co-culture with fresh mice splenocytes.

SiRNA transfection

The DNMT1-targeting siRNAs and controls were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (TriFECTa RNAi Kit, catalog no. 510363041). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Scientific catalog no. 11668030) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1×106 BMCs for each replicate were cultured in MDSC-Derivation Medium in a 6-well plate at day 0. MDSC-Derivation Medium was changed on day 2 and again on day 4 without streptomycin to optimize the following transfection. At day 6, each well received 50 pmol of siRNA in 100 µL of Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 51985034) and incubated before analysis.

Western blot

EVs from different conditions (EV.DNMT1, EV.MOCK, EV.GFP) were collected as described above. EVs were resuspended in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min. Lysed EV samples were centrifuged at 16,000×g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then collected and quantified by Bradford assay. Each 25 µL (30 µg) sample was mixed with 5 µL of 6×Laemmli buffer, while for Hsp70 detection β-mercaptoethanol was added and boiled at 95°C for 5 min to ensure reducing conditions. Samples were loaded onto 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free gels (Bio-Rad) alongside a protein ladder (Precision Plus Protein Kaleidoscope, Bio-Rad, Cat#161–0375). Gels were run at 80 V for 10–15 min, followed by 110 V at 400 mA for~1 hour until the dye front reached the bottom. Gels were activated using the ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad) with 45 s UV exposure. Proteins were transferred to 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore Immobilon) using the Thermo Fisher Pierce G2 Fast Blotter. Membranes were blocked with 1% non-fat dry milk in PBS/Tween (0.1%) for 1 hour at room temperature to prevent non-specific antibody binding. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C. Immunostaining was obtained using anti CD63 (BD Pharmingen catalog. no #564221), CD81 (Thermo Scientific, catalog. no MA5-32333), Hsp70 (BD Pharmingen, catalog. no #610608). Following three 10 min washes in Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (TBST), membranes were incubated for 2 hours at room temperature with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) -conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3000 in TBST) (Goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP, Cat# NA934). Membranes were developed using Clarity Western ECL reagent (Bio-Rad, Cat#170–5061). Chemiluminescent signals were imaged using the ChemiDoc MP System.

EV sample preparation and analysis by transmission electron microscopy

The sample preparation and analysis were performed by HiPREP Core in FIMM Technology Centre, University of Helsinki. EVs were prepared for Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) by loading to carbon-coated and glow-discharged 200 mesh copper grids with pioloform support membrane. EVs were fixed with 2.0% PFA in NaPO4 buffer, stained with 2% neutral uranyl acetate, further stained, and embedded in uranyl acetate and methyl cellulose mixture (1.8/0.4%). EVs were viewed with transmission EM using TEM Hitachi HT7800 operating at 100 kV. Images were taken with Gatan Rio9 bottom mounted CMOS-camera, model 1809 (Gatan, USA) with 3,072×3,072 pixels image size and no binning.

Results

DNMT1 plays a critical role in MDSC differentiation and survival

MDSCs are a heterogeneous myeloid cell population that in murine settings are divided into granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs) (defined as CD11b+, Ly6G+, Ly6Clo) and monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) (defined as CD11b+, Ly6G−, and Ly6Chi).24 In order to understand the effect of HMAs on MDSCs, we isolated and plated murine BMCs from tumor-free mice at day 0, and induced their differentiation to MDSCs in the presence of IL-6 and GM-CSF (added to the cell culture at day 0, 2, and 4).25 The HMA DAC was added at days 0, 2, and 4 at 0.1 and 1 µM, while control cells received dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)(untreated) (figure 1A); 1 µM DAC is considered a clinically relevant dose, linked to impact on DNA methylation.26 27 On the other hand, 0.1 µM DAC is associated with higher immunological effects in vitro.28 At day 6, the total cell viability was reduced in all DAC-treated cells (figure 1B,C). The cell differentiation results were analyzed by flow cytometry. In particular, the untreated cells were represented by 70% of CD11b+ cells, while of this population, the percentages of G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs populations were 42% and 1.1%, respectively (online supplemental figure S1). To compare the total number of cells among the different conditions, G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs were analyzed as a percentage of singlets. G-MDSC levels significantly decreased following DAC treatment, from 7.60%±2.96% in untreated samples to 0.82%±0.25% with DAC 0.1 µM and 1.03%±0.11% with DAC 1 µM (n = 3) (figure 1D,E). On the other hand, the reduced amount of M-MDSCs limited the evaluation of the treatment (online supplemental figure S2A).

To evaluate the treatment efficacy on established MDSCs, we proceeded to test the effect of DAC on MDSCs at a later stage of differentiation (0.1 and 1 µM at days 5 and 6). In these experimental settings, we compared DAC to DNMT1 downregulation by siRNA transfection (siRNA-1 and siRNA-2 both targeting murine DNMT1 at day 6) (figure 1F). Control cells were transfected with an siRNA targeting GFP (siRNA-ctrl) (figure 1G). At day 8, only DAC at 1 µM significantly reduced BMC viability compared with the siRNA control (siRNA-ctrl: 91.66%±1.15%; DAC 1 µM: 82.33%±1.53%; p=0.0099, n=3) (figure 1H). Viability in other groups was as follows: siRNA-1: 85.00%±4.00%; siRNA-2: 89.00%±2.00%; DAC 0.1 µM: 85.33%±1.52%. No significant differences were observed among these conditions.

Despite this observation, both siRNA transfection and DAC treatment negatively affected the total percentage of G-MDSCs (figure 1I) while only DAC treatment resulted in lower M-MDSCs at both concentrations (online supplemental figure S2B). The siRNA transfection efficacy was evaluated by magnetically sorting MDSCs (CD11b+, Gr-1+) at day 8 and tested for the DNMT1 nuclear levels by ELISA, revealing a trend in DNMT1 downregulation efficacy on siRNA-1 (73.02%±0.27%) and siRNA-2 (69.86%±0.96%) compared with DAC 0.1 (81.74%±0.09%) and 1 µM (84.12%±0.26%) (n=3) (figure 1J). These results show that siRNAs targeting DNMT1 can interfere with MDSC persistence in vitro, similarly to DAC. Furthermore, DNMT1-targeting siRNAs can efficiently reduce DNMT1 levels in MDSCs.

OAd.shDNMT1 reduces DNMT1 levels and genome methylation in breast cancer 4T1 cells

Based on our observations, we selected siRNA-2, which produced the highest decrease in DNMT1 expression (figure 1J). We subsequently cloned a gene expression cassette containing a U6 (polymerase-III) promoter and the siRNA-2 sequence (in an shRNA structure) within an oncolytic human AD5/3 D24 backbone (OAd.shDNMT1) as previously described22 (figure 2A). At the same time, a control oncolytic adenovirus (OAd) expressing an shRNA against GFP was generated and implemented for reference (OAd.shGFP). Regarding viral production, OAd.shDNMT1 did not significantly differ from OAd.shGFP in terms of infective units (IFU) (OAd.shDNMT1: 4.05× 10¹⁰ IFU/mL; OAd.shGFP: 2.64× 10¹⁰ IFU/mL; p=0.066; n = 4) (figure 2B). To assess the biological potential of our viruses, different cancer cell lines were seeded on day 0, infected on day 1, and subsequently evaluated through different assays (figure 2C). OAd.shDNMT1 and OAd.shGFP showed similar oncolysis after 96 hours in human breast cancer MDA-MB-436 or murine breast cancer 4T1 and colon cancer CT26 (figure 2D). As expected, murine cancer cell lines did not show sensitivity to human OAds, showing toxicity only at the highest concentration29 and almost no toxicity at 100 MOI. To assess the functionality of OAd.shDNMT1, we also measured the DNMT1 levels in 4T1 cells, 48 hours after infection at 100 MOI. This treatment induced a trend of DNMT1 level reduction comparable to that observed in the 4T1 positive control (siRNA-2 transfection) (figure 2E). Furthermore, the analysis of the total amount of genomic 5-mC on OAd.shDNMT1 treatment of 4T1 cells showed reduced levels of DNMT1 only at 100 MOI (48 hours and 72 hours after infection) (figure 2F). Conversely, OAd.shGFP did not show any significant effect on 5-mC (10, 100 MOI at 48 hours and 72 hours) while DAC drastically reduced whole genome methylation levels at 48 hours and 72 hours compared with untreated cells. For this experiment, 1 µM DAC was chosen as a positive control since a more direct effect on DNA methylation compared with DNMT1 downregulation mediated by siRNA.30 These results suggest that OAd.shDNMT1 can reduce DNMT1 protein levels and subsequently affect the whole genome methylation of 4T1 cells.

OAd.shDNMT1 effect is mediated by sEVs secreted by infected cancer cells

It has been shown that OVs deliver small RNAs to non-infected cells through sEVs.14 This delivery route might enable targeted gene modulation in tumor-supporting cells within the TME. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated whether sEVs isolated from OAd.shDNMT1 (EV.DNMT1), OAd.shGFP (EV.GFP)-infected or uninfected 4T1 (EV.MOCK) could be internalized by differentiated murine BMCs. The analysis by microscopy revealed stained areas around the nuclei of BMCs, suggesting the internalization of CFSE-labeled sEVs (figure 3A) and comparable uptake among the conditions (online supplemental figure S3). Importantly, sEVs were assessed for their identity and purity. Among the experimental conditions, sEVs showed comparable expected sizes (~100 nm) (online supplemental figure S4A) and EV marker expression (Hsp70, CD63 and CD81) (online supplemental figure S4B). Furthermore, electron microscopy revealed typical cup-shaped EV particles of 30–100 nm size in all the conditions, confirming the identity of the isolated sEVs (online supplemental figure S5). Importantly, the presence of the sh.DNMT1 within the isolated sEVs was confirmed by reverse-transcriptase PCR of small RNAs (online supplemental figure S6). Subsequently, EV.DNMT1 or EV.GFP (derived from OAd.shDNMT1 or OAd.shGFP-infected cells, respectively) were used to treat the BMCs in the late stage of differentiation (day 5 and 6) and compared with siRNA-2 transfection (day 6) as a positive control for MDSC reduction (following the protocol in figure 1F). A~12% statistically significant reduction in viability was observed in siRNA-2-treated cells (83.33%±1.52%) compared with all other conditions (Mock: 94.66%±3.05%; EV.DNMT1: 96.66%±1.52%; EV.GFP: 96.00%±1.00%) (figure 3B, left panel; p<0.001; n=3). Additionally, we observed that both EV.DNMT1 and siRNA-2 treatments induced a statistically significant reduction in the total percentage of G-MDSCs compared with EV.GFP (p=0.0089 and p=0.0011, respectively) (Mock, 8.1%±0.63%; siRNA-2, 2.5%±0.23%; EV.DNMT1, 3.3%±0.28%; and EV.GFP, 5.7%±0.58%, n=3) (figure 3B, right panel). A similar trend was observed in M-MDSCs (online supplemental figure S2C). Additionally, the inclusion of OAd.shDNMT1 in the BMC differentiation did not alter MDSC levels (online supplemental figure S7). In an independent experiment, the same treatments induced the reduction of both nuclear DNMT1 levels (48 hours after treatment) (figure 3C, left panel) and whole genomic 5-mC methylation in magnetically sorted MDSCs (72 hours after treatment) (figure 3C, right panel). However, in this and previous experiments, EV.DNMT1 or siRNA-2 treatment did not eradicate MDSCs. We then evaluated the suppressive ability of these cells on treatment. MDSCs exposed to sEVs or siRNA were magnetically sorted and co-cultured with mouse splenocytes (figure 3D). Poly: IC-stimulated splenocytes (stimulated) and unstimulated splenocytes were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. All groups were cultured in the presence of anti-CD3/CD28 beads to induce T-cell minimal activation. After 72 hours, IFN-γ levels from the culture medium were detected by ELISA, revealing higher IFN-γ release in the EV.DNMT1 and siRNA-2 co-cultures compared with their controls (EV.GFP and siRNA-ctrl, respectively) (figure 3E). These results show that sEVs carrying shRNA against DNMT1 are effective in reducing DNMT1 protein levels and the immunosuppressive ability of MDSCs.

To further investigate our approach, we tested whether OAd.shDNMT1 could affect the MDSC differentiation in vitro. Several cancer cell lines, including 4T1, produce and secrete GM-CSF and IL-6, and other components that are required for MDSCs differentiation.4 Therefore, we hypothesized that OAd.shDNMT1-infected 4T1 cells might impair (via sEVs) MDSCs induction when co-cultured with murine BMCs. Additionally, to further address the importance of sEVs in the activity of OAd.shDNMT1 we included a 4T1 Rab27a KO cell line14 in our experiments. Rab27a is a protein that is required for the formation of different classes of vesicles, and its depletion abolishes the secretion of sEVs, as confirmed by NTA (figure 3F). Also, the toxicity on OV infection of 4T1 Rab27a KO (figure 3G) revealed a similar profile to infected 4T1 cells (figure 2D). Subsequently, we compared the basal ability of 4T1 and 4T1 Rab27a KO in inducing MDSCs in culture. 4T1 or 4T1 Rab27a KO were co-cultured at day 0 with murine BMCs at a 1:20 ratio without cytokines for 6 days. The differentiation of murine BMCs as reported in figure 1F (GM-CSF+IL-6) was used as a control. As a result, co-culture with either 4T1 (57.60%±2.68%) or 4T1 Rab27a KO (47.53%±2.73%) cells led to a similar expansion of G-MDSCs compared with the classical GM-CSF+IL-6 protocol (44.90%±3.25%) (4T1 vs GM-CSF+IL-6: p=0.071; 4T1 Rab27a KO vs GM-CSF+IL-6: p=0.99; n = 3) (figure 3H). Once the MDSC induction levels of the two cell lines were determined, we repeated the same experiment with pre-infected cancer cells using OAd.shDNMT1 or OAd.shGFP. In the absence of viral infection, 4T1 cells induced a significantly higher expansion of G-MDSCs compared with 4T1 Rab27a KO cells (p<0.0001) (figure 3I). On infection with OAd.shDNMT1, the number of G-MDSCs was significantly reduced in the 4T1 co-culture (p<0.0001). Conversely, infection with OAd.shGFP resulted in a higher G-MDSC percentage in the 4T1 co-culture compared with the 4T1 Rab27a KO co-culture (p=0.0001), mirroring the trend observed in the uninfected condition (n=3) (figure 3I). These experiments suggest that sEVs are a necessary component for the effect of OAd.shDNMT1 in reducing the levels of MDSC in vitro.

OAd.shDNMT1 reduces G-MDSCs infiltration in the tumor, bone marrow, and spleen in 4T1 but not in Rab27a KO mice

To further examine the role of sEVs in the OAd.shDNMT1 mechanism of action, we assessed the virus effect on MDSC levels in syngeneic tumor models. Balb/c mice were implanted with 4T1 or 4T1 Rab27a KO cells subcutaneously. To ensure the possibility of analyzing murine tissues at later time points (given the fast progression of 4T1 tumors), mice were treated when their tumor reached approximately 30 mm3 (the first day of the treatment was defined as day 0). Mice were treated intratumorally with OAd.shDNMT1 or PBS at days 0, 2, 4, and 6 PT and sacrificed at days 8, 15, and 21 PT to assess the G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs presence in the tumor by flow cytometry (figure 4A). Untreated mice at each time point were used as controls (Mock). Given that the 4T1 systemically induces MDSC accumulation, we also evaluated their levels in the bone marrow and spleen9 31 (online supplemental figure S8). In the same tissues, the baseline levels of MDSCs were assessed in tumor-free mice as a control (dotted line). On treatment with OAd.shDNMT1, the levels of G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs in the 4T1 tumor infiltrate showed a slight reduction at the final time point; however, this trend was not observed in the 4T1 Rab27a KO model (figure 4B). Throughout this study, bone marrow MDSC levels remained consistent until day 21, on which viral treatment resulted in a reduction of G-MDSC only in the 4T1 model (no significant effect was observed on the M-MDSC population). Conversely, no treatment effect was observed in the 4T1 Rab27a KO model for all MDSC populations (figure 4C). In the spleen, a more notable reduction of G-MDSC levels was identified from day 8, which remained stable throughout the study in 4T1 but not in 4T1 Rab27a KO (figure 4D). Similarly, in the 4T1 model, M-MDSC levels appeared reduced from days 8 to 15, though this difference was not maintained by day 21. All the 4T1 Rab27a KO-bearing mice maintained similar levels of both G-MDSC and M-MDSCs regardless of the treatment (figure 4D). Furthermore, the levels of G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs in both the bone marrow and spleen of Mock 4T1 Rab27a KO were slightly reduced compared with 4T1-bearing mice in the last time points, in alignment with the results obtained in figure 3I. These findings suggest that cells from 4T1-bearing mice are more sensitive to intratumoral treatment with OAd.shDNMT1 compared with 4T1 Rab27a KO cells, particularly at later time points, in terms of MDSC accumulation in the tumor, bone marrow, and spleen.

OAd.shDNMT1 has a more pronounced effect on tumor growth in the more immunogenic CT26 mouse model compared with the highly immunosuppressive 4T1

To assess the relevance of our model, we evaluated the intratumoral reduction of MDSCs following OAd.shDNMT1 treatment in more challenging tumors, using DAC as a positive control32 and OAd.shGFP as a negative reference. Mice were subcutaneously implanted with 200,000 4T1 cells in the right flank and treated when their tumor reached approximately 70 mm3 (the first day of the treatment was defined as day 0) (figure 5A). Mice were divided into four treatment groups and received four intraperitoneally injections of DAC (0.5 mg/kg) or four intratumoral administrations of OAd.shDNMT1, OAd.shGFP, or PBS (Mock). On the day of sacrifice, tumor tissue was collected, and MDSC infiltration was evaluated. In these settings, both OAd.shDNMT1 and DAC reduced intratumoral G-MDSCs, but not M-MDSCs, relative to the Mock mice (figure 5B). Tumor growth analysis showed a slight reduction (however statistically significant) in volume for OAd.shDNMT1-treated mice compared with the Mock. However, no significant differences were observed between DAC, OAd.shGFP, and PBS (Mock), indicating that these treatments did not markedly impact tumor growth in this model (figure 5C) (online supplemental figure S9). Flow cytometry analysis (n=5 biological replicates) was performed to assess CD3+ lymphocyte infiltration across all treatment groups in 4T1 tumors (figure 5D,E). The percentages of CD3+ cells were as follows: Mock, 0.21%±0.05%; OAd.shDNMT1, 0.43%±0.09%; OAd.shGFP, 0.65%±0.29%; and DAC, 0.41%±0.21%. Neither OAd.shDNMT1 (p=0.290) nor DAC (p=0.358) significantly enhanced T-cell recruitment compared with Mock. However, OAd.shGFP treatment led to an increase in CD3+ lymphocyte infiltration compared with Mock (p=0.011). The TME of 4T1 is highly immunosuppressive33 and interferes with T-cell recruitment, resulting in limited T-cell recognition and elimination of tumor cells. Given the low lymphocyte infiltration within the 4T1 tumors, we tested our hypothesis in a more favorable model, using murine CT26 colon cancer cells,34 nonetheless associated with high levels of MDSCs21 (figure 5F). In this case, both OAd.shDNMT1 and DAC reduced G-MDSC and M-MDSC levels in the tumor (figure 5G). Regarding tumor growth, all treatments led to a statistically significant reduction in volume compared with the Mock group. Additionally, OAd.shDNMT1 showed a significant difference compared with OAd.shGFP or DAC (figure 5H) (online supplemental figure S9). Compared with the 4T1 model, baseline CD3+ T-cell infiltration in Mock-treated CT26 tumors was approximately three times higher (0.78%±0.35%). In this model, treatment with OAd.shDNMT1 (4.30%±1.40%), OAd.shGFP (3.05%±0.61%), and DAC (2.26%±0.93%) further increased intratumoral CD3+ T-cell levels (figure 5I,J, left panel; n=5 biological replicates). In addition, intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration was significantly higher in all virally treated groups compared with Mock and DAC (Mock: 0.32%±0.23%; OAd.shDNMT1: 1.20%±0.15%; OAd.shGFP: 1.07%±0.31%; DAC: 0.61%±0.24%) (n=5 biological replicates). These data confirm that the higher immunogenicity of the CT26 model correlates with a greater treatment susceptibility. In particular, the presence of intratumoral lymphocytes, together with a reduction of G-MDSCs and tumor volume, is highly enhanced in the presence of OAd.shDNMT1 in the CT26 model, while less pronounced in the 4T1.

OAd.shDNMT1 synergizes with anti-PD1 treatment in vivo

To enhance the antitumoral effectiveness of T cells, we repeated our experiment using the 4T1 or 4T1 Rab27a KO tumor models in conjunction with anti-PD-1 (αPD-1). The same experimental schedule was followed, with αPD-1 administered every other day for a total of four treatments (figure 6A). Among all treatment groups, OAd.shDNMT1 was the only condition that significantly reduced intratumoral G-MDSC levels (p=0.014) (Mock: 4.56%±1.66%; OAd.shDNMT1: 1.87%±0.75%; OAd.shGFP: 3.71%±1.26%; DAC: 4.54%±0.99%; n=5) (figure 6B). While not statistically significant, a trend toward reduced M-MDSC infiltration was also observed in the OAd.shDNMT1-treated group compared with DAC (p=0.055) (Mock: 0.55%±0.41%; OAd.shDNMT1: 0.17%±0.05%; OAd.shGFP: 0.60%±0.26%; DAC: 0.65%±0.20%). These results were accompanied by a statistically significant decrease in tumor growth in OAd.shDNMT1-treated mice compared with the average tumor volume of other groups (figure 6C, online supplemental figure S10). The addition of αPD-1 increased the baseline infiltration of lymphocytes and CD8+T cells in tumors across all αPD-1-treated groups (figure 6D,E). However, in the 4T1 Rab27a KO model, αPD-1 failed to enhance the effects of OAd.shDNMT1 on tumor growth (figure 6F, online supplemental figure S10) or intratumoral MDSC infiltration (figure 6G), despite the presence of intratumoral CD3+ and CD8+ T cells (figure 6H). Considering this outcome, we hypothesized that directly providing EV.DNMT1 within 4T1 Rab27a KO tumors would overcome the inability of these cells to produce and deliver sEVs to the TME. To this aim, Rab27a-KO tumor-bearing mice received four intratumoral administrations (109 sEVs for each injection) of EV.DNMT1, EV.GFP, EV.MOCK or PBS (MOCK) (online supplemental figure S11A). All the groups also received four administrations of anti-PD-1, since the positive impact of ICIs in the previous model. We observed a general reduction in the tumor volume of mice treated with EV.DNMT1 at day 17 PT (online supplemental figure S11B–D). Furthermore, this treatment was also associated with reduced intratumoral levels of G-MDSCs and increased CD8 T-cell infiltration (online supplemental figure S11E). To further explore this approach, we repeated the virus administration in combination with αPD-1 in the CT26 mouse model following the same schedule (figure 6A). Notably, the combination of OAd.shDNMT1 and αPD-1 led to complete tumor eradication by day 11 PT, keeping the mice tumor-free until the endpoint (day 20 PT) (figure 6I, online supplemental figure S10). In contrast, αPD-1 did not significantly improve outcomes in other groups treated with OAd.shGFP, DAC, or alone. In an independent survival experiment with CT26-bearing mice, 100% of OAd.shDNMT1-treated mice were still alive at day 45 PT, whereas only one DAC-treated mouse survived, and no mice from the Mock or OAd.shGFP groups remained (figure 6J). To evaluate the antitumor immunological activation, we conducted an ELISpot analysis on murine splenocytes using the gp70-derived epitope AH135 and CT26-derived whole cell lysate 20 days post-intervention. While AH1 stimulation did not provide conclusive results, whole-cell lysate in the OAd.shDNMT1 group resulted in higher IFN-γ release compared with OAd.shGFP and DAC-treated groups (figure 6K). In another independent experiment, CT26-injected mice treated according to the protocol in figure 6A were rechallenged with a tumor injection of 200,000 CT26 cancer cells on the left flank at day 18 PT. A tumor volume of 30 mm3 was set as the detection threshold. Remarkably, after 10 days, 0% of OAd.shDNMT1-treated mice exhibited tumor growth, potentially suggesting the presence of systemic immunity, which was not observed in the other groups (DAC, 60%; OAd.shGFP, 80%; Mock, 80%) (figure 6L). These findings highlight the potential of OAd.shDNMT1 to enhance antitumor immunity when combined with αPD-1 therapy. However, this combination was less effective in a model lacking functional sEVs.

Discussion

One of the significant challenges in treating highly aggressive tumors such as TNBC is the profound immunosuppressive effects of MDSCs within the TME. The elevated presence of these cells correlates with an increasingly poor prognosis and reduced responsiveness to chemotherapy and immunotherapy.36 37 Current approaches in targeting MDSCs in clinical trials are based on promoting MDSC differentiation, focusing on survival pathways such as the JAK-STAT3 cascade, or anti-MDSCs adoptive cell therapy.38 Among other potential approaches, several preclinical studies showed the efficacy of HMAs in reducing the tumorous and systemic infiltration of MDSCs,10 correlating with the amelioration of anticancer immune responses in the presence of ICI. In this study, we developed a novel therapeutic approach using an engineered OAd that reshapes the immune suppressive profile of the TME via sEVs. In particular, our approach exploited cancer cells for the production and spread of loaded sEVs to remodel cancer-TME communication and suppress the MDSCs via RNA interference (RNAi) of DNMT1 (figure 1). In our in vitro experiments, sEVs obtained from OAd.shDNMT1-infected cancer cells not only reduced MDSC levels similarly to DAC, which is a well-established HMA treatment, but also diminished their suppressive capabilities toward T cells (figure 3). On the other hand, when directly treated with OAd.shDNMT1, we did not observe any significant differences in G-MDSC frequency between the virally infected conditions and the Mock control, suggesting that viral transduction is likely suboptimal in murine MDSCs, possibly due to the presence of innate antiviral sensing and clearance mechanisms. To our knowledge, no published studies report efficient transduction of murine myeloid cells or MDSCs using human OAds, including chimeric Ad5/3 vectors. We think that the development of vectors (eg, viruses, EVs) capable of efficiently targeting immunosuppressive myeloid populations by RNAi, such as MDSCs, could significantly broaden the therapeutic potential of cancer immunotherapy and improve combination strategies. However, when applying RNAi for gene regulation, other aspects should be taken into consideration. First, our study did not evaluate the direct effect of shDNMT1-delivering sEVs on cancer cells or other immune players within the TME apart from MDSCs. Second, we observed that the transient downregulation of DNMT1 in cancer cells (figure 2) can remodel the whole genome methylation and gene expression, potentially affecting how cancer cells interact with their environment.16 Single-cell RNA sequencing and epigenomic studies would be required to obtain preliminary hints on the complex and still unraveled relationship that exists between cancer, immune cells, and epigenetic regulation, especially in the presence of epigenetic remodeling agents such as HMAs.

In vivo, systemic administration of DAC in tumor-bearing mice has been associated with a reduction of MDSCs across multiple systemic compartments, including the bone marrow, blood, spleen, and lungs.9 In our study (figure 4), we observed a similar systemic reduction of MDSCs in the spleen and bone marrow of OAd.shDNMT1-treated 4T1 mice following intratumoral administration. One possibility is that the reduction of MDSCs within the tumor itself leads to a broader systemic effect, as tumors actively sustain MDSC accumulation in peripheral organs. Alternatively, shDNMT1-containing sEVs released from the tumor could disseminate systemically, modulating immune cell populations at distal sites. While our findings suggest that intratumoral OAd.shDNMT1 treatment can influence MDSC levels beyond the primary tumor, the extent to which this effect is mediated by sEVs versus an indirect tumor-driven mechanism remains to be fully elucidated.

In addition, one possible explanation for the reduction of MDSCs observed in vitro (figure 3) and in vivo (figure 4) following OAd.shDNMT1 treatment is that the virus may alter cytokine production and secretion in 4T1 cells. However, our data show that, unlike 4T1 cells, OAd.shDNMT1-infected 4T1 Rab27a KO cells retain the same ability to induce MDSC accumulation as their uninfected counterparts, supporting the hypothesis of the role of functional sEVs in this model. Interestingly, sEVs might also have an important role in inducing MDSCs, since Rab27a KO cells showed reduced capacity to induce the accumulation of MDSCs in vitro (figure 3) and in vivo (figure 4) compared with 4T1 cells in the absence of viral infection. Translated in vivo, the ability to remodel the cancer-surrounding cells through therapeutic intervention might promote a more favorable immune landscape. Importantly, as previously reported in vivo and clinical data,39 the presence of T cells within the TME is crucial for a robust immunotherapy response; in the 4T1 model (figure 5), despite the reduction of MDSCs, the OAd.shDNMT1 and DAC groups showed a limited T-cell infiltration when compared with the Mock group. On the other hand, in the more immunogenic CT26 model, the combination of reduced intratumoral MDSCs (OAd.shDNMT1 and DAC) with a higher lymphocyte presence (figure 5) generated a better tumor control in all the treatments vs Mock, an effect which was more pronounced in the OAd.shDNMT1 group. The results from our study suggest that the incorporation of sEVs-mediated remodeling approaches into ICI-based regimens can significantly improve their therapeutic success in preclinical models of TNBC and colon carcinoma (figure 6). In the TNBC-4T1 model, the use of ICI correlated with better intratumoral lymphocyte infiltration (in all the groups) and with increased control of the tumor growth in the OAd.shDNMT1 group exclusively in the 4T1 model (figure 6). In this and previous studies,9 40 we observed that the systemic administration of DAC can affect the intratumoral accumulation of MDSCs; however, for the first time, we observed that the presence of anti-PD-1 interfered with this effect (figure 6B). Further analysis of treatment schedules and drug doses is required to elucidate the result that the combinatorial therapy can produce. In the CT26 model, our findings indicate that the addition of anti-PD-1 did not result in amelioration from the tumor control point of view in Mock, OAd.shGFP, and DAC groups. However, it induced complete tumor regression in mice treated with OAd.shDNMT1. Furthermore, this approach not only facilitated complete tumor clearance but also established a state of lasting immunity against tumor rechallenge. Potentially, given the same ability to reduce MDSC cells at the tumor level, the higher intratumoral infiltration associated with the viral administration (as shown in figure 5 in the absence of ICI) could explain the superior effect of OAd.shDNMT1 versus DAC. Although we did not observe the full response in our 4T1 model (figure 6), in human settings where the oncolysis is maintained, the synergistic interaction between OVs, ICI, and remodeling of the TME via sEVs could be further enhanced against TNBC. Also, the potential epigenetic remodeling of cancer cells might be exploited for the detection of tumor-associated and cryptic antigens to be included in cancer vaccines. However, it is important to keep in mind that the variability in responses observed between the 4T1 and CT26 murine models highlights the inherent complexities of tumor-immune interactions within the same species. Moreover, the reliance on murine models requires cautious interpretation when translating these results to human clinical contexts, given that human TNBC or colorectal carcinoma may exhibit unique immunological features and microenvironmental interactions.

In summary, the application of OVs engineered to deliver shRNA targeting DNMT1 via sEVs provides a promising therapeutic approach to enhance antitumor immunity. The ability of EVs to transfer genetic material to recipient cells enhances intercellular communication and offers a non-invasive route for targeted therapy. This approach not only underscores OVs’ versatility but also positions EVs as potential carriers for gene therapies, serving to amplify the therapeutic reach and efficacy. The observed synergy with immune checkpoint blockade therapies further describes the potential for combinatorial treatments in overcoming immunosuppression of the TME.
