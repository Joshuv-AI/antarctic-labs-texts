The association between weight-adjusted waist circumference index and risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality: a retrospective analysis based on the NHANES database
Authors: Wentai Yang, Maoping Wang, Xian Huang, Guoxin He, Tingting Qi, Qing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNowadays, obesity has become a public health issue. Weight-adjusted waist circumference index (WWI) is gaining attention as a new obesity indicator. Evidence regarding the association between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among the general adult population in the United States remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 3,081 U.S. adults from the 1999–2018 NHANES database. Weighted Cox regression, trend chi-square test, Kaplan-Meier analysis, and comprehensive predictive performance assessments were used to explore the association between WWI and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality, and validate WWI’s predictive value versus BMI/waist circumference/body weight. Subgroup and interaction analyses were also performed.ResultsWWI was positively associated with all-cause (Model 3 HR = 1.944, p < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (Model 3 HR = 1.854, p = 0.013), with a significant linear upward trend in mortality risks with increasing WWI quartiles (all p < 0.001). WWI had higher AUC than traditional indices, with significant DeLong test differences, stable long-term predictive performance, and superior net benefit in clinically relevant DCA threshold ranges.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a significant association between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among the general adult population in the United States, Our findings suggest that older people, women, low-income and low-educated people, smokers have higher WWI, and people with high WWI also have higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, Integrating WWI into clinical risk assessment may help identify high-risk populations and guide targeted interventions to reduce mortality risk.Keywords: Weight-adjusted waist circumference index, cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality, body mass index

Introduction

Obesity as a global public health issue has received increasing attention, and its assessment indicators are associated with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. Traditionally, body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) have been used to assess obesity risk, but these indicators have limitations in distinguishing muscle mass from fat distribution. For example, BMI is difficult to accurately reflect abdominal fat accumulation; while waist circumference can indicate abdominal obesity, it fails to account for the effects of weight changes [1,2]. In recent years, a novel obesity indicator—weight-adjusted waist index (WWI)—has been proposed, calculated as waist circumference divided by the square root of body weight. By dynamically adjusting the weight factor, WWI more accurately reflects changes in body composition and thus provides more sensitive prediction of obesity risk, particularly superior to BMI and WC in assessing muscle and fat mass [3,4].

Numerous prospective cohort studies have confirmed a significant association between WWI and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. In the U.S. population, studies based on National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data indicate that elevated WWI independently predicts increased risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality [3,5]. Studies in southern China and urban Chinese communities similarly show elevated WWI correlates with increased all-cause mortality risk in elderly populations, with this indicator demonstrating superior predictive value for long-term mortality [1,4]. Similarly, among patients with cardiovascular disease, stroke survivors, and individuals with diabetes or prediabetes, WWI positively correlates with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality risk, highlighting its utility in chronic disease management [6–8]. Notably, compared to other obesity measures such as BMI, WWI demonstrates superior discriminatory power in distinguishing mortality risk [1,9].

Although existing evidence highlights the predictive value of WWI, most studies have focused on specific populations (such as Asian or specific regions in the United States) or specific disease subgroups (such as patients with cardiovascular disease). This limitation means that there is still a lack of consistent evidence regarding whether the relevant findings can be generalized to broader adult populations. Some studies indicate that the associations in non-Asian populations or general populations still require further exploration, particularly the comparative mechanisms between waist-to-hip ratio and traditional indicators such as WC or BMI remain incompletely elucidated [4,5,10]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the associations between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in adults through systematic analysis of large-scale cohort data, to fill existing research gaps and provide more comprehensive epidemiological evidence.

Materials and methods

Data sources

All data used in this study were obtained from the NHANES database, which is publicly accessible through its official website. Detailed descriptions of the survey methodology can be found at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/. This survey was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Study population

Between 1999 and 2018, we initially included 101,316 participants. We first excluded individuals under 20 years of age (n = 46,235), excluded participants who were pregnant or had unknown pregnancy status with missing relevant data (n = 23,194), excluded participants with missing weight or waist circumference data (n = 2,783), excluded participants with missing covariate data (n = 26,019), and excluded participants with missing survival data (n = 4), ultimately including 3,081 participants in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Flowchart of the study design.

Weight adjustment waist circumference index calculation method and outcome variables

WWI is calculated by dividing waist circumference (centimeters) by the square root of body weight (kilograms) [11,12]. This index is derived from the routine physical examination data section of the NHANES database. To ensure data accuracy, anthropometric measurements were performed by trained medical professionals and certified recorders using standardized height and weight measurement equipment and calibrated rulers. During weight measurement, participants were required to wear standardized examination clothing, stand barefoot on an electronic scale with arms naturally hanging at their sides. Waist circumference was measured using a soft tape placed horizontally at the waist, specifically at the level of the iliac crest, along the midline extending from the axilla [13]. The primary outcome parameters assessed in this study were all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. Survival data were obtained from the NHANES publicly linked mortality file (as of December 31, 2019), which was cross-referenced with the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and the National Death Index (NDI) using a probabilistic matching algorithm. Participants with recorded deaths in the National Death Index, regardless of cause of death, were included in the all-cause mortality analysis. Individuals with no death records by December 31, 2019 were classified as survivors. Conversely, for deceased participants, death was attributed to CVD if the cause of death was classified according to International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes I00 to I09, I11, I13, I20 to I51, or I60 to I69 [14].

Covariate collection

This study investigated basic demographic variables, including age, gender (male/female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, etc.), education level (≤high school, high school, >high school), and poverty income ratio (PIR). Additionally, lifestyle variables were assessed, including alcohol consumption and smoking behavior. Participants were categorized as drinkers or non-drinkers based on reported alcohol consumption status. Smoking status was divided into two groups: non-smokers and smokers. Diabetes is defined as: self-reported, physician-diagnosed diabetes. Hypertension is defined as: self-reported hypertension or hypertension diagnosed by a physician. Physical examination variables included BMI (kg/m2, calculated as weight divided by height squared), waist circumference (cm), and weight (kg). Laboratory variables included urinary albumin (mg/L), urinary creatinine (mg/dL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), serum creatinine (mg/dL), serum uric acid (μmol/L), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mmol/L), triglycerides (TG, mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, mmol/L), and other related indicators. For a detailed overview of the measurement protocols for each variable, please refer to www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Statistical analysis

Given the complex sampling framework of NHANES data, this study strictly adhered to the standardized procedures established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). By integrating sample weights and cluster stratification factors, the completeness and reliability of the research results were ensured. The analysis employed a composite weighting strategy: sample weights from the 1999–2000 and 2001–2002 cycles were incorporated into the four-year weight at a ratio of 2/10, while two-year weights from the 2003–2018 cycles were incorporated at a ratio of 1/10. When conducting statistical descriptions and selecting analytical methods for continuous variables, normality testing was first performed to confirm data distribution characteristics. Data conforming to normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) and analyzed using parametric test methods, including analysis of variance (ANOVA). Conversely, for data not conforming to normal distribution, median and interquartile range (Median and IQR) were used for description, and non-parametric test methods such as the Kruskal-Wallis test were applied. For categorical variables involving complex sampling designs with stratification and clustering, the Rao & Scott second-order corrected chi-square test was used for comparative analysis.

For ordered categorical variables (WWI quartiles) and binary outcome variables (CVD mortality, all-cause mortality status), a trend chi-square test was performed to assess the linear trend of outcome risks with increasing WWI levels. The Z value and corresponding two-tailed P value were reported to quantify the statistical significance of the trend, with p < 0.05 considered indicative of a significant linear trend.

This study first converted WWI from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (quartiles), dividing the included data into four groups: Q1 (8.51–10.43), Q2 (10.43–10.96), Q3 (10.97–11.49), and Q4 (11.49–13.62). In both cases of converting WWI into a continuous variable and a categorical variable, we employed weighted Cox regression models to investigate the association between WWI and all-cause mortality as well as cardiovascular mortality. To control for the effects of covariates, we constructed three models: Model1was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, and PIR; Model3 adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, and PIR, Drinking status, Smoking status, Urinary albumin, Urinary creatinine, ALT, AST, Serum uricacid, Serum creatinine, TC, HDL, TG, LDL, Diabetes, Hypertension.

To validate the strength of the association between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, as well as the robustness of the study findings, We conducted subgroup analyses while performing interaction tests by applying the Cox regression model.

In the current study, we employed the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method to determine survival probabilities, while utilizing the nonparametric log-rank test to evaluate survival functions and identify intergroup differences. Data modeled through this approach were graphically presented as KM curves. To investigate the nonlinear association between the continuous variable WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality risk, we implemented restricted cubic spline (RCS) techniques.

The predictive ability of WWI in forecasting cardiovascular and all-cause mortality was evaluated using a comprehensive set of analytical methods, including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, quantitative area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence interval (CI) analysis, DeLong test for ROC curve comparison, time-dependent ROC curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) against body weight (WT), waist circumference (WC), and body mass index (BMI). Traditional ROC curves combined with quantitative AUC and 95% CI calculation were used to assess overall predictive accuracy; the DeLong test was applied to formally verify the statistical significance of AUC differences between WWI and traditional anthropometric indices. Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the dynamic predictive stability over follow-up time (50–250 days), reflecting the consistency of performance in long-term risk monitoring. DCA was further conducted to quantify the clinical utility of each model by calculating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities (0–0.75), which directly reflects the practical value of the model in guiding clinical decision-making. The AUC served as an indicator of comprehensive statistical performance, with elevated values denoting enhanced predictive accuracy. All analytical techniques were implemented using R software (version 4.1.1), and a two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of subjects

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of all participants stratified by WWI quartiles. This study included 3,081 participants, representing 12,852,104 U.S. adults. The table shows significant differences across WWI quartile groups (p < 0.05) in age, gender, race, PIR, education level, smoking status, BMI, waist circumference, weight, urinary albumin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), serum uric acid, serum creatinine, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (Chol), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), diabetes, hypertension, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality. However, alcohol intake and urinary creatinine levels showed no significant differences across WWI quartile groups (p > 0.05). Furthermore, 87.15% of participants were ≤65 years old, with the proportion of those ≤65 years gradually decreasing and those >65 years gradually increasing as WWI increased. Among participants, 27.37% were female and 72.63% were male, with the male proportion gradually decreasing and female proportion correspondingly increasing across WWI quartiles. Non-Hispanic whites comprised the largest racial group at 60.19%. The proportions of non-Hispanic whites, Mexican Americans, and other Hispanic populations gradually increased with higher WWI quartiles, while other racial groups gradually decreased. As WWI quartiles increased, the proportions of high-income individuals, those with high school or higher education, and non-smokers showed declining trends. Participants with higher WWI were more likely to belong to low-income groups, have lower education, and engage in smoking behavior. Additionally, participants with higher WWI values had higher rates of hypertension, diabetes, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality. We found that as WWI values increased, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (Chol), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels showed upward trends, while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels declined with increasing WWI values.

The relationship between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality

When analyzing WWI as a continuous variable, the Cox regression model demonstrated a positive association between WWI and cardiovascular mortality risk in Model 3 (HR = 1.854, 95% CI [1.139, 3.017], p = 0.013) (Table 2). When analyzing WWI as a categorical variable, WWI similarly showed a positive correlation with cardiovascular mortality risk. Compared to the Q1 group, the HR for the Q4 group in Model 1 was 7.891 (95% CI [3.752, 16.595], p < 0.001), P for trend < 0.001. In Model 2, the adjusted HR for the Q4 group was 3.898 (95% CI [1.521, 9.994], p = 0.005). P for trend = 0.014. In Model 3, the adjusted HR for the Q4 group was 4.256 (95% CI [1.015, 17.846], p = 0.048), P for trend = 0.102.

Trend chi-square test further confirmed a significant linear upward trend of outcome risks with increasing WWI quartiles. These results indicate that the risks of CVD and all-cause mortality increase linearly as WWI levels rise, reinforcing the dose-response relationship between WWI and adverse outcomes.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

To validate the robustness of the study results, we conducted subgroup analyses by gender, age, race, education, PIR, smoking, alcohol consumption, and presence of hypertension and diabetes, and further performed interaction tests combined with blood lipid stratification (TC, HDL, TG, LDL quartiles) (Tables 4, 5, Figure 4). The results showed that WWI had a significant interaction with diabetes in terms of all-cause mortality risk (P for Interaction = 0.004), and this interaction was stable across all blood lipid stratifications, with the hazard ratio (HR) of all-cause mortality increasing gradually with the upgrade of blood lipid stratification in diabetic populations. No significant interaction was observed between WWI and gender (p = 0.431), age (p = 0.168), race (p = 0.090), education level (p = 0.975), PIR (p = 0.464), drinking status (p = 0.912), smoking status (p = 0.144) or hypertension (p = 0.924) for all-cause mortality risk (all p > 0.05). For cardiovascular mortality risk, WWI had significant interaction effects with age (P for Interaction = 0.011) and diabetes (P for Interaction = 0.000), and both interactions remained consistent in all blood lipid stratifications: the association between WWI and cardiovascular mortality risk was more significant in people aged ≤65 years, and diabetic populations had a more obvious risk amplification effect of WWI on cardiovascular mortality with the upgrade of blood lipid stratification. No interaction effects were observed between WWI and gender (p = 0.970), race (p = 0.294), education level (p = 0.666), PIR (p = 0.895), drinking status (p = 0.989), smoking status (p = 0.200) or hypertension (p = 0.282) for cardiovascular mortality risk (all p > 0.05).

Predictive ability of WWI for all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality risk

The predictive performance of WWI was systematically validated from statistical accuracy, long-term stability, and clinical utility perspectives (Tables 6–9, Figures 5–9). The ROC curve (Figure 5), quantitative AUC analysis (Tables 6, 7), and DeLong test for ROC curve comparison (Tables 8, 9) comprehensively confirmed that WWI significantly outperformed WT, WC, and BMI in predicting both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with non-overlapping 95% CIs and formal statistical tests verifying the robustness of this advantage. Specifically, for all-cause mortality: WWI achieved the highest AUC value of 0.6765 (95% CI: 0.6469–0.7061), which was markedly higher than BMI (0.5530, 95% CI: 0.5204–0.5856), WC (0.5610, 95% CI: 0.5300–0.5921), and WT (0.5254, 95% CI: 0.4930–0.5577); DeLong tests showed extremely significant differences between WWI and all comparison metrics (all Z > 4.5, p < 0.0001) (Tables 6, 8). For cardiovascular mortality: WWI also achieved the highest AUC of 0.6628 (95% CI: 0.6164–0.7092), far exceeding BMI (0.5576, 95% CI: 0.5058–0.6095), WC (0.5442, 95% CI: 0.4961–0.5923), and WT (0.5416, 95% CI: 0.4897–0.5934); DeLong tests confirmed significant statistical differences (WWI vs BMI: Z = 2.5740, p = 0.0101; WWI vs WC: Z = 4.9103, p < 0.0001; WWI vs WT: Z = 3.2837, p = 0.0010) (Tables 7, 9).

Time-dependent ROC analysis further demonstrated that the WWI model (M1) maintained the highest and most stable time-dependent AUC across the entire follow-up period (50–250 days) for both mortality outcomes (Figures 6, 7), with smaller fluctuations in the AUC curve compared to traditional metrics (M2–M4), indicating superior long-term predictive consistency.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) validated the clinical utility of these statistical advantages (Figures 8, 9). For all-cause mortality, WWI achieved higher net benefit than BMI, WC, and WT across threshold probabilities of 0 to ∼0.35, consistently outperforming the ‘treat all’ and ‘treat none’ strategies; the net benefit of traditional indicators was comparable but lower, with curves overlapping closely with baseline strategies (Figure 8). For cardiovascular mortality, WWI showed superior net benefit in the clinically relevant low-threshold range of 0 to ∼0.15, where clinical decisions for preventive interventions are most commonly made; traditional indicators failed to provide meaningful net benefit in this range, with curves nearly coinciding with the ‘treat none’ strategy (Figure 9). At thresholds exceeding ∼0.35 (all-cause mortality) and ∼0.15 (cardiovascular mortality), the net benefit of all models approached zero, consistent with the limited clinical value of high-risk thresholds in population-based screening. These findings collectively confirm that WWI has superior, statistically significant, stably reliable, and clinically useful predictive power for mortality risk, which is well-aligned with clinical needs for risk stratification and continuous health monitoring.

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the association between WWI and all-cause mortality as well as cardiovascular mortality. By analyzing the NHANES database, we attempted to validate the effectiveness of WWI as a risk prediction indicator. The results demonstrated a positive correlation between WWI and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. These findings provide a foundation for developing more targeted cardiovascular disease prevention strategies.

Our research found that among populations with higher WWI values, elderly individuals, women, low-income groups, those with lower education levels, and smokers were more prevalent, with higher prevalence rates of hypertension and diabetes. The higher proportion of elderly individuals may be related to central obesity and aging processes reflected by WWI. Our study supports this view, as elderly individuals typically exhibit higher WWI levels, which may be due to age-related muscle loss and increased fat redistribution that elevates metabolic risk. The higher proportion of women may stem from increased visceral fat accumulation after menopause, thereby raising WWI values. The higher prevalence in low-income and low-education groups reveals the role of socioeconomic factors in obesity and health inequality—lower socioeconomic status may lead to elevated WWI values through limiting access to medical resources or promoting adverse dietary habits and other behaviors [15]. The findings of this study emphasize the necessity of implementing health interventions targeting low-income populations. The higher proportion of smokers may be related to behavioral risk factors. Smoking, as a known risk factor for metabolic disorders, may increase WWI by promoting abdominal fat accumulation or inflammatory responses. The finding of higher prevalence rates of hypertension and diabetes is consistent with results from multiple literature studies [15–17]. Individuals with higher WWI scores may face elevated metabolic risk, which is associated with inflammatory pathways or insulin resistance mechanisms.

The innovation of this study lies in the first systematic assessment of the relationship between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in the U.S. population. Compared with previous studies, this research utilized the rich data from the NHANES database, which has a large sample size and representativeness, enabling a more comprehensive reflection of the health status of the U.S. adult population. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between WWI experience and cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, this study further confirmed the validity of WWI experience as a prognostic assessment indicator, particularly its unique role in evaluating the risk of all-cause mortality. This finding provides a new perspective for further exploration of obesity and its associated risk factors, and provides more precise assessment tools for clinical practice.

This study found that WWI is positively correlated with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, a phenomenon that can be explained both at the physiological mechanism level and at the indicator characteristic level. First, WWI is defined as waist circumference divided by the square root of body weight, a formula derived from the weight-adjustment concept, which can more accurately reflect the distribution of visceral fat rather than relying solely on weight or waist circumference data, thereby capturing the core risk factors of metabolic disorder. Visceral fat accumulation directly promotes inflammatory responses, insulin resistance, and lipid abnormalities, which in turn accelerate atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events, ultimately leading to death [18,19]. This study found that the prevalence of diabetes increases with rising WWI, and combined with the interaction between WWI and diabetes in the subgroup analysis, it suggests that high WWI may amplify the metabolic stress mechanisms associated with diabetes. Similarly, the linear relationship of restricted cubic splines further indicates that there is no safe threshold for WWI elevation; any degree of increase in WWI may directly promote the process of death.

The predictive advantage of WWI compared to traditional indicators such as BMI or waist circumference lies in its ‘weight-adjusted’ characteristic. Unlike BMI, which relies solely on the weight-to-height ratio and ignores fat distribution, WWI provides more refined assessment. BMI may lead to classification errors because it cannot distinguish between muscle mass and fat type. Similarly, while waist circumference can measure abdominal fat, it does not account for overall body weight and cannot differentiate between visceral fat surrounding internal organs and subcutaneous fat located beneath the skin. This makes WWI a more comprehensive tool for assessing health risks related to body composition. The ROC curve in this study validated the superiority of WWI over body weight, waist circumference, and BMI, which stems from its integration of central obesity and weight adjustment factors, and its ability to more sensitively reflect changes in body composition—this is particularly critical for elderly populations, as aging-related muscle loss is often accompanied by increased visceral fat, which traditional indicators frequently fail to adequately assess [1,18]. This study effectively controlled for potential confounding factors in Model 3, but the observed trend in cardiovascular mortality did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.102). This result may stem from the smaller sample size of cardiovascular death cases or the presence of residual confounding factors such as subclinical cardiovascular disease [6,19]. However, the robustness of the overall linear relationship still strongly supports using WWI as a core risk assessment tool. Overall, the findings of this study emphasize the unique value of WWI in quantifying obesity-related mortality risk, particularly in identifying high-risk subgroups for diabetes or elderly populations.

WWI, as a novel anthropometric indicator, more accurately reflects central obesity by integrating dynamic changes in weight and waist circumference [20,21]. In predicting cardiometabolic comorbidities, WWI demonstrates significant prospective associations, aiding in the identification of high-risk patient populations [20]. Research has found that elevated WWI levels are associated with an increased risk of depression, and this association is partially mediated by chronic conditions such as hypertension and coronary heart disease [22]. This suggests that clinical care should prioritize enhanced mental health screening and chronic disease management for patients with abnormal WWI levels. However, WWI relies on precise waist circumference measurements, and inconsistencies in operating procedures across different medical institutions may compromise the comparability of results [21]. The risk of coronary heart disease is influenced by a combination of genetic, metabolic, and behavioral factors [23,24]. WWI must be integrated with other indicators (such as TyG) to enhance stratification efficacy [25], but its clinical implementation poses significant challenges. In summary. The application of WWI aids in classifying patients’ coronary artery disease risk and provides guidance for clinical care practices.

The results of this study are highly consistent with existing literature and extend the application scope of WWI in mortality risk research. First, the positive correlation between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in this study is consistent with results from large American cohort studies. Analysis based on NHANES data (2005–2014, n = 26,882) also reported similar correlations between WWI and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, further supporting WWI as an independent predictor, with its mechanism of action potentially related to inflammatory responses triggered by changes in body composition [3]. Similarly, analysis of individuals with diabetes or prediabetes in NHANES (n = 6,551) found that elevated WWI directly increased the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (HR approximately 1.5–2.0), emphasizing the amplifying effect of metabolic disorder status, which is consistent with the subgroup interaction results of this study [8]. This association has also been verified in Asian population studies, with a prospective cohort analysis of southern Chinese populations (n = 12,447) confirming that WWI predicts mortality better than BMI and waist circumference, indicating its universal applicability across different ethnicities [1].

However, this study differs from existing literature in certain aspects. Although previous studies also analyzed the relationship between body mass index and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in American adults, they only analyzed data from 2005 to 2014, did not perform subgroup analyses, and did not compare results with traditional obesity indicators [3]. In contrast, this study encompasses data from 1999 to 2018, with a significantly expanded sample size. Furthermore, we employed multiple statistical methods including restricted cubic splines, subgroup analyses, and ROC curves, which strengthened the argumentative power of our conclusions. Another study on patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease indicated that WWI showed a linear positive correlation with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease mortality [18]. However, that study’s subjects were MASLD patients, whereas this study’s subjects were the general American adult population, which is the main difference. Although there are numerous similar studies with varying study populations, their results all support a positive correlation between body mass index and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease mortality [8,16, 19,26].

Despite the substantial evidence provided by this study, there remain several limitations. First, although the study design was based on a retrospective cohort, only a single baseline measurement of WWI was used rather than continuous follow-up. This approach may introduce errors, such as fluctuations in weight or waist circumference over time, thereby obscuring the effects of dynamic changes in body weight index. Second, although Model 3 adjusted for common variables such as smoking and BMI, it did not account for factors such as depression or dietary habits, which may weaken the strength of causal inference. Furthermore, although endpoint events (including all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality) were matched through the National Death Index, the study did not distinguish between specific types of death (such as myocardial infarction or stroke), which limits the analysis of cardiovascular subtypes. Finally, since the study population consisted of general adults in the United States, it remains unclear whether the results can be generalized to other populations.

Conclusion

These research findings have important implications for clinical practice. This study revealed a significant association between WWI and all-cause mortality as well as cardiovascular mortality, suggesting the potential of WWI as an effective prognostic assessment indicator. Our findings indicate that elderly individuals, women, those with low income and low education levels, and smokers have higher WWI, and those with elevated WWI are accompanied by higher prevalence rates of hypertension and diabetes. These findings emphasize the necessity of implementing health intervention measures in the aforementioned populations. The results suggest that clinicians should incorporate WWI into risk assessment. Particularly for high-risk populations, early identification and intervention of elevated WWI levels can help reduce the incidence of cardiovascular events and mortality risk. Future research should further explore the applicability of WWI across different populations to better guide clinical practice and optimize patient management.
