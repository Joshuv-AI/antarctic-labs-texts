Recovery Trajectories and Predictors of Post-Extubation Dysphagia in Intensive Care Unit Patients Following Orotracheal Intubation: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Limi Shan, Haifang Wang, Weixia Yu, Jianzheng Cai, Yuyu Wang, Yingying Zhang, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-extubation dysphagia (PED) is a prevalent and debilitating complication in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, yet the longitudinal heterogeneity of swallowing recovery remains poorly understood. This study was aimed to characterize distinct recovery trajectories of swallowing function in ICU patients with PED and to identify the clinical predictors associated with each pattern. This longitudinal observational study utilized convenience sampling to enroll ICU patients from a tertiary hospital. Swallowing function was evaluated using the Standard Swallowing Assessment (SSA) at seven time points post-extubation, at 4–6, 24, 48, and 72 h and 7, 14, and 28 days. Latent recovery trajectories were identified using growth mixture modeling (GMM), and independent predictors of group membership were determined by multivariate logistic regression. Of 495 intubated patients, 248 (54.98%) developed PED and were included; 209 completed all follow-up assessments. Three distinct trajectories emerged: Group HS (high level, slow improvement; 10.0%), Group HR (high level, rapid improvement; 22.5%), and Group LE (low level, early recovery; 67.5%). For both Group HR and Group HS, membership was predicted by older age, neurological diagnosis, higher peak inspiratory pressure, longer intubation duration, and early pharyngeal pain. In addition, membership in Group HR was uniquely associated with an APACHE II score of 10–14 and exposure to fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Post-extubation swallowing recovery follows distinct trajectories shaped by physiological, procedural, and disease-related factors. Introduction

Invasive mechanical ventilation is the most common form of technical support in intensive care units (ICUs), and over 96% of ICU patients receive oral orotracheal intubation [1, 2]. This invasive intervention is a straightforward procedure that effectively improves respiratory function in critically ill patients by rapidly establishing an artificial airway. However, it exerts mechanical compression and irritation on the oropharyngeal and laryngeal mucosal tissues, and the resulting trauma can lead to localized tissue edema and neuropraxia, thus impairing the muscles and nerves involved in swallowing [3, 4]. Consequently, some patients exhibit clinical manifestations of swallowing dysfunction, a condition clinically termed intubation-associated dysphagia or post-extubation dysphagia (PED) [5]. This condition is a distinct, intubation-associated phenotype of dysphagia that arises from airway instrumentation and critical illness, as it primarily results from the structural injury caused by the endotracheal tube disrupting the coordinated swallowing mechanism [6]. It is defined by characteristic features such as laryngeal injury, tongue weakness, impaired pharyngeal sensation, choking or coughing during liquid intake, repetitive swallowing, and post-swallow voice changes [7, 8]. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a PED incidence of 36% (95% CI: 29.0%–41%, P < 0.001) [7]. Brodsky et al. [9] found that 49% of 775 ICU patients undergoing orotracheal intubation developed PED. This high incidence imposes a substantial burden on patients, families, and the healthcare system [10, 11]. For example, Hongo et al. [12] found that patients with PED tend to develop aspiration pneumonia (17.2% vs. 5.6%). McIntyre et al. [13] demonstrated that the average length of stay in ICU is significantly longer for patients with PED compared to those without (5 days vs. 3 days, P < 0.001), and PED is associated with higher per capita cost of hospitalization (AUD 42,685 vs. 20,840, P < 0.001). Schefold et al. [14] found that in a prospective observational study that PED is independently associated with 28-day and 90-day mortality rates (P < 0.01). Accordingly, promoting the recovery of swallowing function represents a key target for reducing PED‑related complications and improving patient outcomes.

The swallowing function of PED patients involves a dynamic recovery process [15–17]: in the early post-extubation period, swallowing impairment is primarily driven by reversible factors such as pharyngeal and laryngeal edema, mucosal inflammation, and pain resulting from airway instrumentation, and as these acute changes resolve, swallowing function improve rapidly in many patients but can be delayed or incomplete in some patients (those at high risk for PED). Understanding the trajectory of swallowing recovery is crucial for implementing appropriately timed rehabilitation interventions. Orotracheal intubation can directly injure pharyngeal and laryngeal structures, causing mucosal damage, ulceration, vocal cord paralysis, and other conditions [6]. In the early post-extubation period, patients often experience significant pharyngeal and laryngeal edema and pain [15]. Swallowing function typically recovers once these symptoms subside, but patients possessing underlying neuromuscular dysfunction and sensory impairment may experience a slow functional recovery [16, 18]. Most current studies employ cross-sectional surveys to assess PED at isolated time points. For example, Tang et al. [19], Mukdad et al. [20], Yamada et al. [21], and Ceruti et al. [17] measured the PED incidence at 4–6 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 7 days post-extubation, reporting PED incidence of 22.6%, 40%, 22.1%, and 54.8%, respectively. These cross-sectional studies indicate that prevalence varies across intervals but do not reveal the dynamic longitudinal changes in the swallowing function of individuals. Some longitudinal studies have also tracked the overall changes in PED patients. For example, Leder et al. [15] studied a cohort of 202 patients and found that patients passing the swallowing assessment at 1, 4, and 24 h post-extubation were 166 (82.2%), 177 (87.6%), and 185 (91.6%), respectively. Tsai et al. [16] found in a 21-day follow-up of 151 adult critical care patients that the recovery of swallowing function was significantly better in younger patients (< 65 years) than in older patients (≥ 65 years) at 7, 14, and 21 days post-extubation but not at 2 days. However, these longitudinal studies have limited consideration for population heterogeneity.

In clinical research, growth mixture modeling (GMM) has been widely utilized to capture heterogeneity in symptom progression and recovery across diverse contexts, such as cognitive decline in neurodegenerative diseases, psychological responses to trauma, and functional recovery following surgery [22]. This person-centered statistical approach classifies patients into subgroups with distinct trajectories based on shared characteristics to address patient heterogeneity [23]. In this way, it allows for the identification of latent classes within a population that follow different developmental paths over time, rather than assuming a single average growth curve for all individuals [24]. By uncovering underlying recovery patterns, GMM provides a data-driven foundation for developing targeted prognostic tools and precision-based rehabilitation strategies tailored to the unique needs of different patient subgroups. In the ICU context, GMM has been used to study the trajectories of heart rate [25], lactate [26], fluid balance [27], C-reactive protein [28], and platelet count [29]. It has also been applied to characterize dysphagia‑related trajectories in stroke survivors and to model patient‑reported dysphagia trajectories in cancer patients undergoing treatment [30], but it has not been used to investigate PED patients.

For ICU patients with PED, the recovery of swallowing function depends on various exposure factors [16, 18]. Analyzing the predictive factors is crucial for enabling clinicians to identify patients prone to slow recovery and provide precise rehabilitation. Nevertheless, because swallowing is a rapid process that requires precise coordination among the brain, nerves, and muscles [6], the symptoms of PED include choking on water, voice changes, ineffective swallowing, and throat pain [7, 8, 21], and it is not straightforward to establish its predictive factors. An appropriate theoretical framework is needed to strengthen the scientific rigor of such investigations [9, 31]. The symptom science model (SSM), proposed by the National Institute of Nursing Research (NINR) [32], is designed for the assessment and management of complex symptoms and guides the development and clinical implementation of multi-level, targeted interventions. The original SSM (2015) includes four core elements: complex symptom presentation, phenotypic characterization, biomarker discovery (later reframed as biobehavioral factors), and clinical applications, and the revised SSM 2.0 (2022) further adds three contextual factors (social determinants of health, patient-centered experience, and policy/population health) [33]. In the ICU context, the SSM has been increasingly utilized to explore complex symptom clusters such as pain, agitation, and delirium, thus helping researchers map clinical severity to biobehavioral responses [34]. However, to the best of our knowledge, SSM has not been explicitly applied to the investigation of dysphagia, either within or outside the ICU.

Therefore, this study employs a longitudinal design to characterize the distinct swallowing recovery trajectories of ICU patients with PED over a 28-day period. We used GMM to account for clinical heterogeneity and uncover latent subgroups and applied the SSM 2.0 framework to systematically investigate the predictors of trajectories. In this way, this work provides a data-driven foundation for identifying high-risk patients early in the ICU course, thus facilitating the delivery of personalized, precision-based rehabilitation to mitigate the long-term clinical and economic burdens of PED.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

This longitudinal investigation examined ICU patients who developed PED and assessed their swallowing function at seven distinct post-extubation time points: 4–6 h (T1), 24 h (T2), 48 h (T3), 72 h (T4), 7 days (T5), 14 days (T6), and 28 days (T7). These time points were selected after a thorough review of the literature [7, 15–21]. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Table S1) [35], adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of a tertiary hospital (No. 2023066).

Participants

We recruited ICU patients from March 2023 to November 2023 at a tertiary hospital, by convenience sampling. Patients were screened by nurses while still intubated, and after extubation, a nurse utilized the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) [36] to assess consciousness and determine the patient’s ability to cooperate. Meanwhile, a physician evaluated the patient’s readiness for oral intake. Patients passing all preliminary assessments underwent swallowing function evaluation by trained nurses using the Standard Swallowing Assessment (SSA), a hierarchical clinical tool designed for the sequential screening of swallowing function [37]. We initially obtained written informed consent from the patient’s legal caregiver, and once patients regained consciousness and the capacity to communicate post-extubation, a researcher provided detailed study information directly to the participants and obtained individual informed consent.

Eligible patients (1) were ≥ 18 years, (2) received orotracheal intubation while in the ICU, and (3) had a diagnosis of PED based on an SSA score > 18 within 4–6 h post-extubation [6, 37]. Patients were excluded if they (1) had pre-existing dysphagia, (2) received postoperative orders for fasting or water restriction, and (3) were unable to communicate or follow instructions. Withdrawal occurred if, during the follow-up period, the patient refused further participation, required reintubation or tracheotomy, or died.

We determined the target sample size based on GMM requirements [38]. A minimum sample size of 200 is required for using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) as the primary selection criterion for GMM [39]. We projected 20% loss to follow-up and established a final enrollment target of 240 cases.

Data Collection

We collected comprehensive patient data across several domains. Demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and the presence of pre-existing comorbidities (hypertension and diabetes). Variables determined at the time of ICU admission encompassed the primary diagnosis, the total number of diagnoses, and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, and laboratory data including blood calcium (Ca2+), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), blood glucose (GLU), hemoglobin (HB), and serum albumin (ALB). Tracheal intubation-related variables included peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak) and tidal volume (VT) recorded within 24 h of intubation, as well as the total duration of orotracheal intubation. Treatment-related data comprised the use of vasopressors, sedatives, or analgesics within 24 h of intubation, fiberoptic bronchoscopy performed within 24 h of intubation, and the duration of nasogastric/jejunal feeding tube placement prior to extubation. Finally, initial clinical assessments recorded within 4–6 h post-extubation (T1) included global limb muscle strength, measured via the medical research council (MRC) scale, and pharyngeal pain, measured via the numerical rating scale (NRS). To support symptom‑focused analyses, patient characteristics were organized according to both conventional clinical categories and SSM domains (Table 1).

Instruments

The Standardized Swallowing Assessment (SSA), originally developed in 1993 for stroke patients, is a hierarchical clinical tool used to evaluate swallowing safety through three sequential stages [40]. Despite its primary design as a screening instrument, the SSA is increasingly applied in research to provide a semi-quantitative index of dysphagia severity [6, 19, 37, 41]. The assessment protocol used in this study comprised the following (Table S2):

Step 3 was administered only if the patient successfully passed Step 2, and if a patient failed Step 2, the maximum score was automatically assigned items in Step 3. In this way, the total score (18–46 points) could be used to characterize the severity of swallowing disorder across patients [42]. In the ICU setting, the SSA has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, with an AUC of 0.841 (95% CI: 0.754–0.927, P < 0.01), 81.1% sensitivity, 85.7% specificity, 76.9% positive predictive value, and 88.5% negative predictive value [43].

The APACHE II system is routinely used to quantify baseline illness severity and prognostic risk in critically ill patients [44]. It aggregates acute physiology, age, and chronic health components to reflect the greatest physiological derangement within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Scores range from 0 to 71, with higher values indicating greater severity. In this work, for analytic purposes, we assigned patients into three bins of APACHE II score (< 10, 10–14, and ≥ 15) because (1) a score of ≥ 15 has been consistently associated with increased in-hospital mortality, prolonged organ support, and poor long-term outcomes [45, 46] and (2) the distinction between < 10 and 10–14 enables more granular assessment of functional recovery across varying levels of baseline illness severity.

Data Analysis

Considering the dynamic nature of PED recovery, we hypothesized that the SSA scores across the seven time points would reveal distinct latent trajectory groups. GMM was performed using Mplus 8.3.0 to estimate growth factors and mean curves for each class while accounting for individual variation through factor variances [35]. Optimal model selection relied on both statistical fit and clinical interpretability using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, the Lo–Mendell–Rubin (LMR) test, and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT).

Among the most widely used model selection indices, including AIC, BIC, and entropy, BIC and entropy are particularly favored based on recommendations for reporting latent-class mixed-effect models [47]. For AIC, BIC, and aBIC, lower values indicate superior model fit. Classification accuracy was assessed using entropy, which ranges from 0 to 1, with values > 0.80 indicating that individuals are accurately classified and that latent classes are adequately distinct. The LMR and BLRT tests were used to provide statistical evidence for increasing the number of classes; typically, significant P values (P < 0.05) indicate that a k-class model provides a significantly better fit than a k − 1 class model. To ensure statistical stability and representativeness, models were excluded if any latent class contained less than 5% of the total samples [39]. The optimal model was ultimately selected by comprehensively evaluating fit indices (log-likelihood, AIC, BIC, aBIC, and entropy), the results of likelihood ratio tests (LMR and BLRT), class size requirements, and clinical interpretability.

Descriptive, univariate, and multivariate analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0. Continuous data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared across trajectory groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as median [P25, P75] and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical data were expressed as case counts (ratio) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. In the univariate analysis of trajectory membership, variables satisfying P < 0.10 were entered into a multinomial logistic regression to identify independent predictors, and those with established clinical relevance (e.g., age) were entered regardless of P value. The default threshold of statistical significance was P < 0.05.

Results

Participant Enrollment and Baseline Characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the enrollment of participants. Initially, 495 patients were screened following orotracheal intubation. After excluding 44 patients who had pre-existing dysphagia (n = 13) or were unable to follow instructions (n = 31), 451 patients underwent initial swallowing function evaluation. Among them, 203 did not meet the criteria of PED. Consequently, 248 patients were enrolled at T1 (4–6 h post-extubation). Over the 28-day follow-up period, 39 patients (15.7%) were lost to follow-up due to death (n = 9), re-intubation (n = 13), fasting orders (n = 8), rejection/withdrawal (n = 7), or telephone errors (n = 2). A total of 209 patients completed the final assessment on day 28 (T7). In this cohort, female patients were predominant (126/209, 60.3%), and the median age was 65.68 years (Table 2). Nearly half of them (93/209, 44.5%) had an APACHE II score of ≥ 15. The median duration of orotracheal intubation was 65.00 h. Univariate analysis revealed that the following measures differed (P < 0.05) between patients who completed follow-up (n = 209) and those who did not (n = 39): diabetes, number of diagnoses, APACHE II score, blood calcium, blood glucose, duration of orotracheal intubation, nasogastric/jejunal tube placement, and global limb muscle strength (Table 2).

Discussion

Interpretation of Baseline Characteristics and Attrition

This study included a total of 248 orally intubated patients in the ICU who developed PED. Among them, 209 completed all follow-up assessments and 39 were lost to follow-up, which gives an overall attrition rate of 15.72%. Compared to the patients who completed, those lost to follow-up had diabetes, higher APACHE II scores, a greater number of diagnoses, lower blood calcium, higher blood glucose, and longer durations of both orotracheal intubation and nasogastric/jejunal tube placement. Presumably, prolonged orotracheal intubation indirectly reflects the increased disease severity of the patients lost to follow-up [14]. Additionally, during the follow-up period, the presence of a nasogastric/jejunal tube can cause a significant foreign body sensation during swallowing [8], which indirectly increases the psychological burden related to deglutition and may contribute to attrition among PED patients. Furthermore, patients with diabetes or glucose metabolism disorders can experience systemic cellular metabolic abnormalities and disrupted protein synthesis, which may impair the capacity to repair damaged laryngeal tissues and, in severe cases, lead to secondary ulcer. In summary, patients lost to follow-up were characterized by severe illness, longer tube indwelling times, and a higher proportion of metabolic disorders [48]. In clinical practice, early assessment and rehabilitation for swallowing function in such patients, along with enhancing clinical management and their confidence in the recovery process, are of paramount importance.

Trajectory Patterns and Recovery Heterogeneity

This study identified three distinct recovery trajectories in ICU patients with PED. Many patients (141/209, 67.5%) fell into Group LE (low level, early recovery), which featured swift functional improvement within 72 h post-extubation. Leder et al. found that most (185/202; 91.6%) adult patients in the ICU passed the Yale Swallow Protocol as early as 24 h post-extubation and argued that delaying swallowing assessment in post-extubation individuals is not necessary [15]. However, the median intubation duration is 34 and 96 h for those who pass and those who fail, respectively. A shorter duration of orotracheal intubation is likely associated with reduced pharyngeal tissue injury [49, 50].

Group HR (high level, rapid improvement, 47/209, 22.5%) initially presented with severe dysphagia. Although the swallowing function improved over time, it still did not return to normal at 28 days post-extubation. In a prospective, longitudinal cohort study involving 13 ICUs at four hospitals, Brodsky et al. [18] observed that of the prevalence of dysphagia at ICU discharge reached 32% (37/115), but 77% (27/35, two died within 3 months of discharge) regained normal swallowing function within 6 months. Presumably, the trajectory of Group HR is attributable to severe initial disease and prolonged intubation, as these factors contribute to muscle atrophy, sensory impairment, and potential damage to the glossopharyngeal and recurrent laryngeal nerves, thereby delaying functional recovery [51, 52]. Compared to Group LE, Group HR had a higher number of diagnosis, higher APACHE II score, higher blood glucose, longer orotracheal intubation, and longer nasogastric/jejunal tube placement (Table 3). At T1, the patients also exhibited worse global limb muscle strength and pharyngeal pain.

Group HS (high level, slow improvement, 21/209, 10.0%), the smallest of the three groups, maintained high SSA scores throughout the study period. The patients had the worst condition upon ICU admission, featuring higher APACHE II scores (16/21, 76.2% having 15 or higher) and number of diagnosis (17/21, 81.0% having 5 or more). Their severe condition may prolong the duration of endotracheal intubation and increase the risk of complications such as arytenoid dislocation or glossopharyngeal nerve injury [52]. While the percentage of patients in Group HS was relatively small, their prolonged dysphagia symptoms underscore the critical importance of early identification and rehabilitative intervention in the ICU setting. Although both Group HR and Group HS had higher initial SSA scores than Group LE, only Group HR had largely intact underlying physiological recovery mechanisms compared to Group LE, and Group HS included the highest-risk population for chronic post-extubation complications. Therefore, clinical caregivers should prioritize early identification of predictors associated with divergent recovery trajectories to enable feed‑forward, preventive management. Although active swallowing exercises cannot be performed during intubation, early recognition of patients likely to follow high‑risk recovery trajectories allows initiation of preventive, swallowing‑related care prior to extubation, such as meticulous oral care and sensorimotor stimulation aimed at preserving afferent input and neuromuscular integrity. After extubation, these patients can further benefit from targeted rehabilitation strategies, including pharyngeal electrical stimulation and supervised exercises involving the jaw, tongue, and cervical musculature [53].

Predictors of Recovery Trajectories

The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent predictors that distinguish these recovery patterns (Table 4). For both Group HR and Group HS, potent predictors included older age, neurological disease, higher Ppeak, longer intubation duration, and greater pharyngeal pain at T1. In older adults, age‑related motor and sensory decline of the pharynx, often compounded by poor nutritional status, limits mucosal repair and delays recovery of swallowing function [16, 54]. Reduced physiological reserve associated with presbyphagia further constrains compensatory capacity, amplifying the swallowing consequences of intubation‑related trauma [3, 4, 55]. These changes are characterized by sarcopenia of the deglutition muscles, resulting in diminished tongue pressure, weakened pharyngeal contraction, and impaired laryngeal sensitivity with attenuation of the cough reflex. Neurological impairment disrupts the complex cortical and subcortical coordination required for safe swallowing. Damage to neural pathways responsible for sensorimotor integration can delay swallow initiation and impair coordination of the upper esophageal sphincter [56]. In the context of PED, intubation-related mechanical trauma likely acts as a “second hit” to an already compromised neural system, limiting the capacity for rapid neuroplastic recovery. Consistent with this mechanism, Schefold et al. [14] reported substantially lower recovery rates at hospital discharge among PED patients with neurological diagnoses compared to those with cardiovascular disease (7/39 vs. 15/28), with neurological patients exhibiting a more than five-fold increased odds of non-recovery (OR = 5.27, 95% CI 1.75–15.92). Peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak) reflects airway pressure and lung compliance during mechanical ventilation. Suboptimal ventilator settings or inadequate sedation may increase Ppeak and promote patient–ventilator asynchrony, thereby intensifying friction between the orotracheal tube and pharyngeal tissues. Such friction-related injury may initiate ischemia–reperfusion processes and inflammatory responses, potentially leading to tissue fibrosis and persistent PED [52]. Elevated Ppeak may also alter subglottal pressure dynamics and increase ventilatory effort, which impairs the glottal closure reflex and renders the swallowing mechanism more susceptible to sustained dysfunction. Prolonged orotracheal intubation may induce pharyngeal edema, inflammation, and muscular injury [3, 4]. The cumulative mechanical load imposed on the vocal folds and pharyngeal constrictor muscles can disrupt the sensorimotor feedback required for timely restoration of safe swallowing. Extended intubation increases the duration of mucosal pressure and irritation, potentially leading to laryngeal desensitization and attenuation of the cough reflex, a phenomenon often described as post-extubation laryngeal blunting. Over time, this sustained structural insult may shift the clinical course from a transient inflammatory process toward persistent neuromuscular dysfunction. Higher levels of pharyngeal pain have been associated with more severe swallowing-related mucosal injury [4, 57]. Such trauma may provoke local inflammatory and mucosal responses, which can be exacerbated by prolonged intubation and potentially progress to fibrosis and persistent dysphagia. Beyond structural injury, acute nociceptive input may interfere with the sensorimotor gating required for effective deglutition. Severe pain can also promote maladaptive compensatory behaviors, including swallowing avoidance or altered bolus transit timing, effectively disrupting neuromuscular coordination. This pain-mediated inhibition may act as a functional barrier to early rehabilitation, delaying recovery of the coordinated muscle activity necessary for airway protection. These mechanistic insights underscore the need for targeted, proactive intervention in patients at highest risk for delayed swallowing recovery. ICU clinicians should prioritize early identification of vulnerable individuals—particularly older adults, patients with neurological disease, and those exposed to prolonged or high-pressure ventilation—and implement preventive strategies before irreversible neuromuscular dysfunction develops. Timely optimization of ventilator settings and prompt response to elevated Ppeak may reduce mechanical friction and mucosal injury during intubation. Following extubation, early and aggressive management of pharyngeal pain is critical to mitigate nociceptive inhibition and prevent maladaptive swallowing behaviors that impede rehabilitation. Integrating these preventive measures with targeted, supervised swallowing and neuromuscular exercises may enhance the recovery of coordinated airway-protective function and reduce the risk of persistent dysphagia in the most susceptible patient subgroups.

Two factors (fiberoptic bronchoscopy and an APACHE II score of 10–14) were uniquely associated with membership in Group HR and were not predictive of Group HS. The acute mechanical effects of bronchoscopy include transient compression or traction of the hypoglossal and pharyngeal nerves, particularly in the presence of concurrent tracheal or nasogastric/jejunal tubes. The resulting “multiplicative pressure” within the pharynx may induce localized ischemia, mucosal injury, and temporary neuropraxia, leading to marked initial swallowing impairment. However, such injury is typically reversible once mechanical stress is relieved and local edema resolves, allowing patients with preserved physiological reserve to demonstrate a rapid improvement slope characteristic of Group HR. Similarly, an APACHE II score of 10–14 reflects intermediate severity, suggesting that patients are sufficiently ill to likely experience substantial initial dysphagia due to complex treatments, potential disease progression, and prolonged intubation [58]. However, these patients do not exhibit the profound systemic collapse or multi-organ failure seen in patients with higher APACHE II scores. In this group, psychological factors may contribute to elevated early swallowing severity in addition to physiological stressors, and anxiety related to ICU admission and fear following an initial choking episode during post-extubation oral intake may transiently inhibit swallowing performance and inflate early SSA scores. Because these patients retain greater neurological and systemic reserve than those in the Group HS, they are more capable of overcoming both functional and psychological barriers once stabilized, resulting in rapid improvement rather than persistent impairment. These findings indicate that patients with a high level of initial PED but on a trajectory of rapid recovery are characterized not by irreversible injury, but by acute, potentially modifiable insults. For this subgroup, clinical priorities should therefore shift from anticipating prolonged impairment to actively removing transient barriers to recovery. In practice, this includes minimizing avoidable mechanical stress during fiberoptic bronchoscopy through judicious procedural indication and careful management of airway and cuff pressures, thereby reducing reversible pharyngeal compression and neuropraxia. Early, confidence-building interventions are key to patients with intermediate illness severity (APACHE II 10–14) as they may show exaggerated early swallowing impairment driven by anxiety, and structured, success-oriented swallowing trials with close supervision and positive reinforcement may help counter fear-induced inhibition and facilitate improvement. Targeting these modifiable procedural and psychological factors may therefore accelerate recovery in this population.

Limitations

While this study provides a robust framework for identifying trajectories of swallowing function, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, our study included a subset of patients with neurological diseases as the primary diagnosis (43/248), which makes it challenging to definitively distinguish whether the observed swallowing dysfunction was caused primarily by orotracheal intubation, the underlying neurological condition, or their combination. Consequently, caution is required when interpreting these findings within a broad ICU population. Second, participant composition influenced our results in several ways. The three trajectory groups differed substantially in size, with Group HS (high level, slow improvement) accounting for only 10.0% of the cohort. Longitudinal follow-up was restricted to patients with confirmed PED at the 4–6-hour post-extubation assessment, and patients without early PED were not monitored for delayed-onset dysphagia. Persistent PED may have been underestimated due to attrition, as 39 patients lost to follow-up exhibited greater illness severity. Third, although our 28-day follow-up provided significant longitudinal insights, the full recovery of swallowing function was not attained at the 28-day mark for both Group HR and Group HS. Lastly, we did not collect data on endotracheal tube size or the specific conditions of intubation (e.g., emergency vs. elective), both of which are identified in the literature as potential risk factors [3]. The absence of these variables limits the comprehensiveness of our risk factor analysis.

To address these limitations, future research should prioritize multicenter prospective studies with larger and more diverse cohorts to enhance the statistical power and generalizability of identified recovery trajectories, particularly for the minority Group HS. Patient selection criteria should be refined to enable clearer differentiation between dysphagia attributable primarily to intubation-related trauma and that arising from pre-existing or concurrent neurological deficits. In addition, follow-up protocols should be expanded in both scope and duration: screening should include those initially asymptomatic to capture delayed-onset dysphagia, and observation periods should extend beyond 28 days to investigate late-stage recovery or persistence of dysfunction. Finally, incorporating a broader range of procedural and clinical variables, such as endotracheal tube diameter, cuff pressure monitoring, and the urgency of intubation, will be essential for developing more granular and comprehensive predictive models of post-extubation swallowing recovery.

Conclusions

This study utilized GMM to move beyond static assessments of PED and successfully identified three distinct recovery trajectories: Group HS (high level, slow improvement), Group HR (high level, rapid improvement), and Group LE (low level, early recovery). Our findings demonstrate that recovery is not a uniform process but is instead a heterogeneous phenomenon driven by a complex interplay of patient age, illness severity (APACHE II), respiratory mechanics (Ppeak), and procedural trauma (intubation duration and fiberoptic bronchoscopy). The identification of neurological disease and early pharyngeal pain as potent predictors of persistent dysfunction provides a critical window for early risk stratification. This work thus offers a robust foundation for personalized clinical management.

To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to apply longitudinal trajectory modeling to PED recovery. The results advocate for a transition toward stage-specific, proactive rehabilitation. High-risk individuals may benefit from a multi-modal strategy: starting with pre-extubation “feed-forward” sensory stimulation—such as pharyngeal electrical stimulation and ice chip therapy—and transitioning into structured post-extubation training focusing on neuromuscular resistance and airway protection. By aligning the intensity and timing of rehabilitative interventions with a patient’s predicted recovery path, clinicians can move toward a precision-medicine approach in the ICU, ultimately mitigating the long-term morbidity and functional decline associated with post-extubation dysphagia.
