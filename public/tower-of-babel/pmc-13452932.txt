18F-FDG PET/CT Metabolic Characteristics of Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma Across AJCC/UICC TNM-8 and TNM-9 Staging Systems: A Retrospective Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Yun Zhang, Yan Tang, Yuxiao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 9th edition of the AJCC/UICC TNM staging system (TNM-9) has introduced key updates for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) compared with TNM-8. However, there is limited data on the metabolic basis and performance of TNM-9 evaluated using 18F-FDG PET/CT. We aimed to investigate the association between PET/CT metabolic parameters and TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging systems, and evaluate the metabolic homogeneity across TNM-9 substages.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective cross-sectional study enrolled 822 NPC patients who underwent pre-treatment 18F-FDG PET/CT. Uptake, volumetric, glucose-normalized, and body composition-normalized metabolic parameters of the primary tumor (T) and the most metabolically active lymph node (LN) were compared across the TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging systems, and comparisons were also performed between the substages in the TNM-9 system. Multicollinearity diagnosis using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and ordinal logistic regression were performed to identify independent stage-related parameters.ResultsBaseline age, fasting blood glucose and 18F-FDG dose were comparable across stages (all P>0.05), while weight differed significantly in both staging systems (both P<0.001). All metabolic parameters differed significantly among stages in both systems (all P<0.001). TNM-9 showed more coherent metabolic distributions compared with TNM-8, whereas TNM-8 exhibited distinct metabolic overlap between certain stages. Metabolic homogeneity was observed in volumetric parameters between TNM-9 stage Ia and Ib, and in all parameters between stage IVa and IVb. T/LN metabolic tumor volume (TMTV/LNMTV) and T/LN lean body mass-normalized maximum standardized uptake value (TSULmax/LNSULmax) were independent predictors in both systems, with a slightly higher McFadden R2 for TNM-9 (0.16 vs. 0.13).ConclusionsPET/CT metabolic parameters provide supportive evidence for the biological basis and clinical utility of TNM-9 in NPC. TNM-9 demonstrated superior metabolic homogeneity and slightly better model fit compared with TNM-8. TMTV, TSULmax, LNMTV, and LNSULmax can serve as promising complementary biomarkers for NPC staging.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor with a highly concentrated geographic distribution. Approximately 133,354 new cases and 80,008 deaths occur globally each year, accounting for 0.7% of all new cancer cases and 0.8% of cancer-related deaths, respectively. More than 83% of cases are concentrated in Asia, with a prominent male predominance in East Asia. Projections show a continuous rise in the incidence and mortality of NPC by 2040, with the most substantial increase expected in Asian populations, highlighting the necessity for optimized clinical staging systems to support precise diagnosis and treatment.1,2

Clinical staging serves as the cornerstone of decision-making for NPC management. In 2025, the 9th edition AJCC/UICC TNM staging system (TNM-9) was implemented worldwide to overcome the limitations of the 8th edition (TNM-8) and improve the accuracy of prognostic evaluation. Key updates of the TNM-9 system include: reclassifying nonmetastatic NPC into a three-tier system (Stages I–III), replacing the four-tier nonmetastatic classification (stages I–IVa) in TNM-8; subdividing stage I into stage Ia (T1-T2N0) and Stage Ib (T1-T2N1); and categorizing all metastatic disease as Stage IV, which is further stratified into Stage IVa (M1a, ≤3 metastatic lesions) and Stage IVb (M1b, >3 metastatic lesions) based on metastatic burden, representing the first subclassification of M1 stage according to the number of metastatic lesions. 3 Although TNM-9 was designed for optimized clinical applicability, the internal metabolic consistency of its stages and substages has not been thoroughly investigated using imaging modalities, representing a critical gap in current research.

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) play complementary and irreplaceable roles in NPC management. MRI is the gold standard for primary tumor evaluation due to its excellent soft-tissue resolution. 18F-FDG PET/CT shows high diagnostic accuracy in detecting cervical lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis, and is recommended by major oncology guidelines for the comprehensive initial staging of NPC.4,5 In patients with locally advanced NPC, routine PET/CT for initial staging is associated with improved long-term distant control. 6 18F-FDG PET/CT-derived metabolic parameters provide critical metabolic evidence for clinical decision-making in oncology. These indicators have been widely validated in evaluating tumor biological aggressiveness, 7 identifying the risk of lymph node metastasis (LNM), 7 monitoring treatment response, 8 facilitating personalized therapeutic strategies,9,10 and predicting patient survival prognosis.11,12 Taken together, existing evidence supports that these metabolic parameters can be used to characterize metabolic heterogeneity in NPC. Beyond the widely used maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), various volumetric parameters and normalized indices have been developed to overcome the limitations of single-point uptake metrics and comprehensively characterize tumor metabolism, including metabolic tumor volume (MTV), total lesion glycolysis (TLG), 13 lean body mass-normalized SUVmax (SULmax),14,15 body surface area-normalized SUVmax (SUSmax), 16 and glucose-normalized SUVmax/SUVmean/TLG (GNmax, GNmean, GNTLG). 17 However, these normalized and volumetric parameters remain underutilized in studies investigating stage-related metabolic differences in NPC, and their distribution patterns across the new TNM-9 staging system have not been systematically explored.

Previous studies on PET/CT metabolic parameters and NPC staging have notable limitations. Most studies either simply divided patients into early and advanced stages,18,19 focused on a small number of metabolic parameters, or only analyzed the TNM-8 system. No report has incorporated a full spectrum of metabolic parameters including uptake values, volumetric parameters, glucose-normalized and body composition-normalized indices to systematically analyze their differential characteristics in TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging systems. Moreover, no study has addressed the metabolic heterogeneity of key TNM-9 substages (Stage Ia/Ib, IVa/IVb), which are essential for understanding the revised logic of TNM-9.

Therefore, we conducted a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study including 822 pathologically confirmed patients with NPC who underwent standardized pretreatment 18F-FDG PET/CT and MRI. This study aimed to systematically analyze the distributional differences of full-spectrum metabolic parameters of the primary tumor (T) and the most FDG-avid LNM across stages and substages of TNM-8 and TNM-9, and identify key metabolic parameters associated with clinical staging. This will provide a metabolic imaging perspective for the clinical application of the TNM-9 staging system.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center, retrospective cross-sectional study conducted at Jiangsu Cancer Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Cancer Hospital and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 20 The need for individual informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient identifiers (e.g., name, medical record number, contact information) were deidentified prior to data analysis to protect patient privacy. Patients with histologically confirmed NPC who underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT for initial staging between June 2016 and August 2021 were enrolled retrospectively. All consecutive patients meeting the predefined inclusion criteria were enrolled, with no arbitrary exclusion beyond the specified criteria. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was an observational study leveraging consecutive clinical data. Thus, the sample size was determined based on the availability of eligible patients during the study period.

Patient Selection

Patients with NPC who underwent 18F FDG PET/CT at Jiangsu Cancer Hospital between June 2016 and August 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) histologically confirmed squamous cell carcinoma of the nasopharynx by endoscopic biopsy; (3) underwent neck MRI prior to any treatment; (4) clinical staging based on AJCC/UICC TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging systems. The exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete visualization of the nasopharyngeal lesion on PET images (e.g., due to motion artifact, inadequate field of view, or severe image noise); (2) history of other malignant tumors. A total of 1015 patients with NPC were initially assessed. PET/CT Imaging Protocol

PET-CT imaging was performed on a Discovery 710 scanner (GE Medical Systems) in all patients. Following a ≥6-hour fast and confirmation of normoglycemia (blood glucose < 11 mmol/L), 18F-FDG (0.1–0.2 mCi/kg) was intravenously administered. After a 50–70-minute uptake period (during which patients consumed 1000 mL of water and voided before scanning), a vertex-to-mid-thigh scan was acquired with the following parameters:

CT: 140 kV, automatic mA, 0.8 s rotation time, 3.75 mm slice thickness;

PET: 2 minutes per bed position for 6–7 beds.

Iterative reconstruction (VUE Point FX + SharpIR; 2 iterations, 24 subsets) with CT attenuation correction was applied. All images were analyzed on an AW 4.6 workstation (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The scanner underwent quarterly maintenance and calibration, and semi-annual phantom verification (NEMA NU-2 2012) was performed to ensure SUV stability (±5% of the standard value) throughout the study period.

PET/CT Image Analysis and Parameter Quantification

Images were independently analyzed by two radiologists with over 10 years of experience in nuclear medicine and head-neck imaging. Volumes of interest (VOI) were delineated using a semi-automatic approach: the AW 4.6 workstation software first generated an initial automatic contour based on the 42% SUVmax threshold algorithm, and the radiologists then performed manual adjustments to ensure complete coverage of the T and the most FDG-avid LNM, while excluding adjacent normal tissues (e.g., muscles, normal brain tissue). 21 Interobserver agreement for metabolic parameter measurement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC values > 0.75 indicated good agreement, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Metabolic parameters were extracted for the T and the most FDG-avid LNM.

Metabolic Parameter Definitions

Uptake metabolic parameters

SUVmax/SUVmean: The maximum and mean standardized uptake value within the VOI.16,22

Volumetric metabolic parameters

MTV: The total tumor volume of voxels exceeding the 42% SUVmax threshold 21 ; TLG: Calculated as MTV × SUVmean.

Glucose-normalized metabolic parameters

GNmax/GNmean: The glucose-normalized equivalents of SUVmax and SUVmean, respectively. 17 GNmax/GNmean = SUVmax/SUVmean × (blood glucose level (mmol/L)/5.56); GNTLG: Calculated as MTV × GNmean.

Body composition-normalized metabolic parameters

SULmax: The lean body mass-normalized SUVmax14,15; SUSmax: The body surface area-normalized SUVmax16.

Metabolic parameters of T and LN, with T and LN added before the abbreviations of the metabolic parameters respectively: TMTV, TSUVmax, TSUVmean, TTLG, TGNmax, TGNmean, TGNTLG, TSULmax, TSUSmax, LNMTV, LNSUVmax, LNSUVmean, LNTLG, LNGNmax, LNGNmean, LNGNTLG, LNSULmax, LNSUSmax.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All graphs were generated using R (v4.5.1; https://www.r-project.org/). The following statistical methods were applied systematically:

Continuous variables (metabolic parameters, demographic data) were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A P-value >0.05 indicated compliance with a normal distribution. Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance for normally distributed continuous variables across clinical stages. A P-value >0.05 indicated equal variances among groups. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, non-normally distributed continuous data as median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical data (e.g., sex distribution) as counts (percentages).

For continuous variables with normal distribution and homogeneous variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare between-group differences in metabolic parameters across multiple TNM stages. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing. For continuous variables with normal distribution and heterogeneous variance, Welch’s ANOVA (a corrected ANOVA) was used as an alternative to one-way ANOVA, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Games–Howell test (a robust correction for unequal variances). For variables with non-normal distribution, Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to compare differences across multiple stages, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

Two-group pairwise comparisons (Stage Ia vs. Ib, IVa vs. IVb) were performed using independent samples t-test for variables with normal distribution and homogeneous variance, while Mann–Whitney U test was used for those with non-normal distribution or heterogeneous variance.

Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) test was used to compare the distribution of categorical variables (e.g., sex) across TNM stages. Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were <5. Chi-square partition with Bonferroni correction was applied to perform pairwise comparisons of categorical variables across stages, with the significance level adjusted to P < 0.05/k (k = number of pairwise comparisons) to control Type I error.

Prior to regression modeling, multicollinearity among metabolic parameters (independent variables) was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A VIF value >10 indicated severe multicollinearity; highly collinear variables were excluded from the regression model to avoid biased estimates. Tolerance values (1/VIF) were also reported to confirm collinearity status (tolerance <0.1 indicated severe collinearity).

Ordinal logistic regression was performed to assess the independent association between metabolic parameters and ordinal clinical stage (TNM-8/TNM-9 stages as the ordinal dependent variable). Only noncollinear metabolic parameters (VIF ≤ 10) were included in the final model. Model fit was evaluated using the McFadden R2 value. Regression coefficients (β), odds ratios (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported to quantify the strength of associations.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P-value <0.05 (after correction for multiple comparisons where applicable) was considered statistically significant.

Multicollinearity Diagnosis and Ordinal Logistic Regression

Summary of Key Results

All metabolic parameters differed significantly across stages in TNM-9 and TNM-8 systems. In TNM-9, several primary tumor parameters were similar between stage III and IV, whereas some lymph node parameters were comparable between stage II and III. In TNM-8, multiple primary tumor parameters showed no significant differences between stage I and II as well as IVa and IVb. Parameters including TSULmax, LNSUVmax, LNSUVmean, LNGNmax, LNGNmean, LNSULmax, and LNSUSmax showed significant differences in all pairwise comparisons in both systems. TNM-9 stage Ia and Ib had similar volumetric metabolic parameters, and stage IVa and IVb showed homogeneous metabolic profiles. After controlling for multicollinearity, TMTV, TSULmax, LNMTV, and LNSULmax were independently associated with clinical stage in both systems, with a slightly higher McFadden R-squared for the TNM-9 model.

Discussion

In this study we aimed to evaluate 18F-FDG PET/CT metabolic parameters in NPC and investigate their associations with the TNM-8 and updated TNM-9 staging systems. The main focus was assessing metabolic homogeneity within key TNM-9 substages and identifying core parameters related to clinical staging.

Metabolic Distribution Patterns of TNM-9 Versus TNM-8 Clinical Staging

All metabolic parameters showed significant differences across clinical stages in TNM-8 and TNM-9, confirming that tumor glucose metabolic characteristics are closely related to NPC progression. Distinct metabolic parameter distribution patterns were observed between the two staging systems. In TNM-9, several primary tumor parameters (e.g., TMTV) had no significant differences between stage III and IV. TNM-9 stage III corresponds to TNM-8 stage IVa (non-metastatic disease), and Stage IV to TNM-8 Stage IVb (distant metastatic disease). 3 The lack of difference in primary tumor parameters between TNM-9 stage III and IV may indicate that the metabolic burden of the primary lesion reaches a plateau when distant metastasis occurs. In contrast, apart from LNMTV, which showed no significant difference between stage I and stage II, all other metabolic parameters of LN demonstrated significant differences in pairwise comparisons. Metabolic parameters of LN exhibited superior stratification performance, which may be attributed to the fact that the TNM-9 classifies extranodal extension of lymph nodes into N3 category. 3

In TNM-8, multiple primary tumor and LN parameters showed no significant differences in pairwise comparisons. This metabolic overlap indicates TNM-8’s subclassification is inconsistent with NPC metabolic biological characteristics. TNM-8 classifies non-metastatic disease into stage I-IVa, leading to metabolic incoherence between stages, while TNM-9 simplifies non-metastatic disease into stage I-III, which is more consistent with the progressive metabolic changes of NPC.

Notably, TSULmax and LNSULmax showed significant differences in all pairwise stage comparisons in both systems, which is a key advantage over traditional non-normalized parameters. Specifically, body weight varied significantly among different clinical stages in our cohort, which may introduce confounding bias and compromise the comparability of weight-based conventional SUVmax measurements. In contrast, SULmax is normalized to lean body mass, a correction that effectively eliminates the effect of interindividual variations in body weight and fat distribution on PET metabolic quantification.14,15 Although body composition-corrected SUL parameters have been seldom investigated in NPC, they have been well documented and extensively validated as robust metabolic biomarkers in a variety of other solid tumors, including lung cancer, lymphoma, and breast cancer.23-25 By minimizing anthropometric-related measurement bias, SULmax serves as a more stable and objective indicator of tumor metabolic activity, thereby conferring more reliable stage-stratifying capacity throughout the disease course of NPC.

Metabolic Distribution Across TNM-9 Substages

A key novel finding of this study is the verification of metabolic homogeneity within TNM-9 key substages, which provides direct experimental evidence for the scientific basis of the TNM-9 staging revisions for NPC.

For TNM-9 Stage Ia (T1–T2N0) and Ib (T1–T2N1), no significant differences were observed in primary tumor volumetric metabolic parameters, including TMTV, TTLG, and TGNTLG. In contrast, uptake-related parameters such as TSUVmax and TGNmax were significantly different between these two subgroups. This metabolic pattern is highly consistent with the subclassification rationale of TNM-9 stage I. As stage Ia and Ib share identical T categories, the metabolic volume parameters, which reflect primary tumor burden, remained stable. Notably, the significantly higher metabolic uptake in stage Ib suggests that elevated metabolic activity in the primary tumor may contribute to the development of N1 lymph node metastasis. In line with this observation, a previous mechanistic study in nasopharyngeal carcinoma demonstrated that EBV-LMP1 drives robust metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis in primary tumors through the IGF1/mTORC2/PDHE1α signaling axis. This hypermetabolic phenotype promotes nuclear translocation of PDHE1α, enhances snail-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and ultimately increases cell motility and invasiveness. Notably, high expression of phosphorylated PDHE1α, a key indicator of enhanced metabolic activity, was significantly associated with advanced N stage and lymph node metastasis in clinical NPC specimens. 26 Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic and clinical evidence that increased metabolic activity in the primary tumor directly promotes regional lymph node metastasis in NPC. This further supports that the TNM-9 subdivision of stage I based on lymph node status is biologically rational and aligned with the metabolic characteristics of early-stage NPC.

For TNM-9 stage IVa (M1a, ≤3 metastatic lesions) and IVb (M1b, >3 metastatic lesions), all primary tumor and LNM metabolic parameters had no significant differences. TNM-9 unifies all distant metastatic NPC into Stage IV, corresponding to TNM-8 Stage IVb. 3 This finding indicates that the metabolic load and activity of primary and LNM lesions reach a plateau when NPC progresses to distant metastasis. The number of metastatic lesions does not cause significant changes in the metabolic characteristics of local lesions. This metabolic homogeneity supports the TNM-9 revision of grouping all distant metastatic NPC into stage IV and subclassifying by metastatic burden, reflecting the consistent metabolic nature of distant metastatic disease regardless of the number of metastatic lesions.

Correlations Between Metabolic Parameters and TNM-8 and TNM-9 Clinical Staging

TMTV, TSULmax, LNMTV, and LNSULmax were identified as the four metabolic parameters independently associated with clinical stage in both the TNM-8 and TNM-9 systems. These four parameters have distinct biological connotations related to NPC progression. TMTV and LNMTV reflect the invasive range and metabolic burden of primary and metastatic lymph node lesions, respectively. TSULmax and LNSULmax, after body composition normalization, objectively characterize the maximum metabolic activity of primary tumors and metastatic lymph nodes. Collectively, they cover the volumetric load and maximum metabolic activity of primary tumors and LNM, and eliminate interindividual measurement bias via body composition normalization, forming a comprehensive metabolic parameter panel for NPC staging. Unlike SULmax, MTV has been widely applied in NPC research, mainly focusing on treatment response evaluation and prognostic assessment.8,11,12

The ordinal logistic regression model constructed with the four core parameters showed that the TNM-9 McFadden R2 (0.16) was slightly higher than that of TNM-8 (0.13). Although the difference is modest, it is meaningful in large-sample analysis, indicating that TNM-9 has a stronger correlation with NPC metabolic characteristics and a better model fit. This superior fit is attributed to TNM-9’s targeted revisions to the limitations of TNM-8. TNM-9 simplifies non-metastatic NPC from a four-tier to a three-tier system, eliminates staging overlap, subclassifies stage I and IV based on clinically meaningful indicators, and ensures each stage demonstrates distinct metabolic characteristics that align with our parameter distribution. These revisions make TNM-9 more consistent with the progressive metabolic changes of NPC, thus improving its association with metabolic parameters. This result provides the first metabolic imaging evidence for the improved clinical applicability of TNM-9 in NPC compared with TNM-8.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, it is a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study, with potential selection bias due to the homogeneous clinical characteristics and treatment protocols of patients. The findings may not be directly generalizable to other populations with different ethnic backgrounds and clinical practices. Second, the sample size of some key subgroups is relatively small, especially TNM-9 stage IVa and IVb (22 patients each) and TNM-8 stage I (18 patients), which may reduce the statistical power of subgroup analysis and the ability to detect subtle metabolic differences. Third, no formal sample size calculation was performed, which may impact the robustness of the results for stage comparisons with small effect sizes. Fourth, VOI delineation for metabolic parameter quantification relied on a single 42% SUVmax threshold algorithm; the generalizability to other thresholding methods remains to be validated, even though the method has good interobserver agreement (ICC > 0.75). Fifth, as long-term clinical survival data were not included, the prognostic value of the four core parameters combined with TNM-9 staging could not be assessed. Sixth, only the most metabolically active LNM was analyzed, which does not fully reflect the overall lymph node metastatic metabolic burden in NPC.

Future Research Directions

Several extensions of this study are warranted to address the above limitations and deepen the understanding on the subject. First, it is necessary to conduct prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes to validate the findings, reduce selection bias, and improve generalizability to diverse NPC populations. Second, long-term follow-up survival data should be incorporated to examine the prognostic value of TMTV, TSULmax, LNMTV, and LNSULmax combined with TNM-9 staging, and identify high-risk patients within the same TNM stage for personalized treatment stratification. Third, comparative studies using different VOI thresholding algorithms to evaluate the impact of threshold selection on parameter quantification and staging performance should be performed, and a standardized method for NPC metabolic parameter assessment must be established. Fourth, analyze all metastatic lymph nodes to fully characterize the lymph node metastatic metabolic burden and its association with NPC staging and prognosis. Fifth, conduct external validation of the ordinal logistic regression model in independent cohorts to lay the foundation for its clinical application. Sixth, integrate these metabolic parameters with MRI radiomic features, pathological indicators, and molecular biomarkers to construct a comprehensive multimodal model for NPC staging and prognosis prediction, further improving the precision of NPC clinical management.

Clinical Implications and Novelty

This study offers three distinct novelties that address lacunae in prior research: (1) a large cohort (n = 822) enabling fine stratification across the full TNM stage spectrum, (2) the first verification of metabolic homogeneity within TNM-9 substages, and (3) systematic demonstration of the superior stage-stratifying ability of TSULmax and LNSULmax—expanding the utility of body composition-normalized metrics in NPC. These findings translate to critical clinical value: (1) TMTV, TSULmax, LNMTV, and LNSULmax complement traditional imaging and pathology to improve staging accuracy, particularly for ambiguous cases; (2) body composition-normalized parameters outperform uncorrected SUV and should be prioritized in clinical/research settings; (3) the metabolic homogeneity and better model fit of TNM-9 validate its rationality and clinical applicability; (4) distinct metabolic profiles between TNM-8 and TNM-9 support aligning staging criteria with tumor biology, which will inform future TNM system revisions.

Conclusion

We systematically investigated the association between 18F-FDG PET/CT metabolic parameters and TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging systems in a large NPC cohort, identifying TMTV, TSULmax, LNMTV and LNSULmax as core parameters independently associated with clinical staging. All metabolic parameters differed across stages in both systems, with body composition-normalized parameters (SUL) showing superior stage-stratifying ability. Metabolic homogeneity within TNM-9 stage I and IV substages validates the scientific basis for its revised classification. TNM-9 has a slightly better model fit with metabolic parameters, supporting its improved clinical applicability over TNM-8. The four identified parameters can act as complementary staging biomarkers, and body composition-normalized parameters are recommended for routine NPC metabolic assessment. This study provides the first metabolic imaging evidence for the clinical application of TNM-9 in NPC, fills critical research gaps, and lays a foundation for precise staging and personalized treatment of NPC in the TNM-9 era.
