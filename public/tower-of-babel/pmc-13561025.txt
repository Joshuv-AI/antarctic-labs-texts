Effect of intradialytic exercise on cardiovascular structural and functional parameters in maintenance haemodialysis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhi Xiao, Chao Fu, Weiwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with end-stage kidney disease who undergo maintenance haemodialysis (MHD).1 Unlike the general population, cardiovascular death in these patients is not solely driven by atherosclerotic plaque rupture but is predominantly characterised by a distinct phenotype known as uremic cardiomyopathy.2 This pathological process involves structural maladaptations, such as left ventricular hypertrophy and dilation, alongside functional impairments including myocardial stunning and arterial stiffness.3 4 These structural and functional abnormalities are strongly associated with an increased risk of sudden cardiac death and heart failure, which accounts for a substantial proportion of mortality in the dialysis population.5

Physical inactivity is highly prevalent among patients receiving haemodialysis and contributes significantly to cardiovascular deconditioning and poor clinical outcomes.6 To address this issue, intradialytic exercise (IDE) has been introduced as a practical therapeutic strategy that uses the sedentary treatment time for active rehabilitation. While there is a consensus that exercise training improves general physical fitness and health-related quality of life,7 8 its potential to modify the underlying cardiovascular disease process remains a subject of debate.

From a pathophysiological perspective, aerobic exercise is hypothesised to induce reverse cardiac remodelling and improve vascular compliance by enhancing endothelial function and reducing sympathetic overactivity.9 However, clinical evidence regarding the effects of IDE on objective cardiovascular parameters is inconsistent. Recent high-quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) using cardiac MRI suggested that intradialytic cycling could regress left ventricular mass and improve vascular stiffness.10 In contrast, other studies reported improvements in functional capacity without significant changes in cardiac structure or resting haemodynamics.11 12 Furthermore, previous systematic reviews have largely focused on functional performance tests or blood pressure alone, often pooling home-based and intradialytic interventions, which may obscure the specific physiological effects of exercise performed concurrently with dialysis.13 14

Given these conflicting findings and the critical prognostic value of cardiac structure and function, there is a clear need to synthesise the available evidence on objective cardiovascular endpoints. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effects of IDE on cardiac remodelling, vascular function and cardiorespiratory fitness in patients receiving MHD.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.15 The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration No. CRD420261336421. No major deviations from the prespecified protocol occurred with respect to the eligibility criteria, outcomes, literature search, study selection, data extraction or quantitative synthesis.

Data sources and search strategy

Two independent reviewers (ZX and WWL) searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to January 2026. The search strategies were developed using database-specific controlled vocabulary terms and free-text terms related to haemodialysis, end-stage kidney disease, exercise interventions and RCTs. The complete search strategies for all databases were provided in online supplemental table S1. We also screened reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies to identify additional eligible trials. No language restrictions were applied to the search.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote software (V.X9, Clarivate Analytics) for duplicate removal. Duplicate records were initially identified and removed using the automated duplicate detection function of EndNote, followed by manual verification by two independent reviewers (ZX and WWL). Titles and abstracts were then independently screened by the same reviewers according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Population: adult patients (≥18 years) receiving MHD for end-stage kidney disease; (2) Intervention: structured exercise training performed during the haemodialysis session, including aerobic, resistance or combined modalities; (3) Comparison: usual care or non-exercise control; (4) Outcomes: reported at least one quantitative measure of cardiac structure, cardiac function, vascular function or cardiorespiratory fitness and (5) Study design: RCTs or controlled clinical trials. We excluded studies involving home-based exercise or those that did not report sufficient data for extraction.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (WWL and CF) independently extracted data using a standardised form. Extracted information included study characteristics (author, year, country), participant demographics (sample size, age, dialysis vintage), intervention details (frequency, intensity, time, type), control group conditions, follow-up duration and outcomes of interest. Risk of bias was assessed by two reviewers (ZX and WWL) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) for randomised trials.16 In accordance with RoB 2 guidance, the assessment was performed for each outcome result contributing to the meta-analyses rather than assigning a single overall judgement to an entire study. Five domains were evaluated: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome and bias in selection of the reported result. Each outcome result was classified as low risk, some concerns or high risk of bias according to the RoB 2 algorithm. Any discrepancies arising between the two reviewers during the assessment process were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third investigator to achieve consensus.

Outcome measures

The outcomes were pre-specified and categorised into primary and secondary endpoints. The primary outcomes focused on cardiovascular structure and function where cardiac structure was assessed by left ventricular mass index (LVMI) and left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) while cardiac and vascular function were assessed by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and pulse wave velocity (PWV). Secondary outcomes included cardiorespiratory fitness measured by peak oxygen uptake (Peak VO2) alongside resting haemodynamics including systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using STATA software (V.16.0, StataCorp, TX, USA). For each outcome, data were harmonised to consistent units of measurement across studies, and the change from baseline, calculated as the postintervention value minus the baseline value, was used as the effect measure. When studies directly reported the mean change and corresponding SD, these data were used. For studies that did not report the SD of the change, we imputed it using the baseline SD, final SD, and a conservative correlation coefficient (r=0.5), following the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.17 Mean difference (MD) was used for all continuous outcomes, as data were synthesised using consistent units of measurement. For studies reporting multiple postbaseline assessments, the assessment conducted at the completion of the scheduled exercise intervention was selected for meta-analysis; interim assessments and assessments conducted after completion of the intervention were not included. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. As a general guide, I² values of 0%–40% were considered to indicate heterogeneity that might not be important, 30%–60% moderate heterogeneity, 50%–90% substantial heterogeneity and 75%–100% considerable heterogeneity. These ranges were interpreted as approximate rather than rigid thresholds, taking into account the magnitude and direction of effects and the strength of evidence for heterogeneity.18 Given the anticipated variability in exercise protocols and patient characteristics, a random-effects model was applied for all analyses.19 Potential sources of substantial or considerable heterogeneity were considered for exploration using subgroup analyses or meta-regression where sufficient studies were available. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test where the number of included studies exceeded 10.20 Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Evidence from RCTs was initially rated as high certainty and was assessed across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. The certainty of evidence was categorised as high, moderate, low or very low, with reasons for downgrading documented for each outcome.

Patient and public involvement

As the current study was a systematic review based on published data, patients and the public were not involved in the study design, recruitment and conduct.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 2145 records were identified through electronic database searching. After removing duplicates, 1628 records were screened based on titles and abstracts, of which 1578 were excluded. The full text of the remaining 50 articles was assessed for eligibility. Thirty-six studies were subsequently excluded for reasons including ineligible intervention types (n=12), lack of outcome data of interest (n=10), inappropriate study design (n=8) or insufficient data for extraction (n=6). Finally, 14 studies10 11 21–32 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1).

Flow diagram of study selection according to the PRISMA guidelines. CENTRAL, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials; PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

The characteristics of the 14 included studies are summarised in online supplemental table S2. These studies were published between 2008 and 2025 and conducted across multiple regions, including Europe, North America, Asia, South America and Australia. The total pooled sample consisted of 934 patients with end-stage kidney disease receiving MHD, with sample sizes in individual studies ranging from 17 to 335 participants. The mean age of participants ranged from approximately 43–66 years. Dialysis vintage varied widely among studies, ranging from 14 months to over 10 years. Regarding the intervention protocols, all exercise sessions were supervised and performed during haemodialysis. The majority of studies used aerobic exercise alone, primarily cycling or treadmill walking. Four studies implemented a combined exercise protocol incorporating both aerobic and resistance training. The frequency of exercise was consistently set at three times per week across all trials. Session duration typically ranged from 30 to 60 min, with exercise intensity prescribed mostly using the Borg rating of perceived exertion scale (target 11–14) or percentage of peak capacity. The control groups in all studies received usual haemodialysis care without a structured exercise programme. The duration of the interventions varied from 3 months to 1 year.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed at the outcome level according to the RoB 2 framework (table 1). For LVMI, two of five assessments (40.0%) were judged to be at low overall risk of bias and three (60.0%) as having some concerns. For LVEDV, two of three assessments (66.7%) had some concerns and one (33.3%) was at high risk of bias. For LVEF, five of six assessments (83.3%) had some concerns and one (16.7%) was at high risk. For PWV, one of four assessments (25.0%) was at low risk, two (50.0%) had some concerns and one (25.0%) was at high risk. For Peak VO2, five of six assessments (83.3%) had some concerns and one (16.7%) was at high risk. All assessments contributing to SBP and DBP were judged as having some concerns.

Effects of intradialytic exercise on primary outcomes

Cardiac structure

Data on LVMI were available in five studies.10 11 25 29 31 The pooled analysis revealed no significant difference in the change in LVMI between the IDE group and the control group (MD=−1.19 g/m²; 95% CI −5.63 to 3.24; p=0.597), with no observed heterogeneity (I²=0.00%, p=0.537) (figure 2). Conversely, data from three studies10 11 23 assessing LVEDV demonstrated a significant increase in LVEDV in patients performing IDE compared with controls (MD=14.15 mL; 95% CI 2.06 to 26.24; p=0.022), with no observed heterogeneity (I²=0.00%, p=0.518) (figure 3). For LVMI, two of five contributing outcome-level risk-of-bias assessments were judged to be at low overall risk and three as having some concerns. For LVEDV, two of three assessments had some concerns and one was at high risk. Study weights were distributed across the contributing trials, with no single study dominating either pooled estimate.

Forest plot comparing the effects of intradialytic exercise vs control on Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Volume (LVEDV). WMD, weighted mean difference.

Cardiac and vascular function

Regarding cardiac function, six studies10 11 23 25 27 28 reported LVEF. The meta-analysis showed a statistically significant improvement in LVEF favouring the exercise group (MD=2.21%; 95% CI 0.28 to 4.15; p=0.025). Moderate heterogeneity was observed among these trials (I²=38.16%, p=0.152) (figure 4). Five of the six outcome-level assessments contributing to the LVEF meta-analysis had some concerns regarding risk of bias, and one was at high risk. No single study contributed a disproportionately large weight to the pooled estimate.

Forest plot comparing the effects of intradialytic exercise vs control on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

Vascular function, assessed by PWV in four studies,10 24 29 32 showed a significant improvement. The pooled results indicated a significant reduction in PWV (MD=−1.36 m/s; 95% CI −2.58 to −0.13; p=0.031) in the IDE group, indicative of attenuated arterial stiffness. Heterogeneity was low and non-significant (I²=22.97%, p=0.273) (figure 5). Of the four outcome-level assessments contributing to the PWV meta-analysis, one was at low risk of bias, two had some concerns and one was at high risk. No single study dominated the pooled estimate.

Effects of intradialytic exercise on secondary outcomes

Cardiorespiratory fitness

Peak VO2 was reported in six studies.21 22 25–27 30 The pooled analysis demonstrated a significant improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness in the exercise group (MD=2.76 mL/kg/min; 95% CI 0.08 to 5.43; p=0.044). However, considerable heterogeneity was detected for this outcome (I²=90.07%, p<0.001) (figure 6) Five of the six outcome-level assessments contributing to the Peak VO2 meta-analysis had some concerns regarding risk of bias, and one was at high risk. No single study contributed a disproportionately large weight to the pooled estimate.

Forest plot comparing the effects of intradialytic exercise vs control on peak oxygen uptake (Peak VO2).

Resting haemodynamics

The effects of IDE on resting blood pressure were mixed. The pooled analysis of four studies10 24 30 31 revealed no significant reduction in SBP (MD=−8.76 mm Hg; 95% CI −20.38 to 2.86; p=0.139), accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (I²=73.20%, p=0.011). In contrast, DBP was significantly reduced in the exercise group across four studies (MD=−4.54 mm Hg; 95% CI −7.57 to −1.51; p=0.003), with no observed heterogeneity (I²=0.00%, p=0.492) (figure 7). All outcome-level assessments contributing to the SBP and DBP meta-analyses were judged as having some concerns regarding risk of bias. Study weights were distributed across the contributing trials, with no single study dominating either pooled estimate.

Forest plots comparing the effects of intradialytic exercise vs control on resting haemodynamics. (A) Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and (B) diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Publication bias

As the number of included studies for each outcome was fewer than ten, funnel plots and Egger’s tests were not formally conducted to evaluate publication bias, in accordance with Cochrane guidelines.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence varied across outcomes according to the GRADE assessment (online supplemental table S3). Moderate-certainty evidence was identified for LVMI, LVEF, PWV and DBP, whereas the evidence for LVEDV, Peak VO2 and SBP was rated as low certainty. Downgrading was primarily attributable to concerns identified in the outcome-level risk-of-bias assessment, substantial heterogeneity for Peak VO2 and SBP, and imprecision where CIs crossed the null or encompassed effects ranging from trivial to potentially clinically important.

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive evaluation of IDE effects across the cardiovascular and muscular systems in patients receiving MHD. The synthesised evidence demonstrates that concurrent exercise during dialysis predominantly confers cardioprotection through functional and haemodynamic optimisations rather than through the short-term reversal of established uremic myocardial hypertrophy. This functional improvement is evidenced by an enhanced LVEF and a concurrent reduction in arterial stiffness and DBP alongside a robust increase in overall cardiorespiratory fitness. Interestingly these functional gains occur despite a lack of significant regression in the LVMI and an absence of SBP reduction which collectively highlights the nuanced physiological adaptations elicited by this intervention. The certainty of evidence was moderate for LVMI, LVEF, PWV and DBP and low for LVEDV, Peak VO₂ and SBP. Accordingly, findings supported by low-certainty evidence should be interpreted cautiously, particularly where heterogeneity or imprecision reduced confidence in the pooled estimates.

The observed dissociation between improved systolic function and the lack of significant regression in LVMI highlights the complex nature of uremic cardiomyopathy.33 Left ventricular hypertrophy in patients with end-stage kidney disease develops in response to persistent pathophysiological factors, including chronic haemodynamic overload, anaemia, arteriovenous fistula-related high-output circulation and myocardial fibrosis.3 34 These structural determinants may persist despite exercise training and may be less readily reversible than functional abnormalities. In contrast, functional improvements may occur more rapidly through enhanced ventricular filling and improved vascular compliance, without requiring a measurable reduction in myocardial mass. In addition, the relatively small sample sizes of the contributing trials may have limited the ability to detect modest changes in LVMI, while variation in volume status at the time of cardiac assessment may have influenced structural measurements. The relatively limited intervention duration in most trials may have further reduced the opportunity for established myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis to regress.

Notably, our analysis revealed a significant increase in LVEDV following exercise, which occurred concurrently with the improvement in ejection fraction. In heart failure paradigms, ventricular dilation is often viewed as pathological. However, in the context of exercise training, this structural change more likely reflects a physiological adaptation similar to the athlete’s heart phenomenon.35 36 Aerobic exercise improves myocardial compliance and enhances venous return.37 An improved diastolic filling capacity allows the heart to use the Frank-Starling mechanism more effectively. This mechanism translates increased myocardial fibre stretch into a more forceful systolic contraction, consequently leading to a higher ejection fraction.38 Thus, the observed increase in end-diastolic volume should not be interpreted as pathological remodelling. Instead, it serves as a favourable physiological adaptation to aerobic conditioning that directly supports enhanced systolic pump function.

Our meta-analysis demonstrated a clinically significant reduction in PWV, which is the gold-standard measure of arterial stiffness. Exercise-induced shear stress is known to upregulate endothelial nitric oxide synthase, thereby improving endothelial function and reducing vascular tone.39 40 This improvement in vascular compliance perfectly aligns with the significant reduction observed in DBP, a parameter primarily dictated by peripheral vascular resistance.

Intriguingly, despite the reduction in DBP and PWV, SBP remained unchanged. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the fundamental determinants of systemic blood pressure. While diastolic pressure reflects peripheral resistance, systolic pressure is largely driven by cardiac output and large-artery stiffness.41 The significant enhancement in systolic ejection fraction, combined with an improved stroke volume via increased end-diastolic volume, leads to a higher cardiac output. This augmented cardiac output likely counterbalances the blood pressure lowering effect of peripheral vasodilation, thereby maintaining SBP levels.42 Additionally, structural medial calcification of large arteries is ubiquitous in the dialysis population and is highly resistant to short-term interventions.43 This structural rigidity further limits the potential for dramatic reductions in systolic pressure. Clinically, this haemodynamic profile of maintained systolic pressure with lowered diastolic pressure and improved ejection fraction is highly favourable. It indicates that IDE unloads the peripheral vasculature without compromising critical organ perfusion pressure.

The improvement in Peak VO2 suggests a potential clinically meaningful benefit of IDE in patients undergoing MHD. Previous foundational cohorts have demonstrated that even a 1 mL/kg/min increase in Peak VO2 is associated with improved survival and reduced cardiovascular mortality in the dialysis population.44 However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the certainty of evidence for Peak VO2 was rated as low according to the GRADE assessment due to substantial heterogeneity and imprecision. The substantial heterogeneity observed for this outcome is expected given may be explained by varying baseline fitness levels of patients, different modalities of exercise, and the dose-response relationship inherent to exercise physiology. The enhancement in Peak VO2 may reflect improvements in central cardiac performance and peripheral adaptations, including enhanced skeletal muscle capillary density and mitochondrial oxidative capacity.45 46 Further large-scale RCTs are warranted to determine whether improvements in Peak VO2 induced by IDE translate into long-term cardiovascular benefits.

The strength of this study lies in its strict focus on objective cardiovascular imaging and functional parameters, which avoids the subjective bias associated with patient-reported questionnaires. Furthermore, by isolating IDE, we reduced the heterogeneity often seen in meta-analyses that pool home-based and in-centre interventions. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample sizes of the included trials were relatively small, which may limit the statistical power to detect modest changes in left ventricular mass. Second, cardiovascular parameters are highly sensitive to volume shifts. Although most studies attempted to standardise imaging times, residual confounding by volume status cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, the limited number of studies contributing to individual outcome analyses precluded reliable subgroup analyses or meta-regression to explore heterogeneity. Differences in exercise modality, intensity, duration and participant characteristics may have contributed to the observed variability, particularly for outcomes with moderate to considerable heterogeneity. Finally, the follow-up duration of the included studies limits our ability to ascertain whether these functional improvements translate into long-term reductions in cardiovascular events and mortality.

Conclusion

IDE may improve cardiovascular function, arterial stiffness and cardiorespiratory fitness in patients receiving MHD. However, its effects on LVMI and SBP remain uncertain. Given that the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low and that several outcomes were limited by risk-of-bias concerns, heterogeneity and small numbers of contributing studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further large-scale, well-designed RCTs with longer follow-up are needed to clarify the long-term cardiovascular effects of IDE.
