The mediating role of brain structural-functional connectivity coupling in linking paternal education to BMI and depressive scores in young adults
Authors: Yuhui Wang, Cancan Lin, Wenjie Xia, Xiao Zhang, Shutang Zhao, Tiefeng Ji, Xubo Wang, Defu Zhang, Yue Wang, Yuxin Du, Hao Yu, Shanling Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParental education is associated with body mass index (BMI), mental health, and neurodevelopment in young adults, yet the neurobiological mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated whether parental education is related to BMI and depressive scores through brain structural-functional connectivity (SC-FC) coupling.MethodsThis study included 490 healthy young adults who underwent resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) scans. Parental education was measured as years of schooling, BMI by weight/height2, and depressive scores by the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI). SC and FC were derived from DTI and rs-fMRI, respectively. SC-FC coupling was quantified using Spearman’s correlation between SC and FC matrices. Associations among variables were examined using Pearson correlation and mediation analyses.ResultsPaternal education was significantly correlated with BMI (r = 0.10, P < 0.05) and depressive scores (r = -0.09, P < 0.05). Additionally, paternal education, BMI, and depressive scores were positively associated with SC-FC coupling in the right inferior parietal lobule (IPL, r = 0.19, corrected P < 0.001; r = 0.12, corrected P < 0.05; r = 0.10, corrected P < 0.05, respectively). Mediation analysis showed that the right IPL statistically mediated the associations of paternal education with both BMI (indirect effect = 0.01, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.001–0.04) and depressive scores (indirect effect = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.001–0.03).ConclusionIn this cross-sectional study, paternal education was associated with BMI and depressive scores, with SC-FC coupling in the right IPL statistically mediating these associations. No corresponding effects were observed for maternal education. These findings suggest a potential neural pathway linking socioeconomic factors to physical and mental health outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08360-y.

Introduction

Parental education is a crucial environmental factor that profoundly influences the physical and mental health of offspring through various pathways, including family environment, parenting styles, and educational expectations [1, 2]. Higher parental education levels provide a significant protective effect on children’s health [3]. Research has reported that completing 12 years of education reduced under-five mortality by 31.0% for mothers and 17.3% for fathers compared to uneducated parents [4]. Additionally, higher parental education levels have been associated with lower body mass index (BMI), reduced risk of depression, greater psychological resilience, and improved overall mental health in children [5–7]. Despite robust behavioral evidence, the neurobiological substrates mediating these associations remain poorly understood. While many studies have treated parental education as a unitary construct, emerging evidence suggests that maternal and paternal education may have differential effects on offspring outcomes, potentially due to differences in parenting roles, time spent with children, and cultural expectations. For instance, maternal education was found to contribute to increases in total gray matter volume and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex gray matter volume [8]. Therefore, in this study, we examined maternal and paternal education separately.

The developing brain exhibits remarkable plasticity during childhood [9, 10], rendering it highly sensitive to environmental inputs such as parental education. Although the substantial temporal gap between childhood exposure and adult outcomes raises legitimate concerns about confounding by the offspring’s own education or adult socioeconomic status, longitudinal and twin studies have demonstrated that parental education predicts adult brain structure and mental health independently of these later-life factors [11]. One explanation for this enduring association is that early-life environments become biologically embedded during critical periods, with neurodevelopmental effects that persist into adulthood [12]. Life-course models extend this view by proposing that early-life socioeconomic exposures establish durable neurobiological vulnerabilities that are not fully reversed by subsequent attainment [13, 14]. Consistent with these frameworks, neuroimaging studies have documented structural and functional correlates of parental education in offspring. At the structural level, higher parental education has been associated with increased gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex [11] and greater cortical surface area [15]. At the functional level, parental education has been linked to functional connectivity of the salience network [16], enhanced activation of executive systems [17], and, in cases of lower parental education, reduced cortico-striatal activity [18]. Together, these findings indicate that parental education is associated with coordinated variations in both brain structure and function, raising the possibility that their interrelation may constitute a pathway linking early-life socioeconomic circumstances to physical and mental health outcomes in adulthood.

Critically, the brain regions that are sensitive to parental education, such as the prefrontal cortex, parietal lobule, and reward-related circuits, also play key roles in regulating eating behavior, body weight, and mood. Previous studies have shown that alterations in these regions are associated with higher BMI and depressive symptoms. For example, reduced gray matter volume and altered functional connectivity in the prefrontal cortex and inferior parietal lobule (IPL) have been observed in individuals with obesity and in those with depressive symptoms [19, 20]. This overlap suggests that parental education may influence BMI and depressive symptoms through its effects on the development and function of these shared neural substrates. Therefore, we propose that the interplay between structural and functional brain organization in these regions could serve as a pathway linking parental education to both physical and mental health.

Depression and obesity frequently co-occur and share common neurobiological substrates, including alterations in the prefrontal cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and reward-related circuits [21–23]. Both conditions have also been linked to lower parental education, suggesting that they may arise from shared environmental and neural pathways [24, 25]. Therefore, examining BMI and depressive score together within a single framework may help identify the neural mechanisms linking socioeconomic disadvantage to physical and mental health.

Structural connectivity (SC) refers to the white matter architecture derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), while functional connectivity (FC) refers to the temporal correlations in brain activity derived from resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI). Structural-functional connectivity (SC-FC) coupling provides a novel framework to investigate these mechanisms, quantifying the alignment between white matter architecture and functional co-activation patterns [26]. As an integrative neural marker, SC-FC coupling has elucidated pathophysiology in depression [27] and neurodevelopmental disorders [28], but its role in mediating environmental influences remains unexamined. Unlike traditional brain measures such as gray matter volume or functional connectivity alone, SC-FC coupling captures the degree to which functional interactions align with underlying structural pathways [29], reflecting neural efficiency and integrative capacity that may be particularly sensitive to environmental factors like parental education.

This study investigated the underlying neural mechanisms through which parental education influences BMI and depressive scores in young adults. A total of 490 healthy young participants were included. Sparse SC was derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and a fully connected SC matrix was then derived using the cosine similarity method [30, 31]. FC was obtained from rs-fMRI. SC-FC coupling was assessed using Spearman’s correlation between fully connected SC and FC. Pearson correlation and mediation analyses were employed to examine the relationships between parental education, BMI, depressive scores, and brain SC-FC coupling. Given the shared neurobiological substrates of depression and obesity in regions such as the frontal and parietal cortex [21–23], we hypothesized that SC-FC coupling in these regions might serve as a neural substrate linking parental education to both BMI and depressive scores. This study provides new evidence for the neural pathways linking parental education to BMI and depressive scores and offers practical guidance for preventive interventions targeting obesity and depression.

Methods

Participants

A total of 522 healthy participants were recruited in Beijing between August 2014 and July 2016, with 490 meeting the quality control and eligibility criteria for inclusion in the final analysis (details are provided in the Quality Control section of the Supplementary Materials and in Figure S1). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Institute of Mental Health (2013-32), and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been described previously [32]. Briefly, the inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 45 years; (2) right-handedness; (3) Chinese Han. MRI scans for all participants were independently reviewed by trained radiologists, confirming the absence of structural abnormalities. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) a history of psychiatric or neurological disorders; (2) a history of substance use disorder, head trauma, or loss of consciousness; (3) any condition deemed unsuitable for MRI scanning.

Measurements

Parental education level was determined through participants’ reports of the years of formal schooling completed by each of their parents.

Anthropometric measurements included height and weight, which were used to calculate BMI according to the standard formula (weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared).

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI) [33], a 21-item self-report instrument assessing the severity of depressive symptomatology over the preceding two weeks. Each item consists of four graded response options (scored 0–3) measuring emotional, cognitive, somatic, and behavioral manifestations of depression. To ensure reliability, the BDI was administered by trained psychiatric professionals within 24 h of enrollment, following standardized protocols for both administration and scoring. BDI scores were used as a continuous measure of depressive symptomatology and were not employed as an inclusion or exclusion criterion. All participants were free of diagnosed psychiatric disorders, assessed by psychiatrists using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-TR Axis I Disorders, Research Version, Non-patient Edition (SCID-I/NP).

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

MRI acquisition

All imaging data were acquired using a 3.0T GE Discovery MR750 scanner. High‑resolution T1‑weighted, resting‑state functional MRI (rs‑fMRI), and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequences were obtained from each participant. Detailed acquisition parameters are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

MRI preprocessing

The rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using DPARSF [34], including slice timing correction, head motion correction, nuisance signal regression, spatial normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing, and temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). DTI data were preprocessed using PANDA [35], which incorporates tools from Diffusion Toolkit and FSL. The preprocessing involved brain extraction, correction for head motion and eddy currents, generation of fractional anisotropy (FA) maps, and whole‑brain deterministic tractography using the Fiber Assignment by Continuous Tracking algorithm. Tractography was terminated when FA fell below 0.2, when streamlines exited the brain mask, or when the angular deviation exceeded 45°. Further details on MRI Preprocessing are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Functional and structural network construction

Network nodes were defined using the 246-region Brainnetome Atlas [36]. FC was computed as Pearson’s correlation between the time series of each node pair, and the resulting correlation coefficients were Fisher’s z-transformed to obtain a symmetric 246 × 246 FC matrix. SC was reconstructed from DTI tractography: two nodes were considered connected if at least three fiber bundles linked them, and connection weights were normalized by the summed surface areas of the two regions, yielding a sparse 246 × 246 weighted SC matrix. To estimate relationships between regions without direct structural connections, a fully connected SC matrix was derived using the cosine similarity method [30, 31]. Further details on network construction are available in the Supplementary Materials.

SC-FC coupling

SC-FC coupling was quantified using Spearman rank correlation analyses between the values of each row in the SC matrix (1 × 246) and the corresponding row in the FC matrix (1 × 246). This process generated a subject-specific SC-FC coupling profile (1 × 246), with each element indicating the degree of alignment between structural and functional connectivity in a specific brain region.

Associations between parental education, brain SC-FC coupling, BMI, and depressive scores

Partial correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the pairwise associations between parental education, BMI, depressive scores, and SC-FC coupling across 246 brain regions while controlling for covariates, including participants’ age, sex, and their own years of education. The p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction across all 246 brain regions tested. Finally, mediation analyses were performed using the mediation package [37] in R software to investigate the mediating role of SC-FC coupling in the pathways linking parental education to BMI and depressive scores.

Statistical analyses

Preliminary statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 20). Analyzed variables included age, sex, school year, parental education, BMI, BDI scores, and head motion parameters from rs-fMRI. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Supplementary analysis

To further investigate the potential modifying effects of BMI levels and sex on the observed associations, we conducted stratified analyses by categorizing participants into distinct subgroups. For BMI stratification, participants were classified into four categories according to Chinese diagnostic criteria: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2, 12.86%, n = 63), normal weight (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m2, 67.14%, n = 329), overweight (24 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m2, 10.20%, n = 79), and obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2, 9.80%, n = 19). This classification enabled the examination of potential dose-response relationships across BMI categories. For sex stratification, participants were classified into male (n = 250) and female (n = 240) subgroups. Additionally, to explore the associations of offspring socioeconomic status (SES) with parental education, as well as with offspring own BMI and depressive scores, we performed analyses to account for these relationships.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Significant sex differences were observed in BMI, age, years of education, and maternal education (Table 1). Specifically, males had higher BMI than females (t = 10.09, P < 0.001), were slightly older (t = 2.39, P = 0.017), and reported fewer years of schooling (t = − 2.08, P = 0.038). Maternal education years were lower in males than in females (t = − 3.19, P = 0.002). No significant sex differences were found for paternal education or BDI scores (both P > 0.05). Regarding the BDI scores, the results showed that most participants reported minimal depressive scores: 467 participants (95.3%) scored between 0 and 13, 18 participants (3.7%) had mild depression (scores 14–19), 5 participants (1.0%) had moderate depression (scores 20–28), and none had severe depression (scores 29–63).

SC-FC coupling distribution

The highest SC-FC coupling was observed in the medial ventral occipital cortex (bilateral ventromedial parieto-occipital sulcus) and the superior frontal gyrus (bilateral medial area 10), with mean maximum R2 values ranging from 0.41 to 0.39. Conversely, the lowest coupling was identified in the superior parietal lobule (right rostral area 7), inferior temporal gyrus (right intermediate lateral area 20), thalamus (right rostral temporal thalamus), and the superior parietal lobule (left intraparietal area 7, hIP3), with mean minimum R2 values ranging from 0.22 to 0.20. Mediation effects of the right IPL on the relationships of paternal education with BMI and depressive scores

The mediation effects of SC-FC coupling in the right IPL are presented in Fig. 2A (for BMI) and Fig. 2B (for depressive scores). Specifically, the indirect effect of the paternal education on BMI through the right IPL was significant (indirect effect = 0.01, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.001–0.04). The path coefficients were significant both from paternal education to IPL (β = 0.15, P < 0.001) and from IPL to BMI (β = 0.05, P < 0.001), while the direct effect of paternal education on BMI was also significant (β = 1.10, P < 0.001, Fig. 2A). This finding suggested that the right IPL primarily mediates the influence of the paternal education on BMI.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the associations among parental education, BMI, depressive scores, and SC-FC coupling. The findings indicated that paternal education was associated with both BMI and depressive scores through SC-FC coupling in the right IPL. These results offer preliminary insights into the neurobiological pathways that may link paternal education to offspring physical and mental health.

Association of paternal education with offspring BMI, depressive scores, and brain SC-FC coupling

The significant positive association between paternal education and the offspring’s BMI indicated that offspring with more highly educated fathers tend to exhibit higher BMI levels. This finding contrasts with the previous studies, which have generally reported that higher parental education is associated with lower BMI in children [24, 38, 39]. Such negative correlations had been attributed to variables including screen time, physical activity, and dietary habits [40, 41]. However, the positive association in this study may be related to factors such as racial differences [42], family structure [43], intergenerational rearing practices [44], and genetic predispositions [45], among others. Further research was warranted to elucidate these underlying mechanisms.

Additionally, this study found a negative association between paternal education and offspring depressive symptoms, consistent with the view that higher paternal education is associated with reduced depressive tendencies in offspring. In alignment with this finding, previous research demonstrated the protective role of parental education in adolescent mental health [46–48]. Additionally, parental education appeared to influence mental health through multiple pathways, including parental expectations [49], parenting styles [50], and family environment [51]. Future research should explore these potential mechanisms while considering developmental stages, family structure, cultural norms, and social support systems.

Moreover, the positive associations of paternal education, BMI, and depressive scores with SC-FC coupling in the right IPL suggest a close relationship between these factors and brain connectivity. Previous studies have also shown strong associations between parental education [11, 15], BMI [52–54], and depressive scores [55–57] with brain structure and function. The convergence of these factors on SC-FC coupling patterns raises the possibility of an integrative mechanism by which socioeconomic, metabolic, and psychological factors collectively shape brain network dynamics. Future research should investigate genetic, epigenetic, and environmental mechanisms underlying the associations with SC-FC coupling.

The lack of significant correlations between depressive scores and BMI contrasts with previous findings. Some studies suggested a U-shaped relationship between depressive scores and BMI [58–60]. The discrepancy between this study and prior research may be due to sociocultural contexts, measurement methods, and mediating factors, such as family economic status. Future studies should further examine these complexities.

Mediating effects of SC-FC coupling in the right IPL

The SC-FC coupling in the right IPL was found to statistically mediate the association between paternal education and offspring health, providing correlational evidence consistent with intergenerational health transmission.

In the model examining offspring BMI, paternal education showed a positive correlation with BMI, with SC-FC coupling in the right IPL partially mediating this relationship, representing 11% of the total effect. Dysfunction within the IPL, a hub region involved in the attention and decision-making process [19, 61], impaired the regulation of eating behaviors, which may in turn be related to unhealthy habits. Additionally, the IPL dysfunction was linked to body image disorders [62], underscoring its crucial role in self-perception and emotion regulation. Given the plasticity of the IPL, paternal education may relate to its function through shaping health perceptions [18, 62]. Nonetheless, the SC-FC coupling in the right IPL accounted for only a minor portion of the overall effect, suggesting that it represented merely one of numerous potential neural and behavioral mechanisms. Paternal education also indirectly impacts the offspring’s BMI through factors such as dietary habits and physical activity [63, 64]. Consistent with this notion, functional abnormalities in the right IPL have been associated with various depression-related neural characteristics, including increased regional homogeneity [65], metabolic abnormalities in the parietal cortex [66], and reduced fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations [67].

Higher paternal education was associated with a more supportive environment, which may foster the development of executive functions and self-regulation, and this could be related to a lower risk of obesity and depression [68]. Besides, the paternal education may be linked to health outcomes through epigenetic modifications [69] and lifestyle factors, such as encouraging physical activity and promoting healthy behaviors [70]. Future research should incorporate multimodal imaging and longitudinal designs to better understand the neurobehavioral pathways that may connect education to health outcomes.

Absence of maternal education and sex effects on offspring outcomes

Our previous study, which used the same dataset as the current work, found that maternal education contributed to increases in total gray matter volume and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex gray matter volume [8]. This study did not identify any significant associations between maternal education and offspring SC-FC coupling or depressive scores. These findings align with those of a large cohort study [71], yet they contrast with previous research on neurocognitive outcomes [72]. This discrepancy suggests that such relationships may be influenced by parenting factors [1] or cultural contexts [73]. The specificity of paternal effects observed in our study may reflect the particular role of fathers in shaping health-related behaviors and cognitive development in certain cultural contexts, where paternal education might be more closely tied to household economic resources and occupational status. Alternatively, maternal education effects might operate through different neural or behavioral pathways not captured by SC-FC coupling in the right IPL, or may emerge at different developmental stages than those examined here.

Similarly, we observed no sex differences in SC-FC coupling or depressive scores, diverging from previous reports of sex-dimorphic neural organization [74] and depression risk [75], possibly due to methodological variations in sample characteristics, cultural contexts, or developmental timing. These null findings underscore the importance of considering contextual moderators and alternative pathways in intergenerational research while highlighting the need for future studies to examine interactive effects of parental education combinations, mediating mechanisms within home environments, and longitudinal developmental trajectories across diverse populations to clarify these complex relationships. The results suggest maternal education effects were more context-dependent than paternal effects, and that sex differences in brain-behavior relationships might have emerged only under specific conditions or developmental stages not captured in our sample.

Notably, the mediation effects remained materially unchanged after adjusting for offspring socioeconomic status, ruling out confounding by the offspring own socioeconomic circumstances. This finding aligns with life‑course models, which propose that early‑life socioeconomic exposures can have lasting neurobiological effects not fully overridden by adult attainment [13, 14, 76]. Even so, the direct path from paternal education to BMI remained significant, suggesting that right IPL SC‑FC coupling captures only one of several pathways, and that separate structural or functional metrics warrant further investigation.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of our study limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the observed associations between parental education and offspring outcomes, necessitating future longitudinal investigations to establish temporal relationships. Secondly, while we controlled for key covariates, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured socioeconomic factors and lifestyle factors that could jointly influence both parental education and offspring outcomes. Thirdly, the BDI captures short-term emotional states rather than persistent depressive pathology, limiting its usefulness in assessing the enduring effects of stable factors like parental education. It should be noted that our study merely reflects low mood in a healthy population, not clinical depression, although depression may exhibit similar associations. Future studies should integrate multi-source data to enhance assessment validity. Lastly, our results may not fully account for developmental dynamics, as age-related neurobiological changes and evolving socioenvironmental influences could differentially impact SC-FC coupling and mental health across the lifespan. Future research should employ lifespan approaches to delineate critical periods of susceptibility and investigate how these relationships manifest across distinct developmental stages. These limitations highlight important avenues for more rigorous examination of the complex interplay between intergenerational educational attainment and neurobehavioral outcomes.

Conclusions

This study observed an association in which SC-FC coupling in the right IPL statistically mediated the relationship between paternal education and both BMI and depressive scores, consistent with the possibility of a shared neural pathway that may link socioeconomic factors to physical and mental health outcomes. These preliminary findings are consistent with the hypothesis that brain SC-FC coupling may serve as a neural correlate underlying these associations. Notably, no significant associations were found between maternal education and SC-FC coupling or depressive scores, and the mediation pathways observed for paternal education were not present for maternal education, suggesting the specificity of paternal effects that warrants further research.
