A new general quantum state verification protocol by the classical shadow method
Xiaodi Li

Abstract
Verifying whether a quantum device produces a specific quantum state is a fundamental task in many applications of modern quantum technologies. In the conventional framework of quantum state verification, designing an optimal or efficient protocol for each type of state typically requires intricate, state-specific customization. Recently, Hsin-Yuan Huang et al. introduced a novel approach known as the shadow overlap protocol [ 1 ] , which leverages classical shadows to efficiently verify multiple classes of quantum states simultaneously. In this work, we propose a new verification protocol that integrates key ideas from both the conventional framework and the shadow overlap protocol. To this end, we first reformulate the shadow overlap protocol using the formalism of hypothesis testing, which also underpins the conventional approach, and analyze the similarities and differences between the two. Our framework extends the capabilities of the shadow overlap protocol while addressing some of its limitations, yielding improved sample complexity and a more natural treatment of structured quantum states. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our protocol through applications to GHZ states and stabilizer states.



I
Introduction

With the rapid developments of quantum technologies, people can manipulate much more complex and larger quantum systems, whose sizes are out of reach of the simulations of classical computers. Hence, ensuring a quantum state prepared by a quantum device is exactly an interesting target state has become an urgent problem. Such problem, called
quantum state verification
(QSV) or certification, has wide implications in quantum computation, quantum communication, and other quantum technologies.

The traditional quantum state tomography methods can’t be used to solve the QSV problem, as they need exponential copies of the prepared states with respect to the number of qubits to obtain a classical description of the state. To circumvent such a predicament, Sam Pallister et al. proposed a general framework, called the PLM framework, for verifying the prepared quantum states in
[
2
]
. An explicit verification strategy in this framework consists of a sequence of tests constructed by local projective measurements with binary outcomes, if the prepared states pass all such tests, we will accept the statement that the device produces the target state correctly. The sample complexity, i.e., the number of prepared states, of such a general framework is approximately proportional to the inverse of the spectral gap of an operator corresponding to the strategy, called the
strategy operator
. Hence, applying this framework to verify a specific type of state reduces to constructing an explicit protocol or a strategy operator, which has the maximal spectral gap.

Prior to the introduction of the PLM framework, several related works
[
3
,
4
]
had already explored the verification of maximally entangled states. Notably, the authors of
[
4
]
first addressed the quantum state verification under the assumption that the states produced by the quantum device are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.). In contrast, the PLM framework considers a more general setting in which the states may be adversarial and are therefore not necessarily i.i.d.
After the PLM framework, there are also many developments in the direction of QSV. The optimal protocol (strategy operator) with the maximal spectral gap has been constructed for states, like the Bell states, the maximally entangled states, the bipartite pure state, the GHZ states and the stabilizer states
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, and some near-optimal or efficient protocols are also proposed for some more complicated states like the W states, the Dick states, the phased Dick states, graph states and hypergraph states
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.
In addition to the mentioned protocols, there are also some works trying to extend the PLM framework to more complicated or more practical circumstances, like the LOCC measurement
[
6
,
14
,
9
,
15
,
10
]
, the adversarial scenario
[
16
,
17
]
, the blind measurement-based quantum computation
[
18
]
.

However, most previous works have focused on constructing verification protocols tailored to specific types of quantum states, limiting their applicability. A protocol designed for one class of states typically cannot be extended to others. Given the vast number of state classes with special structures, it is impractical to design optimal or efficient protocols for each type individually. Moreover, states with special structures constitute only a small subset of the full Hilbert space, leaving the problem of verifying generic states—those lacking special structures—as an open and challenging question.

Fortunately, Hsin-Yuan Huang et al. proposed a new, different protocol in
[
1
]
, which can verify almost all Haar random pure quantum states and some special states efficiently.
This protocol, called the
shadow overlap protocol
(SOP), unifies the ideas of randomized Pauli measurement and the classical shadow tomography
[
19
,
20
]
, then possesses the advantages of application in practical experiments.
However, the SOP also has certain limitations. First, for some structured states such as GHZ states, applying the SOP requires additional unitary transformations, which are not inherently integrated into the protocol and must be handled separately. Second, the protocol relies on a complicated operator constructed by querying the amplitudes of the target state in the computational basis, which lacks a clear physical interpretation.

In this paper, we explore the similarities and differences between the SOP and the PLM framework, and propose a new protocol, so-called the directly partial shadow overlap (DPSO) protocol, which remedies some shortcomings of the SOP by combining the ideas of both frameworks.
More explicitly, we reinterpret the SOP in the view of hypothesis test and compare it with the PLM framework.
Then we construct the DPSO protocol within the same framework as SOP, under the assumption that the states generated by the quantum device are i.i.d.
Compared with the SOP, our protocol has lower sample complexity and broader applicability, being able to handle GHZ states or other special states more naturally. When the level of our protocol is equal to
1
, the level-
1
DPSO protocol includes the SOP, so our protocol also retains the capability to verify almost all Haar random pure states.

The organization of our paper is following. In Section
II
, we introduce the PLM framework of QSV briefly. In Section
III
, we reformulate the shadow overlap protocol in the formalism of hypothesis testing, then discuss similarities and differences between two frameworks in details. Next, in Section
IV
, we propose our protocol explicitly and compute the sample complexity. Finally, in Section
V
, we apply our protocol to the stabilizer state and the GHZ state.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we will very briefly introduce the PLM framework of QSV proposed in
[
2
]
and its subsequent developments.
The task of quantum state verification is to determine if a quantum device produces a particular target state accurately, and a verification protocol provides us an explicit method to assess whether the output of this device is the target state rather than other states. More explicitly, given a target state
|\psi\rangle
, a quantum device produces a sequence of states
\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{N}
, which may be i.i.d. or adversarial, then we need to accept one of the following statements: (1) the device produces the target state exactly, i.e.,
\rho_{i}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
for all
1\leq i\leq N
, or (2) the device can’t produce the target state exactly, meaning
\langle\psi|\rho_{i}|\psi\rangle\leq 1-\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<1
for all
i
.

II.1
The PLM framework of QSV

In the PLM framework of QSV, the null hypothesis
H_{0}
is: the states produced by the quantum device satisfy
\langle\psi|\rho_{i}|\psi\rangle\leq 1-\epsilon
for all
i
, and the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
is: the device produces the target state exactly, i.e.,
\rho_{i}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
for all
i
.

As illustrated in Protocol
1
, an explicit protocol in the PLM framework of QSV will proceed as the following. (1) When
i
runs from
1
to
N
, the verifier draws
\{E_{j},\mathds{1}-E_{j}\}
from a set
\mathcal{E}
of binary-outcome projective measurements with probability
p_{j}
, then applies it to
\rho_{i}
with the outcomes of
E_{j},\mathds{1}-E_{j}
corresponding to ”Pass” and ”Fail” respectively.
(2) If a ”Fail” is obtained for some
i
, then the protocol ends, and we accept the null hypothesis
H_{0}
and ”reject” the quantum device. Otherwise,
H_{1}
is accepted, and we say the device is ”accepted”, meaning the device produces the target state
|\psi\rangle
.

.

There is a constraint on the test operator

for all

, which guarantees the target state always passes the test.
As for the produced state
\rho_{i}
, the probability of
\rho_{i}
passing the test is

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[\text{"Pass"}]=\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathop{\rm Tr}(E_{j}\rho_{i})=\mathop{\rm Tr}(\Omega\rho_{i}),

(1)

where
\Omega:=\sum_{j}p_{j}E_{j}
is called the

and satisfies

and
\Omega|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
. Since
\rho_{i}
satisfies
\langle\psi|\rho_{i}|\psi\rangle\leq 1-\epsilon
, the worst-case probability of
\rho_{i}
passing the test is

\displaystyle\max_{\rho,\langle\psi|\rho_{i}|\psi\rangle\leq 1-\epsilon}\mathop{\rm Tr}(\Omega\rho)=1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon,

(2)

where
\nu(\Omega)
is the spectral gap of strategy operator
\Omega
. If we assume the eigenvalues of
\Omega
are in the non-increasing order as
1=\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{2}(\Omega)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(\Omega)\geq 0
with
d
being the dimension of the Hilbert space, then
\nu(\Omega):=1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega)
.

On the assumption of
E_{j}|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
, the Type II error, which is the probability of accepting
H_{0}
given
H_{1}
is true, is always
0
. Therefore, there is a unique optimal acceptance set or decision rule in the view of the classical hypothesis testing
[
21
,
22
]
, which is just the rule shown in Protocol
1
.
The Type I error, the probability of accepting
H_{1}
given
H_{0}
is true, is just the probability of all
N
states
\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{N}
passing the test,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[\text{"Accept"}]=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{\rm Tr}(\Omega\rho_{i})\leq[1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon]^{N}.

(3)

Then by requiring the probability
\text{Pr}[\text{"Accept"}]
being smaller than a positive constant
\delta
,
we can get the lower bound of the sample complexity,

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln[1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon]}\approx-\frac{\ln\delta}{\nu(\Omega)\epsilon},

(4)

i.e., the number of
\rho_{i}
’s to verify if the device accurately produces the target state in the fidelity precision
\epsilon
with confidence
1-\delta
. Eq.(
4
) tells us that the sample complexity increases when the spectral gap
\nu(\Omega)
decreases, then for a specific target state, our goal is to design a strategy operator
\Omega
with the spectral gap being as large as possible. If the spectral gap is maximal, the corresponding strategy operator
\Omega
or protocol is
optimal
. If the quantum device produces
n
-qubit states, we also say a protocol is
efficient
if the inverse of the corresponding spectral gap
\nu(\Omega)
is upper bounded by a polynomial of
n
.

II.2
QSV with LOCC measurements

There have also been many improvements on the PLM framework of QSV, amongst which is the substitution of the local measurements by the measurements composed of local operations and classical communications (LOCC)
[
6
,
14
,
9
,
15
,
10
]
.
In
[
2
]
, the test operator
E_{i}
’s are constructed by local projective measurements of each party of the system and there are no classical communications among different parties.
If the classical communications among the parties are allowed, there are more types of test operators can be constructed. Taking the one-way LOCC strategy operator
\Omega^{\rightarrow}
of a bipartite system as an example, Alice performs a local measurement
\{M_{a|j}\}
with probability
p_{j}
on her subsystem, then Alice sends
j
and the measurement outcome
a
to Bob, and then Bob makes the measurement
\{K_{a|j},\mathds{1}-K_{a|j}\}
on his subsystem based on
j,a
.
So
\Omega^{\rightarrow}
has the following form,

\displaystyle\Omega^{\rightarrow}=\sum_{j}p_{j}E_{j}^{\rightarrow},\quad E_{j}^{\rightarrow}=\sum_{a}M_{a|i}\otimes K_{a|i}.

(5)

The above procedure can also be generalized to two-way LOCC and many-round two-way LOCC.
One special example of one-way LOCC strategy operators is proposed in
[
23
]
. In
[
23
]
, the verifier is given a
n
-qubit state produced by a quantum device and apply a randomized local Pauli measurements to
n-1
qubits randomly. After obtaining the measurement outcomes
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n-1})
, the verifier applies a projective measurement to the remaining qubit, where the projective measurement is just the reduced state of
|\psi\rangle
with respect to the outcome
\boldsymbol{s}
. Astonishingly, such simple protocol is proved to be valid for many types of states numerically or analytically in
[
23
]
.

III
The shadow overlap protocol and hypothesis testing

III.1
The shadow overlap protocol

Parallel to advancements in the PLM framework for QSV, Hsin-Yuan Huang et al. recently proposed a new general protocol termed
shadow overlap protocol
(SOP) in
[
1
]
, which certifies nearly all Haar random pure states. This protocol utilizes the classical shadow method
[
20
]
and employs a classical query model
\mathcal{Q}(\psi)
, which is a classical description of the target state
|\psi\rangle
and stored in a classical computer.
At first glance, the SOP appears very different from the PLM framework, but it also shares many similarities with the PLM framework, particularly with the previous mentioned protocol in
[
23
]
. Note that the SOP is designed to handle only i.i.d. states produced by the quantum device.
Here, we briefly review the SOP and reformulate it by using the terminologies of

to unify its description and the PLM framework and facilitate comparison.

III.2
The hypothesis testing

In this subsection, we reformulate the SOP in the form of hypothesis testing and discuss two types of error and the sample complexity in details. Some results can be directly applied to our protocol later.

First, we choose the same null and alternative hypotheses as those in the PLM framework. Before considering two type of errors, we need to select a decision rule, which determines which hypothesis is accepted according to the value of estimator
\hat{\omega}_{\text{a}}

[
21
,
22
]
.
If
H_{1}
is true,
\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)=1
, then as an estimator of
\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)
,
\hat{\omega}_{a}
fluctuates around
1
. While if
H_{0}
is true,
\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)\leq 1-\nu(L)\epsilon
with
\nu(L)
being the spectral gap of
L
, then
\hat{\omega}_{a}
fluctuates around
\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)
but is less than
1-\nu(L)\epsilon
probably. Hence, we tend to reject
H_{0}
and accept
H_{1}
if
\hat{\omega}_{a}>1-\nu(L)\epsilon
.
So we set the
decision rule
as:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\text{reject}~H_{0}&\text{if}~\hat{\omega}_{a}>t_{0},\\
\text{reject}~H_{1}&\text{if}~\hat{\omega}_{a}\leq t_{0},\end{array}\right.

with
1-\nu(L)\epsilon<t_{0}<1
.
In fact, different values of
t_{0}
generate different tests, but it will be much simpler when setting
t_{0}=1-\nu(L)\epsilon/2
as will be seen.

Next we try to compute the Type I and Type II errors.
The Type I error is

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[\text{reject}~H_{0}|H_{0}]=

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}\geq t_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}\geq 1-\nu(L)\epsilon+t_{0}-1+\nu(L)\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}\geq\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)+t_{0}-1+\nu(L)\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\omega}_{a}]\geq t_{0}-1+\nu(L)\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{2N[t_{0}-1+\nu(L)\epsilon]^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}\right\},

(14)

where in the third line, we used the fact
\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)\leq 1-\nu(L)\epsilon
, and in the last line, we used the result of the application of the Hoeffding’s inequality to
\hat{\omega}_{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\omega}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\omega}_{a}]\geq t\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{2Nt^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}\right\},

(15)

with
t>0
and
a\leq\hat{\omega}_{i}\leq b
for all
i
.
While the Type II error is

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[\text{reject}~H_{1}|H_{1}]=

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}\leq t_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\omega}_{a}]\leq t_{0}-1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{2N(1-t_{0})^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}\right\},

(16)

where we used the result
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\omega}_{e}]=\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)=1
for
H_{1}
and the result of the Hoeffding inequality

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left\{\hat{\omega}_{a}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\omega}_{a}]\leq-t\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{2Nt^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}\right\}.

(17)

The upper bounds of Type I and Type II errors in Eq.(
14
) and Eq.(
16
) show that the speeds of both types of error approaching
0
are exponentially fast with respect to
N
. But if
N
is fixed, when
t_{0}
approaches
1
, the Type II error becomes larger and the Type I error becomes smaller. It’s just the reverse case when
t_{0}
approaches
1-\nu(L)\epsilon
.
There is a tradeoff between the two errors, so we have to let one error be smaller than a small positive constant
\chi
and compute the minimum of another error, just as what we should do in the standard hypothesis testing
[
21
,
22
]
. After requiring the second minimal error to be smaller than
\delta
, then we can compute the sample complexity as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem III.1
.

Suppose that the Type II error
\beta:=\rm{Pr}[\rm{reject}~H_{1}|H_{1}]
is smaller than
\chi
with
0<\chi<1
, then the Type I error
\alpha:=\rm{Pr}[\rm{reject}~H_{0}|H_{0}]
takes the optimal (minimal) value
\alpha^{*}
when

\displaystyle t_{0}=1-(b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\ln\chi^{-1}}{2N}}.

(18)

After requiring the
\alpha^{*}\leq\delta
, the sample complexity
N
is

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2\nu^{2}(L)\epsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\ln\chi^{-1}}+\sqrt{\ln\delta^{-1}}\right)^{2}.

(19)

Proof.

Fixing
\beta
being smaller than
\chi
, i.e., the upper bound of
\beta
is not larger than
\chi
, we can easily compute the condition about
t_{0}
as

\displaystyle t_{0}\leq 1-(b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\ln\chi^{-1}}{2N}}.

(20)

As
\alpha
decreases when
t_{0}
increases, so
\alpha
is minimal when
t_{0}=1-(b-a)\sqrt{\ln\chi^{-1}/(2N)}
. Then we expect the Type I error being smaller than
\delta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{2N}{(b-a)^{2}}\left[\nu(L)\epsilon-(b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\ln\chi^{-1}}{2N}}\right]^{2}\right\}\leq\delta,

and we can compute the sample complexity as

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2\nu^{2}(L)\epsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\ln\chi^{-1}}+\sqrt{\ln\delta^{-1}}\right)^{2}.

(21)

∎

In fact, we can also fix the Type I error
\alpha
and compute the optimal Type II error
\beta
, but we get the same sample complexity as Eq.(

).
If we require both types of error to be smaller than one small constant like
\delta
, the situation is much simplified, and
t_{0}
takes the value

,
then we can compute the sample complexity as

\displaystyle N\geq 2(b-a)^{2}\frac{\ln\delta^{-1}}{\nu^{2}(L)\epsilon^{2}}.

(22)

In the following, we will always let
t_{0}=1-\nu(L)\epsilon/2
.

Returning to the discussion of the SOP, the decision rule is: if the estimated shadow overlap
\hat{\omega}_{\text{e}}\geq 1-\epsilon\nu(L)/2
,
then we conclude that the quantum device produces the target state.
According to Eq.(

\hat{\omega}
is bounded as

as proved in
[
1
]
, then we get the explicit sample complexity

\displaystyle N\geq 2^{4\ell-1}\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{2}\nu^{2}(L)}.

(23)

Although the sample complexity

scales exponentially with the level
\ell
, in practice the level

can be set to a small number e.g.,
1
or
2
. In Fig.

The differences between the above sample complexity with that of PLM framework will be discussed below.

III.3
Comparison with the PLM framework

There are many striking similarities between the SOP and the PLM framework.
First, the operator
L
defined in Eq.(
10
) has the same properties as the strategy operator
\Omega
in the PLM framework:
0\leq L\leq\mathds{1}
and
L|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
.
Second, the procedure of randomized measurement in the SOP is very similar with that in
[
23
]
, especially the case of
\ell=1
.

However, there are also some fundamental differences between the SOP and the PLM framework.
First, the SOP assumes that the quantum device produces the i.i.d. states, but the
\rho
’s can be adversarial in the PLM framework.
Secondly, the measurement applied in each run of the SOP is not a LOCC measurement but rather a local Pauli measurement, as there is no classical communication among
n
parties before performing the Pauli measurements. This fundamental distinction sets it apart from the LOCC measurements described in Eq.(
5
). Although the Pauli measurements are performed on
n
qubits in two steps during the first stage of the SOP, in a real experiment, they can instead be executed simultaneously, with the classical shadow computed in the second stage.
Thirdly, although we have reformulated the SOP using hypothesis testing terminology, the resulting form differs from standard hypothesis testing as described in
[
21
,
22
]
. The SOP more closely resembles parameter estimation, as its objective is to estimate
\mathop{\rm Tr}(L\rho)
.
In the PLM framework,
\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Omega)
is the probability for
\rho
passing a test and the decision rule is determined by the number of ”pass”. While in the SOP, we need to estimate
\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho L)
and make a decision based on the estimated value.
Fouthly, as shown in Eq.(
23
), the sample complexity of the SOP appears significantly larger than that of the PLM framework due to the exponential term
2^{4\ell-1}
. However, when
\ell
is set to a small value, this term becomes negligible compared to other factors.
Regarding other factors, the sample complexity
N
of the SOP scales as
(\epsilon^{2}\nu^{2}(L))
, which is the consequence of the Hoeffding’s inequality. While this scaling is less favorable, it enables the SOP to certify a broader class of states. In other words, there is a trade-off between the capability of uniformly verifying different types of state and the sample complexity of the protocol.

In addition to its capability of certifying a broader class of states, particularly nearly all Haar random pure states, another advantage of the SOP is its reliance on local Pauli measurements. These measurements are significantly easier to implement experimentally compared to LOCC measurements.

IV.1
Description of the verification procedure

In general, our DPSO protocol is similar with the SOP in

Compared with the SOP, there are several key changes in DPSO protocol.

•

In the DPSO protocol,

•

For the randomly selected

•

We also require a classical description of the target state

The last two differences are more important. The second modification enables us to handle a wider range of states with special structures, such as the GHZ states and stabilizer states. The third adjustment opens the possibility of constructing the optimal protocol, where the strategy operator achieves the optimal spectral gap.
Hence, when the level

[
23
]
, then the DPSO protocol can also verify almost all Haar random pure states, just as the SOP
[

]

Finally, it is important to emphasize that the measurement applied in each run of the DPSO protocol is not an LOCC measurement, but rather a randomized Pauli measurement. Therefore, the DPSO protocol should not be regarded as an adaptive version of the SOP.

The intuition behind the DPSO protocol is that if the state
\rho
produced by a quantum device equals to or approximates the target state
|\psi\rangle
, then after applying a random partial measurement to a random subset of the qubits, two ensembles of the reduced states will be the same or very similar. It’s possible that after applying only Pauli-

Now we compute the expression of the strategy operator in the DPSO protocol. After applying the Pauli measurements
\sigma_{l_{1}}^{(j_{1})},\cdots,\sigma_{l_{n-r}}^{(j_{n-r})}
to qubits
\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{n-r}\}
and obtaining the outcomes
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n-r})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n-r}

\rho

\displaystyle\zeta_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}=\frac{\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}}{\text{Tr}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}})},

where
\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}=\langle\boldsymbol{z}|\rho|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle
and
\text{Tr}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}})
is the probability of obtaining outcomes
\boldsymbol{z}
.
For qubits
K
, we apply random Pauli measurements and obtain the outcomes
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{r})
, then we can construct the classical shadow of
\zeta_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}=\otimes_{a=1}^{r}(3|s_{a}\rangle\langle s_{a}|-I),

(25)

where
|s_{a}\rangle
is the eigenstate corresponding to the outcome
s_{a}
of the Pauli measurement applied to qubit

.
By the definition of the classical shadow
[
20
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}]=\zeta_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}.

(26)

Corresponding to the random Pauli measurements
(J,\boldsymbol{l})
and the outcomes
\boldsymbol{z}

|\psi\rangle
for the qubits
K

\displaystyle|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle=\frac{|\widetilde{\phi}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle}{\||\widetilde{\phi}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\|},

(27)

with

The computation of

is represented as a matrix product state, or under a more powerful query model than that considered in
[
20
]
. Further discussion of this topic is provided in Appendix

Hence, the shadow expectation of
\hat{\omega}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\omega}]=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{K}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}]|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\langle\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Tr}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}})}\text{Tr}_{K\cup J}\left(\rho|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{z}|\otimes|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\langle\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Tr}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}})}\text{Tr}\left(\rho\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{z}}\right),

(28)

where

\displaystyle\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{z}}:=|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{z}|\otimes|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\langle\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}|

(29)

is a projector.
Hence,
\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\omega}]
has a clear physical meaning, proportional to the probability of projecting
\rho
to the state
|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\otimes|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle
.

We further consider the average of
\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\omega}]
over different outcomes
\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}\text{Tr}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}})\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\omega}]=\text{Tr}\left(\rho\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}\right),

(30)

where
\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}
is the summation of
\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{z}}
over all possible outcomes
\boldsymbol{z}
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{z}|\otimes|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\langle\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}|.

(31)

The operator

in the PLM framework, so we call

as the

The test operator

satisfies a nice property,

\displaystyle\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}|\psi\rangle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{z}|\otimes|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\langle\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}|\right)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\otimes|\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle\langle\phi_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}|\widetilde{\phi}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle.

(32)

The expectation value of the shadow overlap
\hat{\omega}

\displaystyle\omega=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\omega}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{J}p_{J}\sum_{\boldsymbol{l}}q_{\boldsymbol{l}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}\text{Tr}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{K,\boldsymbol{z}})\mathbb{E}_{\text{sh}}[\hat{\omega}]=\text{Tr}\left(\rho\Omega\right),

then the average

In the above equation, we have defined the operator

\displaystyle\Omega:=\sum_{K}\sum_{\boldsymbol{l}}p_{K}q_{\boldsymbol{l}}\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}=

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle 0\leq\Omega\leq\mathds{1},\quad\Omega|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle.

Hence, the operator

V
Stabilizer formalism

In order to illustrate the DPSO protocol, we apply it to the stabilizer state. We can show that our protocol can deal with the stabilizer state, including the GHZ state, more naturally than the SOP.
The problem of verification of the stabilizer state has been solved in the PLM framework elegantly
[
2
,
8
]
, and the protocols are optimal or near-optimal. So we don’t seek to find an explicit optimal DPSO protocol for verifying a specific stabilizer state but provide a method to determine the optimal strategy operator.

V.1
Pauli measurement and the test operator

Now we formalize the DPSO protocol into a more formal form by using the symplectic representation of Pauli measurements, which will be very useful to deal with the stabilizer state.
For a single qubit, the Pauli measurements
X,Z
and
Y
correspond to the vectors
(1;0),(0;1)
and
(1;1)
in the symplectic space
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
, while the trivial measurement, i.e., the identity or no measurement applied, corresponds to
(0;0)
.
Each
composite
Pauli measurement on
n
qubits is specified by a symplectic vector
\mu=(\mu^{\mathrm{x}};\mu^{\mathrm{z}})
in the symplectic space
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, the Pauli measurement applied to qubit
j
is
\mathrm{i}^{\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{x}}\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{z}}}X_{j}^{\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{x}}}Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{z}}}
.
The
weight
of a composite Pauli measurement on
n
qubits is the total number of non-trivial Pauli measurements applied to a single qubit, i.e., the total number of
j
with
(\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{x}},\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{z}})\neq(0,0)
. A composite Pauli measurement is
complete
if its weight equals
n
. However, only the non-complete composite Pauli measurements are involved in our protocol.

Considering the DPSO protocol
3
,
let’s focus on a composite Pauli measurement
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
with weight
t=n-r
and assume the non-trivial single Pauli measurements are applied to the set of qubits
J=\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}\}
. There are
t
non-trivial Pauli operators like
\mathrm{i}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}X_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}}Z_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}
with
a=1,\cdots,t
, then these Pauli operators generate a stabilizer group
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
with cardinality
|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|=2^{t}
.
Applying the composite Pauli measurement to a state
\rho
, the outcome of qubit
j_{a}
is
(-1)^{v_{a}}
with
v_{a}\in\{0,1\}
, then the measurement outcomes of
t
qubits correspond to a
t
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}
.
For each vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, we can construct a new stabilizer groups
\mathcal{T}_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}}
generated by such Pauli operator
(-1)^{v_{a}}\mathrm{i}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}X_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}}Z_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}
with
a=1,\cdots,t
, its corresponding projector is

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}}=

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{a=1}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\left[I+(-1)^{v_{a}}\mathrm{i}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}X_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}}Z_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}(-1)^{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{u}),

(39)

where
W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{u})
is a Pauli operator like

\displaystyle W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{u})=\bigotimes_{a=1}^{t}\left(\mathrm{i}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}X_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{x}}}Z_{j_{a}}^{\mu_{j_{a}}^{\mathrm{z}}}\right)^{u_{a}}.

(40)

Note that
\mathcal{T}_{\mu,\boldsymbol{0}}=\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
and
\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{0}}=\Pi_{\mu}
.
Given a composite Pauli measurement
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, the symplectic vector
\mu
has included the information about the set of qubits
J
and the Pauli measurements applied to these qubits, so we don’t need to use the combination of
J
or
K
with
\boldsymbol{l}
, used in the last section, to label the measurement.

After applying the composite Pauli measurement
\mu
to a state
\rho
and obtaining the outcome
\boldsymbol{v}
, the reduced state is

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}}=\frac{\text{Tr}_{J}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})}{\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})},

(41)

which is just another form of Eq.(
24
).
As for the strategy operator
\Omega
, we can first rewrite the test operator
\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}
as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes\frac{\text{Tr}_{J}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})}{\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})}.

(42)

Note that the reduced states corresponding to some outcomes
\boldsymbol{v}
may be
0

42
), so we don’t need to worry the denominator. The test operator
\Omega_{\mu}
in Eq.(
42
) has another more useful form, as summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem V.1
.

Given a weight-
t
composite Pauli measurement
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, the non-trivial local Pauli measurements are applied to the set of qubits
J=\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}\}

the test operator
\Omega_{\mu}
has the following form

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2^{t}\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu})}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\mu}}T\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{J}(\rho T),

(43)

where

is an element of the stabilizer group
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
corresponding to
\mu
.

Proof.

Before deriving Eq.(
43

(1) If the reduced state
\text{Tr}_{J}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})\neq 0
in Eq.(

\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})\neq 0
and

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})=\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu}).

(44)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\cap\mathcal{S}|}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|}&\text{if}~(-\mathcal{T}_{\mu})\cap\mathcal{S}=\emptyset,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}
. Since
|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\cap\mathcal{S}|/|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|
is independent with
\boldsymbol{v}
, it’s easy to get the Eq.(

When
\rho
is a non-stabilizer state, it can be expanded as a linear combination of some stabilizer states as
\rho=\sum_{i}c_{i}\sigma_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}c_{i}\text{tr}(\sigma_{i}\Pi_{\mu})=\text{tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu}),

(47)

where we have used the fact that
\text{tr}(\sigma_{i}\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})=\text{tr}(\sigma_{i}\Pi_{\mu})
when
\text{tr}(\sigma_{i}\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})\neq 0
.
(2) The second result is the equation,

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}(-1)^{(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}=2^{t}\delta_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}.

(48)

It’s easy to see that if
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{w}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}(-1)^{\boldsymbol{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}1=2^{k},

(49)

otherwise

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}(-1)^{(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}=0,

(50)

because for any
\boldsymbol{v}
letting
(-1)^{(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}=1
, we can flip one bit of
\boldsymbol{v}
and get a vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
satisfying
(-1)^{(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}}=-1
.

To prove Eq.(
43
), we directly substitute Eq.(
39
) into Eq.(
42
) and derive

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2t}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}(-1)^{(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\frac{1}{\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu,\boldsymbol{v}})}

\displaystyle W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{u})\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}[\rho W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2t}\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu})}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}(-1)^{(\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\right)

\displaystyle W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{u})\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}[\rho W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2t}\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu})}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}2^{t}\delta_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{u})\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}[\rho W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{t}\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu})}\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{t}}W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w})\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}[\rho W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{t}\text{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu})}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\mu}}T\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}(\rho T)

(51)

where in the second and third lines, we have used the previous two results. The last line comes from the fact that
W_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w})
is just an element of
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
and the summation runs over all elements of
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
.
∎

V.2
The stabilizer state

When the target state
\rho
is a stabilizer state, the test operator can be further simplified.
As indicated in the proof of Theorem
V.1
, when
\rho
is a stabilizer state with stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
,

\mathop{\rm Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mu})=|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\cap\mathcal{S}|/|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|
,
then the test operator
\Omega_{\mu}
becomes,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\mu}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}T\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}(TS).

(52)

Because
S,T
are both Pauli strings,
\text{Tr}_{J}(ST)\neq 0
if and only if
S
and
T
have the same Pauli matrices at the qubit sites
\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{t}\}
, this motivates us to define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mu}:=\left\{S\in\mathcal{S}|\exists T\in\mathcal{T}_{\mu},\text{Tr}_{J}(TS)\neq 0\right\}.

(53)

The key point is that
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
is a stabilizer subgroup of
\mathcal{S}
, which has been known in
[
25
,
26
]
, then
\Omega_{\mu}
is the projector into the stabilizer code of
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
. This fact is summarized rigorously in the following corollary.

Corollary V.1
.

For a stabilizer state
\rho
with stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
, the test operator can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2^{n-t}|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}R=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}|}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}R.

(54)

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
, we can see that there is an one-to-one correspondence between the set of pairs
(T,S)\in\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\times\mathcal{S}
satisfying
\text{Tr}_{J}(TS)\neq 0
with the group
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
, then

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\mu}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}T\otimes\text{Tr}_{J}(TS)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{t}}{2^{n}|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n-t}|\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{\mu}|}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}R,

(55)

so
\Omega_{\mu}
is proportional to the projector into the stabilizer code of
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
.
Since
\Omega_{\mu}
is also a projector, known already in the last section, then we conclude that

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}|}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}R.

(56)

∎

From the above Corollary, we can easily derive the cardinality of the group,
|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}|=2^{n-t}|\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\cap\mathcal{S}|
.
Compared to the similar results in
[
8
]
, our results actually encompass theirs as a special case of
t=n
.
For
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{S}
and all elements of
\mathcal{S}
commute with each other, therefore all test operators
\Omega_{\mu}
commute with each other and can be diagonalizable simultaneously.

Assuming the stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
has
n
generators as
\{g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\}
, then we can construct a new stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
by the generators
\{(-1)^{w_{1}}g_{1},\cdots,(-1)^{w_{n}}g_{n}\}
with each
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, and the stabilizer state
|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle
is the common eigenstate of
g_{i}
with eigenvalue
(-1)^{w_{i}}
for
i=1,\cdots,n
. There are
2^{n}

such stabilizer states
|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle
and they form an orthonormal basis in the Hilbert space, called the
stabilizer basis
of
|\mathcal{S}\rangle
.
Then we consider the matrix elements of
\Omega_{\mu}
in the stabilizer basis,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}}|\Omega_{\mu}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}|}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}}|R|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}}\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{w}}

(57)

where in the second line we write
R=g_{1}^{v_{R,1}}\cdots g_{n}^{v_{R,n}}=\boldsymbol{g}^{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}}
with
\boldsymbol{v}_{R}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{g}^{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{v}_{R}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle
.
Hence, all
\Omega_{\mu}
’s are diagonal in the stabilizer basis of
\mathcal{S}
as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|

(58)

with
\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}
denoting the diagonal element.
Further, the diagonal elements of
\Omega_{\mu}
are either
0
or
1
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|\Omega_{\mu}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{if}~(-\mathcal{R}_{\mu})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}=\emptyset,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

which follows from Eq.(
V.1
) and the fact
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{S}
.

The special structure of
\Omega_{\mu}
can simplify the optimization problem defined in Eq.(
38
) drastically.
Having defined
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}
as the set of symplectic vector
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
with weight
t
, the strategy operator
\Omega
becomes

\displaystyle\Omega=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}}p_{\mu}\Omega_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\sum_{\mu\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}}p_{\mu}\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|,

(61)

with

\displaystyle\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{\mu\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}}p_{\mu}\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{\mu\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}}p_{\mu}\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|\Omega_{\mu}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle,

(62)

so the eigenvalues of
\Omega
are
\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}
’s, which are just the summation of those probabilities
p_{\mu}
such that the overlap of the corresponding test operator
\Omega_{\mu}
with the stabilizer state
|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle
doesn’t vanish.
Since the target state
|\mathcal{S}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}|
is the eigenstate of
\Omega
with eigenvalue
1
, then
\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{0}}=1
.
Hence, we only need to know which
\boldsymbol{w}
let
\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}
be the second-largest eigenvalue of
\Omega
. So the optimization problem Eq.(
38
) reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{\{p_{\mu}\}}\max_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n},\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\sum_{\mu\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}}p_{\mu}\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|\Omega_{\mu}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle.

(63)

The above optimization problem can be further reduced into a linear programming as done in
[
8
]
.

Although we have reduced the problem of finding the optimal strategy operator
\Omega
to the optimization problem Eq.(
63
), solving this problem is not an easy task as the number of
\mu
with weight
t
is exponentially large,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{t}3^{t}=\frac{3^{t}n!}{t!(n-t)!}.

(64)

Fortunately, the physical interesting target states have many symmetries, for example,
the GHZ state. The symmetries of the target state can reduce the number of test operators significantly. Furthermore, taking into the consideration of the symmetries of the target state can also boost the spectral gap of the strategy operator
[
6
,
9
]
.

Actually, there have been several explicit protocols for verifying the stabilizer state in
[
2
,
5
,
8
]
.
So compared with finding the optimal strategy operator, sometimes we are more interested in the special strategy operator
\Omega_{\text{u}}
corresponding to the uniform sampling, since we don’t need to solve an optimization problem.
For example, without considering the symmetries of the target state, we let all
p_{\mu}
’s are equal, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{t}3^{t}}=\frac{t!(n-t)!}{3^{t}n!},

(65)

then the eigenvalues of
\Omega_{\text{u}}
are

\displaystyle\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}=\frac{t!(n-t)!}{3^{t}n!}\sum_{\mu\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})_{t}}\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|\Omega_{\mu}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle.

(66)

The second largest
\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}
is the one such that the number of non-zero
\langle\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}|\Omega_{\mu}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle
is second-largest. Although for a general stabilizer state, the second-largest number is hard to compute, but for some special stabilizer state like GHZ state, it’s possible to obtain such a number analytically.

V.3
The GHZ state

In this subsection, we consider one type of state with very special structure, the GHZ state.
The
n
-qubit GHZ state is defined as

\displaystyle|\text{GHZ}_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle^{\otimes n}+|1\rangle^{\otimes n}),

(67)

it’s stabilizer group
\text{Stab}(|\mathrm{GHZ}_{n}\rangle)
is generated by

\displaystyle g_{1}=X^{\otimes n},\quad g_{a}=Z\otimes I^{\otimes(a-2)}\otimes Z\otimes I^{(n-a)}

(68)

for
2\leq a\leq n
, and the group elements of
\text{Stab}(|\mathrm{GHZ}_{n}\rangle)
can be classified into two types up to the permutation of
n
qubits:
Z^{\otimes 2i}\otimes I^{\otimes(n-2i)}
,
(-1)^{i}Y^{\otimes 2i}\otimes X^{\otimes(n-2i)}
for
i=0,1,\cdots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

It’s obvious that
|\text{GHZ}_{n}\rangle
is invariant under the action of any permutation
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
of
n
qubits, then if two different composite Pauli measurements are the same up to a permutation of
n
qubits, they don’t result in any difference in the physical sense, hence the probabilities
p_{\mu}
for sampling such two measurements should be the same.
However, for the labeling of
n
qubits, the test operators for the composite Pauli measurements being the same up to some permutations actually are different, so we need to take the average of them together.
More explicitly, two symplectic vectors
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
labeling two composite Pauli measurements are equivalent, denoted as
\mu\sim\mu^{\prime}
, if they can be transformed into each other by applying some permutation
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Each equivalence class
[\mu]
can be distinguished by the number of Pauli-
X
,
Y
and
Z
measurements,
(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})
. For each equivalence class
[\mu]
labelled by
(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})
, we sum over all corresponding test operators and divide it by the number of test operators in this class as

\displaystyle\Omega_{[\mu]}=\frac{1}{|[\mu]|}\sum_{\mu\in[\mu]}\Omega_{\mu}=\frac{m_{x}!m_{y}!m_{z}!}{\binom{n}{t}t!}\sum_{\mu\in[\mu]}\Omega_{\mu},

(69)

with
m_{x}+m_{y}+m_{z}=t
. Then the strategy operator becomes

\displaystyle\Omega=\sum_{[\mu]}p_{[\mu]}\Omega_{[\mu]}

(70)

and the diagonal value is written as

\displaystyle\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{[\mu]}p_{[\mu]}\frac{1}{|[\mu]|}\sum_{\mu\in[\mu]}\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}.

(71)

If we further consider the uniform sampling in the set of equivalence classes, then

p_{[\mu]}=2/[(t+1)(t+2)]
, for the number of equivalence classes is
\binom{t+2}{t}
.
Hence, the diagonal value becomes

\displaystyle\gamma_{t,\boldsymbol{w}}=\frac{2}{(t+2)(t+1)}\sum_{[\mu]}\frac{m_{x}!m_{y}!m_{z}!}{\binom{n}{t}t!}\sum_{\mu\in[\mu]}\gamma_{\mu,\boldsymbol{w}}.

(72)

V.3.1
3
-qubit GHZ state

Here we consider the
3
-qubit GHZ state, the generalization to the
n
-qubit case is straightforward.
When we apply the DPSO protocol
3
to the
3
-qubit GHZ state
|\text{GHZ}_{3}\rangle
, there are two different cases, corresponding to level-
1
and level-
2
.

Level-
1

According to the previous discussions, we need to compute all test operators
\Omega_{\mu}
with
\mu
having weight
2
.
For simplicity, we only compute the expressions of
\Omega_{K,\boldsymbol{l}}
with
K=\{3\}
(
J=\{1,2\}
) and
\boldsymbol{l}\in\{1,2,3\}^{2}
. The remaining test operators can be obtained through symmetry considerations via appropriate index permutations.
Having obtained all test operators, we can consider two strategy operators corresponding to two different uniform sampling.

•

The first one is the strategy operator corresponding to the naively uniform sampling, the expressions of whose eigenvalues are given in Eq.(
66
). In this case, we conclude the spectral gap is
4/9
.

•

The second one is the strategy operator corresponding to the uniform sampling over the equivalence classes. There are totally
6
classes:
(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})=(2,0,0)
,
(0,2,0)
,
(0,0,2)
,
(1,1,0)
,
(1,0,1)
or
(0,1,1)
. Finally, we conclude the spectral gap is
1/2
.

Level-
2

In the level-
2
protocol, we need to compute the test operators
\Omega_{\mu}
with
\mu
having weight
1
. As before, we consider two cases of uniform sampling. For the naive uniform sampling, we can conclude the spectral gap is
2/3
. While after considering the symmetries of
|\mathrm{GHZ}_{3}\rangle
, we obtain the spectral gap as
2/3
too.

V.3.2
n
-qubit GHZ state

The simple example of
|\mathrm{GHZ}_{3}\rangle
leads us to a general result about the spectral gaps of the strategy operators of two uniform sampling cases.

Theorem V.2
.

For two different uniform sampling over all symplectic vectors
\mu
with weight
t=(n-r)
and all symmetric equivalence classes
[\mu]
, the second-largest eigenvalue of the level-
r
strategy operator
\Omega
always corresponds to the eigenvector
\mathcal{S}_{(1,\boldsymbol{0}^{n-1})}
in the stabilizer basis of
|\mathrm{GHZ}_{n}\rangle
. For the two cases, the second-largest eigenvalues are
1-(2/3)^{n-r}
and
(n-r)/(n-r+2)
, then the spectral gaps are

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{n-r},\quad\frac{2}{n-r+2}

(73)

respectively.

The proof of this theorem is provided in the Appendix
B
.
It’s astonishing to find that after considering the symmetries of
|\mathrm{GHZ}_{n}\rangle
, the spectral gap of the strategy operator for the uniform sampling is boosted from exponentially small to the inverse linear for
n
. The weight of Pauli measurements used in the level-
r
protocol is
t=n-r
, then the spectral gap becomes
2/(t+2)
, which is independent of the qubit number
n
! We also see that increasing the level
r
doesn’t improve the sample complexity.

Compared with the known optimal protocols in the literature
[
2
,
7
,
8
]
, the sample complexity of our verification protocol is not optimal and grows linearly with the number of qubits
n
. However, this trade-off comes with the advantage that the protocol is easier to implement and can accommodate a broader class of quantum states simultaneously.
There are also some papers addressing the verification of GHZ states from the perspectives of self-testing and cryptography
[
27
,
28
]
. These approaches are based on a fundamentally different assumption that the quantum devices or participants in the protocol may be untrusted, then the sample complexity of these protocols are much larger than our protocols, since we assumes that the measurement outcomes are fully reliable.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new protocol, called DPSO protocol, for verifying of a pure state under the assumption of the i.i.d., which generalizes the ideas of the SOP introduced in
[
1
]
and
[
23
]
. The DPSO protocol inherits the key advantage of the SOP, namely the ability to verify almost all Haar-random pure states, while extending its applicability to a broader class of states in a more natural manner. Moreover, our protocol achieves lower sample complexity compared to the SOP. In particular, we demonstrate that the DPSO protocol exhibits a concise structure when applied to stabilizer states, just as
[
8
]
.
Besides, we also formulate the SOP in the form of hypothesis testing and discuss the relationships between the PLM framework and the SOP in details. The SOP deals with the problem of QSV in a new different view.
In general, the sample complexity of the DPSO (SOP) is worse than that of the PLM framework with respect to the infidelity
\epsilon
and the spectral gap
\nu(\Omega)
. The DPSO protocol assumes the states produced by the quantum device is i.i.d., while the PLM framework can deal with more general scenarios where the states can be adversarial.
However, the advantage of the DPSO protocol is that it’s possible to verify many types of states uniformly instead of designing different strategy operators in the PLM framework for different types of states.

In general, the verification of quantum states can be classified into two categories. The first involves verifying generic states in the Hilbert space, such as Haar random pure states. These generic states lack special structures, making verification challenging within the ordinary PLM framework, while the SOP or the DPSO protocol provides a general approach to address the problem.
The second category pertains to verifying states with special structures. These special states often possess many symmetries, necessitating the design of explicit optimal or near-optimal protocols. For many types of special states, there have been some very efficient or optimal protocols in the original PLM framework.
Even within the SOP formalism or our protocol, it is crucial to select appropriate probability distributions for different Pauli measurements or to solve an optimization problem, in order to get an optimal protocol.

Considering the current situation of the research of QSV, several challenges remain for the SOP or DPSO protocol to address in the future. First, it’s possible that this protocol can handle more special states, beyond stabilizer states to include non-stabilizer states. Second, it’s possible that the DPSO protocol with high level
r
can also verify almost all Haar random pure states, as the level-
1
SOP protocol
[
1
]
. Third, in the real-world experiments, the physical noise is unavoidable, then we need to consider the consequence of physical noise for the DPSO protocol. Fourth, it is also of interest to consider combining the SOP or DPSO protocols with self-testing techniques to address verification problems in scenarios similar to that discussed in
[
28
]
.
Hence, Addressing these challenges is an important direction for future research.
