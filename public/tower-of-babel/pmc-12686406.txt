Deep learning model of post-translational modification regulating liquid-liquid phase separation
Authors: Xiaokun Hong, Jiyang Lv, Zhengxin Li, Junjie Zhu, Jiayi Li, Mueed Ur Rahman, Ting Wei, Junxi Mu, Hai-Feng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) drives the formation of various membraneless organelles, which are crucial for biological processes and disease development. Despite the significant regulatory effects on LLPS of protein post-translational modifications (PTMs), specific data resource and predictor are still lacking. First, we constructed a well-curated database of PTM regulation on liquid-liquid Phase Separation (PTMPhaSe) (https://ptmphase.sjtu.edu.cn) that contains manually curated complete experimental evidence. Second, we developed graph neural network-based deep learning model (named PhosLLPS) to predict functional phosphorylation sites regulating LLPS, which achieved better identification performance (AUC = 0.9116) than four baseline models and the existing FuncPhos-SEQ method. Meanwhile, human proteome-scale predictions for functional phosphorylation sites were performed with PhosLLPS. PhosLLPS is now freely available in web server (https://ptmphase.sjtu.edu.cn/Predictor). By bridging the gap between PTM regulation and LLPS, these resources could contribute to a better understanding of the molecular function of LLPS and facilitate further drug development for LLPS-related diseases. Subject terms: Phosphoproteins, Phosphorylation

Introduction

Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) refers to the process by which proteins, DNA, RNA, and other macromolecules coalesce into liquid-like condensates1,2. Phase-separated condensates, also called membraneless organelles (MLOs), include stress granules3, P-bodies4, Cajal bodies5, spindle apparatus6, nuclear speckles7, etc., which are involved in numerous biological processes (such as gene expression4, protein degradation8, stress response3) and the development of disease9–11.

Multivalent weak interactions between proteins and other macromolecules drive the LLPS process, such as cation-pi, pi-stacking, pi-pi, electrostatic, and protein-RNA interactions12–14. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) could promote or inhibit LLPS by regulating structure, size, charge, hydrophobicity and other properties of phase-separating proteins15–17. For example, O-GlcNAc modification at T1306 of SynGAP inhibited LLPS by blocking its interaction with PSD-9518. Phosphorylation of multiple sites within the intrinsically disordered region of IFI16 activated LLPS, further driving innate immune signaling19.

Due to the critical biological role of LLPS, several LLPS-related databases have been developed in recent years, such as PhaSepDB20, DrLLPS21, LLPSDB22, PhaSePro12 and RNAgranuleDB23. PhaSepDB database only contains 4 types of PTMs and lacks information on PTM sites within protein structures20. DrLLPS database primarily annotates PTM information for LLPS-related proteins by integrating data from 8 different PTM databases21. The LLPSDB database offers experimentally validated PTM sites (such as methylation, phosphorylation, and acetylation) for LLPS-related proteins but lacks information regarding the regulatory effects of PTMs on LLPS22. The PhaSePro database provides experimental data on both RNA-dependent and PTM-dependent LLPS12. However, it remains challenging for users to browse datasets by specific PTM types and to comprehend how PTMs influence LLPS. Lastly, the RNAgranuleDB database curate proteins associated with stress granules (SGs) and P-bodies (PBs)23. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a specific resource for PTM regulation on LLPS.

Traditional experimental techniques are labor-intensive and cost-intensive, several computational methods for predicting functional phosphorylation sites have been developed, such as FuncPhos-SEQ24 and MMFuncPhos25. FuncPhos-SEQ incorporated phosphosite motif information and network features from protein-protein interactions (PPIs) to predict functional phosphorylation sites24. MMFuncPhos, a multimodal deep learning prediction framework, integrated evolutionary information from protein sequences with local graph structural features25. However, the functional phosphorylation dataset employed in current methodologies (encompassing diverse biological processes including signal transduction, enzyme regulation, and subcellular localization) inherently limits mechanistic specificity, such as regulating LLPS.

In this study, we have established a well-curated database named PTMPhaSe, specifically designed for PTM regulation on LLPS. PTMPhaSe contains complete experimental evidence extracted from literature and also integrates sequence and structure annotations. In addition, based on phosphorylation set, we also developed GNN-based deep learning model to predict functional phosphorylation sites regulating LLPS. This database and predictor could help researchers gain better understanding of the molecular mechanisms of LLPS and facilitate further drug development for LLPS-related diseases.

Results

Database content

The data in PTMPhaSe were manually curated from literature based on PubMed, PTM databases (Uniprot26, iPTMnet27, dbPTM28, EPSD29, CPLM30, PhosphoSitePlus31, BIOGRID32, and PTMD33), and LLPS databases (PhaSepDB20, DrLLPS21, LLPSDB22, PhaSePro12, and RNAgranuleDB23) (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). In total, 855 entries were compiled by integrating records from these databases.

Currently, PTMPhaSe contains 733 non-redundant PTM sites from 164 proteins across 14 organisms that regulate LLPS associated with 82 diseases (8 A). These LLPS proteins were classified into 18 types of MLOs21,34, in which droplet, stress granule, nuclear speckle, P-body, Promyelocytic Leukemia protein (PML) nuclear body, and spindle apparatus are the most abundant (Fig. 1B). A total of 14 types of PTMs have been experimentally identified as regulators of LLPS (Fig. 1C, G), mainly including phosphorylation, methylation, acetylation, SUMOylation, ADP-ribosylation, and ubiquitination. In addition, some rare modifications (such as glutathionylation, hypusination, NEDDylation, N-myristoylation, S-nitrosylation, and hydroxylation) have also been observed to regulate LLPS (Fig. 1C). The primary regulatory PTM sites are serine (S), lysine (K) and arginine (R), comprising 47.89%, 17.60%, and 17.19% of the total, respectively (Fig. 1D). Moreover, nearly 40% of PTM sites are located in low complexity domains (LCD) (Table S1, Supporting Information) and 82.69% are found in “Loop” (intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs)) (Table S2, Supporting Information), indicating that PTMs may regulate LLPS by modulating LLPS-related domains35.

To gain deeper insights into the intrinsic characteristics of PTM-modified LLPS proteins, a total of 164 LLPS proteins were categorized based on their biological functions (Fig. 1E). Many of these proteins are involved in nucleic acid binding and transcriptional regulation, indicating that LLPS proteins likely play a role in gene expression (Fig. 1E). The most frequently associated 10 diseases can be classified into two main categories: neurodegenerative diseases (Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) and neurodegenerative diseases) and genetic diseases (Fragile X syndrome (FXS)) (Fig. 1F).

PTMs have two regulatory roles on LLPS, including “promotion” and “inhibition”. Among the identified PTMs, phosphorylation is the most prevalent modification, with records of inhibition exceeding promotion (Fig. 1G), suggesting that phosphorylation generally inhibits LLPS. Similarly, methylation and acetylation also tend to inhibit LLPS (Fig. 1G). Conversely, ADP-ribosylation appears to promote LLPS (Fig. 1G). For ubiquitination, the number of cases promoting LLPS (number: 14) is nearly equal to those inhibiting it (number: 12) (Fig. 1G). Given that PTMs can occur at various residues, the regulatory effects of each residue were further examined (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). Specifically, phosphorylation of serine residues, methylation of arginine residues, and acetylation of lysine residues predominantly exert inhibitory effects on LLPS (Fig. S2A–C). In contrast, SUMOylation of lysine residues primarily promotes LLPS (Fig. S2D).

Performance evaluation of PhosLLPS

Based on above dataset, we presented a computational model, PhosLLPS, designed to predict functional phosphorylation sites that regulate liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). This model leveraged protein language model and Transformer-based Graph Neural Network to extract features. Protein sequences could be embedded by pre-trained protein language models. Optimizing the window size of the protein sequence could notably assist in feature selection and improve prediction performance36,37. To determine the optimal window size, we conducted experiments across a range of sizes: 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 21, and 41 (Fig. 2A). The window size of 15 achieved the best performance compared to the other sizes (Fig. 2A). We also compared the predictive performance of four pre-trained protein language models, including EMS2, ProtT5, TAPE and ProtBert (Fig. 2B). The appropriate highest ACC (value: 0.9057), AUROC (value: 0.9667), AUPR (value: 0.9688), F1-score (value: 0.9037), MCC (value: 0.8133) and Precision (value: 0.9338) were achieved when using ESM2 embedding features (Fig. 2B). The AUROC of final model (named PhosLLPS) in validation set was 0.9116 (Figs. 2C and S3, Supporting Information). To assess the contribution of graph neural network (GNN), an ablation experiment was conducted (Figs. 2D and S4, Supporting Information). Compared to PhosLLPS, the AUROC, AUPR, and MCC of w/o GNN decreased by 0.0592, 0.0568, and 0.1413, respectively (Fig. 2D). To further evaluate our PhosLLPS method, four baseline deep learning models (GATConv, GCNConv, Transformer, and LSTM) and FuncPhos-SEQ were employed using same features as PhosLLPS model (Table 1 and Fig. S5, Supporting Information). The PhosLLPS model outperformed the other predictive models across six widely used metrics (Table 1).

Human proteome-scale prediction for functional phosphorylation sites

We first performed a length distribution analysis of the 18,308 human proteome proteins. The results showed that most proteins contain fewer than 1000 residues, with only a small portion of proteins having a length exceeding 1500 residues (Fig. 3A). Among all three types of phosphorylation sites, the number of functional phosphorylation sites was significantly fewer than that of non-functional phosphorylation sites (Fig. 3B). In addition, the predicted probability of functional sites and non-functional sites were distributed from 0.72 to 0.96 and 0.76–0.98, respectively (Fig. 3C), which suggests the prediction model can effectively distinguish between functional and non-functional sites. We also analyzed the distribution of pLDDT of these functional and non-functional sites. Functional sites are more frequently located in low confidence regions (pLDDT <30 and 30<pLDDT <50) than non-functional sites, which indicates functional sites are more likely to be located in the dynamic regions or intrinsic disordered regions of proteins. This pLDDT distribution of two kinds of phosphorylation sites is also in accord with Xie et al. work25. Finally, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the relationship between functional phosphorylation sites and diseases using data from the PhosphoSitePlus31 and PTMD databases33. The results showed that functional phosphorylation sites are significantly enriched in disease-related sites (Fig. 3F). This finding further supports the close relationship between protein phosphorylation modifications, liquid-liquid phase separation, and related diseases, providing potential targets for disease diagnosis and drug development.

Database interfaces of PTMPhase

Quick search and advanced search were implemented on the ‘Home’ and “Search” page, respectively. On the “Home” page, users can easily search the database by entering keywords such as Gene, Uniprot, PTM, or Organism. The “Search” page allows users to apply one or more filter conditions (Fig. 4A). Search results are displayed in a table format, with sections for “Organism,” “Gene,” “Uniprot,” “PTM,” “PMID” and “Detail”. Detailed results can be viewed by clicking the “Show” button. For browse function, users can easily browse data by specifying “Organism”, “PTM”, or “MLOs” class, with 14 subclasses for “Organism”, 18 subclasses for “PTM”, and 14 subclasses for “MLOs” (Fig. 4B).

The typical result page consists of four sections, including protein overview, protein features, PTM on liquid-liquid phase separation, and high-quality validated protein-protein interactions (Fig. 4C). Basic information of protein, such as gene name, protein name, organism, is provided in “Protein overview” section. Additionally, users can rotate, translate, zoom, and switch between different structures. Protein disorder features (IUPred2A score) and structural local confidence (pLDDT score) are displayed in the “protein features” section. The ‘PTM on liquid-liquid phase separation’ section consists of three parts, including complete experimental evidence, sequence, and structure annotations. Protein-protein interactions involving LLPS proteins were annotated using the HINT database38.

Predictor interfaces of PTMPhase

The above PhosLLPS prediction model was implemented in PTMPhase (https://ptmphase.sjtu.edu.cn/Predictor) to permit the interactive prediction of functional phosphorylation sites (Fig. 5). The complete task request includes protein sequence, positions of phosphorylation sites, email address and job name (Fig. 5A). Once the job is submitted, users can check status in “Queue” page and receive an email informing submission successfully (Fig. 5B). Once the job is completed, an email containing unique result links (URLs) will be sent to the user’s mailbox immediately. The typical results are presented in a tabular format, including columns for position, residue, prediction (pred), and probability (prob). Total running time is also provided (Fig. 5C).

Case study

Case study 1

RNA-binding protein FUS, a well-known LLPS protein, which can undergo self- or partner-dependent liquid-liquid phase separation16,39. Users can search PTMPhase database to explore all experimentally identified PTM sites that regulate LLPS of FUS. Full-length human FUS contains two domains crucial for liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS): the Low complexity domain (LC) (residues: 17–104, 106–261) and the RNA recognition motif (RRM): (residues: 286–367)40. A total of 45 PTM sites of FUS have been experimentally validated to regulate LLPS, of which 23 sites and 2 sites (K315 and K316)41 are located in the LC region and RRM domains, respectively. In addition, glutathionylation at C447 could enhance LLPS of FUS42, while PAD-mediated citrullination at R514 and R518 can inhibit LLPS through affecting LLPS regions directly (residues: 1–526)43 (Fig. 6A).

Case study 2

TDP-43, another canonical LLPS protein, participates in various stages of RNA biogenesis and processing44. At present, total 15 phosphorylation sites have been identified to regulate LLPS45–48, including S48, S332, S333, S373, S375, S379, S387, S389, S393, S395, S403, S404, S407, S409 and S410 (Fig. 6B). The PhosLLPS model has successfully predicted above 15 functional sites with high probability (Table S6, Supporting Information). Among the 49 phosphorylation sites of TDP-43, the model identified an additional 29 potential regulatory sites and 5 non-functional sites (Table S6, Supporting Information). Phosphorylation of S92, S273, and S292 has been linked to breast cancer49, while phosphorylation of T88 and S91 shows a strong association with cervical cancer50. Additionally, phosphorylation of T8, T153, S305, and S317 is extensively investigated in neuroblastoma models51–53. Collectively, these nine sites represent potential therapeutic targets, as their phosphorylation modifications may contribute to disease progression by regulating LLPS.

In summary, PhosLLPS demonstrates significant potential in identifying novel phosphorylation sites involved in regulatory processes, thereby elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying LLPS and facilitating the development of targeted therapeutic interventions.

Discussion

The dynamic imbalance of LLPS is a key pathological mechanism in neurodegenerative diseases and cancer42. Post-translational modifications of proteins, as critical regulatory factors54, still lack systematic database and predictive tools. To address above challenges, we developed PTMPhase database (Fig. 7) and PhosLLPS method (Fig. 8) in this work. PTMPhaSe is the first comprehensive database of experimentally validated PTM regulation on LLPS. And the PhosLLPS, is the GNN-based deep learning method for predicting functional phosphorylation sites regulating LLPS.

Data collection and annotation of PTMPhaSe.

All PTMPhaSe data were curated manually from literatures based on PTM databases (A) and LLPS databases (B). Sequence (C) and structure annotations (D) have been systematically integrated into the PTMPhaSe.

The overall architecture of the PhosLLPS.

Node features (ESM2 embeddings) and edge features (the adjacency matrix) were integrated to construct the graphs. These graphs were then processed through TransformerConv layers and fed into an MLP to generate the final predictions.

To better understand the role of PTMs in regulating LLPS, “enzyme-substrate-disease” relationships have been established in our database. Targeting PTM enzymes of LLPS proteins directly might present a novel therapeutic approach for LLPS-associated diseases55. The regulatory effects of various PTM types and residues on LLPS have been carefully curated from the literature (Fig. 1, Figure S1, S2, Supporting Information). However, the regulatory tendencies of PTMs might be potentially biased due to the well-studied LLPS proteins. We have introduced a mode of PTM function, encompassing both direct and allosteric effects by correlating PTM sites with LLPS regions. Notably, 92% of PTM sites were located in LLPS regions, suggesting that the most of PTM sites regulate LLPS through direct effects on the multivalent weak interactions in these regions14. The secondary structures and associated domains of the PTMs were also analyzed (Tables S1–S2, Supporting Information).

PTMs can dynamically modulate LLPS in a time- and space-dependent manner under both physiological and pathological conditions17,56. And multiple PTM sites might be functionally associated through cross-talk pattern based on spatial proximity57. Thus, by examining the spatially labeled positions of PTM sites, researchers can further investigate the additive, cooperative, or competitive effects of PTM regulation on LLPS within LLPS proteins and between LLPS proteins and their partner proteins58. RNA, as an LLPS partner, can also influence LLPS through its N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification59. Exploring the functional associations between PTMs of LLPS proteins and m6A modifications in RNAs for the regulation of LLPS represents a promising research avenue.

Several force fields and models have been developed for LLPS, including Martini60, stickers and spacers61, HyRes model62, and Coarse-grained (CG) model63, which could capture multiple weak interactions and the tendencies of secondary structures. However, there is still a gap in simulation models that combine both modification specificity and generalization capacity64. Our PTM data could provide a foundation for developing a PTM-specific LLPS simulation model, enabling adjustments to PTM-related parameters and extensive testing. Additionally, the atomic-level regulatory mechanisms of PTMs on LLPS could be further explored using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and Markov state models (MSM)65,66.

We proposed a first GNN-based deep learning model, named PhosLLPS, for predicting functional phosphorylation sites regulating LLPS. The feature space of the protein sequence is derived from the pre-trained protein language model ESM-2 and subsequently fed into a GNN for feature extraction and classification (Fig. 8). PhosLLPS has demonstrated excellent performance on the validation set, achieving an AUC of 0.9116 (Fig. 2C). Additionally, graph-based models (PhosLLPS, GATConv, and GCNConv) demonstrated better performance than non-graph-based models (Transformer and LSTM) (Table 1), highlighting the advantage of graph neural networks in feature extraction and modeling. For FuncPhos-SEQ, the ACC, AUROC, and AUPR were nearly 0.5 (Table 1), suggesting that this state-of-the-art method cannot effectively distinguish LLPS-associated phosphorylation sites. For convenience, we also developed a user-friendly web server (https://ptmphase.sjtu.edu.cn/Predictor) (Fig. 5). Hub phosphorylation sites and LLPS-associated disease targets can be obtained by constructing phosphorylation site-kinase network67 based on large-scale prediction of our web server.

PTMPhaSe can be improved in the following aspects. First, experimental evidence of RNA modifications that modulate LLPS will be added. Second, records of PTMs on partner proteins that regulate the LLPS of target proteins will be included. Third, the phenomena (such as size, number, composition, and distribution) and underlying mechanisms of MLOs affected by PTMs will be detailed. Fourth, prediction structures in the PTMPhase database will be replaced when high-quality experimentally-determined structures are released in the PDB database. We are committed to maintaining and updating the database with newly identified evidence of PTM regulation on LLPS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have established PTMPhaSe, the first comprehensive database of experimentally validated post-translational modification (PTM) regulation on liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). This database contains 733 non-redundant PTM sites representing 14 PTM types from 164 proteins across 14 organisms. Subsequently, we developed PhosLLPS, a graph neural network (GNN)-based deep learning model, which demonstrated robust predictive performance in 5-fold cross-validation. Compared with four baseline models and FuncPhos-SEQ, our model exhibited superior capability in identifying functional phosphorylation sites that regulate LLPS. To facilitate accessibility, we have integrated the PhosLLPS model into a user-friendly web server (https://ptmphase.sjtu.edu.cn/Predictor). We anticipate that this database and predictor will serve as valuable resources for both biologists and bioinformaticians, enhancing our understanding of LLPS mechanisms and supporting the development of PTM-targeted therapeutics for LLPS-related diseases.

Methods

Data collection and curation

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) play two regulatory roles on liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS): “promotion” and “inhibition”18,19,68–73. The data in the PTMPhaSe database were manually curated from the literature. The literature mining process was conducted in three ways: (i) retrieving LLPS records and associated literature from 8 well-curated PTM databases (Uniprot26, PhosphoSitePlus31, dbPTM28, EPSD29, CPLM30, PTMD33, iPTMnet27, BIOGRID32); (ii) retrieving PTM records and associated literature from 5 well-curated LLPS databases (PhaSepDB20, DrLLPS21, LLPSDB22, PhaSePro12, RNAgranuleDB23); (iii) retrieving relevant literature from PubMed (up to July 2024) using keywords and combinations: liquid-liquid phase separation, phase separation, phase transition, Cajal body, stress granule, P-body, processing body, nuage, P granule, nuclear speckle, PTM, post translational modification, modification, phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, SUMOylation, ubiquitylation and glycosylation (Fig. 7).

Nearly 6000 relevant references were initially obtained and subsequently narrowed down to 224 through carefully checking complete experimental evidence. The experimental evidence included upstream enzymes, PTM types and sites, effects, LLPS regions, LLPS partners, membraneless organelles (MLOs), detection methods (Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), turbidity assay, sedimentation assay, etc.), and LLPS-associated diseases. PTMs were included in the dataset only if they occurred on driver proteins and had a demonstrated effect on LLPS, either promoting or inhibiting it. PTMs occurring on client or regulator proteins, or those on driver proteins without any measurable impact on LLPS, were excluded.

Protein sequence and structural feature analysis

Protein sequence analysis

All canonical protein sequences were downloaded from Uniprot database26. And the sequence window (upstream and downstream ten residues around the PTM sites)36,74 was further extracted using the Python script. Disordered protein regions were predicted using IUPred2a software75, which assigned a disorder propensity score to each residue. Functional domains were annotated using the SMART database76. Additionally, the action modes of PTMs were defined based on their locations relative to LLPS regions: (i) direct effects: PTM sites located within LLPS regions; (ii) allosteric effects: PTM sites located outside LLPS regions.

Protein structure annotation

Protein structures were downloaded from PDB database77 and AlphaFold protein structure database78,79. First, proteins with PTM sites were searched in the PDB database according to the following criteria: (i) structures must contain PTM sites; (ii) single-chain protein structure was preferred; (iii) among multiple single-chain structures, the one with the highest resolution was chosen. Second, the unmatched proteins with PTM sites were obtained using the AlphaFold database. Each residue in these AlphaFold structures was assigned a predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) value, which indicates the local confidence of the prediction78,79.

Secondary structure analysis

The secondary structure propensity of PTM sites was determined using the Dictionary of Protein Secondary Structure algorithm (DSSP)80. For simplification, the eight secondary structure categories were further classified into four types74: (i) Helix: G (helix), I (π-helix), H (α-helix); (ii) Sheet: B (β-bridge), E (β-sheet); (iii) Turn: T (turn); (iv) Loop: S (bend) and C (coil).

Dataset preparation for model construction

The construction of the positive dataset is mainly based on the following three sources: (i) Extracting functional phosphorylation sites from our constructed database; (ii) matching the protein phosphorylation modification database (Uniprot26, PhosphoSitePlus31, dbPTM28) with the LLPS regions of proteins to extract functional phosphorylation sites related to the regulation of LLPS; (iii) matching the protein phosphorylation modification database with the LLPS regions of proteins predicted by dSCOPE software81 to extract functional phosphorylation sites related to the regulation of LLPS. All other phosphorylation sites in the same protein were regarded as negative sites. By this way, we obtained 24,393 functional sites and 25,594 non-functional sites. In order to avoid overfitting, similar sites were also removed by PSI-CD-HIT with a threshold of 40%36. Finally, we have obtained 13,670 functional sites and 13,238 non-functional sites. The entire dataset was randomly split into a train set and a validation set with a 9:1 ratio using stratified sampling. The train set contains 12,303 functional sites and 11,914 non-functional sites, while the validation set includes 1367 functional sites and 1,324 non-functional sites (Table S3, Supporting Information). For deep learning, functional and non-functional sites were labeled as “1” and “0”, respectively.

Model construction and evaluation

Model architecture

We presented a computational model, PhosLLPS, designed to predict functional phosphorylation sites that regulate liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). This model leveraged a Transformer-based Graph Neural Network (TransformerConv)37 to extract features. The overall architecture of PhosLLPS was illustrated in Fig. 8. In a protein sequence graph, each node corresponds to an amino acid residue, and each edge represents the relationship between pairs of residues (residual contacts). First, the complete protein sequence (with L amino acids) is input into the pre-trained protein language model ESM2 to obtain features with dimensions L × 1280. These features are further processed to extract 15 × 1280-dimensional features as node features (where 15 represents the 7 residues upstream and downstream of the phosphorylation site). A full adjacency matrix of size 15 × 15 is used as the edge features of the graph. Next, the graph is processed through three TransformerConv layers, each utilizing a self-attention mechanism. Finally, the processed features are fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to generate the final prediction results.

Model training and validation

The deep neural network was built with PyTorch library and trained on Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6248 CPU. The model was trained on train set and evaluated on validation set. The model was trained for 100 epochs to minimize the CrossEntropyLoss.

Gaussian noise was also introduced to the features to enhance the generalization and robustness of the model82. Five-fold cross-validation of the training set was used to optimize hyperparameters, such as learning rate, batch size, and dropout rate (Table S5, Supporting Information). Finally, we selected the optimal set of parameters as the parameters of the model, with batch size of 128, TransformerConv layers of 3, noise std = 0.12, learning rate of 1.4e-5, weight decay of 1.0e-3, dropout rate1 of 0.3, dropout rate2 of 0.1, and dropout rate3 of 0.5.

Evaluation metrics

Six typical classification metrics were used to evaluate the predictive performance, including accuracy (ACC), the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR), F1-score, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and precision.

where TP, TN, FP, and FN represent true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively

State-of-the-art (SOTA) method

FuncPhos-SEQ24 represents state-of-the-art (SOTA) method for predicting universal functional phosphorylation sites. To evaluate the performance of FuncPhos-SEQ, all protein sequences and corresponding phosphorylation sites from the validation set should be submitted to the web server (http://funcptm.jysw.suda.edu.cn/seq). Following these prediction processes, standard evaluation metrics can be systematically calculated.

Database and web implementation

The web interfaces were developed using JavaScript, HTML, and CSS. The web frame was built with Bootstrap 4 and Flask. 3Dmol.js plugin was used to visualize and manipulate protein structures83. All tables and figures on the website were rendered with the ECharts plugin84.
