Hematological toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors: a pharmacovigilance study
Authors: An-Ju Tan, Jun-Li Lu, Can-Xia Li, Wan-Ying Liu, Yu Yan, Can-Hong Wang, Dun-Chang Mo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveImmune-related adverse events (irAEs) have limited the widespread clinical application of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Hematological toxicities are rare but serious irAEs. This study comprehensively analyzes ICI-related hematological adverse events using the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS).database.MethodsWe extracted ICI adverse events from the FAERS database between January 2004 and January 2025. Disproportionality analyses using four algorithms identified hematological signals. To eliminate the influence of combined medication, we separately conducted disproportionality analysis on all ICI reports and on reports of ICI monotherapy. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to assess the risk factors for hematological adverse events.ResultsOf all ICI reports in FAERS, 3.73% were for hematological adverse events. Among the 14 detected hematological signals, pure red cell aplasia, immune-mediated pancytopenia, acquired hemophilia, and Evans syndrome are rare and severe irAE. Among 6,259 cases of ICI-related hematological AEs, approximately 88.68% were reported by healthcare professionals, and fatal outcomes were reported in 16.49% of cases. The median onset time is 20 days.The median age of patients was 67 years (IQR: 58–73). Male was associated with a significantly lower risk of hematological toxicity compared to females (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.90–0.96; P < 0.05). Patients aged ≥ 65 years also had a reduced risk compared to those < 65 years (65–74 years: OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.62–0.67; ≥75 years: OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.72–0.79; both P < 0.05 ). ICI combination with chemotherapy significantly increased the risk of hematological toxicity (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 3.44–3.69; P < 0.05), while combination with targeted agents reduced the risk (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.69–0.76; P < 0.05).ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is among the first studies to utilize real-world big data for disproportionality analysis focused on ICI monotherapy, uncovering rare and severe hematological irAE. Multivariable logistic regression revealed that following ICI treatment, females are at a higher risk than males for hematological toxicities; likewise, ICI–chemotherapy increases the risk compared to ICI monotherapy. Clinicians should pay more attention to these rare irAEs and identify and treat them as early as possible, especially in patients with high-risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15391-3.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor, FAERS, Pharmacovigilance, Hematological toxicities

Introduction

Immunotherapy has transformed cancer treatment for numerous malignancies. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) against CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1 function by inhibiting the signaling pathways between these receptors and their ligands., thereby enabling T cells to recognize and eliminate cancer cells [1]. Because of their efficacy, ICIs are now therapy for a number of cancers, including various solid tumors and hematological malignancies, with indications expanding significantly over the last decade [2]. These include melanoma, non-renal cell carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer, colorectal cancer (MSI-H/dMMR), endometrial carcinoma, cervical cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, biliary tract cancer, and classical Hodgkin lymphoma. For example, pembrolizumab is the standard first-line treatment for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with high PD-L1 expression (≥ 50%) [3, 4]. Furthermore, nivolumab in combination with ipilimumab has been approved by the FDA for use in treating various cancers. Specifically, it was granted accelerated approval on March 10, 2020, for hepatocellular carcinoma in patients previously treated with sorafenib [5], and later approved as a first-line treatment for unresectable malignant pleural mesothelioma on October 2, 2020 [6]. The FDA also approved atezolizumab (March 2019) and durvalumab (March 2020) for use in combination with chemotherapy as first-line treatment for patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer [7]. In October 2022, tremelimumab plus durvalumab was approved for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma in adults [8].

However, the widespread application of ICIs in oncology brings a spectrum of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) [9–12]. Although most irAEs manifest with mild to moderate symptoms, their severe forms can cause irreversible organ failure or life-threatening complications, thereby presenting major challenges to the clinical application [13]. Although these irAEs primarily impact organs such as the skin, digestive tract, endocrine glands, lungs and liver, they can affect any organ, and the hematological system is no exception [14].

Hematological immune-related adverse events (hem-irAEs) often involve the erythropoietic, leukopoietic, or thrombopoietic lineages and may result in a wide range of clinical manifestations. Among autoimmune disorders affecting the hematopoietic system, autoimmune hemolytic anemia and idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura are the most prevalent [15, 16]. The incidence of hem-irAEs is approximately 0.04–3.6%, with a mortality rate of about 14%, It is a rare but serious irAEs [15, 17]. A retrospective study including 20,128 patients showed that among 82 irAE-related deaths, hematological causes ranked as the fourth most common cause of death (5/82), following respiratory failure, cardiovascular events, and infection [18].

Hematological AEs induced by ICIs are rare but pose potentially fatal risks. Therefore, early detection and optimized management are crucial. While reports on common irAEs are abundant [19–21], reports focusing specifically on hematological irAEs remain relatively scarce.Therefore, using the FAERS database, we used disproportionality analysis for signal detection and multivariable logistic regression to explore risk factors and to provide an in-depth understanding of ICI-related hematological AEs.

Methods

Data source

We utilized data from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System database to perform a pharmacovigilance analysis of ICI-associated hematological AEs. The FAERS is a publicly accessible reporting system that collects adverse events from patients, healthcare providers, and pharmaceutical companies [22]. The data for this study was downloaded from the FAERS database on the official website of the U.S. FDA. As the downloaded data did not contain personal identifiers, ethical approval was not required. We downloaded 85 quarters of data (Q1 2004 to Q1 2025) from the FAERS database and imported it into SAS 9.4 for further processing and analysis.

Data processing procedure

We obtained 22,775,812 reports from the FAERS database. Because of the presence of numerous duplicate reports in FAERS, we performed an initial deduplication by removing reports listed in the deletion files downloaded from FAERS. Subsequently, in accordance with FDA recommendations, we executed further deduplication: for reports with identical CASEIDs but different FDA_DT values, we retained the report with the latest FDA_DT; for reports which both CASEID and FDA_DT were identical, we retained the report with the largest PRIMARYID. Ultimately, the number of reports was reduced to 18,937,868. After this deduplication, we encoded the reported adverse events using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 28.0). The drugs were standardized using the World Health Organization Drug Dictionary (WHO-DD, March 2025version).

Due to the allowance of free-text entries when reporting adverse events in FAERS, this results in a great deal of anomalous and invalid data [23, 24] To ensure data quality, we performed deep cleaning in addition to the routine cleaning. For reports with identical gender, age, weight, event date, adverse reaction, country, and drug, we kept only the record with the largest PRIMARYID. We excluded reports where the adverse event onset time preceded the usage time of the primary suspected drug. Additionally, with the aim of focusing the analysis on PTs directly descriptive of drug adverse events, we chose to exclude those PTs that do not directly describe such events, such as ‘Off label uses’, ‘Product use in unapproved indication’, ‘Drug ineffective’, ‘no adverse event’, ‘Product storage error’, ‘Product dose omission issue’, etc.). After undergoing deep data cleaning, 14,762,592 records remain. ICIs, including anti-PD-1 drugs (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab, and dostarlimab), anti-PD-L1 drugs (durvalumab, atezolizumab and avelumab), and anti-CTLA-4 drugs (ipilimumab and tremelimumab), were employed as keywords to extract reports of adverse events. Signal mining

In pharmacovigilance research, disproportionality analysis serves as a critical methodological approach for identifying potential drug-event associations [25]. A potential signal emerges when the occurrence rate of a specific AE for a specific drug is significantly higher than the background incidence for other drugs in the database and meets a certain thresholds or criteria [26]. Using AE reports from the entire FAERS database as the comparator, we employed four disproportionality analysis algorithms to detect potential hematological AE signals : the reporting odds ratio (ROR) [27], proportional reporting ratio (PRR) [28], Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) [29], and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) [30]. All algorithms were constructed using a fourfold table. Each algorithm possesses unique advantages, and no single algorithm is universally superior to the others [26]. To improve the accuracy of signals and eliminate some false positive signals, PTs of hematological AEs could be defined as ICI-related hematological AEs when they met al.l four algorithm criteria.The calculation methods and thresholds for these algorithms are presented in Table 1. To eliminate the influence of combined medication, we separately conducted disproportionality analysis on all ICI reports and on reports of ICI monotherapy. ICI-related hematological AEs were identified for further analysis (N = 6259).

Descriptive analysis

Following screening, we analyzed the clinical features of ICI-related hematological AEs. This analysis included gender, age, ICI treatment strategy, onset time to AEs, indications, concomitant medication use, serious outcomes and other clinical features.The time-to-onset was calculated as the number of days from ICI initiation to the hematological AE event. Serious outcomes comprised life-threatening events, hospitalization, disability, and death [31].

Statistical analysis

This study employed multivariable logistic regression to explore the risk factors of hematological AEs, such as age, sex, use of combination chemotherapy or targeted therapy. Because FAERS relies on spontaneous reporting, missing values in the sex and age fields are common.Among the 382,327 reports, age data was missing for 87,842 entries (22.98%), and sex data was missing for 34,341 entries (8.98%). Directly deleting records containing missing values could reduce statistical power. To improve the reliability of the results, we used missForest imputation (with the default convergence settings of the missForest package in R.) to fill in the missing values of age and gender prior to conducting multivariable logistic regression, additionally, to ensure the robustness of the findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis without imputation.The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the median time to onset among groups with ICI-related hematological AEs. In this study, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all hypothesis tests were two-tailed. Data analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.3.1).

Results

The occurrence of hematological AEs in ICI users in the FAERS database

Utilizing the FAERS database, we conducted an analysis of hematological AEs in patients receiving ICIs from Q1 2004 to Q1 2025. Following data deduplication and deep cleaning, we identified reports where ICIs were the primary suspected drug and extracted hematological AE cases. Figure 1 illustrates the data processing steps. Among all ICIs reports, hematological AEs accounted for 3.73% (14,254/382,327). Although the proportion of hematological AEs remains relatively small each year (ranging from 2.33% to 4.72% between 2011 and 2024), an upward trend has been observed (Fig. 2). This suggests that hematological AEs constitute a significant part of irAEs and cannot be overlooked.

On reports of ICI monotherapy, a total of 14 hematological signals were detected (Table 3), including immune thrombocytopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, pure red cell aplasia, immune-mediated pancytopenia, acquired hemophilia and Evans syndrome. Among these, immune-mediated pancytopenia had the lowest case count but the highest ROR value. Figure 3 shows a comparison between the two sets of signals.

Descriptive analysis of ICI-related hematological AEs

We screened FAERS for ICI reports and identified 6,259 cases of ICI-related hematological AEs. Statistical analysis of their clinical characteristics (Table 4) revealed that male cases (N = 3,269; 52.23%) were slightly more than female, and the median age was 67 years ( [IQR] 58–73; data available for 5,218 reports). Notably, Cases in Japan constituted the largest proportion (N = 2,472; 39.50%). The reasons for this geographic disparity warrant further investigation but may relate to differences in pharmacovigilance reporting practices or population-specific factors. Fatal outcomes occurred in 16.49% of cases (N = 1,032). Anti-PD-1 agents comprised the majority of ICI treatments (N = 3,447; 55.07%), followed by anti-PD-L1 agents (N = 1,838; 29.37%). ICIs plus chemotherapy were reported in 48.39% of cases (N = 3,029), and ICIs plus targeted therapy were reported in 14.84% (N = 929). Approximately 87.04% of the cases received single-agent immunotherapy, with the remaining 12.96% undergoing dual immunotherapy regimens. The predominant indications among these cases were non-small cell lung cancer (N = 889; 14.20%); triple negative breast cancer (N = 437; 6.98%); and small cell lung cancer (N = 395; 6.31%). Approximately 88.68% of cases were reported by healthcare professionals (Physician: 58.96%; Pharmacist: 22.19%; Other health professional: 7.53%), suggesting generally high reliability of the original reports.

Time to onset analysis

Among the 3,312 reported cases of ICI-related hematological AEs with documented time-to-onset, over 60% (2016/3312) occurred within the first month after ICIs initiation, with a median onset time of 20 days (IQR: 8–63). Significant differences in time-to-onset were observed across treatment regimens (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A). The median onset time was longest with anti-CTLA-4 therapy (35 days, IQR:17–62) and shortest with anti-PD-L1 therapy (14 days, IQR: 9–46). Patients receiving ICIs plus chemotherapy exhibited a 13-day shorter median onset time than those without chemotherapy (14 days vs. 27 days, p < 0.05; Fig. 4B). Similarly, patients receiving ICIs plus targeted therapy showed an 8-day shorter median onset time compared to those without targeted therapy (13 days vs. 21 days, P < 0.05; Fig. 4C). A significant difference was also observed between the single-agent and dual-agent ICIs regimens. The median onset time was significantly earlier in the dual regimen (19 days, IQR: 8-62.2) compared to the single-agent regimen (28 days, IQR: 8-73.2; P < 0.05). However, no significant difference in median onset time was observed between fatal and non-fatal ICI-related hematological AEs (P = 0.14; Fig. 4D).

Influencing factors for ICI-related hematological AEs

The results were consistent between multiple logistic regression following MissForest imputation and following deletion of missing values (Fig. 5). Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that sex, age group, combined use of chemotherapy, and combined use of targeted drugs are potential risk factors of hematological toxicity (Fig. 5). A significantly lower risk was observed in male patients compared to female patients. (OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.90–0.96; P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Patients aged ≥ 65 years also showed reduced risk compared to those < 65 years (65 ≤ age < 75: OR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.62–0.67; age ≥ 75: OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.72–0.79; both P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Combination therapy with targeted agents decreased risk (OR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.69–0.76; P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A), while combination with chemotherapy significantly increased risk (OR = 3.56, 95% CI 3.44–3.69; P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A).

When analyzing the risk of hematological toxicity for “ICI combined targeted therapy,” we found that the cases involving ICI combined with targeted drugs included 41 types of targeted drugs, such as angiogenesis inhibitors (e.g., bevacizumab), EGFR/HER2 inhibitors (e.g., cetuximab, osimertinib, gefitinib, trastuzumab), and MAPK/ERK pathway inhibitors (e.g., dasatinib). Discussion

Although AEs induced by ICIs have been extensively reported and studied [19, 32], Nevertheless, studies investigating hem-irAEs based on large-scale real-world data remain relatively limited. The management of hem-irAEs is challenging and carries a high mortality risk [15]. Therefore, This study conducted an in-depth investigation on hem-irAEs using the FAERS database.To eliminate the influence of combined medication, we conducted disproportionality analysis on all ICI reports and on reports of ICI monotherapy separately. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to perform disproportionality analysis on ICI monotherapy reports, which identified 14 signals, We also explored the time-to-onset and clinical characteristics of hem-irAEs, and performed multivariable logistic regression to explore risk factors for these events.

Hematological adverse events represent a stable yet relatively infrequent category of AEs associated with ICIs. Statistical analysis revealed that from Q1 of 2004 to Q1 of 2025, hematological AEs accounted for 3.73% of all ICI reports, this proportion is slightly higher than the results of previous studies: multiple large-scale retrospective studies have shown that hem-irAEs constitute 0.6%-3.6% of all irAEs [15], Notably, the proportion demonstrated an increasing trend during 2011 to 2024 (Fig. 3).This trend may be attributed to two factors. First, the widespread application of ICI therapy has expanded and diversified the treated patient population, leading to an increase in both the absolute number and reported proportion of rare irAEs, such as hematologic toxicities. Second, with enhanced clinical awareness, physicians’ ability to identify and willingness to report these rare irAEs have improved, thereby increasing the reporting rate. Among 6,259 ICI-related hematological AEs, fatal outcomes were reported in 16.49% of cases. (Table 4), suggesting that hem-irAEs is one of the fatal AEs of ICIs. Prior studies also demonstrate this: A retrospective analysis of 20,128 patients identified 82 irAE-related deaths. Of these, hematological causes constituted the fourth most common category (5 cases), preceded by respiratory failure, cardiovascular events, and infections [18]. Therefore, clinicians should maintain vigilance for this relatively rare but potentially severe toxicity.

Employing the entire FAERS database as the comparator, we conducted disproportionality analyses on reports of ICI monotherapy, detecting 14 hematological signals. including immune thrombocytopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, pure red cell aplasia, immune-mediated pancytopenia, acquired hemophili and Evans syndrome. They are all immune-mediated hematological diseases. Previous studies have been reported the similarly AEs. Rafaela Kramer et al. screened 7,626 cancer patients treated with ICIs from 18 international cancer centers and identified 50 patients with hem-irAEs. Among these, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia were the most common, other hem-irAEs included anemia, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, acquired hemophilia A, aplastic anemia and coagulation deficiency [15]. Davis et al. [33] investigated hematological toxicities associated with ICIs therapy. They identified 168 individual case safety reports documenting various hematological complications, including immune thrombocytopenic purpura, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, hemolytic anemia, aplastic anemia and pure red cell aplasia [33]. The mechanisms underlying ICI-associated hematological toxicities remain incompletely understood. Some researchers propose that ICIs can enhance the cytotoxicity of T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. This heightened activity may lead to an attack on bone marrow cells, including neutrophil precursors, resulting in bone marrow failure and a consequent reduction in trilineage hematopoiesis. Concurrently, ICIs may induce the production of autoantibodies by a range of immune cells, including T cells and NK cells. Immune thrombocytopenia may involve the generation of autoantibodies against platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa. In autoimmune hemolytic anemia, autoantibody production may be related to decreased Treg-mediated immune suppression and/or reduced B-cell activation [34]. The hematological signals identified in this study, such as Immune-mediated pancytopenia, Aplasia pure red cell, acquired hemophilia and Evans syndrome are rare but serious AEs, and require increased attention in clinical practice for early detection and intervention.

We conducted further research on the reports of ICI treatment and employed multivariable logistic regression to explore risk factors of hematological toxicity. Given that the missing value rates for age and gender are 22.98% (87,842/382,327) and 8.98% (34,341/382,327) respectively, directly deleting missing values could compromise statistical power. To enhance the accuracy of our analysis results, we employed MissForest imputation to handle missing values for age and gender prior to conducting multivariable logistic regression. Additionally, the robustness of the results was confirmed through a sensitivity analysis without imputation. We observed that among patients treated with ICIs, males had a reduced risk of hematological AEs relative to females (OR = 0.92,95% CI: 0.90–0.96, P,<0.05). This may be because compared to men, women are more prone to developing autoimmune diseases [35]. In addition, physiological differences between males and females, including hemoglobin and hormone levels, may contribute to a higher susceptibility to hematological disorders in females [36]. However, the precise factors contributing to the gender differences in hematological AEs associated with ICIs remain unclear and warrant further investigation. The elderly (≥ 65 years) had a lower risk of hematological AEs than those aged < 65 years. This finding is in agreement with previous reports by Na Li et al., who described a higher frequency of hematological AEs in younger patients [36]. This might be because in older patients, lower immunity decreases the effectiveness of ICIs while also reducing the risk of immune-related AEs [37]. Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that compared with ICI monotherapy, ICIs plus chemotherapy was associated with a significantly increased risk of hematological toxicity (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 3.44–3.69, P < 0.05). Conversely, ICI plus targeted therapy was associated with a significantly reduced risk of hematological toxicity compared to ICI monotherapy (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.69–0.76, P < 0.05). The finding that ICIs plus chemotherapy significantly increases the risk of hematological toxicity is consistent with previous reports [38, 39]. This may because the myelosuppressive action of chemotherapy directly impairs hematopoietic function, while ICIs may activate the immune system to erroneously attack blood cells. When used in combination, the damage to the hematopoietic system is significantly greater than with either treatment alone. The combination therapy of ICIs and targeted drugs may reduce the risk of hematological toxicity. The specific mechanism remains unclear to date but may involve the following factors: targeted drugs (e.g., tyrosine kinase inhibitors or anti-angiogenic drugs) block tumor cell growth signals or angiogenesis more specifically, resulting in less direct damage to normal hematopoietic cells. In combination therapy, targeted drugs can reduce tumor burden, regulate the tumor microenvironment, and decrease the population of immunosuppressive cells [40]. These effects indirectly reduce the immune attack of ICIs on the bone marrow, thereby lessening hematological toxicity. Since this is a retrospective study utilizing the FAERS database, although imputing missing values for independent variables prior to conducting multivariable logistic regression analysis improved the statistical power, unknown factors (such as patients’ baseline bone marrow function, nutritional status, degree of tumor bone marrow infiltration, drug dosages, presence of infections, or autoimmune diseases) could still introduce bias into the results. Therefore, the risk factors for ICI-related hematological toxicity identified in this study are require further verification.

This study analyzed 6,259 reports of ICIs-related hematological AEs. The median onset time of hem-irAEs was 20 days (IQR 8–63), which is earlier than the 6 weeks previously reported in the literature [15]. Compared to ICIs monotherapy, ICIs combined with chemotherapy significantly accelerated the onset of hematological AEs (27 days vs. 14 days). Similarly, combination with targeted therapy shortened the onset time (21 days vs. 13 days). Furthermore, the onset of hematological toxicity occurred earlier with dual immunotherapy regimens compared to single-agent immunotherapy (19 days vs. 28 days). This suggests that combining ICIs with other agents, such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or another ICI, can lead to a more rapid development of hematological toxicities.The mechanism is currently unclear and may be attributed to accelerated immune activation: combination therapy induces immunogenic cell death of tumor cells, releasing antigens and inflammatory signals. This “primes” the immune system, allowing ICIs to trigger a fulminant response more rapidly, leading to a premature breach of self-tolerance. It is recommended that clinicians closely monitor hematological parameters at an earlier stage during combination therapy.

This study has certain limitations. First, reports in the FAERS database have inherent deficiencies, such as duplicate reports, incompleteness, inaccuracies, and untimeliness. To address these issues, after cleaning the data according to FDA guidance documents, we further deduplicated reports with identical key information (such as: gender, age, weight, event date, adverse reaction, country, and drug), retaining only one record. We also excluded reports where the adverse event onset occurred before drug administration and removed Preferred Terms (PTs) unrelated to the drug’s adverse events. Despite performing substantial data cleaning, we still cannot achieve completely clean data. In particular, reports where adverse events are related to the indication may lead to bias in the results, furthermore, the deep cleaning process itself may have led to the excessive removal of data, thereby introducing another potential source of bias. Second, we cannot ascertain the total number of patients using ICIs from the FAERS database; therefore, the incidence rate of hematological adverse events cannot be calculated. Disproportionality analysis detects signals of imbalance in drug-event reporting but cannot establish a causal relationship between ICIs and hematological adverse events. Third, our study suggested that hematological toxicity increased when ICIs were combined with chemotherapy. However, given that the FAERS database lacks key clinical information (e.g., bone marrow biopsy, drug withdrawal test), we are unable to determine whether the toxicity is primarily attributable to ICIs or the concomitant chemotherapy. Furthermore, due to the inability to obtain patients’ detailed clinical information, we cannot determine whether hematological adverse events were caused by ICIs treatment or by other factors (such as concomitant immune-related diseases, infections, tumor progression), this uncertainty may introduce bias into the results. Finally, as this study is an exploratory retrospective analysis, the findings regarding ICI-associated hematological adverse events still need to beconfirmed through large-scale prospective studies.

Conclusion

Our large-scale pharmacovigilance study confirms that, although uncommon, ICI-associated hematological toxicities are clinically significant and include serious, rare irAEs such as immune-mediated pancytopenia and Evans syndrome. Key risk factors include female sex, age < 65 years, and combination with chemotherapy. These findings underscore the need for heightened clinical vigilance. Early recognition and prompt intervention are crucial for patients presenting with high-risk features. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these risk associations and to develop early detection and management strategies for these potentially life-threatening irAEs.
