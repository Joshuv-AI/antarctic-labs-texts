Searching in trees with heavy group sets of fixed size
Michał Szyfelbein

Abstract
We consider the following generalization of the binary search problem: A searcher is required to find a hidden element x x in a tree T T . To do so, they iteratively perform queries to an oracle about a chosen vertex v v . After each such call, the oracle responds whether the target was found and if not, the searcher receives as a reply the neighbor of v v that lays on the shortest path towards x x . Additionally, each vertex v v may have a different query cost w ⁡ ( v ) w(v) . The goal is to find the optimal querying strategy for the searcher which minimizes the worst case query cost required to find x x . The problem is known to be NP-hard even in restricted classes of trees such as bounded diameter spiders [Cicalese et al. 2016] and no constant factor approximation algorithm is known for the general case. Inspired by recent studies [Dereniowski et al. 2022, Dereniowski et al. 2024], instead of restricted classes of trees, we explore restrictions on the weight function. We introduce the concept of a heavy group set of a vertex H ​ G ​ ( v , w ) HG(v,w) . We show that if for every v ∈ T v\in T : | H ​ G ​ ( v , w ) | ≤ k |HG\left(v,w\right)|\leq k an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) O(\log\log n) -approximation can be found within 2 O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ k ) ⋅ poly ​ ( n ) 2^{O(\log^{2}k)}\cdot\text{poly}(n) time.

Introduction

Searching plays a pivotal role in computer science, serving as a cornerstone for numerous theoretical models and real-world applications. Many computational problems, such as sorting, can be framed as searching for an element within a well-defined set or rely on searching as a fundamental subroutine. The significance of efficient search strategies spans multiple domains, including data management systems, artificial intelligence, and operations research.

One of the most well-known search models is the classical binary search, which efficiently locates a given value within a linearly ordered set. A common application of binary search arises in experimental measurements, where each comparison corresponds to performing a measurement to determine whether the target value is smaller or greater than a chosen threshold. This problem can be formulated as searching within a path, where each comparison operation is modeled as a query to an oracle. In constant time, the oracle returns information on whether a given vertex is the target and, if not, which subpath of the original path contains it.

Let

2.1
Notation and query model

The
Tree Search Problem
consists of a triple
T=\left(V\left(T\right),E\left(T\right),w\right)
, where
w:V\left(T\right)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the cost of querying each vertex. A
query
to an oracle asks about chosen vertex and after time
w(v)
receives an answer. If the answer is affirmative, then
v
is the target, otherwise a neighbor
u
of
v
is returned such
u
belongs to the path between
v
and
x
.

We assume that each considered tree
T
is rooted at some vertex
r\left(T\right)
and additionally, we assume that every input tree is rooted at
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{v\in V\left(T\right)}\left\{w\left(v\right)\right\}
. Ties are broken arbitrarily. By
uv\in E\left(T\right)
we denote an edge connecting vertices
u
and
v
. For any
V^{\prime}\subseteq V\left(T\right)
by
T[V^{\prime}]
we denote the forest, being the subgraph of
T
induced by
V^{\prime}
. Additionally, by
T\langle V^{\prime}\rangle
we denote the minimal connected subtree of
T
containing all vertices from
V^{\prime}
. We denote the set of neighbors of
v\in V\left(T\right)
as
N_{T}\left(v\right)=\left\{u\in V\left(T\right)|uv\in E\left(T\right)\right\}
. We denote the set of neighbors of subtree
T^{\prime}
of
T
as
N_{T}\left(T^{\prime}\right)=\bigcup_{v\in V\left(T^{\prime}\right)}N_{T}\left(v\right)-V\left(T^{\prime}\right)
. By
\deg_{T}\left(v\right)=\left|N_{T}\left(v\right)\right|
we denote the degree of
v
in
T
. By
\mathcal{P}_{T}\left(u,v\right)=T\langle\left\{u,v\right\}\rangle-\left\{u,v\right\}
we denote a path of vertices between
u
and
v
in
T
(excluding
u
and
v
). Analogously for
V_{1},V_{2}\in V\left(T\right)
we define
\mathcal{P}_{T}\left(V_{1},V_{2}\right)=T\langle V_{1}\cup V_{2}\rangle-\left(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\right)
.

2.2
Definition of a decision tree

Definition 1
.

A decision tree is triple
D=(V\left(D\right),E\left(D\right),m)
where
V\left(D\right)
are vertices and
E\left(D\right)
are edges of
D
and
m:V\left(D\right)\to V\left(T\right)
. Let
Q_{D}\left(T,x\right)
be the sequence of queries performed in order to find
x
using decision tree
D
in
T
. We define the cost of searching for target
x
using decision tree
D
in
T
with weight function
w
as:

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T,w,x\right)=\sum_{q\in Q_{D}\left(T,x\right)}w\left(m\left(q\right)\right)

We define the worst case cost of a decision tree
D
in
T
with weight function
w
as:

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T,w\right)=\max_{x\in V}\left\{\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T,x\right)\right\}

Finally, we define the (optimal) cost of
T
with weight function
w
as:

\texttt{OPT}(T,w)=\min_{D}\left\{\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T,w\right)\right\}

A given decision tree is optimal if its cost is equal to
\texttt{OPT}(T,w)
. Let
q\in V\left(D\right)
and
v\in V\left(T\right)
such that
m\left(q\right)=v
. We say that
q
is a query to
v
.

Remark 2
.

We will assume that the target
x\in T
. Let
v\in V\left(T\right)
. Observe that if every
u\in N_{T}\left(v\right)
is queried before
v
, then the query to
v
may be omitted. Whenever
x=v
, after performing all queries to vertices in
N_{T}\left(v\right)
the location of the target
x
is revealed to be
v
.
Such assumption is more general then a version of the problem in which it might happen that
x\notin T
. Whenever dealing with the latter, one can always reduce it to our version of the problem in the following way: Given a tree
T
build a new tree
T^{\prime}
by adding a neighboring leaf
l_{v}
to every vertex
v\in V\left(T\right)
. The weight of each such leaf
l
is
w\left(l_{v}\right)=w\left(v\right)
. Let
D^{\prime}
be the optimal decision tree for
T^{\prime}
(under assumption that
x\in T^{\prime}
). Assume that no
l_{v}
is queried in
D^{\prime}
. If otherwise, replace each such query by a query to
v
. As each vertex
v\in T
is queried in
D
it is a valid and optimal decision tree for
T
.

From now on we will also assume that all weights are normalized, so that the weight of the heaviest vertex is exactly 1. If not, the weights are scaled by dividing them by
\max_{v\in V\left(T\right)}\left\{w(v)\right\}
. Note that such operation does not influence the optimality of a strategy or quality of the approximation.

Furthermore, w. l. o. g. we require that all vertices satisfy the following
star condition
(introduced in
[
11
]
): For every
v\in T
:

w(v)\leq\sum_{u\in N_{T}(v)}w(u)
. If this condition is not fulfilled for some
v
, the optimal decision tree never queries it as any query to
v
may be replaced with a sequence of queries to vertices in
N_{T}\left(v\right)
resulting in a valid decision tree with strictly lower cost. For an input tree, if the star condition is not fulfilled in a finite (and polynomial) time we replace necessary weight by assigning
w(v)=\min\left\{w(v),\sum_{u\in N_{T}(v)}w(u)\right\}
. Following this an observation stated in
[
11
]
is easily obtained:

Observation 3
.

Let
T
be a tree such that
\left|V\left(T\right)\right|>1
and
w:V\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a normalized weight function fulfilling the star condition. Then,
1\leq\texttt{OPT}(T,w)\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
.

3
The parametrized
O\left(\log\log n\right)
-approx. solution

Recall that in the optimal decision tree
D^{*}
for
T
some vertices of
T
might never be queried. However, in order to simplify the solution we prove the following lemma which enables us to assume that all vertices are queried (without introducing much error):

Lemma 4
.

Let
D
be the optimal decision tree such that every
v\in V\left(T\right)
is queried in
D
. Then,
\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T,w\right)\leq 2\texttt{OPT}\left(T,w\right)
.

Proof.

Let
D^{*}
be the optimal decision tree for
T
. Notice that in every
Q_{D^{*}}\left(T,x\right)
at most one query
q
is omitted. We create a decision tree
D^{\prime}
by appending each such missing query
q
to
Q_{D^{*}}\left(T,x\right)
. The additional cost of such query is at most 1, so we get:

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T,w\right)\leq\texttt{COST}_{D^{\prime}}\left(T,w\right)\leq\texttt{COST}_{D^{*}}\left(T,w\right)+1\leq 2\texttt{OPT}\left(T,w\right)

where the first inequality is due to the optimality of
D
, the second is due to the definition of
D^{\prime}
and the last inequality is due to the optimality of
D^{*}
and Observation
3
. The lemma follows.
∎

In the following considerations, by a slight abuse of notation, we will refer to the optimal decision tree as to the one containing queries to all vertices. This does not impact the asymptotical quality of the solution but simplifies the analysis.

3.1
Weight levels and heavy child groups contraction

The main idea of the algorithm is to divide vertices into
weight levels
and process each in a recursive manner. At each level of the recursion the algorithm broadens the set of vertices having queries to them assigned. This continues until every
v\in T
has a query to it scheduled. We consider the following intervals which we call
weight levels
:

1.

Firstly, an interval
\left(0,\frac{1}{\log n}\right]
.

2.

Then, each next interval
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\left(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right]
starts at the left endpoint of the previous interval
\mathcal{I}=\left(a,b\right]
, that is,
a^{\prime}=b
and ends with
b^{\prime}=\min\left\{2b,1\right\}
. This results with the following sequence of intervals:

\left(\frac{1}{\log n},\frac{2}{\log n}\right],\left(\frac{2}{\log n},\frac{4}{\log n}\right],...,\left(\frac{2^{\left\lceil\log\log n\right\rceil-1}}{\log n},1\right]

The algorithm processes each consecutive weight level
\mathcal{I}
by extending the strategy to contain all queries to vertices
v
such that
w\left(v\right)\in\mathcal{I}
.
Once the last interval
\left(\frac{2^{\left\lceil\log\log n\right\rceil-1}}{\log n},1\right]
is processed the algorithm returns a valid decision tree for
T
.

We are now ready to introduce the notions of heavy and light vertices (and queries to them). We say that a vertex
v
(or the query to it) is
heavy
with respect to the interval
\mathcal{I}=\left(a,b\right]
, when
w\left(v\right)>a
. If otherwise, i. e.
w\left(v\right)\leq a
then, the vertex (and the query to it) is
light
with respect to
\mathcal{I}
. Whenever clear from the context, we will omit the term ”with respect to
\mathcal{I}
” and just call the vertices and queries heavy and light.

At last, we introduce a notion of a heavy child group and contraction operation:

A
heavy child group

H_{v}
is a heavy group such that
v
is parent of some vertex
u\in H_{v}
. A
contraction
of a heavy group
H_{v}
is an operation which consists of deleting all of the vertices belonging to
H_{v}
from
T
and connecting every vertex
u
which was a child of some vertex in
H_{v}
to
v
(for example see Figure

). This leads to the following simple observations:

Observation 5
.

Let
T^{\prime}
be a tree created by contraction of a heavy group
H_{v}
in
T
. Then,
\texttt{OPT}\left(T^{\prime}\right)\leq\texttt{OPT}\left(T\right)
.

Observation 6
.

Let
T^{\prime}
be a tree created by contraction of a heavy group
H_{v}
in
T
. Then,
k\left(T^{\prime}\right)\leq k\left(T\right)
.

3.2
Vertex ranking

The vertex ranking of
T
is a labeling of vertices
l:V\to\left\{1,2,...,\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil\right\}
which satisfies the following condition: for each pair
u,v\in V\left(T\right)
whenever
l(u)=l(v)
, then there exists
z\in\mathcal{P}_{T}\left(u,v\right)
for which
l(z)>l(v)
. Such a labeling always exists and can be calculated in linear time by means of the dynamic programming
[
25
,
23
]
.
The vertex ranking directly induces a decision tree for
T
. The procedure is as follows:

1.

Let
z\in T
be a vertex such that for every
v\in V\left(T\right)
:
l\left(r\right)\geq l\left(v\right)
.

2.

Schedule query to
r
as the root of the decision tree
D
for
T
.

3.

For each
T^{\prime}\in T-r
build a decision tree
D_{T^{\prime}}
recursively and hang every
r\left(D_{T^{\prime}}\right)
as a child of query to
v
in
D
.

When the input tree has unitary weights the decision tree built in this way is optimal and never uses more than
\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
queries.

3.3
The main recursive procedure

We are ready to present the main recursive procedure. To avoid ambiguity, let
\mathcal{T}
be the tree processed at some level of the recursion. Alongside
\mathcal{T}
, the algorithm takes as an input the interval
\left(a,b\right]
. For the sake of simplicity, from now on we will say that a set of vertices is a heavy group if it is a heavy group with respect to
v=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v\in V\left(T\right),w\left(v\right)\leq a}\left\{w(v)\right\}
, i. e. all vertices in the heavy groups belong to
\left(a,b\right]
and every vertex in
\left(a,b\right]
belongs to some heavy group. The algorithm (see Algorithm
1
) works in the following manner:

•

The base case happens whenever: for every
v\in\mathcal{T}
:
w\left(v\right)\leq\frac{1}{\log n}
or for every
v\in\mathcal{T}
:
w\left(v\right)>a
, i. e. every vertex is heavy. In such situation a solution is build by disregarding the weights of vertices and building a decision tree by using the vertex ranking of
\mathcal{T}
.

•

If otherwise, all heavy groups in
\mathcal{T}
are contracted, forming a new tree
\mathcal{T}_{C}
and a decision tree
D_{C}
for
T_{C}
is built in a recursive manner. By applying the following proposition, the decision tree
D
for
\mathcal{T}
is build by extending
D_{C}
to contain all necessary queries in
\mathcal{T}
:

Proposition 7
.

There exists the algorithm
ExtendDecTree
which given a tree
T
, a decision tree
D_{C}
for
T_{C}
and an interval
\left(a,b\right]
returns a new decision tree
D
with cost at most:

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(T\right)\leq\texttt{COST}_{D_{C}}\left(T_{C}\right)+\left(2+\frac{b}{a}\right)\cdot\texttt{OPT}\left(T\right)

The algorithm runs in time

2^{O\left(\log^{2}k\right)}\cdot\text{poly}\left(n\right)
.

The algorithm and the proof of the Proposition
7
are provided in Section
4
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
D
be a decision tree build for
\mathcal{T}
in the base of the recursion in

by using the vertex ranking of
\mathcal{T}
. Then:

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)\leq 2\texttt{OPT}(T,w)
.

Proof.

There are two cases:

1.

If

we get that:

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)\leq\frac{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}{\log n}\leq 2\leq 2\texttt{OPT}(\mathcal{T},w)\leq 2\texttt{OPT}(T,w)

where the first inequality is due to the definition of the vertex ranking, the third inequality is due to Observation
3
and the last inequality is due to Observation

.

2.

If for every
{v\in\mathcal{T}}
:
w\left(v\right)>a
then: For every
v\in\mathcal{T}
let
w^{\prime}\left(v\right)=a
(note, that we can choose any cost here since we treat each query as unitary). As
2w^{\prime}\left(v\right)\geq 2a=b\geq w\left(v\right)
we get that
2\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w^{\prime}\right)\geq\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)

\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{T},w^{\prime}\right)\leq\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)\leq 2\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w^{\prime}\right)=2\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{T},w^{\prime}\right)\leq 2\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)

where the equality is due to the optimality of decision tree built using the vertex ranking. The lemma follows.

There exists an

Let

\texttt{COST}_{D}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)\leq\left(4\cdot(d-1)+2\right)\texttt{OPT}\left(T,w\right)+4\texttt{OPT}\left(\mathcal{T},w\right)\leq\left(4d+2\right)\texttt{OPT}\left(T,w\right)

\begin{gathered}\texttt{COST}_{D_{H}}\left(T\langle H\rangle,w\right)\leq\frac{b}{a}\cdot\texttt{COST}_{D_{H}}\left(T\langle H\rangle,w^{\prime}\right)=\frac{b}{a}\cdot\texttt{OPT}\left(T\langle H\rangle,w^{\prime}\right)\leq\\

5
Conclusions and future work

We showed that approximating the Tree Search Problem within a factor of
O\left(\log\log n\right)
is FPT with respect to
k
(the heavy group sets size). Additionally, the running time is quasipolynomial in
k
, making the algorithm more applicable.

The advantage of the solution constructed in this manner is its modular form. This creates a framework for working with the problem for various classes of trees and restrictions of weight functions. The extension procedure may be schemed differently depending on the specific properties of the input. This in turn might allow for more efficiency in dealing with the problem in the real life applications and leads to the better understanding of the approximability of the problem.

The central question in this line of research is whether there exists a constant-factor approximation algorithm that, given an arbitrary tree
T
and weight function
w
, runs in polynomial time. We believe that the approach used in the current state-of-the-art
O\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)
-approximate algorithm
[
11
]
, based on recursive processing of the center of the tree is unlikely to lead to further improvement of the approximation. Instead a solution with a structure similar to the one proposed in this work could potentially reduce the approximation factor to
\text{poly}\left(\log\log n\right)
, since the number of weight levels to be handled is at most
O\left(\log\log n\right)
. This observation suggests a new line of research focused on designing effective extension procedures.

On the hardness side, it remains an open question whether inapproximability bounds can be established for this problem. Currently, the only known result is the trivial nonexistence of a constant additive-factor approximation algorithm. A promising direction for future research is to derive stronger, more sophisticated inapproximability bounds.
