Conversion surgery after immunotherapy and chemotherapy for gastric cancer with peritoneal metastasis or positive peritoneal cytology: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Huayuan Liang, Hailun Huang, Qing Xie, Kaihua Huang, Zhiwei Li, Huimin Zhang, Rou Zhong, Xiao Yan, Min Lai, Liying Zhao, Guoxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The efficacy of conversion therapy for gastric cancer with peritoneal metastasis (P1) and/or positive peritoneal lavage cytology (CY1) treated with immunotherapy remains unclear. This study aimed to clarify clinical outcomes in this setting.Methods:In this retrospective, single-institution cohort study, 144 patients with P1 and/or CY1 confirmed by staging laparoscopy at initial diagnosis received first-line immunotherapy with chemotherapy and/or trastuzumab between November 2019 and March 2024. Patients were divided into a conversion therapy group and a palliative therapy group.Results:Of 144 patients, 75 showed a clinical response and underwent second-look laparoscopy. Negative peritoneal metastasis and cytology were confirmed in 36 patients, all of whom underwent conversion surgery. Compared with the palliative group, the conversion group had significantly longer median progression-free survival (22.9 vs. 5.8 months; P <0.001) and overall survival (33.4 vs. 12.3 months; P <0.001). Among those who had surgery, 27 patients (75.0%, 27/36) achieved R0 resection (complete resection with no residual tumor). Patients with peritoneal cancer index ≤2 had a significantly longer median overall survival than those with peritoneal cancer index >2 (not reached vs. 28.5 months; P <0.006). No serious treatment-related adverse events led to treatment discontinuation or death.Conclusions:Conversion surgery following immunochemotherapy provided a significant survival benefit for gastric cancer patients with P1 and/or CY1. When immunochemotherapy is effective, active staging laparoscopy should be performed to assess surgical feasibility.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a common malignancy of the digestive system, and radical surgery remains the most effective therapeutic option. However, because GC often develops insidiously, most patients present with distant metastases at diagnosis, making curative surgery unfeasible.[1,2] This is particularly the case in advanced GC with peritoneal metastasis (P1) or positive peritoneal lavage cytology (CY1), where survival rarely exceeds one year.[3–5]

Systemic chemotherapy is currently the standard treatment for unresectable or recurrent metastatic GC. In selected patients, combining chemotherapy with primary debulking surgery, hyperthermic intraperitoneal perfusion chemotherapy, or neoadjuvant intraperitoneal and systemic chemotherapy has shown therapeutic benefit.[5,6] Conversion surgery refers to the surgical treatment of tumors initially unsuitable for radical resection, with the aim of achieving R0 resection in patients who respond well to systemic therapy. Previous studies have reported that conversion surgery can significantly improve survival in GC patients with P1 or CY1 who respond to chemotherapy.[7–9] Nevertheless, the efficacy of induction chemotherapy alone remains limited, with relatively few successful R0 resections.

Recent clinical trials, including CheckMate-649, ORIENT-16, and KEYNOTE-811, have demonstrated significant survival improvements with immunochemotherapy in advanced GC.[10–12] However, the clinical efficacy of conversion therapy incorporating immunochemotherapy for GC with P1 and/or CY1 remains unclear.

This study, therefore, conducted a retrospective analysis of GC patients with P1 and/or CY1 who underwent first-line immunochemotherapy, to evaluate the treatment efficacy of conversion therapy based on staging laparoscopy.

Methods

Ethics statement

The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (application number: NFEC2023375) and was conducted in accordance with this committee’s regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.[13]

Patients

This retrospective study included 144 patients with primary GC with P1 and/or CY1 who received immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in combination with chemotherapy and/or trastuzumab at Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University between November 2019 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria were (1) age 18–75 years; (2) pathologically confirmed P1 and/or CY1 from GC; (3) completion of diagnostic staging laparoscopy at our hospital before treatment, with peritoneal cancer index (PCI) recorded if P1 was present. The exclusion criteria were (1) not first-line ICIs; (2) missing PCI records; (3) co-occurrence of other malignancies; (4) palliative surgery; (5) unevaluable lesions; and (6) loss to follow-up.

Treatment strategy

At the institution, the routine practice was to conduct diagnostic staging laparoscopy for most patients at the initial diagnosis of advanced GC. If unresectable distant organ metastases were not detected, patients received either neoadjuvant therapy or radical surgery. When distant metastases were identified, first-line therapy was initiated.

Patients underwent imaging evaluations every 2–4 cycles to assess treatment efficacy and monitor disease progression. Following treatment, improvements in general condition, such as increased strength and weight, were often observed. Blood-based oncological biomarkers and imaging also indicated a reduction in tumor burden, including tumor shrinkage, which could make R0 resection possible. A key criterion for considering surgical intervention was imaging evidence of marked regression or disappearance of peritoneal lesions. At this point, a multidisciplinary team met to assess the need for a second-look laparoscopic exploration, which was compared with pretreatment baseline findings. Treatment response was evaluated by several factors: regression of the primary tumor, reduction in metastatic lymph nodes, disappearance of P1, absence of tumor cells on intraoperative pathology, and negative peritoneal lavage cytology (CY0). If these criteria were satisfied and R0 resection was feasible, conversion surgery was considered.

After successful conversion surgery (in cases without recurrence), continuation of the preoperative regimen was recommended as adjuvant treatment. For patients who still had distant metastases after treatment, palliative drug therapy was continued. In line with treatment guidelines, second-line drug therapy was initiated if tumor progression occurred.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue samples to evaluate the status of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and mismatch repair (MMR). PD-L1 levels were quantified using the PharmDx IHC 22C3 assay and expressed as the combined positive score (CPS), calculated by multiplying the number of PD-L1-stained cells (including tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) by 100. HER2 positivity was defined as IHC 3+ or IHC 2+ with confirmed HER2 DNA amplification through in situ hybridization (ISH). MMR status was assessed by IHC using monoclonal antibodies against mutL homolog 1 (MLH1), mutS homolog 2 (MSH2), PMS1 homolog 2 (PMS2), and mutS homolog 6 (MSH6). Tumors with reduced expression of any of these proteins were classified as MMR deficient, whereas tumors with expression of all four were categorized as MMR proficient. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) status was determined by chromogenic ISH using fluorescein-labeled oligonucleotide probes (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China; ISH-7001) targeting Epstein–Barr virus-encoded RNA (EBER).

Treatment, assessments, response, and toxicity

The ICIs included pembrolizumab (200 mg), sintilimab (200 mg), toripalimab (240 mg), and nivolumab (240 mg). Patients received intravenous infusions every three weeks on day 1 of each treatment cycle. Chemotherapy regimens included: oxaliplatin with calcium levofolinate and 5-fluorouracil (FOLFOX); oxaliplatin with capecitabine (CapeOX); or 5-fluorouracil with leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel. Trastuzumab was recommended for patients with HER2-positive tumors. Dosing and treatment protocols followed established GC guidelines[14] and were adjusted according to individual patient assessments.

The extent of P1 was classified using the PCI described by Jacquet and Sugarbaker.[15] For nonperitoneal metastatic lesions, responses were assessed using RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors) 1.1. For peritoneal metastases, response assessment relied on imaging, laparoscopy, and biopsy to confirm eradication. Adverse events were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0. Clinical staging (cTNM) and pathological staging after conversion therapy (ypTNM, post-neoadjuvant pathological tumor-node-metastasis) were categorized using the American Joint Committee on Cancer / Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) 8th edition TNM system. R0 resection was defined as complete removal of primary and metastatic tumors, with no malignancy at proximal, distal, or circumferential margins. Radiological imaging also confirmed the absence of distant organ metastases. Tumor regression grade (TRG) after immunotherapy was evaluated using the Becker score[16]: TRG 0 (complete response, no residual tumor cells); TRG 1 (almost complete response, only a few residual tumor cells); TRG 2 (partial response, degeneration of residual tumor cells with some remaining); and TRG 3 (extensive residual tumor cells with no degeneration).

Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval from the start of ICI treatment to objective tumor progression or death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from ICI initiation to death or last follow-up. Imaging (CT, MRI, or 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)/fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) positron-emission tomography (PET-CT) was performed every 2–3 months during treatment and, for patients without progression, every three months after treatment discontinuation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the unpaired t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and proportions and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences assessed by the log-rank test. The optimal cut-off value for PCI scores distinguishing high- and low-risk groups was determined using the surv_cutpoint function. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs), and logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs), both with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with P <0.05 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to assess the goodness-of-fit of the logistic model. Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.03 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Of the 144 patients, 75 showed a clinical response and underwent re-laparoscopy. Negative peritoneal metastasis and cytology (P0CY0) was confirmed in 36 patients who subsequently underwent conversion surgery. Patients were therefore divided into the conversion therapy group (n = 36) and the palliative therapy group (n = 108). Compared with the palliative therapy group, the conversion group had lower proportions of patients with PD-L1 <5 (36.1% vs. 60.2%, P = 0.012), signet ring cell carcinoma (47.2% vs. 66.7%, P = 0.038), and distant lymph node metastasis (38.9% vs. 67.6%, P = 0.002).

Among the 36 patients who underwent conversion surgery, baseline status was categorized as P0CY1 (n = 8), P1CY0 (n = 15), and P1CY1 (n = 13) [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865]. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics among these three groups.

Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865 shows the first-line regimens given to patients in the conversion group. A two-drug chemotherapy regimen (oxaliplatin with capecitabine or FOLFOX) was used in 27 patients, while eight HER2-positive patients also received trastuzumab. The ICIs used were sintilimab (n = 12), nivolumab (n = 10), toripalimab (n = 9), and pembrolizumab (n = 5). Twenty-two patients received 3–4 cycles of preoperative treatment, and 14 received 5–8 cycles. After surgery, 25 patients completed more than four cycles of adjuvant therapy.

Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865 summarizes treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Overall, 60 patients (41.7%, 60/144) experienced grade 3/4 TRAEs, mainly myelosuppression. Anemia was the most common, occurring in 28 patients (19.4%, 28/144). There were no significant differences in TRAEs between the conversion and palliative groups.

Outcomes of conversion surgery

Surgical procedures and pathological diagnoses for the 36 patients who underwent conversion therapy are summarized in Table 2. The mean duration of preoperative therapy was 4.2 months (range, 2.6–7.5 months). R0 resection was achieved in 27 patients (75.0%, 27/36). Postoperative complications occurred in seven patients (19.4%, 7/36), all of which were Clavien–Dindo grade I. These included pulmonary infection, abdominal infection, anastomotic leakage, and peritoneal effusion. All complications were successfully managed with conservative treatment, and no treatment-related deaths occurred. Histopathological examination of resected tumors revealed the following responses: grade 0 in 2 patients (5.6%, 2/36), grade 1 in 2 patients (5.6%, 2/36), grade 2 in 17 patients (47.2%, 17/36), and grade 3 in 15 patients (41.7%, 15/36).

Patients who underwent conversion surgery exhibited a median PFS of 22.9 months, which was significantly longer than the 5.8 months observed in patients who received palliative therapy [P <0.001; Figure 2A]. The 1-year PFS rates were 66.7% (24/36) for the conversion group and 8.3% (9/108) for the palliative group. The median OS in the conversion group was 33.4 months, with 7 patients (19.4%, 7/36) showing no recurrence for more than 2 years. In the conversion group, patients with R0 resection had a significantly longer median PFS than those with non-R0 resection (not reached vs. 18.2 months, P = 0.044) [Supplementary Figure 1A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865], whereas no significant difference was observed in OS (not reached vs. 28.5 months, P = 0.331) [Supplementary Figure 1B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865]. Survival analysis of the P0CY1 (n = 8), P1CY0 (n = 15), and P1CY1 (n = 13) subgroups revealed no significant differences in either PFS (P = 0.967) [Supplementary Figure 2A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865] or OS (P = 0.362) [Supplementary Figure 2B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865]. The distribution of treatment regimens is shown in Supplementary Figures 3A–C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865. Relevant analyses on the PCI

Analysis of PCI differences showed that PCI was significantly lower in the conversion group compared with the palliative group (P <0.001) [Figure 3A]. Within the conversion group, there was no significant difference between the P1CY0 and P1CY1 subgroups (P = 0.740) [Figure 3B]. However, PCI was significantly higher in patients with recurrence than in those without recurrence (P = 0.016) [Figure 3C]. These findings suggest that PCI was significantly associated with successful conversion surgery.

Survival analysis identified an optimal cut-off value of 2 for PCI in distinguishing high- and low-risk patients in the conversion group. Patients with PCI >2 had a lower median PFS than those with PCI ≤2 (18.2 months vs. unreached; P = 0.003) [Figure 4A]. Patients with PCI ≤2 also had a significantly longer median OS compared with PCI >2 (not reached vs. 28.5 months; P <0.006) [Figure 4B]. Notably, none of the patients with PCI ≤2 reached the mortality endpoint during the 2-year follow-up. Cox regression analysis further showed that PCI ≤2 was the only factor significantly associated with tumor progression (HR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.18–0.63; P = 0.001) [Supplementary Figure 4A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865] and with mortality (HR, 0.16; 95% CI, 0.04–0.61; P = 0.007) [Supplementary Figure 4B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865].

Risk factors for conversion therapy

Logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors for successful conversion surgery in all patients [Supplementary Table 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C865]. Univariate analysis showed that distant lymph node metastasis, signet ring cell carcinoma, and PCI >2 were risk factors, whereas PD-L1 CPS ≥5 was associated with favorable outcomes. Multivariate analysis confirmed that distant lymph node metastasis (OR, 2.81; 95% CI, 1.08–7.29; P = 0.034) and PCI >2 (OR, 11.38; 95% CI, 4.34–29.85; P <0.001) were independent risk factors. In contrast, PD-L1 CPS ≥5 remained an independent favorable factor (OR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.10–0.73; P = 0.010).

Discussion

This study confirmed that successful conversion surgery in GC patients with P1 and/or CY1 treated with immunochemotherapy and/or trastuzumab significantly prolonged survival. It also identified PCI as a key correlate of conversion surgery. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate repeat laparoscopic exploration to determine PCI and peritoneal lavage cytology to select patients with advanced GC suitable for conversion surgery after immunotherapy. The results demonstrated a clear survival benefit in patients who underwent successful conversion surgery, with a median PFS of 22.9 months and OS of 33.4 months. In addition, PCI ≤2 was a significant correlate of successful conversion surgery and was associated with a favorable prognosis.

Imaging modalities have inherent limitations in accurately staging advanced GC, especially in patients with microperitoneal metastases. In P1 or CY1 cases, some lesions are so small that RECIST assessment is impractical. For tumors treated with immunotherapy, treatment-related inflammation and edema further hinder the evaluation of aggressiveness, and radiographic imaging cannot reliably distinguish between fibrosis and viable tumor tissue, leading to false positives in some patients.[17] In these cases, laparoscopic exploration plays a crucial role in achieving accurate staging and guiding treatment decisions.[18] A notable strength of this study was that all patients underwent diagnostic laparoscopy to confirm PCI and peritoneal cytology. Among them, 75 showed a clinical response and underwent second-look laparoscopy, which confirmed P0CY0 status in 36 patients (48.0%, 36/75). For patients with P1 and/or CY1 who respond to immunochemotherapy, performing a second-look laparoscopy is, therefore, essential for determining prognosis and planning treatment strategies.

The prognosis of patients with P0CY1 is poor, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 10% to 26%, recurrence-free survival rates of 21%, and high recurrence rates after surgery.[19–21] P0CY1 is regarded as a manifestation of metastatic disease and therefore requires systemic therapy. According to the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system, P0CY1 is defined as distant metastasis (M1), and both CY1 and P1 are classified as stage IV GC.[22] Previous studies have shown that patients with P0CY1 who received systemic chemotherapy and were later confirmed laparoscopically as P0CY0 had significantly higher disease-free survival.[23] However, no studies have examined the efficacy of surgical treatment in P0CY1 patients who received effective immunochemotherapy. In our study, 10 patients were initially diagnosed with P0CY1. After immunotherapy and second-look laparoscopy, 8 patients (80.0%, 8/10) achieved P0CY0 and underwent successful conversion surgery, with all achieving R0 resection. By contrast, among 65 patients initially diagnosed with P1, only 28 (43.1%, 28/65) achieved P0CY0 after treatment. These results confirm that P0CY1 should be taken seriously and highlight that immunochemotherapy can achieve a relatively high rate of cytology conversion, increasing the proportion of patients eligible for surgery.

Previous studies have consistently shown that patients with P1 have a limited chance of cure, even after successful conversion surgery following chemotherapy. Yang et al[9] reported that conversion surgery after intraperitoneal and systemic chemotherapy significantly improved prognosis in GC patients with P1, with an R0 resection rate of 54.8% and a median survival of 22.3 months. In another phase II clinical trial of 100 patients, 64 underwent conversion surgery, and the median survival was 30.5 months. However, despite postoperative chemotherapy, recurrence occurred in 58 patients (91%), with about 66% of relapses in the peritoneum.[5] In the present study, conversion surgery after immunochemotherapy produced encouraging results in patients with P1 and/or CY1. The R0 resection rate was 75%. Compared with the palliative group, the surgery group had a significantly longer median PFS (22.9 vs. 5.8 months; P <0.001) and OS (33.4 vs. 12.3 months; P <0.001). After a median follow-up of 20.6 months, 13 patients developed recurrence, and 76.9% (10/13) of relapses were in the peritoneum. These findings suggest that while conversion surgery after effective immunochemotherapy and disappearance of P1 and/or CY1 can extend survival, achieving a complete cure remains difficult. P1 is often characterized by strong invasiveness, and residual micrometastatic lesions may persist despite immunochemotherapy and surgery, leading to recurrence.[24] In addition, the peritoneum may act as a unique immune microenvironment that promotes tumor immune evasion.[25] New strategies are urgently needed to reduce recurrence after conversion surgery. Ongoing randomized controlled trials, such as GASTRICHIP and PREVENT trials, are investigating the safety and benefits of hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy.[26] Furthermore, Li et al[25] identified Killer Cell Lectin Like Receptor D1 (KLRD1)+ natural killer T (NKT) cells as key effectors in GC immunotherapy with P1, supporting the potential development of chimeric antigen receptor NKT therapies targeting P1.

As survival was significantly prolonged in patients who underwent successful conversion surgery, logistic regression was used to identify associated factors. Signet ring cell carcinoma, PD-L1 CPS ≥5, and PCI ≤2 were all linked with the likelihood of successful conversion surgery. Previous studies have suggested that signet ring cell carcinoma is a predictor of recurrence after radical surgery[27] and is strongly associated with peritoneal dissemination and distant lymph node metastasis.[28,29] In this study, signet ring cell carcinoma was common, occurring in 38.2% of cases. Among patients in the conversion group, those with signet ring cell carcinoma (42.1%, 8/19) showed higher recurrence rates compared with those without this histological subtype (29.4%, 5/17). These findings highlight the need for close postoperative surveillance in patients with signet ring cell adenocarcinoma after conversion surgery.

Neither the ATTRACTION-04 study nor the CheckMate-649 study reported significant improvement in outcomes for patients with P1.[30,31] In Chinese data from the CheckMate-649 trial, patients with P1 who received immunochemotherapy had a median OS of 9.5 months.[10] However, subgroup analyses of these patients were not performed in those studies. In the present study, among 66 patients with P1 and/or CY1 who had CPS ≥5, 23 patients (34.8%, 23/66) successfully underwent conversion surgery. In contrast, only 13 patients (16.7%, 13/78) with CPS <5 underwent conversion surgery. These results confirm that not all GC patients with P1 and/or CY1 are ineligible for surgery. Specifically, patients with CPS ≥5 who respond favorably to immunochemotherapy may achieve P0CY0 status, allowing surgical intervention and improving survival. We also examined the subgroup of patients who were HER2-positive. Of the 12 patients in this subgroup, 8 (66.7%, 8/12) successfully underwent conversion surgery. The combination of immunochemotherapy and trastuzumab demonstrated strong efficacy, consistent with the findings of the KEYNOTE-811 study.[11] Patients with P1 and/or CY1 who receive immunochemotherapy and trastuzumab therefore appear to have a higher likelihood of successful conversion surgery. Ongoing follow-up of these patients will allow further evaluation in future analyses.

The PCI is a quantitative scoring system used to evaluate the extent of P1 in GC patients and is directly linked to OS and prognosis.[32,33] However, the optimal PCI threshold for guiding systemic therapy remains uncertain. Previous studies have mainly used thresholds of 6 or 12 to examine prognostic factors.[34,35] Hao et al[36] reported that patients with a PCI below 15 had higher response rates to chemotherapy. In the present study, patients with PCI ≤2, who underwent conversion surgery, had a significantly longer median OS than those with PCI >2 (not reached vs. 28.5 months; P <0.006). This analysis provides the first evidence that PCI ≤2 is associated with superior survival after successful conversion surgery. Biologically, a lower PCI reflects a smaller tumor burden, which correlates with reduced release of immune-suppressive factors. This allows immune cells, such as T cells and natural killer cells, to interact with tumor cells more effectively and exert cytotoxic effects.[37] In contrast, a high tumor burden may create a hypoxic and acidic microenvironment that impairs immune cell infiltration.[38] Previous studies have also classified peritoneal metastatic lesions into two subtypes: the T-cell “exclusive” subtype and the T-cell “exhausted” subtype. Patients with a lower PCI are more likely to have the “exclusive” subtype, which is associated with stronger and more durable responses to immunotherapy.[39]

Concerning safety, both hematologic and non-hematologic toxicities were tolerable and manageable throughout treatment. Myelosuppression was the most frequently observed adverse event. The duration of surgery, blood loss, and postoperative morbidity were acceptable compared with previous studies.[40,41] All adverse events were effectively managed with conservative treatment, and no perioperative deaths occurred.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study with potential selection bias, which limits the generalizability of the findings, as some patient groups (such as those with severe conditions or incomplete records) may be underrepresented. Second, treatment approaches were not fully consistent, particularly with respect to ICIs. This partly reflects the insensitivity and tumor heterogeneity commonly seen in patients with P1 and/or CY1, which also makes prospective clinical studies in this population difficult to conduct.

In conclusion, conversion surgery following first-line immunochemotherapy and/or targeted therapy represents a promising treatment option for GC patients with P1 and/or CY1. When immunochemotherapy is effective, active staging laparoscopy should be performed to evaluate the feasibility of conversion surgery.
