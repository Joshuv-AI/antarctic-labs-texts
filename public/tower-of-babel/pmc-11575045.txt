Biomechanical changes in the lumbar and hip joint after curved periacetabular osteotomy
Authors: Yongzhen Qu, Hao Tang, Ning Lu, Wenjin Li, Ping Xu, Taoyong Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe alignment of the spine and pelvis significantly impacts overall body balance; therefore, alterations in hip and lumbar spine biomechanics following curved acetabular osteotomy (CPO) can help surgeons optimize acetabular correction. To achieve this goal, we conducted patient-specific finite element analyses to compare hip and lumbar disc contact pressure (CP) between patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) and healthy individuals. Additionally, we examined the influence of CPO on the CP of both the hip and lumbar discs in patients with DDH.MethodsWe conducted finite element analyses of the hip and lumbar spine before and after CPO and compared them with those of a healthy human model. Subsequently, we simulated CPO on the preoperative model. Nonlinear contact analysis was employed to calculate the CP of the acetabular cartilage and lumbar discs during a single-leg stance.ResultsThe maximum and average acetabular CP in patients with DDH were 5.4 MPa and 4.5 MPa, respectively. The average CP for the five lumbar discs were 3 MPa, 2.5 MPa, 2 MPa, 3.5 MPa, and 4.4 MPa. In contrast, the maximum and average acetabular CP in normal subjects were 3.7 MPa and 2.1 MPa, respectively, and the average CP of their lumbar discs were 1 MPa, 2 MPA, 1.88 MPa, 2.1 MPa, and 2.1 MPa, respectively. After CPO, the maximum and average CP of the hip decreased, as did the average CP of the lumbar discs. The maximum and average compressive stress of the acetabulum decreased to 3.79 MPa and 2.3 MPa, respectively, and the average compressive stress of the five intervertebral discs decreased to 1.96 MPa, 0.79 MPa, 0.78 MPa, 1.13 MPa, and 3.14 MPa, respectively.ConclusionOur finite element analysis indicated that CPO effectively normalizes hip contact pressure while reducing lumbar disc contact pressure. However, further investigation is required to elucidate the specific biomechanical mechanisms underlying these changes.

Introduction

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is a prevalent hip disease that has emerged as one of the leading causes of secondary osteoarthritis in the hip [1]. Curved acetabular osteotomy (CPO) is a commonly employed treatment method. In numerous studies, finite element analysis, which simulates the distribution of stress within the hip joint before and after surgery, has been used to explore the optimal osteotomy angles for optimal clinical efficacy [2–4]. However, hip and lumbar diseases often coexist. In 1983, Offierski and MacNab introduced the concept of hip-spine syndrome, and the alignment between the spine and pelvis has been studied by numerous scholars since [5–8]. However, the biomechanical effects on the lumbar spine following acetabular reorientation remain undetermined.

Several studies have confirmed alterations in lumbar lordosis (LL), pelvic incidence (PI) and pelvic tilt (PT) following periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) in patients with DDH. However, this study primarily focuses on changes in spinopelvic parameters instead of the change in biomechanics between the preoperative and postoperative periods in patients with DDH. Additionally, there are no studies investigating the biomechanical changes in the lumbar spine following PAO for DDH. Therefore, it is imperative to explore the biomechanical characteristics of the lumbar spine in DDH patients before and after surgery.

Clinicians who gain a comprehensive understanding of the biomechanical changes in the hip joint and lumbar spine following CPO can better understand the biomechanical behavior of the lumbar spine in patients with DDH. By understanding the preoperative biomechanical behavior of the lumbar-pelvic-hip joint complex before surgery, surgeons can better understand the change in the biomechanics of the lumbar-pelvic-hip joint complex postoperatively and thereby enhance their understanding of spinopelvic compensation relationships, individualize the method used for acetabular correction, and ultimately enhance clinical efficacy. We conducted finite element analyses based on full-length standing lumbar pelvic radiographs to (1) determine any differences in the CP of the lumbar disc and the CP of the acetabulum between patients with DDH and normal individuals and (2) identify changes in the lumbar disc CP following CPO.

Materials and methods

Patients

The study was approved by the institutional ethics review board (approval number, Review-PJ-Sec-2023-247). A patient and a volunteer were involved in the study and all participants who were enrolled in this study provided written informed consent and received detailed information regarding the dose of radiation necessary for the procedure.

Between November 2022 and December 2023, one 21-year-old female with DDH (height: 153 cm, weight: 45 kg, BMI: 19.22) volunteered to participate in the study. The AP view of pelvic revealed a lateral center edge angle (LCEA) of 15°. The patient was diagnosed with right hip dysplasia, and the postoperative LCEA was 34° (Fig. 1). Standing AP view of lumbar pelvis full-length X-ray and supine lumbar pelvis CT images (matrix: 512 × 512, field of view: 260–696 mm, and slice thickness: 1 or 2 mm) were acquired before the operation. CT images were acquired three months after the operation using the same parameters as before. No spinal or hip joint lesions were detected in a 25-year-old healthy female volunteer (height: 162 cm, weight: 51 kg, BMI: 19.4) with no prior history of waist, hip joint or lower limb trauma or disease. Lumbar pelvis CT images (matrix: 512 × 512, field of view: 260–696 mm, and slice thickness: 1 or 2 mm) were acquired with the patient in the supine position.

Preoperative and postoperative AP view of patients with DDH

Model generation

According to previously described methods [9, 10], 3D models of the lumbar spine, pelvis, and hip joint surfaces were generated using Mimics21.0 software. Subsequently, Geomagic Wrap 2021 software was utilized for mesh redrawing, smoothing, denoising, surface optimization, and surface fitting. The intervertebral discs, pubic cartilage, sacroiliac cartilage, acetabular cartilage, and femoral cartilage were constructed with SolidWorks 2022 software. Finally, ANSYS was used to reconstruct soft tissues such as muscles and ligaments to generate a lumbar pelvis and hip joint model. The material properties of the cortical and cancellous bone, annulus fibrosus, nucleus pulposus ligaments, and joint structures in the FEA model were obtained from previous studies [11–18].

CPO simulation

Osteotomy of the preoperative DDH model was simulated using 3-matic Research 13.0 software. The osteotomy technique employed was based on Naito’s CPO method [19]. A C-shaped osteotomy line was made near the anterior inferior iliac spine, extending to the end of the quadrilateral body, but no farther than 1.5 cm from the greater sciatic notch. Ischial ramus osteotomy was performed immediately adjacent to the acetabulum, ensuring to maintain the integrity of the posterior column. The excised acetabular block was then rotated laterally around the fitting center of the femoral head surface as its spherical center, resulting in three simulated osteotomy models with LCEAs measuring 30°, 35°, and 40° (Fig. 2).

From left to right, acetabulum, LCEA increased to 30°, LCEA increased to 35°, and LCEA increased to 40° in DDH patients before operation

Boundary and loading conditions

Pre-CPO and post-CPO nonlinear contact analyses were performed, and the joint contact area, maximum CP, and average CP of the acetabular cartilage were also computed. In addition, the lumbar disc CP was calculated. The human walking cycle is divided into a swing phase and a support phase, with the single leg support phase characterized by maximum force at the hip joint. In this study, a simulation in which the patient stood on one leg was conducted; in accordance with the study conducted by Bergmann et al., it was determined that standing on one leg resulted in an approximately 2.38 times increase in body weight and maximum pressure on the hip joint. To replicate this scenario, constraints were applied to the upper surface of the L1 vertebral body and pubic symphysis along with the distal femur, while a resultant force equivalent to 2.38 times the body weight was exerted at the center of the femoral head (Fig. 3).

Results

The acetabular contact area, maximum CP, and average CP of the patients with DDH before and after CPO and after simulated CPO are presented in Table 1. The contact area between the acetabulum and femoral head in DDH patients prior to CPO was approximately half that of individuals without DDH, while the average pressure was roughly twice as high. After CPO, there was a significant increase in the contact area and a notable decrease in the average contact pressure. Analysis of the simulated CPO models revealed that as the LCEA gradually increased, the contact area gradually expanded; moreover, both the maximum and average pressures decreased progressively. When the LCEA was corrected to 35°, the maximum contact pressure decreased to 3.73 MPa while maintaining an average contact pressure closest to that observed in normal individuals at 2.0 MPa. Figure 4 illustrates the force cloud diagram of the acetabulum. In patients with DDH, there were substantial changes in the stress distribution gradient within the acetabular cartilage, with a pronounced concentration on the anterolateral side—significantly differing from that observed in normal individuals. However, following CPO, these stress distribution gradients became smoother, and the stress concentration significantly decreased.

Discussion

Many studies have revealed that PAO can enhance the biomechanics of the hip joint. In our study, the preoperative acetabular contact area in patients with DDH was less than half that of individuals without DDH, while the contact pressure exceeded twice that of normal subjects, which is consistent with previous literature [4]. Additionally, acetabular stress tended to be concentrated at the acetabular edge. In our clinical model after osteotomy, we observed a significant increase in the acetabular contact area compared to that before surgery, along with a notable decrease in contact pressure and substantial improvement in hip joint biomechanics. In addition, our study revealed that there were no significant differences in the force exerted on the L1-L5 intervertebral discs in individuals with a normal spine, and this force was uniformly distributed, which is consistent with previous findings [20, 21]. However, compared with normal individuals, patients with DDH experienced significantly greater stress on the L1–L5 intervertebral discs, particularly on the L1/L2, L4/L5, and L5/S1 lumbar discs. Consequently, patients with DDH are more susceptible to lumbar disc herniation and other related disorders than their counterparts without DDH. The results of the study be Balik et al. also support the results of our study. They conducted a study involving 39 patients with DDH and unequal lower limb lengths and 43 patients suffering from low back pain. Their study revealed a statistically significant difference in the incidence of disc herniation between the DDH group and the control group [22]. There are multiple etiologies of low back pain in patients with DDH. Some researchers propose that the pathogenesis of low back pain in patients with DDH is attributed to compensatory mechanisms involving the spine, pelvis, and lower extremities. Harada et al. reported that the sacral slope (SS) and pelvic incidence (PI) values in patients with DDH were significantly higher than those in healthy individuals [23]. An increased lumbar lordosis angle and increased pelvic anteversion may induce alterations in the biomechanical interactions between the spine and pelvis, consequently leading to corresponding clinical manifestations such as low back pain in certain patients with DDH.

Additionally, we observed a right-sided concentration of force on the L2/L3 intervertebral discs in patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), whereas normal subjects exhibited a left-sided concentration. This finding confirms the biomechanical alterations in the lumbar spine resulting from the spino-pelvic compensatory mechanism in patients with DDH. Upon examination of the cloud chart depicting intervertebral disc stress, we noted that patients with DDH predominantly experienced posterior concentrations of stress, particularly affecting the L1/L2, L4/L5, and L5/S1 intervertebral discs. These findings align with clinical observations indicating the susceptibility of the abovementioned discs to lumbar disc herniation [24]. Second, the clinical model showed a reduction in stress on the lumbar intervertebral discs, particularly the L4/L5 intervertebral discs, after osteotomy compared to their preoperative state. This finding suggested that CPO can change the biomechanics of the lumbar spine in patients with DDH. During simulated CPO, as the contact area between the femoral head and acetabulum increases, the CP of the hip joint and intervertebral discs gradually decrease. However, compared with those of a normal human model, the biomechanical changes in the intervertebral disc are not consistent with those of a normal human model; specifically, the pressures exerted on both the L1/L2 and L5/S1 intervertebral discs are greater than those exerted on normal intervertebral discs. This may be attributed to the following: 1. The spino-pelvic compensatory mechanism. Delay et al.’s follow-up study on patients with bilateral DDH after bilateral PAO revealed a slight reduction in postoperative pelvic anteversion [5]. 2. The soft tissue balance also plays a role in this process. When we increase femoral head coverage during CPO, the inevitable rotation of the acetabular osteotomy block toward the lateral side causes the femoral head to shift inward, potentially altering the moment arms of the psoas major and iliacus muscles.

Limitations of the study include the following: Although the model was constructed based the full-length film of the lumbar pelvis in the standing position, there may be some discrepancies between the model and the actual human body while in the standing position, and the soft tissues were simplified during modeling. In our future work, we plan to conduct biomechanical experiments using three-dimensional MR images to determine the material parameters for various muscles and soft tissues. By incorporating these parameters into our finite element model, we aim to improve the accuracy of the simulation and analysis so that the model more closely resembles the human body. Additionally, this study focused on only one patient with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) whose spine did not have scoliosis. Therefore, it is necessary to establish and analyze different DDH patient models based on varying degrees of dislocation to examine changes in lumbar spine stress associated with disease severity. Last, single leg standing was the only posture examined in this study, which does not dynamically reflect biomechanical characteristics such as spinal flexion, extension, rotation, and lateral bending. Further research should encompass multiple angles, positions or complete gait cycles to obtain a more comprehensive understanding.

In this study, modeling and finite element mechanical analysis showed that the biomechanics of the lumbar spine in patients with DDH patients were significantly different from those in normal people. Compared with that in normal people, the force on the intervertebral disc in patients with DDH was significantly greater, and the force on the L4/L5 and L5/S1 intervertebral discs in patients with DDH was greater, which may cause degenerative diseases of the lumbar spine in patients with DDH, Low back pain is associated with quality of life and function [25] CPO not only reduces the contact pressure of the hip joint but also reduces the contact pressure of the lumbar intervertebral discs in patients with DDH. These results are of great value for improving the clinical outcome of CPO and, to some extent, delaying, or even preventing, lumbar degenerative diseases; however, the mechanism of the specific mechanical changes involved needs further study, Such as metabolic or serological indicators [26].

Conclusions

The findings from our finite element analysis indicate that CPO effectively normalizes hip contact pressure while reducing lumbar disc contact pressure. However, further investigation is required to elucidate the specific biomechanical mechanisms underlying these changes.
