Two-Year Changes in Astigmatism in Myopic Children Wearing Lenslet-ARray-Integrated Spectacle Lenses
Authors: Binbin Su, Pauline Cho, Haodi Xiao, Shichen Liu, Qi Yu, Fan Lu, Jun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the effects of Lenslet-ARray-Integrated (LARI) spectacle lenses on refractive and corneal astigmatism in myopic children.DesignA secondary analysis of data from a randomized, double-masked trial.ParticipantsPertinent data were retrieved from the files of 209 participants from the previous 2-phase LARI study, which reported the efficacy of LARI lenses on myopia control in children aged 6 to 12 years.MethodsData from participants of the LARI study (phase I: wearing LARI lenses or single-vision (SV) lenses for 12 months; phase II: all wore LARI lenses for another 12 months) were retrieved and analyzed. Participants who wore LARI lenses for 2 years were categorized as the LARI group, whereas those who switched from SV to LARI lenses were categorized as the SV lens switched over to LARI lens in the second year (SV-LARI) group. Data retrieved included cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction and refractive astigmatism, axial length, and corneal astigmatism at 6-month intervals, and the vector components (J0 and J45) of refractive and corneal astigmatism were calculated for analysis.Main Outcome MeasuresChanges in refractive and corneal astigmatism, J0, and J45.ResultsNo significant differences were found in increased refractive astigmatism, refractive J0 and J45, corneal astigmatism, and corneal J0 and J45 (all P > 0.05) between the LARI and SV groups in phase I. For participants who wore LARI lenses for 2 consecutive years, there were no significant differences in changes in refractive astigmatism and refractive J0 between phases I and II (P = 0.23 and P = 0.41), but change in J45 in phase II increased by –0.03 D (P = 0.003), and a smaller increase in corneal astigmatism was observed in the second year (0.07 ± 0.36 D vs. 0.20 ± 0.34 D, P = 0.02). In the SV-LARI group, a higher change in refractive astigmatism was observed in phase II (–0.12 ± 0.45 D, P = 0.03), but there were no significant differences in changes in refractive J0 and J45, corneal astigmatism, and corneal J0 and J45 between phases I and II (all P > 0.05).ConclusionsWearing LARI lenses for 2 years did not seem to lead to any clinically significant additional progression in refractive and corneal astigmatism.Financial DisclosuresProprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Astigmatism, Myopia control, Spectacle lens, J0, J45

Myopia has become a public health problem worldwide, especially among children and adolescents.1 Studies show that the prevalence of myopia in children and adolescents is increasing rapidly, particularly in East Asian countries, such as China, Japan, and Singapore.2, 3, 4 Epidemiological prediction models indicate that by 2050, approximately 740 million children and adolescents (about 40% of those aged 5 to 19 years) around the world will be myopic.5

Astigmatism is another common refractive error, which usually coexists with myopia. Astigmatism is related to the structure of the eyeball and is mainly influenced by the cornea and crystalline lens.6 It can be divided into refractive astigmatism, corneal astigmatism, and internal astigmatism. Studies have found that higher astigmatism7, 8, 9 or against-the-rule astigmatism8 in early childhood is associated with an increased likelihood of myopia onset or progression. A 5-year cohort study also showed association between high baseline astigmatism and against-the-rule astigmatism with axial elongation in compound myopic eyes.10

In recent years, some specially designed spectacle lenses, such as defocus incorporated multiple segments (DIMS),11 highly aspherical lenslets (HAL),12,13 and lenslet-array-integrated (LARI) lenses,14 have been proven to have significant myopia control effects, and are increasing in popularity for myopia prevention and control in clinical practice.15 However, previous studies have mainly focused on the effects of these lenses on spherical equivalent refraction and axial length, with less attention paid to their potential impact on astigmatism. There were some anecdotal reports that some children experienced an increase in astigmatism after wearing specially designed spectacle lenses and this has raised concern among parents. A retrospective study has shown that refractive astigmatism increased significantly after wearing HAL lenses for 1 year, and the refractive astigmatic changes in HAL group was higher than that in the single-vision (SV) group, which may be related to increased corneal astigmatism.16 However, another prospective cohort study demonstrated that children wearing DIMS lenses had a slightly smaller increase in refractive astigmatism over 12 months than those wearing SV lenses, with no significant difference in corneal astigmatic changes between the 2 groups.17

There is currently no consensus on whether wearing specially designed spectacle lenses will have adverse effects on astigmatism. The purpose of this study was to determine changes in refractive and corneal astigmatism with the use of LARI lenses using combined data from the LARI study.14,18 In addition, factors that may influence the changes were explored.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Pertinent data was retrieved from the files of participants of the LARI study14,18 (randomized, double-masked, controlled clinical trial [Chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2200057210]). The study was conducted from July 2022 to August 2024 at Wenzhou Medical University Affiliated Eye Hospital, and the study design has been described in detail elsewhere.14,18 Briefly, participants were of age 6 to 12 years, with spherical equivalent refraction between –4.00 D and –1.00 D in both eyes, astigmatism ≥ –1.50 D, and anisometropia ≤1.00 D. The LARI lenses used included Lenslet-ARray-Integrated spectacle lens with lenslets of +3.00 D addition power (PLARI) and Lenslet-ARray-Integrated spectacle lens with lenslets of –3.00 D addition power (NLARI) (Shanghai Gino Optical Spectacle Co Ltd).

Phase I (12 months) of the LARI study involved 3 randomly assigned groups: PLARI, NLARI, and SV, each with 80 participants. Over 95% of the participants completed the study. In phase II (12 months), the participants in each group were randomly divided into 2 subgroups, wearing either PLARI or NLARI lenses. Participants who wore LARI lenses in both phases of the study were categorized as LARI group, and SV participants who switched to wear PLARI or NLARI lenses were categorized as single-vision lens switched over to Lenslet-ARray-Integrated lens in the second year (SV-LARI) group.

This study was in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the Wenzhou Medical University Affilicated Eye Hospital (2021-241-K-211-03). Written informed consent from participants and their parents were obtained prior to participation. Data from 209 participants who completed both phases of the LARI study were retrieved for analysis. Axial length was measured by IOLMaster700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG). Corneal topographic data were obtained with Medmont E300 (Medmont International Pty Ltd), and flat and steep keratometry values were derived by identifying the minimum (flattest) and maximum (steepest) corneal curvature values within an 8 mm diameter circular zone centered on the corneal vertex. Corneal astigmatism is the difference between flat keratometry and steep keratometry, expressed as a negative value, with the axis being the direction of the flat keratometry. Cycloplegic refractive astigmatism was measured by Topcon KR-8800 (Topcon Corporation). Both refractive and corneal astigmatism were decomposed into J0 and J45 components through Thibos vector analysis.19 J0 was the horizontal or vertical vectors of astigmatism, calculated as: (-astigmatism power/2) × cos(2 × astigmatism axis); J45 was the oblique vectors of astigmatism, calculated as: (-astigmatism power/2) × sin(2 × astigmatism axis).

Statistical Analyses

SPSS statistics software, version 27 (IBM Corp) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc) were used for statistical analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Only data from the right eye of participants were analyzed. Chi-square test was used for categorical data. Analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous data from >2 groups, where appropriate, whereas unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison between 2 groups. For comparisons within group, Friedman test or repeated measures analysis of variance with the Bonferroni post hoc test, as appropriate, were used. Mixed linear model was used to compare for differences in changes in astigmatism between phases I and II, adjusting for initial age, gender, sphere, refractive astigmatism, refractive J0, refractive J45, and corneal astigmatism. In addition, the generalized linear model was used to conduct an exploratory analysis of the associations between changes in refractive astigmatism and age, gender, baseline ocular parameters, changes in corneal astigmatism, axial elongation, and treatment groups. To ensure model stability and avoid multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all predictors (baseline age, gender, sphere, refractive astigmatism, refractive J0, refractive J45, corneal astigmatism, changes in corneal astigmatism, axial elongation, and treatment groups). The mean VIF across all variables was 1.5, with individual VIF values ranging from 1.1 (gender) to 2.1 (baseline J0). No VIF exceeded the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating minimal collinearity and supporting the reliability of regression coefficients.

Results

Baseline Parameters among 3 Groups of Participants

A total of 209 participants completed both phases of the LARI study: 142 in the LARI group (71 from the PLARI group and 71 from the NLARI group in phase I) and 67 in the SV-LARI group (SV group in phase I). There were no significant differences in baseline parameters among the PLARI, NLARI, and SV groups (all P > 0.05), except that baseline refractive J0 in the NLARI group was greater than that in the SV group (P = 0.03) (Table 1). At the end of 12-month visit, before commencing phase II, no significant differences were found between groups (all P > 0.05), except that spherical equivalent refraction and refractive J0 in the LARI group were greater than those in the SV-LARI group (P = 0.003 and P = 0.04), and axial length was smaller than that of the SV-LARI group (P = 0.02) (Table 1).

Because no significant differences were found in the changes in refractive astigmatism, refractive J0, refractive J45, flat keratometry, steep keratometry, corneal astigmatism, corneal J0, and corneal J45 among the SV, PLARI, and NLARI groups in phase I (all P > 0.05) (Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), the PLARI and NLARI groups were combined and referred to as LARI group hereafter.

Twenty-Four-Month Trends in Astigmatism and Corneal Curvature

Over the 24-month period, both SV-LARI and LARI groups showed a significant increase in refractive astigmatism, refractive J0, corneal astigmatism, and corneal J0 from baseline to 24 months (all P < 0.001), while flat keratometry decreased significantly across the same time points (both P < 0.001) (Figure S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). There were no significant changes in refractive J45, corneal J45, and steep keratometry in both groups (all P > 0.05).

At baseline, 12 months, and 24 months after lens wear, refractive astigmatism and refractive J0 both exhibited significant moderate positive correlation with their respective corneal counterparts (corneal astigmatism and corneal J0, respectively) (Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). In contrast, refractive J45 had a weak, but significant, positive correlation with corneal J45.

Changes in Refractive Astigmatism, Flat and Steep Keratometry, and Corneal Astigmatism in Phase I

The 12-month changes in refractive astigmatism in LARI group were slightly higher than that in SV-LARI group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.29) (Table 2). No significant differences were found in the changes in refractive J0, refractive J45, flat keratometry, steep keratometry, corneal astigmatism, corneal J0, and corneal J45 in phase I between the LARI and SV-LARI group (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Comparison of Changes in Astigmatism between Phases I and II

After model adjustment, the mixed linear model analysis showed no significant differences in the changes in refractive astigmatism, refractive J0, flat keratometry, steep keratometry, and corneal J45 between phases I and II in the LARI group (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). The change in refractive J45 during phase II was slightly negative compared with phase I, with an adjusted difference of –0.03 ± 0.14 D (P = 0.003) (Table 2). In addition, compared with phase I, the increases in corneal astigmatism and corneal J0 in phase II were significantly reduced, with an adjusted difference of 0.10 ± 0.49 D (P = 0.02) and –0.10 ± 0.35 D (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

In the SV-LARI group, the increase in refractive astigmatism during phase II was greater than that in phase I, with an adjusted difference of –0.12 ± 0.45 D (P = 0.03) (Table 2). However, the increase in corneal J0 during phase II was smaller than that in phase I, with an adjusted difference of –0.10 ± 0.38 D (P = 0.04) (Table 2). No significant differences were observed in the changes in refractive J0, refractive J45, flat keratometry, steep keratometry, corneal astigmatism, and corneal J45 were observed between phases I and II (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Distribution of Participants with Increases in Refractive Astigmatism

Figure 1 shows the proportion of participants with increase in refractive astigmatism over 24 months. At the end of phase II, the proportions of participants with a more negative shift in refractive astigmatism of ≥0.50 D were 44.4% and 50.7% in the LARI and SV-LARI groups, respectively. There were no significant differences in the proportion of participants with an increase in refractive astigmatism between the 2 groups (P = 0.45).

In the LARI group, the proportion of participants with increase in refractive astigmatism in phase II was similar to that in phase I. However, in the SV-LARI group, a higher proportion of participants showed a more negative refractive astigmatic change of ≥0.50 D in phase II (P = 0.04).

Exploratory Association Analysis for Factors Affecting Changes in Refractive Astigmatism

Changes in refractive astigmatism (both in phase I and over 2-year follow-up) were significantly correlated with baseline refractive astigmatism, baseline corneal astigmatism, and the concurrent changes in corneal astigmatism (all P < 0.001). No significant associations were observed between refractive astigmatism changes and gender, baseline sphere, axial elongation, or treatment groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 3 and Table S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Changes in refractive astigmatism over 2 years were significantly correlated with baseline age (P = 0.02), while those in phase I were unrelated to baseline age (P = 0.07).

Discussion

This study found that both the SV-LARI and LARI groups showed comparable increases in refractive and corneal astigmatism over 2 years. The SV-LARI group had a slightly faster progression in refractive astigmatism after switching from SV to LARI, while continual LARI lens wear showed similar refractive astigmatism progression in year 2 as year 1. Exploratory association analysis revealed that refractive astigmatism progression was correlated with baseline refractive astigmatism, baseline corneal astigmatism, and increases in corneal astigmatism.

In recent years, various specially designed spectacles have been proven to have significant myopia control effects.15 However, research on their impact on astigmatism remains relatively limited. A retrospective study found HAL lenses led to a greater increase in refractive astigmatism compared to SV lenses after 1 year.16 Nevertheless, the research results by Wang et al17 were the opposite, showing the refractive astigmatism progression in the DIMS group within 12 months was slightly lower than that in the SV group. Current randomized clinical trial showed that the increases in refractive astigmatism in the LARI group at 12 months was –0.15 D, which was not statistically different from the SV group, and was similar to the reported results for HAL (–0.15 D)16 and DIMS (–0.17 D).17 However, the progression of refractive astigmatism by about 0.12 D after switching from SV to LARI lenses was similar to the findings of Xu et al.16 The exact mechanism behind this phenomenon was not yet clear, but this difference was small and not likely to be of clinical significance. The increase in refractive astigmatism during the second year of LARI lens wear was consistent with that in the first year. Further comparison revealed that the total increases in refractive astigmatism in the SV-LARI group and the LARI group over 2 years were similar to the 2-year data of the SV group in 2 low concentration atropine randomized controlled trials (–0.33 D20 and –0.38 D21). Taken together with available published data, this exploratory comparison indicates that the progression of refractive astigmatism over 2 years with LARI lenses does not appear to exceed that of SV lenses.

The current study revealed a significant increase in refractive astigmatism after wearing LARI lenses, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear. The prevalence and magnitude of refractive astigmatism in children may be age-related.22, 23, 24, 25 Zhang et al25 reported that refractive astigmatism increased with age in primary school children, but not in kindergarten and junior high school children. The participants in the current study were aged 6–12 years old and mainly in primary school, so their refractive astigmatism may increase with age. It is also possible that the increase in refractive astigmatism is correlated with baseline astigmatism and corneal astigmatism progression. In this study, corneal astigmatism increased significantly over the 2-year follow-up, consistent with findings from pediatric epidemiological surveys.25,26 Refractive astigmatism was significantly correlated with corneal astigmatism, suggesting that increases in corneal astigmatism may contribute to refractive astigmatism progression.23 Moreover, mixed linear model analysis revealed that both 1-year and 2-year refractive astigmatism progression were significantly associated with baseline refractive astigmatism, baseline corneal astigmatism, and corneal astigmatism progression, consistent with findings from Wang et al.17 In the current study, no significant independent association was found between treatment group and refractive astigmatism changes after adjusting for the progression of corneal astigmatism, suggesting that the impact of LARI treatment on refractive astigmatism may be mainly mediated through changes in corneal astigmatism. Therefore, it is necessary to consider children’s initial refractive astigmatism, initial corneal astigmatism, and changes in corneal astigmatism when interpreting the changes in refractive astigmatism. Children with low initial refractive astigmatism, high corneal astigmatism, and greater progression of corneal astigmatism are more likely to experience an increase in refractive astigmatism after wearing LARI lenses. In addition, wearing myopia control spectacle lenses may cause asymmetric choroidal thickening,27,28 leading to asymmetric changes in retinal morphology, thus changing intraocular astigmatism, which may also impact changes of refractive astigmatism.

Astigmatism is a vector with value and direction, and using vector analysis can provide a more comprehensive observation of its changes. The results of this study showed that whether wearing SV or LARI lenses, or switching SV to LARI lenses, there was a similar increase in refractive J0, while refractive J45 remained stable. Consistent with the results of our study, previous epidemiological survey results also showed that the progression of refractive astigmatism in children mainly due to an increase in J0, with no significant change in J45.29,30 In addition, in 2 randomized controlled trials of low-concentration atropine, the changes in refractive J0 and J45 in the SV group were also consistent with those in this study.20,21 These results further support that, compared with SV lenses, LARI lenses do not appear to exert a more severe impact on refractive astigmatic changes.

The strength of the current study lies in its randomized, controlled, and double-blinded trial design, and vector decomposition of astigmatism can comprehensively evaluate the effects of wearing LARI lenses and SV lenses on astigmatism in myopic children. A major limitation of this study was the lack of a concurrent 2-year SV control group. Due to ethical considerations, all participants in the SV group were switched to LARI lenses in the second year. As a result, direct comparisons between the SV and LARI groups were only feasible during the first year. To understand the 2-year astigmatic changes in the SV wearers, this study cited 2-year astigmatic changes data from SV participants of two 2-year clinical trials, which was used solely for exploratory reference.20,21 In addition, it is unclear if similar findings would be obtained with different myopia control ophthalmic lenses and in children of different ethnicities. Further studies may be warranted.

Conclusions

Significant increases in refractive and corneal astigmatism were observed in children wearing LARI lenses over the 2-year follow-up. Moreover, increases in refractive astigmatism were significantly correlated with baseline refractive astigmatism, baseline corneal astigmatism, and progression of corneal astigmatism. However, compared with SV lenses, LARI lenses did not appear to induce any clinically significant additional progression in refractive or corneal astigmatism.
