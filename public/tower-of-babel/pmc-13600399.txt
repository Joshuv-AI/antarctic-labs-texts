Crisis as Catalyst: The Future of the NIH Driving Scientific Discovery
Authors: Ana A Weil, Sarah M Baker, Frederick S Buckner, Geoffrey S Gottlieb, Kevin Hybiske, David M Koelle, Tara B Reid, Michelle C Sabo, Chetan Seshadri, Javeed A Shah, Wesley C Van Voorhis, Helen Y Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe National Institutes of Health (NIH) has an unparalleled record of advancing biomedical science. Today, 2 critical challenges threaten its future impact. We propose concrete steps to restore the NIH's trajectory and preserve its leadership in scientific discovery.Keywords: National Institutes of Health, early-Stage investigator, study section, physician-scientist

Biomedical research funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) has transformed human health. NIH-supported discoveries have reshaped oncologic care, improved prevention and treatment of HIV and other infectious diseases, advanced therapies for autoimmune conditions, and extended life expectancy. These transformative improvements in human health unfolded over decades of sustained investment in scientific infrastructure and the biomedical workforce.

The NIH's role as a driver of scientific innovation now faces 2 major threats. First is the shifting interpretation of the NIH's value by the current administration. Over the past year, policy changes have moved NIH priorities toward implementation, replication of research, and short-term gains to demonstrate “delivery of results to the public” [1]. This approach is not likely to yield the needed paradigm-shifting discoveries that often require investment in risk over a long time horizon. Furthermore, many of the recently introduced reforms undermine longstanding infrastructure and norms. These include increased political influence in funding decisions, delays in grant review and disbursal of awards, and reductions in overall funding amounts [2]. The proposed changes also introduce significant barriers to foreign collaboration, such as rules around foreign co-authorship and new mechanisms for research studies to be done in other countries. Many of these actions have been executed in a manner perceived to be arbitrary, negatively affecting morale and retention, particularly among early career scientists [3]. The activities of the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, in particular, have contributed to one of the most disruptive periods in NIH history [4]. Current trends suggest that this imposed chaos will continue, at least in the short term, and the long-term consequences have yet to be fully realized.

Second, even before the destructive strategy employed by the current administration, accumulated structural inefficiencies threatened the NIH's continued success. For example, regulatory requirements have become more complex over decades, resulting in time reallocated to administrative tasks and the oversight processes rather than science [5]. Research using vertebrate animals, international collaborations, and human subjects research have been especially affected [6, 7]. Although regulations are essential for protecting research participants and performing ethical research, the now extensive bureaucracy required has not resulted in proportional benefits [7]. One example is the inclusion of ancillary documents such as extensive human subjects information and other nonscored components of grants that are submitted on the initial due date; this could be delayed until after the initial review. Another proposed solution is improved coordination across agencies with overlapping mandates and streamlining regulatory processes at clinical trial sites and their institutions.

The overall increase in administrative burden has occurred in the setting of growing competition for NIH funding amid relatively stagnant budgets. One indicator is the steadily increasing age at which investigators obtain their first major NIH award. According to NIH data, scientists now typically receive their first R01 grant in their early 40 seconds, reflecting prolonged training and delayed independence. Furthermore, the top 10% of investigators receive as much as 40% of total NIH funding, a concentration that has grown over time [8]. These trends have made the current model of supporting investigator-initiated research strained due to delay in promotion timelines and discouragement of new investigators. A National Academies report has outlined approaches for shifting NIH policies toward incentivizing progress rather than creating barriers, including increasing the number of awards for postdoctoral or early career researchers [9]. Although programs supporting Early-Stage Investigators and reforms such as the Simplified Review Framework have the potential to improve these issues, structural incentives within peer review continue to favor more seasoned investigators and lower-risk, incremental projects over bold, high-impact ideas. In practice, this dynamic encourages investigators to pursue safer work to remain competitive for funding. To demonstrate that a proposed idea is a “sure thing,” extensive pilot data that has already been peer-reviewed and published is required for the most common investigator-initiated research grants. Even R21 grants, which were designed to fund more exploratory studies, are recognized as only competitive with substantial preliminary results.

Together, these challenges threaten the long-term pipeline of transformative biomedical innovation. The current state of the NIH presents a unique opportunity to address longstanding inefficiencies, reimagine a more sustainable research enterprise, and reaffirm the vital role of the NIH. We feel that this is a pivotal moment to rebuild the NIH for the future, through a thoughtful process that preserves the best of the American scientific enterprise (Table 1).

Addressing these issues requires a renewed vision that embraces uncertainty and prioritizes scientific impact over predictability. We propose that the NIH focus on funding the most impactful science by reforming both incentives and resource allocation. Identification of the strongest proposals should continue to rely on peer review by subject-matter experts that are free of political interference. Today, the time commitment and inadequate compensation for serving in an NIH study section disincentivizes highly qualified investigators from participating. The review process should also be modified to more highly reward originality, ambition, and potential impact, even at the cost of a higher likelihood of failure or negative results. Study section reviewers should be explicitly encouraged to value high-risk, high-reward proposals. Existing NIH programs provide a foundation for these reforms. The High-Risk, High-Reward Research Program is specifically designed to fund breakthrough ideas with limited preliminary data but currently has funding rates of only 4%–10%. Expanding such programs by increasing paylines should be a priority.

Physician-scientists represent another critical group for investment based on their past track record of transformative discoveries. They uniquely bridge clinical care and laboratory research but face significant structural disincentives such as low pay and prolonged training periods relative to their clinical peers. Strengthening career development pathways, including offering salaries competitive with full-time clinical peers, would improve retention in the physician-scientist pathway. For example, at our institution, physician-scientists supported by K awards earn approximately 35% less than their clinical counterparts at the same career stage. This presents a particular challenge for investigators who are often also starting families at this point in their careers and must make practical decisions about costs of housing and childcare. Career development awards that support early investigators are essential, but their funding levels often fall short of the resources required for bridging to independence. Increasing the salary and research support for technicians, equipment and supplies, and other costs of doing research in these programs could encourage investigators to pursue greater risks in research earlier in their careers and strengthen the overall scientific pipeline.

Additional reforms should focus on increasing transparency and flexibility in NIH funding decisions. The NIH has begun moving in this direction through initiatives such as the Unified Funding Strategy, which considers factors beyond paylines, including career stage and existing funding. However, concerns remain regarding how transparently this flexibility will be applied. Of particular concern is the new introduction of policies granting political appointees greater influence over funding decisions, which may increase bias and reduce trust if not properly managed. Greater transparency in NIH decision-making would strengthen confidence in the system. This could include communication to the public around funding priorities and perceived research gaps, public access to nonconfidential portions of council meetings, and sharing NIH feedback received from stakeholders, including researchers, patients, and industry.

Revitalizing the NIH's role in advancing science will require thoughtful and deliberate disruption, led by scientists and policymakers whose goal is to create the most impactful science. Mechanisms designed to assess proposals primarily on creativity and impact even if high risk, rather than conventional scoring criteria, could better support transformative science. Equally important is reducing administrative burden, particularly in grant submission and reporting requirements, to allow researchers to devote more time and funding to scientific discovery. This will require increased investment, a true and transparent commitment to funding breakthrough science, and a renewed emphasis on scientific creativity. These steps are essential to maintaining U.S. leadership in biomedical discovery and ensuring continued progress in improving human health for decades to come.
