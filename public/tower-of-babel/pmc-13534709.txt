Biomimetic diffusion metamaterials enabled by physical-geometric duality
Authors: Fubao Yang, Yuhong Zhou, Gaole Dai, Cheng-Wei Qiu, Jiping Huang, Liujun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Structural design typically faces a fundamental trade-off between advanced functionality and practical manufacturability, a challenge that is especially acute in freeform metamaterials, where omnidirectional performance often demands intricate fabrication. Inspired by the angle-preserving growth of leaves, we address this long-standing challenge by establishing a physical-geometric duality for metamaterial design. This duality enables geometric transformations to be derived directly from material parameters and fields obtained via physical equivalence. The resulting diffusion metamaterials exhibit a distinctive behavior: despite strong anisotropy and heterogeneity in thermal conductivity, isotherms and heat streamlines remain mutually orthogonal. This property effectively reduces the realization of complex thermal conductivity tensors to the direct alignment of laminated composites along local field lines without optimization. We experimentally demonstrate a freeform thermal cloak that achieves omnidirectional invisibility. Our work establishes a biomimetic design paradigm that reconciles functionality with manufacturability, with broad implications for thermal management and diffusive transport.

Introduction

Metamaterials, artificially engineered structures composed of unit cells densely packed into effective media, exhibit properties unattainable in natural media1–5, providing a transformative platform for manipulating physical fields. Precise control of diffusion is a persistent challenge across domains ranging from microscale transport in soft matter6 and phononic conduction7,8 to macroscopic mass and energy transfer9–11. Thermal metamaterials12,13 have yielded remarkable functions, including cloaking14–17, concentrating18,19, rotating20,21, and camouflaging22,23. Increasingly complex thermal environments and diverse heat-control requirements call for freeform metamaterials. However, structural design typically faces a trade-off between functionality and manufacturability. Freeform thermal metamaterials epitomize this tension. The fundamental contradiction between omnidirectional functionality and straightforward fabrication remains a significant and long‑standing challenge. These two mutually exclusive advantages mainly result from two distinct design paradigms.

Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\kappa }_{{\rm{P}}}$$\end{document}κP is isotropic, heat streamlines and isotherms are inherently orthogonal, enabling direct fabrication. In these designs, the inherent dependence of physical equivalence on the external temperature field restricts them to unidirectional functionality. Especially for freeform geometries, if the external temperature field changes direction or becomes nonuniform (e.g., generated by point heat sources), performance inevitably deteriorates. Despite the advantage of direct fabrication, achieving omnidirectional functionality remains unattainable.

Although this trade-off appears intractable, a geometric insight from leaf growth suggests a promising solution. While the local growth rate is inhomogeneous and anisotropic, the global evolution minimizes elastic stresses by preserving local angles37–39 (left panel of Fig. 1c). This geometric analogy inspires the construction of diffusion metamaterials: just as angle-preserving deformations suppress elastic stresses in leaves, analogous transformations may eliminate the off-diagonal components of anisotropic thermal conductivity. If we can identify transformation matrices associated with angle-preserving mappings, it may provide a fundamental pathway to reconcile omnidirectional functionality with direct realization.

Here, we resolve this long-standing contradiction by establishing a physical-geometric duality that links the disparate methods of physical equivalence and geometric transformation (Fig. 1c). Specifically, the physical parameters (thermal conductivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\kappa }_{{\rm{P}}}$$\end{document}κP and temperature TT) of a unidirectional device explicitly prescribe the geometric transformation described by a Jacobian matrix required for its omnidirectional counterpart. This duality yields a distinctive class of metamaterials, termed diffusion duality metamaterials, designed via the proposed physical-geometric duality. A key feature of these metamaterials is that, despite strong anisotropy and heterogeneity, the conductivity tensor is diagonal in the local coordinate system defined by isotherms and streamlines, which remain mutually orthogonal throughout the device. Specifically, the resulting thermal conductivity tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\bf{\upkappa}_{\mathrm{D}}}}$$\end{document}𝛋D can be expressed in the diagonal form with eigenvalues of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\kappa }_{\mathrm{P}}$$\end{document}κP and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\kappa }_{0}^{2}/{\kappa }_{\mathrm{P}}$$\end{document}κ02/κP, and its principal axes are naturally aligned with the local streamline/isotherm directions. This alignment makes it direct to implement spatially varying anisotropic thermal conductivity by orienting laminated composites along the local field-line directions. Omnidirectional functionality and straightforward fabrication are thus compatible, as verified by simulations and experiments. Beyond the single function and steady state, the core Jacobian matrix also contributes to direction-dependent functions, transient regimes, and wide diffusion and wave systems where transformation theory applies. Our work offers a general platform for designing advanced freeform metamaterials without compromising performance or manufacturability.

Universality for arbitrary unidirectional inputs

A remarkable advantage of the physical-geometric duality is its compatibility with arbitrary unidirectional inputs. For proof, we integrate our paradigm with topology optimization, a powerful method but traditionally limited to designing unidirectional devices due to its dependence on the external temperature field.

We first employ topology optimization to generate a unidirectional thermal cloak with a highly asymmetric geometry (Fig. 4a). Although this device performs well under a horizontal temperature gradient (Fig. 4b), its functionality degrades when the gradient becomes vertical, leading to severe distortion of the background field (Fig. 4c). By applying the physical-geometric duality directly to the topology optimization-generated fields, we analytically derive the required Jacobian matrix and the corresponding anisotropic conductivity tensor (Fig. 4d). The resulting metamaterial exhibits a precise omnidirectional cloaking effect (Fig. 4e, f). To demonstrate manufacturability in such complex geometries, we discretize the continuous tensor using local streamlines and isotherms (Fig. 4g) and realize the device with only four isotropic materials. The discretized cloak maintains omnidirectional invisibility (Fig. 4h, i). Discussion

Our duality paradigm features unified mathematical structures and theoretical predictions. The derived Jacobian matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{\Lambda }}$$\end{document}Λ bears a formal resemblance to pseudoconformal mappings42,43. Unlike pseudoconformal mappings, which are typically restricted to specific geometries, our approach enables omnidirectional functionalities with freeform geometries. As demonstrated by the topology-optimized case, this framework can process physical fields from any sources, whether analytical derivation or numerical optimization44, to produce omnidirectional devices with arbitrarily asymmetric geometries that are intractable for coordinate-transformation-based methods. Another striking prediction is that a unidirectional metamaterial functioning in the orthogonal direction requires an isotropic thermal conductivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\kappa }_{0}^{2}/{\kappa }_{\mathrm{P}}$$\end{document}κ02/κP, which is characterized by an interchange of heat streamlines and isotherms (Supplementary Information Note 3). This prediction stems from the inherent orthogonality between the streamline and isotherm axes, which ensures that their respective heat pathways are physically decoupled. Notably, recent works on field-line-guided coordinate-transformation strategies have elegantly utilized coordinate mappings along field trajectories to decouple the functionalities of heat flux and temperature45, with subsequent extensions achieving synergistic electrothermal multi-field control46. Specifically, the mutual orthogonality in our framework applies to the initial background field (and its orthogonal counterpart). When subjected to other external fields, the internal field pathways no longer align with the local principal axes, rendering the streamlines and isotherms non-orthogonal. Nevertheless, this behavior is a natural physical consequence of the tensor’s high anisotropy and does not compromise the device’s omnidirectional functionality.

Since the physical-geometric duality is rooted in the Jacobian transformation, it readily extends from thermal management to broader physical systems where transformation theory applies, including mass transport47,48, charge diffusion49, light propagation50, hydrodynamic systems51, and static magnetic fields52. Conflict between performance and manufacturability similarly constrains designs in these domains. As a proof-of-concept, we provide an example of an omnidirectional direct current electric cloak designed using the proposed duality framework in Supplementary Information Note 7. Our paradigm offers a universal solution to this long-standing barrier, supporting future development of metamaterials that unite freeform geometries, omnidirectional performance, and direct fabrication across a wide spectrum of diffusion and wave phenomena.

Experimental realization

The model size is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$19\times 19 \, {{\rm{cm}}}^{2}$$\end{document}19×19cm2 with a thickness of 22 cm. Based on Fig. 3c, we selected actual materials for the sample. The specific material selections and their corresponding parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1. We used computer numerical control (CNC) wire cutting to fabricate the metallic skeleton of the sample, as shown in Fig. 3d. The skeleton was placed in a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$19\times 19\times 2 \, {{\rm{cm}}}^{3}$$\end{document}19×19×2cm3 bakelite box with the top cover removed, and different types of silicone grease were filled into the framework according to the design specifications.

To experimentally realize the specific thermal boundary conditions used in the simulations, we combined thermostatic water baths, highly conductive copper connectors, and thermal insulation materials. Two thermostatic water baths were utilized to establish constant temperature differences (acting as boundaries): one was filled with an ice-water mixture (cold source), and the other was equipped with a heating rod (hot source). For the uniform linear background and line heat source cases (Fig. 3j, k), two copper plates were placed in direct contact with the target sample edges and submerged in the water baths to generate uniform isothermal boundary conditions. For the point heat source case (Fig. 3l), four 1-cm-wide copper strips were placed at the diagonally adjacent short edges of the sample and submerged to achieve localized point-source conditions. Furthermore, to equivalently realize adiabatic boundary conditions, foam and thermal insulation glue were added to all sides of the sample. Supplementary Fig. S3 shows top and side views of the sample, the heating arrangements for line and point heat sources, and an overview of the experimental setup.
