1.
Introduction

Many authors have explored the idea of associating to a given Banach space or Banach lattice a real function or a numerical parameter, in order to analyze some specific geometric properties of that space (or lattice). For instance, J. Gao introduced a parameter in
[
11
]
, now known as the James constant, which quantifies the non-squareness of the unit ball. The James constant has been widely studied by numerous researchers (see
[
16
]
and the references therein for a comprehensive overview).

Another parameter, introduced by J. J. Schäffer in
[
27
]
and perhaps less well known, is the so-called Schäffer constant, defined for a Banach space
X
as

\lambda(X)=\inf\{\max\{\|x+y\|,\|x-y\|\}\,\colon\,\|x\|=\|y\|=1\}.

This constant is related to the concept of uniform non-squareness introduced by Schäffer in 1970. As K. Jarosz demonstrated in
[
15
]
, the Schäffer constant has applications in the study of vector-valued extensions of the Banach–Stone theorem. Furthermore, in the pursuit of vector-valued lattice extensions of the Banach–Stone theorem, E. M. Galego and the first author introduced a lattice version of the Schäffer constant in
[
10
]
for a real Banach lattice
X
, namely:

\lambda^{+}(X)=\inf\{\max\{\|x+y\|,\|x-y\|\}\,\colon\,\mbox{$x,y\geq{\bf 0}$ and $\|x\|=\|y\|=1$}\}.

We refer to this parameter here as the
lattice Schäffer constant
. Notice that
\lambda(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
for every Banach lattice
X
. Moreover, the equality does not hold in general (see examples below).

The aim of this paper is to study the lattice Schäffer constant, as well as its companion parameter:

\displaystyle\beta(X)=\inf\{\|x\vee y\|\,\colon\,\mbox{$\|x\|=\|y\|=1$, $x,y\geq{\bf 0}$ and $x\wedge y={\bf 0}$}\}.

We begin by outlining the organization of the paper. In Section
2
, we review key facts about Banach lattices, and recall the definitions of the lower and upper moduli of uniform monotonicity of a Banach lattice

X
, denoted by
\delta_{m,X}(\cdot)
and
\sigma_{X}(\cdot)
, respectively. We also gather some key facts about these moduli and their corresponding characteristics,
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)
.

In Section
3
, we examine the relationship between the lattice Schäffer constant and the Riesz angle of a Banach lattice,
\alpha(X)
, as introduced by J.M. Borwein and B. Sims in
[
5
]
. Additionally, we compute the value
\lambda^{+}(X)
and
\beta(X)
for several Banach lattices
X
.

In Section
4
we show that the parameters
\lambda^{+}
and
\beta
are preserved when passing to the second dual and to the ultrapower. Further, these quantities can be computed using finite dimensional sublattices only.

Section
5
explores some connections between our parameters and the “global” properties of Banach lattices, such as their convexity, concavity, or being a KB-space.

Finally, in Section
6
we connect the lattice Schäffer constant to the moduli of monotonicity.
For instance, we prove that for every Banach lattice
X
, the inequality
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq 2-\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)
holds. Also we show that
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)<1
if and only if
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
. As a consequence, we prove that if
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)+\alpha(X)<2
, then
X
is superreflexive.

2.
Preliminaries

The standard terminology and notation of Banach space and Banach lattice theory will be used and can be found in
[
1
]
and
[
23
]
. The Banach spaces considered here are assumed to be real.
If
X
is a Banach lattice,
X^{+}
denotes the positive cone of X. If
X
is a Banach space,
S_{X}
and
B_{X}
stand for the unit sphere and the closed unit ball, respectively. Also, we set
S_{X}^{+}=S_{X}\cap X^{+}
and
B_{X}^{+}=B_{X}\cap X^{+}
. If
X
and
Y
are Banach lattices, a bijective operator
T\colon X\to Y
is called
Banach lattice isomorphism
if

T(x\vee y)=Tx\vee Ty
for all
x,y\in X
. A
Banach lattice isometry
is a Banach lattice isomorphism which is also an isometry.

We briefly recall the definition of the ultrapower of a Banach space (see e.g.
[
12
]
for more information). Let
X

be a Banach space and let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-trivial ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
. If
(a_{n})
is a bounded sequence of real numbers, it can be shown that there is an unique real number
a
such that
for each
\varepsilon>0
, the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,\colon\,|a_{n}-a|<\varepsilon\}
belongs to
\mathcal{U}
. The number
a
is called the
\mathcal{U}
-limit of the sequence
(a_{n})

and it is denoted by
\mathcal{U}-\lim a_{n}
.

Let
\ell_{\infty}(X)
be the Banach space of all bounded sequences
(x_{n})
in
X
endowed with the natural norm. The set
c_{0,\mathcal{U}}(X)=\{(x_{i})\in\ell_{\infty}(X)\,\colon\,\mathcal{U}-\lim\|x_{i}\|=0\}
is a closed subspace of
\ell_{\infty}(X)
. The
ultrapower of

X

following
\mathcal{U}
(or
along
\mathcal{U}
) is defined as
X_{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\ell_{\infty}(X)\big/c_{0,\mathcal{U}}(X)
with the quotient norm. The elements of
X_{\mathcal{U}}
are denoted by
(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}
.
An instructive exercise is to show that the norm of
(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\in X_{\mathcal{U}}
is given by
\|(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\|=\mathcal{U}-\lim\|x_{n}\|
.

Finally, if
X
is a Banach lattice, the formulas

\displaystyle(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\vee(y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq(x_{n}\vee y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\wedge(y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq(x_{n}\wedge y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}

for each
(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}},(y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\in X_{\mathcal{U}}
define a natural Banach lattice structure on the ultrapower
X_{\mathcal{U}}
. The following fact is a consequence of definitions.

Fact 2.1
.

If
X
is a Banach lattice, then the map
x\in X\mapsto(x,x,\ldots)_{\mathcal{U}}\in X_{\mathcal{U}}
is a Banach lattice isometry.

Now we introduce two useful functions. Let
X
be a Banach lattice. For
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
we set

\displaystyle\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|x+\varepsilon y\|-1\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}^{+}\},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\inf\{1-\|x-y\|\,\colon\,{\bf 0}\leq y\leq x,\|x\|\leq 1,\|y\|\geq\varepsilon\}.

These functions are called the
upper modulus of monotonicity
and the
lower modulus of uniform monotonicity
of
X
, respectively. It is known that
\delta_{m,X}
is non-decreasing in
[0,1]
and continuous in
[0,1)
. Associated with these modules, we have the following numbers called
characteristics of monotonicity
of
X
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\varepsilon\in[0,1)\,\colon\,\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)=0\},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\varepsilon\in[0,1)\,\colon\,\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)=0\}.

We say that
X
is
uniformly monotone
if
\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)>0
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. It is known that

X
is uniformly monotone if and only if
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)>0
if and only if
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)>0
. This is a consequence of the
inequalities (see
[
13
, Theorem 1]
):

(2.1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)\leq 2\varepsilon_{0,m}(X).

For more information on these objects, see
[
8
]
.

For a proof of the following statements, see
[
18
]
and
[
22
, Theorem 2.1]
, respectively.

Fact 2.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{m,X}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)}{1-\delta_{m,X}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)}\leq\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)}{1-\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)}

Fact 2.3
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. For all
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\delta_{m,X}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)}{1+\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. The map
\varepsilon\in[0,1]\mapsto\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)\in[0,1]
is 1-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

If
t,s\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle|\|x+ty\|-\|x+sy\||\leq|t-s|.

for all
x,y\in S_{X}^{+}
. By taking infima, we conclude that
|\sigma_{X}(t)-\sigma_{X}(s)|\leq|t-s|
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. Then,
\sigma_{X}(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X))=0
.

Remark 2.6
.

This work examines quantitative characterizations of monotonicity in a Banach lattice. These are connected with the geometry of its unit ball. We do not study these connections here; an interested reader is referred to e.g.
[
14
]
.

3.
Some parameters associated to Banach spaces and Banach lattices

In this section, we introduce the lattice Schäffer constant and explore its relationship with various other constants associated with Banach spaces and Banach lattices. Additionally, we provide examples of the lattice Schäffer constant for specific Banach lattices.

Recall that if
X
is a Banach space, the James and Schäffer constants of
X
are defined respectively by

\displaystyle J(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\inf\{\|x-y\|,\|x+y\|\}\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}\},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\lambda(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\max\{\|x-y\|,\|x+y\|\}\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}\}.

If
X
is a Banach lattice, then similar parameters may be defined using its order structure. For instance,
[
20
]
and
[
29
]
defined the
Riesz angle
of a Banach lattice
X
:

\displaystyle\alpha(X)=\sup\{\|x\vee y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in B_{X}^{+}\}.

This parameter was used in
[
5
]
to study fixed point properties.

In this paper we shall focus on the following parameter, defined in
[
9
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. The lattice Schäffer constant of
X
is the number

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)=\inf\{\max\{\|x-y\|,\|x+y\|\}\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}^{+}\}.

Clearly
\lambda^{+}({\mathbb{R}})=2
and
\alpha({\mathbb{R}})=1
. We shall be concerned with lattices of dimension at least
2
.
Begin by providing alternative formulas for
\lambda^{+}(X)
and
\alpha(X)
(which also make clear that
\alpha(X)
is the lattice counterpart of the James constant
J(X)
).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice, with
\dim X\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|x+y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}^{+}\}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|\,|x|+|y|\,\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|\,|x-y|\vee|x+y|\,\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|x+y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in X^{+},\,\min\{\|x\|,\|y\|\}\geq 1\}.

Also, we have

\displaystyle\alpha(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|x\vee y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in B_{X}^{+},x\wedge y={\bf 0}\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|x-y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}^{+}\}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|\,|x|-|y|\,\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|\,|x-y|\wedge|x+y|\,\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|\,|x-y|\wedge|x+y|\,\|\,\colon\,x,y\in B_{X}\}.

Proof.

(
3.2
): The first identity follows because
|x-y|\leq x+y
for all
x,y\in X^{+}
. The second one is consequence from the fact
that
\|\,|x|\,\|=\|x\|
for each
x\in X
. Now, since
|x|+|y|=|x-y|\vee|x+y|
for all
x,y\in X
, we obtain
the third equality. Finally, if
D(X)=\inf\{\|x+y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in X^{+},\,\min\{\|x\|,\|y\|\}\geq 1\}
, then

D(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
. To prove the opposite inequality, if
\varepsilon>0
is given, there exist
x,y\in X^{+}
with

\min\{\|x\|,\|y\|\}\geq 1\}
such that
\|x+y\|<D(X)+\varepsilon
. Notice that

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\left\|\frac{x}{\|x\|}+\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right\|\leq\|x+y\|<D(X)+\varepsilon.

From the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
we conclude that
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq D(X)
.

(
3.2
): Clearly
\alpha(X)\geq\sup\{\|x\vee y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in B_{X}^{+},x\wedge y={\bf 0}\}
. To prove the converse, recall that, by
[
29
]
(see
[
30
]
for a different proof),
\alpha(X)=\alpha(X^{**})
. Being a dual space,
X^{**}
is
\sigma
-order complete, hence by
[
30
]
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exist disjoint
u,v\in B_{X^{**}}^{+}
so that
\alpha(X^{**})<\|u\vee v\|+\varepsilon
.
By
[
3
]
, for such
u,v
there exist disjoint
x,y\in B_{X}^{+}
so that
\|x\vee y\|+\varepsilon>\|u\vee v\|
. Thus,
\alpha(X)=\alpha(X^{**})<\|x\vee y\|+2\varepsilon
, and we obtain the first formula for
\alpha(X)
by taking
\varepsilon
arbitrarily small.

Since
x+y=|x-y|
for all
x,y\in X^{+}
with
x\wedge y={\bf 0}
, the second equation holds. The third equation follows since
||x|-|y||=|x-y|\wedge|x+y|
for all
x,y\in X
; a similar reasoning can be used for the fourth one.
To prove the last equation, let
C(X)=\sup\{\|\,|x-y|\wedge|x+y|\,\|\,\colon\,x,y\in B_{X}\}
. Clearly
\alpha(X)\leq C(X)
. For the reverse inequality, notice that for each
x,y\in X
, the map
t\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\|\,|x-ty|\wedge|x+ty|\,\|
is convex and even, and hence it is non-decreasing. Thus if and
\varepsilon>0
is given, we have for some
x,y\in B_{X}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
that

\displaystyle C(X)-\varepsilon

\displaystyle<\|\,|x-y|\wedge|x+y|\,\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\,\left|x-\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right|\wedge\left|x+\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right|\,\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\,\left|\frac{x}{\|x\|}-\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right|\wedge\left|\frac{x}{\|x\|}+\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right|\,\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(X).

Therefore, by taking
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
we obtain that
C(X)\leq\alpha(X)
.
∎

From the previous proposition, we obtain:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. Then

\displaystyle\lambda(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\alpha(X)\leq J(X).

Consequently, if
\lambda(X)=\sqrt{2}
, then
\lambda^{+}(X)=\alpha(X)=\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

We will check the middle inequality of the centered formula, since other paers are similar. If
x,y\in X^{+}
satisfy
\|x\|=\|y\|=1
and
x\wedge y={\bf 0}
, since
x+y=x\vee y+x\wedge y=x\vee y
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\|x+y\|=\|x\vee y\|\leq\alpha(X).

For the “consequently” statement, recall that
J(X)\lambda(X)=2

[
16
, Theorem 2]
.
∎

Now in connection with the lattice Schäffer constant, we introduce another parameter for the Banach lattice
X
with
\dim X\geq 2
, namely

\displaystyle\beta(X)=\inf\{\|x\vee y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}^{+},\,x\wedge y={\bf 0}\}=\inf\{\|x+y\|\,\colon\,x,y\in S_{X}^{+},\,x\wedge y={\bf 0}\}.

Clearly,
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\beta(X)\leq\alpha(X)
for every Banach lattice
X
. By Corollary
3.3
,
\lambda^{+}(X)=\beta(X)=\alpha(X)
when
\lambda(X)=\sqrt{2}
. The equality
\lambda^{+}(X)=\beta(X)
also holds in the two-dimensional case:

Proposition 3.4
.

If
X
is a two-dimensional Banach lattice, then
\beta(X)=\lambda^{+}(X)
.

Proof.

We can and do identify
X
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see
[
26
, Section II.3]
), and assume the normalization
\|(1,0)\|=\|(0,1)\|=1
. Clearly
\beta(X)=\|(1,1)\|
. We shall show that
\lambda^{+}(X)\geq\|(1,1)\|
; as we already know that
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\beta(X)
, our goal will then be reached.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\beta(X)>\lambda^{+}(X)
. By replacing
B_{X}
with
B_{X}+\varepsilon B_{\ell_{2}^{2}}
, and scaling to preserve normalization, we can assume that
X
is smooth, that is, for every
x\in X\backslash\{0\}
there exists a unique
f\in S_{X^{*}}
with
f(x)=\|x\|
; write
f_{x}=f
. It is easy to observe that, if
x
is positive, then so is
f_{x}
. By e.g.
[
7
, Chapter 2]
, for any
y\in X
,
\|x+ty\|=\|x\|+tf_{x}(y)+o(t)
as
t\to 0
.

By compactness, we can find
x,y\in S_{X}^{+}
so that
\|x+y\|=\lambda^{+}(X)
. As
x
and
y
are not disjoint, at least one of them, say
x
, has full support.

Notice that
f_{x}=f_{z}
, where
z=x+y
. Indeed, otherwise, pick
u
with
f_{x}(u)=0
and
f_{z}(u)<0
. For
t
small,
x+tu
is still positive, and
\|x+tu\|=1+o(t)
. Take
x^{\prime}_{t}=(x+tu)/\|x+tu\|
, then
x^{\prime}_{t}=1+tu+o(t)
, and
\|x^{\prime}_{t}+y\|=1+tf_{z}(u)+o(t)
, which is less than
\|x+y\|=\lambda^{+}(X)
for
t
small, which cannot happen.

Thus,
f_{x}=f_{z}
. If
y
has full support, we similarly conclude that
f_{y}=f_{z}
, hence
\lambda^{+}(X)=f_{z}(x+y)=\|x\|+\|y\|=2
. By Theorem
5.1
below,
X=\ell_{1}^{2}
, and
\lambda^{+}(X)=2=\|(1,1)\|=\beta(X)
.

Now suppose
y
does not have full support, say
x=(a,b)
and
y=(1,0)
, with
a,b>0
. As noted above, for
x=(a,b)
and
z=(a+1,b)
we have
f_{x}=f_{z}=f=(u,v)
. We shall show that
\|(1,1)\|\leq\|(a+1,b)\|
, which then implies
\beta(X)=\|(1,1)\|\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
.

Consider first the case of
a\leq b
. Observe that
f
norms both
(a,b)
and
(a+1,b)
, hence also
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=(a+1,b)/\|(a+1,b)\|
. For
t\in[0,1]
,

1\geq\|(1-t)(a,b)+t(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\|\geq f\big((1-t)(a,b)+t(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=1,

hence all the “
\geq
” signs above are in fact equalities. As
a^{\prime}\geq b^{\prime}
, one find
t
so that
(1-t)(a,b)+t(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
is a multiple of
(1,1)
, hence
f
also norms
(1,1)
. That is,
\|(1,1)\|=f((1,1))=u+v
. Clearly
v\leq 1
, and
1=\|x\|=f(x)=au+bv
, hence

\|(1,1)\|=f\big((1,1)\big)=u+v\leq 1+u=(au+bv)+u=f\big((a+1,b)\big)=\|(a+1,b)\|.

Now suppose
a>b
. Let
w=(1-b)/a
(note that
a\vee b\leq 1\leq a+b
, hence
0\leq w\leq 1
), and consider
g=(u,1)\in X^{*}
. Then
g((a,b))=1=g((0,1))
.

Note that
(0,1),(a,b)\in S_{X}
. Denote by
L
the straight line passing through these two points, then
g|_{L}=1
. Further, denote by
S
the segment connecting
(0,1)
with
(a,b)
. By convexity,
S\subset B_{X}
, while
L\backslash S
does not meet the interior of
B_{X}
.

As
a<b
,
S
contains
(1,1)/t
, for some
t>1
. Then
\|(1,1)\|\leq t
. On the other hand,
1=g((1,1)/t)
, so
t=g((1,1))=w+1
, hence
\|(1,1)\|\leq w+1
.

Now find
s>1
so that
(a+1,b)/s\in L\backslash S
. Then
\|(a+1,b)\|\geq s
. However,

1=g((a+1,b))/s
, and so,
s=g((a+1,b))=(a+1)w+b=(aw+b)+w=1+w
. Thus,
\|(a+1,b)\|\geq 1+w\geq\|(1,1)\|
.
∎

Example
3.5
and Remark
3.6
show that Proposition
3.4
fails in dimensions higher than
2
.

Example 3.5
.

There exists a
3
-dimensional Banach lattice
X
for which
\lambda^{+}(X)<\beta(X)
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{3}
be endowed with the lattice norm

\|(x,y,z)\|=\Big(|x|+\frac{|z|}{2}\Big)\bigvee\Big(|y|+\frac{|z|}{2}\Big)\bigvee\Big(\frac{2}{3}|x|+\frac{2}{3}|y|+\frac{|z|}{3}\Big)\bigvee\frac{5}{6}\big(|x|+|y|\big),\quad(x,y,z)\in X.

We show that

\beta(X)=\frac{15}{11}>\frac{4}{3}\geq\lambda^{+}(X).

First we estimate
\lambda^{+}(X)
. Take norm one vectors

u=\Big(\frac{4}{5},0,\frac{2}{5}\Big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad v=\Big(0,\frac{4}{5},\frac{2}{5}\Big).

Then
\|u+v\|=4/3\geq\lambda^{+}(X)
. Next we compute
\beta(X)
, by finding an upper estimate for
\|u+v\|
when
u,v\in S_{X}^{+}
and
u\wedge v={\bf 0}
. By symmetry, it suffices to consider two cases: (i)
u=(a,b,0)
,
v=(0,0,2)
, and (ii)
u=(a,0,c)
,
v=(0,1,0)
.

Case (i): We have
a+b=6/5
. Hence,

\|u+v\|=\|(a,b,2)\|\geq\frac{2}{3}(a+b)+\frac{2}{3}=\frac{22}{15}>\frac{15}{11}>\frac{4}{3}.

Case (ii): Observe that
a+c/2=1
. Therefore,

\|u+v\|=\|(a,1,c)\|\geq\Big(1+\frac{c}{2}\Big)\bigvee\frac{5}{6}(a+1).

The minimum of the right hand side under the constraint
a+c/2=1
(that is,
c/2=1-a
) occurs when

\frac{5}{6}(a+1)=1+\frac{c}{2}=2-a.

Then
a=7/11
, and we obtain
\|u+v\|=15/11
.

Remark 3.6
.

Suppose
X
is as in Example
3.5
. Let
W=L_{1}(E,\Sigma,\mu)
for some
\sigma
-finite measure space
(E,\Sigma,\mu)
, and let
Z=X\oplus_{1}W
. Then
\lambda^{+}(Z)<\beta(Z)
.

To establish this, it suffices to show that
\lambda^{+}(Z)=\lambda^{+}(X)
and
\beta(Z)=\beta(X)
.
To this end, pick
x,y\in S_{Z}^{+}
. Write
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
, with
x_{1}\in X,x_{2}\in W
. Similarly, we have
y=(y_{1},y_{2})
. Note that
\|x\vee y\|=\|x_{1}\vee y_{1}\|+\|x_{2}\|+\|y_{2}\|
.

Consider
x^{\prime}=x_{1}/\|x_{1}\|,y^{\prime}=y_{1}/\|y_{1}\|\in S_{X}^{+}
. Then

\|x^{\prime}\vee y^{\prime}\|\leq\|x_{1}\vee y_{1}\|+\|x^{\prime}-x_{1}\|+\|y^{\prime}-y_{1}\|=\|x_{1}\vee y_{1}\|+\|x_{2}\|+\|y_{2}\|\leq\|x\vee y\|.

If, in addition,
x
and
y
are disjoint, then so are
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
. Thus, in the definition of
\lambda^{+}(Z)
and
\beta(Z)
, we can restrict ourselves to taking the infimum over appropriate
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in X
.

Remark 3.7
.

Clearly
1\leq\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\beta(X)\leq 2
for any Banach lattice
X
, and Example
3.5
shows that the middle inequality can be strict. We do not know the maximal values of
\beta(X)/\lambda^{+}(X)
or
\beta(X)-\lambda^{+}(X)
.

Now we show examples of
\lambda^{+}(X)
for some particular Banach lattices
X
.

Example 3.8
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
(E,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space.
If
X=L_{p}(E,\Sigma,\mu)
, we have
\lambda^{+}(X)=\beta(X)=2^{1/p}
. Indeed, if
f,g\in S_{X}^{+}
, from
the elementary inequality
(u+v)^{p}\geq u^{p}+v^{p}
for all
u,v\geq 0
we obtain

\displaystyle\|f+g\|^{p}=\int_{X}[f(t)+g(t)]^{p}\,d\mu\geq\int_{X}f(t)^{p}\,d\mu+\int_{X}g(t)^{p}\,d\mu=2.

Thus,
\lambda^{+}(X)\geq 2^{1/p}
. Now, if
f\wedge g={\bf 0}
, then
\|f+g\|^{p}=\|f\|^{p}+\|g\|^{p}=2
.
Whence,
\beta(X)\leq 2^{1/p}
. To summarize,
2^{1/p}\leq\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\beta(X)\leq 2^{1/p}
, yielding the desired result.

Remark 3.9
.

Notice that if
1\leq p<2
and
X=(\ell_{p},\|\cdot\|_{p})
, then
\lambda(X)=2^{1-1/p}<2^{1/p}=\lambda^{+}(X)
.

Example 3.10
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
be endowed with the lattice norm

\displaystyle\|(x,y)\|=\max\{\|(x,y)\|_{\infty},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|(x,y)\|_{1}\},\quad(x,y)\in X.

Since
\lambda(X)=\sqrt{2}
, from Corollary
3.3
we obtain
\lambda^{+}(X)=\sqrt{2}
.

Example 3.11
.

Let
1\leq a<\sqrt{2}
and let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the lattice norm

\displaystyle\|x\|_{a}=\max\{\|x\|_{2},a\|x\|_{\infty}\},\quad x\in X.

From
[
16
, Theorem 2 and Example 5]
we obtain
\lambda^{+}(X)\geq\lambda(X)=\sqrt{2}/a
.
Moreover, if
x=(1/a,0)
and
y=(0,1/a)
, we have
x,y\in S_{X}^{+}
,
x\wedge y={\bf 0}
and
\|x+y\|=\sqrt{2}/a
. Therefore

\beta(X)\leq\sqrt{2}/a
. Thus,
\lambda^{+}(X)=\beta(X)=\sqrt{2}/a
.

4.
Stability results

In this section we show that
\beta
and
\lambda^{+}
are stable under small perturbations, as well as under passage to the second dual or to the ultrapower of a lattice.

To state our first result, recall that a Banach lattice
X
has the Principal Projection Property (PPP for short) if for every
x\in X
, the principal band generated by
x
is a projection band (see
[
2
, p. 38]
for facts concerning such lattices).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. If
X
has the PPP, then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lambda^{+}(Y)\,\colon\,\mbox{$Y$ is a finite dimensional sublattice of $X$}\},\quad\mbox{and}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\beta(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\beta(Y)\,\colon\,\mbox{$Y$ is a finite dimensional sublattice of $X$}\}.

Proof.

We only show (
4.1
), as (
4.2
) is handled similarly.

Clearly,
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\inf\{\lambda^{+}(Y)\,\colon\,\mbox{$Y$ is finite dimensional sublattice of $X$}\}.
To prove the converse, fix
A>\lambda^{+}(X)
. Let
x,y\in S_{X}^{+}
be such that
\|x+y\|<A
.
By
[
2
, Theorem 2.8 and the discussion preceding it]
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find disjoint components of
z=x+y
, say
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}
, so that there exist
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in S_{X}^{+}\cap Y
, where
Y=\mathrm{span}\,[z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}]
, with
\|x-x^{\prime}\|,\|y-y^{\prime}\|<\varepsilon
.
Then
\lambda^{+}(Y)\leq\|x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}\|<\|z\|+2\varepsilon<A+2\varepsilon
. As
A>\lambda^{+}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
are arbitrary, the conclusion
\inf\{\lambda^{+}(Y)\,\colon\,\mbox{$Y$ is finite dimensional sublattice of $X$}\}\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
follows.
∎

The following Lemma improves the inequality proved in
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach lattices and
T\colon X\to Y
be a Banach lattice isomorphism. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|}\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(Y)\leq\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\lambda^{+}(X)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|}\beta(X)\leq\beta(Y)\leq\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\beta(X).

Proof.

Again, we only deal with the inequality for
\lambda^{+}
, as the case of
\beta
requires only minor adjustments.

Let
x,y\in S_{X}^{+}
be given. Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\|T\|}{\|Tx\|}x+\frac{\|T\|}{\|Tx\|}y\leq\max\left\{\frac{\|T\|}{\|Tx\|},\frac{\|T\|}{\|Ty\|}\right\}(x+y)\leq\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|(x+y).

Thus,

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(Y)\leq\left\|\frac{Tx}{\|Tx\|}+\frac{Ty}{\|Ty\|}\right\|\leq\left\|\frac{\|T\|}{\|Tx\|}x+\frac{\|T\|}{\|Tx\|}y\right\|\leq\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\|x+y\|.

So,
\lambda^{+}(Y)\leq\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\lambda^{+}(X).

∎

To go on, we say that
Y
contains a lattice copy of
X
if
X
is Banach-lattice isomorphic to a sublattice of
Y
;
we also say that
Y
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
X
if for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
a sublattice
Z_{\varepsilon}
of
Y
and a Banach-lattice isomorphism
T_{\varepsilon}\colon X\to Z_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|T_{\varepsilon}\|\|T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon.
The next corollary is an inmediate consequence of Proposition
4.2
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach lattices. If
Y
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
X
, then
\lambda^{+}(Y)\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
and
\beta(Y)\leq\beta(X)
.

If
X
and
Y
are Banach lattices, recall that
X
is said to be
lattice finitely representable
in
Y
is for every finite
dimensional sublattice
E
of
X
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a sublattice
F
of
Y
and a Banach lattice isomorphism
T\colon E\to F

such that
\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
. The following fact, established in
[
3
]
, will be used in the proof of the upcoming theorem.

Fact 4.4
.

For every Banach lattice
X
, the bidual
X^{**}
is lattice finitely representable in
X
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-trivial ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
and
X
be a Banach lattice. Then

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)=\lambda^{+}(X^{**})=\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta(X)=\beta(X^{**})=\beta(X_{\mathcal{U}}).

Proof.

Here as well, we only prove the statements involving
\lambda^{+}
. Begin from the first equality. Since
J_{X}\colon X\to X^{**}
is an into Banach lattice isometry, we have
\lambda^{+}(X^{**})\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
. On the other hand,
by
[
2
, Theorem 1.47]
, any
\sigma
-order complete Banach lattice has the PPP, hence any dual Banach lattice must have this property. Consequently, by Proposition
4.1
we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X^{**})=\inf\{\lambda^{+}(Y)\,\colon\,\mbox{$Y$ is finite dimensional sublattice of $X^{**}$}\}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be given and
Y
be a finite dimensional sublattice of
X^{**}
such that
\lambda^{+}(Y)<\lambda^{+}(X^{**})+\varepsilon
. By Fact
4.4
there is
a sublattice
Y_{0}
of
X
and a Banach lattice isomorphism
T\colon Y\to Y_{0}
such that
\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
. From Proposition

4.2
it follows that

\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(Y_{0})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\lambda^{+}(Y)<(1+\varepsilon)(\lambda^{+}(X^{**})+\varepsilon).

By the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon>0
we conclude that
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(X^{**}).

Now we prove that
\lambda^{+}(X)=\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}}).
From Fact
2.1
and Corollary
4.3
we have

\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}})\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
. Now let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Thus there are two elements
(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}},(y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\in X_{\mathcal{U}}

with
\|(x_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\|=\|(y_{n})_{\mathcal{U}}\|=1
such that
\|(|x_{n}|)_{\mathcal{U}}+(|y_{n}|)_{\mathcal{U}}\|=\mathcal{U}-\lim\|\,|x_{n}|+|y_{n}|\,\|<\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}})+\varepsilon
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{U}
be such that
\|\,|x_{n}|+|y_{n}|\,\|<\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}})+\varepsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Also let
B\in\mathcal{U}
be such that

1-\varepsilon<\min\{\|x_{n}\|,\|y_{n}\|\}
for each
n\in B
. If
m\in A\cap B
, then

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\left\|\frac{|x_{m}|}{\|x_{m}\|}+\frac{|y_{m}|}{\|y_{m}\|}\right\|\leq\left\|\frac{|x_{m}|}{1-\varepsilon}+\frac{|y_{m}|}{1-\varepsilon}\right\|<\frac{\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}})+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we obtain
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(X_{\mathcal{U}}).

∎

Remark 4.6
.

The proof of Theorem
4.5
shows that (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) hold for any Banach lattice
X
(no need to assume the PPP).

5.
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\lambda^{+}
describe “global” properties of Banach lattices

Even though the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\lambda^{+}
are calculated “locally”, they can be used to describe “global” properties of Banach lattices.
To present our first result in this direction, recall that a Banach lattice
X
is an abstract
L_{p}
-space,
1\leq p<\infty
, if
\|x+y\|=\|x\|+\|y\|
for each
x,y\in X
disjoint
(see
[
19
, Definition 1, p. 131]
). If
p=1
,
X
is said to be an abstract
L
-space.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\lambda^{+}(X)=2
.

(ii)

\beta(X)=2
.

(iii)

X
is an abstract
L
-space.

Proof.

Since
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\beta(X)\leq 2
, it follows that (i) implies (ii). Suppose that (ii) holds.

Claim 5.2
.

If
x,y\in X
and
\|x+y\|=\|x\|+\|y\|
, then
\|\alpha x+\beta y\|=\alpha\|x\|+\beta\|y\|
for
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
.

We may assume that
\beta\geq\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\|\alpha x+\beta y\|=\|(\alpha-\beta)x+\beta(x+y)\|\geq\beta(\|x\|+\|y\|)+(\alpha-\beta)\|x\|=\alpha\|x\|+\beta\|y\|,

and the claim is proved.

Let
x,y\in X^{+}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
be such that
x\wedge y={\bf 0}
. The definition of
\beta(X)
gives that
\displaystyle\left\|\frac{x}{\|x\|}+\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right\|=2
. By Claim
5.2
we have
\left\|a\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}+b\dfrac{y}{\|y\|}\right\|=a+b
for all
a,b\geq 0
. In particular, if
a=\|x\|
and
b=\|y\|
, we obtain
\|x+y\|=\|x\|+\|y\|
as required. Therefore, we prove (iii).

Finally, if (iii) is valid, then by
[
19
, Theorem 1, p. 133]
we have
\|x+y\|=\|x\|+\|y\|
for all
x,y\in X^{+}
. Thus,
\lambda^{+}(X)=2
.
∎

Example
3.8
implies that
\lambda^{+}(X)=\beta(X)=2^{1/p}
when
X
is an abstract
L_{p}
-space. The converse is false, per Example
3.10
. So, Theorem
5.1
cannot be modified to characterize abstract
L_{p}
-spaces.
However, a weaker result (showing that the parameters
\lambda^{+}
and
\beta
can describe global properties of Banach lattices) still holds.
To state the upcoming result, we recall (see e.g.
[
21
, Section 1.f]
) that a Banach lattice
X
satisfies a lower (resp. upper)
q
-estimate with constant
M
(
1\leq q\leq\infty
) if the inequality

\displaystyle M\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\|\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}\|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\quad(\mbox{respectively,}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\|\leq M\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}\|^{q}\right)^{1/q}),

holds for every family of pairwise disjoint elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
. For a Banach lattice
X
, we denote by
s(X)
the infimum of all
q\in[1,\infty]
for which
X
has lower
q
-estimate (with some constant);
S(X)
stands for the supremum of all
q
for which an upper
q
-estimate holds.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
X
is an infinite dimensional Banach lattice, then

(5.1)

2^{1/s(X)}=\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)=\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z).

Here, the supremum is taken over all sublattices
Z\subset Y
with
\dim Z\geq 2
, and the infimum – over all
n
-dimensional sublattices
Y\subset X
. Similarly,

(5.2)

2^{1/S(X)}=\lim_{n}\sup_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\inf_{Z\subset Y}\alpha(Z).

Proof.

We shall establish (
5.1
), as (
5.2
) is handled similarly.

By the local reflexivity results of
[
3
]
(for the duality of upper and lower estimates, see also
[
21
, Proposition 1.f.5]
), both the left and the right hand sides remain the same if we can replace
X
by
X^{**}
. We therefore assume that
X
is order complete.
Then for every
n
-dimensional sublattice
W\subset X
there exists a sublattice
Y
of dimension
n+1
so that
W\subset Y\subset X
, hence

\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y={n+1}}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)\geq\inf_{W\subset X,\dim W=n}\sup_{Z\subset W}\lambda^{+}(Z),

and similarly with
\beta
. Thus, the sequences
\big(\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)\big)_{n}
and
\big(\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z)\big)_{n}
are increasing in
n
, hence convergent.

We use a version of Krivine’s Theorem
[
28
]
: if
W
is an infinite dimensional Banach lattice, then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exist disjoint positive norm one
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in W
so that, for any scalars
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
,

(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\big(\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|^{s(W)}\big)^{1/s(W)}\leq\big\|\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}w_{i}\big\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\big(\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|^{s(W)}\big)^{1/s(W)}.

For
W
as above, Example
3.8
gives
\lambda^{+}(W)\leq\beta(W)\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{2}2^{1/s(W)}
. In (
5.1
), we can take
Y=Z
to be the
k
-dimensional
W
as above. Thus,

2^{1/s(X)}\geq\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)\quad\mbox{and}\quad 2^{1/s(X)}\geq\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z).

To prove the converse, we use an “ultraproduct trick” (see e.g.
[
25
, Theorem 3.6]
): for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N=N(k,\varepsilon)
so that, for any
n\geq N
, and any norm one disjoint
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
there exist disjoint norm one
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
in the span of
x_{1},\ldots x_{n}
, so that, for some
p\in[1,\infty]
, the inequality

(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\big(\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}\leq\big\|\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}z_{i}\big\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\big(\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}

holds for all scalars
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
.
In particular, if
Y
is an
n
-dimensional sublattice of
X
, it contains a further sublattice
Z
spanned by
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
as above; then

\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{-2}2^{1/p}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z)\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{-2}2^{1/p}.

Fix
q>s(X)
, then
X
has lower
q
-estimate with the constant
C_{q}
. Using the definition of lower
q
-estimate on
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
, we obtain
k^{1/q}\leq C_{q}(1+\varepsilon)k^{1/p}
. Consequently,

\frac{1}{p}\geq\frac{1}{q}-\frac{\ln((1+\varepsilon)C_{q})}{\ln k},

and so,

\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z),\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z)\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{-2}2^{1/q}2^{-\ln((1+\varepsilon)C_{q})/\ln k}.

Taking
n
to be large enough, we can make
\varepsilon
and
k
to be as small as possible and as large as possible, respectively. Thus,

2^{1/q}\leq\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)\quad\mbox{and}\quad 2^{1/q}\leq\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z).

As
q>s(X)
is arbitrary, we conclude that

2^{1/s(X)}\leq\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\lambda^{+}(Z)\quad\mbox{and}\quad 2^{1/s(X)}\leq\lim_{n}\inf_{Y\subset X,\dim Y=n}\sup_{Z\subset Y}\beta(Z),

thus establishing (
5.1
).

To establish (
5.2
), a similar reasoning is involved; instead of Example
3.8
one uses the equality
\alpha(L_{p})=2^{1/p}
from
[
5
]
.
∎

Now we characterize the Banach lattices
X
with
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
.

Theorem 5.4
.

A Banach lattice
X
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}^{2}
if and only if
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
.

Proof.

If
X
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}^{2}
, by Corollary
4.3
we obtain
1\leq\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\lambda^{+}(\ell_{\infty}^{2})=1
. Conversely, assume that
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
and let
c>1
be given. Our goal is to find a lattice isomorphism
T=T_{c}:\ell_{\infty}^{2}\to X
so that
\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|<c
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
so that
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}<c
. From the definition of
\lambda^{+}(X)
, there are
x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon}\in S_{X}^{+}
such that
1\leq\|x_{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon}\|<1+\varepsilon
.
If
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle|ax_{\varepsilon}+by_{\varepsilon}|\leq|a|x_{\varepsilon}+|b|y_{\varepsilon}\leq\max\{|a|,|b|\}(x_{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon}).

So,
\|ax_{\varepsilon}+by_{\varepsilon}\|\leq\max\{|a|,|b|\}(1+\varepsilon)
. Also, as
x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon}\in S_{X}^{+}
, we have
\max\{|a|,|b|\}\leq\|ax_{\varepsilon}+by_{\varepsilon}\|
if
a
and
b
have the same sign.

Now note that
x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}=2x_{\varepsilon}-(x_{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon})
, hence

(5.3)

\|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 2\|x_{\varepsilon}\|-\|x_{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Let
z_{\varepsilon}=x_{\varepsilon}\wedge y_{\varepsilon}
,
x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=x_{\varepsilon}-z_{\varepsilon}
, and
y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=y_{\varepsilon}-z_{\varepsilon}
. Clearly
x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon},y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
are positive and disjoint.
Further,
x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}-y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}
, hence
x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|
. By (
5.3
),

\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|=\|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon
.

We clearly have
x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\leq x_{\varepsilon}
, hence
\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|\leq 1
. In fact,
\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+z_{\varepsilon}\|=\|x_{\varepsilon}\|=1
,
while

\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+2z_{\varepsilon}\|\leq\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+2z_{\varepsilon}\|=\|x_{\varepsilon}+y_{\varepsilon}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon.

By the triangle inequality,

\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 2\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+z_{\varepsilon}\|-\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+2z_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon,

and similarly,
\|y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon
. For any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\|ax^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+by^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|\leq\||a|x_{\varepsilon}+|b|y_{\varepsilon}\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\max\{|a|,|b|\},

and also,

\|ax^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+by^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|\geq\max\{|a|\|x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|,|b|\|y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\|\}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\max\{|a|,|b|\},

Thus, the operator

T:\ell_{\infty}^{2}\to X:(a,b)\mapsto ax^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+by^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}

has the desired properties.
∎

In contrast to Example
3.5
, we obtain:

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. Then
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
if and only if
\beta(X)>1
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\beta(X)
,
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
implies
\beta(X)>1
. Conversely, if
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
, then, by Theorem
5.4
,
X
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}^{2}
, hence, by Corollary
4.3
,
\beta(X)\leq\beta(\ell_{\infty}^{2})=1
.
∎

6.
Connection between the lattice Schäffer constant and other parameters

We begin by relating the lattice Schäffer constant
\lambda^{+}(X)
and the characteristic of monotonicity
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X).

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. For each
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)+2-\varepsilon.

Consequently,
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)\leq 2-\lambda^{+}(X).

Proof.

If
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
is given, by Lemma
2.4
we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(X)-1-\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)=|\sigma_{X}(1)-\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)|\leq 1-\varepsilon.

Thus,
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)+2-\varepsilon.
The second inequality follows by taking
\varepsilon=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0}(X)
in (
6.1
) and recalling that
\sigma_{X}(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X))=0
(Corollary
2.5
).
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. Then,
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0}(X)<1
if and only if
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0}(X)<1
and let
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0}(X)<a<1
. Then
\sigma_{X}(a)>0
. If
x,y\in S_{X}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\|x+y\|\geq\|x+ay\|\geq 1+\sigma_{X}(a).

Consequently,
\lambda^{+}(X)\geq 1+\sigma_{X}(a)>1
. Conversely, if
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
, then
\sigma_{X}(1)>0
. By Lemma
2.4
there is

a<1
such that
\sigma_{X}(a)>0
. Thus,
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0}(X)<a<1
.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

If
X
is a Banach lattice, then

\displaystyle\delta_{m,X}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}\right)=\frac{\lambda^{+}(X)-1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}.

Consequently,
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}
. Moreover,
\dfrac{1}{1-\delta_{m,X}(1/2)}\leq\lambda^{+}(X)
.

Proof.

Plugging
\varepsilon=1
into Fact
2.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{m,X}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}\right)=\frac{\lambda^{+}(X)-1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}.

Now, the first inequality holds when
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
. Now, if
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
, the above equation implies that
\delta_{m,X}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}\right)>0
.
Whence,
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)\leq 1/\lambda^{+}(X)
.

On the other hand, from Fact
2.2
and Lemma
2.4
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{m,X}(1/2)}{1-\delta_{m,X}(1/2)}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1^{-}}\frac{\delta_{m,X}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)}{1-\delta_{m,X}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)}\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1^{-}}\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)=\lambda^{+}(X)-1.\qed

Remark 6.4
.

In
[
17
]
and
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
it is claimed that if
X
is Banach lattice, then

(6.2)

\displaystyle\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)}{1+\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)},\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in[0,1)$.}

However, this formula is false. Indeed, if (
6.2
) were true, from
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
and Fact
2.4
we would have

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1^{-}}\delta_{m,X}(\varepsilon)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1^{-}}\frac{\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)}{1+\sigma_{X}(\varepsilon)}=\frac{\lambda^{+}(X)-1}{\lambda^{+}(X)}.

Thus,
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)=1/\lambda^{+}(X)
, which is false when
X
is uniformly monotone.

One can also reach the same conclusion by noting that
\delta_{m,L_{1}(0,1)}(\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon=\sigma_{L_{1}(0,1)}(\varepsilon)
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. Then
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
if and only if
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)<1
.

Proof.

If
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
, by Theorem
6.3
we have
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)<1
. On the other hand, if
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
, by Theorem
5.4
,
X
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}^{2}
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and find a Banach lattice isomorphism
T=T_{\varepsilon}\colon\ell_{\infty}^{2}\to X
such that
\|T\|=1
, and
\|T^{-1}\|<1+\varepsilon
. Let
x=T(1,1)
and
y=T(0,1)
, then,
{\bf 0}\leq y\leq x
,
\|x\|\leq 1
, and
\|y\|\geq 1-\varepsilon
.
Therefore,
\delta_{m,X}(1-\varepsilon)\leq 1-\|x-y\|=1-\|T(1,0)\|\leq 1-(1-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
. Passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 1
, we conclude that
\delta_{m,X}(1-)=0
, and so,
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)=1
.
∎

To state the next corollary, recall that
X
is a
KB-space
if every monotone sequence in
B_{X}
is norm convergent. It is known that
X
is a KB-space
if and only if
X
contains no sublattice isomorphic to
c_{0}

[
23
, Theorem 2.4.12]
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. If
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
, then
X
satisfies a lower
q
-estimate for some
q\in(1,\infty)
. In particular, if
\lambda^{+}(X)>1
, then
X
is a KB-space.

Proof.

If
\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)<1
, by
[
4
, Theorem 2.3(2)]

X
satisfies a lower
q
-estimate for some
q\in(1,\infty)
.
The conclusion follows from Proposition
6.5
. For second part, notice that a Banach lattice satisfying a lower
q
-estimate cannot contain a lattice copy of
c_{0}
, hence it must be a KB-space
by the aforementioned result.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

The second part of Theorem
6.6
follows also from Corollary
4.3
. Indeed, If
X
is not a KB space, then, by
[
23
, Theorem 2.4.12]
, it contains a sublattice isomorphic to
c_{0}
. By
[
6
, Theorem 2]
,
X
contains lattice-almost isometric copies of
c_{0}
.
Since
\lambda^{+}(c_{0})=1
, we obtain
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
by Corollary
4.3
.

On the other hand, being a KB-space does not imply that
\lambda^{+}>1
as the following example shows. Let
(p_{n})
be a sequence in
(1,\infty)
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}=\infty
and consider the Banach lattice
X=(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{p_{n}}^{2})_{2}
. Since
X
is reflexive,
X
is a KB-space. On the other hand,
\lambda^{+}(X)\leq 2^{1/p_{n}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence,
\lambda^{+}(X)=1
.

Now we give a condition to obtain superreflexivity of
X
in terms of the characteristic
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)
.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. If
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)+\alpha(X)<2
, then
X
is superreflexive.

Proof.

Since
1\leq\alpha(X)\leq 2
, we obtain
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)<2-\alpha(X)\leq 1
. From Proposition
6.2

it follows that
\lambda^{+}(X)>1.
By Corollary
6.6
,
X
satisfies a lower
q
-estimate for some
q\in(1,\infty)
.
On the other hand, since
\alpha(X)<2
,
X
satisfies an upper
p
-estimate for some
p\in(1,\infty)
by
[
4
, Theorem 2.3]
.
Thus,
X
is superreflexive by
[
21
, Theorem 1.f.10]
.
∎

In
[
24
, Theorem 3.2]
it is proved that if
2\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)+\alpha(X)<2
, then
X
is uniformly non-square. Notice that if
2\varepsilon_{0,m}(X)+\alpha(X)<2
, by (
2.1
) we also have
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)+\alpha(X)<2
. This leads to the natural question:

Question 6.9
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. Is
X
uniformly non-square provided that
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{0,m}(X)+\alpha(X)<2
?
