Eliminating Incoherent Noise: A Coherent Quantum Approach in Multi-Sensor Dark Matter Detection
Jing Shu, Bin Xu, Yuan Xu

Abstract
We propose a novel dark matter detection scheme by leveraging quantum coherence across a network of multiple quantum sensors. This method effectively eliminates incoherent background noise, thereby significantly enhancing detection sensitivity. This is achieved by performing a series of basis transformation operations, allowing the coherent signal to be expressed as a combination of sensor population measurements without introducing background noise. We present a comprehensive analytical analysis and complement it with practical numerical simulations. These demonstrations reveal that signal strength is enhanced by the square of the number of sensors, while noise, primarily due to operational infidelity rather than background fluctuations, increases only linearly with the number of sensors. Our approach paves the way for next-generation dark matter searches that optimally utilize an advanced network of sensors and quantum technologies.

II
Dark matter detection with a single quantum sensor

The interaction Hamiltonian describing the coupling between a sensor mode and the background bosonic DM field in the interaction picture is expressed as:

{H}_{\alpha}={\alpha\Psi\left(\hat{a}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{r}t}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{r}t}\right)}/{\sqrt{2}},

(1)

where
\displaystyle\alpha
denotes the coupling constant,
\displaystyle\hat{a}
(
\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator of the sensor mode, and
\displaystyle\omega_{r}
is the resonant frequency of mode
\displaystyle\hat{a}
. The oscillating bosonic DM field is represented by:

\Psi=\Psi_{0}\cos(m_{\Psi}t+\phi_{\Psi}),

(2)

where
\displaystyle\Psi_{0}
is the field amplitude,
\displaystyle m_{\Psi}
is the mass of the boson corresponding to its Compton frequency, and
\displaystyle\phi_{\Psi}
is a random phase. Further details on the DM field model are provided in the Supplemental Material (SM).

For weak signals on resonance (
\displaystyle\omega_{r}=m_{\Psi}
), the quantum sensor, initialized in its ground state
\displaystyle|g\rangle
, evolves according to the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) into the state

|\beta\rangle\equiv U_{DM}(\beta)|g\rangle\simeq\left(1-\frac{|\beta|^{2}}{2}\right)|g\rangle+\beta|e\rangle,

(3)

which is a superposition of the ground state
\displaystyle|g\rangle
and the excited state
\displaystyle|e\rangle
. Here,
\displaystyle U_{DM}(\beta)=e^{-iH_{\alpha}\tau}=e^{\beta\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}\hat{a}}
, and
\displaystyle\beta=-\frac{i}{2\sqrt{2}}\alpha\Psi_{0}e^{i\phi_{\Psi}}\tau
represents the displacement amplitude with
\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}\ll 1
, and
\displaystyle\tau<\min\{T_{\Psi},T_{s}\}
is the signal integration time, which is shorter than both the coherence time of the DM field
\displaystyle T_{\Psi}
and that of the sensor
\displaystyle T_{s}
. Terms of higher order than
\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}
in the expansion have been neglected.

The DM signal in an ideal quantum sensor is quantified by the excitation probability, defined by the expectation value of the observable
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{P}\equiv|e\rangle\langle e|
:

P_{e}=\langle\beta|\mathcal{O}_{P}|\beta\rangle\simeq|\beta|^{2}.

(4)

However, in reality, detection is impeded by various types of noise. Major noise sources during the detection process include decoherence due to thermal relaxation/excitation and dephasing, along with SPAM errors. As demonstrated in Refs.
[
38
,
39
]
, SPAM errors can be significantly reduced to negligible levels by employing a repeated QND measurement and a voting protocol. We now focus on the challenges posed by thermal decoherence and dephasing errors.

In order to properly represent the ensemble average over the noise, it is recommended to utilize the quantum density matrix of the qubit

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\rho=|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|\simeq&\begin{pmatrix}1-|\beta|^{2}&\beta^{*}\\
\beta&|\beta|^{2}\end{pmatrix}&\begin{matrix}\end{matrix}\end{array},

(5)

where
\displaystyle\rho_{ij}=\langle i|\rho|j\rangle
is the density matrix element.

The effect of the decoherence errors can be expressed as

\rho\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{T_{1}}\rho_{gg}+p_{\rm reset}p_{0}&p_{T_{2}}\rho_{ge}\\
p_{T_{2}}\rho_{eg}&p_{T_{1}}\rho_{ee}+p_{\rm reset}p_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(6)

where
\displaystyle p_{T_{1}}=e^{-\tau/T_{1}}
and
\displaystyle p_{T_{2}}=e^{-\tau/T_{2}}
are the probability for a qubit to relax and dephase during an integration time
\displaystyle\tau
,

\displaystyle T_{1}
and
\displaystyle T_{2}
are the thermal relaxation and dephasing times,

\displaystyle p_{0}
and
\displaystyle p_{1}=1-p_{0}
are the populations of the ground and excited state at equilibrium, and
\displaystyle p_{\rm reset}=1-p_{T_{1}}
is the probability for a qubit to reset to an equilibrium state.

From Eq. (
4
), the excitation probability in the presence of noise is given by:

\text{Tr}[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\cdot\mathcal{O}_{P}]=p_{T_{1}}|\beta|^{2}+p_{\text{reset}}p_{1},

(7)

where the signal is suppressed by a factor of
\displaystyle p_{T_{1}}
, and a noise level of
\displaystyle p_{\rm reset}p_{1}
is added. The SNR is given by

SNR=\frac{S}{B}=\frac{p_{T_{1}}|\beta|^{2}}{p_{\rm reset}p_{1}},

(8)

where
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle B
represents the signal strength and background noise, respectively. The signal significance denoted by the signal over the fluctuation of the background is given by

\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}}=\frac{p_{T_{1}}|\beta|^{2}}{\sqrt{p_{\rm reset}p_{1}}}.

(9)

III
Coherence-based detection with multiple quantum sensors

We intend to use multiple quantum sensors operating simultaneously to improve detection sensitivity. As long as the sensors are situated within the DM coherence length, they will experience coherent excitations. This contrasts with noisy excitations, which are inherently incoherent. For simplicity, let us consider two identical sensors,
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
, that operate independently without interactions between them and carry the same signal strength. However, the noise affecting each sensor may differ.

The density matrix of the entire system can be expressed as a tensor product:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}=\mathcal{E}_{A}(\rho^{(1)})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho^{(1)}),

(10)

where
\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}=|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|
is the density matrix of a single sensor, either
\displaystyle A
or
\displaystyle B
, and decoherence errors affect each independently. The observable for measuring the total excitation in each sensor is represented by the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{P}\equiv|e_{A}g_{B}\rangle\langle e_{A}g_{B}|+|g_{A}e_{B}\rangle\langle g_{A}e_{B}|
, where the subscript
\displaystyle P
indicates that the operator measures population of the excited states. The expectation value of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{P}
is given by:

Tr[\rho^{(2)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{P}]\approx(p_{T_{1}}^{A}+p_{T_{1}}^{B})|\beta|^{2}+p_{reset}^{A}p_{1}^{A}+p_{reset}^{B}p_{1}^{B},

(11)

assuming
\displaystyle p_{1}^{A/B}\ll 1
.
Both the signal and noise rates double as anticipated. For
\displaystyle N
identical sensors operating independently, both the signal and noise rates scale linearly with
\displaystyle N
. The SNR and the signal significance can be derived from Eq. (
8
) and (
9
)

SNR\simeq\frac{p_{T_{1}}|\beta|^{2}}{(1-p_{T_{1}})\bar{n}_{r0}},\quad\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}}\simeq\frac{N^{\frac{1}{2}}p_{T_{1}}|\beta|^{2}}{\sqrt{(1-p_{T_{1}})\bar{n}_{r0}}}.

(12)

III.1
Coherence-based detection

We now turn to the off-diagonal element of the density matrix, namely the coherence, and devise an observable
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C}=|g_{A}e_{B}\rangle\langle e_{A}g_{B}|+|e_{A}g_{B}\rangle\langle g_{A}e_{B}|
, where the subscript
\displaystyle C
indicates the coherence-channel, whose expectation value is given by

Tr[\rho^{(2)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C}]=2p_{T_{2}}^{A}p_{T_{2}}^{B}|\beta|^{2},

(13)

which possesses comparable signal strength, but is completely noise free.

By implementing a unitary transformation
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C}
to diagonalize the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C}
, quantum coherence can be converted into population measurements that can be detected by quantum sensors. The unitary matrix
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C}
is not unique. As an example,

\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
0&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(14)

where the matrix act on wave vectors with basis
\displaystyle\{|gg\rangle,|ge\rangle,|eg\rangle,|ee\rangle\}
, from top to bottom,
so that

\mathcal{O}^{\prime(2)}_{P}=\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C}\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C}\mathcal{U}^{(2)\dagger}_{C}=|g_{A}e_{B}\rangle\langle g_{A}e_{B}|-|e_{A}g_{B}\rangle\langle e_{A}g_{B}|.

(15)

We now have

Tr[\rho^{(2)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C}]=Tr[\rho^{\prime(2)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{\prime(2)}_{P}]=\bar{n}^{\prime}_{g_{A}e_{B}}-\bar{n}^{\prime}_{e_{A}g_{B}},

(16)

where
\displaystyle\rho^{\prime(2)}=\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C}\rho^{(2)}_{C}\mathcal{U}^{(2)\dagger}_{C}
is the transformed density matrix,
\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\prime}_{i_{A}j_{B}}=\langle i_{A}j_{B}|\rho^{\prime(2)}|i_{A}j_{B}\rangle
represents the population of state
\displaystyle|i_{A}j_{B}\rangle
with
\displaystyle i,j
equals
\displaystyle e
or
\displaystyle g
. For the signal part,
\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\prime}_{g_{A}e_{B}}=[(p_{T_{1}}^{A}+p_{T_{1}}^{B})/2+p_{T_{2}}^{A}p_{T_{2}}^{B}]|\beta|^{2}
,
\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\prime}_{e_{A}g_{B}}=[(p_{T_{1}}^{A}+p_{T_{1}}^{B})/2-p_{T_{2}}^{A}p_{T_{2}}^{B}]|\beta|^{2}
. For the noise part,
\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\prime}_{g_{A}e_{B}}=\bar{n}^{\prime}_{e_{A}g_{B}}=(p_{reset}^{A}p_{1}^{A}+p_{reset}^{B}p_{1}^{B})/2
. Thus, for two detectors, after the DM signal undergoes a unitary transformation, we expect the signal to be concentrated in one detector, while the noise is evenly distributed between the two detectors. By subtracting the signals in the two detectors, we obtain the entire coherence signal and ideally zero noise.

For
\displaystyle N
multiple quantum sensors, there are generally
\displaystyle{N(N-1)}/{2}
coherence channels. Nevertheless, here we utilize only a subset of these coherence channels to define an observable. We divide the whole system into two parts, each containing
\displaystyle N/2
sensors (assuming
\displaystyle N
is even), and only consider the coherence between these two portions. As we will see in Section
III.2
, this definition results in reduced overall noise. The whole observable we are concerned is defined as the sum over the
\displaystyle{N^{2}}/{4}
coherence channels between sensors
\displaystyle I(N\geq I>N/2)
and
\displaystyle J(N/2\geq J\geq 1)

\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{C}=\sum_{I>N/2\geq J}\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C_{IJ}}=\sum_{I>N/2\geq J}|e_{I}^{(N)}\rangle\langle e_{J}^{(N)}|+h.c.,

(17)

where
\displaystyle|e_{I}^{(N)}\rangle\equiv|g_{1}\dots e_{I}\dots g_{N}\rangle
describes the system where the
\displaystyle I
-th sensor is in the excited state, while all other sensors remain in their ground states.
The signal is indicated by the expectation value

Tr[\rho^{(N)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{C}]=\sum_{I>N/2\geq J}2p_{T_{2}}^{I}p_{T_{2}}^{J}|\beta|^{2},

(18)

which is noise-free and enhanced by
\displaystyle N^{2}/2
, where
\displaystyle\rho^{(N)}=\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho^{(1)})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{(1)})\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{E}_{N}(\rho^{(1)})
is the density matrix of
\displaystyle N
sensors.

Quantum coherence can also be extracted in a similar way by executing a unitary transformation
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}
to diagonalize
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{C}
, measuring the population and post-possessing the data. In general,
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}
for
\displaystyle N=2^{s}
can be decomposed by a series of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{IJ}}
’s in a divide-and-conquer manner which is illustrated in Fig
2

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{N/2,N}}(\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{N/4,N/2}}\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{3N/4,N}})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\cdots(\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{12}}\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{34}}\cdots\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{N-1,N}}),

(19)

which only needs
\displaystyle s=\log_{2}N
steps, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{IJ}}
is the unitary transformation of Eq. (
14
) acting on the Hilbert space
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{J}
of sensors
\displaystyle I
and
\displaystyle J
.
The transformed observable is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{\prime(N)}_{P}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{C}\mathcal{U}^{(N)\dagger}_{C}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{N}{2}\left(|e_{N}^{(N)}\rangle\langle e_{N}^{(N)}|-|e_{N/2}^{(N)}\rangle\langle e_{N/2}^{(N)}|\right).

(20)

Despite the perfect binary tree structure of the coherence signal transfer route as illustrated in Fig.

,
\displaystyle N
does not necessarily be an order of 2. For a general
\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}

\displaystyle(N-1)

\displaystyle\lceil\log_{2}N\rceil
steps, with the structure forming a complete binary tree. The signals obtained in the final sensor are noise-free and enhanced by a factor of
\displaystyle N^{2}/2

III.2
Implementation and infidelities

Due to hardware infidelities and environmental interactions, the application of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}
often introduces additional errors.
Here, we use the depolarizing Pauli error model to mimic the hardware infidelity, which involves Pauli operations that can lead to errors with a certain probability.
However, when depolarizing errors act uniformly, as with the symmetric depolarizing channel, hardware infidelities do not contribute to background noise. Additional noise is introduced only when the depolarizing error acts unevenly on subsystem
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
.
The expectation value in Eq. (
16
) is then altered to be

\displaystyle\displaystyle Tr

\displaystyle\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{AB}(\rho^{\prime(2)})\cdot\mathcal{O}^{\prime(2)}_{P}]=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}{2}(1-p_{reset}^{A}p_{1}^{A}-p_{reset}^{B}p_{1}^{B})+\cdots,

(21)

where
\displaystyle\cdots
represents the signal approximately equal to
\displaystyle 2p_{T_{2}}^{A}p_{T_{2}}^{B}|\beta|^{2}
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{AB}(\rho)=\mathcal{D}_{A}(\mathcal{D}_{B}(\rho))
denotes the combination of the depolarizing error on sensors
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
with different strength
\displaystyle\lambda_{A}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{B}
. The infidelity of the depolarizing channel can be expressed as
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}_{1}}\sim\lambda
.

On the other hand, the interaction between the sensors and the environment can be modeled by the thermal relaxation and dephasing errors described in Eq. (
6
) with an interaction time of
\displaystyle\tau_{g}\ll T_{1}
. The corresponding expectation value can be calculated as

\displaystyle\displaystyle Tr[\mathcal{E}_{AB}(\rho^{\prime(2)})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\cdot\mathcal{O}^{\prime(2)}_{P}]=\frac{\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{B}}{2}(p_{1}^{B}-p_{1}^{A})

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon_{B}-\epsilon_{A}}{2}(p_{T_{1}}^{A}p_{1}^{A}+p_{T_{1}}^{B}p_{1}^{B})+\cdots,

(22)

where the infidelity
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}_{2}}=\epsilon_{A/B}=1-e^{-\tau_{g}/T_{1}^{A/B}}\simeq{\tau_{g}}/{T_{1}^{A/B}}
, and
\displaystyle p_{1}^{A/B}\sim\bar{n}_{r}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{AB}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{A}(\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho))
is the combination of the error channels
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{A}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B}
.
Therefore, the background noise added by the thermal decoherence channel is of the order of
\displaystyle O(\bar{n}_{r}\tau_{g}/T_{1})
.

Now, we can estimate the potential sensitivity when considering hardware infidelities in various situations. In coherence-based detection, the signal scales as
\displaystyle N^{2}p_{T_{2}}^{2}|\beta|^{2}/2
,
while background noise is proportional to
\displaystyle N\bar{\mathcal{F}}/2
, which is added only in the last implementation of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(2)}_{C_{IJ}}
. This is because the errors added in the previous steps can all be considered as incoherent noises between the two portions that we divide the system into, and are canceled in the last step following the same story as the two-sensor case.
If there are significant uneven depolarizing errors, they will dominate the background noise. we thus have a conservative estimation of the SNR and the signal significance for the coherence-based detection.

SNR\simeq N\frac{p_{T_{2}}^{2}|\beta|^{2}}{\bar{\mathcal{F}}},\quad\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}}\simeq N^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{p_{T_{2}}^{2}|\beta|^{2}}{\sqrt{2\bar{\mathcal{F}}}}.

(23)

In some special cases where only the symmetric depolarizing channel is present, the error budget is dominated by the decoherence error channel, which is limited only by the operation time
\displaystyle\tau_{g}
. From Eq. (
22
), we see that the background noise is further suppressed by a factor of
\displaystyle\bar{n}_{r}
. In such cases, we have

SNR\simeq N\frac{p_{T_{2}}^{2}|\beta|^{2}}{\bar{n}_{r}\bar{\mathcal{F}}},\quad\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}}\simeq N^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{p_{T_{2}}^{2}|\beta|^{2}}{\sqrt{2\bar{n}_{r}\bar{\mathcal{F}}}}.

(24)

The thermal excitation at very low temperatures typically saturates to a small number
\displaystyle\bar{n}_{r0}

IV
Outlook

In this work, we propose a novel DM detection scheme that utilizes the quantum coherence channels among sensors. This approach surpasses conventional population-based sensor measurements due to its immunity to incoherent background noise. Disabling interactions between sensors during the detection process ensures that noise from excitation events remains uncorrelated across sensors. Conversely, quantum sensors within their coherence length and time are coherently excited by bosonic DM fields, thus providing a inherently noise-free channel. In a network comprising
\displaystyle N
sensors, there exist a total of
\displaystyle O(N^{2})
coherence channels. The noise-free coherence signals are extracted by initially applying a unitary transformation and subsequently performing a population measurement in the transformed basis. This transformation is achieved through
\displaystyle O(N)
two-body operations within a duration of
\displaystyle O(\tau_{g}\log_{2}N)
. Consequently, the resulting signal strength scales as
\displaystyle O(N^{2})
, with only an additional
\displaystyle O(N)
noise, resulting in a signal-to-noise ratio that scales linearly with
\displaystyle N
. The DM detection sensitivity is primarily limited by the infidelities of the basis-transformation operations rather than incoherent background noise. Additionally, the logarithmic scaling of the operation time mitigates constraints associated with scalability.

In addition to employing vacuum states as the initial probe states, a range of nonclassical states—including Fock states
[

,
56
]
, Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger states
[

,
45
]
, squeezed states
[

,
58
,

]
, and Schrödinger cat states
[
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
]
—emerge as promising candidates for enhancing the capabilities of DM detection. Further investigation into the integration of these exotic quantum states within our coherence-based method hold the potential to substantially improve the DM detection sensitivity.

Furthermore, by leveraging the alternating strong electromagnetic fields confined in superconducting cavities
[
65
,
66
]
or the Nb-based Josephson junctions and transmon qubits capable of tolerating strong magnetic fields
[

,

, our approach facilitates advanced measurements of axions and ultra-high-frequency gravitation waves
[

under these strong magnetic field conditions.

I
Detection schemes with resonant oscillators

In this section, we focus on the electromagnetic resonant (cavity and superconducting circuit) and mechanical resonant (trapped ion) systems and set up a parameterization for the interaction between quantized resonant modes and potential signals, such as an effective current.
We follow Ref.
[
36
]
for a similar derivation of the Hamiltonian of electromagnetic resonant systems.

I.1
Resonant cavity

The electromagnetic fields within a cavity of volume
\displaystyle V
are quantized bound states.
In the Coulomb gauge, the vector potential can be parameterized as

\vec{A}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{r}^{n}}}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{r}\,)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{r}^{n}\,t}+h.c..

(S1)

where the sum is taken over various modes labeled by
\displaystyle n
, and
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}
(
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}
) it the annihilation (creation) operator for a mode characterized by an eigenfrequency
\displaystyle\omega_{r}^{n}
and a wave function
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{r}\,)
.
The electric and magnetic fields of the cavity modes are given by

\vec{E}=-\partial\vec{A}/\partial t,\qquad\vec{B}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{A}.

(S2)

Considering a perfect conductor as the boundary, each mode is determined by the vacuum Maxwell’s equation
\displaystyle\Box\vec{A}=0
inside the receiver cavity, along with the boundary condition
\displaystyle\vec{B}_{\perp}=0
and
\displaystyle\vec{E}_{\parallel}=0
. Furthermore, the wave functions must satisfy the orthonormality condition:

\int_{\text{V}}\vec{\epsilon}_{m}^{\ *}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}=\delta_{mn}.

(S3)

Using Eqs. (
S1
-
S3
), the free Hamiltonian for cavity modes can be written as:

\begin{split}H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\text{V}}\left(\vec{E}^{2}+\vec{B}^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}=\sum_{n}\omega_{r}^{n}\left(\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right).\end{split}

(S4)

A cavity mode is generated resonantly once its frequency falls within the resonant bandwidth and its wave function spatially overlaps with the effective current
\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm eff}
originating from bosonic fields. The interaction Hamiltonian concerning the spatial component of the effective current
\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\rm eff}
is given by a linear coupling with the vector potential
\displaystyle\vec{A}
:

\begin{split}H_{\text{int}}=\int_{\text{V}}\,\vec{A}\cdot\vec{J}_{\rm eff}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}=\sum_{n}\sqrt{\frac{V}{2\omega_{r}^{n}}}\,G_{n}\,\bar{J}_{\rm eff}\,\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{r}^{n}\,t}+h.c..\end{split}

(S5)

where
\displaystyle G_{n}
is the geometric overlap factor between
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\rm eff}
:

G_{n}\equiv\frac{{\int}_{V}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\cdot\vec{J}_{\rm eff}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}}{\sqrt{\int_{V}|\vec{J}_{\rm eff}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}}}.

(S6)

and
\displaystyle\bar{J}_{\rm eff}
denotes the average current density within the cavity:

\bar{J}_{\rm eff}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}|\vec{J}_{\rm eff}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}}.

(S7)

On the other hand, the time component of
\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm eff}
, corresponding to an effective charge density
\displaystyle\rho_{\rm eff}
, exclusively excites the irrotational mode
[
72
]
, which does not experience resonant enhancement within the cavity context.

I.2
Superconducting Quantum Circuits

Before we introduce the nonlinear quantum circuits, we first quantize the Hamiltonian of an LC resonator characterized by its inductance
\displaystyle L
and capacitance
\displaystyle C
encompassing its charging and inductive energy

H_{0}=\frac{Q^{2}}{2C}+\frac{\Phi^{2}}{2L}

(S8)

where
\displaystyle Q
is the charge accumulated in the capacitor and
\displaystyle\Phi
is the magnetic flux traversing the inductor, serving as a pair of canonical conjugate variables, which
are then promoted to noncommuting observables obeying the commutation relation

\displaystyle[\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q}]=i
.

Introducing the standard annihilation and creation operators,

\hat{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2Z_{r}}}(\hat{\Phi}-iZ_{r}\hat{Q}),\qquad\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2Z_{r}}}(\hat{\Phi}+iZ_{r}\hat{Q})

(S9)

with
\displaystyle Z_{r}=\sqrt{L/C}
being the characteristic impedance, the previous Hamiltonian takes the usual form

H_{0}=\omega_{r}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(S10)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{r}=1/\sqrt{LC}
is the resonant frequency.

Superconducting qubits, known as artificial atoms, are anharmonic oscillators in the form of non-linear LC circuits.
The nonlinearity is contributed by replacing the geometric inductance
\displaystyle L
of the LC oscillator with a single Josephson junction, which is a non-dissipative and nonlinear inductor, or two parallel junctions forming a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID).
The Hamiltonian of the circuit is modified by the presence of the Josephson junction taking the place of the linear inductor

H_{0}=\frac{\hat{Q}^{2}}{2C}-E_{J}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{\Phi_{0}}\hat{\Phi}\right)=4E_{C}\hat{n}^{2}-E_{J}\cos\hat{\varphi}

(S11)

with
\displaystyle\Phi_{0}=h/2e
the flux quantum and
\displaystyle E_{J}
the Josephson energy, which is fixed for a single Josephson junction and flux-tunable for a SQUID. We also define the charge number operator
\displaystyle\hat{n}=\hat{Q}/2e
, the phase operator
\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}=(2\pi/\Phi_{0})\hat{\Phi}
, and the charging energy
\displaystyle E_{C}=e^{2}/2C
.

In the transmon regime (
\displaystyle E_{J}/E_{C}\gg 1
)
[
73
]
, the variance of the phase is relatively small
\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\varphi}\ll 1
, therefore the last term of Eq. (
S11
) can be truncated to its first nonlinear correction

H_{0}\approx 4E_{C}\hat{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}E_{J}\hat{\varphi}^{2}-\frac{1}{4!}E_{J}\hat{\varphi}^{4}

(S12)

with the first two terms corresponding to an LC circuit of
capacitance
\displaystyle C
and inductance
\displaystyle E_{J}^{-1}(\Phi_{0}/2\pi)^{2}
, which can be expressed by the annihilation and creation operators

\hat{\varphi}=\left(\frac{2E_{C}}{E_{J}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}),\qquad\hat{n}=\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{E_{J}}{2E_{C}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a})

(S13)

Making use of the rotating-wave approximation finally gives the Hamiltonian

H_{0}\approx\omega_{r}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\frac{E_{C}}{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a}

(S14)

with
\displaystyle\omega_{r}=2\sqrt{2E_{C}E_{J}}-E_{C}
the transition frequency between the ground and first excited states, and the last term of Eq. (
S14
) denotes a Kerr nonlinearity.

Transmon qubits have a strong coupling to electric fields
[
74
]
.
In the presence of an effective current density, the observed electric field generated inside an electromagnetic shield is determined with the standard methods for driven cavities as in Sec.
I.1

[
29
]
, which is given by

\bar{E}_{\rm eff}=\kappa\frac{\bar{J}_{\rm eff}}{\omega}

(S15)

with
\displaystyle\omega
the driving frequency of the effective current,
\displaystyle\bar{J}_{\rm eff}
the average current density within the shield, and an enhancement factor

\kappa=\left|\sqrt{V}\sum_{n}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}-\omega^{2}}G_{n}\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\cdot\hat{n}_{C}\right|

(S16)

where
\displaystyle n
runs over all resonant modes,
\displaystyle\omega_{n},G_{n},\vec{\epsilon}_{n}
are the the resonant frequency, geometric factor and mode function respectively,
\displaystyle\hat{n}_{C}
is the normal vector of the conductor plate.
On the other hand, we simply have
\displaystyle\kappa=1
for the unshielded case.

The interaction Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\rm int}=\hat{Q}\bar{E}_{\rm eff}d=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}\omega}\sqrt{C\omega_{r}}\kappa{\bar{J}_{\rm eff}}d\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{r}t}+h.c.,

(S17)

where
\displaystyle d
is the effective distance between two conductor plates.

I.3
Trapped ion

Our analysis applies not only to electromagnetic resonant systems, but also generalizes to other kinds of quantum sensors, e.g., mechanical oscillators.
The motion of an ions in a Penning trap or Paul trap can be described in terms of three independent harmonic oscillator modes, realizing the mechanical oscillator. The free Hamiltonian given by

H_{0}=\sum_{n}\omega^{n}_{r}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)

(S18)

with
\displaystyle\omega^{n}_{r}
the mechanical resonant frequency of the
\displaystyle n
-th vibrational mode.
Mechanical oscillators such as trapped ions can serve as a high-Q resonator to measure small displacements due to weak forces and electromagnetic fields. The interaction Hamiltonian between the vibrational modes and the electric field induced by an effective current is

H_{\rm int}=e\vec{E}_{\rm eff}\cdot\hat{\vec{x}}=\sum_{n}\frac{eE_{\rm eff}^{n}}{\sqrt{2m_{\rm ion}\omega^{n}_{r}}}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega^{n}_{r}t}+h.c..

(S19)

where
\displaystyle\hat{\vec{x}}
is the three dimensional position operator,
\displaystyle\vec{E}_{\rm eff}
is the induced electric field at the location of the ion,
\displaystyle m_{\rm ion}
is the ion mass.

III
Qubit noise models

In the main text we mainly addressed three kinds of error sources: (i) hardware infidelities in the form of depolarizing Pauli error, (ii) state preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors, and (iii) decoherence in the form of thermal relaxation/excitation and dephasing errors. In this section we discuss the quantum channels that we use to model each error.

The noise acting on a qubit can be described by a trace-preserving quantum operation
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}
, which can be expanded in an operator-sum representation with operation elements
\displaystyle\{E_{i}\}
. The density matrix of the qubit
\displaystyle\rho
evolves under the acting of noise as

\rho\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sum_{i}E_{i}\rho E_{i}^{\dagger}

(S34)

The combination of two error channels
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{AB}=\mathcal{E}_{A}\circ\mathcal{E}_{B}
where
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{A}(\rho)=\sum_{i}A_{i}\rho A_{i}^{\dagger}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho)=\sum_{j}B_{j}\rho B_{j}^{\dagger}

is given by

\mathcal{E}_{AB}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{A}(\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho))=\sum_{ij}(A_{i}B_{j})\rho(A_{i}B_{j})^{\dagger}

(S35)

The Choi-matrix
[
75
]
provides an alternative representation for a quantum channel. For a single qubit, the Choi-matrix of the quantum channel
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}
is defined as

C^{(1)}=\sum_{i,j}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes\mathcal{E}(|i\rangle\langle j|)

(S36)

The evolution of the density matrix
\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}
with respect to the Choi matrix
\displaystyle C^{(1)}
can be described as

\rho^{(1)}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(\rho^{(1)})=\mathrm{Tr}_{1}[C^{(1)}(\rho^{(1)T}\otimes I)]

(S37)

where
\displaystyle T
represents transpose and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{1}
is the partial trace over subsystem 1 in which the density matrix
\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}
resides.

For multipule qubits the Choi matrix
\displaystyle C^{(n)}
can be constructed similarly by the tensor products.

III.1
The depolarizing channel

The depolarizing channel simulates the bit-flip and phase-flip errors due to operation infidelities within a certain process, which are represented by the following Pauli operators

D_{0}=\sqrt{1-\frac{3}{4}\lambda}I_{2},\quad D_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\sigma_{x},\quad D_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\sigma_{y},\quad D_{3}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\sigma_{z}

(S38)

The noise operation on a single qubit
\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}
gives

\mathcal{D}(\rho^{(1)})=\sum_{i=0}^{3}D_{i}\rho D_{i}^{\dagger}=(1-\lambda)\rho^{(1)}+\frac{\lambda}{2}I

(S39)

For the example we considered in the main text, the depolarizing channel
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{AB}
was devised to apply on sensor
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
with different parameters
\displaystyle\lambda_{A}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{B}
, where the two-qubit system evolves as

\rho^{\prime(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{AB}(\rho^{\prime(2)})=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{3}(D_{i}^{A}D_{j}^{B})\rho^{\prime(2)}(D_{i}^{A}\otimes D_{j}^{B})^{\dagger}

(S40)

The expectation value becomes

Tr[\mathcal{D}_{B}(\rho^{\prime(2)})\cdot\mathcal{O}^{\prime(2)}_{P}]=\frac{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}{2}(1-p_{reset}^{A}p_{1}^{A}-p_{reset}^{B}p_{1}^{B})+2p_{T_{2}}^{A}p_{T_{2}}^{B}|\beta|^{2}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{A}+\lambda_{B}}{2}+\frac{p_{T_{1}}^{A}+p_{T_{1}}^{B}}{4p_{T_{2}}^{A}p_{T_{2}}^{B}}(\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{B})\right),

(S41)

under the influence of
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{AB}
, with background noise of order
\displaystyle O(\lambda)
added.

In the contrast, the symmetric depolarizing channel applying to
\displaystyle n
-qubits with parameter
\displaystyle\lambda
gives

\mathcal{D}(\rho^{(n)})=(1-\lambda)\rho^{(n)}+\frac{\lambda}{2^{n}}I

(S42)

Tr[\mathcal{D}(\rho^{\prime(N)})\cdot\mathcal{O}^{\prime(N)}_{P}]=(1-\lambda)Tr[\rho^{\prime(N)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{\prime(N)}_{P}],

(S43)

which only leads to a
\displaystyle(1-\lambda)
suppression on the signal strength without adding additional noises.

III.2
State Preparation and Measurement (SPAM) Channel

The SPAM quantum channel can be simply represented by a simple Pauli X error with the following Kraus operators

S_{0}=\sqrt{1-p_{2}}I,\quad S_{1}=\sqrt{p_{2}}X

(S44)

where
\displaystyle p_{2}
is the is the probability that the measurement (state preparation) is incorrect.
The effect of the SPAM channel can be expressed as

\mathcal{S}(\rho^{(1)})=S_{0}\rho^{(1)}S_{0}+S_{1}\rho^{(1)}S_{1}

(S45)

III.3
Thermal Decoherence and Dephasing Channel

The single qubit thermal relaxation channel is parameterized by relaxation time constants
\displaystyle T_{1}
and
\displaystyle T_{2}
with relation
\displaystyle T_{2}\leq 2T_{1}
, gate time
\displaystyle t
, and excited state thermal population
\displaystyle p_{1}
.

If
\displaystyle T_{2}\leq T_{1}
, the error can be expressed as a mixed reset and Pauli
\displaystyle Z
error channel, where the single qubit reset quantum error channel is given by the map

\rho^{(1)}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\rho^{(1)})=(1-p_{1})|0\rangle\langle 0|+p_{1}|1\rangle\langle 1|.

(S46)

The effect of the thermal decoherence and dephasing channel can be expressed as

\rho^{(1)}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(\rho^{(1)})=\frac{p_{T_{1}}+p_{T_{2}}}{2}\rho^{(1)}+\frac{p_{T_{1}}-p_{T_{2}}}{2}Z\rho^{(1)}Z+p_{\mathrm{reset}}\mathcal{R}(\rho^{(1)})

(S47)

where
\displaystyle p_{T_{1}}=e^{-t/T_{1}}
and
\displaystyle p_{T_{2}}=e^{-t/T_{2}}
are the probability for a qubit to relax and dephase during time
\displaystyle t
,
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{reset}}=1-p_{T_{1}}
is the probability for a qubit to reset to an equilibrium state.

While for
\displaystyle T_{1}\leq T_{2}\leq 2T_{1}
, the error can only be expressed by the following Choi matrix

C^{(1)}=\sum_{i,j}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes\mathcal{E}(|i\rangle\langle j|)=\begin{pmatrix}1-p_{1}p_{\mathrm{reset}}&0&0&p_{T_{2}}\\
0&p_{1}p_{\mathrm{reset}}&0&0\\
0&0&(1-p_{1})p_{\mathrm{reset}}&0\\
p_{T_{2}}&0&0&1-(1-p_{1})p_{\mathrm{reset}}\end{pmatrix}

(S48)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(|0\rangle\langle 0|)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-p_{1}p_{\mathrm{reset}})|0\rangle\langle 0|+p_{1}p_{\mathrm{reset}}|1\rangle\langle 1|

(S49)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(|0\rangle\langle 1|)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=p_{T_{2}}|0\rangle\langle 1|

(S50)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(|1\rangle\langle 0|)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=p_{T_{2}}|1\rangle\langle 0|

(S51)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(|1\rangle\langle 1|)

The background noise added due to the thermal relaxation channel is on an order of

IV
Multiple sensors containing all coherence

For
\displaystyle N
quantum sensors there are totally
\displaystyle{N(N-1)}/{2}
coherence channels.
In contrast of the particular observable we considered in the main text, the one containing all coherence channels can be defined as

\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{CA}=\sum_{I>J}\mathcal{O}^{(2)}_{C_{IJ}}=\sum_{I\neq J}|E^{(N)}_{I}\rangle\langle E^{(N)}_{J}|,

(S56)

which gives noise-free signals enhanced by a factor of
\displaystyle N(N-1)
:

Tr[\rho^{(N)}\cdot\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{CA}]=2\sum_{I>J}p_{T_{2}}^{I}p_{T_{2}}^{J}|\beta|^{2}.

(S57)

It can be diagonalized in the same way by the unitary transformation
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}
:

\mathcal{O}^{\prime(N)}_{PA}=\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{CA}\mathcal{U}^{(N)\dagger}_{C}=N|E^{(N)}_{N}\rangle\langle E^{(N)}_{N}|-\sum_{I=1}^{N}|E^{(N)}_{I}\rangle\langle E^{(N)}_{I}|.

(S58)

Although the observable contains more coherence channels than
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{C}
, the background noise for the measurement of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{CA}
would increase as
\displaystyle O(N^{2})
instead of
\displaystyle O(N)
if we consider the hardware infidelities in implementations of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C}
.
This is because errors due to all steps of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(N)}_{C_{IJ}}
will contribute to the final measurement result of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{CA}
, while for
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{(N)}_{C}
only the last one does.
