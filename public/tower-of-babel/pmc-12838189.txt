Physician-reported outcomes associated with collaboration between home healthcare institutions and after-hours house-call services in Japan
Authors: Yu Sun, Ryota Inokuchi, Masao Iwagami, Nobuo Sakata, Ayaka Sakamoto, Nanako Tamiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Japan, home healthcare is a system in which physicians regularly visit patients at home to provide medical care. Additionally, the physicians are required to be available for 24-hour emergency home visits upon patient request. However, numerous single-physician home healthcare providers have been overburdened, particularly with after-hours consultations. This study aimed to examine physicians’ perceptions of changes associated with collaboration between medical institutions providing home healthcare and after-hours house-call (AHHC) services.MethodsA questionnaire survey was conducted in 57 clinics and hospitals providing home healthcare that began collaborating with AHHC providers in Japan between January 2021 and September 2022. This study analyzed the changes in the management of after-hours consultations by healthcare providers before and after collaboration, along with its advantages and drawbacks. Furthermore, we also compared job satisfaction, workload, quality of life (QOL), and fatigue before and after collaboration, using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to determine changes in each scale.ResultsThe study included 25 physicians from 21 institutions, with a response rate of 37%. The percentage of home visits for after-hours consultations increased after collaboration. While 56–88% (16–22/25) of physicians noted that the mental and physical burden of AHHC was reduced, 48% (12/25) of physicians noted an increase in the burden of sharing patient information. The results revealed physicians perceived improvements in job satisfaction (p = 0.015), QOL (p = 0.026), and fatigue (p = 0.015) after collaboration; however, there was no significant change in workload (p = 0.209).ConclusionOur findings indicate that physicians perceived improvements in job satisfaction and QOL and reduced fatigue after the collaboration with AHHC services. However, this result also suggests that it is necessary to simplify the sharing of patient information and reduce the burden of collaboration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03120-5.

Background

Population aging significantly affects healthcare and long-term care delivery worldwide [1], driving a renewed interest in home healthcare services [2, 3]. With the rise in patient demand and the need to alleviate the burden on general practitioners (GPs), the organization of primary care has evolved, with various models of after-hours house-call (AHHC) services emerging [4, 5]. In several developed countries, private AHHC services have become common, with an increasing number of general practitioners in Australia utilizing these services [6, 7].

In Japan, which has the world’s most advanced and rapidly aging society in the world [8], the government has been promoting a shift in healthcare from hospitals to the community, particularly to home healthcare. To support this transition, the government introduced home care support clinics/hospitals (HCSCs) between 2006 and 2008, which are required to offer a 24-hour on-call home visit system upon patients’ request. Enhanced HCSCs were introduced in 2012 to further enhance home healthcare by providing higher-quality care with a focus on emergency home visits and end-of-life care [9, 10]. These enhanced HCSCs receive higher fees than conventional ones but must meet stricter requirements, including employing three or more full-time doctors, having conducted at least 10 physician-led emergency home visits in the past year, and providing at least four home-based end-of-life cases during that period [9, 10]. While the number of HCSCs initially increased, it plateaued in recent years, and only 26% of HCSCs were enhanced HCSCs in 2022 [10]. Furthermore, owing to the high burden of maintaining a 24-hour on-call system, many non-HCSC general clinics provide home healthcare [11]. Approximately 70% of these general clinics and conventional HCSCs are run by single-physician practitioners [12], making the 24-hour on-call system burdensome [13]. This phenomenon highlights the need for systems that reduce after-hours workload, particularly for single-physician practitioners providing home healthcare.

Under these conditions, a large, private AHHC service was introduced in 2016, followed by the launch of collaborative efforts with home healthcare institutions from 2021. This initiative aimed to alleviate the burden on physicians providing home healthcare while improving patients’ access to care at night and on holidays. While prior studies in other countries have shown that after-hours GP cooperatives reduce physician burden and improve patient satisfaction [4, 14, 15], the effects of collaboration between Japanese medical institutions and private AHHC services remain unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine changes in home healthcare delivery and physician-reported outcomes associated with collaboration between home healthcare institutions and AHHC services, and to evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of such collaboration.

Methods

Home healthcare and AHHC medical services in Japan

Japanese citizens are covered by a universal health insurance system, which includes home healthcare services. In Japan, home healthcare involves regular home visits by physicians from one of the three types of medical institutions: general clinics, conventional HCSCs, and enhanced HCSCs. Patients receiving regular physician-led home visits can call their physician when their condition suddenly worsens and request emergency home visits.

Japan’s AHHC service provides phone consultations and triage, including dispatching a physician for a home visit or calling an ambulance. We previously reported on a private AHHC service in Japan [16–19]. Initially, the AHHC was designed for patients who did not receive regular home healthcare. However, since 2021, the AHHC services have begun collaborating with medical institutions to provide after-hours care (from 18:00 to 06:00 on weekdays and 24 h on weekends and holidays) to patients already receiving home healthcare. Two methods of collaboration exist between primary physicians and AHHC services. In the first model, the primary physician receives the initial call and, if necessary, requests a home visit from the AHHC. In the second model, the AHHC service manages all after-hours phone support, including triage and arrangement of home visits, when needed.

Study design and participants

This study targeted 57 home care medical institutions collaborating with Fast DOCTOR Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), an AHHC service provider, between January 2021 and September 2022. The questionnaire survey was conducted in October 2022. Fast DOCTOR Ltd. had contracted with 99 institutions by this time, although 14 had discontinued collaboration. The survey focused on 57 institutions that maintained collaboration and agreed to share their data with the University of Tsukuba.

The survey included two types of questionnaires: one addressing the institution itself and the other targeting each physician working there. The questionnaire was distributed by post to medical institutions providing home healthcare. Each institution received a single institutional questionnaire, while all physicians engaged in home healthcare at the institution were asked to complete the physician-specific questionnaire. As we did not have data on the exact number of physicians per institution, we assumed a maximum of five and accordingly sent five physician-specific questionnaires per institution. The first author (Sun) developed the questionnaires based on previous research and refined them through three rounds of discussion with the co-authors (Tamiya, Inokuchi, and Sakamoto).

Survey items

Institutional questionnaire

This questionnaire gathered information about the institution’s characteristics, including how it collaborated with the AHHC service (e.g., whether the AHHC service handled the initial call during after-hours or if the institution managed it directly) and the number of physicians employed (both full-time and part-time). The questionnaire also addressed changes in the institution’s revenue and physician salaries after the collaboration, the frequency of phone consultations and emergency home visits before and after the collaboration, and how after-hours consultations were managed.

Physician-specific questionnaire

The physician-specific questionnaire gathered demographic information, including age, gender, employment status, position, marital status, presence of children, specialty, and years of experience in home healthcare. It also required physicians to report the benefits and drawbacks of the collaboration for both themselves and their patients, using predetermined options based on prior research and discussions among researchers. The physicians were inquired whether they believed that the benefits of collaboration outweighed the drawbacks and whether they would recommend such collaboration to other institutions.

Additionally, the questionnaire assessed changes in the physicians’ job satisfaction, workload, quality of life (QOL), and fatigue levels using a five-point Likert scale. Job satisfaction and workload were measured using the Japan Hospital Physician Satisfaction Scale (JHPSS) [20], which includes two global scales and six subscales. The “Global Job Satisfaction” scale, one of the JHPSS global scales, was used to measure overall job satisfaction, while the “Burden and Business” subscale was used to assess workload. Both scales comprise five items each, with total scores converted to a 0–20 point range, where higher scores indicate greater satisfaction or a lower workload. The QOL and fatigue were assessed using single-item measures on a five-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicated higher QOL and lower levels of fatigue. The specific questions for each category are presented in Appendix 1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the aggregated data. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate the changes in physicians’ job satisfaction, workload, QOL, and fatigue levels before and after collaboration. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using STATA version 15.0 (Stata Corp., Texas, USA).

Results

Institutional questionnaire

Responses were received from 21 institutions (response rate: 37%). Among these, 47.6% delegated initial calls to the AHHC service during after-hours, while 38.1% were handled by their own institutions. Seventeen (81%) institutions had only one full-time physician, although the number of part-time physicians varied (Table 1).

Changes before and after collaboration

It was noted that one institution transitioned from a general clinic to a conventional HCSC after collaboration, while another upgraded from a conventional HCSC to an enhanced HCSC. Most institutions reported no changes in revenue or physicians’ salaries following collaboration (Table 1). Figure 2 illustrates the changes in after-hours emergency home visits and phone consultations before and after the collaboration with AHHC. The results indicate that while the frequency of phone consultations remained largely unchanged, there was an increase in the number of after-hours emergency home visits following the collaboration. Additionally, in terms of how after-hours consultations were managed, most medical institutions reported making more emergency home visits whenever possible, while reducing their reliance on providing instructions to visiting nurses, requesting ambulances, and referring patients to emergency rooms after the collaboration.

Physician questionnaire

Responses were obtained from 25 physicians. Most respondents were male (76%), with an average age of 53.2 (± 10.5) years. Most were full-time physicians (88%) and directors (64%); regarding their marital status, it was observed that many were married and living with their children. There were variations in both specialties and years of experience in home healthcare (Table 1).

Benefits and drawbacks of collaboration

Table 2 shows the result responses regarding the benefits and drawbacks for physicians and patients owing to collaboration The most frequently noted benefits for physicians were: “reduced mental burden (88%)”, “easier planning of after-hours schedules (64%)”, “reduced physical burden (56%)”, and “improved QOL (48%)”. For patients, the frequently noted benefits were: “patients and their families feel more reassured (48%)” and that “the proportion of cases where home visits could be arranged during after-hours increased (44%)”. Contrastingly, 48% of physicians reported an increased burden related to sharing patient information. Fewer than 10% of the physicians noted any drawbacks for patients. Overall, 84% of the physicians believed that the benefits outweighed the disadvantages, and 68% said that they would recommend collaboration with other institutions.

Changes in job satisfaction, workload, quality of life, and fatigue

Table 3 shows the changes in job satisfaction, workload, QOL, and fatigue before and after collaboration. It was noted that physicians’ job satisfaction increased significantly after collaboration (pre-collaboration mean: 14.9 vs. post-collaboration mean: 15.4, p = 0.015). The QOL also showed significant improvement (3.36 vs. 3.60, p = 0.026), and fatigue levels decreased (2.64 vs. 3.04, p = 0.015). Although there was a trend toward reduced workload after collaboration, the change was not statistically significant (8.92 vs. 9.16, p = 0.209).

Discussion

This study evaluated physicians’ perceptions of changes associated with collaboration between home healthcare institutions and AHHC services. Following the collaboration, most medical institutions reported increased emergency home visits and decreased requests for ambulances and emergency room visits. Additionally, physicians perceived improvements in job satisfaction and QOL and reduced fatigue levels. Collaboration was seen as particularly beneficial in reducing mental and physical burdens, allowing for better work-life balance. However, the increased burden of sharing patient information was noted as a drawback, with no significant change in the overall workload.

Nearly all institutions included in this study were already HCSCs before the collaboration, indicating that they were equipped to manage 24-hour on-call systems. However, with 81% of the institutions employing only one full-time physician, the number of calls they could handle was likely limited. Collaboration with the AHHC services increases the availability of human resources, enabling better emergency management. In fact, 44% of physicians reported an increase in cases in which home visits could be arranged during after-hours. Some institutions have even upgraded to enhanced HCSCs after collaboration, possibly because of the increased frequency of emergency home visits. Previous studies have shown that patients using HCSCs (especially enhanced ones) receive more home visits and experience fewer hospitalizations than those receiving care from general clinics [9, 21]. The current study supports this finding, with participants highlighting an “increased number of cases in which treatment could continue at home without hospitalization.” Avoiding hospitalization, particularly for older patients, may help maintain physical and cognitive functions while reducing medical costs [22–24]. Therefore, strengthening the 24-hour home visit system through collaboration with services, such as AHHC providers, could be a viable solution for medical institutions with a limited number of full-time physicians.

Collaboration also provides several benefits for physicians, including reduced mental and physical burdens and better separation of professional and personal lives. Previous studies have reported similar findings, highlighting that group practices improved work-life balance, job satisfaction, and quality of care [25, 26]. However, a frequently mentioned drawback is the increased burden of sharing patient information, with no significant reduction in the overall workload. The need to manually input patient information into the information-sharing tool as part of the collaboration with the AHHC provider likely contributed to this burden. In March 2023, an automated system was introduced for certain electronic medical records, allowing patient information to be automatically reflected in the sharing tool. This change may have reduced the burden; however, further investigation is required to achieve a detailed result.

There are several limitations in our study. First, selection bias may be at play, as the medical institutions and physicians who responded to the survey may have been more likely to perceive collaboration with AHHC services as effective. Second, as we did not collect data on the number of physicians at each institution, we were unable to calculate the response rate for the physician-targeted questionnaire. Third, recall bias may have influenced the reporting of various measures before collaboration. To minimize recall bias, future studies should conduct surveys at the beginning of the collaboration and after its implementation. Fourth, to assess the benefits to patients more accurately, future research should include surveys targeting patients themselves. Finally, because this study employed a cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. The findings represent physicians’ perceptions of changes associated with collaboration, rather than objectively measured effects, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study is the first in Japan to investigate the collaboration between medical institutions providing home healthcare and AHHC services. It provides valuable insights that can contribute to the future development and improvement of home healthcare systems.

Conclusion

Physicians perceived improvements in their job satisfaction and QOL, and perceived reduced fatigue after the collaboration between home care medical institutions and AHHC services. The most notable benefits were the reduction of mental and physical burdens and enhanced work-life balance. However, the increased burden of patient information-sharing has been identified as a drawback. These findings provide valuable insights for the future development of home healthcare systems in Japan.
