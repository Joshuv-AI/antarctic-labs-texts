The relationship between homocysteine and post-stroke depression in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Chao Zhang, Guojuan Chen, Peng Ding, Lei Xiang, Wei Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepression after a stroke is the most frequent and burdensome neuropsychiatric post-stroke complication. This study aimed to determine the relationship between post-stroke depression (PSD) and the levels of homocysteine (HCY) in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (SICH).MethodWe collected data from patients with hemorrhagic stroke (HS) admitted to the hospital and recorded their demographic and clinical characteristics. We also searched for information regarding HAM-D17 (Hamilton Depression) scores and HCY levels at 3 m, the use of antidepressant medications and folic acid during the follow-up period in the group of patients diagnosed with PSD and hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) in the acute phase.ResultA total of 1,852 patients were included. 642 (34.7%) patients with PSD and 598 (32.3%) patients with HHcy, 364 (19.7%) patients with both conditions. A link between depression and low HCY level has similarly been found in patients with HS (OR, 1.549; 95% CI, 1.358–1.768). Additionally, left-sided stroke, anterior circulatory stroke, intraventricular hemorrhage, symptoms of paralysis or dysarthria, and higher NIHSS scores occurred more often in the PSD group. Compared to the antidepressant medications (ADM) group, HAM-D17 scores decreased significantly in the ADM plus folic acid group at the end of 3 m (P = 0.036).ConclusionOn the basis of our data, PSD was significantly more frequent in patients with HHcy in patients with SICH at the acute phase. The location of hemorrhage and the severity of the disease are significantly correlated with the incidence of PSD. Oral doses of folic acid and ADM showed significant improvements in the HAM-D17 scores for the patients with comorbid PSD and HHcy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04951-0.Keywords: Homocysteine, Folic acid, Post-stroke depression, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Treatment

Introduction

Along with an aging social population, stroke is the largest contributor to global mortality and the leading cause of both death and premature death (accounting for more than 40%) in China [1, 2]. The World Health Organization has recently reported that depression is the leading cause of disability worldwide [3]. Depression and stroke are the two major causes of socioeconomic burden, and depression after stroke is the most burdensome and common neuropsychiatric post-stroke complication [4, 5]. The prevalence of post-stroke depression (PSD) ranges from 18% to 55% [3, 6, 7]. A previous study indicated that hemorrhagic stroke is associated with higher odds of depression than ischemic stroke in the acute phase [8]. Therefore, it is important to assess patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (SICH) when studying PSD.

Lower homocysteine (HCY) levels are correlated with a higher risk of severe symptoms of depression [9]. The literature suggests that patients with depression have lower serum levels of folic acid (FA) and that FA supplementation improves the efficacy of antidepressant medications (ADM) [10]. A meta-analysis found that HCY levels were a risk factor for PSD in patients with ischemic stroke at the acute phase [11]. Furthermore, HCY levels have a significant negative effect on global cognition in patients with depression [12]. However, most studies of the PSD either only focus on ischemic strokes or do not distinguish between ischemic and hemorrhage stoke in the further analysis. Our study collected data from a large retrospective clinical trial to describe the relationship between HCY and PSD, and to clarify the collaborative treatment effect of ADM and FA in patients with SICH.

Method

Study design and population

A total of 3,832 patients with hemorrhagic stroke (HS) admitted to Tianjin Huanhu Hospital between Jan. 1st 2021 and Jan. 1st 2022 were included in our study, which was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Review Board. All patients were diagnosed with HS, and high-density imaging was performed using Computed Tomography (CT). In the secondary part, we collected the follow-up data of patients (n = 364) diagnosed with PSD and hyperhomocysteinemia(HHcy) in the acute phase of HS from the Cerebrovascular Disease Up System of our hospital.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 80 years; (2) patients who met the diagnostic criteria for acute HS; (3) patients without a history of depression who did not receive ADM; (4) complete data of relevant variables; and (5) willingness to participate in the study and provision of signed written informed consent. Patients were excluded on the basis of one of the following criteria: (1) surgery (e.g. decompressive craniotomy and surgical hematoma evacuation); (2) the patients were diagnosed with HS caused by brain trauma, hemangioma, or tumor; (3) the patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage; (4) the patients with severe symptoms, i.e., scoring ≤ 12 on the Glasgow Coma Scale, severe organ dysfunction, such as heart, liver, and kidney, as well as those who cannot complete the original 17-item Hamilton’s Depression Scale (HAM-D17) [13].

Definition of confounders and intermediary variables

The observational variables were HCY and HAM-D17 scores. All patients were interviewed using a structured questionnaire (HAM-D17) and their HCY levels were collected within 7 days of hospitalization. Patients with HCY > 15 mmol/L were diagnosed with HHcy and those with HAM-D17 >17 were diagnosed with post-stroke depression (PSD). We collected details about health conditions, comorbidities, clinical characteristics, and neuroimaging results. In addition, we searched for the following information for three months from onset in patients with HHcy and PSD. We collected information on the HAM-D17 score and HCY level at 3 m, using the situation of ADM and FA during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26 was used for statistical analysis of the data, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The HCY and HAM-D17 scores were expressed as mean standard deviation. We analyzed the count data by the chi-square test and analyzed quantitative data by the independent samples t-test. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to identify the association between HHcy levels and PSD. We used the independent samples t-test to compare data before and after treatment.

Following these exclusions, 1,852 participants were included in the analysis, out of which 1247 (67.3%) patients were male, with a mean age of 59.10. In total, 557 (30.1%) and 408 (22.0%) patients were smokers and drinkers, respectively. There was no significant difference between patients with and without depression. According to the imaging findings, in the PSD group, there were 367 cases (57.2%) in the cortex and 162 (25.2%) in the basal ganglia, and the difference was statistically significant in multivariable logistic regression. Additionally, left-sided stroke (OR, 1.749; 95% CI, 1.465–2.089), anterior circulatory stroke (OR, 0.720; 95% CI, 0.595–0.871), and intraventricular hemorrhage (OR, 1.551; 95% CI, 1.315–1.829) occurred more often in the PSD group (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Treatment responses

Among the 1,852 patients with HS, 642, 598, and 364 had PSD, HHcy, and PSD combined with HHcy, respectively. During the follow-up phase, 116, 142, and 34 patients received ADM and FA, ADM, FA, respectively, and 47 patients without any concerned treatment. The mean HAM-D17 scores at 3 m post stroke were 9.13 points (SD 3.74 points), 10.23 points (SD 4.65 points), and 12.76 points (SD 3.24 points) in the antidepressants plus folic acid (FA), antidepressants, and FA groups, respectively. Furthermore, the mean HCY levels were 7.43 points (SD 3.32 points), 18.29 points (SD 7.43 points), and 8.65 points (SD 3.84 points) in the mentioned three groups, respectively (Fig. 3, supporting Table 1). Compared with the ADM group, the HAM-D17 scores decreased significantly in the ADM plus FA group at the end of three month (P = 0.036).

Discussion

We all got 1,852 participants included in the analysis, 642 (34.7%) patients with PSD and 598 (32.3%) patients with HHcy, 364 (19.7%) patients with both conditions. Patients with PSD have a significantly higher prevalence of HHcy. The location of intracerebral hemorrhage, the presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, the extent of functional impairment, and the severity of the disease are significantly correlated with the incidence of PSD. At the 3-month follow-up, patients with PSD complicated by HHcy who received combined treatment with FA and ADM showed significant improvements in the HAM-D17 scores.

In a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies, Ayerbe et al. indicated that 55% of hospitalized stroke patients were depressed at a certain point after a cardiovascular disease [7] and approximately 33% of patients face depression in the first year after their stroke [14]. Cheng et al. found that the cumulative incidence rate of PSD was 33.5% three months after the stroke [15]. The development of PSD (especially for patients with SICH) is the most burdensome and frequent neuropsychiatric complication [16–18]. It is necessary for us to recognizing the risk factors, which can guide closer monitoring the symptoms of PSD for the high-risk groups, and may lead to more intensive preventive interventions.

The etiology of PSD is complex in origin and progression. The results of this study indicate that PSD was significantly more frequent in patients with HHcy. The levels of HCY were associated with an increased risk of depression, as confirmed in previous studies [19, 20]. Serum levels of HCY are a biological marker of the risk of PSD; patients with higher HCY levels are considered to have a risk 6.05 times higher than the patients with lower levels [21]. The pathophysiology of the association between HCY and PSD is multifactorial and likely involves several factors. First, HCY increases oxidative stress and cytotoxic effects, leading to neuronal damage and the induction of apoptosis in the hippocampus. HCY has adverse effects on neurotransmitters and causes endothelial dysfunction, then causing cognitive and affective disorders in patients, by impair the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factors (BDNF) [22]. Secondly, methionine, the precursor of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), is synthesized from HCY by methionine synthase. The reduction of SAM leads to reduced generation of monoamines and neurotransmitters requiring methyl groups in the generation process. Therefore, HCY influences monoamine metabolism in the brain. Decreased monoamine levels play an important role in depression pathophysiology. Third, the MTHFR C677T polymorphism, which can impair homocysteine metabolism, has been shown to be overrepresented among patients with depression [23], and HCY affects DNA methylation, which strengthens the association. Another animal experiment indicated that HCY could aggravate depression-like behaviors via NMDAR-mediated synaptic alterations [24].

For patients with PSD, early detection and suitable management are essential to provide best treatment. The optimal treatment is a combination of stroke-focused treatments, pharmacological, and psychosocial [3]. According to our results, FA had a positive effect on individuals with PSD and alleviated their depressive symptoms. Similarly, previous studies have indicated that higher folate levels are associated with a better response to ADM (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI) and tricyclic [25, 26], whereas, oral doses of folic acid and adjusted dietary patterns can improve treatment outcomes in depression [27].

The frequency of PSD in our study was 34.67%, and this result was comparable to that reported in previous large studies. We found that the presence of depression was associated with the extent of disability, and that patients with PSD had higher NIHSS scores. The incidence of PSD is higher in patients with intraventricular extension of intracerebral hemorrhage. Notably, the extension of intraventricular hematoma is strongly associated with a poor prognosis [28]. Compared to any other symptoms of impaired function, the symptom of paralysis and dysarthria make them prone to depression [29]. We also found that cortical or basal ganglia lesions and left-sided stroke occurred more often in the patients with PSD during the acute phase. Douven et al. reported that cortex and basal ganglia lesions are associated with depression [8]. This may be attributable to distinct underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, non-hypertensive hemorrhages are observed more frequently in lobar intracerebral hemorrhages [30]. And compared with deep subcortical hemorrhages, lobar intracerebral hemorrhage are associated with a more severe early prognosis [31]. Numerous studies have found a significant association between PSD and the left hemisphere lesion location in patients [32, 33]. Our study showed an increased prevalence of depression among patients with anterior circulatory stroke and intraventricular hemorrhage. Recent studies have indicated that PSD is associated with damage to the neuronal network damage [8, 34]. Further studies are required that focus on more advanced imaging techniques and a broader range of imaging markers to evaluate the association between depression and SICH.

However, this study has limitations that could affect the application of the results. This was a single-center retrospective study with a limited number of participants and a short follow-up period, which may have affected the estimates and was a source of potential bias. Second, we did not collect data on the degree of recovery of neurological tissue loss and functional decline after stroke, which has been proven to be a factor in PSD [3]. Third, there are multiple biomarkers (i.e., CRP, IL-6, TNF-a) in predicting PSD in stroke, which should be further exploration the prospective relationship between HCY and multiple biomarkers. Hence, these errors should not bias the conclusions regarding the relationship between HCY and PSD.

Furthermore, the relationship between post-stroke sequelae, long-term functional impairment, and PSD should be explored. Additional biomarkers also need to be identified to predict the relationship between HCY and PSD. In future studies, long-term, multicenter, prospective research should be conducted to further elucidate the relationship between HCY and PSD.

Conclusion

We believe our study provides valuable insights into the demographic and clinical characteristics of depression in acute SICH patients and highlights the pertinent issues regarding the association for serum HCY level and depression, respectively. We report a strong association between NIHSS scores and PSD. Individuals with PSD are more likely to develop SICH with symptoms such as paralysis, dysarthria, cortical or basal ganglia lesions, left-sided stroke, anterior circulatory stroke, and intraventricular hemorrhage. These data suggest that elevated HCY levels are related to the risk of developing depression after stroke onset. Homocysteine-lowering treatments may provide potential therapeutic routes to reduce the effect of depression in PSD patients. Further long-term prospective studies are required to confirm our findings.
