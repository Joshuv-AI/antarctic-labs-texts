Exploring collaboration preferences of health care professionals and patients regarding treatment decision making in severe asthma: a qualitative study in The Netherlands
Authors: Maria Achterbosch, Hinke M van der Werf, Laura Mortelmans, Tinne Dilles, Boudewijn J H Dierick, Liset van Dijk, Job F M van Boven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShared decision making (SDM) is advocated as an approach for patient-centred asthma care. However, this approach may not always be feasible or preferred by patients and/or healthcare professionals (HCPs). Knowledge and insights into whether and how the preferred collaboration style in medical decision making is discussed and managed during consultations for severe asthma are limited.ObjectivesTo investigate how HCPs’ and patients’ preferred collaboration styles in treatment decision making are experienced, discussed and managed during consultations.MethodsQualitative research using semi-structured interviews with HCPs specialised in severe asthma and a focus group with severe asthma patients in The Netherlands. Inductive thematic analysis was used, with results reported according to the COREQ checklist.ResultsFour themes concerning HCPs’ and patients’ experiences were identified: (1) overall preference for SDM, yet (2) ambiguity of SDM’s content, (3) variation in application of SDM and (4) friction between medical focus and patient autonomy in SDM. A fifth theme concerned the discussion and management of collaboration style preferences: limited metacommunication. HCPs and patients seem unable to identify each other’s preferences in collaboration styles. Overall, a lack of communication regarding how to collaborate in making treatment decisions was found. Varying views on meaning and content of collaboration styles, especially SDM, and HCPs’ medical focus seemed to contribute to the lack of communication.ConclusionWith the lack of communication regarding preferences in collaborating, HCPs and patients are likely to miss out on effective collaboration. Future studies should explore how views and preferences regarding HCP-patient collaboration can be bridged.

Introduction

Shared decision making (SDM) is advocated as the most appropriate approach for collaboration and medical decision making in care for patients with chronic diseases such as asthma.16 SDM is referred to as ‘an approach where clinicians and patients share the best available evidence when faced with the task of making decisions, and where patients are supported to consider options, to achieve informed preferences’.7 Notably, advocacy for SDM in policy and daily practice has been based on both moral and functional grounds. First, with the integration of patient preferences and values within SDM, patients’ autonomy is recognised and safeguarded.7 Second, SDM positively affects clinical and patient outcomes, such as asthma medication adherence and satisfaction with care.811 However, SDM is not always the preferred or feasible strategy for healthcare providers (HCPs) and patients.35 6 1215 Both HCPs and patients experience various barriers when it comes to SDM. HCPs experience lack of time, patients’ inability to participate or limiting clinical situations (eg, emergency situations).1416 Patients can assume their role to be passive rather than active or feel a lack of medical knowledge to participate in SDM.2 A discrepancy between patients and HCPs in recognising and experiencing each other’s preferred way of collaboration, and discrepancies between HCPs’ intentions in collaboration and their performance have also been reported.13 17 18 Interestingly, while also being a critical prerequisite for SDM and its effectiveness, preferences for collaboration between HCP and patient are typically not considered in both SDM models and effectiveness studies.1923 If and how preferences in collaboration approaches influence experienced SDM and how these preferences are recognised and managed is underexplored.

Severe asthma is an interesting area to explore preferences in this collaboration given its high clinical burden (ie, having more exacerbations and lower symptom control), the relatively frequent contact with HCPs and broad available therapeutic arsenal (including inhalers, biologics and bronchial thermoplasty).24 25 Also, the spectrum of available treatment options for this particular patient group is increasing, for example, multiple novel biologics.2629 As such, these patients face more treatment decisions compared with patients with some other chronic conditions and compared with the general asthma population.30 31 The severe asthma population differs from the general asthma population in having more intense and prolonged periods of asthmatic symptoms, for example, having more frequent and more severe exacerbations. We therefore aimed to investigate how HCPs’ and patients’ preferred collaboration styles in treatment decision making are experienced, discussed and managed during severe asthma consultations.

Methods

Study design

Phenomenological qualitative research approach using semi-structured interviews with HCPs followed by a focus group with patients with severe asthma. This study is reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for qualitative research (online supplemental materials I).32

Study population

HCPs treating patients with severe asthma and patients with severe asthma were included. Severe asthma was defined as ‘asthma which requires treatment with high dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) plus a second controller (and/or systemic corticosteroids) to prevent it from becoming ‘uncontrolled’, or which remains ‘uncontrolled’ despite this therapy’.33

Recruitment and inclusion criteria

Convenience sampling was used to recruit HCPs. Inclusion criteria were: pulmonologist or respiratory nurse/specialist/physician assistant, actively involved in treatment of severe asthma patients, and proficiency in Dutch language. Participants were contacted through severe asthma expertise centres throughout the Netherlands, the research team’s network and social media.

Patients with severe asthma were also selected through convenience sampling. They were approached via patient organisations, the study team’s network and social media. Patient inclusion criteria were doctor-diagnosed severe asthma, ≥18 years old and proficiency in Dutch language. Patients with cognitive impairment were excluded.

Sample size

Sample size for was determined by data saturation, as is common for qualitative research. An inductive thematic saturation method was used to determine data saturation.34 35 Interviews and interview analysis were performed simultaneously. Data saturation was considered to be reached when<5% new information (ie, new themes) was found in the last interview compared with previous interviews.36 After the interviews, a focus group with patients was conducted to compare HCPs’ perspectives with patients’ perspectives. For the focus group, we aimed for 6–11 participants.37

Setting

Face-to-face interviews with HCPs took place at a location preferred by the interviewee. If this was not possible, interviews were performed online. The interview was piloted with three HCPs and adjusted accordingly in content and duration (aiming for 30–45 min). Face-to-face interviews were audio recorded, online interviews video recorded. During the interviews, only the interviewer of the research team (MA) and the interviewee were present. For the focus group, there was one researcher as moderator (HMvdW) present and an additional study team member (MA) as observer and for notetaking and technical aspects. Field notes were made of all interviews. The focus group was conducted online via Microsoft Teams to limit patients’ health risks and inconvenience of travel.

Data collection

HCPs’ and patients’ characteristics were collected using REDCap Software. HCP characteristics included age, gender, education and work, communication or SDM training, cultural background, language preference and experienced overall degree of SDM. Patient characteristics included age, gender, cultural background, language preference, socioeconomic background, health literacy, clinical characteristics, relation with their HCP and experienced overall degree of SDM.

The Shared Decision Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q-9), containing nine items with answering scale from 0 to 5 was used to measure the degree of SDM.38 A higher total score means a higher degree of SDM. The Health Literacy Questionnaire (HL-EU-6) was used to measure health literacy,3942 with higher scores meaning higher degrees of health literacy. To measure the overall feeling of connection between patient and HCP, the Inclusion of Other in the Self-scale was used.43

Interview guide

The HCP interview topic guide (table 1) was based on relevant literature. In table 1, the various topics are shown. The literature on these topics has been used to guide the directions of the interview questions and sub-questions (online supplemental materials II). One introduction question concerning patients’ severe asthma care and consultation characteristics, and two closing questions for participant remarks and advice were added. The same topic guide and the HCP interview findings were used for the patient focus group questions. Interview recordings and transcripts were reviewed by a second researcher (HMvdW). Subsequently, the interview questions were adjusted and refined by a discussion among the study team. This process was repeated after the first three interviews.

Data analysis

Interviews were analysed using inductive thematic analysis. First, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by professional transcribers from an independent company. Transcripts were not returned to the participants. Subsequently, interview scripts were coded using Atlas.ti Software by two research team members (MA and HMvdW). The interview topic guide was used as a coding tree for the initial axial coding. Subsequently, an inductive approach was used to identify the overarching themes related to the research question. Themes and subthemes were coded openly.

Coding of the transcripts was performed by two researchers (MA, HMvdW) independently and subsequently discussed in order to identify the underlying themes. The identified themes were also discussed with the complete study team for feedback. Disagreements between or questions concerning the analysis were resolved with a third researcher or during study team meetings.

Study quality considerations

The researcher (MA) who conducted the interviews, focus group and analysis is a female sociologist and received training in qualitative research methods from the University of Oxford. She has working experience as a registered nurse in a pulmonary ward and previously studied the topic of collaboration and SDM. The other researcher (HMvdW) who conducted the focus group and analysis is also a female, works as a sociologist researcher (PhD) and teacher and had previous experience with qualitative research. This researcher had no experience working with asthma patients although she was also trained as a nurse. Participants received basic knowledge concerning the research topic and the researchers did not meet prior to the interview or focus group.

Data were pseudonymised using a subject identification code list.

Patient and public involvement

This research was co-produced with patients, carers or members of the public. For the development and design of the study, healthcare professionals and experts from an asthma patient association were involved. They informed the study team on the relevance of the study objectives and the interview topic guide. In addition, the patient association was involved in the recruitment of the participating patients.

Results

Interview and focus group characteristics

In total, 11 of the 14 HCPs approached participated in the interviews (mean duration: 44 min, range 36–57 min) conducted between June 2024 and October 2024. The average duration of the interviews was 44 min (range 36–57 min). Six interviews were conducted face-to-face. The other interviews were online. After the ninth interview, data saturation was reached. The 10th and 11th interviews were already planned and used to verify if indeed no new themes could be identified. The online focus group included six patients (duration: 1 hour and 50 min). One patient dropped out due to unforeseen circumstances and another patient dropped out due to sickness and treatment appointments.

Participant characteristics

Seven HCPs were pulmonologists, three were nurse specialists and one was a pulmonary nurse (table 2), with a mean age of 48.5 years, being mostly female and with significant experience in severe asthma treatment. Most HCPs received extracurricular education or training in communication skills or SDM. Overall, they rated themselves with an average of 33.3 out of 45 on the SDM-Q-9 scale.

Main themes

We identified five main themes concerning how and whether HCPs and patients experience, discuss and manage collaboration style preferences. The themes concerning HCPs’ and patients’ experiences were (1) overall preference for SDM, (2) ambiguity of SDM’s content, (3) variation in application of SDM and (4) friction between medical focus and patient autonomy in SDM. The fifth theme concerned the discussion and management of collaboration style preferences and was (5) limited metacommunication (figure 1). Important concepts explained

Overall preference for SDM

Both HCPs and patients stated unanimously to prefer SDM as collaboration style in making treatment decisions. The motivation for this preference varied; however, greatly between HCPs. Some HCPs stated SDM made them feel in control, while others stated it to be in line with their own preference if they would be patients. Several HCPs assumed that SDM would motivate patients and enhance their treatment adherence and health outcomes. Furthermore, one HCP stated that they did not have a clear motivation for the preference for SDM, and another HCP stated it is simply outdated to prefer a more paternalistic approach.

I think what is most important for shared decision-making is, above all, hopefully promoting therapy adherence and improving the health of the patient. (specialised nurse, male, early 60s)

I think that maybe it was different in earlier generations, but you are not there as the specialist who tells the patient what to do. I think that is no longer appropriate in this day and age. (pulmonologist, female, late 40s)

Patients had one aligned motivation for preferring SDM: the assurance of their autonomy and with this ownership and control of their own disease and treatment. Besides the importance of including their perspective, patients highlighted the equal importance of HCPs’ medical expertise and patients’ own expertise. Some patients added that this way they are more motivated and can more easily cope with side effects because the decision for a treatment is their own.

I actually have only one thing why I find that shared decision-making is so important. It is about the quality of my life. That is it. (patient, female, late 50s)

If I decide to do something together with my doctor and we really do it together. Then I can also bear the side effects better because it was a very conscious choice, then I can conform to that more easily than when it is imposed on me because I know very consciously that this is my choice. (patient, male, late 50s)

Ambiguity of SDM’s content

According to HCPs, core elements of their preferred collaboration style, that is, SDM, included medical substantive ones such as making the correct diagnosis, selecting relevant treatments and educating the patient. Other SDM elements concerned the HCP-patient relationship and include (i) the importance of getting to know the patient and (ii) levelling with the patient. A few other elements were prominent in all interviews: ‘informing the patient on treatment options’ and ‘discussion of the treatment’s pros and cons’. Less mentioned were ‘activating the patient’, ‘setting the consultation agenda’ and ‘formulating patients’ main health issues or goals’.

Those are also situations where you try to let the patient take the lead to some extent, as you work together to determine, for example, what would be a practical device for the patient to improve compliance and whether there are any side effects. (nurse specialist, female, early 40s)

I think it is just important that a patient is well informed. (pulmonologist, female, early 40s)

And it always starts with making them feel as comfortable as possible, so they feel free to share their story. And if I know that someone mentions a hobby, like saying they play football, I always ask: which club? (nurse specialist, male, early 60s)

Patients’ perspectives regarding the content of their preferred collaboration style, that is, SDM, were more uniform. In line with HCPs, patients found the discussion of treatment goals and its pros and cons essential to SDM. However, they put a stronger emphasis on the importance of listening to them as patients and getting to know them.

It is so important that you feel that you are being heard. (patient, male, late 50s)

I think it is important that the doctor knows which things in life are important to me and what I want to achieve. (patient, female, late 50s)

There was some variation in what SDM entails in practice. For example, some HCPs presented all treatment options while others made a pre-selection considering the patient’s personality and social context. Also, the interpretation of the discussion of treatment options varied from explaining the treatment advice for the patient to a dialogue with patients on how the different treatment options related to their preferences and situation. Regarding the final decision, some HCPs proposed their treatment advice and obtained patients’ agreement. Other HCPs provided all treatment options and let the patient make the final decision.

So then I present it and leave the decision to the patient, who then goes along with it. (nurse specialist, female, mid 40s)

I provide a proposal and what I think is very good, or what we as a group have determined is good, during a multidisciplinary meeting (MDM). (pulmonologist, male, early 60s)

The expected patient role by HCPs varied from a more active role (eg, preparing questions and goals) to not having any expectations. Besides these variations, the need for patients to be honest was often stated. HCPs’ own role was mainly described as the medical content (eg, on treatments) specialist. Patients found that HCPs, apart from using their medical expertise, were mainly expected to listen to the patient and take into account patients’ personality and experiences when discussing treatments. In contrast to HCPs, patients allocated themselves unanimously a more active role in the SDM process, for example, by providing all relevant personal information needed to provide advice and guidance.

My role is to ensure that I can provide the doctor with the information he or she needs as best as possible so that he or she can then coach me properly or inform me about the options available. (patient, female, late 40s)

HCPs have limited ideas about how their colleagues deploy SDM. Only one HCP stated to be aware that they differ in views and application of SDM. They stated that some of these differences could possibly be explained by the specific roles and tasks (eg, nurse vs pulmonologist). Concerning their own knowledge and performance, some HCPs expressed doubt on what SDM is and whether they execute it as supposed to. Overall, most HCPs demonstrated limited awareness regarding the different interpretations and content given to SDM, and regarding the differences between SDM, informed consent, motivational interviewing and informing.

One doctor does something different than another doctor. (pulmonologist, male, early 50s)

It has something to do with providing good information and I don't know if providing good information is always the same as shared decision making. (nurse specialist, male, early 60s)

Variation in experience and application of SDM

Overall, HCPs were under the impression that most patients cooperated within SDM when they initiated this. They also mentioned that most patients acted according to the role they envisaged for them. Patients stated that they had mixed experiences. One patient experienced an obvious increased use of SDM in consultations over the years, and another patient stated that they almost always experienced SDM nowadays.

Yes, in recent years. Within the early years, absolutely not. When I asked what is going to happen, what is going on, I simply did not get an answer, but that has changed in the last 10 years… (patient, female, mid 60s)

Both HCPs and patients mentioned that practice deviates from their own ideas about the content of and roles within SDM due to various barriers. HCPs’ barriers varied from their own mood to organisational factors and not feeling the necessity for SDM. Also, HCPs voiced preconditions such as patients being therapy adherent and honest in their intentions and goals.

I can sometimes be curt or blunt or a bit clumsy or… I'm not often like that, but it does happen sometimes. (nurse specialist, male, early 60s)

Because those are much more existential choices in oncology. (pulmonologist, male, early 60s)

Barriers experienced by patients in practising SDM were mostly related to HCPs’ communication skills and their relationship with HCPs (eg, using medical jargon, lack of feeling safe). Some patients stated not feeling heard and experiencing a power imbalance as reasons for not acting or being able to act as intended within SDM. Patients found trust in HCPs that they will not misuse their power in the relationship an important precondition for SDM.

That has to do with the character trait of the therapist, because with another therapist it looks different. (patient, female, mid 60s)

It also has to be a kind of safety indeed and what I notice is indeed that I sometimes still let myself be overruled. And yes, that something has actually already been decided, while you may have other thoughts yourself. (patient, female, mid 50s)

Generally, HCPs demonstrated limited awareness of the inconsistency between their intentions and performance. For example, preferring and working according to SDM, but still making the final treatment choice themselves or already deciding on the patient’s goals beforehand. No indications for this unawareness between intentions and performance were found for patients.

I don't let the patient make the choice. (pulmonologist, female, late 30s)

Look, where we want to go is usually quite clear. I think everyone generally agrees on that. It’s achieving better asthma control. (pulmonologist, female, early 50s)

Friction between medical focus and patient autonomy in SDM

Overall, HCPs demonstrated a strong emphasis on their medical expertise regarding their preferred collaboration style, that is, SDM. As such, HCPs also had a perception of ‘knowing what was best for the patient’ in terms of their health. Their application of this perception within SDM was evident in statements about, that is, preference for SDM, described content of SDM, barriers for SDM and the choice for treatment and the treatment goals.

And then making decisions together can ultimately lead to better asthma control. (nurse specialist, female, early 40s)

In principle, I make the decision about the final treatment, because I still think I have more knowledge. (pulmonologist, female, early 60s)

HCPs showed a feeling of conflict and discomfort within the execution of SDM because they felt medically responsible. Some HCPs made remarks feeling conflicted in collaborating when patients had different treatment goals or preferences for alternative treatments with possible worse health outcomes. Other HCPs simply mentioned it to be their responsibility to guide or correct patients into a better health.

Ultimately, a patient always decides for himself what he does. But if he doesn't know what the possible consequences of not doing it are, then… Yes, I certainly find that difficult with these severe asthma patients. (nurse specialist, female, early 40s)

These are all things I think to myself: for someone who doesn't have health problems, a kids' camp week already seems quite exhausting. Let alone if you're doing it during a lung attack. But well, in the end, it's her decision. And for now, I thought: I don't want to take that away from her either. (pulmonology care nurse, female, mid 30s)

Then I can of course really provide some direction, because sometimes I think: otherwise I really do the patients an injustice. (pulmonologist, female, late 40s)

Though some HCPs demonstrated the feeling of conflict or discomfort, it was not mentioned as a barrier for collaboration with patients or for preferring SDM. However, this was the case for patients. Patients identified HCPs’ medical focus and a paternalistic attitude as barriers for SDM. For example, they sometimes experienced that their personal knowledge and expertise were not considered to be equally important as the HCP’s medical knowledge within making the treatment decision. Moreover, patients expressed that occasionally it occurred that their goals or needs were dismissed by HCPs, and they therefore felt there was no room for SDM.

Sometimes you say something, and it is simply ignored. (patient, female, mid 60s)

I once asked the question, I want to get back on a racing bike, and the answer I got was, ‘well, maybe you should forget about that.’ (patient, female, late 50s)

Limited or no metacommunication

Most HCPs did not actively consider patients’ preference for a specific collaboration style in advance. They sometimes recognised or felt resistance towards SDM by patients’ verbal and/or non-verbal cues. However, they demonstrated not to identify patients’ collaboration style preferences with those cues. Instead, they identified deviance from their expected patient roles or patient-related barriers. For example, patients acting passively or not being proficient in the Dutch language served as an indication for not preferring SDM. The most frequently voiced indications were patients actively requesting them to make the treatment decision for them, and patients disagreeing with their treatment advice.

Actually, you just assume it. I don’t specifically address it during my consultations. (nurse specialist, female, early 40s)

Sometimes you can tell from a patient’s facial expression that they don’t understand anything at all, or that they don’t like it, or that they’re uninterested. (pulmonologist, female, late 40s)

Just tell me what to do, what to take. You're the doctor. You've studied for it, I haven't. (pulmonologist, male, early 50s)

Patients demonstrated a lack of consideration for HCP collaboration style preferences too. Patients considered shortcomings in SDM to be related to HCPs’ lack of communication skills or not feeling a good connection with the HCP. None of the patients stated that they explicitly declared their preference in how to collaborate or asked HCPs about their preference.

Sometimes it’s also due to miscommunication in word choice. The doctors tend to use too much jargon or technical language. I’m fairly familiar with it, but occasionally I come across something where I think, what on earth are you talking about? (patient, female, mid 60s)

With no identification of collaboration style preferences, HCPs and patients did not manage differences in preference for collaboration approaches. Given that SDM preference was not actively asked or agreed on, HCPs managed and adapted the style based on what they did identify during the consultation. How HCPs managed these varied from person to person. For example, taking over the treatment decision-making and persuading the patient into the ‘right’ treatment option was frequently stated strategies for managing disagreements with patients. In contrast, other HCPs stated that they would refuse to make the final decision. The utmost mentioned management strategy was ending collaboration with a patient. This was being applied by HCPs when the patient held on to a treatment that was deemed to be not medically meaningful, or when patients didn’t meet their preconditions for SDM, that is, not showing up at appointments.

So, saying it, explaining it very well, and just keep doing that and saying, as I’m saying it now: I really wish it for you, because these kinds of resources are specifically there to solve this kind of problems. (pulmonologist, female, late 40s)

And if those people insist on it, I think: this really doesn't fit. Then you should find another doctor who might be willing to give it to you. But I at least try to bring someone along with reasonable arguments. However, I’m not going to do something that I don't consider medically appropriate. (pulmonologist, female, late 30s)

The latter strategy of HCPs was also mentioned as a management strategy by patients. In case of feeling a lack of trust or not feeling good about a HCP in general, some patients with a preference for SDM stated they would switch to another physician. Other strategies of patients concerned with managing the pre-existing HCP-patient relationship. For example, patients aimed to show their HCP respect by acknowledging their greater medical expertise and need for this expertise to manage their disease. Of all participants, only one participant stated to once have explicitly discussed the collaboration process with the HCP after feeling friction in the collaboration process. Patients stressed that this response was not representative for most patients since this demands considerable self-confidence.

So I do look things up, but I present it as a kind of question, so it doesn't come across as too direct for a doctor, because I have the impression that they prefer it that way. But I do try not to sit in the doctor's chair, which is why I think I remain humble enough in that sense, so they don't feel attacked. (patient, female, mid 30s)

I noticed at some point that the collaboration just wasn't going well and that it was becoming more of a one-way street. So, I just made an appointment to talk about how we could work together. (patient, female, late 50s)

With HCPs stating how they managed differences in preference for collaboration styles, some HCPs demonstrated doubt about whether and how these strategies fitted into SDM. They questioned if they stayed true to their concept of SDM when managing preferences. Other HCPs did not give any indication of awareness that their strategies conflicted with their aforementioned meaning and content of SDM.

So I don't really know to what extent that is still shared decision making, because I just say openly and honestly what I think about something. (pulmonologist, male, early 50s)

However, I don't necessarily see that as not collaborating, because in essence, you are still working together. It's just that I am the one making the decision for the patient, and I think that is also a form of collaboration. (pulmonology care nurse, female, mid 30s)

Yes, I think in that case, you need to adopt a slightly different approach, and perhaps sometimes you need to say a bit more, like: you know, this really is important for you. And then you move a bit away from the collaborative approach and take on more of a role where you say: you really have to do this to avoid having an asthma attack. (pulmonologist, female, early 60s)

Discussion

Although both HCPs and patients with severe asthma demonstrated a strong preference for SDM, preferences for collaboration style are not or little considered nor being discussed during their consultations. In other words, there is no metacommunication on how to collaborate in making treatment decisions. As such, discrepancies in preferred and practiced collaboration approach are not well managed by HCPs and patients. Two major concerns were identified in this study. First, inconsistencies in SDM’s meaning, content and ideas on role division of collaboration style make it difficult for both HCPs and patients to recognise their own and the other party’s preferences. Second, HCPs demonstrated a strong overall medical focus and have—often unconsciously—a paternalistic mindset which seems to contribute to lack of metacommunication, limited application of true SDM and difficulties in identifying collaboration styles and preferences.

Interpretation of finding

As expected, a high preference for SDM was found in this study.6 17 18 Patients main motivation for SDM was found to be protecting their own autonomy, maintaining their own responsibility and receiving care that is most suitable for them. This fits the argumentation that SDM is of great importance and ethically the most appropriate approach.7 HCPs’ motivations, on the other hand, are mostly based on another argument to adopt SDM: the positive contribution SDM has to clinical and patient outcomes.811

HCPs’ and patients’ motivation for their collaboration approach preference, together with the existing ambiguity of SDM, seems to be important for understanding the different content given to SDM and preferring SDM or not. In this study, we found significant variation in the description and content of SDM mostly among HCPs, but also between HCPs and patients. With no less than 40 unique SDM models and 19 SDM measurement instruments existing, it is not surprising that there is great variation in content and execution of SDM.19 44 45 Subsequently, the meaning of—and preference for—SDM has been widely debated in the literature.6 18 44 A systematic review on patient preferences for SDM pointed out that a great variety of SDM measurements has been used.6 Also, having a preference for being (actively) involved in the decision-making process is often used interchangeably with having a preference for SDM.6 17 18 In line with previous studies, we found that these are not identical concepts.6 17 46 For example, patients stated they can still feel like it is SDM as long as they are actively involved in the decision-making process, but not making the final decision. In other words, making the final treatment decision together was not recognised as crucial to SDM by many participants, in contrast to several definitions and models of SDM.7 19 46 47

In this study, we also found indications that motivation for SDM can also play a role in explaining the content of SDM. With HCPs’ main motivation being to enhance the patient’s health, they put a strong focus on education on treatment options and discussing pros and cons in relation to clinical outcomes, as was also found previously.48 Patients on the other hand, stated respect for their autonomy as the main motivation for SDM. With this, being listened to, feeling trust and respecting their personal goals were stated to be essential as content of SDM.19 Notably, it was found that working towards patients’ personal goals is often mentioned more implicitly and referred to as considering ‘patient’s values’ or ‘treatment preferences’.19 49 As such, the discussion of the patients’ goals, preferences and values can be easily neglected by HCPs in both their meaning and practice of SDM.2 19 48 50 51

Patients typically had varying experiences with collaboration and SDM. Overall, patients noticed an increase in experienced degree of SDM over the years while HCPs stated they saw many actively involved patients. These phenomena can be explained by considering the increased attention to SDM and patient self-management in guidelines and medical education.1 52 53 It is evident that the different experiences are influenced by a lack of implementation and execution of SDM.2 14 16 52 54 Indeed, HCPs and patients both stated to deviate from their intentions due to well-known barriers (ie, time constraints, feeling to have too little medical knowledge, lack of trust) and these barriers are assumed to result in the suboptimal execution of SDM in daily practice.2 14 16 However, the still existing differences in experiences can also be partly explained by the aforementioned variations in meaning and content HCPs and patients give to SDM.

Along with the variety in experiences in collaboration and SDM, it is known that HCPs and patients have difficulties with self-assessment and recognising each other’s intentions for collaboration.6 17 18 Reaffirmed in this study, it was found that HCPs are inclined to unknowingly collaborate more according to other approaches than SDM. Also, it is challenging for HCPs to differentiate SDM from informed consent or motivational interviewing.18 We add to this knowledge with the finding that there seems to be overall limited consideration for collaboration style preferences and therefore (unjustly) interpretations are being made. For example, cultural differences may influence the preference for and the experienced degree of SDM, but cultural prejudices and cultural-related barriers (ie, language, health literacy) can and seem to be easily misinterpreted by HCPs as not preferring SDM.5557

Lastly, we found indications that the medical focus and paternalistic view of HCPs could undermine putting SDM in practice and limit metacommunication. The friction between medical focus and patient autonomy in SDM is no new finding.2 18 58 59 Studies have shown that although HCPs want to collaborate with patients and also often feel like they apply SDM, they do not.18 This was also one of the most prominent aspects of the inconsistency between HCPs’ intentions and performance of SDM found in this study. In various ways, HCPs demonstrated a sense of ‘knowing what is best for the patient’ even though they intended to apply SDM and stated to recognise the importance of the patients’ experiences and knowledge. The task of providing medical expertise and the feeling of responsibility for the patients’ health are found to contribute to HCPs’ paternalistic tendency.59 60 Patients recognised the importance of and need for HCPs’ medical knowledge, but also recognised HCPs’ paternalistic attitude as a fundamental barrier for SDM, in line with earlier work.2 The dismissal of patients’ goals was a prominently named indication for patients experiencing paternalism and a lack of SDM. Moreover, with the medical focus, HCPs seem to underestimate their role as communicator and collaborative partner and lack the needed skills for these roles.

Recommendations

Awareness of different collaboration styles and involvement methods can help both parties to voice their preferences more effectively. An overview of existing educational materials for HCPs and patients and how the different collaboration styles are presented is needed, alongside research into their effectiveness in enhancing collaboration during consultations. Practical tools, such as asking simple questions like ‘How would you like to be involved?’ and ‘What do you need to participate?’ at the start of consultations, could raise awareness about collaboration. While such questions are included in SDM tools like the SDM-Q-9 and OPTION Scale, they are not used as standard practice yet.38 61 Further refinement and input from health communication experts are needed to integrate these strategies effectively.

The original purpose of SDM—promoting patient autonomy—seems often overshadowed by its framing as a tool to improve clinical outcomes. This approach can reinforce paternalism, as health goals are often predetermined by HCPs, leaving little space for patient-driven objectives. To counter this, SDM training must (re)focus on safeguarding patient autonomy, ensuring that medical education emphasises this principle. Additionally, SDM barriers such as HCPs’ sense of responsibility for patients’ health must be addressed. Furthermore, placing greater emphasis on patients’ goals within SDM and training HCPs in ‘mentalizing’ could possibly help to overcome unconscious or unintended paternalism. Mentalising is the ability to engage with one’s own and others’ thoughts, emotions and desires.62 HCPs trained in mentalising demonstrate improved skills in identifying and clarifying patients’ needs and goals, leading to more meaningful collaboration and better outcomes.62 Also, the promotion and use of updated SDM models in which patients goals have a prominent place are recommended.50

Strength and limitations

A strength of this study is that both HCPs’ and patients’ perspectives were included. Since HCPs are assumed to be trained in and expected to be able to professionally collaborate with patients, the focus of this study was on HCPs. To not neglect the patient perspective, and to account for the time and resources available for this study, it was decided to perform one focus group with patients instead of also more labour-intensive individual interviews. Second, we targeted a specific but relevant population for whom new treatment options, requiring effective communication and collaboration between HCPs and patients, have recently emerged. Although the specificity of the subpopulation may limit generalisability to other chronic patient groups, the identified themes likely extend beyond severe asthma.

The study also has limitations. Selection bias may be present. Most participating HCPs had additional training in communication and SDM, potentially portraying a more favourable view of daily practice. Similarly, patients—recruited via a patient association—were likely more assertive and engaged than the general population.63 Information on the reasons for no response was not collected. The study design also had limitations. First, patients were interviewed in a group, unlike HCPs. Group settings may suppress conflicting opinions due to the majority effect, leading to more uniform responses.64 65 However, group dynamics can also be advantageous, as participants may help each other recall details or refine opinions.64 Finally, participants were asked about collaboration style preferences, introduced via three models: the paternalistic model, the informed model and SDM as a middle ground. While this framing simplified the discussion, it may have limited nuanced responses, excluding other approaches like the professional-as-agent model. To mitigate this, participants were encouraged to describe the content of their preferred style, yielding additional insights into their preferences.

Conclusion

When there is no communication about perspectives on and preferences in collaborating, HCPs and severe asthma patients are likely to miss out on effective collaboration. Subsequently, the benefits of good collaboration on the process of care and health outcomes are suboptimal. Even though most patients and HCPs seem to prefer SDM, there is an obvious need for both parties to learn how to identify and communicate their own and each other’s collaboration style preferences and expectations. Addressing HCPs’ feeling of responsibility and the often-unconscious paternalistic mindset is crucial for increasing understanding, implementing and experiencing SDM. Likewise, in SDM, more emphasis on joined-goal setting is crucial. HCPs should ‘take off that doctor’s coat at the table for a moment’ to get to those joined goals, as one patient stated. Most importantly, more research and guidance is needed into how different views on and preferences in collaboration can be bridged to improve collaboration in daily practice.
