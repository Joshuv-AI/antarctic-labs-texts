Phthalates exposure and longitudinal trajectories of body composition and regional fat distribution in children: shifting towards “apple-shaped” body and the role of physical activity
Authors: Jieyu Liu, Di Gao, Yanhui Li, Manman Chen, Xinli Song, Yi Zhang, Ruolin Wang, Jianuo Jiang, Zhiying Song, Xinxin Wang, Jun Ma, Yanhui Dong, Zhiyong Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effects of phthalate esters (PAEs) exposure on longitudinal changes in body composition and body fat distribution are not understood, especially in children. The study aimed to assess how PAEs influence the long-term changes in body composition (fat mass index (FMI), body fat percentage (BF%), fat-free mass index (FFMI), bone mineral density (BMD_Z), lean mass (LM) and total body water (TBW%)) and body regional fat distribution, and how physical activity moderate these effects.MethodsWe tracked 519 children in a child growth and development cohort from May 2017 to October 2020 in Xiamen city of China, measuring PAE metabolites at the outset and assessing body composition through bioelectrical impedance analysis at six visits. Generalized estimating equations were used for the relationship between PAEs and repeated body composition measurements. Group-based trajectory modeling and multinomial logistic regressions were employed to examine how PAEs exposure influenced the trajectories of body composition. Further, stratified analysis with an interaction term was used to investigate how physical activity influences such relationships.ResultsChildren (8.62 years) presented significant correlations between mono-n-butyl PAEs exposure with the “largest” trajectory of FMI, BF% and FFMI, with the ORs of 1.69, 1.70 and 2.17, respectively (P < 0.05). Whereas exposure to mono-iso-butyl phthalate was negatively linked to the “largest” trajectory of LM (OR = 0.51, 95%CI = 0.28, 0.93). Most PAE metabolites were significantly related to the “largest” trajectory of BF% in the trunk and upper limbs (P < 0.05), but moderate-to-high physical activities could relieve the increased BMD trajectories caused by mono-2-ethylhexyl PAEs exposure (P for interaction = 0.023) and decrease regional BF% in the trunk and upper limbs.ConclusionsPAEs exposure was linked to larger trajectories of fat mass, BF% and BMD, a smaller LM trajectory, and fat accumulation in the trunk and upper limbs, suggesting an “apple-shaped” body. Physical activity help relieve the increased BMD driven by PAEs and fat buildup in the upper body related to PAEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25738-x.

Background

Phthalate esters (PAEs), widespread environmental pollutants, are commonly found in our surroundings due to their persistence and resistance to breakdown. As a result, they are often detected in food products, even in low concentrations. Although legislative measures have reduced PAEs exposure to some degree [1], children still face considerable exposure due to behaviors like mouthing and crawling, especially when PAEs are present in toys.

As endocrine-disrupting “obesogens”, PAEs have been widely studied for their potential effects on childhood anthropometric measures [2, 3]. However, evidence from longitudinal studies regarding the association between PAEs exposure and childhood obesity remains inconsistent, with some studies suggesting positive associations [4], while others have found no significant relationship [5], or even inverse associations [6]. However, studies using bioelectrical impedance analysis to investigate the effects of PAEs exposure on childhood body composition have produced inconsistent findings, frequently examining only a narrow set of indicators. One research had indicated potential influences of gestational and childhood PAEs exposure on adolescent lean mass [7]; but another study did not detect similar significant relations between urinary PAEs and fat mass index in children aged 8–16 years [8]. Additionally, low molecular weight PAEs biomarkers have been cross-sectionally associated with lower bone mass in males [9], a finding echoed in a Boston-area cohort of girls [10]. The broader associations between childhood PAEs exposure and comprehensive body composition indicators, including lean mass, bone mineral density (BMD), and total body water, remain uncertain, as most studies rely on single-point assessments of body composition.

Childhood growth trajectories play a crucial role in influencing lifelong health and mortality risk, potentially providing more valuable clinical insights than isolated or aggregate measures [11]. Significantly, prenatal PAE metabolite levels have been associated with child adipose trajectories [12], but a cohesive understanding of their impact on other key body composition, such as lean mass, BMD and total body water, remains elusive. Longitudinal environmental health studies that fail to consider these growth trajectories may overlook important patterns linked to life stages that could increase the risk of cardiometabolic complications in the future.

Individuals with a “pear-shaped” body type generally accumulate fat in the hips, thighs, and lower abdomen, and tend to have a lower risk of metabolic complications. In contrast, those with an “apple-shaped” body type store fat in both subcutaneous and visceral areas above the waist, which is associated with a higher risk of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [13]. The distinct risk profiles of “apple” versus “pear” body types are believed to stem from the different functions and sensitivities of adipocytes in visceral fat compared to subcutaneous fat. However, the effects of environmental endocrine disruptors on the development of these body types are still under-researched. Given the potential cardiovascular risks linked with “apple-shaped” obesity in children, it’s crucial to further explore how these environmental factors may influence pediatric populations.

Our hypothesis posits that childhood exposure to environmental PAEs may disrupt body composition indicators under normal growth conditions. To investigate this, we aim to elucidate whether exposure to PAEs of child correlates with longitudinal alterations in various body composition indicators and their trajectories, while also assessing the differential effects on regional fat accumulation. Additionally, we will explore how physical activity might modify these relationships, highlighting its public health significance.

Materials and methods

Included study population

The child growth and development cohort, conducted from May 2017 to October 2020 in Xiamen city of China, aimed to investigate the impact of environmental factors on childhood BP. The initial phase, initiated in May 2017, involving a random selection of four schools, which included both the primary (grades 1–6) stages and middle school levels (grades 7–9) [14]. Subsequently, a baseline survey was launched in October 2017, enrolling students from two of these schools who were not exhibiting signs of puberty, yielding a cohort of 1430 participants. Among them, 829 children submitted their urine samples for PAEs detection. Participants who opted out of urine collection or lacked body composition measurements were excluded from the study. The comparison between included and excluded population was shown in Table S1. Over the course of the cohort, six follow-up visits were included during 2017–2020 to collect longitudinal data on body composition indicators from the children (Figure S1). The research was conducted in strict compliance with ethical standards, with approval obtained from the Medical Ethical Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (Number: IRB00001052-17026). We obtained the written informed consent from all participating students and their parents or legal guardians.

Anthropometric and body composition assessments

During the study, six follow-up visits, including the baseline, involved detailed anthropometric measurements for each participant. Children’s height and weight were recorded using a portable stadiometer (model TZG, China) and a lever-type weight scale (model RGT-140, China). To ensure accuracy and minimize variability, each parameter was measured twice per visit, with the average value used in the final analysis to improve data reliability.

Body composition was evaluated by trained medical professionals using a bioelectrical impedance bone densitometer (InBody770, Biospace, Seoul, Korea), adhering to standardized protocols and equipment guidelines. To standardize the assessment of children’s body composition, the fat mass index (FMI) was determined by quantifying the fat mass in kilograms—derived from the product of weight and body fat percentage (BF%)—divided by the square of the individual’s height (kg/m2). The fat-free mass (FFM) was calculated by subtracting BF% from 100%, then multiplying the results by the total body weight. Then the fat-free mass index (FFMI) was determined through dividing FFM by the square of person’s height (kg/m2). The percentage of lean mass (LM) was obtained by dividing lean mass by body weight. Bone mineral density (BMD) of the whole body (excluding the head) was expressed as age-, sex-, and ethnicity-specific BMD Z-scores [15]. Total body water percentage (%) was measured to reflect overall fluid content. In addition to whole-body measurements, regional body fat percentage was assessed for the trunk, upper limbs, and lower limbs to enable a detailed longitudinal analysis of body composition changes.

Measurements and detection of PAE metabolites

Quantification of PAE metabolites was carried out by collecting urine samples from children at baseline. The urine collection process is detailed in a separate publication [16]. We employed ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) to detect and quantify seven distinct PAE metabolites including mono-methyl phthalate (MMP), mono-ethyl phthalate (MEP), mono-n-butyl phthalate (MnBP), mono-iso-butyl phthalate (MiBP), mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP), mono-2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl phthalate (MEHHP), and mono-2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl phthalate (MEOHP). When performing tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis, an AB Sciex API 4000 LC/MS/MS system (Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) was employed. The ion source was set to Turbo Spray, and multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used for scanning to ensure sensitive and specific detection of target analytes. To minimize contamination from experimental equipment during the study, glassware was prioritized for use and rinsed with ultrapure water. Other plastic lab consumables—including pipette tips—were made of polypropylene, a material free of phthalates. To keep track of reagent quality and address unavoidable background contamination in the experimental process, each batch of experiments included reagent blanks as well as low- and high-concentration quality control samples. For metabolite concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD) (Table S2), a value of their corresponding LOD divided by √2 to these samples was assigned, effectively managing data below the detection threshold. PAEs were further classified into low molecular weight PAEs (LMWP), and high molecular weight PAEs (HMWP) exemplified by di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), according to their carbon atoms in alcohol chains as well as molecular weight (Table S3) [17, 18]. To provide a comprehensive assessment of PAE exposure, we calculated the molar sums of LMWP (∑LMWP), DEHP (∑DEHP), and total PAEs (∑PAEs) across their respective metabolites [19]. Moreover, since the health effects of PAEs exhibited clear sex-specific characteristics, we classified each PAE metabolite into low exposure (< 50th ) and high exposure (≥ 50th ) group according to the sex-specific 50th percentile limits of log-transformed values.

Physical activities definition and covariates

Self-administered questionnaires were used to gather data on physical activities and other covariates at each visit, as previously published [16, 20]. Participants reported the frequency (days) and duration (hours and minutes) of moderate to vigorous-intensity physical activities (MVPA) they engaged in over the past week following the guideline of International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [21], which is recommended for large-scaled population monitoring among adults aged 18–65 years [22]. Due to the open-ended questions of IPAQ-SF, and appropriate guidance would be given by project members. The average daily MVPA time was then calculated using the formula: average daily time = (total days of activity × total duration) / 7. According to the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, children are advised to accumulate at least 60 min of MVPA on average each day [23]; additionally, China’s Ministry of Education has urged schools to adjust their schedules to guarantee students one hour of MVPA each day [24]. Since most of the included children in the study did not meet the recommendation for MVPA, we classified individuals into three groups based on the data distribution and MVPA guidelines: low (< 30 min/day), moderate (30 ~ 60 min/day), high (≥ 60 min/day). Considering the small sample sizes of the “moderate” and “high” groups, we merged the “moderate” and the “high” group into a “moderate-to-high” group for the subsequent stratified analysis.

We distributed self-administered questionnaires to collect data on demographical and lifestyle factors. High paternal or maternal educational attainment was considered when either the father or mother had a “college degree, or a bachelor degree or above”, a high household income was defined as ≥ 8000 Chinese yuan (CNY) per month. Following the protocol detailed in the 2004 Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System Survey [25], the survey questionnaire assessed dietary habits. Specifically, the daily intake of vegetables, fruits, sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), and meat was computed using the formula: average daily intake=(servings on consumption days × consumption frequency)/7. Additionally, the frequency of dairy product consumption over the prior 7 days was documented. In an effort to obtain more accurate information, parents were responsible for completing questionnaires for children in grades 1–3. For children above the 4th grade, they filled out the questionnaires themselves as directed by their class teachers.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square (χ2) test and Student’s t-test were employed to examine the differences between each categorical and continuous variable. Logarithmic transformations were used to correct for the skewed distribution of the original PAE values. The transformed PAE values allowed for the examination of the distribution of their geometric means. Pearson’s correlation coefficient matrix was employed to assess the relationships among the different PAE metabolites. First, we used generalized estimating equation (GEE) models with linear regression to estimate the effects of PAEs on body composition changes. These models provided estimates of the changes in outcomes per doubling of exposure, along with 95% confidence intervals (CI), accounting for the repeated measurements within individuals. Generalized linear models and GEE models were further employed to evaluate the relations between exposure to PAEs and regional BF% including of both lower limbs, both upper limbs and trunk, considering both single time-point and repeated measures.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify distinct trajectories in body composition and regional body fat percentage. To address the left-skewed distribution of body composition indices, natural log transformations were applied to all measures before identifying these trajectories. The resulting trajectory assignments served as outcomes in the analysis. Log-transformed body composition indicators from each survey were included in the models to capture how trajectory groups of each indicator evolved with age. Multinomial logistic regressions were employed to analyze the relationships between childhood exposure to PAEs and trajectory patterns, with the “smallest trajectory” class serving as the reference category. The model included some confounders including sex, age at baseline visit (in analyses of body composition in which the outcome was not age-standardized), single-child, parental education attainment levels, monthly household income, puberty stage at baseline as well as physical activities, fruit consumption, vegetables consumption, sugar-sweetened beverages intakes, meat intakes, and the dairy products consumption. A Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) model was employed to thoroughly examine the effects of a mixture of PAE metabolites on body composition trajectories. We used a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler, running for 10,000 iterations, to obtain precise estimates of the model parameters. The group posterior inclusion probability (groupPIP) was used to assess the relative importance of variables across different groups, with values ranging from 0 to 1. Additionally, the conditional posterior inclusion probability (condPIP) measured the significance of specific components within these groups.

Given the significant impact of physical activities on body composition, we further investigated how physical activities influences the relationships between environmental chemical exposure and body composition indicators and their trajectories. This was done through a stratified analysis (low physical activities, moderate-to-high physical activities), with an interaction term involving physical activities included in the models to compute the p-value for the multiplicative interaction. The model was adjusted for sex, age at baseline visit (in analyses of body composition in which the outcome was not age-standardized), single-child, parental education attainment levels, monthly household income, puberty stage at baseline as well as fruit consumption, vegetables consumption, sugar-sweetened beverages intakes, meat intakes, and the dairy products consumption. Analysis was performed using STATA 15.0 (StataCorp, 2017), SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., NC, USA), and R software (version 4.0.3; developed by John Chambers and colleagues; located in Jersey City, NJ, USA). Significance tests were conducted at the 0.05 significance level using two-sided tests.

Results

Basic characteristics

During the study, a total of 519 children (49.13% boys) of a mean age of 8.62 years old participated in six follow-up visits. Nearly a third of the families, 29.29%, were single-child households. Over the entire follow-up period, 80.54% of the children experienced puberty. The educational attainment of parents was high, with over half holding a college or bachelor’s degree or higher (Table 1). A majority of the PAE metabolites were detected in over 80% of the population, with the exception of mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) (Table S4). mono-2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl phthalate (MEHHP) and mono-2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl phthalate (MEOHP) exhibited a notably high correlation with each other (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.93, Figure S2).

PAEs exposure longitudinally associated with body composition

Figure 1 illustrates the longitudinal associations between PAE metabolites and repeatedly measured body composition indicators using GEE models. Significant positive correlations were observed for MEHP and ∑DEHP with FMI, with estimates of 0.08 (95%CI = 0.04, 0.13), 0.19 (95%CI = 0.02, 0.35), respectively (P < 0.05). Additionally, exposure to mono-n-butyl phthalate (MnBP), MEHP, and ∑DEHP were found to significantly elevate BF%, with estimates of 0.74 (95%CI = 0.07, 1.40), 0.36 (95%CI = 0.17, 0.55), as well as 0.80 (95%CI = 0.15, 1.46) (P < 0.05). Furthermore, we found that exposure to MEHP was inversely related to percent LM (Estimates=-0.26, 95% CI=-0.48, -0.04, P < 0.05) and total body water% (Estimates=-0.27, 95% CI=-0.40, -0.13, P < 0.05). In terms of FFMI, significant increases were observed with exposure to all PAE metabolites (all P < 0.05) (Table S5). Trajectories of body composition in relation to PAEs exposure

Our study delineated three distinct trajectories of each body composition indicator during childhood; the body composition of school-age children tends to stabilize during this period (Fig. 2). Table S6 presents the summarized data regarding the likelihood of assignment to each class, while Table S7 presents the percentages of body composition trajectories by PAEs exposure group. Exposure to the MEHP was linked to a higher probability of being in the “largest” trajectory class for both FMI (OR = 1.90, 95%CI = 1.12, 3.22, P < 0.05) and BF% (OR = 2.05, 95%CI = 1.22, 3.46, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Similarly, exposure to ∑LMWP (OR = 1.84, 95%CI = 1.10, 3.07, P < 0.05) and the total PAEs (OR = 1.83, 95%CI = 1.10, 3.05, P < 0.05) also showed significant associations with the “largest” trajectory class of BF%. In contrast, MiBP exposure was inversely related to the odds of the “largest” trajectory class for percent LM (OR = 0.51, 95%CI = 0.28, 0.93, P < 0.05). Furthermore, when children were exposed to high concentrations of MEP, ∑LMWP and the total PAEs, the estimated ORs of the “largest” trajectory of BMD-Z was 1.78, 1.72 and 1.67 (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). The BKMR model yielded probabilities of inclusion, which are outlined in Table S8. Notably, our analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between PAE mixtures at or exceeding the 55th percentile and percent LM trajectory when compared to concentrations at the 50th percentile (P < 0.05), as illustrated in Figure S3.

Associations between PAEs exposure and regional BF%

For the trajectories of regional BF%, a “smallest”, a “medium”, and a “largest” class was also identified (Figure S4). Divided by PAEs exposure group, the percentages of trajectory groups are shown in Table S9. Similarly, Most PAE metabolites were significantly related to the “middle” or “largest” trajectory of BF% in the trunk and upper limbs (P < 0.05). However, the significant associations of most PAE metabolites with the “largest” trajectory of both lower limbs disappeared, in addition to MEHP exposure (Fig. 4). The age-by-effect interactions indicated no significant effects between PAEs exposure and ages on the long-term trajectories of developmental stage of regional body composition (Table S10).

We further investigated the differences in regional fat distribution in relation to MEHP exposure. At the baseline, MEHP exposure was significantly linked to increased regional BF%, particularly in the trunk and upper limbs with a steeper slope (Fig. 4). In each single visit, the effects on BF% of upper limbs and trunk were also more pronounced compared to both lower limbs (Fig. 4). GEE models revealed a positive relationship between MEHP exposure and repeated measured BF% in the trunk (Estimates = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.21, 0.63, P < 0.05) and upper limbs (right upper limb: Estimates = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.17, 0.55; left upper limb: Estimates = 0.37, 95% CI = 0.18, 0.55, P < 0.05) during follow-up, while the estimates for lower limbs were smaller (Figure S5).

Stratified analyses by physical activity group

The geometric mean of total PAEs was higher in group of more physical activities (Table S11). When adjusting for sex, age at baseline visit (in analyses of body composition in which the outcome was not age-standardized), single-child, parental education attainment levels, monthly household income, puberty stage at baseline as well as dietary habits, it should be noted from GEE models that in the group engaging in moderate-to-high physical activities, the effect sizes, of associations between MEHP exposure with longitudinal repeated measured increased whole BF% (as well as regional BF%), decreased percent LM, and increased BMD-Z, declined to the point of losing statistical significance, also, physical activities and MEHP exposure had a significant interaction effect on repeated measured BMD-Z (P for interaction = 0.025), high MEHP exposure and less physical activity can significantly increase BMD-Z (Figure S6). Stratified analysis for trajectories was also performed, with the prevalence rates detailed in Table S12. Similarly, the interaction analyses showed that increased physical activity could relieve the increased BMD trajectories driven by MEHP exposure (P for interaction = 0.023, Fig. 5, Table S13). Besides, more physical activities could alleviate almost all medium and larger trajectories of regional BF% in the trunk and upper limbs associated with MEHP exposure (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Our study marks a pioneering effort in comprehensively assessing the quantitative impact of childhood exposure to PAEs on the longitudinal developmental trajectories of various body compositions and regional fat distributions, as well as examining the role of physical activities. Exposure to PAEs was positively linked to a higher probability of following the “largest” trajectory for FMI, BF%, FFMI, and BMD-Z. Conversely, there was a negative link with the “largest” trajectory for percent LM. We firstly proposed that PAEs exposure primarily led to fat accumulation in the trunk and upper limbs, suggesting an “apple-shaped” body. Enhancing physical activity could relieve the increased BMD driven by MEHP exposure and reduce fat buildup in the upper body resulting from PAEs exposure. There is a critical need for precise intervention action focused on limiting exposure to PAE and encouraging activities especially for preventing cardiovascular damage caused by “apple-shaped” body.

Existing literature on the relationship between PAE exposure and childhood adiposity presents inconsistent findings. For instance, some studies have reported a negative correlation between prenatal PAEs exposure and children’s BMI Z-scores [12], while others have found no significant associations between either prenatal or postnatal PAEs exposure and obesity [26]. These discrepancies might be due to differences in biomarker levels, the timing of exposure during development, or the types of metabolites studied. To date, only one study has investigated how PAEs exposure influences adiposity trajectories in childhood. This research found that higher levels of prenatal di-isononyl phthalate metabolites were linked to a higher likelihood of being classified in the “low-high” adiposity category, though this effect was specific to prenatal exposure [12]. Our results revealed a positive association between childhood PAEs exposure and body fat percentage trajectories in the “largest” class. This is likely because PAEs can influence peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), leading to increased visceral fat and overall adipose tissue [27].

Previous research on PAEs exposure and body composition has seldom addressed its impact on children’s lean mass. However, a recent cross-sectional study found a link between higher urinary PAE metabolite concentrations and decreased lean mass [28]. One longitudinal cohort study identified a notable association between higher prenatal PAEs exposure and a decrease in the skeletal muscle index, especially in female children [26]. This finding aligns with our findings. The observed reduction in percent lean mass could be attributed to the anti-androgenic effects of PAEs, as androgens play a crucial role in muscle development [29]. Besides, PAEs can decrease insulin-like growth factor-1, a key promoter of muscle growth [30], and potentially modulate PPAR leading to muscle protein loss [31].

The impact of environmental endocrine disruptors such as PAEs on bone health has been investigated in a limited number of studies, with somewhat inconsistent findings. For instance, some research has reported that low molecular weight PAEs are associated with higher bone mineral content in 6-year-old children, while exposure to di-n-octylphthalate has been linked to lower bone mineral content in 10-year-olds [32]. Additionally, positive correlations between maternal PAE biomarker levels and offspring BMD have been documented [33], and PAE exposure may also be associated with accelerated bone maturation in girls [34]. Collectively, these findings suggest that PAEs may disrupt the systemic hormonal regulation underlying bone remodeling and skeletal formation processes [35]. For example, higher estrogen levels, which PAEs can mimic, are known to accelerate bone maturation [36]. It is widely recognized that bone maturation and increased BMD exhibit a natural synchrony during adolescence. Hormones that promote bone maturation, such as estrogen and growth hormone, simultaneously stimulate bone mineralization (the deposition of calcium and phosphorus), leading to a temporary increase in BMD [37]. Given estrogen’s well-documented role in promoting epiphyseal plate closure—a key hallmark of bone maturation—the estrogenic activity of PAEs may be closely tied to increased BMD. This potential association stems from PAEs’ shared endocrine-disrupting mechanism: by binding to estrogen receptors in skeletal tissues, they mimic endogenous estrogen, thereby interfering with normal bone development and metabolism.

The significant correlation between PAEs exposure and reduced total body water suggested a potential impact on body fluid levels. Some studies have found short-term exposure to particulate matter to be notably linked with increased body water [38, 39], while others, have not observed such significant relationships [40]. The mechanisms underlying these associations are not yet fully understood. PAEs exposure might increase oxidative stress, potentially disrupting ion channels or gradients in cell membranes and affecting osmotic pressure [41]; alternatively, .PAEs may act as cytotoxic agents which could subsequently raise osmotic pressure [42].

Our study further revealed that PAE-induced fat accumulation was predominantly concentrated in the trunk and upper limbs, resembling an “apple-shaped” body configuration. Previous research has shown that individuals with this body shape, marked by increased abdominal fat, face a higher risk of developing insulin resistance [43]. This phenomenon may be linked to the unique physiological characteristics of subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissues. A study involving 100 Mexican adolescents showed that exposure to heavy metals could result in the accumulation of both subcutaneous and visceral fat [44], further, elevated trunk fat levels were associated with an unfavorable lipid profile, while higher levels of fat in the leg region indicated a more favorable lipid profile [45].

Enhanced physical activity has the potential to relieve the increased BMD driven by PAE exposure and improve the distribution of regional body fat. Mechanistically, PA exerts a dual regulatory effect on skeletal homeostasis: on one hand, mechanical loading from exercise stimulates bone remodeling and enhances osteoblastic activity, which helps maintain bone quality without excessive mineral deposition; on the other hand, it modulates the endocrine microenvironment by reducing the sensitivity of bone tissues to exogenous estrogenic disruptors like PAEs. Collectively, these findings indicate that physical activity offers a feasible non-pharmacological intervention to relieve the increased BMD trajectories driven by PAEs during critical developmental periods. Additionally, increased vigorous physical activity was linked to reduced levels of visceral fat in adolescents [46]. It is acknowledged that adipose tissue in the gluteofemoral region can promote a protective adipokine profile by releasing higher levels of beneficial adipokines [47]. Consequently, engaging in physical activities is essential for maintaining a healthy body composition and preventing potential future cardiovascular damage.

We offer an in-depth analysis of how childhood exposure to PAEs affects long-term changes in different body composition metrics. Using a bioelectrical impedance bone densitometer, we assessed the effects of PAEs on FMI, BF%, FFMI, percent LM, BMD-Z, and total body water% across multiple visits, and explored the patterns of regional body fat accumulation over time. Nonetheless, there are some limitations to consider. One limitation is the reliance on a single urine test to assess chemical exposure, which captures only a short-term snapshot. Employing average levels from multiple time points could enhance the precision and accuracy of exposure estimation. Second, while we assessed regional fat distribution, further research is necessary to define body types using waist-to-hip ratio indices, enabling a more precise identification of an “apple-shaped” or “pear-shaped” body. Third, the reliance on self-reported physical activity data may introduce bias; employing age-specific accelerometer data could offer a more accurate assessment. In addition, we used the IPAQ-SF to assess physical activity, which might not be completely appropriate for pediatric population, lead to measurement error and recall bias. However, acceptable reliability and validity of the IPAQ-SF were detected. Fourth, some other important dietary variables—such as nutrient intake and energy intake—are closely associated with body composition. However, these variables were not included in the survey of this study. Future research requires more rigorous cohort designs to validate the findings of this study. Last but not least, it is reported that the long-term trajectory of body composition shifts from a relatively higher proportion of fat mass in early childhood to increased muscle mass accumulation during puberty, accompanied by age-specific fluctuations in BMD and lean body mass. However, due to the narrow age range of the included child population—limited to children from only two primary schools—this study may fail to reflect the complete cycle of children’s body composition growth.

Conclusions

Our study reveals a significant connection between PAEs exposure and long-term changes in body composition, underscoring its broader public health and environmental policy implications. Higher PAEs levels were notably linked to the most pronounced increases in FMI, BF%, FFMI, and BMD-Z, while showing a negative association with the largest trajectory for percent LM. Additionally, exposure to most PAE metabolites was associated with increased fat accumulation in the upper trunk and limbs, suggesting an “apple-shaped” body. Nonetheless, enhanced physical activity has the potential to relieve the increased BMD driven by MEHP exposure and reduce upper body’s fat accumulation associated with PAEs exposure. This research highlights the urgent need for a robust public health strategy that includes strict environmental regulations, promotes lifestyle changes for healthier habits, and implements educational initiatives to raise awareness, particularly regarding the cardiovascular risks associated with an “apple-shaped” body.
