Comparison of Structure Preserving Schemes for the Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes Equations with Degenerate Mobility and Adaptive Mesh Refinement Journal: Journal of Computational Physics
Jimmy Kornelije Gunnarsson, Robert Klöfkorn

Abstract
The Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes (CHNS) system utilizes a diffusive phase-field for interface tracking of multi-phase fluid flows. Recently structure preserving methods for CHNS have moved into focus to construct numerical schemes that, for example, are mass conservative or obey initial bounds of the phase-field variable. In this work decoupled implicit-explicit formulations based on the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methodology are considered and compared to existing schemes from the literature. For the fluid flow a standard continuous Galerkin approach is applied. An adaptive conforming grid is utilized to further draw computational focus on the interface regions, while coarser meshes are utilized around pure phases. All presented methods are compared against each other in terms of bound preservation, mass conservation, and energy dissipation for different examples found in the literature, including a classical rising droplet problem.



1
Introduction

At its core, the Cahn-Hilliard (CH) equation
employs a phase-field variable
\psi\in[-1,1]
, representing the local concentration of
components in a binary mixture, to track the evolution of diffuse interfaces
between phases
[
14
]
. Contrary to classical Level-Set
[
43
]
or Volume-of-Fluid
methods
[
42
]
, the diffuse-interface approach eliminates the need for explicit
interface tracking making it particularly adept at handling complex topological
changes during phase separation and evolution of interfaces.
For fluid-dynamical applications, there exist coupling schemes
with the Navier-Stokes (NS) equations to simulate multiphase flows
[
31
,
37
]
.

This coupling was first discussed in
[
31
]
where
the "model H" representation was used. Although this proposed model lacks thermodynamic
consistency and treatments of unequal fluid densities
[
47
]
,
this has since the turn of the century been studied by sophisticated
and careful analysis to provide proper physical representations
[
40
,
1
,
13
,
46
,
20
]
.
An overall review of these models can be found in
[
47
]
.

Recently, structure preserving methods for such binary fluid flows based on the CH and CHNS quations
have been of particular interest in ensuring physical consistency
when using a phase field method
[
2
,
4
,
29
,
38
]
.
These works typically consider the following properties (or a subset of these):

1.

Energy Dissipation
: The free energy of the system decreases monotonically,
aligning with the second law of thermodynamics and ensuring that the system evolves toward a lower-energy steady state.

2.

Mass Conservation
: The phase-field mass is constant in the domain over the entire time interval.

3.

Bound Preservation
: The phase-field takes values within a
given interval, typically
[-1,1]
, ensuring there are unphysical over-or undershoots in concentration.

The interest to study schemes that fulfill all three of these properties
stems from experimental applications where numerical results are used to
train and improve image reconstruction algorithms. For example, in
X-ray multi-projection imaging (XMPI)
[
55
,
51
]
such simulations serve as
ground truth to evaluate the quality of the reconstruction algorithm which tends
to break down when the phase-field is out-of-bounds.

While most schemes presented in the literature, even if recently introduced
[
16
]
,
usually obey energy dissipation, there is considerable less work on mass conservation or boundedness of the phase
field, even though theoretical results on boundedness of the
phase-field variable were proven in
[
21
]
for a degenerate mobility function
M:\psi\to 1-\psi^{2}
.
For other mobility formulations, especially when
M
is non-degenerate,
one often encounters over- or undershoots which in conjunction with fluid flow
based on the NS equations may lead to unphysical densities.

Recent efforts to address such challenges have increasingly turned to Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods,
which offer several advantages over classical Finite Element methods (FEM), with early attempts published
in
[
27
,
49
]
. DG methods excel due to the addition of fluxes and the incorporation of upwind-like techniques combined with
limiting strategies to facilitate boundedness as has been studied in
[
2
,
4
,
39
,
33
]
. Existing work on structure-preserving DG schemes for CH
can be categorized into schemes that employ projections onto piecewise
constant spaces
[
2
,
4
,
29
]
, or schemes that use an
auxiliary variable
[
39
,
33
,
50
]
. A third category are methods that
rely on limiters as a post-process correction to obtain structure
preservation
[
38
,
25
]
. However, there is currently a lack
of comparison of these methods and the settings in which they are optimal.

In this paper we provide a thorough comparison in terms of the three highlighted
structure preservation principles for schemes that fulfill the following
requirements:

1.

The considered scheme is easy to implement and can be expressed in the Unified Form Language (UFL)
[
9
]
.
For example, we did not find a way to easily incorporate the scheme suggested in
[
25
]

in our UFL based code
[
19
,
17
]
. Otherwise this would have been an interesting candidate.
But since UFL is used by many users of various simulation software packages we think this
is a reasonable constraint to consider.

2.

The scheme fulfills at least two of the three structure preservation properties.
This is the case for most schemes, in particular for standard Finite
Element schemes if implemented carefully.

3.

The scheme can be used in combination with parallel-adaptive grid refinement.
This is the case for almost all schemes available in
the literature.

4.

The scheme works well in a split-setting, i.e. where CH and NS are solved
separately. While fully coupled schemes yield more accurate results they are also more complicated
to implement and precondition. This seems reasonable since many application packages are usually
utilizing a split approach.

In addition, we provide improvements for previously suggested DG formulations,
for example, a weighted DG method to overcome difficulties when the phase-field approaches the bounds,
i.e.
|\psi|\to 1
. All methods presented in this paper are tested and compared
in terms of energy dissipation, mass conservation, and boundedness
for a variety of problems ranging from standard test problem found in the literature
to problems closer to applications.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
In Section
2
a comprehensive overview of the
CHNS equation system is provided, including its motivation from thermodynamic
principles, its physical interpretation, and the role of degenerate mobility
in enforcing the bound preservation. In Section
3
the discretization
spatial and temporal are presented. This section includes
proofs on mass conservation, bound preservation, and energy consistency in the
discrete setting. In Section
4
an extensive set of numerical
experiments is presented, ranging from benchmark problems with analytical solutions to complex,
application-driven scenarios. These tests demonstrate the scheme’s robustness,
accuracy, and ability to handle sharp interfaces and long-time dynamics, with
comparisons to existing methods. We then conclude the paper with a discussion
of the results presented in this study,
highlighting the advantages of the proposed methods and its potential
to advance phase-field modelling for systems of higher complexity. Lastly, we
emphasis further improvements.
An Appendix contains the UFL forms of the suggested methods which should make it
easy for other user of the UFL language to implement the suggested methods.

2.1
Governing equations

For the dynamics of the phase-field

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{u}\psi-\frac{\omega\sigma}{\varepsilon}M(\psi)\nabla\upsilon\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle\upsilon-W^{\prime}(\psi)+\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\psi

For Eqs. (

When the velocity field

is obtained from the NS equations. Following the NS formulation
in
[
1

with a source term

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}\rho(\psi))+\nabla\cdot(\rho(\psi)\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{J}(\psi,\upsilon))+\nabla\cdot(P\mathbb{I}-2\mu(\psi)D(\boldsymbol{u}))

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T},

(5)

where
D(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\right)

,

is the pressure,
\mathbb{I}
is the
d\times d
identity tensor, and the following phase-field-dependent quantities are present:

\rho(\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\big((1+\psi)\rho_{1}+(1-\psi)\rho_{2}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\big((1+\psi)\mu_{1}+(1-\psi)\mu_{2}\big),

(6)

correspond to the volume-averaged density and viscosity, respectively, and

\boldsymbol{J}(\psi,\upsilon):=\frac{\omega\sigma(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})}{2\varepsilon}M(\psi)\nabla\upsilon,

(7)

is the mass flux. For practical purposes we denote the overall physical mass
of the fluid mixture
at a given

m_{\rho}(t):=\int_{\Omega}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)dx,\quad t\in[0,T].

(8)

Theorem 2.1

(Equivalence to physical mass diffusion)
.

The function

corresponds to the diffusion of the conserved physical
mass

in time as a
consequence of the Eqs.(
1
)—(
2
) if
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=0

Proof.

The
integrand of Eq. (

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\psi+\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{u}\psi-\frac{\omega\sigma}{\varepsilon}M(\psi)\nabla\upsilon\right)dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\psi dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\mathbf{u}\psi-\frac{\omega\sigma}{\varepsilon}M(\psi)\nabla\upsilon\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}ds,

(9)

due to the boundary conditions of
\upsilon
in Eq. (
4
) and with
the assumption that
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
we obtain that

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\psi=0
. If
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
then there is no density
exchange, thus we assume that
\rho_{1}>\rho_{2}

\partial_{t}\rho(\psi)+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\rho(\psi)=-\nabla\cdot J,

in Eqs. (

∎

2.2
Physical laws

From Eqs. (

obtained, for example,

Definition 2.1

(Phase-field mass)
.

The mass
m_{\psi}
of the phase-field
\psi
at a given time
t\in[0,T]
is defined as

m_{\psi}(t):=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\psi(x,t)dx}{|\Omega|}.

(22)

with
|\Omega|=\int_{\Omega}1dx
, then for
\psi\in[-1,1]
we obtain
m_{\psi}\in[-1,1]
.

Remark 2.3

(Mass conservation)
.

As a direct consequence of Theorem
2.1
we obtain that the phase-field

\psi
in Eq. (
17
) is mass conservative in the sense that

m_{\psi}(t)=m_{\psi}(0)\quad\forall t\in(0,T].

(23)

Ultimately, this is equivalent to the physical mass
m_{\rho}
from (
8
)
being conserved over time, i.e.
m_{\rho}(t)=m_{\rho}(0)\ \forall t\in(0,T]
.

Furthermore, we state that energy dissipation is present.

Theorem 2.2

(Energy dissipation)
.

The non-dimensional free energy functional

\mathcal{E}_{tot}[\psi,\boldsymbol{u}]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho(\psi)|\mathbf{u}|^{2}+\frac{1}{CnWe}\left(W(\psi)+\frac{1}{2}Cn^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{Fr}\rho(\psi)\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}\cdot\mathbf{x}\,dx

(24)

is dissipative. The non-dimensional dissipation rate is:

\frac{d\mathcal{E}_{tot}(t)}{dt}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{M(\psi)|\nabla\upsilon|^{2}}{CnWePe}+\frac{\mu(\psi)}{Re}|D(\mathbf{u})|^{2}\right)dx\leq 0,\quad\forall t\in(0,T].

(25)

Proof.

Taking the time derivative of
\mathcal{E}_{tot}
in Eq. (
24
) and
using the CHNS equations (
18
), along with the boundary conditions
in (
4
) and Remark
2.1
yields
the result.
∎

Theorem 2.3

(Bound preservation,
[
21
]
)
.

Suppose that the boundary
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz continuous and introduce
the essential supremum norm

||\psi(\mathbf{x},t)||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}|\psi(\mathbf{x},t)|,\quad t\in[0,T].

(26)

The degenerate mobility function
M:\psi\to 1-\psi^{2}
and energy density
W

guarantee bound-preservation in the weak sense for
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
following Eq. (
17
):

||\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)||_{\infty}\leq 1,\quad\forall t\in(0,T],

(27)

given that
||\psi(\boldsymbol{x},0)||_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Proof.

A proof is given in
[
21
, Theorem 1]

for the non-advective CH equations and
[
21
, Theorem 5]
for
the advective CH and CHNS equations.
∎

3
Discretization

Let the spatial domain
\Omega
be partitioned into a union of
M
non-intersecting
elements
K
forming a mesh
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\cup_{i=1}^{M}K_{i}
. Then we denote
by
\Gamma_{i}
with unit normal
\boldsymbol{n}
the set of all intersections between
two
elements of the grid
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, and the set of all
intersections, also with the boundary of the domain
\Omega
, is denoted by

\Gamma
.
For each element
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}

we define the local mesh width as

h_{K}:=\text{diam}(K)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in K}||\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}||.

(29)

For an intersection
e\in\Gamma
we denote the adjacent elements with
K^{-}_{e}
and

K^{+}_{e}
(
K^{-}_{e}=K^{+}_{e}
for
e\in\Gamma\cap\Gamma_{i}
) and
define

h_{e}:=\frac{\min\{|K^{-}_{e}|,|K^{+}_{e}|\}}{|e|}\quad\forall e\in\Gamma.

(30)

The global mesh width is then defined as
h=\max_{e\in\Gamma}h_{e}
.
For our discretization methods we require, that the mesh has a certain
regularity.

Remark 3.1

(Regularity)
.

If the mesh
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
is a tessellation wherein all
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
are regular quadrilaterals or triangles then there exists a constant
C_{{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}>1
only dependent on the shape of
K
and dimension
d
of
\Omega
such that

\min\{h_{K^{+}_{e}},h_{K^{-}_{e}}\}\leq C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{e}\quad\forall e\in\Gamma.

(31)

For instance when
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have for quadrilaterals a sharp constant
C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\sqrt{2}
, and for isosceles right triangles we have a sharp constant
C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=4
.

3.2
Discontinuous Galerkin formulation

In this section we consider an equal order DG formulation for the broken approximation

We formulate some auxiliary functions

discretize the Laplacian in Eq. (

a:V_{h,\psi}\times V_{h,\upsilon}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle a(\psi_{h},\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\nabla\psi_{h}\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx

\displaystyle+\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{h_{H}}[{\psi_{h}}][{\xi}]-\{{\nabla\psi_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}[{\xi}]-\theta\{{\nabla\xi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}[{\psi_{h}}]\right)\,ds

(36)

where

h_{H}:=\frac{2h_{K_{e}^{-}}h_{K_{e}^{+}}}{h_{K_{e}^{-}}+h_{K_{e}^{+}}}

(37)

is the harmonic average of the local grid-width
h_{K}
over the intersection
e
,
and the penalty parameter
\eta_{1}
satisfies (cf.
[
5
]
):

\eta_{1}\geq\frac{k(k+d-1)(\theta+1)^{2}}{4},

(38)

and
\theta\in[-1,1]
denotes the IPG type where
\theta=1
is used for
Symmetric IPG (SIPG),
\theta=0
corresponds to Incomplete IPG (IIPG), and
finally
\theta=-1
for the Non-symmetric IPG (NIPG) (see for example
[
5
]
).
A SIPG scheme for degenerate mobility was previously presented in
[
39
]

with a common factor
M(\{{\psi_{h}}\})
as its treatment for the mobility for
the consistency term. To lift the constraint on averaging over the degenerate
mobility
M
for the consistency terms, we instead consider a generalization
by only requiring symmetry for the second and third argument in the trilinear form

b:[0,1]\times V_{h,\upsilon}\times V_{h,\psi}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle b(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}M(\psi_{h})\,\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx+\sum_{e\in\Gamma}\int_{e}\frac{\eta\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))}{h_{H}}[{\upsilon_{h}}][{\varphi}]

\displaystyle-\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}\,[{\varphi}]-\theta\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\varphi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}\,[{\upsilon_{h}}]ds,

(39)

where
\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))
is understood to be related to a diffusion flux over the intersection
e
.
Moreover, we can form the bilinear form
\tilde{b}(\cdot,\cdot)=b(M(\psi_{h}),\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 3.1

(Trace inequality,
[
45
]
)
.

For each intersection
e\in\Gamma_{i}
shared by two elements
K^{-}_{e}
and
K^{+}_{e}

there exists a constant
C_{t}>0
independent of the mesh width
h
such that
for all
\varphi_{h}\in V_{h}
and
e\in\Gamma_{i}
the following inequality holds:

||\nabla\varphi_{h}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}\leq\frac{|e|}{|K_{e}^{\pm}|}C_{t},||\nabla\varphi_{h}||_{L^{2}(K_{e}^{\pm})}^{2},

(40)

where, in the special case of
K_{e}
being a quadrilateral or triangle,
C_{t}=\frac{k(k+d-1)}{d}
(see further treatments and formulations in
[
5
]
and
[
22
]
).

Remark 3.2

(Equivalence of local mesh width)
.

Since we only consider the case of regular quadrilateral and triangular tessellations on the mesh
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, we have the inequality:

\frac{|e|}{\min\{|K^{+}_{e}|,|K^{-}_{e}|\}}\leq C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\max\{h^{-1}_{K^{+}_{e}},h^{-1}_{K^{-}_{e}}\},\quad\forall e\in\Gamma_{i},

(41)

following a similar argument as in Rem.
3.1
. Moreover, we observe that
h_{H}\leq 2\min\{h_{K^{+}_{e}},h_{K^{-}_{e}}\}
is equivalent to
\max\{h^{-1}_{K^{+}_{e}},h^{-1}_{K^{-}_{e}}\}\leq 2h^{-1}_{H}
. Therefore,
Lemma
3.1
also holds by replacing
\frac{|e|}{|K_{e}|}
with
2C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}h^{-1}_{H}
in the inequality over each edge
e\in\Gamma_{i}
in Eq.(
40
).

For the following theorem we derive coercivity of the
bilinear form
\tilde{b}(\cdot,\cdot)=b(M(\psi_{h}),\cdot,\cdot)

from Eq. (
39
). In particular, we can not rely on the estimate
in

[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
since it assumes a piece-wise constant diffusion
over
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
. To ensure that coercivity holds,
we require that the mobility
M(\psi_{h})
is strictly positive, which can be unconditionally
achieved by regularization as
M_{\delta}(\psi_{h})=\max\{M(\psi_{h}),\delta\}
for
some small
\delta>0
(we found that
\delta=10^{-20}
is sufficient in practice). For the remainder of this paper we will suppress the
subscript
\delta
for brevity.

Theorem 3.2

(Coercivity)
.

The bilinear operator
\tilde{b}(\cdot,\cdot)=b(M(\psi_{h}),\cdot,\cdot)
from Eq. (
39
)
is coercive if
M(\phi_{h})>0
given a sufficiently large penalty parameter
\eta>0
independent of the mobility
M(\psi_{h})
and where
\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))
is dependent on
M(\psi_{h})
.

Proof.

We introduce the DG semi-norm:

||\upsilon_{h}||^{2}_{DG}:=||\sqrt{M(\psi_{h})}\nabla\upsilon_{h}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}^{2}+\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}\frac{\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))}{h_{H}}[{\upsilon_{h}}]^{2}ds,

(42)

and consider the inequality:

b(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\upsilon_{h})\geq\tilde{C}||\upsilon_{h}||^{2}_{DG},\quad\forall\upsilon_{h}\in V_{h,\upsilon},

(43)

for some
\tilde{C}>0
. For the trilinear form Eq. (
39
) we have:

\displaystyle b(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\upsilon_{h})

\displaystyle=||\sqrt{M(\psi_{h})}\nabla\upsilon_{h}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}^{2}+\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}\frac{\eta\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))}{h_{H}}[{\upsilon_{h}}]^{2}ds

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}(1+\theta)\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}[{\upsilon_{h}}]ds.

Firstly, we absolutely estimate the expression
\int_{e}\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}[{\upsilon_{h}}]ds
. By using the triangle inequality
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we obtain:

\left|\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}[{\upsilon_{h}}]ds\right|\leq\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}||\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}||_{L^{2}(e)}||[{\upsilon_{h}}]||_{L^{2}(e)},

(44)

then from the Young inequality we get the intersection-wise estimate:

\displaystyle 2||\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}||_{L^{2}(e)}||[{\upsilon_{h}}]||_{L^{2}(e)}

\displaystyle\leq h_{H}\epsilon||\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\epsilon h_{H}}||[{\upsilon_{h}}]||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2},

for an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
. Again, using the triangle inequality:

4||\{{M(\psi_{h})\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}\leq||M(\psi_{h}^{+})\nabla\upsilon_{h}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}+||M(\psi_{h}^{-})\nabla\upsilon_{h}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2},

(45)

moreover

||M(\psi_{h}^{\pm})\nabla\upsilon_{h}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}\leq||\max\{M(\psi_{h}^{+}),M(\psi_{h}^{-})\}||^{2}_{L^{\infty}(e)}||\nabla\upsilon_{h}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2},

(46)

then, using the trace inequality in Lemma
3.1
and since
M(\psi_{h})>0
we have the estimate:

\displaystyle||\nabla\upsilon_{h}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}\frac{|e|}{|K_{e}^{\pm}|}||\nabla\upsilon_{h}||_{L^{2}(K_{e}^{\pm})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{t}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{H}^{-1}\frac{||\sqrt{M(\psi_{h})}\nabla\upsilon_{h}||^{2}_{L^{2}(K_{e}^{\pm})}}{\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{e}^{\pm}}{\min}M(\psi_{h})}.

To investigate a global estimate we introduce the local contrast:

\lambda(M(\psi_{h}),K_{e}^{\pm}):=\frac{\sum_{e\in\partial K_{e}}||\max\{M(\psi_{h}^{+}),M(\psi_{h}^{-})\}||^{2}_{L^{\infty}(e)}}{\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{e}^{\pm}}{\min}M(\psi_{h})},

(47)

for each
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
. Then due to the local contrast function

\lambda(M(\psi_{h}),K)
we define the global contrast
\lambda^{\star}:=\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}{\lambda(M(\psi_{h}),K)}

and note that
0<\lambda^{\star}\leq M^{-1}(||\psi_{h}||_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})})

and
\lambda^{\star}
is bounded thanks to the regularization
\delta
. Then, upon summing over every
e\in\Gamma_{i}
we obtain the bounds
for
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
:

\left(1-\frac{(1+\theta)C_{t}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lambda^{\star}\epsilon}{4}\right)||\sqrt{M(\psi_{h})}\nabla\upsilon_{h}||_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}\geq 0.

(48)

Eq. (
48
) is unconditionally positive for

\theta=-1
independent of
\epsilon
, we therefore proceed with
\theta\in(-1,1]

and
\epsilon\leq\frac{4}{(1+\theta)C_{t}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lambda^{\star}}
to obtain:

\int_{e}\left(\frac{\eta\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))}{h_{H}}-\frac{(1+\theta)}{2\epsilon h_{H}}\right)[{\upsilon_{h}}]^{2}ds\geq 0,

(49)

thus to ensure that the jump term is positive we require that

\eta\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))\geq\frac{(1+\theta)}{2\epsilon}\geq\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}C_{t}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lambda^{\star}}{8},

(50)

and in particular to adhere to standard estimates (i.e.
[
5
,
45
]
)
of the penalty parameter we
consider

\eta\geq\frac{dC_{t}(1+\theta)^{2}}{4},

(51)

and for
\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))
we consider the following estimate:

\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))\geq\frac{\lambda^{\star}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}{2d}.

(52)

Then for each term there are two positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

b(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\upsilon_{h})\geq C_{1}||\sqrt{M(\psi_{h})}\nabla\upsilon_{h}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}^{2}+C_{2}\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}\frac{\eta\Lambda_{e}(M(\psi_{h}))}{h_{H}}[{\upsilon_{h}}]^{2}ds,

(53)

with
\tilde{C}=\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
we arrive at the desired result,

b(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\upsilon_{h})\geq\tilde{C}||\upsilon_{h}||^{2}_{DG},\quad\forall\upsilon_{h}\in V_{h,\upsilon},

(54)

which concludes the derivation.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

A similar coercivity from Thm.
3.2
holds for the SWIP formulation
in Eq. (

) following:

||\{{M(\psi_{h})}\}_{H}\{{\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}\leq||\{{\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}||_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}\left|\left|\{{M(\psi_{h})}\}^{2}_{H}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}(e)},

which is well-defined since

\displaystyle\lambda(M(\psi_{h}),K)

for coercivity.

Finally, the advection term is discretized using standard upwinding

c(\boldsymbol{u},\psi_{h},\varphi)=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\psi_{h}\,dx-\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}\left(\{{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}_{\oplus}\psi_{h}^{+}+\{{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}}\}_{\ominus}\psi_{h}^{-}\right)[{\varphi}]ds.

We present two schemes for the DG-FEM discretization of Eqs.(

)-(

)
. Firstly, the SIPG formulation

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\psi_{h},v\right\rangle-c(\boldsymbol{u},\psi_{h},\varphi)+Pe^{-1}b(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\varphi)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T}\quad\forall\varphi\in V_{h,\psi},

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T}\quad\forall\xi\in V_{h,\upsilon},

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\psi_{h},v\right\rangle-c(\boldsymbol{u},\psi_{h},\varphi)+Pe^{-1}b_{M}(M(\psi_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\varphi)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T}\quad\forall\varphi\in V_{h,\psi},

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T}\quad\forall\xi\in V_{h,\upsilon},

3.3
The Acosta-Soba upwinding scheme

The Acosta-Soba upwinding (ASU) scheme is a structure-preserving scheme which
was first studied for the convection CH equations
[
2
]
, and has
then been extended to the coupled CHNS equations
[
4
]
. The scheme
is constructed by introducing an auxiliary discontinuous piece-wise constant
variable

w_{h}\in\underbrace{\left\{v\in L^{2}({\mathcal{T}_{h}}):v|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}^{0}(K),\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\right\}}_{:=V_{h,w}},

(71)

which we use for an upwinding formulation, and it is used as the variable in
the dynamical part of Eq. (
17
). The variable
w_{h}
serves as a low-order
approximation of the physical phase-field
\tilde{\psi}_{h}\in\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}\subset H_{1}(\Omega)
, with
\Pi_{0}\psi_{h}=w_{h}
provided
by mass lumping.
The mobility trilinear form is given by the upwind mobility trilinear form:

\displaystyle b_{AS}\left(M(w_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\varphi\right):=

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\Gamma_{i}}\int_{e}(\{{-\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}_{\oplus}(M^{\uparrow}(w_{h}^{+})+M^{\downarrow}(w_{h}^{-}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\{{-\nabla\upsilon_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{+}}\}_{\ominus}(M^{\uparrow}(w_{h}^{-})+M^{\downarrow}(w_{h}^{+})))[{\varphi}]ds,

(72)

where

M^{\uparrow}(w)=M(w_{\ominus}),\quad\text{and}\quad M^{\downarrow}(w)=M(w_{\oplus})-M(0),

(73)

and in particular

M^{\uparrow}(w)+M^{\downarrow}(w)=M(w).

(74)

Then the ASU scheme is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}w_{h},\bar{w}\right\rangle-c(\boldsymbol{u},w_{h},\bar{w})+Pe^{-1}b_{AS}(M(w_{h}),\upsilon_{h},\bar{w})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\bar{w}\in V_{h,w},

\displaystyle\left\langle\upsilon_{h},\xi\right\rangle-\left\langle W(\tilde{\psi}_{h}),\xi\right\rangle-Cn^{2}a(\tilde{\psi}_{h},\xi)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\xi\in\tilde{V}_{h,\upsilon},

(75)

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\psi}_{h},\varphi\right\rangle_{ML}-\left\langle w_{h},\varphi\right\rangle_{ML}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\varphi\in\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}.

Theorem 3.4

(Weak mass conservation for the ASU scheme
[
2
]
)
.

The approximative physical phase-field
\tilde{\psi_{h}}
obeying Eq. (
75
)
is weakly mass conservative. In particular the mass-lumping guarantees that

\left\langle\tilde{\psi_{h}},1\right\rangle_{ML}=\left\langle w_{h},1\right\rangle_{ML},

(76)

ensures mass conservation for
\tilde{\psi_{h}}
when
w_{h}
is mass conservative.

Proof.

A proof for uniform triangular grid is found in
[
2
]
.
The proof for the conformally adaptive triangular grid follows similarly in
combination of Thm.
3.6

∎

Remark 3.8
.

Weak mass conservation for
w_{h}
follows similarly to the proof of Thm.
3.6
.
Overall, the authors in
[
2
]
provide excellent arguments for
the mass conservation of each field for a non-adaptive grid.

w_{h}\in V_{h,w}

3.4
Time discretization

Consider a discretization of
N
equidistant time increments
\tau:=\frac{T}{N}

of the time interval
(0,T]
. We introduce a discrete time sequence
(t^{n})_{i=0}^{N}

where
t^{n}:=n\tau
to perform discrete time evolution of
\varphi_{h}(t^{n})

at specific time steps. From now onwards we denote

\varphi_{h}^{n}:=\varphi_{h}(t^{n})
for brevity.

For the time discretization of the CHNS equations, we consider the approximation:

\partial_{t}\varphi^{n}\approx\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{q}\alpha_{j}\varphi^{(n-j)}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{q+1}),\quad\quad\sum_{j=0}^{q}\alpha_{j}=0,

(77)

for some appropriate choice of
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
for
j=0,\ldots,q
which
approximates the time derivative to order
\tau^{q}
. In this paper we only consider
the implicit Euler formulation, i.e.
q=1
and
\alpha_{0}=-\alpha_{1}=1
.
Moreover, an implicit-explicit (IMEX) discretization is utilized. IMEX is used
for the treatment of the non-linear energy potential
W^{\prime}(\psi)
using the Eyre
approach
[
48
,
24
]
:

\Phi(\psi_{h}^{(1)},\psi_{h}^{(2)})=\Phi^{+}(\psi_{h}^{(1)})-\Phi^{-}(\psi_{h}^{(2)}),

(78)

where
\Phi^{+}
and
\Phi^{-}
correspond to a convex-concave decomposition for
the energy potential
W^{\prime}(\psi)
in terms of the phase-field variable. In particular
for SIPG/SWIP we use the non-linear Eyre decomposition
[
48
,
24
]
:

\Phi^{+}(\psi_{h})=\psi_{h}^{3},\quad\Phi^{-}(\psi_{h})=\psi_{h},

(79)

and the linear Eyre decomposition for ASU:

\Phi^{+}(\psi_{h})=\psi_{h},\quad\Phi^{-}(\psi_{h})=2\psi_{h}-\psi_{h}^{3}.

(80)

Moreover, the evaluation
\Phi(\psi_{h},\psi_{h})=W^{\prime}(\psi_{h})
recovers the formulation
previously presented in Eq. (
2
). Finally, an implicit treatment is
used for the mobility
M(\cdot)
and advection term, while the weights in Eq. (
3.2
)
are treated explicitly. Thus, the general SIPG/SWIP schemes are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{q}\alpha_{j}\left\langle\psi_{h}^{(n-j)},v\right\rangle-c(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\psi_{h}^{n},\varphi)+Pe^{-1}b_{M^{n-1}}(M(\psi_{h}^{n}),\upsilon_{h}^{n},\varphi)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle\upsilon_{h}^{n},\xi\right\rangle-\left\langle\Phi^{+}(\psi_{h}^{n})-\Phi^{-}(\psi_{h}^{n-1}),\xi\right\rangle-Cn^{2}a(\psi_{h}^{n},\xi)-\left\langle S(\psi_{h}),\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(81)

for
\varphi,\xi\in V_{h,\psi}\times V_{h,\upsilon}
, where
S(\psi_{h})
is some stabilization
function due to IMEX couplings, serves
as a candidate for a general scheme. However, as is noted in
[
53
]
,
a proper treatment for the unconditional energy stability with BDF2 requires
the addition of a Douglas-Dupont-type regularization term
S(\psi_{h})=A\tau\Delta(\psi^{n}_{h}-\psi_{h}^{n-1})
for
A\geq 0
[
53
]
. However, this is not necessary
for our IMEX scheme by using a first-order in time formulation.

Finally, the discretization of the ASU scheme
[
2
,
4
]
is given by

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}\left\langle w_{h}^{n}-w_{h}^{n-1},\bar{w}\right\rangle-c(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},w_{h}^{n},\bar{w})+Pe^{-1}b_{AS}(M(w_{h}^{n}),\upsilon_{h}^{n},\bar{w})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle\upsilon_{h}^{n},\xi\right\rangle-\left\langle\Phi^{+}(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})+\Phi^{-}(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n-1}),\xi\right\rangle-Cn^{2}a(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n},\xi)

\displaystyle=0,

(82)

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n},\varphi\right\rangle_{ML}-\left\langle w_{h}^{n},\varphi\right\rangle_{ML}

\displaystyle=0,

for
(\bar{w},\xi,\varphi)\in V_{h,w}\times\tilde{V}_{h,\upsilon}\times\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}
.

Remark 3.10
.

The authors in
[
4
]
utilized some modification of the CHNS to
properly prove bound-preservation for a coupled system. It is outside the scope
of this paper to derive a proof which necessarily has energy stability in the
decoupled setting for the ASU scheme. Consequently, only tests which utilize
a similar form to
the one in the pre-print
[
3
]
will be used as numerical evidence.

3.5
Scaling limiter

Figure 1:
An example of a scaling limiter for
\psi=1.1\tanh(10(x-0.5))
.

For the SIPG and SWIP scheme we enforce boundedness of the phase-field
\psi_{h}

by applying an element-by-element scaling limiter.
This limiter was first suggested in
[
54
]
and successfully applied
in
[
15
,
17
,
38
]
.
The general idea is to scale the phase-field on each element such that the constraints
on minimum and maximum values of the phase-field are respected.
For
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
we denote
\psi_{K}={\psi_{h}}_{|K}
the restriction of
\psi_{h}

to element
K
and define the following projection operator

\Pi_{s}:V_{h}\longrightarrow V_{h}
with

\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}\int_{K}\Pi_{s}[\psi_{K}]\cdot\varphi=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}\int_{K}\widehat{\psi_{K}}\cdot\varphi\quad\forall\varphi\in V_{h}

(83)

with the scaled phase-field

\widehat{\psi_{K}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\alpha_{K}\big(\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x})-\overline{\psi_{K}}\big)+\overline{\psi_{K}}

(84)

and

\overline{\psi_{K}}
being the mean value of
\psi_{K}
. Note that by construction this leads
to a mass conservative scheme since the scaled part has mean
value zero and therefore
\widehat{\psi_{K}}
has the same mean value as
\psi_{K}
.
The scaling factor is

\alpha_{K}:=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Lambda_{K}}\big\{1,\left|\frac{\overline{\psi_{K}}-\psi_{min}}{\overline{\psi_{K}}-\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right|,\left|\frac{\overline{\psi_{K}}-\psi_{max}}{\overline{\psi_{K}}-\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right|\big\}

(85)

for the combined set of all quadrature points
\Lambda_{K}
used for evaluation
of the bilinear
forms of the given scheme.
\psi_{min}=-1
and
\psi_{max}=1
are the bounds
that
\psi_{h}
should obey.
Note, for linear polynomials it suffices to evaluate
\psi_{K}
at the vertices
of element
K
to find
\alpha_{K}
. For higher polynomial degrees, however, more
quadrature points have to be considered which is slightly more expensive.

This scaling procedure is simple to implement, only needs the solution of element-local
systems in (
83
)
and renders our proposed SIPG and SWIP schemes to be bound preserving.

Remark 3.11
.

Alternative limiting methods have been carefully studied previously for the
CH equations in
[
25
,
32
,
38
]
and references therein.

Remark 3.12

(Cell Averages)
.

As pointed out in
[
38
]
, depending on the choice of the mobility formulation
the DG scheme might not
obey the bounds even for the cell averages in which case the scaling limiter
cannot work correctly.
In
[
38
]
constant and degenerated mobility formulations are studied.
The modification of cell averages leads to violation of mass conservation which
in our setting is not a desirable outcome and is therefore not considered.
For the degenerate mobility formulation (
3
) a provable result
is available (see Theorem
2.3
)
and one can therefore expect the DG scheme to obey these bounds at least for
the cell averages. For this reason only
the degenerate mobility formulation is considered in this work.
Furthermore, in numerical experiments considered in this paper no violation
of the bounds for the cell averages has been encountered.

3.6
Standard Finite-Element Schemes

The standard continuous Galerkin Finite Element Method (FEM) scheme can be
recovered by choosing
the continuous space
\tilde{V}_{h}
instead of the DG space
V_{h}
in Eq.
(
64
). It’s easy to see that the skeleton terms, for example,
in Eq. (
3.2
) or (
63
) vanish since the jump terms
of the involved basis functions are zero. Several modifications are necessary
to render the FEM scheme mass conservative and bound preserving.
For this purpose we formulate the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Bounded and mass conservative lumped
L^{2}
-projection)
.

Consider the mass-lumped

L^{2}
-projection
\Pi_{ML}:V_{h,\psi}\to\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}

given by

\left\langle\psi_{h},w\right\rangle_{ML}=\left\langle\tilde{\psi}_{h},w\right\rangle_{ML}\quad\forall w\in\tilde{V}_{h,\psi},

(86)

where
\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}
is spanned by a piece-wise linear Lagrange basis over

{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, and the mass-lumped inner product is defined by nodal quadrature so
that
the Lagrange basis is orthogonal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{ML}
.
If
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
is a convex polygon then the following holds if
\psi_{h}
is
piece-wise linear over each
element
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
:

i)

||\tilde{\psi}_{h}||_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}\leq||\psi_{h}||_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}
, and

ii)

m_{\psi_{h}}=m_{\tilde{\psi}_{h}}
.

Proof.

Let
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be the Lagrange basis functions spanning
\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}
,
where
N
is the total number of degrees of freedom in
\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}
corresponding
to nodes in
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
.
By noting that the Lagrange basis functions satisfy
\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=\delta_{ij}

at the nodes
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, we further denote the set of elements

K
with the node
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
as
I_{i}=\{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\partial K\}
and the support of
\varphi_{i}
is given by
\text{supp}(\varphi_{i})=\cup_{K\in I_{i}}K
.
With
\psi_{K}
we denote the restriction of
\psi_{h}
to element
K
and
for the basis expansion
\tilde{\psi_{h}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\psi}_{i}\varphi_{i}
the coefficients
\tilde{\psi}_{i}
satisfy

\tilde{\psi}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{K\in I_{i}}|K|\,\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}{|\text{supp}(\varphi_{i})|},

(87)

due to the nodal quadrature.
As a consequence we obtain a weighted average over the elements
K
in
I_{i}
,
so that

\min_{K\in I_{i}}\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\leq\tilde{\psi}_{i}\leq\max_{K\in I_{i}}\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

(88)

in particular
||\tilde{\psi}_{h}||_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|\tilde{\psi_{h}}|\leq||\psi_{h}||_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})}

which concludes part
i)
of the proof, boundedness, as
linear Lagrangian basis functions attain their extrema over
K
at the nodes.
For part
ii)
, mass conservation, we observe that

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tilde{\psi}_{h}dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\psi}_{i}\int_{{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}\varphi_{i}dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\sum_{K\in I_{i}}|K|\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}{|\text{supp}(\varphi_{i})|}\frac{|\text{supp}(\varphi_{i})|}{C_{K}},

(89)

where the expression for
\tilde{\psi}_{i}
from Eq.(
87
) was
inserted in the last step.
C_{K}
denotes the number of nodes per element
K
.
Rearranging the summation we arrive at

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{K\in I_{i}}\frac{|K|}{C_{K}}\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}|K|\left(\sum_{i:K\in I_{i}}\frac{1}{C_{K}}\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right),

(90)

where the inner sum is over all nodes
i
for a given element
K
, totalling to
C_{K}
nodes.
Since
\psi_{K}
is affine ensures the weighted nodal average over the element
K
is equivalent
to the cell average,

\sum_{i\in K}\frac{1}{C_{K}}\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\psi_{K}dx,

(91)

and thus

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tilde{\psi}_{h}dx=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}|K|\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\psi_{h}dx=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\psi_{h}dx,

(92)

which concludes part
ii)
of the proof, mass conservation. In particular this means

m_{\psi_{h}}=m_{\tilde{\psi}_{h}}
following Def.
2.1
.
∎

To render the FEM scheme bound preserving we introduce the
limited FEM (FEM-L) scheme. The continuous solution is projected
into a DG space with equal polynomial degree.
This step is exact since the continuous space is contained within the DG space.
The above described scaling limiter is then applied and
the result is projected back into the continuous space using the mass lumped
projection
\Pi_{ML}
described in (
86
).
The last step involves the solution of a linear system (mass matrix) which is
diagonal and can be inverted easily.
This entire procedure is detailed in Algorithm
3
.

Remark 3.13

(Mass conservation for the FEM-L projection)
.

FEM-L scheme is mass conservative during the limiting procedure, because

\tilde{V}_{h,\psi}\subset V_{h,\psi}
which means that the
L^{2}
projection
of the continuous solution onto the DG space is exact, and thus, mass conservative.
As stated in Eq. (
85
) the limiting of the DG solution is mass
conservative
since the mean value of the solution is preserved.
Finally, by using the mass-lumped
projection
\Pi_{ML}
to project back to the continuous space
we obtain mass conservation as a direct consequence of Thm.
3.6
.

Corollary 3.6.1

(Bound preservation of FEM-L)
.

The FEM-L scheme is bound preserving provided that the
FEM phase-field for each element
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
satisfies

\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\tilde{\psi_{h}}dx\in[-1,1]
.

Proof.

Upon projecting
\tilde{\psi_{h}}
onto
\psi_{h}
they share the same cell mean value

\overline{\psi_{K}}
, and are equivalent due to Remark
3.13
. Then the scaling
limiter in Eq. (
85
) guarantees that the DG phase-field
\psi_{h}
is
bound preserving when
\overline{\psi_{K}}\in[-1,1]
, even if the FEM phase-field violates
||\tilde{\psi_{h}}||_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq 1

for some
K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
.
Finally, applying
\Pi_{ML}
to project back to the continuous space preserves the
bounds as shown in Thm.
3.6
.
∎

For adaptive simulations a second modification is necessary. Lagrange interpolation
can no longer be used for data transfer during adaptation,
since this is not mass conservative during the coarsening step.
Here, again the solution is projected to and from a DG space in the same way
as
for the limiting. The continuous solution is projected into a DG space before
the adaptation takes place. During the adaptation both the DG and FEM solution
are transferred using
L^{2}
projection for DG and Lagrange interpolation for
FEM. After the adaptation the DG solution is then projected back to the FEM space. Like before this means solving a diagonal linear system which is trivial.

Another issue with the FEM scheme, as pointed out in
[
50
]
, is
the problem
arising with advection stabilizations such as SUPG which are altering the
equations and thus might lead to incorrect solutions. This could potentially
cured with so-called Vertex Centered Finite Volume discretization for the
advection term which can be combined with a FEM discretization for the
diffusion terms. An examples can be found in
[
36
]
. Many other
works on that
topic exist.

3.7
The Incremental Pressure Correction Scheme

The Incremental Pressure correction scheme (IPCS) is a splitting scheme used
as a time integration method to numerically solve the NS equations, which
is based on projection method with two auxiliary variables for the velocity

\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
, which is not necessarily solenoidal but follows the dynamical
equation of the velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
in Eq. (
19
), and an auxiliary
pressure difference
\delta P
. Following a Helmholtz decomposition
[
44
]
,
it is obtained that the auxiliary pressure difference
\delta P
satisfies

[
28
]
:

\alpha_{0}\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{n}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\right)+\tau\nabla\delta P^{n}=0,

(93)

and

\delta P^{n}=P^{n}-P^{n-1}+\frac{\mu(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})}{Re}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{(n)}.

(94)

Using Helmholtz decomposition on Eq. (
93
) allows decoupling
into two equation systems as
\boldsymbol{u}
is solenoidal. We obtain, due to the solenoidal
constraint
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{n}=0
, that

-\alpha_{0}\nabla\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}+\tau\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla\delta P^{(n)}}{\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})}\right)=0,

(95)

which allows for solvability of each respective variable.

Without loss of generality, it is assumed that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
and

\delta P
share the same boundary conditions as
\boldsymbol{u}
and
P
respectively.
The scheme is formulated to project an auxiliary velocity
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
onto
a space where the physical velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies the solenoidal constraint
and to ensure stability in the numerical solution. The RIPCS consists of four
main steps: solving the momentum equation for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
, solve for the
pressure difference
\delta P
, solving for the physical velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
, and
updating the pressure
P
.

For the discretization we consider a Taylor-Hood
\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathbb{P}^{1}
inf-sup stable FEM basis space
for the velocity

[V_{h,\boldsymbol{u}}]^{d}
, where each component of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
is in
V_{h,\boldsymbol{u}}
, and pressure
V_{h,P}
respectively. Before
proceeding, the skew-symmetric formulation

\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}\right),

(96)

is introduced. Because
\boldsymbol{u}
is solenoidal, this does not modify the strong
formulation of the governing equations in Eq. (
20
).

Remark 3.14
.

While
\boldsymbol{u}
is solenoidal, this does not mean that the FEM approximation
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}

necessarily
is solenoidal everywhere in
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\times(0,T]
. We refer to
[
44
]

for a scheme that provides this property with the usage of Raviart-Thomas elements.

Denoted by
\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\in[V_{h,\boldsymbol{u}}]^{d}
are the FEM velocity
field and auxiliary velocity field respectively, while
\tilde{P}_{h},\delta P_{h},\in V_{h,P}
represented the FEM approximation of the pressure and auxiliary
pressure difference. Moreover, the zero-mean pressure field will be represented
by
P_{h}
. The auxiliary velocity field
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n}
is the solution of
the weak form of Eq. (
12
):

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}\left(\alpha_{0}\left\langle\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_{j}\left\langle\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-j},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\right)+\left\langle\boldsymbol{J}^{n}\nabla\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle-\left\langle P_{h}^{n}\mathbb{I},D(\boldsymbol{v})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}}+0.5\left(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}}\right)\right),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{h,\boldsymbol{u}}

(97)

\displaystyle+2Re^{-1}\left\langle\mu(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})D(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}}),D(\boldsymbol{v})\right\rangle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle+Fr^{-1}\left\langle\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})\hat{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle.

To ensure consistency and particularly to
maintain continuity of the density
\rho
and density-flux
\boldsymbol{J}
,
we reconstruct a continuous phase-field
\tilde{\psi_{h}}=\Pi_{ML}\psi_{h}
whenever a DG method
is used to compute the phase-field.
In light of Thm.
3.6
, the projection of
\psi_{h}
under
\Pi_{ML}

is both mass-conservative and bound preserving.

Continuing,
\delta P^{n}
is found via Eq. (
95
):

\tau\left\langle\frac{\nabla\delta\tilde{P}_{h}^{n}}{\alpha_{0}\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})},\nabla q\right\rangle=-\left\langle\nabla\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n},q\right\rangle,\quad\forall q\in V_{h,P}.

(98)

However, since Eq. (
98
) is a weak Poisson equation with Neumann
boundary conditions
\mathbf{n}\nabla\cdot\delta P_{h}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, the auxiliary variable
\delta P_{h}
may only be uniquely defined in

V_{h,P}
up to a time-dependent constant
C^{n}
. This problem is solved shortly
for the physical pressure
P_{h}^{n}
. Then
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}
is found through

\alpha_{0}\left\langle\rho(\tilde{\psi}_{h}^{n})\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n}\right),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle-\tau\left\langle\nabla\delta P^{n}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in[V_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}]^{d}.

(99)

Then, the (non-unique) pressure
\tilde{P}_{h}^{n}
is recovered from the weak form
of Eq. (
94
)

\left\langle\tilde{P^{n}}_{h},q\right\rangle=Re^{-1}\left\langle\mu(\tilde{\psi}^{n}_{h})\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{(n)},q\right\rangle-\left\langle\delta P_{h}^{n}+P_{h}^{n-1},q\right\rangle,\quad\forall q\in V_{h,P}.

(100)

Lastly, to find a unique representation of the pressure
P_{h}^{n}
the following
equation is applied:

P_{h}^{n}=\tilde{P}_{h}^{n}-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{T}_{h}}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tilde{P}_{h}^{n}dx

(101)

which is equivalent to finding the pressure
P
in the solution space
V_{h,P}\cap L^{2}_{0}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})
for solutions of Eq. (
100
) as a post-processing
measure.

A comprehensive comparison of the considered schemes was done
with respect to energy dissipation, mass
conservation, and boundedness for different test cases ranging from pure
Cahn-Hilliard examples to coupled Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes examples.
In Table
5
we list schemes and their behavior with
respect to the mentioned criteria. While energy dissipation is provided by all
schemes tested, mass conservation together with boundedness is only provided by
ASU and the limited FEM or DG schemes. Here, ASU has no clear extension to
higher order basis functions and also showed slightly worse performance than the DG
schemes. On the other hand, ASU has some clear advantages in fully coupled
settings, since no post-processing has to be done for this scheme. The FEM-L scheme might not be optimal
for cases with strong advective fields but certainly is a good improvement
for projects already considering a FEM based scheme for Cahn-Hilliard.
Among the DG schemes the SWIP-L scheme is more robust and overall
faster due to better conditioning of the resulting system matrices.
One downside of the DG schemes is the typical need for good preconditioning
methods which do not really surface in the test cases studied
in this work. All structure preserving schemes are fairly easy to implement in
frameworks based the Unified Form Language (UFL) and therefore also in other FEM
based software frameworks. Most of the presented test cases were utilizing grid
adaptation demonstrating the capabilities of the schemes in this regard.

Based on the findings presented in this paper, we will utilize the SWIP-L or
whenever only moderate advection takes place the FEM-L scheme
for further studies of multiphase fluid flow.

An natural continuation of this work is the extension of SWIP-L to a grid- and space-adaptive approach
which should allow low order approximations in areas where the solution is constant
and high order (
k>1
) approximations where fluid interface is present.
