Scalar behavior for a complex multi-soliton arising in blow-up for a semilinear wave equation
Asma Azaiez, Jacek Jendrej, Hatem Zaag

Abstract
This paper deals with blow-up for the complex-valued semilinear wave equation with power nonlinearity in dimension 1. Up to a rotation of the solution in the complex plane, we show that near a characteristic blow-up point, the solution behaves exactly as in the real-valued case. Namely, up to a rotation in the complex plane, the solution decomposes into a sum of a finite number of decoupled solitons with alternate signs. The main novelty of our proof is a resolution of a complex-valued first order Toda system governing the evolution of the positions and the phases of the solitons.

Introduction

We consider the occurrence of blow-up for the following complex-valued one-dimensional semilinear wave equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{t}u=\partial^{2}_{x}u+|u|^{p-1}u,\\
u(0)=u_{0}\mbox{ and }u_{t}(0)=u_{1},\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
u(t):x\in\mathbb{R}\to u(x,t)\in\mathbb{C}
,
p>1
,

u_{0}\in H^{1}_{loc,u}
and
u_{1}\in L^{2}_{loc,u}
with

\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{loc,u}}=\displaystyle\sup\limits_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{|x-a|<1}|v(x)|^{2}dx
and
\|v\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{loc,u}}=\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{loc,u}}+\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{loc,u}}

The Cauchy problem is locally wellposed. By energy arguments, Levine showed in
[
21
]
the existence of blow-up solutions.

Equation (

[

[

Since many of those more physical examples take
the form of systems, we are interested in the complex-valued case in
(

The idea of reducing the description of the dynamical behavior of superpositions of soliton-like objects

Without aiming to be exhaustive, let us mention the works on the Ginzburg-Landau gradient flow

\displaystyle=\frac{p(p-1)}{2\int_{-1}^{1}\rho(y)dy}\kappa_{0}^{p-1}2^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{-\frac{2p}{p-1}}(z)e^{\frac{2z}{p-1}}\tanh zdz>0,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{p-1}{(p+3)\int_{-1}^{1}\rho(y)dy}\kappa_{0}^{p-1}2^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{-\frac{2p}{p-1}}(z)e^{\frac{2z}{p-1}}dz.

By a simple integration by parts, we see that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{-\frac{2p}{p-1}}(z)e^{\frac{2z}{p-1}}dz=p\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{-\frac{2p}{p-1}}(z)e^{\frac{2z}{p-1}}\tanh zdz,

therefore,

\frac{A}{B}=\frac{(p+3)}{2}.

The aim of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Take
k\geq 2
.
If
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are
C^{1}
functions satisfying
(
4.2
), (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) for
s
large
enough, then, there exist
\delta>0
,
\zeta_{00}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta_{00}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
j=1,\dots,k
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{j}(s)

\displaystyle=\zeta_{00}+\kappa_{j}\log s+\alpha_{k,j}+O(s^{-\delta}),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\theta_{j}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\theta_{00}+j\pi+O(s^{-\delta})\quad(\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi),

where
\kappa_{j}=\frac{p-1}{2}\left(j-\frac{k+1}{2}\right)
and

\alpha_{k,j}
were defined right before (
1.11
).

Remark
.

Another way to express (
4.5
) would be to write
\zeta_{j}(s)=\zeta_{00}+\bar{\zeta}_{j}(s)+O(s^{-\delta})
, where
(\bar{\zeta}_{1}(s),\dots,\bar{\zeta}_{k}(s))
is the solution of system (
1.11
)
with zero center of mass defined in the introduction right before (
1.11
). Note that
the number
\delta
in Proposition
4.1
may differ from the number
\delta
used in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
).

4.2
Preliminaries

For any
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we define the
following functional

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\cos(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j+1}-\zeta_{j})}.

(4.6)

In (
4.10
), we will see that this is a Lyapunov functional, if
one neglects the error terms of order
J^{1+\delta}
in system
(
4.3
)-(
4.4
), before showing it is indeed a Lyapunov
functional, without neglecting those terms (see (
4.22
)
below). Let us compute

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta_{j}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\zeta_{j}}\big(\cos(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j+1}-\zeta_{j})}+\cos(\theta_{j}-\theta_{j-1})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j-1})}\big)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{p-1}\cos(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j+1}-\zeta_{j})}+\frac{2}{p-1}\cos(\theta_{j}-\theta_{j-1})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j-1})}

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\theta_{j}}\big(\cos(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j+1}-\zeta_{j})}+\cos(\theta_{j}-\theta_{j-1})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j-1})}\big)

(4.8)

\displaystyle=-\sin(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j+1}-\zeta_{j})}+\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{j-1})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j-1})}.

These formulas are valid also for
j=1
and
j=k
, if we apply the
usual conventions recalled in the remark right after the statement of
Proposition
2.8
.
Our differential system can thus be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(p-1)A}{2}\,\partial_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s))+O(J(s)^{1+\delta}),

(4.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-B\,\partial_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s))+O(J(s)^{1+\delta}).

As we have announced earlier, if we neglect the error term of order
J^{1+\delta}
, these relations immediately imply
that
\mathcal{E}
is a Lyapunov functional of the system, which is the crucial observation for our analysis.
To be more precise, noting that
|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mathcal{E}|+|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{E}|\lesssim J
from (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s))

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s)),\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}(s)\rangle+\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s)),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(s)\rangle

(4.10)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(p-1)A}{2}\,|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}-B\,|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+O(J^{2+\delta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{(p-1)A}|\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}|^{2}-\frac{1}{B}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}|^{2}+O(J^{2+\delta}).

Here, and everywhere in this section, the brackets
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the standard Euclidean inner product.
We also denote
\cos\boldsymbol{b}:=(\cos b_{1},\ldots,\cos b_{k-1})
,
\sin\boldsymbol{b}:=(\sin b_{1},\ldots,\sin b_{k-1})
,

\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}:=(\mathrm{e}^{-a_{1}},\ldots,\mathrm{e}^{-a_{k-1}})
etc.
Moreover,
\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}
denotes the
vector
with components
\cos b_{j}\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j}}
,
similarly for
\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}
etc.

Set

a_{j}(s):=\frac{2}{p-1}(\zeta_{j+1}(s)-\zeta_{j}(s))\mbox{ and }b_{j}(s):=\theta_{j+1}(s)-\theta_{j}(s).

(4.11)

With this notation, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta_{j}}\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{p-1}\cos b_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j}}+\frac{2}{p-1}\cos b_{j-1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j-1}},

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=-\sin b_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j}}+\sin b_{j-1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j-1}}.

Observe from (
4.3
) that

a_{j}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{2A}{p-1}\Big(\cos(b_{j+1}(s))\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j+1}(s)}-2\cos(b_{j}(s))\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j}(s)}+\cos(b_{j-1}(s))\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j-1}(s)}\Big)+O(J(s)^{1+\delta}).

(4.12)

In shorter form,

\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{2A}{p-1}\Delta^{D}\big(\cos\boldsymbol{b}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}(s)}\big)+O(J(s)^{1+\delta}),

(4.13)

where
\Delta^{D}
is defined as follows.

Definition 4.2
.

The (positive)
discrete Dirichlet Laplacian
in dimension 1 is the matrix

\Delta^{D}=(\Delta_{jl})\in\mathbb{R}^{(k-1)\times(k-1)}

defined by the conditions

\Delta_{jj}=2
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
,
\Delta_{j,j+1}=\Delta_{j+1,j}=-1
for
j=1,\ldots,k-2

and
\Delta_{jl}=0
if
|j-l|\geq 2
.

Similarly as (
4.13
), we have

\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}(s)=-B\Delta^{D}\big(\sin\boldsymbol{b}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}(s)}\big)+O(J(s)^{1+\delta}).

(4.14)

For later use, we study in more detail the matrix
\Delta^{D}
. First, note that the associated quadratic form is

\langle\boldsymbol{v},\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(v_{j}-v_{j-1})^{2},\quad\text{for }\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\text{ and }v_{0}=v_{k}=0.

(4.15)

Denote
\boldsymbol{1}:=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j=1}^{k-1}
with
\sigma_{j}:=\frac{j(k-j)}{2}
.
By an elementary computation, one checks that

\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{1}.

(4.16)

We also denote
\Pi:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}:\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=0\}
,

c_{0}:=\frac{1}{\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle}=\frac{12}{(k-1)k(k+1)},

(4.17)

P_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle}{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|^{2}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}

(4.18)

the orthogonal projection of
\boldsymbol{v}
on
\Pi
, and
P_{1}\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{v}-c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\boldsymbol{1}

the projection of
\boldsymbol{v}
on
\Pi
along the direction
\boldsymbol{1}
.
Observe that
P_{1}\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}=\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}-c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\boldsymbol{1}=\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}-c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\boldsymbol{1}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

The matrix
\Delta^{D}
is positive definite. There exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}

\langle\boldsymbol{v},P_{1}\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{v},\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle-c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle^{2}\geq c_{1}|P_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}.

Proof.

Consider the matrix
\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}=(\widetilde{\Delta}_{jl})
given by
\widetilde{\Delta}_{jl}:=2\delta_{jl}+c_{0}-\Delta_{jl}
.
The desired inequality is equivalent to

\langle\boldsymbol{v},\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\leq 2|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}-c_{1}|P_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}.

(4.19)

Noting that the matrix
\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}
has positive entries,
the Perron-Frobenius theorem applies, and we have the existence of an
eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\tau}
with positive entries such that
\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\alpha\boldsymbol{\tau}
where
\alpha>0
is
a simple eigenvalue and the largest in modulus. Since
\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}

is symmetric, hence real diagonalizable in some orthogonal basis, it
has no other eigenvector with positive entries, up to a multiplying factor. Since

\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=2\boldsymbol{\sigma}+c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\rangle\boldsymbol{1}-\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=2\boldsymbol{\sigma}

(4.20)

and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
has positive entries, it follows by uniqueness that

\alpha=2
and we can take
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
Moreover, all the eigenvalues of the
restriction of
\widetilde{\Delta}^{D}
to the hyperplane
\Pi
are strictly less
than
2
(in modulus).
Decomposing the inner product in (
4.19
) into a
contribution along
\sigma
and another in
\Pi
, (
4.19
)
follows. This concludes the proof of Lemma
4.3
.
∎

Using (
4.18
), we get the following consequence of the lemma:

Corollary 4.4
.

For some
c_{2}>0
and for all
v\in\mathbbm{R}^{k-1}
, it holds that
\langle\boldsymbol{v},\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\geq c_{2}|v|^{2}
.

4.3
Differential inequalities for Lyapunov functionals

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
the function
a_{j}(s)-\log s
is bounded.

Proof.

By a slight abuse of notation, we denote
\mathcal{E}(s):=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s))
introduced in (
4.6
). Using the expressions (
4.7
) and (
4.8
)
of the derivatives of
\mathcal{E}
, together with (
4.15
) and Corollary

4.4
, we see that

|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mathcal{E}|^{2}+|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{E}|^{2}\gtrsim|\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}|^{2}+|\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}|^{2}\gtrsim\langle\boldsymbol{1},\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle^{2},

(4.21)

where the last inequality follows from equivalence of norms.
By (
4.10
), the last bound and the definition
(
4.1
) of
J(s)
, for all
s
large enough we have

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(s)\leq-C^{-1}J(s)^{2}.

(4.22)

We also have
|J^{\prime}(s)|\leq CJ(s)^{2}
from
(
4.1
), (
4.11
) and (
4.12
), thus
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(s)\leq-C^{-2}|J^{\prime}(s)|
. Recalling that both
J(s)
and
\mathcal{E}(s)
go to
0
as

s\to\infty
from (
4.2
) and (
4.6
), we may
integrate in
s
to obtain

\mathcal{E}(s)\geq C^{-2}\int_{s}^{\infty}|J^{\prime}(\tau)|\mathrm{\,d}\tau\geq C^{-2}J(s).

(4.23)

Next, arguing as for (
4.21
), then applying Lemma
4.3
with
\boldsymbol{v}=\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}
, we get

\displaystyle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mathcal{E}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{(p-1)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\cos b_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j}}-\cos b_{j-1}\mathrm{e}^{-a_{j-1}})^{2}=\frac{4}{(p-1)^{2}}\langle\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}},\Delta^{D}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}})\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4c_{0}}{(p-1)^{2}}\langle\boldsymbol{1},\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle^{2}=\frac{4c_{0}}{(p-1)^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{2}.

Hence, (
4.10
) and (
4.23
) yield

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(s)\leq-\frac{2Ac_{0}}{p-1}\mathcal{E}(s)^{2}+O(J(s)^{2+\delta})\leq\Big({-}\frac{2Ac_{0}}{p-1}+O(J(s)^{\delta})\Big)\mathcal{E}(s)^{2}.

Dividing both sides by the positive number
\mathcal{E}(s)^{2}
and using the Chain Rule, we have

(\mathcal{E}(s)^{-1})^{\prime}\geq\frac{2Ac_{0}}{p-1}+O(J(s)^{\delta}),

(4.24)

and an integration in time yields

\mathcal{E}(s)^{-1}\geq\Big(\frac{2Ac_{0}}{p-1}+o(1)\Big)s,

implying

\mathcal{E}(s)\leq\Big(\frac{p-1}{2Ac_{0}}+o(1)\Big)s^{-1}.

(4.25)

The bound (
4.23
) yields

J(s)\leq Cs^{-1}.

(4.26)

Plugging this into (
4.24
) and integrating in time leads to

\mathcal{E}(s)^{-1}\geq\Big(\frac{2Ac_{0}}{p-1}+O(s^{-\delta})\Big)s,

thus we can improve (
4.25
) and write

\mathcal{E}(s)\leq\frac{p-1}{2Ac_{0}}s^{-1}+O(s^{-1-\delta}).

(4.27)

The bound (
4.26
) also implies by definitions
(
4.1
) and (
4.11
) of
J(s)
and
a_{j}(s)
that

a_{j}(s)\geq\log s-C,\qquad\text{for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}.

(4.28)

In order to obtain the upper bound, we decompose

\boldsymbol{a}(s)=r(s)\boldsymbol{1}+\boldsymbol{z}(s),\qquad r(s)=c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{a}(s)\rangle,\qquad\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{z}(s)\rangle=0.

(4.29)

Using the equation (
4.13
) satisfied by
\boldsymbol{a}(s)
, we have

r^{\prime}(s)\boldsymbol{1}+\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}(s)=\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}(s)={-}\frac{2A}{p-1}\Delta^{D}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{a}(s)})+O(s^{-1-\delta}).

Taking the inner product with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, then using the fact that

\Delta^{D}
is symmetric and that
\Delta^{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{1}
(use
(
4.16
)), we see by definitions (
4.6
) and (
4.17
)
of
\mathcal{E}
and
c_{0}
that

r^{\prime}(s)=\frac{2Ac_{0}}{p-1}\mathcal{E}(s)+O(s^{-1-\delta}).

(4.30)

Integrating in time and using (
4.27
), we get

r(s)\leq\log s+C.

But
r
is a weighted mean of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}
by definition
(
4.17
) of
c_{0}
, so (
4.28
) implies that we also have the desired upper bound

a_{j}(s)\leq\log s+C,\qquad\text{for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}.

(4.31)

This concludes the proof of Lemma
4.5
.
∎

We further study the evolution in the variables
(r,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{b})
.
From Lemma
4.5
and by definition (
4.29
) of
r

and
\boldsymbol{z}
, we know that
r(s)-\log s
and
\boldsymbol{z}(s)
are bounded functions.
In particular, (
4.26
) holds and we may
rewrite (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2A}{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-r(s)}\Delta^{D}\big(\cos\boldsymbol{b}(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}(s)}\big)+O(s^{-1-\delta}),

(4.32)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-B\mathrm{e}^{-r(s)}\Delta^{D}\big(\sin\boldsymbol{b}(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}(s)}\big)+O(s^{-1-\delta}).

Applying
P_{1}
to the first equation above, we obtain

\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{2A}{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-r(s)}P_{1}\Delta^{D}\big(\cos\boldsymbol{b}(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}(s)}\big)+O(s^{-1-\delta}).

(4.33)

Consider the functional
\mathcal{F}:\Pi\times\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{b}):=-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\cos(b_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-z_{j}}=-\langle\cos\boldsymbol{b},\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle.

(4.34)

Note that

\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{z}(s),\boldsymbol{b}(s))=\mathrm{e}^{r(s)}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}(s)).

(4.35)

It turns out that, even though
\mathrm{e}^{r(s)}
grows linearly,
\mathcal{F}
is still a Lyapunov functional, up to error terms.
In order to see this, we write

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}}\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{z}(s),\boldsymbol{b}(s))=\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime},\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime},\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle

(4.36)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2A}{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-r}\langle P_{1}\Delta^{D}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}),\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle-B\mathrm{e}^{-r}\langle\Delta^{D}(\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}),\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle+O(s^{-1-\delta})

\displaystyle\lesssim-\mathrm{e}^{-r}\big(|P_{\sigma}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}})|^{2}+|\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|^{2}\big)+O(s^{-1-\delta}),

where in the last step we use Lemma
4.3
and Corollary

4.4
. We need the following fact.

Lemma 4.6
.

Set
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}:=-\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}+c_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}\rangle\boldsymbol{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.

1.

For any
R>0
and
\epsilon>0
small enough there exists
\eta=\eta(R,\epsilon)>0
such that if

\boldsymbol{z}\in\Pi,\;\;|\boldsymbol{z}|\leq R,\qquad|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}|+\min(|\cos\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{1}|,|\cos\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{1}|)\geq\epsilon,

(4.37)

then

|P_{\sigma}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}})|+|\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|\geq\eta.

2.

There exist
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that if

|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}|+|\boldsymbol{b}-\pi\boldsymbol{1}|\leq\epsilon_{0},

then

|P_{\sigma}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}})|+|\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|\geq C^{-1}\big(|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}|+|\boldsymbol{b}-\pi\boldsymbol{1}|\big).

Proof of (i).

If
|\sin b_{j}|\gtrsim 1
for some
j
, then the conclusion is immediate.
Hence, we can assume
|\sin b_{j}|\ll 1
, which implies
\min(|\cos b_{j}-1|,|\cos b_{j}+1|)\ll 1
for all
j
.
We claim that, if the lemma was to fail, this has to be either
1
for all
j
, or
-1
for all
j
.
Indeed, if the sign changes, then the angle between the directions of
\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is bounded from below,
implying
|P_{\sigma}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}})|\gtrsim|\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|
, from where the conclusion follows quite
easily.

Assume
|\cos\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{1}|\ll 1
(the other case follows by changing
\boldsymbol{b}
to
\pi\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{b}
).
Upon shifting the components of
\boldsymbol{b}
by multiples of
2\pi
, we can assume
|\boldsymbol{b}-\pi\boldsymbol{1}|\ll 1
.
In particular, (
4.37
) implies
|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\epsilon
.
We have
\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}={-}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}+(\boldsymbol{1}+\cos\boldsymbol{b})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}
, thus

\displaystyle|P_{\sigma}(\cos\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}})|+|\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|

\displaystyle\geq|P_{\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|-|(\boldsymbol{1}+\cos\boldsymbol{b})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|+|\sin\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|

(4.38)

\displaystyle\geq|P_{\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|+\frac{1}{2}|(\boldsymbol{b}-\pi\boldsymbol{1})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|,

where the last inequality follows from
|\boldsymbol{b}-\pi\boldsymbol{1}|
being small.
We only need to show that
|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\epsilon
implies
|P_{\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|\geq\eta
.

We can rewrite the condition
|P_{\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}|\leq\eta
as follows:

\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{u},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\ \boldsymbol{u}\in\Pi,\ |\boldsymbol{u}|\leq\eta.

Note that
\lambda
is of size
1
. We have

|{-}\boldsymbol{z}-\log(\lambda\boldsymbol{\sigma})|=|\log(\lambda\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{u})-\log(\lambda\boldsymbol{\sigma})|\lesssim|\boldsymbol{u}|\lesssim\eta.

The vector
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}
is in fact determined by the expression

\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}=-\log(\lambda_{\mathrm{cr}}\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, where

\lambda_{\mathrm{cr}}
is the unique value for which
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}\in\Pi
. We thus get from the above

|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}+(\log\lambda-\log\lambda_{\mathrm{cr}})\boldsymbol{1}|\lesssim\eta.

Taking the inner product with
\sigma
we find out that
|\log\lambda-\log\lambda_{\mathrm{cr}}|\lesssim\eta
,
which yields
|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}|\lesssim\eta
and finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of (ii).

By (
4.38
), it suffices to check that

Remark 4.7
.

The function

\boldsymbol{z}\mapsto\langle\boldsymbol{1},\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle
, defined for
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Pi
,
is strictly convex and tends to infinity when
|\boldsymbol{z}|\to\infty

(use the fact that whenever
|\boldsymbol{z}_{n}|\to\infty
for some sequence

\boldsymbol{z}_{n}\in\Pi
, then,
z_{n,j(n)}\to-\infty
for some choice of
j(n)
).
Thus it has a unique critical point
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}
, which is its global minimum.
The Euler-Lagrange equation yields
\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{cr}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
where
\lambda_{\mathrm{cr}}\in\mathbb{R}
is the unique number for which
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}}\in\Pi
.

The function
\mathcal{F}:(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{b})\mapsto-\langle\cos\boldsymbol{b},\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{z}}\rangle
,
defined in (
4.34
) on the set
\{(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{b})\in\Pi\times\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\}
,
has a discrete set of critical points
(\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}},\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{cr}})
, where
\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{cr}}

is any vector such that
\cos\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{cr}}=\boldsymbol{1}
or
\cos\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{cr}}=-\boldsymbol{1}
.
A priori,
\cos\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{cr}}
could be a vector whose components include both
1
and
-1
, but it is not so.
Indeed, from the Euler-Lagrange equation,
if the function
\boldsymbol{z}\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{\iota_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{-z_{j}}

has a critical point on
\Pi
, then all the signs
\iota_{j}
are the
same.

Note that
(\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathrm{cr}},\pi\boldsymbol{1})
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{b})
,
related to
\max_{\boldsymbol{b}}\min_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{b})
.

(Convergence to the saddle point).

(Polynomial convergence rate.)
