Large-party limit of topological entanglement entropy in Chern-Simons theory
Simran Sain and Siddharth Dwivedi Department of Physics, School of Physical Sciences, Central University of Rajasthan, Ajmer, 305817, India

Abstract
We investigate the topological entanglement entropy of quantum states arising in the context of three-dimensional Chern-Simons theory with compact gauge group G G and Chern-Simons level k k . We focus on the quantum states associated with the T d ​ m , d ​ n T_{dm,dn} torus link complements, which is a d d -party pure quantum state, and analyze its large-party limit, i.e., d → ∞ d\to\infty limit. We show that the entanglement measures in this limit will receive contributions only from the Abelian anyons, and non-Abelian sectors are suppressed in the large-party limit. Consequently, the large-party limiting value of the entanglement entropy has an upper bound of ln ⁡ | Z G | \ln|Z_{G}| , where | Z G | |Z_{G}| is the order of the center of the group G G . As an explicit example, we perform quantitative analysis for the simplest case of the SU(2) group and T d , d ​ n T_{d,dn} torus link to obtain the large-party limit of the entanglement entropy. We further investigate the semiclassical ( k → ∞ k\to\infty ) limit of the entropies after taking the large-party limit for this particular example.

Introduction

The underlying theory for our study will be the (2+1)-dimensional Chern-Simons theory with a compact gauge group
G
and the Chern-Simons level
k
, which is a non-negative integer. This theory is defined on a 3-manifold
M
with action given by
[
13
]

S(A)=\frac{k}{4\pi}\int_{M}\text{Tr}\left(A\wedge dA+\frac{2}{3}A\wedge A\wedge A\right)\penalty\ ,

(1.1)

where the gauge field
A=A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
is a connection on the trivial SU(
N
) bundle over
M
. This theory has gauge-invariant operators, which are called Wilson lines. For an oriented knot
\mathcal{K}
embedded in
M
, the Wilson line is defined by taking the trace of the holonomy of
A
around
\mathcal{K}
:

W_{R}(\mathcal{K})=\text{Tr}_{R}\,P\exp\left(i\oint_{\mathcal{K}}A\right)\penalty\ ,

(1.2)

where the trace is taken under the representation
R
of SU(
N
). One can compute the partition function by integrating over the gauge invariant classes of connections, which results in a topological invariant of
M
. When there are no knots or links, the partition function
Z(M)
of the bare manifold is given as:

Z(M)=\int e^{iS(A)}dA\penalty\ .

(1.3)

When there are knot insertions in
M
, one must insert the corresponding Wilson loop operators in the integral. Given a link
\mathcal{L}
made of disjoint oriented knot components, i.e.
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{d}
, the partition function of
M
in the presence of
\mathcal{L}
can be obtained by modifying the path integral as:

Z(M;\mathcal{L})=\int e^{iS(A)}\,W_{R_{1}}(\mathcal{K}_{1})\ldots W_{R_{d}}(\mathcal{K}_{d})\,dA\penalty\ .

(1.4)

In this article, we will consider manifolds
M
with boundary
\Sigma
. For this, we must incorporate the boundary condition
A\rvert_{\Sigma}=Q
while performing the path integral. In such cases, the partition function is interpreted as the wave function of a quantum state, which is given as:

\ket{\Psi}\equiv Z_{Q}(M;\mathcal{L})=\int_{A\rvert_{\Sigma}=Q}e^{iS(A)}\,W_{R_{1}}(\mathcal{K}_{1})\ldots W_{R_{n}}(\mathcal{K}_{n})\,dA\penalty\ .

(1.5)

This quantum state is an element of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
associated with the boundary
\Sigma
. In this work, we will be dealing with the torus boundary, i.e.,
\Sigma=T^{2}
. The basis states for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}
are obtained by doing the Chern-Simons path integral on a solid torus with Wilson lines inserted along its non-contractible cycle. These Wilson lines can carry only certain allowed representations of the group
G
for a given level
k
. These are called the ‘integrable representations’. For example, for SU(
N
) group, the integrable representations are those representations
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{N-1}]
which satisfy
0\leq a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots+a_{N-1}\leq k
and
a_{i}\geq 0
. Here,
a_{i}
are the Dynkin labels of the highest weight of the representation.

Generic
d
-party torus link state
\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}

Let us consider the torus link state
\ket{T_{p,q}}
where
d=\text{gcd}(p,q)
. Without loss of generality, set
p=dm
and
q=dn
with
\text{gcd}(m,n)=1
. The torus link
T_{dm,dn}
is made of
d
torus knots
T_{m,n}
such that the linking number between any pair of knots is
mn
. This is a generic
d
-party torus link state which lives in the tensor product of
d
copies of
\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}
and can be linearly expanded as:

\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}=\sum_{R_{1}}\sum_{R_{2}}\cdots\sum_{R_{d}}C_{R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{d}}\,\ket{e_{R_1},e_{R_2},\ldots,e_{R_d}}\penalty\ ,

(2.1)

There is a nice topological way of extracting the values of the coefficients
C_{R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{d}}
as explained in
[
2
]
and illustrated in
[
8
,
9
]
, and in this case, they will be given as:

C_{R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{d}}=Z(S^{3};[T_{dm,\,dn}]_{R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{d}})\penalty\ ,

(2.2)

where the right hand side is the
S^{3}
partition function in the presence of the torus link
T_{dm,\,dn}
where the
d
knots carry the representations
R_{1},R_{2},\ldots R_{d}
of the gauge group
G
. These partition functions have already been obtained in the works of
[
12
,
3
]
and can be written in terms of the modular
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
matrices, which form the unitary representation of the modular group SL(2,
\mathbb{Z}
).
The partition function is given as:

Z(S^{3};[T_{dm,\,dn}]_{R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{d}})=\sum_{P,Q,R}\frac{(\mathcal{S}_{PQ})^{*}\,\mathcal{S}_{0R}\,(\mathcal{T}_{RR})^{n/m}\,\mathcal{X}_{QR}(m)}{(\mathcal{S}_{0P})^{d-1}}\left(\mathcal{S}_{R_{1}P}\,\mathcal{S}_{R_{2}P}\ldots\mathcal{S}_{R_{d}P}\right)\penalty\ .

(2.3)

The coefficients
\mathcal{X}_{QR}(m)
are integers that occur in the expansion of the traces of powers of the holonomy operator
U
as:

\text{Tr}_{Q}(U^{m})=\sum_{R}\mathcal{X}_{QR}(m)\,\text{Tr}_{R}(U)\penalty\ .

(2.4)

These coefficients can be obtained by performing the Adams operation on the characters associated with the irreducible representations of the group
G
. For the SU(2) group, these coefficients have been explicitly computed in
[
8
]
. A computationally friendly formula for the matrix elements of
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
can be found in
[
7
]
. Although the quantum state (
2.1
) after substituting (
2.3
) looks cumbersome, it can be written in a cleaner fashion by changing the basis of individual Hilbert spaces. Since we do not want the entanglement measures to change, we must use unitary transformed basis states for each Hilbert space. Since
\mathcal{S}
matrix is unitary, we can invoke the following basis change for the
i^{\text{th}}
Hilbert space:

\ket{e_{R_i}}=\sum_{P_{i}}(\mathcal{S}_{R_{i}P_{i}})^{*}\ket{f_{P_i}}\penalty\ .

(2.5)

It is a simple exercise to see that in the new basis
\ket{f_{P_i}}
of the
i^{\text{th}}
Hilbert space, the quantum state can be linearly expanded as:

\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}=\sum_{P,Q,R}\frac{(\mathcal{S}_{PQ})^{*}\,\mathcal{S}_{0R}\,(\mathcal{T}_{RR})^{n/m}\,\mathcal{X}_{QR}(m)}{(\mathcal{S}_{0P})^{d-1}}\,\underbrace{\ket{f_{P},\,f_{P},\,\ldots,\,f_{P}}}_{d}\penalty\ .

(2.6)

It can be written in a compact way as:

\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}=\sum_{P}\frac{(\mathcal{S}^{*}\,\mathcal{X}(m)\,\mathcal{T}^{n/m}\,\mathcal{S})_{P0}}{(\mathcal{S}_{0P})^{d-1}}\,\ket{f_{P},\,f_{P},\,\ldots,\,f_{P}}\penalty\ .

(2.7)

In the next section, we will analyze the entanglement structure of this state in the
d\to\infty
limit.

2.1
Entanglement measures in large-party limit for generic group

Let us study the quantum state in (
2.7
) for a compact gauge group
G
and level
k
. Our first step must be to normalize the state. But before that, we would like to convert the isolated factor of
\mathcal{S}_{0P}
into a quantum dimension using the formula
\text{dim}_{q}P=\mathcal{S}_{0P}/\mathcal{S}_{00}
. We will see the implications of this conversion later. So after normalization, the state (
2.7
) will become:

\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{NF}}}\,\sum_{P}\frac{(\mathcal{S}^{*}\,\mathcal{X}(m)\,\mathcal{T}^{n/m}\,\mathcal{S})_{P0}}{(\text{dim}_{q}P)^{d-1}}\,\ket{f_{P},\,f_{P},\,\ldots,\,f_{P}}\penalty\ ,

(2.8)

where NF is the normalization factor given as:

\text{NF}=\langle T_{dm,\,dn}|T_{dm,\,dn}\rangle=\sum_{R}\absolutevalue{\frac{(\mathcal{S}^*\, \mathcal{X}(m)\,\mathcal{T}^{n/m}\, \mathcal{S})_{R0}}{(\text{dim}_qR)^{d-1}}}^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.9)

The structure of the state immediately tells us that the total density matrix
\rho_{\text{total}}=\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}\bra{T_{dm,\,dn}}
will be a diagonal matrix of order
(n_{0})^{d}
. Here
n_{0}\equiv\text{dim }\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}
is the dimension of the Hilbert space for a given value of
k
, and it essentially counts the number of integrable representations of the group
G
at level
k
. This counting can be explicitly found in
[
7
]
. Of all the diagonal elements, only the
n_{0}
number of diagonal elements of
\rho_{\text{total}}
will be non-zero, and others will be 0. In order to study the entanglement measures, we would like to bi-partition the total system as
(A|B)=(d^{\prime}|d-d^{\prime})
, where Alice’s subsystem has
d^{\prime}
parties and Bob’s subsystem has
(d-d^{\prime})
parties. The corresponding bi-partitioning of the total Hilbert space will be:

\underbrace{\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}}_{d^{\prime}}\,\otimes\,\underbrace{\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}}_{d-d^{\prime}}

(2.10)

Tracing out Bob’s system will give us the reduced density matrix on Alice’s system. The diagonal structure of the total density matrix ensures that the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}
will also be a diagonal matrix, with only
n_{0}
non-zero entries and all other entries being zero. Since the diagonal entries are also the eigenvalues of
\rho_{A}
and the zero eigenvalues do not contribute to the entanglement measures, the entanglement structure is insensitive to how many parties are traced out. So without loss of generality, we can assume the bi-partition
(A|B)=(1|d-1)
. Now, tracing out
B
will give a reduced density matrix of order
n_{0}
whose eigenvalues will be given as:

\lambda_{R}=\frac{F_{R}}{\text{NF}}=\frac{1}{\text{NF}}\absolutevalue{\frac{(\mathcal{S}^*\, \mathcal{X}(m)\,\mathcal{T}^{n/m}\, \mathcal{S})_{R0}}{(\text{dim}_qR)^{d-1}}}^{2}\penalty\ ,

(2.11)

where each integrable representation gives one eigenvalue. Our goal is to study the large-party limit, i.e.,
d\to\infty
limit of the entanglement entropy. Let us first examine how the eigenvalues themselves behave in this limit. We will assume that
m
and
n
are finite integers and only
d\to\infty
. From (
2.11
), we see that the explicit dependence of
d
appears only in the term
(\text{dim}_{q}R)^{d-1}
. Let us rewrite (
2.11
) by separating out the
d
dependence:

\lambda_{R}=\frac{M_{R}}{\sum\limits_{P}M_{P}\,(\text{dim}_{q}R/\text{dim}_{q}P)^{2d-2}}\quad;\quad M_{R}=\absolutevalue{(\mathcal{S}^*\, \mathcal{X}(m)\,\mathcal{T}^{n/m}\, \mathcal{S})_{R0}}^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.12)

Now, in our theory, we will have both Abelian and non-Abelian anyons at a given level
k
. The Abelian anyons correspond to those integrable representations
R=R_{a}
which have unit quantum dimension, i.e.
\text{dim}_{q}R_{a}=1
. For non-Abelian anyons at a given value of
k
, the quantum dimension is always greater than 1. So, we can write the following:

\sum_{P}M_{P}\,\left(\frac{\text{dim}_{q}R}{\text{dim}_{q}P}\right)^{2d-2}=\sum_{P\,\in\,\text{Abelian}}M_{P}\,\left(\text{dim}_{q}R\right)^{2d-2}+\sum_{P\,\in\,\text{non-Abelian}}M_{P}\,\left(\frac{\text{dim}_{q}R}{\text{dim}_{q}P}\right)^{2d-2}\penalty\ .

(2.13)

Therefore, for large
d
, we will get the following limit:

\lim_{d\to\infty}\,\sum_{P}M_{P}\,\left(\frac{\text{dim}_{q}R}{\text{dim}_{q}P}\right)^{2d-2}=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{P\,\in\,\text{Abelian}}M_{P}&\text{, \quad when $R\in$ Abelian}\\[8.5359pt]
\infty&\text{, \quad when $R\in$ non-Abelian}\end{cases}\penalty\ .

(2.14)

Thus, the large-party limit of the eigenvalues will be given as:

\lambda_{R}^{\text{LP}}\,\,\equiv\,\,\lim_{d\to\infty}\lambda_{R}\,\,=\,\,\begin{cases}\dfrac{M_{R}}{\sum_{P\,\in\,\text{Abelian}}M_{P}}&\text{, \quad when $R\in$ Abelian}\\[8.5359pt]
0&\text{, \quad when $R\in$ non-Abelian}\end{cases}\penalty\ ,

(2.15)

where it is assumed that
M_{R}
itself does not diverge for any
R
and any
k
, which is true and can be easily verified using numerical checks. The superscript ‘LP’ in
\lambda_{R}^{\text{LP}}
is a notation to say that these are the eigenvalues in the large-party limit. Thus, we see that only those eigenvalues that correspond to Abelian anyons survive. So we get our result:

Result-1:

The topological entanglement measures for the state
\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}
in the large-party limit, i.e.,
d\to\infty
limit, receive contributions only from the Abelian anyons of the theory, and the non-Abelian sectors are suppressed in the large-party limit.

We also know that the Abelian anyons are in one-to-one correspondence with the center
Z_{G}
of the gauge group
G
. Hence, the number of Abelian anyons is equal to the order of
Z_{G}
. This gives us the following result.

Result-2:

The large-party limit, i.e.
d\to\infty
limit, of the entanglement entropy for the state
\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}
will satisfy the bound:

0\leq\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}\leq\ln|Z_{G}|
.

For example, if we consider the SU(
N
) group with level
k
, the Abelian anyons correspond to the following representations:

\text{Abelian anyons}\in\left\{[0,0,\ldots,0]\,\,,\,\,[k,0,0,\ldots,0]\,\,,\,\,[0,k,0,\ldots,0]\,\,,\,\,\ldots\,\,,\,\,[0,0,\ldots,0,k]\right\}\penalty\ .

(2.16)

Hence, in the large-party limit, only those eigenvalues
\lambda_{R}
will survive for which
R
is one of the representations listed above. The value of the entropy
\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}
will, of course depend on
k,
but it will always satisfy the bound
0\leq\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}\leq\ln N
for any
k
.

In the following, we will perform a quantitative analysis for one example: the link state is
\ket{T_{d,\,dn}}
and the gauge group is the SU(2) group. For this particular example, we will study the large-party entanglement entropy for finite
k
and will also analyze its large
k
behavior.

2.2
An example: Quantum state
\ket{T_{d,\,dn}}
and SU(2) group

Let us consider the
m=1
case, so that
n
can be taken as any positive integer. From the equation (
2.4
), we can see that
\mathcal{X}_{QR}(1)=\delta_{QR}
. Hence,
\mathcal{X}(1)
will be an identity matrix. Therefore, we can write:

M_{R}=\absolutevalue{(\mathcal{S}^*\,\mathcal{T}^{n}\, \mathcal{S})_{R0}}^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.17)

The Abelian anyons for the SU(2) group and level
k
are
R=[0]
and
R=[k]
, which are the trivial and spin
k/2
representations, respectively. Hence, in the
d\to\infty

\lambda_{0}=\frac{M_{0}}{M_{0}+M_{k}}\quad;\quad\lambda_{k}=\frac{M_{k}}{M_{0}+M_{k}}\penalty\ .

(2.18)

The entanglement entropy will be given as:

\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}=-(\lambda_{0}\ln\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{k}\ln\lambda_{k})\penalty\ .

(2.19)

In the remainder of this section, we will analyze this entanglement entropy for finite
k
as well as for large

.

2.2.1
Large-party entanglement entropy for finite
k

The modular
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
matrix elements for SU(2) group are given below:

\mathcal{S}_{ab}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{k+2}}\,\sin\left[\frac{(a+1)(b+1)\pi}{k+2}\right]\quad;\quad\mathcal{T}_{ab}=e^{\frac{i\pi a(a+2)}{2(k+2)}}e^{-\frac{i\pi k}{4(k+2)}}\,\delta_{ab}\penalty\ .

(2.20)

Using this, we can numerically compute the numbers
M_{0}
and
M_{k}
and obtain the eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{k}
and hence the large-party entanglement entropy
\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}
. Although we do not have a closed-form expression for the entropy as a function of
k
, we present certain observations in the following.

Result-3:

For the state
\ket{T_{d,\,dn}}
and the group SU(2), the large-party limit of the entanglement entropy shows the following behavior:

\bullet
The states
\ket{T_{d,\,dn}}
are maximally entangled large-party states for odd
n
.

\bullet
The states
\ket{T_{d,\,dn}}
are separable large-party states for even
n
and odd
k
.

The first part of the result is a consequence of the fact that for odd values of
n
, we will have
M_{0}=M_{k}
for any value of
k
, which we have checked numerically. Therefore,
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{k}=1/2
giving the maximum entropy
\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}=\ln 2
. The second part of the result is because for even
n
and odd
k
values, one of the
M_{0}
or
M_{k}
vanishes. As a result, one of the eigenvalues is 0, and the other is 1, resulting in vanishing entropy.
For even
n
and even
k
values, the entropy varies between 0 and
\ln 2
with no fixed pattern at first glance. We present the plots of the
\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}
vs
k
for

in the Figure

.

2.2.2
Large-party entanglement entropy at large
k

Now, let us analyze the semiclassical, i.e., large
k
behavior of the large-party entanglement entropy. For this, we must first obtain the large
k
asymptotics of
M_{0}
and
M_{k}
. Let us define
M_{0}=|L_{0}|^{2}
and
M_{k}=|L_{k}|^{2}
so that we will have:

L_{0}=(\mathcal{S}^{*}\,\mathcal{T}^{n}\,\mathcal{S})_{00}\quad;\quad L_{k}=(\mathcal{S}^{*}\,\mathcal{T}^{n}\,\mathcal{S})_{k0}\penalty\ .

(2.21)

We can rewrite these functions as follows:

L_{0}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{0a}T_{aa}^{n}\mathcal{S}_{a0}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{0a}^{2}T_{aa}^{n}\quad;\quad L_{k}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{ka}T_{aa}^{n}\mathcal{S}_{a0}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}(-1)^{a}\,\mathcal{S}_{0a}^{2}T_{aa}^{n}\penalty\ ,

(2.22)

where we have used the fact
\mathcal{S}_{ka}=(-1)^{a}\mathcal{S}_{0a}
. Now we will substitute the
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
matrix elements of SU(2) group and define
K=(k+2)
so that we can write:

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n(K-2)}{4K}}}{K}\sum_{a=0}^{K-2}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{(a+1)\pi}{K}\right]\,e^{\frac{i\pi na(a+2)}{2K}}

\displaystyle L_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n(K-2)}{4K}}}{K}\sum_{a=0}^{K-2}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{(a+1)\pi}{K}\right]\,e^{i\pi a}e^{\frac{i\pi na(a+2)}{2K}}\penalty\ .

(2.23)

In the large
K
limit, we can convert the sum in
L_{0}
and
L_{k}
to a sum of integrals using the Poisson summation formula, which we have discussed in Appendix A. Defining
x\equiv(a+1)/K
and using the formula (
A.7
), we can write:

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle\,\,\sim\,\,2\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n(K-2)}{4K}}\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n}{2K}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\,\int_{0}^{1}\sin^{2}(\pi x)\,e^{iK\left(\frac{n\pi x^{2}}{2}-2\pi sx\right)}dx

\displaystyle L_{k}

\displaystyle\,\,\sim\,\,2\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n(K-2)}{4K}}e^{i\pi}\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n}{2K}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\,\int_{0}^{1}\sin^{2}(\pi x)\,e^{iK\left(\frac{n\pi x^{2}}{2}+\pi x-2\pi sx\right)}dx\penalty\ .

(2.24)

So now, our aim is to obtain the large
K
asymptotics of the integrals

\displaystyle I_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sin^{2}(\pi x)\,e^{iK\,\phi_{s}(x)}\,dx\quad;\quad\phi_{s}(x)=\frac{n\pi x^{2}}{2}-2\pi sx

\displaystyle J_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sin^{2}(\pi y)\,e^{iK\,\Phi_{s}(y)}\,dy\quad;\quad\Phi_{s}(y)=\frac{n\pi y^{2}}{2}+\pi y-2\pi sy\penalty\ .

(2.25)

We will use the method of the stationary phase for this, which we have discussed in Appendix B. To apply the method of the stationary phase, we need to find the stationary points in the two cases. For the integrals
I_{s}
and
J_{s}
, the stationary points are
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
at which
\phi_{s}^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
and
\Phi_{s}^{\prime}(y_{0})=0
respectively. These points are given as:

x_{0}=\frac{2s}{n}\quad;\quad y_{0}=\frac{2s-1}{n}\penalty\ .

(2.26)

Let us consider the integral
I_{s}
first. Since
n
is a fixed positive integer, the stationary points that lie in the integration range
[0,1]
will occur only for
s=0,1,2,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Here
\left\lfloor a\right\rfloor
is the Floor function, which gives the greatest integer less than or equal to
a
. Since the leading order contribution in the large
K
asymptotics of
I_{s}
comes only from the stationary points that are within the integration range
[0,1]
, we will only consider the contribution of the integrals
I_{s}
where
s=0,1,2,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Thus our original function
L_{0}
and hence
M_{0}
can be approximated as:

L_{0}\,\,\sim\,\,2\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n(K-2)}{4K}}\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n}{2K}}\sum_{s=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}I_{s}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_{0}\,\,\sim\,\,4\absolutevalue{\sum_{s=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor} I_s}^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.27)

Now consider the integral
J_{s}
. Here the stationary points that lie in the integration range
[0,1]
will occur only for
s=1,2,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rfloor
. Hence, we will have:

L_{k}\,\,\sim\,\,2\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n(K-2)}{4K}}\,e^{i\pi}\,e^{-\frac{i\pi n}{2K}}\sum_{s=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rfloor}J_{s}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_{k}\,\,\sim\,\,4\absolutevalue{\sum_{s=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rfloor} J_s}^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.28)

For each stationary point
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, we can apply equations (
B.6
), (
B.11
), or (
B.16
), depending upon whether the stationary point coincides with the endpoints of the integration (which are 0 and 1), or if it is in the interval
(0,1)
. It should be noted here that the function
f(x)=\sin^{2}(\pi x)
and its derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
vanish at both the endpoints, i.e., at
x=0
as well as
x=1
. As a result, the boundary stationary points will only contribute at the order
k^{-3/2}
in both
I_{s}
and
J_{s}
. Also, since both
f(x)
and
f^{\prime}(x)
have non-zero values for
x\in(0,1)
, the stationary points in the interval
(0,1)
will contribute at the order
k^{-1/2}
in both
I_{s}
and
J_{s}
.

There is another subtle point that needs to be mentioned here. In the equations (
B.6
), (
B.11
) and (
B.16
), we notice an exponential term
\exp[iK\phi(x_{0})]
. In our case, this term will be
\exp[-2i\pi(k+2)s^{2}/n]
in
I_{s}
and
\exp[-i\pi(k+2)(2s-1)^{2}/(2n)]
in
J_{s}
respectively. These are not overall phase factors, as they are
s
-dependent and hence the physical results will be sensitive to the value of
k
modulo
n
. Therefore, we must consider
k\equiv r\,(\text{mod $n$})
where
r=0,1,2,\ldots,(n-1)
. For each value of
r
, the large
k
analysis may yield different leading-order coefficients (for the same
n
), and we must keep track of these distinct leading-order coefficients. Our analysis gives the following form of the large
k
behavior of
M_{0}
and
M_{k}
:

M_{0}\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{C(r,n)}{k}&\text{for $n\geq 3$}\\[8.5359pt]
\dfrac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{3}}&\text{for $n=1$}\\[8.5359pt]
\dfrac{\pi^{2}\left((-1)^{r}+1\right)^{2}}{4k^{3}}&\text{for $n=2$}\end{cases}\quad;\quad M_{k}\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{D(r,n)}{k}&\text{for $n\geq 3$}\\[8.5359pt]
\dfrac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{3}}&\text{for $n=1$}\\[8.5359pt]
\dfrac{4}{k}&\text{for $n=2$}\end{cases}\penalty\ .

(2.29)

Here is a brief explanation of these results. For
n=1
, we have
M_{0}\sim 4\absolutevalue{I_0}^{2}
and
M_{k}\sim 4\absolutevalue{J_1}^{2}
. For this case, the stationary point of
I_{0}
is
x_{0}=0
, and the stationary point of
J_{1}
is
y_{0}=1
. Since these are the boundary points and
\sin^{2}(\pi x)
and its derivative vanish at these points, the integrals
I_{0}
and
J_{1}
both go as
k^{-3/2}
, giving a
k^{-3}
behavior of
M_{0}
and
M_{k}
for
n=1
. The relevant formulae here will be (
B.6
) for
I_{0}
and (
B.16
) for
J_{1}
.

For
n=2
, we will have:
M_{0}=4\absolutevalue{I_0+I_1}^{2}
and
M_{k}=4\absolutevalue{J_1}^{2}
. For the case of
M_{0}
, the stationary points of
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
are
x_{0}=0
and
x_{0}=1
respectively. Both being the boundary points, we will get the expected
k^{-3}
behavior of
M_{0}
. However, as discussed earlier, the asymptotics will be sensitive to

. So we may get different results for
r=0
(i.e., when
k
is even) and for
r=1
(i.e., when
k
is odd). In fact, we see that for
r=1
, the leading order coefficient vanishes. This is a consequence of our earlier discussion (after Result-3) that for even
n
and odd

, one of
M_{0}
or
M_{k}
identically vanishes, and for
n=2
, it is
M_{0}
that vanishes for odd

\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}^{\infty}\equiv\lim_{k\to\infty}\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}=-\left[\lambda_{0}^{\infty,\,\text{LP}}\ln\left(\lambda_{0}^{\infty,\,\text{LP}}\right)+\lambda_{k}^{\infty,\,\text{LP}}\ln\left(\lambda_{k}^{\infty,\,\text{LP}}\right)\right]\penalty\ .

(2.33)

At the end, we would like to point out certain identities involving
C(r,n)
and
D(r,n)
. Here they are.

•

For
n=\text{odd}
, we will have:
C(r,n)=D(r,n)
for any
r
.

Proof:
Let
n
be an odd integer. So,
\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor=(n-1)/2
. Now consider the coefficient
D(r,n)
and let us change the summation variable to
s^{\prime}=\frac{n+1}{2}-s
. So we will have:

D(r,n)=\dfrac{8}{n}\absolutevalue{\sum\limits_{s'=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}} e^{-\frac{i \pi(r+2) (2s'-n)^2}{2n}} \sin^2\left(\frac{2\pi s'}{n}-\pi\right)}^{2}=\dfrac{8}{n}\absolutevalue{\sum\limits_{s'=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}} e^{-\frac{2i \pi(r+2) s'^2}{n}} \sin^2\left(\frac{2\pi s'}{n}\right)}^{2}\penalty\

(2.35)

where we expanded
(2s^{\prime}-n)^{2}=4s^{\prime 2}+n^{2}-4ns^{\prime}
and notice that only the first term will appear in the phase. This final expression is identical to
C(r,n)
, and hence the proof.

•

For
n=\text{even}
, one of the
C(r,n)
or
D(r,n)
will be 0 for odd values of
r
.

Proof:
Let
r
be an odd integer where
r=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1
. Also, let
n=2u
. We can write the coefficients as
C(r,n)=\frac{8}{n}\absolutevalue{S_C}^{2}
and
D(r,n)=\frac{8}{n}\absolutevalue{S_D}^{2}
where:

S_{C}=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{u-1}e^{-\frac{i\pi(r+2)s^{2}}{u}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi s}{u}\right)\quad;\quad S_{D}=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{u}e^{-\frac{i\pi(r+2)(1-2s)^{2}}{4u}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi-2\pi s}{2u}\right)\penalty\ .

(2.36)

In the sum
S_{C}
, change the variable to
s^{\prime}=u-s
. Further, in
S_{D}
, change the variable to
s^{\prime}=u+1-s
. In new variables, we can simplify the phases and the
\sin
terms. Comparing it with the
S_{C}
and
S_{D}
written in old variable
s
, we obtain the following identities:

S_{C}=e^{-i\pi ru}\,S_{C}\quad;\quad S_{D}=e^{-i\pi r(u+1)}\,S_{D}\penalty\ .

(2.37)

Since
r
is odd, we clearly see that
S_{C}
vanishes when
u
is odd, and
S_{D}
vanishes when
u
is even. Thus, one of the
C(r,n)
or
D(r,n)
will be 0 when
r
is odd, and
n
is even.

The above two identities tell us that
\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}^{\infty}=\ln 2
for odd values of
n
. Further, for
n
even and
k
odd values,
\text{EE}_{\text{LP}}^{\infty}=0
. These were expected from our earlier Result-3.

3
Conclusion

In this work, we have analyzed the large-party limit of the entanglement entropies associated with generic torus link states
\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}
. These quantum states are prepared in three-dimensional Chern-Simons theory with compact gauge group
G
at Chern-Simons level
k
. The state
\ket{T_{dm,\,dn}}
is associated with the link complement
S^{3}\backslash T_{dm,\,dn}
and can be explicitly calculated using the group theoretic and topological tools available at our disposal. These states are
d
-party quantum states living in a
d
-fold tensor product of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

The entanglement measures associated with this state do not depend on the choice of bi-partition. For simplicity, we therefore consider the bi-partition
(1|d-1)
, where we trace out the
(d-1)
parties, leaving a reduced system consisting of a single party. We compute the entanglement entropy for this reduced system and analyze its behavior as the total number of parties tends to infinity, i.e., in the limit
d\to\infty
. This is what we mean by the large-party limit of the entanglement entropy in this paper.

We have shown that, in the large-party regime, the contributions to the entanglement measures originate exclusively from Abelian anyons, while the non-Abelian sectors are suppressed. This leads to a universal upper bound on the entanglement entropy in the large-party limit, given by
\ln|Z_{G}|
, where
Z_{G}
denotes the center of the gauge group.

We illustrated these general results explicitly for the case
G=\mathrm{SU}(2)
, where the large-party behavior of the entanglement entropy for the state
\ket{T_{d,\,dn}}
was explicitly obtained. After taking the large-party limit, we have further examined the semiclassical (
k\to\infty
) behavior of the entropies and found that the resulting values remain finite and consistent with the expected dominance of Abelian sectors.

These results provide new insight into the large-party structure of quantum entanglement in topological quantum field theories and clarify the role played by the Abelian sectors of the theory. It would be interesting to extend this analysis to generic link complements and to test whether this behavior persists as a universal feature of topological quantum entanglement in more general settings.
