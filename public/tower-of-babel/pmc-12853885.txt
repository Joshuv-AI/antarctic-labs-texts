Association of cervical cancer and intrauterine device use: a narrative review
Authors: Christine M Corrêa, Luiz C Zeferino, Cassia T Juliato, Luis Bahamondes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough intrauterine devices (IUDs) are widely used as a contraceptive method, their association with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia remains a subject of debate. Some studies suggest a potential protective effect, possibly through local immune modulation, while others report no significant association. Evidence, however, remains scarce and inconsistent, particularly regarding long-term IUDs use. Our study aims to address these gaps by exploring the relationship between IUDs use and cervical cancer within the broader context of cervical cancer prevention. We conducted a narrative review by searching the PubMed, Scopus, LILACS, and Cochrane databases, utilizing a combination of text and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. The search terms included “intrauterine contraception,” “intrauterine devices,” “hormonal intrauterine device,” “IUD,” “LNG-IUS” (levonorgestrel intrauterine system), and “cervical cancer,” “cervical lesions,” and “cervical cancer risk,” for publications available up to July 2024. Our review included 16 studies of fair to good quality, employing rigorous methodologies and addressing key confounding variables to evaluate the association between IUDs use and the risk of cervical cancer. Among these, eight studies reported that IUD use was associated with a lower risk of cervical cancer, while six studies identified a trend toward reduced risk, although without significant differences. Conversely, two studies found an association between IUD use and a higher risk of cervical cancer. A critical limitation in many of these studies was the lack of information regarding the specific type of IUD used by the study populations. These findings underscore the need for further research to clarify the association between IUDs use and cervical cancer risk, with a focus on long-term outcomes and stratification by IUD type.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04239-4.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Intrauterine devices, Narrative review, Risk factors

Background

Previous studies have investigated the relationship between intrauterine device (IUD) use and the development of cervical cancer, yielding conflicting results. Some research suggests that copper (Cu)-IUDs may induce localized inflammatory responses that enhance immune surveillance, potentially lowering the risk of cervical dysplasia. Conversely, other studies report no significant association or a slight increase in cervical cancer risk, possibly influenced by confounding factors such as sexual behavior or exposure to HPV. The type of IUD may also play a role, with hormonal IUDs potentially exerting different effects due to alterations in cervical mucus and endometrial changes. However, critical gaps remain in the literature, including limited data on long-term outcomes, insufficient stratification by IUD type, and variations across diverse populations. Our study aims to address these gaps and clarify the association between IUD use and cervical cancer risk, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of this complex relationship.

Cu- and levonorgestrel (LNG)-IUDs are highly effective and long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) methods [1]. The introduction of different LNG-IUDs in many markets was probably an element which helped with the increased use of IUDs. The LNG 52-mg IUD is associated with non-contraceptive benefits such as the reduction of bleeding in women with heavy menstrual bleeding [2]. Recently, several regulatory agencies approved the use as contraceptive up to 8 years and the TCu380A IUD is approved up to 10 years; however, it was tested as a contraceptive without replacement until menopause in women over 35 years old [3].

Cervical cancer is considered a sexually transmitted infection (STI), and the high-risk HPV is the main cause of cervical cancer and its precursor lesions. In fact, most HPV infections are transient, clearing spontaneously, and the actual risk is represented by the persistent infections. Therefore, other factors are involved in the risk or in cervical carcinogenesis as risk factors favoring the acquisition or persistence of HPV infection, such as age of sexual debut, multiparity, lifetime number of sexual partners, long-term use of combined hormonal contraceptives, tobacco smoking, and immune system deficiency [4, 5]. It is more often diagnosed in women living in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) and settings with lower access to healthcare services with high rates of death, rather than in resource-high income nations and populations [6].

A low risk of cervical cancer and IUD use was described in 1976 [7]; however, the results of several reports have been inconsistent, and, in many cases, there is a lack of control for confounding variables, such as coitarche age, number of partners and consistent use of male or female condoms [8–11]. The main report came from Castellsagué et al. [8], who conducted a study with pooled analysis of over 20,000 women to evaluate the relationship between IUD use and the risk of acquiring HPV infection and progression to cervical cancer. After adjusting for confounding variables like age, schooling, cohabitation status, level of cervical cytology, parity, condom use and number of sexual partners, the study found an inverse association between IUD use and the risk of cervical cancer (Odds Risk [OR], 0.55; 95% Confidence Interval [CI], 0.42;0.70; p < 0.000). Specifically, the risk of squamous cell carcinoma was reduced (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.43;0.72; p < 0.000), and the risk for adenocarcinoma and adenosquamous carcinoma was also lower (OR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.22;0.97; p = 0.035).

However, a recent study showed that IUD use was associated with an increased risk of developing precursor lesions such as intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 (CIN 3) [12]. Due to the debate about the benefits of IUD use concerning cervical cancer risk, the objective of our narrative review is to evaluate the association between IUD and the risk of cervical cancer.

Materials and methods

We conducted a narrative review. This approach employed a comprehensive bibliographic research strategy and a systematic methodology to evaluate the evidence regarding the association between IUD use and the risk of cervical cancer and its precursor lesions. Narrative reviews provide a critical analysis of existing literature on a topic, help researchers understand current knowledge, identify gaps, and suggest directions for future research and it is valuable for continuing education and offering new perspectives on established fields [13–15].

For our review, we employed a custom checklist to assess the overall relevance and rigor of the included studies, particularly when standardized tools were not applicable. This checklist focused on the alignment of study objectives with the review’s research question, the transparency and replicability of the methodology, and the clarity and depth of the reported findings. This systematic approach ensured that only studies meeting adequate quality and relevance standards were included in the evidence synthesis presented in this review.

We searched the PubMed, Scopus, LILACS, and Cochrane databases using a combination of text and MeSH terms to identify English-language publications reporting on IUD use and cervical cancer or its precursor lesions. The search terms included “intrauterine contraception,” “intrauterine device,” “hormonal intrauterine device,” “IUD,” “LNG-IUS” AND “cervical cancer,” “cervical lesions,” and “cervical cancer risk.” The search covered all publications from database inception through July 2024. Duplicates, reviews, case reports, case series, and abstracts without full-text availability were excluded.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Among the 163 records screened, 16 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). These comprised 7 case–control studies, 5 cohort studies, 3 pooled or multicenter analyses, and 1 population-based retrospective study. The studies covered a broad geographic range, including Europe, North America, Asia, and Africa. Sample sizes varied widely, from 272 to over 700,000 participants. Most studies focused on adult women aged 18–50 years. However, variation in study design and population characteristics contributed to the heterogeneity of findings. Reporting on IUD types was inconsistent; only six studies clearly distinguished between Cu- and hormonal IUDs. These 16 studies were of fair to good quality. Many of the studies were not associated with any abnormal Pap tests performed after IUD placement, suggesting a low risk of cervical cancer. Of these, eight studies [7, 8, 10, 11, 16–19] showed results in which IUD use was associated with a low risk of cervical cancer and six studies [4, 20–24] reported data in which IUD use was associated with a trend towards a low risk of cervical cancer; however, there was no statistical significance and two studies [12, 25] provided data in which IUD use was associated with a high risk of cervical cancer. Most studies did not report on the specific types of IUDs used by the study populations; some focused-on copper IUDs, although several may have included hormonal-IUD users.

Characteristics of studies in which IUD use is associated with low risk of cervical cancer and precursor lesions

Of the eight studies which reported a low risk of cervical cancer associated with IUD user (Table 1), four were case–control [7, 16–18], three were cohort studies [10, 11, 19] and one used a pooled analysis design from two large studies [8]. Of the four case–control studies, all of them included histologically confirmed cases [7, 16–18]. Cases were identified through population-based registries [7, 8, 10, 19] or hospital records [11, 16–18] and controls were most often population-based; one study used population-, clinic- and hospital-based control subjects [8] while another used one hospital- and one community-control series [16]. These studies typically featured large samples, robust adjustment for HPV and screening history, and often included long-term IUD users.

Both retrospective cohort studies conducted in Denmark [10] and USA [11] included Cu-IUD and levonorgestrel 52 mg IUD. One recent study focused on the levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD [19]. While only three studies [10, 11, 19] reported on the type of IUDs used, it is likely that other studies primarily included inert (an IUD which their use was stopped decades ago) and Cu-IUD.

A study conducted in China reported on 272 patients with histologically confirmed invasive squamous cell cervical cancer diagnosed between 1989 and 1991 and 893 population-based control subjects. Women who were younger than 33 years old at their first use of an IUD experienced a decreased risk of cervical cancer (OR = 0.5, 95% CI 0.3;0.9). The risk was further reduced among those who used an IUD for 11 years or more (OR = 0.4, 95% CI 0.2;1.0). Therefore, young age at the initial IUD use and longer duration of use were both associated with a significantly lower risk of cervical cancer [17].

The major study from Castellsagué and co-workers suggested that IUD use is associated with lower risk in cervical carcinogenesis and that the cellular immunity triggered by the device might be one of several mechanisms that could explain those findings [8]. After this, a large retrospective cohort study was conducted in Denmark [10], including Cu-IUD- and levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD-users, compared with combined oral contraceptive (COC) users. When Cu-IUD- and levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD-users were compared with COC users, the risks of CIN3 + were lower: aRR 0.58 (95% CI 0.52;0.65) and aRR 0.63 (95% CI 0.57;0.69), respectively.

A nationwide study analyzing only levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD use included 514,719 women aged 18 to 50 years from the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register; they were randomly matched with 1,544,157 women who did not use the levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD at a ratio of 1:3. The use of levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD was associated with a 9% reduced risk of cervical cancer (adjusted HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.84;0.99) compared to women who did not use that IUD [19].

Characteristics of studies in which IUD use is associated with a trend to low risk of cervical cancer and precursor lesions

Of the six identified studies in which the authors only reported a trend towards a low risk of cervical cancer and IUD use (Table 2), five were case–control studies [20–24] and one was a prospective cohort [4]. All six studies included histologically confirmed results of cervical lesions [4, 20–24]. Case patients were identified through population-based cancer registries [24] and hospital records [20–23]. Most control subjects were population-based [20, 21, 24] and two studies were hospital-based control studies [22, 23].

In a large prospective cohort study including 367,993 women in 10 European countries, cases of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 (CIN 3)/carcinoma in situ (CIS) and invasive cervical cancer (ICC) were identified through several methods, including population- and hospital-based cancer registries, health insurance records, and pathology registries [4]. Only two studies specified the exposure to two types of IUD [21], while the others did not report the IUDs used by the study participants [4, 20, 22, 23].

A US-based case-control study included 479 cases with incident invasive cervical cancer identified through 24 hospitals from 1982 to 1984 and 789 population-based control subjects [21]. Having ever used an IUD was not associated with cervical cancer (OR = 0.8, 95% CI 0.5;1.2), and the risk did not vary with the duration of IUD use. In examinations by IUD type in this study, Cu-IUD use was associated with a non-significant decreased risk (OR = 0.6, 95% CI 0.3;1.2), while no effect was seen for inert IUDs (OR = 1.1, 95% CI 0.9;1.7) when compared with never users.

One of the studies conducted in northern Italy included 720 patients with histologically confirmed invasive cancer diagnoses and 820 hospital-based control subjects [22]. A total of 20 (2.8%) cases and 38 (4.6%) controls had used an IUD. Using an IUD was associated with a non-significant decreased risk for cervical cancer (OR = 0.6, 95% CI 0.3;1.0), with no difference in effect by duration or time since last use. However, the estimated OR was statistical significance (possibly due to the small number of users) and no trend in risk was found with duration of use.

Among the 308,036 women included in the final analysis of the single multicenter prospective cohort study in Europe [4], 1,065 cases were identified: 261 ICC cases and 804 CIN3/CIS cases. Concerning IUD use, a non-significant reduced risk of ICC with ever used an IUD was found in the nested case-control analysis (OR = 0.6). Analyses restricted to all cases and HPV seropositive controls yielded similar results, revealing a significant inverse association with IUD for combined CIN3/CIS and ICC (OR = 0.7).

One of the six studies reported the analysis of both Cu-IUD and levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD. This case-control study with recent IUD use found a trend toward a protective effect of Cu-IUDs, which may be associated with CIN2 but not CIN3, for the levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD [24]. The observed association between the levonorgestrel 52-mg IUDs and CIN2 + was modest but warrants further investigation. The absence of statistical significance in these studies may reflect insufficient sample size or underpowered analyses rather than a lack of association.

Characteristics of studies in which IUD use is associated with high risk of cervical cancer and precursor lesions

Although previous studies have reported the relationship between IUD use and a low risk or trend towards a low risk of cervical cancer and precursor lesions, inverse results have been reported by two studies of our review [12, 25] (Table 3). However, these data are mostly from older studies, and more recent epidemiological studies on the effect of IUDs on cervical carcinogenesis are missing.

A clinic-based case-control study among American Indian women [25] included 628 patients with histologically confirmed CIN 1, CIN 2, and CIN 3. The study initially reported that ever-use of an IUD (OR 3.0; 95% CI, 1.4;6.1) and current use (OR 4.1; 95% CI, 1.1;14.6) were associated with CIN 2/3. However, after adjusting for confounders such as age, number of sexual partners, age at first intercourse, parity, smoking, and oral contraceptive use in a multivariable regression model, ever-use of an IUD was no longer significantly associated with CIN 2/3. While the regression results provide a more robust analysis, this study was included in our review because its unadjusted findings contribute to the broader discussion of conflicting evidence regarding the relationship between IUD use and cervical pathology. Additionally, the study’s limitations—such as the absence of data on specific IUD types—underscore the need for further research in this area.

The other study, a population-based retrospective cohort conducted in the Netherlands [12], included 702,037 women with a median follow-up of 9.7 years, of which 6,705 (0.96%) and 559 (0.08%) women developed histologically confirmed CIN3 and cervical cancer, respectively. IUD use was associated with an increased risk of developing CIN3+ (RR 1.51, 95% CI 1.32;1.74) but did not significantly influence the risk of cervical cancer (RR 1.21, 95% CI 0.71;2.07). The types of IUDs used by the participants were either Cu-IUD or levonorgestrel 52-mg IUD. The findings in this group should be interpreted cautiously, as they were based on older data or lacked adequate adjustment for confounding variables.

Confounding variables

A central challenge in evaluating the association between IUD use and cervical cancer risk is the variability in how individual studies controlled confounding factors. As summarized in Table 4, the most controlled confounders were age at sexual debut, parity, number of sexual partners, tobacco use, and HPV status. However, the extent of control varied widely across studies, with only a few accountings for the full spectrum of relevant variables. Studies reporting a lower risk of cervical cancer associated with IUD use generally demonstrated stronger control for key confounders, particularly HPV infection and Pap smear screening history [8, 10, 11, 19]. This suggests that the observed protective association may be more robust in analyses with more comprehensive adjustment. Conversely, studies reporting no significant association or a higher risk [12, 25] often employed less comprehensive control of confounding variables or presented unadjusted results, limiting the interpretability of their findings. Overall, our analysis suggests that differences in study outcomes may be partially attributable to inconsistencies in how key risk factors were addressed. This underscores the importance of standardized confounder control in future research to enable more reliable comparisons. Discussion

Several studies have indicated a lower risk of cervical cancer among users of both Cu-and levonorgestrel 52-mg IUDs. Some reports also suggest a trend toward reduced cervical cancer risk with IUD use, even when the results are not statistically significant, as the 95% confidence intervals cross 1.0. Although many of the studies included in our review do not differentiate between types of IUDs, we acknowledge this as a key limitation and emphasize that Cu- and hormonal-IUDs may have distinct effects on the cervical environment and immune response.

The strongest evidence linking IUD use and low risk of cervical cancer and precursor lesions comes from two large, well-designed studies [8, 12]. These studies are notable for their robust methodology, comprehensive adjustment for confounders, and generalizable findings. Additionally, Averbach et al. [24] provide insights into the biological mechanisms underlying these associations. In contrast, findings from older studies (e.g., Sandmire et al. [7], William et al. [23]) and small regional studies (e.g., Hammouda et al. [18], Yi et al. [19]) require cautious interpretation due to methodological limitations and potential unmeasured confounding. Collectively, this body of evidence underscores both the complexity of assessing IUD-related risks and the need for further research employing contemporary methodologies.

Because of the limited extent of association between IUD use and the risk of cervical cancer in the studies, we considered a variety of sources for a spurious relationship. We consider early sexual debut and the number of lifetime sexual partners to be confounding variables that can determine whether a woman will or will not develop cervical cancer and precursor lesions. The reduced risk of cervical cancer associated with IUD use most likely reflects the influence of uncontrolled confounding variables because many women could present low risk profiles (e.g. were of high social class, had few births, more recent Pap smear evaluations, and low rates of positivity to HPV). In addition, the low risk of cervical cancer in users of IUDs is probably associated with Pap smear screening because IUD users may have more contact with healthcare services and more opportunities for screening or treatment as well because women who had been screened positive should not be a control. Furthermore, it is important to consider that may be a healthcare provider bias because they may not suggest an IUD in women with high-risk features, such as multiple partners.

One of the mechanisms for the low risk of cervical cancer associated with IUD use could be that the IUD generates an inflammatory response or immune modulation in the endocervical canal, which could lower the risk of HPV infection, or that the IUD strings in the endocervix produce regular exfoliation of the cervical tissue, possibly acting as a protective cofactor in cervical carcinogenesis. In addition, it is possible that IUDs might exert this protective effect through the induction of a reactive, chronic, low-grade, sterile inflammatory response in the endometrium, endocervical canal and cervix that could modify the course of HPV infections by promoting changes in the local mucosal immune status. It is possible that these IUD-related mechanisms induce an immune deviation with a Th1-type biased immune response, which might affect IUD users’ risk of HPV persistence, progression to cervical cancer, or both. Also, for hormonal IUDs, the release of progestin into the uterus might affect the natural history of HPV infection [8].

Another possible explanation for the protective effect of IUDs against cervical cancer is elimination of pre-invasive cervical lesions when the device is inserted or removed. The traumatic manipulation of the cervical tissue during IUD placement or removal and during its use induces a combination of acute and chronic inflammatory effects, as well as a long-lasting immune response, as noted in patients after cervical punch biopsies. This hypothesis would better explain the immediate protective effect found for short-term users and the observation that there was no difference in the protective effect following long-term IUD use [8, 9].

Although these possible mechanisms are speculative, they emphasize our limited knowledge and the need for other studies to explore the underlying mechanisms by which IUDs might be associated with a low risk or a trend towards a low risk of cervical cancer.

The study design influences the findings. It is common for the history of contraception use to be determined at the time of detection of the disease in case-control studies or at the time of enrollment in prospective clinic-based investigations. This leads to confounding results where contraceptive methods which may increase risk (such as IUDs or other hormonal contraceptive methods) are being compared with methods with potential protective effects, such as use of male or female condoms or diaphragms. Therefore, the “high risk” group is then compared with a “low risk” (or protected) group. However, women who are currently using one type of contraceptive method are likely to have tried one or more of the other methods. Designs allowing for analysis of the lifetime use of various contraceptives (ideally weighted by exposure) would best model the risk or protection offered by each method.

Furthermore, although we were concerned that IUD users might have greater access to Pap smear screening and thus a higher probability of disease detection, associations were observed among both those with and without recent Pap smears, arguing against a detection bias. In fact, controlling the interval since last Pap smear considerably strengthened the observed associations.

We believe that rigorous investigation, even in case of observational studies, may reveal patterns of low risks of IUD use and cervical cancer. That information is needed, particularly as it pertains to disease burden in developing countries and settings. In summary, we consider that the available data is conflicting, and it does not indicate an increased risk of cervical cancer and its precursor lesions.

Limitations

Our review is subject to several limitations. First, only four databases (PubMed, Scopus, LILACS, and Cochrane) were searched, potentially excluding relevant studies indexed elsewhere. Second, the narrative nature of this review—while allowing for flexibility—lacks the methodological rigor and reproducibility of a systematic review or meta-analysis. Third, publication bias may be present, as studies with significant findings are more likely to be published. Finally, although we attempted to account for confounding variables, differences in study quality, population characteristics, and outcome definitions may limit the generalizability of our findings. As this is a narrative review, formal risk of bias assessment tools such as ROBINS-I or ROBINS-II were not used, as these tools are specifically designed for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Conclusions

The review identified 16 articles on the proposed topic, eight of which demonstrated a low risk of cervical cancer, and its precursor lesions associated with IUD use. Based on the available literature, there is no evidence to suggest an increased risk of cervical cancer or its precursor lesions among IUD users. The current body of evidence does not indicate a consistent increase or decrease in cervical cancer risk associated with IUD use. Findings remain inconclusive, partly due to heterogeneity in study designs and populations, as well as limited information on the type of IUD used. Future systematic reviews and meta-analyses are warranted to clarify this association using standardized methodologies and formal risk-of-bias assessments.
