Efficient, Low-Regret, Online Reinforcement Learning for Linear MDPs
Philips George John, Arnab Bhattacharyya, Silviu Maniu, Dimitrios Myrisiotis, Zhenan Wu

Abstract
Reinforcement learning algorithms are usually stated without theoretical guarantees regarding their performance. Recently, Jin, Yang, Wang, and Jordan (COLT 2020) showed a polynomial-time reinforcement learning algorithm (namely, LSVI-UCB) for the setting of linear Markov decision processes, and provided theoretical guarantees regarding its running time and regret. In real-world scenarios, however, the space usage of this algorithm can be prohibitive due to a utilized linear regression step. We propose and analyze two modifications of LSVI-UCB, which alternate periods of learning and not-learning, to reduce space and time usage while maintaining sublinear regret. We show experimentally, on synthetic data and real-world benchmarks, that our algorithms achieve low space usage and running time, while not significantly sacrificing regret.



1
Introduction

In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent interacts with an environment by taking actions and receiving rewards.
The objective is to either reach a goal, or maximize cumulative reward over time.
Some well known applications are robot control, game playing, and recommendation systems.
The main components of reinforcement learning are the agent and the environment, which continuously interact with each other.
These algorithms learn by trial and error, and the agent’s behavior is shaped by the rewards it receives.

Research on RL has seen many breakthroughs since the Deep Q-Learning paper
[
MKS+13
]
.

[
MKS+15
]
introduced the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm, which utilized a convolutional neural network to approximate the action-value function
Q
.

[
MBM+16
]
introduced the Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C) algorithm, which enabled parallelization of RL training across multiple CPU cores.

[
SWD+17
]
introduced the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, which is a policy optimization method for RL.
PPO strikes a balance between sample efficiency and stability, making it widely adopted in various RL applications.

[
SSS+18
]
introduced AlphaZero, an RL algorithm that achieved superhuman performance in the game of Go, chess, and shogi.
AlphaZero demonstrated the power of RL in learning complex strategies without prior human knowledge.

Most RL algorithms use a lot of space, prohibiting their use in cases where the available memory is limited.
Some approaches, such as aggregated memory for RL, aim to improve sample efficiency and resilience to noise by using order-invariant functions in conjunction with standard memory modules.
Additionally, the size of the replay buffer is a crucial hyperparameter in experience replay, which can significantly impact the speed of learning and the quality of the resulting policy.

Closer to our focus,
[
JYWJ23
]
introduced and analyzed the Least-Squares Value Iteration with Upper Confidence Bound (LSVI-UCB) algorithm.
The original algorithm, namely LSVI, uses
linear function approximation
for the design of optimal policies for problems with a
very large state space
.
LSVI-UCB adds
provable
performance guarantees based on the notion of
regret
, generally used in the context on multi-armed bandits.
Currently, this is among the few approaches that provide theoretical bounds on RL regret.
The issue with LSVI-type algorithms, and more generally those that are based on linear models, is that they require a large amount of memory to store the samples and perform regressions for estimating the
Q
-values.
This is a significant limitation in practice, as the memory requirements can be prohibitive for large-scale problems, or when one desires to implement these algorithms on low-powered embedded devices, such as those used in Internet of Things scenarios.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this paper we propose two variants of the LSVI-UCB algorithm whose objective is to minimize space usage.
The reasons why we focus on LSVI-UCB, compared to other existing approaches, are as follows:
(i) The model used by the LSVI-UCB, linear regression, is simple but still widely applicable in real-world scenarios; and
(ii) LSVI-UCB is an approach that is founded on theoretical guarantees
[
JYWJ23
]
, something that many of the heuristic approaches currently employed in practice (such as those based on neural networks, for example) are lacking.

To achieve better space and time efficiency, we modify LSVI-UCB to alternate periods of learning with periods where samples are discarded, along with a careful reset of the workspace according to the number of episodes that have elapsed.

In particular, our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We prove that an LSVI-UCB variant where the learning intervals are of
fixed size
,
LSVI-UCB-Fixed
(
Algorithm

2
), achieves a practical space-to-regret trade-off by appropriately resetting the work space after a fixed number of episodes has passed.
This variant yields better running time and space usage than LSVI-UCB, while maintaining sublinear regret.

2.

The second variant that we introduce
adapts
the length of the learning intervals by keeping track of a proxy measure, that is faster to compute compared to the actual regression step, based on the deviation of the projection matrix used in LSVI-UCB.
We show that this variant, namely
LSVI-UCB-Adaptive
(see
Algorithm

3
), minimizes space usage as well and that it has better running time than LSVI-UCB.
As in
Algorithm

2
, this algorithm too utilizes workspace resets that alleviate space usage and empirically keeps regret within reasonable bounds.

3.

We show experimentally that our algorithms minimize space usage and keep regret low, even for arbitrary linear MDPs.

Regarding the significance of the problems that we are considering, one might wonder whether linear MDPs exist in real-world scenarios.
Orthogonally to the above contributions, we take first steps in this direction by taking a real-world benchmark and linearizing (that is, transforming the space-action state to a linear one, by learning a linear relation) it to show that our algorithm can be applied to more general benchmarks (see
Section

5.1
).
As an aside, linearizing real-world problems may be a problem of independent interest.

Other Related Work

We continue in this section the discussion of related work that we started in
Section

1
.

Linear MDPs.

[
LCC+21
]
focus on a scenario with value-based linear representation, which postulates linear realizability of the optimal Q-function (also called the “linear
Q^{*}
problem”).
They make progress towards understanding this linear
Q^{*}
problem by investigating a new sampling protocol, which draws samples in an online/exploratory fashion but allows one to backtrack and revisit previous states.

[
PTP+21
]
study the role of the representation of state-action value functions in regret minimization in finite-horizon MDPs with linear structure.

[
WCS+22
]
obtain first-order regret bounds scaling not as the worst-case but with some measure of the performance of the optimal policy in the linear MDP setting.

[
HCZ+22
]
propose a formulation for deployment-efficient reinforcement learning (DE-RL) from an “optimization with constraints” perspective.
They are interested in exploring an MDP and obtaining a near-optimal policy within minimal deployment complexity, whereas in each deployment the policy can sample a large batch of data.

[
ZRY+22
]
study an alternative definition of linear MDPs that automatically ensures normalization while allowing efficient representation of learning via contrastive estimation.

[
HZZG23
]
focus on episodic time-inhomogeneous linear Markov decision processes whose transition probability can be parameterized as a linear function of a given feature mapping, and they propose the first computationally efficient algorithm that achieves nearly optimal regret.

UCRL and UCRL2.

[
AO06
]
present a learning algorithm (UCRL) for undiscounted reinforcement learning.
They use upper confidence bounds to show that UCRL achieves logarithmic online regret in the number of steps taken with respect to an optimal policy.

[
AJO08
]
present a variant of the UCRL algorithm, namely UCRL2.
UCRL2 defines a set of statistically plausible MDPs given the observations so far, and chooses an optimistic MDP (with respect to the achievable average reward) among these plausible MDPs.
Then it executes a policy which is (nearly) optimal for the optimistic MDP.

Bandits.

A simpler setting than that of reinforcement learning is multi-armed-bandits, where multiple states are not considered (that is, there is only one state).
However, many of the techniques developed for bandits can be adapted to reinforcement learning, especially the notion of regret for linear bandits.
A comprehensive resource on bandits is
[
LS20
]
.

[
PK23
]
propose a new algorithm that combines concepts from the reinforcement learning domain of multi-armed bandits and random search strategies from the domain of genetic algorithms to solve discrete stochastic optimization problems via simulation.
In particular, their focus is on noisy large-scale problems, which often involve a multitude of dimensions as well as multiple local optima.

[
DVLH23
]
consider the challenging scenario of contextual bandits with continuous actions and large input “context” spaces, e.g., images.
They posit that by modifying reinforcement learning algorithms for continuous control, they can outperform hand-crafted contextual bandit algorithms for continuous actions on standard benchmark datasets, i.e. vector contexts.

[
Lin24
]
presents an overview of recent advancements of reinforcement learning and bandits, and discusses how they can be effectively employed to solve speech and natural language processing problems with models that are adaptive and scalable.

Sketching.

One possible approach to reducing the space complexity of reinforcement learning is to use sketching, especially in the case of linear MDPs.

[
BVB12
]
investigate the application of hashing to linear value function approximation.

[
AKL17
]
describe a framework for multitask deep reinforcement learning guided by policy sketches.
To learn from sketches, they present a model that associates every subtask with a modular subpolicy, and jointly maximizes reward over full task-specific policies by tying parameters across shared subpolicies.

[
TSBV18
]
introduce a new sub-linear space sketch — the Weight-Median Sketch — for learning compressed linear classifiers over data streams while supporting the efficient recovery of large-magnitude weights in the model.
This enables memory-limited execution of several statistical analyses over streams, including online feature selection, streaming data explanation, relative deltoid detection, and streaming estimation of pointwise mutual information.

[
SWYZ23
]
propose a novel sketching scheme for the first order method in large-scale distributed learning setting, such that the communication costs between distributed agents are saved while the convergence of the algorithms is still guaranteed.
Using their framework, they develop algorithms for federated learning with lower communication costs.

Other LSVI or LSVI-UCB Extensions.

[
SSX21
]
present the first provable Least-Squares Value Iteration (LSVI) algorithm that achieves runtime complexity sub-linear in the number of actions.
They formulate the value function estimation procedure in value iteration as an approximate maximum inner product search problem and propose a locality sensitive hashing (LSH) type data structure to solve this problem with sublinear time complexity.

[
ZCVJ22
]
consider reinforcement learning in episodic MDPs with linear function approximation under drifting environments.
They first develop the LSVI-UCB-Restart algorithm, an optimistic modification of least-squares value iteration combined with periodic restart, and establish its dynamic regret bound when variation budgets are known.
They then propose a parameter-free algorithm, Ada-LSVI-UCB-Restart, that works without knowing the variation budgets, but with a slightly worse dynamic regret bound.

Adaptivity.

[
WZG21
]
study RL with linear function approximation under the adaptivity constraint.
They consider two popular limited adaptivity models:
The batch learning model and the rare policy switch model, and propose two efficient online RL algorithms for episodic linear Markov decision processes, where the transition probability and the reward function can be represented as a linear function of some known feature mapping.

[
XWY24
]
take the first step in studying general sequential decision-making under the two adaptivity constraints.
They provide a general class called the Eluder Condition (EC) class, which includes a wide range of reinforcement learning classes.
Then, for the rare policy switch constraint, they provide a generic algorithm to achieve a
\widetilde{O}\!\left(\log K\right)
regret on the EC class. For the batch learning constraint, they provide an algorithm that provides a
\widetilde{O}\!\left(\sqrt{K}+K/B\right)
regret with the number of batches
B
.

Let

For a vector

, we denote by

the
transpose of
v

For vectors

dot product of
x
and

Theorem 1

(Sherman-Morrison)
.

Suppose

is an invertible square matrix and

are vectors.
Then
A+uv^{\top}
is invertible if and only if

and

\left(A+uv^{\top}\right)^{-1}=A^{-1}-\left(A^{-1}uv^{\top}A^{-1}\right)/\left(1+v^{\top}A^{-1}u\right).

We denote by

the
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is the set of all vectors in

with non-negative entries that sum to

.
We use standard asymptotic notation, such as

and
\Omega\!\left(\cdot\right)
, whereby
\widetilde{O}\!\left(\cdot\right)
and
\widetilde{\Omega}\!\left(\cdot\right)

3.1
Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)

We adapt here the formalization of
[
JYWJ23
]
.
We define an
episodic Markov Decision Process (episodic MDP)
as a tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},H,\mathbb{P},r)
, where

\mathcal{S}
is the set of
states
,

\mathcal{A}
is the set of possible
actions
,

H\in\mathbb{N}
is the
planning horizon
,

\mathbb{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
,
\mathbb{P}_{h}:S\times A\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, is the set of
transition functions
; where
P_{h}
maps each state-action pair
(s_{h},a_{h})
to a distribution over
\mathcal{S}
which is used to sample the next state
s_{h+1}
,

r=\{r_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
,
r_{h}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta([0,1])
, is the set of
reward functions
, where
R_{h}
maps a state-action pair
(s_{h},a_{h})
to a distribution over
[0,1]
which is used to sample the stochastic reward
r_{h}
.

For a
linear MDP
that we consider in this paper, we use the definition of
[
JYWJ23
]
, i.e., an MDP whose transition and reward functions for each step
h
can be expressed as the dot product of a mapping depending only on state and action with a step-specific vector.

Definition 2

(Linear MDP)
.

The tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},H,\mathbb{P},r)
is a linear MDP with a feature map
\phi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if for any
h\in[H]
, there exist
d
unknown (signed) measures
\mu_{h}=(\mu_{h,1},\dots,\mu_{h,d})
over
\mathcal{S}
and an unknown vector
\theta_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that for any
(x,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{h}\!\left(\cdot|x,a\right)=\left\langle\phi\!\left(x,a\right),\mu_{h}\right\rangle
and
r_{h}\!\left(x,a\right)=\left\langle\phi\!\left(x,a\right),\theta_{h}\right\rangle
.

Note that since the reward functions are bounded in
[0,1]
, the value functions are bounded in
[0,H]
.
Moreover, we set
T:=HK
.

We are interested in analyzing how an agent interacts with some MDP.
Initially, the agent starts at some initial state
s_{1}
chosen by an adversary.
At each step
h\in[H]
, the agent observes the state
s_{h}\in\mathcal{S}
, picks an action
a_{h}\in\mathcal{A}
, receives a reward
r_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})
, and the MDP evolves into a new state
s_{h+1}
that is drawn from the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{h}(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h})
.
The episode terminates when
s_{H+1}
is reached.

A
policy

\pi:\mathcal{S}\times H\to\mathcal{A}
is a mapping such that
\pi(s,h)
gives a probability distribution on
\mathcal{A}
conditioned on the MDP state being at state
s
at the
h
-th step.
For each
h\in[H]
, we define the value function
V_{h}^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
as the expected value of cumulative rewards received under policy
\pi
when starting from some arbitrary state at the
h
-th step.
That is,

V_{h}^{\pi}\!\left(x\right):=\Exp\!\left[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}r_{h^{\prime}}\!\left(s_{h^{\prime}},\pi\!\left(s_{h^{\prime}},h^{\prime}\right)\right)|s_{h}=x\right]

for all
x\in\mathcal{S}
and
h\in\left[h\right]
.
We also define the action-value function
Q^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
as the expected cumulative rewards when policy
\pi
comes in only one step later, and we are fixed with action
a
at current state
s
.
That is,

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{\pi}\!\left(s,a\right):=r_{h}\!\left(s,a\right)+\Exp\!\left[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h+1}^{H}r_{h^{\prime}}\!\left(s_{h^{\prime}},\pi\!\left(s_{h^{\prime}},h^{\prime}\right)\right)|s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a\right]

for all
\left(s,a\right)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and
h\in\left[H\right]
.

An important property of linear MDPs is that, for any policy, the action-value functions are linear in the feature map
\phi
, so it suffices to focus on linear action-value functions.

Proposition 3

(
[
JYWJ23
]
)
.

For a linear MDP, for any policy
\pi
, there exist weights
\left\{w_{h}^{\pi}\right\}_{h}\in[H]
such that for any
(s,a,h)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times[H]
, we have
Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)=\left\langle\phi\!\left(s,a\right),w_{h}^{\pi}\right\rangle
.

Since the action spaces and the episode length are both finite, there always exists an optimal policy
\pi
which gives the optimal value
V_{h}^{*}(s)=\sup_{\pi}V_{h}^{\pi}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
h\in[H]
.
The existence of such optimal policies is a topological consequence of fixed point theorems applied to the Bellman transformations
[
Put14
]
.

Bellman equations
capture the conditions under which a policy is optimal.
They are as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{*}\!\left(s,a\right)

\displaystyle=r_{h}\!\left(s,a\right)+\Exp_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}\left(\cdot|s,a\right)}\!\left[V_{h+1}^{*}\!\left(s^{\prime}\right)\right];\hskip 11.49994ptV_{h}^{*}\!\left(s\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{*}\!\left(s,a\right);\quad V_{H+1}^{*}\!\left(s\right)=0.

This implies that the optimal policy
\pi
is a greedy policy with respect to the optimal action-value function
\left\{Q_{h}\right\}_{h\in[H]}
.

We shall now consider the total accumulated regret of an interaction between an agent and the environment.
For every
k\geq 1
the adversary picks the initial state
s_{1,k}
and the agent chooses policy
\pi_{k}
.
The difference between
V_{1}^{\pi_{k}}(s_{1,k})
and
V_{1}(s_{1,k})
is the expected regret of the agent at the
k
-th episode.
Therefore, the total expected regret is equal to

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(V_{1}^{*}\!\left(s_{1,k}\right)-V_{1}^{\pi_{k}}\!\left(s_{1,k}\right)\right).

5.1
Environments (Datasets)

We run our experiments on

versions of some standard RL environments (from OpenAI

Finite action spaces are required for LSVI-UCB itself and also for our variants, which leads us to focus on the

We use the
-ram

We implement our RL algorithms in Python.
The algorithms themselves are implemented using only

The linearization process and the representations used in the linearized environments are implemented using

Synthetic Linear MDP Environments

We run most of our experiments on a synthetic linear MDP environment, with
\mathcal{S}=[500]
,
\mathcal{A}=[15]
,

(feature-embedding dimension) and

(planning horizon).
This synthetic MDP is generated with random features (

Similarly, the reward weight vectors (

Linearized Environments

The linearization process is done using the Contrastive Representation Learning (CTRL) approach of
[

]
.
We run their training algorithm with exploration for

We obtain the linear environments used in our experiments by

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied
provably
space efficient variants of the LSVI-UCB algorithm, and show that they perform well in terms of accumulated regret.
Our experimental results support our findings, and show that significant gains can be achieved without significantly sacrificing regret for both of the variants.

Going forward, we plan to study the possible space savings obtainable with other reinforcement learning algorithms, and also test our proposed methods on other reinforcement learning benchmarks by using our linearization approach.

A.1

We recount here some fundamental notions that are needed in this supplementary material.
Let

If

If

A.2
Some Useful Results

The following results will come handy in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 10
.

If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
A,A^{\prime}\succ 0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, then

\left|\left\|x\right\|_{A}^{2}-\left\|x\right\|_{A^{\prime}}^{2}\right|\leq\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|A-A^{\prime}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\left|\left\|x\right\|_{A}^{2}-\left\|x\right\|_{A^{\prime}}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=(x^{\top}Ax)-(x^{\top}A^{\prime}x)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}(Ax)-x^{\top}(A^{\prime}x)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}((A-A^{\prime})x)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x\right\|_{2}\left\|(A-A^{\prime})x\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|A-A^{\prime}\right\|_{2\to 2}.\qed

We require the following property of sub-sampling from a large set of independent samples.

Lemma 11
.

Let
L:=\left(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right)
be a sequence of vectors sampled independently from
\mathcal{D}
, a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
p
be a distribution on
[n]
, and denote
\|p\|_{2}^{2}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}p(i)^{2}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the distribution that arises from sampling
m\leq\sqrt{{\delta}/{\|p\|_{2}^{2}}}
vectors from
L
independently according to the distribution
p
is the same as the distribution that arises from sampling
m
vectors independently from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

If we sample
z\in L
with
\Prob\!\left[z=v_{i}\right]=p(i)
, then for any Borel set
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\Prob\!\left[z\in B\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Prob\!\left[z\in B|z=v_{i}\right]\Prob\!\left[z=v_{i}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Prob\!\left[v_{i}\in B\right]\cdot p(i)=\Prob\!\left[v_{1}\in B\right],

since the
v_{i}
’s are identically distributed.
This shows that
z
has the same distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
If we sample
z_{1},z_{2}
from
L
independently according to
p
and get
z_{1}=v_{i}
,
z_{2}=v_{j}
for
i,j\sim p
, the probability that
i=j
is given by
\|p\|_{2}^{2}
.
Hence, if we take
m
independent samples, the probability that there is at least one collision is upper bounded by
\binom{m}{2}\|p\|_{2}^{2}\leq m^{2}\|p\|_{2}^{2}
.
So, for
m\leq\sqrt{{\delta}/{\|p\|_{2}^{2}}}
, this probability is upper-bounded by
\delta
.
If no collisions happen in the
m
samples from
p
, then the samples are independent by construction of
L
.
∎

We now prove a version of
Lemma

11
for continuous distributions.

Lemma 12
.

Let
L:=\left(v_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a sequence of vectors sampled independently from
\mathcal{D}
, a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the index set
\mathcal{I}
is
non-discrete
.
Let
p
be a
continuous
distribution on
\mathcal{I}
.
Then, with probability
1
, the distribution that arises from sampling
m
vectors from
L
independently according to the distribution
p
is the same as the distribution that arises from sampling
m
vectors independently from
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

If we sample
z\in L
by sampling
i\sim p
and taking
z=v_{i}
, then for any Borel set
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\Prob\!\left[z\in B\right]=\int_{x\in\mathcal{I}}\Prob\!\left[z\in B|z=v_{x}\right]\cdot p(x)\ \mathrm{d}x=\Prob\!\left[v_{1}\in B\right],

where the last step is valid since since

does not depend on

.
This shows that

follows the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
If we sample

from

Theorem 13

(See

ALPTJ12

.

Consider a random vector

\Sigma

Drawing upon

we show the following lemma.

Lemma 14
.

Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
be drawn from
\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\Sigma\right)
.
Then the minimum eigenvalue of
\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}
is at least
k\cdot\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\Sigma\right)/100

1-2\exp\!\left(-O\left(\sqrt{d}\right)\right)

Let
\overline{\Lambda}_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}

It would suffice to show that
\lambda_{\rm min}(\overline{\Lambda}_{k})\geq\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma)/100
with high probability.
Consider the Cholesky decomposition of
\Sigma

\Sigma=UU^{\top}
for some matrix
U
.
We may now write
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
as
Uz_{1},\ldots,Uz_{k}
, respectively, where
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
are i.i.d. samples.

Then we have that

\displaystyle\overline{\Lambda}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}Uz_{i}z_{i}^{\top}U^{\top}

\displaystyle=U\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}z_{i}^{\top}\right)U^{\top}

\displaystyle=UD_{k}U^{\top},

whereby
D_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}z_{i}^{\top}

1-2\exp\!\left(-O\left(\sqrt{d}\right)\right)

We shall now continue as follows.
For a matrix

\lambda_{\min}\!\left(A\right)=\min_{x:\left\|x\right\|=1}x^{\top}Ax

Note that

\left\|U^{\top}x\right\|^{2}=\left(U^{\top}x\right)^{\top}U^{\top}x=x^{\top}UU^{\top}x=x^{\top}\Sigma x\geq\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\Sigma\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\overline{\Lambda}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}(UD_{k}U^{\top})

\displaystyle=\min_{x:\left\|x\right\|=1}x^{\top}UD_{k}U^{\top}x

\displaystyle=\min_{x:\left\|x\right\|=1}\left(U^{\top}x\right)^{\top}D_{k}U^{\top}x

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\min_{x:\left\|x\right\|=1}\frac{\left(U^{\top}x\right)^{\top}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)}}D_{k}\frac{U^{\top}x}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)}}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\min_{z:\left\|z\right\|=1}z^{\top}D_{k}z

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}(D_{k})\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma).

Since

\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)-O\!\left(\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\right)O\!\left(\sqrt{\beta}\right)\\
\leq\lambda_{\min}\!\left(D_{k}\right)\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)

by

\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\overline{\Lambda}_{k}\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma)-O\!\left(\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma)\right)O\!\left(\sqrt{\beta}\right).

What is left is to show that
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)-O\!\left(\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\right)O\!\left(\sqrt{\beta}\right)
is at least
\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma)/100
with high probability.
The latter follows by the value of
\beta=d/k
and the preceding discussion.

A.3.1
Convergence of
\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}
Implies Convergence in the Action-Value Function

Define

\Lambda_{h,k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\phi(s_{h,i},a_{h,i})\phi(s_{h,i},a_{h,i})^{\top}+\lambda I

for any
h
and
k
.
We will say that
LSVI-UCB (
Algorithm

1
) converges at
k_{c}
if, for all
k\geq k_{c}
,

\left\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}-\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq c/k^{2},

for some
c>0
(that depends on
d
).

Let
w_{h,k}
be the weight vector learned by LSVI-UCB at step
h
of episode
k
.
We bound the difference between
w_{w,k}
and
w_{h,k+1}
.

Lemma 15
.

We have that

\displaystyle\left\|w_{h,k}-w_{h,k+1}\right\|_{2}\leq O_{d}(1/k),

for all
k\geq\sqrt{K}
, with high probability.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\psi_{h,k}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)r_{h}\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h+1}\!\left(s_{h+1,i},a\right)

and

z_{h,k}:=r_{h}\!\left(s_{h,k},a_{h,k}\right)+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h+1}\!\left(s_{h+1,k},a\right).

Note that
z_{h,k}\in[0,H+1]
by the bounded reward assumption.
We have that
w_{h,k}=\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\psi_{h,k}
, and

\displaystyle w_{h,k+1}-w_{h,k}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)\big(r_{h}\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)+\max_{a}Q_{h+1}\!\left(s_{h+1,i},a\right)\big)-w_{h,k}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\left(\psi_{h,k}+z_{h,k}\phi\!\left(s_{h,k},a_{h,k}\right)\right)-w_{h,k}

\displaystyle=\!\left(\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}-\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\right)\psi_{h,k}+z_{h,k}\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k}).

Hence, using the triangle inequality and
0\leq z_{h,k}\leq H+1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|w_{h,k+1}-w_{h,k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\!\left(\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}-\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\right)\psi_{h,k}\right\|+(H+1)\left\|\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\right\|.

To bound the first term, note that

\left\|\psi_{h,k}\right\|_{2}\leq(H+1)\cdot(k-1)\cdot O(\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}),

and hence by our assumption we have that

\left\|\!\left(\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}-\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\right)\psi_{h,k}\right\|_{2}\leq O_{d}\!\left(\frac{(H+1)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}{k}\right).

To bound the second term, note that
\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}
is equal to

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx_{\rm F}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)\phi\!\left(s_{h,i},a_{h,i}\right)^{\top}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx_{\rm F}\left(k\Sigma\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{k}\Sigma^{-1},

by setting
\lambda=o\!\left(1\right)
.

So we get that
\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\approx_{\rm F}\frac{1}{k}\Sigma^{-1}\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})
.
Let us now take into account the fact that (with high probability)
\left\|\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\right\|_{2}\leq O\!\left(\sqrt{\Tr\!\left(\Sigma\right)}\right)
and the assumption that
\left\|\Sigma^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}=O_{d}\!\left(1\right)
.
In this case, we have

\left\|\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{k}\cdot O_{d}\!\left(\sqrt{\Tr\!\left(\Sigma\right)}\right),

and so by the discussion above we get that

\displaystyle\left\|w_{h,k}-w_{h,k+1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(H+1)\cdot O_{d}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}{k}\right)+(H+1)\cdot\frac{1}{k}\cdot O_{d}\!\left(\Tr\!\left(\Sigma\right)\right).

This concludes the proof as
H,\Tr\!\left(\Sigma\right)=O_{d}\!\left(1\right)
.
∎

If LSVI-UCB converges at

\left\|Q_{h,k}-Q_{h,k^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq O_{d}\!\left(\log k^{\prime}-\log k+\frac{\beta}{K^{1/4}}\right),

For any

(2)

A.3.2
Convergence of
\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}
Under Some Assumptions

We begin by stating some assumptions.

Assumption 19
.

At each step
h
in each episode
k
, the state
s_{h,k}
is sampled from a distribution
p_{\mathcal{S}}
on
\mathcal{S}
.
That is, for all
(s,a)
and

it is the case that
\mathbb{P}_{h}(s,a)=p_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Also, either
(a)

\mathcal{S}
is non-discrete (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{s}
) and
p_{\mathcal{S}}
is a continuous distribution on
\mathcal{S}
, or
(b)

\mathcal{S}
is finite and
\left\|p_{\mathcal{S}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1/T^{4}
.

Note that
Assumption

19
is not (necessarily) true for general linear MDPs, but it is motivated by the notion of
occupancy measure

\nu^{\pi}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}\!\left(s_{t},a_{t}\right)
(for some discount factor
0<\gamma<1
), relative to a policy
\pi
, and the potential practical use of generative models (such as deep learning networks) to sample states given some state-action pair as input.

Assumption 20
.

For each
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, the feature-vector
\phi(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is drawn independently from a non-degenerate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma\succ 0
is positive-definite matrix such that
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)=c_{\min}>0
.

We now show the following straightforward proposition.

Proposition 21
.

With probability at least
1-1/K
, for all
h\in[H]
and
k\in[K-1]
, we have

\left\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}-\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq O_{d}(1/k^{2}).

Proof.

By
Assumption

19
and
Assumption

20
, using either
Lemma

11
or
Lemma

12
(depending on whether
\mathcal{S}
is discrete or continuous), for any
h\in[H]
, with probability at least
1-1/T^{2}
, the values
\phi(s_{h,1},a_{h,1}),\dots,\phi(s_{h,K},a_{h,K})
are i.i.d samples from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
.
By a union bound, this holds for all
h
simultaneously with probability
1-1/K^{2}
.

By construction, we have that
\Lambda_{h,k+1}=\Lambda_{h,k}+\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})^{\top}
, so the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity gives, for
u:=\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})
and
w:=\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})
, that

\displaystyle\left\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}-\Lambda_{h,k+1}^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}uu^{\top}\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}}{1+u^{\top}\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}u}\right\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{ww^{\top}}{1+u^{\top}\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}u}\right\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|ww^{\top}\right\|_{2\to 2}}{{\left\|1+u^{\top}\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}u\right\|}_{2\to 2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|ww^{\top}\right\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\|w\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\cdot\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\left\|\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\right\|_{2}^{2},

by the fact that
\left\|1+u^{\top}\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}u\right\|_{2\to 2}\geq 1
.

What is left is to bound the quantities
\left\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}
and
\left\|\phi(s_{h,k},a_{h,k})\right\|_{2}^{2}
.
To bound
\left\|\Lambda_{h,k}^{-1}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}
our approach is to lower bound the smallest eigenvalue of
\Lambda_{h,k}
by
\Omega(k)
, with high probability.
By the definition of
\Lambda_{h,k}
(see
Algorithm

2
) and
Lemma

14
, we get that the minimum eigenvalue of
\Lambda_{h,k}
is at least
\left(k-1\right)\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\Sigma\right)+\lambda=\Omega\!\left(k\right)
(by appropriately setting
\lambda
).

We now turn to bounding
\left\|\phi\!\left(s_{h,k},a_{h,k}\right)\right\|_{2}
.
Since
\phi\!\left(s_{h,k},a_{h,k}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\Sigma\right)
, it is a standard result that
\left\|\phi\!\left(s_{h,k},a_{h,k}\right)\right\|_{2}=O\!\left(\sqrt{\Tr\!\left(\Sigma\right)}\right)
, with high probability.
The result follows from the fact that
\Tr\!\left(\Sigma\right)=O_{d}\!\left(1\right)
.
∎
