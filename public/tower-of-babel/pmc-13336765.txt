Evaluation of Mothers’ Recollection of the Consistency of Information from Antenatal Consultations with Their Postnatal Experiences
Authors: Cosima Babinet, Hélène Gauthier-Moulinier, Nicolas Romain-Scelle, Marine Butin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe primary aim of the study was to assess mothers’ perception of the consistency of information retained from antenatal consultations relative to their experience of their neonate’s hospitalization. Secondary aims included evaluating the quality of information delivered on fetal status, management, and prognosis, and identifying factors associated with perceived inconsistencies between retained information and experience.MethodsThis questionnaire-based study involved the mothers of neonates treated in a tertiary neonatal intensive care unit (Hôpital Femme-Mère-Enfant, Lyon, France). Mothers were included if they had attended a prenatal diagnosis consultation and gave birth between January 2020 and December 2022 to a newborn admitted to the NICU. The main dependent variable was the perceived consistency of information provided during prenatal consultations compared with mothers’ experiences of hospitalization. A descriptive statistical analysis was performed, and quantitative variables were summarized as medians and qualitative variables as counts and percentages.ResultsOf 106 eligible mothers, 64 (60%) responded to the questionnaire. Among the latter, 68% (43/63) reported that the information received during antenatal consultations was mostly or fully consistent with their experience of their neonate’s hospitalization. Among the 20 mothers who reported disparities (32%), 7 (35%) reported inconsistencies with information provided by other specialists and 5 (25%) reported not receiving information on their newborn’s condition or prognosis.ConclusionThis study highlights the need to improve the consistency and clarity of information provided during antenatal consultations, particularly in a multidisciplinary context, and to better convey prognostic uncertainties. Further studies are required on the impact of these interventions on maternal experiences, preferably with a prospective design to limit recall bias.

Introduction

Consultations with a neonatologist are offered to pregnant women if a fetal congenital malformation is identified or if the circumstances of the birth (e.g., prematurity) or the pregnancy (e.g., maternal infection, medication, or toxic exposure) require the newborn to be hospitalized. In addition, depending on the context, expectant mothers may also be referred to a specialist in the relevant field (e.g., surgeon, pediatric pulmonologist, or pediatric nephrologist). The purpose of these consultations is to explain the diagnosed condition and its potential repercussions, prognosis, and management after birth. Prenatal consultations have been shown to significantly reduce parental anxiety prior to birth, irrespective of the severity of the diagnosed congenital malformation [1]. The information provided in these consultations must be adapted to each situation and must be clear and realistic, particularly with regard to postnatal management, prognosis, and risk of complications [2, 3].

However, the information retained from these consultations may differ from mothers’ experiences of their neonate’s hospitalization. These discrepancies may be due to a revised diagnosis and/or prognosis after birth; for example, Kukora et al. [4] reported that prognoses were inaccurate in 7.7% of cases. On the other hand, patients may not retain the information from consultations exactly as it was provided. Finally, there may be inconsistencies between the information given by neonatologists and that provided by other specialists.

Studies are lacking on the information retained by parents from antenatal diagnosis consultations, and how this aligns with their experiences of the hospitalization of their neonate. Filling this knowledge gap is crucial to improve these consultations for parents and ensure continuity of care from the pre- to postnatal periods.

The primary aim of this questionnaire study was to assess mothers’ perceptions of the consistency of information retained from antenatal consultations with respect to their experience of their neonate’s subsequent hospitalization. Secondary aims were to evaluate the quality of information delivered on fetal status, management, and short- and long-term prognosis, and to identify factors associated with perceived inconsistencies between retained information and experience.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This questionnaire-based survey was conducted in the level III maternity unit of the Hôpital Femme Mère Enfant (HFME) University Hospital in Lyon, France. Mothers were eligible for inclusion if their neonate was prenatally diagnosed with a congenital malformation or condition that was anticipated to require admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) between January 2020 and December 2022.

Expectant mothers in this situation were systematically offered the opportunity to meet a senior neonatologist from the NICU, to provide information on their child’s condition, management, and expected outcome. The study questionnaire was retrospectively sent to these mothers if they have attended at least one antenatal consultation with a senior neonatologist and if their neonate was subsequently hospitalized in the NICU during the study period. The exclusion criteria were death of the neonate during hospitalization, conditions with a high risk of death in the first years of life (including chromosomal abnormalities), low French proficiency, and maternal age <18 years at the time of the study.

Questionnaire

Mothers who met the inclusion criteria were contacted after discharge from hospital from August 2024 to January 2025 by email to complete the questionnaire. If they did not respond to two consecutive emails, they were contacted by telephone by one of the study investigators.

Since no validated questionnaire on this topic has been published to date, a questionnaire was designed to assess the quality of the information received during antenatal consultations and its perceived consistency with maternal experiences after birth during their infant’s hospitalization. The questionnaire (online suppl. Material 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552256) consists of 16 items and takes around 15 min to complete. It was created with input from the Collectif Interassociatif Autour de la Naissance (CIANE) pregnancy and childbirth advocacy group. At the end of the questionnaire, mothers were invited to comment or elaborate on their responses in a free-text field. Additional data on neonatal (condition, hospitalization duration, management, and possible complications) and maternal (number of prenatal consultations, presence or not of an accompanying person, term of pregnancy at the time of the consultation, consultation with another specialist) characteristics were obtained from electronic medical records (ICCA, Philips®, Suresnes, France, and Easily®).

Study Objectives

The main dependent variable of the study was how mothers evaluated the consistency of information provided during the prenatal consultations relative to their experience of postnatal hospitalization. This was evaluated in the eighth item of the questionnaire, to which the possible responses were “not at all,” “somewhat,” “mostly,” or “completely” consistent. The secondary aims were to identify factors associated with perceived inconsistencies between retained information and experience, based on questionnaire items 1–6 (on prenatal consultations), 7–13 (on the quality of information provided on fetal status and management), and 14–16 (on the quality of information provided on short- and long-term prognosis).

Statistical Analyses

Participants were divided into two groups based on their response to item 8 of the questionnaire. Mothers who replied “completely” or “mostly” were classed in the “consistent information” group, while those who replied “somewhat” or “not at all” were classed in the “inconsistent information” group. A descriptive statistical analysis was performed, with quantitative variables presented as medians and qualitative variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were conducted descriptively through visual comparison of distributions, without the use of inferential statistical tests. The free-text section enabled mothers to provide additional details about their experiences and to clarify why they replied “somewhat” or “not at all” to item 8. A descriptive, criterion-based approach was used to review the free-text comments. Comments were classified solely based on explicit statements, without further qualitative interpretation or thematic analysis.

Results

Among the 209 mothers eligible for inclusion who gave birth between January 2020 and December 2022 in the maternity department, 106 were finally included in the study (Fig. 1). The characteristics of the 106 mothers and their newborns (n = 107) are presented in Table 1. The median gestational age at the first prenatal consultation was 34 weeks. Ninety-two (87%) of the mothers also had a consultation with another specialist. The median gestational age at birth was 38 weeks. Ninety-six neonates (90%) were hospitalized for a congenital malformation, half of them for a malformation of the digestive system. Answers to the main items of the questionnaire

Regarding mothers’ satisfaction with prenatal consultations (questionnaire items 1–6), 95% (61/63) felt the duration of the consultation was appropriate. Sixteen percent of mothers would have liked a second consultation before birth, and 48% would have preferred a joint consultation with the specialist. Fifty-five percent of mothers visited the neonatology unit, of whom 97% found the visit useful, and 31% found it stressful.

Regarding satisfaction with the information provided during prenatal consultations, 86% of mothers (55/64) considered the information somewhat or very useful. Mothers recalled being informed about their child’s illness, planned treatments, risk of complications, and prognosis in 81%, 84%, 80%, and 67% of cases, respectively. Mothers who had a consultation with another specialist evaluated the information received as fully consistent in 47% of cases and mostly consistent in 33% of cases. The information retained about lengths of hospital stay and their child’s medical status and quality of life was reported as close to reality in 36%, 57%, and 57% of cases, respectively.

Table 3 compares neonatal and maternal characteristics between the “consistent information” and “inconsistent information” groups. The “inconsistent information” group contained higher proportions of neonates with thoracic malformations (21% vs. 8%) and neonatal complications (55% vs. 33%). The proportions of mothers who reported inconsistent information between the neonatologist and the specialist and a lack of information on the disease or prognosis were higher in the “inconsistent information” group (35 vs. 8% and 25 vs. 7%, respectively). Optional free-text comments were provided by 29 respondents. Four mothers (14%) reported a change in diagnosis, and 11 (38%) a less severe postnatal prognosis than announced before birth.

Discussion

In this questionnaire-based study, two-thirds of mothers who had an antenatal consultation with a neonatologist reported that the information received was mostly or entirely consistent with their experience of their neonate’s hospitalization. Inconsistent experiences were more frequent when mothers felt that some information had not been covered or when information from other specialists conflicted with the information provided by the neonatologist.

Several factors may have influenced the perceived consistency between retained information and maternal experiences of neonatal hospitalization. Our results suggest that inconsistent experiences were associated with discrepancies in the information provided by different specialists. While there are no studies in the literature comparing the information provided by different specialists on the same condition, it is reasonable to suspect that this information is not identical. In prenatal consultations, neonatologists focus more on the immediate neonatal period, while other specialists also cover longer term outcomes, possibly leading to different take-home messages. Joint consultations would have been preferred by half of the responding mothers in our study. These could improve consistency and reduce parental stress, and have been shown to enhance pre- and postnatal care [5, 6]. Pre-consultation coordination could help harmonize professional messaging.

Furthermore, mothers may have considered the information insufficient if it was not all provided by the neonatologist during the consultation, either because of omission, or because the information was provided with a degree of uncertainty. This uncertainty surrounding antenatal conditions may lead to a perception among mothers that they did not receive clear information. For example, estimating hospital lengths of stay is more difficult for infants with a congenital defect (90% of our population) than for premature ones [7, 8]. Similarly, although information about long-term prognoses and quality of life is known to have a strong effect on maternal perceptions, roughly a third of mothers in this study reported that these topics were not discussed. This is no doubt at least partly because this information is highly uncertain and varies between pathologies. Complications during hospitalization have also been reported to have a negative impact on quality of life; however, these cannot be predicted during prenatal consultations [9, 10]. To improve communication in uncertain situations, Simpkin et al. [11] recommended first assessing individuals’ informational preferences and their ability to understand uncertainty. They also emphasize the use of multiple formats to communicate risk and ambiguity (qualitative descriptions, graphical displays, and proportions), and the importance of clarifying the type of uncertainty involved, its implications, and the potential consequences if uncertainty persists after birth [11].

Another factor which our results suggest contributes to “inconsistent” experiences is the perception by mothers that the information provided before birth was either insufficient or changing. This may be due in part to poor recall in the aftermath of the consultation. Diagnoses of congenital defects are known to cause psychological distress [12–14] and inherently carry substantial diagnostic and prognostic uncertainty, which is itself a major source of stress [15]. Malin et al. [16] found a correlation between uncertainty and post-traumatic stress symptoms, and studies have shown that acute stress has a negative effect on memory retention [17, 18]. The large amounts of information provided in long prenatal consultations coupled with the stressful nature of this information, therefore making it particularly difficult to memorize every point covered. Moreover, the course of each hospitalization likely influenced maternal responses, both during and after the event [19, 20]. To help mothers retain the information provided, they could be given a written summary at the end of the consultation. This has been shown to improve parental satisfaction in prenatal consultations for genetic disorders [21]. A virtual or in-person visit of the NICU could also help mothers prepare for their neonate’s hospitalization, as previously reported [22].

Clear and comprehensive information is crucial in emotionally charged contexts. When precise data (e.g., hospital length of stay) cannot be provided, this should be recognized and presented in a nuanced manner. In the case of severe fetal anomalies, clinicians should avoid overly pessimistic language. Forth et al. [23] compared optimistic and pessimistic framings of serious preterm complications, both realistic and honest, and found that parents overwhelmingly preferred the optimistic approach, which also promoted a more accurate understanding of the medical situation.

While many studies have examined parental experiences in neonatal intensive care, this is, to our knowledge, the first to compare maternal experiences of hospitalization with their recollections from antenatal consultations. The 60% response rate is similar to those reported in previous studies in this area [24]. To maximize the response rate, we used email and telephone reminders, measures which have already been shown to be effective [25]. The main limitations of this study are its retrospective questionnaire design, with a 2- to 5-year interval between the gathering of responses and the studied events. The responses are therefore likely compromised by recall bias, especially as hospitalization and complications may have had a negative effect on recollections of prenatal discussions. This recall bias explains the presence of inconsistencies in some of the responses, which could not be clarified because no follow-up of questionnaire responses was performed. We chose to not compare questionnaire responses with written consultation reports to focus on what mothers retained, as potential improvements in care pathways must account for the recall bias identified in this study. Another limitation is the exclusion of mothers with a high risk of infant death. Although their perspectives on information delivery would have been valuable, they were nonetheless excluded because of ethical concerns regarding post-discharge contact. A dedicated questionnaire would be more appropriate for these mothers. Finally, the single-center nature of the study means its results are not necessarily representative of other NICUs. Overall however, our results identify areas for improvement that could benefit all neonatologists providing this type of consultation.

Conclusion

This study provides insights to enhance the quality of antenatal counselling. Aligning professional communication between specialists and providing tailored, transparent information that acknowledges diagnostic uncertainty should be key priorities. The provision of a written summary at the end of prenatal consultations could help with memorization. Future research should focus on surveying mothers at discharge, ideally prospectively, to limit recall bias, and on multicenter designs for broader generalizability.
