Concurrent Tongue and Cervical Lymph Node Amyloidosis Without Systemic Disease
Authors: Chelsea O Onyeji, Alycia M Boutrand, Lewis Overton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmyloidosis of the head and neck is often associated with systemic involvement. However, this report describes a rare presentation of localized amyloidosis involving both the tongue and cervical lymph nodes in a patient without systemic disease. It highlights the importance of a thorough workup in differentiating amyloidosis from metastatic malignancy.Keywords: amyloidosis, cervical lymph node, head and neck, localized amyloidosis, tongue amyloidosis

Introduction

Amyloidosis is characterized by the extracellular accumulation of insoluble fibrils which exhibit green birefringence under polarized light [1]. Primary amyloidosis (AL type) results from deposition of immunoglobulin light chains, and is typically associated with plasma cell dyscrasias [2]. Secondary amyloidosis (AA) is primarily driven by chronic inflammation, while the senile systemic form of amyloidosis (ATTR) originates from deposits of the non‐mutated transthyretin protein [2].

Head and neck amyloidosis is most commonly associated with AL subtype, whether local or systemic [1]. With the larynx the most frequently involved site [2], tongue involvement is less common, often presenting as progressive macroglossia and with systemic involvement [3]. Cervical lymphadenopathy in the context of amyloid deposition is exceedingly uncommon and is also considered a marker of systemic disease [2, 4].

Here we present the case of a 53‐year‐old man diagnosed with amyloidosis of the tongue with concurrent PET‐avid cervical lymphadenopathy. While amyloidosis in the head and neck is frequently associated with systemic disease, this case highlights a rare presentation of localized AL amyloidosis affecting both the tongue and regional lymph nodes. The multifocal yet localized nature of the disease emphasizes the critical role of histopathologic confirmation and amyloid typing in differentiating amyloidosis from regionally metastatic malignancy, particularly when radiologic findings are suspicious for cancer.

Case History/Examination

A previously healthy 53‐year‐old man was evaluated by his dentist for persistent oral discomfort and was found to have a left‐sided posterior tongue lesion. The patient had no history of tobacco use and reported no symptoms of macroglossia. An excisional biopsy showed acellular material consistent with a localized amyloid nodule. Laboratory follow‐up with serum free light chain analysis showed normal kappa and lambda levels. Additionally, immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, and IgM), serum protein electrophoresis, and C‐reactive protein were all within normal limits.

Contrast Computed Tomography (CT) Neck Soft Tissue with IV Contrast showed enlarged left level 2 lymph nodes (Figure 1A). A Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scan demonstrated several hypermetabolic and enlarged lymph nodes in the left neck at level 2 raising suspicion for neoplasm. However, no other abnormal uptake was seen (Figure 1B).

(A) PET‐CT showing hypermetabolic activity within the cervical lymph nodes. (B) Contrast‐enhanced CT of the neck demonstrating enlarged left cervical lymph nodes.

A core needle biopsy of the enlarged lymph node was negative for malignancy, and flow cytometry was not performed due to insufficient sample volume. Given the nondiagnostic nature of the specimen, the patient was referred to our Otolaryngology clinic for further evaluation.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

At our clinic, physical examination was notable for left‐sided lymphadenopathy. The patient reported that the lymphadenopathy was intermittent, with overall discomfort but without other symptoms, including no weight loss, fever, or night sweats. No abnormalities of the oral cavity were observed. A FNA of the left neck lymph node was repeated and histologic findings showed rare groups of atypical cells, raising concern for a neoplastic process. Given the persistent lymphadenopathy, cytologic atypia, and the inability to further characterize the specimen, the decision was made to proceed with a left‐sided neck dissection for definitive diagnosis.

Histopathological examination after neck dissection demonstrated acellular material consistent with amyloidosis in 2 of 10 lymph nodes, confirmed by Congo red staining (Figure 2). Numerous CD138 positive plasma cells were present with kappa light chain‐predominant, suggesting AL amyloidosis (Figure 3).

Excisional histology of the cervical lymph node reveals acellular, waxy material suggestive of amyloid deposition (left) confirmed by Congo Red staining with characteristic green birefringence under polarized light (right) at 400× magnification.

Excisional histology of the cervical lymph node showing CD138 positive plasma cells at 400× magnification.

Further immunohistochemical analysis was positive for CD117, a proto‐oncogene marker, and CD56, which is seen in some leukemias and lymphomas. EBV in situ hybridization was negative. There was no evidence of metastatic carcinoma. Definitive amyloid typing was performed and confirmed AL (kappa)‐type amyloid deposition. Follow up laboratory tests demonstrated no abnormal findings consistent with systemic disease or other lymphoproliferative disorder.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Thus, although plasma cell dyscrasias versus malignancy were initially considered in the differential diagnosis, the final diagnosis was AL‐type amyloidosis involving the tongue and regional lymph nodes. The patient continues to be closely monitored.

Discussion

Localized amyloidosis in the head and neck is rare and typically involves the larynx. When the tongue is affected, it classically presents with macroglossia, a finding often associated with systemic AL amyloidosis [4]. In contrast, our patient's presentation of a non‐enlarging tongue lesion without obstructive symptoms was an atypical clinical picture for amyloid deposition at that site. Historically, lymph node involvement has been associated with systemic spread and is often accompanied by hallmark laboratory evidence, such as serum IgG M‐protein spikes or urinary Bence Jones proteins [5]. These systemic markers were absent in our patient, further supporting the diagnosis of localized multifocal AL amyloidosis.

PET‐CT imaging demonstrated hypermetabolic activity in the cervical lymph nodes, initially raising concern for metastatic malignancy. However, subsequent histopathologic evaluation revealed no evidence of neoplasia, highlighting the nonspecific nature of fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in amyloidosis, where metabolic avidity may reflect inflammation or plasma cell infiltration rather than true malignancy. Additionally, the pronounced metabolic activity in the lymph nodes displays the utility of cross‐sectional imaging in screening for distant site involvement in amyloidosis.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is frequently employed in the evaluation of head and neck malignancies and should be discussed in the context of amyloidosis. While contrast‐enhanced MRI is well established in cardiac amyloidosis, its role in head and neck amyloidosis is less clearly defined. In cardiac amyloidosis, diffuse subendocardial or transmural late gadolinium enhancement is characteristic on MRI [6]. To contrast, head and neck amyloidosis case reports describe MRI findings that are nonspecific and closely resemble neoplastic or inflammatory processes [7, 8]. This limits MRI's diagnostic specificity. Nonetheless, MRI may provide superior soft‐tissue characterization and delineation of lesion extent, particularly in complex anatomic regions such as the tongue base [7].

Histopathologic confirmation remains the cornerstone of amyloidosis diagnosis, with Congo red staining demonstrating the classic apple‐green birefringence under polarized light. The FNA result of rare atypical cells, without sufficient material for immunohistochemical analysis, showcased the limitations of cytology alone in amyloidosis workup. Definitive diagnosis was achieved only after the excisional biopsy of both the tongue and lymph nodes revealed acellular material and plasma cell depositions.

Given the persistent lymphadenopathy and cytologic atypia, it was critical to evaluate for underlying systemic and localized plasma cell processes, such as plasma cell dyscrasias and hematologic malignancies. Although the PET‐CT raised concern for regionally metastatic disease, there was no evidence of dissemination, and laboratory findings, including serum protein electrophoresis, free light chain analysis, and flow cytometry, were not supportive of a systemic plasma cell disorder.

Furthermore, the differential diagnoses in this case can go beyond epithelial malignancy to include mesenchymal tumors, particularly sarcomas of the tongue base. Giant pleomorphic sarcoma, although rare, has been reported to present as an infiltrative tongue base mass with imaging features concerning for malignancy [9]. Such tumors may demonstrate rapid growth, metabolic avidity, and local invasion, overlapping with the radiographic appearance seen in our patient. This reinforces the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and pursuing tissue diagnosis when imaging findings are equivocal.

Overall, this case challenges the assumption that multifocal involvement, particularly of lymph nodes, is indicative of systemic disease and underscores the importance of thorough evaluation rather than presumption based on multifocality alone.

Limitations

Advanced imaging modalities such as contrast‐enhanced MRI were not obtained. This limited radiographic comparison between amyloidosis and malignant soft‐tissue processes. Additionally, certain specialized laboratory tests, such as mass spectrometry‐based amyloid subtyping and comprehensive hematologic flow cytometry, were not available on site and therefore not performed. Despite these limitations, definitive diagnosis was achieved through histopathologic evaluation and immunohistochemical analysis, which remain the diagnostic gold standard (See Abbreviation section for all text abbreviations).

Conclusion

This rare instance of multifocal, truly localized AL amyloidosis involves both the tongue and cervical lymph nodes. It demonstrates the importance of histopathologic confirmation and thorough evaluation, as localized amyloidosis can present with features concerning for malignancy but represents a benign, non‐systemic process.
