Abstract
We propose a channel estimation protocol to determine the uplink channel state information (CSI) at the base station for an intelligent reflecting surface (IRS) based wireless communication. More specifically, we develop a channel estimation scheme in a multi-user system with high estimation accuracy and low computational complexity. One of the state-of-the-art approaches to channel estimation is the deep learning-based approach. However, the data-driven model often experiences high computational complexity and, thus, is slow to channel estimation. Inspired by the success of utilizing domain knowledge to build effective data-driven models, the proposed scheme uses the high channel correlation property to train a shallow deep learning model. More specifically, utilizing the one coherent channel estimation, the model predicts the subsequent channel coherence CSI. We evaluate the performance of the proposed scheme in terms of normalized mean square error (NMSE) and spectral efficiency (SE) via simulation. The proposed scheme can estimate the CSI with reasonable success of lower NMSE, higher SE, and lower estimation time than existing schemes.

II
System Model and Problem Description

We consider a single IRS for a communication system with
K
users and one base station (Multiple Input Single Output (MISO)), as shown in Fig.
1a
. The set of the users is denoted by
\mathcal{K}=\{U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{K}\}
. The base station (BS) consists of
M
antennas, while each user has a single antenna. The IRS consists of
N
reflecting elements.

Let the reflection column vector be
\mathbf{r}=[\mathcal{B}_{1}e^{j\theta_{1}},\mathcal{B}_{2}e^{j\theta_{2}}....\mathcal{B}_{N}e^{j\theta_{N}}]^{T}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is the amplitude and
\theta_{n}
is the phase shift of the
n
-th element in the IRS. The diagonal reflection matrix
\mathbf{R}=\diag({\mathbf{r}})\in\mathcal{C}^{N\times N}
stores the phase state of the reflection surface. Let denote the channel gain from user
U_{k}
to IRS by
\mathbf{f_{k}}
. As
\diag(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{f_{k}}=\diag(\mathbf{f_{k}})\mathbf{r}
, the channel gain from user
U_{k}
to BS through IRS can be written as
\mathbf{G}\diag({\mathbf{r}})\mathbf{f_{k}}=\mathbf{G}\diag({\mathbf{f_{k}}})\mathbf{r}
, where
\mathbf{G}
is the channel gain from IRS to BS. The uplink CSI for a user
U_{k}
can be written as
\mathbf{H_{k}}=[\mathbf{d_{k}},\mathbf{B_{k}}]\in\mathcal{C}^{M\times(N+1)}
, where
\mathbf{d_{k}}
is the channel gain of the direct path between user
U_{k}
and the BS, and

\mathbf{B_{k}}=\mathbf{G}\diag({\mathbf{f_{k}}})\in\mathcal{C}^{M\times N}
is the cascaded channel gain from user
U_{k}
to BS through IRS. We consider a time-division duplex system for channel estimation, Where a time period
T
is divided into channel estimation and data transmission. This study aims to estimate CSI, which we donate as
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
, with high accuracy and low estimation latency. Although we provide a scheme to estimate the uplink CSI, the downlink CSI can be obtained by the conjugate transpose of the uplink channels via exploiting the channel reciprocity as the time division duplex (TDD) system is assumed
[
7
,
19
]
.

II-A

Channel Estimation Protocol

The uplink channel estimation protocol is described in Fig.
1

K

L(L>K)

\mathbf{u}_{k}=[u_{k,1},u_{k,2},...u_{k,L}]
where,
\mathbf{u_{i}}^{H}\mathbf{u_{j}}=P_{t}L\quad\text{if}\quad i=j
and
0

Here, the power of each symbol is
P_{t}
. The channel estimation steps consist of
C

C=N+1
. In each sub-frame, the different reflection patterns are set in
C
sub-frames that are presented as
\mathbf{\wedge}=[\mathbf{R_{1}},\mathbf{R_{2}},....\mathbf{R_{C}}]^{T}
. The reflection phases are kept constant in each sub-frame and changed in the next sub-frame. Each user
U_{k}\in\mathcal{K}
sends their same pilot signal
\mathbf{u_{k}}
in each subframe. Thus, the
l
-th,
l\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,L\}
received pilot signal at the BS in the
c
-th sub frame,
\mathbf{s_{c,l}}\in\mathcal{C}^{M\times 1}
, can be written as
\mathbf{s_{c,l}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{p_{c}}u_{k,l}+\mathbf{n}_{c,l}
, where,
\mathbf{n}_{c,l}\in\mathcal{C}^{M\times 1}
is complex white Gaussian noise and
\mathbf{p_{c}}=[1,\mathbf{r_{c}}]^{T}\in\mathcal{C}^{(N+1)\times 1}
. The selection of choice
N
,
K
, and
L
depends on the system size and available energy at the BS. Nevertheless, our proposed algorithm is independent of the choice of those parameters.

Now combining
L
length pilot signals, we can write the received signal at BS at
c
-th subframe,
\mathbf{S_{c}}\in\mathcal{C}^{M\times L}
, as follows:

\mathbf{S_{c}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{H_{k}}\mathbf{p_{c}}\mathbf{u_{k}}^{T}+\mathbf{N_{c}}.

The proposed channel estimation is based on the fact that a sufficient correlation exists between the subsequent coherence time slots when the CSI changes slowly. The dataset used in this study (described in section
IV
) showed an average
40\%
correlation (Pearson’s Linear Correlation) between the successive coherence intervals. We aim to use this inherent coherent property of the CSI to build our data-driven model. A reasonable accurate channel estimation (refereed as a reference signal in our study) in one coherence time can be useful to estimate the next channel coherence time if a proper
mismatch
is found with respect to the prior accurate channel estimation. To understand it better, consider two highly correlated signals
\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
and
\mathbf{H_{1}}
. We can estimate the
\mathbf{H_{1}}
if we know
\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
by solving the linear regression problem:
\mathbf{H_{1}}=a\mathbf{H_{Ref}}+b
by properly modeling slope
a
and intercept
b
. Assuming the existence of a strong correlation, we allow slope parameter
a=1
and let the model learn the intercept
b
, which we refer to as a mismatch channel vector. More specifically, we propose a data-driven model that estimates the mismatch channel vector corresponding to a prior channel estimation, which we refer to here as the reference CSI
\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
. The details of the selection reference CSI
\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
are described in Sec.
IV-C
. In the next section, we will describe the algorithmic steps for the channel estimation.

III-A

Basic Concept

Our goal is to train our data-driven model to learn the mapping between the mismatch of the received signal
\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X_{Ref}}
with the mismatch of the CSI,
\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
. Here,
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{H}
are arbitrary noisy received signal and CSI, respectively.
\mathbf{X_{Ref}}
is the noisy reference received signal of the corresponding reference CSI
\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
.
To train the model, we will use the received training signal
\mathbf{X_{Train}}
and ground truth CSI
\mathbf{H_{Train}}
(the details of the dataset are given in Sec.
IV
). More specifically, we will train the deep learning model with (
\overline{\mathbf{X_{Train}}}
,
\mathbf{H_{Train}}
), where
\overline{\mathbf{X_{Train}}}=\mathbf{X_{Train}}-\mathbf{X_{Ref}}
is the mismatch received signal. With a proper selection of training hyper-parameters and adequate model convergence, the model will learn, with reasonable accuracy, how the
\mathbf{H_{Ref}}
needs to be changed (addition operation) to predict the training CSI,
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{Train}=\mathbf{H_{Ref}}+\overline{\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{Train}}
. Here
\overline{\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{Train}}=f_{MM}(\overline{\mathbf{X_{Train}}})
is the predicted mismatch CSI and
f_{MM}()
is the well-trained mismatch model. The loss function,
f_{NMSE}
, to train the neural network is given by

f_{NMSE}(\mathbf{H}_{Train},\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{Train})=\frac{\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{Train}-\mathbf{H}_{Train}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{H}_{Train}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}.

(4)

Equ. (
4
) will also be used as the evaluation metric for the comparison with other models (Section
IV
). As for the training scheme, the same iterative procedure is executed until the NMSE converges sufficiently.

=

-

and
H_{Ref}

1

IV-A

Performance Evaluation

To verify the effectiveness of the proposed model, we use two evaluation metrics, namely, NMSE and spectral efficiency (SE). NMSE captures how the channel estimation diverges from the true value. Here, SE captures the effect of poor channel estimation on designing beam forming vector
\mathbf{w_{k}}
and IRS configuration vector
\mathbf{r_{k}}

[
22
]
. We calculate the SE in bps/Hz using the following formula.

SE=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{k})}{K},

(5)

where,
\gamma_{k}
is the SNR of user
U_{k}
. The SNR
\gamma_{k}
is given by
\gamma_{k}=\frac{|\mathbf{r}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{B}^{H}_{k}+\mathbf{d_{k}}^{H})\mathbf{w_{k}}|^{2}}{\sum_{i\neq k}^{K}|(\mathbf{r}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{B}^{H}_{k}+\mathbf{d_{k}}^{H})\mathbf{w_{i}}|^{2}+\sigma^{2}}
,
where,
\sigma^{2}
is the noise variance of the propagation environment,
\mathbf{B}_{k}
and
\mathbf{d}_{k}
are the perfect channel state information. Beamforming vector
\mathbf{w}_{k}
and IRS configuration vector
\mathbf{r}_{k}
are optimized, for user
U_{k}
, based on the our estimated
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{Test}
. The beamforming matrix
\mathbf{W}_{opt}=[\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}...\mathbf{w}_{K}]\in\mathcal{C}^{M\times K}
is computed by using the alternating optimization method (first Lagrangian dual transform, followed by fractional programming method) described in
[
23
]
. Finally,
\mathbf{R}_{opt}=[\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}...\mathbf{r}_{K}]\in\mathcal{C}^{N\times K}
is calculated through a closed form solution by applying the iterative reflection coefficient updating.

V
Conclusion and Future Work

We have adapted the correlation character among the channel state information into our deep learning model to make a shallow network with minimum parameters. As for the performance matrices, we have considered NMSE and spectral efficiency. In both evaluation metrics, the proposed model shows results comparable to those of the existing approach. Moreover, the proposed algorithm performs better in low SNR regions, which makes it suitable for use in situations with low energy availability or extremely noisy environments.
