Computational pathology with dynamic convolutional and adaptive kernels
Authors: Taymaz Akan, Richa Aishwarya, Md Shenuarin Bhuiyan, Steven A Conrad, John A Vanchiere, Mohammad Alfrad Nobel Bhuiyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractData processing and learning have become essential to the advancement of medicine, with pathology and lab medicine being no exception. Integrating scientific research with clinical informatics into clinical practice facilitates novel methodologies for patient care. Computational pathology is a burgeoning subspecialty in pathology that promises a better-integrated solution to histopathological images and clinical informatics. Deep-learning methods in computational pathology have demonstrated considerable advances in automated histopathological image analysis. However, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) face fundamental limitations when dealing with the significant morphological heterogeneity present in disease tissues. Conventional CNNs use fixed convolutional kernels, which restrict their effectiveness in adaptively extracting features from histopathological images that exhibit diverse pathological patterns, staining intensities, and tissue architecture. To address this substantial limitation, we present an optimized variant of Omni-Dimensional Dynamic Convolution (ODConv) networks for distinguishing diseased tissue from healthy tissue. Compared with prior dynamic convolution methods that attend to a single kernel dimension, ODConv applies multi-dimensional attention across spatial positions, input channels, output channels, and kernel candidates, enabling more flexible and adaptive feature extraction. We evaluated our approach on wheat-germ agglutinin-stained and hematoxylin and eosin-stained skeletal muscle images from multiple disease models, including G93A*SOD1 transgenic mice (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis) and Akita mice (Type I diabetes). ODConv, trained entirely from scratch without ImageNet pretraining, achieved competitive classification performance relative to seven fine-tuned pretrained architectures across both staining modalities, demonstrating the effectiveness of omni-dimensional dynamic kernels in learning discriminative morphological representations directly from domain data. The study reports strong statistical agreement metrics, proving effective class balance handling and stable decision boundaries. These findings confirm ODConv as a strong computational pathology framework that advances automated diagnosis of neurodegenerative and metabolic skeletal muscle disorders.Keywords: Computational pathology, Dynamic convolution, Deep learning, Histopathological image analysis, Skeletal muscle pathology

Introduction

Histopathological image analysis plays a crucial role in disease diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning.1 The accurate classification of tissue sections based on morphological features is fundamental to understanding disease mechanisms and guiding clinical decision-making. Skeletal muscle pathologies manifest as changes in myofiber size (hypertrophy or atrophy), reorganization of central nuclei as an indicator of muscle injury or regeneration, changes in myofiber shape, disruption in sarcomere structure, changes in connective tissue, loss of blood flow, and an increase in the number of inflammatory cells.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Pathologists use myopathic manifestations to differentiate between healthy and diseased muscles.7 Pathological images are useful in disease diagnosis because they provide detailed visual information about tissue morphology and cellular structures. This is critical for detecting abnormalities such as cancerous cells, degenerative diseases, and inflammatory conditions.7, 8 However, manual examination of histopathological images is time-consuming, subjective, and prone to inter-observer variability. The application of deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has shown tremendous promise in automating histopathological image analysis, offering the potential for consistent, objective, and rapid diagnostic support.9, 10, 11, 12

Despite the remarkable success of CNNs in computer vision tasks, their application to histopathological image analysis faces unique challenges.13 Histopathological images exhibit substantial morphological heterogeneity, with disease manifestations varying considerably across samples due to biological variability, tissue preparation artifacts, and staining inconsistencies.14 Diseased tissues often present subtle and complex patterns that differ significantly from healthy tissue architecture. For instance, in neurodegenerative disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) modeled by G93A*SOD1 transgenic mice, skeletal muscle exhibits characteristic pathological features including increased central nucleation, elevated numbers of smaller myofibers, and morphological alterations from polygonal to rounded fiber shapes.7 Similarly, metabolic disorders such as Type I diabetes, modeled by Akita mice, demonstrate skeletal muscle dysfunction with associated structural alterations.15, 16 These diverse and often subtle pathological changes demand highly discriminative feature representations that can capture fine-grained morphological variations while maintaining robustness to biological and technical variability.

Transfer learning has become a widely adopted paradigm in medical image analysis because most clinical datasets are significantly smaller and more heterogeneous than natural image collections.17 Instead of optimizing a model starting from randomly initialized parameters, transfer learning initializes the network with weights learned from large-scale datasets such as ImageNet.18 These pretrained weights encode general low- and mid-level visual representations that are transferable to domains with limited data. This strategy consistently provides faster convergence, higher stability during optimization, and improved performance, particularly in histopathological image classification tasks, where texture structures and color variations can be substantial. Training from scratch is usually ineffective in such settings because insufficient data lead to unstable gradients and overfitting. Pretrained models therefore form a reliable backbone, whereas the final layers are adapted to the target domain through fine tuning.

Fine tuning can be performed at different depths depending on the size of the dataset and the severity of domain shift. When the available data are limited, only the final classification layers are trained, keeping the remaining convolutional blocks frozen to preserve general visual features. As the dataset size increases or when the appearance distribution deviates considerably from natural images, deeper layers are gradually unfrozen. Full fine tuning of all layers is typically preferred for in-domain adaptation in histopathology because the color distribution, micro-structures, and morphological patterns differ significantly from ImageNet content. In this configuration the network can adjust its internal representations to domain-specific features associated with cellular boundaries, connective tissue patterns, and pathological changes.

Despite the advantages of transfer learning, standard CNNs still employ static convolutional kernels that remain fixed across all input samples during inference. Once trained, the same convolutional weights are applied uniformly to every image, regardless of its specific characteristics or complexity.19 This static nature imposes a fundamental limitation: the network cannot adaptively modulate its feature extraction strategy based on input-specific properties. In the context of histopathological image analysis, where samples may exhibit dramatically different morphological patterns, pathological severities, and staining intensities, this inflexibility constrains the model's ability to extract optimally discriminative features for each individual sample. The fixed kernels must represent a compromise that works reasonably well across all training samples but may be suboptimal for any particular input sample.

Recent research has introduced dynamic convolution as a mechanism to address this limitation, showing that adaptive or dynamic convolutional kernels can improve feature extraction by modulating convolutional behavior according to the input. Dynamic convolution methods, such as CondConv20 and DyConv,21 compute convolutional kernels as input-dependent linear combinations of multiple kernel candidates, where the combination weights are determined by attention mechanisms conditioned on the input features. This input-dependent kernel generation enables the network to adapt its feature extraction process to each sample, potentially capturing more discriminative representations. However, existing dynamic convolution approaches apply attention mechanisms along only one dimension of the convolutional kernel space—specifically, the dimension corresponding to the number of kernel candidates. The other three critical dimensions of the kernel space remain unattended: the spatial dimension (kernel height and width), the input-channel dimension, and the output-channel dimension. This selective attention strategy results in all weights within each kernel candidate sharing the same attention scalar for a given input, fundamentally limiting the granularity of adaptation and the richness of contextual information that can be captured.

To further extend this line of work, we adopt a simplified implementation of Omni-Dimensional Dynamic Convolution (ODConv),19 a more generalized and comprehensive dynamic convolution mechanism that applies attention along all four dimensions of the convolutional kernel space. Compared with earlier dynamic convolution methods, ODConv leverages a multi-dimensional attention mechanism with a parallel strategy to learn four complementary types of attention: spatial attention modulating individual locations within each kernel, input attention weighting different input channels, output-channel (filter) attention regulating different convolutional filters, and kernel attention determining the contribution of multiple kernel candidates. By progressively applying these four types of attention in a location-wise, channel-wise, filter-wise, and kernel-wise manner, ODConv enables convolution operations to be dynamically adapted with respect to all spatial locations, all input channels, all filters, and all kernels for each input sample. This comprehensive attention mechanism substantially enhances the feature extraction capability of convolutional operations, enabling the network to capture richer contextual cues and learn more discriminative representations.

In this work, we present a comprehensive application of ODConv-based neural networks for binary classification of histopathological images of skeletal muscle tissue, distinguishing between diseased and healthy samples.

The main contribution of this study is the development and evaluation of a simplified ODConv-based computational pathology pipeline for skeletal muscle histopathology, supported by extensive comparisons with strong pretrained baselines, statistical significance analysis, qualitative visualization, and ablation experiments across two staining modalities.

Our study addresses several key objectives. First, we demonstrate that ODConv can significantly improve classification accuracy compared to standard convolutional approaches, particularly in the challenging domain of histopathological image analysis were morphological variability and subtle pathological features demand highly adaptive feature extraction. Second, we conduct rigorous k-fold cross-validation to ensure robust and generalizable performance estimates, providing comprehensive statistical analysis across multiple evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and Cohen's kappa. Third, we evaluate the model's performance across two distinct staining protocols—wheat-germ agglutinin (WGA) staining that delineates cell membranes and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining that provides broader tissue architecture visualization—demonstrating the versatility of the approach across different histological imaging modalities. Fourth, we analyze the model's ability to discriminate pathological features associated with different disease models, including neurodegenerative (G93A*SOD1) and metabolic (Akita) disorders, establishing the potential for broad applicability in computational pathology.

We presented the experimental results, including k-fold cross-validation performance, comparative analysis across staining modalities, and comprehensive statistical evaluation. Later, we discussed the implications of our findings, advantages and limitations of the ODConv approach for histopathological image analysis, and potential directions for future research. Animals and muscle tissue

We used G93A*SOD1 mice and C57BL/6-Ins2Akita/J (Akita) mice on C57Bl/6 as diseased muscle models. G93A*SOD1 non-carrier and wild-type mice were used as healthy muscle controls. The mice were housed in properly maintained cages in a temperature-controlled, well-maintained environment following a 12-h light-dark cycle. The mice were fed a regular chow diet ad libitum and water. All animals were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), a commercial research animal supplier, and were not privately owned. All the animals were cared for according to the procedures in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, the animal protocol approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of LSU Health-Shreveport, and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. A previously published article explained animal and muscle tissue processing.22

Wheat-germ agglutinin staining and imaging

We used diseased and non-diseased mice on a C57BL/6 background to obtain skeletal muscles for WGA staining, as described previously.7 We anesthetized the mice using isoflurane and isolated five different skeletal muscles, i.e., Quadriceps (Quad), Gastrocnemius (Gastro), Soleus (Sol), Extensor Digitorum Longus (EDL), and Tibialis Anterior (TA) from both limbs. All mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Isospire™, Dechra Veterinary Products, KS, USA) overdose (5% concentration), and anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of a pedal withdrawal reflex before euthanasia by exsanguination and pneumothorax. The isolated muscles were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin to make tissue blocks. The paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were cut into thin serial sections of 5 μm. The cut tissue sections were subjected to deparaffinization and hydration followed by antigen retrieval by boiling at 100 °C in 10 mmol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Then, the sections were blocked at room temperature for 1 h in a blocking solution (1% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% cold water fish skin gelatin, and 1% Tween 20 in PBS). The blocked sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 WGA (5 μg/mL) for 1 h at room temperature to stain the cell membrane. We used DAPI (in 1 × PBS for 5 min) to counterstain nuclei. Finally, the stained slides were washed using 1XPBS and mounted using Vectashield mounting media. The stained tissue sections were observed and imaged using a Nikon A1R high-resolution confocal microscope (20× objective lens). The images were analyzed using Nikon NIS Elements software version 4.13.04, respectively.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and microscopy

We conducted H&E staining to evaluate overall muscle architecture, myocyte arrangement, and immune cell infiltration. We utilized 5 μm thin serial sections from all five skeletal muscles, namely the quadriceps, gastrocnemius, soleus, EDL, and TA, of G93A*SOD1 transgenic mice, as described in Section 2.2. This was followed by staining with H&E in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (H&E staining kit; H3502; Vector Laboratories). The stained muscle sections were mounted with Cytoseal XYL mounting medium (Thermo Scientific), followed by brightfield imaging using an Olympus BX40 microscope (Olympus Life Science, Waltham, MA, USA) at a magnification of 20×. The detailed protocol for this staining method has been described in previous studies.23

Histopathological images and labeling

The final dataset included five different muscle types per subject, including WGA-stained muscle sections from Akita, G93A*SOD1, and wild-type mice as dataset 1, and H&E-stained muscle sections from the control and G93A*SOD1Tg mice as dataset 2. We used skeletal muscle (quadriceps, gastrocnemius, TA, EDL, and soleus) sections from healthy and diseased mice. We used wild-type (Wt) mice on a C57BL/6 background as a control and G93A*SOD1 and Akita mice for diseased mice. G93A*SOD1 mouse is a widely used model for ALS, a neurodegenerative disorder.24 G93A*SOD1 mice exhibit pathological features like increased central nuclei number, increased number of smaller muscle fibers, and mostly deformed and somewhat round muscle fibers in the skeletal muscle.7 These features contrast the healthy muscle fiber features like nested polygonal shape, presence of peripheral nuclei, and diverse myofiber.6 Another disease model we used in our study is C57BL/6-Ins2Akita/J (Akita) mice. Akita mice are widely used as a Type I diabetes model.16 Studies have shown skeletal muscle pathology with muscle dysfunction. Both staining methods help in evaluating muscle sections in terms of their pathological characteristics. We stained muscle sections from all these mice with WGA to mark the plasma membrane and, hence, understand the morphological features in terms of the shape and cross-sectional area of the skeletal muscle. We divided the histopathological images from G93A*SOD1 and Akita mice into two categories (Disease and Healthy) and used them for training, testing the parameters, and prediction performance. A total of 409 WGA-stained skeletal muscle images were used in this study, comprising 153 diseased samples (from G93A*SOD1 and Akita mice) and 256 healthy controls (from wild-type C57BL/6 mice). In addition, a total of 905H&E-stained skeletal muscle images were included, comprising 298 diseased samples (from G93A*SOD1Tg mice) and 607 healthy controls.

Material and methods

Conventional CNNs utilize static convolutional kernels that are fixed for all input samples during inference. Recent advancements in dynamic convolution have shown that the performance of models can be significantly improved by computing convolutional kernels as a weighted combination of multiple kernel candidates, where the weights are determined by input-dependent attention mechanisms. Existing dynamic convolution methods, such as CondConv20 and DyConv,21 however, focus on a single dimension of the kernel space (the kernel number dimension) and ignore the other three critical dimensions: input channels, output channels, and spatial kernel size. This limitation restricts the capacity of dynamic convolution to capture rich contextual information and its expressive power. To address these limitations, ODConv, a generalized dynamic convolution mechanism applies attention along all four dimensions of the convolutional kernel space. ODConv has demonstrated not only substantial improvement in feature extraction capability but also maintained computational efficiency.

ODConv formulation

where Wi denotes the ith convolutional kernel among n kernel candidates; αwi∈R represents the scalar attention for kernel Wi along the kernel dimension; αsi∈Rk×k denotes the spatial attention applied to each k×k convolutional filter; αci∈Rcin represents the attention along the input-channel dimension; αfi∈Rcout denotes the attention along the output-channel (filter) dimension; ⊙ represents element-wise multiplication operations along different dimensions; and ∗ denotes the convolution operation.

Multi-dimensional attention mechanism

The core innovation of ODConv lies in its multi-dimensional attention mechanism that learns complementary attentions along all four dimensions of the kernel space in parallel. These four types of attention provide distinct yet complementary information

Spatial attention (αsi)

Assigns different attention values to convolutional parameters at each of the k×k spatial locations within each filter. This enables the network to emphasize or de-emphasize specific spatial positions in the convolutional kernel based on input characteristics.

Input-channel attention (αci)

Modulates the cin input channels of each convolutional filter with distinct attention values. This allows the network to selectively weight input channels according to their relevance for the current input sample.

Output-channel (filter) attention (αfi)

Applies different attention scalars to each of the cout convolutional filters. This mechanism is conceptually similar to channel attention in squeeze-and-excitation networks but operates on the convolutional kernels rather than output features.

Kernel attention (αwi)

Determines the contribution of each of the n convolutional kernel candidates to the final aggregated kernel. This dimension-level attention enables the network to select or blend different kernels based on input characteristics.

Implementation details

Comparison with the original ODConv

The original ODConv formulation employs parallel attention branches across kernel, input channel, output channel, and spatial dimensions, each with independent fully connected layers and temperature-scaled Softmax normalization. In contrast, our implementation adopts a streamlined design by using a shared feature embedding followed by multiple lightweight attention heads, replacing temperature-scaled Softmax with L1 normalization for spatial attention, and simplifying grouped convolutions to standard convolutions. These modifications reduce computational complexity and improve implementation clarity while preserving the core multi-dimensional adaptive kernel mechanism.

In this work, we implement a streamlined version of ODConv that preserves the core multi-dimensional dynamic kernel generation mechanism while simplifying the attention computation pipeline. Our implementation maintains the five-dimensional attention structure (αK,αO,αI,αH,andαw) proposed in the original ODConv, enabling dynamic adaptation across kernel space, output channels, input channels, and spatial dimensions. The attention module includes a global average pooling layer that consolidates spatial information, succeeded by a shared fully connected layer utilizing ReLU activation, which maps the input features to a diminished dimensional space with a reduction ratio of r = 1/4. Subsequently, five separate linear head branches compute the respective attention weights: a fully connected layer with output size K and softmax activation computes αK for kernel space attention; layers with output sizes cout and cin /groups with sigmoid activation generate α0 and α1 for output- and input-channel attention, respectively; and layers with output sizes kh and kw with sigmoid activation compute αH and αw for spatial attention. Unlike the original ODConv, we normalize the spatial attention weights (αH and αw) using simple L1 normalization to sum to one across their respective dimensions, rather than temperature-based SoftMax, and employ a single shared MLP with separate heads instead of the more complex parallel branch architecture. Furthermore, we simplify grouped convolution operations to improve code clarity. These changes reduce implementation complexity while retaining ODConv's fundamental ability to generate sample-specific dynamic kernels via multi-dimensional attention, making the approach more suitable for integration into various network architectures. The fundamental principle of aggregating K static kernels via learned five-dimensional attention weights remains intact, guaranteeing that our model leverages the adaptive representational capacity of omni-ODConv. The workflow of the proposed multi-dimensional attention mechanism and dynamic convolution process is illustrated in Fig. 1, which shows how five attention heads jointly generate input-adaptive kernels for ODConv.

Overview of the multi-dimensional attention mechanism used in the ODConv layer. Given an input feature map, global average pooling followed by a lightweight MLP generates five complementary attention heads: kernel attention, output-channel attention, input-channel attention, height-wise spatial attention, and width-wise spatial attention. These attention weights are combined to compute dynamic convolutional kernels, which are then applied to the input feature map to produce the output representation. This design enables input-adaptive kernel generation with fine-grained control across channels and spatial dimensions.

To balance model complexity and performance, we used a reduction ratio of r = 1/4 for the attention mechanism. We set K = 4 kernel candidates per ODConv layer. ODConv blocks were applied in the main feature extraction layers (layer1, layer2, and layer3), whereas the initial stem and residual downsample projections used standard convolutions for computational efficiency.

Comparison with the original ODConv formulation. The original ODConv19 employs four independent parallel attention branches—one per attention dimension—each with its own dedicated fully connected layers and normalization strategy. Spatial attention weights (αH, αw) are normalized using temperature-scaled Softmax, which allows the sharpness of the attention distribution to be learned during training, providing finer control over spatial selectivity. Grouped convolutions in the original implementation enforce structured sparsity across input channels, which reduces parameter coupling and can improve generalization on large-scale datasets. Our simplified implementation departs from this design in three ways: (1) we replace the parallel branch architecture with a single shared MLP followed by five separate linear heads, reducing implementation complexity and parameter overhead while preserving the five-dimensional attention structure; (2) we substitute temperature-based Softmax with L1 normalization for spatial attention, which enforces a sum-to-one constraint without a learnable temperature parameter—this slightly reduces the flexibility of spatial attention sharpness but improves training stability on small datasets; and (3) we replace grouped convolutions with standard convolutions for clarity and portability. These simplifications reduce the total parameter count and implementation complexity, making the approach more practical for domain-specific applications with limited data, at the cost of some of the fine-grained attention control available in the full ODConv formulation. The ablation study in Section 4.5 confirms that these simplifications do not substantially degrade classification performance in the histopathological setting evaluated here.

Experiments

We employed a variety of pretrained convolutional architectures on our datasets,25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31 each with its own set of pros and cons. DenseNet121 employs dense connectivity, allowing each layer to reuse all preceding feature maps. This improves gradient flow and feature propagation, which is beneficial when training on small datasets, but the cumulative feature maps increase memory consumption. EfficientNetV2-S employs compound scaling and progressive learning techniques to optimally balance depth, width, and resolution. It often attains high accuracy with minimal computational expenditure, although its training dynamics are comparatively more intricate. Xception and Inception-V3 use factorized or multiscale convolution blocks to capture both fine and coarse structures. These architectures work well for heterogeneous tissue appearances, but they require higher input resolutions and computational budgets. ResNet50 continues to be a prevalent backbone due to its residual connections, which stabilize the optimization process and enable deeper networks to converge effectively. ConvNeXt-Base enhances the ResNet architecture through refined normalization, convolutional blocks, and training methodologies, delivering performance comparable to transformer models, albeit with significantly greater parameter counts. In contrast, VGG19 provides simple sequential architectures with high representational capacity, but they are extremely parameter-heavy and computationally inefficient when compared to modern alternatives.

The proposed ODConv-based method introduces dynamic convolutional kernels that adjust to each input instance. In contrast to traditional CNNs with static filters, ODConv adjusts kernel weights across spatial, channel, and feature dimensions based on the attributes of the input image. Histopathological images display significant variations in texture, staining intensity, and micro-architectural patterns, with dynamic filtering being more adept at capturing this variability. The adaptability of ODConv enables the model to prioritize pertinent structures while diminishing irrelevant background elements more efficiently than static convolutions. This property is especially significant for tissues exhibiting heterogeneous appearances, fragmented structures, and localized staining variations. The proposed ODConv architecture provides a versatile and robust alternative to traditional CNN backbones, while maintaining a low parameter count and consistent optimization performance.

A diverse set of pretrained convolutional architectures was used to provide a thorough and fair comparison to the proposed ODConv-based network. As shown in Table 1, the evaluated models differ significantly in parameter count, input resolution, publication era, and core architectural design principles. Our dynamic convolution backbone is ODConv, whereas DenseNet121, EfficientNetV2-S, Xception, Inception-V3, ResNet50, ConvNeXt-Base, and VGG19 are established baselines that capture dense connectivity patterns, efficient scaling strategies, depth-wise separable filtering, multi-scale feature extraction, residual learning, modernized CNN formulations, and deep sequential architectures. This diversity ensures that the experimental evaluation covers a broad range of modeling approaches commonly used in medical image analysis.

Network architecture

We constructed our classification network using ODConv blocks organized in a residual learning framework. Each ODConv block consists of two sequential ODConv layers with batch normalization and ReLU activation, followed by a residual connection. The network comprises an initial stem layer (standard 3 × 3 convolution with 64 output channels), followed by three stages with each stage containing two ODConv blocks. The stages have progressively increasing channel dimensions (64, 128, and 256) with spatial downsampling via strided convolutions (stride 2) applied at the beginning of stages 2 and 3. Global average pooling and a fully connected layer produce the final binary classification output.

Training configuration

The network was trained on single NVIDIA L40S GPU using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 and weight decay of 0.05. We employed cosine annealing learning rate scheduling over 100 epochs with a batch size of 16. The model was trained in a 20-fold cross validation setting (n_splits = 20). All images were resized to 128 × 128 pixels and normalized using the mean and standard deviation required by the pretrained model. Cross-entropy loss was used as the optimization objective.

No data augmentation or preprocessing steps were performed on skeletal muscle images. This decision was made to preserve the pathological features required for disease differentiation. Skeletal muscle pathologies are distinguished by subtle but biologically significant morphological changes, such as nuclei centralization as a marker of regeneration or injury, variable myofiber size (hypertrophy or atrophy), irregular myofiber shapes, disruption of sarcomere organization, connective tissue changes, loss of vascular integrity, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Conventional augmentation methods, such as rotation, flipping, scaling, or intensity changes, may obscure or distort these critical structures, potentially resulting in the loss of clinically relevant data. As a result, the models were trained and evaluated using the original images, with no modifications.

H&E staining

H&E staining produces high contrast representations of overall architecture, including fiber boundaries, cytoplasmic content, and inflammatory infiltrates. These global and well-defined structures benefit from deep, high-capacity networks. All strong baselines, including DenseNet121, EfficientNetV2S, Xception, InceptionV3, and ResNet50, achieved high accuracies above 0.987. ODConv obtained an accuracy of 0.9812 ± 0.0189, precision of 0.9762 ± 0.0242, recall of 0.9796 ± 0.0232, and F1 score of 0.9771 ± 0.0220. The ROC AUC value reached 0.9966 ± 0.0094, indicating strong separability between healthy and diseased tissue. MCC (0.9555 ± 0.0428) and Cohen kappa (0.9543 ± 0.0439) further confirmed consistent agreement between predictions and ground-truth labels.

All high performing convolutional networks, including DenseNet121, EfficientNetV2S, Xception, InceptionV3, and ResNet50, produced strong results with accuracies above 0.987 and ROC AUC values above 0.997. ResNet50 delivered the highest non-dynamic convolution performance with an accuracy of 0.9911 ± 0.0147 and ROC AUC of 0.9999 ± 0.0004. Whereas ODConv obtained slightly lower raw accuracy than the top-performing pretrained baselines (e.g., ResNet50: 0.9911 vs ODConv: 0.9812), it showed comparable or superior performance on agreement metrics such as MCC and Cohen's kappa in several pairwise comparisons, and achieved these results without any pretraining. VGG19 exhibited inconsistent performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.8255 ± 0.1446 and significant variance across all metrics.

WGA staining

Comparable patterns were identified in the WGA-stained fluorescence dataset, which exhibits more nuanced morphological boundaries and a reduced total sample size. High-performing baseline models including DenseNet121, EfficientNetV2S, Xception, InceptionV3, and ResNet50 also demonstrated robust performance on this dataset, with the majority of accuracies spanning from 0.972 to 0.985 and ROC AUC metrics exceeding 0.994. ResNet50 achieved the highest raw accuracy among static models (0.9854 ± 0.0224) and also led on precision, recall, F1, MCC, and kappa compared to ODConv on this dataset. Nevertheless, ODConv—trained from scratch with no ImageNet pretraining—remained within a competitive range, particularly on agreement metrics, which is notable given the significant difference in initialization advantage. VGG19, like the H&E dataset, had low accuracy (0.8304 ± 0.1422) and high variance, indicating that shallower or older architectures struggle with complex fiber boundaries in WGA-stained sections. ODConv again achieved very competitive performance, with an accuracy of 0.9415 ± 0.0290, precision of 0.9371 ± 0.0388, recall of 0.9378 ± 0.0380, and F1 score of 0.9320 ± 0.0336. Spatial receptive-field placement: 3 × 3 vs 7 × 7

We first evaluated the effect of replacing the default 3 × 3 ODConv kernels with larger 7 × 7 kernels in different stages of the network. The goal was not to enlarge the model globally, but to determine where expanded spatial context is beneficial.

The baseline ODConv configuration, in which all spatial convolutions use 3 × 3 kernels, achieved the best overall performance. Introducing a large kernel in the stem reduced performance. The stem receives raw pixel-level information, where broad spatial filters tend to amplify staining variation, edge noise, and background intensity rather than morphological cues. This causes a subtle decrease in fold-averaged accuracy and increases fold-to-fold variance.

Increasing kernel size in the first residual block (layer1) produced competitive results, occasionally improving robustness in folds containing mild staining differences or blurred fiber borders. At this depth, spatial structure is more coherent and ODConv can effectively aggregate kernel candidates without amplifying noise. In contrast, enlarging the final block (layer3) consistently degraded performance. At this semantic stage, embeddings are already compact; a 7 × 7 kernel disperses discriminative features and weakens fine-grained separation between normal and diseased muscle morphology. Combining large kernels in both the early and late stages further deteriorated accuracy, indicating that global receptive-field expansion inhibits ODConv's selective kernel aggregation.

Overall, these experiments show that the model benefits from localized receptive-field expansion, but global enlargement undermines discriminative attention at deeper layers. The optimal strategy remains using 3 × 3 dynamic kernels throughout, which allows sample-specific adaptation without deteriorating local structural detail.

Replacing ODConv with standard convolution

To determine whether gains arise from architecture depth or simply from residual topology, we replaced all ODConv layers with fixed 3 × 3 Conv2D layers while keeping the network identical in channel widths, block counts, and downsampling structure. This variant experienced a marked decline in performance across all metrics. Accuracy declined by approximately 10 %, and the model exhibited large fold-level instability, particularly on folds containing heterogeneous fiber size or staining intensity.

These results demonstrate that ODConv is not merely a heavier backbone, nor a surrogate for depth scaling. Its kernel-space, input-channel, output-channel, and spatial attention enable adaptive kernel construction conditioned on each sample. Removing this mechanism collapses generalization capacity and confirms that the observed improvements stem from omni-dimensional adaptation rather than architectural size.

Control of confounding factors

Ablation studies often suffer from changes to parameter count, network width, or residual depth, making it unclear whether improved performance arises from adaptive modeling or simply from larger capacity. In our experiments, model depth, width, and number of blocks were fixed. The only modified components were kernel size and convolution type. This design ensures a causal interpretation: performance differences are attributable to where ODConv is applied, not how large the model is.

Discussion

This study presents an automated framework for the analysis of pathological images to assess skeletal muscle morphology in two prevalent murine diseases: ALS and diabetic myopathy. These conditions exhibit nuanced and gradual alterations in fiber organization, nuclear positioning, fiber morphology, and localized structural anomalies, rendering automated detection exceedingly challenging. Traditional methods for evaluating skeletal muscle, including NIH ImageJ (v1.6.0),32, 33 depend on manual labeling, thresholding, and region-specific quantification. Although widely used, these manual workflows are time consuming and sensitive to user-specific choices, which leads to inter- and intra-observer variability and limits scalability for large experimental studies.

Our findings demonstrate that the simplified ODConv-based models used in this study provide a reliable computational alternative for muscle pathology classification in both H&E and WGA staining modalities. Histopathological images have a high level of biological and technical variability due to tissue preparation, staining protocols, animal-specific differences, and imaging conditions. Static convolutional kernels offer fixed feature extraction behavior that remains unchanged across samples. This creates a constraint, particularly for muscle tissues, where disease manifestations can vary from well-defined structural alterations to very subtle deviations in fiber contour or nuclear arrangement.

The performance gains observed with ODConv can be attributed to its omni-dimensional adaptive kernel design, implemented here in a simplified ODConv-based framework for histopathological image analysis. ODConv adjusts spatial weights, input-channel interactions, output-channel contributions, and kernel combinations for each individual image. This lets the model respond to signals that are specific to a location or sample without relying on global, one-size-fits-all filters. The dynamic filtering process facilitates finding subtle morphological signatures, such as borderline atrophy, minor rounding of fibers, changes in connective tissue, and early nuclear shifts that are often missed by static CNNs. These characteristics are particularly relevant in ALS and diabetic myopathy, where pathology may develop gradually and in spatially heterogeneous patterns.

The qualitative visualizations support this interpretation. Attention overlays and class activation maps show that ODConv consistently gives consideration to fiber boundaries, local texture features, and areas with irregular morphology in both H&E- and WGA-stained samples. The low-level activation maps and dynamic attention plots show that ODConv changes how it responds at different levels of representation, both spatially and across channels. These qualitative findings corroborate the quantitative results, indicating that ODConv exhibited competitive performance relative to state-of-the-art pretrained baselines in both staining modalities.

Another significant aspect of this study is that ODConv was trained entirely from scratch, whereas all baseline models were fine-tuned from pretrained weights obtained from large-scale natural image datasets. Pretraining generally offers a significant benefit, particularly in biomedical imaging applications where datasets tend to be limited in size and demonstrate substantial intra-class variability. Although there is a variation in the initialization approach, ODConv demonstrated performance that is competitive with several pretrained models, particularly on agreement metrics such as MCC and Cohen's kappa, and without the benefit of ImageNet pretraining.

This outcome highlights the effectiveness of adaptive convolution mechanisms such as ODConv in extracting discriminative features directly from domain data, even when the dataset size is modest. The statistical analyses using pairwise Wilcoxon signed rank tests further indicate that ODConv consistently remains competitive with state-of-the-art pretrained baselines in both accuracy and F1 Score.

This study illustrates the adaptability of ODConv across diverse staining protocols, in addition to its classification performance. H&E offers pronounced structural contrast, whereas WGA accentuates membrane delineations and nuanced contour variations. ODConv's capacity to adjust its convolutional responses to various modalities suggests that such adaptive filtering strategies may generalize to other histological staining techniques and tissue types characterized by significant structural variability.

Whereas the findings are encouraging, some concerns remain. This study focuses on two disease models; the integration of additional muscle disorders or human biopsy data would provide further validation. Second, whereas qualitative visualizations make things easier to understand, adding more advanced tools for understanding, like attention rollout or token attribution, may give you more information.
