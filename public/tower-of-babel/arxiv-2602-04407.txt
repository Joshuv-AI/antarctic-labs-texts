Introduction

The Boltzmann equation describes the time evolution of the particle density of a collisional rarefied gas in the position and velocity phase space. Its success, together with the kinetic theory it gave rise to, represents an undisputed achievement in physics.

In this note we present the result by
21
, who derive the Boltzmann equation starting from a microscopic system of hard spheres, for times much larger than the mean free time (the average time between two collisions of a given particle): the derivation holds on the same time interval on which there is a smooth solution of the Boltzmann equation.

Let us start by describing the main theorem informally. A precise statement requires a number of definitions and will appear on page
4.1
(Theorem
4.1
). Consider
N
hard spheres moving in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, initially “independent” and identically distributed according to a distribution
f^{0}=f^{0}(x,v)
. Assume that the Boltzmann equation with initial data
f^{0}
admits a smooth solution
f
on a time-span
[0,T]
. Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
, the (random) fraction of hard spheres in any set
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
converges in probability to
\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}f(t)
, in the limit when the number of particles
N\to\infty
and their diameter
{\varepsilon}\to 0
in such a way that the mean free time is of order 1.

The problem of deriving the Boltzmann equation from microscopic systems has been addressed by many authors in the past century.
An initial key step is
the enlightening work of
35
on the low-density scaling, where he
wrote down
an effective differential equation for the first correlation function in the low-density regime.
The first mathematical result on this problem goes back to
17
and to the celebrated theorem of
37
, who proved that in the special case of the hard-sphere system, the asymptotic factorisation of the correlation functions (known in kinetic theory as the
propagation of chaos
property) can be established by a careful study of trajectories. In particular, Lanford demonstrated that there is no contradiction between the reversibility in the microscopic
laws of mechanics and the irreversibility exhibited by Boltzmann’s equation. The proof was an important breakthrough and after a number of subsequent works until the early 2000’s, the details of the argument were completed and the result refined and generalized

1
The Boltzmann equation

The Boltzmann equation, devised by Boltzmann in 1872, is the cornerstone of kinetic theory. It provides a statistical description of a gas constituted of identical particles moving in the
d
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
)
with uniform rectilinear motion between each elastic binary collision (see Section
2.1

below for more).

Under the assumption that the particles are independent and identically distributed (this is referred to as the “chaos assumption”), their common distribution
f
is expected to be a real-valued function
depending on
t\in\mathbb{R}
(time),
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(position) and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(velocity) satisfying the Boltzmann equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\underbrace{\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\!+\!v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)}_{\tiny\mbox{transport}}\!=\!\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\!\!\big(\underbrace{f(t,x,v_{\star}^{\prime})f(t,x,v^{\prime})}_{\tiny\mbox{gain term}}-\underbrace{f(t,x,v_{\star})f(t,x,v)}_{\tiny\mbox{loss term}}\big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\big((v-v_{\star})\cdot\omega\big)_{+}\,dv_{\star}\,d\omega\,\,,\\
&f(0,x,v)=f^{0}(x,v)\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where the “pre-collisional” velocities
(v^{\prime},v_{\star}^{\prime})
in the “gain” term on the right-hand side are defined by the scattering laws

v^{\prime}\coloneqq v-\big((v-v_{\star})\cdot\omega\big)\,\omega\,,\qquad v_{\star}^{\prime}\coloneqq v_{\star}+\big((v-v_{\star})\cdot\omega\big)\,\omega\,.

(2)

Figure 1:
The hard-sphere scattering law.

Note that

\displaystyle v+v_{\star}

\displaystyle=v^{\prime}+v_{\star}^{\prime}\,,

(3)

\displaystyle|v|^{2}+|v_{\star}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|v^{\prime}|^{2}+|v_{\star}^{\prime}|^{2}\,,

which express the fact that collisions are elastic and thus momentum and energy are
conserved during collisions. The initial value of
f
is prescribed to be a given function
f^{0}
. We have used the notation
a_{+}
for
\max(0,a)
.

In the following we shall use the shorthand notation

f_{\star}\coloneqq f(v_{\star})\,,\quad f^{\prime}\coloneqq f(v^{\prime})\quad\mbox{and}\quad f^{\prime}_{\star}\coloneqq f(v_{\star}^{\prime})\,,

so that the right-hand side of (
1
) becomes

Q(f,f)(v)\coloneqq\int\big(f^{\prime}f_{\star}^{\prime}-ff_{\star}\big)\big((v-v_{\star})\cdot\omega\big)_{+}\,dv_{\star}\,d\omega\,.

(4)

1.1
Conservation laws and H-Theorem

From (
3
) and using the well-known fact (see for instance
17
) that the mappings
(v,v_{\star})\mapsto(v_{\star},v)
and
(v,v_{\star},\omega)\mapsto\left(v^{\prime},v_{\star}^{\prime},\omega\right)
have unit Jacobian determinants, one can show formally that for any test function
\varphi
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and with the notation introduced at the end of the previous paragraph,

\int Q(f,f)\,\varphi\,dv=\frac{1}{4}\int[f^{\prime}f^{\prime}_{\star}-ff_{\star}](\varphi+\varphi_{\star}-\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{\prime}_{\star})\,\big((v-v_{\star})\cdot\omega\big)_{+}\,dv\,dv_{\star}\,d\omega\,.

In particular, it can be shown that

\int Q(f,f)\varphi\,dv=0\quad\mbox{for all $f$ regular enough}

if and only if
\varphi(v)
is a collision invariant, meaning that
\varphi(v)
is a linear combination of
\{1,v^{1},\dots,v^{d},|v|^{2}\}
. Thus, successively multiplying the Boltzmann equation (
1
) by the collision invariants and then integrating in velocity gives rise formally to the
local conservation laws
:

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}\int f(t,x,v)\,dv+\nabla_{x}\cdot\int f(t,x,v)v\,dv=0

\displaystyle;

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}\int f(t,x,v)v\,dv+\nabla_{x}\cdot\int f(t,x,v)v\otimes v\,dv=0

\displaystyle;

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}\int f(t,x,v)\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\,dv+\nabla_{x}\cdot\int f(t,x,v)\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}v\,dv=0

\displaystyle.

The second line should be understood component-wise as

\forall 1\leq i\leq d\,,\quad{\partial}_{t}\int f(t,x,v)v^{i}\,dv+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{j}\int f(t,x,v)v^{i}v^{j}\,dv=0\,.

Taking
\varphi=\log f
and using the additivity of the logarithm shows that the collision operator is
dissipative
in the sense that

D(f)\coloneqq-\int Q(f,f)\log f\,dx\,dv\geq 0

with equality if and only if
\log f
is a collision invariant.
This leads to the entropy inequality

\int f\log f(t,x,v)\,dx\,dv+\int_{0}^{t}D(f)(s)\,ds\leq\int f^{0}\log f^{0}(x,v)\,dx\,dv\,,

(5)

known since Boltzmann as the H-Theorem. Note that this increase in the entropy, defined by
\displaystyle\int-f\log f(t,x,v)dxdv
(which is strict unless
f
is an equilibrium, i.e. a spatially homogeneous Gaussian distribution) reflects the fact that Boltzmann’s equation is
time irreversible
, in contrast with the particle system: we refer to Paragraph
1.3
below for more on this point.

1.2
Existence theories

In this paragraph we give a short presentation of three approaches to solving the Cauchy problem (
1
): the question is to know whether given
f^{0}
, there exists a unique solution to the equation, and what is its life span.

Renormalised solutions

For many partial differential evolution equations, a natural approach to constructing solutions is to exploit the a priori controls provided by the equation in order to design an approximation scheme.

We have presented in the previous paragraph the conservation laws associated with the Boltzmann equation (
1
). Unfortunately
such a priori information on the solution, typically the log-linear bound (
5
) — also known as an
L\log L
bound — is not sufficient to make sense of the collision term which is quadratic. By introducing a notion of
renormalised solution
(a function with bounded entropy satisfying a formally equivalent equation obtained by taming concentrations) and exploiting the entropy inequality (
5
),
27
nevertheless succeeded in proving the
global existence
of renormalised solutions to (
1
). However, their uniqueness is not known to date, nor the fact that they satisfy (
1
) even in the sense of distributions.

Fixed-point methods

In such a situation where the a priori bound does not provide enough information to conclude, the strategy usually employed is to change perspective and consider the PDE as a fixed point problem to be solved. More precisely, using the so-called Duhamel formula we can rewrite the equation (
1
) in integrated form:

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{t}f^{0}+\mathcal{B}_{t}\big(f(t),f(t)\big),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}\big(f(t),f(t)\big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{t-s}Q\big(f(s),f(s)\big)\,ds,

where
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is the free transport operator that attaches to any function
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
the function

\mathcal{S}_{t}g(x,v)\coloneqq g(x-vt,v)\,.

Solving (
6
) reduces to looking for a Banach space in which the operators
\mathcal{S}_{t}
and
\mathcal{B}_{t}
operate continuously.
A typical result, based on the Cauchy-Kowalewskaya theorem (as in
60
or
45
for instance) states that if
f^{0}
is a continuous function in a weighted space
L^{\infty}_{\beta}
in velocities, meaning that
f^{0}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and satisfies

\|f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\beta}}\coloneqq\Big\|f_{0}\exp(\frac{\beta}{2}|v|^{2})\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}<\infty

(7)

for some
\beta>0
, then there exists a constant
C_{\beta}>0
(depending only on
\beta
) such that the solution of (
6
) has a unique continuous solution on
[0,T]
, with

T=\frac{C_{\beta}}{\ \ \,\Big\|f_{0}\exp(\frac{\beta}{2}|v|^{2})\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}}\,\cdotp

Actually not only is the solution continuous, but it is also stable with respect to perturbations of the initial data, in the same function space.
Unlike the renormalised solutions framework presented above, this type of result does not make any use of the particular structure of the nonlinear term (especially of the cancellations between the gain and loss terms): it suffices for the nonlinear term to be quadratic and to produce a linear loss in velocity
(actually this loss is triggered by the defect of integrability in (
4
) for large relative velocities
v-v_{*}
).

To exaggerate somewhat, by this method one is roughly solving an equation of the type

\partial_{t}f=f^{2}\,.

Such an equation is of course known to have a solution blowing up in finite time, so the only way to improve the well-posedness of (
6
) is to use more information on the structure of the nonlinear term.

Perturbative methods

Using the entropy seems difficult as it provides too little a priori smoothness and decay on the solution, so a third strategy consists in exploiting known global solutions and then to act perturbatively. For instance, since the Maxwellian

M_{\beta}(v)\coloneqq\frac{\beta^{d/2}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-\beta\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)

is a stationary solution, one can try to solve (
6
) in the vicinity of
M_{\beta}
. Assuming the initial data takes the form

f^{0}=M_{\beta}(1+g^{0})

with
g^{0}
smooth and small enough in an appropriate function space (with strong enough decay in velocities), then one can produce a unique solution
f(t)=M_{\beta}(1+g(t))

for all positive times

(
62
)
.
This approach crucially uses the fact that the collision operator
2Q(M_{\beta},\cdot)
is coercive in
L^{2}(M_{\beta}^{-1}dv)
, which is inherited from the dissipative nature of the equation. Another possibility is considering solutions which do not depend on the space variable
(
13
)
.

1.3
The Loschmidt paradox and the loss of information

What went down in history as the most famous criticism of Boltzmann’s theory is an argument due to
42
(see
18
, for an historical account). It confronts two indisputable facts: the microscopic laws of classical mechanics are reversible, yet the kinetic world displays an arrow of time, as the H-Theorem indeed shows.

The apparent contrast can be interpreted as related to information: the irreversible evolution corresponds to a gradual loss of microscopic detail. Entropy measures the level of information, and the entropy production quantifies the amount of information discarded when the complete microscopic state of
the particle system is replaced by a reduced statistical description.

In fact, a mathematical investigation of the transition from particle systems to the kinetic description allows one to complement this discussion with a more pragmatic perspective. It allows indeed to quantify the defect of chaos (see Section
3.2
below), and to relate the entropy production to the growth of microscopic correlations (see
59
).

2
Short time derivation

In this section we shall present Lanford’s theorem, which ensures the validity of the Boltzmann equation (
1
) starting with a system of hard spheres, for a short time. It also provides a control on correlations, and therefore on the chaos approximation, i.e. the asymptotic factorisation of the correlation functions.

2.1
The hard-sphere system and the Liouville equation

We
assume throughout that the gas is made of small identical particles, which are spheres of diameter
\varepsilon\ll 1

interacting only through contact (see Figure
2
). This is referred to as a
hard-sphere gas
.

Figure 2:
The hard-sphere dynamics and the deflection condition.

If the gas consists of
N
particles, its state is described by the collection
(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
of their positions and the collection
(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
of their velocities.
Knowing the state of the gas at a certain instant (say
t=0
), we can describe its state at any time
t\in\mathbb{R}
by solving the following system of ordinary differential equations:

\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}=v_{i}\,,\quad\frac{dv_{i}}{dt}=0\quad\hbox{as long as \ }|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|>\varepsilon\quad\hbox{for \ }1\leq i\neq j\leq N\,,

(8)

with specular reflection condition at each collision: if
|x_{i}(t)-x_{i}(t)|=\varepsilon
, then

\begin{cases}v_{i}(t^{+})\coloneqq v_{i}(t^{-})-\big((v_{i}(t^{-})-v_{j}(t^{-}))\cdot{\omega}\big)\,{\omega}\\
v_{j}(t^{+})\coloneqq v_{j}(t^{-})+\big((v_{i}(t^{-})-v_{j}(t^{-}))\cdot{\omega}\big)\,{\omega}\end{cases}

(9)

where
{\omega}\coloneqq(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))/\varepsilon
is the unit vector in the direction of the relative positions at the collision time
t
. As pointed out in (
2
)-(
3
), this condition ensures that the collisions are elastic: momentum and energy are conserved. Note that (
8
) has a unique global solution
\Big((x_{1},\dots,x_{N})(t),(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})(t)\Big)
outside a set of initial data which has Lebesgue measure zero (see
1
, for example).

The dynamics of the particles is deterministic, and we choose the initial state of the gas randomly following a grand-canonical formalism (see, for example,
56
). The grand-canonical formalism is known to be technically convenient when dealing with correlations at low density (see van
3
).

We therefore assume that the number of particles
\mathcal{N}
in the gas is random, with a Poisson-like distribution with parameter
\mu_{\varepsilon}>0
to be determined. Given
N
particles, we denote by
{\mathbf{x}}_{N}\coloneqq(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
the vector representing the position of each of these particles and by
{\mathbf{v}}_{N}\coloneqq(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
the vector representing their velocities. We set
{\mathbf{z}}_{N}\coloneqq({\mathbf{x}}_{N},{\mathbf{v}}_{N})
. As a shorthand notation we shall write also
\mathbf{z}_{H}
for the set
(z_{i})_{i\in H}
when
H
is a subset of
\{1,\dots,N\}
. For simplicity let us assume that the
initial density distribution
W^{\varepsilon,0}_{N}
corresponds to almost independent and identically distributed particles

\frac{1}{N!}W^{\varepsilon,0}_{N}(\mathbf{z}_{N})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}^{\varepsilon}}\,\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{N}}{N!}\,{\textbf{1}}_{\mathcal{D}^{\,\varepsilon}_{\!N}}(\mathbf{z}_{N})\;\prod_{i=1}^{N}f^{0}(z_{i})\,,

(10)

where
\mathcal{D}^{\,\varepsilon}_{\!N}
encodes the exclusion condition

\mathcal{D}^{\,\varepsilon}_{\!N}\coloneqq\Big\{\mathbf{z}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2dN}\,\colon\,|x_{i}-x_{j}|>\varepsilon\,\,\forall i\neq j\Big\}

and the partition function is defined by

\mathcal{Z}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq 1+\sum_{N\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{N}}{N!}\int_{\mathcal{D}^{\,\varepsilon}_{\!N}}d\mathbf{z}_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}f^{0}(z_{i})\,.

(11)

Note that the small correlation due to the initial exclusion is harmless in the analysis that will follow and we will ignore it most of the time.
The distribution of a single particle
f^{0}
is assumed to be a continuous and Lipschitz probability density on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
satisfying (
7
) for some
\beta>0
.

The probability of an event
A
relative to the measure
(
10
)

will be denoted by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\varepsilon}(A)
, and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\varepsilon}
will be the expectation. In particular, the expectation of the number of particles
\mathcal{N}
is determined asymptotically by
\mu_{\varepsilon}
in the sense that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\varepsilon}[\mathcal{N}]=1\,.

We assume from now on that the gas is
dilute
by setting

\mu_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-(d-1)}\,.

(12)

Since the size of a cylinder based on a sphere of diameter
\varepsilon
is proportional to
\varepsilon^{d-1}
, this choice of scaling guarantees that the mean free time between collisions of a given particle is of the order of one, meaning that it is essentially independent of
\varepsilon
.

The Newtonian system satisfied by the particlest translates, at the level of the distribution function
W^{\varepsilon}_{N}(t)
for all times
t
, into the following Liouville equation

\partial_{t}W^{\varepsilon}_{N}+\mathbf{v}_{N}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}W^{\varepsilon}_{N}=0\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\hbox{in }\,\,\,\mathcal{D}^{\,\varepsilon}_{\!N}\,,

(13)

with specular reflection condition at the boundary of
\mathcal{D}^{\,\varepsilon}_{\!N}
.

2.2
Lanford’s theorem

We are interested in the typical behaviour of a particle in the hard-sphere gas.
We recall that, according to the grand-canonical description given in Section
2.1
, the randomness comes through the initial configuration of particles
\left(z_{1}(0),\cdots,z_{\mathcal{N}}(0)\right)
, chosen according to the probability measure (
10
). The main object of investigation becomes then the discrete random field

\Pi^{\varepsilon}(h,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{N}}h(z_{i}(t))

(14)

defined on bounded test functions
h\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.
This random field is introduced, by following the original intuition of Boltzmann, to “count” the number of particles in little boxes of the one-particle phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
The scope of Lanford’s theorem is to prove a law of large numbers for this random field; namely that its distribution concentrates, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to the deterministic limit driven by the Boltzmann equation:

\Pi^{\varepsilon}(h,t)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(t)h(x,v)dxdv\;.

(15)

To do this, we are led to study the first and second moments of
\Pi^{\varepsilon}
, that means in particular computing the expectation (with respect to (
10
))

{\mathbb{E}}_{\varepsilon}\left[\Pi^{\varepsilon}(h,t)\right]\eqqcolon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t,x,v)h(x,v)\,dxdv

(16)

where the right hand side defines the first correlation function
f_{1}^{\varepsilon}
.
Due to the invariance under permutations of the density distribution
W_{N}^{\varepsilon}(t)
, it is not hard to see that this amounts to studying its projection on the one-particle space:

f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t,z_{1})=\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\,\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{1}{p!}\,\int dz_{2}\dots dz_{p+1}\,W_{p+1}^{\varepsilon}(t,\mathbf{z}_{p+1})\,.

(17)

The evolution of
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)
over time is therefore dictated by that of
W^{\varepsilon}_{N}(t)
for all integers
N
.
It is also easy to see that the equation satisfied by
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}
is not closed :
if we define the correlation function of order
k

to be the joint probability of
k
typical, selected particles

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{\varepsilon}\Big[\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{k})}h\big(z_{i_{1}}(t),z_{i_{2}}(t),\dots z_{i_{k}}(t)\big)\Big]

(18)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dk}}f^{\varepsilon}_{k}(t,z_{1},\dots z_{k})h(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\,dz_{1}\dots dz_{k}

then the equation satisfied by
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}

involves the second correlation function
f^{\varepsilon}_{2}
, whose equation itself involves
f^{\varepsilon}_{3}
, and so on.
We more generally obtain that the equation on
f^{\varepsilon}_{k}
can be written

\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}_{k}+\mathbf{v}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}f^{\varepsilon}_{k}=C^{\varepsilon}_{k,k+1}f^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\hbox{in }\,\,\,{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}_{k}\,,

(19)

with a specular reflection condition at the boundary of
{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
. This set of equations on
(f^{\varepsilon}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is referred to as the BBGKY hierarchy. The collision operator
C^{\varepsilon}_{k,k+1}
comes from the boundary term in Green’s formula and is defined as follows:

\displaystyle(C_{k,k+1}^{\varepsilon}f^{\varepsilon}_{k+1})(\mathbf{z}_{k})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int f^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\langle i\rangle},x_{i},v^{\prime}_{i},x_{i}+\varepsilon\omega,w^{\prime})\big((w-v_{i})\cdot\omega\big)_{+}\,d\omega dw

(20)

\displaystyle-\int f^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\langle i\rangle},x_{i},v_{i},x_{i}+\varepsilon\omega,w)\big((w-v_{i})\cdot\omega\big)_{-}\,d\omega dw\,,

where
(v^{\prime}_{i},w^{\prime})
is recovered from
(v_{i},w)
through the scattering laws (
9
), and with the notation

\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\langle i\rangle}\coloneqq(z_{1},\dots,z_{i-1},z_{i+1},\dots,z_{k})\,.

Note that if
C_{1,2}
denotes the formal limit of
C_{1,2}^{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, then the right-hand side
Q(f,f)
of the Boltzmann equation (
4
) is none other than
C_{1,2}(f^{\otimes 2})
.

The key result derived by
37
is the convergence for short times of
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)

to
f(t)
, the solution of the Boltzmann equation with initial data
f^{0}
.

{theo}

[
37
]

Under assumption (
7
), there exists a time
T_{L}>0
depending on
C_{0}
and
\beta
such that, for any
t\in[0,T_{L}]
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)=f(t)

(21)

uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.

{rema}

[More on Lanford’s convergence result]
The formula
(
10

2.3
A strategy of the proof

Lanford’s proof, progressively refined and made more quantitative by many authors over the years, is nowadays a classical argument in perturbation theory: we refer for instance to
61
;
58
;
16
;
15
;
63
;
33
;
52
. The original proof is built upon the hard-sphere hierarchy (
19
). The guiding idea is:

(
0^{\prime}
)

Express the correlation function
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)
in terms of the initial correlation functions
f^{\varepsilon}_{k}(0)
, using a series expansion in “collision histories”, labelled by binary tree graphs, obtained by iterating the Duhamel formula associated with (
19
) backwards in time. Each term corresponds to the backward flow of a finite group of particles that are successively added in a collisional configuration and then evolve under the reduced hard-sphere dynamics.

The argument then proceeds in three steps.

(
1^{\prime}
)

Prove that, for sufficiently short times, the series expansion of
f^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t)
constructed in Step (
0^{\prime}
) is absolutely convergent, uniformly in
\varepsilon
, in weighted
L^{\infty}
spaces such as (
7
).

(
2^{\prime}
)

Prove that in the low-density scaling (
12
), almost all collision histories have no recollision, meaning that no collision occurs other than the ones prescribed by the collision tree constructed in Step (
0^{\prime}
).

(
3^{\prime}
)

The rest of the argument is simple: neglecting recollisions, the measure of which tends to zero, one sees that the expansion reduces termwise to the iterated Duhamel formula for the Boltzmann equation, and by inspection that
f^{\varepsilon}_{k}(t)\;\to\;f^{\otimes k}(t)
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The proof we present in Section
3
below is not the original proof by Lanford, though the two proofs share many similarities. The main difference lies in the way the functions
f^{\varepsilon}_{k}(t)
are explicitly represented. In place of a backward iterated formula, the guiding idea is a
cluster expansion
of trajectories as in
(
6
)
, in turn inspired from previous work on cumulant methods
(
53
;
8
)
. The plan is the following:

(0)

Implement a
dynamical cluster expansion
associated to the hard-sphere dynamics. This leads to express
f^{\varepsilon}_{k}(t)
as an expansion over cluster trajectories labelled by complex

collision graphs
(encoding all possible interactions between the particles).

(1)

Obtain uniform bounds on the expansion (as in (
1^{\prime}
) above).

(2)

Reduce to minimally connected (tree) graphs, by showing that one can discard recollisions. This implies the chaos property as in (
2^{\prime}
) above.

(3)

Identify the Boltzmann equation after the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Overall, the cluster expansion strategy is more complicated than Lanford’s original method. In fact, it requires to understand and exploit subtle cancellations among graphs with different signs (both in Steps (1) and (3)). The interest of presenting this strategy is that it prepares for the proof of the main result of this paper in Section
4
, which will overcome the time restriction to
[0,T_{L}]
arising from Step (1).

5
Consequences and perspectives

The paper by
21
is a crucial advance in deriving macroscopic laws rigorously from microscopic models using the Boltzmann equation as an intermediate description, which was one of the questions addressed by Hilbert in his sixth problem. Hydrodynamic models such as the compressible Euler equations, or the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations can indeed be recovered as asymptotics of the Boltzmann equation in the fast relaxation limit, i.e. when the collision process is much faster than the transport. In order to complete the program and make a connection with particle systems, one therefore needs to derive the Boltzmann equation for times which are much longer than the mean free time.

As mentioned in Remark
4.1
, although it provides a convergence for times which are much longer than the mean free time, Theorem
4.1
is not exactly the result we need, because the functional setting imposed by (
34
) prevents us from considering initial distributions which are fluctuations around an equilibrium or satisfy at least a bound from below on the macroscopic density, which is required for all hydrodynamic limits. Furthermore, as noted in Remark
4.1
, Theorem
4.1
does not provide a quantitative estimate on the time of validity of the Boltzmann approximation, which would be necessary to make explicit the scaling laws leading to the hydrodynamic limits from the particle system via the Boltzmann approximation.

The recent preprint by
22
aims at addressing these two objections. It relies on a similar proof, where the use of
11
is adapted with a new cutting algorithm. It states the convergence from the hard-sphere dynamics to the compressible Euler equations, and incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, under strong regularity assumptions (which are known to be violated in the presence of compressible shocks for instance).

It is worth stressing that the direct hydrodynamic limit from Hamiltonian particle systems, with no smallness in the density, is far from being understood and involves different kinds of problems. This limit should lead to the Euler and the Navier--Stokes equations for real fluids

For a recent discussion on the comparison of the different limit procedures, see e.g.
54
.
. At present, a full mathematical result has been obtained only for stochastic dynamics (see

However, like Lanford’s theorem, these results hold only for short times (except at equilibrium where both the linear fluctuating Boltzmann equation, and the linear fluctuating Stokes-Fourier equations are derived globally in time in

Another important problem, which remains fully open in the field, is the low-density limit of microscopic systems in which the interaction potential is not compactly supported, which is more relevant from the physical point of view
(for some first partial results in this direction, see

The main difficulty in this case is that the cross section (encoding the rate of the jump process
(v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{\star},\omega)\mapsto(v,v_{*},\omega)

A natural question is whether this type of cancellations could be exploited indirectly by assuming the existence of a regular solution, as in the argument by Deng, Hani and Ma presented in Section
