Towards quantum topological data analysis: torsion detection
Nhat A. Nghiem

Abstract
Topological data analysis (TDA) has become an attractive area for the application of quantum computing. Recent advances have uncovered many interesting connections between the two fields. On one hand, complexity-theoretic results show that estimating Betti numbers—a central task in TDA— is NP \rm NP -hard, indicating that a generic quantum speedup is unlikely. On the other hand, several recent studies have explored structured, non-generic settings and demonstrated that quantum algorithms can still achieve significant speedups under certain conditions. To date, most of these efforts have focused on Betti numbers, which are topological invariants capturing the intrinsic connectivity and “holes” in a dataset. However, there is another important feature of topological spaces: torsion. Torsion represents a distinct component of homology that can reveal richer structural information. In this work, we introduce a quantum algorithm for torsion detection—determining whether a given simplicial complex contains torsion. Our algorithm, assisted by a low complexity classical procedure, can succeed with high probability and potentially offer exponential speedup over the best-known classical approach.

While most of these works focus on Betti numbers, they capture only part of the topological information. For a given simplicial complex, the Betti numbers measure the rank of the free part of the homology group. However, homology also contains another component: torsion. Intuitively, Betti numbers count the number of independent “holes” or cycles that cannot be filled, whereas torsion measures those cycles that become trivial (i.e., contractible) after a finite number of traversals. Together, the free part and the torsion part give a more complete characterization of a topological space. For example, a sphere and a Klein bottle differ not only in their Betti numbers but also in that the Klein bottle has torsion while the sphere does not. Yet, most existing TDA algorithms, classical or quantum, typically work over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, effectively hiding torsion information. Readers interested in a more formal discussion of this issue are referred to Appendix
D
, which explains how the choice of coefficients can obscure torsion.

Motivated by this gap, our work explores the potential of quantum computation for torsion detection. Specifically, given a simplicial complex
K
, we ask: Does
K
contain torsion? Our algorithm draws on several mathematical tools. The key theoretical foundations are classical results from algebra, including the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups and the universal coefficient theorem, both of which imply that torsion is reflected in the dimensions of certain homology groups. Thus, our strategy reduces to estimating the dimensions of these groups—a task for which we propose a quantum procedure. Our method builds on recent advances in state preparation, block-encoding, and the quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) framework. We show that, with suitable input, a quantum computer can detect torsion significantly faster than known classical algorithms.

Topological Data Analysis

Topological data analysis is a data analysis tool based on algebraic topology. Topology studies shape (e.g., how many holes a configuration have) while algebra studies arithmetic (what are properties of a symbolic set under certain operational rules). Algebraic topology is a mathematical framework that studies the topological space using algebraic methods. This is partly possible because, from the view of topology, certain objects are “the same”, or more precisely, equivalent, up to a special type of transformation called homeomorphism. Intuitively, it is a deformation, or stretching without breaking, e.g., see the figure below for a simple illustration in 1-dimension.

Thus, by reducing the seemingly complicated configuration to simpler ones, one can hope to gain its topological information easier.

III
A Survey of Quantum Topological Data Analysis

Quantum algorithms for topological data analysis (briefly called quantum TDA), specifically estimating Betti numbers, began with the work in
lloyd2016quantum
, a.k.a LGZ algorithm. Their algorithm contains the following key components as well as related assumptions.

1.

Encoding simplexes into computational basis states.
Let the simplicial complex of interest
K
having
N
data points, denoted as
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{N}
. Then a
r
-simplex
\sigma_{r}

\in K
is encoded into a
N
-qubit string of Hamming weight
r+1

\ket{\sigma_{r}}
as follows: if
v_{i}\in\sigma_{r}
then the value of
i
-th bit is 1; otherwise, it is 0.

2.

Oracle for verifying simplexes.
For any
r
(
1\leq r\leq N
), there is an oracle
O_{r}
that acts as follows:

\displaystyle O_{r}\ket{0}\ket{\sigma_{r}}=\begin{cases}\ket{1}\ket{\sigma_{r}}\text{\ if $s_{r}\in$ K }\\
\ket{0}\ket{\sigma_{r}}\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

3.

Entry-computability of boundary operators
\{\partial_{r}\}_{r=1}^{N}
.
There are specifically two oracles. The first one computes the location of nonzero entries (for a given row/column). The second one computes the value of those entries. This assumption is essentially analogous to those appeared the context of Hamiltonian simulation
berry2007efficient
;
berry2012black
;
berry2014high
;
berry2015hamiltonian
. In fact, the LGZ algorithm uses these quantum simulation algorithms as a subroutine.

With the above inputs, LGZ algorithm combines several well-known ones, including Grover’s search algorithm (multi-solution version), quantum simulation, and quantum phase estimation, to construct a quantum procedure that estimates the
r
-th normalized Betti number
\frac{\beta_{r}}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
(where we remind that
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is the set of
r
-simplexes in
K
), up to some additive accuracy.

The LGZ algorithm has ignited many subsequent attempts, toward many directions. The work in
ubaru2021quantum
proposed to replace the Grover’s search subroutine within LGZ algorithm by a method called rejection sampling. In addition, they also proposed replacing the quantum phase estimation subroutine with the stochastic rank estimation method, which was first introduced in
ubaru2016fast
;
ubaru2017fast
. These two replacements resulted in a certain improvement over the LGZ algorithm, and as claimed in
ubaru2021quantum
, it is very suitable for near-term era. Recent work
berry2024analyzing
provides an in-depth analysis of quantum advantage in estimating Betti numbers. In particular, they introduced new ways to replace Grover’s search algorithm (with Dicke state preparation) and amplitude estimation (using Kaiser window). They also provided specific type of graphs/complexes for which exponential quantum speedup is possible.

In addition to the two above, the work in
hayakawa2022quantum
;
mcardle2022streamlined
also features another progress. Instead of estimating Betti numbers (as in
lloyd2016quantum
;
berry2024analyzing
;
ubaru2021quantum
), the authors in
hayakawa2022quantum
;
mcardle2022streamlined
focus on persistent Betti numbers and develop the corresponding quantum algorithms. We remind the reader that the Betti numbers capture the number of holes in a given complex. Persistent Betti numbers, on the other hand, capture the number of holes that survive over different
filtration
. To get more insight about filtration, one can imagine that a simplicial complex is built on simplexes, which are again built on data points and pairwise connectivity. Filtration roughly means denser and denser connectivity.

Aside from the algorithmic aspect, quantum TDA is also attracting attention from the complexity aspect. In particular, the work in
schmidhuber2022complexity
has shown that, provided pairwise connectivity, computing Betti numbers is
\#\rm P
-hard, while estimating them is
\rm NP
-hard. A similar result obtained in
crichigno2024clique
, showing that estimating Betti numbers
\rm QMA
-hard, while exactly computing them is
\rm BQP
-hard. These results have implied a strong barrier on the potential of quantum exponential advantage in TDA. However, the work
gyurik2022towards
has shown that the problem of estimating normalized Betti numbers is classically hard, which leaves a certain hope for quantum TDA. In fact, recent efforts
lee2025new
;
nghiem2023quantum
;
nghiem2025hybrid
have shown that in structured settings, e.g., triangulated manifold
nghiem2023quantum
, mutual relation between a pair of simplexes
lee2025new
, or a graph with bounded degree
nghiem2025hybrid
, quantum computers can still offer a significant advantage.

IV
Our Contribution

The aforementioned works have featured a significant amount of interest in quantum TDA. Here, we make a relevant, yet fundamentally different progress toward this exciting direction. Instead of seeking the Betti numbers of a given complex, we are curious about the
torsion
of such the complex. To elaborate on this concept, we recall that earlier we mentioned that a
r
-chain is formed by taking a (formal) linear combination of
r
-simplexes. The collection of these chains form the
r
-chain group/space
C_{r}
. If the coefficients of the linear combination are drawn from real field
\mathbb{R}
, then
C_{r}
behaves as a linear vector space, for which the Betti numbers
\beta_{r}
can be estimated by probing the spectrum of corresponding Laplacian operators. In particular, we remark that all the works mentioned in the previous section are based on homology
over
\mathbb{R}
(see also Appendix
C
). If instead of
\mathbb{R}
, the coefficients are drawn from the integer
\mathbb{Z}
, which is a ring, then it leads to a distinct separation.

First, over
\mathbb{Z}
,
C_{r}
is no longer a vector space. Rather, it is an Abelian group (or
\mathbb{Z}
-module). Second, because of this,
\partial_{r}
is no longer a linear mapping between vector spaces. Third, as a result,
H_{r}
is no longer a space but an Abelian group. Since it is an Abelian group, there exists a fundamental result in algebra (see Theorem
2
in the Appendix
B
), showing that
H_{r}
can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle H_{r}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{r}}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{r_{i}}\right)

(5)

where
r_{i}\mid r_{i+1}
refers to the divisbility relation. We refer the readers to Appendix
B
for more a formal review of abstract algebra. The first part
\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{r}}
is called
free part
, while the second part
\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{r_{i}}\right)
is
torsion part
. To this end, we have sufficient information to state the key objective of our work, which is:

Given a simplicial complex
K
, does it have torsion ?

We remark a few things. In the setting
\mathbb{Z}
,
H_{r}
is no longer a vector space and, therefore, the notion of
\rm dim\ H_{r}
is no longer relevant. Rather, it is
\rm rank\ H_{r}
that is of interest. However, quantum computation is built on quantum mechanics, which is naturally built on the complex field
\mathbb{C}
. Thus, it is of fundamental constraint that prevents existing quantum algorithms from handling the torsion part appearing in the above formula.

Our solution to the above question and the corresponding quantum algorithm rely on a few things. We leave the detailed treatment in the Appendix
D

F

G

\displaystyle H_{r}(Q)\cong H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q\oplus\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),Q\right)

\mathbb{F}_{p}

A detailed analysis on the complexity of our algorithm, in both oracle and non-oracle is provided in the Appendix

Potential advantage.
To compare, we point out that the classical algorithm for detecting torsion can still rely on examining the behavior of homology groups
H_{r}
over different fields. A naive way is to compute the dimension of kernel of
\Delta_{r}
directly, e.g., via exact diagonalization or Gaussian elimination. For a complex having
|\mathcal{S}_{r}|

r
-simplexes, this method has complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(|\mathcal{S}_{r}|^{3}\right)
. Alternatively, we can use the classical algorithm developed

;

(see Algo.

). Instead of diagonalizing
\Delta_{r}
, we perform a sequence of matrix-vector products

for

with

. The rank, and thus, the kernel of

can be found by finding the rank of

, with a success probability

for

.

As indicated in the Theorem

, quantum algorithm can offer a variety of speed-ups, depending on the value of
\mathcal{C}
as well as

. If both of them are of the order

V
Discussion
\&
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed and analyzed a quantum algorithm for detecting torsion. Starting from the mathematical properties, specifically the algebraic structure of the homology groups, we outline a strategy for revealing the torsion from the complex. Our quantum algorithm is built upon severally recent advances in quantum algorithms, including
nghiem2025refined
;
lee2025new
;
gilyen2019quantum
, and the classical probabilistic method for rank estimation
kaltofen1991wiedemann
;
eberly2017black
. As been analyzed, with a certain assist from classical computers and probabilistic guarantee, our quantum algorithm can provide a significant speed-up compared to the classical counterpart.

As mentioned in the introduction, the potential of quantum computation in TDA has been somewhat limited by the complexity-theoretic result of
schmidhuber2022complexity
. However, the result of this work has revealed that a fundamentally related problem can still be beneficial with quantum computers. Our work has suggested that there should be more room and opportunities for exploring quantum advantage in TDA, beyond the issue of Betti numbers. Below, we list several prospects that we think it is of high value.

•

An important factor within our algorithm is the condition number
\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}
. We point out that in
ubaru2021quantum
(Section 4.3) and
berry2024analyzing
(Section IV), the authors explicitly discussed and constructed some types of complexes that the combinatorial Laplacian
\Delta_{r}
admitting low condition number. The complexes provided there are apparently does not have torsion. So, how to generalize these examples to include low condition numbers plus torsion, is of worth pursuing.

•

Our work has provided a quantum algorithm for detecting torsion. However, the structure of torsion can be complicated, e.g., of the form
\bigoplus_{i}Z_{r_{i}}
, and our algorithm cannot determine these factors
\{r_{i}\}
. The classical approach is to compute the Smith normal form for the boundary, or Laplacian operator. However, this approach is very computationally expensive. A direct translation of this procedure to quantum setting is not known to us. Even if there is, we suspect that the cost is high. Therefore, developing an efficient quantum algorithm for finding these invariant factors is of great interest.

•

TDA has been proposed for a while, and the corresponding algorithms have been applied in many contexts. However, from a complexity-theoretic viewpoint, it just has been recently established
crichigno2024clique
;
schmidhuber2022complexity
that Betti numbers are difficult to estimate, and it is even harder to compute them exactly. Whether if there exists an analogous result regarding the torsion and its structure is unclear to us.

We believe that all these questions can foster new development toward quantum TDA. We conclude our work here and leave them for future investigation.

Abstract Algebra

In this section, we provide an overview of algebra, showing essential concepts to understand the meaning of torsion. As a consequence, we aim to comprehend the role of coefficients and how it can affect the Betti numbers – a central problem within TDA, as mentioned in the main text. A more detailed introduction to abstract algebra can be found in standard textbook, e.g.,
[

Definition 2

(Group)
.

A group
\mathcal{G}
is a set equipped with an operation, denoted as
*

1.

Closure:
if
a,b\in\mathcal{G}

.

2.

a*(b*c)=(a*b)*c

3.

.

4.

Every element

.

A group is said to be Abelian if the group operation between two elements commute, i.e., for any

, we have that

. Abelian group is very common in many contexts, e.g., any vector space is an Abelian group.

(Torsion)
.

Given an Abelian group
\mathcal{G}

(

•

•

.

•

(rm,n)=r(m,n)=(m,rn)
.

The following properties are imposed within tensor product:

1.

(m_{1}+m_{2})\otimes n=m_{1}\otimes n+m_{2}\otimes n
.

2.

m\otimes(n_{1}+n_{2})=m\otimes n_{1}+m\otimes n_{2}
.

3.

(rm)\otimes n=m\otimes(rn)
.

It can be seen that any Abelian group
\mathcal{G}
, with group operation
(*)
, is a
\mathbb{Z}
-module, by defining the action
nx=x*x*x*\cdots*x
(n times) for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in\mathcal{G}
. We point out a few properties regarding computing tensor product between
\mathbb{Z}
-modules and other module:

Lemma 11

(Tensor product of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules)
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers. Then it holds that:

•

\mathbb{Z}^{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}N\cong N^{r}
for arbitrary
\mathbb{Z}
-module
N
.

•

\mathbb{Z}_{m}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\rm gcd(m,n)}
.

•

\mathbb{Z}_{m}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q=0
for
Q
being a field of characteristic zero.

•

\mathbb{Z}^{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q\cong Q^{r}

For illustration, we provide the following examples.

Example 1.
Let
M=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{4}
, and
N=\mathbb{Z}_{6}
be
\mathbb{Z}
-modules. Then we have the following.

\displaystyle M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}N

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{4})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{6}

(29)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{6})\oplus(\mathbb{Z}_{4}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{6})

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}_{6}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(31)

Example 2.
Let
M=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{4}
and
N=Q
. Then we have:

\displaystyle M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}N

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{4})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q

(32)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q)\oplus(\mathbb{Z}_{4}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q)

(33)

\displaystyle=0

(34)

From the above example, we see that whenever a
\mathbb{Z}
-module is tensor producted with a field (of characteristic zero), then the resulting module is zero. Subsequently, we will point out that this accounts for the fact that homology built on real coefficients miss the torsion part.

Appendix C
Algebraic Topology

This appendix provides a concise overview of the fundamental concepts in algebraic topology relevant to our work. We primarily follow the exposition of Nakahara
[
56
,
27
]
, to which we refer interested readers for comprehensive treatment of the subject.

Definition 9

(Simplex)
.

Let
p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be geometrically independent points where
m\geq r
. The
r
-simplex
\sigma_{r}=[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]
is defined as:

\sigma_{r}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:x=\sum_{i=0}^{r}c_{i}p_{i},\quad c_{i}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{r}c_{i}=1\right\},

(35)

where the coefficients
(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})
are called the barycentric coordinates of
x
.

Geometrically, a 0-simplex
[p_{0}]
represents a point, a 1-simplex
[p_{0},p_{1}]
represents a line segment, a 2-simplex
[p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}]
represents a triangle, and higher-dimensional simplices generalize this pattern to higher dimensions.

An
r
-simplex can be assigned an orientation. For instance, the 1-simplex
[p_{0},p_{1}]
has orientation
p_{0}\to p_{1}
, which differs from
[p_{1},p_{0}]
. Throughout this work, we adopt the convention that for an
r
-simplex
[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]
, the indices are ordered from low to high, indicating the canonical orientation.

Definition 10

(Face and simplicial complex)
.

For an
r
-simplex
[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]
, any
(s+1)
-subset of its vertices defines an
s
-face
\sigma_{s}
where
s\leq r
. A simplicial complex
K
is a finite collection of simplices satisfying:

1.

Every face of a simplex in
K
is also in
K

2.

The intersection of any two simplices in
K
is either empty or a common face of both simplices

The dimension of
K
is
\dim(K)=\max\{r:\sigma_{r}\in K\}
.

Definition 11

(Chain group)
.

Let
K
be an
n
-dimensional simplicial complex. The
r
-th chain group
C_{r}^{K}
is the free abelian group generated by the oriented
r
-simplices of
K
. For
r>\dim(K)
, we define
C_{r}^{K}=0
. Formally, let
S_{r}^{K}=\{\sigma_{r,1},\sigma_{r,2},\ldots,\sigma_{r,|S_{r}^{K}|}\}
denote the set of
r
-simplices in
K
. An
r
-chain is an element of the form:

c_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{|S_{r}^{K}|}c_{i}\sigma_{r,i}

(36)

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are real coefficients. The group operation is defined by:

c_{r}^{(1)}+c_{r}^{(2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{|S_{r}^{K}|}\left(c_{i}^{(1)}+c_{i}^{(2)}\right)\sigma_{r,i},

(37)

making
C_{r}^{K}
a free abelian group of rank
|S_{r}^{K}|
.

In fact, since the coefficients
\{c_{i}\}
belong to a field
\mathbb{R}
, the group
C_{r}^{K}
is also a vector space. In the following, we use the Abelian group/vector space interchangeably. The boundary operator
\partial_{r}:C_{r}^{K}\to C_{r-1}^{K}
is fundamental to homological algebra.

Definition 12

(Boundary operator)
.

For an
r
-simplex
[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]
, the boundary operator is defined as:

\partial_{r}[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,\hat{p_{i}},\ldots,p_{r}],

(38)

where
\hat{p_{i}}
indicates that vertex
p_{i}
is omitted, yielding an
(r{-}1)
-simplex.

For an
r
-chain
c_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{|S_{r}^{K}|}c_{i}\sigma_{r,i}
, we extend linearly:

\partial_{r}c_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{|S_{r}^{K}|}c_{i}\partial_{r}\sigma_{r,i}.

(39)

The boundary operators form a chain complex:

0\xrightarrow{}C_{n}^{K}\xrightarrow{\partial_{n}}C_{n-1}^{K}\xrightarrow{\partial_{n-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}C_{0}^{K}\xrightarrow{\partial_{0}}0.

(40)

Definition 13

(Cycles, boundaries, and homology)
.

An
r
-chain
c_{r}
is called an
r
-cycle if
\partial_{r}c_{r}=0
. The collection of all
r
-cycles forms the
r
-cycle group
Z_{r}^{K}=\ker(\partial_{r})
. Conversely, an
r
-chain
c_{r}
is called an
r
-boundary if there exists an
(r{+}1)
-chain
d_{r+1}
such that
\partial_{r+1}d_{r+1}=c_{r}
. The set of all
r
-boundaries forms the
r
-boundary group
B_{r}^{K}=\textnormal{im}(\partial_{r+1})
.

A fundamental property of boundary operators is that
\partial_{r}\circ\partial_{r+1}=0
, which ensures that every boundary is also a cycle, i.e.,
B_{r}^{K}\subseteq Z_{r}^{K}
. This inclusion allows us to define the
r
-th homology group/space as the quotient group/space
H_{r}^{K}=Z_{r}^{K}/B_{r}^{K}
, which captures the notion of cycles that are not boundaries.

Definition 14

(Betti numbers)
.

The
r
-th Betti number is defined as:

\beta_{r}=\textnormal{dim}(H_{r}^{K})=\textnormal{dim}(Z_{r}^{K})-\textnormal{dim}(B_{r}^{K}).

(41)

In the group-theoretic language, the above dimension, e.g.,
\textnormal{dim}(H_{r}^{K})
, is replaced by
\rm rank(H_{r}^{K})
. As we mentioned, we use the notion of group/space interchangeably. For computational purposes, we can utilize the combinatorial Laplacian:

\Delta_{r}=\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}+\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r},

(42)

where
\partial_{r}^{\dagger}
denotes the adjoint of
\partial_{r}
. A fundamental result in algebraic topology establishes that:

H_{r}^{K}\cong\ker(\Delta_{r}),

(43)

providing a direct method for computing Betti numbers via kernel dimension.

A central theorem in algebraic topology states that homology groups constitute topological invariants
[
27
]
:

Theorem 3

(Topological invariance of homology)
.

If two topological spaces
X
and
Y
are homeomorphic, then their homology groups are isomorphic:
H_{r}^{X}\cong H_{r}^{Y}
for all
r\geq 0
. Consequently, their Betti numbers are equal:
\beta_{r}^{X}=\beta_{r}^{Y}
.

This invariance property makes Betti numbers powerful tools for topological classification and forms the mathematical foundation for their applications in topological data analysis.

Appendix D
Role of Coefficients

In the above, we have built the chain group/space by taking (formal) linear combination of simplexes, with coefficients coming from a field. More concretely, we have defined the
r
-chain as:

c_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{|S_{r}^{K}|}c_{i}\sigma_{r,i}

(44)

where
\{c_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}
. Instead, if we choose
\{c_{i}\}
from the set of integers
\mathbb{Z}
, then the resulting set
C_{r}^{K}\equiv\{c_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{|S_{r}^{K}|}c_{i}\sigma_{r,i}\}
is not a vector space, but just an Abelian group. We emphasize the subtlety that a vector space is inherently an Abelian group, but an Abelian group is not necessarily a vector space (as the multiplication by a scalar might not be properly defined, and also the scalar does not necessarily come from a field).

Changing the coefficients results in the change in the algebraic structure of homology groups. Suppose that we begin with the coefficients
\{c_{i}\}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then the
k
-th homology group
H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
is an Abelian group (where we specify
\mathbb{Z}
to explicitly imply the choice of coefficients from
\mathbb{Z}
). According to Theorem
2
,
H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
admits the following decomposition:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\cong\rm Free\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\oplus\rm Torsion\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)

(45)

where the free part
\rm Free
generally has the form
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
in which
r
is called the rank, or the Betti numbers. The torsion part
\rm Tor
is generally of the form
\mathbb{Z}_{d_{1}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{d_{2}}\oplus\cdots
with the divisibility relation
d_{i}|d_{i+1}
. We can see that the
\rm Tor
part is the main difference between homology with integer coefficients and real coefficients. To be more specific, if we change the choice of coefficients
\{c_{i}\}
from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}
, then there is a so-called universal coefficient theorem, which relates the change in the structure of homology group (under corresponding coefficients’ type):

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\cong H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\oplus\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{R}\right)

(46)

where the last term
\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{R}\right)
is the
torsion product functor
, which is defined as:

Definition 15

(Torsion product functor)
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers, and
m
be some integers. Then for a cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and another
\mathbb{Z}
-module
R
,
\rm Tor(\mathbb{Z}_{m},R)\cong[r\in R|\ mr=0]
. In other words, it is the
n
-torsion subgroup of
R
.

We remark that this
\rm Tor
is different from the
\rm Torsion
that we used earlier. For convenience, we point out the following useful properties associated with
\rm Tor
, and refer the readers to standard textbook for derivation.

Lemma 12

(
\rm Tor
of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules)
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers,
p
be a prime number and
R
be some
\mathbb{Z}
-module.

•

\rm Tor(\mathbb{Z},R)=0

•

\rm Tor\left(\bigoplus_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{m_{i}},R\right)=\bigoplus_{i}\rm Tor\left(\mathbb{Z}_{m_{i}},R\right)
.

•

Let
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
be the finite field of size
p
. Then:

\displaystyle\rm Tor\left(\mathbb{Z}_{m},\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}_{p}\text{\ if p $\mid$ n}\\
0\text{\ if p $\nmid$ n}\\
\end{cases}

(47)

•

For arbitrary
m
(not necessarily a prime), it holds that:

\displaystyle\rm Tor(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\mathbb{Z}_{m})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\rm gcd(m,n)}

(48)

•

For
R=\mathbb{Q}
– a divisible group,
\rm Tor\left(\mathbb{Z}_{m},R\right)=0
for any finite
m
.

Thus, a direct use of the above lemma leads us to:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\cong H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}

(49)

To proceed, we consider the term
H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
, which is:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\cong\left(\rm Free\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\oplus\rm Torsion\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}

(50)

\displaystyle=\left(\rm Free\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\right)\oplus\left(\rm Torsion\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\right)

(51)

We recall the properties from Lemma
11
that, for any integers
m
,
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q=0
for any field
Q
of characteristic zero. At the same time, the torsion part
\rm Torsion\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)
is
\cong\mathbb{Z}_{d_{1}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{d_{2}}\oplus\cdots
, which results in

\displaystyle\left(\rm Torsion\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\right)=0

(52)

For the first part
\rm Free\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
, we can use the first property of Lemma
11
, and also
\rm Free\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, which results in:

\displaystyle\rm Free\big(H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}^{r}

(53)

which implies that

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{r}

(54)

suggesting that
H_{r}(\mathbb{R})
behaves like a vector space. Thus, the above deduction reveals how the choice of coefficients influences the algebraic structure of homology, and that the typical choice of coefficients from
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
may hide the torsion part.

Appendix F
Key insight

In the following, we describe the insight, which is a continuation of the previous section, that underlies our subsequently main algorithm to detect the possible torsion of a given simplicial complex, denoted
K
. Throughout the following, we omit this simplex notation
K
and naturally impose this setting. We recall from the previous section the universal coefficient theorem:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\cong H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\oplus\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{R}\right)

(55)

which reflects the change in algebraic structure upon a change in the coefficients from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}
. If instead of
\mathbb{R}
, we choose another ring/field
Q
, then the formula still holds:

\displaystyle H_{r}(Q)\cong H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q\oplus\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),Q\right)

(56)

According to Theorem
2
,
H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{r}}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{r_{i}}\right)

(57)

Then we have that the above formula is:

\displaystyle H_{r}(Q)\cong\left(\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{r}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{r_{i}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}Q\right)\oplus\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),Q\right)

(58)

To make the above group become a vector space (since linear algebra is a more natural language in quantum computation), we choose
Q
to be some field. For a reason that will be clear later, we choose
Q=\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
for
p
being a prime. Then for
Q=\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the above equation can be written as:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong\left(\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{r}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{r_{i}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)\oplus\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

(59)

Again, by Theorem
2
, we have:

\displaystyle H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{q}}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{q_{i}}\right)

(60)

Via Lemma
12
, we have that:

\displaystyle\rm Tor\left(H_{r-1}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\cong\rm Tor\left(\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{q}}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{q_{i}}\right),\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle\cong\rm Tor\left(\left(\bigoplus_{i}Z_{q_{i}}\right),\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

(62)

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{i,q_{i}\mid p}\mathbb{F}_{p}

(63)

By Lemma
2
, and also the fact that
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\left(\bigoplus_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{k_{i}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{i,k_{i}\mid p}\mathbb{F}_{p}

(64)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{Z}^{\beta_{r}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\beta_{r}}

(65)

Gathering everything, we have:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\beta_{r}}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i,r_{i}\mid p}\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i,q_{i}\mid p}\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)

(66)

We recall from the previous section that if we choose the field
Q\equiv\mathbb{R}
, then:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{\beta_{r}}

(67)

It means that, the rank of the
k
-th homology space over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
field contains additional factors from the torsion part, i.e.,:

\displaystyle\rm dim\left(H_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\right)=\beta_{r}+t_{r}+t_{r-1}

(68)

where
t_{r},t_{r-1}
is the sum of the cyclic summands (of
k
-th homology group/space and
(k-1)
-th homology group/space, respectively) of order divisible by a prime
p
. We recall from the previous section
D
that:

\displaystyle H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{\beta_{r}}

(69)

which implies

\displaystyle\dim\left(H_{r}(\mathbb{R})\right)=\beta_{r}

(70)

Our strategy is built on this, as we proceed to estimate the dimension of
k
-th homology group over two different fields
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and then compare them. If their dimensions are equal, then it indicates that there is no torsion. Otherwise, there is torsion. The problem then is, what value of
p
should we choose ? We recall that
p
is a prime, and it is of a known fact that arbitrary integers can be decomposed as products of different primes. Thus, in general, without knowing the order of summands in advance (which is apparent, because if we know the order already, then the torsion is already known), the only strategy is to try as many primes as possible, e.g.,
p=2,3,5...,7
, etc. In the following section, we construct a quantum algorithm to execute the estimation of the desired dimension, and subsequently, the comparison.

Appendix G
Quantum Algorithm

G.1
Input model

As seen from the main text, our input model being similar to the recent work
[
25
]
, namely, the non-oracle model. Subsequently, we will also discuss how the oracle model can be used within our algorithm. Let
\mathcal{S}_{r}=\{\sigma_{r_{1}},\sigma_{r_{2}},...\}
denotes the set of
r
-simplexes in the complex of interest
K
. For some
r
, let
\mathscr{D}_{r}
denotes the matrix of size
|\mathcal{S}_{r-1}|\times|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
, which encodes the structure of the given complex as follows. For a given column index, say
j
(with
1\leq j\leq|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
), we have:

\displaystyle(\mathscr{D}_{r})_{ij}=\begin{cases}1\text{ \ if $\sigma_{r_{i}}\cap\sigma_{(r-1)_{j}}\neq\emptyset$ }\\
0\text{ \ if $\sigma_{r_{i}}\cap\sigma_{(r-1)_{j}}=\emptyset$ }\end{cases}

(71)

Intuitively, the matrix
\mathscr{D}_{r}
encodes the local, mutual relations between all
r
-simplexes and
(r-1)
-simplexes. In addition, as a
r
-simplex shares
r+1

(r-1)
-simplexes, then for a given column, there are
(r+1)
nonzero entries. The assumption we make in our work is that the entries of
\{\mathcal{D}_{r}\}
are classically known. We remark that the classically known entries is in the sense, or perspective of Lemma
8
, where the entries can be efficiently loaded into a (large) quantum state.

As also discussed in
[
25
]
, whether this model arises in practical scenario is unknown. However, in reality, we can achieve this model by directly specifying the simplexes. The method of specifying simplexes was also suggested in
[
23
]
as a mean to overcome a high-complexity subroutine required in the original LGZ algorithm. For instance, let
K
be the complex of interest and
v_{0},v_{1},....,v_{N}
be the
N
data points, or vertices in
K
. If we pick:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{3}=[v_{0},v_{1},v_{3}],[v_{2},v_{3},v_{7}],[v_{8},v_{9},v_{16}],

(72)

as 3-simplexes
\in K
, and:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}=[v_{0},v_{1}],[v_{1},v_{3}],[v_{0},v_{3}],[v_{2},v_{3}],[v_{3},v_{7}],[v_{2},v_{7}],[v_{8},v_{9}][v_{8},v_{16}],[v_{9},v_{16}],[v_{5},v_{7}],[v_{6},v_{8}]

(73)

as 2-simplexes in
\in K
. Then we organize the above information as a matrix of size
|\mathcal{S}_{2}|\times|\mathcal{S}_{3}|
, resulting in the matrix
\mathscr{D}_{3}
. The same construction holds for different orders, resulting in the matrices
\{\mathscr{D}_{r}\}
. In general, we can reformulate our problem as follows.

Given the classical knowledge of the matrix
\mathscr{D}_{r}
of size
|\mathcal{S}_{r-1}|\times|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
, with the property that any column has
r+1
nonzero entries being 1. At the order
r
, does the simplicial complex
K
with structured encoded in this matrix have torsion ?

G.2
Procedure

Earlier, we have mentioned the strategy for detecting torsion, which is based on comparing the dimension of
r
-th homology space/group
H_{k}
over two fields,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Over
\mathbb{R}
, the dimension of
H_{k}(\mathbb{R})
is in fact the
r
-th Betti number commonly used in the literature. Yet, over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the dimension of
H_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
can change, depending on whether the complex of interest has torsion. In the following, we discuss in detail the quantum algorithm for estimating the dimension of
H_{k}
over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Estimating
\beta_{r}
over real field
\mathbb{R}
.
We first recall that the boundary operator is generally defined as:

\partial_{r}[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,\hat{p_{i}},\ldots,p_{r}],

(74)

The classical knowledge of matrix
\mathscr{D}_{r}
translates to the classical knowledge of
\partial_{r}
directly, almost by definition. According to the above definition of boundary operator,
\partial_{r}
is of size
|\mathcal{S}_{r-1}|\times|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
. In the
j
-th column of
\partial_{r}
, which corresponds to the
j
-th
r
-simplex
\sigma_{r_{j}}
, the location of nonzero entries are those
(r-1)
-simplexes that are faces of
\sigma_{r_{j}}
. The value of these non-zero entries is either 1 or -1. Thus, from
\mathscr{D}_{r}
, we simply need to change the value of nonzero entries at certain locations from 1 to -1.

To proceed, we have seen earlier that the problem of estimating
r
-th Betti number, or normalized Betti number eventually reduce to estimating the kernel of the combinatorial Laplacian:

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}=\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}+\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r},

(75)

This has already been applied in many previous works, e.g.,
[
18
,
26
,
19
,
23
,
20
,
25
]
. Thus, we refer the interested readers to each of them for concrete details. Here, we point out that all these works have different input assumptions and that our input model is similar to
[
25
]
(to some degrees, similar to
[
26
]
as well). Hence, it is most appropriate to use their result, as they also deal with a real field. We recapitulate the main procedure as well as the main result in
[
25
]
with regard to the estimation of the
r
-th Betti number over the real field
\mathbb{R}
as follows.

Algorithm 1

(
[
25
]
)
.

Let
\{\mathscr{D}_{r}\}
be the classical specification of the simplicial complex
K
of interest, having total
N
data points.

1.

Use the classical knowledge of
\mathscr{D}_{r},\mathscr{D}_{r+1}
and Lemma
7
to construct the block-encoding of
\frac{1}{||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r},\frac{1}{||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}
. The Frobenius norm of
\partial_{r}
is
\sqrt{(r+1)|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
.

2.

Use the block-encoding arithmetic recipes Lemma.
3
,
4
to construct the block-encoding of
\frac{1}{2||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}}\Delta_{r}
.

3.

Use stochastic rank estimation method proposed in
[

,
35
,
20
]
(see also Algorithm
3
) to estimate
\frac{\rm rank\Delta_{r}}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
, which can then be used to infer
\frac{\dim\rm Ker(\Delta_{r})}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}=\frac{\beta_{r}}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
.

Output:
Estimation of
r
-th normalized Betti numbers
\frac{\beta_{r}}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
.

To be more concrete, the first and second steps proceed as follows. From the block-encoding of
\frac{1}{||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r}
, we use Lemma
4
to transform the block-encoded operators:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{1}{||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r}

(76)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}

(77)

Then we use Lemma

to construct the block-encoding of the above two operators on the right:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2||\partial_{r}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}||_{F}^{2}}\Delta_{r}

(78)

According to the analysis provided in
[
25
]
, the above algorithm yields the following result:

Lemma 13
.

The Algorithm
1
outputs an estimation of the
r
-th normalized Betti number
\frac{\beta_{r}}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
to additive precision
\epsilon
with time complexity

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(r\lvert\mathcal{S}_{r}^{K}\rvert)\log(r^{2}\lvert\mathcal{S}_{r}\rvert\lvert\mathcal{S}_{r+1}^{K}\rvert)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(79)

In addition, we point out that in the same work
[
25
]
, the authors also proposed an alternative way to estimate Betti numbers, based on the tracking of different homology classes.

Estimating
\beta_{r}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
We remind the readers that the boundary operator is:

\partial_{r}[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}]=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{i}[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,\hat{p_{i}},\ldots,p_{r}],

(80)

Over real field
\mathbb{R}
, complex field
\mathbb{C}
, or integer ring
\mathbb{Z}
,
-1
is the inverse element of
1
in the usual sense that we use. Over finite field, however, things are different. The inverse element, say of
1
, is no longer
-1
, because in this case, for example,
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, that
-1
does not even belong to this set. Instead, we need to rely on the definition provided in the Appendix
B
. It states that the inverse of an element, say
x
, of an Abelian group
\mathcal{G}
(with group operation
+
) is another element
y
such that
x+y=0
where
0
is the identity element. We consider the case
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, which contains of two element
\{0,1\}
. Then it can be seen that
1+1=0
(module 2), thus suggesting that in this group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
1
is its own inverse element. More generally, let
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv(0,1,2,...,p-1)
(with the group operation taken modulo
p
), then the inverse element of
1
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is
p-1
. Thus, over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the entries with value
1
remains the same, and value
-1
in the boundary map
\partial_{r}
is replaced by
p-1
, and all the subsequent arithmetic operation needs to be done modulo
p
. However, we point out that equivalently, we can define:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\left(\frac{-(p-1)}{2},\cdots,-1,0,1,\cdots\frac{p-1}{2}\right)

(81)

In this setup, the value of
1,-1
remain the same, only that the arithmetic operation needs to be done modulo
p
. It makes things a bit more convenient for the representation of a boundary operator, as we will see shortly. For completeness, we use
\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}
to denote the boundary operator
\partial_{r}
modulo
p
. It is not hard to see that the classical knowledge of
\{\mathscr{D}_{r}\}
translates into the classical knowledge of
\{\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\}
.

The first two steps of Algorithm
1
can still be applied to obtain the block-encoding of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}

(82)

Previously, we have pointed out that over
\mathbb{R}
, the Frobenius norm of
\partial_{r}
is
\sqrt{(r+1)|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
. Over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
as defined above, as the value of
1,-1
remain the same, then Frobenius norm is the same
||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}=\sqrt{(r+1)|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
. The next goal is to estimate the dimension of
r
-th homology group
H_{r}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, which is also the main challenge when we change from real field
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. To be more specific, at the third step of Algorithm
1
, there is an application of stochastic rank estimation method originally derived from
[
34
,
35
]
, and this method only works on real, or complex field
\mathbb{C}
. Likewise, other quantum algorithms for estimating Betti numbers, such as
[
18
,
19
,
23
]
, are based on quantum simulation and quantum phase estimation, which are built naturally on complex field
\mathbb{C}
.

To this end, we point out that there is a relevant attempt in developing classically randomized algorithms to estimate the rank of a matrix
[
44
,
45
]
over finite field. The idea and mechanism of these algorithms are very similar to the stochastic rank estimation method, as they also generate a sequence of vectors with random entries. The rank of the matrix of interest, say
A
, is deducted from the action of
A
on these randomized vectors. We leave the details of these algorithms in the Appendix
I
, where we will provide a concrete procedure of two algorithms to estimate rank, in the infinite case (like
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
; see Algorithm
3
) and the finite case (like
\mathbb{F}_{p}
; see Algorithm
4
). In the following, we provide a quantum algorithm for estimating the rank of a block-encoded matrix, based on the classical algorithm (see Algo
4
in Appendix
I
).

We recall that we have the block-encoding of:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}

(83)

which is of dimension
|\mathcal{S}_{r}|\times|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
. According to Algorithm
4
, the first step is to generate random vectors of dimension
|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
, with i.i.d. entries over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. This is a nontrivial task, as generating a random state is still a topic of research
[
57
,
58
]
. However, these methods are designed in the infinite field
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
regime. To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of any direct method for generating a quantum state with i.i.d. entries over a finite field. To circumvent this, we propose to use a classical generator, which draws elements from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with equal probability. With the classically randomized numbers from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, an application of the Lemma
8
is straightforward to prepare the corresponding state. For subsequent usage, we summarize the above procedure, including a classical randomization of numbers from finite field and preparing the corresponding quantum state in the following lemma:

Lemma 14
.

Let
x
be a
n
-dimensional vector with i.i.d. entries randomly (and classically) drawn from some finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Then there is a quantum circuit of depth
\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)
that prepares the state
\ket{\textbf{x}}=\frac{\textbf{x}}{||\textbf{x}||}
.

The next step, e.g., Step 3 in Algorithm
4
, is to estimate an inner product (modulo
p
) of the form:

\displaystyle\braket{\textbf{x}_{1},\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\textbf{x}_{2}}

(84)

for
\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}},\ket{\textbf{x}_{2}}
being some state proportional to vectors
\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2}
with randomly i.i.d. entries from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. What we have above is the block-encoding of:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}

(85)

An application of Lemma
10
allows us to use the above block-encoding and obtain the block-encoding of:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}

(86)

where
\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}
is the condition number of the boundary operator
\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}
. Denote the unitary block-encoding of the above operator as
U_{r}
. Upon acting this unitary on
\ket{\bf 0}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}
, then according to Definition
1
, we have:

\displaystyle U_{r}\ket{\bf 0}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}=\ket{\bf 0}\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}+\ket{\rm Garbage}

(87)

Next, we prepare the state
\ket{\bf 0}\ket{\textbf{x}_{2}}
by using Lemma
14
and append ancilla qubits initialized in
\ket{\bf 0}
. Then we use Hadamard/SWAP test/or amplitude estimation subroutine to estimate the inner product:

\displaystyle\bra{\bf 0}\bra{\textbf{x}_{2}}U_{r}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\bf 0}\bra{\textbf{x}_{2}}\left(\ket{\bf 0}\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}+\ket{\rm Garbage}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle=\bra{\bf 0}\bra{\textbf{x}_{2}}\ket{\bf 0}\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}

(89)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{||\textbf{x}_{2}||||\textbf{x}_{1}||\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}\textbf{x}_{2}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\textbf{x}_{1}

(90)

where from the first line to the second line we have used the property that the state
\ket{\rm Garbage}
is orthogonal to
\ket{\bf 0}\ket{\phi}
for arbitrary
\ket{\phi}
. The value of
\textbf{x}_{2}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\textbf{x}_{1}
can be inferred from the above estimation:

\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{2}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\textbf{x}_{1}=||\textbf{x}_{2}||||\textbf{x}_{1}||\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}\bra{\bf 0}\bra{\textbf{x}_{2}}U_{r}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}

(91)

We remark an important detail that since we are dealing with finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the above estimation needs to be done modulo
p
. Thus, after we estimate the above inner product, we need to take modulo
p
, then we obtain
\textbf{x}_{2}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\textbf{x}_{1}
(mod
p
).

Now we are ready to provide a formal description of our quantum algorithm for torsion detection.

Algorithm 2

(Quantum algorithm for torsion detection)
.

Let
\{\mathscr{D}_{r}\}
be the classical specification of the simplicial complex
K
of interest.

1.

Use the classical knowledge of
\mathscr{D}_{r},\mathscr{D}_{r+1}
and Lemma
7
to construct the block-encoding of

\frac{1}{||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}(\partial_{r}^{\dagger})^{\rm mod\ p}\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p},\frac{1}{||\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}(\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger})^{\rm mod\ p}

2.

Use the block-encoding arithmetic recipes Lemma.
3
,
4
to construct the block-encoding of

\frac{1}{2||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}

3.

Use Lemma
10
to transform the above block-encoded operator into the unitary block-encoding
U_{r}
of:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}

(92)

4.

Use Lemma
14
to generate
t
different quantum states
\ket{v_{1}},\ket{v_{2}},...,\ket{v_{t}}
with randomly i.i.d. entries drawn from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Likewise, generating
s
different quantum states
\ket{u_{1}},\ket{u_{2}},...,\ket{u_{s}}
with randomly i.i.d. entries drawn from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

5.

For all
i=1,2,...,t
, and
j=1,2,...,s
, estimate the overlaps:

\displaystyle\bra{\bf 0}\bra{u_{i}}U_{r}\ket{v_{j}}

(93)

6.

For all
i=1,2,...,t
, and
j=1,2,...,s
, estimate:

\displaystyle u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}=||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}\bra{\bf 0}\bra{u_{i}}U_{r}\ket{v_{j}}

(94)

followed by taking modulo
p
. Then we obtain
u_{i}^{\dagger}U_{r}v_{j}
(mod
p
)

7.

Classically build the matrix
M
with
M_{ij}=u_{i}^{\dagger}U_{r}v_{j}
(mod
p
). Then find its rank
\hat{r}
via classical Gaussian elimination modulo
p
. For convenience, we denote
\hat{r}_{p}
as the estimator of the rank of
\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}
.

8.

Repeat the above algorithm for various values of prime
p
, we obtain a sequence of estimations
\hat{r}_{p_{1}},\hat{r}_{p_{2}},....,\hat{r}_{p_{K}}
.

9.

Compare the values
\hat{r}_{p_{1}},\hat{r}_{p_{2}},....,\hat{r}_{p_{K}}
and also the value of
\frac{\rm rank\Delta_{r}}{|\mathcal{S}_{r}|}
estimated via Algorithm
1
.

Output:
If there are differences
\longrightarrow
complex
K
has torsion (at order
r
). Otherwise, it does not have torsion.

G.3
Extension to oracle-based input model

We recall that the input information to our algorithm is the classical knowledge of matrices
\{\mathscr{D}_{r}\}
encoding the mutual relation between
r
-simplexes and
(r-1)
-simplexes. This classical knowledge allows us to leverage the result of
[
49
]
(see Lemma
7
) to block-encode the operator that is
\varpropto\Delta_{r}
, as well as subsequently,
\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}
. As mentioned earlier, existing works on quantum TDA have different input assumptions. Many, if not most of them, such as
[
18
,
19
,
22
,
20
]
, encode the simplexes of the given complex into the computational basis states of appropriate qubits system. For instance, assuming that the complex
K
has
N
data points. Let
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{N}
denotes these
N
data points, or 0-simplexes. Then a 1-simplex
[v_{i},v_{j}]
is encoded to the computational basis state of
N
-qubits system, that has the bit at position
i
and
j
equal to
1
, and 0 otherwise. Likewise, a 2-simplex
[v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}]
is encoded to the basis state that has the bit
1
at position
i,j,k
, and 0 otherwise. Generally, a
r
-simplexes is encoded into a string of Hamming weight
r+1
. In addition, another enabling component of these algorithms is the oracle
O_{r}
, which acts on a string
\sigma_{r}
of Hamming weight
r+1
as follows:

\displaystyle O_{r}\ket{0}\ket{\sigma_{r}}=\begin{cases}\ket{1}\ket{\sigma_{r}}\text{\ if $\sigma_{r}\in$ K }\\
\ket{0}\ket{\sigma_{r}}\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(95)

One can see that the above oracle is similar to the oracle in the Grover algorithm. In fact, the algorithm in
[
18
,
19
,
22
,
20
]
employs a multi-solution version of Grover’s search algorithm as a subroutine.

The integration of the above oracle with our algorithm is quite straightforward as a corollary of a technique introduced
[
22
]
. Specifically, in Section 6, they show the following:

Lemma 15

(Section 6 of
[
22
]
)
.

Provided the oracle
O_{r}
as above, then there is a quantum circuit of depth
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
that is an exact block-encoding of
\frac{\partial_{r}}{N(r+1)}
.

Thus, we can use the above result to obtain the block-encoding of:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{r}}{N(r+1)},\frac{\partial_{r}^{\dagger}}{N(r+1)},\frac{\partial_{r+1}}{N(r+2)},\frac{\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}}{N(r+2)}

(96)

Then we use Lemma
1
,
3
to construct the block-encoding of:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N(r+1)(r+2)}\left(\partial_{r}^{\dagger}\partial_{r}+\partial_{r+1}\partial_{r+1}^{\dagger}\right)=\frac{1}{2N(r+1)(r+2)}\Delta_{r}

(97)

In the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the boundary operator has the same representation
\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}\equiv\partial_{r}
. Thus, the above block-encoded operator is also

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N(r+1)(r+2)}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}

(98)

It can be seen that the above block-encoding is similar to what we have in the second step of Algorithm
2
. Therefore, we can execute a similar procedure.

Appendix H
Complexity and Error Analysis

In this section, we provide a detailed discussion on the overall error and complexity of the algorithm outlined above (the non-oracle case). We will follow exactly each step of the algorithm
2
and explicitly detail the complexity of the recipes used in each step. An analysis for the oracle caes will be given afterward.

H.1
Analysis for non-oracle case

Step 1:
We first point out that the operator
\mathscr{D}_{r}
has size

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\right)

Because the circuit depth of the unitary that block-encodes the left-hand side operator is

\epsilon
-approximated block-encoding of

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(\frac{||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}||\partial_{r+1}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)\right)

||\partial_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}||_{F}^{2}=(r+1)|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
for all

. So the above complexity is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(\frac{r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|}{\epsilon}\right)\right)

(103)

At this step, we use Lemma
8
to prepare the states (of dimension
|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
) with randomly i.i.d. entries drawn from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, which use a quantum circuit of depth:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\log(|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\right)

(104)

Step 5 & 6:
At these two steps, we need to use the block-encoding from the previous two steps, inside the Hadamard/SWAP/amplitude estimation subroutine to estimate the desired overlaps. Suppose that we desire to estimate the overlaps to precision
\epsilon
, then we can use the recent results
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
which bypass the need of the quantum Fourier transform compared to the traditional methods. Provided that the circuit depth of the block-encoding of
\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}
and of
\ket{u_{i}},\ket{v_{j}}
are, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(\frac{r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|}{\epsilon}\right)\right),\ \mathcal{O}\left(\log|\mathcal{S}_{r}|\right)

(105)

Thus, the complexity, or circuit depth, of estimating:

\displaystyle\bra{\bf 0}\bra{u_{i}}U_{r}\ket{v_{j}}=\frac{1}{||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}

(106)

to a precision
\epsilon
is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(\frac{r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|}{\epsilon}\right)\right)

(107)

We note that, because the block-encoding of
\frac{\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}}{\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}
is actually
\epsilon
-approximated (see Definition
1
), thus the error would accumulate linearly. In other words, we obtain the estimation of
\bra{\bf 0}\bra{u_{i}}U_{r}\ket{v_{j}}
with accuracy
\epsilon+\epsilon=2\epsilon
. In order to obtain an estimation of
u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}
with a precision, say
\epsilon^{\prime}
, we need to choose
2\epsilon||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}=\epsilon^{\prime}
, which leads to:

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}}

(108)

and thus the circuit depth is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{3}\log^{4}\left(\frac{r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|}{\epsilon}\right)\right)

(109)

We remark that, ideally, the value of
u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}
is an integer. So, the estimation of
u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}
can be done with precision
\epsilon^{\prime}=1/2
, then from such estimation we can round it to the nearest integer. Afterward, we can take modulo
p
to obtain
u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}
(mod
p
). As
\epsilon^{\prime}
(and thus
\epsilon
) is of
\mathcal{O}(1)
, the eventual quantum circuit depth for obtaining
u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}
(mod
p
) is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{3}\log^{4}\left(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|\right)\right)

(110)

We remark that we need to evaluate
u_{i}^{\dagger}\Delta_{r}^{\rm mod\ p}v_{j}
(mod
p
) for
i=1,2,...,t
,
j=1,2,...,s
, so we need to repeat the above circuit
s\cdot t
times.

Step 7 & 8 & 9:
These steps are classical, and it takes negligible cost. Indeed, performing Gaussian elimination (mod
p
) on a matrix of size
m\times n
has complexity
\mathcal{O}(mn)

[
59
]
. So the complexity for finding the rank of
M
(mod
p
) is
\mathcal{O}\left(st\right)
. As provided in Algo.
4
, the value of
s,t
is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log_{p}\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
, where
\delta
is the failure probability of the above algorithm.

Summary.
To sum up, to succeed with probability
1-\delta
, our algorithm makes use of a quantum circuit of (maximum) depth

\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{i,j}\left(||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\right)\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|\right)\right)

and need to repeat such circuit
\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{2}_{p}\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
times, followed by a classical routine of complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{2}_{p}\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
. To simplify further, we can define
\mathcal{C}\equiv\max_{i,j}\{||u_{i}||,||v_{j}||\}_{i,j=1}^{s,t}
. So the above complexity is

\mathcal{O}\left(\mathcal{C}^{2}\log(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}|)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(r|\mathcal{S}_{r}||\mathcal{S}_{r+1}|\right)\right)

H.2
Analysis of the norm
\max_{i,j}\left(||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\right)

An important factor within the above complexity is
\max_{i,j}\left(||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\right)
. To remind, the entries of
u_{i},v_{i}
are randomly drawn from a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\left(-\frac{p-1}{2},\cdots-1,0,1,\cdots,\frac{p-1}{2}\right)
. It can be seen that such norm
||u_{i}||,||v_{i}||
can be (very) small when the entries are dominant by zero, i.e., the vector
u_{i},v_{i}
are (very) sparse. As the entries are selected probabilistically, a natural question to ask is, with what probability are these
l_{2}
norm
||u_{i}||,||v_{j}||
sufficiently small ? To this end, we state the following result and subsequently prove it:

Lemma 16
.

Let
\textbf{x}\equiv(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{N}
with randomly i.i.d. entries, e.g., for
1\leq i\leq N
,
x_{i}
is drawn from
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\{-\frac{p-1}{2},-\frac{p-1}{2}+1,...,-1,0,1,\frac{p-1}{2}+1,\frac{p-1}{2}\}
with probability
\frac{1}{p}
. Define
||\textbf{x}||=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}}
. Then we have

•

(Weak version) For some
S\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
S^{2}\geq\frac{N(p^{2}-1)}{12}
, the probability that
||\textbf{x}||\leq S
is:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(||\textbf{x}||\leq S)\geq 1-\frac{N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{2}}{N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{2}+(S^{2}-\frac{N(p^{2}-1)}{12})^{2}}

(111)

•

(Stronger version) For any
S\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(||\textbf{x}||\leq S)\geq\Phi\left(\frac{S^{2}-\frac{N(p^{2}-1)}{12}}{\sqrt{N}\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)}\right)-\frac{0.56(p-1)^{6}}{2^{6}\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{3}\sqrt{N}}

(112)

where
\Phi
is the standard normal CDF.

Proof:
Our proof makes use of well-known results from probability theory
\&
statistics. We define the following variable:

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{F}_{p}

(113)

\displaystyle Z_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{2}

(114)

\displaystyle||\textbf{x}||^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}

(115)

For convenience, we denote
\mathbb{F}_{p}\equiv\{-\frac{p-1}{2}+k\}
for
k=0,1,...,p-1
. Then it can be seen that:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(x_{i}=-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)=\frac{1}{p}

(116)

for any
i=1,2,...,N
and
k=0,1,...,p-1
. We compute the mean and the variance of
Z_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\rm Pr(x_{i}=-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k\right)^{2}

(117)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{1}{p}\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k\right)^{2}

(118)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}

(119)

\displaystyle\rm Var(Z_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\rm Pr(x_{i}=-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\frac{\left(\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k\right)^{2}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})\right)^{2}}{N-1}

(120)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}

(121)

With regard to
||\textbf{x}||^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(||\textbf{x}||^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})

(122)

\displaystyle=N\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}

(123)

\displaystyle\rm Var(||\textbf{x}||^{2})

\displaystyle=N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rm Var(Z_{i})

(124)

\displaystyle=N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)

(125)

We also point out that:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(||\textbf{x}||\leq S)=\rm Pr(||\textbf{x}||^{2}\leq S^{2}).

(126)

The weak version of the lemma above is a consequence of the one-sided Chebysev-Cantelli inequality, which states that, for a random variable
Y
drawn from a probability distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(Y\geq\mu+t)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+t^{2}}

(127)

In our case, we need to replace:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=||\textbf{x}||^{2}

(128)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(||\textbf{x}||^{2})=N\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}

(129)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\rm Var(||\textbf{x}||^{2})=N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)

(130)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=S^{2}-\mu

(131)

Then we have:

\displaystyle\rm Pro(||\textbf{x}||^{2}\geq S^{2})\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\big(S^{2}-\mathbb{E}(||\textbf{x}||\big)^{2}}

(132)

which implies that:

\displaystyle\rm Pro(||\textbf{x}||^{2}\leq S^{2})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\big(S^{2}-\mathbb{E}(||\textbf{x}||\big)^{2}}

(133)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{2}}{N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{2}+(S^{2}-\frac{N(p^{2}-1)}{12})^{2}}

(134)

thus completing the proof of the weak version of Lemma
16
.

The strong version, on the other hand, is a consequence of the Berry-Esseen-type normal bound. It states that for a random variable
Y
drawn from a probability distribution with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
, defining the variable
X=\frac{S^{2}-\mu}{\sigma}
, then it holds that:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(Y\leq S^{2})\geq\Phi(x)-\frac{C\rho}{\sigma^{3}/N}

(135)

where
C\leq 0.56
is a constant, and
\rho\equiv\mathbb{E}\big(|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})|\big)^{3}
. Again, we replace:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=||\textbf{x}||^{2}

(136)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(||\textbf{x}||^{2})=N\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}

(137)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\rm Var(||\textbf{x}||^{2})=N\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)

(138)

Now we consider specifically the term
\rho\equiv\mathbb{E}\big(|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})|\big)^{3}
, showing that it is upper bounded. We have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})|\big)^{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\rm Pr(x_{i}=-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\Big|\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\right)^{2}-\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}\Big|^{3}

(139)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{1}{p}\Big|\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\right)^{2}-\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}\Big|^{3}

(140)

We observe that, for all
k
:

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\right)^{2}-\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}\leq\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)^{2}

(141)

Thus:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{1}{p}\Big|\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}+k)\right)^{2}-\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}\Big|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)^{6}

(142)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)^{6}

(143)

So we have:

\displaystyle\rho\equiv\mathbb{E}\big(|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})|\big)^{3}\leq\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)^{6}

(144)

So from the inequality:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(Y\leq S^{2})\geq\Phi(x)-\frac{C\rho}{\sigma^{3}/N}

(145)

we can deduce that:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(||\textbf{x}||^{2}\leq S^{2})\geq\Phi\left(\frac{S^{2}-\frac{N(p^{2}-1)}{12}}{\sqrt{N}\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)}\right)-\frac{0.56(p-1)^{6}}{2^{6}\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{3}\sqrt{N}}

(146)

which is inherently equivalent to:

\displaystyle\rm Pr(||\textbf{x}||\leq S)\geq\Phi\left(\frac{S^{2}-\frac{N(p^{2}-1)}{12}}{\sqrt{N}\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)}\right)-\frac{0.56(p-1)^{6}}{2^{6}\left(\frac{p^{4}}{180}-\frac{p^{2}}{36}+\frac{1}{45}\right)^{3}\sqrt{N}}

(147)

Combining the above bounds and the complexity from previous section, we have that the probability for
\max_{i}\{||u_{i}||\},\max_{j}\{||v_{j}||\}
, and thus
\mathcal{C}
, having bounded
l_{2}
-norms obey the above inequality, with
N
replaced by
|\mathcal{S}_{r}|
.

H.3
Analysis for oracle case

While the above complexity analysis is for the non-oracle case, the complexity for the oracle case is almost identical. The only difference is that, in the
Step 1
and
Step 2
, we use the result of
[
22
]
to obtain the block-encoding of
\frac{\Delta_{r}}{2N(r+1)(r+2)}
. The complexity of these two steps is
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}\right)
, which means that the complexity at the
Step 3
is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\left(\frac{N(r+1)(r+2)}{\epsilon}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\frac{Nr}{\epsilon}\right)

(148)

The remaining steps and corresponding complexity can be deduced in a straightforward manner. The final circuit depth is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\left(N^{2}\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}^{2}\log^{4}\big(Nr\big)+\log|\mathcal{S}_{r}|\right)\kappa_{\Delta_{r}}\max_{i,j}\left(||u_{i}||\cdot||v_{j}||\right)\right)

(149)

The total of iterations is
st=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
for a success probability
1-\delta
.

Appendix I
Classical Randomized Algorithm for Rank Estimation

For convenience, we provide a neat summary, or pipeline of the two algorithms for rank estimation over infinite and finite field, respectively. We refer the interested readers to the correspondingly original works for a more rigorous treatment.

The first one is the stochastic rank estimation proposed in
[
34
,
35
,
20
]
. The method is based on the property that
\rm rankA\equiv\Tr\left(h(A)\right)
where
h(x)
is a step function for
x\in(-1,1)
. The step function is then approximated by Chebyshev polynomial
h(x)\approx\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}T_{j}(x)
where
T_{j}(.)
is the Chebyshev polynomial.

Algorithm 3

(Rank estimation over
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{C}

[
34
,
35
]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}/\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian matrix.

1.

Draw
s
random vectors
u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with i.i.d. entries
\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{C}
.

2.

For each
i=1,2,...,s
, compute
\braket{u_{i},T_{j}(A)u_{i}}
where
T_{j}(.)
is the Chebyshev polynomial.

3.

Compute:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{s}u_{i}^{\dagger}T_{j}(A)u_{i}

(150)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{j}u_{i}^{\dagger}T_{j}(A)u_{i}

(151)

Output:

\hat{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{j}u_{i}^{\dagger}T_{j}(A)u_{i}
is the estimator for the rank of
A
.

Guarantee:
For
s=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),m=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta\epsilon}\right)
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\big|\hat{r}-r\big|\leq\epsilon

(152)

As can be seen, the above method is based on the approximation of
\rm rank(A)
as a trace of certain function of
A
(the step function). The trace is then approximated by a polynomial of corresponding matrix. An important ingredient in the above algorithm is the first step. As pointed out in
[
34
,
35
]
, any random vector with zero mean and i.i.d. coordinates can work. To obtain such a sequence of random vectors, the authors in
[
20
]
proposed to randomly draw columns from the Hadamard matrix. As emphasized in
[
20
]
, this choice works very well in practical setting. To achieve this, they began with the state
\ket{0}^{\log n}
, followed by a random application of NOT gate in each of the
\log n
qubits, and then Hadamard gates to all
\log n
qubits. Repeating this process
s
times results in random Hadamard states
\ket{u_{1}},\ket{u_{2}},...,\ket{u_{s}}
, which is sufficient for the purpose of trace and rank estimation in the above algorithm. To be more precise, since the states
\ket{u_{1}},\ket{u_{2}},...,\ket{u_{s}}
are normalized, the estimation
\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{j}\bra{u_{i}}T_{j}(A)\ket{u_{i}}
actually approximate
\frac{\Tr A}{N}
.

It can be seen that the above discussion does not hold in the finite case, as we cannot approximate the trace and rank via Chebyshev polynomials. To this end, it turns out that there are multiple classical algorithms tailored for finite case. In the following, we provide a summary/pipeline of the method introduced in
[
44
,
45
]
.

Algorithm 4

(Rank Estimation Over
\mathbb{F}_{p}

[
44
,
45
]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n\times n}
be a matrix of size
m\times n
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Let
r
denotes the rank of
A
.

1.

Draw
t
random vectors
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. Define
V
be a matrix of size
n\times t
, with
[v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{t}]

2.

Draw
s
random vectors
u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}

U
be a matrix of size
s\times n
, with
[u_{1}^{T},u_{2}^{T},...,u_{s}^{T}]

3.

Define a matrix
M
of size
s\times t
with entries:

\displaystyle M_{ij}=u_{i}^{T}Av_{j}\text{ \ (mod p)}

(153)

Or equivalently

\displaystyle M=UAV

(154)

4.

Find rank
\hat{r}
of

(via Gaussian elimination mod p).

Output:

\hat{r}
