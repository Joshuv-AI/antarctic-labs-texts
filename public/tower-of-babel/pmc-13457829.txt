Understanding the mechanisms underlying obesity induced tumorigenesis: therapeutic perspectives to manage dysregulated lipid metabolism
Authors: Uzma Faridi, Rashid Mir, Naseh A. Algehainy, Zeyad M. Alharbi, Faris J. Tayeb, Jameel Barnawi, Mohammed M. Jalal, Malik A. Altayar, Mamdoh Moawadh, Syed Khalid Mustafa, Mohammad Muzaffar Mir, Jaber Alfaifi, Ibrahim Abdullah Almazni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity has emerged as one of the most significant global public health challenges of the twenty-first century and is now firmly established as a major risk factor for multiple cancer types. Hypertrophic adipose tissue generates chronic low-grade inflammation, adipose hypoxia, and fibrosis, which leads to activation of IL-6/STAT3, NF-κB, and PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling. Hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance increase oncogenic pathways, while dysregulated adipokines by elevated leptin and reduced adiponectin enhance proliferation, angiogenesis, epithelial mesenchymal transition, and immune evasion. Obesity further remodels the tumor microenvironment by promoting extracellular matrix deposition, angiogenesis, immunosuppressive cell expansion, and metabolic competition that impairs antitumor immunity. Emerging evidence also implicates obesity-driven epigenetic reprogramming, altered microbiome composition, and metabolic heterogeneity as key determinants of tumor aggressiveness and treatment response. Conventional oncologic therapies rarely account for host metabolic status, which may contribute to variable therapeutic efficacy in obese patients. This review integrates mechanistic and translational evidence to define actionable vulnerabilities within obesity-induced tumorigenic pathways. We evaluate targeted metabolic interventions, cytokine blockade, adipokine modulation, immune checkpoint optimization, microbiome-directed strategies, and precision stratification frameworks. The present review outlines a multidimensional therapeutic roadmap to improve prevention, treatment response, and long-term outcomes in obesity-associated malignancies.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity has risen dramatically over recent decades, reaching epidemic levels in both developed and developing countries [1, 103]. Obesity, defined by excessive adipose tissue accumulation and commonly measured by using body mass index (BMI), It is strongly associated with increased morbidity and mortality [46]. While BMI is widely used, it does not fully reflect fat distribution or metabolic risk. Many earlier studies focused on BMI without considering metabolic heterogeneity. Another facet of this is often a failure to consider diet composition, e.g., high BMI and a high fat diet were often treated as equivalent [73]. The broader literature identifies the non-equivalence of these factors, e.g., some individuals may be normal BMI, yet ingest a high fat diet [15, 104]. There are also epidemiological studies that strongly suggest that high BMI is protective against hormone-responsive breast cancer in pre-menopausal women, but this is reversed post-menopause [28]. These observations highlight the need to consider metabolic phenotype, dietary factors, and hormonal context when interpreting the asscociation of obesity and cancer. Obesity has long been associated to cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome [38]. More recently, considerable evidence has established obesity as an independent risk factor for cancer incidence, progression, and mortality [66, 81]. Epidemiological studies consistently indicated strong association between obesity and numerous cancers, including postmenopausal breast, colorectal, endometrial, pancreatic, hepatocellular, renal cell carcinoma, and esophageal adenocarcinoma [2, 80]. Large cohort studies and meta-analyses report a dose-dependent relationship between increasing BMI and cancer risk and mortality [101]. Obesity also worsens prognosis of the disease and contributs to higher recurrence rates, metastasis, and reduced overall survival [17, 66]. The link between obesity and cancer is multifactorial, involving systemic metabolic disturbances and alterations within adipose tissue and the tumor microenvironment (TME) [51, 52, 62, 80, 86]. Adipose tissue functions as an endocrine and immune organ [8, 79]. In obesity, it undergoes hypertrophy, hypoxia, fibrosis, and immune cell infiltration, promoting a pro-tumorigenic environment [79]. Chronic low-grade inflammation is a central mechanism. Obese adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1, leading to activation of NF-κB and STAT3 pathways that promote genomic instability, angiogenesis, and resistance to apoptosis [5, 31, 41, 50, 59, 86]. Hyperinsulinemia and dysregulation of the insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) axis further contribute. Insulin resistance induces compensatory hyperinsulinemia, stimulating tumor proliferation and increasing bioavailable IGF-1 [38, 84]. Activation of insulin and IGF-1 receptors triggers oncogenic PI3K/Akt/mTOR and Ras/MAPK pathways, commonly altered in cancer [8, 40, 48, 49, 87]. Adipokine imbalance also plays an important role as Obesity increases leptin and reduces adiponectin levels [79]. Leptin promotes proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis via JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK signaling [32, 45, 53, 98]. In contrast, adiponectin exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative effects, and its reduction removes a protective mechanism against tumorigenesis [26]. Hormonal dysregulation is particularly significant in postmenopausal women, where adipose tissue becomes the main source of estrogen through increased aromatase activity [12]. Elevated estrogen stimulates tumor growth in hormone-sensitive tissues, especially breast and endometrial cancers [52, 58]. Obesity also impairs anti-tumor immunity. Excess adiposity reduces cytotoxic T-cell and NK cell function, increases T-cell exhaustion, and expands immunosuppressive populations such as regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [19, 68, 77]. These changes weaken immune surveillance and facilitate tumor immune evasion [13]. Beyond increasing cancer risk, obesity adversely affects treatment response. It is associated with greater tumor aggressiveness, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy [17, 34]. Altered pharmacokinetics, inflammation, hypoxia, and immune dysfunction reduce therapeutic efficacy [52, 109]. Although improved responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors have been reported in some obese patients, this “obesity paradox” remains incompletely understood [67, 68]. Recognizing obesity as a modifiable driver of tumorigenesis has prompted development of targeted strategies, including metabolic modulation, anti-inflammatory therapies, adipokine targeting, immune reprogramming, and lifestyle interventions [30]. Mechanistic links between obesity and tumorigenesis

Chronic inflammation and cytokine signaling

One of the defining hallmarks of obesity is the presence of chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation, which plays a central role in obesity-associated tumorigenesis [50, 86]. As adipocytes expand in size during obesity, they experience hypoxia endoplasmic reticulum stress, and increased rates of apoptosis and necrosis, leading to the recruitment of immune cells, particularly macrophages, into adipose tissue [52, 77]. These macrophages surround dead or dying adipocytes, forming characteristic crown-like structures that are a pathological hallmark of inflamed obese adipose tissue [22]. Both infiltrating immune cells and hypertrophic adipocytes secrete elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and C-reactive protein (CRP) [59, 79]. Obesity-associated inflammation is characterized as metainflammation, a chronic, low-grade inflammatory condition caused by metabolic excess rather than acute infection. In hypertrophic adipose tissue, nutrient overload, lipotoxicity, and hypoxia promote sustained activation of innate immune pathways which leads to macrophage activation [92]. This metabolically triggered inflammation prolongs insulin resistance and maintains persistent NF-κB and STAT3 signaling, therefore causing the activation of proliferative and survival pathways in pre-malignant and malignant cells [75]. Sustained cytokine production and metainflammation leads to persistent activation of key inflammatory signaling pathways such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and signal transducer and activator of transcription-3 (STAT3), which are well-established drivers of cancer initiation and progression [41, 47]. These pathways promote genomic instability inhibit apoptosis enhance angiogenesis, and facilitate epithelial–mesenchymal transition, thereby supporting malignant transformation and tumor growth [5, 42, 86]. Chronic obesity-associated inflammation not only promotes proliferative signaling but also disrupts key regulators of cell-cycle and apoptosis. Persistent activation of NF-κB and STAT3 has been shown to suppress tumor suppressor pathways involving cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors such as p16(INK4a) and p21(Cip1/Waf1), thereby facilitating unchecked G1/S cell-cycle progression and bypass of senescence checkpoints [70]. Inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress further impair p53-dependent repair process and weakens DNA damage surveillance mechanisms. In parallel, obesity-related inflammatory signaling can attenuate extrinsic apoptotic pathways, including tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) receptor signaling [29]. Downregulation or inactivation of TRAIL receptors reduces caspase activation and apoptosis sensitivity, enabling survival of genetically unstable cells. Collectively, inflammation-driven suppression of cell-cycle arrest and apoptotic signaling establishes a permissive molecular environment that supports malignant transformation and tumor progression in obese individuals [91].

Insulin resistance and the IGF-1 axis

Obesity is strongly associated with insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia, which constitute a major mechanistic link between excess adiposity and cancer development [38, 84]. Elevated circulating insulin levels exert direct mitogenic effects on tumor cells by activating insulin receptors, which are often overexpressed in malignant tissues [97]. In parallel, hyperinsulinemia suppresses insulin-like growth factor binding proteins (IGFBPs), thereby increasing bioavailable IGF-1 [84]. Both insulin and IGF-1 activate downstream oncogenic signaling cascades, most notably the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway and the Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which regulate cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, and angiogenesis [48, 49]. Chronic activation of these pathways in obese individuals creates a permissive metabolic environment that favors tumor initiation and progression and contributes to resistance against anticancer therapies [30, 52].

Adipokine dysregulation

Adipose tissue functions as an active endocrine organ that secretes adipokines involved in metabolic regulation, inflammation, angiogenesis, and immune responses [79, 80]. Obesity profoundly alters adipokine profiles, characterized by increased secretion of leptin and reduced levels of adiponectin [26, 74].

Leptin exerts pro-tumorigenic effects by promoting cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and metastasis through activation of JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways [32]. Elevated leptin levels have been linked to poor prognosis in breast, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers [79]. In contrast, adiponectin has anti-inflammatory, insulin-sensitizing and anti-proliferative properties, and its decline in obesity removes an important protective mechanism against tumorigenesis [19, 26]. The imbalance between leptin and adiponectin strongly favors cancer development and progression in obese individuals.

Sex hormones and aromatase activity

In obese individuals, adipose tissue becomes a major extragonadal site of estrogen biosynthesis due to increased expression and activity of the aromatase enzyme [12]. This mechanism is particularly relevant in postmenopausal women, where ovarian estrogen production has ceased and adipose tissue-derived estrogen becomes the dominant source [58]. Elevated circulating estrogen levels promote the development and progression of hormone-sensitive malignancies, including breast and endometrial cancers, by stimulating estrogen receptor–mediated transcriptional programs that enhance cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis [14, 21, 52]. Obesity-related inflammation further amplifies estrogen production by upregulating aromatase expression via cytokine-mediated signaling, creating a feed-forward loop that accelerates tumor growth [12].

Immune dysfunction and the tumor microenviroment

Obesity induces profound alterations in both innate and adaptive immune responses, contributing to immune dysfunction within the tumour microenvironment (TME) [13, 19]. Excess adiposity is associated with T-cell exhaustion, reduced cytotoxic activity of natural killer (NK) cells, and impaired antigen presentation [19]. Additionally, obesity promotes the expansion of immunosuppressive cell populations, including regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which inhibit effective anti-tumour immune responses [86]. Metabolic competition within the TME further compromises immune cell function, as tumour cells outcompete immune cells for glucose and other nutrients [13]. Collectively, these obesity-driven immune alterations weaken immune surveillance and facilitate tumour immune evasion, contributing to disease progression and therapeutic resistance.

Obesity-associated adipose tissue hypoxia and HIF-1α signaling in tumor progression

Adipose tissue expansion during obesity overtakes the vascularization, which results in regional hypoxia within hypertrophic fat depots [99]. This hypoxic microenvironment recognized as a critical driver of obesity induced tumorigenesis. Reduced oxygen tension stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a major transcriptional regulator that orchestrates angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling [64]. In obese adipose tissue, persistent HIF-1α activation promotes the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), enhances the angiogenesis and induces the glycolytic enzymes, favoring aerobic glycolysis, Importantly, obesity-driven HIF-1α activation does not remain confined to adipose tissue [44]. Paracrine signaling between hypoxic adipocytes and adjacent tumor cells amplifies malignant progression. Hypoxia enhances tumor cell survival through metabolic adaptation, shifting cancer cells toward glycolytic dependency while simultaneously increasing lactate production, which acidifies the tumor microenvironment and impairs cytotoxic immune cell function [20, 102]. Also, the HIF-1α cooperates with NF-κB and STAT3 signaling to sustain chronic inflammation and promote resistance to apoptosis [4]. In addition to HIF-1α activation, hypoxic adipose tissue in obesity promotes excessive deposition of extracellular matrix components, particularly collagen VI [72]. Proteolytic cleavage of collagen VI generates endotrophin, a bioactive fragment that enhances fibrosis, macrophage recruitment, and TGF-β signaling within adipose depots. Endotrophin further amplifies epithelial-mesenchymal transition and angiogenic responses, thereby strengthening the pro-tumorigenic crosstalk between dysfunctional adipocytes and adjacent malignant cells [57]. This obesity-associated adipose tissue remodeling contributes to a mechanically rigid and cytokine-enriched microenvironment that facilitates tumor progression [65].

Environmental endocrine disruptors and obesity-associated tumorigenesis

A growing number of EEDs have been shown to be obesogenic. These endocrine disruptors also known as obesogens, promote adipogenesis, lipid accumulation, and metabolic imbalance which leads to obesity [6, 54]. Compounds such as Bisphenol A and Phthalates alter transcriptional regulators like PPARγ, enhancing adipocyte differentiation and long-term fat storage. Chronic exposure may predispose individuals to obesity, a key risk factor for metabolic disease and cancer [18]. Heavy metals further expand this metabolic disruption [19, 25]. Lead exposure has been associated with lipid accumulation in liver, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Lead-induced fatty liver contributes to systemic insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and chronic inflammation, conditions that favor cellular proliferation and genomic instability [100, 106]. In the same way. Arsenic exposure promotes metabolic dysfunction through inflammatory pathway activation and impaired glucose homeostasis. Arsenic-mediated inflammation increases reactive oxygen species production, disrupts insulin signaling cascades, and enhances epigenetic modifications linked to carcinogenesis [78]. Environmental toxicants disrupt endocrine and metabolic homeostasis, leading to obesity and insulin resistance. The resulting pro-inflammatory and hyperinsulinemic condition creates a microenvironment that promote the tumor initiation and progression, particularly in hormone-sensitive tissues.

Metabolic targeting strategies in obesity-driven cancer

Obesity is characterized by systemic metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, dyslipidemia, and altered energy sensing pathways, all of which contribute to tumor initiation and progression. These metabolic disturbances promote sustained proliferative signaling, resistance to cell death, and metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at correcting obesity-associated metabolic abnormalities have emerged as promising approaches to mitigate cancer risk and progression in obese individuals [52, 59].

Targeting insulin and IGF-1 signaling

Insulin resistance is a central feature of obesity and results in compensatory hyperinsulinemia, which has been strongly linked to increased cancer incidence and mortality. Elevated insulin levels directly stimulate tumor cell proliferation through activation of the insulin receptor and indirectly enhance insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling by reducing circulating IGF-binding proteins [38, 84]. Activation of the insulin/IGF-1 axis promotes oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK, leading to increased cell survival, growth, and angiogenesis [97]. Metformin, a first-line antidiabetic drug, has received considerable attention for its potential anticancer effects. Metformin primarily reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis and improves peripheral insulin sensitivity, resulting in lower circulating insulin levels. In both preclinical and clinical studies, metformin has been shown to suppress tumor growth and reduce cancer incidence, particularly in obesity-associated malignancies such as breast, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers [30, 33, 49, 82]. Mechanistically, metformin inhibits mitochondrial complex I, leading to reduced ATP production and activation of AMPK, which in turn suppresses m TOR signaling and induces energetic stress in cancer cells [30]. Beyond metformin, therapeutic strategies targeting the IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R have also been explored. Monoclonal antibodies and small-molecule inhibitors against IGF-1R have demonstrated antitumor activity in preclinical models,however, clinical outcomes have been variable, partly due to compensatory signaling through insulin receptors and metabolic toxicity [38, 84]. These findings highlight the complexity of targeting insulin and IGF-1 signaling in cancer and underscore the need for patient stratification based on metabolic status.

mTOR and AMPK modulation

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism that integrates signals from nutrients, growth factors, and energy status. In obesity, chronic nutrient excess and hyperinsulinemia lead to persistent activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, thereby promoting anabolic metabolism, protein synthesis, and tumor growth [48, 86]. Sustained mTOR activation has been observed in multiple obesity-associated cancers and is associated with poor prognosis. Pharmacological inhibition of mTOR using rapalogs, such as everolimus and temsirolimus, has shown clinical efficacy in certain cancers. These agents can counteract obesity-driven tumorigenesis by limiting cancer cell growth and proliferation. However, their use is often limited by feedback activation of upstream signaling pathways and metabolic side effects, including insulin resistance [48, 49]. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a cellular energy sensor and acts as a metabolic tumor suppressor by inhibiting anabolic pathways and promoting catabolic processes. In obesity, AMPK activity is often suppressed due to nutrient excess and chronic inflammation, contributing to metabolic dysregulation and cancer progression [50, 74]. Activation of AMPK, either pharmacologically or through lifestyle interventions, has been shown to inhibit tumor growth by suppressing mTOR signaling, reducing lipid synthesis, and impairing cancer cell metabolic flexibility [13] (Fig. 2). Agents that activate AMPK, including metformin and other emerging small molecules, represent promising strategies to counteract obesity-associated metabolic tumorigenesis. Modulation of EMT and MET

Metastatic spreading is one of the primary causes of cancer-related mortality all over the world. The accumulating evidences suggest that obesity significantly enhances metastatic potential through induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [10]. Obesity-driven chronic inflammation, hyperinsulinemia, and adipokine dysregulation converge to activate EMT transcriptional programs [69]. Increased level of IL-6 activates STAT3, which directly induces EMT-associated transcription factors including Snail, twist amd ZEB1 [35, 55, 61, 105]. Concurrently, leptin signaling stimulates JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK pathways, reinforcing EMT and promoting cytoskeletal reorganization, cell motility, and invasion) [3]. These molecular changes enable epithelial cancer cells to acquire mesenchymal characteristics, increasing migratory capacity and resistance to anoikis [96]. Importantly, EMT in the context of obesity is metabolically supported. Insulin/IGF-1 signaling enhances glucose uptake and lipid biosynthesis, providing the energetic and structural resources necessary for cell migration and invasion. Obesity-associated lipid accumulation may also fuel β-oxidation pathways in metastatic cells, enhancing survival in circulation. Following dissemination, mesenchymal tumor cells undergo mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) to colonize distant organs [39]. Obesity-induced systemic inflammation and altered stromal niches facilitate this plasticity by creating pre-metastatic niches enriched in cytokines, adipocytes, and immunosuppressive cells. Thus, obesity does not merely increase tumor incidence,it actively drives metastatic competence through metabolic and inflammatory reinforcement of EMT-MET dynamics [27].

Genetic predisposition and obesity-related epigenetic remodeling

Obesity affects cancer not only through inflammation and metabolism but also by influencing genes and how they are regulated. Some people may already carry genetic mutations (like in PIK3CA, KRAS, or MYC) that can lead to cancer. In people with obesity, these mutations may become more harmful [7, 108]. For example, high insulin levels can over-activate growth pathways like PI3K/Akt, while inflammation can speed up cancer in cells with KRAS mutations. A high-nutrient environment also supports the growth and spread of cancer cells driven by MYC [56]. Obesity also alters gene regulation without changing the DNA sequence itself, a process known as epigenetic remodeling. Long-term inflammation, oxidative stress, and changes in cellular metabolites can modify DNA methylation, histone proteins, and microRNA expression [60, 95]. Key metabolic molecules such as acetyl-CoA and NAD⁺ directly affect how genes are turned on or off [107]. In obesity, altered nutrient levels disturb these processes, promoting gene expression patterns that support tumor growth, blood vessel formation, and immune escape. Furthermore, obesity can cause certain cells to enter a senescent state, where they stop dividing but release inflammatory factors known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [36]. These factors create a tumor-supportive environment. Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory interventions

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a defining feature of obesity and a critical driver of obesity-associated tumorigenesis. Excess adipose tissue undergoes hypoxia, adipocyte hypertrophy, and cell death, leading to immune cell infiltration and sustained production of pro-inflammatory mediators. These inflammatory signals not only promote tumor initiation but also shape the tumor microenvironment to favor immune evasion, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance [50, 52, 86].

Targeting inflammatory cytokines

Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) are markedly elevated in obese individuals and play a central role in linking adipose tissue inflammation to cancer development. These cytokines activate oncogenic signaling pathways, including NF-κB and STAT3, which promote DNA damage, inhibit apoptosis, and enhance tumor cell survival and proliferation [24, 41]. IL-6 has emerged as a particularly important mediator of obesity-related tumorigenesis. Elevated IL-6 levels correlate with poor prognosis in several cancers, including breast, colorectal, and hepatocellular carcinoma [79, 90]. Pharmacologic blockade of IL-6 signaling using monoclonal antibodies such as tocilizumab has shown antitumor effects in preclinical models by reducing tumor-associated inflammation and suppressing STAT3 activation [24]. Similarly, TNF-α inhibitors, widely used in inflammatory diseases, have demonstrated potential in reducing obesity-associated cancer progression by limiting inflammatory cell recruitment and angiogenesis [63]. Although cytokine-targeted therapies hold promise, their translation into oncology requires careful consideration due to potential immunosuppressive effects. Ongoing research focuses on combining cytokine inhibition with chemotherapy or immunotherapy to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse outcomes [5, 41].

Immune checkpoint modulation in obese patients

Obesity profoundly alters immune function, leading to T-cell exhaustion, impaired natural killer (NK) cell activity, and expansion of immunosuppressive populations such as regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [19, 68]. These alterations weaken immune surveillance and facilitate tumor progression. Paradoxically, emerging clinical evidence suggests that obese patients may exhibit improved responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly therapies targeting programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) or its ligand PD-L1. Several retrospective studies have reported enhanced survival outcomes in obese patients treated with ICIs for melanoma, lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma [23, 67]. This phenomenon, often termed the “obesity paradox,” may be driven by chronic antigenic stimulation and increased baseline PD-1 expression on T cells in obese individuals, rendering them more responsive to checkpoint blockade [19]. However, obesity-associated inflammation may also exacerbate immune-related adverse events, highlighting the need for personalized immunotherapy strategies based on metabolic and inflammatory status [51]. Adipokine-targeted and hormonal therapies

Adipose tissue functions as an active endocrine organ, secreting adipokines and hormones that regulate metabolism, inflammation, and cell growth. Obesity disrupts adipokine homeostasis, creating a hormonal environment that favors tumourigenesis [59, 79].

Leptin signaling inhibition

Leptin levels increase proportionally with adipose mass and are consistently elevated in obese individuals. Beyond its role in appetite regulation, leptin exerts potent pro-tumorigenic effects by stimulating cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis through activation of JAK/STAT, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways [97]. High leptin expression has been associated with aggressive tumor behavior and poor prognosis in breast, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers [16]. Targeting leptin signaling represents an attractive therapeutic strategy in obesity-related cancer. Experimental approaches include leptin receptor antagonists and inhibitors of downstream signaling pathways, which have demonstrated tumor-suppressive effects in preclinical models [76, 88]. However, translating these strategies into clinical practice remains challenging due to leptin’s systemic metabolic functions.

Restoring adiponectin activity

In contrast to leptin, adiponectin levels are reduced in obesity and exhibit anti-inflammatory, insulin-sensitizing, and anti-proliferative properties. Adiponectin inhibits tumor growth by activating AMPK, suppressing mTOR signaling, and reducing oxidative stress [26, 74]. Low adiponectin levels have been linked to increased risk of breast, colorectal endometrial, and liver cancers [87]. Microbiome and lifestyle-based interventions

Gut microbiota modulation

The gut microbiome plays a critical role in regulating host metabolism, immune function, and inflammation. Obesity is associated with gut dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial diversity and altered metabolic capacity, which contributes to systemic inflammation and cancer risk [43]. Microbiota-derived metabolites can influence immune responses and modulate the efficacy of anticancer therapies, including immunotherapy [37, 109]. Interventions such as probiotics, prebiotics dietary modification, and fecal microbiota transplantation are being explored as adjunctive strategies to mitigate obesity-driven tumorigenesis. Early studies suggest that microbiome modulation may enhance antitumor immunity and improve responses to immunotherapy [90, 109].

Weight loss, diet and physical activity

Lifestyle interventions remain foundational in addressing obesity-associated cancer risk. Sustained weight loss through caloric restriction, bariatric surgery, or increased physical activity has been shown to reduce systemic inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity, and lower circulating levels of pro-tumorigenic hormones [66, 90]. Observational and interventional studies indicate that physically active cancer patients experience improved treatment tolerance, reduced recurrence risk, and enhanced survival [94].

Precision medicine and future directions

The growing recognition of obesity as a biologically active modifier of cancer risk, progression, and treatment response has highlighted the need for precision medicine approaches tailored to obese patients. Advances in genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and systems biology now allow a more comprehensive characterization of tumor biology alongside host metabolic and inflammatory status. Integrating these data has the potential to transform obesity-driven cancer management from a one-size-fits-all paradigm to a personalized therapeutic strategy [52, 79]. Precision oncology in obese patients requires stratification beyond traditional tumor-centric biomarkers. Metabolic phenotypes, including insulin resistance, circulating adipokine profiles, systemic inflammatory markers and body composition metrics, may significantly influence treatment efficacy and toxicity. Emerging evidence suggests that obese patients represent a heterogeneous population, with distinct metabolic subtypes that differentially impact tumor behavior and therapeutic outcomes [49, 66]. Incorporating metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers into clinical decision-making could help identify patients most likely to benefit from targeted metabolic or immunomodulatory interventions. Genomic and epigenomic alterations induced by obesity further complicate tumor biology. Chronic inflammation and metabolic stress can drive epigenetic reprogramming, oxidative DNA damage, and altered gene expression patterns that promote tumor aggressiveness and resistance to therapy [41, 86]. Understanding how these obesity-associated molecular changes intersect with oncogenic drivers may inform rational combination therapies that simultaneously target tumor-intrinsic pathways and obesity-related host factors. A critical future direction lies in the integration of metabolic interventions with standard cancer therapies. Combining agents such as metformin, AMPK activators, anti-inflammatory drugs, or microbiome-modulating strategies with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy may enhance treatment efficacy while mitigating resistance mechanisms [30, 109]. Importantly, obesity may alter drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, underscoring the need for optimized dosing strategies that account for body composition rather than body weight alone [93]. Despite promising preclinical and observational data, a major limitation in the field is the lack of large-scale, prospective clinical trials specifically designed to evaluate obesity-targeted cancer therapies. Most oncology trials do not stratify patients by metabolic status or adequately capture obesity-related variables, limiting the generalizability of findings to obese populations [66]. Despite of substantial progress in understanding obesity-driven tumorigenesis still several important knowledge gaps remain unanswered. Earlier studies largely relied on body mass index (BMI) as the primary measure of obesity, which fails to capture metabolic heterogeneity among patients [11, 15, 71]. Consequently, many oncology trials have not adequately stratified patients based on metabolic status, limiting interpretation of therapeutic outcomes in obese populations [83, 85]. Now the Recent researches are addressing the limitation by incorporating more refined stratification approaches, including body composition analysis (visceral versus subcutaneous adiposity), assessment of insulin resistance, circulating inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and C-reactive protein, and classification of sarcopenic obesity [9]. Emerging strategies now extend beyond conventional measures and include adipokine profiling (e.g., leptin/adiponectin ratio), metabolomic signatures, epigenetic biomarkers, microbiome-defined subgroups, and integrated tumor-immunometabolic phenotyping [89]. These approaches recognize obesity as a biologically heterogeneous condition rather than a uniform risk factor. Future clinical trials should incorporate such multidimensional stratification frameworks to better define therapeutic responsiveness and personalize treatment strategies for obese cancer patients [52].

Conclusion

Obesity-induced tumorigenesis represents a multifaceted and dynamic process driven by the convergence of metabolic dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, hormonal imbalance, adipokine dysregulation, immune impairment, and alterations in the tumor microenvironment. As the global prevalence of obesity continues to rise, the burden of obesity-associated cancers is expected to increase substantially, posing significant challenges to cancer prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. Accumulating evidence clearly demonstrates that obesity not only increases cancer incidence but also worsens disease progression, therapeutic resistance, and overall prognosis, underscoring the urgent need for novel and integrative therapeutic strategies. Traditional cancer therapies have largely focused on tumor-intrinsic genetic alterations, often overlooking the critical role of host metabolic and inflammatory status. This tumor-centric approach may partially explain the suboptimal outcomes observed in obese cancer patients. The emerging understanding of obesity as an active biological driver of tumorigenesis has shifted the therapeutic paradigm toward targeting obesity-related mechanisms alongside conventional oncologic treatments. Interventions aimed at correcting insulin resistance, modulating the IGF-1 axis, inhibiting mTOR signaling, and activating AMPK have shown considerable promise in counteracting metabolic drivers of cancer growth. Similarly, targeting inflammatory cytokines and reshaping immune responses has opened new avenues for enhancing antitumor immunity and improving responses to immunotherapy in obese patients. Adipokine- and hormone-targeted therapies further highlight the importance of addressing the endocrine function of adipose tissue in cancer progression. The imbalance between leptin and adiponectin, as well as increased estrogen production in obesity, creates a pro-tumorigenic hormonal milieu that can be therapeutically exploited. In parallel, growing insights into the gut microbiome and its role in immunometabolism regulation have revealed novel opportunities for adjunctive interventions that may enhance treatment efficacy and reduce obesity-associated cancer risk. Lifestyle-based strategies remain foundational in addressing obesity-driven cancer, serving not only as preventive measures but also as important adjuncts to cancer therapy. Weight loss, dietary modification, and physical activity have demonstrated beneficial effects on systemic inflammation, insulin sensitivity, immune function, and treatment tolerance. Integrating these interventions into standard oncology care has the potential to improve both cancer-specific outcomes and overall patient health. In conclusion, effectively confronting obesity-induced tumorigenesis demands a fundamental shift in oncology from a purely tumor-centric model to an integrated host-tumor framework that recognizes metabolic dysfunction as a critical driver of cancer biology. Obesity must no longer be viewed as a secondary comorbidity but as a modifiable determinant of cancer initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Adding the metabolic assessment, inflammatory profiling, and biologically informed stratification into routine cancer care is improtant. Future progress will be hugely dependent on precision-based clinical trials specifically designed for metabolically distinct patient populations, along with sustained interdisciplinary collaboration bridging oncology, endocrinology, immunology, and metabolic science. Only through this integrated and proactive approach can we meaningfully reduce the escalating global burden of obesity-associated cancers and improve long-term patient outcomes.
