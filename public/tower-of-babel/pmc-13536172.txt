Environmental impact of NanoNeedle arthroscopy versus conventional knee arthroscopy: a hybrid carbon footprint analysis
Authors: Evan Argintar, Chiara-Maria Homann, Rosie Hillson, Ingeborg Steinbach, Christopher W Gee, Stephen Rossettie, Alex Burnett, Richard Wigham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from procedure room NanoNeedle arthroscopy (NA) and its associated clinical pathway, compared with conventional arthroscopy (CA) delivered in an operating room, focusing on non-complex knee arthroscopy procedures.DesignHybrid carbon footprint analysis using process-based life-cycle methods combined with economy-wide input-output data that include environmental impact factors for medical devices and components of patient care pathways. Three care pathways were evaluated: (1) CA in an OR with preoperative MRI, (2) NA in a procedure room with MRI and (3) NA in a procedure room without MRI. Emissions were calculated for all pathway components, including patient and staff travel, diagnostic imaging, medical devices, the procedure itself and postoperative care.SettingUK National Health Service-based care pathway model for non-complex knee arthroscopy procedures.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was GHG emissions (kgCO2e) per procedure pathway. Additional analyses assessed emissions by pathway component, adoption scenarios, conversion rates from NA to CA and sensitivity analyses for selected assumptions and input parameters.ResultsThe CA pathway generated 77.0 kgCO₂e per procedure, whereas the NA with MRI produced 45.0 kgCO2e and NA without MRI 35.3 kgCO2e, representing reductions of 42% and 54% of kgCO2e, respectively. Major emission contributors for CA were patient travel, consumable items and general anaesthesia; for NA pathways, patient travel and the device itself were the leading contributors. Adoption modelling indicated that using NA without MRI for 5000–20 000 knee arthroscopy procedures could yield annual CO2e savings ranging from 208 tons to 834 tons.ConclusionKnee arthroscopy delivered via the NA procedure room pathway has the potential to substantially reduce environmental impact compared with conventional OR-based care. As healthcare providers and policymakers increasingly consider environmental sustainability, technologies that enable alternative care models, such as NA in a procedure room setting, may represent a promising approach for reducing the carbon footprint of selected procedures.Keywords: Knee, Clinical Decision-Making, Health policy

Introduction

The environmental impact of the healthcare sector is significant, accounting for an estimated 5% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.1 In response, several healthcare systems have implemented sustainability strategies to reduce their environmental footprint. The National Health Service (NHS) in England was the first national health system to publish net-zero carbon commitments.2 Countries including Canada,3 Australia4 and Sweden5 have since introduced their own environmental initiatives, reflecting a global shift toward greener healthcare delivery.

Within healthcare, orthopaedics is recognised as one of the top three carbon-intensive specialties,6 largely due to its reliance on energy-demanding operating rooms (ORs). These environments contribute significantly to healthcare-related emissions, generating 20–33% of a hospital’s total waste and consuming three to six times more energy than other clinical areas,7 driven by high energy consumption,1 8 heating, ventilation, air conditioning (HVAC) demands,1 8 extensive sterilisation protocols1 and the use of volatile gases in general anaesthesia.1 8 As awareness of this environmental impact grows, clinicians and professional bodies are increasingly advocating for more sustainable models of care. Recent examples include the UK’s Getting It Right First Time programme, which published guidance on decarbonising a hip replacement pathway,9 and initiatives from Scotland’s National Centre for Sustainable Delivery aimed at reducing the environmental impact of orthopaedic surgical procedures and their associated care pathways.10

Knee arthroscopy is a high-volume orthopaedic procedure, accounting for hundreds of thousands of cases annually in the USA and the UK.11 12 Traditionally, these interventions have been performed in the OR under general anaesthesia, thereby contributing to the environmental footprint of orthopaedic surgical care.1 Identifying opportunities to reduce emissions while maintaining patient safety and clinical efficacy of this high-volume procedure could therefore have a positive environmental impact.

One promising innovation in this field is NanoNeedle arthroscopy (NA), an ultra-minimally invasive alternative to conventional arthroscopy (CA). NA facilitates both diagnostic and lower-complexity therapeutic procedures under local anaesthesia in a procedure room setting rather than a full OR, thereby supporting a range of environmental, operational and cost efficiencies.13–16 The environmental efficiencies of procedure room surgical care have been exemplified by Rougereau et al, who demonstrated that rotator cuff repair performed in a procedure room reduces GHG emissions by 12.2% due to the use of local rather than general anaesthesia.17 NA may also eliminate the need for an MRI, thereby streamlining the patient care pathway, improving time to treatment and reducing resource utilisation.15 16 18–22 The NA approach is also associated with favourable clinical and patient benefits, including faster recovery, minimal post-procedure pain and less disruption to daily activities.16 22

Despite these benefits, the sustainability of NA has been questioned due to its single-use design,1 and its environmental impact in terms of GHG emissions remains unassessed. While several environmental studies have been conducted to ascertain the overall carbon emissions of surgical procedures and specific devices,17 23 24 we are unaware of any orthopaedic environmental studies that consider both the procedure and the broader clinical pathway, including pathway changes introduced by new devices or technologies. This is particularly important, as NA may fundamentally alter the care pathway, potentially offsetting the environmental impact of its single-use design. Hence, this study evaluates GHG emissions from procedure room NA and its associated clinical pathway, compared with CA delivered in an OR, focusing on non-complex knee arthroscopy procedures. We hypothesise that pathway-level changes enabled by NA will mitigate or outweigh the environmental burden associated with its single-use design.

Methods

A hybrid carbon footprint analysis was conducted of the medical devices and patient care pathway components to estimate and compare the GHG emissions associated with CA in an OR and NA in a procedure room (details in online supplemental data). This was accomplished with a cradle-to-grave process-based analysis, which, in the case of medical devices, encompassed primary product and packaging material production, transport, disposal and, for reusable items, onsite sterilisation. A top-down environmentally extended input-output analysis for pharmaceuticals, except for sevoflurane (table 2), was carried out, converting costs into GHG emissions. GHG emissions of preoperative and postoperative appointments were based on the Sustainable Healthcare Coalition’s respective emission factors.25 The analysis was performed by the Centre for Sustainable Healthcare (CSH), an independent, non-profit organisation that is experienced in environmental assessments in the healthcare sector.26

Setting

This analysis was based on the UK NHS setting and focused on non-complex knee arthroscopy procedures. Non-complex procedures were defined as diagnostic arthroscopy and therapeutic interventions that do not require implants, including chondroplasty, synovectomy, loose body removal and partial meniscectomy. Care pathways were based on assumed clinical practice, described previously by Munn et al and Abbas et al,15 16 which is considered to be broadly consistent across sites.

Procedure pathways

The following procedure pathways were considered for carbon footprint analysis. Pathways 1 and 3 were analysed as previously described by Munn et al,16 while an additional NA pathway (pathway 2), including a preoperative MRI as described by Abbas et al,15 was incorporated to reflect potential variations in clinical practice (figure 1).

Assumptions

Several assumptions were required to complete this carbon footprint assessment as is typical of life cycle and hybrid life cycle assessment approaches. The assumed lifetime usage of a conventional arthroscope and its ancillary equipment was 180 uses based on time-to-repair data from the manufacturer. HVAC energy demand was assumed to scale proportionally per m²; the OR environment was assumed to be 55 m2,27 and the procedure room was assumed to be 30 m2.28 Devices were assumed to be disposed of via high-temperature incineration, and packaging was assumed to be disposed of at low-temperature incineration. All other consumable and reusable items (online supplemental tables 3 and 4) were assumed to be disposed of as clinical waste at end of life. The method of sterilisation evaluated in this analysis was steam sterilisation. It was also assumed that reusable equipment sterilisation was performed in the hospital, not at an off-site facility. All core surgical instruments, apart from the NanoNeedle diagnostic sheath kit, were considered reusable, composed of stainless steel and assigned a lifetime of 500 uses and included items such as graspers, forceps or clamps. Any materials contributing <0.05 g to the medical devices were excluded from the analysis, as the resulting carbon emissions were assumed to be negligible. Average travel emissions per patient journey were sourced from the Greener NHS.25 For staff travel, commute distance was derived from the Health Outcomes Travel Tool,29 while transport mode distributions were based on the UK National Travel Survey,30 consistent with predominantly car-based travel (online supplemental table 5). Staff travel emissions were calculated using standardised UK emission factors31 within the CSH Travel Carbon Calculator32 and scaled according to staffing levels and average patient counts per procedure. Staffing levels were assumed to differ between the OR and procedure room settings according to workflow requirements,16 with fewer staff required in the procedure room setting (online supplemental table 1). Further details on calculation assumptions and emission factors are provided in the final column of table 1.

Adoption rates

A series of adoption scenarios were developed to estimate the potential GHG emission impact associated with broader adoption of the NA pathway. These scenarios modelled the replacement of the CA pathway with the NA pathway in the treatment of 5000, 10 000, 15 000 and 20 000 patients, representing both conservative and more widespread uptake.

Scenario and sensitivity analysis

To reflect potential real-world clinical practice, the possibility of conversion from the NA to CA was also considered. Per Munn et al16 and expert opinion, conversion from NA to CA occurs if the identified pathology cannot be managed by NA alone. In this scenario, both sets of equipment are used in a single procedure. To assess the impact of pathway conversions on GHG emissions, conversion rates of 5% and 10% were applied to the adoption scenarios.

To account for the possibility that pathway-level changes may not be realised in clinical practice, an additional conservative scenario analysis was conducted, in which only the procedure and procedure-related components were modified from CA to NA, while all other pathway components were held constant.

Per-device emissions of the reusable arthroscopy equipment were modelled over a range of assumed lifetimes to capture real-world variation and to verify the robustness of the analysis. Beyond the base case assumption of 180 uses, sensitivity analyses were performed for 50 uses, 100 uses, 300 uses and 400 uses.

Additionally, one-way sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the influence of selected individual input parameters on total pathway emissions. Each parameter was varied independently by ±20% around the base-case value to reflect plausible input uncertainty, while all other pathway parameters were held constant. The parameters assessed were OR size, defined as the incremental difference between the OR and procedure room size; OR energy use; time on the observation ward and staff travel distance.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in this research. As this study was an environmental modelling analysis based on secondary data sources, direct patient involvement was not considered appropriate for the study design.

Results

Procedure pathways

The assessment showed that the CA approach generated 77.0 kgCO2e per pathway, whereas the NA pathways produced 45.0 kgCO2e (with MRI) and 35.3 kgCO2e (without MRI). The NA pathways resulted in 42% (with MRI) and 54% (without MRI) reductions in kgCO2e emissions relative to the CA pathway (table 2; figure 2).

Travel and surgical procedure were the pathway elements that contributed the most to GHG emissions across all three care pathways (figure 2). Absolute emissions from travel were highest in the CA pathway (23.4 kgCO2e) and lower in the NA pathways (14.7 kgCO2e and 10.4 kgCO2e, respectively). Emissions from the procedure component remained constant in the two NA pathways at 21.7 kgCO2e, compared with 37.1 kgCO2e in the CA pathway (table 2).

At a more granular level, in the CA pathway, the largest individual contributors to overall GHG emissions were patient travel (22%), surgical equipment (15%) and anaesthetic gases (11%). For the NA pathway with MRI, the highest contributors were surgical equipment (31%), patient travel (28%) and MRI (12%). In the NA pathway without MRI, the main sources of emissions were surgical equipment (40%), patient travel (24%) and PPE (10%) (table 2).

Per-Device emissions

Per-device emissions were higher for NA and its ancillary equipment than for CA and its ancillary equipment (online supplemental table 7). Transport (59%) and primary product manufacture (30%) were the main contributors to per-device emissions for CA. Similarly, transport was the main contributor to per-device emissions for NA, accounting for 90% of emissions. For all other components, primary product manufacture, packaging material manufacture and disposal, emissions from CA were proportionately higher than for NA (table 3).

Scenario modelling

Limited pathway-change scenario

Total emissions in the limited pathway-change (NA) scenario were 50.8 kgCO2e per pathway, corresponding to a 26.1 kgCO2e, or 34.0%, reduction compared with the CA pathway (online supplemental table 8).

Adoption rates

The modelled adoption rates of performing NA in place of CA showed potential GHG savings ranging from 160 tons of CO2 for 5000 procedures to 640 tons of CO2 for 20 000 procedures in the NA pathway including MRI. Carbon savings were higher for the NA pathway performed without MRI, ranging from 208 tons of CO2 for 5000 procedures to 834 tons of CO2 for 20 000 procedures (figure 3).

Sensitivity analysis

5% conversion from NA to CA resulted in a 7% reduction in annual GHG emission savings at all abovementioned adoption rates, while 10% conversion led to a reduction of 15% in savings across all adoption rate scenarios (table 4). Threshold analysis showed that at a 65% conversion rate from NA to CA, the NA pathway no longer generated an environmental benefit.

Discussion

This comparative analysis is consistent with existing literature, demonstrating a substantial reduction in GHG emissions for both NA procedure room pathways compared with CA. NA in a procedure room presented a more favourable carbon profile, achieving a reduction of up to 41.7 kgCO2e, which amounts to approximately 50% per procedure. This benefit was primarily due to decreased energy use, lower HVAC demands, reduced staffing and the elimination of general anaesthesia. This highlights the potential for significant environmental benefits when performing knee arthroscopy with the NA pathway in a procedure room, compared with CA in an OR.1

Of the three pathways analysed, CA exhibited the highest total GHG emissions, primarily driven by surgical equipment (excluding PPE and anaesthetics), anaesthesia and staff travel. At the surgical procedure level, total emissions were also more favourable for the NA pathways (21.7 kgCO2e) compared with the CA pathway (37.1 kgCO2e).

Emissions from surgical equipment were a major contributor across all pathways, although absolute emissions were slightly lower in the CA pathway. This is attributable to lower per-use device emissions of the reusable arthroscope used in CA. In the NA with MRI pathway, MRI was a significant contributor to GHG emissions, consistent with existing scientific evidence.1 In contrast to the existing literature, the assumed carbon emissions of the MRI were noticeably lower, as the electricity emission factor was adjusted for the UK.33 Patient travel was a consistent and substantial contributor in all three groups.

Device-related emissions for the CA were substantially lower per use than those for NA, based on a defined lifetime of 180 procedures. As lifetime usage is expected to differ greatly by clinic, pathology and surgeon experience level, the difference in device-related emissions between the CA and NA will vary depending on the number of uses of the CA before it is retired. This is likely to be more relevant for smaller-diameter scopes with shorter operational lifespans. Because 180 lifetime uses was a key assumption of this analysis, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to address this uncertainty and evaluate the robustness of the pathway-level emission differences. Varying the assumed device lifetime from 50 uses to 400 uses produced a maximum difference of 0.7 kgCO2e in the CA pathway, indicating that pathway-level factors exert a substantially greater influence on emissions than lifetime assumptions. Moreover, it is important to note that carbon emissions associated with both devices are influenced by geographic factors, such as international transport. For instance, emissions associated with device shipments from the USA to the EU would not apply to procedures performed entirely within the USA.

These findings contribute to the ongoing discussion around the environmental impact of single-use medical devices. Although traditionally associated with increased waste and emissions, this study suggests that single-use devices like the NanoNeedle Scope, which enable pathway optimisation, can offer net environmental benefits when evaluated within the full pathway context. At an adoption rate of 10 000 procedures, the NA pathway could reduce annual emissions by an estimated 320 tons of CO2, which equates to approximately 58 700 MRIs (based on the assumed 5.45 kgCO2e per MRI). Even when accounting for a 10% conversion rate to CA due to incomplete treatment with the NA pathway, the carbon savings remained substantial, with annual reductions estimated at 272 tons of CO2. This is further supported by a threshold analysis, which confirmed that the NA pathway remained the lower-emission option up to a conversion rate of 65%, beyond which the environmental benefits became negligible.

While conventional arthroscopes have lower per-device emissions due to their reusable nature, this analysis highlights the importance of considering the wider clinical pathway to accurately capture the environmental impact of medical devices, particularly when a pathway change is enabled. Consistent with other literature, the primary driver of this advantage is moving into a less resource-intensive procedure room environment.1 17 This was further supported by the limited pathway-change scenario analysis, in which NA in the procedure room still resulted in a 34.0% reduction in emissions compared with CA when holding all non-surgical pathway elements constant. Rougereau et al similarly demonstrated the positive environmental impact of procedure room surgery in orthopaedics, showing a 12.2% reduction in GHG emissions in rotator cuff repair when performed in the procedure room compared with the OR.17 The differences in observed GHG reductions between this study and our analysis are likely due to the methodological differences, as Rougereau et al’s analysis was largely based on cost conversion and data from the literature. Their results also validate those reported by Munn et al, who demonstrated a significant reduction in waste generated between the pathways in favour of NA in a procedure room.16

Although this evaluation considered a routine postoperative follow-up within the analysis, it should be noted that several clinics are moving towards patient-initiated follow-up (PIFU) rather than a mandated postoperative visit, with the goal of lessening demand on outpatient services.34 NA does not require general anaesthesia in this application, which may facilitate the PIFU approach. Patients can see the procedure and recorded images in real time and discuss the findings immediately post procedure. This enables more effective bedside counselling, which could in turn help reduce pathway emissions by avoiding the need for a postoperative in-person visit. The choice of anaesthetic agent can also influence the environmental impact of surgical pathways. The global warming potential of different anaesthetic gases is well documented, with organisations such as the Royal College of Anaesthetists highlighting the role of anaesthetic choice in reducing emissions.35 In this evaluation, sevoflurane was selected to reflect typical UK practice; however, this assumption may not apply across all settings. The use of agents with lower or higher global warming potential, such as total intravenous anesthesia with propofol or desflurane, would decrease or increase emissions, respectively.1 This potential variability should therefore be considered when interpreting the results.

This work illustrates that single-use products can be part of a sustainable approach, particularly when aligned with efficient clinical pathways; their impact depends on how and where they are used. There is growing evidence supporting NA for several knee indications, as well as other joints and soft-tissue procedures, including the shoulder, wrist and ankle.17 36–38 Expansion of such techniques may present further opportunities to reduce healthcare’s carbon footprint. Prior work has shown that outpatient procedure rooms are a safe and effective setting for non-complex arthroscopic procedures, all with added benefits of efficiency, reduced recovery time and lower overall cost.15 16 22 Despite these advantages, it is important to recognise that NA also has inherent limitations. These include a learning curve associated with operating a 0 degree camera and challenges in achieving the traditional viewing angles required to address more complex pathologies.20 Although ongoing technological advancements continue to improve usability and enhance the overall patient experience, CA remains essential for a broad spectrum of procedures. At present, NA is best suited for less complex indications, where its minimally invasive profile offers the greatest benefit. This distinction is important when interpreting the environmental findings, as the potential carbon benefits of NA are linked to indications where its use is clinically suitable.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this analysis. Carbon footprint assessments inherently rely on assumptions, and some degree of uncertainty is unavoidable. In this study, the lifetime use of 180 procedures for the CA was a key assumption; however, the accompanying sensitivity analysis demonstrated that varying this assumption had only a minimal impact on overall pathway emissions. This was consistent with the additional one-way sensitivity analyses, in which variation in selected pathway parameters resulted in only a modest change of up to 1.75 kgCO2e in total pathway emissions. The assumed 10% conversion rate from NA to CA may be lower than true clinical practice, potentially increasing the magnitude of carbon savings attributed to the NA pathway. The analysis was conducted in a UK setting, and results may differ in regions with alternative care pathways, different anaesthetic choices, energy mixes or equipment supply chains. Some upstream data were not included due to limited availability, such as the energy and resources required to manufacture both scopes and associated ancillary equipment, as well as the sterilisation of the NanoNeedle Scope at the manufacturing facility. Similarly, the material composition, transport and packaging associated with the arthroscopy stack (for CA) and console (for NA) were excluded. Given the complexity of the arthroscopy tower and the potential for off-site sterilisation, these exclusions are likely to favour the conventional arthroscope. A small number of data points were supplemented by clinical expert opinion, resulting in some reliance on lower-level evidence. Nonetheless, across all scenarios tested, the relative differences between the CA and NA pathways remained consistent, supporting the robustness of the overall findings.

Conclusion

Our analysis suggests that, under the assumptions applied, knee arthroscopy delivered via the NA procedure room pathway has the potential to substantially reduce GHG emissions compared with conventional OR-based care. Performing clinically appropriate, non-complex arthroscopy cases in a procedure room using NanoNeedle may help lower emissions by avoiding the higher energy and resource demands of the OR. As healthcare providers and policymakers increasingly consider environmental sustainability, technologies that enable alternative care models, such as NA in a procedure room setting, may represent a promising approach for reducing the carbon footprint of selected procedures.
