Worldwide governance indicators: Cross country data set 2012–2022
Authors: Sofik Handoyo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) constitute an extensive dataset that measures the quality of public governance at the national level. The WGI framework is structured around six key governance dimensions: voice and accountability, political stability and absence of violence, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption. The WGI is an invaluable tool for policymakers, researchers, and practitioners interested in comparative governance and institutional analysis.The dataset spans from 2011 to 2022 and covers diverse geographical regions such as East Asia & Pacific, Europe & Central Asia, Latin America & Caribbean, Middle East & North Africa, North America, Sub-Saharan Africa, Western Europe, and South Asia. The data is collected systematically, including data source identification, extraction, filtering, validation, and organization. The data is presented in a balanced panel structure to facilitate longitudinal analysis, allowing researchers to explore the dynamics of change within units over time in a standardized manner.Policymakers use these indicators to make informed decisions and plan better. International development organizations employ them to allocate resources and assess the impact of aid. Financial institutions and businesses leverage WGI to evaluate investment risks and market entry conditions. Scholars and researchers find WGI valuable for empirical studies involving cross-country comparisons and longitudinal analyses. Civil society organizations use these metrics to advocate for governance reforms. Furthermore, the indicators are also useful in public discourse for promoting transparency and accountability.Keywords: Voice and accountability, Political stability and absence of violence, Government effectiveness, Regulatory quality, Rule of law, Control of corruption

Background

The dataset in this paper offers a comprehensive set of metrics on six key public governance dimensions: voice and accountability, political stability and absence of violence, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption. The Voice and Accountability metric evaluates the extent to which a country's citizens can participate in selecting their government [1]. It also measures the freedom of expression, association, and media. The Political Stability and Absence of Violence dimension assesses the likelihood of political instability and politically motivated violence, including terrorism [1]. The Government Effectiveness metric gauges the quality of public services, civil service capacity, and the independence of the bureaucratic apparatus from political pressure [1]. The Regulatory Quality dimension examines the government's ability to formulate and implement policies and regulations that promote private sector development [1]. The Rule of Law metric evaluates the extent to which agents have confidence in and abide by the rules of society, including the quality of contract enforcement, property rights, the police, and the courts [1]. Finally, the Control of Corruption dimension measures the extent to which public power is exercised for private gain, encompassing petty and grand forms of corruption [1].

The primary objective of this dataset is to help advance political science, economics, and public administration research by providing a deeper understanding of patterns and factors that influence public governance. The dataset is a valuable resource for policymakers, academics, and stakeholders interested in public governance and public affairs, as it has significant implications for policy analysis, formulation, and evaluation. By analyzing public governance indicators such as Voice and Accountability, Political Stability and absence of violence, Government Effectiveness, Regulatory Quality, Rule of Law, and Control of Corruption, we can identify the differences and similarities in public governance styles and efficiency across different nations and regions. This analysis forms a foundation for accurate forecasting of public governance trends, which can assist in informed decision-making. By utilizing the extensive and multifaceted information available in this dataset, we can contribute significantly to the body of knowledge on public governance. The goal is to describe the current state of public governance and inform strategies to help improve public governance frameworks, leading to more sustainable and equitable societal systems.

Data Description

About the data

The Worldwide Governance Indicators, commonly known as WGI, is a research dataset that summarizes the quality of governance in various countries worldwide. Developed by the World Bank, these indicators have been instrumental in studying governance and institutional quality across the globe. The WGI comprises six broad dimensions of governance: voice and accountability, political stability, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption.

The data set presented in this manuscript can be accessed publicly through the Mendeley data repository (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/bfrkzf5k64/1). The data is identified with the file name Worldwide Governance Indicators 2012-2022.xlsx. The data presented in that file consists of six dimensions of public governance as previously mentioned. The data is structured into a balanced panel data format from 2012 to 2022. It covers 180 countries members of the World Bank. Additionally, the data also identified each country in the data set based on its region (East Asia & Pacific, Europe & Central Asia, Latin America & Caribbean, Middle East & North Africa, North America, Sub-Saharan Africa, Western Europe, and South Asia)

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

The development of worldwide governance Indicators by the World Bank

The Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) is a robust research database introduced by the World Bank in collaboration with several academic and international organizations. Developed by Daniel Kaufmann, Aart Kraay, and Massimo Mastruzzi [2,3], the WGI project reports aggregate and individual governance indicators for 215 economies from 1996 to the present, based on over 30 individual data sources produced by a variety of survey institutes, think tanks, non-governmental organizations, international organizations, and private sector firms [3]. The WGI is organized into six principal dimensions designed to capture the multifaceted nature of governance: Voice and Accountability, Political Stability and absence of violence, Government Effectiveness, Regulatory Quality, Rule of Law, and Control of Corruption [3].

Voice and Accountability measure the extent to which a country's citizens can participate in governance, including freedom of expression, association, and free media [1]. Political Stability assesses the likelihood that the government will be overthrown through unconstitutional means, including politically motivated violence and terrorism [1]. Government Effectiveness evaluates the quality of public services, the quality of the civil service, the degree of its independence from political pressures, and the quality of policy formulation and implementation [1]. Regulatory Quality captures the ability of the government to formulate and implement sound policies and regulations that permit and promote private sector development [1]. Rule of Law measures the extent to which agents have confidence in and abide by the rules of society, including the quality of contract enforcement, property rights, the police, and the courts [1]. Control of Corruption measures the extent to which public power is exercised for private gain, including petty and grand forms of corruption and “capture” of the state by elites and private interests [1].

The methodology of the WGI employs an unobserved components model, effectively aggregating multiple sources of governance data into a single composite indicator for each of the six dimensions. The individual data sources are standardized to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, ensuring comparability across diverse metrics. The model generates point estimates along with standard errors to measure the uncertainty associated with each indicator [1]. The WGI framework relies on statistical methodologies that standardize data from multiple sources to generate comparable indicators across countries. Each of the six governance indicators is accompanied by a margin of error, reflecting the range of uncertainty around the point estimate. The indicators are also normalized to range from approximately -2.5 to 2.5, with higher values corresponding to better governance outcomes [4]. Measurement of WGI indicators.

These indicators are often measured through survey data, expert assessments, and statistical models sourced from multiple institutions, including international organizations, NGOs, and private sector firms. The WGI aggregates these into a composite index for each of the six dimensions, typically presented as a score allowing cross-country and over-time comparisons.

Regarding application, WGI serves multiple stakeholders, including governmental bodies, non-governmental organizations, researchers, and policymakers. It is extensively used to identify governance bottlenecks that may inhibit development, quantify the effectiveness of policy interventions, and benchmark performance against global standards or over time. Moreover, several international organizations and financial institutions leverage WGI metrics in determining eligibility criteria for grants or assessing investment risks [5]

Despite its widespread use, it is worth noting that the WGI is not without its criticisms. Questions have been raised about the reliability and validity of the constituent data and the potential for cultural biases. Consequently, it is generally advised that WGI should be used in conjunction with other data and insights for a more holistic understanding of governance issues [6].

Data collection process

Collecting Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) data involves an intricate, multi-step process that requires thorough attention to detail. Fig. 3 illustrates the step-by-step process for collecting and presenting data. In the data source identification phase (Step 1), the avenue of WGI's official website (https://www.govindicators.org) was explored. The website is official from the World Bank Organization. The website briefly describes the WGI, components of WGI, the type of option data available, and how to cite. Keeping track of the specific version or data release is imperative, as WGI is updated yearly.

Process of collecting and presenting data.

The subsequent step, data extraction (Step 2), involves determining the optimal retrieval format, whether a data set for STATA (statistical software) or a data set for Excel (Microsoft Office software). STATA is a powerful statistical software for data analysis, management, and graphics [7,8]. Bulk downloads were utilized for acquiring data. The data was presented in Excel data set format. The consideration for presenting data in Excel format is accessibility and compatibility with most common computers and users [9].

Data filtering (Step 3) was conducted to select particular data as intended. WGI was released by the World Bank in 1996. However, In this paper, the selected data only covers 11 years. The consideration to select only the last 11 years of data is related to the requirement to fulfill balanced panel data. In balanced panel data, each unit is observed for the same number of periods, making the data set homogeneous. This simplifies both the statistical analysis and the interpretation of results. A balanced structure allows for the utilization of simpler estimation techniques, as it avoids the complications associated with missing data points [10]. Additionally, Balanced panel data facilitate comparative analyses across entities or over time, as each entity is observed for the same number of periods [11]

Moving to data validation (Step 4), the extracted data must be cross-referenced with other official metadata to ascertain the accuracy of variables and their values. Quality checks should be run to identify any inconsistencies. Data extracted in Excel format underwent validation against other sources. Data validation was performed using random sampling. Sample data from Excel format was cross-checked with other official data formats, such as web interactive data and STATA data. This procedure was conducted to ensure the data obtained is valid and consistent with other format data.

Finally, data organization (Step 5) is crucial for setting the stage for meaningful analysis. The data should be arranged in well-structured spreadsheets with clearly defined rows and columns, indicating variables, timeframes, and geographical scope. It may be necessary to transform the data into a specific format to be compatible with the intended analytical tools. This paper presents WGI data in a balanced data panel format (2011-2022). Furthermore, the data is also associated with geographic group location (East Asia & Pacific, Europe & Central Asia, Latin America & Caribbean, Middle East & North Africa, North America, Sub-Saharan Africa, Western Europe, and South Asia).

Added value to the primary data

The manuscript draws on primary data sources that provide raw indicators related to worldwide governance. However, these indicators are not presented in a format suitable for direct comparative and longitudinal analysis, as they are not organized into a balanced panel data structure. In contrast, the secondary data presented in the manuscript have undergone a transformative process to convert them into a balanced panel data structure. This is critical since balanced panel data offers researchers greater opportunities to conduct rigorous empirical analyses, especially in public governance.

Restructuring the data enables researchers to perform more complex and accurate analyses. This includes examining the variations in governance indicators within and between countries from 2012-2022. The restructuring of data also allows for more efficient estimation methods and controls for unobserved heterogeneity, thus improving the quality and scope of inferential and descriptive studies in the field of public governance.

In addition to the primary data, the dataset includes each country's geographic location or region, providing valuable information. Researchers can use geographic location as a crucial control variable in governance studies, allowing them to consider regional differences in governance practices and outcomes. This additional information enhances the dataset's usability and expands its potential for multifaceted analyses.

Limitations

One of the most prominent criticisms pertains to the validity and reliability of the indicators. Aggregating multiple and diverse data sources into single composite indicators can introduce validity issues, particularly if the individual sources have their own methodological shortcomings [6]. The reliability issue is also raised, given that data might be inconsistent over time or across different contexts. Another concern is the potential bias in the data sources, often based on expert opinion and perception-based surveys. Perceptions can be influenced by many factors that may not necessarily correlate with actual governance performance, thereby casting doubt on the representational accuracy of the indicators [12]. The WGI's focus on country-level data has also been criticized for neglecting sub-national variations in governance, an oversight that could be crucial for policy decisions and interventions [13]. Furthermore, there is the issue of normativity; the indicators are often construed as being built upon Western-centric norms and values of what constitutes 'good governance,' which may not be universally applicable or may even reinforce neo-colonial power dynamics [14]. Thus, while the WGI is an influential tool for various forms of analysis and decision-making, it is imperative to consider these criticisms and limitations in any application or interpretation of its data.

From the author's perspective, Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) do not represent the universal value of the global nation. Recognizing the limitations of applying a universal set of governance metrics across diverse socio-political landscapes is essential. While the WGI framework is valuable in facilitating comparative analyses, it tends to standardize governance into quantifiable measures. However, this quantification may disregard the nuances of governance values and principles as they are contextually realized in individual nations. Governance is largely influenced by a country's historical trajectory, culture, and societal norms. The values emphasized in one nation's governance model may not necessarily align with or be prioritized in another. For example, while Western democracies may prioritize political freedoms and individual liberties as core governance principles, other societies might prioritize collective welfare, societal harmony, or economic stability. Applying a uniform governance evaluation metric risks imposing values that may not be universally shared, leading to ethical and epistemological challenges.

It is important to note that a set of universally applicable indicators may unknowingly perpetuate cultural imperialism by favoring the governance models of certain nations over others. This can create a power dynamic that undermines the principle of sovereignty and self-determination for nations whose governance models do not align with the predominant indicators. Therefore, while the Worldwide Governance Indicators can be useful for macro-level comparative analyses, they should be used alongside more context-sensitive approaches. These approaches should recognize the diversity of governance models and consider the unique socio-cultural factors that shape governance in different nations. Only through a more nuanced and contextualized evaluation can we truly understand and assess the complexities of governance.
