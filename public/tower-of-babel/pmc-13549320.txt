Dermoscopic CIELab Colorimetry for Longitudinal Monitoring of Fixed Target Lesions in Psoriasis: Objective Quantification of Background Color and Vascular Density
Authors: Ziyuan Tian, Yang Han, Zhou Zhuang, Ke Xue, Yong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeObjective longitudinal monitoring of a single psoriasis lesion remains underdeveloped. We evaluated the methodological feasibility of a quantitative fixed-target-lesion framework that derives continuous surface-color and vascular signals from dermoscopy, using CIELab as the measurement space rather than the clinical endpoint.Patients and MethodsThis single-center retrospective longitudinal study included 28 patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis receiving biologic therapy (176 dermoscopic observations). Linear mixed-effects models (LMM) tested the longitudinal association between a* and PASI, and cumulative logit mixed models (CLMM) tested associations with ordinal dermoscopic scores. Data-driven a* thresholds were then applied unchanged to a separate mild-psoriasis cohort from the same center, device platform, and institutional database (BW cohort; 14 patients, 42 visit-level observations with concurrent scores) as a within-center transferability assessment.Resultsa* was positively associated with PASI in the random-slope LMM (β = 0.928, p < 0.001). Data-driven a* tiers showed moderate agreement with expert background-color grading in the primary cohort (quadratic weighted κ = 0.538) and higher agreement in the unchanged-threshold BW assessment (κ = 0.722; ±1-level agreement 95.2%). The BW analysis was interpreted as within-center transferability, not external validation. Automated vessel-signal counts were associated with ordinal vessel-density scores (CLMM OR = 2.53 per SD, p < 0.001) and remained associated after adjustment for a* (OR = 2.13 per SD, p < 0.001). Exploratory treatment-group findings were treated as hypothesis-generating only.ConclusionThese findings provide proof-of-concept support for an objective, quantitative framework for monitoring fixed psoriatic target lesions. a* and rule-based vessel-signal counts are candidate lesion-level measures that may complement conventional scores, but clinical utility and cross-center or cross-device generalizability remain unestablished.

Introduction

Assessment of psoriasis severity and treatment response still relies mainly on composite clinical scores such as PASI, BSA, and PGA.1–4 PASI is widely used, but it is relatively complex and shows both inter-observer and intra-observer variability.5,6 Global indices such as PASI and BSA also do not fully capture lesion-level change or individual disease burden.7,8 This limitation is most apparent near clearance, when whole-body scores compress toward low values. Objective continuous complementary tools are therefore needed.

Dermoscopy provides non-invasive visualization of psoriatic lesions, but most studies remain descriptive or ordinal.9–12 Image-processing and deep-learning studies have added objectivity,13–16 yet many aim to reconstruct PASI or its subscores. A separate methodological need is a repeatable framework that converts the surface color and vascular appearance of the same prespecified lesion into continuous, interpretable variables over time.

CIELab represents color through continuous parameters: a* (green-red), b* (blue-yellow), and L* (lightness).13,17 Here it served as a measurement space rather than a clinical endpoint: a* captured surface redness, and local CIELab contrasts supported rule-based vessel-signal detection. Prior work suggests that colorimetry can characterize psoriatic erythema.18,19 However, absolute values remain device dependent unless acquisition is standardized and formal color calibration is performed.

Our objective was to evaluate the methodological feasibility of a quantitative fixed-target-lesion framework that integrates continuous background color and automated vascular signals. We tested longitudinal relationships with conventional clinical and dermoscopic scores and examined whether primary-cohort a* thresholds retained agreement when applied unchanged in a separate same-center cohort. The second-cohort analysis was treated as a within-center transferability assessment rather than external validation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a single-center retrospective secondary analysis of longitudinal dermoscopic data from an institutional research database. The database is a multi-disease repository maintained under ethics approval No. 2020–130-K83, and the plaque psoriasis subset analyzed here has not been reported previously. Twenty-eight patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis received biologic therapy between 2021 and 2023 (anti-IL-12/23p40, n = 20; anti-IL-23p19, n = 8). All eligible records were included. Because this was a retrospective feasibility study, no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. For quantitative analyses, an observation was defined as one visit-level assessment of the prespecified target lesion, represented by the immersion-mode image used for quantitative measurement. A total of 176 observations were available. CIELab parameters and vessel-signal counts were extractable from all 176 observations; PASI was available for 175, ordinal dermoscopic scores for 159, and analyses requiring both for 158. The remaining 17 observations from eight patients lacked scorable images at those visits but those patients contributed scored images at other visits. Analysis-specific sample sizes are reported with each result.

A separate mild-psoriasis dermoscopy cohort (BW cohort; 14 patients; topical therapy) was drawn from the same center, the same FotoFinder device platform, and the same institutional database. The BW source cohort comprised 14 patients and 87 dermoscopic observations. For the prespecified transferability analysis, each patient contributed three scored visits (V01–V03), yielding 42 visit-level observations with concurrent ordinal dermoscopic scores. Because the source and acquisition setting were shared, this cohort was used only to assess within-center transferability of the unchanged a*-based thresholds and was not considered an external-validation cohort. Cohort selection and analysis-specific sample sizes are summarized in Supplementary Figure S1.

The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of China-Japan Friendship Hospital (Approval No. 2020–130-K83) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The parent project, under which the data were originally collected, was registered as a non-interventional study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of original data collection. The present sub-study used only de-identified records.

Data Acquisition

At each visit, trained clinicians assessed PASI, BSA, and PGA/sPGA using a standardized protocol. Dermoscopic images of the same fixed target lesion were acquired with a FotoFinder system in both non-contact (dry, 30×) and immersion (IM-30×) modes. To reduce hair interference and improve segmentation stability, one representative hair-free psoriatic lesion was selected per patient and followed throughout the study. Non-contact images preserved scale morphology and were used for scale-related scoring. Immersion images reduced scale interference and better exposed vascular and color information; all quantitative analyses were therefore based on immersion-mode images. Light-source settings were kept constant within the center, images were exported at native settings without post-hoc enhancement or white-balance correction, and images with heavy scale obscuration, glare, or blurring were excluded.

Dermoscopic Ordinal Scoring

Because no internationally standardized dermoscopic scoring system for psoriasis is available, key features were scored on ordinal scales modeled on PASI subscores. Background color (erythema), vessel density, scale thickness, and scale amount were graded from 0 to 4. Hemorrhage and pigmentation were graded from 0 to 2, and hypopigmentation from 0 to 1. Scale-related dimensions were scored on non-contact images; all other dimensions were scored on immersion images. Assessors were blinded to group allocation and clinical scores. Two independent assessors scored all images, and inter-rater weighted kappa exceeded 0.84 for all dimensions (n = 216). Primary analyses used one assessor’s scores. These scores describe one fixed target lesion and should not be interpreted as whole-body disease activity.

CIELab Extraction and Vessel Signal Detection

A custom rule-based image-processing pipeline was applied to immersion-mode images in Python 3.13.0 (NumPy 2.1.3, SciPy 1.16.1, scikit-image 0.26.0, OpenCV 4.10.0, Pillow 11.1.0). Dark vignetting was detected from the image corners and cropped automatically. An effective skin mask was then defined by grayscale thresholding (60–240), and images were converted from sRGB to CIELab (D65 illuminant). Scale was segmented with a combined brightness and color rule based on the 85th percentile of skin L* together with a* and hue offsets from their skin means. Scale area percentage was defined as 100 × scale pixels divided by effective skin-mask pixels. From the non-scale background region of interest, we extracted L* (lightness), a* (green-red axis), b* (blue-yellow axis), and an erythema index defined as (R−G)/(R+G).

Because erythema is a core feature of psoriatic lesions, a* was pre-specified as the primary longitudinal candidate. b* and L* were exploratory dimensions. Changes in b* may reflect post-inflammatory color shifts or pigment-related processes, so interpretation was kept cautious. Segmentation results were visually verified.

An automated vessel-signal detection algorithm was then applied to the same images. Because small vessel foci often lie beside scale, the non-scale mask was re-derived with a local L* z-score criterion. Under immersion dermoscopy, dotted vessels appear redder and darker than the surrounding background. We therefore used a difference-of-Gaussians contrast measure: for each channel, the local deviation was the difference between Gaussian filtering at σ = 2 and σ = 15 pixels, and the vessel score was defined as a* deviation minus L* deviation in CIELab units. Pixels with locally elevated b* were excluded to reduce post-inflammatory pigmentation. A fixed threshold of 6.0 was applied to the vessel score in all images, and connected components were retained as vessel signals when their area was 6–600 pixels and their circularity (4πA/P2) exceeded 0.12. Outputs were vessel-signal count and vessel area density.

All thresholds, morphological operations, and safeguard rules are summarized in Supplementary Figure S2. All spatial parameters are expressed in pixels and are therefore specific to the magnification and sensor resolution used here. Immersion-mode contact dermoscopy shields the field from ambient light and uses the instrument’s internal light source, which helped keep acquisition conditions stable across visits at this single center. No per-image color calibration was applied, so absolute CIELab values remain instrument specific and would require calibration before transfer across platforms. The algorithm is rule-based, deterministic, and does not rely on supervised training data.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were organized in three tiers. The LMM of a* versus PASI and the CLMM of vessel-signal count versus ordinal vessel-density score were the primary analyses; the a*-based stratification and its application to the BW cohort were secondary; and all treatment-regimen comparisons, b* and L* analyses, and Spearman correlations were exploratory, unadjusted for multiplicity, with p values reported descriptively.

Mixed-effects models accounted for within-patient correlation and unequal visit numbers. LMM assessed the longitudinal association between a* and PASI with patient as a random intercept. When convergence permitted, a sensitivity model also included a random slope; if the likelihood-ratio test (LRT) supported better fit, the random-slope model was reported as primary. CLMM assessed associations between CIELab parameters and ordinal scores, with the ordinal score as outcome and patient as random intercept. Spearman rank correlation was used only as supplementary descriptive analysis.

K-means clustering (k = 5) on a* values from the 159 scored primary-cohort observations generated objective stratification thresholds without using ordinal-score input. Midpoints between adjacent cluster centers served as cutoffs. Weighted Cohen’s kappa (quadratic weights) assessed post-hoc agreement with expert scores in the primary cohort. The thresholds were then applied unchanged to the 42 scored BW observations to assess transferability under the same-center, same-platform conditions.

Per-patient slope analyses used zero-intercept OLS regression of Δa*, Δb*, and ΔPASI on time and required at least two post-baseline visits. The primary time axis was visit sequence number (approximately monthly); actual days from baseline (days/30.44) served as sensitivity analysis. The association between Δa* and ΔPASI slopes was assessed by Pearson correlation. LMM and CLMM repeated-measures models used actual days/30.44 as the time variable.

Exploratory between-group analyses by treatment regimen used LMM for PASI trajectories, with treatment group, time (actual months), and the group × time interaction as fixed effects. Sensitivity analyses added a random slope for time or treated time as a categorical visit variable. For patient-level summary measures, including baseline characteristics and individual slopes, we used the Mann–Whitney U-test and reported rank-biserial correlations. Slope comparisons were further examined with 10,000-iteration permutation and leave-one-out analyses. All tests were two-sided (α = 0.05).

Ordinal Score Validity and the Case for Continuous Quantification

LMM showed that higher ordinal scores were associated with higher PASI (28 patients, 158 observations). Each 1-grade increase in background color was associated with a 3.89-point increase in PASI (β = 3.89, 95% CI 3.32–4.45, p < 0.001). Vessel density (β = 3.71, 95% CI 2.85–4.56, p < 0.001) and scale thickness (β = 4.84, 95% CI 3.83–5.84, p < 0.001) showed similar positive associations, supporting the ordinal scoring system (Table 2). In CLMM, each 1-unit increase in a* was associated with higher background-color grade (OR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.25–1.52, p < 0.001). b* was also positively associated with pigmentation score (OR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.12–1.50, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Overlaying continuous CIELab values on ordinal grades showed the limits of the ordinal system. The a* distributions for BG = 0 and BG = 1 overlapped substantially, the range within BG = 4 was wide, and mean a* values for BG = 2 and BG = 3 were nearly inverted. Thus, ordinal grading captures the overall trend but lacks resolution for fine-grained continuous lesion-level quantification (Figure 2C and D).

Data-Driven a* Stratification and Within-Center Transferability Assessment

To explore whether a* could support objective background-color grading, we applied k-means clustering (k = 5) to a* values from 159 primary-cohort observations without using ordinal-score input. Five tiers (L0-L4, ascending a*) were obtained, with midpoint cutoffs T1 = 20.86, T2 = 25.09, T3 = 29.70, and T4 = 34.59 (silhouette coefficient = 0.550). In the primary cohort, agreement between a*-derived tiers and expert BG_COLOR scores was moderate (weighted κ = 0.538), with ±1-level agreement of 76.7%. Discordance was driven mainly by the wide a* spread within BG = 4, again reflecting the limited resolution of the ordinal scale.

Applying the same thresholds unchanged to the same-center BW cohort (42 visit-level observations from 14 patients) yielded a quadratic weighted κ of 0.722, exact agreement of 38.1%, and ±1-level agreement of 95.2%. The confusion matrix remained concentrated near the diagonal: BG = 0 mapped mainly to L0, BG = 2 to L2, and BG = 3–4 to L3-L4. The higher κ than in the primary cohort may reflect differences in severity, score distribution, or other cohort features, but the small sample does not allow a firm explanation.

These findings show that the primary-cohort thresholds retained agreement with expert grading when applied in a second cohort under the same institutional and acquisition conditions. They support within-center transferability only and do not establish external validity, cross-device portability, or broader clinical threshold performance (Figure 2E and F).

Δa* and Δb* slopes were significantly negatively correlated (visit r = −0.647, p = 0.0004; days r = −0.711, p < 0.001), suggesting that fading redness was accompanied by increasing yellow-brown tone (Figure 3C).

Automated Vessel-Signal Quantification

CLMM showed that higher algorithm-derived vessel-signal counts were associated with higher expert vessel-density grades (0–4; 28 patients, 159 observations). In the unadjusted model, each 1-SD increase in vessel-signal count (SD = 314 counts) was associated with higher vessel-density grade (OR = 2.53 per SD; OR = 1.0030 per count, 95% CI 1.002–1.004, p < 0.001). After adjustment for a*, the association remained (OR = 2.13 per SD; OR = 1.0024 per count, 95% CI 1.001–1.004, p < 0.001), indicating that vessel-signal count captured information not fully explained by background redness (Table 3).

LMM also showed a positive longitudinal association between vessel-signal count and PASI (β = 0.0092, 95% CI 0.0042–0.0141, p < 0.001; 28 patients, 175 observations) (Table 3). During treatment, mean vessel-signal count declined from 436 at baseline to 240 at the last visit (Δ = −196; paired t-test t = −3.11, 95% CI −326 to −66, p = 0.005; 26 pairs) (Table 3).

In supplementary Spearman analyses, vessel-signal count showed a moderate within-patient positive correlation with vessel-density score (median per-patient ρ = 0.578, IQR 0.184–0.866; 83% of 23 evaluable patients showed a positive direction). The pooled correlation between vessel-signal count and PASI was ρ = 0.267 (p = 0.0003), and the median per-patient correlation was ρ = 0.450 (IQR 0.180–0.810) (Table 3).

Descriptive baseline imbalances were present. The anti-IL-12/23p40 group had thicker scales (median 4 vs 2, rank-biserial r = +0.80, p = 0.0002) and more scales (median 4 vs 3, r = +0.63, p = 0.002), whereas the anti-IL-23p19 group had higher background-color grades (median 4 vs 3, r = −0.60, p = 0.011) and more hemorrhage (median 1 vs 0, r = −0.54, p = 0.011). Baseline CIELab parameters and vessel-signal counts did not differ significantly (Table 4). These imbalances further limit any causal or regimen-specific interpretation.

Twenty-six patients entered the slope analysis (anti-IL-23p19 n = 8; anti-IL-12/23p40 n = 18). Using visit sequence as the primary time axis, Δa* slopes did not differ significantly (−0.91 ± 0.66 vs −2.20 ± 2.84 per visit; rank-biserial r = −0.35, p = 0.174; Figure 4B). Δb* slopes differed in the primary analysis (+0.62 ± 1.05 vs +3.34 ± 3.17 per visit; rank-biserial r = +0.79, p = 0.002; Figure 4C), with permutation p = 0.026 and leave-one-out p values of 0.0004–0.003 (Figure 4D). In the actual-time sensitivity analysis, the Mann–Whitney p value was 0.008, whereas the permutation result was borderline (p = 0.054; leave-one-out range 0.003–0.017) (Table 4). Given the small non-randomized subgroups and baseline imbalances, this signal should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Descriptive PASI response rates at visits 6 to 8 were numerically higher in the anti-IL-23p19 subgroup, but no comparison reached statistical significance (Fisher exact p ≥ 0.070), and denominators varied by visit because of differing follow-up lengths (Table 4).

Within-group Δa*–Δb* coupling was negative in the anti-IL-12/23p40 subgroup (visit version r = −0.637, p = 0.005; actual-time version r = −0.717, p = 0.001) but not in the anti-IL-23p19 subgroup (visit version r = −0.08, p = 0.851). These exploratory analyses show that separate color dimensions can be examined within the framework, but they do not establish incremental clinical value or treatment-specific biological effects.

Cross-Sectional Descriptive Summary

Cross-sectional descriptive values stratified by PASI severity and response milestones showed that a* generally decreased as severity declined, whereas b* showed the opposite trend (Supplementary Table S2). For example, observations with PASI > 20 had mean a* of 28.30 ± 6.23 and b* of 21.80 ± 6.79, whereas observations with PASI 0–3 had mean a* of 23.09 ± 5.35 and b* of 33.09 ± 10.83. Because patients contributed unequal numbers of observations and strata mixed patients and treatment stages, these values are descriptive only and should not be interpreted as reference ranges or used for longitudinal inference.

Descriptive pooled summaries of CIELab parameters, erythema index, scale area percentage, and vessel-signal counts across all observations are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

The main contribution of this study is methodological. We present a framework for repeated objective measurement of one prespecified psoriatic plaque using continuous surface-color and rule-based vascular-signal features. CIELab is the measurement space, not the central clinical claim. The study does not establish a new severity scale or a clinically validated biomarker. Instead, it asks whether lesion-level quantitative signals can be derived and related to conventional assessments over time. Among the evaluated measures, the longitudinal a*-PASI association provided the clearest proof-of-concept evidence.

Mixed-effects analysis identified an average positive longitudinal association between a* and PASI while allowing this association to vary between patients. This supports convergence at the dataset level, but it does not imply individual-level prediction of whole-body severity. The nonsignificant correlation between patient-specific Δa* and ΔPASI slopes reinforces that distinction and may partly reflect the scale difference between one target lesion and global PASI. The mixed model and the slope analysis therefore address complementary questions rather than validating each other.

The a*-based stratification analysis further showed that a continuous redness variable contains an ordered structure broadly compatible with clinical grading. Agreement was moderate in the primary cohort and higher when the unchanged thresholds were applied to the BW cohort. Because both cohorts came from the same center, device platform, and institutional database, the BW analysis should be interpreted only as a within-center transferability assessment. Its small size, milder disease distribution, and 42 visit-level observations preclude conclusions about external validity or threshold superiority.

The rule-based vessel-signal algorithm provided a second quantitative dimension within the same framework. Its association with ordinal vessel-density scores persisted after adjustment for a*, and the count declined during treatment. These findings support methodological feasibility, but the output remains an image-derived two-dimensional proxy rather than a direct measure of vascular depth, caliber, histology, or blood flow. We therefore retain the term vessel-signal count rather than equating it with a validated vascular-density biomarker.

The between-group Δb* finding was exploratory. Although the difference persisted in several sensitivity analyses, the comparison involved small non-randomized groups with baseline phenotypic imbalance, and the actual-days permutation result was borderline. The result should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a treatment-specific effect. The weaker relationship between b* and ordinal pigmentation also suggests that the biological meaning of b* remains uncertain.20,21

A possible use case for fixed-target-lesion monitoring is the near-clearance stage, when global scores compress toward low values.3,22,23 However, this study did not directly test incremental discrimination, clinical decision impact, or responsiveness in near-clear patients. The results should therefore be read as feasibility evidence for a complementary lesion-level measure, consistent with the multidimensional nature of psoriasis assessment.3,7,8,24–26

Objective imaging methods for psoriasis probe different physical dimensions at different depths and scales rather than competing on a single accuracy scale. High-frequency ultrasound measures plaque architecture, including skin thickness and the subepidermal low-echogenic band. Optical coherence tomography can quantify epidermal thickness and depth-resolved vascular features such as vascular density, plexus depth, vessel diameter, and vessel count.11,21,27 Laser speckle contrast imaging measures microvascular perfusion as a functional wide-field signal and has identified perilesional perfusion patterns associated with later plaque expansion.28 Reflectance confocal microscopy provides high-resolution information on epidermal architecture, inflammatory cells, dermal papillae, and papillary-dermal vascularization.29 AI-based image analysis is an analysis layer rather than a separate acquisition modality, and published psoriasis applications have largely focused on lesion segmentation or reconstruction of PASI and its subscores.14–16

Against this background, the present dermoscopic framework occupies a deliberately narrow niche. Surface color cannot quantify induration or plaque thickness, which ultrasound and OCT can measure. Vessel-signal count is an en-face two-dimensional proxy without depth or caliber information. Static chromatic contrast is not equivalent to perfusion measured by LSCI, and the method provides no cellular information comparable to RCM. In addition, because the measurement is chromatic rather than structural, it is intrinsically more dependent on illumination geometry, camera spectral response, and white-balance behavior than depth-resolved structural modalities such as HFUS or OCT. This dependence is the reason that formal color calibration, which was not performed here, is a prerequisite for cross-device or cross-center use of absolute CIELab values and thresholds.

Its main strengths are deployability and interpretability rather than superiority. The framework uses a dermoscope already present in routine workflow, applies a rule-based algorithm without annotated training data, and returns continuous parameters with explicit color or morphologic meaning. Unlike supervised systems trained to reproduce PASI labels, these parameters are defined independently of expert severity grades, although they remain anchored to a single lesion rather than to the whole-body coverage possible with photographic AI systems. The modalities are best regarded as complementary: combined dermoscopy-ultrasound studies have shown that surface background, vascular, and scale findings covary with ultrasound-derived plaque features during biologic treatment.21

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective single-center analysis of 28 patients, and no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. The treatment subgroups were especially small, treatment allocation was not randomized, and baseline phenotypic differences were present, so all between-group findings are exploratory. Patient and lesion diversity was limited: the primary cohort included moderate-to-severe patients receiving biologic therapy, whereas the BW cohort included milder disease treated topically; the study was not designed to cover the full range of skin tones, anatomical sites, psoriasis phenotypes, or treatment settings. Only one fixed target lesion was monitored per patient, which supports within-lesion tracking but cannot represent whole-body activity or between-lesion variation. All images were obtained at one center on one device platform under relatively controlled conditions, and no standardized color target or formal device calibration was used; absolute CIELab values and thresholds may therefore shift with illumination, camera response, white balance, or processing settings. The rule-based algorithm also depends on image quality, segmentation, and predefined thresholds, and vessel-signal output is not depth resolved and is not equivalent to perfusion or histologic vascular density. Finally, the BW cohort provides only a within-center transferability check, not external validation, and the biological interpretation of b* remains provisional.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides proof-of-concept evidence for an objective, quantitative framework for monitoring one fixed psoriatic target lesion over time. Within this framework, a* showed the clearest longitudinal relationship with PASI, and rule-based vessel-signal counts provided a complementary surface vascular measure. Applying unchanged a* thresholds to the same-center BW cohort supported limited within-center transferability but did not constitute external validation. The findings support methodological feasibility, not established clinical utility. Prospective multicenter studies with formal color calibration, multiple device platforms, diverse patients and anatomical sites, and multi-lesion assessment are needed to test robustness, generalizability, responsiveness, and clinical value. The main practical appeal of the approach is that dermoscopic hardware is already embedded in routine dermatology practice, although this is offered as a direction for future work rather than a conclusion supported by the current data.
