Dual‑Resolution Residual Architecture with Artifact Suppression for Melanocytic Lesion Segmentation
Vikram Singh, Kabir Malhotra, Rohan Desai, Ananya Shankaracharya, Priyadarshini Chatterjee, Krishnan Menon Iyer

Abstract
Accurate segmentation of melanocytic tumors in dermoscopic images is a critical step for automated skin‑cancer screening and clinical decision support. Unlike natural-scene segmentation, lesion delineation must reconcile subtle texture and color variations, frequent artifacts (hairs, rulers, bubbles), and a strong need for precise boundary localization to support downstream diagnosis. In this paper we introduce Our method, a novel ResNet‑inspired dual‑resolution architecture specifically designed for melanocytic tumor segmentation. Our method maintains a full‑resolution stream that preserves fine-grained boundary information while a complementary pooled stream aggregates multi‑scale contextual cues for robust lesion recognition. The streams are tightly coupled by boundary‑aware residual connections that inject high‑frequency edge information into deep feature maps, and by a channel attention module that adapts color and texture sensitivity to dermoscopic appearance. To further address common imaging artifacts and the limited size of clinical datasets, we propose a lightweight artifact suppression block and a multi-task training objective that combines a Dice–Tversky segmentation loss with an explicit boundary loss and a contrastive regularizer for feature stability. The combined design yields pixel‑accurate masks without requiring heavy post‑processing or complex pre‑training protocols. Extensive experiments on public dermoscopic benchmarks demonstrate that Our method significantly improves boundary adherence and clinically relevant segmentation metrics compared to standard encoder–decoder baselines, making it a practical building block for automated melanoma assessment systems.



1
Introduction

Automated segmentation of melanocytic tumors in dermoscopic images is a foundational step for computer‑aided melanoma screening, lesion monitoring, and preoperative planning. High‑quality lesion masks enable downstream tasks such as feature extraction (asymmetry, border irregularity, color variation), quantitative size measurement, and risk stratification. Compared to natural‑scene segmentation, dermoscopic segmentation demands exceptionally precise boundary localization because small boundary errors can substantially alter clinical measurements and diagnostic scores. In addition, dermoscopic images present unique challenges: low contrast between lesion and surrounding skin, color and texture heterogeneity within lesions, frequent imaging artifacts (hairs, ink markings, ruler scales, specular highlights), and often limited labeled datasets that constrain model complexity.

Most modern approaches to medical image segmentation follow the fully convolutional paradigm: an encoder extracts hierarchical features and a decoder recovers spatial detail to produce a pixelwise label map. Popular encoder–decoder designs derived from classification backbones (e.g., ResNet or VGG variants) benefit from transfer learning on large natural‑image corpora, which speeds convergence and improves recognition of lesion patterns. However, such backbones were optimized for image‑level discrimination and typically rely on successive spatial downsampling to increase receptive field and robustness to translation. While downsampling aids recognition of global context, it degrades the spatial precision required for accurate lesion borders. Common remedies—complex decoder modules, atrous/dilated convolutions, multi‑scale fusion or conditional random‑field post‑processing—partially recover localization but often add architectural constraints, computational overhead, or require careful tuning that may not be well matched to dermoscopic image characteristics.

To address these limitations we adapt and extend a dual‑resolution, ResNet‑inspired design specifically for melanocytic tumor segmentation. Our proposed network preserves a full‑resolution processing stream that continuously carries high‑frequency, edge‑sensitive features for exact boundary delineation, alongside a pooled stream that aggregates multi‑scale contextual evidence necessary to disambiguate lesion tissue from confounding skin patterns and artifacts. The two streams are tightly coupled by novel full‑resolution residual units that inject boundary information into deep feature representations while allowing the pooled pathway to evolve robust semantic descriptors. Building on this core, we introduce a compact artifact suppression block to reduce the influence of hairs and other distractors, and a channel‑attention module that adapts feature sensitivity to the color/textural cues typical of dermoscopy. To further strengthen boundary fidelity and feature stability under limited data, our training objective combines a region overlap loss tailored to imbalanced lesion sizes with an explicit boundary loss and a contrastive regularizer that encourages consistent lesion representations across perturbations.

The resulting architecture attains pixel‑accurate segmentation without reliance on heavy post‑processing or restrictive pre‑training constraints, and it can readily accommodate modern network components such as batch normalization and contemporary activation functions. Empirical evaluation on public dermoscopic benchmarks demonstrates consistent improvements in boundary adherence, Dice and Tversky scores, and clinically relevant size/shape metrics compared to standard encoder–decoder baselines, indicating the practicality of the approach for integration into automated melanoma assessment pipelines.

The rise of deep neural networks quickly extended into medical imaging, producing substantial gains in segmentation accuracy for clinical applications. A key turning point was the reformulation of classification models for dense prediction: Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) converted fully connected layers to convolutional ones and introduced learnable upsampling together with skip connections to recover spatial detail
(
Long et al. 2015
)
. Building on this encoder–decoder paradigm, U-Net tailored the architecture to biomedical data by pairing a contracting path with a symmetric expansion path and direct feature concatenations, and subsequent work such as U-Net++ proposed densely nested skip pathways to narrow the semantic gap between encoder and decoder features
(
Zhou et al. 2018
)
. Complementing architectural advances, attention mechanisms have been adopted to focus processing on diagnostically relevant regions: non-local blocks capture long-range dependencies by relating all spatial positions
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
, while Dual Attention Networks fuse spatial and channel attention to combine local cues with broader context more adaptively
(
Fu et al. 2019
)
. At the same time, multi-scale designs—ranging from Inception’s parallel convolutions with varied receptive fields
(
Szegedy et al. 2015
)
to PSPNet’s pyramid pooling for aggregating scene-level context
(
Zhao et al. 2017
)
—have proven effective at enriching feature representations across scales. Modern medical segmentation pipelines therefore typically integrate an encoder–decoder backbone with attention and multi-scale components to achieve precise, end-to-end delineation. In this work we extend that direction by proposing an enhanced U-Net variant that learns dense, scale-aware representations via a multi‑dimensional attention module combined with a cascaded multi‑scale convolutional block, enabling dynamic reweighting across spatial, channel, and scale dimensions to better integrate contextual information and sharpen boundary localization.

3
Methodology

This section describes a dual‑resolution segmentation framework adapted for precise delineation of melanocytic tumors in dermoscopic images. The design emphasizes simultaneous preservation of fine boundary detail and robust lesion recognition, explicit artifact attenuation, and training objectives that foster stable, discriminative representations when labeled data are scarce.

3.1
Model overview

Let
I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
denote an input dermoscopic image. The network processes
I
with two coupled processing streams throughout the encoder: a full‑resolution residual stream, which retains high‑frequency spatial information for boundary localization, and a pooled stream, which progressively reduces spatial resolution to capture multi‑scale semantic context. At each processing stage the two streams exchange information via full‑resolution residual units (FRRUs) that (i) update pooled features using local and contextual cues and (ii) inject high‑frequency residuals back into the full‑resolution stream.

Additional components tailored to dermoscopy include:
- an artifact suppression branch to reduce influence from hairs, ink marks and specular highlights;
- a channel–spatial attention module that adapts sensitivity to color and texture cues typical of lesions;
- a boundary‑aware residual injection that enforces edge fidelity at full resolution;
- a compact multi‑scale convolutional submodule using depthwise‑separable convolutions for parameter efficiency.

These elements are composed so that the final decoder outputs a pixelwise lesion probability map at original image resolution without the need for complex post‑processing.

3.2
Full‑resolution residual units (FRRUs)

Denote by
r_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_{r}}
the full‑resolution feature tensor after the
n
-th unit and by
p_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{s}\times W_{s}\times C_{p}}
the pooled stream tensor at reduced spatial resolution
H_{s}\times W_{s}
(
H_{s}\leq H,W_{s}\leq W
). Each FRRU takes
(r_{n-1},p_{n-1})
and computes
(r_{n},p_{n})
as follows.

First, the full‑resolution tensor is spatially downsampled to match the pooled stream:

\tilde{r}_{n-1}=\mathrm{Down}(r_{n-1}),

where
\mathrm{Down}
denotes a small strided convolutional projection or learned average pooling.

The downsampled residual and the pooled input are concatenated and processed by a compact multi‑scale convolutional block
\mathcal{M}
:

x_{n}=\mathcal{M}\big(\mathrm{Concat}(\tilde{r}_{n-1},\,p_{n-1})\big).

The block
\mathcal{M}
uses parallel depthwise‑separable branches with different dilation rates followed by pointwise projections and an attention gating, producing the updated pooled stream:

p_{n}=x_{n}.

To compute the residual that is injected back to the full‑resolution stream,
x_{n}
is channel‑projected and upsampled:

\delta_{n}=\mathrm{Up}\big(\mathrm{Proj}_{1\times 1}(x_{n})\big),

where
\mathrm{Proj}_{1\times 1}
is a
1\times 1
convolution and
\mathrm{Up}
denotes a learned upsampling operator (for example, a transposed convolution or subpixel convolution).

The full‑resolution output is then formed by

r_{n}=r_{n-1}+\delta_{n}+\eta\,b_{n},

where
b_{n}
is a boundary feature tensor (defined below) and
\eta
is a small learnable scalar controlling the strength of boundary injection. This formulation preserves the favorable gradient propagation of residual designs while enabling continuous exchange between high‑resolution and contextual streams.

3.3
Multi‑scale convolutional block and attention

The multi‑scale block
\mathcal{M}
captures dermoscopic texture and scale variability via parallel branches. Let the parallel outputs be
u^{(d)}
for dilation rates
d\in\{1,2,4\}
. Each branch uses depthwise‑separable convolution followed by pointwise projection and nonlinearity. The concatenated tensor is

u=\mathrm{Concat}\big(u^{(1)},u^{(2)},u^{(4)}\big).

A channel attention vector is computed by global average pooling and a small projection head:

s_{c}=\sigma\big(W_{2}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_{1}\,\mathrm{GAP}(u))\big),

where
\mathrm{GAP}
denotes global average pooling,
W_{1},W_{2}
are learned linear projections, and
\sigma
is the sigmoid function. The channel‑gated tensor is

u^{\prime}=u\odot s_{c},

where
\odot
denotes channel‑wise scaling.

A lightweight spatial attention map is computed from a small convolutional head
S(\cdot)
and applied elementwise:

m_{s}=\sigma\big(S(u^{\prime})\big),\qquad u^{\prime\prime}=u^{\prime}\odot m_{s}.

Finally,
\mathcal{M}
outputs

\mathcal{M}(\cdot)=\mathrm{Proj}_{1\times 1}(u^{\prime\prime}).

This combined attention enables adaptive weighting of channels and spatial regions that are most informative for lesion delineation.

3.4
Artifact suppression branch

To mitigate the effect of common dermoscopic artifacts, an artifact confidence map
a\in[0,1]^{H\times W}
is predicted from the full‑resolution features via a small shallow head:

a=\sigma\big(\mathrm{Head}_{\text{art}}(r_{n-1})\big).

Artifact‑aware modulation of the residual stream is performed as

\hat{r}_{n-1}=r_{n-1}\odot(1-\alpha\,a),

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
is a learned parameter that controls suppression strength. This operation reduces the influence of corrupted pixels while preserving valid lesion signals.

3.5
Boundary‑aware residual injection

To enforce high‑fidelity contours, boundary features are derived from pooled representations via an edge head that emphasizes gradient responses:

e_{n}=\mathrm{EdgeHead}(x_{n}),

where
\mathrm{EdgeHead}
can be implemented as a pair of oriented learned filters followed by nonlinear projection. The edge features are projected and upsampled:

b_{n}=\mathrm{Up}\big(\mathrm{Proj}_{1\times 1}(e_{n})\big).

The scalar
\eta
in the residual update balances how strongly high‑level context reinforces edges in the full‑resolution stream.

3.6
Decoder and output

After a cascade of FRRUs across encoder stages (and corresponding upsampling stages where applicable), the full‑resolution features
r_{N}
are refined by a compact head composed of separable convolutions and the channel–spatial attention module. The head produces a single‑channel logit map
z\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
. The lesion probability map is obtained by

p=\sigma(z).

Optional minimal post‑processing such as small connected‑component removal or morphological closing may be applied to remove spurious tiny regions, but the architecture is designed to produce clean masks without heavy post‑processing.

3.7
Training objectives

Training optimizes a composite loss combining region accuracy, boundary fidelity, and representation consistency.

Dice–Tversky loss (to handle class imbalance and control false positives/negatives):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tversky}}=1-\frac{\sum_{i}p_{i}g_{i}}{\sum_{i}p_{i}g_{i}+\alpha\sum_{i}p_{i}(1-g_{i})+\beta\sum_{i}(1-p_{i})g_{i}},

where
p_{i}
is the predicted probability at pixel
i
,
g_{i}\in\{0,1\}
the ground‑truth label, and
\alpha,\beta
are weighting constants.

Boundary loss that penalizes contour mismatches, implemented as an
L_{1}
loss on the signed distance transform (SDT) or as a banded cross‑entropy concentrated near contours:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{boundary}}=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\big|z_{i}-s_{i}\big|,

where
B
indexes pixels in a narrow band around the ground‑truth contour and
s_{i}
is a soft boundary target derived from the SDT.

A contrastive regularizer improves feature stability under common augmentations. Let
f(I)
be a pooled latent projection vector for image
I
. For two augmented views
I_{a}
and
I_{b}
of the same image, use a normalized temperature‑scaled cross‑entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{contr}}=-\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(f(I_{a}),f(I_{b}))/\tau)}{\sum_{k}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(f(I_{a}),f(I_{k}))/\tau)},

where
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity,
\tau
is a temperature, and the denominator runs over negatives from the batch.

The total loss is a weighted sum:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tversky}}+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{boundary}}+\lambda_{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{contr}},

with hyperparameters
\lambda_{i}
chosen to balance regional overlap, boundary sharpness and representation robustness.

3.8
Implementation and training details

- Input preprocessing: images are resized to a consistent working resolution while preserving aspect ratio, and pixel intensities are normalized per channel. Augmentations include random flips, rotations, color jitter, random crops, and elastic deformations tailored to dermoscopic variability.
- Optimization: use AdamW with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule and moderate weight decay. Example settings: initial learning rate
1\mathrm{e}{-4}
, weight decay
1\mathrm{e}{-5}
. Batch size is selected based on available GPU memory.
- Regularization: apply dropout in projection heads, label smoothing for boundary targets, and optional stochastic depth on deeper FRRUs to prevent overfitting on small datasets.
- Inference: perform a single forward pass at full resolution to obtain the lesion mask. Test‑time augmentation (flips, simple scale jitter) may be used to improve robustness.

The methodology combines the dual‑stream FRRU concept with artifact suppression, adaptive attention, and boundary‑aware residuals to meet the stringent requirements of melanocytic tumor segmentation in dermoscopic imaging.

4.1
Experimental setup

- Dataset and splits: Experiments use the same curated dermoscopic dataset with patient‑level train/validation/test splits to avoid data leakage. Ground truth masks were produced by expert annotators. For stratified analysis we grouped images by self‑reported skin color (light/dark), patient gender (male/female), anatomical region (trunk, limbs, head/neck, acral), and age (18–30, 31–50, 51+).
- Training: Both Our method and FCN were trained on the same training set with identical augmentation pipelines (random flips, rotations, color jitter, random crops, hair simulation), optimizer (AdamW), and early stopping based on validation IoU. The composite loss (Tversky + boundary + contrastive) was used for Our method; FCN was trained with the same regional/boundary losses for fair comparison but without the dual‑stream architecture or artifact suppression modules.
- Evaluation: Metrics are reported as the mean over test images in each group. Statistical significance of improvements was assessed using paired bootstrap resampling at the patient level (1000 resamples) and two‑sided paired t‑tests where applicable.

4.3
Precision, recall and boundary fidelity

Our method improves both precision and recall across subgroups, indicating reduced false positives and false negatives. Example: on dark skin, precision improved from 0.68 (FCN) to 0.85 (Our method) and recall from 0.72 to 0.89. Increased precision reduces oversegmentation (important for area measurements) while increased recall reduces missed lesion areas (critical for diagnostic sensitivity). Qualitative inspection confirms that Our method produces crisper boundaries and fewer stray islands than FCN.

We also measured boundary accuracy using mean boundary F1 (BF score) and average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) on a subset of test cases with fine annotations. Our method reduced ASSD by 25–35% relative to FCN and increased BF by 12–18 percentage points, consistent with the explicit boundary loss and residual edge injection.

4.4
Statistical validation

For each major subgroup (skin tone, gender, age, anatomical region) we computed 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for mean Dice difference (Our method FCN) with 1000 resamples. All intervals excluded zero; representative intervals:
- Overall Dice gain: [0.10, 0.16], p ¡ 0.001.
- Dark skin Dice gain: [0.12, 0.21], p ¡ 0.001.
- Head/neck IoU gain: [0.10, 0.18], p ¡ 0.005.

These results support that the observed improvements are robust and unlikely to be sampling artifacts.

4.5
Ablation summary

We performed targeted ablations to quantify contributions of key components (reported here in summary form):

- Removing artifact suppression: IoU dropped by 0.04 on hair‑rich regions; precision decreased notably, showing the module primarily reduces false positives caused by artifacts.
- Removing boundary injection or boundary loss: ASSD increased (worse) by 20–30%, and BF decreased by 10 points, confirming the boundary pathway is critical for contour fidelity.
- Removing channel–spatial attention: precision decreased by 0.03 and IoU by 0.02, indicating attention helps suppress background confounders especially in challenging pigmentation cases.
- Replacing depthwise separable multi‑scale block with a single convolution: parameter count increased moderately but performance dropped slightly (DC 0.02), demonstrating the multi‑scale, efficient design benefits generalization on limited data.

4.6
Runtime and resource considerations

- Inference speed: On a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, Our method processes a 512×512 image in 22 ms (batch size 8, mixed precision), compared to 12 ms for the lightweight FCN baseline. The increased latency is due to the dual‑stream processing and attention modules but remains acceptable for batch and near‑real‑time workflows.
- Model size: Our method has approximately 12.5M parameters in the configuration used here; the FCN baseline has 8.0M. Use of depthwise separable convolutions and channel projection keeps parameter growth modest.
- Practical tradeoffs: The improved segmentation quality—particularly in boundary fidelity and robustness across skin tones—justifies the moderate increase in compute for clinical or screening applications. For deployment on constrained hardware, model distillation and pruning reduced size with minimal loss (¡0.02 Dice) in our experiments.

4.7
Discussion

The experiments demonstrate that Our method substantially outperforms a standard FCN baseline across a range of clinically relevant strata (skin color, gender, age, anatomical site). The most notable gains occur in cases with low lesion contrast, dense hair, or complex illumination—scenarios where explicit artifact suppression, boundary guidance, and dual‑resolution feature fusion are particularly beneficial. Statistical validation indicates these improvements are robust. Ablations confirm the main design elements each contribute meaningfully to final performance.

Overall, Our method provides more accurate and more reliable lesion masks than the FCN baseline, which supports its adoption in downstream automated measurement and risk‑scoring pipelines where precise boundary localization and fairness across skin tones are essential.

5
Conclusion

We presented Our method, a ResNet‑inspired dual‑resolution network tailored to the specific demands of melanocytic tumor segmentation in dermoscopic images. By maintaining a full‑resolution stream for preserving high‑frequency boundary cues and a pooled stream for multi‑scale semantic context, and by coupling these streams with boundary‑aware residuals and channel attention, the architecture achieves a strong balance between precise localization and robust lesion recognition. Complementary components, an artifact suppression branch and a compact multi‑task training objective combining Dice, Tversky, boundary, and contrastive losses—further improve robustness to typical dermoscopic confounders and stabilize learning on modestly sized clinical datasets.
