The bidirectional relationships among multimorbidity, depression, and catastrophic health expenditures in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: random-intercept, cross-lagged panel model
Authors: Yan Wang, Zhiwei Wang, Yuhan Wang, Jing Yu, Shiji Xia, Yanna Li, Yushi Che, Yiqiao Wang, Xinyuan Li, Yunfang Liu, Lijuan An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe evolving global disease landscape, in conjunction with the significant impact of an aging population, has led to mental‒physical multimorbidity, imposing unprecedented pressures on healthcare systems and economies. This study aimed to investigate the interrelationships among multimorbidity, depression, and catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) and to test whether the intensity of CHE mediates these links.MethodsThe analysis employed data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which conducted a longitudinal survey from 2011 to 2018, tracking 5,274 participants aged 45 years and older over a seven-year timeframe. Multimorbidity was ascertained through self-reported data from participants, whereas depression was evaluated via the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10). The intensity of CHE was calculated as the ratio of out-of-pocket (OOP) payments to the capacity to pay (CTP), adjusted for a catastrophic threshold of 40%. The relationships among the three variables were analysed via an extension of the random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM), which includes covariates to predict the observed variables. Mediation via the intensity of CHE was tested using 5,000 bootstrap resamples.ResultsAt the between-person level, multimorbidity and depression were positively correlated (Model 1 r = 0.349; Model 2 r = 0.246; both p < 0.001), whereas the intensity of CHE showed negligible between-person associations with either variable. At the within-person level, all variables showed significant autoregressive stability, with multimorbidity demonstrating the strongest persistence (β = 0.808 in Model 1 and 0.936 in Model 2). Cross-lagged associations were clearly asymmetric, with prior multimorbidity exerting the largest prospective effects on the intensity of CHE (β = 3.028) and subsequent depression (β = 0.646 in Model 1 and β = 0.789 in Model 2), whereas prior depression and prior intensity of CHE had much smaller effects on later multimorbidity. Mediation analyses indicated that the intensity of CHE (T) partially mediated the association from multimorbidity (T‑1) to depression (T + 1) (indirect effect = 0.063, 95% CI [0.042, 0.084]), but showed negligible mediation for the reverse pathway from depression (T‑1) to multimorbidity (T + 1) (indirect effect = 0.001, 95% CI [0.000, 0.001]).ConclusionsThe study identified asymmetric bidirectional relationships among multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE in Chinese middle-aged and older adults, with effects predominantly running from multimorbidity to increased intensity of CHE and later depression; the intensity of CHE explained only a small portion of the multimorbidity→depression effect and virtually none of the depression→multimorbidity pathway. Policies that integrate multimorbidity management with routine depression screening could help reduce the combined physical, psychological, and financial burdens among middle-aged and older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27383-4.

Introduction

In light of the evolving global disease landscape and the substantial consequences of population aging, noncommunicable chronic diseases (NCDs) have become a predominant threat to global health [1]. The prevalence of multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions [2], has been rising rapidly among middle-aged and older adults [3–5]. Notably, beyond the decline in physical health, the impact of multimorbidity is closely and bidirectionally linked with psychological disorders [6–8]. Additionally, it directly increases medical expenses and economic strain on households [9, 10], thereby establishing a multifaceted risk at the systemic level.

To facilitate analysis, this study proposes a tripartite framework comprising the dimensions of physical health, economic burden, and psychological health. In this framework, physical health is measured by multimorbidity, psychological health by depression, and economic burden by catastrophic health expenditure (CHE). Prior research has preliminarily outlined the bidirectional relationship between each pair within this triad, providing the theoretical foundation for the present research. First, regarding the bidirectional relationship between multimorbidity and depression, multimorbidity has been shown to increase the risk of depression through pathways such as increased physiological burden, functional limitations, and reduced social participation [8, 11, 12]. Conversely, depression may exacerbate the progression of multimorbidity and adversely affect patient’ prognosis through pathways involving maladaptive health behaviors, neuroendocrine dysregulation, and reduced social support [13, 14]. Second, in terms of the cyclical feedback between multimorbidity and CHE, multimorbidity directly elevates healthcare utilization and costs, substantially increasing the likelihood that households will incur CHE [15–19]. Concurrently, the economic strain caused by CHE can exacerbate disease burden by limiting access to care, delaying care seeking, and limiting adherence to long-term treatment, thus reinforcing a “health‒poverty” cycle [20, 21]. Third, the bidirectional relationship between depression and CHE has also been documented. Depression increases household medical financial burdens by increasing healthcare utilization and impairing economic decision-making capacity [22]. In turn, major healthcare-related economic shocks can act as acute stressors that trigger or exacerbate depression [23, 24]. Nevertheless, most prior studies have been limited to static analyses or bidirectional examinations of pairwise relationships. Few studies have integrated all three dimensions into a unified, dynamic analytical framework. Given the pairwise associations among these three domains, catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) may mediate the relationship between multimorbidity and depression (e.g., multimorbidity → CHE → depression or depression → CHE → multimorbidity), thereby offering an important opportunity to examine interconnected pathways across physical health, mental health, and economic risk.

In this study, we focus on middle-aged and older adults in China (aged ≥ 45 years), use four waves of longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), conducted in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018. This study employs a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to analyse the interrelationships among multimorbidity, depression, and CHE, controlling for covariates including age, gender, marital status, area of residence, education level, and after-tax income. Compared cross-lagged panel model (CLPM), the RI-CLPM explicitly separates stable between-person differences from dynamic within-person processes through random intercepts, thus reducing the interference of between-person differences in lagged effect estimation [25, 26]. The research specifically seeks to address the following questions: (1) After distinguishing within-person processes from stable between-person differences, do the bivariate relationships between multimorbidity and depression exhibit cross-lagged effects across the T1–T4 waves? (2) Do the bidirectional relationships among multimorbidity, depression, and CHE exhibit cross-lagged effects over the T1–T4 waves? Are both within-person (individual-level dynamic changes) and between-person (differences in average levels across individuals) effects statistically significant? (3) For within-person dynamics, does the mediating effect of CHE on the relationship between multimorbidity and depression remain consistent over time? This methodological approach effectively captures both dynamic changes and lagged effects, thereby offering a more robust framework for understanding the intricate relationships among these variables.

Methods

Data collection and population

The CHARLS is a large, longitudinal cohort study of individuals over age 45 in China that includes information such as health status, health care and insurance, and demographics [27]. In this study, we used the data of Harmonized CHARLS (Version D), which covers four waves: 2011 (T1), 2013 (T2), 2015 (T3), and 2018 (T4), with a total of 25,586 observations. Although the database has been updated through 2020, data from 2020 were excluded from this study due to the potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on individuals’ physical and mental health. The development of the Harmonized CHARLS was funded by the National Institute on Aging (R01 AG030153, RC2 AG036619, R03 AG043052). Details of the surveys are available on the official website (https://g2aging.org/). The study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki, the original CHARLS was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University, and all participants signed informed consent at the time of participation [28].

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of our analysis, we conducted rigorous screening of the sample, excluding participants under the age of 45 (N = 8443) and those with missing or abnormal data for key variables (N = 11,869). Specifically, we excluded participants with missing multimorbidity information (N = 2,307), missing depression information (N = 6,389), or missing CHE information (N = 3,173). As a result, the final sample comprises 5,274 individuals with balanced panel data spanning from 2011 to 2018. Measurements

Multimorbidity

We assessed the multimorbidity of participants on the basis of 12 self-reported disease inquiries, which included the following question: have you been diagnosed with any of the following conditions by a doctor: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart problems, stroke, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive diseases, arthritis, or asthma? The respondents were given a score of “1” if they reported having an illness and “0” if they did not. Furthermore, this study quantified multimorbidity by counting the number of reported diseases without considering the severity of each condition [29].

Intensity of CHE

The intensity of CHE serves as an indicator of the impact of out-of-pocket (OOP) payments on household well-being [30]. Criteria for depression

The 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D-10) scale is employed to assess depression. The total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depression [32]. The CES‑D‑10 has been validated and shown to be reliable among Chinese adults (Cronbach’s α up to 0.89) [7]; in this study, longitudinal confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the CES‑D‑10 demonstrated strict longitudinal invariance across waves (see Supplementary Appendix Table S2).

Covariates

In our analysis, we controlled for four timeinvariant covariates at the 2011 baseline, including age, gender (male = 1, female = 2), region of residence (rural = 0, urban = 1), and educational attainment (less than lower secondary = 1, upper secondary and vocational training = 2, tertiary = 3). And we controlled for two time-varying covariates in each wave, including marital status (married = 1, unmarried = 2), and earnings after tax.

Statistical analysis

First, we conducted a descriptive analysis of the study population. Continuous variables are described using the mean and standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables are described using frequency and percentage.

Second, on the basis of the skewed distribution of the study variables, we used Spearman correlation analysis among multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE within and across waves.

Third, RI-CLPMs were constructed: Model 1 examined longitudinal bidirectional relationships between multimorbidity and depression, and Model 2 extended this specification to include the intensity of catastrophic health expenditure (CHE). In both models, respondent age, gender, region of residence, and educational attainment were included as time-invariant covariates. Marital status and after-tax earnings were entered as time-varying covariates. All covariates were specified as predictors of the observed variables across the T1–T4 waves. Specifically, the testing of Models 1 and 2 involved three nested specifications for each substantive analysis (Models 1a–1c and Models 2a–2c). Initially, an unconstrained baseline model was fitted in which all paths were free to vary across waves (Models 1a/2a). Then, a “several‑constrained” model was fitted that imposed equality constraints on all cross‑lagged paths and all autoregressive paths across time (Models 1b/2b). Finally, a fully constrained model was fitted that constrained cross‑lagged paths, autoregressive paths, and occasional covariances to be time‑invariant (Models 1c/2c). Additionally, the CLPMs were estimated as auxiliary models.

All the models were estimated in Mplus 8.0 via robust maximum likelihood (MLR). Global fit was evaluated with CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR; values of CFI ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR ≤ 0.08 were considered acceptable, and CFI ≥ 0.95 with RMSEA ≤ 0.06 and SRMR ≤ 0.08 were considered indicative of good fit [33]. Because χ2 is sensitive to sample size, nested model comparisons rely primarily on incremental fit indices: a more constrained model is retained if changes in fit meet commonly used invariance criteria (ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015; ΔSRMR is also inspected, with ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 considered supportive of invariance [34]). When the fit was statistically equivalent between nested models, the more parsimonious specification was selected.

Following model selection, mediation was examined specifically via the retained specification of Model 2. We tested whether the intensity of CHE mediated the longitudinal association between multimorbidity and depression by estimating indirect paths from (a) multimorbidity T-1 to depression T + 1 via the intensity of CHE T and (b) depression T-1 to multimorbidity T + 1 via the intensity of CHE T. Indirect effects were tested via percentile bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples to obtain 95% confidence intervals; an indirect effect was deemed statistically significant if its 95% CI did not include zero. The same set of covariates was included in all the mediation models.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Across the four survey waves (T1 = 2011, T2 = 2013, T3 = 2015, T4 = 2018), Table 1 shows that the analysed and excluded samples differed significantly in all covariates except gender. Sociodemographically, the analysed sample was younger, more often married, more likely to reside in rural areas, and had a higher proportion with upper secondary/vocational education (all p < 0.001); earned income was similar at T1 (p = 0.059), higher in the analysed group at T2 (p = 0.004), and lower at T3–T4 (both p < 0.001). Random intercept cross-lagged panel model between multimorbidity and depression

As shown in Table 3, for Model 1, the unconstrained baseline model (Model 1a) fitted the data but was not more parsimonious. The several‑constrained model (Model 1b), which imposed equality constraints on all cross‑lagged and autoregressive paths, produced only negligible deterioration in fit relative to the baseline (ΔCFI < 0.010; ΔRMSEA < 0.015). Furthermore, constraining occasional covariances (Model 1c) did not yield substantive improvement. Therefore, on the basis of model fit and parsimony, Model 1b was retained as the final RI‑CLPM for Model 1.

Figure 2 presents the RI-CLPMs between multimorbidity and depression. At the between-person level, we found a positive correlation between the random intercepts of multimorbidity and depression (r = 0.349, p < 0.001), indicating that individuals with higher average multimorbidity across waves also reported higher average depression compared to those with lower average multimorbidity. At the within-person level, all the autoregressive paths for multimorbidity and depression were significant (multimorbidity T on multimorbidity T-1: β = 0.808, p < 0.001; depressionT on depressionT-1: β = 0.097, p < 0.001), meaning that each additional chronic condition at T‑1 predicted a 0.808 increase in the number of conditions at T, and each one‑point increase in depression at T‑1 predicted a 0.097 increase in depression at T. For the cross-lagged paths, the paths from multimorbidity T-1 to depression T (β = 0.646, p < 0.001) and from depression T-1 to multimorbidity T (β = 0.011, p < 0.001) were significant, meaning that each additional chronic condition at T‑1 predicted a 0.646 increase in depression at T, and each one‑point increase in depression at T‑1 predicted a 0.011 increase in the number of conditions at T. Thus, the effect of multimorbidity on subsequent depression was substantially stronger than the reverse effect; moreover, prior multimorbidity had a larger influence on both subsequent multimorbidity and subsequent depression than prior depression did.

At the within-person level, significant autoregressive effects were found for multimorbidity (β = 0.936, p < 0.001), depression (β = 0.111, p < 0.001), and the intensity of CHE (β = 0.255, p < 0.001), which indicated high stability across these variables at the within-person level. Additionally, all cross-lagged paths were found to be statistically significant, further highlighting the dynamic interrelationships among these factors. Specifically, when comparing effect sizes within each outcome, the intensity of CHE at T was most strongly predicted by prior multimorbidity (β = 3.028), followed by prior depression (β = 0.372), with its own autoregression being smaller (β = 0.255). Depression at T was primarily predicted by prior multimorbidity (β = 0.789), followed by its autoregression (β = 0.111), and only minimally by prior intensity of CHE (β = 0.021). Multimorbidity at T was dominated by its autoregressive effect (β = 0.936), with small contributions from prior depression (β = 0.011) and prior intensity of CHE (β = 0.002). Overall, the model indicates robust within-person stability and dynamic, bidirectional associations among multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE, with prior multimorbidity exerting the largest prospective influence on both the intensity of CHE and depression.

Notably, the results of the mediation analyses (standardized indirect effects) revealed two significant indirect paths via the intensity of CHE: multimorbidity T‑1 → intensity of CHE T → depression T + 1 (indirect effect = 0.063, 95% CI [0.042, 0.084]) and depression T‑1 → intensity of CHE T → multimorbidity T + 1 (indirect effect = 0.001, 95% CI [0.000, 0.001]). Thus, the intensity of CHE partially mediates the prospective effect of multimorbidity on later depression, whereas the mediated effect from depression to later multimorbidity is minimal.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis — exclusion of extreme values

Because observations with multimorbidity > 9 or CES‑D‑10 > 21 represent extreme values within the sample distribution and may disproportionately influence parameter estimates owing to their high leverage, we excluded all participants meeting either criterion (n = 745) and re-estimated the models. After exclusion, the reciprocal associations among multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE remained statistically significant. Model fit remained acceptable (CFI = 0.951 > 0.95; RMSEA = 0.056 < 0.06; SRMR = 0.06 < 0.08), indicating that the main findings were not driven by these extreme cases.

Sensitivity analysis — exclusion of the T1 wave

Considering the notably low mean intensity of CHE at T1, we conducted an additional analysis excluding T1 and re‑estimated the bidirectional models using T2–T4 only. The direction and statistical significance of the key parameter estimates were similar to those in the primary analysis. The reduced‑wave models also demonstrated good fit (CFI = 0.986 > 0.95; RMSEA = 0.028 < 0.06; SRMR = 0.024 < 0.08), further supporting the robustness of the results.

Discussion

Previous research has demonstrated bidirectional associations between multimorbidity and depression, as well as unidirectional associations between CHE and either multimorbidity or depression. However, the longitudinal relationships and dynamic developmental trends among multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE have remained obscure. To fill this gap in research, the present study used an extended version of the RI-CLPM [25] to disentangle the within- and between-person effects of multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE among middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years and older in China via multiwave data from CHARLS 2011–2018.

Relationships between multimorbidity and depression

After adjusting for stable, trait‑like differences between individuals, Model 1 revealed a positive relationship between multimorbidity and depression at both the between‑person and within‑person levels. At the between-person level, the positive correlation between the random intercepts indicates that individuals with higher average multimorbidity tended to have higher average levels of depression. At the within‑person level, changes in multimorbidity and changes in depression were bidirectionally related, but the associations were markedly asymmetric. Specifically, increases in multimorbidity predicted subsequent and substantially larger increases in depression, whereas the reverse pathway—from elevated depression to increases in multimorbidity during the observed periods—was statistically significant but minimal in magnitude.

These results are consistent with the results of a prospective cohort study of Chinese older adults [35], and can be plausibly explained by bidirectional pathways linking multimorbidity and depression. On the one hand, individuals with multimorbidity often face substantial physical limitations [36], pain [37], and disability [38], which can severely reduce their quality of life and lead to social isolation [39, 40]. These challenges contribute to heightened psychological distress and increased vulnerability to mental health issues, thus amplifying the risk of depression and complicating the process of recovery. In contrast, depression can exacerbate multimorbidity via biological pathways, including increased inflammation and physiological dysregulation [41–43]. It also impairs self‑care behaviours, reduces treatment adherence, and undermines an individual’s ability to manage their health [44, 45]. These effects tend to manifest gradually and are more likely to intensify disease severity or progression rather than precipitate new diagnoses within short timeframes.

Based on these findings, we suggest the systematic incorporation of routine depression screening for the management of multimorbidity. At the primary care level, this involves regular assessment of patients with multimorbidity to facilitate early detection of mild-to-moderate symptoms, alongside the provision of initial interventions, including psychoeducation and adherence support [46]. Furthermore, the establishment of a structured referral system is essential to facilitate prompt specialist consultation for cases that are complex, severe, or resistant to treatment. For such patients, management by a multidisciplinary team is advised to develop personalized care plans and deliver coordinated, comprehensive care [47, 48].

Relationships among multimorbidity, depression, and the intensity of CHE

The Model 2 results show that, at the between-person level, the intensity of CHE was negligibly correlated with both multimorbidity and depression when comparing individuals. In contrast, multimorbidity and depression remained positively correlated across individuals. These observations highlight that the processes connecting multimorbidity, depression, and CHE function primarily through intraindividual changes over time rather than reflecting fixed differences between individuals.

Conceptually, multimorbidity seems to function as an antecedent factor within the observed triadic relationship. Increases in multimorbidity within individuals demonstrated stronger prospective associations with subsequent depression or intensity of CHE than the autoregressive effects observed for these two variables. In addition, depression has a greater impact on CHE than vice versa. A possible mechanism is that worsening multimorbidity leads to increased pain, functional limitations, and care needs, thereby increasing the risk of depression; meanwhile, both multimorbidity and depression increase healthcare utilization and out-of-pocket expenses, which in turn increase the intensity of CHE [22, 49–52]. In contrast, the prospective effects of depression and the intensity of CHE on multimorbidity were small in our analyses. Compared with the effects of financial strain and psychological distress on incident physical multimorbidity, the influence of existing multimorbidity itself appeared greater, which is consistent with the findings reported by Peng [53]. In addition, the mediation analysis initially suggested an asymmetric pathway, with the indirect effect from increasing multimorbidity to subsequent depression via the intensity of CHE being greater than the reverse indirect effect. However, although both indirect effects reached statistical significance, their magnitudes are very small. In practical terms, the intensity of CHE accounts for only a minor portion of the effect of multimorbidity on later depression and virtually none of the reverse effect.

These findings underscore the importance of preventing additional disease accumulation and actively managing existing multimorbidity. This necessitates the adoption of patient-centered, integrated clinical protocols that encompass routine screening for high-risk disease clusters, coordinated care directed by primary care physicians, systematic deprescription overseen by clinical pharmacists, functionally graded rehabilitation programs, and proactive longitudinal monitoring [54, 55].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several key strengths. First, it utilizes multiwave longitudinal data to explore the dynamic associations among multimorbidity, depression, and CHE thoroughly, overcoming the limitations of previous cross-sectional studies. The use of multiwave data further minimizes the potential for retrospective recall bias. Second, by employing the RI-CLPM, the study differentiates between between-person variations and within-person effects, thus reducing the interference of between-person differences in lagged effect estimation, and clarifying that the processes connecting multimorbidity, depression, and CHE function primarily through intraindividual changes over time. Finally, the study extends the understanding of the mediating role of CHE in the relationship between multimorbidity and depression.

However, the study also has several limitations. First, multimorbidity was operationalized as a simple count of chronic conditions and therefore does not capture condition severity, duration, patterns or clustering, or potential temporal changes in diagnostic criteria; this constrains inferences about mechanisms that may differ by disease severity, chronicity or specific multimorbidity profiles. Second, key variables—including multimorbidity, depression, out-of-pocket expenditures (OOP), and capacity to pay (CTP)—were primarily based on self-reported data and survey instruments, which may be susceptible to recall bias and measurement error. Third, household budget surveys typically do not collect information on unmet medical needs or informal payments. Consequently, the intensity-of-CHE measure used in this study may fail to identify households that report no out-of-pocket spending not because they lack need for healthcare, but because they forwent care or made informal payments. This measurement gap may lead to an underestimation of the prevalence and severity of financial hardship and could attenuate the observed associations between CHE and health outcomes. Fourth, although the covariates included in this study were selected on the basis of an extensive literature review and expert consultation, other pertinent factors—such as type of health insurance, functional limitations, lifestyle behaviors, and social support—that may influence multimorbidity, depression, and CHE were not included, indicating the need for further investigation in future research. Finally, the complete-case analysis may have introduced some selection bias, warranting cautious interpretation — particularly for between-person findings. In terms of key variables, the analytic sample exhibited higher multimorbidity overall and higher CHE in later waves, potentially leading to an overestimation of the random-intercept correlation between multimorbidity and CHE. Some differences in depression were also observed, with lower levels at T1 but higher levels at T4 in the analytic sample, leaving the direction and magnitude of bias for depression-related between-person effects uncertain. In terms of demographics, the analytic sample tended to be younger, better educated, more often married, more likely to be rural, and varied in income across waves. Importantly, all these characteristics were adjusted for as covariates in the models, lending greater confidence in the between-person conclusions drawn. Turning to within-person effects, included and excluded participants showed broadly similar longitudinal trends in multimorbidity, depression, and CHE, suggesting limited impact on within-person effect estimates. To further strengthen representativeness, future studies should pursue more complete follow-up and adopt appropriate methods for handling missing data (e.g., multiple imputation, inverse probability weighting).

Conclusion

Using four waves of CHARLS (2011–2018) and RI-CLPMs, this study examined between- and within-person dynamics among multimorbidity, depression and intensity of CHE in Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years. At the between-person level, individuals with more multimorbidity presented higher average levels of depression. Within-person analyses revealed substantial short-term stability in terms of multimorbidity, with increases in multimorbidity exerting stronger prospective influences on subsequent depression and the intensity of CHE. Furthermore, the mediation analysis indicates that the intensity of CHE accounts for only a minor portion of the effect of multimorbidity on later depression and virtually none of the reverse effect. These findings underscore the importance of integrating multimorbidity management to alleviate the intertwined physical, psychological, and economic burdens faced by this population. Considering the observational design of the study, the dependence on self-reported health conditions, the utilization of a basic count of conditions to characterize multimorbidity, and missing data may have introduced bias and limited the generalizability of the findings, necessitating caution when applying results to all Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Future investigations should incorporate intervention-based methodologies, extended follow-up periods, stratified analyses on the basis of disease clusters and severity levels, and validation across diverse countries and a wider spectrum of age groups.
