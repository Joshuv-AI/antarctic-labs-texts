Enhanced integrin-mediated adhesion and proliferation of Schwann cells using highly aligned, dual-functional fibrous scaffolds
Authors: Yin Mei Chan, Nicola G. Judge, Rebecca K. Willits, Matthew L. Becker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing biomaterials that combine tunable mechanical properties with precise biochemical functionalization is critical for advancing peripheral nerve regeneration. Here, we report the synthesis and characterization of a novel family of allyl-containing semi-aromatic polycaprolactone copolymers, synthesized via a one-pot ring-opening copolymerization. By varying monomer composition, copolymers possessing a range of thermal and mechanical properties were obtained. Aligned fiber scaffolds were fabricated by touch-spinning and functionalized with bioactive peptides RGD and YIGSR via strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition and thiol-ene click chemistries, yielding dual-functionalized fiber scaffolds with spatiotemporal control of functionalization densities. Schwann cell cultures revealed enhanced 67 kDa laminin receptor activity on YIGSR-functionalized scaffolds and significantly increased proliferation on softer materials decorated with peptides, demonstrating the synergistic influence of matrix mechanics and bioactive cues. This modular platform offers tunable scaffold properties and controlled biochemical functionalization to elicit favorable Schwann cell responses, providing a promising strategy for next-generation nerve regeneration materials.

Introduction

Integrins, principal heterodimeric transmembrane receptors, are key regulators that facilitate cell-adhesion1. Many fundamental physiological processes, including cell migration, proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation are influenced by integrin-mediated adhesion2. In particular, cell migratory speed is controlled as a function of cell-substrate interaction, where cells form new attachments at the leading edge and detach at the rear to drive locomotion3. In tissue regeneration, cell migration and proliferation are critical to the healing process. For example, in cutaneous wound healing, the repair of skin lesions relies on the ability of cells to migrate from the wound edges to restore the tissue integrity4. In the case of peripheral nerve transection injury, reparative Schwann cells migrate to and within the injured site to guide regrowing axons from proximal to distal nerve ends5. Interestingly, the timely collaboration between the proliferation and migration of Schwann cells has been shown to be critical during myelinating processes6.

In peripheral nerves, Schwann cells are encased by a specialized basement membrane comprised of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins including collagen, proteoglycans, and laminin7. Collectively, the maturation of Schwann cells and subsequent myelination processes is determined by the binding of integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}β1 to basement membrane ligands, where loss of integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}β1 in Schwann cells results in severe defects in radial lamellipodia formation critical to migration7,8. In particular, laminin-1 and laminin-2, primary components of the basement membrane, have been shown to improve Schwann cell migration in vitro9. The functional pentapeptide motif YIGSR is in the laminin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}β1 chain and binds to the 67 kDa laminin receptor10. RGD is a peptide sequence found in Fibronectin-III repeat 1011. The activation of fibronectin is most efficiently induced by the binding of RGD to integrins, including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}αV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}β311,12. Both YIGSR and RGD have been shown to play a role in supporting Schwann cell processes when tethered to synthetic materials13,14. Specifically, as substratum gradients are known to drive haptotactic migration, several studies have elucidated the influence of laminin-derived concentration gradient profiles on Schwann cell migratory activity9,15–17. Gradients of tethered YIGSR peptides have been reported to increase overall speed of migrating Schwann cells on flat substrates9. In contrast, gradients of RGD have demonstrated little influence on directional migration, though RGD did positively increase adhesion and proliferation9.

Native ECM contributes further physical guidance cues for infiltrating cells, such as topographical factors, that flat substrates do not offer. Aligned nanofibers are of particular interest for nerve regenerative applications because they mimic and enhance the topographical guidance naturally provided by blood vessels and the Bands of Büngner during healing. Many studies have demonstrated that peptide-functionalized fiber scaffolds can improve neuroregenerative cell functions, including increasing cell elongation, differentiation, and migratory speeds18,19. As such, we developed a dual-functionalized fibrous scaffold presenting YIGSR and RGD motifs. Substrates of 2D aligned nanofibers possessing a 10% allyl-containing polycaprolactone copolymer (polymer A10) with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α-alkyne terminated ends were fabricated via touchspinning20,21. RGD was uniformly tethered to the fibers via strained-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) at a defined average concentration of 170 pmol/cm2. YIGSR was applied in defined concentration profiles via a thiol-ene addition mechanism to elucidate the synergistic impacts of YIGSR and RGD on Schwann cell integrin binding activity and proliferation. Such peptide-functionalized fibers can be used alongside implantable conduits to bridge nerve transection injuries clinically.

Additionally, the stiffness of substrates have been shown to impact Schwann cell spreading, proliferation, and focal adhesion maturation22,23. While studies have shown improved Schwann cell proliferation on gel substrates with Young’s moduli between 7.45–8.76 kPa22,23, no study to date has elucidated changes in Schwann cell behavior with changing mechanical properties of fibers exhibiting storage moduli ranging from 1–100 MPa20. Therefore, the need for modulation of fiber mechanical properties within the region of native nerve tissue is of primary importance. When considering polyester-based materials, the most reported substrates with regards to Schwann cell migration are poly(glycolic acid) (PLGA), poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and polycaprolactone (PCl). While PCl is the softest and most ductile, exhibiting the lowest modulus, the values are 2–3 orders of magnitude higher than that of native nerve24. Alongside this mechanical profile misalignment, polyesters are inherently hydrophobic which can limit cell adhesion and interactions with implanted materials25. Schwann cell proliferation and binding interactions with the most and least stiff dual-functionalized fibers were explored to uncover a baseline understanding of cell-substrate relationships in terms of mechanical properties and bioactivity. Furthermore, proliferation was increased on all peptide-containing conditions on the less stiff material (A10EE10), with the dual-functionalized samples exhibiting the greatest proliferation percentages.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterization of A/SAEE-PCL

Polymers were synthesized in a one pot ring-opening copolymerization of ε-caprolactone, 3-methyl-2,3-dihydro-5H-1,4-benzodioxepin-5-one (SAEE) (Scheme S1), and 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone (Scheme S2), using 4-dibenzocyclooctyne as the initiator and Mg(BHT)2(THF)2 as the catalyst (Scheme S3-6). The respective polymer compositions were confirmed using 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Figs. S1–12). The molar percentage of each monomer in the resulting polymers was controlled by varying the feed ratio of monomer added, as verified by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 1A). A 10% allyl monomer incorporation was confirmed by the integration ratios (1:18) of the single methine epsilon proton of the 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone unit and the two methylene epsilon protons of ε-caprolactone (δ = 4.9 ppm and 4.1 ppm, respectively). DIBO initiation was confirmed by the presence of resonance δ = 5.57 ppm, representing the benzylic, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α-ether carbon.

SAE incorporation was determined by the resonances at δ = 5.25 (methine), 4.26 (methylene), and 1.37 ppm (methyl). The percent incorporation of each monomer was confirmed using the ratios of the methine peak of SAEE to the methylene epsilon proton resonances of ε-caprolactone, with ratios 0.1:22, 0.7:27, and 1.1:27 representing A10 EE1, A10 EE5, and A10 EE10, respectively. Tension DMA analysis of mechanical properties of the bulk material at 25 °C and 37 °C (Figs. S16 and 17) displayed significant decreases in storage modulus (G’) with increased SAEE monomer content, affording a softer and more rubber-like material, yielding 192 ± 10 MPa for polymer A10 and 53 ± 4 MPa for A10EE10 (Fig. 2D). The polymers displayed frequency-dependent storage moduli (Fig. 2C), which can be attributed to the materials’ semi-crystalline nature post-thermal processing.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the successful synthesis and functionalization of a new family of tunable copolymers—A/SAEE-PCL—exhibiting bioactive peptides to influence Schwann cell behavior. Each copolymer was made using a three monomer system (ε-caprolactone, 3-methyl-2,3-dihydro-5H-1,4-benzodioxepin-5-one (SAEE), and 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone) with Mg(BHT)2(THF)2 as the catalyst28. This catalyst has been used in the synthesis of caprolactone copolymers that have independently contained either 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone21 or the SAEE monomer26, but has not been used in a one-pot three monomer system until now. Initial investigations looked to optimize monomer concentration and catalyst loading. As SAEE monomer preferentially polymerizes in the bulk, reactions were trialed at high concentrations, though at concentrations above 3 M ɛ-caprolactone and 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone, polymerization resulted in gelation and formation of oligomeric species. Higher catalyst loading (5 eq. relative to the initiator) also resulted in low SAEE monomer conversion. The optimized reaction conditions were found to be 2 M ɛ-caprolactone and 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone and 2 M equivalents of catalyst.

Manufacturing material characteristics, such as topological, mechanical, and thermal properties, is a critical aspect of scaffold design for tissue engineering applications29–31. Aligned nanofibers are highly mimetic of natural neural microenvironments and provide high surface areas for cell attachment and proliferation32–35. For cases of nerve transection injuries, studies have shown aligned fibers serve as topographical directional cues for neurite outgrowth of isolated cells and whole DRG explants36,37. Similarly, Schwann cells on fibers have been shown to advantageously restrict migration to the axis of fiber alignment through contact guidance37. As touch-spinning has previously shown to yield fiber scaffolds of high alignment and uniform diameters37, touch-spinning was chosen for this study. While several caprolactone copolymers have previously been touch-spun, including polyethylene oxide20 and poly(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}ε-caprolactone-co-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}ε-allyl-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\varepsilon$$\end{document}ε -caprolactone)21, we have demonstrate successful fabrication of aligned fibers made with variations of A/SAEE-PCL.

By varying monomer contribution, A/SAEE-PCL copolymers representing a broad range of thermal and mechanical properties were obtained. While increasing SAEE monomer incorporation resulted in decreased Tm and Tc, Td remained significantly greater for all polymers, still allowing for a broad temperature range of thermal stability. In addition, a materials’ mechanical properties, particularly with regards to matrix stiffness, has shown to effect adhesion and proliferation of a number of cell types38–40. However, many studies elucidating effects of stiffness on cell behavior utilize gels and other soft materials exhibiting storage moduli on the kPa scale22,23. Uniquely, we investigated the effects of stiffness on Schwann cell adhesion and proliferation on innately stiffer fibers that exhibited storage moduli on the MPa scale. By increasing the molar incorporation of SAEE monomer from 0 to 10 mol%, the storage moduli reflected an almost 70% decrease at 25 °C. The same analysis was done at 37 °C to elucidate material performance at physiological conditions and showed a similar drastic decrease of around 90%. All storage moduli decreased at physiological temperatures compared to ambient, as expected.

In addition to matrix stiffness, cell proliferation is influenced by integrin-mediated adhesion2. YIGSR and RGD are standard peptides that have been commonly tethered to synthetic scaffolds to introduce bioactivity for nerve regenerative applications9,41. In particular, our previous publications have demonstrated the uniform dual-functionalization of RGD and YIGSR to DIBO-terminated ketone-containing PCL14. A subsequent study by Motta et al. reported that while gradients of RGD did not induce haptotaxis, concentration gradients of YIGSR did have a distinct effect9. As such, in this study, we continued to utilize DIBO-terminated copolymers to allow for the uniform addition of RGD peptides via SPAAC, but advantageously introduced allyl side chains to tether YIGSR through thiol-ene click mechanisms, allowing for spatiotemporal control over functionalization density. Through fluorescently-tagged peptides and fluorescence imaging, we demonstrated that DIBO-A/SAEE-PCL can be successfully functionalized with both uniform RGD and patterns of YIGSR, including gradient profiles, that can be investigated in future works.

To understand the synergistic impacts of variable fiber stiffness, and RGD and YIGSR presence, fibers of the softest (A10) and stiffest (A10EE10) materials were uniformly functionalized with either none, one, or both of the peptides. Schwann cells were then seeded on the scaffolds for 48 hours and subsequently probed for 67 kDa laminin receptor (YIGSR) and integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}αV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta 3\,$$\end{document}β3(RGD) activity. Similar to previous results done by Zheng et al., presence of 67 kDa laminin receptor was not evident for scaffolds without YIGSR, but was enhanced on scaffolds with YIGSR for both A10 and A10EE10 fibers14. Laminin-coated controls also demonstrated greater cells expressing 67 kDa laminin receptor labeling, which can be expected due to YIGSR being a laminin-derived peptide42. Interestingly, integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}αV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta 3$$\end{document}β3 labeling was present across all scaffolds at similarly high percentages, and no significant differences in integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}αV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta 3$$\end{document}β3 labeling across functionalization conditions and polymers was observed. While integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}αV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta 3$$\end{document}β3 is a prominent member of the RGD-recognizing integrin family, it can also bind to various other extracellular matrix proteins, potentially explaining its constant expression throughout the study43. Finally, cells were stained for Ki67 to identify actively proliferating cells44. On A10 fibers, all conditions reflected similar proliferation percentages with no significant differences. Excitingly, on the softer A10EE10 fibers, proliferation significantly increased on all peptide-functionalized scaffolds, regardless of which peptide or combination was present. Junka et al. saw similar results, incorporating chitosan and laminin within the fiber scaffold increased cell proliferation after a 4-day incubation period compared to control PCL fibers45. Non-functionalized A10 and A10EE10 fibers exhibited limited proliferation and no differences between the two, highlighting the key impact of bioactive peptides on synthetic scaffolding. However, future studies will look to elucidate any effects that may be present over longer incubation periods such as those seen by Cooper et al. where statistically enhanced proliferation was seen after 3 days on PCL/chitosan fiber scaffolds46.

This study presents the synthesis, functionalization, and characterization of a novel family of A/SAEE-PCL copolymers engineered for nerve regeneration applications. By leveraging a one-pot three-monomer strategy and Mg(BHT)₂(THF)₂ catalysis, we optimized polymerization conditions to incorporate SAEE monomer and 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone into polycaprolactone, while preserving control over copolymer composition, processability, mechanical properties, and post-fabrication functionalization capabilities. These materials were effectively fabricated into aligned fiber scaffolds via touch-spinning and subsequently functionalized with RGD and YIGSR peptides. RGD was uniformly tethered via SPAAC on the DIBO-terminated copolymers, while YIGSR was tethered via thiol-ene reactions to the alkene side chains introduced by 6-allyl-ε-caprolactone. The advantageous use of thiol-ene reactions allowed for spatiotemporal control of functionalization densities across the scaffold. Functionalization notably enhanced 67 kDa laminin receptor activity on YIGSR-containing scaffolds and increased Schwann cell proliferation on softer peptide-containing scaffolds, underscoring the synergistic effects of matrix mechanics and biochemical signals. Together, these findings highlight the potential of A/SAEE-PCL scaffolds as a modular platform for studying Schwann cell responses and for developing next-generation nerve regenerative technologies. Future work investigating spatial gradients of YIGSR in combination with uniform RGD and their effects on directional cell migration and axonal regeneration in vitro and in vivo would be of interest.

Monomer synthesis

3-Methyl-2,3-dihydro-5H-1,4-benzodioxepin-5-one (SAEE) monomer was synthesized according to previously published procedures26. KOH (40.00 g, 360 mmol), H2O (13 mL) and salicylic acid (25.00 g, 181 mmol) were added sequentially to a high pressure round bottom flask (250 mL) under continuous stirring. Propylene oxide (38.00 mL, 543 mmol) was added dropwise, the vessel sealed and was heated to 70 °C and allowed to react for 48 h. The crude product was acidified (pH 2) with 2 M HCl and extracted with diethyl ether. The combined organic layers were washed 2 M HCl (x2) followed by brine (x2) and the resulting organic layer dried over MgSO4 and concentrated in vacuo. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy

1H-NMR (600 MHz) and 13C-NMR (150 MHz) were recorded using a Bruker Avance III spectrometer. Chemical shifts were recorded in parts per million (ppm) relative to the CDCl3 reference resonance at δ = 7.26.

Size exclusion chromatography (SEC)

Molecular mass and molecular mass distribution were determined using an EcoSEC HLC-8320GPC (Tosoh Bioscience LLC., King of Prussia, PA) equipped with a TSKgel GMHHR-M mixed bed column and refractive index (RI) detector. Poly(styrene) was used as the standard. Samples (2.5 mg/mL) were prepared using HPLC-grade chloroform (amylene stabilized) for injection with a 0.5 mL/min flow rate at 40 °C.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Thermal transitions (Tm and Tc) were determined from the centers of the transitions acquired by DSC (first cooling and second heating cycle) on a TA Instruments Discovery DSC250 (TA Instruments–Waters L.L.C., New Castle, DE) using Trios Software Analysis. Samples (5–10 mg) were used and heated to 200 °C at 10 °C/min before cooling to −80 °C at 5 °C/min per cycle.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Sample degradation temperatures (Td) were determined using a TA Instruments Discovery TGA 550. Each sample was prepared by loading material (5–10 mg) onto a platinum TGA pan. Under N2 gas flow, the temperature was ramped to 120 °C at 10 °C/min and held isothermally for 15 minutes. The temperature was then equilibrated at 30 °C and finally ramped to 200 °C at 10 °C/min. Td was determined as the temperature at 10% mass loss.

Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA)

Oscillation strain sweeps in tension were performed on each polymer composition (N = 1) to identify the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) for subsequent measurements. The frequency was maintained at 1 Hz while the strain varied from 0.1 to 1%. The LVR was determined as the strain values between which the measured storage modulus remained constant. Oscillation frequency sweeps in tension were performed (N = 5) on melt pressed bars for each polymer composition. The strain was maintained at 0.5% and the frequency varied from 0.1 to 100 Hz. Storage modulus (G’) was determined as the G’ at 10 Hz. These measurements were conducted at 37 °C and 25 °C. Oscillation temperature ramps were performed on each polymer composition. The strain was maintained at 0.1% and the frequency was maintained at 1 Hz while the temperature ramped from −80 °C to 20 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min. Tg was determined as the temperature corresponding to the peak tan (δ) value.

Film fabrication

Films were prepared on a Bubble Magic 5” × 5” Pneumatic Heat Press (70 psi) between Teflon sheets ~20 °C above the sample melting temperature (as determined by DSC) for ~5 minutes. Fast cooling was performed by removing the pressed film and stacking cold metal plates atop it on the bench until cool. Liquid N2 was used to cool films as polymer crystallization temperatures (Tc) were around room temperature.

Fabrication and characterization of touch-spun aligned nanofibers

An illustration of the touch-spinning setup is shown in Fig. 3A. Polymer solutions (25 w/w%) were made using chloroform and drawn into a glass syringe (1 mL). The syringe was fixed with a blunt-end needle (18 G) and loaded onto a syringe pump. The polymer solution was extruded out of the syringe at a constant flow rate (5 µL/min) for all touch-spun substrates. The syringe pump was positioned so that the tip of the needle was in tangential contact with the rotating, perpendicular rods connected to the rotating disk. Borosilicate coverslips were used as collection plates for subsequent functionalization and in vitro studies. The resulting fibers were sputter coated and imaged using SEM. Fiber diameter and directionality were characterized using the measurement function and directionality plugin of NIH FIJI.

Gradient patterns were designed on Adobe Illustrator and used for the fabrication of a chromium neutral density filter on quartz purchased from Advance Reproductions Corporation (North Andover, MA). The gradient pattern used herein was defined as 100% to 0% transparency over a 25 mm distance.

The fibers were allowed to spontaneously react with the peptide for 15 mins at ambient conditions. The fibers were then rinsed with and soaked in DI H2O for 15 min, H2O/MeOH (4:5 v/v) for 30 min, and soaked again in DI H2O for a final 15 min. Separately, coverslips with touch-spun aligned nanofibers were submerged in a solution of the appropriate thiol (0.8 mM) and lithium phenyl (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphinate (LAP) (0.3 mM) in H2O:MeOH (4:5 v/v). Quantification of YIGSR and RGDS concentration

Scaffolds functionalized with CYIGSRK-FITC and N3-KRGDSK-TAMRA were used to quantify concentration of immobilized peptides. For each gradient type, 3.5 mm of fibers were removed from both the left and the right side of the scaffold because of aggregation and physical absorbance along the edges of the scaffold. The remaining fibers were divided into three equal sections (6 mm in width). along the fiber direction (6 mm wide sections) and weighed. For the fiber samples where half of the scaffold was exposed to UV light for thiolene functionalization, the scaffolds were divided at the interface of functionalized and non-functionalized to yield two equal sections (9 mm wide sections). The fibers per section were then dissolved in 300 µL of HFIP and the fluorescence was read using a Shimadzu UV-3600i UV-Vis-NIR Spectrophotometer. FITC was measured at excitation λex = 449 nm and emission λem = 511 nm, while TAMRA was measured at excitation λex = 552 nm and emission λem = 578 nm. All samples were kept at the same weight (0.22 mg) and concentration was calculated using a calibration curve. Three replicates were tested and measured for each pattern.

Substrate preparation and sterilization for in vitro studies

Loctite silicone sealant was manually extruded through a plastic syringe to build layered walls on glass and fiber substrates and left to dry. The substrates were then affixed to a four-well rectangular dish. Ethylene oxide (EtO) (Andersen Sterilizer, Haw River, NC) was used to sterilize the substrates via a 12 h sterilization cycle, followed by a 2 h aeration period. Sterilized substrates were then left for a minimum 72 h to allow residual EtO to diffuse out before in vitro studies.

In vitro cell studies

Primary Schwann cells were isolated at Northeastern University (Boston, MA). Male Schwann cells were cultured in proliferation media (DMEM, forskolin (5 µM), FBS (10% vol/vol), N2 supplement (1% vol/vol), L-glutamine (1×), and BPE (14.2 µg/mL))47 and seeded at a density of 10,000 cells/cm2 in poly-l-lysine-coated (15 µg/mL) T25 flasks for general culturing. Cells used in experiments were under passage 5.

Schwann cells were seeded on functionalized fiber scaffolds at a density of 10,000 cells/cm2 in proliferation media (above) and incubated for 48 hours. After 48 hours, the cells were fix with PFA in PBS (4% v/v), rinsed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton (X-100). Cells were then permeabilized using sodium borohydride (1 mg/mL in PBS) twice for 10 min each. Samples were washed with PBS three times and then incubated with 7.5% w/v BSA in PBS at ambient conditions for 1 h before washing once with 1% w/v BSA and thrice with PBS. The primary 67 kDa laminin receptor (1:250 in 1% BSA in PBS) was then aliquoted onto samples and left on a shaker with gentle rocking. Statistical analysis

Fiber diameters and concentration quantification of tethered peptides were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Statistical differences regarding storage modulus and fluorescent labeling were analyzed via ordinary two-way ANOVA.
