Healing wounds with words: Narrative Exposure Therapy for chronic PTSD
Authors: Rudi Haryadi, Rizka Apriani, Riskiyana Prihatiningsih, M. Ramli, Nur Hidayah, Muslihati
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We are writing to address critical issues highlighted in the recent study by Jasim et al. on the long-lasting effects of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in Yazidi women living in Northern Iraqi camps.1 This research underscores the severe and enduring impact of PTSD, which warrants immediate attention and action from both the scientific community and policymakers. Our discussion will focus on two pivotal areas: the long-term impact of PTSD on families and communities and the efficacy of Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET) as a treatment strategy.

Firstly, the long-term impact of PTSD extends beyond the individual, significantly affecting families and entire communities. According to existing scientific literature, PTSD can lead to a range of chronic health problems, including cardiovascular, metabolic, and autoimmune diseases,2 which can diminish the overall well-being and life expectancy of affected individuals.3 These health issues often create a ripple effect, placing additional burdens on families and caregivers who are already struggling with the emotional and psychological fallout of trauma.

Children of PTSD sufferers are particularly vulnerable. Studies have shown that these children are at a higher risk of developing emotional and behavioral problems, including anxiety, depression, and conduct disorders.4 This can be attributed to the lack of emotional availability and consistent parenting from traumatized parents,5 who may be dealing with their own overwhelming symptoms of PTSD. The intergenerational transmission of trauma is a well-documented phenomenon,6 leading to a cycle of psychological distress that can perpetuate across generations if not adequately addressed.

Communities with a high prevalence of PTSD, such as those comprising displaced Yazidi populations, often experience social fragmentation.7 Trust and social cohesion may be severely undermined, as individuals with PTSD may become isolated due to their symptoms,8 leading to a breakdown in community support structures. This fragmentation exacerbates the difficulties faced by these communities, making it even more challenging to provide comprehensive support and foster resilience.

In addressing these profound impacts, it is crucial to implement effective therapeutic interventions. Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET) has emerged as a compelling treatment for PTSD, particularly in populations that have experienced multiple and complex traumas,9 such as the Yazidi women. NET is a short-term, evidence-based therapeutic approach that integrates elements of cognitive-behavioral therapy with narrative therapy, allowing individuals to construct a coherent narrative of their traumatic experiences.10

The efficacy of NET in reducing PTSD symptoms is well-supported by scientific evidence. Studies have demonstrated that NET can significantly decrease PTSD symptoms, improve overall psychological functioning, and enhance quality of life.11 One of the compelling rationales for NET is its structured yet flexible approach, which accommodates the needs of individuals who have endured severe and prolonged trauma. By helping patients chronologically reconstruct their life stories, NET facilitates the integration of traumatic memories into a cohesive narrative, reducing the fragmentation of memory that is a hallmark of PTSD.10

Furthermore, NET is particularly suited for use in resource-limited settings, such as refugee camps, due to its relatively brief duration and structured protocol.10 This makes it a viable option for large-scale implementation, where resources and access to mental health professionals may be limited. Additionally, the communal nature of many displaced populations can be leveraged in the group-based delivery of NET, fostering social support and collective healing.12

A compelling aspect of NET is its dual focus on individual healing and social acknowledgment of trauma. By constructing and sharing their narratives, individuals not only process their trauma but also contribute to collective memory and acknowledgment of their experiences.10 This can be particularly empowering for communities like the Yazidi, who have faced systematic attempts to erase their cultural identity and experiences. NET thus serves as both a therapeutic intervention and a means of preserving cultural memory and resilience.13

In conclusion, the long-term impacts of PTSD on families and communities are profound and far-reaching, necessitating urgent and effective interventions. Narrative Exposure Therapy offers a promising solution, with its strong empirical support, suitability for resource-limited settings, and dual focus on individual and collective healing. We advocate for the implementation of NET in communities affected by mass trauma, such as the Yazidi women in Northern Iraqi camps, to mitigate the intergenerational transmission of trauma and foster resilience and recovery.
