An Overview of Continuing Medical Education/Continuing Professional Development Systems in the Middle East and North Africa: A Mixed Methods Assessment
Authors: Lawrence Sherman, Samar Aboulsoud, Kathy Chappell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aims of this assessment were to describe the requirements for physicians to engage in CME/CPD; explore perceptions of in-country SMEs of their CME/CPD systems; describe perceptions of in-country physicians about interprofessional continuing education (IPCE) and independent CME/CPD; and provide recommendations that may be adopted to improve quality and effectiveness. An assessment of CME/CPD systems in the Middle East and North Africa was conducted using a mixed-methods approach that included 1:1 interviews with in-country SMEs and an electronic survey capturing qualitative and quantitative data from practicing in-country physicians. The results of this assessment were strongly influenced by Egypt and Israel in the Middle East, and Algeria and Morocco in North Africa. The CME/CPD systems demonstrate wide variation from absent/immature systems to robust/mature systems. Strategies to improve the quality of the CME/CPD systems range from implementing basic standards in North Africa to evaluating the impact of CME/CPD in practice in the Middle East. The maturity of CME/CPD systems seems to affect physician awareness, independence from the influence of pharmaceutical companies over education, and IPCE, with more mature systems having a positive relationship to awareness, independence and engagement in IPCE. Maturity of CME/CPD systems is less tied to physician perceptions of value of CME/CPD, hours of participation, perceptions of what is missing from current systems, and preferred formats of education.

Introduction

The world’s population relies on the healthcare professional (HCP) workforce to keep them healthy, to receive care when they are ill or injured, and to help them manage chronic disease. It is therefore critical that practicing HCPs have opportunities to engage in high-quality, evidence-based continuing medical education (CME)/continuing professional development (CPD) that meets their learning needs and helps them maintain the necessary knowledge and skills over their professional careers.

This manuscript describes the current state of the CME/CPD systems in 12 countries in the Middle East and North Africa as described by in-country SMEs via focused interviews as well as input from in-country physician practitioners from 16 countries via an electronic survey. This assessment includes the countries of Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Israel, Jordan, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Oman, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Yemen in the Middle East. This assessment also includes the countries of Algeria and Morocco in North Africa. The report compares the state of the CME/CPD systems in these countries to best practices in CME/CPD systems globally.

This manuscript is one of a series of assessments that also include China, Europe, and Latin America following a similar format [1]. This manuscript builds on previous regional assessments funded by independent educational grants from Pfizer in the country of Japan and within the Asian-Pacific countries of Hong Kong, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Taiwan, and Thailand [2,3].

Overview

The countries that are represented by in-country SMEs in this assessment (Appendix A) have physician per 1000 people ratios that vary widely, ranging from 0.7 physicians/1000 people in Egypt (2019) to 3.7 physicians/1000 people in Israel (2021) [4].

Medical Education Systems

With the exception of the KSA, academic preparation for physicians in these countries follows two general paths and both begin after successful completion of secondary school. The first path requires an average of 6–7 years to complete and confers a medical degree. The first path incorporates medical school and practice-based training (internship or general practice). The second path requires 8 years to complete and confers both a baccalaureate degree and a medical degree. Physicians graduating from medical school in the second path often complete internship and/or residency programmes as a requirement to graduate. Four of the 12 countries in this assessment have both paths available concurrently. In the KSA, physicians are prepared through a baccalaureate degree programme only and must also complete internship and/or fellowship programmes to practice medicine. Graduates in all countries can specialise through residency and/or fellowship training. Tuition is heavily subsidised by the government of each country or is free. The practice of medicine in each country is governed by a national body, most often a Ministry of Health.

CME/CPD Requirements for Physicians

There is no requirement for physicians to participate in CME/CPD as a condition of licensure and to practice medicine in 5 of 12 countries in this assessment (Algeria, Israel, Lebanon, Morocco, and Oman). One country (Egypt) recently passed a law requiring physicians to participate in CME/CPD after 2020. In six countries, physicians must engage in CME/CPD as a condition of licensure and to practice medicine (Kingdom of Bahrain, UAE, Jordan, KSA, Kuwait, and Qatar).

For countries requiring participation in CME/CPD as a condition of licensure and to practice medicine, credits are awarded in hours, points, or units. Hours are calculated as time spent engaged in the educational activity, while points or units are calculated using a matrix as defined by the individual regulatory body and are generally based on the type of educational activity and/or length of time engaged in the educational activity [5] More points and/or credits are awarded to educational activities that are perceived as more valuable/impactful or those that are longer. For example, more points may be awarded to a physician who participates in a medical symposium or teaches within a school of medicine as compared to reading a journal article. Additionally, more credits may be awarded for an educational activity lasting 8 hours as compared to one lasting 1 hour. In countries without mandatory participation, physicians are still encouraged to engage in CME/CPD, and participation may be recommended by or enforced by the employer.

In most countries in this assessment, there is a regulatory body for CME/CPD even if participation is not required as a condition of licensure or to practice medicine. The regulatory body may be the same governing body for the practice of medicine (licensure) or may be driven by a medical professional association.

CME/CPD Provider System

The CME/CPD provider systems, defined as organisations that develop CME/CPD educational activities, in these countries are primarily regulated by a medical professional association or speciality organisation.

Types of organisations that can provide CME/CPD include medical associations, hospitals and health departments, colleges and universities, and approved training organisations. Pharmaceutical companies have influence over the CME/CPD systems through direct provision, control, or funding in six of the countries in this assessment while the remaining countries have implemented regulations to separate influence and control.

Physicians in these countries in the Middle East and North Africa have an opportunity to participate in CME/CPD in multiple types of formats that may include, but are not limited to, live and web-based educational programmes, conferences and scientific meetings, simulation, skills or practice-based education, and scholarly publications. Physicians can also receive credit for self-directed learning activities based on the rules or regulations as established by the professional association or governmental body.

Review of the Literature on CME/CPD in the Middle East and North Africa

A focused review of the literature on CME/CPD in the Middle East and North Africa was conducted using electronic databases (MEDLINE, PubMed, and CINAHL), with inclusion criteria of availability in English, full text, research studies, and published in a peer review journal within the past 15 years. The literature review was expanded to include one seminal article published in 2006 regarding the historical background in the region and expanded to include theses and conference presentations identified by the Regional SME. A summary of the articles reviewed is provided below.

Historical Background

A Committee for CME/CPD in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) was established in 200 to coordinate and promote the CME/CPD initiatives among the GCC countries. The Committee facilitated awareness about CME/CPD and introduced the concept of mutual recognition of CPD credits within the GCC countries. Furthermore, there was discussion regarding the formalisation of GCC Alliance in CPD [6].

System Comparisons

A comparative analysis of CME/CPD frameworks and accreditation systems was undertaken by Hussain et al. in 2016. The principle findings can be summarised into four themes: differences in governance structures, discrepancies in the total credit requirements as well as between different health professions, the presence of an accreditation system, and the mandatory participation in CME/CPD activities for maintenance of licensure.

CME/CPD: Challenges and Barriers

Our literature review identified a number of research papers that addressed challenges and barriers to the development and implementation of CME/CPD. A cross-sectional study that was conducted amongst primary care physicians in Qatar reported lack of time and cost as the main challenges to participation in CME/CPD activities [7]. In an earlier study of the primary care physicians in Alahsa, KSA, researchers found that poor teaching methodologies, irrelevance of topics and physicians’ inclination to self-learning, dissatisfaction with available CME resources and lack of computers in the workplace, high patient load, and multiple work responsibilities were barriers to participation in CME [8]. A Jordanian study concluded that CPD practice is jeopardised by the lack of mandatory laws in addition to challenges related to staff shortages, work overload, lack of time, and cost [9].

In her research of the status of CME in the KSA governmental hospitals, Alghamdi [10] described the major CME challenges and categorised them into four main themes: management, including the lack of appreciation of decision makers about the importance of CME, and their influence over the planning of learning programmes; lack of resources and poor access to medical libraries; CME budget constraints which amplified the influence of pharmaceutical industry on CME practice; cultural diversity of physicians, including different physicians’ educational backgrounds, and resistance to change their performance after the completion of a CME activity or programme.

Perceptions, Good Practice and Lessons Learnt

A study of the perceptions of CME activities among primary care physicians in Bahrain reported a positive overall experience and recommended that financial support and protected time should be secured to further enhance the CME practice. The study highlighted the need for a formal CME committee in health institutions to support and provide continuous oversight for CME activities within the institution [11].

Capacity Building in the Health Professions

One publication outlined a cooperative strategy among Gulf Coast Countries (GCC) in the Middle East to address critical shortages of nationally trained HCPs and an over-reliance on an expatriate health care workforce [12]. Following reports issued by the World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for the Eastern Mediterranean in 2014, 2016 and 2017, and a call for capacity building of the health care workforce in the GCC, the authors describe the process of analysing the current gaps in capacity and identifying areas of success that could be replicated. The authors cite the development of CME/CPD activities as crucial to increasing health care capacity. Specific recommendations include conducting country-specific needs assessments, post-remediation programmes, assessment of the impact of CME/CPD on healthcare professional and patient levels, assessing the number of trained HCPs working in the country, and identifying areas requiring additional improvement [12]. Recommendations also included an emphasis on incorporating culturally appropriate CME/CPD activities and implementing a multidisciplinary approach. The authors share numerous strategic partnerships across GCC countries as well as strategic partnerships between GCC and the WHO, and between the GCC and non-GCC organisations.

Design and Implementation of a CPD System for Healthcare Professionals in Egypt

In 2018, the Egyptian government approved legislation requiring the establishment of a regulatory authority for CPD and expectations of participation in CPD as a condition of relicensure for physicians [13]. Subsequently, the regulatory authority created a provider accreditation model that established criteria for educational quality, learning outcomes, independence from influence of commercial interest organisations, and tracking of learners. The authors share outcomes that include the number of accredited CPD providers, number of CPD activities, the types of organisations that achieved accreditation, and the steps taken to ensure independence between commercial interest organisations and CME/CPD. The authors describe how the strong regulatory framework and emphasis on quality education are leading to the aim of achieving international recognition.

Limitations of the Literature Review

The majority of the literature analysed explored the perceptions of primary care physicians in different countries in the Middle East. It is recommended to consider a more inclusive research approach that explores perspectives of physicians in tertiary care and other HCPs. Published CME/CPD research is particularly lacking in some Northern African countries e.g. Algeria, Tunisia and Morocco, and much of it was published more than ten years ago.

Mixed-Methods Assessment Strategy

To evaluate each country in greater depth and to assess the overall perception of the CME/CPD systems in the Middle East and North Africa from a broad group of physicians, a mixed-methods assessment strategy was employed in a three-pronged approach that included 1:1 interviews with In-Country SMEs facilitated by a Regional SME, and an electronic survey to capture both quantitative and qualitative data from in-country physicians (Appendix B). The survey that was used to capture data from in-country physicians was used in two previous assessments conducted in Japan and the Asia-Pacific region; therefore, the results of this assessment provide an opportunity to compare results globally [2,3]. The survey captured demographic data as well as self-report data from in-country physicians that reflected characteristics of the CME/CPD system in their countries, perceptions of industry-supported CME/CPD, and perceptions of how well the current CME/CPD systems were meeting physicians’ educational needs.

In this assessment, independent CME/CPD was defined as: CME/CPD for which financial, or in-kind, contributions given by a commercial interest, which is used to pay all or part of the costs of an activity. The definition of roles and requirements when commercial support is received are outlined in the Accreditation Council of Continuing Medical Education (ACCME) Standards for Commercial Support and although this is a United States (US)-based organisation, it is accepted as the standard for defining roles and responsibilities. The commercial supporter cannot be involved in the planning, delivery, or evaluation of the educational activities and all decisions are made by the educational provider [17].

This assessment was led by an independent health professions educator (HPE) with 27 years of experience in global CPD and IPCE, who also holds an international development role within the Association for Medical Education in Europe (AMEE). The HPE recruited a regional SME from Egypt to assist with the study in the Middle East. The Egyptian SME facilitated the recruitment of in-country SMEs from the Middle East, reviewed the survey to ensure contextual relevance, and supported survey dissemination in the region. The HPE also recruited a regional SME from Algeria to assist with the study in North Africa. The Algerian SME facilitated the recruitment of in-country SMEs from North Africa and supported survey dissemination in the region. The Algerian SME also translated the survey into French and provided back-translation of free text responses into English.

Each in-country SME participated in a 1:1 focused interview with the HPE and regional SME to provide an overview of their CME/CPD system. In-country SMEs used PowerPoint presentations and open discussion to deliver the content. Interviews (Appendix C) lasted between 30 and 60 minutes and were guided by the HPE using a standard set of questions. All interviews were conducted in English and translation was not required. Interviews were also attended by a doctorally-prepared researcher who transcribed notes for the subsequent report/manuscript. Country-level data were summarised in table format and sent back to the in-country SME for validation. In-country SMEs confirmed the data and/or made changes to ensure the data were accurate.

The electronic survey that was used to capture quantitative and qualitative data from in-country physicians throughout the Middle East and North Africa was provided in English to the in-country physicians throughout the Middle East and in French to the in-country physicians in North Africa. Per the regional SMEs, physicians throughout the Middle East were comfortable responding to the survey in English while physicians in North Africa would be more apt to respond if the survey was translated. Throughout the countries, the survey was disseminated electronically using SurveyMonkey® by the in-country SMEs who also engaged partner organisations if needed. Purposive, heterogeneous sampling was used to access respondents as the aim of this assessment was to capture the perceptions of physicians who represented diversity across specialities, practice settings, age, gender, and experience. Respondents self-selected to participate and no remuneration for participation was given. There were 12 countries represented by regional SMEs and a total of 16 countries that had respondents to the survey.

Institutional Review Board (IRB) Approval

This assessment was conducted in accordance with the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki and in accordance with local statutory requirements.

Dissemination

The survey in the Middle East and North Africa was opened on 27 November 2020 for a total of 20 weeks and 4 days, and was closed on 20 April 2021. When necessary, reminders were sent to maximise participation. The survey remained open for the extended period to allow partner organisations to disseminate and to maximise the number of respondents.

Following the survey close, data were downloaded from SurveyMonkey® and free text items were translated from French into English by the Algerian SME.

Data Analysis

Middle East Cohort

For the Middle East cohort, the original data file included 550 rows of respondent data including quantitative data and free text responses in English. All personal identifiers including IP address were immediately removed from the data file and analysis was performed only using the deidentified file. A case number was created for each respondent row to track and ensure accuracy during the analysis process. To ensure duplicates were not counted more than once, the researchers deduplicated the survey responses before conducting the analysis.

A review of countries reported by respondents was conducted to ensure that all respondents practiced in the Middle East. A total of 12 cases were removed from the data set due to a respondent who practiced outside the Middle East. A total of 60 cases were removed from the data set due to respondents who failed to enter any country name into the free text box.

There were 104 cases that were removed from the original data file because the respondent failed to answer the item “Do you have formal training/coursework in medical education” (e.g. the item was left blank; N = 37) or the respondent failed to continue the survey after that item (e.g. no further data were entered by the respondent; N = 67). Both Cohorts

Quantitative analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistical software Version 28 and included descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, %). Analysis was conducted on the qualitative data collected via the survey (e.g. open response questions) who aggregated and summarised comments by overall theme as they related to the survey item. The six-step process used for thematic analysis was: familiarisation, coding, generating themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing up themes was followed for this analysis [18]. Qualitative analysis was conducted on the interview responses and open-ended questions on the survey.

Results

Physicians from across 16 countries throughout the Middle East and North Africa responded to the electronic survey. The following is an aggregate summary of the quantitative and qualitative data captured through the survey. Qualitative data provided by in-country SMEs via focused interviews that reflect their perspectives are also presented in summary.

Quantitative Survey Data

Demographics (Table 1)

A total of 374 physicians from the Middle East and 45 physicians from North Africa were included in this analysis. In the Middle East, the largest volume of respondents were from Israel (N = 168) and Egypt (N = 126). In North Africa, most respondents were from Morocco (N = 35). In both cohorts, more respondents practiced in general practice than in any other single speciality or sub-speciality. In the Middle East, a substantial number of respondents practiced in internal medicine or an internal medicine subspeciality, in paediatrics, or in obstetrics/gynaecology. There were no respondents who practiced in surgery, a surgery subspeciality, or in obstetrics/gynaecology from the North Africa cohort.

In the Middle East, more respondents practiced in a hospital than in any other practice setting, while 23% reported “other” as their practice setting. Free text responses in “other” included primarily private hospitals and clinic settings. In North Africa, 57.7% of respondents practiced in a hospital setting, and a substantial number of respondents practiced in a location classified as “other”. Free text responses to “other” reflected private practice or clinic settings and speciality clinic settings.

Most respondents in both cohorts practiced in large cities. In the Middle East cohort, most respondents graduated from medical school more than 25 years ago, and the remaining respondents were evenly distributed through the remaining categories. In the North Africa cohort, respondents were more evenly distributed among the categories of years since graduation.

Most respondents practiced clinically and reported seeing patients. In the Middle East cohort, the average number of patients seen per day for those in clinical practice averaged 23.21 (SD 16.92); and in the North Africa cohort, the average number of patients seen per day was slightly higher at 28.26 (SD 18.20).

When asked if they had responsibility for teaching medical students, resident doctors, clinical specialists (consultants) or “other”, most respondents in both the Middle East and North Africa cohorts reported being responsible for “other”. As the survey item was asked as a single response choice, those who selected “other” reported not being responsible for teaching at all, being responsible for teaching multiple groups within medicine, or being responsible for teaching within medicine and with other health professionals.

Preparation as Life-Long Learner and Consultant

More respondents from the Middle East cohort (40.9%) reported that their undergraduate training prepared them to be a lifelong learner as compared to respondents from the North Africa cohort (24.5%) and reported that their postgraduate training prepared them to practice as a consultant (Middle East: 53.7%; and North Africa: 42.2%).

Qualitative Survey Data

Overall, qualitative data collected via the electronic survey substantiated the quantitative survey findings. In the Middle East cohort, some respondents reported that the CME/CPD systems in their countries were good, and they had no additional suggestions. When offering suggestions, respondents reported that they recommended reducing costs, increasing the CME/CPD opportunities to better align with their areas of clinical speciality, and to implement mandatory requirements. Respondents from the North Africa cohort reported a need to better fund CME/CPD for physicians.

Qualitative Data from the In-Country SME 1:1 Interviews

Discussion from the in-country SME interviews expanded the overall findings of this assessment.

Middle East Cohort (Kingdom of Bahrain, Dubai (UAE), Egypt, Israel, Jordan, KSA, Kuwait, Lebanon, Oman, and Qatar)

In-country SMEs in the Middle East cohort focused primarily on improving the existing structure, improving quality, and measuring impact within their CME/CPD systems as their systems were already well established. In-country SMEs in several countries recommended expanding their accreditation systems, increasing more active learning engagement strategies, implementing evaluation strategies to assess impact of participation in CME/CPD, and better aligning CME/CPD with country-level practice gaps and health care needs. In-country SMEs also recommended developing more independent funding sources for physicians to participate in CME/CPD, implementing protected time for education, and continuing to expand interprofessional continuing education.

North Africa Cohort (Algeria and Morocco)

As compared to the Middle East cohort, the in-country SMEs recognised that their CME/CPD systems are in very early stages of maturity. As there is no regulation or oversight of CME/CPD in Algeria and Morocco, in-country SMEs recommended implementing a structured system that is based on standards, is regulated, and is widely available at little to no cost for physicians. The CME/CPD in Algeria and Morocco is currently driven primarily by the pharmaceutical industry and content is focused on clinical areas of therapeutic interest, therefore there is little to no content that is tied to practice gaps or country-level health needs. In-country SMEs recommended developing funding sources outside of the pharmaceutical industry, tying CME/CPD requirements to licensure, increasing faculty development support, expanding reach, and implementing methods to evaluate effectiveness. Interprofessional continuing education was not seen as a priority by the in-country SMEs at this time.

Discussion

Results from this assessment of CME/CPD systems across the Middle East and North Africa and from In-country SME interviews depict systems that are more robust and structured in some countries while in other countries, they are in the very early stages of maturity.

Opportunities to expand scope, depth and impact therefore vary accordingly.

Regional Overview

Across the countries reviewed in this assessment, academic preparation for physicians is relatively consistent. Countries offer single- or dual-model paths that incorporate internship or residency (practice-based) training and are heavily subsidised by the government or are free. A national body governs the licencing of physicians to practice medicine. The number of physicians per 1000 people varies widely with Bahrain, Egypt and Morocco having significantly fewer physicians as compared to the other countries in this assessment. The countries with fewer physicians per 1000 people may need to depend on a more diverse workforce to meet health care needs of the population and may need to explore or invest in more opportunities for interprofessional continuing education.

The CME/CPD systems in the Middle East countries are more robust and structured as compared to the countries in North Africa. The majority of countries in the Middle East require physicians to participate in CME/CPD as a condition of licensure and although Israel, Lebanon and Oman do not require participation, physicians are strongly encouraged to engage in continuing education. In the North Africa countries included in this assessment, there are no structured CME/CPD systems and there are no requirements for physicians to engage in continuing education to practice medicine.

Of the countries evaluated in this assessment, only the countries in the Middle East cohort have established formal CME/CPD systems which include governing bodies, standards, credit systems, and tracking mechanisms. In the countries with formal CME/CPD systems, there is a CME/CPD credit system that is used to validate and/or track physician participation regardless of whether the credit is required to practice medicine. There are also incentive and/or employer-mandated requirements for physicians to engage in continuing education thus ensuring that there is a commitment to lifelong learning. The credit systems in countries with formal CME/CPD systems reflect hours of participation in an educational activity or credit that is awarded in self-directed learning. The currency of CME/CPD may be awarded in hours, units or credits. The currency can then be used to determine if a physician has met expected requirements, and if not, a governing body determines what actions would be taken against a physician’s licence and/or ability to practice medicine. In the countries without formal CME/CPD systems, there is no regulation, no standards, no formal credit system, and no mechanisms to track participation as there are no repercussions for not engaging in continuing education, although physicians may be awarded a certificate of attendance.

Regardless of whether there is a formal CME/CPD system, physicians in all countries can access a variety of different formats for educational activities or self-directed learning. The CME/CPD systems, when regulated, are done so by the same governing body for the practice of medicine or are led by a medical professional association.

In countries with mandatory CME/CPD as a condition of licensure, pharmaceutical companies may not control the content of educational activities; however, pharmaceutical companies are permitted to provide funding support that can be used by the educational provider to independently develop and provide CME/CPD. In countries without mandatory CME/CPD as a condition of licensure, there is more influence by pharmaceutical companies over content. In countries without structured CME/CPD systems, pharmaceutical companies provide the majority of continuing education directly to physicians. In-country SMEs noted that implementation of accreditation systems such as the ACCME in the US have led to the separation of pharmaceutical influence over control of content.

The availability and interest in interprofessional continuing education (IPCE) is one of the aspects of the CME/CPD systems in these countries that demonstrates the greatest variation. In some countries, IPCE is the predominant method of designing and delivering continuing education while in other countries, there is an absence of any investment in team-based education.

Best Practices in CME/CPD Systems

The CME/CPD systems that support physicians and other HCPs to engage in life-long learning vary widely across different countries yet are critical to improving health care practice, and patient and population health outcomes. Best practices for CME/CPD systems should demonstrate that they are based on adult learning principles, are aligned with contemporary learning science frameworks, include mechanisms to ensure quality and independence, provide educational opportunities in multiple formats and via multiple modes of delivery, and are easily accessible and affordable. Regulatory mechanisms should be driven by the profession, include methods to validate learner participation, and should be clearly understood by all stakeholders. Systems should incorporate strategies for self-assessment, be based on identified practice gaps for individuals and health care teams, and include evaluation of change (learner, practice, and health outcomes). There should be an investment in developing HCPs as lifelong learners, interprofessional team members, and teachers.

Comparison of CME/CPD Systems in the Middle East and North Africa to Best Practices

This assessment of the CME/CPD systems in the Middle East and North Africa reflects wide variation and comparison of the systems to best practices is best accomplished by separating the two cohorts.

Middle East

Overall, the CME/CPD systems in the Middle East are mature. They are structured and regulated through governmental agencies, or medical associations. Participation in CME/CPD is required as a condition of licensure in the majority of countries, or there are strong incentives for physicians to engage. There are formal credit systems even when participation is not mandatory, and physicians have access to multiple different types of educational opportunities. There are a variety of types of organisations that can provide CME/CPD, and physicians can engage in both educational provider-directed CME/CPD as well as self-directed education for credit. There is a commitment in most countries to limit the influence of pharmaceutical companies over control of CME/CPD through implementing accreditation systems such as ACCME standards, or the profession has implemented ethical standards for physicians that address independence. Since most countries have mature systems, there is a focus on increasing the quality of education and better aligning education with practice gaps. There is a focus on improving faculty skills, moving to more active learning engagement strategies, and expanding patient-focused, clinically relevant content. IPCE, while seen as a priority across countries, is still evolving. Although IPCE is rare in some countries, which may reflect more hierarchical systems, in other countries it is the predominate style of continuing education.

North Africa

There are no structured CME/CPD systems in Algeria and Morocco. There are no requirements for physicians to engage in continuing education and there is no established credit or tracking system; however, physicians may be awarded certificates of attendance. The pharmaceutical industry funds and provides most CME/CPD for physicians and content is primarily in their therapeutic areas of interest. There is a lack of alignment between education that is provided and practice or country-level needs with the exception of alignment in certain areas that are driven by socio demographic and epidemiological data, such as a focus on diabetes and heart disease.

Limitations

There are significant limitations to this assessment. As the survey was disseminated using a snowball strategy, it was not possible to calculate an accurate response rate. Using the number of physicians in the Middle East and North Africa, the response rate was very low, and the results may not accurately reflect the perspectives of the broader physician community. The results predominantly reflect the countries of Israel and Egypt in the Middle East and Algeria and Morocco in North Africa. The SMEs participating in the assessment may have a limited or biased perspective of the CME/CPD system and therefore may not reflect the broader physician community, although efforts were made to include SMEs who were actively engaged in and able to critically assess their country systems. A more rigorous sampling strategy would have increased validity of the results. Finally, the assessment was conducted during the global COVID-19 pandemic which could have impacted survey participation.

Conclusions

This mixed-method assessment conducted across the Middle East and North Africa describes the current state of the CME/CPD systems in the regions. The results of this assessment were strongly influenced by Egypt and Israel in the Middle East, and Algeria and Morocco in North Africa. The CME/CPD systems demonstrate wide variation from absent/immature systems to robust/mature systems. Strategies to improve the quality of the CME/CPD systems range from implementing basic standards in North Africa to evaluating the impact of CME/CPD in practice in the Middle East.

Maturity of CME/CPD systems seems to impact physician awareness, independence from the influence of pharmaceutical companies over education, and IPCE with more mature systems having a positive relationship with awareness, independence and engagement in IPCE. Maturity of CME/CPD systems is less tied to physician perceptions of value of CME/CPD, hours of participation, perceptions of what is missing from current systems, and preferred formats of education.
