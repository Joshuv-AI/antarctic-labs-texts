Sum of Gaussian vectors and large sets
Antoine Song

Abstract
. We show that for some constant κ > 0 \kappa>0 , any centered κ \kappa -subgaussian random variable is equal to the sum of three standard Gaussian random variables, confirming a conjecture of M. Talagrand. We also prove that given Λ ≥ 1 \Lambda\geq 1 , any centered random vector X X in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} such that ‖ X ‖ ≤ Λ \|X\|\leq\Lambda almost surely and ‖ Cov ⁡ ( X ) ‖ ≤ Λ 2 ​ e − Λ 2 \|\mathrm{Cov}(X)\|\leq{\Lambda^{2}}{e^{-\Lambda^{2}}} is equal to the sum of a universal number of standard Gaussian random vectors. How expressive are sums of Gaussian vectors?

2.1.
Basic properties of sums of Gaussian vectors

In this subsection, we develop some general tools for studying sums of non-independent Gaussian random vectors. In preparation for the main lemma of this subsection, we note the following:

Lemma 2.1

(Linear map)
.

If
G
is a standard Gaussian random vector in
\R^{n}
, then for any linear operator
A
with
\|A\|\leq 1
,
there are two standard Gaussian random vectors
X,Y
in
\R^{n}
such that

A(G)=\frac{X+Y}{2}.

Proof.

The singular value decomposition of
A
, viewed as an
n
-by-
n
matrix, yields
A=USV^{T}
where
U,V
are orthogonal matrices and
S
is diagonal with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
, since
\|A\|\leq 1
.
By rotational symmetry of standard Gaussian random vectors, we can assume without loss of generality that
A=S
.
Note that
A(G)
is then given by a random vector of the form
(Z_{1},...,Z_{n})
where
Z_{i}
are independent real-valued Gaussian random variables with variance at most
1
.
Thus, it becomes clear that to prove the lemma, it is enough to show that any real-valued Gaussian random variable with variance at most
1
is the average of two standard Gaussian random variables.
Let
Z^{\prime}
be a real-valued Gaussian random variable with variance
\sigma^{2}\leq 1
. Let
X_{1}
be a standard Gaussian real-valued random variable, which we can decompose as
X_{1}=X_{\sigma^{2}}+X_{1-\sigma^{2}}

where
X_{\sigma^{2}},X_{1-\sigma^{2}}
are independent Gaussian random variables centered at
0
and with variances
\sigma^{2}
,
1-\sigma^{2}
respectively.
Now, consider the random variable
Y_{1}:=X_{\sigma^{2}}-X_{1-\sigma^{2}}.

Then
Y_{1}
is, like
X_{1}
, a standard Gaussian random variable. But observe that
\frac{1}{2}(X_{1}+Y_{1})=X_{\sigma^{2}}
has the same distribution as
Z^{\prime}
. That finishes the proof.

∎

Recall the notation
\sum^{q}\mathcal{G}(\R^{n})
for

).

Next, we state the main lemma of this subsection:

Lemma 2.4

(Lipschitz map)
.

Let
\Psi:\R^{n}\to\R^{n}
be a Lipschitz map with Lipschitz constant at most
C_{\mathrm{Lip}}>0
, and let
G
be a standard Gaussian random vector in
\R^{n}
.
Then there are two standard Gaussian random vectors
X,Y
in
\R^{n}
such that

\Psi(G)-\E[\Psi(G)]=C_{\mathrm{Lip}}\frac{X+Y}{2}.

Proof.

Our proof relies on Itô’s formula, in a way inspired by the proof of
[
Pis16
, Theorem 1.5]
.
By rescaling
\Psi
by a factor
\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{Lip}}}
, it is enough to show the statement when
\Psi
is 1-Lipschitz.
Let
\{B_{t}\}
be the standard Brownian motion on
\R^{n}
starting at
0
. Note that
\Psi(G)-\E[\Psi(G)]
has same probability distribution as
\Psi(B_{1})-\E[\Psi(B_{1})].

By Itô’s formula
[
RY99
, Theorem 3.3]
, we have

\Psi(B_{1})-\E[\Psi(B_{1})]=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla(P_{1-t}\Psi)(B_{t}).dB_{t}.

Here,
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the heat semigroup, and if
\Psi
is the vector-valued map
(\Psi_{1},...,\Psi_{n})
, then
P_{1-t}\Psi
denotes the vector-valued map
(P_{1-t}\Psi_{1},...,P_{1-t}\Psi_{n})
and
\nabla(P_{1-t}\Psi)(x)
is the linear map whose matrix has
i

(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}P_{1-t}\Psi_{i}(x),...,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}P_{1-t}\Psi_{i}(x))
.
Since
\Psi
is 1-Lipschitz,
P_{1-t}\Psi
is also 1-Lipschitz and so

\|\nabla(P_{1-t}\Psi)\|\leq 1.

This means by
[
RY99
, Proposition 2.13]
that the random variable
\Psi(G)-\E[\Psi(G)]
is the limit in probability of random variables of the following form:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}(G_{N,j})

where
G_{N,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{N}I_{n})
are independent and for each
j\geq 1
,
A_{j}
is a certain random linear operator with operator norm at most 1 depending only on
G_{N,1},...,G_{N,j-1}
.
By Lemma
2.1
, for each
j=1,...,N
, conditioned on
A_{j}
(which only depends on

where
k=1,...,j-1
),

A_{j}(G_{N,j})=\frac{X_{N,j}+Y_{N,j}}{2}

where
X_{N,j},Y_{N,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{N}I_{n})
.
Since the latter holds no matter what the values of
G_{N,k}
are for
k=1,...,j-1
, this gives well-defined random vectors
X_{N,1},...,X_{N,N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{N}I_{n})
which are independent, and similarly random vectors
Y_{N,1},...,Y_{N,N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{N}I_{n})
which are independent.
Summing in
j
, we get

\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}(G_{N,j})=\frac{X+Y}{2}

where
X:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{N,j}
and
Y:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}Y_{N,j}

X,Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
.
∎

(Bounded support)

The next lemma says that a sum of Gaussian random vectors with small covariance can be rewritten as a sum of standard Gaussian random vectors:

Lemma 2.8

(Normalization)

For any integer
q\geq 3

Note that, since a sum of

Recall that if

G_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,bI_{n})

G_{a}+G_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(a+b)I_{n})
.
Thus, to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that for any
q\in\{3,4,5\}
and any
\tau_{i}\in(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
where
i\in\{1,...,q\}
, there are random vectors
W_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(1-\tau_{i}^{2})I_{n})
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{q}W_{i}=0
.
We explain the case
q=3
only, since the other cases are similar (and since we will only need that case later). Fix
a:=\frac{1}{2}((1-\tau_{1}^{2})+(1-\tau_{2}^{2})-(1-\tau_{3}^{2}))
. Note that
a\geq 0
,
(1-\tau_{1}^{2})-a\geq 0
and
(1-\tau_{2}^{2})-a\geq 0

\tau_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
for

. Consider
W_{1}=W_{1}^{\prime}+W_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, and
W_{2}=W_{2}^{\prime}+W_{2}^{\prime\prime}

and

and

The following lemma is technically a key piece of the proof of Theorem

Lemma 2.9

(Density bounds)
.

For some universal
\kappa\in(0,1)
, if
S
is a real-valued
\kappa
-subgaussian variable, then there is a standard Gaussian random variable
G
such that
S+G
has distribution
f(x)dx
where

is a piecewise continuous function that satisfies

C^{-1}\varphi(x-y_{0})\leq f(x)\leq C\varphi(x-y_{0})

for some
y_{0}\in[-1,1]
depending on
S
and for some universal constant

.

Proof.

Let
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, which we will fix in the proof. For simplicity, we assume that
S
is a discrete variable with density
\sum_{i=1}^{I}p_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
where
I>0
is an integer,
p_{i}\in(0,1)
,
\sum_{i=1}^{I}p_{i}=1
, and
x_{i}\in\R
. The general case can then be deduced by approximating the variable

in the weak topology by such discrete variables, and use compactness.

Consider some
y_{0}\in[-1,1]
and some
\nu>0
for the moment.
Given
j=1,...,I
,
if
x_{j}\notin[-1,1]
, set

g_{j,0}(x):=0,\quad g_{j,1}(x):=\varphi(x-x_{j})

and if

where
\mathds{1}_{j,y_{0}}
denotes the function equal to
\mathds{1}_{[y_{0},\infty)}
if
y_{0}<x_{j}
and equal to
\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,y_{0}]}
if

.
If
\nu\in(0,1/2)
is smaller than some universal positive constant, then

for all

, all

and all choices of

There is a random vector
X
supported on
S
such that

X\sim-X\quad\text{and}\quad\|\Cov X\|\leq\tau^{-1}.

Proof.

By a standard compactness argument, it is enough to treat the case where
S
is finite. The lemma then follows from the von Neumann minimax theorem, in a similar fashion as
[
DGLN19
, Theorem 2]
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}
be the set of symmetric positive semidefinite
n
-by-
n
matrices with trace equal to
\tau
, and let
\Delta_{S}
be the simplex of symmetric probability measures on
S
. These are compact convex sets and any
\mu\in\Delta_{S}
has average
0
. The minimax principle applied to the affine function

f:\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}\times\Delta_{S}

f(Q,\mu):=\Tr(Q\Cov(\mu))

gives

\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{S}}\Tr(Q\Cov(\mu))=\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{S}}\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}\Tr(Q\Cov(\mu)).

But
\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}\Tr(Q\Cov(\mu))=\tau\|\Cov(\mu)\|
, and

\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{S}}\Tr(Q\Cov(\mu))=\min_{s\in S}\Tr(Qss^{T})=\min_{s\in S}s^{T}Qs
, so

\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}\min_{s\in S}s^{T}Qs=\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{S}}\tau\|\Cov(\mu)\|.

From this equality, the equivalence is clear.

∎

Proof of Corollary
0.6
.

An ellipsoid
E=\{x^{T}Qx\leq 1\}
with trace
\Tr E=\Tr Q=\frac{1}{10}
has Gaussian measure
\gamma_{n}(E)=\mathbb{P}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z_{i}^{2}\leq 1]=\mathbb{P}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}10\lambda_{i}Z_{i}^{2}\leq 10]
where the
\lambda_{i}
’s are the eigenvalues of
Q
and the
Z_{i}
’s are independent standard Gaussian variables. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}10\lambda_{i}=1
,
[
SB03
, Corollary 3]
implies that

\gamma_{n}(E)\geq\mathbb{P}[Z_{1}^{2}\leq 10]\geq\frac{1}{2}

where the second inequality follows from well-known estimates for the Gaussian measure.
Moreover by Lemmas
1.2
and
1.3
, if a set
A
satisfies
\gamma_{n}(A)\geq\frac{2}{3}
then
\gamma_{n}(A^{\prime})\geq\frac{2}{3}
where
A^{\prime}:=A_{(k_{0})}\cap(-A_{(k_{0})})
and
k_{0}>0
is a universal integer. The point here is that
A^{\prime}
is symmetric.

In view of these preliminary remarks, in order to prove the corollary, it is sufficient to show that for some universal integer
q_{0}>0
, for a symmetric set
A\subset\R^{n}
with
\gamma_{n}(A)\geq\frac{2}{3}
,
there is an ellipsoid
E
in
\R^{n}
with

(33)

\Tr E=\frac{1}{10}\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}E\subset A_{(q_{0})}.

Let
q>0
be the integer given in Theorem
0.4
.
By Lemmas
1.2
and
1.3
, for some universal
k_{1}\geq 2
,

(34)

\gamma_{n}(A_{(k_{1})})>1-\frac{1}{10q}.

Suppose towards a contradiction that (
33
) fails for
k_{0}=10k_{1}q+2
.
Then for any ellipsoid
E
with
\Tr E=\frac{1}{10}
,
E\cap\R^{n}\setminus\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log n}}A_{(10k_{1}q+2)}\neq\varnothing
. By Lemma
3.3
, there is a random vector
X_{0}
in
\R^{n}
whose support
\supp X_{0}
is contained in the closure of
\R^{n}\setminus\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log n}}A_{(10k_{1}q+2)}
,
such that
X_{0}\sim-X_{0}
and
\|\Cov(X_{0})\|\leq 10
.
Note that by Lemma
1.2
,
A_{(10k_{1}q)}
is strictly contained in the interior of
A_{(10k_{1}q+2)}
, meaning that

(35)

\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\supp X_{0}\subset\R^{n}\setminus A_{(10k_{1}q)}.

Set

X_{1}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{\|X_{0}\|^{2}\leq 100n\}}X_{0}

which satisfies
X_{1}\sim-X_{1}
like
X_{0}
. In particular
X_{1}
is centered.
By the covariance bound for
X_{0}
and Markov’s inequality,
X_{1}=X_{0}
with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
. Besides,
\|\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}X_{1}\|\leq\sqrt{\log n}
almost surely, and
\|\Cov(\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}X_{1})\|\leq\frac{\log n}{n}
, so applying Theorem
0.4
to the centered random vector
\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}X_{1}
,

(36)

\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}X_{1}=10(G_{1}+...+G_{q})

for some standard Gaussian random vectors

.
By (
34
) and the union bound, we deduce that
G_{i}\in A_{(k_{1})}

and

36
), there are points

and

, with

\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}x=10(a_{1}+...+a_{q})\in 10A_{(k_{1}q)}\subset A_{(10k_{1}q)}.

But this contradicts (

This finishes the proof.

3.3.
Further discussions

3.3.1.
Largest slices

One can show that Corollary
0.6
and the standard Lemma
1.3
imply an optimal bound for the largest sections of sums of large sets: there exists an integer
q>0
such that
for any
\theta\in(0,1)
,
if
A
is a closed set in
\R^{n}
with
\gamma_{n}(A)\geq\frac{2}{3}
, then there is a linear subspace
H
with
\dim H=\lfloor\theta n\rfloor
and
c=c(\theta)>0
such that

(37)

\forall k\geq q,\quad\gamma_{H}\big(A_{(k)}\cap H\big)\geq 1-n^{-ck^{2}}.

Up to the constant
c>0
, this bound is optimal: consider the Gaussian measure of sections of centered hypercubes
[
BGMN05
, Theorem 10]
.
This remark also provides an indirect way to verify that the factor
\sqrt{{n}^{-1}{\log n}}
in Corollary
0.6
is sharp up to uniform constants already for centered hypercubes
(if
\sqrt{{n}^{-1}{\log n}}
could be replaced by a much larger constant, then it would lead to a much better bound than (
37
), a contradiction with
[
BGMN05
, Theorem 10]
).

3.3.2.
From convex bodies to ellipsoids

If Problem
0.1
were known to have a positive answer, then Corollary
0.6
would immediately follow from the beautiful result
[

, Theorem 2.11.9]
, which implies that given a convex body

in

with

, there is a sequence of halfspaces

such that for some universal

\R^{n}\setminus c_{0}K\subset\bigcup_{k\geq 1}H_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\|x_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log(k+1)}}.

One could show from Corollary

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

The proof argument closely follows
[
Tal
, Lemma 2.11]
.
Clearly, it is enough to show the statement when
n
is an even integer, which we will assume below.
We want to estimate the sum over
i=1,...,n
of

(38)

\displaystyle\E[|X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}|^{2}]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[|X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}|>t]\,t\,dt.

By symmetry of the question, it is enough to estimate the sum over
i=1,...,n/2
.
Fix
i\in\{1,...,n/2\}
and
t>0
. Under these assumptions,
r_{i}\leq 0
.
Set
Z_{k}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}<r_{i}-t\}}
.
These random variables are independent.
Note that
X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}<-t
if and only if the number of elements in
\{k;\,X_{k}<r_{i}-t\}
is at least
i
, namely

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}<-t]=\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\geq i].

Set
a_{k}:=\mathbb{P}[Z_{k}=1]
, and
a:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}
. By our assumption on
\theta_{i}
and the definition of
r_{i}
,

a=n\Phi(r_{i}-t)\in(0,i).

Write

(40)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\geq i]&=\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}(Z_{k}-a_{k})\geq i-a]\\
&\leq\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}e^{-\lambda(i-a)}\Pi_{k=1}^{n}\E[e^{\lambda(Z_{k}-a_{k})}].\end{split}

Next, for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

\E[e^{\lambda(Z_{k}-a_{k})}]=a_{k}e^{\lambda(1-a_{k})}+(1-a_{k})e^{-a_{k}\lambda}\leq e^{a_{k}\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda}}

(this can be checked by showing that for all
x\geq 0
and
\lambda\geq 0
,
xe^{\lambda}+(1-x)\leq e^{x\lambda+x\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda}}
, which in turn can be proved by comparing the derivatives). Hence, by (
40
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\geq i]\leq\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}e^{-\lambda(i-a)+a\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda}}.

If
a\in[\frac{i}{7},i)
, we choose
\lambda=\frac{i-a}{6a}
and get

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\geq i]\leq e^{-(i-a)^{2}/12a}

where we used
\lambda=\frac{i-a}{6a}\leq 1
and
e^{\lambda}\leq 3
.

If
a\in(0,\frac{i}{7})
, we choose
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\log(\frac{i-a}{2a})
and get

(42)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}\geq i]&\leq e^{-\frac{(i-a)}{4}\log(\frac{i-a}{2a})}\\
&=(\frac{2a}{i-a})^{\frac{i-a}{4}}\leq(\frac{2a}{i-a})^{\frac{i}{8}}\leq(\frac{4a}{i})^{\frac{i}{8}}\end{split}

where in the first line we used that
\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda}\leq\lambda e^{2\lambda}
so that
e^{-\lambda(i-a)+a\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda}}\leq e^{\lambda[-(i-a)+ae^{2\lambda}]}
, and in the second line we used that
\frac{2a}{i-a}\leq 1
, and
\frac{i-a}{4}\geq\frac{i}{8}
, and
\frac{2a}{i-a}\leq\frac{4a}{i}
.

Set

\xi_{i}(a):=e^{-\frac{(i-a)^{2}}{12a}}\quad\text{ if $a\in[\frac{i}{7},i)$},

\xi_{i}(a):=(\frac{4a}{i})^{\frac{i}{8}}\quad\text{ if $a\in(0,\frac{i}{7})$}.

Combining (
39
), (
41
) and (
42
), we obtain

(43)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}<-t]\,t\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))\,tdt.

The estimate of the integral (
43
) is where there is an issue with the proof of
[
Tal
, Lemma 2.11]
.
We will use the following basic inequalities:

(44)

\displaystyle\text{for any $x\leq 0$,}\quad\frac{|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Phi^{\prime}(x)\leq|x|\Phi(x)\leq\Phi^{\prime}(x)\quad(\text{Mills ratio inequalities})

(45)

\displaystyle\text{for any $x\leq 0$, $t>0$,}\quad\Phi(x-t)\leq e^{-c(1+|x|)t}\Phi(x)\quad\text{where $c>0$ is universal.}

To check (
45
), recall that the Gaussian cumulative distribution function
\Phi
is log-concave, so
\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u)}{\Phi(u)}
is non-increasing in
u
, and thus
\frac{\Phi(x-t)}{\Phi(x)}=\exp(-\int_{x-t}^{x}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u)}{\Phi(u)}du)\leq\exp(-t\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\Phi(x)}).

Since
\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\Phi(x)}\geq\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(0)}{\Phi(0)}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}
, and since we have
\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\Phi(x)}\geq|x|
by the lower bound in the Mills ratio inequalities, we further deduce that
\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\Phi(x)}\geq c(1+|x|)
for some universal
c>0
, which gives (
45
).
Next, recall that since
i\leq n/2
, we have
r_{i}\leq 0
. Besides, by the Mills ratio inequalities (
44
), one checks that

(46)

\displaystyle(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{10}(\log\frac{n}{i}+1).

By (
45
) and the definition of
r_{i}
,

(47)

\displaystyle n\Phi(r_{i}-t)\leq ie^{-c(1+|r_{i}|)t}.

Define
t_{i}>0
to be the unique number such that
n\Phi(r_{i}-t_{i})=\frac{i}{7}
. In particular,
0<t_{i}\leq\frac{\log 7}{c}
by (
47
), and

(48)

\displaystyle\begin{split}n\Phi(r_{i}-t)\in[\frac{i}{7},i)\quad\text{if $t\in(0,t_{i}]$,}\\
n\Phi(r_{i}-t)\in(0,\frac{i}{7})\quad\text{if $t\in(t_{i},\infty)$}.\end{split}

Besides, note that both functions
a\mapsto e^{-\frac{(i-a)^{2}}{12a}}
and
a\mapsto(\frac{4a}{i})^{\frac{i}{8}}
are non-decreasing in
a
. So by (
48
), (
47
) and (
45
),

(49)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{if $t\in(0,t_{i}]$,}\quad\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))&=\exp({-\frac{(i-n\Phi(r_{i}-t))^{2}}{12n\Phi(r_{i}-t)}})\leq\exp({-\frac{i^{2}(1-e^{-c(1+|r_{i}|)t})^{2}}{12ie^{-c(1+|r_{i}|)t}}})\\
&\leq\exp({-\frac{i(1-e^{-c(1+|r_{i}|)t})^{2}}{12}})\leq\exp({-\frac{ic^{2}(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}t^{2}}{12}}),\\
\text{if $t\in(t_{i},\infty)$,}\quad\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))&=(\frac{4n\Phi(r_{i}-t)}{i})^{\frac{i}{8}}=(\frac{4n\Phi(r_{i}-t_{i}-(t-t_{i}))}{i})^{\frac{i}{8}}\\
&\leq(\frac{4n\Phi(r_{i}-t_{i})e^{-c(1+|r_{i}|)(t-t_{i})}}{i})^{\frac{i}{8}}\leq(\frac{4}{7}\exp[{-c(t-t_{i})}])^{\frac{i}{8}}.\end{split}

Thanks to (
49
) and since
t_{i}\leq\frac{\log 7}{c}
, we can now estimate the integral (
43
):

(50)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))\,tdt&=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))\,tdt+\int_{t_{i}}^{\infty}\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))\,tdt\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\exp({-\frac{ic^{2}(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}t^{2}}{12}})\,tdt+\int_{t_{i}}^{\infty}(\frac{4}{7}\exp[{-c(t-t_{i})}])^{\frac{i}{8}}\,tdt\\
&=\frac{6}{ic^{2}(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}}(1-\exp[-\frac{ic^{2}(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}t_{i}^{2}}{12}])+\int_{t_{i}}^{\infty}(\frac{4}{7})^{\frac{i}{8}}\exp[-\frac{ci}{8}(t-t_{i})]\,tdt\\
&\leq\frac{6}{ic^{2}(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}}+(\frac{4}{7})^{\frac{i}{8}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp({-\frac{c}{8}s})\,(s+\frac{\log 7}{c})ds.\end{split}

Finally we can bound (
38
): summing the integrals (
49
) over all
i=1,...,\frac{n}{2}
, we obtain using (
50
) that for some universal constants
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\E[|X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}|^{2}]&=2\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{i}-r_{i}<-t]\,t\,dt\\
&\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi_{i}(n\Phi(r_{i}-t))\,tdt\\
&\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\frac{1}{i(1+|r_{i}|)^{2}}+C^{\prime}\\
&\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\frac{10}{i(\log\frac{n}{i}+1)}+C^{\prime}\quad\quad\text{(use (\ref{r_iestimate})}\\
&\leq C^{\prime\prime}(\log\log n+1).\end{split}

This is enough to conclude the proof.
