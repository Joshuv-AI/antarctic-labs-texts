Environmental Scan of Tools and Frameworks Assessing Climate-Related Health Vulnerabilities in Older Populations
Authors: Connie Cai Ru Gan, Cicely K. Nurse, Pratik Chaturbhai Sohaliya, Patricia Chiao-Tzu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesClimate change is intensifying health inequities, particularly among older adults who face heightened vulnerability to climate-related hazards. With the global senior population estimated at one billion and projected to increase by 20% by 2050, there is urgent need for health systems to identify and respond to their specific risks. This environmental scan aims to map and evaluate existing tools and frameworks used to assess climate-related health vulnerabilities in older adults, with a focus on informing equitable healthcare planning and system resilience.Research Design and MethodsFive electronic databases (ProQuest, Scopus, PubMed, CINAHL, Embase) and grey literature were searched. The PRISMA checklist was utilised to guide the review process and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool was used to appraise the studies. A total of 23 studies were identified and included in this review.ResultsOf the 23 identified tools and frameworks, only two were explicitly designed for older adult populations and predominantly focused on heat-related risk assessments, accounting for 74% of climate hazards addressed. Most tools relied on general population indicators, potentially under-estimating age-specific vulnerability related to functional decline, care dependency, and social isolation. Studies were concentrated in East Asia and the Pacific and North America, with no representation from South Asia or the Middle East and North Africa, highlighting significant regional gaps. The majority of studies remain anchored in pre-2021 IPCC vulnerability models with limited update of newer risk-based frameworks.Discussion and ImplicationsThis environmental scan highlights gaps for age-specific vulnerability assessment tools that capture the unique climate-risks facing older adults. Future work should explicitly adopt and clarify IPCC frameworks with standardised domain classifications, and integrate geriatric-specific indicators to improve risk assessment accuracy for this growing demographic.

Introduction

Climate change is driving a global health threat that disproportionately affects sub-populations, exacerbating existing health inequities and increase healthcare system burden. 1 These climate impacts can directly or indirectly affect our health. Direct pathways, for example, include injury and mortality from heatwaves, storms, wildfires, floods, and droughts, while indirect pathways disrupt human systems and ecosystems, therefore compounding adverse health effects.2,3 It is estimated that climate change will cause an additional 250,000 fatalities each year between 2030 and 2050, due to food shortages, insect-borne diseases, and heat-related illnesses alone. 4 However, some population groups face greater health risks from climate hazards than others due to intersecting environmental, biological, and social factors that shape differential exposures and adaptive capacity. 5

Older adults are one population group that is more susceptible to and has a higher risk of adverse health effects from the impacts of climate change. 6 This is due to a number of factors that includes physiological age-related changes as well as socioeconomic, demographic, and contextual factors.7-9 Physiologically, ageing is accompanied by impaired thermoregulation due to reduced sweat gland density and diminished cardiovascular response, decreased renal reserve limiting the ability to maintain fluid balance during heat exposure, and leading to higher rates of multimorbidity and polypharmacy that further compromise adaptive physiology. These biological changes interact with climate hazards in ways that confer disproportionately greater risk to the older population.8,9 However, the continued rise in global life expectancy, coupled with decreasing fertility rates and momentum of past growth, is causing global populations to rapidly age. 10 By 2050 the proportion of people aged 60 years and over is predicted to double. 11 Consequently, a growing ageing population will be exposed to heightened health risks of climate change. This view is consistent with the findings of the 2020 Lancet Countdown Report, which argues that there have been an additional 475 million exposure events to heatwaves in 2019 due to ageing populations combined with increasing heatwave events. 12 Furthermore, the report found that the previous two decades saw a near twofold increase in heat-related deaths worldwide among people aged 65 and older, with 296,000 fatalities in 2018 alone.

Despite the rising risks, health systems lack targeted tools and frameworks to assess and respond to older adults’ climate-related vulnerabilities. Health vulnerability and adaptation assessments can provide an understanding of climate change-related health risks to inform adaptation strategies and responses.4,13 These assessment tools can be applied at the national level (e.g., Climate and Health Country Profiles developed by the WHO) or at the local level (e.g., local vulnerability assessment developed by Simcoe Muskoka District Health Unit, Central Ontario, Canada).14,15 The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)and WHO have been influential in the development of guidance and frameworks used to understand the climate change risks to health. 16 Early vulnerability frameworks developed by the IPCC17,18 conceptualised vulnerability as a function of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity to climate change impacts. In more recent IPCC reports (2023), vulnerability has been reconceptualised to be one of three components—alongside exposure and hazards—which, when combined, result in the risk of adverse events and consequences. This transitions the framework from being vulnerability-focused to risk-focused, therefore acknowledging that many impacts are triggered by hazardous events. 19 The revised framework closely aligns with disaster risk reduction research (such as Crichton’s Risk Triangle) and is intended to encourage research that aims to understand the likelihood of adverse consequences, whilst emphasising the importance of exposure and vulnerability. 19 However, the revised framework has been poorly adopted, possibly due to little discussion on how to practically operationalise the theory. 20

This environmental scan aims to identify and synthesise existing tools and frameworks to assess climate-related health vulnerabilities among older adults, highlighting common domains and gaps to inform equitable health service planning and delivery. Technical guidance on how to conduct an assessment was first published by the WHO in 2003, following this numerous other guidelines and toolkits have been developed.13,16,21 The most recent WHO guidance published in 2021 17 details a six-step process, which includes: a planning stage to establish the project boundaries and team; a vulnerability assessment to identify climate-sensitive health outcomes, climate risks, and vulnerable populations; a capacity assessment of the healthcare system and relevant services; an assessment of future risks; an adaptation assessment to identify relevant policies, programmes, and activities; and finally synthesising the information into appropriate forms for input into policies, planning, and reporting. 4 Following the re-conceptualisation, the IPCC define vulnerability as “the propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected,” additionally emphasising that vulnerability “encompasses a variety of concepts and elements, including sensitivity or susceptibility to harm and lack of capacity to cope and adapt” (2022, p.43). Yet despite the increase uptake of vulnerability assessment, a preliminary literature review revealed that there has not been a study to systematically examine common domains and indicators of health vulnerability assessment tools and methods specifically for older adults.

Method

This environmental scan was guided by the IPCC AR6 vulnerability-focused framing 22 , where vulnerability comprises sensitivity and adaptive capacity, distinct from exposure (presence in hazard-affected areas) and hazards (climate-related events). Domains were operationally defined as follows.• Population Climate Sensitivity: Indicators of susceptibility to harm (e.g., age ≥65 years (or defined by the study), pre-existing conditions, low income)• Adaptive Capacity: Resources mitigating impacts (e.g., healthcare access, air conditioning ownership, social capital)• Exposure: Spatial/temporal proximity the distribution of climate hazards (e.g., population density in heat-vulnerable areas, urban heat island zones)• Hazards: Climate events and environmental stressors (e.g., extreme heat, flooding, air quality)• Climate Impacts: Health outcomes and system effects (e.g., heat stress hospitalisations, mortality)

When indicators spanned multiple plausible domains (e.g., urban heat island as either exposure or hazard; healthcare access as either adaptive capacity or sensitivity), we retained the domain assigned by the original study and discussed any ambiguities among all authors. Indicators like the urban heat island (UHI) effect illustrate domain overlapping challenges. Zhu & Yuan (2023) classified UHI as “exposure” (older adults in high-density heat-trapping areas), while Bernhardt et al. (2023) treated it as a “hazard” (built environment amplifying temperatures). Our extraction protocol followed the primary study’s classification, revealing 12/23 tools with multi-domain indicators.

This environmental scan process was guided and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.23-25 The review protocol is registered with PROSPERO: CRD42024570715.

Search Strategy

The literature search concentrated on identifying all primary studies that either developed or utilised tools and frameworks designed to assess vulnerabilities resulting from climate-related changes. The following databases were accessed through Griffith University Library to gather relevant studies: ProQuest, Scopus, PubMed, CINAHL, and Embase. For the grey literature search, the WHO, IPCC, and government documents were manually searched and reviewed in March to April 2024. The keyword combination (“health vulnerability assessment tool*” AND “climate change” AND “elderly population*”) and the complete list of alternative terms used in literature search is provided in Appendix 1. All search outcomes were exported to Covidence, where duplicates were eliminated through both automated and manual processes. Results were then screened against the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Selection Criteria

In the first selection stage, the title and abstract of each article were reviewed and assessed. Papers were excluded if they: (a) did not cover health vulnerability assessment in the context of climate change; (b) did not mention, use, develop, or apply any assessment tool or framework for vulnerability assessment related to climate events; (c) focused on disaster-related impacts other than climate change; (d) addressed issues other than human health; (e) could not be retrieved through Griffith University Library databases; (f) were published before January 2000; (g) were published in languages other than English. A full-text review of selected papers was conducted in the second selection stage.

In the second selection stage, full-text papers were included if they: (a) involved health vulnerability assessment tools or frameworks in the context of climate change; (b) used primary or secondary data based on census or population databases to guide their analysis; (c) focused on population health or employed a human health perspective; (d) were peer-reviewed journal article or official reports.

Quality Analysis

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist was used to comprehensively appraise the quality of the included studies, as well as evaluate the relevance of each. The selected studies had employed various study designs, including quantitative research (such as cross-sectional studies), qualitative research, mixed methods, and literature reviews. A score of 1 was assigned for each “Yes” response, and a score of 0 was assigned for a “No”, “unclear”, or “Not applicable” response in the checklist. The total score of ≥5 was used as the cut-off to determine the quality of study for inclusion.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

The focus of the data extraction was to review and collect key domains and indicators used in health vulnerability assessment across identified studies. The collected data were inserted into the spreadsheet with the following columns: A) Author (publication year); B) Study region or country where the assessment was carried out; C) Target population, whether it was developed in the general population or specifically for the older adult population D) Study design/data collection method (e.g., primary or secondary data) E) Tool Name F) Tool type (e.g., vulnerability mapping, or conceptual framework for vulnerability assessment and adaptation) G) Climatic Hazards Covered (whether it is specific to heatwaves, floods, or non-specific to particular climatic events) H) Key Domains/Indicators) Health issues/risks (what were the health issues considered during the assessment?) J)Output/s or the assessment approach (e.g., vulnerability map, hot-spot analysis, recommendations for adaptation).

Due to the unavailability of original data, quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible; furthermore, it was outside the scope of the research objectives. Therefore, an environmental scan focusing on a narrative systematic review was employed to present the findings. Common domains and components from each tool were treated as emerging themes, which were further discussed. Indicator frequency was used as a descriptive metric to identify patterns in how vulnerability is operationalised across tools, rather than as a measure of the relative importance or causal strength of individual indicators. Higher frequency reflects prevailing methodological conventions and the availability of routinely collected data particularly census-derived demographic and socioeconomic variables rather than normative prioritisation. As a result, indicators that are clinically relevant but less systematically captured in population datasets may be under-represented.

Results

Initially, 1753 publications were found from five electronic databases, with 1740 comprising scientific literature, 6 grey literatures, and 7 from citation searches. All the retrieved references were imported into Covidence, where 97 duplicates were removed. For the remaining 1,656 studies, title and abstract screening was carried out. Out of 1619 irrelevant papers, 1172 were eliminated during title screening because they focused on disease outcomes such as air quality impacts or specific clinical conditions, rather than on the tools, methods, or frameworks used to assess vulnerability. While infection and disease burdens are relevant climate-health concerns, this scan specifically sought assessment processes and instruments rather than epidemiological studies targeting specific health outcomes. Furthermore, 447 articles were excluded from the abstract screening because there was absence of a health vulnerability assessment tool or framework. In the second selection phase, 37 papers were retrieved for full-text review. Out of these, 14 studies were found to be irrelevant. 7 studies were excluded for addressing climate change impacts in general without explaining vulnerability domains and indicators. 4 studies were removed because they focused on disaster-related health risks without discussing health vulnerability domains and indicators. One study was excluded for concentrating on country-level progress of addressing climate change issues. One duplicate study was removed and one study was excluded for validating past tools. Quantitative Synthesis and Mapping of Studies

Most of the selected studies (n= 15) were secondary data analysis studies, five were frameworks, one was a mixed-methods study, and one was a case study. There was a variety of methods employed to select the indicators used to evaluate health vulnerability to climate change. The most common approach included an initial literature review that identified various components for vulnerability assessment and expert opinion. The studies were found in five out of seven world regions, as designated by the World Bank (2024) 26 : East Asia and Pacific (n= 7; 30.5%), North America (n= 7; 30.5%), Europe and Central Asia (n= 3; 13%), Latin America and the Caribbean (n= 2; 8.5%), and Sub-Saharan Africa (n= 1; 4.5%). Therefore, no studies were reported in the regions of South Asia or the Middle East and North Africa. A total of three studies (13%) were non-location specific and are treated as universal frameworks in the regional analysis to prevent distortion of geographic representation. The majority of studies were conducted in high-income countries (n= 15; 65%), while upper-middle-income countries were reflected in 17.5% of studies (n= 4), and lower-middle-income countries were reflected in 4.5% of studies (n= 1). One study broadly researched Europe, but did not specify which countries. Therefore, in addition to the previous three studies with no specific location, income status could not be determined in a total of four studies (17.5%). Data-driven tools and indices for assessing climate-related health vulnerability identified in the environmental scan

The majority of studies addressed a specific climate hazard (n = 20; 87%). Heat-related impacts were the most frequently addressed issue (n = 17; 74%), followed by studies assessing flood-related risks and diseases associated with water (n = 3; 13%). Three studies, which were all descriptive studies, focused on all related climate risks (13%). In terms of vulnerability assessments for older adults, only two studies (9%) specifically focused on evaluating vulnerabilities within this age group, while the majority of studies (n = 21; 91%) considered vulnerabilities across all age groups, including older adults.

The indictors and related measurements used in all the studies were identified then grouped into related codes and domains. The major domains identified in this environmental scan include: population and sector climate sensitivity; action, responses, and measures to climate change; adaptive capacity; climate impacts; exposure; and hazards. These domains are crucial for understanding and assessing the multifaceted nature of climate change related vulnerability assessments. Demographic Sensitivity

The IPCC (2022) defines vulnerability as “the propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected” (p.43), which includes how sensitive or susceptible different elements are to harm. In this study, population climate sensitivity encompasses elements that affect the extent to which a population is susceptible to harm due to climate change.

Demographic indicators were frequently used, with age, especially the youngest (0—14 years) and oldest age groups (≥55 years, ≥60 years, and ≥65 years) appearing most often. Other demographic-related indicators included household composition (living along, single-parent households, no children, and marital status). Older adults (≥65 years) living alone were coded independently, as this indicator intersected both age and household composition categories and was frequently used. Additional indicators in this category included citizenship status (foreign born); ethnicity and race (non-white, black, racial minority groups, people of colour, Hispanic, and ethnic groups); Indigenous status; migration status; and people with limited English. Indicators for gender and sex were not frequently used.

Health Sensitivity

Health indicators related to climate sensitivity included having a disability (non-specific disability or receiving welfare for a disability); being pregnant or breastfeeding; taking medication; and being dependent on care or living in residential care. Having a pre-existing health or mental health condition was a frequently used indicator, categorised as non-specific or specific. Specific pre-existing health conditions included respiratory diseases, obesity, diabetes, asthma, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, renal diseases, immunocompromised, and arthritis. Indicators relating to health behaviours in this domain included substance use, nutrition, and health literacy.

Housing and Socioeconomic Sensitivity

Population sensitivity indicators relating to the social determinates of health included housing-related indicators (housing quality, rental accommodation, overcrowding, homelessness, public housing, and housing insecurity); employment (unemployment and occupational exposure); socioeconomic status indicators (poverty, low income, receiving welfare, and low socioeconomic status); material conditions (access to communication technologies, vehicle ownership, access to transport, access to air conditioning, and land ownership); and education (completed primary or secondary levels of education, illiterate, high drop-out rate, and barriers to accessing education).

Community Sensitivity

Indicators at the interpersonal level of the community and social environment included social cohesion (neighbourhood stability, discrimination, and crime); social connection (social isolation and new residents); and gender norms.

Population climate sensitivity data were often obtained from censuses (e.g., China’s Sixth National Census) and national surveys (e.g., the Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System in the United States).

Sector Climate Sensitivity

Similar to the previous domain, sector climate sensitivity describes elements related to the sensitivity and susceptibility of harm to economic sectors due to climate change. This study found that industries sensitive to climate change included transport, energy, water, and agricultural sectors. The majority of indicators were from theoretical frameworks; however, the tool by Wright et al. (2023) included indicators relating to the water, sanitation, and hygiene sector.

Action, Responses, and Measures to Climate Change

This domain includes strategies to mitigate or adapt to the impacts of climate change. The majority of indicators were top-down governance strategies related to adaptation. The most frequent indicators included disease surveillance; programme planning, monitoring, and evaluation; educational programs for individuals, communities, first responders, and health professionals; national strategies; and laws and regulations, e.g., minimum building standards. Additionally, some indicators related to political stability, including political participation measured using voter turnout. Nearly all the indicators in this domain were from frameworks.

Adaptive Capacity

Aligned with the IPCC definition, adaptive capacity describes the ability to adjust to the impacts of climate change (2022). This domain includes assets that can be utilised to support adaptation during change. 28

Healthcare System

Geographical proximity to health facilities was most commonly used in secondary data analysis as a measurement denoting access to health care. Additional secondary data measurements included the number of hospital beds and physicians per inhabitant; and coverage of the population either with or without health insurance. Studies also considered the availability of medical supplies and the capacity of the health workforce as factors relating to the healthcare system. Health system capacity can be assessed through involving key stakeholders and utilising available hospital data.

Measures in Addressing the Social Determinants of Health

High income and tertiary education, improved housing quality, and access to transport and communication technology were considered indicators of adaptive capacity. These data were obtained through national surveys (e.g., American Community Survey) and censuses (e.g., Instituto Nacional de Estadística de Chile).

Improving the Community and Social Environment

Local knowledge systems, household resources, and social capital were indicators relating to the community and social environment. These were all from theoretical frameworks.

Built Environment

The presence or proximity to green and blue spaces (normalised difference vegetation index, green vegetation, enhanced vegetation index, pervious surfaces, green vegetation fraction) were considered to increase adaptive capacity. Data to measure green spaces were often from satellite imagery, such as Landsat data or from the Terra Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS). Blue spaces were measured using data from the United States Geological Survey and Globeland 30 dataset. Other indicators of adaptive capacity within the built environment included public spaces and recreational areas (swimming pools, schools, libraries, malls, cooling centres), the presence of cooling infrastructure (shade, ventilation, and air cooling), and critical infrastructure (relating to water, transport, energy, and technology sectors).

Exposure

The exposure domain (distinct from hazards per IPCC AR6) includes indicators that measure the presence of people, livelihoods, and human and natural resources, which are in areas that could be adversely impact by climate change (IPCC, 2022). The dynamics and distribution of populations were measured using population density, distributions of ages, indicators of rurality, human mobility, and population change. One study 29 used geotagging of Tweets to gauge human activity in an area. However, the use of geotagged social media data introduces a systematic bias as adults aged 85 and over, who represent the fastest-growing and most climate-vulnerable age group, are the least likely to use smartphones and social media platforms. Land use and land cover were measured using various classifications and indices (e.g., the Simpson index for land use diversity), building density, and building height. To measure terrain indexes, studies used digital elevation model data. Areas favourable for vector habitats was also included in this domain.

Climate Impact

Climate impacts describe the impacts of climate change and climate variability on the natural environment and human systems. Framework studies presented a range of theoretical health-related indicators (vector, water, food, and air-borne diseases, heat stress, parasitic diseases, cardiovascular diseases, dehydration, morbidity, mortality, and issues relating to maternal and child health). While other studies measured heat stress (previous episodes, symptoms of heat stress, and emergency department visits for heat stress); mortality (summer death rate); hospital visits; dengue rates; and the optimum temperature threshold. Climate variability indicators included those relating to temperature (land surface temperature, anthropogenic heat, temperature, wet bulb globe temperature, climate variability, air temperature) and rainfall variability. Data were obtained from the National Environment Agency weather station, Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM), and remotely sensed data from Landsat and MODIS. Framework studies presented indicators relating to climate-driven impacts on human systems (food insecurity, water stress, socioeconomic impacts, and impacts to infrastructure).

Hazard

In this study, the “hazards” domain (as per IPCC AR6: physical climate events) includes extreme weather events (extreme heat, floods, heavy rain, storms and cyclones., drought, bushfires, and multi-hazards events); air quality (ambient air quality, air pollution, ozone level, and aeroallergens); ground ozone levels; and environmental stressors. Human-induced events related to the urban heat island effect and its drivers (sky view, impervious surfaces) (IPCC, 2022).

A common output of the assessment tools or frameworks from the included studies was to identify at-risk populations (including older adults) and determine the areas of their vulnerability through spatial mapping and high-risk zones.

Discussion

This environmental scan examined the included tools while documenting how the primary studies operationalised exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity, hazards, and climate impacts. Although the newer IPCC risk-focused models (hazards × exposure × vulnerability = risk) offer advantages for multi-hazard analysis, most included tools remain anchored in pre-2021 vulnerability conceptualisations. This scan highlights the diversity and complexity of tools and frameworks used to assess climate-related health vulnerability in older populations.

A central finding of this review is that climate-health vulnerability tools rarely adopt an older adult specific lens, only two studies explicitly designed around ageing populations. As a result, relying on general population tools can systematically under-estimate older adults’ vulnerability, as these tools often assume uniform physiological resilience, independent functioning, and equal capacity to access resources. For example, sweating onset occurs at higher temperatures in older adults, so standard heat indices may underestimate their heat risk. More broadly, vulnerability in later life is shaped by age-related functional decline, higher prevalence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy, dependence on caregivers and formal services, and heightened sensitivity to social isolation. These interacting factors are not adequately captured by population-level socioeconomic or demographic proxies alone.

The comprehensive lists of indicators from the sensitivity, exposure, adaptive capacity, hazards, impacts, and action domains can help identify potential vulnerability to climate change and suggest how planning can be strengthened to mitigate them. While multiple tools have been adapted to suit specific geographic or data contexts,30-32 their scope and application vary widely. Several focus narrowly on a single hazard such as heatwaves or floods; while others attempt to capture multi-hazard vulnerability. However, the predominance of single-hazard approaches limits the ability to understand the compounded risks older adults face in increasingly volatile climate conditions. 33

Exposure, Hazards, and Climate Impacts in Climate Health Vulnerability Assessments

Indicators such as the urban heat island (UHI) effect illustrate the complexity of assessing climate health vulnerability, as they can span multiple domains. UHI can be perceived as exposure due to the high density of people and infrastructure in urban environments. However, it can also be interpreted as a hazard, because the locally elevated temperatures can be attributed to built environment, which amplify human-induced heat events. Furthermore, we found that the diversity of methods and indicators used to measure UHI highlighted a lack of standardisation among vulnerability assessments, which complicated comparisons across studies. For example, Zhu and Yuan (2023) calculated UHI using the sky view factor and temperature changes created by air conditioning, therefore incorporating anthropogenic heat emission in their analysis to inform stakeholders of neighbourhood-level health risks to older adults. In comparison, Bernhardt et al. (2023) developed a simple screening tool, intended to be utilised by health professionals to screen for heat-related illness. In this screening tool, the UHI is defined as an area that has limited tree cover and green space, and includes the presence of impervious surfaces. These examples highlight the need for fit-for-purpose tools and indicators that are tailored to the objectives of the vulnerability assessment.

Climate-driven health outcomes provide empirical evidence of observed impacts and can be used to validate vulnerability assessment tools.34,35 Validation approaches differed between data-driven and theoretical studies. Five of the data-driven studies included validation using the following indicators to test the relationship between climate variables and health outcomes: emergency department visits for heat stress36,37; summer death rate 38 ; monthly hospital visits 39 ; and dengue rate. 40 In comparison, framework studies presented a broader range of theoretical indicators.

This highlights a mismatch between data-driven and framework studies, which could be due to privacy regulations restricting access to health data, therefore constraining validation. 35 Furthermore, the emphasis on heat-related validation indicators reflects the dominant focus on single hazards in climate vulnerability assessments, potentially overlooking older adults, who may experience compounded risks due to increased risk of disease, pre-existing health conditions, and reduced capacity to respond to climate stressors.41,42

A further methodological concern is the reliance on land surface temperature (LST) as the primary heat metric in many included tools. LST reflects thermal radiation from surfaces and can substantially overestimate or misrepresent the physiological heat load experienced by individuals, particularly older adults with impaired thermoregulatory capacity. Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT), which integrates ambient temperature, humidity, wind, and radiation, more closely reflects perceived heat stress and aligns better with clinical thresholds for heat illness. Validation using emergency department visits captures only overt clinical crises. The cumulative toll of sub-threshold heat exposure including social withdrawal, reduced mobility, dehydration, and worsening chronic conditions, remains invisible to outcome-based validation approaches.

A number of studies included indicators of population density and distribution,36,38-40,43-46 two studies specifically included population density of older adults.47,48Age-specific population data can inform the design and implementation of climate strategies aimed at reducing vulnerability in older adults, such as those related to public health policies, housing, and urban planning.47,49 Christenson et al. (2017) and Foroutan et al. (2024) highlighted that older adults who live alone and/or in remote regions face increased vulnerability due to limited healthcare access and transportation, making it less likely for them to receive assistance when needed. Zhu and Yuan (2023) evaluated high-risk zones by considering the proportion and density of older adult populations. The presence of higher proportions of older adults and reduced healthcare accessibility in non-urban areas suggests the need for risk mitigation plans or interventions that specifically target older adults in these regions.50,51

Dimensions and Determinants of Population Sensitivity

Population sensitivity indicators primarily focused on the individual, with indicators relating to individual health, social determinants of health, and demographic factors. Health-related factors were included in all tools. Disability and pre-existing health conditions were common indicators; health conditions were often specified (e.g., diabetes, asthma), while disabilities were not. As previous research has indicated, future vulnerability assessments should explore how specific disabilities are impacted by climate change, rather than reporting people with disability as one homogenous group. 52 Méndez-Lázaro et al. (2018) and Wright et al. (2023) explain that existing health issues (e.g., limited mobility, and weakened immune systems) make older adults more susceptible to the adverse effects from climate change. However, Lindsay et al. (2023), and Connon and Hall (2021) found that disabling environments, such as social, physical, and structural limitations, were the driving causes of vulnerability during extreme weather events for people with disabilities and pre-existing health conditions, rather than individual impairments. Additionally, our study found that those dependent on care, including residing in residential aged care, 36 and taking medication were reported in this category. Four studies incorporated health behavioural indicators, such as substance and alcohol use, poor nutrition and undernourishment in their assessments,4,32,36,53,54 which are crucial in determining both the recovery from and vulnerability to the effects of climate events. 55

Socioeconomic Sensitivity indicators related to education; housing and homelessness; income, economic status, and poverty; material conditions; and occupation and employment. Poverty, low education, poor housing quality, unemployment, and not having access to communication technologies (e.g., internet and phone) were the most frequently used indicators of sensitivity. The intersection of the social determinants of health compounded an individual’s vulnerability.30,37,38,40,56,57 Poverty was often cited as a barrier to accessing air conditioning36,37,44,54,56,58; as well as associated with an increased risk or mortality and hospital admission.46,56,57 Older adults need to be specifically included in climate adaptation strategies, as they experience increased physiological sensitivity to temperature 59 and age is one of the significant predictors of digital inclusion. 60 These factors can increase older adults’ health risks during heatwaves and limit their access to early warnings during extreme weather events.59,61,62

Demographic sensitivity included age; race and ethnicity; household composition; migration status; language spoken; indigenous status; citizenship; and gender and sex. A particularly notable gap is the near-absence of sex- and gender-disaggregated vulnerability indicators. Older women are disproportionately affected by climate hazards: they have longer life expectancy, higher rates of social isolation, lower incomes and pension coverage, and are more likely to reside in residential aged-care. These factors amplify their climate vulnerability. Future tools should explicitly stratify assessment by sex and incorporate gender norms as structural determinants of adaptive capacity.

Regarding age, older and younger age groups were considered to be the most sensitive to the health impacts of climate change. Older adults were mostly defined as people aged 65 years and above; however, other categories included adults aged over 85 years, 36 60 years,39,57 and 55 years. 30 Higher rates of morbidity and mortality in non-white racial minority populations during climate-related events were cited as the justification for selecting indicators relating to race and ethnicity.36,37,44,54,56,58 Two of these studies highlighted the underlying structural factors that contribute to this disparity, including racialised residential segregation, known as redlining, 54 racism, and a lack of political power. 44 While these studies identify key population groups and factors associated with vulnerability, they often stop short of examining the underlying causes of that vulnerability. For example, in the US Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino populations disproportionately reside in areas with less tree canopy, limited access to cooling strategies, and higher exposure to urban heat islands and exposure to pollutants, due to historical redlining and systemic disinvestment infrastructure. 63 While several included studies highlighted racial disparities in climate-related health outcomes particularly within the US context, these patterns reflect broader upstream structural processes that operate globally.

Marginalised Indigenous communities in the Global North, for example, often experience heightened exposure to climate hazards due to historical dispossession, underinvestment in infrastructure, and limited access to healthcare and decision-making processes. Similarly, in many parts of South Asia, caste-based social stratification and informal urban settlement patterns shape differential exposure to heat, flooding, and environmental stressors, while constraining adaptive capacity through restricted access to housing, services, and social protection (Cissé et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). Recognising these structural determinants underscores the need for vulnerability assessments to move beyond individual-level indicators and explicitly account for governance, discrimination, and historically entrenched inequalities across diverse global settings.

Future vulnerability assessments should therefore move beyond individual-level factors to include upstream, structural determinants, such as governance, policy, and discrimination. 7

Adaptive Capacity and Action as Critical Domains in Vulnerability Assessment

The adaptive capacity domain plays an equally significant role in climate-health vulnerability assessments, alongside the other domains in evaluating overall vulnerability. However, we found there to be a conceptual overlap at times with indicators relating to the social determinants of health in the population sensitivity domain. For example, low education attainment was considered to increase sensitivity; whereas, higher education was a factor of adaptive capacity. A similar relationship could be said for indicators within the health system: where barriers and facilitators to accessing health care can either support adaptive capacity or diminish it. Likewise, the presence or absence of public, recreational, and cooling spaces (e.g., swimming pools, libraries, malls, shade, and cooling centres), critical infrastructure (e.g., transport, energy, and water), and green and blue spaces can either enhance or limit adaptive capacity. Access to these factors contribute to the overall resilience of population, as effective utilisation of these resources enables people to protect their health during climate-related events.

To reduce conceptual ambiguity and avoid double-counting, future climate–health vulnerability assessments would benefit from adopting a clear conceptual roadmap. One practical approach is to explicitly align indicator selection with the IPCC AR6 risk framework, while treating exposure and hazards as analytically distinct from vulnerability (Estoque et al., 2023). Within this structure, indicators commonly associated with both sensitivity and adaptive capacity such as education, income, housing quality, and access to healthcare can be coherently organised under a Social Determinants of Health (SDH) lens (Li et al., 2023). Under this approach, SDH indicators are interpreted according to their functional role in the assessment: factors that increase susceptibility to harm are treated as sensitivity, whereas those enabling anticipation, coping, or recovery are treated as adaptive capacity. Making this distinction explicit at the design stage can improve transparency, reduce conceptual overlap, and enhance comparability across studies.

With populations worldwide shifting toward older age structures, the strategies and action taken to adapt to the health impacts of climate change are of increasing importance. Wilhelmi and Hayden (2010) described that neglecting adaptive capacity in vulnerability assessments could lead to inaccurate findings. A critical and consistently overlooked vulnerability is polypharmacy. Older adults frequently take multiple medications that independently or synergistically impair climate-related physiological adaptation.64,65 Diuretics reduce plasma volume and increase susceptibility to dehydration during heat events. 66 Antihypertensives (particularly beta-blockers) blunt the cardiovascular response to heat stress by limiting cardiac output and heart rate elevation. Anticholinergic agents (including many antihistamines and tricyclic antidepressants) impair sweating and psychotropic medications can reduce thirst perception and impair behavioural thermoregulation. 67 Despite this well-established pharmacological evidence, none of the 23 tools included in this scan incorporated medication use as a specific pharmacokinetic vulnerability indicator reflecting a significant gap between clinical geriatric knowledge and public health vulnerability assessment practice.

A notable gap in the reviewed literature is the absence of governance-related indicators in most tools, except for those designed as guidance frameworks. This omission is significant, as governance plays a critical role in shaping health system responses, resource allocation, and community resilience. Furthermore, while adaptation is a central theme in many tools, there is a need for greater emphasis on mitigation strategies that address root causes and reduce long-term exposure to climate risks.

Actions, responses, and measures to climate change primarily focussed on top-down strategies (e.g., laws, regulations, and national strategies); local strategies were also considered, albeit less frequently. Future vulnerability assessments should give greater attention to indicators relating to the influence of local governments, as this level of governance can be pivotal to the success of climate adaptation strategies. 68 Furthermore, Mason et al. (2021) argue that decision-making and effective leadership are crucial elements before, during, and following a climatic event. Therefore, the inclusion of diverse population groups including older adults in decision-making processes is vital for addressing their needs in emergencies. Failure to involve these groups in decision-making can heighten their vulnerability, leading to potential inadequacies in health policy and development and planning. Beyond governance, neighbourhood-scale social infrastructure warrants greater attention as a determinant of adaptive capacity for older adults. Key elements include the density and accessibility of age-friendly community programmes, the strength of informal social networks and caregiver availability, neighbourhood walkability, access to cooling centres, and community-level social cohesion. These proximal, place-based factors are often more actionable for local governments and more directly experienced by older adults than national-level government indicators, yet they remain largely absent from current vulnerability frameworks.69,70

Access to green and blue spaces provides a range of physical and mental health benefits, which can support human health and wellbeing during climate-related events. 71 We found several studies that quantified the density and health of vegetation and blue space through satellite imagery.29,38,39,43,46,57,58 The use of satellite imagery is a valuable tool for understanding how vegetation impacts the adaptive capacity of vulnerable communities and offers insights into areas where vegetation can reduce vulnerability (e.g., decrease heat exposure by lowering surface temperatures). This data could be helpful for informing urban planning and mitigation strategies to enhance resilience and reduce heat-related risks. However, satellite-derived vegetation indices may overestimate the adaptive benefits of green space for older adults. A high canopy score does not guarantee that the space is physically accessible. Features such as level pathways, seating, shade, and proximity to public transport determine whether older adults with mobility limitations can actually use these environments. Future tools should incorporate accessibility-weighted green space metrics rather than relying solely on remote sensing indices or examining the relevant features independently.

Another challenge is the diversity of indicators used across tools, which complicates efforts to standardise assessments or compare results across context. Despite this, three domains – exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity serve as a common thread, offering a foundation for tool adaptation and development. These domains are deeply intertwined with individual level factors (e.g., physiological status, health conditions), social determinants of health (e.g., access to care), and broader equity considerations in the context of climate exposures.

Limitations

Heterogeneity in study designs, the databases used for assessment tools, and geographical focus poses significant challenges to identify common findings (e.g., domains and indicators) or draw generalised conclusions across the included studies. In addition, limiting inclusion of English-language publications after 2000 may have limited our findings. Only two studies explicitly focused on older adults, and most of the studies used historical data, which may not fully capture the rapidly changing dynamics of climate change impacts and related topics. The majority of low-income countries lack comprehensive census data, which may create information gaps when synthesising universal vulnerability assessment tools.

Survivor bias in historical datasets poses an additional challenge: individuals who experienced severe climate events, survived and remained in the community were more likely to be captured in longitudinal records, while the most vulnerable who died, were institutionalised, or were never registered in the health systems, are systematically absent. This may lead to an underestimation of risk in historical vulnerability assessments. Furthermore, the increasing use of digital data sources including electronic health records, social media, and smartphone-derived mobility data introduces structural bias concerning the oldest-old (aged ≥85 years), who have the lowest rates of digital participation. Vulnerability assessments relying on these sources excluding rapid growing and most at-risk older adults from risk models. Political and economic determinants, as well as governance, were also understudied, and several measures relied on simple count-based indicators, which may fail to capture capture underlying vulnerability and contextual complexity.

Conclusion

After comparing and synthesising the 23 tools and frameworks, we identified the common domains, along with indicators across various geographical areas applicable for assessing climate vulnerabilities among older populations. The integration of multiple indicators such as race, ethnicity, age, existing health conditions, and living alone can enhance understanding of climate change impacts on health. However, there is a need to consider health system capacity and the involvement of diverse population groups, including older adults in adaptation planning, which may have a positive impact throughout the vulnerability evaluation process.

The scarcity of older-adult-specific tools means that current vulnerability assessments likely under-estimate climate risks for ageing populations, particularly by failing to capture functional status, care dependency, social isolation, and medication-related sensitivity. Heat-related impacts have been extensively studied, with numerous tools available to assess these risks. Therefore, further research is needed to explore other climate-related impacts, such as floods, droughts, bushfires, and cyclones. Integration of social vulnerability indicators such as race/ethnicity, rural/remote locations and isolation into assessments can enhance the understanding of how the health of the older adult population is differentially impacted by climate change. Many studies may have overlooked the adaptive capacity domain in their analysis. However, Wilhelmi and Hayden (2010) and WHO (2021a) emphasise that adaptive capacity should be evaluated in greater depth, with a focus on health system preparedness, community resources, availability of emergency services, and protective health programmes. It is crucial to involve diverse population groups including older adults in decision-making of emergency response processes to ensure that the needs of vulnerable populations are addressed. To advance equitable climate vulnerability assessments, future tools must integrate these domains with a stronger emphasis on intersectionality and systemic inequities. This includes accounting for how age intersects with other factors such as disability, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, and how these intersections influence health outcomes during climate events. Instead of choosing or developing a single comprehensive tool, future research should consider developing modular vulnerability assessment frameworks that permit hazard-specific weighting of domains such as care dependency, functional status, polypharmacy, and social isolation. For example, a flood vulnerability assessment may weight physical mobility and evacuation capacity most heavily, while a heatwave tool may prioritise thermoregulatory indicators and medication-related risks. This modular approach would enable context-sensitive assessments and move beyond the current single-hazard monoculture that characterises most existing tools.
