RPS6KA3/RSK2-mediated phosphorylation of DRAM2 promotes lysosomal targeting and autophagic flux in melanoma
Authors: Ga-Eun Lee, Soo-Bin Nam, Eunyoung Moon, Yang Hoon Huh, Hye Sun Park, Seungjin Na, Jin Young Kim, Eun Hee Han, Hana Cho, Jung Hoon Choi, Yong-Yeon Cho, Geul Bang, Cheol-Jung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMacroautophagy/autophagy is a critical process for maintaining cellular homeostasis and has emerging implications in cancer biology. DRAM2 (DNA damage regulated autophagy modulator 2), a transmembrane protein enriched at lysosomal membranes, has been implicated in autophagy regulation; however, the upstream mechanisms governing its trafficking and function remain unclear. In this study, we identified RPS6KA3/RSK2, a stress-responsive kinase downstream of the MAPK pathway, as a novel upstream kinase of DRAM2. RPS6KA3/RSK2 interacted with and phosphorylated DRAM2 at Ser263 within its cytosolic tail. This phosphorylation was required for AP3D1/AP-3-dependent trafficking of DRAM2 to the late endosomal-lysosomal pathway, thereby facilitating autolysosome formation and sustaining autophagic flux. In contrast, the non-phosphorylatable DRAM2S263A mutant failed to bind AP3D1/AP-3, exhibited defective lysosomal trafficking, and was partially redistributed toward plasma membrane-proximal compartments, where it enhanced exosome secretion. Bioinformatic analyses revealed a strong positive correlation between RPS6KA3/RSK2 and DRAM2 expression in melanoma tissues, and elevated DRAM2 expression was associated with poor patient prognosis. Depletion of RPS6KA3/RSK2 or DRAM2 impaired autophagic flux and inhibited melanoma cell proliferation. Similarly, expression of the DRAM2S263A mutant suppressed melanoma progression in vitro and in vivo by disrupting autophagy. Moreover, DRAM2 protein levels were elevated in skin cancer tissues compared to normal tissues. Collectively, our findings uncover a phosphorylation-dependent trafficking switch that bifurcates DRAM2 function between autophagy and exosome secretion, and establish the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis as a critical regulator of melanoma progression. Introduction

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved catabolic process that enables eukaryotic cells to degrade and recycle cytoplasmic contents, including damaged organelles and misfolded proteins, via lysosomal degradation [1,2]. A hallmark of this process is the formation of autophagosomes, double-membraned vesicles that sequester intracellular material and fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes [1,3,4]. Lysosomes, as terminal degradative compartments, are thus indispensable for the completion of autophagic flux. This fusion event and subsequent degradation rely on a cohort of lysosomal membrane proteins, such as LAMP1 and LAMP2, which support lysosomal function and membrane dynamics [5,6]. Recent studies have underscored that the localization and activity of these membrane proteins are finely regulated by post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, which can alter their trafficking and thereby impact autophagy efficiency and vesicle sorting [7–9]. These observations highlight the lysosomal membrane not only as a structural boundary but also as a regulatory platform essential for orchestrating autophagic signaling.

DRAM2 (DNA damage regulated autophagy modulator 2) is a six-transmembrane domain protein predominantly localized to the lysosomal membrane [10,11]. Prior studies have implicated DRAM2 in the modulation of autophagic flux [10,11]. Notably, DRAM2 has no known enzymatic activity, and its function is presumed to rely on interactions with other proteins or post-translational modifications [12,13]. It has been reported to facilitate LC3-I to LC3-II conversion and to contribute to autophagic processes, suggesting a role in the regulation of autophagic flux [10,14]. However, how DRAM2 is regulated at the post-translational level, particularly in terms of phosphorylation, membrane trafficking, or sorting, and how such modifications influence its function under physiological or pathological conditions remain unexplored. Given the central role of lysosomal membrane proteins in coordinating autophagy and vesicle trafficking, understanding the upstream regulation of DRAM2 is crucial for elucidating its broader role in membrane dynamics and cell fate decisions.

RPS6KA3/RSK2 is a serine/threonine kinase that functions downstream of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway and plays a central role in diverse cellular processes, including cell growth, survival, and stress responses [15,16]. RPS6KA3/RSK2 has been shown to mediate transcriptional regulation by phosphorylating transcription factors such as CREB and FOS, thereby influencing gene expression programs critical for cell proliferation and differentiation [17,18]. Recent studies have also implicated RPS6KA3/RSK2 in the regulation of autophagy. For instance, RPS6KA3/RSK2 has been reported to enhance autophagic activity through mTOR-independent mechanisms, including phosphorylation of autophagy-related substrates and modulation of lysosomal biogenesis [19]. In the context of cancer, RPS6KA3/RSK2 is frequently overexpressed or activated in melanoma, where it promotes cell proliferation, survival, and resistance to targeted therapies, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target [20–22]. UV-induced activation of RPS6KA3/RSK2 promotes cell survival by protecting against apoptosis and contributes to UV-mediated skin carcinogenesis [23,24]. Conversely, RPS6KA3 knockdown significantly suppresses the proliferation of skin cancer cells and inhibits neoplastic transformation in human skin cells [23]. These oncogenic properties are often linked to its ability to dynamically phosphorylate downstream substrates in response to external stress signals, suggesting that RPS6KA3/RSK2 May serve as a critical signaling hub that integrates autophagy regulation with tumorigenic processes.

Melanoma is a highly aggressive form of skin cancer whose global incidence continues to rise, particularly among younger populations [25]. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, advanced-stage melanoma remains associated with poor prognosis and limited long-term survival [26,27]. Increasing evidence suggests that autophagy plays a context-dependent role in melanoma, functioning as a tumor-suppressive mechanism during early stages but supporting tumor survival and progression in established disease. In particular, melanoma cells rely on autophagy to adapt to microenvironmental stressors such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and therapeutic pressure [28–30]. Given the central role of lysosomes in autophagy, alterations in lysosomal membrane proteins and their trafficking pathways may critically influence melanoma development and treatment response.

Here, we identify a previously unrecognized regulatory mechanism involving the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis, which controls the intracellular trafficking and lysosomal targeting of DRAM2 through site-specific phosphorylation and thereby modulates autophagic flux. Our findings reveal that this phosphorylation-dependent trafficking switch not only governs the functional fate of DRAM2 but also contributes to melanoma cell proliferation and tumor progression through the regulation of autophagy, thereby offering new mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic targets in autophagy-related cancer biology.

Results

RPS6KA3/RSK2 interacts with DRAM2

Although DRAM2 has previously been implicated in the regulation of autophagy, most studies have focused primarily on its transcriptional regulation rather than on its post-translational modifications [10,12]. In contrast, it is well established that phosphorylation of autophagy-related proteins such as BECN1/Beclin-1, LC3B, and SQSTM1/p62 plays a critical role in modulating autophagic activity. These observations led us to hypothesize that DRAM2 May also undergo phosphorylation and that this modification could regulate its role in autophagy. To explore this possibility, we first utilized the NetPhos 3.1 prediction algorithm (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetPhos-3.1/) to identify potential phosphorylation sites across the DRAM2 sequence. This analysis yielded twelve candidate residues with high phosphorylation potential (Table S1). Considering that DRAM2 is a six-transmembrane domain protein and that phosphorylation typically occurs on cytoplasmically exposed regions of membrane proteins, we focused on residues predicted to reside in the cytoplasmic tail or intracellular loops. Based on this topological constraint, three residues (Ser82, Thr245, and Ser263) were shortlisted for further analysis. To predict potential upstream kinases responsible for modifying these residues, we employed GPS 5.0 kinase prediction software. Ser263, located in the C-terminal cytoplasmic tail of DRAM2, was predicted to be a target of AGC kinases, with the highest score-to-cutoff (S:C) ratio (10.915), suggesting it as the most promising site for regulatory phosphorylation. Thr245 was predicted to be phosphorylated by ACVRL1/ALK1, a member of the TKL/STKR kinase family, while Ser82 was predicted to be phosphorylated by CSNK2/CK2 (casein kinase 2), a member of the CMGC family. Given the lysosomal localization of DRAM2 and the known cytosolic activity of AGC kinases, Ser263 emerged as the most likely regulatory phosphorylation site (Table S2). To identify DRAM2-interacting kinases, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS) using an anti-DRAM2 antibody (Figure 1A). Among the proteins co-immunoprecipitating with DRAM2, we identified RPS6KA3/RSK2, a serine/threonine kinase of the AGC family, as a candidate. Notably, ACVRL1/ALK1 and CSNK2/CK2 were not detected in the DRAM2 affinity isolation. To validate the interaction between DRAM2 and RPS6KA3/RSK2, we conducted co-immunoprecipitation assays under both exogenous and endogenous expression conditions. In exogenous assays, HA-tagged RPS6KA3/RSK2 was co-expressed with Flag-tagged DRAM2 in HEK293T cells, and DRAM2 was found to co-immunoprecipitate with RPS6KA3/RSK2 (Figure 1B). Consistent with this result, endogenous co-immunoprecipitation analyses demonstrated that native RPS6KA3/RSK2 interacts with DRAM2 (Figure 1C and Figure S1A, B). The specificity of the DRAM2 antibody was confirmed using DRAM2 knockdown cells (Figure S1C, D). Under identical input conditions, endogenous DRAM2 was readily detectable in HEK293T cells (Figure 1C). Basal DRAM2 expression was also observed across multiple human cancer cell lines (Figure S1E). Because DRAM2 is a multi-pass transmembrane protein, we speculated that RPS6KA3/RSK2 May interact with cytoplasmically exposed regions of DRAM2. To gain structural insight into the potential interaction interface between DRAM2 and RPS6KA3/RSK2, we performed an in silico protein-protein docking analysis using Discovery Studio software. The computational docking model predicted that the cytoplasmic regions of DRAM2 could form multiple stabilizing interactions with RPS6KA3/RSK2, including hydrogen bonds, carbon-hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, sulfur-X interactions, and hydrophobic contacts, with a predicted binding energy of −73.924 kcal/mol (Figure 1D and Table S3). Although this computational model does not define the precise binding interface, it supports the feasibility of a direct interaction between the two proteins. To further explore the regions of DRAM2 potentially involved in this interaction, we attempted to map the binding interface using truncation mutants. However, deletion of TM-containing segments caused severe mislocalization (Figure S1F and G), indicating that such mutants do not retain physiologically relevant conformations for binding analysis. Collectively, these results identify RPS6KA3/RSK2 as a DRAM2-interacting kinase.

RPS6KA3/RSK2 phosphorylates DRAM2 at Ser263

Given that RPS6KA3/RSK2 interacts with DRAM2 and functions as a serine/threonine kinase, we next examined whether DRAM2 could serve as a phosphorylation substrate of RPS6KA3/RSK2. Sequence analysis of DRAM2 revealed a putative RPS6KA3/RSK2 consensus recognition motif (RxxpS/T) spanning residues 258–263 (RTRLLS), with Ser263 as the likely phospho-acceptor site (Figure 2A). Consistently, kinase prediction analysis also indicated Ser263 as a putative phosphorylation site (Figure 1). To test this, we performed in vitro kinase assays using active RPS6KA3/RSK2 and a bacterially expressed DRAM2 CD4 (residues 240–266) fragment containing the Ser263 site. Phosphorylation was detected using antibodies specific to both RxRxxpS/T and RxxpS/T motifs (Figure 2B). To further verify the phosphorylation site, we generated a phospho-deficient DRAM2 mutant (S263A). In vitro kinase assays showed complete loss of phosphorylation in the S263A mutant, confirming Ser263 as the primary phosphorylation site targeted by RPS6KA3/RSK2 (Figure 2C). To evaluate whether DRAM2 phosphorylation occurs in cells under physiologically relevant conditions, we performed Phos-tag SDS-PAGE analysis. Endogenous DRAM2 displayed a detectable mobility shift under basal conditions, and this shift was increased upon RPS6KA3/RSK2 overexpression, indicating enhanced phosphorylation (Figure 2D). Furthermore, UVB irradiation, a physiologically relevant activator of RPS6KA3 signaling, was associated with increased RPS6KA3/RSK2 activation and a corresponding increase in DRAM2 phosphorylation, as reflected by a significant increase in the ratio of phosphorylated to non-phosphorylated DRAM2 (Figure 2E). Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of RPS6KA3/RSK2 using BI-D1870 markedly reduced the phosphorylated DRAM2 band without affecting total DRAM2 levels, confirming that DRAM2 phosphorylation is dependent on RPS6KA3 kinase activity (Figure 2F). To confirm Ser263 phosphorylation at the peptide level, we performed LC-MS/MS analysis of DRAM2 immunoprecipitated from cells co-expressing RPS6KA3/RSK2. The MS spectrum revealed a phospho-modification at Ser263 (Figure 2G,H). Additionally, synthetic phosphopeptides corresponding to the region containing Ser263 (“TRLLpSRDI”) yielded matching MS/MS fragmentation profiles, further validating Ser263 as a bona fide phosphorylation site (Figure S2). Taken together, these results demonstrate that RPS6KA3/RSK2 directly phosphorylates DRAM2 at Ser263 both in vitro and in cellular contexts.

RPS6KA3/RSK2-mediated phosphorylation of DRAM2 governs AP-3-dependent lysosomal trafficking

Membrane proteins are frequently sorted to distinct intracellular destinations through phosphorylation-dependent trafficking mechanisms that regulate their progression through the endosomal system [7,31–34]. Based on this principle, we hypothesized that phosphorylation of DRAM2 at Ser263 by RPS6KA3/RSK2 modulates its intracellular trafficking rather than merely defining its final subcellular localization. To test this hypothesis, we first examined the subcellular distribution of wild-type (WT) DRAM2 and Ser263 mutants by immunofluorescence microscopy. WT DRAM2 showed prominent colocalization with the lysosomal marker LAMP1 and a characteristic perinuclear distribution. In contrast, the phospho-deficient DRAM2S263A mutant exhibited markedly reduced overlap with LAMP1 and appeared more broadly dispersed throughout the cytoplasm, with numerous DRAM2-positive puncta extending toward peripheral and plasma membrane-proximal regions (Figure 3A). We next examined whether these localization differences arise from altered trafficking through the endosomal pathway. Quantitative colocalization analysis revealed that WT DRAM2, the S263A mutant, and the phospho-mimetic S263D mutant exhibited comparable overlap with the early endosomal marker EEA1 (Figure 3B,C), indicating that entry into early endosomes and initial sorting are largely preserved regardless of Ser263 phosphorylation status. In contrast, analysis of colocalization with the late endosomal marker RAB7 revealed a clear phosphorylation-dependent difference: WT DRAM2 displayed substantial overlap with RAB7-positive compartments, this overlap was markedly reduced in cells expressing DRAM2S263A, whereas the S263D mutant exhibited the highest degree of RAB7 colocalization among the three constructs (Figure 3D,E). In contrast, neither WT DRAM2 nor the S263A or S263D mutants showed appreciable colocalization with the recycling endosome marker Rab11, indicating that altered recycling trafficking does not contribute to the observed differences in DRAM2 distribution (Figure S3). These results indicate that phosphorylation at Ser263 promotes efficient progression of DRAM2 from early endosomes into RAB7-positive late endosomal compartments.

Lysosomal membrane proteins are typically sorted to lysosomes through defined cytosolic targeting motifs, including tyrosine-based (YXXØ) and dileucine-based ([E/D]XXXLØ) motifs, in which Ø denotes a hydrophobic residue such as L, I, M, V, or F, and which are recognized by adaptor protein (AP) complexes such as AP-3 [35–37]. Moreover, phosphorylation events adjacent to these motifs are known to modulate adaptor recognition and intracellular trafficking of lysosomal proteins [9,32,35,38]. Sequence analysis of DRAM2 revealed a conserved dileucine motif (ERTRLL) within its C-terminal cytosolic domain, immediately followed by Ser263, the RPS6KA3/RSK2 phosphorylation site, forming the sequence ERTRLLS (Figure 3F). Given that AP3D1/AP-3 recognizes dileucine-based motifs to mediate lysosomal targeting, we hypothesized that phosphorylation at Ser263 regulates the interaction between DRAM2 and AP3D1/AP-3. Consistent with this model, co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated robust association of AP3D1/AP-3 with WT DRAM2, whereas this interaction was markedly reduced with the DRAM2S263A mutant (Figure 3G). This phosphorylation-dependent interaction was further supported by immunofluorescence analysis, which revealed colocalization of endogenous AP3D1/AP-3 with WT DRAM2, but not with DRAM2S263A (Figure 3H). Collectively, these findings indicate that phosphorylation of DRAM2 at Ser263 facilitates AP-3-dependent recognition of a C-terminal dileucine sorting motif, thereby promoting efficient trafficking of DRAM2 into the late endosome-lysosome pathway. Loss of this phosphorylation impairs AP-3 engagement, resulting in defective lysosomal sorting and redistribution of DRAM2-positive puncta toward peripheral compartments and plasma membrane-proximal compartments.

Loss of Ser263 phosphorylation reroutes DRAM2 to the plasma membrane and enhances exosome secretion

Because impaired lysosomal sorting of DRAM2S263A leads to its redistribution toward plasma membrane-proximal compartments, we next focused on the functional consequences of DRAM2 accumulation at the plasma membrane. To directly assess whether loss of Ser263 phosphorylation promotes delivery of DRAM2 to the plasma membrane, we first examined the subcellular distribution of WT DRAM2 and the S263A mutant using plasma membrane labeling with CellBrite 640. While WT DRAM2 showed limited association with the plasma membrane, DRAM2S263A displayed prominent DRAM2-positive puncta localized along plasma membrane-proximal regions (Figure 4A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the number of plasma membrane-associated DRAM2 puncta per cell in the S263A mutant compared with WT DRAM2 (Figure 4B). To further corroborate these observations, we performed immuno-electron microscopy to visualize DRAM2 localization at ultrastructural resolution. In cells expressing WT DRAM2, gold particles were predominantly detected on lysosomal and autolysosomal membranes, with minimal presence at the plasma membrane (Figure 4D and Figure S4A). In contrast, DRAM2S263A exhibited a marked increase in gold particle density at the plasma membrane, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in autophagic vesicle-associated labeling (Figure 4D and Figure S4A). Quantification revealed a significant enrichment of DRAM2 at the plasma membrane and a decrease in autophagic compartment association in the S263A condition (Figure 4E,F), consistent with defective lysosomal targeting. Given the established role of the plasma membrane in extracellular vesicle biogenesis and release, we next examined whether plasma membrane-associated DRAM2 influences exosome secretion. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) demonstrated that cells expressing DRAM2S263A released significantly higher numbers of extracellular vesicles compared with cells expressing WT DRAM2 or vector control (Figure 4G,H). This increase was not attributable to differences in cell number or viability, indicating a specific effect on vesicle secretion (Figure S4B, C). Consistently, immunoblot analysis further confirmed the identity and purity of the isolated exosomes, as evidenced by enrichment of the canonical exosome markers CD63 and HSPA/HSP70 and the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum marker CANX/Calnexin. Notably, while WT DRAM2 was detectable in exosomal fractions, DRAM2S263A was incorporated into exosomes at substantially higher levels (Figure 4I). Collectively, these results indicate that loss of Ser263 phosphorylation diverts a subset of DRAM2 from the lysosomal pathway toward the plasma membrane, where it functionally contributes to enhanced exosome secretion. This finding identifies plasma membrane-associated DRAM2 as a downstream functional consequence of impaired lysosomal sorting caused by defective RPS6KA3/RSK2-mediated phosphorylation.

RPS6KA3/RSK2 is essential for autophagy regulation

Since RPS6KA3/RSK2-mediated phosphorylation of DRAM2 at Ser263 governs its lysosomal targeting, we next investigated whether RPS6KA3/RSK2 is functionally required for autophagy regulation. To this end, we utilized Rps6ka3+/+ and rps6ka3-/- mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) derived from rps6ka3 knockout mice. Western blot analysis revealed that loss of RPS6KA3/RSK2 resulted in a substantial reduction in LC3B-II levels accompanied by an accumulation of SQSTM1/p62 protein, consistent with impaired autophagic degradation (Figure 5A). Reintroduction of RPS6KA3 into knockout MEFs via lentiviral transduction restored LC3B-II levels and reduced SQSTM1/p62 accumulation, confirming a critical role for RPS6KA3/RSK2 in maintaining autophagic activity (Figure 5B). To directly assess autophagic flux, we treated cells with the lysosomal V-ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BafA1). In Rps6ka3+/+ MEFs, BafA1 treatment induced robust accumulation of LC3B-II and SQSTM1/p62, reflecting active autophagic flux. In contrast, rps6ka3-/- MEFs exhibited attenuated LC3B-II accumulation and persistently elevated SQSTM1/p62 levels upon BafA1 treatment, indicating defective autophagic flux at the late degradative stage (Figure 5C). Quantitative analysis further supported a significant impairment of autophagic turnover in the absence of RPS6KA3/RSK2. Consistently, starvation-induced autophagy triggered by Earle’s balanced salt solution (EBSS) led to efficient SQSTM1/p62 degradation in wild-type MEFs but failed to do so in rps6ka3-/- cells (Figure 5D). Moreover, LysoTracker staining revealed that EBSS-induced lysosomal acidification was markedly reduced in rps6ka3-/- MEFs compared to wild-type controls, suggesting compromised lysosomal function upon loss of RPS6KA3/RSK2 (Figure 5E). Collectively, these data demonstrate that RPS6KA3/RSK2 is required for efficient autophagic flux, particularly at the late lysosomal degradation stage, thereby establishing RPS6KA3/RSK2 as a key upstream regulator of autophagy.

RPS6KA3/RSK2 and DRAM2 are co-upregulated in melanoma and correlate with poor prognosis

Our findings that RPS6KA3/RSK2 phosphorylates DRAM2 at Ser263 to promote its lysosomal localization and autophagic function prompted us to investigate the potential oncogenic relevance of this signaling axis in human cancers. Given that autophagy can support tumor survival and progression, we analyzed the expression patterns of RPS6KA3/RSK2 and DRAM2 across various cancer types using the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html). Among all tumor types examined, skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) exhibited one of the highest and most tumor-specific levels of RPS6KA3/RSK2 expression, with significant upregulation in tumors compared to matched normal skin (Figure 6A). We next assessed DRAM2 transcript levels in SKCM and found that both DRAM2 and RPS6KA3 were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues relative to normal skin (Figure 6B). Importantly, correlation analysis revealed a positive association between RPS6KA3 and DRAM2 expression in melanoma patients, suggesting a potential transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulatory link between these two genes (Figure 6C). To assess clinical relevance, we analyzed patient survival data and found that high DRAM2 expression was significantly associated with reduced overall survival in SKCM, consistent with a tumor-promoting role (Figure 6D). At the protein level, western blot of melanoma cell lines demonstrated co-expression of total and phosphorylated RPS6KA3/RSK2 (p-S359/T363) along with DRAM2 (Figure 6E). These findings highlight a melanoma-specific co-expression pattern of RPS6KA3/RSK2 and DRAM2, and support the notion that their coordinated activity promotes melanoma progression by regulating autophagy.

The RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis promotes melanoma cell proliferation by enhancing autophagic flux

To investigate the functional relevance of the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis in melanoma, we employed both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown approaches. Treatment with BI-D1870 markedly suppressed proliferation of A375SM melanoma cells (Figure 7A). In parallel, colony formation assays revealed that BI-D1870 treatment significantly impaired the clonogenic capacity of melanoma cells (Figure 7B). To directly assess whether RPS6KA3/RSK2 regulates autophagic flux in melanoma cells, we next examined autophagy under starvation conditions using EBSS. In control cells, EBSS treatment induced LC3B-II accumulation accompanied by a reduction in SQSTM1/p62 levels, consistent with active autophagic flux. In contrast, depletion of RPS6KA3/RSK2 blunted EBSS-induced LC3B-II accumulation and prevented efficient SQSTM1/p62 turnover, indicating impaired starvation-induced autophagic flux (Figure 7C and Figure S5A). To further validate these findings at the level of autophagosome maturation, we employed a tandem mCherry-EGFP-LC3B reporter assay. Under EBSS treatment, control cells exhibited a pronounced increase in mCherry-only puncta, reflecting efficient autophagosome-lysosome fusion and autolysosome formation. By contrast, RPS6KA3 knockdown significantly reduced the formation of mCherry-only puncta, supporting a defect in autophagic flux downstream of autophagosome formation (Figure S5B). Consistent with these autophagy defects, RPS6KA3 knockdown significantly reduced melanoma cell proliferation (Figure 7D). We next examined the functional contribution of DRAM2, a downstream effector of RPS6KA3/RSK2. Knockdown of DRAM2 in both A375SM and A375P cells significantly suppressed cell proliferation (Figure 7E,F) and abrogated anchorage-independent colony growth in soft agar assays (Figure 7G). These phenotypes closely resembled those observed upon RPS6KA3/RSK2 inhibition, supporting the notion that DRAM2 acts downstream of RPS6KA3/RSK2 to promote melanoma cell proliferation. To assess the in vivo relevance of DRAM2, we performed xenograft experiments using DRAM2-depleted A375SM cells. DRAM2 knockdown dramatically inhibited tumor growth and significantly reduced tumor mass compared to controls (Figure 7H–J). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of a melanoma tissue microarray (TMA) showed elevated DRAM2 protein expression in tumor tissues relative to normal skin (Figure 7K), highlighting its clinical relevance in melanoma pathogenesis. Together, these results establish that the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis plays a critical role in melanoma cell proliferation and tumorigenicity through the regulation of autophagy flux.

Loss of DRAM2 Ser263 phosphorylation impairs autophagic flux and suppresses melanoma tumorigenesis

To determine the functional significance of DRAM2 phosphorylation at Ser263 in melanoma progression, we established A375SM cells stably expressing either WT DRAM2 or a non-phosphorylatable mutant (S263A). Western blot analysis showed that expression of WT DRAM2 increased LC3B-II levels, while DRAM2S263A expression reduced LC3B-II and led to accumulation of SQSTM1/p62, indicating impaired autophagic flux (Figure 8A). To directly assess autophagic flux, cells were subjected to EBSS-induced starvation and analyzed using a tandem mCherry-EGFP-LC3B reporter. Under these conditions, DRAM2 WT-expressing cells displayed a higher proportion of mCherry-only puncta, indicative of efficient autophagosome maturation and autolysosome formation. In contrast, DRAM2S263A-expressing cells exhibited a marked reduction in red-only puncta accompanied by accumulation of yellow puncta, consistent with defective progression to autolysosomes (Figure 8B). To evaluate the role of the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis under stress-relevant conditions, cells were exposed to UVB irradiation, a known activator of RPS6KA3 and autophagy. Following UVB exposure, WT DRAM2-expressing cells showed reduced SQSTM1/p62 levels, consistent with enhanced autophagic flux. In contrast, DRAM2S263A-expressing cells exhibited accumulation of LC3B-I and sustained high levels of SQSTM1/p62, indicative of defective autophagy (Figure 8C). Time-course analysis further confirmed progressive SQSTM1/p62 degradation in WT DRAM2 cells after UVB treatment, whereas DRAM2S263A-expressing cells showed sustained SQSTM1/p62 levels (Figure 8D). Consistently, tandem mCherry-EGFP-LC3B imaging revealed a robust increase in red-only LC3 puncta in WT DRAM2 cells following UVB exposure, whereas DRAM2S263A-expressing cells showed markedly fewer autolysosomal puncta, further indicating impaired autophagic flux (Figure 8E). Functionally, WT DRAM2 significantly promoted anchorage-independent colony formation in soft agar, while the DRAM2S263A mutant failed to support colony growth (Figure 8F). To assess the in vivo relevance of Ser263 phosphorylation, we performed xenograft experiments in nude mice. Tumors derived from DRAM2S263A-expressing cells were significantly smaller in both volume and mass compared to those from WT DRAM2 expressing cells (Figure 8G–I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that phosphorylation of DRAM2 at Ser263 is required for efficient autophagic flux under stress conditions and is a critical determinant of melanoma cell proliferation and tumorigenicity.

Discussion

A growing number of proteins have been shown to undergo lysosomal trafficking upon phosphorylation near sorting motifs. For instance, RAB29/Rab29 is phosphorylated by PRKC/PKC to promote its lysosomal targeting [31], PI4K2A/PI4KII is phosphorylated by GSK3 to enable AP3D1/AP-3-mediated delivery to lysosomes [32], and CLDN2/claudin-2 undergoes lysosomal degradation upon phosphorylation at Ser208 [33]. Together, these studies illustrate a regulatory principle in which phosphorylation adjacent to sorting signals modulates adaptor binding and intracellular trafficking decisions. In this study, we identified RPS6KA3/RSK2 as a previously unrecognized upstream kinase that directly phosphorylates the lysosomal membrane protein DRAM2 at Ser263. Rather than acting solely as a static localization signal, Ser263 phosphorylation governs late endosomal-lysosomal trafficking of DRAM2 and thereby determines its functional role in autophagy. When phosphorylated at Ser263, DRAM2 engages the AP3D1/AP-3 adaptor complex via its adjacent dileucine motif, enabling efficient progression through RAB7-positive late endosomes and targeting to lysosomes [39], which promotes autophagosome-lysosome fusion and autophagic flux [40]. In contrast, the non-phosphorylatable DRAM2S263A mutant fails to bind AP3D1/AP-3, exhibits defective late endosomal trafficking, and is partially redistributed toward plasma membrane-proximal compartments, where it enhances exosome secretion. While previous studies have focused primarily on transcriptional regulation of DRAM2 through TP53/p53 [41,42], our findings demonstrate that DRAM2 is also regulated at the post-translational level through phosphorylation-dependent trafficking control. To our knowledge, DRAM2 represents a previously unrecognized example of a lysosomal membrane protein whose sorting signal-proximal phosphorylation dynamically regulates adaptor engagement and lysosomal targeting, highlighting an additional layer of spatial regulation at the lysosomal membrane.

Our study further uncovers a compartment-specific function of RPS6KA3/RSK2 in autophagy regulation. Although members of the RPS6KA/RSK family have been implicated in cytoplasmic autophagy suppression via MTOR/MTORC1 signaling [43,44], our data reveal a spatially distinct function for RPS6KA3/RSK2 at the lysosomal membrane. Through phosphorylation of DRAM2, RPS6KA3/RSK2 selectively promotes late-stage autophagic processes, including autophagosome-lysosome fusion and flux completion. This duality underscores the context-dependent nature of kinase signaling, where subcellular localization of both the kinase and its substrate determines the functional outcome. These results expand our understanding of how autophagy is differentially regulated through spatial signaling networks and suggest that RPS6KA3/RSK2 May coordinate multiple stages of the autophagy pathway in a compartmentalized manner.

Our findings indicate that RPS6KA3/RSK2 interacts with DRAM2 and phosphorylates Ser263 within its C-terminal cytoplasmic region. This post-translational modification enhances the binding affinity of DRAM2 for the AP-3 adaptor complex, in line with established mechanisms of phospho-adaptor recognition. In the absence of Ser263 phosphorylation, DRAM2 fails to efficiently enter the late endosomal-lysosomal pathway and is partially redirected toward plasma membrane-proximal compartments, where it promotes exosome secretion, potentially through effects on multivesicular body dynamics [45–47]. Although the precise molecular mechanism linking DRAM2 trafficking to exosome biogenesis remains to be elucidated and represents a limitation of the present study, the growing interest in exosome-mediated tumor communication underscores the translational relevance of this observation. Importantly, although the specific DRAM2S263A substitution used here has not been reported as a recurrent patient-derived mutation, cancer genomics databases (e.g., COSMIC and cBioPortal) reveal multiple somatic variants distributed across DRAM2, including mutations affecting or flanking the dileucine sorting motif. In this context, the S263A mutant serves as a mechanistic probe to interrogate the functional importance of the Ser263-proximal regulatory region rather than as a direct model of a single clinical mutation.

At the cellular and clinical levels, we observed strong co-expression of RPS6KA3/RSK2 and DRAM2 in melanoma, with elevated DRAM2 expression correlating with poor patient survival. Functional assays demonstrated that both RPS6KA3/RSK2 activity and DRAM2 phosphorylation are required to sustain autophagic flux and melanoma cell proliferation. Notably, DRAM2 knockdown or expression of the S263A mutant phenocopied RPS6KA3/RSK2 loss, indicating that DRAM2 acts downstream of RPS6KA3/RSK2. The relevance of this signaling axis was further validated in vivo, where DRAM2S263A expression or RPS6KA3/RSK2 inhibition suppressed melanoma tumor growth. These results position the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 axis as a key regulator of late-stage autophagy, linking membrane trafficking decisions to tumorigenic outcomes.

Unlike many autophagy regulators that act at the initiation or nucleation stages [48–50], the RPS6KA3/RSK2-DRAM2 pathway selectively modulates late-stage autophagy by ensuring proper lysosomal trafficking of DRAM2. This late-stage specificity may offer a potential therapeutic advantage by suppressing tumor-promoting autophagy while sparing essential basal autophagy. More broadly, our study highlights how phosphorylation-dependent trafficking decisions can bifurcate the fate of membrane proteins between degradation and secretion pathways. Such spatial regulation may represent a general mechanism by which stress-responsive kinases coordinate autophagy and vesicle-mediated communication in cancer. In summary, our findings reveal a phosphorylation-dependent trafficking mechanism that links RPS6KA3/RSK2 signaling to DRAM2 function, autophagic flux, and melanoma progression (Figure 9).

Schematic summary of the proposed mechanism. Upon UVB exposure, RPS6KA3/RSK2 phosphorylates DRAM2 at Ser263, enabling its AP-3-mediated trafficking to lysosomes. This facilitates autolysosome formation and enhances autophagic flux, thereby supporting melanoma cell survival. In contrast, the non-phosphorylatable S263A mutant fails to interact with AP-3, mislocalizes to the plasma membrane, increases exosome release, impairs autophagy, and reduces cell viability.

Computational protein-protein docking simulation

To evaluate the interaction interface and binding energy (ΔG) between DRAM2 and RPS6KA3/RSK2, a protein-protein docking simulation was performed using Discovery Studio (version 2021, insillico). The 3D structure of DRAM2 was predicted using AlphaFold (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/), while the N-terminal kinase domain (NTKD) structure of RPS6KA3/RSK2 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3G51). Protein-protein docking was conducted using the ZDOCK rigid-body algorithm to generate multiple binding poses by evaluating shape complementarity and desolvation energy. The top-ranked poses were refined using the RDOCK protocol, which applies CHARMm-based minimization and recalculates desolvation and electrostatic interactions to improve binding accuracy and eliminate steric clashes. The best-scoring complex was further solvated with explicit water molecules and optional counterions to mimic physiological conditions. Final energy minimization was performed to eliminate van der Waals clashes and stabilize the complex. To identify the key interacting regions, the docking interface between the NTKD of RPS6KA3/RSK2 and the cytosolic loop of DRAM2 was modeled based on complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), following the rational design principles of antibody-antigen interactions. The final complex satisfied structural complementarity and minimized desolvation energy, supporting the predicted interaction between RPS6KA3/RSK2 and DRAM2.

IP-LC-MS/MS analysis

To identify kinases that interact with DRAM2, immunoprecipitation followed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (IP-LC-MS/MS) was performed. HEK293T cells (3 × 106 cells/100-mm dish) were seeded and incubated overnight. Cells were transfected with a Flag-tagged DRAM2 plasmid and harvested 24 h post-transfection. Cell lysis was performed using EBS lysis buffer, and protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay. For immunoprecipitation, 1 mg of total protein was incubated with anti-Flag-conjugated Sepharose beads at 4°C overnight with gentle rotation. After extensive washing, the beads were prepared for subsequent protein digestion and mass spectrometry analysis. Tryptic peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) coupled with an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were loaded onto a trap column (Acclaim PepMap 100 C18, 75 μm × 2 cm, 3 μm particle size) and separated on an analytical column (EASY-Spray column, 75 μm × 25 cm, 2 μm particle size, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a linear gradient of 5–32% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) over 95 min at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Full MS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 60,000, with a scan range of m/z 400–1,600. The most intense precursor ions were selected for fragmentation by higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 30%, and fragment ions were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 60,000.

In-gel digestion

Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained gel bands were excised, destained with 50% acetonitrile in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8.0 and dehydrated in 100% acetonitrile. Proteins were reduced with 10 mM DTT (Sigma-Aldrich, D9779) at 56°C for 45 min and alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, I1149) at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. After dehydration, gel pieces were rehydrated with 10 ng/μL trypsin (Promega, V5280) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, incubated on ice for 30 min, and digested overnight at 37°C. Peptides were extracted with 50% acetonitrile, 5% formic acid, dried in a SpeedVac, and stored at −20°C until LC-MS/MS analysis.

Synthetic peptide validation

To validate the phosphorylation site of the peptide TRLLpSRDI, a synthetic phosphorylated peptide was custom-synthesized by DANDI Cure Co. (Republic of Korea) and analyzed under the same LC-MS/MS conditions as the experimental sample. Tandem mass spectra of both the synthetic and endogenous peptides were acquired in positive ion mode. Fragment ions were annotated with b- and y-type ions, as well as neutral loss peaks of phosphoric acid (−97.977 Da; PL ions), to confirm site-specific phosphorylation.

Exosome purification using gradient ultracentrifugation (UC)

To isolate exosomes from WT DRAM2- and DRAM2S263A-expressing cells, HEK293T (3 × 106 cells/100-mm dish) were seeded and incubated overnight. The cells were transfected with Flag-tagged empty vector (mock), WT DRAM2, and DRAM2S263A. After 24 h, the cell media exchanged with fresh medium containing 10% of exosome-depleted FBS (System Biosciences, EXO-FBS-50A-1). After 24 h, the conditioned media were centrifuged at 500 ×g for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected. From each media sample (26 mL), 2 mL was taken for nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, NS300, Malvern Instruments, UK). The remaining media were concentrated using Amicon 30K filters (Merk, UFC9030) down from 2.3 mL to 2.4 mL. After concentration, 200 μL of each sample were used for NTA. The samples were further purified by gradient ultracentrifugation, and 500 μL fractions were collected. Purified fractions were analyzed for NTA and western blotting. Exosome counting and analysis were carried out by a professional operator at the Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI) using a nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) system (instrument model code: OC-103).

Quantification of red and yellow puncta was performed using ImageJ (version 1.54).

LysoTracker Staining

To investigate autophagy induction, LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Invitrogen, L7528) was used. Rps6ka3+/+ and rps6ka3-/- mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells (3 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 35-mm confocal dishes (SPL, 101,350) and incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The LysoTracker probe was initially diluted in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, D8418) to a stock concentration of 1 mM and subsequently diluted to a final concentration of 50 nM in complete growth medium protected from light, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with EBSS for 2 h to induce autophagy, followed by incubation in LysoTracker-containing medium for 30 min under standard culture conditions. After staining, cells were washed once with fresh growth medium, and the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Fisher, 62,249) for 15 min at 37°C. Finally, cells were washed twice with growth medium and visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 710, Carl Zeiss).

In vitro kinase assay

To validate RPS6KA3/RSK2-mediated phosphorylation of DRAM2, an in vitro kinase assay was performed. E. coli BL21 cells were transformed with either pGEX-5X-1-DRAM2 240–266 wild-type or pGEX-5X-1-DRAM2 240–266 S263A mutant expression vectors. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Sigma-Aldrich, I6758) at 37°C for 4 h. The expressed proteins were purified using glutathione-conjugated beads and eluted with 10 mM reduced glutathione. Purity and size of the proteins were confirmed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining (LPS solution, CBC006). The purified DRAM2 peptides were then incubated with recombinant human active RPS6KA3/RSK2 protein (Abcam, ab268950), ATP (Cell Signaling Technology, 9804), and kinase reaction buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, 9802) at 30°C for 30 min. Phosphorylation was detected by western blotting using phospho-specific antibodies against R×R××pS/T and R××pS/T motifs.

Phos-tag assay

Phos-tag gel electrophoresis was performed to evaluate the phosphorylation status of DRAM2. Briefly, HEK293T cells (3 × 106 cells/100-mm dish) were seeded and incubated overnight at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Cells were then transfected with HA-RPS6KA3/RSK2, Flag-DRAM2 WT, or Flag-DRAM2 S263A expression vectors. After 24 h, cells were harvested and lysed using EBC lysis buffer. Whole-cell lysates were subjected to electrophoresis using a Phos-tag acrylamide gel (SuperSep™ Phos-tag™; Fujifilm, 198–17981). Following electrophoresis, the gels were washed three times in 10 mM EDTA-containing transfer buffer with gentle agitation for 20 min to chelate manganese ions and deactivate the Phos-tag reagent, then re-soaked in EDTA-free transfer buffer for 10 min prior to protein transfer. Gels were transferred to PVDF membranes and probed with appropriate antibodies to detect phosphorylated DRAM2.

Crystal violet staining for foci formation

To examine melanoma cell death induced by BI-D1870, a selective RPS6KA3 inhibitor, a foci formation assay was performed. A375P and A375SM cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The following day, cells were treated with BI-D1870 at the indicated concentrations and incubated for 7 days. At the endpoint, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS, and then stained with crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich, V5265) for 15 min at room temperature with gentle rocking. After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS and air-dried overnight. The stained plates were scanned using an Epson flatbed scanner (Professional mode), and the intensity of crystal violet staining was quantified using ImageJ software (Ver. 1.54k).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using A375P and A375SM cells. Cells (1 × 104) stably infected with sh-RPS6KA3 or sh-DRAM2 were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The following day (0 h), 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (DOJINDO Molecular Technologies, CK04) was added to each well using a repeater pipette, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation with CCK-8 for approximately 1 h at 37°C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured every 24 h using a TECAN microplate reader (Infinite® M Plex).

Soft agar colony formation assay

To evaluate colony-forming ability under anchorage-independent conditions, A375P cells with DRAM2 knockdown were used. Bottom agar layer of 0.5% agar composed with Basal Medium Eagle (BME; Sigma-Aldrich, B9638) containing 10% FBS, 1X L-glutamine (Gibco, 25,030,081), and 0.5% gentamicin (Gibco, 15,710,064). Cells (8 × 103 /well) were seeded into 6-well plates containing a top layer of 0.3% agar composed of BME with 10% FBS, 1X L-glutamine, and 0.5% gentamicin. The plates were incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 10–14 days. Colonies were visualized under a microscope with a camera, and both colony size and number were quantified. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Mouse xenograft

To examine DRAM2-mediated melanoma tumor growth, a xenograft assay was performed. DRAM2 knockdown A375SM cells and DRAM2S263A-expressing SK-MEL-28 stable cells (5 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice. Starting one week after injection, tumor size and body weight were measured every three days using vernier calipers, up to 30 days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (1/2) × (length × width2). At the endpoint, mice were euthanized using CO2 inhalation, and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis.

Immunohistochemistry on tissue microarrays (TMA)

To stain the tumor microarray (TMA) slides, immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed. The slides derived from de-identified human melanoma specimens were purchased from US Biomax (catalog no. SK804). Briefly, slides were deparaffinized by washing three times with xylene, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (100%, 90%, 80%, and 70%), each for 3 min, three times. After washing with distilled water, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 M citrate buffer by boiling the slides for 10 min. Slides were then blocked with 3% BSA in 1× PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody against DRAM2 was applied and incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle rocking. The following day, the biotin-streptavidin-HRP system was applied, and DAB (3,3‘-diaminobenzidine) staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (abcam, ab64264). Slides were dehydrated through 95% and 100% ethanol (three times, 3 min each), followed by xylene treatment, and coverslipped with mounting medium. Stained tissues were visualized and analyzed using a TissueFAXS (Ver. 4) microscope system (TissueGnostics; KBSI equipment code AS46), and DAB intensity was quantified by HistoQuest analysis software (Ver. 4).

Immuno-electron microscopy (immuno-EM)

To evaluate the WT DRAM2 and DRAM2S263A cellular localization, the HeLa cells (1 × 106 cells) were seeded into 100-mm culture dish and incubated overnight. The cells were transfected with GFP-tagged DRAM2 WT and S263A subsequently incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were fixed either in 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.05% glutaraldehyde in PBS (pH 7.4). The fixed cells were embedded in 10% gelatin (AURION, 900.033) and small blocks were infused with 2.3 M sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich, S0389) overnight and then frozen in liquid nitrogen. Ultrathin cryo sections (50 nm) were cut at −120°C with a cryo-ultramicrotome (UCT7, Leica, Vienna, Austria). A rabbit monoclonal anti-GFP antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2956) was used at a dilution of 1:10, followed by protein A – gold (Cell Microscopy Core, Netherlands) for detection. All antibodies and gold conjugates were diluted in 0.1% BSA-c (AURION, 900.099) in PBS. The immunostained sections on the grid were then examined at KBSI using the Bio-HVEM System (OC101, JEM-1400 Plus and JEM-1000BEF, JEOL) operating at 120 kV.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise specified. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests (Microsoft Excel), and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9). Detailed statistical parameters, including the number of replicates and exact p-values, are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
