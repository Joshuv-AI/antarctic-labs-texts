The Index for Preventive and Interceptive Orthodontic Treatment Needs (IPION): a scoping review
Authors: Jeannette Dino, Noor Sam Ahmad, Ahmad Shuhud Irfani Zakaria, Alida Mahyuddin, Huay Woon You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Index For Preventive and Interceptive Orthodontic Treatment Needs (IPION) was developed to assess early orthodontic treatment needs (EOT) in mixed dentition. This scoping review aimed to map the extent, characteristics and nature of the existing evidence on the development of IPION and its application in identifying the prevalence of EOT and malocclusion in mixed dentition. This scoping review also investigated the reported IPION modifications, limitations and gaps in literature. Following PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a scoping review of the literature was conducted in July 2025 across 4 databases (EBSCO, Proquest, Pubmed, Scopus, Web of Science) and additional sources (journal reference list). Eligible sources include population-based and institutional observational studies which applied the IPION among children between the ages of 6 to 12 years. Data were extracted and literature was reviewed by 5 independent reviewers using a predefined extraction template and the findings were synthesized narratively. Twelve of 47 identified records (1999 to 2025) from North America, Asia, and Africa were included. EOT prevalence ranged from 20 to 96% across institutional and population-based studies. IPION modifications across different studies included modifications on the assessments of caries, overbite, overjet, missing permanent incisors, IPION component categories and addition of IPION score for 12-year-olds. Limited studies evaluated the validity of these modifications. There is a growing application of the IPION in evaluating the prevalence of EOT and malocclusion in mixed dentition. Future research is warranted to address its limitations and enhance its utility as a comprehensive epidemiological instrument.Keywords: IPION, Preventive orthodontics, Interceptive orthodontics, Early orthodontic treatment, Mixed dentition

Introduction

Malocclusion is a condition which leads to discrepancies in skeletal, dental and soft tissue relationships in the transverse, vertical and sagittal planes. This can occur as the primary teeth transition into permanent teeth in the mixed dentition stage (Fleming et al. 2008; Proffit 1986). Early screening with subsequent preventive or interceptive measures is crucial (Kerosuo 2002) as up to 50% of malocclusion can be improved and some fully corrected at this stage (Sinniah et al. 2023).

Orthodontic indices for epidemiological use have evolved from early qualitative classifications (Angle 1899; Björk et al. 1964) to quantitative tools such as the Summer’s Occlusal Index and Grainger’s Treatment Priority Index (Grainger 1967; Summers 1971). Subsequently, several countries introduced indices determine treatment need and eligibility for publicly funded orthodontic care (Jolley et al. 2010; Steinmassl et al. 2017).

Most established indices, however, were developed for permanent dentition and various limitations were identified when these indices were applied in mixed dentition. Commonly used indices include the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) (al Nimri and Richardson 2000; Mohamed et al. 2014), the Index of Complexity, Outcome and Treatment Need (ICON) (Kirschneck et al. 2023) and the Dental Aesthetic Index (DAI) (Vedovello et al. 2020, 2025).

These indices often do not account for dental caries and premature loss of primary teeth, which contribute to arch length loss and developing malocclusion in mixed dentition (Borzabadi-Farahani 2011; Grippaudo et al. 2007; Khan et al. 2024; Ngan et al. 1999). The IOTN’s “missing and displaced teeth” components were not applicable in this transitional stage and the lack of assessments for crowding, midline deviation, caries and early loss of teeth in mixed dentition, potentially underestimates treatment needs (al Nimri and Richardson 2000; Mohamed et al. 2014; Rapeepattana et al. 2019). Modifications, such as incorporating Little’s Index of Irregularity and primary teeth crossbites assessments, have been proposed (Kerosuo et al. 2013). On the other hand, the ICON places strong emphasis on aesthetics, which may be unreliable in mixed dentition (Daniels and Richmond 2000; Kaygisiz et al. 2016). Meanwhile, studies utilizing the DAI had to undertake modifications such as the omission of “missing teeth” component as unerupted teeth are common in mixed dentition (Borzabadi-Farahani 2011). Several authors found that the Summers Occlusal Index does not account for skeletal and functional components in the assessment of early orthodontic treatment needs (Grippaudo et al. 2007) as well as difficulties in molar relationship assessments in dentition due to drifting of permanent first molars secondary to extensive caries on deciduous molars (Tang and So 1992). As a result, the IPION was developed by Coetzee in 1999 for mixed dentition, with age-specific versions for 6- (IPION6) and 9-year-olds (IPION9) (Coetzee 1999).

Despite two decades of research, the existing body of literature remains fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous, with substantial variation in study design, population characteristics, outcome reporting, and modifications to the IPION. These differences limit direct comparability and meaningful synthesis. Systematic reviews are typically designed to address focused questions and generate pooled estimates from relatively homogeneous data, whereas scoping reviews are more appropriate for examining broad and diverse bodies of evidence and for identifying key concepts, gaps, and methodological variation (Arksey and O’Malley 2005; Munn et al. 2018; Tricco et al. 2018). In the context of IPION, important aspects of the evidence base, including scoring variations, index modifications, and reported limitations, have not been comprehensively mapped. Therefore, a scoping review was undertaken to systematically map the extent, characteristics, and research gaps relating to IPION and to inform future research, including areas where more focused systematic reviews or validation studies may be warranted (Munn et al. 2018; Peters et al. 2020).

Objectives

The objectives of this scoping review are: (1) To map the use of the Index for Preventive and Interceptive Orthodontic Treatment Needs (IPION) among children in mixed dentition (6 to 12 years) in epidemiological and institutional studies assessing early orthodontic treatment need and malocclusion prevalence, (2) to identify the modifications of IPION reported in the literature, (3) to identify reported limitations and proposed recommendations of IPION in its application to mixed dentition populations and (4) to identify the recommendations proposed in literature to address the identified limitations.

Methods

Protocol and registration

A scoping review of the published literature was performed based on PRISMA-ScR guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis extension for Scoping Reviews) (Tricco et al. 2018). The methodology was defined a priori, although a formal protocol was not registered. A scoping review design was selected to accommodate the broad objectives of mapping heterogeneous evidence on IPION rather than conducting a focused effectiveness synthesis.

Research questions

This review was conducted based on population, concept and context (PCC) framework according to the guidance by JBI Scoping Review Methodology Group (Pollock et al. 2023) outlined in Table 1. This scoping review was structured around the following questions: (1) What is the existing evidence regarding the application of IPION in assessing EOT and prevalence of malocclusion? (2) What are the modifications to the IPION identified in the literature? (3) What limitations in the use of the IPION are highlighted in existing studies? (4) What recommendations have been proposed to overcome the limitations of IPION?

Scoping review eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria and search strategy

Eligibility criteria were established according to the PCC framework, with the inclusion and exclusion criteria summarized in Table 1. Non-original publications (e.g. editorials, commentaries, book chapters, conference abstracts without full data) were excluded, as they do not provide primary data suitable for evidence mapping. No language or geographical restrictions were applied. Search terms were developed through team discussion and keyword identification. The preliminary search strategy was tested across selected databases and refined through iterative review until a final search strategy was developed.

Screening and study selection

The literature search was conducted independently by two authors (JD and NSA) on 26 July 2025 across EBSCO, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using the keywords “Index for Preventive and Interceptive Orthodontic Treatment Need,” “IPION,” or “mixed dentition.” Searches covered publications from 1 January 1999 (year of index development) to 26 July 2025, with no restriction on publication status. Duplicate articles were first eliminated using the EndNote reference management software Ver. 20 (The EndNote Team 2013) followed by manual verification by 2 authors (JD and NSA). Titles and abstracts were screened independently by 2 authors (JD and NSA) and potentially eligible studies were retrieved for full-text assessments. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with arbitration from a third author (ASI) where necessary. Full-text articles were independently reviewed by five authors (JD, NSA, ASI, AM, YHW) against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. Data extraction was conducted using a pre-piloted data charting form. Reference lists were screened through reverse and forward searches to identify additional eligible studies.

Data extraction

Inductive analysis was carried out by the reviewers to establish a data extraction framework, based on the following parameters: Author’s name(s), publication year, study characteristics (country, setting, design, IPION types, sample size, age, gender), data collection methods, study outcomes, IPION modifications and limitations identified. The preliminary extraction form was piloted on two randomly selected studies (approximately 5–10% of included articles) (Mak and Thomas 2022) and further refined using two additional studies prior to full data extraction. Inter- and intra-examiner agreement were assessed on the day of extraction and reassessed after two weeks. Extracted data from all included studies are presented in Table 2.

Critical appraisal

No formal critical appraisal of included studies was undertaken. This is consistent with the purpose of a scoping review, which aims to map the extent and characteristics of the evidence rather than to evaluate methodological quality or produce pooled estimates (Arksey and O’Malley 2005; Tricco et al. 2018). Given the heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting, formal risk-of-bias assessment was not considered appropriate. Instead, methodological characteristics were systematically charted and descriptively reported to support interpretation of findings.

Results

Paper selection

A total of 42 publications were identified through database searching (n = 39) and additional sources including reference lists screening (n = 2) and author-based searching (n = 1). After removal of 10 duplicates, 32 records remained for initial screening. After exclusion at the title and abstract screening stage (n = 11), 21 records proceeded to full-text assessment for eligibility. At full-text review, 8 articles were excluded due to not applying the IPION index (n = 6), incomplete data reporting (n = 1) and 1 article was an oral poster presentation with the exact study design and results as a published article. 13 articles that met the inclusion criteria were selected for this scoping review. The flowchart of the inclusion process was reported according to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines (Fig. 1). Two articles based on the same study population were combined during synthesis to avoid double counting, yielding 12 unique studies from 13 included publications.

Characteristics of the included studies

The year of the publication ranged from 1999 to 2025 and included patients aged 6 to 12 years of age. One single center study (8.3%) was conducted whereby the original author developed the index (Coetzee 1999), whereas 33.3% single-center studies (n = 4), one dual-centre study (8.3%) and 58.3% population-based studies (n = 7) were identified. The geographical distribution of studies included two from North America (Canada and the United States), six from Asia: South Asia (India), Southeast Asia (Thailand), two from the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, Syria), and two from African nations (South Africa and Tanzania). 16.7% (n = 2) of the studies used only IPION9 (Madiraju et al. 2024; Rapeepattana et al. 2019) while both IPION6 and IPION9 were used in 83.3% of the studies (n = 10). One study (8.3%) added a modification of IPION12 for children 12 years of age (Nerurkar and Kamble 2023). Characteristics of the included studies were seen in Table 2.

The IPION was developed by Coetzee by examining existing study models and records of past dental examination to assess all components using an IPION ruler designed for the study, depicted in Fig. 1 (Coetzee 1999). Weighting factors and scoring protocol were assigned according to the consensus of 10 orthodontists, whereas validity and reproducibility were assessed by a group of 30 clinicians and dental health care auxiliaries consisting of 10 orthodontists, 10 general dentists and 10 oral hygienists.

8.3% of the studies (n = 1) lacked information on the number of investigators, examiner training and calibration procedures (Pushparekha et al. 2025), while a study did not mention the number of examiners involved (Galui and Pal 2021). Additionally, 41.7% of the studies (n = 5) performed examiner calibration but did not describe the calibration process in detail (Burhan and Nawaya 2016; Khan et al. 2024; Nerurkar and Kamble 2023; Tungaraza et al. 2019; Vudata et al. 2023). A single investigator was identified in 33% (n = 4) of the studies, whereas two investigators were involved in 41.7% of the studies (n = 5).

The children included in these studies were between 6 and 12 years old. In studies which used only IPION9, the age group was between 7.8 and 9.2 years. In studies which utilized both IPION6 and IPION 9, the age group ranged from 5.9 to 12 years old. Half of the studies recruited children with the exact age of 6 and 9 years for IPION6 and IPION9 respectively (n = 6), while the age for 50% of recruited subjects were within a range of up to ± 2.17 years for each group measured with IPION6 and IPION9 individually.

Research outcomes of the included studies

Tables 3 and 4 illustrate the outcomes of the included studies. The pioneer study of developing IPION was conducted by Coetzee with the aim to create an index to quantify preventive and interceptive orthodontic treatment needs (PITxN) in mixed dentition (Coetzee 1999). 83.3% of the studies investigated PITxN of the study population (n = 10) while 75% of the studies investigated malocclusion features according to different levels of IPION categories (n = 9).

The prevalence of PITxN in North American regions ranged from 20.6 to 87.27% (Haider 2013; Karaiskos et al. 2005), whereas PITxN in Asia was between 20.0 to 96.0% and the prevalence of African nations was 67.2%. From which, the range of moderate PITxN was 20.0–29.3% while and that of definite PITxN was 35.5–60.92%.

Studies investigating only IPION9 were identified in Asia with reported PITxN ranging from 88.7 to 96.0%. Some studies which investigated both IPION6 and IPION9 a found that the prevalence of PITxN in the regions of North America, Asia and Africa was 20.9% to 95.6% for IPION6 and 68.6% to 91.9% for IPION9.

The mean IPION9 final score was only quantified in two studies. A Thai study identified the following mean scores (SD) for the treatment categories of “no”, “moderate” and “definite”: 2.50 (2.4), 10.80 (2.9), 42.27 (19.5) (Rapeepattana et al. 2019). Another study in India assessed the mean overall scores for IPION6 at 9.66 and 10.65 for IPION9 (Nerurkar and Kamble 2023). A median IPION score (IQR) of 3.0 (3.0–4.0) was identified in 1 study (Nerurkar and Kamble 2023).

Malocclusion traits commonly identified using IPION in these communities were variable. Commonly identified features for the primary component (caries and early loss) were caries C, caries D, caries E and caries 6. This was followed by abnormal first permanent molar relationships, increased overbite, increased overjet, anterior crossbite, posterior crossbite and crossbite tendency, anterior open bite, impeded eruption of first permanent molar, lower first permanent molar tipping, lip incompetency and active frenum.

Among institutional studies, Haider (2013) reported that caries experience of D and E were most prevalent at 26% and 29% respectively. In Saudi Arabia, caries of D and E were among the highest at 77% and 70.3% respectively (Madiraju et al. 2024) whereas a prevalence of 94.6% for interproximal caries of CDE6 was seen in India (Khan et al. 2024).

In population-based studies, some identified the prevalence of overall caries for IPION6 at 38.4% and 49.5–91.6% for IPION9 (Rapeepattana et al. 2019; Vudata et al. 2023). Caries prevalence for individual tooth within IPION6 was 32.3% for primary canines (C) (Tungaraza et al. 2019), 47.3% for primary first molars (D) and 43.9% for primary second molars (E). Corresponding prevalence for IPION9 was 32.3–77% for C, 33.5–77.8% for D (Rapeepattana et al. 2019; Tungaraza et al. 2019), 75% for E and 20.8% for permanent first molars. Early loss of primary teeth assessed (CDE) ranged from 43.1 to 46.7% (Pushparekha et al. 2025; Rapeepattana et al. 2019). Some studies adopted different quantification data for caries prevalence, such as caries “involving 1 tooth”, “more than one tooth”, “more than 2 teeth” and “involving 4 teeth” as seen in Table 2 (Karaiskos et al. 2005; Pushparekha et al. 2025; Tungaraza et al. 2019).

Varying prevalence of other common malocclusion features were as also seen. Abnormal permanent molar relationships in population-based studies ranged from 11 to 29.9% for IPION6 (Karaiskos et al. 2005; Tungaraza et al. 2019) and between 45.5 and 54.5% for IPION9 (Karaiskos et al. 2005; Rapeepattana et al. 2019), with predominantly class II permanent molar relationships at 11%–32% for IPION6, and between 36 and 44% for IPION9, while an institutional study identified a prevalence of 54% (Madiraju et al. 2024).

Increased overbite was observed in population-based studies with the following prevalence: 24% for IPION6 and 23.2–55.7% for IPION9. Institutional studies identified a prevalence of 17% for IPION6 and 50% for IPION9.

The prevalence of increased overjet of more than 5.0 mm in population-based studies for IPION6 were 11% and 17.5% for IPION9. Institutional studies identified a prevalence of 14.3% (Haider 2013). Overjet of more than 3.0 mm was identified in population-based studies at 20.5% for IPION6 and between to 36% to 43% for IPION9 (Rapeepattana et al. 2019; Tungaraza et al. 2019) as well as at 44% for an institutional study which investigated treatment need using only IPION9 (Madiraju et al. 2024).

Anterior crossbite was observed with a prevalence of 10.5% for IPION6 and 7–23.9% for IPION9 for population-based studies (Karaiskos et al. 2005; Rapeepattana et al. 2019). Meanwhile, reported prevalence for anterior crossbite among institutional studies were 8.6–20% for IPION6 and 20–27% for IPION9 (Khan et al. 2024).

Population-based studies have identified posterior crossbite and crossbite tendencies with a prevalence of 3.1 to 7.9% for IPION9 (Rapeepattana et al. 2019; Vudata et al. 2023), whereas the corresponding prevalence in institutional studies for 9.4% for IPION6 and 14.6–19.5% for IPION9 (Haider 2013; Khan et al. 2024).

Anterior open bite was seen in population-based studies with a prevalence of 10% for IPION6 and 6.7–10% for IPION9 (Karaiskos et al. 2005; Tungaraza et al. 2019), as well as an overall IPION prevalence of 6.7% in one study (Pushparekha et al. 2025). The prevalence of impeded eruption of permanent first molar identified using IPION9 was 13.7% in an institutional study (Khan et al. 2024) and 1.3% for a population-based study (Vudata et al. 2023).

Lower first permanent molar tipping was seen in one population-based study with a prevalence of 23% whereas upper first permanent molar rotation was identified at 13.7% (Rapeepattana et al. 2019). Lip incompetency was identified at a prevalence of 13.7% for a population-based study investigating IPION9 (Tungaraza et al. 2019) while two studies found that less than 9% of children investigated with IPION6 and IPION9 presented with active frenum (Pushparekha et al. 2025).

Data collection methods and IPION modifications

A variety of data collection methods and IPION modification across studies were observed in Table 5. Clinical examinations and measurements were done using either a ruler (Burhan and Nawaya 2016; Haider 2013; Karaiskos et al. 2005; Tungaraza et al. 2019), the Michigan probe (Khan et al. 2024; Rapeepattana et al. 2019) or the CPITN probe (Galui and Pal 2021; Madiraju et al. 2024). Study models and intraoral photographs were used in 25% of the studies (n = 3); however, within the studies some used both methods and others used only one of either study models or intraoral photographs (Coetzee 1999; Haider 2013; Nerurkar and Kamble 2023; Rapeepattana et al. 2019). 25% of the studies conducted assessments using modified IPION which excluded caries assessment (n = 3).

Haider in 2013 included both restorations and caries for the caries component as this design was more suitable to the setting of a treatment seeking population (Haider 2013). 16.7% of the studies compared IPION level of treatment need with inclusion and exclusion of the caries component (n = 2). A study in Thailand classified missing permanent incisor(s) only when the permanent premolar or canine in the same arch was present and assessed abnormal overbite when there was an “overlap of the 1/3 of the crown height of the lower incisors” instead of using the standard orthodontic reference of “2/3 overlap” of the lower incisor crown height. Also, the IPION index was split into 2 categories: (1) caries and early loss component and (2) Occlusal and functional component to streamline PITxN identification during referral. This was different than the original five component classification by the original author (Rapeepattana et al. 2019). Karaiskos et al. established a minimum IPION score of 5 as an indicator of treatment (Karaiskos et al. 2005). Nerurkar et al. added an IPION12 index assessing 12-year-old age group using IPION9 criteria (Nerurkar and Kamble 2023). 33.3% of the studies assessed modified IPION with removal of caries and early loss components (n = 4).

A study suggested and investigated categorization of IPION criteria into “caries and early loss”, “occlusal” and “functional” components to streamline treatment approaches (Rapeepattana et al. 2019).

It has been suggested that the IPION may underestimate PITxN, as it accounts for space loss due to molar tipping or rotation, potentially overlooking mild to moderate cases that still require space management. Incorporating parameters which aids in space analysis and management such as average permanent tooth size measurements in the mixed dentition has been suggested (Rapeepattana et al. 2019).

A Canadian study proposed assigning higher scores to caries, early tooth loss, and crossbite components to better reflect the true severity of malocclusion. These features were commonly observed among the children studied, yet many were classified under the IPION category of no treatment need, causing those who require interventions to miss out on orthodontic care, since only 28% of the children were identified as requiring treatment according to the index (Karaiskos et al. 2005). Several studies pointed out that radiographic investigations were not conducted to identify caries severity, abnormal eruption patterns or anomalies (such as congenitally missing tooth and hypodontia) which could lead to underestimation of PITxN (Galui and Pal 2021; Khan et al. 2024; Nerurkar and Kamble 2023; Rapeepattana et al. 2019; Vudata et al. 2023).

Coetzee (1999) and Rapeepattana et al. (2019) recommended adjustments to the cut off value of the IPION score for categorizing those with PITxN in countries with limited resources according to individual needs. Although various studies incorporated modifications to the use of the IPION, additional research was suggested to assess accuracy and reliability of both original and modified versions of the IPION (Rapeepattana et al. 2019).

IPION scoring protocol

The original IPION index has two versions: IPION6 for the children who are 6 years of age and IPION9 for 9-year-old children. IPION6 includes 17 criteria, while IPION9 assesses 20. Each criterion was assigned a score based on its presence and severity. This was then multiplied by a specific weighting factor as outlined in Table 7. The weighted scores were summed to produce the final IPION score, which determined the three levels of treatment need as follows: “no treatment need (scores 0 to 5)”, “moderate treatment need (scores 6 to 14)”, and “definite treatment need (score greater than 15). Originally, these criteria were broadly categorized into five components: primary, anterior, posterior, occlusion, and soft tissue. An IPION ruler was also designed to aid in measurement (Coetzee 1999).

Discussion

Application of IPION in mixed dentition

This scoping review mapped 12 unique studies published between 1999 and 2025 evaluating the Index for Preventive and Interceptive Orthodontic Treatment Needs (IPION) in mixed dentition populations. Most included studies were cross-sectional, with a predominance of population-based designs, although several institutional investigations were also identified. Across diverse geographic regions, the reported prevalence of preventive and interceptive orthodontic treatment need (PITxN) was consistently substantial, frequently exceeding 60% and reaching up to 96% in some cohorts. However, considerable heterogeneity was observed in sampling strategies, age categorization, outcome reporting, and index modification.

The evidence demonstrates that IPION has been applied primarily as an epidemiological screening tool to quantify early orthodontic treatment needs in children aged 6 to 12 years. Its age-specific versions (IPION6 and IPION9) were generally selected according to developmental stage rather than chronological age alone, reflecting the biological variability in dental maturation This approach is clinically defensible, as interceptive orthodontic planning is often guided by dental development rather than strict age thresholds (Keski-Nisula et al. 2003).

The consistently high PITxN prevalence reported across regions suggests that mixed dentition malocclusion traits and caries-related space loss remain common public health concerns. However, prevalence estimates varied markedly between institutional and population-based studies. Institutional cohorts, often comprising treatment-seeking children, tended to report higher treatment need, whereas school-based samples showed broader variability. These differences likely reflect referral patterns, underlying caries burden, and population characteristics rather than inconsistencies of the index itself.

Although IPION was originally developed as a structured, weighted scoring system incorporating caries, occlusal, and soft tissue components (Coetzee 1999), its application across studies demonstrates adaptation to local clinical contexts. This reinforces its practicality as a screening instrument but also introduces variability in interpretation.

Modifications to IPION

A notable finding of this review is the frequency of modifications to the original index. Several studies excluded the caries component when assessing treatment need, particularly in populations where restorative care had already been provided (Haider 2013; Madiraju et al. 2024). Others altered overbite assessment, reclassified components into simplified categories, introduced referral cut-off scores, or developed an IPION12 extension (Karaiskos et al. 2005; Nerurkar and Kamble 2023; Rapeepattana et al. 2019).

While these adaptations were intended to enhance clinical relevance or feasibility, only limited evidence exists regarding their external validation. The absence of standardized evaluation of modified versions restricts comparability across studies and formal validation is required.

Limitations identified across included studies

Epidemiological surveys commonly rely on clinical examination alone, and prior evidence suggests that radiographs may increase caries indices only modestly at the population level (Poorterman et al. 2000). Thus, the omission of radiographs is consistent with the screening purpose of IPION.

Other reported concerns include the inability of IPION to specify the types of interceptive intervention required, limited assessment of oral habits, absence of skeletal and midline evaluation, and potential underestimation of space loss not directly related to molar tipping or rotation (Khan et al. 2024; Rapeepattana et al. 2019).

Methodological variation in examiner calibration and reporting quality was also observed. While calibration was frequently mentioned, detailed procedures were often insufficiently described, which may affect reproducibility across settings.

Limitations of this scoping review

The number of included studies was limited (12 studies), restricting the breadth of available evidence. Geographic representation was uneven, with clustering in certain regions and limited data from others. All included studies were observational and predominantly cross-sectional, which limits inference regarding predictive validity or longitudinal outcomes. Substantial heterogeneity in study design, sampling methods, examiner calibration reporting, and outcome definitions limited direct comparison across studies. In keeping with scoping review methodology, a narrative synthesis was conducted with no formal critical appraisal was undertaken. Consequently, conclusions reflect the mapped evidence rather than graded quality assessment.

Conclusion

The IPION has been increasingly applied over the past two decades to screen early orthodontic treatment needs in mixed dentition populations across diverse clinical and community settings. Current evidence supports its utility primarily as an epidemiological screening tool rather than a definitive decision-making instrument. While the original validation study demonstrated acceptable reproducibility and construct validity (Coetzee 1999), multiple studies varied considerably in methodology and incorporated modifications. Therefore, the strength of evidence for validity remains context-dependent and is strongest when IPION is used according to its original design. Future research should prioritize validation of modified versions, development of standardized referral thresholds, and large-scale population-based investigations to enhance generalizability. With careful refinement and standardization, IPION has the potential to contribute meaningfully to public health planning and resource allocation for preventive and interceptive orthodontic care.
