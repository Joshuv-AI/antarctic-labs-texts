Designing unique mechanical modes through an extension of the quantum hopping method
Authors: Haoran Nie, Xiangying Shen, Lei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe introduce an extension of the hopping method, typically used in quantum systems, to mechanical networks for constructing dynamical matrices. This innovative and efficient approach facilitates the design of a family of mechanical metamaterials that reveals fresh insights into the relationship between topological structure and mechanical function. By tuning hopping interactions and onsite terms, we uncover unique topological phenomena, including boundary-localized and bulk-constrained vibrations, the ability to isolate pure topological modes, and the realization of topological edge states near zero frequency. It not only bridges quantum and mechanical domains but also provides a framework for designing mechanical metamaterials, which may impact applications in material science and engineering.

The hopping method is a fundamental approach widely used in condensed matter physics to derive the Hamiltonian of the tight-binding model. In this model, the wave-function of electrons in a crystalline lattice is described by a linear combination of localized atomic orbitals. Electrons have the ability to hop between tightly bounded orbitals of atoms, allowing for the construction of a Hamiltonian based on these hopping amplitudes. These hopping amplitudes indicate the degree of electron delocalization and play a crucial role in determining the energy band structure of the system. The hopping method is a straightforward and intuitive framework for understanding the electronic properties of materials (1–4), and its simple format leads to convenient computation in theoretical investigations.

Mechanical networks are ubiquitous in various engineering and industrial areas (5, 6). With special designs, such networks can exhibit unusual elastic responses, such as negative Poisson ratio (7–9), negative compressibility (10), nonreciprocity (11), topological boundary modes (12), ultra-high stiffness (13), and adjustable moduli and Poisson ratio (14, 15). In particular, topological properties, originally studied in condensed matter physics under the quantum framework, have been extended to classical systems (16–18). Such studies have produced numerous innovative and fascinating outcomes, such as topological acoustics (19), mechanical topological insulators (20–25), topological soft modes and self-stress states (12, 26–29), phononic crystals (30, 31), and amorphous or non-Hermitian mechanical systems (32–38). However, traditional Newtonian methods face challenges of low computational efficiency and high complexity when analyzing large-scale, disordered mechanical networks. These limitations hinder the in-depth study and design of mechanical metamaterials. Therefore, finding a unified analysis approach in both quantum and mechanical systems could potentially facilitate the novel design of metamaterial from the quantum area to classical system.

In general, a key approach in mechanical networks is to construct the dynamical matrix D (39, 40), whose eigenmodes are vibrational normal modes with the eigenvalues the square of frequencies ω2. D plays a similar role as the quantum Hamiltonian and vibrational modes can be solved by the equation Du=ω2u, which can be derived from the Newton’s equations (Materials and Methods). Traditionally, obtaining the dynamical matrix of a mechanical network requires solving corresponding Newton’s equations. Inspired by the hopping method from quantum systems, we develop a mechanical hopping framework specifically tailored for mechanical metamaterials. Using this framework, we demonstrate an efficient computational process and introduce designs for mechanical materials with unique topological properties based on connectivity, hopping interactions, and mechanical onsite potentials. Through the design and verification of these structures, we showcase both the theoretical significance and practical value of our approach.

Hopping Method and Its Computational Advantages

We suppose spring-mass network with small oscillations, where force (i.e., stress) and deformation (i.e., strain) exhibit a linear relation. where θij is the angle between eij and x axis. A 3×3 matrix can be similarly obtained in 3D. We can get the net force on node i after summing up forces from all bonds at node i: [3]Fi=∑j≠iFij=∑j≠itijΘij·(uj−ui)=∑j=1NKijuj

with the off-diagonal matrix element Kij=tijΘij (j≠i) from the first term and the diagonal element Kii=−∑j≠itijΘij=−∑j≠iKij from the second term.

When nodes i and j are not connected, we simply put Kij=0. Thus we get the matrix equation for the entire system: F=K·u. The matrix element Kij=tijΘij (j≠i) can be understood as stiffness tij multiplying a tensor Θij which contains the geometric details. From K we can then obtain the dynamic matrix, D=−M−1K, with M the mass matrix. Then the characteristic equation reads:[4]Du=ω2u,

where ω is the eigen-frequency of the system.

From the former process we can construct mechanical network in a hopping method like the tight-binding model, with Kij analogous to the hopping interaction between sites i and j, and the displacement ui mimicking the wave function. However, please note a major difference in the diagonal elements: For a quantum system, the diagonal element Hii represents the onsite energy independent of other sites; while for a mechanical system, Kii=−∑j≠iKij couples with all connected sites.

The hopping method for constructing the dynamical matrix emphasizes interactions over nodes, and builds the D matrix directly from interaction data, following the outlined approaches. This method not only offers computational advantage but also introduces three key hopping-model perspectives for designing mechanical networks: connectivity, hopping interaction, and on-site potential. Through these perspectives, we explore the method’s applicability to the design of classical mechanical systems.

Design from Connectivity Perspective

Apart from the computational speed advantage, the hopping method provides multiple important insights. We begin with the connectivity perspective. Because the network’s topology is governed by connectivity, we can design real-space structures with desired topological properties through connectivity. More specifically, we construct a connectivity matrix Dc by ignoring geometrical information of edges, i.e., setting Θij=1. The resulting Dc is an N×N matrix, which is much simpler than the dynamical matrix D (dN×dN with d the dimension).

We compare the mode spectrum of the connectivity matrix Dc with the full matrix D in Fig. 2. We choose the connectivity of valley Hall insulator (VHI) as a typical example, in which each node is connected to three neighbors by identical bonds (Fig. 2A, Left panel). We construct Dc and plot its spectrum in Fig. 2A, Right panel. We also compute the spectra from the full dynamical matrix D in Fig. 2B–D, from structures with the same connectivity but different geometric configurations.

The red point can be twisted by δx and δy to give different configurations with the same connectivity. The twisting is δx=δy=0 in (B), δx=0.8a,δy=0 in (C), and δx=−0.2a,δy=0.3a in (D). (E and F) The topological edge modes of structure (C and D), respectively. Despite geometric difference, all spectra demonstrate similar two-Dirac-cone topological feature due to their identical connectivity. Based on this, we further design a system with two bulk regions with a “Z” shaped boundary in the Middle. The two bulk regions share identical connectivity but have opposite mass distributions, similar to the VHI. The Z-shaped boundary features a topologically protected edge mode, akin to the VHI, as illustrated in Fig. 2 E and F, which correspond to the configurations shown in Fig. 2 C and D. Note that we adopt a fixed boundary condition at the horizontal and vertical edges of the system, where the nodes are connected to fixed points. These nodes are still movable, while the fixed points (which are immovable) are not shown in the figure. The Top and Bottom parts of the “Z” originate from the fixed boundary encountering two distinct types of sublattices, where one type vibrates strongly while the other remains nearly static (20, 23) (see more details in SI Appendix).

Therefore, the geometric details (Θij) of a system do not influence its topology; instead, it is connectivity (Dc) that determines these intrinsic characteristics. Moreover, this property extends seamlessly between ordered and disordered systems. In Fig. 2 G and H, we present two structures with the same Dc that exhibit identical topological edge states, even though one structure is ordered Fig. 2G while the other is disordered Fig. 2H (see SI Appendix for more details). Thus, the connectivity perspective provides a significant design freedom to achieve similar topological features with distinct configurations in both ordered and amorphous systems, as long as they share the same connectivity matrix Dc.

Design from Hopping Interaction Perspective

Besides connectivity, the hopping interaction can also be tuned to induce novel properties. We manipulate the hopping interaction in a hexagonal lattice for demonstration: The hopping interaction changes from a simple scalar constant tij into a tensor: tij=tijgab=txtytxtyij, with δt=tx−ty. Such mechanical interaction can be realized by anisotropic springs that exhibit different responses in different directions.

Once the anisotropy is introduced, the Dirac cones at points K and K′ become tilted in opposite directions, as indicated by the blue arrows in Fig. 3A. Simultaneously, the Γ point at the center splits into two Dirac cones, each tilting in opposite directions, as illustrated by the red arrows in Fig. 3A. Moreover, the degree of tilting is positively correlated to δt=tx−ty, as shown in Fig. 3B. By centering on the Dirac points and following a small circle’s circumference (see the gray circle in Fig. 3A at one of the K′ points), we plot the eigenvalue versus the circumferential angle in Fig. 3C: Evidently, the evolution at the K and K′ points, as well as the Γ− and Γ+ points, is precisely opposite. If the Dirac cone is not distorted, the resulting curves should appear as horizontal straight lines. Conversely, due to the distortion, the cone’s inclination transforms the straight lines into opposite wavy patterns.

More interestingly, when we join two bulk regions with opposite anisotropy interactions, a special boundary can be formed with rather unique properties. As depicted in the Upper panel of Fig. 3E, two bulk regions, where interactions are characterized by δt>0 (red bonds) and δt<0 (blue bonds), form an intermediate boundary. This boundary gives rise to boundary modes, as indicated by the red curves in the spectral plot of Fig. 3E. The intense vibrations at this boundary are clearly showcased in the red box of Fig. 3E. In addition to the boundary modes, this system also generates rather unique bulk modes that are confined to vibrating exclusively within either half of the bulk, unable to penetrate into the other half due to the presence of intermediate boundary, as illustrated in the blue box of Fig. 3E. In contrast, the normal bulk modes shown in Figs. 1D and 3F exhibit vibrations throughout the entire bulk.

This phenomenon demonstrates a unique confining effect arising from the hopping interaction perspective, achieved by opposite bulk hopping-interaction anisotropy. This effect can confine vibrations not only at the boundary through boundary modes but also within desired bulk regions through bulk modes, providing significant flexibility for tuning vibrations in both the boundary and bulk. The tilted Dirac cone achieved in mechanical systems may also serve as a potential platform to explore nonlinear valley Hall effects and valleytronics in mechanical metamaterials (45).

Moreover, if the anisotropic spring is designed to exhibit stronger stiffness in one particular direction (such as the x-direction) than in others and is applied to disordered systems, such a system could display nematic-like properties-positional disorder combined with orientational order imposed by the engineered interaction. Additionally, the disordered system could be designed with specific connectivity, as mentioned in the last section, to achieve a preferred topological property. This design integrates both the hopping interaction and connectivity perspectives, potentially enabling unique topological behaviors in liquid crystal-like systems and stimulating significant theoretical and experimental interest.

Design from On-Site Potential Perspective

The third design perspective we aim to demonstrate involves manipulating the mechanical on-site potential. Fig. 4A, Left panel displays a quasi-one-dimensional structure based on a hexagonal lattice. Its mirror symmetry at the center creates a boundary characterized by nontrivial topological modes. Fig. 4A, Right panel demonstrates how to implement the onsite potential by adding an additional on-site spring directly connected to the ground. Since the hopping interaction is controlled by the spring between different nodes and the on-site potential is controlled by the on-site spring connected to the ground, they can be controlled independently.

It has two trivial boundary modes in the Left and Right ends and a nontrivial topological boundary mode in the Middle due to the mirror symmetry at center. The Right panel shows a vertical cross-section to demonstrate the on-site potential. (C) Mode spectrum with on-site potential with stiffness tonsite=Ax and corresponding excitations. The linear tonsite breaks the degeneracy and produces two red curves of trivial boundary modes, which vibrate mainly at either the Left or the Right end (see the Top and Bottom boxes). They can separately couple to the topological mode and form two types of mixed modes (see the two Middle boxes). (D) Screening the trivial boundary modes with on-site potential. The eigenmode spectrum is depicted in Fig. 4B. There is a pair of degenerate trivial boundary mode bands marked by the red curve, which is associated with the Left and Right boundaries. Additionally, a nontrivial topological mode band, highlighted in green, originates from the central boundary. The activation of these modes is shown within the corresponding boxes in Fig. 4B.

For the trivial boundary modes (Top box), a stronger vibration is evident at both ends. Conversely, for the nontrivial mode (Middle box), a larger amplitude is observed centrally. Due to the proximity of frequencies between the trivial and nontrivial boundary modes, they can be simultaneously excited, leading to a mixed hybridization state. This state features pronounced amplitudes at both the center and the two ends, as depicted in the Bottom box. Such mixing poses challenges for the experimental investigation and practical application of pure nontrivial topological states.

To tackle this challenge, we introduce an on-site potential to every site, as inspired by the hopping approach. The mechanical on-site potential is designed to increase the spring constants of on-site springs linearly along the x direction: tonsite(x)=Ax. This inhomogeneous tonsite breaks the degeneracy of the trivial boundary modes and results in two red curves in Fig. 4C. These modes mainly vibrate at either the Left or Right end, as shown by the Top and Bottom boxes in Fig. 4C. These two branches of trivial modes can separately couple to the topological modes and form two types of mixed modes, as shown in the two Middle boxes.

Moreover, by employing two different on-site potentials, tonsite=Ax and tonsite=−Ax, we can screen out the trivial boundary modes and preserve the pure nontrivial topological mode only. The modes excited at system edges are referred to as “trivial” because they arise from the physical boundary rather than the topological boundary carefully designed between two bulks with different topological properties. However, these “trivial” modes can be excited alongside the nontrivial topological modes, leading to hybridization. When an excitation excites both trivial and nontrivial boundary modes, after passing one on-site potential region tonsite=Ax, the trivial boundary mode at the Left edge gets screened out due to frequency change by the potential (i.e., one degenerate red curve breaks into two different curves at different frequencies). Similarly, when it continues to pass another potential region tonsite=−Ax, the trivial mode at the right edge gets screened out due to its frequency change as well. Eventually only the nontrivial topological mode in the Middle remains, as shown in Fig. 4D. Thus, the on-site potential manipulation has significance for screening the trivial modes and keeping only the pure nontrivial topological mode for research and application purposes.

We further verify this effect by simulating mechanical networks without (Fig. 4E, Left) and with (Fig. 4E, Right) on-site potential manipulation. The systems are stimulated at 33.7 Hz to excite the topological state (however, multiple nearby states are also excited), and the results are recorded at steady state when t=9.7s. By comparing the transport region within the gray box, it is evident that the on-site potential manipulation results in a more concentrated and pure topological mode excitation. This clearly demonstrates the effectiveness of the on-site potential manipulation.

Inspired by this on-site potential approach, in Fig. 5, we demonstrate another example of manipulating in-gap states using on-site potential, this time within a single bulk material. The onsite potential can be designed by adjusting the on-site spring constant as a function of real space position. By applying linearly varying on-site potentials to the two sublattices, p and q (which have different masses), we show that it is possible to create localized states in specific regions, such as the Left (B), Middle (C), and Right (D) regions. This approach offers significant flexibility for the system, as it can be implemented within a single bulk material without requiring a topological boundary.

In addition, by analyzing the spectrum from (A) to (C), we observe that the flat in-gap states can be changed into curved profile and fill the gap, enabling an insulator-to-conductor-like transition. This example demonstrates that desirable localized states and insulator-to-conductor transitions can be achieved by adjusting the on-site potential within a single bulk material. Our approach is also applicable to various boundary configurations, such as armchair boundary termination (see SI Appendix for details), offering a versatile platform for metamaterial design.

Furthermore, the mechanical on-site potential can be designed in a more flexible manner, as it does not necessarily have to be positive. For instance, negative on-site potential can be achieved by using precompressed springs. By simply applying a constant negative on-site potential in the VHI system, we achieve a downward shift of the ω spectrum, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Negative on-site potential can be achieved using precompressed springs connected to fixed points nearby (see one design in Fig. 6A and more details in SI Appendix). These precompressed springs exert a force that pushes the nodes away from their equilibrium points, thereby creating a negative potential.

Negative potential induces near-zero topological frequency. The edge states of the original structure without negative potential are distributed from 32 to 35 Hz (Fig. 4). (B) Upper panel exhibits the eigenmode spectrum under a constant negative onsite potential, where topological edge states are indicated by red curves. Clearly the curves approach near-zero frequency. The Lower panel shows vibration distribution of a topological state near zero frequency. (C–E) show simulation results of the topological edge states with negative onsite potential. When appropriately chosen, the band gap can be shifted close to 0 Hz. In this scenario, the negative eigenvalues correspond to decaying states, while the positive eigen-spectrum, which is our focus, is shown in Fig. 6B. In this situation, the frequency of the topological edge state is reduced to nearly 0 Hz, compared to 36 Hz in the system without the negative on-site potential. This ultra-low-frequency topological state is achieved across various predesigned boundary patterns, as shown in Fig. 6C–E, demonstrating its broad and robust applicability. Note that such zero-frequency modes are distinct from the “topological zero modes” or “soft/floppy modes” discussed in the previous study (46), which arise due to intrinsic structure. In contrast, the zero-frequency modes in our case are caused by the negative onsite potential.

The results in Fig. 6 show that a simple negative on-site potential enables mechanical topological vibrational states with near-zero frequencies. This approach, based on on-site potential manipulation in the hopping model, offers an effective strategy for designing low-frequency topological metamaterials without increasing system mass, an approach practically desired. By drastically lowering the frequency of topological edge states, it highlights significant potential for low-frequency vibration isolation and damping, offering a promising avenue for designing advanced vibration-damping metamaterials.

Conclusion and Discussion

In conclusion, inspired by the quantum hopping model, we propose a hopping paradigm for constructing mechanical metamaterials. We demonstrated that this approach not only enhances the computational efficiency of mechanical network analysis but also offers fresh perspectives for creating innovative structures through connectivity, hopping interactions, and mechanical on-site potentials.

Beyond ordered structures, our method also provides a promising framework for designing amorphous network metamaterials. For instance, the same connectivity enables the realization of similar topological edge modes in both disordered and ordered systems, and properties akin to those of nematic liquid crystals can be achieved by manipulating anisotropic on-site potentials. Meanwhile, our method offers the possibility of achieving vibration isolation and realizing the nonlinear Valley Hall effect in mechanical systems, which is conducive to the further exploration of these phenomena. Also note that the localized modes we create are fundamentally linear phenomena, built upon the small vibrational approximation. In contrast, gap solitons and other self-localized states (47, 48) are inherently nonlinear phenomena, which are entirely different from our linear modes.

These findings highlight the potential of our hopping-based approach as an exciting and versatile framework for designing mechanical metamaterials in both ordered and disordered configurations. With this framework, we may further explore the effects of onsite damping, driving, and nonlinear effects, which could lead to rich physics and phenomena.
