Prevalence and characteristics of disabilities in physicians in Canada: an analysis of Canadian Census data
Authors: Michael J. Quon, Boris Kralj, Andrew Neitzel, Manish M. Sood, Arthur Sweetman, Maya A. Gibb, Daniel T. Myran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Despite the importance of equity and accessibility with respect to the medical workforce in Canada, limited data exist on physicians with disabilities. We aimed to estimate the prevalence and types of disabilities among physicians and compare them with other occupations.Methods:We performed a population-based cross-sectional study using weighted data from the 2021 mandatory Canadian long-form Census, obtained from Statistics Canada. Participants included those working in Canada, categorized into physicians, non–health care professionals (lawyers, engineers, professors), nurses, and the remaining working population. We calculated the prevalence of self-reported disabilities overall and by type (mental health, visual, hearing, mobility or physical, cognitive or learning) among physicians. We compared variation in disability by age, sex, and specialty. We compared physicians to other occupations using age–sex standardization and calculated adjusted odds ratios (OR).Results:Of 16 468 900 individuals, 89 760 (0.6%) were physicians (mean age [standard deviation] 48.1 yr [13.0], 54.5% male). The prevalence of respondents reporting a disability always or often was 5.0%, including mental health (2.0%), visual (1.8%), hearing (0.7%), mobility or physical (0.6%), and cognitive or learning (0.3%) disabilities. Overall, the prevalence of disability increased with age, primarily owing to increasing hearing and mobility or physical disabilities, whereas mental health disabilities were more common at younger ages. Male physicians (4.5%) reported lower (adjusted OR 0.75, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.71 to 0.80) prevalence of disability than female physicians (6.0%). The age–sex standardized prevalence of disabilities in physicians (5.3%) was lower than in non–health care professionals (7.8%; adjusted OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.44 to 1.53), nurses (9.2%; adjusted OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.69 to 1.79), and the remaining working population (10.8%; adjusted OR 2.21, 95% CI 2.15 to 2.27). When we broadened disability to include impairments reported sometimes, the overall prevalence of disability among physicians increased to 17.0%.Interpretation:One in 20 practising Canadian physicians reports always or often experiencing a disability, which was less than other occupations. These Census-based estimates provide a national benchmark to inform efforts to improve accessibility and sustainability in the physician workforce.

Physician health and well-being are critical to workforce sustainability and patient care. Disability remains an understudied component of physicians’ health.1 Physicians may either enter their training with disability or acquire it during their education or careers. Physicians with disabilities frequently face discrimination, inaccessible workplaces, and inadequate support.2 Not retaining physicians with disabilities in the workforce means losing skilled providers with valuable perspectives,3 negatively affecting human health resources. Improving accessibility in medicine is important.2 However, despite the importance of disability in medicine, limited data exist on the prevalence, types, and demographics of physicians with disabilities or how disability affects physicians’ practices.

Estimates of the prevalence of disability among physicians vary widely, and some derive from highly selected samples. The 2019 National Sample Survey of Physicians (n = 6000) provides the most comprehensive US estimate; 3.1% of physicians reported a disability.4 A convenience sample of 3846 Canadian physicians and residents completed the 2021 National Physician Health Survey, and 20.5% reported having either a disability or a chronic health condition. Disability estimates to date have been limited by nonrepresentative samples with voluntary reporting, heterogeneous disability definitions, limited information on the physician characteristics and, in the case of Canadian data, not disaggregating disability and other chronic health conditions.4–6 Accurate prevalence data are critical for understanding the scale of physician disability and informing interventions in medical education and health system planning.

To address these gaps, we sought to calculate the prevalence of disabilities among physicians in Canada overall and by specific types of disability, to assess variation in disability by age, sex, and specialty among physicians and to compare physicians to other occupations.

Methods

Study design, participants, and data sources

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of all working individuals who completed the Canadian long-form Census in 2021. The Statistics Canada (StatCan) Census is completed every 5 years, response is legally mandatory, and it has a 97.4% response rate.7 The long-form Census, completed by a randomly selected 25% of the population living in Canada, collects information about individual characteristics including sex, age, race or ethnicity, immigration status, education, occupation, residence, and disability.8 All Census estimates are weighted to represent the population living in private dwellings in Canada; the actual number of respondents is not provided by StatCan. Counts were rounded to the nearest 5, based on StatCan release policies.9 We accessed the data through StatCan’s Research Data Centre at McMaster University.

Occupation and respondent characteristics

We analyzed those who responded to occupation questions as physicians, non–health care professionals (professors, lawyers, engineers, which we call “other professionals”), nurses (registered nurses, registered practical nurses, nurse practitioners), and the remainder of the working population. We selected other professionals because their income and education are similar to physicians. We selected nurses for their shared patient care roles, institutions, and clinical workplaces. We determined respondent characteristics based on Census responses: sex (male, female), age (grouped in 5-year intervals), race or ethnicity (White, South Asian, Chinese, Black, Arab, and other), immigrant status (immigrant, not immigrant), and urban (a “population centre” that has a population > 1000 and ≥ 400 people per square kilometre) and rural (outside of a population centre). We analyzed 3 different groups of physicians as reported in the occupation classification of the Census: general practitioners and family physicians (family physicians), specialists in clinical and laboratory medicine (all other physicians), and specialists in surgery (surgeons).

Outcome

Our primary outcome was disability, which was determined by a multistep Census question adapted from the Washington Group Short Set (WGSS), an internationally recognized standard for measuring functional limitations.10,11 For our analysis, we defined “disability” as a difficulty or impairment affecting activities of daily living reported to occur “always” or “often” (other response options were “sometimes” and “no”).12 Similar to previous analyses using the WGSS, we did not include disabilities reported to occur “sometimes,” to improve the specificity of our disability cohort.11 We classified physicians who reported multiple disabilities by the disability with the highest frequency; for example, a physician reporting 1 disability always and another sometimes was considered to always have a disability. Types of disability included mental health (e.g., emotional, anxiety, depression, bipolar, substance abuse, anorexia), visual (difficulty seeing even when wearing glasses or contact lenses), hearing (difficulty hearing even when using a hearing aid), mobility or physical (difficulty walking, using stairs, using hands or fingers, or doing other physical activities), cognitive or learning (difficulty learning, remembering or concentrating) (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260075/tab-related-content).8,11 We included disabilities when impairments lasted or were expected to last for 6 months. In sensitivity analysis, we evaluated disabilities reported “always” and “always, often, and sometimes.” We also examined each disability type individually. We included people with chronic health conditions (e.g., asthma, hypertension, diabetes) in our study cohort; these conditions were not classified as disabilities because not all individuals with a chronic condition experience functional impairment.

Statistical analysis

We estimated descriptive statistics for physicians with disabilities based on the weighted long-form Census data provided by Statistics Canada.13 We determined prevalence estimates by the proportion of physicians, other professionals, nurses, and remaining workers who reported at least 1 disability among each group. We analyzed subgroups by sex, age, race or ethnicity, physician group, and geographic location. We used pairwise comparisons with the χ2 tests of independence to examine whether sociodemographic characteristics differed significantly across physicians reporting disabilities, always or often and never.

We used direct standardization against the general population of Canada in 2021 to generate age–sex standardized prevalence of disabilities for physicians and other occupations. We estimated 2 logistic regressions to calculate odds ratios (OR), with 95% confidence intervals (CI), for each of 6 dependent variables: any disability type, and each of the 5 disability types reported always or often. The first set of regressions compared the prevalence of disability across occupations and was adjusted for age, and sex (with physicians as the referent group). The second set of regressions compared disability prevalence by sex adjusted for age (with female as the referent group), by age adjusted for sex (with age < 35 yr as the referent group), and by specialty adjusted for age and sex (with family physicians as the referent group). We performed statistical analyses using STATA version 19.5 SE and generated figures using RStudio version 2025.09.0.

Ethics approval

Research approval for this project was completed internally at Statistics Canada, and external ethics approval is not required for projects at the Research Data Centre at McMaster University.

Results

We analyzed data representing a weighted population of 16 468 900 individuals who were working in Canada in 2021, of whom 89 760 (0.6%) were physicians (mean [standard deviation] age 48.1 [13.0] yr, 54.5% male). A total of 4460 (5.0%) physicians reported disabilities “always” or “often,” and 10 805 (12.0%) reported disabilities “sometimes” (Table 1). Among all physicians with disabilities reported “always” or “often,” and allowing multiple responses, the most common reported disability was mental health (40.2%), followed by visual (36.7%), hearing (14.8%), mobility or physical (12.8%), and cognitive or learning (6.2%) disabilities. More than 1 disability was reported by 8.7% of physicians with disabilities. Chronic conditions were reported by 65.5% of physicians who reported disabilities “always” or “often,” compared with 13.3% of those reporting disabilities “sometimes” or 14.6% of those never reporting disabilities. We found statistically significant differences in all physician characteristics (p = 0.02 for urban v. rural; p < 0.001 for all others) between physicians reporting disability always or often, sometimes, and never (Table 1). However, some of the differences (e.g., geographic location) were small.

Differences reported in disability by physician subgroups and medical specialty

The overall sex-standardized prevalence of any disability reported “always” or “often” by physicians increased with increasing age, rising from 5.2% in those younger than 35 years to 6.0% in those aged 65 to 69 years (sex-adjusted OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.31) (Table 2 and Figure 1). The prevalence of hearing disabilities increased with age, rising from 0.1% in physicians younger than 35 years to 2.0% in physicians aged 65 to 69 years (adjusted OR 13.16, 95% CI 8.42 to 20.55), and mobility or physical disabilities rising from 0.1% in physicians younger than 35 years to 2.0% in physicians aged 65 to 69 years (adjusted OR 14.47, 95% CI 9.03 to 23.18). Reported mental health disabilities decreased with age, from 3.2% among physicians younger than 35 years to 1.3% in those aged 65 to 69 years (adjusted OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.44). The prevalence of cognitive or learning disabilities (0.4%) and visual disabilities (1.7%) in physicians younger than 35 years did not increase with age.

Prevalence of disability and types of disabilities among physicians by age.

The age–sex standardized prevalence of any disability reported “always” or “often” among physicians varied across specialties, but absolute differences were small. Compared with the disability prevalence in family physicians (5.0%), the prevalence was marginally lower in surgeons, at 4.8% (age- and sex-adjusted OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.00), and marginally higher in all other physicians at 4.9% (adjusted OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.18) (Table 2). The prevalence of mental health (2.10%) and mobility or physical (0.61%) disabilities in family physicians was slightly higher than those reported by surgeons, at 1.85% (adjusted OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.63) and 0.58% (adjusted OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.80). The prevalence of visual (1.8%) and cognitive or learning (0.3%) disabilities in family physicians was not significantly different than in surgeons (1.8%, adjusted OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.19; and 0.29%, adjusted OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.69), whereas hearing disabilities in family physicians (0.7%) were marginally higher than among surgeons (0.7%) (adjusted OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.87). The prevalence of mental health (2.1%) disability in family physicians was marginally higher than in all other physicians (2.0%) (adjusted OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.32). The prevalence of visual (1.8%), hearing (0.7%), mobility or physical (0.6%), and cognitive or learning (0.3%) disabilities in family physicians was not different from all other physicians.

Difference in disability by occupation

The age–sex standardized prevalence of any disability reported as occurring “always” or “often” in physicians (5.3%) was lower than other professionals (7.8%, adjusted OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.44 to 1.53), nurses (9.2%; adjusted OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.69 to 1.79), and the remaining working population (10.8%; adjusted OR 2.21, 95% CI 2.15 to 2.27) (Table 3 and Figure 2). We observed similar patterns for mental health, visual, hearing, mobility or physical, and cognitive or learning disabilities.

Sensitivity analysis

In physicians, the age–sex standardized prevalence of disabilities reported “always” (2.8%) was lower than for other professionals (4.2%; adjusted OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.44 to 1.57), nurses (4.9%; OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.59 to 1.72), and the remaining working population (5.6%; adjusted OR 2.10, 95% CI 2.03 to 2.19). Among physicians with disabilities “always” causing impairment, the most common reported disability was visual (1.3%), followed by mental health (0.7%), mobility or physical (0.5%), hearing (0.3%), and cognitive or learning (0.04%) (Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260075/tab-related-content).

In physicians, the age–sex standardized prevalence of disabilities reported “always,” “often,” or “sometimes” (18.1%) was lower than other professionals (26.2%; adjusted OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.50 to 1.56), nurses (29.4%; adjusted OR 1.80, 95% CI 1.76 to 1.83), and the remaining working population (32.2%; adjusted OR 2.12, 95% CI 2.09 to 2.15) (Appendix 2). When we analyzed any frequency of impairment among physicians, we found that the most common reported disability was mental health (8.1%), followed by visual (5.0%), hearing (4.6%), mobility or physical (4.0%), and cognitive or learning (2.2%) (Appendix 2).

Interpretation

In this study of nationally representative Census data, we found that 5% of working physicians reported a disability “often” or “always,” and 12% reported a disability “some-times.” Hearing and mobility or physical disabilities increased with age, whereas mental health disabilities decreased with age. Disabilities were more prevalent in female than male physicians. Disabilities were less common among physicians than other professionals (professors, lawyers, engineers), nurses, and the general workforce.

Despite the importance of workforce inclusion, voluntary reporting, small sample sizes, or a focus on trainees have limited the available data by which to calculate the prevalence of disability among physicians.4–6,10 We leveraged Canada’s mandatory Census long-form questionnaire (97.4% response rate) to provide a nationally representative, population-based estimate of disability among practising physicians. The 5.0% rate of physicians reporting a disability in our study is higher than that reported in US studies.4,6 This may be partly because the Census uses a functional definition of disability — that is, participants must report difficulty with activities of daily living. The largest US study required self-disclosure of disability, defined by a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits 1 or more major life activities.11 When we limited our disability definition to physicians reporting disability “always,” the prevalence (2.8%) was comparable to reports from the United States, which identified disability in 3% of physicians.4,6 Our prevalence estimate rose to 17% when we included physicians reporting disability “sometimes,” in addition to “always” and “often.” These findings highlight that intermittent disability might be more common in the physician population than previously estimated.

Among physicians, the prevalence of disabilities increased with advancing age, which was related primarily to increasing reports of hearing and mobility or physical disabilities. In contrast, mental health disabilities (the most common disability overall) declined with advancing age. This finding is consistent with previous US survey data14 and may reflect either generational differences in mental health or attrition over time from practice in physicians with mental health disabilities.15 The prevalence of cognitive or learning disabilities — which includes conditions such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, traumatic brain injury, or cognitive impairment — did not increase with age. As this category includes conditions with different age distributions, similar prevalences across physician age groups in our study may reflect higher rates of neurodevelopmental conditions among younger physicians, along with higher frequency of cognitive impairment among older physicians. The absence of an increase in cognitive or learning disabilities with age could reflect either attrition from practice or underreporting owing to stigma or awareness. Although disability rates increased with age, the prevalence in physicians younger than 35 years remained notable (5.18%), most commonly owing to mental health disabilities (3.2%), followed by visual (1.7%) and hearing disabilities (0.1%). These values may more closely approximate the prevalence of disability in medical trainees, although some may have acquired disabilities in early career.

We observed small differences in rates and types of disabilities across physician characteristics and specialties. Surgeons were less likely to report disabilities than family physicians and all other physicians, possibly related to self-selection away from surgical residencies or lower residency match rates.17 Differences related to practice environments, professional demands, or culture could contribute to different health outcomes.18,19 Higher prevalence of disability was reported in females (6.0%) than in males (4.5%) (Table 2). Prevalence of reported disability varied across racial groups, from 3.5% in South Asian physicians to 5.4% in White physicians, which underscores the importance of further understanding how various demographics may affect self-reported disability among physicians. These patterns may reflect the impact of intersectional identities, including differences in stigma, discrimination, barriers,12 or availability of support. Physicians reporting disabilities “always” or “often” also reported chronic health conditions at much higher rates than those without a disability (65.5% v. 14.6%), underscoring additional health needs that accompany persistent disabilities in physicians.

The prevalence of disabilities was lower in physicians than in other professionals with similar levels of education and income, and nurses who share similar patient care roles and workplaces. Despite working in similar clinical environments, nurses may experience greater occupational physical and psychosocial exposures than physicians, potentially contributing to a higher risk of work-related disability.

These findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses restricted to individuals reporting disability “always.” These findings suggest that the lower prevalence of disability in physicians is unlikely to be explained solely by socioeconomic differences or working in a clinical environment. The difference compared with nurses suggests that other factors contribute to the lower prevalence of disability among physicians, such as barriers to enrolment in medical school, better baseline health, or greater workplace attrition in physicians where adequate accommodations may be lacking. In addition, the lower prevalence may reflect underreporting driven by persistent stigma and licensure concerns.16

Disabilities are an important yet often overlooked contributor to physician workforce diversity.2 Ensuring accessible work environments and equitable policies can promote the wellbeing and retention of physicians with disabilities and support those seeking to return to practice.3,22 Beyond benefits to the physicians themselves, advancing accessibility might enhance patient care by fostering a workforce that better reflects the populations it serves.

Limitations

Disability was self-reported, and stigma and professional concerns may have led to underestimates for physicians and other occupations with disclosure concerns.15 Although our disability definition was based on an internationally recognized standard for measuring disability, the WGSS, the definition is intended for population-level comparisons and screening and has not been validated specifically in physicians.11,23 In our study, consistent with WGSS practices, we did not consider respondents reporting disability “some-times” in the primary analysis. Whereas restricting analysis to disability reported “always” or “often” increases specificity, this prevents inclusion of the full spectrum of disability, which leads to an underestimate of individuals with disability.12 We considered anyone reporting a mental health condition as having a disability, but did not include chronic health conditions in our definition of disability because the Census question did not clarify whether the condition was associated with functional impairment. Not all individuals with a mental health condition are disabled, and a chronic health condition can cause disability. These decisions could have biased our estimate of physician disability prevalence in either direction. Other types of disabilities, including all forms of physical disabilities, or neurodivergent conditions, may not be fully captured by the Census questions.10,12,24 Our cross-sectional design did not allow us to address temporal questions, to better understand how the prevalence of physician disability changed over educational and career stages. The Census can be completed in different formats, but ongoing reports indicate persistent barriers for respondents with sensory and cognitive disabilities, possibly contributing to underreporting.25 Finally, the Census captures only working physicians and not those who left the workplace owing to disability, likely resulting in an underestimate of disabilities with greater impairment.

Conclusion

In this national study of working people in Canada, we found physician disability to be reported “always” or “often” by 1 in 20 individuals, with small differences in prevalence by disability type, age, and specialty. Strengthening efforts by health systems, professional organizations, and regulators to dismantle structural barriers and support physicians with disabilities through equitable training, accessible workplaces, and inclusive policies is essential for diversifying the physician workforce. Advancing physician diversity and addressing structural ableism demands coordinated institutional and organizational commitments, alongside system-level reforms at local, provincial, and national levels.
