Extended Reality in Rehabilitation Medicine: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jie Hao, Yao Yao, Ka‐Chun Siu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThis systematic review synthesizes current evidence on the application of extended reality (XR) technologies, comprising virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR), in rehabilitation medicine, assessing their effects across diverse rehabilitation domains and clinical outcomes.MethodsA comprehensive search of three biomedical databases was conducted, yielding 58 randomized controlled trials that examined XR interventions in geriatric, neurological, orthopedic, and pediatric rehabilitation. Methodological quality was assessed using the Physiotherapy Evidence Database scale. Results were qualitatively synthesized according to intervention characteristics and outcome measures.ResultsThe findings indicate that XR technologies can enhance rehabilitation outcomes, including improvements in motor and cognitive function, pain management, psychological well‐being, and quality of life. XR interventions were shown to improve balance, strength, and cognitive and psychological function in older adults; facilitate motor recovery and reduce neuropathic pain in neurological populations; support postoperative rehabilitation and conservative interventions in orthopedic patients; and enhance engagement and therapeutic goals attainment in pediatric patients.ConclusionXR technologies present a promising and innovative approach to rehabilitation, offering personalized and engaging therapeutic experiences. Clinicians should leverage XR technologies to empower patients and optimize clinical outcomes.Keywords: function, health, immersion, physiatry, simulation, therapy

Introduction

The rapid evolution of science, technology, and engineering has revolutionized human society and catalyzed significant innovations in healthcare. Artificial intelligence and simulation technologies are increasingly being developed and adopted in clinical research and practice to improve access and quality of healthcare. Among them, extended reality (XR) is a burgeoning field. XR is defined as the spectrum of computer‐generated immersive technologies, which encompasses virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR) [1]. VR allows users to be completely immersed in a simulated environment by using an interactive device and a head‐mounted display. AR superimposes digital components to the real world, and users can view and interact with virtual objects and information as if they were in the real‐world. MR connects the physical and virtual worlds and allows digital objects to interact with their surroundings. According to a report by the International Market Analysis Research and Consulting Group [2], the global market size of XR in healthcare reached USD 6.0 billion in 2023 and is expected to have a compound annual growth rate of 29.02% from 2024 to 2032.

Rehabilitation medicine aims to restore function, reduce disability, and improve the quality of life in individuals with various health conditions. People with cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, neurological, or other chronic diseases may suffer from short‐ to long‐term impairments and functional deficits that impede independence in personal life and social participation. Rehabilitation is an essential component of healthcare, and it was estimated that 2.4 billion people with disabilities have demands for rehabilitation [3]. In addition, in light of the global aging population with an increased burden of disability related to chronic diseases, the need for rehabilitation is projected to grow in the next decades [4]. Given the growing and unmet needs for rehabilitation [5], it is imperative to incorporate the advances in the state‐of‐the‐art technologies into rehabilitation medicine to optimize function and promote the well‐being of people with disabilities.

In rehabilitation medicine, XR technologies provide a unique platform for enhancing physical and cognitive recovery. Conventional rehabilitation relies on intensive physical, occupational, and speech therapies which involve repetitive therapeutic exercises or task‐specific training delivered by therapists. For many patients and their families, participating in rehabilitation programs can be a long‐term and tedious process. XR holds the promise to transform conventional rehabilitation paradigms by enabling patients to perform a variety of interactive activities that are customized to patients’ needs in an immersive environment [6]. Patients can receive real‐time multimodal feedback on their performance, facilitating skill acquisition and functional recovery. By creating a novel rehabilitation experience, XR can increase patients’ motivation and engagement, improving adherence to therapeutic regimens [7]. Therapists can leverage XR's capacity to produce adaptable and patient‐specific environments to foster the efficiency and effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions. Besides, XR plays an instrumental role in pain management by diverting patients’ attention from painful movements or procedures to relaxing and aesthetic environments [8], improving patients’ comfort and tolerance to rehabilitation.

Although VR, AR, and MR differ in their levels of immersion, interaction with the physical environment, and technological implementation, they can be conceptualized as part of the broader XR continuum. These technologies share common therapeutic principles relevant to rehabilitation, including immersive visualization, interactive task‐based training, and the provision of real‐time multimodal feedback to facilitate motor and cognitive recovery. Synthesizing VR, AR, and MR within a unified XR framework allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how immersive technologies are applied across diverse rehabilitation contexts. Recent literature has reviewed the application of XR to various medical specialties, including cardiology [9], spine medicine [10], radiology [11], ophthalmology [12], orthopedic surgery [13], neurosurgery [14], and primary care [15]. However, no reviews have systematically investigated how XR can be used in rehabilitation medicine. This systematic review aims to identify, critically appraise, and synthesize current evidence regarding the application of XR technologies in rehabilitation medicine, with a focus on clinical outcomes, such as motor and cognitive function, mood, pain reduction, functional recovery, and quality of life. By synthesizing the available literature, this review can provide insights into the therapeutic potential of XR, and inform future development and implementation of XR in rehabilitation medicine.

Methods

Reporting of this systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [16]. The protocol of this systematic review was registered at the International prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42024604134.

Three bibliographic databases, PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, were searched using strategies designed for each database. The search strategies of PubMed are described in the Appendix. Databases were searched from inception to December 30, 2024. The search strategies combined controlled vocabularies and title/abstract keywords on search themes of XR, VR, AR, MR, and rehabilitation. The literature search was limited to human studies reported in English in peer‐reviewed journal articles. Records retrieved from each database were then exported to Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd., Melbourne, Australia) for duplication removal.

The inclusion criteria of this systematic review were generated based on the PICOS framework [17]: (1) Population: patients with a disability or functional deficit who are candidates for rehabilitation services; there were no restrictions on age, gender, or diagnosis; (2) Intervention: XR technologies (VR, AR, or MR) were used for rehabilitation; (3) Comparison: conventional rehabilitation, usual care, or waitlist control; (4) Outcome: performance‐based or patient‐reported outcome measures related to motor, sensory, cognitive, or perceptual function; psychological outcomes; activities of daily living ability and functional independence; disability; quality of life; pain; or biomechanical parameters; and (5) Study design: longitudinal experimental studies designed as randomized controlled trials. Studies were excluded in this systematic review if they met any of the following criteria: (1) they were evaluated as fair or poor in quality assessment; (2) study design was a qualitative study, case study, cross‐sectional laboratory‐based study, or research protocol; or (3) study was conference proceeding or abstract‐only.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for potential eligibility and reviewed full‐text articles to determine final eligibility. For each included study, they extracted data on participant characteristics, sample size, intervention in the experimental and control groups, outcome measures, and main findings. Throughout this process, any discrepancies were resolved through group discussion with a third senior reviewer until a consensus was reached.

For each randomized controlled trial, the risk of bias was assessed by the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale, a valid and reliable tool used to evaluate the methodological quality of clinical trials in rehabilitation medicine literature to inform clinical decision‐making [18]. There are 11 items on this scale: Item 1 is classified as “yes or no,” and items 2–11 each count for one point, resulting in a total score range of 0–10. Higher scores indicate better study quality, with scores of 9–10 commonly interpreted as reflecting excellent, 6–8 as good, 4–5 as fair, and 0–3 as poor quality [19]. Two reviewers independently assessed the quality of each included study and reached a consensus with the third reviewer on any identified discrepancies in their rating. We decided to exclude studies with low‐quality (fair or poor as assessed by the PEDro) from data synthesis in priori to avoid the risk of bias.

We qualitatively evaluated clinical and methodological heterogeneity among included studies and the risk of biases that could impact the interpretation of the evidence. We qualitatively synthesized included studies according to domains of rehabilitation medicine (geriatric, neurological, orthopedic, and pediatric rehabilitation) and types of outcome measures to summarize the effects of XR interventions on clinical outcomes in different populations. We decided a priori not to perform a meta‐analysis due to substantial heterogeneity across studies. Specifically, heterogeneity was observed in XR intervention characteristics (e.g., type of technology, hardware platforms, and training protocols), intervention dosage and duration, patient populations with diverse clinical conditions and severity levels, and outcome measures spanning motor, cognitive, psychological, and functional domains.

Results

A total of 6175 records were identified from the three bibliographic databases. After removing duplicates, 4499 remaining records were screened by titles and abstracts. Subsequently, 164 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 106 studies were excluded due to wrong intervention (n = 85), wrong outcomes (n = 7), wrong study design (n = 3), and inadequate methodological quality (n = 11). Ultimately, 58 articles were included in this systematic review. The PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1) delineates the study identification process and reasons for excluding articles.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only.

The 58 included randomized controlled trials report the application of XR in geriatric, neurological, orthopedic, and pediatric rehabilitation. In particular, neurological conditions include Parkinson's disease, spinal cord injury, stroke, and vestibular dysfunction. Orthopedic conditions include neck pain and post‐operative rehabilitation. Among the 58 studies, 42 used VR, 13 used AR, and 3 used MR. XR technologies were incorporated into rehabilitation programs for upper extremity, lower extremity, balance and postural control, mobility and gait, kinematic, cognitive, perceptual, and vestibular‐specific training. For outcome measures, performance‐based outcomes included measures such as range of motion, balance ability, postural and gait parameters, and cognitive and perceptual performance, whereas patient‐reported outcomes included pain, psychological distress, self‐efficacy, balance confidence, and self‐reported health and functional status. Half of these studies assessed the occurrence of adverse events, and 10 reported adverse events related to XR, including headache, dizziness, motion sickness, fatigue, and eye symptoms. Lee et al. [20] reported a participant with an ischemic stroke due to vertebral artery had a transient ischemic attack in the AR group after 3 weeks of training. The 58 included randomized controlled trials were scored 6–8 on the PEDro scale. Less than half of studies (n = 22) performed concealed allocation. Nine studies did not have adequate follow‐up (key outcome measures were obtained from less than 85% of the participants from initial allocation), and 10 did not use the intention‐to‐treat analysis. Geriatric Rehabilitation

Ten randomized controlled trials investigated the effects of XR in geriatric rehabilitation. Two [22, 23] studies reported that VR exergaming program as an add‐on intervention to usual care significantly improved balance ability as measured by the Tinetti scale; in addition, one [23] of them demonstrated the significant effects of VR exergaming on functional strength (measured by the Five times sit‐to‐stand test) and mobility (measured by the Timed Up and Go test). Lima et al. [26] reported that for older adults with balance deficits, VR exergaming produced similar benefits on balance‐related outcomes (static and dynamic balance, functional reach, dizziness, and fear of falling) as dose‐matched conventional multimodal circuit exercises. Furthermore, Qiu et al. [29] investigated the application of VR to enhance Tai Chi among 300 community‐dwelling older adults. They found that compared to traditional Tai Chi, VR‐enhanced Tai Chi exhibited a significantly greater reduction of depression as measured by the Geriatric Depression Scale.

Three studies used AR‐based exercise programs for community‐dwelling older adults. Ku et al. [25] reported that the AR group demonstrated significantly better improvements in balance (measured by the Berg Balance Scale), mobility (measured by the Timed Up and Go test), and fall risk index (measured by posturography). Similarly, Jeon et al. [24] showed significant improvements in favor of AR on balance, lower extremity strength, mobility, endurance, and exercise self‐efficacy. Besides, Zhu et al. [30] reported that a 4‐week AR memory training program significantly improved working memory among Chinese healthy older adults.

Three studies [21, 27, 28] focused on older adults with cognitive and memory impairment. Optale et al. [27] compared VR memory training to dose‐matched music therapy among older adults with memory deficits. They found that the VR memory training group demonstrated significant improvements in verbal memory, long‐term recall, and global cognition; whereas the control group showed progressive decline. Similarly, Park et al. [28] reported that MR‐based cognitive training was superior to dose‐matched computer‐assisted cognitive training in improving visuospatial working memory. Appel et al. [21] applied VR to patients with dementia in an acute care hospital. VR films were displayed to patients for up to 20 min every 1–3 days to promote nature visualization and psychological well‐being. VR was overall acceptable and enjoyable to patients. Results found that VR had a significant effect on reducing aggressiveness, including physical aggression and loud vociferation, while the effects on other behaviors and psychological symptoms, falls, length of stay, and quality of life were comparable to standard care.

Neurological Rehabilitation

Thirty‐one randomized controlled trials investigated the effects of XR in neurological rehabilitation. Neurological conditions included Parkinson's disease, spinal cord injury, stroke, and vestibular dysfunction.

Gulcan et al. [71] used an AR‐augmented treadmill for gait training among patients with Parkinson's disease. The study group received AR gait training combined with conventional therapy, and the control group received conventional therapy only. The training was applied to both groups three sessions per week for a total of 6 weeks. They found that both groups showed similar amounts of improvement in the Berg Balance Scale, the Activity‐Specific Balance Confidence Scale, but the AR group demonstrated more improvements on spatiotemporal gait parameters. Another study by Schuch et al. [31] reported that a 5‐week VR balance training program did not produce significantly different outcomes in functional mobility compared to a structured exercise program for Parkinson's disease.

Three studies applied VR to motor rehabilitation for patients with incomplete spinal cord injury. An et al. [32] reported that VR soccer games significantly improved the results of the chair stand test, Timed Up and Go test, 10‐meter walk test, and kick speed test compared to conventional rehabilitation. Goel et al. [72] reported that VR training produced significantly improved sitting balance compared to functional electrical stimulation. For upper extremity function [33], VR demonstrated significant improvements in grip and pinch strength, and muscle strength of elbow and wrist extensors. One study [73] used VR to manage neuropathic pain experienced by patients with spinal cord injury. Participants reported significantly lower pain intensity after viewing nature scenes in VR compared to 2D laptop screen application; an increased level of presence was associated with greater pain reduction.

Thirteen studies used VR for stroke rehabilitation. The majority of these studies used VR interventions for the upper extremity and hand. VR‐based mirror therapy demonstrated significantly improved manual dexterity than conventional rehabilitation [39, 74]. Four studies [34, 38, 42, 43] reported that VR significantly promoted motor function of the upper extremity. Conversely, Crosbie et al. [36] and Huang et al. [40] reported no significant difference in upper extremity function between the VR and conventional therapy groups. Ogun et al. [43] found significant between‐group differences in self‐care skills (measured by Performance Assessment of Self‐care Skills) but not functional independence (Functional Independence Measure). For lower extremity function, Kang et al. [68] reported that VR treadmill training improved balance and gait significantly compared to regular treadmill training, and Pelaez‐Velez et al. [44] reported that VR exergames significantly improved trunk control, lower extremity, balance, and mobility. Moreover, two studies [35, 45] found VR‐based spatial neglect training more effective than conventional rehabilitation. For patients with mild post‐stroke cognitive impairment, Liu et al. [63] reported that VR puzzle game therapy demonstrated significant improvements in executive and visual‐spatial function.

Six studies used AR for stroke rehabilitation. Evron et al. [37] employed AR mobility exercises with the Selfit system, which projected different scenarios to the floor and captured patients’ movements with motion sensors. They reported that the AR group demonstrated significantly better improvement in the Timed Up and Go test; both therapists and patients demonstrated high levels of satisfaction with the AR system. Similarly, AR postural control training [41] yielded significantly better gait parameters than conventional rehabilitation; AR gait adaptation training [47] demonstrated superior effects on obstacle avoidance, balance ability, and functional mobility compared to regular training. Lee et al. [20] found that an AR‐enhanced home exercise program produced better outcomes of balance and fear of falling compared to a written and pictorial home exercise program. Li et al. [69] reported that adding a game‐based cellphone AR system training to conventional occupational therapy was effective in improving both upper extremity and cognitive function. Furthermore, Stammler et al. [46] used AR‐based active exploration training for patients with unilateral spatial neglect, and they found that the AR training produced significantly better outcomes than standard therapy, which remained stable for 2 months after the completion of treatment.

One study used MR for stroke rehabilitation. Duff et al. [67] developed the Adaptive MR Rehabilitation system for upper extremity training, which provided precise kinematic measurements and engaged multimodal feedback for self‐assessment during therapeutic activities. They found that both the MR and control groups demonstrated improved outcomes on upper extremity function scales, while only the MR group showed a consistent improvement in the kinematic parameters of reaching, indicating better quality of movement.

For vestibular dysfunction, studies used VR for benign paroxysmal positional vertigo [48, 51] and unilateral vestibular hypofunction [49, 50, 70]. Both Ozdil et al. [48] and Yan et al. [51] reported that VR was effective in mitigating the residual symptoms, including balance impairments and dizziness, after the canalith repositioning procedure. A VR‐based vestibular exercise program, including substitution, habituation, and balance exercises, demonstrated improvements in vestibular‐ocular reflex, balance ability, and dizziness symptoms in both short‐ [70] and long‐term [50]. Stankiewicz et al. [49] reported that VR vestibular rehabilitation was as effective as conventional vestibular rehabilitation, with significantly higher levels of patient satisfaction.

Orthopedic Rehabilitation

Eleven randomized controlled trials investigated the effects of XR in orthopedic rehabilitation. Among them, five studies used VR for neck pain. Cetin et al. [75] compared VR training to motor control exercises for chronic neck pain. Compared to the control group, the VR group demonstrated more positive effects on exercise compliance, balance control, and kinesiophobia. Similarly, Tereja et al. [55] reported that VR demonstrated significant effects in reducing kinesiophobia; while there were no significant between‐group differences in pain intensity, neck function, and psychological distress (pain catastrophizing, fear‐avoidance beliefs, and anxiety). Two studies compared VR‐based kinetic training to conventional kinetic training for chronic neck pain, at an in‐clinic and a home‐based setting, respectively. For the in‐clinic training study [52], both VR and control demonstrated significant improvements in neck pain intensity, neck function, and range of motion throughout the intervention, with only a few between‐group differences detected. For the home‐based training study [53], VR demonstrated significant advantages in cervical motion velocity, pain intensity, health status, and movement accuracy. Another study applied self‐VR kinetic training to pilots with flight‐associated neck pain [54]. It found that compliance of the participants was poor, and no differences were observed in self‐reported measures related to neck pain and cervical strength.

Four studies focused on postoperative rehabilitation. Shim et al. [56, 57] used AR to augment home exercise programs and applied it to patients post‐total knee arthroplasty [56] and rotator cuff repair [57]. In both studies, the AR‐based home exercise program was compared with the conventional home exercise program with a brochure. They found that all physical and function‐related outcomes were significantly improved in both groups for patients post‐total knee arthroplasty, with no significant between‐group differences. For patients post‐rotator cuff repair, AR showed better improvement in shoulder function. Mazurek et al. [76] reported that for older adults post‐total hip or knee arthroplasty, eight VR sessions demonstrated significantly reduced psychological distress and improved functional independence. Furthermore, for older adults post‐total knee arthroplasty, VR balance training produced better early balance ability and knee function compared to the control group [58].

Two studies used VR exergames for conservative management of orthopedic conditions. Ozlu et al. [59] reported that the addition of VR training produced significant improvements in pain reduction, lower extremity function, and balance ability for patients with osteoarthritis. For patients with adhesive capsulitis [60], VR exercises combined with joint mobilization demonstrated significantly better outcomes in pain and range of motion compared to joint mobilization only.

Pediatric Rehabilitation

Six randomized controlled trials investigated the effects of XR in pediatric rehabilitation. Four studies used XR for upper extremity rehabilitation. Bortone et al. [61] reported for children with neuromotor impairments (cerebral palsy and developmental dyspraxia), VR significantly improved manual dexterity and movement quality of upper extremity. Similarly, for children with hemiplegic cerebral palsy, VR‐mediated gamified rehabilitation significantly improved upper extremity function and quality of life [65]. For children with hand burns [62], the intervention group performed AR‐enhanced exercises with a printed booklet, and the control group received a printed booklet only. Results revealed that the AR group demonstrated significantly better hand function as measured by the Jebsen‐Taylor hand function test. Furthermore, Malick et al. [64] allocated children with hemiplegic cerebral palsy into three groups, each group receiving an AR game. Following 8 weeks of intervention, all the groups demonstrated significant improvements in upper extremity range of motion and muscle strength.

Two studies used XR in cognitive training programs. Shen et al. [66] developed an innovative VR interactive cognitive training for executive function rehabilitation and examined its feasibility and efficacy among children with traumatic brain injuries. Participants reported adequate satisfaction with this system regarding pleasure and motivation; low levels of physical exertion and simulator sickness. Results also supported the efficacy of this system, particularly for children with moderate and severe brain injuries. Liu et al. [77] presented an MR aquarium, which enhanced a real aquarium with a virtual underwater environment, to children with autism spectrum disorder. They found that MR training can effectively attract and maintain the attention of children, bring better ease of use, provide multi‐sensory feedback, and assist cognitive rehabilitation.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence regarding the application of XR technologies, comprising VR, AR, and MR, in rehabilitation medicine. The 58 included randomized controlled trials cover a wide range of patient populations, interventions, and clinical outcomes across geriatric, neurological, orthopedic, and pediatric rehabilitation. More studies used VR than AR and MR. XR can be implemented in all stages of rehabilitation in different settings, including acute care hospitals, inpatient and outpatient rehabilitation, residential care facilities, and patients’ homes. Overall, the findings indicate that XR has the potential to enhance various aspects of rehabilitation outcomes, including motor and cognitive function, pain management, post‐surgical recovery, psychological well‐being, and quality of life.

The results of this review highlight several promising applications of XR in rehabilitation. In geriatric populations, XR demonstrated substantial benefits in improving balance, strength, mobility, and cognitive function. These findings align with previous research supporting the positive effects of serious games on physical [78] and cognitive [79] aspects of older adults. XR provides immersive and interactive environments that enhance the effectiveness of game‐based learning and training processes, fostering engagement and motivation for rehabilitation interventions. A study [80] demonstrated that gamified interventions can be used to improve the motivation for rehabilitation and facilitate the transition from hospital to home for older adults. Moreover, XR's capacity to improve psychological outcomes, such as reducing depression through VR‐enhanced Tai Chi [29], underscores its potential to address the mental health needs of geriatric patients. However, it should be recognized that older adults may encounter potential barriers when using XR in rehabilitation, such as visual and hearing deficits, limited digital literacy, psychological barriers, and resistance to change. It is crucial to take these factors into consideration through adaptive design and customization to make XR more inclusive and effective for older adults.

Neurological rehabilitation, which accounted for the largest body of evidence in this review, demonstrated that XR interventions can facilitate recovery of motor function in stroke, spinal cord injury, and Parkinson's disease; reduce neuropathic pain in spinal cord injury; mitigate cognitive and perceptual deficits in stroke; and enhance treatment effects of vestibular dysfunction. Current neuroscience research suggests rehabilitation professionals use timely, intensive, and highly repetitive task‐specific practices to harness experience‐dependent neural plasticity and promote functional independence [81]. Compared to conventional rehabilitation approaches that can be tedious and labor‐intensive, XR technologies open the door to immersive and engaging therapeutic experiences that enable highly controlled environments for targeted task practice and skill relearning. Moreover, XR technologies can support therapists by reducing their workload, as they allow for self‐directed rehabilitation sessions [20] that patients can engage in independently or with minimal supervision. Therapists can use XR to monitor progress, track patient performance, and adjust treatment plans based on clinical outcome data, enabling them to focus more on personalized interventions and strategic modifications. Furthermore, A systematic review [82] synthesizes the effects of VR interventions on cortical reorganization in stroke rehabilitation, elucidating the underlying neurophysiological mechanism of VR. Future studies should investigate the effects of AR and MR on neural plasticity to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of their mechanism in neurological rehabilitation.

In orthopedic rehabilitation, VR and AR interventions were primarily used to aid postoperative rehabilitation and facilitate conservative management of chronic musculoskeletal conditions including neck pain, knee osteoarthritis, and frozen shoulder. Overall, VR demonstrates positive effects on reducing pain and disability among patients with chronic neck pain, consistent with the findings in a previous systematic review [83]. However, for a group of pilots with flight‐associated neck pain, there were no significant differences between self‐VR kinetic training and control groups in symptom improvement due to high baseline kinematic performance and low adherence [54]. The negative findings of this study indicate the importance of considering specific impairments, devising targeted interventions, and securing patients’ compliance to attain optimal outcomes. An AR‐based digital rehabilitation system (UINCARE Home+) demonstrated positive outcomes in patients post‐total knee arthroplasty [56] and rotator cuff tear [57]. The AR system provides different levels of therapeutic exercises based on the postoperative timeframe, and uses a three‐dimensional depth camera to capture patients’ movements and provides real‐time feedback. Therapists can leverage these technological advantages to improve efficiency in remote therapeutic monitoring.

Pediatric rehabilitation also showed positive outcomes with XR, particularly in improving upper extremity function in children with neuromotor impairments and enhancing cognitive function in children with brain injuries or autism spectrum disorders. Immersive and gamified exercises can help children achieve therapeutic goals through interactive tasks that keep them engaged, supporting more sustained participation than traditional therapy alone. The external focus of attention provided by XR can facilitate intrinsic motivation and implicit motor learning [84]. Studies have shown that it was feasible to implement an intensive bimanual training program in VR for children with hemiplegic cerebral palsy [85], and apply the training program in the home setting [86]. While developing XR rehabilitation programs for children, it is important to consider the active ingredients of computer play‐based interventions [87], including intensive practice, task specificity, flexibility to individualization, feedback, social play equalization, neural plasticity changes, problem‐solving, motivation, and external support. A thoughtfully designed XR program also needs to balance the entertainment feature and the therapeutic effectiveness of the technology to maximize its acceptance and utility in the pediatric population.

The findings from this review have important implications for clinical practice. XR technologies offer an innovative, patient‐centered approach to rehabilitation that can be tailored to individual characteristics, providing augmented feedback, and promoting active patient engagement. For clinicians, the ability to use XR to simulate various therapeutic scenarios or exercises, while simultaneously tracking patient performance, can enhance the precision and efficiency of rehabilitation interventions. Importantly, safety and tolerability should be carefully considered when implementing XR‐based rehabilitation interventions. Across the included studies, adverse events were typically reported passively rather than systematically assessed using predefined monitoring procedures. These safety considerations may be particularly important for older adults and individuals with neurological disorders, who may be more susceptible to cybersickness, sensory conflict, or balance instability when exposed to immersive environments. Accordingly, future clinical implementation of XR interventions should incorporate structured safety screening, monitoring protocols, and appropriate supervision to ensure safe and effective use in rehabilitation settings.

Practical implementation issues should also be considered when integrating XR technologies into clinical practice. Clinicians may require training to operate XR systems, interpret system‐generated feedback, and incorporate XR‐based exercises into individualized rehabilitation programs. Infrastructure requirements, including compatible hardware, technical support, and adequate space, may influence the feasibility of XR deployment in clinical settings. In addition, integrating XR interventions into existing clinical workflows requires careful planning to ensure that these technologies complement routine therapy. Adoption may also be limited in low‐resource or rural settings where access to advanced equipment and technical expertise is restricted. Improving accessibility may depend on the development of affordable XR technologies and implementation models adapted to resource‐limited healthcare environments.

Future research should further clarify the role of XR in rehabilitation across different clinical contexts. Key research priorities include identifying which patient populations may benefit most from XR interventions and determining the most relevant outcome measures for evaluating their effectiveness. In addition, there is a need to standardize the reporting of XR intervention characteristics, including hardware, training protocols, and dosage parameters, to improve comparability across studies. Comparative effectiveness studies are also warranted to evaluate differences between VR, AR, and MR modalities. Furthermore, future work should examine the cost‐effectiveness of XR‐based rehabilitation and investigate long‐term outcomes to better understand the sustainability of treatment effects.

This systematic review has some limitations. First, the number of included studies and associated sample sizes are limited, especially for studies using AR and MR. Further high‐quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to investigate the specific effects of different XR technologies to inform clinical practice. Second, only articles in English were searched and included in this review. Potentially eligible studies in other languages could be missed. Third, studies rated as “fair” or “poor” on the PEDro scale were excluded a priori to reduce the risk of bias and improve the methodological rigor of the synthesized evidence. However, this decision may introduce selection bias and potentially limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, fewer than half of the studies performed concealed allocation, and most studies did not blind participants or therapists, which could introduce potential bias. In addition, some studies had incomplete follow‐up or did not use intention‐to‐treat analysis. These methodological limitations may lead to an overestimation of treatment effects and should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review.

Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates the significant potential of XR technologies, including VR, AR, and MR, to enhance rehabilitation outcomes across diverse patient populations and clinical contexts. The evidence suggests that XR may improve motor and cognitive functions, pain management, psychological well‐being, and overall quality of life, with promising applications identified in geriatric, neurological, orthopedic, and pediatric rehabilitation. Overall, XR technologies offer a transformative approach to rehabilitation that emphasizes patient‐centered care and enhances therapeutic engagement, demonstrating the potential to redefine rehabilitation practices and improve outcomes for diverse patient populations.
