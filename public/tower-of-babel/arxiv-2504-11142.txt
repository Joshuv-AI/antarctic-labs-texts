On the dimension of the boundaries of attracting basins of entire maps
Krzysztof Barański and Bogusława Karpińska and David Martí-Pete and Leticia Pardo-Simón and Anna Zdunik

Abstract
. We study the dimension of the boundaries of periodic Fatou components of transcendental entire maps. We prove that if U U is an immediate component of the basin of an attracting periodic point ζ \zeta of period p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 of a transcendental entire function f : ℂ → ℂ f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C} from the Eremenko–Lyubich class ℬ \mathcal{B} , such that deg ⁡ f p | U = ∞ \deg f^{p}|_{U}=\infty and Sing ⁡ ( f p | U ) ¯ \overline{\Sing(f^{p}|_{U})} is a compact subset of U U , then the hyperbolic (and, consequently, Hausdorff) dimension of the boundary of U U is larger than 1 1 . The same holds if U U is an immediate component of the basin of a parabolic p p -periodic point ζ \zeta , under an additional assumption ζ ∉ Sing ⁡ ( f p ) ¯ \zeta\notin\overline{\Sing(f^{p})} . We also show that if U U is a bounded immediate component of an attracting basin of a transcendental entire function f f , then the hyperbolic dimension of the boundary of U U is larger than 1 1 . In particular, this implies that the boundary of a component of an attracting basin of a transcendental entire function is never a smooth or rectifiable curve.



1.
Introduction and results

For an entire function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, its set of stability
\mathcal{F}(f)
, known as the
Fatou set
, is defined as the set of points
z\in\mathbb{C}
, for which the sequence
\{f^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of the forward iterates of
f
forms a normal family in some neighbourhood of
z
; the
Julia set

J(f)
, which is the locus of chaotic behaviour of
f
, is the complement of
\mathcal{F}(f)
. The set
\mathcal{F}(f)
is open, and each of its connected components is called a
Fatou component
of
f
. We say that
U
is a
periodic
Fatou component of period
p\geq 1
, if
f^{p}(U)\subset U
, and
U
is
invariant
if
f(U)\subset U
. If
U
is a Fatou component which is never mapped into a periodic component by a forward iterate of
f
, then we say that
U
is a
wandering domain
. In this paper we study the dimension of the boundaries of Fatou components, which are parts of
attracting
or
parabolic basins
of periodic points of
f
. Recall that a periodic point
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
with
f^{p}(\zeta)=\zeta
for some
p\geq 1
is
attracting
if
|(f^{p})^{\prime}(\zeta)|<1
and
parabolic
if
|(f^{p})^{\prime}(\zeta)|=e^{2\pi i\theta}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
. The
attracting
(resp.
parabolic
)
basin
of
\zeta
is defined as the set of points
z\in\mathbb{C}
, such that
f^{pn}(z)\to\zeta
as
n\to\infty
. An
immediate
component
U
of an attracting (resp. parabolic) basin is a (periodic) component such that
U
(resp. the boundary of
U
) contains an element of the orbit of
\zeta
. We denote the boundary of
U
by
\partial U
.

The Hausdorff dimension (denoted by
\dim_{H}
) of the Julia sets of transcendental entire maps has been widely studied, starting from the seminal paper by McMullen
[
29
]
on the exponential family
E_{\lambda}(z)=\lambda e^{z}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
By a result of Baker
[
3
]
, the boundary of every Fatou component
U
of a transcendental entire function
f
contains non-trivial continua, so
\dim_{H}\partial U\geq 1
, and therefore
\dim_{H}J(f)\geq 1
. It was a long-standing open question whether there exists a transcendental entire map
f
with
\dim_{H}J(f)=1
. This was answered affirmatively by Bishop
[
17
]
, who constructed a transcendental entire function with a multiply connected wandering domain
U
, whose boundary consists of countably many
C^{1}
Jordan curves and satisfies
\dim_{H}\partial U=1
. In
[
37
,
40
]
, Stallard presented examples of transcendental entire maps
f
with
\dim_{H}J(f)=t
for every given
t\in(1,2)
. As in
[
29
]
it was proved that
\dim_{H}J(E_{\lambda})=2
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, one can see that the Hausdorff dimension of the Julia set of a transcendental entire function can attain every value in
[1,2]
.

The functions studied in
[
37
,
40
]
, as well as the exponential maps, belong to the well-known
Eremenko–Lyubich class

\mathcal{B}
, that consists of all transcendental entire maps
f
, for which the closure of the set of its critical and asymptotic values, denoted by
\Sing(f)
, is bounded. In
[
38
]
, Stallard proved that
\dim_{H}J(f)>1
for every
f\in\mathcal{B}
. Note that
\dim_{H}J(f)=2
for
f\in\mathcal{B}
with a finite, or ‘not too fast infinite’ order of growth, as proved in
[
6
,
14
,
36
]
.

In the context of the dynamics of holomorphic maps, it is natural to consider also another kind of dimension, mainly
hyperbolic dimension
. Recall that the hyperbolic dimension of a set
A\subset J(f)
is given by

\dim_{hyp}A=\sup\{\dim_{H}X\colon X\subset A\textup{ is an expanding conformal repeller}\}.

Here, by an expanding conformal repeller we mean a compact forward-invariant set
X
such that
f
is
uniformly expanding
on
X
, that is, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
|(f^{k})^{\prime}|_{X}|>1
. Obviously,
\dim_{hyp}A\leq\dim_{H}A
. However, the two dimensions need not coincide. Indeed, there exist transcendental entire maps
f
, for which
\dim_{hyp}J(f)
is an arbitrary small (positive) number (see
[
11
]
).
In
[
41
]
, Urbański and Zdunik proved that for exponential maps
E_{\lambda}
with a periodic attracting point, there holds
\dim_{hyp}J(E_{\lambda})<\dim_{H}J(E_{\lambda})=2
. In fact, the hyperbolic dimension of the Julia set of a hyperbolic
1
1

1

A transcendental entire map
f
is
hyperbolic
, if the set
\overline{\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}f^{n}(\Sing(f))}
is bounded and disjoint from
J(f)
.
transcendental entire map
f
is equal to the unique zero of the topological pressure function
t\mapsto P(f,t)
, as proved in
[
10
]
, which enables to find more examples of this kind (see e.g.
[
39
]
). On the other hand, there are examples of hyperbolic transcendental entire maps
f
, where
\dim_{hyp}J(E_{\lambda})=\dim_{H}J(E_{\lambda})=2
(see
[
35
]
).

Much less is known about the Hausdorff and hyperbolic dimension of the boundaries of particular Fatou components, which are subsets of the Julia set. Note that if a Fatou component
U
is completely invariant, then the Julia set of
f
coincides with the boundary of
U
and, consequently,
\dim_{H}J(f)=\dim_{H}\partial U
. Such a situation occurs, for example, within the exponential family, if the map
E_{\lambda}
has a fixed attracting point
\zeta
, which happens e.g. for
\lambda\in(0,1/e)
. Recall that in this case
\dim_{H}J(f)=\dim_{H}\partial U=2
, where
U
is the attracting basin of
\zeta
.

In
[
9
]
, Barański, Karpińska and Zdunik proved that if the exponential map
E_{\lambda}
has a periodic attracting point
\zeta
of period
p>1
, then
1<\dim_{H}\partial U<\dim_{H}J(E_{\lambda})=2
for an immediate component
U
of the attracting basin of
\zeta
. However, outside the exponential family, little is known on the dimension of the boundaries of Fatou components of transcendental entire maps. The following seems a natural question.

Question
.

Let
U
be an immediate component of an attracting basin of a transcendental entire function. Is it true that
\dim_{H}\partial U>1
?

One can naturally distinguish three types of
p
-periodic components of basins
U
of entire transcendental maps
f
: the ones with
\deg f^{p}|_{U}=\infty
, which are necessarily unbounded, the unbounded ones with
\deg f^{p}|_{U}<\infty
, and the bounded ones, which always satisfy
\deg f^{p}|_{U}<\infty
(cf. Proposition
2.1
). All the three types of basins actually appear among maps from class
\mathcal{B}
, see Figure
1
.

U
of period
3
with
\deg f^{3}|_{U}=\infty
for
f(z)=(2+\pi i)e^{z}
(see
[
16
]
). Center: an unbounded invariant component
U
with
\deg f|_{U}=2
for
f(z)=e^{-z}+z-1
(see
[

,
43
]
). Right: a bounded invariant component
U
for
f(z)=(-21+3i)z^{2}e^{z}
(see
[

]
).

In this paper we answer the above question in affirmative for attracting basins of the first type for maps from class
\mathcal{B}
(under a natural hyperbolicity condition) and attracting basins of the third type for arbitrary transcendental maps. In fact, instead of the Hausdorff dimension, we estimate the hyperbolic dimension of the boundaries of the considered basins. Here and in the sequel we denote by
\Sing(g)
the set of singular values (critical and asymptotic values and their accumulation points) of a holomorphic map
g
defined on some domain in
\mathbb{C}
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a transcendental entire function from class
\mathcal{B}
, and let
U
be an immediate component of the basin of an attracting periodic point
\zeta
of period
p\geq 1
, such that
\deg f^{p}|_{U}=\infty
and
\overline{\Sing(f^{p}|_{U})}
is a compact subset of
U
. Then the hyperbolic dimension of the boundary of
U
is larger than
1
.

In fact, the result extends to the case of parabolic basins as follows.

Theorem B
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a transcendental entire function from class
\mathcal{B}
, and let
U
be an immediate component of the basin of a parabolic periodic point
\zeta
of period
p\geq 1
, such that
\deg f^{p}|_{U}=\infty
,
\overline{\Sing(f^{p}|_{U})}
is a compact subset of
U
, and
\zeta\notin\overline{\Sing(f^{p})}
. Then the hyperbolic dimension of the boundary of
U
is larger than
1
.

Remark 1.1
.

In fact, the proofs of Theorems A–B show that in both cases, for every
r>0
there exists a compact expanding conformal repeller
X\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|>r\}
of Hausdorff dimension larger than
1
, contained in
\partial U
. Therefore, the
eventual hyperbolic dimension
(in the sense of
[
21
]
) of the boundary of
U
is at least
1
.

The proofs of Theorems A–B consist in constructing a suitable expanding conformal repeller
X\subset\partial U
of Hausdorff dimension larger than
1
. We follow a general idea introduced in
[
8
]
, where it was proved that the hyperbolic dimension of the Julia set is larger than
1
for transcendental entire maps from the class
\mathcal{B}
and, more generally, for meromorphic maps with logarithmic tracts. The method was later used and developed e.g. in
[
9
,
15
,
28
,
42
]
. Note that to ensure that the expanding repeller
X
is contained in
\partial U
, we need a careful modification of the construction, based on an analysis of topological and combinatorial structure of the considered basin.

Remark 1.2
.

In
[
9
]
it was proved that for an immediate component
U
of the basin of an attracting periodic point of period
p>1
of an exponential map, the set of points in the boundary of
U
which escape to infinity under iteration of the map, has Hausdorff dimension
1
. In
[
12
]
, Bergweiler and Ding generalized this result for some functions of finite order with a finite set of singularities.

Out next result concerns the case of bounded immediate components
U
of attracting basins of transcendental entire functions. Since in this case the map is defined on a neighbourhood of the closure of
U
, the situation is more similar to the one for polynomial and rational maps. In this context, Zdunik proved in
[
44
,
45
]
that for a polynomial
f
with a simply connected basin
U
of infinity, the following dichotomy holds: either
\partial U
is an analytic curve, or
\dim_{H}\partial U>1
. This result was later extended to arbitrary attracting basins of rational functions by Przytycki
[
32
, Theorem A]
. In
[
25
]
, Hamilton proved that if the Julia set of a rational function
f
is a Jordan curve in the Riemann sphere, then either it is an analytic curve, or
\dim_{H}J(f)>1
.

A classical result of Brolin
[
19
, Lemma 9.1]
says that if
U
is an invariant component of an attracting basin of a rational map
f
, and
\partial U
is an analytic Jordan curve or an analytic arc, then
\partial U
is, respectively, a circle or an arc of a circle in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. These two cases actually appear within the space of rational maps, and correspond, respectively, to
f
being conformally conjugate to a finite Blaschke product with an attracting fixed point, or
f
being semiconjugate to a finite Blaschke product
B
by the map
\phi(z)=z+1/z
(which includes the case when
f
is a Chebyshev polynomial and
B(z)=z^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
), see
[
30
, Lemma 15.5 and Chapter 7]
. In the case of a Jordan curve, the Brolin result was then generalized by Azarina
[
2
]
, who showed that in fact, if
\gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
is an invariant analytic Jordan curve for an entire map
f
, then
f
is conformally conjugate to a finite Blaschke product, or
\gamma
is a level curve of an invariant Siegel disc of
f
.

In this paper, combining the Brolin–Azarina result with a theorem of Przytycki
[
32
, Theorem A’]
, which generalizes the aforementioned Zdunik’s dichotomy to the case of so-called RB-domains (see Definition
11.1
), we show the following.

Theorem C
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and let
U
be a bounded immediate component of an attracting basin of a periodic point of
f
. Then the boundary of
U
has hyperbolic dimension larger than
1
.

Remark 1.3
.

Since the hyperbolic dimension of the boundary of a Fatou component is preserved by branches of
f^{-1}
, the boundaries of all components of periodic basins considered in Theorems A–C have hyperbolic dimension larger than
1
.

In the context of transcendental entire maps, there are few examples of Fatou components with a smooth boundaries. It is known that for several families of transcendental entire functions there exist Siegel disks, whose boundaries are
C^{\infty}
Jordan curves (see
[
1
,
24
]
). Recently, Boc Thaler
[
18
]
showed that every regular simply connected domain whose closure has no bounded complementary components, is a wandering domain of some transcendental entire function, which provides examples of wandering domains with smooth boundaries.

For transcendental entire functions, Baker and Weinreich
[
5
, Theorem 4]
(see also
[
27
]
) proved that if
U
is an unbounded immediate component of an attracting basin, then its boundary in the Riemann sphere is not a (Jordan) curve. This together with Theorem C and Remark
1.3
immediately implies the following.

Corollary D
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and let
U
be a component of an attracting basin of a periodic point of
f
. Then the boundary of
U
is not a smooth or rectifiable curve.

As an example, consider the bounded immediate component
U
of an attracting basin, shown on the right-hand side of Figure
1
. Although
U
appears to have a smooth boundary, Corollary D implies that
\partial U
is neither smooth nor rectifiable.

Remark 1.4
.

Note that Corollary D is not true for transcendental meromorphic maps. Indeed, for
f(z)=\lambda\tan z
,
\lambda>1
, the upper and lower half-plane are completely invariant basins of attracting fixed points, and their common boundary
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, equal to the Julia set of
f
, is an analytic Jordan curve in the Riemann sphere. Concerning other types of Fatou components,
Hamilton
[
26
]
proved that there exist invariant Baker domains
U
of meromorphic maps, such that
\partial U
is a non-smooth rectifiable Jordan curve.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section
2
contains the preliminaries. The proof of Theorem A, divided into several parts, is presented in Sections
4
–
9
. Since the proof is rather involved, before going into the details, in Section
3
we include its short description. In Section
10
we explain how to adapt the proof of Theorem A to the case of parabolic basins, which shows Theorem B. Finally, in Section
11
we prove Theorem C, with a proof of the Brolin–Azarina lemma for transcendental attracting basins included in the appendix.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
General notation

By
\overline{A}
and
\partial A
we denote, respectively, the closure and boundary (in
\mathbb{C}
) of a set
A\subset\mathbb{C}
. The open disc of radius
r>0
, centered at
z\in\mathbb{C}
, is denoted by
\mathbb{D}(z,r)
. The open unit disc is denoted by
\mathbb{D}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
we write

\mathbb{H}_{>x}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}(z)>x\}

and define
\mathbb{H}_{<x}
,
\mathbb{H}_{\geq x}
,
\mathbb{H}_{\leq x}
analogously. We also set

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq\sigma}=\{s\in\mathbb{Z}:s\geq\sigma\}

for
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}
. We denote by
\exp
the exponential map
z\mapsto e^{z}
on
\mathbb{C}
.

2.2.
Hyperbolic metric

For a simply connected domain
V\subset\mathbb{C}

we denote by
d\rho_{V}
the
hyperbolic metric
on
V
, which is a conformal Riemannian metric given by
d\rho_{V}=\frac{2|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|}{1-|\varphi(z)|^{2}}|dz|
, where
\varphi\colon V\to\mathbb{D}
is a Riemann map. The Schwarz–Pick lemma (see e.g.
[
20
]
), asserts that for a holomorphic map
g\colon V\to V
, one has
g^{*}(d\rho_{V})\leq d\rho_{V}
, where the inequality is strict unless
g
is univalent.

2.3.
Distortion of univalent maps and Koebe’s theorems

The
distortion
of a univalent map
g
on a set
A\subset\mathbb{C}
is defined as
\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in A}\frac{|g^{\prime}(z_{1})|}{|g^{\prime}(z_{2})|}
. Recall the classical Koebe distortion and one-quarter theorems (see e.g.
[
20
]
).

Koebe distortion theorem
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{D}(z_{0},r)\to\mathbb{C}
be a univalent holomorphic map, for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
. Then for every
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\lambda r)}
, where
0<\lambda<1
,

\frac{1-\lambda}{(1+\lambda)^{3}}\leq\frac{|g^{\prime}(z)|}{|g^{\prime}(z_{0})|}\leq\frac{1+\lambda}{(1-\lambda)^{3}}.

Koebe one-quarter theorem
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{D}(z_{0},r)\to\mathbb{C}
be a univalent holomorphic map, for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
. Then
\mathbb{D}(f(z_{0}),r|f^{\prime}(z_{0})|/4)\subset f(\mathbb{D}(z_{0},r))
.

2.4.
Conformal repellers and Bowen’s formula

For a holomorphic map
g\colon V\to\mathbb{C}
on an open set
V\subset\mathbb{C}
, an
expanding conformal repeller

\Lambda\subset V
is a compact set, such that
g(\Lambda)\subset\Lambda
and
|(g^{k})^{\prime}|_{\Lambda}|>1
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The classical
Bowen’s formula
(see e.g.
[
33
]
) asserts that the Hausdorff dimension of an expanding conformal repeller
\Lambda
is equal to the unique zero of the (strictly decreasing)
topological pressure function

t\mapsto P(g|_{\Lambda},t)
, where

P(g|_{\Lambda},t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\sum_{w\in g^{-n}(z)\cap\Lambda}|(g^{n})^{\prime}(w)|^{-t}

for
z\in\Lambda
.

2.5.
Standard facts on entire functions

We will use Liouville’s theorem, which states that every bounded entire function is constant, and Picard’s theorem, which asserts that a transcendental entire function attains every complex value, with at most one exception, in any neighbourhood of infinity.

Finally, let us recall a useful fact on the degree of an entire function on simply connected domains.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
13
, Proposition 2.8]
)
.

Let
f
be a non-constant entire function, let
U\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain and let
V
be a component of
f^{-1}(U)
. Then either
f
maps
V
onto
U
as a proper map with a finite degree, or
V
is unbounded and the set
f^{-1}(z)\cap V
is infinite for every
z\in U
with one possible exception. In the second case,
V
contains an asymptotic curve corresponding to an asymptotic
value in
U
, or
V
contains infinitely many critical points of
f
.

3.
Summary of the proof of Theorem A

As the proof of Theorem A is rather involved, we present here its short description, introducing notation and highlighting the main ideas and steps of the proof.

The whole proof, divided into several parts, covers Sections
4
–
9
. In Section
4
we describe the topological structure of the basin
U
showing, in particular, that there exists a topological disc
D\subset U
, such that
E=f^{-1}(D)\cap U
is an unbounded simply connected domain containing
\overline{D}
, and each component of
U\setminus\overline{E}
is a simply connected domain, mapped by
f
onto
U\setminus\overline{D}
as an infinite degree covering (see Lemma
4.1
). This implies (see Subsection
5.1
) that, assuming
\zeta=0
, one can lift
f
on
U\setminus E
by the exponential map to

\Phi\colon\exp^{-1}(U\setminus E)\to\exp^{-1}(U\setminus D),

such that the inverse branches of
\Phi
, denoted by
G_{s}
,
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, form an iterated function system on
\exp^{-1}(U\setminus D)
.

In Subsection
5.1
, for a chosen component
\mathcal{V}
of
U\setminus\overline{E}
and
\omega=\partial\mathcal{V}\cap U
, we define a system of curves in
\exp^{-1}(U)
, consisting of a logarithmic lift
\Gamma
of
\partial D
, logarithmic lifts
\Gamma_{s}
,
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, of
\omega
, and some curves
\gamma_{s}
connecting
\Gamma
to
\Gamma_{s}
. The union of all these curves, together with their images under successive iterates of the branches
G_{s}
, is a connected tree-like subset of
\exp^{-1}(U)
(see Lemma
5.1
).

In Subsection
5.2
, using the assumption
f\in\mathcal{B}
, we consider another logarithmic lift
F
of the map
f
near infinity, defined on the union of lifted tracts of
f
over infinity. Note that due to the possible existence of the critical points of
f
with critical values that lie outside of
U
, the two lifts
\Phi
and
F
need not coincide on the intersection of their domains of definition.

The relations between
\Phi
and
F
are described in Section
6
. In Lemma
6.1
we define some points
v_{s}\in\Gamma_{s}
, located at the boundary of a domain
T
, where the inverse branches of
F
are defined (see (
8
)). We set

v_{s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}}=G_{s_{0}}\circ\cdots\circ G_{s_{n-1}}(v_{s_{n}})\in\exp^{-1}(U).

In Lemma
6.3
and Corollary
6.4
we show that
v_{s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}}\in T
provided
s_{1}\geq\sigma
for a fixed (sufficiently large) number
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}
. The most important part of Section
6
is given by Lemma
6.5
, which describes the relations between inverse branches of
\Phi
and
F
. In particular, it shows that for
s_{0},s_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
there exist an inverse branch
H_{s_{0},s_{1}}
of
F
and a number
k(s_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that

(1)

H_{s_{0},s_{1}}(v_{k(s_{1}),s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}})=v_{s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}}

for
s_{2}\geq\sigma
. We denote
T_{s_{0},s_{1}}=H_{s_{0},s_{1}}(T)
. The branches
H_{s_{0},s_{1}}
have good contracting properties, as described in Lemma
6.6
.

In Sections
7
–
8
we construct a suitable Cantor conformal expanding repeller
X\subset\partial U
for the map
f
. To this end, working in the logarithmic coordinates, we define an iterated function system
\{\phi_{j,l}\}_{(j,l)\in\mathcal{J}}
on a rectangle

K=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}(z)\in[r-1,r+1],\;\textup{Im}(z)\in[y-d,y+d]\}\subset\mathbb{H}_{>b}

for some
y\in\mathbb{R}
and large
b,d>0
, with
r>b+1
, where
\phi_{j,l}
are suitable high iterates of the branches
H_{s_{0},s_{1}}
. The rectangle

Appendix A
Proof of the Brolin–Azarina lemma for transcendental attracting basins

Assume that
U
is a bounded invariant component of the basin of attraction of a transcendental entire function
f
, and suppose to the contrary that
\partial U
is an analytic Jordan curve. Let
\zeta\in U
be the attracting fixed point of
f
. Consider a Riemann map
\varphi
from
\mathbb{D}
onto
U

such that
\varphi(0)=\zeta
. Since
\partial U
is a Jordan curve, the map
\varphi
extends homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and, by the analyticity of
\partial U
and Schwarz reflection, it extends to a univalent map

\varphi\colon\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\to\mathbb{C},

where
\mathbb{D}_{\rho}=\mathbb{D}(0,\rho)
for some
\rho>1
. By Proposition
2.1
, the map

(36)

B=\varphi^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi

is a Blaschke product of degree
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, with an attracting fixed point at
0
, that is

B(z)=az^{m}\prod_{k=1}^{d-m}\frac{z-a_{k}}{1-\overline{a_{k}}z},

where
1\leq m\leq d
,
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
|a|=1
and
a_{k}\in\mathbb{D}
. Note that
B
extends to the Riemann sphere
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
as a rational map of degree
d
, with an attracting fixed point at
\infty
, such that
B(1/\overline{z})=1/\overline{B(z)}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. In particular,
B(\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and

(37)

\text{for every compact set }K\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}\text{ there exists }n\geq 0,\text{ such that }B^{-n}(K)\subset\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

Moreover, the Schwarz–Pick lemma implies
B^{-1}(\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
By (
36
), if
\rho
is chosen sufficiently close to
1
, then

(38)

\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi\quad\text{on }\mathbb{D}_{\rho}.

See Figure
10
.

Figure 10.
The relation between the maps
B
and
f
.

Claim
1
. The map
\varphi
can be extended to a transcendental entire function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
. In particular,
B
has no finite poles.

Proof of Claim
1
. First, we extend
\varphi
to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the union of the forward orbits of the critical values of
B
contained in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Note that
\mathcal{P}
is at most countable discrete subset of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
(provided
\rho
is chosen sufficiently close to
1
). In particular,
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
is a domain containing
\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
. Note that all inverse branches of
B^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are defined in a neighbourhood
W
of
z
. Hence, by (
37
), there exists
n\geq 0
and an inverse branch
g
of
B^{n}
defined on
W
, such that
g(z)\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. We set

\varphi=f^{n}\circ\varphi\circ g\quad\text{on }W.

We show that
\varphi
is a well-defined holomorphic function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
. For this, we should check that if
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
and
g_{1},g_{2}
are inverse branches of
B^{n_{1}}
and
B^{n_{2}}
, respectively, defined in a neighbourhood of
z
, such that
g_{1}(z),g_{2}(z)\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, then
\varphi_{1}(z)=\varphi_{2}(z)
for

\varphi_{1}=f^{n_{1}}\circ\varphi\circ g_{1},\qquad\varphi_{2}=f^{n_{2}}\circ\varphi\circ g_{2}.

To see it, connect
z
to a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
by a simple curve
\gamma\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
and extend the branches
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
holomorphically to a neighbourhood of
\gamma
. Take
v\in\gamma
close to
z_{0}
, such that
v\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and let
v_{1}=g_{1}(v)
,
v_{2}=g_{2}(v)
. As
B^{-1}(\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, we have
v_{1},B(v_{1}),\ldots,B^{n_{1}-1}(v_{1})\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
and
v_{2},B(v_{2}),\ldots,B^{n_{2}-1}(v_{2})\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
. Hence, (
38
) implies

\varphi_{1}(v)=f^{n_{1}}(\varphi(v_{1}))=\varphi(B^{n_{1}}(v_{1}))=\varphi(v)=\varphi(B^{n_{2}}(v_{2}))=f^{n_{2}}(\varphi(v_{2}))=\varphi_{2}(v).

Hence, by holomorphicity,
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
on
\gamma
. In particular,
\varphi_{1}(z)=\varphi_{2}(z)
. This shows that
\varphi
is well-defined and holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
. It is obvious by definition that
\varphi
is the extension of
\varphi|_{\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}
defined previously. In this way we have extended
\varphi
holomorphically to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{P}
.

To show that
\varphi
extends holomorphically to
\mathbb{C}
, consider a point
z\in\mathcal{P}
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is discrete,
\varphi
is defined in
W\setminus\{z\}
for a small neighbourhood
W
of
z
, and by (
37
), we have
B^{-n}(W\setminus\{z\})\subset\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
for some
n>0
, so
\varphi(W\setminus\{z\})\subset f^{n}\circ\varphi(\overline{\mathbb{D}_{\rho}})
by (
38
). Hence,
\varphi
is bounded in
W\setminus\{z\}
, so it extends holomorphically to
W
. This shows that
\varphi
extends to
\mathbb{C}
, so it is an entire function.

To prove
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
, consider first
z\in B^{-1}(\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P}))
. Then
z,B(z)\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P})
, so by (
37
), there exists an inverse branch
g
of
B^{n}
defined in a neighbourhood of
z
with
g(z)\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and an inverse branch
h
of
B
defined in a neighbourhood of
B(z)
with
h(B(z))=z
. Then by the definition of
\varphi
,

\varphi(B(z))=f^{n+1}(\varphi(g(h(B(z)))))=f(f^{n}(\varphi(g(z))))=f(\varphi(z)).

Hence, we have
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
on
B^{-1}(\mathbb{C}\setminus(\overline{\mathbb{D}}\cup\mathcal{P}))
. Together with (
38
), this implies
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
on
B^{-1}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{P})
. Since
B^{-1}(\mathcal{P})
is a discrete subset of
B^{-1}(\mathbb{C})
, by continuity we have
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
on
B^{-1}(\mathbb{C})
.

Therefore, to show
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
on
\mathbb{C}
, it is sufficient to check
B^{-1}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}
, which means that
B
has no finite poles. To prove it, suppose otherwise and consider a pole
p\in\mathbb{C}
of
B
. Then
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
in
W\setminus\{p\}
for some neighbourhood
W
of
p
. Consider a sequence
w_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
,
w_{n}\to\infty
, and note that one can find a sequence
z_{n}\to p
, such that
B(z_{n})=w_{n}
. Since
z_{n}\in W\setminus\{p\}
for large
n
, we have

\varphi(w_{n})=\varphi(B(z_{n}))=f(\varphi(z_{n}))\to f(\varphi(p)).

This shows
\lim_{w\to\infty}\varphi(w)=f(\varphi(p))\in\mathbb{C}
, so
\varphi
extends to a rational map on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
, with
\varphi(\infty)\in\mathbb{C}
. This implies
\varphi(\overline{\mathbb{C}})\subset\mathbb{C}
, which is impossible by Liouville’s theorem. Consequently,
B
has no finite poles, which shows
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
on
\mathbb{C}
.

Finally, note that
\varphi
is transcendental, because otherwise, it is a (non-constant) polynomial
\varphi
and, consequently,
\varphi\circ B
is rational, while
f\circ\varphi
is transcendental, which contradicts the relation
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
. This ends the proof of the claim.
\blacktriangle

By Claim 1,
B
has no finite poles, so
B
must be of the form
B(z)=az^{d}
, with
|a|=1
and
d>1
. By applying a linear change of variable we can assume
a=1
, so

B(z)=z^{d}.

Claim
2
. For
r>0
let

\gamma_{r}=\varphi(\partial\mathbb{D}_{r})

for
\mathbb{D}_{r}=\mathbb{D}(0,r)
. Then for every
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,

\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}f^{-n}(f^{n}(\varphi(z)))

is dense in
\gamma_{|z|}
. Moreover, for every
r>0
, either
\gamma_{r}\subset J(f)
or
\gamma_{r}\subset\mathcal{F}(f)
.

Proof of Claim
2
. Let
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\xi^{d^{n}}=1
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
|\xi z|=|z|
and

B^{n}(\xi z)=\xi^{d^{n}}z^{d^{n}}=z^{d^{n}}=B^{n}(z).

Hence, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are
d^{n}
elements of
B^{-n}(B^{n}(z))
, situated at equal distances in the circle
\partial\mathbb{D}_{|z|}
, and so the set

\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}B^{-n}(B^{n}(z))

is dense in
\partial\mathbb{D}_{|z|}
. Since
f
and
B
are semiconjugate by
\varphi
, the set
\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}f^{-n}(f^{n}(\varphi(z)))
is dense in
\gamma_{|z|}
, which proves the first part of the claim.

Suppose now
\gamma_{r}\not\subset\mathcal{F}(f)
and take
z\in\gamma_{r}\cap J(f)
. Then

\bigcup_{n\geq 0}f^{-n}(f^{n}(z))\subset J(f)

by the invariance of
J(f)
, and since this union is dense in
\gamma_{r}
and
J(f)
is closed, we obtain
\gamma_{r}\subset J(f)
. This proves the second part of the claim.

\blacktriangle

Claim
3
. There is no
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
with
\varphi(z)=\zeta
.

Proof of Claim
3
. Suppose to the contrary that there exists
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\varphi(z)=\zeta
. Since
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
, we can assume
|z|>1
. Then
\zeta=\varphi(z)\in\gamma_{|z|}
and, by Claim 2 and since
\zeta=f^{n}(\zeta)
for all
n
, the set

\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}f^{-n}(\zeta)=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}f^{-n}(f^{n}(\varphi(z)))

is dense in
\gamma_{|z|}
. This is a contradiction, because an attracting fixed point cannot have preimages in a small punctured neighbourhood of itself. Hence,
there is no
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\varphi(z)=\zeta
.

\blacktriangle

Claim
4
. The immediate component
U
of the attracting basin of
\zeta
is completely invariant.

Proof of Claim
4
.
Suppose
U
is not completely invariant. Then there exist a component
U_{1}
of
f^{-1}(U)
and a component
U_{2}
of
f^{-1}(U_{1})
, such that
U,U_{1},U_{2}
are pairwise disjoint. Since
\varphi
is entire, there exist
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\varphi(z_{1})\in U_{1}
,
\varphi(z_{2})\in U_{2}
. Fix
r>1
such that
r>\max(|z_{1}|,|z_{2}|)
. Then
\overline{U}\subset\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{r})
,
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
intersect
\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{r})
and, by the maximum principle,
\partial\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{r})\subset\gamma_{r}
. Suppose
U_{j}
intersects
\gamma_{r}
for some
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then by Claim 2,
\gamma_{r}\subset U_{j}
, in particular
\partial\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{r})\subset U_{j}
. Hence,
U_{j}
is not simply connected, which is impossible, as
U_{j}
is a component of a preimage of the simply connected component
U
under an entire map. Therefore,
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\subset\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{r})
, so
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are bounded. By Proposition
2.1
, we have
f(U_{2})=U_{1}
,
f(U_{1})=U
, so there exist
w_{1}\in U_{1}
,
w_{2}\in U_{2}
, such that
f(w_{2})=w_{1}
,
f(w_{1})=\zeta
.
By Picard’s theorem, there exists
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\varphi(z_{1})=w_{1}
, or there exists
z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\varphi(z_{2})=w_{2}
. Note that in the second case the semiconjugation
\varphi\circ B=f\circ\varphi
implies
\varphi(B(z_{2}))=f(w_{2})=w_{1}
, so in fact, the first case always holds. Consequently, by the semiconjugation relation, we have
\varphi(B(z_{1}))=\zeta
. Note that
B(z_{1})\neq 0
, because otherwise
z_{1}=0
and
U_{1}\ni z_{1}=\varphi(z_{1})=\varphi(0)=\zeta\in U
. Therefore, we have a contradiction with Claim 3 for
z=B(z_{1})
. Hence,
U
is completely invariant.
\blacktriangle

Finally, observe that Claim 4 provides a contradiction with Picard’s theorem: since
U
is bounded and
f
is a transcendental entire function,
f
cannot omit more than one point of
\mathbb{C}
in any neighbourhood of
\infty
. This finishes the proof of the Brolin–Azarina lemma for transcendental attracting basins (Lemma
11.4
).
