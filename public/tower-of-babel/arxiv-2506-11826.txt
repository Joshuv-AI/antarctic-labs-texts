I
Introduction

The concordance model of cosmology, commonly referred to as the Lambda Cold Dark Matter (
\Lambda
CDM) model,
has proven to be a highly robust theoretical framework for understanding the evolutionary history of the universe
(
P. Bull et al. 2016
)
. In this model, the mechanisms governing the formation of large-scale structure (LSS)
(
P. Bull et al. 2016
)
, and the cosmic microwave background (CMB) are well defined
(
M. S. Turner 2022
)
). Furthermore, the
\Lambda
CDM model provides an excellent fit to a variety of different cosmological observations, such as the
Planck
CMB temperature (TT), polarization (EE), and cross-power (TE) spectra
(
G. Efstathiou 2024
)
, as well as data from galaxy redshift surveys characterizing LSS and the expansion history of the universe inferred from type Ia supernovae
(
M. S. Turner 2022
)
, to name a few. However, it is precisely these cosmological probes which pose empirical challenges to the model’s validity, since the values of certain cosmological parameters inferred from these probes differ from one another. For example, the value of the Hubble constant
H_{0}
determined via the distance ladder approach and the one inferred from CMB fluctuations differs by more than
5\sigma

(
A. G. Riess 2020
)
; this has become known as the Hubble tension. Moreover, there is also tension between the value of the weighted amplitude of matter fluctuations,
S_{8}
, as determined from the CMB and from late-time probes, such as weak lensing, redshift space distortions, and cluster counts
(
L. Perivolaropoulos & F. Skara 2022
)
; this is known as the growth tension. The
\Lambda
CDM model also faces theoretical difficulties, such as its reliance on yet poorly understood cosmic constituents, such as dark matter and dark energy, which currently lie beyond the scope of the Standard Model of particle physics
(
M. S. Turner 2022
)
, and numerous other problems, such as the cosmic coincidence problem, as well as the cosmic age, synchronicity, horizon, and flatness problems
(
J. Casado 2020
)
. While the inflationary period addresses the horizon and flatness problems
(
A. H. Guth 1981
)
, the others remain unresolved. Given these theoretical and empirical challenges, exploring alternative cosmological models seems prudent.

The present study examines a family of models referred to as coasting cosmologies. The defining feature of these models is that scale factor,
a(t)
, is a linear function of cosmic time (
a\propto t
), although its realization varies from model to model
(
J. Casado 2020
)
. Coasting cosmologies can be subdivided into strictly linear models, where
a\propto t
holds at all times, and quasi-linear models, where the universe transitions from a
\Lambda
CDM model to a
a\propto t
expansion sometime after recombination (for a review, see
J. Casado 2020
). Coasting models are also diversified by having different spacetime geometries. For example, the
R_{\mathrm{h}}=c\,t
model (
F. Melia & A. S. H. Shevchuk 2012
,
F. Melia 2020
), which is strictly linear, suggests a flat geometry (with curvature parameter
k=0
), while the eternal coasting model
M. V. John & K. B. Joseph 2023
also admits spherical (
k>0
) or hyperbolic geometries (
k<0
). Strictly linear coasting cosmologies naturally resolve the horizon problem because the particle horizon diverges with
a\propto t
, and they reduce the synchronicity problem to a trivial consequence of linear expansion, as
a\propto t\Rightarrow Ht=1
. Furthermore, strictly linear coasting cosmologies are also free from the flatness problem
(
J. Casado 2020
)
. However, strictly linear models also face various theoretical challenges. They cannot easily account for the observed abundances of light elements, while also lacking a robust model for the origin of the CMB’s anisotropies and properties (see
P. Raffai et al. 2024
and references therein). In contrast, quasi-linear coasting models do not suffer from these problems, since they are essentially
\Lambda
CDM models prior to recombination, while also resolving the cosmic coincidence and fine-tuning problems
(
J. Casado 2020
)
. Coasting cosmologies are also demonstrably consistent with a variety of empirical data (for an extensive list, see Table 2 in
F. Melia 2018
), and are even slightly favored over the
\Lambda
CDM model by a number of observables
(
P. Raffai et al. 2025
)
. In light of their ability to resolve various theoretical issues while remaining consistent with empirical data, further investigations of coasting, and specifically quasi-linear coasting models appears justified.

While many aspects of specific coasting cosmologies have been extensively studied, the question of the growth of LSS has received less attention. The time evolution of LSS growth is expressed by the growth factor,
D(z)
(see Section
II
).
E. W. Kolb 1989
derived an expression for
D(z)
for a quasi-linear coasting universe in which linear expansion is realized through the dominance of an exotic component called K-matter, which has an equation of state parameter
w_{\mathrm{K}}=-1/3
.
F. Melia 2017
performed a similar derivation in the context of the
R_{\mathrm{h}}=c\,t
model. Each of these derivations relied on their specific realizations of linear expansion to obtain an expression for
D(z)
, yielding similar but distinct results. For the purposes of this work, we will derive an expression for
D(z)
without making use of any property of a coasting universe other than the
a(t)\propto t
functional form of its scale factor within the redshift range covered by empirical data.

To test the consistency of a theoretical
D(z)
with observed LLS growth, it is expedient to use empirical data from redshift space distortion
(
A. J. S. Hamilton 1998
)
measurements, expressed as the density-weighted growth rate,
{f\sigma_{8}(z)\equiv f(z)\sigma_{8}(z)}
, where
\sigma_{8}(z)
is the amplitude of mass fluctuations in a sphere with a comoving radius of
8\text{\,}{\mathrm{\mathit{h}}}^{-1}\text{\,}\mathrm{Mpc}
(where
h\equiv H_{0}/$100\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$
), and
f(z)
is the growth rate
(
D. Huterer 2023a
)
. Both
\sigma_{8}
and
f
are directly linked to
D
, since
{\sigma_{8}(z)\equiv\sigma_{8}(z=0)D(z)}
and
{f(a)\equiv\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!{\ln{D}}/\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!{\ln{a}}}

(
D. Huterer 2023a
)
, which makes them ideal probes. However, empirical determinations of
\sigma_{8}
and
f
rely on measurements of galaxy clustering and peculiar velocities, and as such, they reflect the matter overdensity in galaxies, rather than the total matter overdensity
(
D. Huterer 2023a
)
. The relationship between the two overdensities can be expressed using the galaxy bias factor,
b
, which is both scale- and redshift dependent
(
D. Huterer 2023a
)
. The empirical values of
\sigma_{8}
and
f
using data from galaxies (
\sigma_{8,\mathrm{g}}
and
f_{\mathrm{g}}
) are then related to the true values as
{\sigma_{8,\mathrm{g}}=b\cdot\sigma_{8}}
and
{f_{\mathrm{g}}=f/b}

(
R. J. Turner 2025
)
. Obtaining
\sigma_{8}
from
\sigma_{8,\mathrm{g}}
or
f
from
f_{\mathrm{g}}
requires knowledge of the scale-dependence of
b
(which usually has to be determined directly from the given galaxy sample), as well as
a priori
information on the redshift-dependence of
b
, since the redshift-dependencies of
b
and
D
are degenerate
(
D. Huterer 2023a
)
. However
{f_{\mathrm{g}}\sigma_{8,\mathrm{g}}}
is free from galaxy bias, and
{f_{\mathrm{g}}\sigma_{8,\mathrm{g}}=f\sigma_{8}}
, making it a preferable observable over both
\sigma_{8}
and
f
.

F. Melia 2017
used
f\sigma_{8}
data published between 2011 and 2015 to perform a consistency test. As there are now
f\sigma_{8}
values for over forty distinct redshifts in the
z\in[0,2]
range, we will test our expression’s consistency with
f\sigma_{8}
data using a larger dataset, collated by
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
.

By fitting our expression for
f\sigma_{8}(z)
to the data, we can constrain the present value of the density parameter of matter,
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
, and the amplitude of mass fluctuations,
\sigma_{8}(z=0)
. Using these, we can constrain the weighted amplitude of matter fluctuations, which is defined as
(
D. Huterer 2023a
)

S_{8}\equiv\sigma_{8}(z=0)\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}}{0.3}}.

(1)

Obtaining a value for
S_{8}
in coasting cosmologies allows us to examine whether coasting models can resolve the growth tension. Lastly, we will also perform this analysis for a
\Lambda
CDM model with
Planck
2018
“TT,TE,EE+lowE+lensing”
(which we hereafter shorten to
“baseline”
) best-fit parameters
(
Planck Collaboration 2020a
)
, enabling us to determine whether the data exhibits a preference for either model.

Our treatment of LSS growth in coasting cosmologies will be limited to quasi-linear models. Strictly linear models face significant challenges in explaining the shape of the linear matter power spectrum,
P(k)
. Notably,
P(k)
is not monotonic with
k
and, based on CMB observations, exhibits a maximum at the characteristic length scale corresponding to matter-radiation equality,
k_{\mathrm{eq}}
(see
Planck Collaboration 2020b
for a visual representation). In the
\Lambda
CDM model, this extremum arises because perturbations of length scales exceeding that of the particle horizon (super-horizon perturbations) evolve at a different rate than those of length scales lying inside the horizon (sub-horizon perturbations) during the radiation-dominated epoch, whereas both super- and sub-horizon perturbations grow at the same rate during the matter-dominated era
(
T. Padmanabhan 1993
)
. The result is that the scale-independent primordial power spectrum produced by inflation is modified in a scale-dependent manner. This explanation does not apply to a strictly linear coasting universe, since it experiences uniform expansion throughout its history (without an inflationary period to seed the initial fluctuations) and lacks super-horizon and sub-horizon perturbations due to the absence of a particle horizon. On the other hand, the peak in the matter power spectrum is identified based on analyzing CMB anisotropies within the context of the concordance model. Therefore, it is possible that we would not observe a peak if the CMB fluctuation spectrum were constructed using a strictly linear model. However, until a model of the fluctuation spectrum is produced for strictly coasting universes, we will not be able to make that determination. In contrast, formation of the CMB, its important features, as well as those of the matter power spectrum, can be explained by the same mechanisms as in the concordance model, since the transition to coasting expansion occurs after recombination in quasi-linear models. Additionally, given that the available data cover a redshift range of
z\in(0\,,2)
, our results are specifically applicable to a universe experiencing linear expansion within this redshift interval (with linear expansion beginning only at some threshold redshift
z_{\mathrm{th}}<z_{*}
, where
z_{*}
is the redshift at recombination), without consideration of the expansion history prior to
z=2
. Moreover, as our treatment of quasi-linear cosmologies aims to be as general as possible, we will perform consistency tests for coasting models with hyperbolic, flat, spherical geometries, corresponding to curvature parameters
{k=\{-1,0,+1\}}
in
H^{2}_{0}c^{-2}
units.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we derive a closed form expression for
D(z)
and
f\sigma_{8}(z)
for coasting cosmologies in a manner consistent with the fluid treatment outlined by
D. Huterer 2023b
. In Section
III
, we describe the method used to fit our formula to
f\sigma_{8}(z)
data collated by
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
. We then explain how this procedure is adapted to the
\Lambda
CDM model, as well as the method used to assess the models’ consistency with the data. In Section
IV
, we discuss the results of the fit and the aforementioned test, with especial attention given to their implications for the
S_{8}
tension. Finally, in Section
V
, we summarize our conclusions and outline possibilities for expanding upon the analyses performed herein.

II
Growth of Large Scale Structure in Coasting Cosmologies

The evolution of LSS is captured by the density contrast function,
\delta
, defined as

\delta(\boldsymbol{r},\,t)=\frac{\delta\rho(\boldsymbol{r},\,t)}{\overline{\rho}(t)},

(2)

where
\overline{\rho}
is the matter density of the homogeneous background, and
\delta\rho
is the local deviation, representing an over- (
\delta\rho>0
) or underdensity (
\delta\rho<0
) relative to the background; their parameters are proper position,
\boldsymbol{r}
, and cosmic time,
t
. It is often expedient to work with
\delta
in Fourier space. The differential equation governing the evolution of the various Fourier modes of
\delta
is the following
(
D. Huterer 2023b
)

\frac{\partial^{2}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\partial t^{2}}+2\,\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\partial\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\partial t}=\left(4\pi G\,\overline{\rho}-\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\,|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\overline{T}}{a^{2}}S_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(3)

where
G
is the gravitational constant,
\boldsymbol{k}
is the comoving wavevector associated with the given Fourier mode,
c_{\mathrm{s}}
is the adiabatic sound speed,
\overline{T}
is the temperature of the background, and
S_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the corresponding Fourier mode of the specific entropy,
S
. Since we are working with quasi-linear coasting cosmologies, we can use the fact that inflation typically predicts isentropic initial conditions to set
S_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0

(
D. Huterer 2023b
)
. Moreover, since the Jeans length, which is defined as
{\lambda_{\mathrm{J}}\equiv c_{\mathrm{s}}\sqrt{\pi/(G\overline{\rho})}}
, corresponds to a mass scale on the order of
10^{5}
solar masses after recombination, whereas the astronomical objects of interest have masses on the order of
10^{12}
solar masses or greater
(
D. Huterer 2023b
)
, we can assume a length scale
\lambda\gg\lambda_{\mathrm{J}}
, which is equivalent to
c_{\mathrm{s}}\approx 0
. Thus, at late times, when
c_{\mathrm{s}}=0
is a reasonably good approximation, Equation (
3
) reduces to a yet simpler form
(
D. Huterer 2023b
)

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits^{2}\!\delta}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!t^{2}}+2\,\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!\delta}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!t}=4\pi G\,\overline{\rho}\,\delta,

(4)

which is the equation we will solve to obtain
\delta
. We have dropped the
\boldsymbol{k}
subscript because Equation (
4
) does not distinguish between perturbations of different scales (different
|\boldsymbol{k}|
). For convenience, we also make the substitution
\delta(t)=\delta_{0}\,D(t)
, where
D(t)
denotes the
growth factor
, which is normalized to 1 at the present time. Substituting this expression into Equation (
4
), and rewriting the right-hand side using
\bar{\rho}=(3H_{0}^{2}/8\pi G)\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}a^{-3}
, and that for a coasting universe
a(t)=H_{0}\,t
, Equation (
4
) becomes

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits^{2}\!D}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!a^{2}}+\frac{2}{a}\,\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!D}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!a}=\frac{3}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}\,a^{-3}D(a).

(5)

Then, by performing a change of variables,
{y\equiv\sqrt{6\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}/a}=\sqrt{6\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}(1+z)}}
, Equation (
5
) takes the form of a Bessel differential equation, yielding the general solution

D(y(z))=y(z)\left(C_{1}\,I_{1}(y(z))+C_{2}\,K_{1}(y(z))\right),

(6)

where
I_{i}
and
K_{i}
are the
i
th order modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind, respectively. On the relevant interval,
a\in(0,1)
,
I_{1}\left(y\right)
represents the decaying mode, so we neglect it. Furthermore, in view of the normalization condition placed on
D(a)
, one may write the growth factor as a function of redshift as

D(z)=\frac{y(z)}{\sqrt{6\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}}K_{1}\left(\sqrt{6\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}}\right)}K_{1}(y(z)),

(7)

where the dependence on scale factor is replaced by redshift, as

f\sigma_{8}(z)
is typically reported in terms of redshift. Having derived
D(z)
, an expression for
f\sigma_{8}(z)
can be obtained from the formula
(
D. Huterer 2023a
)

f\sigma_{8}(a)=\sigma_{8,0}D(a)\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!\ln{D}}{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!\ln{a}},

(8)

where
{\sigma_{8,0}\equiv\sigma_{8}(a=1)=\sigma_{8}(z=0)}
.
Recasting Equation (
8
) in terms of redshift yields the final form of
f\sigma_{8}(z)
in a coasting universe

\begin{split}&f\sigma_{8}(z)=-\frac{\sigma_{8,0}}{\sqrt{6\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}}K_{1}\left(\sqrt{6\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}}\right)}\cdot\\
&\cdot\Biggl[\frac{y(z)K_{1}(y(z))}{2}-\frac{b^{2}}{4}\biggl(K_{0}(y(z))+K_{2}(y(z))\biggr)\Biggr].\end{split}

(9)

With a closed form expression for
f\sigma_{8}(z)
in hand, we may proceed with performing such preprocessing of the available
f\sigma_{8}
data as is required to render it suitable for curve-fitting.

It ought to be noted that the current work is not the first instance of Equation (
4
) being solved without including components other than matter in
\overline{\rho}
. A solution provided by
D. S. Lemons & W. Peter 1992
was analogous to Equation (
6
), as it was also a linear combination of the same modified Bessel functions. However, their approach, which treated Equation (
5
) as an initial value problem, retained the decaying mode, whereas we consider it negligible.

III
Data and Analysis

The data utilized in this study was originally assembled by
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
to investigate, among other things, the discrepancy between values of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
and
\sigma_{8,0}
derived from redshift space distortion and textitPlanck
(
Planck Collaboration 2020a
)
data. The data compilation of
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
contains a total of sixty-six
f\sigma_{8}(z)
values at various redshifts. However, many of these data points come from galaxy surveys whose galaxy samples overlap, which introduces correlations between data points and their uncertainties, and a full covariance matrix is not available for the whole dataset. As such, for the purposes of the present study, we only used a subset of thirty-five data points, which
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
identified as being largely uncorrelated, as our determining the models’ consistency with the data and whether the data prefer any one model over the others requires reliable knowledge of the uncertainties and correlations present in the dataset.

As outlined by
L. Kazantzidis & L. Perivolaropoulos 2018
, obtaining
f\sigma_{8}(z)
values from redshift space distortion data necessitates assuming a fiducial cosmology to convert redshifts into distances, which makes these values model-dependent. Consequently, such data cannot be used to test models which differ from the one originally assumed. However, one can calculate the value
f\sigma_{8}(z)
would have taken had one assumed a different fiducial model by multiplying the existing data points with a correction factor (
L. Kazantzidis & L. Perivolaropoulos 2018
)
q(z,\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}},\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}})
, where
\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}}
corresponds to the present-day value of the matter density parameter in the fiducial cosmology, and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
is the corresponding value in the model under scrutiny. This is an approximation based on the Alcock-Paczyński effect
(
C. Alcock & B. Paczynski 1979
)
. As
L. Kazantzidis & L. Perivolaropoulos 2018
note, one may find different forms for
q(z,\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}},\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}})
in the literature, but we will use the one already employed in previous studies (namely by
E. Macaulay et al. 2013
,
L. Kazantzidis & L. Perivolaropoulos 2018
,
E.-K. Li et al. 2020
, and
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
)

q(z,\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}},\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}})=\frac{H(z)D_{\mathrm{A}}(z)}{H^{\mathrm{fid}}(z)D^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{A}}(z)},

(10)

where
D_{\mathrm{A}}(z)
is the angular diameter distance. For each data point in our dataset, the fiducial model was a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology (for which the density parameter of radiation was considered negligible), with the specific values of the various parameters differing depending on the source publication. Thus, one may write
H^{\mathrm{fid}}(z)
and
D^{\mathrm{fid}}_{A}(z)
as

H^{\mathrm{fid}}(z)=H^{\mathrm{fid}}_{0}\sqrt{\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}}(1+z)^{3}+(1-Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}})}

(11)

and

D^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{A}}(z)=\frac{c}{H^{\mathrm{fid}}_{0}}\frac{1}{1+z}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{H^{\mathrm{fid}}_{0}}{H^{\mathrm{fid}}(z’)}\mathop{\mathrm{\mathstrut d}}\nolimits\!z’

(12)

respectively, while the relevant quantities for a coasting cosmology are

H(z)=H_{0}(1+z)

(13)

and

D_{\mathrm{A}}(z)=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\frac{1}{1+z}\begin{cases}\sinh{(\ln{(1+z)})},&$k=-1$\\
\ln{(1+z)},&$k=0$\\
\sin{(\ln{(1+z)})},&$k=+1$\\
\end{cases}.

(14)

Substituting Equations (
11
)-(
14
) into Equation (
10
) makes it clear that
q
does not depend on
H^{\mathrm{fid}}_{0}
and
H_{0}
, as these parameters cancel.

The aim of this work is to determine whether coasting cosmologies are consistent with the growth data from redshift space distortion measurements in the redshift range
z\in(0,2)
and whether they provide a better fit compared to the
\Lambda
CDM model. Therefore, the dataset was homogenized with respect to a flat
\Lambda
CDM model with the baseline
Planck
2018 best-fit value
(
Planck Collaboration 2020a
)
of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}

(0.3153)
, as well as three coasting models, by multiplying both the fiducial
f\sigma_{8}
values and their uncertainties by the corresponding
q
. Note that by Equations (
13
) and (
14
), no prior knowledge of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
was required to perform this step for the coasting cases, and the fiducial
\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}}
values were listed by
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
, facilitating the computation of
q(z,\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}},\Omega^{\mathrm{fid}}_{\mathrm{m,0}})
.

Subsequent to recalibration, we fitted Equation (
9
) (for the coasting cases) to the data using the
dynesty
implementation of Nested Sampling (
J. S. Speagle 2020
,
S. Koposov et al. 2024
). The priors of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
and
\sigma_{8,0}
were taken to be uniform distributions on the
(0,2)
interval. The lower bound is motivated by the fact that neither
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
nor
\sigma_{8,0}
can take negative values, as that would be unphysical. The upper bound was chosen to be 2 as neither parameter is expected to exceed it based on independent empirical determinations. The results in Figure
1
demonstrate that the posterior distributions lie within the
(0,2)
interval. This same approach was taken in fitting the corresponding
f\sigma_{8}(z)
for the
\Lambda
CDM case, except that Equations (
11
) and (
12
) were used for both the fiducial and the
“target”
cosmologies, with only the specific parameter values differing between the two. The theoretical
f\sigma_{8}(z)
curve for the
\Lambda
CDM case was constructed from the following two functions. Firstly, the analytical expression for
\sigma_{8}(z)=\sigma_{8,0}D(z)
was obtained by using the fact that
D(a)=ag(a)/g(1)
, where
g(a)
is the growth suppression factor
(
D. Huterer 2023b
)
. For a flat
\Lambda
CDM universe, at late times,
g(z)
can be expressed as
(
H. Mo et al. 2010
)

\begin{split}g(z)&=\frac{5}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(z)\biggl[\Omega^{\frac{4}{7}}_{\mathrm{m}}(z)-\Omega_{\Lambda}(z)+\\
&+\left(1+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(z)}{2}\right)\left(1+\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}(z)}{70}\right)\biggr]^{-1}.\end{split}

(15)

Secondly, there exists a convenient form of
f(z)
for the
\Lambda
CDM universe, called the growth index parametrization
{f(z)=\Omega^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{m}}(z)}

(
L. Wang & P. J. Steinhardt 1998
)
, where
{\gamma=6/11}
is implied by
{w_{\mathrm{\Lambda}}=-1}

(
S. Nesseris & L. Perivolaropoulos 2008
)
.

Evaluation of the models’ consistency with the data and determination of model preference could not be performed using the reduced chi-squared statistic due to the non-linearity of the models’ dependencies on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
and
\sigma_{8,0}
, which prevents reliable determination of the number of degrees of freedom
(
R. Andrae et al. 2010
)
. Instead, we assessed model consistency with the data by computing the residuals of the fits, normalizing them with recalibrated uncertainties, and subjecting them to the Anderson-Darling (AD) test for normality
(
T. W. Anderson & D. A. Darling 1952
)
. A model with the correct parameter values would produce residuals which follow a standard normal distribution by definition
(
R. Andrae et al. 2010
)
. The AD test quantitatively measures how closely the residuals adhere to this expectation, providing a robust method for model comparison. Additionally, as curve fitting was performed using Nested Sampling, we also used the Bayesian evidence computed by the program to establish model preference.

We fitted Equation (
9
) to three coasting models, with curvature parameters
{k=\{-1,0,+1\}}
. The best-fit values of parameters correspond to the median values, while the uncertainties are derived by taking the difference between the 84th and 16th percentiles and the median. Using these parameter values, we calculated the uncertainty-normalized residuals, denoted as
R
, for the recalibrated dataset. The uncertainty-normalized residuals are defined as

R=\frac{(f\sigma_{8}(z))_{\mathrm{data}}-(f\sigma_{8}(z))_{\mathrm{model}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}},

(16)

where
(f\sigma_{8}(z))_{\mathrm{data}}
is the observed value,
(f\sigma_{8}(z))_{\mathrm{model}}
is the model-predicted value, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}
is the uncertainty in the data.
We then conducted an AD test to assess how well these residuals adhere to a standard normal distribution. Using the MATLAB implementation of the AD test
(
The MathWorks Inc. 2024
)
with a 5% significance level, we accepted the null hypothesis that
R
follows a standard normal distribution for all three values of
k
. The test yielded
p
-values of
{p=\{0.655,\,0.713,\,0.778\}}
. The results of the fits are summarized in Table
1
illustrated in Figure
2
. Repeating the analysis for the dataset homogenized with respect to the baseline
Planck
2018
\Lambda
CDM cosmology, we obtained the following best-fit values:
{\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}=0.286^{+0.053}_{-0.046}}
and
{\sigma_{8,0}=0.764^{+0.039}_{-0.035}}
, which deviate from the
Planck
2018 values by
0.55\sigma
and
1.2\sigma
, respectively. The AD test accepted the null hypothesis of a standard normal distribution with a
p
-value of 0.416. These results suggest that both the concordance
\Lambda
CDM model and the coasting models are consistent with the available growth data from redshift space distortion measurements.

In evaluating which model provides a
better
fit to the growth data, we used the base-10 logarithm of the ratio of the probabilities (
p
-values) yielded by the AD test, referred to as the log Bayes factor, defined as
{\log_{10}{\mathcal{B}}\equiv\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{coasting}}/p_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}})}
, to determine model preference. For
{k=\{-1,0,+1\}}
, we found
{\log_{10}{\mathcal{B}}=\{0.198,\,0.234,\,0.272\}}
, respectively. This indicates a weak preference for the coasting models, with the
k=+1
model being preferred overall. However, as
\log_{10}{\mathcal{B}}\in(0,0.5)
in all cases, no preference is substantial
(
R. E. Kass & A. E. Raftery 1995
)
, and all models may be said to fit the data equally well. The best-fit parameters and model comparison results are summarized in Table
1
. It should also be noted, that the coasting models generally yielded higher
p
-values than the flat
\Lambda
CDM model, as seen in Figure
3
. The median
p
-value for the
\Lambda
CDM model is exceeded by {71%, 73%, 77%} of
p
-values for the
{k=\{-1,0,+1\}}
coasting models, respectively, while the maximum
p
-value is exceeded by {12%, 26%, 39%}.

Mirroring the approach of
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
, we also fitted the models to the full dataset to assess whether consistency with the data is preserved and whether the best-fit parameters remain in agreement with those obtained from the subset. All four models remained consistent with the full dataset, though the
p
-values from the AD test were notably lower. The
p
-value for the coasting model with
k=+1
was once again the largest, followed by those of the
k=0
and
k=-1
coasting models, and lastly that of the
\Lambda
CDM model. In terms of best-fit parameters, both
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
and
\sigma_{8,0}
differed by less than
0.5\sigma
from their corresponding values obtained from the subset, across all models.

Additionally, as we used the
dynesty
implementation of Nested Sampling, the program estimated the Bayesian evidence
\mathcal{Z}
for each model. Thus, the data’s preference for one model over another can be quantified by defining
\log_{10}{\mathcal{B}}
as
{\log_{10}{\mathcal{B}}\equiv\log_{10}{(\mathcal{Z}_{1}/\mathcal{Z}_{2})}}
. In contrast to what the normalized residuals suggested,
\mathcal{Z}
was greatest for the
\Lambda
CDM model. Therefore, we computed
{\log_{10}{\mathcal{B}}=\log_{10}{(\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}}/\mathcal{Z}_{k})}}
, which yielded
\{1.78,1.53,1.40\}
for
{k=\{-1,0,+1\}}
. As such, based on the
\mathcal{Z}
values yielded by
dynesty
, we would conclude that the data strongly favor
(
R. E. Kass & A. E. Raftery 1995
)
the flat
\Lambda
CDM model. However, when the distributions of the normalized residuals are inspected, we find that their standard deviation is less than 1 for all models, and most of all for the flat
\Lambda
CDM model. This suggests that the flat
\Lambda
CDM model overfits the data, which the AD test is more sensitive to, resulting in a lower
p
-value.

Subsequent to performing model comparisons, we turned our attention to the tension in the present value of the weighted amplitude of matter fluctuations,
S_{8}
.
This analysis was motivated by the potential for a quasi-linear expansion to resolve the
S_{8}
tension (see Section
I
). Currently, the
Planck
-only value of
S_{8}
, obtained from CMB anisotropy analysis assuming a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology,
{S_{8}=0.834\pm 0.016}

(
Planck Collaboration 2020a
)
, exhibits varying degrees of statistical tension with values derived from late-time probes of LSS, such as weak gravitational lensing. Specifically, results from the Dark Energy Survey Year 3 (DES-Y3),
{S_{8}=0.759^{+0.025}_{-0.023}}

(
A. Amon et al. 2022
)
, and the Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS-1000),
{S_{8}=0.759^{+0.024}_{-0.021}}

(
M. Asgari et al. 2021
)
, exhibit
2.7\sigma
and
2.8\sigma
tensions with the
Planck
2018 value, respectively.

) yielded

These results suggest that a quasi-linear coasting cosmology has the potential to resolve the
S_{8}
tension. As noted by
M. A. Sabogal et al. 2024
, however, all determinations of
S_{8}
are inherently model-dependent. Consequently, the value of
S_{8}
inferred from analyzing the CMB under the assumption of a
\Lambda
CDM model would not directly correspond to the value obtained for a quasi-linear coasting model. Nevertheless, it remains plausible that the
S_{8}
value derived from CMB anisotropy measurements within a quasi-linear coasting framework might closely align with the value predicted by
\Lambda
CDM, given the substantial similarities between the two models at the epoch of recombination. This correspondence would not hold for strictly linear coasting models, which, for example, lack a matter-dominated phase at recombination.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we derived closed-form expressions for the growth factor,
D(z)
(Equation
7
), of universes experiencing an expansion linear in cosmic time, from which the functional form of their density-weighted growth rates could be obtained (Equation
9
). We recalibrated a set of
f\sigma_{8}
data points obtained from redshift space distortion measurements collated by
F. Skara & L. Perivolaropoulos 2020
using a correction factor based on the Alcock-Paczyński effect
(
C. Alcock & B. Paczynski 1979
)
to account for the data’s model dependence and facilitate curve fitting. We performed this recalibration for coasting universes with curvature parameters
{k=\{-1,0,+1\}}
in
H^{2}_{0}c^{-2}
units. Using the
dynesty
implementation (
J. S. Speagle 2020
,
S. Koposov et al. 2024
) of Nested Sampling, we fitted Equation (
9
) to the recalibrated dataset assuming uniform priors on the
(0,2)
interval for both parameters of the fit (
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}
and
\sigma_{8,0}
), and performed the AD test on the resultant uncertainty-normalized residuals of the fit to assess the models’ consistency with the data. The entire process described here was also repeated for a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology as well, for which the data was recalibrated using the
Planck
2018 TT,TE,EE+lowE+lensing value of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m,0}}

(
Planck Collaboration 2020a
)
. Model comparison being performed using the results of the AD tests. The results of the fits and tests are summarized in Table
1
.

We found that all four models are consistent with the available
f\sigma_{8}
data. When model preference is quantified using the AD test, the dataset exhibits a weak preference for coasting models, of which the
k=+1
model is preferred the most, although none of these preferences are substantial. In contrast, when using the Bayesian evidence
\mathcal{Z}
as estimated by
dynesty
, the
\Lambda
CDM model is preferred strongly. This, however, is due to the
\Lambda
CDM model’s overfitting the data, which the AD test is more sensitive to. Thus, we reagard the results of the AD test more reliable for the purposes of model comparison. With regards to the
S_{8}
tension, the values obtained for the flat
\Lambda
CDM model and the coasting model with
k=-1
exhibit an approximately
2\sigma
tension with the
Planck
2018 (TT,TE,EE+lowE) value. The tension is significantly lower for the flat and the spherical coasting models, which produced
1.07\sigma
and
0.52\sigma
discrepancies, respectively, meaning that there is effectively no tension for these two models. Establishing whether the
S_{8}
tension is resolved by linear expansion at late times would require obtaining a value for
S_{8}
from CMB measurements within a coasting cosmology framework, which requires further theoretical development. These considerations highlight the importance of further research into the potential resolution of the
S_{8}
tension.
