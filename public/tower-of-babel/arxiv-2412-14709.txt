Minimal rank of primitively n n -universal integral quadratic forms over local rings Thanks: This work was supported by the National Research Foundation of Korea(NRF) grant funded by the Korea government(MSIT)(RS-2024-00342122) and (NRF-2020R1A5A1016126).
Byeong-Kweon Oh and Jongheun Yoon

Abstract
. Let F F be a local field and let R R be its ring of integers. For a positive integer n n , an integral quadratic form defined over R R is called primitively n n -universal if it primitively represents all quadratic forms of rank n n . It was proved in [ 7 ] that the minimal rank of primitively 1 1 -universal quadratic forms over the p p -adic integer ring ℤ p \mathbb{Z}_{p} is 2 2 if p p is odd, and 3 3 otherwise. In this article, we completely determine the minimal rank of primitively n n -universal quadratic forms over R R for any positive integer n n and any local ring R R such that 2 2 is a unit or a prime.

1.
Introduction

In
1770
, Lagrange proved that the quadratic form
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2}
represents all positive integers, that is, the diophantine equation

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2}=n

has an integer solution
x,y,z
, and
w
for any positive integer
n
. Any (positive definite and integral) quadratic form having this property is called
universal
, as introduced by Dickson and Ross. The concept of universality has been generalized in many directions, including the
n
-universality and the primitive
n
-universality over various rings. Among those, the primitive
n
-universality over local rings is of particular interest in this article, whose precise definition will be given in what follows.

Let
F
be a local field at place
\mathfrak{p}
and let
R
be the ring of integers in
F
. We generally assume that the characteristic of
F
is not two. A quadratic form of rank
n
defined over
F
is a quadratic homogeneous polynomial

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\qquad\text{($f_{ij}=f_{ji}\in F$),}

where the corresponding symmetric matrix
M_{f}=(f_{ij})
, which is called the Gram matrix of
f
, is nondegenerate. We say that
f
is classically integral if the Gram matrix
M_{f}
of
f
is an integral matrix, that is,
f_{ij}=f_{ji}\in R
for any
i
,
j
. Whereas,
f
is called non-classically integral if
f
is an integral polynomial, that is,
f_{ii}
and
2f_{ij}
are in
R
for any
i
,
j
.

For two (integral) quadratic forms
f
and
g
of rank
n
and
m
, respectively, we say that
f
is represented by
g
over
R
if there is an integral matrix
T\in M_{n,m}(R)
such that

M_{f}=T^{t}M_{g}T\text{.}

We say that
f
is isometric to
g
if the above integral matrix
T
is invertible. We say that
f
is primitively represented by
g
if the above integral matrix
T
can be extended to an invertible matrix in
GL_{m}(R)
by adding suitable
(m-n)
columns. In particular, an integer
k\in R
is primitively represented by
g
if and only if there are integers
x_{1}
, …,
x_{m}\in R
such that

k=g(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m})\text{\, and at least one of $x_{i}$ is a unit.}

For a positive integer
n
, an integral quadratic form is called (
primitively
)
n
-
universal
if it (primitively, respectively) represents all integral quadratic forms of rank
n
. A complete classification of
n
-universal quadratic forms is recently published in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, and
[
10
]
. Finding primitively
1
-universal quadratic forms was first considered, as far as the authors know, by Budarina in
[
1
]
. She classified all primitively
1
-universal quaternary quadratic forms when
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for any odd prime
p
, and also obtained some partial classification when
R=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Recently, Earnest and Gunawardana provided a complete classification of all primitively
1
-universal quadratic forms over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for any prime
p
, including results for both classically integral and non-classically integral forms when
p=2
. Budarina also classified in
[
2
]
primitively
2
-universal quadratic forms having squarefree odd discriminant.

The subsequent discussion will be conducted in a more suitable language of quadratic spaces and lattices. A quadratic
F
-space, simply a space, is a finite dimensional vector space
V
over
F
equipped with a symmetric bilinear form

B:V\times V\to F\text{,}

and the corresponding quadratic form
Q:V\to F
is defined by
Q(v)=B(v,v)
for any
v\in V
. A quadratic
R
-lattice, simply a lattice, is a finitely generated
R
-submodule of a quadratic
F
-space. The scale
\mathfrak{s}L
of a quadratic
R
-lattice
L
is the
R
-submodule
B(L,L)
of
F
, and the norm
\mathfrak{n}L
of
L
is the
R
-submodule of
F
generated by
Q(L)=\{Q(v)\mid v\in L\}
. An
R
-lattice
L
is called integral if
\mathfrak{s}L\subseteq R
. Throughout this article, we always assume that any lattice is integral, unless stated otherwise. An (integral)
R
-lattice
L
is called even if
\mathfrak{n}L\subseteq 2R
.

Given a basis
e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}
for a space
V
, the corresponding symmetric
n\times n
matrix
M_{V}=(B(e_{i},e_{j}))
is called the Gram matrix of
V
, and in this case, we write

V\cong(B(e_{i},e_{j}))\quad\text{in}\quad e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\text{.}

The discriminant
dV
of a space
V
is an element of the quotient monoid
F/(F^{\times})^{2}
defined by
dV=(\det M_{V})(F^{\times})^{2}
. We call a space
V
nondegenerate if
M_{V}
is nondegenerate. If a space
V
has the zero Gram matrix, then
V
is called totally isotropic. For a nondegenerate space
V
, all maximal totally isotropic subspace of
V
has the same dimension. Such a dimension is denoted by
\operatorname{ind}V
, called the Witt index of
V
. One may easily show that if
\operatorname{ind}V\geq r
, then
V
is orthogonally split by a
2r
-dimensional hyperbolic space.

Given a basis
e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}
for a lattice
L
, the corresponding symmetric
n\times n
matrix
M_{L}=(B(e_{i},e_{j}))
is called the Gram matrix of
L
, and in this case, we write

L\cong(B(e_{i},e_{j}))\quad\text{in}\quad e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\text{.}

The discriminant
dL
of a lattice
L
is an element of the quotient monoid
F/(R^{\times})^{2}
defined by
dL=(\det M_{L})(R^{\times})^{2}
. We call a lattice
L
nondegenerate if
M_{L}
is nondegenerate. Throughout this article, we always assume that a lattice is nondegenerate, unless stated otherwise. For a symmetric
n\times n
matrix
N
over
R
, we let
\langle N\rangle
(or simply
N
) stand for any
R
-lattice whose Gram matrix is
N
. Moreover, for
\alpha_{1}
, …,
\alpha_{n}\in R
, the notation

\langle\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n}\rangle

stands for the
R
-lattice having a diagonal Gram matrix
\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n})
. Let
\Delta=1-4\rho
be a fixed nonsquare unit in
R
such that
\rho\in R^{\times}
. The symbols
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{A}
stand for binary
R
-lattices whose Gram matrices are

\mathbb{H}\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{A}\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\rho\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \text{respectively.}

In fact,
\mathbb{H}
is the isotropic even unimodular lattice, and
\mathbb{A}
is the anisotropic even unimodular lattice. Scalings of
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{A}
by an integer
\alpha\in R
are denoted by
\alpha\mathbb{H}\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\alpha\\
\alpha&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\alpha\mathbb{A}\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2\alpha&\alpha\\
\alpha&2\rho\alpha\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. For a positive integer
n
,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
denotes the lattice
\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}\dotsb\mathbin{\perp}\mathbb{H}
with rank
2n
. We also use the same symbol
\mathbb{H}
which stands for the hyperbolic plane
F\mathbb{H}
, if no confusion arises.

For two
R
-lattices
L
and
M
, a representation from
L
to
M
is a linear map
\sigma:L\to M
such that

B(\sigma(v),\sigma(v^{\prime}))=B(v,v^{\prime})

for any
v
,
v^{\prime}\in L
. In addition, if
\sigma(L)
is a direct summand of
M
, then such a representation is called
primitive
. If there exists a (primitive) representation
\sigma:L\to M
, then we say that
L
is (primitively, respectively) represented by
M
. If such a representation
\sigma
is bijective then we say that
L
is isometric to
M
, denoted by
\sigma:L\cong M
. An
R
-lattice
M
is called (
primitively
)
n
-
universal
if
M
(primitively, respectively) represents all
R
-lattices of rank
n
. We denote by
u_{R}(n)
the minimal rank of
n
-universal
R
-lattices, and denote by
u^{\ast}_{R}(n)
the minimal rank of primitively
n
-universal (P
n
U, for short)
R
-lattices. For
u_{R}(n)
, the followings are well-known (see, for instance,
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
): If
R
is nondyadic, then

u_{R}(n)=\begin{cases}2n&\text{if $1\leq n\leq 2$,}\\
n+3&\text{if $n\geq 3$;}\end{cases}

If
R
is unramified dyadic, then

u_{R}(n)=\begin{cases}2n+1&\text{if $1\leq n\leq 2$,}\\
n+3&\text{if $n\geq 3$.}\end{cases}

The aim of this article is to determine
u^{\ast}_{R}(n)
completely for any integer
n
and any local ring
R
such that
2
is a unit or a prime.

Let
I
be an ideal in
R
. For any
\alpha
,
\beta\in R
, the congruence
\alpha\equiv\beta\ (\mathrm{mod}\ I)
takes on its usual meaning, namely
\alpha-\beta\in I
. For any
\gamma\in R
, we write

\alpha\equiv\beta\pmod{\gamma}

instead of
\alpha\equiv\beta\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \gamma R)
. Moreover, for any subset
S
,
T
of
R
, the congruence
S\equiv T\ (\mathrm{mod}\ I)
means that
\alpha\equiv\beta\ (\mathrm{mod}\ I)
for any
\alpha\in S
,
\beta\in T
. For instance, we have
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\times})^{2}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)
.

Any unexplained notation and terminology can be found in
[
11
]
or
[
14
]
.

2.
Witt indices of spaces admitting a P
n
U lattice

In this section, we prove some necessary condition for a quadratic space having a primitively
n
-universal
R
-lattice. More precisely, we prove that if
M
is a primitively
n
-universal
R
-lattice, then the space
FM
represents a
2n
-dimensional hyperbolic space. In particular, we have
u^{\ast}_{R}(n)\geq 2n
for any positive integer
n
.
In fact, the result follows from an easy application of a multivariable version of Hensel’s lemma given below. Let
K
be a complete field with respect to an absolute value
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
satisfying the strong triangle inequality, and let
\mathfrak{o}:=\{x\in K:\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq 1\}
.

Let
n\geq 1
and define a norm of
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in K^{n}
by
\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert:=\max_{i}\left\lvert c_{i}\right\rvert
. Denote the derivative matrix and Jacobian of
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1}(\mathbf{X}),\dots,f_{n}(\mathbf{X}))\in K[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]^{n}
by

(D\mathbf{f})(\mathbf{X})=\left(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial X_{j}}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{X})=\det((D\mathbf{f})(\mathbf{X})).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{f}\in\mathfrak{o}[\mathbf{X}]^{n}
and let
\mathbf{a}\in\mathfrak{o}^{n}
satisfy
\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})\rVert<\left\lvert J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{a})\right\rvert^{2}
. Then there is a unique
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{o}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{f}(\bm{\alpha})=\mathbf{0}
and
\lVert\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{a}\rVert<\left\lvert J_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{a})\right\rvert
.

Proof.

See Theorem 3.3 of
[
4
]
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

For
m\geq n\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\in\mathfrak{o}[X_{1},\dots,X_{m}]^{n}
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\mathfrak{o}^{m}
satisfy
\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})\rVert<\left\lvert J_{\mathbf{f},n}(\mathbf{a})\right\rvert^{2}
, where
J_{\mathbf{f},n}(\mathbf{a})=\det\left(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial X_{j}}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
. Then there is an
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{o}^{n}
such that

\mathbf{f}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},a_{n+1},\dots,a_{m})=\mathbf{0}

and
\left\lvert\alpha_{i}-a_{i}\right\rvert<\left\lvert J_{\mathbf{f},n}(\mathbf{a})\right\rvert
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

See Theorem 3.8 of
[
4
]
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For
m\geq n\geq 1
, let
G=(g_{ij})
and
H
be symmetric matrices over
\mathfrak{o}
of size
n
and
m
, respectively, and let
A=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n})=(a_{ij})_{m\times n}
be a matrix over
\mathfrak{o}
such that
G=A^{t}HA
. Suppose that
H
is nondegenerate and
A
is primitive. Then, for any
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})\in\mathfrak{o}^{n}
satisfying

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\lvert g_{in}-\gamma_{i}\right\rvert<4\left\lvert\det H\right\rvert^{2}\text{,}

there is an
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{o}^{m}
such that
A_{1}=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n-1},\bm{\alpha})
is also primitive, and
(g^{\prime}_{ij})=A_{1}^{t}FA_{1}
satisfies

g^{\prime}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\gamma_{j}&\text{if }i=n\text{,}\\
\gamma_{i}&\text{if }j=n\text{,}\\
g_{ij}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We regard
g_{1n}-\gamma_{1}
,  …,
g_{n-1,n}-\gamma_{n-1}
,  and
g_{nn}-\gamma_{n}
as
n
polynomials in
m
variables
a_{1n},\dots,a_{mn}
. Note that
g_{in}-\gamma_{i}=g_{ni}-\gamma_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. To apply Theorem
2.2
, it suffices to show that the derivative matrix has an
n\times n
subdeterminant whose absolute value is greater than or equal to
2\left\lvert\det H\right\rvert
.

First, note that the derivative matrix is
\operatorname{diag}(1,\dots,1,2)A^{t}H
. Hence, it suffices to show that
A^{t}H
has an
n\times n
subdeterminant whose absolute value is greater than or equal to
\left\lvert\det H\right\rvert
. By the primitivity of
A
, we may complete
A^{t}
to an element of
GL_{m}(\mathfrak{o})
, namely

U=\begin{pmatrix}A^{t}\\
\hline\cr C\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

By the theory of modules over a principal ideal domain, there is a
V\in GL_{m}(\mathfrak{o})
such that
UHV=T=(t_{ij})_{m\times m}
is lower triangular. Clearly,
A^{t}HV=(t_{ij})_{n\times m}
has a submatrix (the leftmost one) whose determinant is
d=\prod_{i=1}^{n}t_{ii}
. Then,

\left\lvert d\right\rvert=\left\lvert\prod_{i=1}^{n}t_{ii}\right\rvert\geq\left\lvert\prod_{i=1}^{m}t_{ii}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\det UHV\right\rvert=\left\lvert\det H\right\rvert\text{.}

Now, observe that
d
is a linear combination of
n\times n
subdeterminants of
A^{t}H
. Hence, there exists at least one with absolute value greater than or equal to
\left\lvert\det H\right\rvert
. This proves the lemma.
∎

From now until the end of this section, we do not assume that a lattice is nondegenerate.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
M
be a nondegenerate
R
-lattice. Let
N
and
N^{\prime}
be
R
-lattices of the same rank
n
with Gram matrices
G
,
G^{\prime}
, respectively, such that
G-G^{\prime}\in M_{n}(4(dM)^{2}\mathfrak{p})
. Then
N
is primitively represented by
M
if and only if
N^{\prime}
is primitively represented by
M
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
M
be a nondegenerate
R
-lattice. Then the followings are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{ind}FM\geq n
;

(2)

some lattice
N
of rank
n
with
\mathfrak{s}N\subseteq 4(dM)^{2}\mathfrak{p}
is primitively represented by
M
;

(3)

all lattices
N
of rank
n
with
\mathfrak{s}N\subseteq 4(dM)^{2}\mathfrak{p}
are primitively represented by
M
.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
M
is a primitively
n
-universal nondegenerate
R
-lattice, then
\operatorname{ind}FM\geq n
. In particular,
u^{\ast}_{R}(n)\geq 2n
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the corollary given above.
∎

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
M
be a primitively
n
-universal
R
-lattice of rank

. We have the followings:

(a)
If
m=2n
, then
FM
is hyperbolic.

(b)
If

, then

.

(c)
If

and

, then

is hyperbolic.

As a direct consequence of the above corollary, any primitively

-universal

-lattice of rank

is an

-universal
R
-lattice on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Furthermore, any primitively
n
-universal

-lattice

of rank

is an

-universal

-lattice on the space
\mathbb{H}^{n}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle(-1)^{n}dM\right\rangle
.

Remark 2.8
.

Suppose that an
R
-lattice

, where

)
.

3.
Minimal rank of P
n
U lattices over nondyadic local rings

In this section, we prove that
u^{\ast}_{R}(n)=2n
for any nondyadic local ring
R
and any positive integer
n
. Furthermore, it is shown that, up to isometry, there exist exactly one primitively
2
-universal lattice of rank
4
and exactly two primitively
3
-universal lattices of rank
6
over any nondyadic local ring.

Recall that
\mathbb{H}\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is the even unimodular
R
-lattice on the hyperbolic plane. Let
x,y
be a basis for
\mathbb{H}
such that
\mathbb{H}\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
in this basis. Then,
Q(\alpha x+\beta y)=2\alpha\beta
for any
\alpha
,
\beta\in R
. Now, the proof of the following lemma is quite straightforward.

Lemma 3.1
.

(a)
The
R
-lattice
\mathbb{H}
primitively represents all even integers. In particular, if
R
is nondyadic, then
\mathbb{H}
is primitively
1
-universal.

(b)
If an
R
-lattice
J
primitively represents a lattice
K
of rank
k
, then
\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}J
primitively represents all lattices of rank
k+1
of the form
\left\langle\alpha\right\rangle\mathbin{\perp}K
for any
\alpha\in 2R
. In particular, if
R
is nondyadic, then
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is primitively
n
-universal.

The above lemma shows that, for any nondyadic local ring
R
,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a primitively
n
-universal
R
-lattice of rank
2n
. By combining it with Corollary
2.7
, we conclude that the minimal rank of primitively
n
-universal quadratic
R
-lattices is exactly
2n
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R
be a nondyadic local ring and let
\pi\in R
be a prime.

(a)
Any primitively
2
-universal
R
-lattice of rank
4
is isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

(b)
Any primitively
3
-universal
R
-lattice of rank
6
is isometric to either
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or
\mathbb{H}^{2}\mathbin{\perp}\pi\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

(a) According to
[
10
, Proposition 3.3]
and Corollary
2.7
, any
2
-universal quaternary lattice on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is isometric to
I_{4}\cong\mathbb{H}^{2}
. (b) According to
[
10
, Proposition 3.4]
and Corollary
2.7
, any
3
-universal senary lattice on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is isometric to either
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or
\mathbb{H}^{2}\mathbin{\perp}\pi\mathbb{H}
. It can be easily shown that
\mathbb{H}^{2}\mathbin{\perp}\pi\mathbb{H}
is also primitively
3
-universal.
∎

4.
Minimal rank of P
n
U non-classically integral lattices over unramified dyadic local rings

Hereafter, we assume that
R
is unramified dyadic. Recall that an
R
-lattice
L
is called non-classically integral if
\mathfrak{n}L\subseteq R
. A non-classically integral lattice is called primitively
n
-universal if it primitively represents all non-classically integral lattices of rank
n
. In this section, we prove that the minimal rank of primitively
n
-universal non-classically integral lattices is
2n
. Furthermore, it can be shown that, up to isometry, there exists exactly one primitively
2
-universal non-classically integral lattice of rank
4
.

Clearly, the study of primitively
n
-universal non-classically integral lattices is equivalent to the study of even lattices that primitively represent all even lattices of rank
n
. The following is a supplement of Lemma
3.1
for even lattices over unramified dyadic local ring
R
.

Lemma 4.1
.

(a)
If an
R
-lattice
J
is isotropic, then
\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}J
primitively represents all binary lattices of the form
2^{a}\mathbb{H}
for any nonnegative integer
a
.

(b)
If an
R
-lattice
J
primitively represents
2^{a+1}\epsilon
for a nonnegative integer
a
and a unit
\epsilon\in R
, then
\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}J
primitively represents all binary lattices of the form
2^{a}\mathbb{H}
and
2^{a}\mathbb{A}
. In particular,
\mathbb{H}^{2}
primitively represents all binary lattices of the form
2^{a}\mathbb{H}
and
2^{a}\mathbb{A}
for any nonnegative integer
a
. Hence,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
primitively represents all even lattices of rank
n
.

Proof.

We let
x,y
be a basis for
\mathbb{H}

,
y_{n-1}=\alpha e_{2n-2}
, and
\beta_{n-1}=1
. If
K_{r}\cong\alpha\mathbb{A}
, note that
Q(w_{r})=2\epsilon\alpha
for some unit
\epsilon
in
R
. In that case, let
x_{n-1}=\rho\epsilon^{-1}e_{2n-3}+\alpha e_{2n-2}
,
y_{n-1}=e_{2n-3}
, and
\beta_{n-1}=1
. Now, note that

R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},\beta_{1}y_{1}+\dotsb+\beta_{n-1}y_{n-1}+z]

is a primitive sublattice of
L
that is isometric to
\ell
.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice
\mathbb{H}^{3}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
primitively represents all
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattices of rank
4
except for the quaternary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice
\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{A}
.

Proof.

Let
L\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
and let
\ell
be any
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice of rank
4
not isometric to
\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{A}
. Since
Q^{\ast}(\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\times}\cup 8\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
\ell
is primitively represented by
L
unless any Jordan decomposition
\ell=\ell_{1}\mathbin{\perp}\dotsb\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{t}
(
\ell_{i}
is nonzero for any
1\leq i\leq t
) of
\ell
satisfies
2\mathbb{Z}_{2}\supseteq\mathfrak{n}\ell_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathfrak{n}\ell_{t}\supseteq 4\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, by Lemmas
4.1
and
5.2
. Suppose that
\ell
has such a Jordan decomposition. Assume that
\ell
is orthogonally split by
2^{a}\mathbb{H}
for some nonnegative integer
a
. Since
2^{a}\mathbb{H}
is primitively represented by
\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
,
\ell
is primitively represented by
L
. Hence, we may assume that
\ell
has a proper component. Assume that
\ell\cong\left\langle 2^{a_{1}}\epsilon_{1},2^{a_{2}}\epsilon_{2},2^{a_{3}}\epsilon_{3},2^{a_{4}}\epsilon_{4}\right\rangle
. Since
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\in\{1,2\}
, up to rearrangement, at least one among

2^{a_{1}}\epsilon_{1}+2^{a_{2}}\epsilon_{2}\text{,}\quad 2^{a_{1}}\epsilon_{1}+2^{a_{2}}\epsilon_{2}+2^{a_{3}}\epsilon_{3}\text{,}\quad 2^{a_{1}}\epsilon_{1}+2^{a_{2}}\epsilon_{2}+2^{a_{3}}\epsilon_{3}+2^{a_{4}}\epsilon_{4}

is a multiple of
8
, so that it is primitively represented by
\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
. Hence,
\ell
is primitively represented by
L
by Lemma
6.6
. Finally, assume that
\ell\cong\left\langle 2^{a_{1}}\epsilon_{1},2^{a_{2}}\epsilon_{2}\right\rangle\mathbin{\perp}2^{a_{3}}\mathbb{A}
. Note that
a_{1},a_{2}\in\{1,2\}
and
a_{3}\in\{0,1\}
. If
a_{1}=a_{3}+1
, then
\ell
is split by
2^{a_{3}}\mathbb{H}
. If
a_{1}=a_{3}=1
, then
\ell
is an orthogonal sum of proper components. Hence, we may assume that
a_{1}=a_{2}=2
and
a_{3}=0
. Then,
2^{a_{1}}\epsilon_{1}+2^{a_{2}}\epsilon_{2}
is a multiple of
8
. Hence,
\ell
is primitively represented by
L
by Lemma
6.6
.
∎

Theorem 6.8
.

For any
n\geq 5
, the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice
\mathbb{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
is primitively
n
-universal. In particular,
u^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(n)=2n
for any
n\geq 5
.

Proof.

Suppose that
n=5
. Any
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice of rank
5
is split by a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice of rank
1
. Hence, by Lemma
6.7
, it suffices to show that any
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice
\ell
of the form
\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 2^{a}\epsilon\right\rangle
is primitively represented by
\mathbb{H}^{4}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
. If
a=0
or
a\geq 3
, then
\left\langle 2^{a}\epsilon\right\rangle
is primitively represented by
\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
. If
a=2
, then
\ell\cong\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 20\epsilon\right\rangle
. If
a=1
, then
\ell\cong\mathbb{H}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 10\epsilon\right\rangle
.

Suppose that
n=6
. Any
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice of rank
6
either has a component of rank
1
or is an orthogonal sum of improper
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattices of rank
2
. For the former case, the primitive representability follows directly from the case when
n=5
by Lemma
5.2
. For the latter case, consider a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattice of the form
\ell_{1}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{2}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{3}
, where
\ell_{i}
’s are binary improper
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-lattices. Clearly, it is impossible that
\ell_{1}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{2}\cong\ell_{1}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{3}\cong\ell_{2}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{3}\cong\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{A}
. Hence, we may assume that
\ell_{1}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{2}\not\cong\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\perp}2\mathbb{A}
. Therefore,
\ell_{1}\mathbin{\perp}\ell_{2}
is primitively represented by
\mathbb{H}^{3}\mathbin{\perp}\left\langle 1,-1\right\rangle
, and
\ell_{3}
is primitively represented by
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

The case when
n\geq 7
follows by an induction on
n
. It follows from the case of
n-1
for any odd integer
n
, from the cases of
n-1
and
n-2
for any even integer
n
, by Lemmas
4.1
and
5.2
. This completes the proof.
∎
