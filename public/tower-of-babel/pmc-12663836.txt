From intractable hiccups to optic neuritis: paraneoplastic neuromyelitis optica with leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in the setting of immature ovarian teratoma
Authors: Shannon Zhou, Julie Szczygielski, Boris Winterhoff, Jordan Mattson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This is the first recorded case of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) associated with immature ovarian teratoma.•NMOSD can present early with area postrema syndrome, including intractable hiccups, nausea, and vomiting.•Diagnosis can be complex in the setting of central nervous system metastases.•Early neurologic evaluation and multidisciplinary team involvement are pertinent for timely diagnosis and improved outcomes.Keywords: Neuromyelitis optica, Paraneoplastic syndrome, Ovarian cancer, Teratoma, AQP4, Gynecologic oncology

Introduction

Paraneoplastic syndromes (PNS; singular also PNS) are a rare group of disorders indirectly caused by an underlying malignancy (Henry, 2019). They involve symptoms caused by substances (e.g. cytokines, hormones, growth factors) released by the tumor or the body’s immune response to the tumor. Clinical signs of PNS may precede, coincide with, or manifest after initial malignant diagnosis. Many different subgroups of PNS have been associated with gynecologic malignancy, including neurologic, ophthalmologic, nephrologic, endocrine, dermatologic, rheumatologic, and hematologic subgroups (Viau et al., 2017). Of gynecologic cancers, ovarian cancer is the most commonly associated with PNS, with an incidence rate of approximately 10 % (Ray and Kapoor, 2025).

One type of neurologic PNS is Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder (NMOSD). In NMOSD, aquaporin 4 (AQP4) antigens (or similarly structured antigens) are expressed by cancer cells (Wingerchuk and Lucchinetti, 2022). AQP4 is also natively expressed on astrocytes in the optic nerve, spinal cord, and area postrema. In an attempt to destroy cancer cells, the body makes AQP4-IgG Antibodies (Ab), which attack both the cancer and healthy astrocytes. The attack on healthy tissues results in complement activation, cytotoxicity, and neuronal loss (Wingerchuk and Lucchinetti, 2022). As a result, symptoms include vision loss due to optic neuritis, paraplegia, paroxysmal tonic muscle spasms, sensory dysfunction, bowel/bladder dysfunction due to transverse myelitis, and/or intractable nausea, vomiting, or hiccups due to area postrema syndrome (Wingerchuk and Lucchinetti, 2022).

NMOSD are most commonly found in breast and lung cancers, and are rarely found in ovarian cancers (Sepúlveda et al., 2018). In fact, fewer than 10 cases of NMOSD have been reported in association with ovarian cancers in the medical literature. One recent review identified 6 cases of AQP4-IgG seropositive NMOSD associated with mature ovarian teratoma, while a separate case report described NMOSD associated with ovarian dysgerminoma (Ikeguchi et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2024, Tardo et al., 2022). No cases involving other histological subtypes of ovarian cancer have been clearly documented. We present the first recorded case of paraneoplastic NMOSD and leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in the setting of malignant immature ovarian teratoma.

Case presentation

Initial presentation and management

A 27-year-old female initially presented with fatigue and vertigo, which subsequently progressed to intractable hiccups, nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, and severe hyponatremia. On routine diagnostic CT, she was discovered to have a large pelvic mass seemingly arising from the left ovary, as well as moderate volume ascites and lesions suspicious for peritoneal carcinomatosis. Diagnostic paracentesis revealed clusters of atypical large cells compatible with malignancy. At that time, she was referred to Gynecologic Oncology for further evaluation. Laboratory evaluation revealed the following: CA125 elevated to 429, AFP elevated to 24.3, and LDH elevated to 647. CEA and CA 19–9 were within normal limits.

One month later, she underwent a diagnostic laparoscopy, revealing an enlarged left ovarian mass filling the pelvis, firm omental cake, and miliary disease noted on bowel, right fallopian tube, pelvic and abdominal peritoneum, falciform ligament, and bilateral diaphragms. The procedure was converted to an exploratory laparotomy with left salpingo-oophorectomy, with omental and abdominopelvic biopsies. During laparotomy, there was noted to be extensive involvement of the sigmoid colon and cecum, transverse colon, and tail of the pancreas. Due to this, the decision was made to abort the procedure. Final surgical pathology demonstrated high grade immature teratoma with a glial component. A mixed germ cell component was considered and ruled out with further immunohistochemical staining. A secondary malignant transformation was also considered, such as neuroendocrine carcinoma or primitive neuroectodermal tumor, but was ruled out with further immunohistochemical staining.

Disease sequelae

In the early postoperative period, the patient experienced persistent large volume emesis, as well as nerve pain affecting the neck and upper back with associated tingling in the hands. Additionally, she endorsed vision changes, including blurred vision. Due to this, a brain MRI was ordered. MRI of the brain and spine demonstrated patchy leptomeningeal enhancement of the brainstem and cervical and thoracic spinal cord, concerning for leptomeningeal carcinomatosis. Given the leptomeningeal spread, she was diagnosed with stage IVB immature teratoma of the ovary. On postoperative day 4, she was started on BEP (Bleomycin, Etoposide, Cisplatin) with a plan for 5 cycles and whole brain radiation.

Three weeks postoperatively (2 months after initial symptoms), she presented with acute-onset central scotoma of the right eye, peripheral vision loss of the left eye, and bilateral optic disc edema (right greater left) on MRI (Fig. 1). She also reported pain with bilateral eye movements and increased blurry vision with upward gaze, followed two days later by diplopia and diminished light sensitivity. In the setting of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis and worsening vision, she underwent whole brain radiation with some improvement in vision. During the radiation course, however, she developed new muscle spasms in the lower extremities and abdomen. In the following weeks, spasms progressively worsened, spreading to upper extremities and eventually evolving into full body spasms multiple times per hour and lasting 30–60 s. Given her spasms, vision changes, and history of teratoma, serum AQP4-IgG Ab testing was sent to evaluate for possible paraneoplastic NMOSD. In the interim, spasms continued to increase in frequency and intensity despite multiple symptomatic therapies, with only mild, temporary improvement with tizanidine, oxycodone, diazepam, and duloxetine. The severity of spasms ultimately necessitated the use of a wheelchair due to fall risk. High dose IV methylprednisolone was also trialed in consideration of possible inflammation contributing to spasms in the setting of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, but no improvement was observed.

Ultimately, her AQP4-IgG Ab returned with a 1:100 titer, supporting a diagnosis of paraneoplastic NMOSD. Notably, she was negative for Anti-Yo Ab, Anti-Hu Ab, Anti-Ri Ab, and Anti-TR/DNR Ab, making alternative PNS such as subacute cerebellar ataxia, paraneoplastic encephalomyelitis, paraneoplastic brainstem and cerebellar syndromes, or paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration less likely (Campana and Silva, 2022, Shams’ili et al., 2003, Zaborowski et al., 2014). She then received plasma exchange (PLEX) every other day for 5 days with high dose steroids, baclofen, and diazepam for symptomatic relief. PLEX did not immediately improve spasms. However, as she continued to complete the BEP treatment course and started low-dose carbamazepine 100 mg, spasms notably improved to a few times per day. After an increase to 200 mg of carbamazepine, spasms resolved entirely and she was able to taper off of baclofen and diazepam.

Due to evidence of increased brain metastases on subsequent MRI, she also received 5 sessions of gamma knife radiation. Shortly after completing treatment, she began a steroid taper, after which she developed vertigo and bilateral gaze-provoked horizontal nystagmus. She was restarted on low dose Prednisone, followed by dexamethasone 4 mg after lack of symptomatic improvement. Follow up PET scan (7 months after initial symptom onset) showed positive response to treatment. However, two weeks later, she developed new left-sided facial weakness, dysarthria, left upper and lower extremity ataxia, and gradual left-sided hearing loss. Stroke was ruled out and brain MRI revealed possible progressive disease versus post-treatment effects, including enlarging lesions with increased perfusion in the left middle cerebellar peduncle and left pons. She then received 3 cycles of bevacizumab maintenance therapy before arriving in the emergency department with new dysphagia and hoarseness to a whisper. This was initially suspected to potentially be related to worsening brain metastases versus NMOSD exacerbation. However, MRI showed the etiology to be radiation changes to the left side of the medulla oblongata and inferior cerebellar peduncle (Fig. 2).

Most recently, the patient’s imaging showed stable disease without progressive intracranial enhancement or new spinal cord signal abnormalities. Her vision continues to improve each day in the right eye, though the left eye continues to be somewhat blurry. She is able to watch television but cannot read most words on the screen. There are plans for ocular therapy to strengthen the left eye muscles. Her hearing has declined to profound left-sided sensorineural hearing loss with tinnitus and moderate right-sided sensorineural hearing loss, largely attributed to chemotherapy and radiation. Left-sided facial weakness has remained stable. Although spasms have improved significantly, she continues to experience ataxia and uses a wheelchair to travel longer distances. Steroid weaning has been challenging, as attempts to taper have often been associated with worsening bulbar symptoms. However, rehabilitation efforts have yielded some encouraging progress: strength and mobility have started to improve with physical and occupational therapy, and both dysphagia and speech function are showing improvements with speech therapy.

Discussion

This case represents the first documented instance of NMOSD with concurrent leptomeningeal carcinomatosis associated with immature ovarian teratoma, expanding the histologic scope of gynecologic malignancies linked to paraneoplastic syndromes. Previous reports of NMOSD in the setting of ovarian cancers have been limited to mature teratomas and dysgerminomas.

Clinical presentation and differential diagnosis

A unique and diagnostically relevant feature of this case was the early presentation of area postrema syndrome, characterized by intractable hiccups, nausea, and vomiting. In a study of 35 patients with AQP4-IgG-positive NMO, 43 % had episodes of intractable hiccups and nausea (IHN), and 54 % of these episodes preceded optic neuritis or myelitis (Takahashi et al., 2008). This can occasionally be followed by paroxysmal tonic muscle spasms, which are highly specific for demyelinating disease (Wingerchuk and Lucchinetti, 2022). The spasms typically last 15–60 s and can be mistaken for convulsions. The disease course is usually relapsing, with months to years between attacks. Functional deficits may remain stable or improve during remission, though cumulative stepwise deficits are often observed (Wingerchuk and Lucchinetti, 2022). Our patient’s course was consistent with this pattern. While treatment of the underlying malignancy did improve her symptoms, she continues to experience occasional residual diplopia and uses a walker and wheelchair to aid mobility secondary to spasms. The findings from this case and literature emphasize the importance of recognizing IHN and persistent spasms as potential early clinical markers of NMOSD, especially in the setting of underlying malignancy.

It is important to note that NMOSD may resemble other PNS associated with vision changes, including cancer-associated retinopathy and diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation (Viau et al., 2017). NMOSD may also resemble non-PNS demyelinating diseases like multiple sclerosis, which also can present with optic neuritis. In this case, diagnostic clarity was also complicated by leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, which prompted concern for central nervous system metastases as the etiology of the patient’s neurologic symptoms.

Overlapping clinical features can delay diagnosis, especially in oncology patients where central nervous system metastases are present. This complexity was exemplified again by the new-onset dysphagia and hoarseness during surveillance, which initially was attributed to NMOSD or progression of brain metastases, but ultimately found to be due to radiation-induced necrosis affecting the medulla oblongata, where cranial nerve X nuclei are located. This case reinforces the need to maintain a high index of suspicion for PNS, especially when clinical symptoms and imaging are not fully explained by metastases.

Management in this case required a multidisciplinary approach to address the primary malignancy and PNS. Treatment included symptomatic management of NMOSD with plasma exchange (5 cycles), recurrent courses of high dose corticosteroids, and adjunctive agents like carbamazepine, baclofen, and diazepam for muscle spasms.

Clinical implications and recommendations

This case highlights the importance of early neurology consultation in cancer patients with atypical or unexplained neurologic symptoms. It also underscores the utility of early autoantibody testing to avoid delays in diagnosis. This is especially important for long term outcomes, as earlier treatment can result in more favorable recovery of neurologic losses. In this case, the patient was diagnosed with NMOSD about two months after initial symptoms, resulting in partial functional recovery but continued attenuated vision and use of mobility aids. Ultimately, multidisciplinary care involving gynecologic oncology, neurology, neuroimmunology, radiation oncology, and pathology was essential in this complex case for optimizing outcomes.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that NMOSD can be associated with immature ovarian teratoma, and that overlapping neurologic findings from leptomeningeal carcinomatosis can complicate identification of symptom etiology. PNS, including NMOSD, should be considered as part of a wide differential in the setting of neurologic symptoms and potential malignancy. Initial signs may include intractable hiccups and nausea or vomiting followed by persistent muscle spasms. Early recognition, multidisciplinary approach, and intervention can improve outcomes and minimize long term sequelae.
