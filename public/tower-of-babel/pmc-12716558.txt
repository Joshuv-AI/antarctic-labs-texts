Prognosis of patients hospitalised with primary or secondary pericardial disease: an Australian population-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Timothy Nathan Kwan, Jayant Ravindran, Gemma Kwan, David Brieger, Vincent Chow, Leonard Kritharides, Austin Chin Chwan Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo describe the outcomes and associations of pericardial disease, with a particular focus on the outcomes of patients admitted with primary or secondary pericardial disease.DesignRetrospective observational study.SettingAll public and private hospitals in New South Wales, Australia.ParticipantsHospitalised patients with pericardial disease admitted from 2004 to 2021 that was (a) a primary diagnosis or (b) a secondary diagnosis.MeasuresMortality both in-hospital and during several years of available follow-up.ResultsOut of 45 446 patients diagnosed with pericardial disease, under half (46.8%) had pericardial disease as the primary reason for hospitalisation. Patients in whom pericardial disease was the primary compared with the secondary diagnosis were more commonly male (68.2% vs 59.1%), younger (median 51.2 years vs 66.0 years) and less comorbid (age-adjusted median Charlson Comorbidity Index 1 vs 4). In patients with pericardial disease, adjusted in-hospital mortality was fivefold lower if this was the primary diagnosis (OR 0.21, p<0.001). Malignancy affected 19% of the population and after adjustment was associated with a much higher in-hospital mortality for patients with a primary (OR 4.33) or secondary diagnosis of pericardial disease (OR 2.67). Other negative prognostic factors included older age, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, pulmonary hypertension, heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.ConclusionsPatients with pericardial disease have a low in-hospital mortality of about 1% if this was the primary diagnosis. However, patients in whom it was a secondary diagnosis, especially in the presence of comorbidities such as malignancy, had a much worse prognosis.Keywords: Adult cardiology, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Cardiology

Introduction

Pericardial disease describes a broad range of disorders that involves the pericardium, and includes acute pericarditis, pericardial effusion, pericardial constriction, pericardial cysts and congenital pericardial diseases such as congenital absence of the pericardium.1 Among these, pericarditis accounts for the majority of acute medical presentations.2 It involves inflammation of the pericardial sac and accounts for approximately 5% of non-ischaemic chest pain presentations to hospital and 0.1% of all hospital presentations.1

The European Society of Cardiology previously estimated the most common acute presentation of pericardial disease, pericarditis, occurs in approximately 30 per 100 000 people,1 and although reported numbers vary, this figure was verified in a large recent observational study.3 This and other studies also noted the rate of pericarditis has risen over the past two decades.3

Pericardial disease typically presents with acute chest pain but is usually a self-limiting illness from which major long-term complications seldom develop.4 For example, pericarditis can be recurrent or chronic and can cause life-threatening complications such as cardiac tamponade in <3% of cases and constrictive pericarditis in <0.5% of cases.5

Pericardial disease may be a primary diagnosis but can also be secondary to other diseases which may not have yet been detected. For example, patients with pericardial disease are at higher risk of subsequently being diagnosed with cancer.6 For others, pericardial disease may be the first clinical manifestation of a systemic autoimmune disease that may be diagnosed years later.7 8 How the underlying diagnosis affects the outcomes of patients with pericardial disease is unclear.

This paper provides a longitudinal description of the epidemiology of pericardial disease involving the statewide unselected population of New South Wales (NSW), Australia. We aim to describe the outcomes and associations of pericardial disease with a particular focus on the outcomes of patients admitted with primary or secondary pericardial disease.

Material and methods

Data sources and study design

In this retrospective observational cohort study, data were acquired from the Centre for Health Record Linkage which holds the NSW Admitted Patient Data Collection (APDC) database linked to the NSW Deaths Registry. The APDC database contains information from all admissions in NSW across public and private hospitals. Diagnoses in this database were coded according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10AM). Information on NSW general population density was obtained from the Australian Bureau of Statistics website.9 In the APDC database, each admission has a principal or primary diagnosis with all other diagnoses classified as secondary diagnoses.

Data were collected for admissions with a primary or secondary diagnosis of pericardial disease according to their ICD-10AM code between June 2001 and March 2022 (online supplemental table S1) with the following selection criteria to identify the study cohort: (1) patients were required to have at least 3 years of background data, meaning patients admitted before June 2004 were excluded; (2) patients admitted after September 2021 were excluded as these patients would have less than 6 months follow-up; (3) recurrent or duplicate admissions were excluded so that only the index admission with pericardial disease during the study period was included and (4) patients with residency outside of NSW were excluded to reduce loss to follow-up. The dates from 2001 to 2022 encompassed the entire APDC database at the time of analysis.

Definitions

Background conditions were defined as conditions listed at the index admission or any previous admissions within the follow-up period. The comorbidity burden of each patient was semiquantified using the age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index.10

Patients’ socioeconomic status was approximated with their socioeconomic index for area (SEIFA) score which was based on their Statistical Area Level 2.11 Patients’ remoteness was approximated by their Australian Statistical Geography Standard which is a 5-point scale measuring the remoteness of their address.9

Study variables

Prespecified covariates for inclusion in multivariable analysis were chosen based on existing literature and clinical plausibility: age, sex, socioeconomic status, remoteness, date of admission, whether pericardial disease was the primary diagnosis at index hospitalisation, presence of previous cardiac surgery, acute myocardial infarction, autoimmune disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, heart failure, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, liver disease, malignancy, myocarditis, pulmonary hypertension, smoking and tuberculosis.12,15 The dependent variable mortality was a binary variable coded as 0 for alive and 1 for dead.

All of the prespecified variables were available in the APDC. Comorbidities were defined according to their ICD-10AM code as described above (online supplemental table S1) and were treated as binary categorical variables. Socioeconomic status and geographical remoteness are defined above and were treated as numerical variables. The APDC codes one diagnosis as the primary diagnosis each admission and hence whether pericardial disease was the primary diagnosis at index hospitalisation was immediately accessible from the database.

Statistical analysis

The incidence of pericardial disease was calculated with adjustment for the NSW population density stratified by age and sex. When data for the full year were not available, the NSW population density over that portion of the calendar year was linearly interpolated. A Kaplan-Meier plot was used to quantify the survival of patients with pericardial disease.

The cause of death was acquired from the free text listed cause of death on the death certificate. Pericardial disease was identified through a word search for “pericard*” without case sensitivity.

Logistic regression analysis and Cox regression analysis were performed to identify predictors of mortality and to adjust for these variables. Logistic regression modelled the risk of in-hospital mortality. The date of death, if present, was ascertained from the death registry, and if that was within the dates of admission, the patient was categorised as having in-hospital mortality. Cox regression modelled the risk of death at any time for the entire duration of follow-up. All these prespecified variables were included in the multivariable model if significance was <0.05 in univariable analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed by quantifying variance inflation factors. Model performance was evaluated with the C-index.

Subgroup analysis was performed stratifying patients into those whose primary diagnosis at the index admission was pericardial disease and those in whom pericardial disease was a secondary diagnosis. The difference between these groups was quantified with standard non-parametric techniques: the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

Significance was set at p<0.05. Analysis was performed with R V.4.3.1. Missing data were omitted from analysis with no imputation performed.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in designing, analysing or reporting this research. The study investigated de-identified administrative data, making patient and public involvement less immediately applicable.

Results

There were 45 446 patients diagnosed with pericardial disease between 1 July 2004 and 30 September 2021. Cases excluded at each stage of the study cohort derivation are detailed in figure 1. Just under half of patients diagnosed with pericardial disease had it as a primary diagnosis, with the remainder as a secondary diagnosis (table 1). The most common subgroup of pericardial disease was an ‘unspecified’ disease of the pericardium (table 1). Patients with pericardial disease as a secondary diagnosis had a diverse range of primary diagnoses for hospitalisation with the most common being circulatory disorders, accounting for one-quarter of the entire cohort (table 1). These circulatory disorders included ischaemic heart disease (39%, n=4643), valvular heart disease (15%, n=1728) and heart failure (11%, n=1259). Patients with a secondary diagnosis of pericardial disease more commonly had a pericardial effusion (online supplemental tables S2 and S4).

Two-thirds of the total cohort were male and had a median age of 60 years old. Their index hospitalisation required a median admission duration of 5 days, incurring 5% in-hospital mortality and with one-quarter requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission. The patients were also moderately comorbid with a median Charlson Comorbidity Index of 3. 19% of patients had a background diagnosis of malignancy. Patients had different characteristics depending on whether pericardial disease was a primary or secondary diagnosis (table 2). Patients with a primary diagnosis of pericardial disease had a median admission duration of 2 days with 1.2% mortality and 5% requiring ICU admission. Similarly, they were less comorbid (median Charlson Comorbidity Index 1) and 13% had a history of malignancy.

Pericardial disease occurred at a fairly constant rate in adulthood at any age although in men more than women. By contrast, pericardial disease as a secondary diagnosis had a completely different demographic affecting primarily older individuals and with a more similar incidence in men and women (figure 2). The median age of patients with a primary diagnosis of pericardial disease was 51 years whereas the median age of patients with a secondary diagnosis of pericardial disease was 66 years.

Population demographics of first presentation of pericardial disease, stratified by age and sex. (a) Primary diagnosis pericardial disease. (b) Secondary diagnosis pericardial disease.

The overall age-adjusted incidence of pericardial disease in hospitalised patients has been increasing (online supplemental figure S1). From 2004 to 2021, the incidence of pericardial disease increased from 29 to 45 per 100 000 people (increase of 55%) in the NSW population. Of note, there was no sharp change in the incidence of documented pericardial disease despite the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. A similar overall trend was seen for both sexes and in the crude unadjusted rate (online supplemental figures S1–S4). Primary and secondary diagnoses of pericardial disease followed a similar trend of a gradual rise in incidence (figure 3).

Patients with pericardial disease had a cumulative 5.2% in-hospital mortality, 5.3% 30-day mortality, 10.8% 6 month mortality, 13.5% 1 year mortality, 23.9% 5 year mortality and total cohort mortality of 28.7% (13065/45446) during a median follow-up of 4.8 years. However, the mortality of patients with pericardial disease as a primary diagnosis was much less than that of patients with pericardial disease as a secondary diagnosis (figure 4). For example, 1-year and 5-year mortality for pericardial disease as a primary diagnosis was 6.1% and 12.6% respectively whereas for pericardial disease as a secondary diagnosis 1 year and 5 year mortality were 20.0% and 33.9% respectively.

Kaplan-Meier plot of survival after index diagnosis of pericardial disease. (a) Primary diagnosis pericardial disease. (b) Secondary diagnosis pericardial disease.

Six per cent (810/13065) of deaths in the cohort of patients with known pericardial disease had pericardial disease recognised as a cause or antecedent condition indicating it was not implicated by clinicians in these patients’ eventual demise. Five per cent (610/13065) of deaths in patients with pericardial disease had pericardial disease listed in the principal cause of death.

The in-hospital mortality of patients with a diagnosis of pericardial disease was influenced by their underlying comorbidities. A background history of malignancy was present in one-in-five (n=8752/45446), and these patients had 12.3% in-hospital mortality (OR 2.9 compared with patients with no background of malignancy). In combination with whether pericardial disease was a secondary diagnosis, malignancy was strongly predictive of survival: patients with a background of malignancy and in whom their pericardial disease was a secondary not a primary diagnosis had 15.8% in-hospital mortality. Conversely, in patients with no malignancy and in whom pericardial disease was the primary reason for their index hospitalisation, in-hospital mortality was 0.7%.

Similar variables predicted the prognosis of patients with pericardial disease irrespective of whether this was a primary or secondary diagnosis. For patients with pericardial disease as primary or secondary diagnosis, malignancy was both common and strongly predictive of in-hospital mortality (OR for primary pericardial disease 4.3, OR for secondary pericardial disease 2.7, table 3). Liver disease, chronic kidney disease, heart failure and older age were also strongly predictive of mortality (table 3). Patients with hypertension had a lower risk of in-hospital mortality for patients with pericardial disease as either a primary or secondary diagnosis. In a multivariable model including all patients with either primary or secondary pericardial disease, having pericardial disease as the primary diagnosis was associated with a much lower risk of in-hospital mortality (OR 0.2; online supplemental table S3). The predictive power of this multivariable logistic regression model was strong, with a C-index of 0.83. There were similar findings prior to adjustment (online supplemental table S3).

Discussion

Pericardial disease is an increasingly common phenomenon with a prognosis that is substantially affected by the surrounding clinical context.3 Prognosis in general is poor with one-quarter of these patients admitted to ICU and with high in-hospital mortality of over 5%. In approximately half of patients with pericardial disease, this was a secondary diagnosis during hospitalisation and this cohort is slightly older, has a much higher burden of comorbidities including malignancy in 25% and has a much higher mortality. Patients with comorbidities generally have a poorer prognosis; however, the worst prognosis is seen in patients with a background of malignancy.

These findings motivate the hypothesis that pericardial disease is frequently seen as a marker of disease severity rather than a separate pathology. Across the cohort, there was a surprisingly high mortality despite pericarditis and pericardial disease typically being recognised as a relatively benign condition.4 Moreover, this mortality rapidly accumulated to 11% by 6 months and 24% by 5 years, despite it being believed that pericarditis recurs or has chronic sequelae in under 30% of cases.16 This apparent discrepancy can be explained by recognising that more than half of pericardial disease in the present study was not the primary diagnosis and hence it is likely that other pathologies were associated with these patients’ eventual demise. Pericardial disease frequently emerged as an epiphenomenon of other disease processes, especially malignancy.6 Malignancy affected a large subgroup of this population and was beset by a particularly poor prognosis; however, to a lesser extent, pericardial disease was a harbinger of a poor outcome in other conditions.

Comparison to previous literature

The epidemiological characteristics of pericardial disease were broadly similar to reported literature, although in the present cohort it was slightly more common. Best estimates of pericarditis incidence indicate that it occurs at just under 30 per 100 000 based on data from 2001 to 2005 in Italy.17 18 Similarly, the incidence in the present cohort was 29 per 100 000 in 2004; however, by 2021, it had risen to 45 per 100 000. By contrast, a Finnish study and Swiss study demonstrated a lower incidence of pericarditis disease of only 3.3 or 10.1 per 100 000, respectively.19 20 This may have been lower than the present study because (a) the present study included primary or secondary diagnoses of pericardial disease in contrast to the other population-level studies and (b) the present cohort included all pericardial disease and not just pericarditis, although we suspect that a large proportion of pericardial disease is pericarditis in a hospitalised population. The male predominance and middle age of pericardial patients are consistent with previous literature.19 20

We are not aware of any population-level data that has tracked the incidence of pericardial disease over time. Nevertheless, the rising incidence of pericardial disease observed in the present study is consistent with the recognised growth or emergence of risk factors such as autoimmune disease21 and COVID-19.22

The mortality of 5% seen in the present cohort is much higher than previously reported, although this is probably because, unlike previous studies, the present study was not limited to patients presenting primarily for acute pericarditis. When only pericardial disease as a primary diagnosis is evaluated in the current study, in-hospital mortality is 1% which is identical to previously reported figures.19

We are not aware that mortality rates of patients with a secondary diagnosis of pericardial disease have been reported at a population level. Nevertheless, the high mortality in this population is consistent with previous studies of specific diseases that recognise pericardial disease as a prognostic marker.23 One notable example is that pericardial disease has been recognised to predict the subsequent diagnosis of cancer and to portend a higher mortality in cancer patients.6 Similarly, in COVID-19, acute pericarditis is a negative prognostic marker.24 By contrast, pericardial disease is not recognised as a prognostic marker for myocardial infarctions or lupus.25 26

Limitations

We have presented longitudinal data in a large, unselected population that allows unique and significant insights into the real-world prognosis of patients with pericardial disease. However, there are some limitations of administrative data including the fact that the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes lack precision and many patients in the present study were labelled with unspecified diseases of the pericardium.27 It has been shown that the ICD-10 coding is inaccurate for grouping the type of pericardial disease, although the assignment of pericardial disease in general has a positive predictive value of 92%.28 The database we used also lacks detailed data on imaging findings including echocardiography, laboratory results and medications, all of which would have been useful for describing and prognosticating these patients. This would have been especially helpful for the present cohort because significant interclinician variability exists for which patients are admitted for pericardial disease. Furthermore, given the lack of certain clinical data, it was impossible to employ already validated risk scores in the assessment of patients’ prognosis.12 13 29 30 These scores could not be interrogated in the present study because not all the relevant clinical data were available such as medication use and laboratory investigations. Future research will need to include key laboratory parameters which will enable investigators to evaluate the performance of emerging risk scores and further improve risk stratification. Finally, etiological questions could not be answered from this observational study. Although the high mortality in patients with pericardial disease as a secondary diagnosis allowed us to recognise them as an at-risk group with poor prognosis, our study could not provide the mechanism leading to their death.

Conclusions

Pericardial disease is a growing healthcare concern in Australia. Although pericardial disease and pericarditis are usually regarded as self-limiting disease processes, in half of index cases of pericardial disease, this was not the primary diagnosis. Malignancy was common and present in 20% of patients with pericardial disease. In cases where pericardial disease was a secondary diagnosis, especially when there was a background of malignancy, mortality was high and in excess of 5% during the index admission. Pericardial disease may be an epiphenomenon of other disease processes and portends a poor prognosis. Further research will be required to better elucidate the reasons pericardial disease associates with death and to describe the cause of death in these patients.
