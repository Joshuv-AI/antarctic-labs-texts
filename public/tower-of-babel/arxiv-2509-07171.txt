Z-Curve Plot: A Visual Diagnostic for Publication Bias in Meta-Analysis
František Bartoš, Ulrich Schimmack

Abstract
Publication bias undermines meta-analytic inference, yet visual diagnostics for detecting model misfit due to publication bias are lacking. We propose the z-curve plot, a publication-bias-focused absolute model fit diagnostic. The z-curve plot overlays the model-implied posterior predictive distribution of z z -statistics on the observed distribution of z z -statistics and enables direct comparison across candidate meta-analytic models (e.g., random-effects meta-analysis, selection models, PET-PEESE, and RoBMA). Models that approximate the data well show minimal discrepancy between the observed and predicted distribution of z z -statistics, whereas poor-fitting models show pronounced discrepancies. Discontinuities at significance thresholds or at zero provide visual evidence of publication bias; models that account for this bias track these discontinuities in the observed distribution. We further extrapolate the estimated publication-bias-adjusted models to the absence of publication bias to obtain a posterior predictive distribution in the absence of publication bias. From this extrapolation, we derive three summaries: the expected discovery rate (EDR), the false discovery risk (FDR), and the expected number of missing studies ( N missing N_{\text{missing}} ). We demonstrate the visualization and its interpretation using simulated datasets and four meta-analyses spanning different degrees of publication bias. The method is implemented in the RoBMA R package.



Keywords
Meta-Analysis, Publication Bias, Z-Curve, Model Fit, Diagnostics, Publication Bias Adjustment, Robust Bayesian Meta-Analysis, Bayesian

1
Introduction

An important concern for the validity of meta-analytic conclusions is publication bias, the suppression of statistically non-significant and nonconforming findings
(
Sterling 1959
;
Rosenthal & Gaito 1964
)
. This well-documented suppression
(
Scheel et al. 2021
;
van Zwet & Cator 2021
;
Masicampo & Lalande 2012
, e.g.,)
causes overestimated effect sizes and inflated evidence against the null hypothesis
(
De Winter & Dodou 2015
;
Fanelli et al. 2017
;
Bartoš et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. As such, numerous methods were developed to adjust for publication bias, including selection models
(
Hedges 1984
;
Iyengar & Greenhouse 1988
;
Vevea & Hedges 1995
;
Copas 1999
;
Van Assen et al. 2015
;
Citkowicz & Vevea 2017
, e.g.,)
, regression (asymmetry) based approaches
(
Duval & Tweedie 2000
;
Stanley & Doucouliagos 2014
;
Bom & Rachinger 2019
, e.g.,)
, and Bayesian model-averaging methods
(
Maier et al. 2023
;
Bartoš et al. 2022
)
(also see
Irsova et al. 2023
for selection on standard errors and
Marks-Anglin & Chen 2020
for a historical overview).

Despite these developments, the adoption of advanced adjustment techniques remains limited. Most meta-analyses still rely on funnel plots and Egger’s test
(
Egger et al. 1997
)
before reporting publication-bias-unadjusted (unadjusted) estimates
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
. This analytic approach persists even though funnel plots are unreliable for detecting publication bias
(
Terrin et al. 2005
;
Lau et al. 2006
)
and Egger’s test is often underpowered
(
Renkewitz & Keiner 2019
;
Stanley et al. 2021
)
. Moreover, because these and related regression methods target asymmetry in the sampling distribution, they are vulnerable to “small-study effects”. In other words, the asymmetry-based approaches might falsely indicate publication bias in settings where studies of different sizes target different populations (
Tang & Liu 2000
; e.g., small clinical trials vs. large general-population trials). Any detected asymmetry can therefore reflect genuine heterogeneity rather than publication bias
(
Sterne et al. 2011
)
, which invites dismissal of publication bias concerns.

One reason for continued reliance on outdated methods is the lack of publication-bias-specific model fit diagnostics. Apart from funnel plots (with the limitations noted above), researchers lack visual tools that supplement statistical tests of publication bias (e.g., the selection-model likelihood ratio test in frequentist settings
(
Vevea & Hedges 1995
)
or the inclusion Bayes factor in Bayesian settings
(
Maier et al. 2023
)
). Consequently, publication-bias-adjusted (adjusted) models are often treated as sensitivity analyses, even when statistical tests suggest the presence of publication bias. The lack of applying and interpreting adjusted results is especially alarming since extensive simulations show that unadjusted models perform worse than adjusted models
(
Hong & Reed 2020
, e.g.,)
. Side-by-side visualizations of unadjusted and adjusted model fit may help resolve the issue. If the adjusted model fits better quantitatively and visually, it should form the primary basis for interpretation.

Furthermore, visual model fit assessment complements statistical tests by grounding model evaluation in the observed data. Since statistical tests provide relative evidence, i.e., whether “Model A” fits better than “Model B”, it may still be the case that neither model fits the data adequately
(
Box 1976
)
. Visual inspection can reveal a lack of absolute fit and indicate further necessary revisions to the model specification. Such inspections might allow further development and improvement of existing meta-analytic methods
(
Gelman et al. 2020
)
.

We propose a publication-bias-specific meta-analytic model fit visualization inspired by z-curve
(
Brunner & Schimmack 2020
;
Bartoš & Schimmack 2022
)
. In addition to the visualization, we derive several publication bias metrics describing the discrepancy between the observed and expected data; the expected discovery rate
(
Bartoš & Schimmack 2022
)
(which can be transformed into the false discovery risk
(
Schimmack & Bartoš 2023
;
Sorić 1989
)
) and the expected number of missing studies (defined under selection model adjustment).

The z-curve plot overlays the model-implied sampling distribution of
z
-statistics over the observed distribution of
z
-statistics. Publication bias is indicated by sharp discontinuities—typically at the significance threshold (selection for statistical significance) or at zero (selection for positive results). Meta-analytic models that ignore these discontinuities misfit the data and should not be used for inference; models that respect them provide a better basis for inference. Conversely, the absence of such discontinuities and close agreement between model-implied and observed distributions increases confidence in unadjusted models. In the following sections, we outline the z-curve meta-analytic plots and demonstrate their application on four real examples. The methodology is implemented in the
RoBMA

R
package
(
Bartoš & Maier 2020
)
.

2
Methods

Publication bias can be broadly defined as the suppression of statistically non-significant or nonconforming (i.e., findings in the opposite direction than expected) findings
(
Sterling 1959
;
Rosenthal & Gaito 1964
;
Borenstein et al. 2009
;
Schmid et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. This process alters the distribution of published studies, i.e., studies that “survive” the publication process.

First, publication bias might result in a lack of small studies with small effect size estimates. Because non-significant results are suppressed, achieving statistical significance requires either a large effect size or a small standard error, which forms the motivation behind funnel plots
(

)
. The top panel of Figure

) and moderate heterogeneity (

), with study characteristics following the simulation of Carter and colleagues

. In the absence of publication bias (left), the distribution of effect sizes is approximately balanced across the range of standard errors. In the presence of moderate publication bias (right; defined as in
Carter et al. 2019
), positive results are overrepresented among smaller studies (i.e., studies with larger standard errors). This pattern results from the publication bias in our simulated dataset; in other cases, smaller studies may legitimately target larger effects, resulting in the same asymmetric pattern in the absence of publication bias
(
Tang & Liu 2000
;
Sterne et al. 2011
)
.

Second, publication bias can induce discontinuities in the distribution of test statistics at selection thresholds (using
z
-statistics based on the observed effect sizes and standard errors). Although some outcomes yield discrete distributions of test statistics
(
Viechtbauer 2021
, e.g., binary data;)
, we would not expect sharp discontinuities when aggregating across trials of various sample sizes, especially at the usual selection criteria. The bottom panel of Figure
1
displays a histogram of
z
-statistics based on the same data displayed in the top panel. In the absence of publication bias, the distribution is smooth and shows no conspicuous jumps at the selection thresholds (highlighted by dotted red lines). In the presence of publication bias, a pronounced discontinuity appears at the conventional significance threshold (
\alpha=0.05
corresponding to
\text{z}=1.96
) in the positive direction. Unlike the funnel plot asymmetry, such discontinuities are unlikely under small-study effects alone.

Whereas examining the distribution of effect sizes and standard errors (i.e., the funnel plot) might make detection of publication bias difficult, examining the distribution of
z
-statistics simplifies the process by visualizing the data in a projection that is directly related to how publication bias often operates (i.e., on test statistics rather than effect sizes and standard errors). Publication bias implies that test statistics that are not significant are less likely, and this effect is usually visible at the threshold for significance or direction of the effect.

2.1
Z-Curve Plot

Histograms of large collections of
z
-statistics similar to those in the bottom panel of Figure
1
have become an intuitive display of severe publication bias in recent years.

In parallel, several methods such as z-curve
(
Brunner & Schimmack 2020
)
(see also
Brodeur et al. 2016
;
van Zwet & Cator 2021
) were developed to examine the distribution of
z
-statistics from large sets of studies and assess the quality of the underlying literature
(
Schimmack & Bartoš 2023
;
Gupta & Bosco 2023
, see e.g.,)
. These methods model the distribution of test statistics, typically via mixtures of (truncated) normal distributions, and do not provide standard meta-analytic inference.

Meta-analytic methods instead estimate parameters
\theta
, usually the pooled effect size
\mu
and the between-study heterogeneity
\tau
, via a model
\pi(y\mid\text{se},\theta)
defined on the observed effect size estimates
y_{i}
and their standard errors
\text{se}_{i}
. For example, the random-effects model

y_{i}\sim\text{Normal}(\mu,\text{se}_{i}^{2}+\tau^{2}),

assumes that observed effects are normally distributed around the pooled effect with the total variance equal to the sum of the sampling variances and the additive heterogeneity. Other meta-analytic models, such as meta-regression
(
Baker et al. 2009
;
Stanley & Jarrell 1989
)
, multilevel models
(
Konstantopoulos 2011
;
Van den Noortgate et al. 2013
)
, selection models
(
Hedges 1984
;
Vevea & Hedges 1995
)
, and PET-PEESE
(
Stanley & Doucouliagos 2014
)
extend this framework by adding predictors, parameters, or modified likelihoods. Although these models define a distribution of the observed effect sizes rather than test statistics, they also imply a sampling distribution of the associated
z
-statistics.

In Bayesian settings, the implied distribution of the
z
-statistics based on the fitted model
\text{z}_{\text{pred}}
can be obtained in the following way. First, we obtain the posterior predictive distribution for the effect sizes
\text{y}_{\text{pred}}
,

\pi(\text{y}_{\text{pred}}\mid y,\text{se})=\int\pi(\text{y}_{\text{pred}}\mid\theta,\text{se})\,\pi(\theta\mid\text{y},\text{se})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Then, we perform a change of variables
\text{z}_{\text{pred}}=\text{y}_{\text{pred}}/\text{se}
to obtain the posterior predictive distribution for test statistics,

\pi(\text{z}_{\text{pred}}\mid y,\text{se})=\int\text{se}\,\pi\!\left(\text{se}\,\text{z}_{\text{pred}}\mid\theta,\text{se}\right)\,\pi(\theta\mid\text{y},\text{se})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

The resulting posterior predictive distribution for the
z
-statistics can be visualized alongside the observed distribution of
z
-statistics to assess the model fit. See
Gabry et al. 2019
,
Gelman et al. 2020
, and
Schad et al. 2022
for a detailed introduction to posterior predictive distributions.

2.2
Extrapolation to Pre-Publication Bias

Under adjusted models, such as PET, PEESE, selection models, or their model-averaged ensemble like RoBMA, one can extrapolate from the fitted model to the pre-publication bias state of the literature. We denote the posterior predictive distribution for the original set of studies (prior to publication bias) by
\pi^{*}(\text{y}_{\text{pred}}\mid y,\text{se})
(extrapolated posterior predictive distribution).

For PET and PEESE, the extrapolated posterior predictive distribution is obtained by setting the standard error or sampling variance coefficients to zero (i.e., adjusting the sampling distribution for the bias associated with smaller studies). For selection models with weighted-normal likelihood

\text{Weighted-normal}(\text{y}|\mu,\tau^{2}+\text{se}^{2},\omega)=\text{Normal}(\text{y}|\mu,\tau^{2}+\text{se}^{2})\,w(\omega,\text{y}/\text{se})\,I(\mu,\tau,\omega,\text{se})^{-1},

where a weight function
w
assigns relative publication probabilities
\omega
based on the
z
-statistic
\text{y}/\text{se}
, and the normalizing constant
I
corresponds to

I(\mu,\tau,\omega,\text{se})=\int\text{Normal}(x|\mu,\tau^{2}+\text{se}^{2})\,w(\omega,x/\text{se})\,\mathrm{d}x,

the extrapolated posterior predictive distribution is given by

\pi^{*}_{\text{selection model}}=\text{Normal}(\text{x}^{*}_{\text{pred}}|\mu,\tau^{2}+\text{se}^{2})\,I(\mu,\tau,\omega,\text{se}).

The multiplication by the normalizing constant of the weighted distribution is required to expand the predictive distribution proportionately to the censoring captured by the weight function. Importantly,
\pi^{*}_{\text{selection model}}
is no longer a probability distribution as it does not integrate to 1 with respect to
\text{x}_{\text{pred}}
. Those extrapolated posterior predictive distributions for the effect size
\pi^{*}
can be, again, transformed to the corresponding extrapolated posterior predictive distributions for the
z
-statistics
z^{*}_{\text{pred}}
via a change of variables.

2.3
Additional Statistics

The comparison of the posterior predictive distribution and the extrapolated posterior predictive distribution offers a new perspective on the data and the degree of publication bias. We define two metrics to describe characteristics of this comparison: the expected discovery rate (EDR) and the expected number of missing studies (
\text{N}_{\text{missing}}
).

The expected discovery rate corresponds to the expected proportion of statistically significant results in the absence of publication bias
(
Bartoš & Schimmack 2022
)
. In the meta-analytic settings, it can be computed as the proportion of the extrapolated posterior predictive distribution beyond the conventional two-sided threshold (typically
|z|>1.96
),

\text{EDR}=\text{Pr}(\text{z}^{*}_{\text{pred}}>1.96\,\text{or}\,\text{z}^{*}_{\text{pred}}<-1.96\mid\text{y},\text{se}).

The EDR provides a bias-corrected estimate of the discovery rate (i.e., the percentage of statistically significant results that were obtained before publication). This estimate is valuable because it limits the false discovery rate (i.e., the percentage of statistically significant results that are false positives).
Sorić 1989
developed a simple formula to compute the maximum false discovery rate that is compatible with the discovery rate;
Schimmack & Bartoš 2023
refer to this estimate as the false discovery risk because the actual rate of false positives remains unknown.
2
2

2

The EDR estimate can also be used to specify a new alpha level to achieve a desired false positive risk. This information can complement the interpretation of effect size estimates when there is considerable heterogeneity that suggests some effect sizes are in the wrong direction, zero, or too small to matter.

The expected number of missing studies corresponds to the mismatch between the posterior predictive mass and the extrapolated posterior mass. With the observed number of studies
N
, the expected number of missing studies equals

N_{\text{missing}}=(1-I)\,N.

For non-selection models, the normalizing constant equals to
1
(i.e., no mismatch between the distributions) and the models therefore cannot predict any missing studies. Because
RoBMA
constrains the relative publication weight for statistically non-significant results to be at most
1
, the expected number of missing studies may be positively biased (it is non-negative). Note that this model-based estimate of the expected number of missing studies differs from fail-safe N
(
Rosenthal 1979
)
, which asks how many unpublished null results would render the pooled effect size estimate non-significant.
3
3

3

The
RoBMA
function allows fixing the mean effect at
0
. Estimating the expected number of missing studies under this constraint returns the number of missing studies under the null hypothesis given the fitted publication-bias model.

Returning to the simulated datasets; when publication bias is absent, RoBMA estimates an EDR of
0.27
, 95% CI
[0.24,0.30]
which implies a FDR of
0.14
, 95% CI
[0.12,0.17]
, suggesting that the majority of statistically significant results were obtained with a real effect. The estimated number of missing studies is
3
, 95% CI
[0,50]
, consistent with the absence of publication bias. When publication bias is present, RoBMA estimates an EDR of
0.18
, 95% CI
[0.14,0.24]
. The EDR contrasts with the observed discovery rate (ODR) of
0.57
and implies that nearly 1 out of 4 statistically significant results, FDR =
0.24
, 95% CI
[0.17,0.32]
, could have been obtained without a real effect. Finally, the publication bias is further reflected in the large number of missing studies,
N_{\text{missing}}=868
, 95% CI
[399,2002]
, corresponding to the simulated publication bias.

3
Examples

We illustrate the z-curve plot on four empirical meta-analyses. First, a meta-analysis on the ease of retrieval effects in the few/many paradigm
(
Weingarten & Hutchinson 2018
)
, which exhibits an extreme degree of publication bias. Second, a meta-analysis on social comparison as a behavior change technique
(
Hoppen et al. 2025
)
, which exhibits a strong degree of publication bias
(
Bartoš et al. 2025a
)
. Third, a meta-analysis on ChatGPT’s effect on students’ learning performance
(
Wang & Fan 2025
)
, which shows a moderate degree of publication bias
(
Bartoš et al. 2025c
)
. Fourth, a collection of registered replication reports (RRRs) of framing effects from the Many Labs 2 project
(
Klein et al. 2018
)
, whose design guarantees the absence of publication bias.

In all examples, we compare a Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis with robust Bayesian meta-analysis (RoBMA), which performs Bayesian model averaging over publication-bias-unadjusted models and eight publication-bias adjustments: six selection models that allow selection on the expected direction, marginal significance, and statistical significance (in various combinations), and a Bayesian version of PET-PEESE
(
Bartoš et al. 2022
)
. Code and data for all examples are available as an annotated
R
vignette (
https://osf.io/pm45q/files/osfstorage
).

3.1
Ease of Retrieval Effects in the Few/Many Paradigm

Schwarz et al. 1991
introduced the few/many paradigm to demonstrate that ease-of-retrieval influences frequency estimation
(
Tversky & Kahneman 1973
)
. Recalling more exemplars is more difficult than recalling fewer exemplars and should lead to lower frequency estimates, despite the larger number of recalled exemplars. A meta-analysis by
Weingarten & Hutchinson 2018
of the standard paradigm in settings based on 298 estimates from 111 studies suggested a medium-sized proximal effect of
r=0.25
, 95% CI
[0.22,0.28]

r=0.19
, 95% CI
[0.15,0.23]
, whereas a large-sample replication failed to show a statistically significant effect
(
Yeager et al. 2019
)
.

3.2
Social Comparison as a Behavior Change Technique

Social comparison as a behavior change technique (SC-BCT) is based on our tendency to compare ourselves against others
(
Wood 1996
)
. SC-BCT provides a social standard, prompts individuals to assess their (dis)similarity to that standard, and thereby motivates them to adapt or maintain their behavior. In practice, SC-BCT connects individual feedback with social benchmarks, often delivered repeatedly via email, leaderboards, or letters, to prompt change.
Hoppen et al. 2025
conducted a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating the efficacy of SC-BCT for outcomes in climate-change mitigation, health, performance, and service domains. We examine the main comparison of SC-BCT to passive controls. The original pooled effect was
g=0.17
, 95% CI
[0.11,0.23]
, based on a random-effects model examining 37 trials. Egger’s test performed by the authors was not statistically significant, and the authors deemed publication bias unlikely.

3.3
Effect of ChatGPT on Students’ Learning Performance

Large language models are becoming ubiquitous in our daily lives. To examine their impact in educational settings,
Wang & Fan 2025
performed a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials on the efficacy of ChatGPT for students’ learning outcomes. We examine the main analysis comparing the experimental group (i.e., ChatGPT-based tutoring or learning) and the control group (without ChatGPT) on learning performance (e.g., exam scores, final grades). The authors reported a pooled effect size of
g=0.87
, 95% CI
[0.68,1.05]
, based on a random-effects model with 42 trials. The original authors used funnel plots and fail-safe N to conclude that the possibility of publication bias is relatively small.

4
Discussion

Z-curve plots provide a simple visual assessment of meta-analytic model fit aimed at detecting misfit due to publication bias. Using simulated and empirical datasets, we illustrated how to interpret these diagnostic plots and introduced additional publication bias summaries: expected discovery rate, false discovery risk, and the expected number of missing studies. The methodology is implemented in the
RoBMA

R
package and available for all types of meta-analytic models (i.e., meta-regression, multilevel models, and various types of publication bias adjustments).

A key benefit of z-curve plots is the simultaneous comparison of several models to the same observed data. In this way, z-curve plots complement relative-evidence tests (e.g., likelihood-ratio tests, inclusion Bayes factors) with a sense of absolute fit. The visual model assessment allows practitioners to detect when simple publication-bias-unadjusted models are inappropriate and gain confidence in interpreting complex publication-bias-adjusted models. Furthermore, the visual assessment can help methodologists to diagnose issues of existing publication bias adjustment methods and further refine the meta-analytic methodology.

Z-curve plots are most informative for moderately sized or large meta-analyses. With only a small number of estimates (e.g., 10-30 effect-size estimates), histograms of test statistics are most likely not very informative (unless all test statistics cluster exactly at the significance criterion). In our applications, we modified the default
R
histogram to increase granularity and align bin boundaries with selection thresholds (i.e.,
\pm 1.96
for statistical significance,
\pm 1.64
for marginal significance, and
0
for selection on the expected direction). As with any visualization, these choices introduce some subjectivity; nevertheless, we believe that figures convey useful information about the model fit.

Publication bias may co-occur with questionable research practices (QRPs)
(
John et al. 2012
;
Stefan & Schönbrodt 2023
;
Nagy et al. 2025
;
Mathur 2024
)
. Some QRPs are effectively equivalent to publication bias (e.g., selective reporting); however, others, such as aggressive optional stopping, might create more severe deviations of the distribution of test statistics than publication bias. While this pattern will be clearly detectable on the z-curve plot, it is probably not distinguishable from the similar patterns introduced by publication bias. Unfortunately, publication bias adjustment methods are not usually designed to handle those extreme cases of p-hacking
(
Mathur 2024
, e.g.,)
and often result in underestimating the pooled effect
(
Bartoš et al. 2022
)
. Although we did not examine QRPs in this manuscript, it might be possible that the z-curve plots help identify cases where publication bias adjustment models do not fit the observed data well due to QRPs.

In summary, z-curve plots provide a publication-bias-focused fit diagnostic that integrates with standard meta-analytic workflows. The visualizations complement model comparison tests and provide insight into absolute model fit.
