Automated Multienzyme Digestion Workflows for Confident Characterization of Challenging Posttranslational Modifications in Therapeutic Antibodies by LC–MS/MS
Authors: Nanna Sofie Eskesen, Yann Leblanc, Justine Lempereur, Martin Ørgaard, Elizabeta Madzharova, Trine M Sloth, Simon Krabbe, Alexandra K Rebak, Magali André, Pernille F Jensen, Dan B Kristensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleNon‐reduced trypsin digestion of therapeutic antibodies can generate large disulfide‐linked peptide complexes that exhibit poor chromatographic and mass spectrometric performance. This limits the confident characterization of posttranslational modifications (PTMs) in specific antibody regions, such as the heavy‐chain constant region 1 (CH1) domain, where conventional digestion produces 8‐kDa complexes with poor fragmentation.MethodsWe developed automated multistep and multienzyme digestion workflows using immobilized trypsin, chymotrypsin, and proteinase K on magnetic beads. These workflows were implemented on a robotic platform and evaluated for their ability to characterize engineered cysteine residues in the CH1 domain of antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) intermediates. Analysis was performed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS).ResultsThe multienzyme workflows generated highly orthogonal peptide pools; for instance, selected workflows using trypsin only, trypsin combined with chymotrypsin, and trypsin combined with proteinase K yielded 13%, 14%, and 34% unique peptide identifications, respectively. In the challenging CH1 region, the multienzyme approach identified six distinct cysteine adducts at engineered Cys152. These included cysteinylation, homocysteinylation, and an unexpected, putative process‐related impurity, thioglycolic acid capping, which was validated with unambiguous MS and MS/MS evidence using selected multienzyme workflows. Relative extracted‐ion current‐based levels of these cappings were observed and found to be similar across selected workflows; for instance, relative levels of thioglycolic acid capping from 12% to 15% were observed across selected workflows.ConclusionsThe presented automated multienzyme workflows serve as complementary tools in an analytical toolbox, which can be strategically deployed for targeting challenging regions of biopharmaceutical products. We demonstrate significantly improved precursor signal quality and MS/MS sequence coverage compared to trypsin‐only protocols. The presented workflows are fast (36–92 min total runtime) and utilize commercially available reagents and instrumentation, making them suitable for direct implementation in biopharmaceutical analytical development and quality control laboratories.Keywords: antibody–drug conjugate, automation, cysteine capping, LC–MS/MS, multi‐attribute method, multienzyme digestion, peptide mapping, proteinase K

Introduction

Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) constitute one of the fastest growing classes of biopharmaceutical drugs [1]. The complexity and inherent heterogeneity of these large biomolecules necessitate comprehensive analytical characterization to ensure product quality, safety, and efficacy throughout development and manufacturing. Peptide mapping by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS), also known as the multi‐attribute method (MAM), has become the cornerstone analytical technique for confirming primary sequence, identifying posttranslational modifications (PTMs), and monitoring critical quality attributes (CQAs) in therapeutic proteins [2, 3, 4, 5]. This bottom‐up approach involves enzymatic digestion of the intact protein into smaller peptides, followed by reversed‐phase chromatographic separation and high‐resolution tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis.

Trypsin has long been the protease of choice for peptide mapping due to its high specificity, cleaving peptide bonds C‐terminal to lysine and arginine residues [6, 7]. This predictable cleavage pattern generates peptides with basic C‐termini, which enhances ionization efficiency and produces favorable MS/MS fragmentation patterns. However, the reliance on trypsin alone presents significant challenges for certain protein regions, also in IgG‐based therapeutics. The distribution of lysine and arginine residues in antibodies is non‐uniform, resulting in regions with sparse tryptic cleavage sites. Under non‐reducing conditions—essential for preserving disulfide bonds and characterizing engineered cysteines dedicated to specific conjugation in ADCs—trypsin digestion can produce large, disulfide‐linked peptide complexes that perform poorly in LC–MS analysis [8, 9].

The heavy‐chain constant region 1 (CH1) domain of IgGs exemplifies this challenge. Non‐reduced trypsin digestion generates a disulfide‐linked peptide complex spanning residues 134–210 (EU numbering) with a mass of approximately 8 kDa [10]. Such large peptides exhibit poor chromatographic behavior, reduced ionization efficiency, and incomplete MS/MS fragmentation, compromising confidence of PTM assignments. Variable domains similarly contain long hydrophobic stretches lacking tryptic cleavage sites that generate challenging peptides [11]. These limitations are especially concerning for ADC characterization, where accurate identification and quantification of modifications at engineered cysteine residues are essential for process understanding and product quality control.

Automation of Peptide Mapping Workflows

Traditional peptide mapping workflows are labor‐intensive, involving multiple manual steps including denaturation, reduction, alkylation, buffer exchange, and overnight trypsin digestion [12]. This complexity compromises robustness and poses challenges for method transfer between laboratories, particularly from development to cGMP environments. To address these limitations, automated digestion workflows using immobilized enzymes on magnetic beads have been developed in recent years [13, 14, 15, 16]. These platforms enable precise control of digestion conditions, reduce hands‐on time, improve reproducibility, and minimize protocol‐induced artifacts.

We recently introduced an optimized automated MAM workflow utilizing trypsin immobilized on magnetic beads (SMART Digest) combined with a low‐footprint robotic platform [17, 18]. This workflow demonstrated excellent inter‐laboratory robustness and significantly reduced missed cleavages through a two‐step digestion procedure operating at high and low temperatures. The complete digestion is achieved in less than 60 min, compared to traditional overnight protocols, while maintaining high sequence coverage and minimal protocol‐induced deamidation or other artifacts [18]. We further refined this approach to address acid‐catalyzed deamidation during sample storage and to maintain solubility of hydrophobic peptides through the use of strong chaotropic agents post‐digestion [17]. Importantly, the workflow employs only commercially available, off‐the‐shelf reagents requiring no solution preparation prior to digestion, facilitating straightforward implementation and robust operation.

However, while automation has addressed many workflow challenges, the fundamental limitations imposed by trypsin's cleavage specificity remain. Even with optimized digestion conditions, certain antibody regions yield large peptides that are inherently difficult to analyze by LC–MS/MS, regardless of how well the digestion is performed.

Multistep and Multienzyme Approaches

To overcome the sequence coverage limitations of trypsin, alternative and complementary proteases have been explored. Chymotrypsin, which preferentially cleaves C‐terminal to aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine), has been successfully employed to improve sequence coverage in regions poorly covered by trypsin [19, 20]. Proteinase K, a broad‐specificity serine protease that cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aliphatic and aromatic amino acid residues, has shown promise in proteomics applications [20, 21, 22]. While proteinase K generates more complex peptide mixtures due to its lower specificity, this property can be advantageous for reducing peptide size in regions resistant to tryptic digestion.

Multienzyme digestion strategies offer the potential to overcome the inherent limitations of single‐enzyme workflows. Sequential digestion—where a primary enzyme is followed by a secondary enzyme with complementary specificity—has been shown to significantly increase protein identification rates in proteomics studies. Dau and colleagues demonstrated that predigestion with trypsin improved protein identifications by 21% for chymotrypsin and 731% for proteinase K, suggesting that initial tryptic cleavage creates better substrates for subsequent proteolytic enzymes [21]. Recent work has also shown promise in using dual protease strategies combining trypsin with chymotrypsin for enhanced peptide mapping of mAbs [20]. However, despite these promising results, the systematic application of automated multienzyme workflows to peptide mapping of therapeutic antibodies for MAM applications remains limited. Most biopharmaceutical laboratories continue to rely on trypsin‐only protocols, potentially missing critical information about PTMs in poorly covered regions.

Analytical Challenges in ADC Development

Non‐reducing peptide mapping is particularly important for ADC development and characterization, particularly for ADC formats with engineered cysteine residues dedicated to specific conjugation, improving product stability and homogeneity. During upstream manufacturing of site‐specific ADCs, engineered cysteine residues in antibodies become capped with various thiol‐containing compounds, including cysteine, glutathione, and homocysteine [23, 24, 25]. The type and extent of cysteine capping can significantly impact the efficiency of subsequent reduction and conjugation steps, as different capping species exhibit varying reduction potentials. Comprehensive characterization of these capping modifications is therefore essential for process optimization and ensuring consistent ADC quality. However, the large, disulfide‐linked peptides generated by trypsin digestion in the CH1 region often yield poor MS/MS data, limiting the confidence of capping assignments [26].

Present Study

Here, we extend our automated magnetic bead‐based digestion platform [17, 18] to incorporate multistep and multienzyme workflows for comprehensive peptide mapping of therapeutic antibodies and ADC intermediates. We systematically evaluated sequential digestion strategies combining trypsin with chymotrypsin or proteinase K, all using immobilized enzymes on magnetic beads and the same robotic platform. The workflows were assessed for peptide identification orthogonality and MS/MS coverage quality. We demonstrate the utility of this approach through comprehensive profiling of thiol‐capping modifications at engineered cysteines in the CH1 domain of ADC intermediates, a region that generates large, poorly performing tryptic peptides under non‐reducing conditions. Our results show that multienzyme workflows provide complementary analytical information compared to trypsin alone, enabling high‐confidence PTM characterization in previously challenging antibody regions. All workflows employ commercially available reagents and instrumentation, facilitating implementation in biopharmaceutical quality control and analytical development laboratories.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

mAbs were produced internally at Servier Symphogen. Thermo Scientific SMART Digest kits and associated low pH digestion buffer were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. UHPLC MS‐grade water, acetonitrile, and formic acid were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. MS‐grade (IonHance) difluoroacetic acid (DFA) was purchased from Waters. Both 0.5 M tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) and 8 M guanidine‐HCl (GuHCl) were obtained from Pierce. Ammonium hydroxide was purchased from Avantor. RAPID Slit Seals were obtained from BioChromato.

Analytical Instrumentation and LC–MS Data Management

LC–MS/MS was performed using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Neo UHPLC coupled to a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Ascend BioPharma Tribrid MS equipped with an Ion Max source and a HESI‐II‐probe or an NSI probe. All data was acquired using Thermo Scientific Chromeleon CDS Enterprise version 7.3.2.

Digestion Protocols

Samples were digested by a combination of Thermo Scientific SMART Digest trypsin, SMART Digest chymotrypsin, SMART Digest proteinase K, and SMART Digest pepsin in a Thermo Scientific KingFisher Duo Prime robot using Thermo Scientific BindIt software version 4.0.0.45. Three different digestion protocols were used, which are specified below. The specific combination of enzymes used in each digestion protocol can be found in Table 1.

One‐Step SMART Digestion Protocol

Samples were mixed with 0.2% (v/v) ammonium hydroxide, 0.5% (v/v) formic acid (pH 3.0) in Row A of a KingFisher 96‐deep‐well plate to a final sample concentration of 1 mg/mL. For reduced samples, TCEP was added in Row A to a final concentration of 5 mM. The final digestion volume was 100 μL. The KingFisher Duo digestion program differs from the original publication by only having one digestion step. The digestion was performed for 30 min at 75°C, and the enzyme used can be seen in Table 1 Workflows 1–5 (see full digestion protocol in Figure S1).

After digestion, 8 M GuHCl were added to the sample to a final concentration of 4 M. The 96‐deep‐well plates were covered with a Rapid Slit Seal, mixed by vortexing (1400 rpm, 10 s) using a Thermomixer, centrifuged for 5 min at 1000×g, and the supernatant was extracted into a microwell plate, which was transferred to the Vanquish autosampler for LC–MS/MS analysis.

Two‐Step SMART Digestion Protocol

Samples were mixed with SMART digestion buffer (pH 6.5) in Row A of a KingFisher 96‐deep‐well plate to a final sample concentration of 1 mg/mL. For reduced samples, TCEP was added in Row A to a final concentration of 5 mM. The final digestion volume was 100 μL. The KingFisher Duo digestion program developed by Kristensen et al. consists of two digestion steps [17, 18]. The two digestion steps were performed for 15 min at 75°C and 30 min at 40°C, respectively. The use of protease for each step can be seen in Workflows 6–10 in Table 1 (see full digestion protocol in Figure S2).

After digestion samples were treated as described for the one‐step SMART Digestion Protocol.

Three‐Step SMART Digestion Protocol

Samples were mixed with SMART digestion buffer (pH 6.5) in Row A of a KingFisher 96‐deep‐well plate to a final sample concentration of 1 mg/mL. For reduced samples, TCEP was added in Row A to a final concentration of 5 mM. The final digestion volume was 100 μL. The KingFisher Duo digestion program differs from the original publications by the addition of a third digestion step [17, 18]. The three digestion steps were performed for 15 min at 75°C, 30 min at 40°C, and 30 min at 40°C, respectively. The use of protease for each step can be seen in Workflows 7–12 in Table 1 (see full digestion protocol in Figure S3).

After digestion sample were treated as described for the one‐step SMART digestion protocol.

LC–MS/MS Analysis

Solvent A was 0.1% DFA in water, and Solvent B was 95% acetonitrile with 5% water and 0.1% DFA. The LC method includes a gradient from 2% to 15% Solvent B from 0 to 1 min, followed by a gradient to 60% Solvent B from 1 to 49 min and one wash step. Total run time was 70 min, and the column temperature was 35°C. A flow rate of 0.500 μL/min was used from 0 to 10 min and for the wash step, while a flow rate of 0.300 μL/min was utilized from 10 to 60 min (see Figure S4 for details). The sample load was 0.05 μg throughout.

Samples were analyzed on a μPAC Trapping Column and a reversed‐phase C18 110 cm μPAC Neo HPLC Column with NanoVipers on the Vanquish Neo UHPLC system. See Figure S5 for details on sample loading and column wash and equilibration steps.

LC–MS/MS was performed using data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) on an Orbitrap Ascend Biopharma mass spectrometer. MS was performed in the Orbitrap detector, and MS/MS (EThcD and HCD) was performed in the ion trap detector. Precursors were excluded for 10 s (see Figure S6 for details). LC–MS/MS was performed from 1 to 70 min to identify peptides eluting during the full run, including the wash step.

Data Processing

All LC–MS/MS was processed in Byosphere Enterprise using the Byosphere Virtual Client Version 5.11. Data processing and peptide identification were performed using the parameters defined in Figure S7. Unique peptide sequences, modifications and adducts were counted as separate hits. Redundant spectral matches (same precursor) and different charge states of the same peptide were collapsed.

Results and Discussion

Evaluating Automated Digestion Using Multienzyme and Multistep Workflows

Robotic digestion with enzymes immobilized on magnetic beads provides automated, tightly controlled digestion conditions with facile enzyme removal [13, 17, 18]. In addition, if required digestion can be performed in discrete steps using different enzymes (e.g., trypsin, chymotrypsin, and proteinase K) or conditions (e.g., temperature) [18]. The primary aim of the current study was to address the analytical challenges posed by large disulfide complexes from trypsin digestion under non‐reducing conditions, which are pivotal for characterizing thiol‐cappings and disulfide scrambling at engineered cysteines in ADC intermediates. A representative example is the large tryptic peptide complex derived from Residues 134–210 of the IgG CH1 domain (Figure 1). Characterization of microheterogeneity at the engineered cysteine in this peptide complex is challenging due to its size and the disulfide bridge, which limits MS/MS fragmentation. We therefore evaluated whether automated multistep and multienzyme workflows can generate superior chromatographic, MS, and MS/MS results for IgG regions that are difficult to characterize by trypsin‐only digestion.

A peptide complex composed of 77 amino acids (8015.9336 Da) resulting from trypsin digestion under non‐reduced conditions of IgG CH1 domain with an engineered cysteine at heavy‐chain Position 152 (EU numbering, marked in red) [27]. A canonical disulfide linkage is located between heavy‐chain Cysteine 144 and 200 (EU numbering, marked in blue). Characterization of microheterogeneity on this peptide complex via MS and MS/MS data is challenging due to its large size and presence of disulfide linkage.

In order to evaluate impact of the different digestion workflows summarized in Table 1, the relative intensity of identified peptides was grouped by the C‐terminal amino acid (Table 2). As expected, trypsin‐only workflows are dominated by peptides with C‐terminals K and R. Trypsin‐chymotrypsin combinations show a subtle increase in aromatic C‐terminal residues, particularly in two‐step and three‐step workflows. Chymotrypsin has a relatively mild impact under the conditions used, as evidenced by only a minor decrease in C‐terminal K and R peptides. Digestion with chymotrypsin alone was ineffective under these conditions and resulted in large antibody fragments rather than peptides (data not shown). Consequently, digestion with chymotrypsin alone was not included in the current study. Across all trypsin‐chymotrypsin workflows (5, 9, 10, 12), the abundance of peptides with C‐terminal F range from 1% to 8%. This illustrates a critical observation: The peptide profile depends not only on the enzyme combination but also on the order of application and the number of digestion steps. Each workflow generates a unique peptide landscape and should be considered a distinct tool in an analytical toolbox for targeting specific regions of a biopharmaceutical product. In contrast to chymotrypsin, proteinase K is an effective and active protease, both alone and in combination with trypsin. Proteinase K digestion results in high levels aliphatic (particularly L and S) and aromatic (F, Y, W) amino acids at the C‐terminal position.

Finding Optimal Digestion Workflows for Characterization of Engineered Cys152 in the Challenging CH1 Region of Antibodies

Originally, there was a demand to characterize microheterogeneity at the engineered Cys152 position of an ADC intermediate. This required confident assignment of all cappings at the Cys152 position using non‐reduced peptide mapping. Non‐reduced trypsin digestion results in a large CH1 peptide complex with a canonical disulfide linkage, that has a complex precursor isotopic pattern and poor MS/MS coverage due to the disulfide linkages (Figure 1). Consequently, we systematically subjected ADC intermediates with engineered Cys152 to all digestion workflows in Table 1 and compiled results for all peptide hits covering the Cys152 position (Table 3). Many Cys152‐containing peptides are identified, and the different digestion workflows have highly different peptide distributions. Non‐specific proteases alone, like pepsin (Workflow 1) and proteinase K (Workflow 3) tend to have peptides distributed across multiple major forms, whereas trypsin, as expected, has concise distributions with the expected main form H148‐210 comprising 68% of all identified peptides. However, when combining high specificity trypsin and low specificity proteases, like proteinase K, interesting patterns emerge. The initial digests with trypsin build specificity into the peptide pool, leaving proteinase K limited space to cleave further. This is seen particularly well for Workflow 8 (initial trypsin digest followed by separate trypsin + proteinase K digest), which has a 71% purity of the main peptide (DYFPCPVTVS) containing Cys152, the highest purity seen for any of the digestion workflows (including pure trypsin workflows). This peptide is generally produced a high level from workflows combining trypsin and proteinase K digestion (Workflows 4, 7, 8, and 11), and it displays good MS and MS/MS properties (Figure 3). With its high abundance of DYFPCPVTVS, digestion Workflow 8 is a good example of how multistep, multienzyme workflow can build confidence and quality into LC–MS/MS data where trypsin digestion alone results in challenging and inconclusive results.

High‐Confidence Characterization of Cappings at Cys152 Using Multistep, Multienzyme Digestion Workflows

From Table 3, it is clear that the different digestion workflows provide different peptide evidence for the engineered Cys152 residue. Notably, trypsin‐only digests (Workflows 2 and 6) under non‐reducing conditions will result in large peptide complexes including a canonical disulfide linkage with poor MS/MS properties, even when using EThcD for MS/MS. An unexpected capping with thioglycolic acid (TGA, +89.9776 Da) was indicated at Cys152 using non‐reduced trypsin digestion (Figure 3, top panel). TGA is a reducing agent used in cosmetics and is not normally found in biological systems [28]. To our best knowledge only one paper has previously reported TGA capping in therapeutic mAbs [29]. To validate the unexpected TGA capping, we looked at workflows resulting in high confidence and high‐quality results for the Cys152 position. Digestion Workflow 8 (trypsin digestion followed by trypsin + proteinase K digestion) provides high confidence for TGA capping, since it results in high abundance (71%) of a peptide with excellent MS and MS/MS properties (DYFPC152PVTVS). Indeed, digestion workflows combining trypsin with either proteinase K or chymotrypsin result in peptides with excellent MS and MS/MS properties as shown in Figure 3 (Panel 2), and mapping of TGA capping to Cys152 could thus be achieved unambiguously and with high confidence using these workflows.

In addition to TGA capping, the multistep, multienzyme digestion workflows allow for high‐confidence and high‐quality assignment of all cappings at position Cys152. The results are summarized in Table 4 and a good quantitative consistency is observed across the digestion workflows. Acetylcysteine was only identified (by data‐dependent acquisition) in Workflow 11, most likely since the digestion workflow produces a pure high intensity precursor mass (Figure 3, Panel 2). The dominating capping at Cys152 is cysteinylation, followed by TGA capping, which is quantitated to 12%–15%. The high level of TGA capping is unexpected and currently under active investigation in our laboratories (manuscript in preparation). No free (‐SH) cysteine was detected at Cys152.

Conclusion

We have developed and systematically evaluated automated multistep and multienzyme digestion workflows that overcome the fundamental limitations of trypsin‐only peptide mapping for therapeutic antibody characterization. By combining primary trypsin digestion with secondary chymotrypsin or proteinase K digestion on a robotic magnetic bead platform, we generated highly orthogonal peptide pools (e.g., 13%, 34%, and 14%, unique peptides in Workflows 6, 11 and 12, respectively) that provide complementary analytical information for regions poorly served by trypsin alone. The practical impact of this approach was demonstrated through confident, high‐quality characterization of six distinct thiol‐capping modifications at engineered Cys152 in ADC intermediates, including the unexpected identification of thioglycolic acid capping (12%–15% across selected workflows) as a putative process‐related impurity. This finding, validated with unambiguous EThcD fragmentation, would have remained ambiguous using conventional trypsin‐only digestion due to the poor MS/MS performance of the ~8 kDa disulfide‐linked CH1 peptide complex. The multienzyme workflows thus function as complementary tool in an analytical toolbox—where each digestion protocol generates a unique set of peptides—that can be strategically deployed to target challenging regions of biopharmaceutical products when trypsin digestion falls short. All digestion workflows are automated (36–92 min total runtime). LC–MS/MS is typically performed overnight (depending on the number of samples) and data processing in Byosphere typically takes a few minutes per sample, allowing researchers to prepare, analyze, process and report results in one to two workdays, depending on the number of samples. Only commercially available instrumentation, reagents, and software are used in the current study, facilitating direct implementation in analytical development and cGMP quality control laboratories. The approach is broadly applicable to any antibody‐based therapeutic and is particularly valuable for ADC characterization, biosimilar comparability studies, and comprehensive PTM profiling in regions resistant to conventional tryptic digestion.
