Spinal Mucormycosis Following a Traditional Cupping Procedure in a Diabetic Patient: A Rare Case Report from Somalia
Authors: Hussein Salad Yusuf, Mohamed Said Adan, Abdirisak Ali Abukar, Raqiib Abdirahman Mubarik, Abdiaziz Siad Mohamed, Zakariye Yusuf Mohamed, Mustaf Abdi Ulusow, Sharmarke Hassan Ali, Liban Ade Huseein, Mohamed Osman Dahir, Ismail Mohamoud Abdullahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal mucormycosis is a rare but potentially fatal fungal infection that is often observed in immunocompromised patients, particularly in those with poorly controlled diabetes or other systemic conditions. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial for improving patient outcomes.Case PresentationA 55-year-old male with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus presented with throat pain, chest tightness, and dyspnea. Over the course of one year, the patient experienced repeated hospitalizations for pneumonia and pleural effusion, and subsequently developed a spinal abscess following a traditional cupping procedure. Imaging revealed severe spinal cord compression and abscess formation, extending from the thoracic spine. Initial treatment with antibiotics did not yield a significant improvement, and further imaging suggested possible tuberculosis. However, histopathological examination after thoracic spinal laminectomy confirmed the presence of mucormycosis.ManagementThe patient was initially started on empiric intravenous fluconazole, which was discontinued upon confirmation of mucormycosis. Definitive therapy with intravenous liposomal amphotericin B was then initiated, along with broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics for possible secondary bacterial infection. Thoracic spinal laminectomy was performed; however, despite treatment, no recovery from paraplegia was observed, and the patient was discharged after several months of care.DiscussionMucormycosis can present insidiously, mimicking bacterial or tuberculous infections, making early diagnosis difficult. In this case, delayed identification and treatment of the fungal infection allowed it to spread, leading to severe complications. The importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for fungal infections in immunocompromised individuals is emphasized, particularly when standard therapies fail.ConclusionSpinal mucormycosis is a rare but serious condition that requires prompt diagnosis and aggressive management. This case underscores the need for early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach for managing infections involving both medical and surgical interventions. Timely initiation of antifungal therapy is critical for improving patient outcomes and reduce mortality.

Introduction

Spinal epidural abscesses are an increasingly common clinical pathology, reported in approximately 1 per 100,000 patients.1 Spinal mucormycosis (SM) is rare and potentially fatal, requiring a high degree of suspicion for diagnosis and treatment.2 Its incidence is reported at 1 in 10,000–20,000 hospital admissions in the United States.3 However, the prevalence of this condition can vary according to the geographical location. Early diagnosis and appropriate management are critical.4

Mucormycosis, previously known as zygomycosis or phycomycosis, is a rare opportunistic fungal infection with rapid progression and high mortality. Mucormycosis is most common in patients with seriously compromised immune systems, poorly controlled diabetes, ketoacidosis, hematologic malignancies, immunosuppressive disorders, and solid organ or bone marrow transplantations.5,6 The portals of entry of organisms include inhalation, ingestion, and skin penetration caused by accidental wounds, extensive bums, central venous catheters, and surgery. Mucorales spp. are susceptible to uncontrolled diabetes and diabetic ketoacidosis. Patients with diabetic acidosis who inhale mucor spores may develop pulmonary mucormycosis. However, no obvious invasion sites have been found in some cases.7

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old man with a known history of diabetes mellitus presented to the outpatient department with throat, posterior shoulder, and chest pain associated with dyspnea and diaphoresis for 12 days. He had a history of posterior thoracic trauma. The pain was described as tightness and was associated with muscle spasms.

On examination, his blood pressure was 120/80 mmHg and oxygen saturation was 99%. No neurological deficits were identified. Laboratory investigations were within normal limits except for hyperglycemia and a positive hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Initial imaging was unremarkable. His previous medical history included posterior thoracic trauma, ischemic stroke, and cerebral angiography performed four years earlier. He was managed conservatively and was not admitted.

One year later, he presented to the emergency department with fever, productive cough, shortness of breath, insomnia, dyspepsia, anuria, and generalized body pain of one month duration. He was diagnosed with pneumonia. Laboratory investigations showed marked leukocytosis and inflammation, with a white blood cell count of 38.33 × 109/L and C-reactive protein of 285 mg/L (Table 1). Sputum culture grew Klebsiella spp. Chest radiography is shown in Figure 1. He was treated with moxifloxacin 400 mg once daily and piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g three times daily. Three months later, the patient presented with lower back pain radiating to the right leg and a rounded edematous lesion over the left scapular region following a traditional cupping procedure. Two weeks later, the cupping site developed a subcutaneous collection that progressed to an abscess. Incision and drainage were performed. The superior aspect of the abscess was covered by well-demarcated, firm, white, honeycomb-like necrotic or fibrinous material. Culture of the lesion was negative. The patient received broad-spectrum antibiotics during hospitalization and showed clinical improvement. After stabilization of his vital signs and resolution of the acute symptoms, he was discharged with follow-up instructions.

Two weeks later, he returned with a non-productive cough and chest pain and was admitted with a diagnosis of pneumonia. Thoracic CT demonstrated mild cardiomegaly, peribronchovascular thickening in the central regions of both lungs, intralobular septal thickening, and ground-glass opacities in both lower lobes. Minimal pleural effusion, pleural thickening, and small pleural-based localized collections were also observed. Thoracic CT confirmed left pleural empyema and spondylodiscitis with associated paraspinal and epidural abscesses (Figure 7). Because of the radiological suspicion of spinal tuberculosis, empirical antituberculosis therapy was initiated. However, the patient demonstrated no clinical or radiological response. Thoracic spinal laminectomy and surgical evacuation of the abscess were subsequently performed. The intraoperative appearance raised suspicion of fungal infection (Figure 8). Tissue samples were submitted for histopathological examination, mycobacterial testing, and culture. Antifungal Treatment

Fluconazole was initially administered empirically as an intravenous loading dose of 200 mg followed by 100 mg once daily. Following histopathological confirmation of mucormycosis, fluconazole was discontinued because it does not provide appropriate activity against Mucorales. Liposomal amphotericin B was then initiated as definitive antifungal therapy. The patient also received intravenous moxifloxacin at a dose of 400 mg once daily for antibacterial coverage.

The patient underwent surgical decompression and abscess evacuation in addition to antifungal treatment. Despite these interventions, there was no neurological recovery from paraplegia. The patient was discharged after approximately five months of follow-up. Discussion

Mucormycosis is most commonly observed in patients with severely weakened immune systems, poorly controlled diabetes, and ketoacidosis.6 Mucormycosis can enter the body through inhalation, ingestion, or skin penetration, often due to accidental injuries.7 Local predisposing factors such as burns, trauma, surgery, and needle sticks play a major role.8 In trauma patients, initial cutaneous soil contamination of traumatized tissue leads to fungal proliferation and invasion of subcutaneous tissues, muscles, and fascia.9

Mucormycosis can involve several anatomical sites, including the rhino-orbital-cerebral region, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, skin, and central nervous system, and disseminated disease may involve multiple organs. Rhino-orbital-cerebral and pulmonary disease can be rapidly progressive and life-threatening in a study conducted by Jeican et al described the major clinical forms of mucormycosis and emphasized the characteristic histopathological features of invasive disease, including broad, irregular, aseptate or pauciseptate hyphae and tissue invasion.10

In the present case, the traditional cupping procedure performed on the skin, the patient had diabetes mellitus and developed extensive spinal and paravertebral disease after a cutaneous lesion appeared at the site of a traditional cupping procedure. The temporal relationship between the traditional cupping procedure, development of a cutaneous abscess at the cupping site, and subsequent contiguous paravertebral and spinal involvement raises the possibility that the procedure served as the portal of entry.

The clinical importance of this case lies in the possibility of direct inoculation.

The cupping procedure preceded the development of a localized lesion that subsequently evolved into an abscess. The later imaging findings demonstrated extensive contiguous infection involving the paravertebral tissues, pleural space, epidural compartment, and vertebral structures. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be proven because fungal culture from the initial cutaneous lesion was negative, the temporal and anatomical relationship makes direct inoculation a clinically plausible mechanism.

Our patient was 55 years old, consistent with the average age of 50.2 years reported in the literature.11

The disease was diagnosed approximately two months after the appearance of symptoms (35 days in the first hospital and 23 days in the second), which emphasizes how delayed diagnosis and initiation of proper antifungal treatment contributed to the spread of the disease.12 In our case, the disease was identified approximately 1.5 months after the onset of symptoms (17 days of follow-up with chest radiography in the first hospital and 24 days in the second hospital when the patient was admitted with lower limb weakness).

The diagnosis was challenging because the patient’s presentation overlapped with more common bacterial and tuberculous infections. The patient initially had pneumonia with positive sputum culture for Klebsiella spp., followed by recurrent pleural infection and subsequently extensive spinal disease. Imaging findings were concerning for spinal tuberculosis, leading to empirical antituberculosis treatment. However, there was no clinical or radiological improvement. This progression emphasizes the importance of reconsidering the differential diagnosis when presumed bacterial or tuberculous infection fails to respond to appropriate therapy.

In the present case, histopathology demonstrated broad, irregular, aseptate to pauciseptate fungal hyphae with characteristic morphology, providing the basis for the diagnosis despite negative culture and GeneXpert results. Current diagnostic guidance supports the use of histopathology together with culture and, where available, molecular methods; however, molecular testing for Mucorales is not yet universally standardized.

The thoracic vertebrae are among the most commonly affected areas in mucormycosis spondylitis;12 In the present case, the infection had an unusual distribution involving the skin, paravertebral tissues, pleural space, vertebral structures, and epidural compartment.

Spondylitis was observed at the T3–T4 and T5 vertebral levels. An extensive T3–T6 myelopathy and severe spinal cord compression at T4–T5 were associated with profound lower-limb weakness.

Successful treatment typically involves a combination of antifungal therapies, including dual agents or local or topical antifungals, along with intravenous therapy. In four reported cases, including ours, amphotericin was combined with anti-mucormycosis azoles.13

Other invasive mucor infections have been treated successfully with a combination of local and intravenous amphotericin B, which helps achieve effective fungicidal levels while minimizing the risk of nephrotoxicity.14 Long-term antifungal maintenance therapy following completion of the intravenous amphotericin course and remission of acute infection symptoms has also been recommended.15 The treatment of cutaneous mucormycosis requires aggressive debridement and intravenous amphotericin B.16 As in our case, surgical spine laminectomy and liposomal amphotericin B.

In a previously reported case, the patient underwent laminectomy and antifungal treatment, but a scheduled lobectomy could not be performed because of the deteriorating condition, and the patient ultimately did not respond to treatment.17 In the present case, thoracic spinal laminectomy and surgical evacuation of the abscess were performed.

In this patient, thoracic laminectomy and surgical evacuation of the epidural abscess were performed after failure of empirical antituberculosis treatment and radiological progression. Liposomal amphotericin B was subsequently initiated after histopathological confirmation.

In a study, paraplegia is caused by spinal cord infarction resulting from the obstruction of the anterior spinal artery. Following debridement of the skin and subcutaneous infection, the patient was discharged after three months, although no recovery from paraplegic symptoms was noted.12 Our patient was discharged and after five months of follow-up, no improvement in paraplegia was observed.

A diabetic patient developed a cutaneous abscess after traditional cupping, followed by contiguous pleural/paravertebral and spinal infection. The infection mimicked bacterial infection and tuberculosis, cultures and GeneXpert were negative, but histopathology demonstrated Mucorales-compatible fungal invasion.

Despite surgical decompression and antifungal therapy, the patient did not regain lower-limb motor function during follow-up. The neurological deficit may have been related to the severity and duration of spinal cord compression and myelopathy before definitive treatment. The absence of neurological recovery highlights the potential for permanent disability even when the infection is ultimately recognized and treated.

An important public health consideration is the use of traditional procedures involving disruption of the skin barrier. Cupping is widely practiced in many communities, and inadequate skin preparation, contamination of instruments, or environmental exposure could theoretically facilitate inoculation of pathogenic organisms. This case does not establish that cupping caused mucormycosis; however, it highlights the importance of appropriate infection-prevention practices during traditional procedures, particularly in individuals with diabetes or other conditions associated with increased susceptibility to invasive fungal infection.

Conclusion

Spinal mucormycosis is a rare and potentially life-threatening infection that may mimic tuberculosis or chronic bacterial spondylodiscitis. In patients with diabetes who develop persistent or progressive spinal, paravertebral, or pleuropulmonary infection despite standard antimicrobial therapy, invasive fungal infection should be included in the differential diagnosis.

This case also highlights the diagnostic value of histopathological examination when cultures are negative and demonstrates the potential importance of direct inoculation following procedures that disrupt the skin barrier. Early recognition, multidisciplinary evaluation, surgical source control, and prompt initiation of appropriate antifungal therapy are essential. In regions where, traditional cupping and other skin-penetrating procedures are common, strengthening infection-prevention practices and increasing awareness of invasive fungal infections may have important public health implications.
