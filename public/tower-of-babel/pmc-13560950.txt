The Significant Impact of the Evolution of Porous Architecture in Sol–Gel Processed-Nanoscale ZnO-Bearing Bioactive Glass in a Physiological Environment
Authors: Mahesh Malpani, Saikat Chattopadhyay, Raja Devesh Kumar Misra, Kamakhya Prakash Misra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe incorporation of network stabilizer trace elements such as zinc in bioactive glass is an effective strategy to enhance biological response and modulate degradation kinetics. However, the quantitative relation among ZnO concentration, lattice micro-strain, and evolution of dynamic pore during in vitro mineralization remains unclear.MethodsBioactive glass (BG) film with different concentrations of ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) were synthesized through sol–gel spray pyrolysis method at 100 °C, followed by thermal treatment at 250 °C. In vitro bioactivity was assessed by 28 days of immersion in Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS). Crystallographic evolution, elemental composition, microstructural morphology, and pore distribution were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), and energy‑dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).ResultsThe systematic addition of ZnO NPs controls the matrix dissolution and maintains the integrity of the structure. Quantitative analysis of pore size exhibits large scale degradation of glass matrix in undoped bioactive glass, whereas ZnO NPs embedded BG maintains a controlled micro‑porosity range between 0.4–2.5 μm over 28 days of immersion in EBSS. The value of lattice micro-strain (ε) demonstrates that stability of structure is improved as the concentration of ZnO increases. Furthermore, XRD confirms the progressive nucleation growth of hydroxyapatite (HA) layer, corresponding to characteristic reflection peak at 32.08°. The porous architecture provides high surface area that accelerates rapidly the ion exchange process and nucleation of HA for bone bonding.DiscussionZnO NPs act as an effective network modifier that rapidly inhibits the collapse of BG matrix and stabilizes the bioactive interface. The quantitative analysis reveals that micro-strain stability and surface pore dynamics are systematically controlled by ZnO doping, which demonstrates an optimized framework for enhanced bone interfacial bonding and tissue engineering applications.

Introduction

There is significant interest in bio-ceramics for bone repair and reconstruction. Porous bio-ceramics enhances the mechanical strength at the bone-implant interface. Additionally, they facilitate strong surface interaction and provide a larger surface area between tissue and implant.1,2 When Hench et al3 developed 45S5 bioactive glass (BG) in 1969, the material demonstrated a unique ability to bond with the bone. A few years later, BG was categorized into three types based on their oxide network composition: SiO2-based, B2O3-based, and P2O5-based. Among these, silica-based BG was prioritized because it exhibits excellent bioactivity in promoting osseointegration between living bone tissue and implant.4–6 When the implant is placed in the physiological environment, it forms an HA layer and develops a bioactive attachment with the bone tissue.7,8 Furthermore, silica-based BGs exhibit high bioactivity, good proliferation, and cell adhesion.9 Furthermore, silica-based BGs have several modified chemical compositions, including 58S, 68S, and 77S. Among these, the 58S composition is particularly notable because it exhibits a low tendency for crystallization, while the high content of Ca and P accelerates the rate of HA formation, thereby enhancing cellular mineralization.10,11

Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), triethyl phosphate (TEP), calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca(NO3)2.4H2O), and sodium nitrate (NaNO3) are widely used as standard precursors in sol–gel and melt-derived bioactive glasses. Sol–gel silicate bioactive glasses describe TEOS as the main silica source.12,13 It is a liquid alkoxide that hydrolyses and condenses to form a silicate network of glass. TEP is introduced as the organic phosphorus precursor, providing phosphate (P2O5) to form PO43- groups in glass network. Ca(NO3)2·4H2O is mentioned as calcium source.14 It dissolves in the solution and during heat treatment the nitrate is removed, leaving Ca2+ to enter the glass as a network modifier and to supply calcium for HA formation. NaNO3 is used as sodium source, where Na+ acts as a network modifier that opens the silicate network and increases the reactivity and bioactivity of glass.15

In biomaterials and optoelectronics applications, silicate-based bioactive glasses bearing with zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) are preferred because of their superior physical, thermal, and mechanical properties.16 ZnO exhibits unique combination of properties as an intermediate oxide, notably as a glass network former or a network modifier depending on its concentration. This characteristic impacts the glass matrix. When ZnO NPs are incorporated into the silicate network, they mainly act as network modifiers.17,18 The Zn2+ ions break the strong, bridging oxygen Si-O-Si bonds within the long SiO4 chains. This depolymerization increases the number of non-bridging oxygens (NBOs), which reduces the optical band gap and shifts the absorption edge toward higher energy levels under ultraviolet-visible light.19,20 In tissue engineering, the structural disturbance caused by these NBO sites lowers the glass softening point and accelerates dissolution kinetics on contact with the physiological fluid. This allows for a rapid therapeutic release of Zn2+ ions that simultaneously promotes osteogenesis and provides a strong antibacterial property.21,22 In summary, ZnO NPs facilitate the formation of bioactive glasses that bond with the bone and are potentially osteogenic and antimicrobial.23,24

There are several processing methods for BG such as melt-quench, sol–gel, and spray pyrolysis (SP). Bioglass produced via melting is simple and suitable for mass production, but it is difficult to obtain fine particles and nanostructured materials, and also it is fired at a very high temperature (above 1300°C), so they are characterized by a dense structure and small specific surface area, which limits their properties.25,26 A convenient processing approach is sol–gel. It is chemically versatile, flexible and requires low processing temperatures.27–29 On the other hand, spray pyrolysis is an alternative and complementary to sol–gel process, which is a low-cost, non-vacuum method for producing powder and film and can be utilized to control the morphology of particles. Film can be deposited on the substrates that are easily adaptable for extensive area deposition and industrial manufacturing operation.30,31

The objective of this study is to explore and develop a novel, multi-component sol–gel bioactive glass system (ZnO-Na2O-CaO-P2O5-SiO2) with varying content of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) in the range of 1–10 wt.%. An accompanying aim is to understand how ZnO act as a structural network modifier to break bridging oxygen bonds and create NBOs. It is demonstrated here that nanoscale structural interference in the glass can be used as a chemical mediator to tune the degradation behavior and surface morphology during 28 days of immersion in biological fluid. By controlling this mediator, the surface topography can be controlled from simple, uniform spherical and rod-like structure toward highly organized, quadrilateral porous architecture. This evolution is beneficial, as it enables the design of an advanced tissue scaffold that maximizes cell attachment and provides controlled release of therapeutic zinc ion. This study systematically correlates quantitative lattice micro-strain stability and pore size distribution with in vitro bioactivity, providing a clear mechanistic layout for bone regeneration.

We processed a series of ZnO-bearing bioactive glasses with enhanced bone regenerative properties and bone-bonding bioactivity.

Experimental

Materials and Methods

The 0.08 M solution of zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2.2H2O (99% pure, Rankem LR grade) in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) (99.8% pure, Merck) was used for producing ZnO. To obtain a homogenous solution, the mixture was magnetically stirred for 3 h at room temperature. During this, 0.5 M NaOH aqueous solution was added dropwise until ZnO precipitated. The resulting precipitates were filtered and dried at 50°C for 3 h. To obtain fine particle size, the dry precipitate was milled using a mortar pestle. The resulting powder sample was stored in an air at 400 °C for five hours in a muffle furnace. To further reduce the particle size, the sample was ground again. The synthesis of bioactive glass thin film starts with the sol–gel preparation of precursor solution. First, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) C8H20O4Si at 98% (SRL Private Ltd) was dissolved in isopropyl alcohol at 99% (IPA, Loba Chemie) and stirred continuously for 15 minutes to initiate the hydrolysis. Following this, triethyl phosphate (TEP) (C2H5O)3PO at 98% (Otto Kemi Ltd), sodium nitrate (NaNO3) at 98% (Avarice LR grade), and calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca(NO3)2.4H2O) at 99% (Sigma Aldrich) were sequentially added to the mixture at 15-min interval under continuous stirring to obtain a homogenous distribution of necessary network-modifiers.

To accelerate the hydrolysis and condensation, 1 mL nitric acid (HNO3) at 25% (Emplura) was introduced as a catalyst. Next, the previously synthesized ZnO nanoparticles of size 30 nm were dispersed into the solution. In this manner, there were six samples synthesized with undoped ZnO, referred to as ZNCPS0. Doping with ZnO at 1 wt.%, 2 wt.%, 5 wt.%, 7 wt.% and 10 wt.% is referred to as ZNCPS1, ZNCPS2, ZNCPS5, ZNCPS7, and ZNCPS10. The resulting mixture was stirred for an additional 2h to obtain complete homogenization and a stable bioactive glass precursor solution. After stirring, the solution was filtered to remove agglomerated particles and ensure a well‑dispersed precursor solution. For thin-film deposition, this filtered solution was loaded into a syringe and atomized onto glass slides held at a substrate temperature of 100°C. Finally, all the coated glass slides were transferred to furnace and dried at 250°C for 2 h to ensure complete solvent evaporation, organic residue removal, and film densification. Characterization: Prior to and After Bioactivity

Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV was used to record XRD data in continuous mode from 10° to 80° 2θ at a step size of 0.02° and a scan speed of 4°/min at GI angle 0.5° to determine the nature of phase in sol–gel processed bioactive glass. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Bruker Alpha, USA) was used to analyze the different bonds in bioactive glass in the range 2000–500 cm−1 with a resolution of 2 cm−1, in transmission mode. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) (JEOL-7610F) equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was used to study the morphology and elemental composition of the synthesized bioactive glass film. All samples were coated with ~10Å thick gold prior to SEM studies. Multiple regions were scanned per sample to confirm uniform surface morphology.

In vitro Bioactivity

To examine the in-vitro bioactivity test of synthesized bioactive glass film, buffered saline solution (EBSS) was used. To conduct bioactivity test, a small part of sprayed film was taken individually from each sample (ZNCPS0, ZNCPS1, ZNCPS2, ZNCPS5, ZNCPS7, and ZNCPS10) and added to 5 mL EBSS in a petri dish and stored in an incubator at 37 °C for 14 and 28 days, respectively. After completion the immersion period, the film was characterized.

In ZnO NP-bearing bioactive glasses, Zn2+ ions play a dual structural role in the silicate glass network, acting primarily as a network modifier but also as a network former.39 At low to moderate ZnO content (1 wt.%–5 wt.%), Zn2+ ions mainly function as a network modifier and they occupy interstitial sites in the silicate network and break Si-O-Si bridges, creating NBOs, according to the reaction as given below:40

The breaking of the silicate network increases the number of NBOs and makes the glass more reactive and bioactive. Zn2+ has a high field strength similar to Mg2+, which increases glass density, refractive index, and activation energy while decreasing thermal expansion. At higher concentration of ZnO NPs, Zn2+ enters the glass network in tetrahedral coordination (ZnO4), forming Zn-O-Si or Zn-O-P linkages and participating in network connectivity.41 Molecular dynamics studies have shown that Zn2+ cations are easily incorporated into the free volume of the silicate network, forming Si-O-Zn bonds and stabilizing the structure. This enables ZnO to enhance bioactivity while maintaining structural stability.42

Figure 7a–c shows FTIR spectra of ZnO NPs bearing bioactive glass. Before immersion, peaks demonstrate the basic structure of bioactive glass, in Figure 7a. The peak at 525 cm−1 arises from bending of Si-O-Si bonds, which is characteristic of a silicate glass network. The band observed at 605 cm−1 is due to bending vibrations of phosphate groups (P-O / PO43-), showing that phosphate is part of glass structure. Peaks at 693 cm−1, 754 cm−1, and 782 cm−1 are all related to bending vibrations of Si-O-Si and O-Si-O in SiO4 tetrahedra, confirming that the glass has an amorphous silicate framework.47 The peak observed at 882 cm−1 and 945 cm−1 are from Si-O− stretching with non-bridging oxygen, meaning some silicate bonds are broken by modifiers (Na+, Ca2+, Zn2+), which makes the glass more reactive. The strong peak at 1025 cm−1 is the asymmetric Si-O-Si stretching band, showing that the silicate network is dominant. The band at 1212 cm−1 comes from asymmetric P-O stretching in phosphate units, confirming that phosphate groups are incorporated into network.48 At higher wavenumbers, 1374 cm−1 is related to O-H bending or weak carbonate vibrations, reflecting adsorbed water and early carbonate from air. The peaks at 1446 and 1517 cm−1 are due to C-O stretching in carbonate (CO32-) groups, showing that some carbonate species are already present on glass surface. The development of these spherical morphologies confirms that the material is bioactive and forms an active interface with natural bone and also provides a specific surface area, allowing for a steady and controlled release of essential ions (Zn2+ and Ca2+), which is expected to destroy the bacterial cell membranes and form biofilm without requiring antibiotics.62 When the glass slides are immersed in biological fluid, they begin to dissolve, releasing sodium (Na+), calcium (Ca2+), and zinc (Zn2+) ions. The remaining silica network combines and forms a soft, negatively charged gel layer on the surface. Given that the opposite charges attract, this negative gel pulls the positive calcium and zinc ions along with phosphate fluid (PO43-) ions back down to the surface. This deposition creates a force that promotes the nucleation of small mineral particles.63 To keep their surface energy as low as possible, these new particles grow uniformly outward in all directions, creating three-dimensional shape.64 However, the released zinc ions alter the crystal growth kinetics. Instead of forming long and sharp needles, the zinc acts as a growth-limiting factor that promotes the crystals to form compact geometric building blocks. Ultimately, these blocks stack and interlock tightly together to create a dense layer.

These micro-spheres and porous morphology provide significant advantages to biomedical devices and bone tissue engineering. The high surface area of micro-rough, channelled sphere mimics natural bone topology, allowing proteins to adsorb and guiding bone cells to attach and grow much faster to accelerate healing. Additionally, these porous pathways and building blocks act as reservoirs, enabling slow release of therapeutic Zn2+ ions to provide long-lasting antibacterial and anti-inflammatory protection.65,66

Considering the high surface-area-to-volume ratio of ZnO NPs, their chemical reactivity increases on contact with EBSS. Driven by high surface energy, they dissolve rapidly and release Zn2+ ions into surrounding environment at a fast and controlled rate. As regards bone tissue engineering, the rapid release of ions is crucial, as an increased amount of zinc ions accelerates bone healing by immediately initiating the process of osteoblast formation.67,68 Additionally, it exhibits strong antibacterial effects to prevent infection and regulates the inflammatory response.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a successful way to create advanced bioactive glass films by combining sol–gel chemistry with a spray pyrolysis method. By systematically embedding ZnO NPs to the glass network, we found that zinc acts as a powerful structural switch. It perturbs the silicate network to create NBOs, which control how the glass breaks down and interacts with the biological environment. This change promotes a notable transition in surface appearance. As the ZnO NPs concentration increases, the surface completely changes shape, developing from simple, smooth microspheres and micro-rods to highly unique quadrilateral porous structures. When examined in biological fluid, these changes proved highly effective. Within 14 to 28 days, a new, crystalline bone-like mineral layer (HA) successfully formed across the surface and completely fill the open pores. The lower micro-strain value of HA peaks confirms the high crystallographic stability and minimum internal stress. This ability to develop bone-like minerals with releasing zinc ions makes these glass films an effective material for bone repair and biomedical implants.
