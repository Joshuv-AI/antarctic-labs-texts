Leveraging renewable-energy–electric-vehicle synergies for deep decarbonisation: Technical frontiers, market barriers and policy solutions
Authors: Yufei Zhang, Song Lin, Yuanyuan Wu, Magdalena Radulescu, Haisen Bao, Wenqiong Fan, Yuan Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs nations pursue decarbonization targets, coupling renewable energy with electric vehicles (EVs) has emerged as a promising pathway to enhance grid flexibility, reduce greenhouse‐gas emissions, and drive sustainable mobility. This review synthesises 2013–2023 trends in clean-energy expansion, energy-use carbon intensity, and EV adoption. Regions that expanded wind and solar faster cut carbon intensity more steeply and adopted EVs more quickly. Coordinating clean power with flexibility raised renewable penetration and contained integration costs. Smart charging typically reduced peaks and curtailment by ~ 10–25%. We then map five frontiers that couple renewables with e-mobility. Intelligent bidirectional management delivered 5–8% CO₂ savings at the distribution level. Aggregator and VPP participation unlocked $3,000–$4,500 per EV per year after degradation costs. Hardware and charging-infrastructure innovations trimmed converter losses by 3–5% and stabilised voltage at high EV penetrations. Microgrid and hybrid renewable–V2G designs lifted self-consumption by up to 15% and cut diesel backup by ~ 70%. Lifecycle and circular strategies showed that second-life batteries retained > 80% capacity after ten years, could meet up to 50% of Europe’s stationary-storage needs, and reduced raw-material demand by 7.5% and lifecycle emissions by 10–12%. We then diagnose the main barriers. Standards remain fragmented (ISO 15118, CHAdeMO, GB-T). Bidirectional chargers are costly. Many markets still enforce 1 MW bid floors and 15-min settlements. Interconnection and data rules are often unclear. Finally, we propose a sequenced roadmap: high-resolution pricing, clear aggregation access, harmonised technical and market standards, and cross-sector planning. Research priorities centre on integrated modelling, hardware–software co-design, large-scale pilots, and behavioural and market studies. This roadmap aligns policy, technology, and economics to accelerate a resilient, low-carbon energy–mobility transition.Keywords: Clean energy, Electric vehicles, Vehicle-to-grid, Market design, Bidirectional charging, Policy integration, Circular economy, Climate change, Global warming

Introduction

Climate change stands as one of the most urgent and far-reaching challenges of the twenty-first century. According to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), from 2015 to 2024, each year ranked among the ten warmest on record. In 2024, the global mean was 1.55 °C ± 0.13 °C above the 1850–1900 baseline [1]. Rising sea levels, more frequent extreme weather events, and ecosystem disruptions underscore the need for accelerated global action to curb greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions [2]. Among the principal contributors to anthropogenic GHG emissions are the energy and transportation sectors. The 2024 Emissions Gap Report from the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) estimates that global emissions reached 57.1 Gt CO₂-equivalent in 2023, with transportation alone accounting for approximately 15% due to energy consumption [3]. Transforming energy and transport systems is now seen as essential to reaching net-zero. Many countries, after long-term dependence on fossil energy, have set goals on combating climate change. For example, China’s "Dual Carbon" strategy, which targets carbon peaking by 2030 and carbon neutrality by 2060, has laid out a clear pathway for such transformation [4]. The European Union aims to achieve climate neutral in 2050 [5]. Meeting these goals requires lower-carbon power and rapid transport electrification. Both depend on large-scale deployment of renewables and electric vehicles (EVs).

Over the past decade, both clean energy and EV technologies have experienced significant global growth. EV adoption has accelerated worldwide, driven by ambitious climate targets, rapid innovation in battery technologies, expanding charging infrastructure, and supportive policy frameworks [6]. IEA reports over 14 million EV sales in 2023, nearly 18% of new cars, up from 4% in 2020 [7]. This rapid expansion is particularly evident in regions such as Europe, North America, and China, where governments have implemented comprehensive strategies to promote low-emission transport. China remains a major player, accounting for more than 60% of global EV sales in 2023 [7]. At the same time, falling costs, technological breakthroughs, and strong national commitments to carbon neutrality have accelerated the global energy transition [8] and the renewable energy sector has seen remarkable advancement. In 2023, renewable sources accounted for 30.3% of global electricity generation, up from 29.4% in 2022 which was primarily driven by the rapid expansion of solar and wind power [9]. In 2024, China led clean-energy growth. Solar generation rose by 250 TWh (+ 43%), or 53% of global solar growth. Wind rose by 106 TWh (+ 58% of the global total) [9].Despite this progress, further acceleration is needed to align with the energy transition pathways required for achieving the climate goals of the Paris Agreement [10].

While the climate mitigation potential of EVs and renewable energy is widely recognized, their effective integration into existing energy systems remains a major challenge. A key barrier lies in the mismatch between the growing generation capacity of intermittent renewables and the limited development of corresponding energy storage infrastructure [11]. This imbalance not only jeopardizes grid stability but also constrains the timely utilization of clean electricity [12]. At the same time, the rising electricity demand from EVs further amplifies stress on the power grid, particularly when spatial and temporal mismatches exist between renewable supply and EV charging demand [13]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for flexible, decentralized storage solutions. In this context, vehicles-to-everything (V2X) technology has emerged as a promising approach. V2X refers broadly to leveraging EV batteries to deliver energy services and generate additional value from the battery when the vehicle is idle [14]. By enabling bidirectional energy flow between EVs and the grid, buildings, or homes, V2X allows EVs to serve as distributed energy resources (DERs), capable of load balancing, peak shaving, and renewable energy buffering [14]. A recent simulation study in New England showed that a 25% EV penetration would cause a 19% increase in peak demand which would require significant investment in new generation, transmission, and distribution capacity [15]. This synergistic functionality between EVs and the clean energy not only improves energy efficiency but also enhances the flexibility and resilience of low-carbon power systems.

The integration of renewables and EVs into energy systems faces persistent structural and spatial challenges. First, a mismatch exists between the geographic distribution of renewable energy resources and demand centres, leading to curtailment and inefficient clean electricity utilization. Second, the rising demand for electricity from EVs introduces new strains on grid infrastructure and temporal balancing, particularly when charging behaviour is not aligned with renewable availability. Third, while vehicle-to-everything (V2X) technologies—such as V2G, V2H, V2B, and V2L—present a powerful means of enhancing system flexibility through bidirectional energy flow, they remain constrained by regulatory gaps and insufficient market mechanisms. Recent studies explore V2X modalities and control strategies and show value for grid services and decarbonisation [16, 17]. Yet gaps remain in regional coordination, interoperability, and policy support for flexible EV–grid integration. These issues point to the need for a comprehensive synthesis of how clean energy and electric mobility can be more effectively coupled in practice.

This review proceeds as follows: Sect. 2 examines the spatiotemporal development of clean energy production, carbon intensity of energy consumption, and electric‐vehicle (EV) market evolution to set the context for coupling these technologies; Sect. 3 analyzes current research frontiers—namely, intelligent bidirectional energy management, aggregator‐based market integration, hardware and charging‐infrastructure innovations, microgrid/hybrid renewable‐V2G architectures, and lifecycle/circular‐economy approaches—that are driving synergistic decarbonization; Sect. 4 discusses key barriers, including fragmented communication protocols and legacy market designs that impede technical interoperability, economic viability, and market participation; and Sect. 5 outlines policy implications, spanning regional regulatory models, essential market‐design elements, sequenced implementation strategies, and targeted research priorities to accelerate clean‐energy and EV synergies. We aim to provide a roadmap for researchers and policymakers. The goal is a scalable transition to a low-carbon energy–mobility future.

Clean energy production

As shown in Table 1, European countries have made the most significant progress in clean energy production, with Germany and Norway achieving average annual growth rates exceeding 4.0%, largely driven by the rapid expansion of wind and solar power. This acceleration is closely tied to the European Union’s policy ambitions under the Green Deal and carbon neutrality goals [5] that aims to achieve carbon neutrality in 2050 and reduce the greenhouse gas emissions by 55% in 2030. Emerging economies such as China and India also show substantial growth, with annual increases between 1.5% and 2.0%, reflecting intensified investment in renewables, as promoted in China’s 14th Five-Year Plan [18]. During the 14th Five-Year Plan, China set clear targets to raise non-fossil energy consumption to around 20% by 2025 and accelerated renewables deployment through competitive bidding, green-certificate trading, and transitional subsidies for wind and solar projects. It built out UHV transmission lines to reduce wind/solar curtailment, promoted distributed rooftop PV (“roof-top solar”), and backed R&D for advanced renewables technologies. At the same time, China expanded EV incentives and charging infrastructure and phased out small, inefficient coal units while using its national carbon-trading market to favour cleaner generation. In contrast, Russia, parts of the Middle East, and several African countries show limited progress, with growth rates below 0.3%, likely due to fossil fuel dependency and lack of supportive policy frameworks.

Energy carbon intensity

Table 1 reveals changes in the carbon dioxide intensity of energy use, reflecting the impact of energy structure adjustments on emission reductions. Developed countries such as the United States, Canada, and Germany have reduced their carbon intensity by more than 2.5% annually, primarily due to increased shares of renewables and natural gas. Germany’s success, in particular, is deeply linked to its long-term "Energiewende" strategy [19]. The Energiewende cuts energy carbon intensity by massively scaling up wind, solar, and other renewables while phasing out coal and nuclear, and by boosting energy efficiency in buildings, industry, and transport. It also invests in smart grids, storage, and green hydrogen to replace fossil-heavy energy sources. In contrast, carbon intensity in countries like India and Indonesia has increased modestly (by 0.3–0.5% per year), indicating ongoing reliance on coal to fuel economic growth. This trend highlights the persistent challenge for developing countries in reconciling development goals with climate mitigation, particularly when infrastructure and technological capacity remain limited. Carbon intensity in the Middle East and parts of Africa has remained largely unchanged, underscoring continued dependence on high-emission energy sources and the lack of effective decarbonization pathways.

EV sales share

Table 1 demonstrates the rapid evolution of the electric vehicle market. Norway stands out globally, with EVs accounting for over 60% of EV sales by 2023 and an average annual growth rate above 6%, owing to a long-standing combination of subsidies, zero-emission incentives, and an already clean electricity mix [20]. Norway’s EV incentives include extensive tax and fee exemptions—such as no purchase/import taxes, VAT, or annual road fees—alongside perks like free parking, toll and ferry waivers, and access to bus lanes. Strong public charging infrastructure investment and clear zero-emission vehicle targets (all new cars by 2025) further accelerate electric vehicle adoption. Other European countries such as Germany and the Netherlands have also shown robust growth, with annual increases between 3 and 5%. China, supported by a massive domestic market and vertically integrated EV supply chains, has become the largest producer and consumer of EVs globally. Since 2009, the Chinese government has rolled out a comprehensive package of support measures—including purchase subsidies, exemptions from purchase tax and vehicle & vessel tax—while local authorities have invested heavily in charging infrastructure and established demonstration cities to stoke domestic demand and build economies of scale for OEMs and suppliers [21]. Although the EV market share in China is slightly lower than in Europe, its annual growth rate remains among the highest. In contrast, countries such as India, Russia, and most of Africa still report EV shares below 0.5%, suggesting persistent structural barriers including insufficient charging infrastructure, affordability concerns, and weaker policy support. Overall, there appears to be a strong synergy among the three indicators: regions with fast-growing clean energy production tend to exhibit both declining carbon intensity and rising EV adoption, reinforcing the notion that systemic transformation in both the energy and transport sectors is essential to achieving global climate targets [9].

Summary

Briefly, India’s renewable generation grows steadily (around 1.5%–2.0% annually), yet its energy-related carbon intensity also inches upward by 0.3%–0.5% per year, and EV sales and market share remain very low. By contrast, the United States, Canada, and Germany have aggressively expanded wind and solar capacity, driving down carbon intensity by over 2.5% per year, while simultaneously promoting electric vehicle uptake; European markets such as Norway, Germany, and the Netherlands have seen EV sales grow by 3%–6% annually. This pattern illustrates a clear synergy: regions that rapidly scale clean energy tend also to reduce carbon intensity and experience faster EV adoption, demonstrating how joint advances in power generation and transport electrification are crucial for cutting global carbon emissions.

Research frontiers in the coupling of renewable energy and electric vehicles for carbon reduction

Intelligent bidirectional energy management

Bidirectional energy management has rapidly evolved from simple rule-based charging protocols toward intelligent, optimization-driven control frameworks that align EV charging/discharging with real‐time renewable generation and grid conditions. Rehman et al. [22] developed a mixed‐integer linear programming model for multi-energy microgrids—incorporating EVs, hydrogen networks, and thermal storage—and showed that enabling V2G functionality reduced total system cost by 0.75% and cut CO₂ emissions by 4%, demonstrating that even moderate EV participation can yield both economic and environmental benefits. Similarly, Er et al. [23] formulated a two‐stage stochastic optimization for a multi‐energy system serving fast‐charging and hydrogen refueling stations; their case study revealed that allowing EVs to provide V2G services reduced the annualized total lifecycle cost by up to 1.19% under high load scenarios, while enhancing system reliability through buffered renewable integration.

Beyond optimization models, data‐driven and machine‐learning approaches are gaining traction. Yang et al. [24] proposed a stochastic model combining a modified artificial flora optimization (MAFO) algorithm to schedule EVs in load‐response programs,their results indicate up to a 52% reduction in peak loads, a 48% decrease in total operational costs, and a 15% increase in renewable energy utilization, underlining the potential of learning‐based strategies to manage uncertainties in both renewable output and EV behavior. Moreover, Li et al. [25] introduced an extreme learning machine (ELM)–based online V2G regulator for a U.K. microgrid; by feeding sampled frequency and renewable‐output data into the ELM, their control scheme shifted surplus renewable energy into EV batteries while constraining depth‐of‐discharge to mitigate degradation, thus achieving low‐cost decarbonization without sacrificing battery life.

Collectively, these studies highlight that intelligent bidirectional management—whether via deterministic optimization or machine‐learning algorithms—enables EVs to (i) absorb midday renewable surpluses, (ii) deliver grid services (frequency regulation, voltage support) that displace fossil‐fueled peakers, and (iii) minimize overall system cost. Technoeconomic assessments consistently show that even a 5–10% EV fleet participation can reduce local CO₂ emissions by 5–8% [22, 26], underscoring the pivotal role of real‐time AI‐driven dispatch in unlocking V2G’s full carbon‐reduction potential.

Aggregator-based market integration

An aggregator is an entity that pools distributed energy assets—most importantly fleets of EVs—and manages them as a unified virtual power plant to bid into wholesale and ancillary‐services markets [27]. By coordinating the bidirectional charging and discharging of numerous EVs, aggregators provide fast, distributed flexibility that balances wind and solar intermittency, effectively acting as virtual inertia and reducing reliance on fossil‐fueled peaker plants [28]. Moreover, aggregators align EV charging with periods of high renewable output, thereby increasing renewable self‐consumption, reducing curtailment, and cutting overall CO₂ emissions, which makes them pivotal for coupling clean energy resources with transportation electrification.

Research on aggregator models—virtual power plants that pool fleets of EVs to participate in ancillary‐services markets—has intensified, as these frameworks define the pathway from technical feasibility to commercial viability. Menos-Aikateriniadis et al. [27] introduced an “Energy Reserves” product that allows EV aggregators to communicate day‐ahead flexibility bids for frequency restoration reserves,their simulation showed that the top 25% of ranked aggregators could cover 80% of actual imbalance events without imposing excessive administrative burdens on system operators. In another study, Koltsaklis and Knápek [28] formulated a mixed‐integer unit commitment model co‐optimizing energy and reserve markets with embedded G2V/V2G and stationary battery storage; their findings demonstrate that inclusion of EV aggregates in ancillary‐services markets lowered renewable curtailment and reduced system CO₂ emissions, while delivering up to $3,000–$4,500/year per vehicle in revenue when degradation costs were carefully accounted for [22, 26]. Specifically, the $3,000–$4,500 per EV·yr figure comes from a mixed-integer unit-commitment formulation that co-optimises energy and frequency-reserve products with embedded G2V/V2G [28]. Revenues are reported net of degradation, monetised via cycle-depth-sensitive wear functions, consistent with recent techno-economic treatments [22, 26]. Given dependence on market prices, EV availability, and cycling depth, we interpret this as a scenario-specific upper-bound rather than a system-wide average.

Key to these aggregator investigations is the design of incentive structures that balance grid needs with EV owner interests. Studies recommend dynamic per‐kWh compensation tied to real‐time grid signals—offering higher payments during large frequency deviations and reduced (or even negative) rates during low‐value periods—to maintain battery state‐of‐health (SoH) and secure user participation. Survey‐based analyses reveal that, when guaranteed minimum SoC thresholds and transparent revenue splits are provided, over 60% of EV owners are willing to enroll in V2G programs [29]. Nevertheless, ambiguity persists around tariff structures and communication protocols (ISO 15118 vs. OCPP), delaying market launch. Future work must, therefore, integrate centralized optimization with socioeconomic modeling, ensuring that aggregator‐based V2G aligns with evolving market regulations and consumer behavior.

Hardware and charging infrastructure innovations

Advancements in power‐electronics and control strategies are critical for scalable V2G deployments. Salim and Ameen [30] designed a DC‐coupled dual active bridge (DAB) converter for an EV extreme fast charger (1.4 MW), demonstrating that integration of DC renewable sources at each converter level eliminated the AC‐AC stage, reduced component count, and charged a 540 kWh battery from 20 to 90% SOC in under 19 min, thereby proving that high‐power bidirectional architectures can marry fast charging with grid support. Complementing high-power solutions, Zhang et al. [31] developed a schedulable capacity assessment method for PV‐integrated fast‐charging stations,by modeling the interplay between PV output, stationary storage, and EV charging demand, they showed that colocating a 2 MW solar array with a 500-vehicle V2G depot increased daytime schedulable capacity by 18%, thereby reducing reliance on grid‐tied storage and improving renewable self‐consumption.

At the charger level, Niu et al. [26] proposed a cooperative scheduling strategy in which EVs participating in V2G responded to real‐time wind power forecasts; their improved backbone particle swarm optimization (IBBPSO) reduced wind curtailment to over 92% consumption when user responsiveness was high, while flattening peak‐valley load differences and safeguarding grid profits. Moreover, Cheng and Ching [32] demonstrate that their decentralized feedback‐optimization controller can confine node voltages within a narrow band (approximately ± 3%) even when EV penetration approaches 70%–80%, thereby ensuring voltage stability under high renewable integration.

Together, IBBPSO‐based scheduling strategies and decentralized feedback‐optimization controllers—enabled by advanced DAB converter topologies and robust schedulability frameworks—lay the groundwork for mass‐market V2G by minimizing converter losses by 3–5%, supporting a broad 300–800 V operating range, and guaranteeing stable operation under high EV penetration.

Microgrid integration and hybrid renewable-V2G architectures

Microgrid and hybrid architectures exemplify how EV fleets can serve as distributed energy resources, bolstering local resilience and carbon reduction. Heredero-Peris et al. [33] reported a live validation of an islanded microgrid at the Expoelectric fair, showing that V2G‐enabled EVs, alongside stationary batteries and a Master Inverter, provided grid‐forming services, maintained fault resilience, and stabilized voltage during PV intermittency—without requiring dedicated communication layers between devices. Rehman et al. [22] formulated an optimal sizing framework for a multi‐energy microgrid integrating EVs, hydrogen, and thermal networks; their case study found that V2G participation saved 0.75% in extra microgrid costs and reduced CO₂ emissions by 4%, while hybrid hydrogen storage further cut emissions by 40% and eliminated grid‐purchase costs.

In hybrid renewable‐V2G designs, Gagangras et al. [34] presented a zero‐carbon AC/DC microgrid planning approach leveraging V2G; sensitivity analyses revealed that, under high V2G adoption, microgrids could absorb low renewable output, reducing unit costs by up to 12% compared to no‐EV scenarios and maintaining voltage quality under 2050 carbon‐neutral targets. Similarly, Ali et al. [35] proposed a multi‐objective planning framework for colocated EV charging stations and renewables: their simulations on a 69-bus distribution network showed a 96% reduction in voltage deviations and a 74% drop in energy losses when advanced bidirectional control was employed—highlighting that intelligent siting and dispatch of EVCS alongside wind and solar can drastically improve both reliability and carbon outcomes.

These microgrid and hybrid studies illustrate that, by orchestrating EV fleets with local renewables—via hierarchical control layers and co‐optimization—communities can boost renewable self‐consumption from 68% to over 84%, slash diesel‐generator reliance by 70%, and achieve near‐zero dispatch‐carbon scenarios in islanded contexts.

Lifecycle and circular-economy approaches

As EV adoption accelerates, second-life battery applications and circular-economy strategies become critical to long‐run sustainability. Multiple studies demonstrate that second‐life EV batteries can perform effectively in V2G applications when depth‐of‐discharge (DoD) is properly constrained. Geng et al. [36] model vehicle-to-grid cycling–induced aging for both lithium iron phosphate and nickel manganese cobalt lithium-ion batteries, showing that operation at limited DoD supports multi-year service life consistent with retaining over 80% of nominal capacity at roughly ten years, while lithium iron phosphate–based EV distributed energy storage achieves a levelized cost of storage as low as 0.057 USD per kwh—up to ~ 76% below commercial battery energy storage systems—thereby supporting chemistry-level generalizability and strong cost competitiveness. In a related analysis, Michaelides et al. [37] examined the reuse of EV batteries in stationary storage and found that limiting daily DoD to around 20% results in annual capacity degradation below 1%, enabling 10 years of service with 85%–90% remaining capacity, thus, validating that second‐life V2G systems’ minimal performance loss while delivering significant grid services.

Life‐cycle and material‐flow analyses indicate that repurposing 40–50% of retired EV batteries for stationary storage could meet approximately 30–50% of Europe’s projected capacity by 2040, thereby reducing new battery production by about 7.5% and cutting life‐cycle emissions by 10–12% compared to immediate recycling scenarios [38, 39]. To realize these benefits, policymakers should standardize pack interfaces for easy module extraction, mandate comprehensive battery health‐data logging to streamline second‐life qualification, and offer green incentives—such as tax credits or renewable certificates—to utilities and firms that adopt repurposed battery systems, ensuring that second‐life V2G and circular‐economy approaches deliver maximum environmental and economic value.

Summary

Recent literature [22–24] demonstrates that intelligent bidirectional energy management—leveraging optimization and machine‐learning algorithms—enables EVs to synchronize charging/discharging with real‐time renewable generation and grid conditions, yielding 5–8% reductions in local CO₂ emissions and substantially reducing the need for stationary storage. Concurrently, aggregator‐based market integrationresearch [27, 28] shows that virtual power‐plant models can collectively bid EV fleets into ancillary‐services markets, generating $3,000–$4,500 per vehicle annually while carefully balancing battery degradation costs and user incentives.

On the hardware front, advances in power‐electronics and embedded control [26, 30, 31] have produced multiport, bidirectional converters and decentralized feedback‐optimization loops that reduce converter losses by up to 5%, stabilize voltage/frequency at high EV penetrations, and increase renewable self‐consumption by 15–18%. In microgrid and hybrid renewable‐V2G architectures, field demonstrations [22, 33, 34] confirm that EV fleets can act as fast‐responding reserves, raising renewable self‐consumption from 68% to over 84% and cutting diesel‐generator use by 70%. Finally, lifecycle and circular‐economy approaches [38, 39] reveal that second‐life EV batteries can fulfill 30–50% of Europe’s future storage demand, lower raw‐material needs by 7.5%, and reduce lifecycle emissions by 10–12% at levelized costs as low as USD 0.057/kWh. Collectively, these interrelated advances illustrate a maturing ecosystem in which EVs not only draw clean energy but serve as dynamic, distributed storage assets—synergistically accelerating carbon reduction in both power and transportation sectors.

Challenges of synergise clean energy and EVs

Spatial mismatch of clean energy generation and EV market

Spatial mismatches between new energy supply and demand are a growing challenge in many countries undergoing energy transitions. China illustrates this issue particularly well: while it has rapidly scaled up renewable capacity, much of its wind and solar generation is located in inland provinces like Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Qinghai, far from the coastal population centers with the highest electricity demand [40]. This has resulted in curtailment of surplus renewable energy due to transmission constraints. Despite significant investments in ultra-high-voltage (UHV) transmission lines connecting western generation zones with eastern coastal load centers, these infrastructural efforts have lagged behind the exponential growth in renewable installations [41]. For example, Seven provinces and regions, most with large wind and solar capacity in the northwest and north, exceeded 10% of curtailment in February 2024 due to limited grid absorption capacity [42]. This transmission bottleneck not only represents a waste of clean energy but also undermines the decarbonization potential of electrified end-use sectors. If EVs are charged using electricity derived from fossil fuel sources, particularly in demand-heavy urban regions with limited renewable access, their net climate benefit is significantly diminished. Recognizing this challenge, the Chinese government has implemented a range of policy responses aimed at improving local renewable energy utilization and fostering regionally inclusive electrification. Chief among these is the “New Energy Vehicles to the Countryside” initiative, introduced in 2020 and renewed under the Green and Smart Countryside agenda, which provides purchase subsidies and infrastructure support to promote EV adoption in rural and renewable-rich regions [43]. Simultaneously, China’s 14th Five-Year Plan for Modern Energy System Development emphasizes the importance of integrating generation, storage, grid flexibility, and end-use electrification through the “source-grid-load-storage” approach, with targeted support for western provinces where renewable curtailment remains high [44].

While China’s centrally coordinated planning offers advantages in aligning infrastructure and policy at scale, comparative international experiences offer complementary lessons. Germany’s Energiewende illustrates how regional flexibility markets and legally mandated transmission planning can help resolve geographic disparities between renewable generation and electricity consumption. By enabling local demand response and incentivizing energy storage in areas with high curtailment, Germany has enhanced both system efficiency and stakeholder participation [45]. The United States’ Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), passed in 2022, provides another instructive case [46]. It designates “energy communities”—economically disadvantaged or fossil-fuel-dependent regions—as priority zones for clean energy investment, offering enhanced tax credits and direct incentives for clean electricity, EV infrastructure, and manufacturing. These justice-oriented, place-based approaches are designed not only to decarbonize but also to ensure that the benefits of the energy transition are equitably distributed. By contrast, China’s current subsidy structures and grid planning mechanisms remain largely uniform across provinces, with limited integration of regional socioeconomic or renewable capacity differentials into EV incentives or power dispatch decisions. Moving forward, China could adopt a more differentiated policy architecture—linking EV subsidies to local clean electricity availability, piloting regional electricity utilization markets, and prioritizing green industrial investment in provinces facing high curtailment. Such reforms would not only enhance the carbon efficiency of the national transition but also address concerns of spatial inequality and system imbalance. A regionally inclusive, spatially coordinated, and justice-informed electrification strategy will be critical for realizing a truly sustainable energy future.

Technological and regulatory barriers

Effective integration of clean energy and electric vehicles hinges on robust technical standards and regulatory frameworks to ensure interoperability, reliability, and market viability. For example, without harmonized communication protocols (e.g., ISO 15118–20) and consistent grid‐connection requirements, EVs cannot reliably deliver bidirectional services to balance variable renewable generation [47, 48]. Similarly, current wholesale market rules typically require minimum bid capacities of 1 MW or more, effectively excluding individual EVs (50–100 kW each) unless they form large aggregations—an approach that Koltsaklis and Knápek [28] show increases coordination costs and fails to appropriately value EVs’ rapid-response capabilities.

The convergence of ISO 15118, CHAdeMO, and GB/T standards remains incomplete, creating interoperability gaps that hinder seamless V2G deployment. Although ISO 15118’s Plug & Charge framework provides basic authentication, only about 40% of public chargers today support bidirectional power transfer, reflecting both legacy hardware and software constraints [48]. Bidirectional chargers cost more than twice as much as unidirectional units, which discourages network operators from large‐scale rollouts [49]. While properly limiting depth of discharge can keep annual capacity fade under 2%—as shown in V2G cycling simulations—many stakeholders remain concerned that frequent cycling will degrade batteries prematurely [37]. Each grid‐connection region enforces its own interconnection and safety requirements, so a charger certified in Europe often requires retesting before deployment in North America or Asia, adding further complexity [50].

Emerging efforts are beginning to bridge these gaps. The forthcoming ISO 15118–20 update will unify messaging for secure digital certificates, enhanced state‐of‐charge management, and harmonized bidirectional control, reducing software fragmentation [47]. Open-source initiatives like Open V2G are defining a common firmware stack for charging stations, enabling a single charger to support vehicles from multiple OEMs [51]. On the policy front, the EU’s Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation now mandates that at least 20% of new public chargers be bidirectional by 2025, and California’s recent bidirectional‐charging order requires utilities and OEMs to deliver V2G-capable hardware at scale [38]. When technical standardization, hardware cost reductions, and aligned regulations coalesce, V2X can improve renewable utilization, lower total infrastructure costs, and bolster grid resilience. Achieving this will require ongoing collaboration among standards bodies, utilities, charger manufacturers, and policymakers to ensure that every new installation operates globally, cost-effectively, and with minimal grid‐integration friction.

Legacy market designs further impede V2X integration. Traditional ancillary-services and capacity markets were architected around large, centralized thermal generators rather than distributed, mobile storage assets. As a result, EVs’ rapid sub-second response and stochastic availability are poorly captured by existing products, which settle on 15-min to hourly intervals and value flexibility calibrated for slow-ramping generators. Moreover, most wholesale markets impose a minimum bid requirement of 1 MW or more, effectively excluding individual EVs (typically 50–100 kW each) unless they join an aggregator—introducing coordination overhead and additional costs [52]. Until market products evolve to recognize and reward sub-minute flexibility from aggregated EV fleets, many V2G use cases will remain economically marginal. Only by aligning technical standards (ISO 15118–20, unified charger firmware), cost-effective hardware (cheaper bidirectional converters), and forward-looking market rules (sub-minute ancillary services, lower bid thresholds) can V2X realize its full potential in coupling clean energy with electric transportation.

Policy implications

Regional regulatory models for V2X integration

Global jurisdictions have adopted varied regulatory strategies to address V2X market barriers, each reflecting its own energy architecture and policy priorities. In the European Union, the Clean Energy Package requires member states to establish local flexibility markets that allow distributed assets—including aggregated EV fleets—to bid capacity into system‐balancing auctions. Platforms such as Piclo Flex in the UK and Redispatch 2.0 in Germany illustrate how localized markets facilitate distributed participation, while the ITS Directive (2010/40/EU) provides technical guidelines for cross‐border interoperability [53]. By contrast, the United States has focused on dismantling regulatory roadblocks: FERC Order 2222 obliges regional transmission organizations to permit aggregated distributed resources—including EV aggregators—to compete alongside conventional generators in wholesale markets [54]. China’s state-led approach combines top-down planning with large-scale pilot programs. Its “source-grid-load-storage” initiative strategically deploys V2X assets in areas with acute grid constraints, validating new business models under controlled conditions (NDRC and NEA, 2022). While these three regions differ—market-based (EU), regulatory reform (US), and centralized pilot-led (China)—each offers lessons on how tailored policy frameworks can accelerate V2X adoption and underscore the importance of aligning governance approaches with local technical and market realities.

Essential market design and regulatory elements

To mainstream V2X technologies, policymakers must craft a comprehensive framework addressing multiple dimensions of market design. First, high-resolution dynamic pricing is crucial: transitioning from static, time-of-use rates to five-minute (or shorter) settlement intervals ensures that EV flexibility is accurately valued. California’s SB 233 demonstrates how time-varying tariffs can shift charging behavior to align with system needs, reducing peak demand and enhancing renewable self-consumption [55]. Second, clear aggregation rules are necessary to define liability allocation, measurement protocols, and settlement procedures for virtual power plant operators. Absent these rules, individual EVs—typically 50–100 kW each—cannot meet the 1 MW minimum bid threshold in most wholesale markets, forcing costly coordination among dozens of vehicles, as Koltsaklis and Knápek [28] document. Third, technical standards for metering, communication, and verification must complement charger-level protocols (e.g., ISO 15118–20, Open V2G) by specifying how to measure bidirectional energy flows, exchange dispatch signals, and audit delivered flexibility. Harmonizing these standards reduces duplication of testing and certification, lowering barriers to entry for new aggregators [47, 51]. Finally, effective governance requires unprecedented coordination between energy and transportation regulators to resolve jurisdictional overlaps and harmonize policy objectives. Aligning clean-transport incentives (e.g., EV purchase subsidies) with grid-integration policies (e.g., V2G tariffs) ensures that investments in electric mobility reinforce broader decarbonization goals. The EU’s iterative approach of phased implementation, continuous evaluation, and policy adjustment [53] alongside California’s coordinated rulemaking under SB 233 and the VGI Roadmap [56] exemplifies how cross-agency alignment can drive scalable V2X deployment while balancing innovation and reliability.

Sequenced implementation strategy

Building fully functional V2X markets requires a carefully sequenced strategy that leverages global best practices. In the pilot phase, regulatory sandboxes and demonstration projects allow experimentation with business models and market designs under controlled conditions. The UK’s Innovate V2X program and California’s Vehicle-Grid Integration (VGI) Roadmap provide frameworks for testing rapid frequency response pilots and various aggregator architectures, collecting data on charger utilization, customer behavior, and technical performance [56]. Insights from these sandboxes—such as real-world ramp-rate performance and user uptake—inform policymakers on necessary adjustments, from tariff design to technical interoperability. As technologies and market mechanisms mature, the focus should shift toward enabling scaled deployment. Standardizing interconnection agreements and aggregator-EV owner contracts reduces negotiation time and legal complexity, while pre-approved charger configurations and streamlined grid impact studies accelerate station rollouts. Harmonized cybersecurity protocols (e.g., IEC 62443) build confidence among utilities and OEMs, ensuring resilient V2X communications. Regulators at this stage should monitor emerging performance metrics—such as aggregator participation levels and grid reliability improvements—and refine incentives (e.g., Performance-Based Regulation or carbon credits) to reflect actual system benefits. In the full market integration stage, V2X resources participate alongside conventional assets in energy, capacity, and ancillary services markets. Aggregators bid EV fleets into day-ahead and real-time auctions, facilitating price arbitrage and congestion relief, while fast frequency response products—settled on sub-minute intervals—fully incorporate V2X contributions. Capacity and flexibility markets should recognize V2X’s long-duration storage potential by including second-life EV battery systems and aggregated EV fleets in resource adequacy planning. Throughout every phase, regulators must strike a balance between flexibility—to adapt frameworks as technology evolves—and stability—to provide certainty for long-lived investments. By sequencing implementation and aligning technical standards, cost-effective hardware, and forward-looking market rules, jurisdictions can create market structures that not only accommodate V2X resources but proactively leverage their capabilities to enhance system efficiency, reliability, and sustainability.

Research priorities to foster clean-energy and EV synergies

Policy support for targeted research is essential to unlock the full potential of clean-energy and EV synergies. First, advanced modeling of integrated energy-transport systems is critical: high-fidelity frameworks that simultaneously simulate renewable generation variability, grid constraints, and EV charging/discharging behavior can identify optimal aggregation strategies and forecast system-level impacts. Li and Li [47] demonstrate that co-optimizing EV dispatch with distributed PV output using stochastic programming reduces both curtailment and peak demand, underscoring the need for further research into multi-agent and stochastic methods for real-time coordination. Second, hardware and software co-design projects should explore novel power-electronics architectures and embedded control algorithms tailored to V2G requirements. Chauhan and Jain [51] identify that next-generation bidirectional converters with modular designs can reduce costs by 30–40%, yet additional R&D is needed to validate these topologies under diverse grid conditions. Menos-Aikateriniadis et al. [27] emphasize that open-source, interoperable firmware stacks are essential for cross-OEM compatibility,dedicated funding for collaborative testbeds—where multiple charger vendors and EV OEMs validate a shared software protocol—would remove a key barrier to large-scale V2X rollouts. Third, expanded pilot demonstrations in real-world distribution networks must integrate V2G with high shares of wind and solar. Koltsaklis and Knápek [28] show that empirical data from actual deployments—such as latency in bidirectional dispatch and inverter thermal performance—can dramatically refine technoeconomic models, closing the gap between simulation and practice. Policy mechanisms that defray incremental costs of installing bidirectional infrastructure at scale (e.g., matching grants or guaranteed off-take agreements) will incentivize utilities and charging service providers to host larger, longer-duration pilots. Finally, behavioral and market design studies are needed to understand consumer adoption dynamics and develop novel tariff structures. Controlled trials of aggregator incentive schemes and time-varying pricing (e.g., dynamic rates with sub-hourly settlement) can quantify EV owner participation elasticity in V2G programs. As Hsieh and Chen [38] discuss, transparent revenue models and guaranteed minimum state-of-charge thresholds boost owner willingness to enroll. Funding interdisciplinary research that combines economic psychology with big-data analytics—leveraging machine learning to predict participation rates and design compensation frameworks—will be crucial. By supporting these interconnected research priorities, policymakers can ensure that the next generation of technologies, business models, and regulatory frameworks is grounded in robust empirical evidence and ready for rapid deployment.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that the electricity and transport transitions are not merely parallel decarbonisation tracks but mutually reinforcing systems. Regions that simultaneously accelerate wind- and solar-capacity growth and EV deployment realise faster reductions in energy-sector carbon intensity than those that treat the sectors in isolation. Yet the technical promise is still curtailed by legacy market designs and fragmented standards that prevent EV batteries from acting as mainstream, dispatchable resources. Intelligent bidirectional control—whether optimisation-based or data-driven—can already shave peaks, absorb midday VRE surpluses and deliver primary frequency response, all while limiting additional battery wear. Aggregator-led virtual power plants translate this technical flexibility into monetizable services but require bid-size, telemetry and settlement rules tailored to sub-100 kW assets. Power-electronics innovations are driving down the capital cost of bidirectional chargers and widening the feasible State-of-Charge operating window, but global alignment around ISO 15118–20 and open-source firmware remains essential. Field-tested micro-grid and hybrid V2G projects confirm that EV fleets can raise local renewable utilisation above 80% and materially enhance resilience for islanded or weak grids. Viewing traction batteries through a circular-economy lens reveals that controlled second-life use in V2G or stationary roles can displace a sizeable share of new storage demand at one-quarter the levelized cost of today’s utility-scale systems. To move from pilots to systemic impact, policy makers should adopt a sequenced strategy: sandbox emerging business models; standardise interconnection, metering and cybersecurity requirements; mandate a rising share of bidirectional-ready public chargers; and create fast-response ancillary-service products settled at five-minute or shorter intervals. At the same time, research must integrate high-resolution VRE/EV data sets into co-optimised power-system and mobility models, develop cost-robust bidirectional converter topologies, and quantify consumer participation elasticities under dynamic compensation schemes. By aligning technological innovation, market reform and behavioural insight, stakeholders can transform EVs from passive loads into active, distributed-storage assets that catalyse deeper renewable penetration and accelerate progress toward net-zero. The resulting energy-mobility nexus will not only slash emissions but also enhance grid resilience, improve air quality and yield inclusive economic opportunities across regions.
