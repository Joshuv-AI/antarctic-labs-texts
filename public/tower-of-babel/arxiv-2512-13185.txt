Abstract
. Probabilistic programs encode stochastic models as ordinary-looking programs with primitives for sampling numbers from predefined distributions and conditioning. Their applications include, among many others, machine learning 18 and modeling of autonomous systems 13 . The analysis of probabilistic programs is often quantitative —it involves reasoning about numerical properties like probabilities and expectations. A particularly important quantitative property of probabilistic programs is their posterior distribution , i.e., the distribution over possible outputs for a given input (or prior) distribution. Computing the posterior distribution exactly is known as exact inference . We present our current research using weighted automata 12 , a generalization of the well-known finite automata, for performing exact inference in a restricted class of discrete probabilistic programs. This is achieved by encoding distributions over program variables—possibly with infinite support—as certain weighted automata. The semantics of our programming language then corresponds to common automata-theoretic constructions, such as product, concatenation, and others.

2.
Probability Generating Automata

In a recent paper
5
, we proposed a novel automata-based exact inference method for a certain class of imperative probabilistic programs.
The key idea is to encode joint probability (sub)distributions as weighted automata, and view each program’s semantics as an
automaton transformer
: a function translating an automaton for the input into an automaton for the output distribution.

Formally, the weighted automata we consider are defined over a nonempty subset
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
of an
\omega
-continuous semiring
(\mathbb{S},+,\cdot,0,1)
, where
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
represents the admissible transition weights or labels (e.g.,
12
).
A weighted automaton is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,M,I,F)
, where
Q
is a nonempty finite set of states,
M\in\mathbb{S}^{\prime Q\times Q}
is the transition matrix, and
I\in\mathbb{S}^{\prime 1\times Q}
and
F\in\mathbb{S}^{\prime Q\times 1}
are vectors of initial and final weights, respectively.
The semantics of
\mathcal{A}
—this is not to be confused with the program semantics (see
Section

3
)—is defined as
|\!|{\mathcal{A}}|\!|=IM^{*}F\in\mathbb{S}
, where
M^{*}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}M^{i}
.

We instantiate this abstract framework with the semiring of formal power series with nonnegative real coefficients and the program variables as indeterminates (see
Equation

1
for an example).
We allow only terms of the form
r
and
r\cdot X
, where
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\texttt{+}}
and
X
is a program variable, as transition labels.
We call an automaton
\mathcal{A}
of this form a
probability generating automaton (PGA)
if
|\!|{\mathcal{A}}|\!|
is a PGF: a formal power series whose coefficients sum up to not more than
1
.
We stress that this encoding also admits infinite-support distributions, as our PGA may have loops.

The following is an example of a PGA (unlabeled transitions have weight 1):

\frac{1}{2}
\mathcal{A}
:
\frac{1}{2}X

First, we can see that this PGA encodes an infinite-support distribution, as it involves a (self) loop containing
X
. The semantics of the automaton is the probability generating function

(1)

\displaystyle|\!|{\mathcal{A}}|\!|~=~\frac{1}{2}X^{0}+\frac{1}{4}X^{1}+\frac{1}{8}X^{2}+\cdots~,

which encodes a geometric distribution over

with parameter
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
We can also think of a PGA as a weighted multi-counter system where variables can only be incremented (but not decremented): every time we take an
X
-transition in a PGA, the value of the program variable
X
is incremented.
The probability that
X
has a certain value
n
is then the sum of the weights of all paths having exactly
n

X
-transitions.

PGA constitute the semantic domain of our framework.
Similar to other approaches
3
;
8
;
10

The novelty is that we use common automata-theoretic operations, such as the union, intersection, or concatenation, to represent most of these transformations; see
Equation

In
5

4.
Discussion

The primary benefit we anticipate from further exploring this approach is the potential to leverage the extensive body of automata theory.

For instance, one of the restrictions of
ReDiP

2
;
9
, the language we currently support, are
rectangular guards
; that is, Boolean conditions (e.g., in if-statements) can compare variables only to constants, but not to other variables.
We conjecture that we could extend our approach (to a certain degree) with support for non-rectangular guards by using a more sophisticated automaton model (e.g., pushdown automata and their variants).
Ultimately, this would allow us to broaden the class of programs for which inference can be performed exactly.
