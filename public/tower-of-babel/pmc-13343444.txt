Hepatic congestion and metabolic acidosis caused by aortic dissection with massive pericardial effusion
Authors: Xifu Wang, Yongmei Wang, Yuanyuan Xing, Wenjian Jiang, Lu Dai, Xiang Lian, Zixi Liu, Weijuan Yao, Hongjia Zhang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Main text

Acute aortic syndrome (AAS) is a cardiovascular emergency characterized by damage to the structural integrity of the aortic wall, which can affect any segment of the aorta. Particularly, Stanford A aortic dissection carries an extremely high mortality rate once it occurs.1 Currently, emergency surgical intervention is the preferred treatment strategy for AAS. However, patients who are unable to undergo surgical treatment for various reasons must receive conservative medical management. Although international guidelines provide non-surgical treatment options for AAS, detailed protocols are limited, especially for cases of aortic dissection rupture into the pericardium, leading to significant pericardial effusion. Typically, the primary cause of death in patients with aortic dissection is the dissection rupturing into the thoracic or abdominal cavity and causing massive hemorrhage. However, for patients with aortic dissection complicated by pericardial effusion who receive conservative medical treatment, refractory acidosis and systemic metabolic disturbances could be the underlying causes of mortality.

Rupture of aortic dissection into the pericardium prevents massive hemorrhage and delays sudden death to some extent, creating an opportunity for surgical intervention. However, if timely surgical treatment were not performed, sudden massive pericardial effusion could lead to cardiac tamponade. Particularly when intrapericardial thrombosis occurs, these complications would exacerbates systemic venous congestion, resulting in liver congestion, rapid elevation of transaminases, a sharp reduction in left ventricular output, inadequate renal perfusion, oliguria or anuria, and, ultimately, refractory metabolic acidosis along with circulatory and respiratory failure. Certainly, aortic dissection rupturing into the pericardium does not immediately reach the severe stage of systemic venous congestion. How to stratify the risk for patients with aortic dissection complicated by pericardial effusion and strive for earlier surgical intervention is a critical issue that requires in-depth consideration by vascular surgeons. Here, we report a case of death caused by hepatic congestion and metabolic acidosis after aortic dissection with massive pericardial effusion.

A 65-year-old male experienced chest pain 9 h ago without any apparent inducement and relieved after resting, which did not attract his attention. Subsequently, he experienced a brief episode of loss of consciousness and recovered consciousness after about 1 min without any limb movement or language impairment, and urinary or fecal incontinence. Then, he was taken to the emergency department of our hospital. The patient had a 10-year history of hypertension, with a highest recorded blood pressure of 200/100 mmHg. He did not regularly take antihypertensive medication, and the control of his blood pressure was unclear. On admission, his blood pressure was 129/79 mmHg. The patient appeared anxious and dyspneic, with coarse breath sounds in both lungs and had no obvious dry or wet rales. Heart sounds were dull, with a heart rate of 115 beats per minute and a regular rhythm. No significant murmurs were heard in any valve areas, and there was no obvious edema in the lower limbs.

Cardiac ultrasound indicated large pericardial effusion with normal ejection fraction and ventricular wall motion. Aortic computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed aortic dissection involving the ascending aorta along with massive pericardial effusion (Figures 1A and 1B). Hepatic vein contrast retention was noted, suggesting impaired contrast reflux (Figure 1C). Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis data were as follows: pH 7.438, cLac 9.9 mmol/L, actual base excess (ABE) −13.60 mmol/L, standard base excess (SBE) −14.70 mmol/L, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) 19.00 mmHg, and D-dimer 3,799 ng/mL. Whole-blood analysis indicated white blood cell (WBC) 17.33 × 109/L, neutrophil (NE) 14.59×109/L, and platelet (PLT) 40×109/L. Based on the patient’s clinical presentation and auxiliary examinations, a definitive diagnosis of Stanford A aortic dissection with massive pericardial effusion, hypertension, and metabolic acidosis combined with respiratory alkalosis was established. After a detailed discussion with the family regarding the risks of surgical operation, the family accepted the conservative medical treatment and decided that the surgical operation could be performed once the condition stabilized. However, during treatment, the patient’s condition rapidly deteriorated, with worsening dyspnea and oliguria and gradually blurred consciousness. Ten hours after admission, blood gas analysis showed pH 7.247, Lac 10.5 mmol/L, ABE −17.00 mmol/L, SBE −17.30 mmol/L, and PCO2 20.80 mmHg. Biochemistry analysis showed ALT 4690 U/L, AST 5090 U/L, Cr 326.2 μmol/L, and D-Dimer 1,1881 ng/mL. Abdominal ultrasound revealed liver congestion and dilated hepatic veins (Figures 1D and 1E). Cardiac ultrasound indicated thrombus formation within the pericardium (Figure 1F). Despite active symptomatic treatment to correct acidosis and diuresis and improve liver function, the patient’s acidosis continued to deteriorate. The heart rate gradually slowed down, eventually leading to cardiac and respiratory arrest, and the patient was declared clinically deceased.

Discussion

Aortic dissection is a severe cardiovascular emergency characterized by abrupt onset and rapid progression, often leading to sudden death due to rupture into the thoracic or abdominal cavity. Such patients frequently experience serious complications, including acute cerebral ischemia, acute renal insufficiency, acute intestinal ischemia, massive pericardial effusion, acute myocardial infarction, and acute left heart failure.2 These complications collectively accelerate the patient’s progression toward death, and in some cases, they may lead to loss of the optimal timing for surgical intervention. Currently, the primary management strategies for aortic dissection include analgesia, blood pressure and heart rate control, as well as surgical treatment. With the continuous refinement of aortic surgery techniques, the mortality rate of aortic dissection has shown a significant downward trend. Nevertheless, a subset of patients, due to the severity of their condition, are unable to undergo surgical treatment and thus lose the opportunity for survival. For patients who cannot undergo surgery, we should focus on how to stabilize their condition through conservative medical management in order to create an opportunity for subsequent surgical intervention.

Aortic dissection complicated by pericardial effusion is a fatal complication following aortic dissection, with an incidence of approximately 2.5–7.2 per 100,000 individuals per year,3 and typically requires emergency surgical intervention. Although, compared to rupture into the thoracic or abdominal cavity, rupture into the pericardium may provide some additional time for surgical intervention, the mortality rate among patients who do not undergo early surgical treatment is significantly elevated. Unlike chronic pericardial effusion, pericardial effusion resulting from aortic dissection is an acute event. Without timely pericardial drainage, it can quickly progress to acute cardiac tamponade, which restricts cardiac filling. In addition to clinical symptoms such as dyspnea, manifestations of systemic venous congestion become particularly prominent, especially hepatic congestion. Currently, there are no reports of acute hepatic congestion and metabolic disturbances caused by aortic dissection. There is no consensus in the medical community regarding whether pericardiocentesis should be performed for pericardial effusion induced by aortic dissection.4,5 The primary concern is that, if pericardiocentesis is performed without immediate follow-up surgery, then it may exacerbate the expansion of the dissection and increase the risk of massive hemorrhage. This was also the reason why pericardiocentesis was not aggressively pursued in this case. However, refraining from pericardiocentesis is also detrimental, as it further aggravates systemic venous congestion, particularly when a thrombus forms within the pericardium, leading to a further decrease of cardiac output, oliguria, or even anuria, ultimately resulting in metabolic disturbances and refractory metabolic acidosis. This may be the underlying cause of circulatory failure, cardiac arrest, and respiratory arrest in such patients.

In this case, aortic CTA revealed contrast retention at the level of the hepatic veins (Figure 1C). Ultrasound examinations showed hepatomegaly (Figure 1D) and dilation of the hepatic veins (Figure 1E), indicating impaired systemic venous return and hepatic congestion. The persistently elevated D-dimer levels, gradually decreasing platelet count, and formation of thrombi in the pericardium all suggested a hypercoagulable state. The patient exhibited persistent oliguria, and the metabolic acidosis was difficult to correct. Acidosis, in turn, aggravated liver injury and worsened renal insufficiency, creating a vicious cycle. The patient gradually developed unstable vital signs and drowsiness, ultimately leading to respiratory and cardiac arrest. Based on the above analyses, we think that aortic dissection was the fundamental cause of the patient’s death, while massive pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade, which caused severe systemic venous congestion leading to refractory acidosis, were the direct causes of death.

It is of great significance to analyze the clinical and pathological characteristics of patients with aortic dissection complicated by massive pericardial effusion and could help surgeons determine the optimal timing of surgery. In the early stage of aortic dissection with massive pericardial effusion, the body remains in a compensatory state, and metabolic acidosis and systemic metabolic disturbances have not yet occurred. This represents the optimal window for surgical intervention.

The longer the course of the disease, the faster the progression of lesions, and the more severe the systemic congestion and metabolic disorder, thus losing the opportunity for surgical intervention. Therefore, patients with aortic dissection and massive pericardial effusion should be identified early. Risk stratification based on the time of onset, vital signs, characteristics of the pericardial effusion, acid-base balance from blood gas analysis, and liver function should be performed to facilitate early and aggressive surgical intervention, making every effort to secure the timing for surgery. This is the primary viewpoint of this article, and we hope to draw more attention from surgeons.
