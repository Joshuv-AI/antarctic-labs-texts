Abstract
We report the detection of a new H i absorption system associated with the radio source NVSS J164725+375218 at a redshift of z = 2.327 z=2.327 , identified through a blind survey conducted by the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME). This marks the fifth detection of an associated system at z > 2 z>2 . By analyzing a subset of available data, we conduct a blind survey for H i absorption systems within the redshift range of 0.78 to 2.55 along 202 lines of sight in the declination range of 35 ​ ° 35{\degree} to 60 ​ ° 60{\degree} . We detect three H i absorbers, including two previously known intervening systems and one newly discovered associated system. By fitting the absorption profile with a number of Gaussian functions, we put lower limits on the optical depth, velocity integrated optical depth and the ratio between the H i column density and the spin temperature of the absorption systems. These results represent a validation of CHIME’s absorber search strategy, and demonstrate CHIME’s ability to discover new absorbers even in small subsets of its full dataset.

I
Introduction

Neutral hydrogen 21 cm absorption systems are foreground gas clouds that absorb flux from a background radio source at a wavelength of 21 cm, corresponding to the hyperfine transition of neutral hydrogen (H
i
). The detectability of H
i
absorption relies solely on the column density of the foreground gas and the brightness of the background radio source. This makes it a complement to H
i
emission observations at higher redshifts, where direct detection of emission is difficult because the strength of the emission line decreases with the square of the distance from the emitting gas. The H
i
absorption line serves as a powerful probe of cold gas in the interstellar medium (ISM), providing insights into the structure and evolution of galaxies across cosmic time. In addition to studying galaxies, H
i
21 cm absorption systems can be utilized to investigate the spin temperature of H
i
gas
(
N. Kanekar et al. 2014
)
, explore the temporal and spatial variations of fundamental constants
(
A. M. Wolfe et al. 1976
;
C. L. Carilli et al. 2000
;
J. N. Chengalur & N. Kanekar 2003
)
and directly measure cosmic acceleration
(
H.-R. Yu et al. 2014
)
.

H
i
absorption systems are classified into two main types: associated (or intrinsic) absorbers, which are physically linked to the background radio source, and intervening absorbers, which arise from gas clouds in the disks or halos of foreground galaxies along the line of sight to a background radio source. Associated absorbers are traditionally defined to be systems within 3000
\mathrm{km}

\mathrm{s}
of the emission line redshift of their host source
(
S. L. Ellison et al. 2002
)
. Since associated absorbers probe the neutral gas associated with active galactic nuclei (AGNs), they can help constrain models of AGN formation and evolution. Conversely, intervening absorbers can be used to study the properties of the ISM in high-redshift galaxies
(
R. Morganti & T. Oosterloo 2018
;
R. Dutta 2019
)
.

The current sample of H
i
absorbers, primarily identified from catalogs of damped Lyman-alpha absorbers (DLAs) and Mg
ii
absorbers, is limited by observational biases. Optical surveys for DLAs, which rely on the Lyman-alpha transition in the ultraviolet, are restricted to redshifts
z\gtrsim 1.7
due to atmospheric cut-off, and expensive space-based observations are needed to probe these systems at lower redshifts. Additionally, dust in high-column-density, metal-rich DLAs can obscure background quasars, potentially biasing optical samples against such systems and contributing to the observed lack of metallicity evolution in DLAs.

An unbiased blind radio survey for 21-cm absorption systems overcomes these limitations, as radio waves are unaffected by dust and atmospheric absorption, allowing for a uniform census of cold hydrogen gas across a wide redshift range. Thanks to the enhanced bandwidth coverage and sensitivity of radio telescopes, several blind surveys for H
i
absorbers have been conducted in recent years, including the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Radio Telescope All Sky H
i
survey (FASHI), covering declinations between
-16^{\degree}
to
66^{\degree}
at
z<0.42

(
C.-P. Zhang et al. 2025
)
; the First Large Absorption Survey in H
i
(FLASH), which covers the sky south of declination
40^{\degree}
in the redshift range
0.42<z<1.0

(
J. R. Allison et al. 2020
;
J. R. Allison et al. 2022
)
; and the MeerKAT Absorption Line Survey (MALS), designed to conduct an unbiased survey of H
i
and OH absorption at redshift
z<2
in the declination range of
-40\degree<\delta<30\degree

(
N. Gupta et al. 2018
)
.

To date, only ten detections of H
i
absorbers at
z>2
have been reported, and just four of these are associated H
i
absorbers
(
N. Gupta et al. 2021
;
J. N. H. S. Aditya et al. 2020
;
C. B. Moore et al. 1998
;
J. M. Uson et al. 1991
)
.

S. J. Curran & M. T. Whiting 2010
propose that the scarcity of associated H
i
absorbers may result from the bias in optically selected surveys, which often target luminous AGNs at high redshifts. These AGNs can fully ionize the surrounding neutral hydrogen, leading to the non-detection of the 21 cm absorption line.

The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) Absorber Project aims to increase the sample size of H
i
absorbers at high redshifts by conducting a wide-area blind survey in the redshift range
0.78<z<2.55
. The catalog of H
i
absorbers in this redshift range can then be utilized to characterize the cold gas in galaxies and the environment of AGNs. In this paper, we present the initial results of a spectrally blind search for H
i
absorbers using the CHIME Absorber backend, which involved analyzing four datasets, each covering one month of data. This search resulted in the detection of a new H
i
absorption signal associated with the radio source NVSS J164725+375218 at redshift 2.327, as well as two previously known intervening absorbers.

II
Methods

II.1
Instrument

The CHIME telescope (
8

CHIME Collaboration et al. 2022
) is a transit radio telescope located at the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory near Penticton, BC, Canada. It consists of four cylindrical parabolic reflectors of
20\,$\mathrm{m}$\times 100\,$\mathrm{m}$
each, aligned along the north-south direction. Each reflector contains a linear array of 256 dual-polarization cloverleaf feeds
(
M. Deng & D. Campbell-Wilson 2017
)
connected to low-noise amplifiers, band-pass filters, and analog-to-digital converters. The telescope is sensitive to frequencies from 400
\mathrm{MHz}
to 800
\mathrm{MHz}
, which corresponds to the H
i
line at redshifts between 2.55 and 0.78.

The time-domain data stream arrives in the F-engine, where it is channelized in field-programmable gate arrays by performing a polyphase filter bank (PFB) using a fast Fourier transform (FFT) on 2048 samples at a time, giving frames of 2.56
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
with a spectral resolution of 390.625
\mathrm{kHz}
. After applying a scaling factor and phase offset for each frequency channel for optimal compression
(
J. Mena-Parra et al. 2018
)
, these are rounded to 4+4 bit for transmission to CHIME’s GPU-based X-engine.

After arriving in the GPU nodes, the data stream for CHIME’s Absorber and FRB backends (
9

CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2018
) splits off from those for the Cosmology (
8

CHIME Collaboration et al. 2022
) and Pulsar (
10

CHIME/Pulsar Collaboration et al. 2021
) backends, which have different needs. FFT beam-forming creates 1024 closely spaced beams in a static grid of 256 beams in the North-South (NS) and 4 in the East-West (EW) direction
(
C. Ng et al. 2017
)
. In the NS direction, the beam pointing covers the declinations north of
-10\degree
. In the EW direction, the beams point
(-0.4\degree,0.0\degree,0.4\degree,0.8\degree)
away from the meridian.

H
i
21 cm absorption features are usually narrower than 390.625 kHz, so data for the absorber search needs a higher resolution than CHIME’s native 390.625 kHz channels. For each beam and frequency channel, 128 successive frames of 2.56
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
are accumulated and Fourier-transformed to achieve a spectral resolution of approximately 3.052
\mathrm{kHz}
. These data are squared, integrated to 10
\mathrm{s}
, and then written to disk for archiving.

The ability of CHIME’s Absorber system to continuously scan a large part of the sky at a sufficiently high spectral resolution makes it an excellent instrument for discovering new H
i
21 cm absorbers.

II.2
Observations and data processing

The CHIME Absorber backend began collecting data in February 2021, and we currently have approximately four years of data. Our plan for the search is to process a subset of these data as an initial step, allowing us to understand the characteristics of the pipeline output before conducting a more comprehensive search for 21 cm absorption lines by processing additional data. This paper presents the results of our initial search for 21 cm absorbers utilizing the CHIME Absorber backend. Our objective is to identify H
i
21 cm absorption systems within a redshift range of 0.78 to 2.55, corresponding to frequencies between 400 and 800
\mathrm{MHz}
, and a declination range of
35{\degree}
to
60{\degree}
.

We processed four months of data to conduct this blind survey, covering periods from mid-March to mid-April 2021 and 2023 (hereafter referred to as the April datasets), as well as December 2021 and 2023. During these 4 months, the instrument was more stable with little downtime and fewer transient radio frequency interference (RFI) events. Additionally, the April and December datasets are eight months apart, enabling us to reject many false positives using the expected barycentric shift between the April and December data caused by Earth’s orbital motion.

Each dataset was processed individually using the CHIME Absorber pipeline, which includes RFI excision, sidereal re-gridding which interpolates the data onto a fixed grid in local Earth rotation angle ranging from
0{\degree}
to
360{\degree}
, and correcting for the band-shape which is caused by enhancing the original frequency resolution of CHIME data from 390.625
\mathrm{kHz}
to 3.052
\mathrm{kHz}
. The band-shape correction is performed in two steps: first, each coarse channel with a width of 390.625 kHz is divided by a PFB model. Second, we subtract the spectrum of each pixel in the sky map from the average of its neighboring pixels in right ascension direction. This helps remove truncation noise bias caused by 4+4 bit truncation in the F-engine, which is not accounted for in our PFB model. Finally, the sidereal days are averaged together. The output is a 3D map of the sky for each dataset, comprising spectra from over two hundred thousand pixels, which were subsequently saved for analysis. The daily integration time for each line of sight is approximately
20\,$\mathrm{s}$
, resulting in a total integration time of about 10 minutes per sight line over the course of one month.

After the two-step band-shape correction, non-negligible band-shape residuals remain. Within each coarse channel, these residuals are well-described by a sine function with a wavelength equal to the coarse channel width, with phase and amplitude varying slowly from channel to channel. Since the change in phase and amplitude is gradual, narrow-band RFI or absorption features appear as outliers in amplitude and phase. To address this, we smooth the fitted amplitudes and phases using the arPLS method
(
S.-J. Baek et al. 2015
)
. The residuals are then mitigated by subtracting the sine function, with the smoothed amplitude and phase, from each coarse channel. This approach ensures that outlier features, including absorption features, remain unaffected.

The correction of the spectra for the Earth’s orbital motion (barycentric correction) is also not yet part of the pipeline processing steps. Instead, we calculate the average frequency shift caused by the Earth’s motion over one month along the lines of sight and apply this correction to the averaged spectra. The main drawback of this approach is that a few percent of any very narrow absorption feature is washed out because we average the data before applying the barycentric correction. This effect is minimal for absorption features with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of
\gtrsim 35$\mathrm{kHz}$
, but our search becomes less sensitive to very narrow-band features. In the future, we plan to incorporate the barycentric correction task into the analysis pipeline, ensuring that the spectra are corrected for the Earth’s orbital motion before averaging the data.

Our long-term goal is to process all lines of sight to conduct an untargeted and unbiased blind radio survey for H
i
21 cm absorbers. However, for this initial search, we focused on a limited subset of data and selected only those lines of sight with sufficiently bright background sources. We used the NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS) catalog
(
J. J. Condon et al. 1998
)
and available redshift information taken from the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database (NED)
1
1

1

https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/
to select 202 lines of sight from the processed output. These lines of sight were chosen based on having a background source brighter than 400 mJy at 1.4 GHz and a redshift greater than 0.78 so that they can act as a background source for the absorbers. Requiring an optical redshift measurement introduces a bias toward the radio sources that are less dusty and exhibit strong UV brightness. Note that unlike targeted H
i
21 cm surveys toward DLAs or Mg
ii
absorbers, where the redshift of the absorption feature is known from optical surveys and used to guide H
i
absorption searches in the radio spectrum, our initial search represents a spectrally blind survey, i.e., we have no prior knowledge about the H
i
absorption frequency of the absorber.

A search algorithm was then applied to the spectra of these lines of sight to identify absorption features. This algorithm is based on a likelihood ratio test that compares the likelihood of the null hypothesis that the observed data is due to the noise against the likelihood of the signal being a genuine Gaussian absorption feature. Likelihood ratio values are evaluated by convolving spectra with Gaussian templates with full width at half maximum (FWHM) ranging from 35
\mathrm{kHz}
to 175
\mathrm{kHz}
.

Finally, we select absorber candidates by comparing the search results across different datasets, requiring that the absorption features must appear at similar frequencies in all datasets where the absorption feature is not masked. The spectra of the final candidates are visually inspected, and any features attributed to systematics or RFI are excluded.

II.3
Physical and observable properties

Two main observables in H
i
absorption studies are the fractional change in continuum level due to absorption (
\Delta S/S_{0}
) and the width of the absorption feature. These observables are used to estimate the observed optical depth and the ratio between H
i
column density
{N_{\rm H\,{\textsc{i}}}}
and the spin temperature
T_{\mathrm{s}}
of the absorbing cloud. The optical depth is defined as
\tau=-\ln(1-\Delta S/(fS_{0}))
, where
S_{0}
and
f
represent the continuum level and the covering factor of the background source, respectively. In the optically thin regime (
\Delta S/S_{0}\lesssim 0.3
), the optical depth can be approximated by
\tau\approx\Delta S/(fS_{0})
. The relationship between the true and observed optical depth is
\tau=\tau_{\rm obs}/f
. The
{N_{\rm H\,{\textsc{i}}}}
can then be determined by

{N_{\rm H\,{\textsc{i}}}}=A\times\frac{T_{\mathrm{s}}}{f}\int\tau_{\rm obs}\,dv,

(1)

where
A=1.823\times 10^{18}\,$\mathrm{cm}$
is a constant factor, and
\int\tau_{\rm obs}\,dv
is the velocity-integrated optical depth
(
A. M. Wolfe & G. R. Burbidge 1975
)
.

We fit the absorption profiles using models with a varying number of Gaussian components, selecting the best fit using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC;
G. Schwarz 1978
). The BIC is defined as

\text{BIC}=k\ln(n)-2\ln(\hat{L})

(2)

where
k
is the number of parameters in the model,
n
is the number of data points, and

\hat{L}
is the maximum likelihood value of the model. Models with a lower BIC value are preferred. The fits give the peak absorption frequency
\nu_{\rm abs}
, peak observed optical depth
\tau_{\rm peak}
, and FWHM of each component, which are then used to estimate the quantities
\int\tau_{\rm obs}\,dv
and
f{N_{\rm H\,{\textsc{i}}}}/T_{\mathrm{s}}
for each individual component, as well as for the entire absorption feature. For absorbers with multiple Gaussian components, the total absorption frequency is estimated by taking the weighted average, using the velocity-integrated optical depths as weights.

III.2
Detection of two previously known absorbers

There are a total of 13 known H
i
21 cm absorbers in the redshift range covered by CHIME and within the declination range of
35{\degree}
to
60{\degree}
. Of these 13 absorbers, 5 fall within RFI bands, and 6 are too weak to be detected in this limited initial search. The noise level in one month of data ranges from 35 to 45 mJy across 400-790
\mathrm{MHz}

However, we have successfully re-detected the remaining two previously known absorbers across all our datasets. The first is an absorption feature at a frequency of 431.765
\mathrm{MHz}
toward the source NVSS J031443+431405, first reported by

and 4.94 Jy at

The other detection is an intervening system toward NVSS J235421+455304, which is a quasar with a flux density of 1.8 Jy at 1.4 GHz and located at a redshift of 1.992. The absorption profile is best fitted with two Gaussian components as shown in Figure

IV
Summary and perspective

We present the results of a spectrally blind survey for H
i
21 cm absorption systems along the lines of sight to 202 sources within a declination range of
35{\degree}
to
60{\degree}
. The sources are at a redshift of
z>0.77
, allowing them to serve as backlight for the absorbers. Using the CHIME Absorber pipeline, four datasets, each representing one month of data, are processed individually to produce spectra of 202 lines of sight. We then apply barycentric corrections, a search algorithm, and a validation algorithm as separate steps outside the pipeline to identify final candidates. We detect two previously known intervening absorbers and one new associated system at a redshift of 2.32743. The new detection is only the fifth detection of an H
i
21 cm absorber associated with an AGN at redshift
z>2
. By modeling the absorption profile with a set of Gaussian functions, we establish lower limits on the optical depth, velocity-integrated optical depth, and
f{N_{\rm H\,{\textsc{i}}}}/T_{\mathrm{s}}
for our detections.

The initial search presented in this paper is labeled as a “spectrally” blind search, because although we selected radio sources whose optical counterpart has a known redshift, we have not used any prior information about the expected H
i
absorption frequency of the absorbers. However, there are many bright radio sources in the NVSS catalog whose redshift is unknown, and a fully blind survey must include all such lines of sight. In the near future, we plan to conduct a fully blind survey by processing additional data, targeting fainter background sources with and without redshift information, and increasing sky coverage.
