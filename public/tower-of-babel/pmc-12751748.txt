Innovative application of mefloquine and Methacycline in combating Tet(X3/X4)-positive Escherichia coli infections
Authors: Qianyu Zhou, Jinjing Xue, Jun Li, Zhiying Liu, Yinuo Zou, Rui Dai, Kuan Gu, Yonglin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The advent and global spread of plasmid-carried resistance genes, including the tigecycline resistance gene tet(X3/X4) and the colistin resistance gene mcr-1, present a substantial threat to public health that requires immediate resolution. This study successfully screened and identified commercial anti-parasitic drugs that efficiently inhibit Tet(X4) enzyme activity (IC50 = 79.42 µg/mL), revealing that mefloquine exhibited a synergistic antibacterial effect with tetracyclines against tet(X3/X4)-positive bacteria (FIC < 0.5) and an additive effect with tetracyclines against tet(X3/X4)-negative bacteria (0.5 < FIC < 1, excluding minocycline). Molecular dynamics simulations and checkerboard minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) tests on Tet(X4) mutants in amino acid residues demonstrated that mefloquine’s interaction with the active site of Tet(X4) reduces its activity. The energy contribution of the binding site residues involves PHE221, PHE316, GLY233, PRO315, PHE232, MET212, ALA222 and HIE231, which establish robust interactions with mefloquine (ΔEtotal of ≤ − 0.5 kcal/mol). Moreover, findings from metabonomics, bacterial membrane analysis, ROS detection, NAD+/NADH ratio determination, and intracellular ATP determination indicated that mefloquine may significantly influence bacterial energy metabolism (P < 0.05). Furthermore, in the mouse infection model of E. coli 47EC, the combination of methacycline and mefloquine, demonstrated significant therapeutic advantages, as evidenced by an increased survival rate (from 20.00% to 80.00%), decreased sepsis score, and decreased bacterial load. Our work is the first to reveal that the commercial medicine mefloquine can prolong the efficacy of tetracycline and treat infections caused by drug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae in clinical environments.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The antimicrobial resistance of gram-negative bacteria presents a significant risk to human health and livestock production, with E. coli (Escherichia coli) and A. baumannii (Acinetobacter baumannii) ranking among the top five bacteria in terms of isolation and resistance rates [1, 2]. Tigecycline and colistin are primarily regarded as the final options for treating extensively drug-resistant gram-negative bacterial infections [3, 4]. Regrettably, reports indicate that E. coli strains isolated from animals harbor the plasmid-mediated tigecycline resistance gene tet(X4) and the colistin resistance gene mcr-1, which has emerged as a global epidemic [3]. This indicates a potential outbreak of E. coli that has entirely compromised the final line of protection. Tetracycline antibiotics, including oxytetracycline, methacycline, and tigecycline, are offered in various dose forms for both human and veterinary use and exhibit high oral bioavailability and moderate costs [5]. Before the identification of tet(X3/X4) genes, the predominant mechanism of low-level tigecycline resistance in gram-negative bacteria was typically linked to the overexpression of efflux pumps, such as AdeABC and AcrAB-TolC [5–7]. Conversely, the plasmid-mediated tigecycline resistance gene tet(X3/X4) confers significant bacterial resistance to all tetracycline drugs [8, 9]. Tet(X3/X4) has been identified as monooxygenases involved in the oxidative phosphorylation pathways in bacteria, which are associated with energy metabolism, ATP production, or reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, hence being essential for bacterial viability [8].

In recent years, several adjuvants and inhibitors that have the potential to increase the efficacy of antibiotics or inhibit resistance have been reported, showing promise in the treatment of drug-resistant bacterial infections. β-lactamase inhibitors (such as clavulanic acid, tazobactam, and sulbactam) have been clinically proven to increase the efficacy of β-lactam antibiotics by inhibiting the activity of β-lactamases [10, 11]. For example, our previous research in the lab has shown, Carnosic acid is a potential New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase-1 inhibitor, although carnosic acid did not display direct antibacterial activity, the combination of carnosic acid and meropenem still showed bactericidal activity after the loss of bactericidal effect of meropenem [12]. Flavonoids, a class of natural polyphenolic substances widely present in fruits, vegetables, and tea, not only significantly reduce the use of antibiotics in combination but also decrease or even reverse bacterial drug resistance [13]. We have discovered some natural compounds that could inhibit the resistant enzyme with good synergy antibacterial [12, 14]. Such as the combination of honokiol and polymyxin had a significantly synergistic effect against MCR-1-positive Enterobacteriaceae in vivo/vitro, and the nature compounds may not continue to induce new resistance, and it can reduce the dosage of combined antibiotics [14].

Consequently, the identification of Tet(X3/X4) or enzymes associated with oxidative phosphorylation pathways presents a feasible approach for addressing high-level resistance to tetracycline antibiotics in gram-negative bacteria. This work revealed that mefloquine is a possible inhibitor of Tet(X4) that influences bacterial energy metabolism and exhibits synergistic efficacy with tetracyclines against Tet(X3/X4)-positive bacteria. Mefloquine, an aryl amino acid, is frequently the preferable choice for malaria prevention in travelers to endemic regions, as recognized by numerous worldwide health standards [15, 16]. The precise mechanism of the anti-malarial action of mefloquine primarily involves the inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase and glutamic acid decarboxylase, therefore impacting nucleic acid synthesis and resulting in DNA damage [17]. Doxycycline, a tetracycline antibiotic, is one of four anti-malarial medicines that can be taken with mefloquine to augment its therapeutic impact [16, 18]. Delineated administration dosages and substantial pharmacologic efficacy create optimal conditions for the use of tetracycline antibiotics in conjunction with mefloquine against Tet(X3/X4)-positive bacterial infections.

Materials and methods

Sacrifice methods Experimental animals appearing in this article were anesthetized before being killed. Tribromoethanol (M2910, Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology) with a dose of 16 μl/g was injected intraperitoneally into mice. After coma, mice were killed by neck breaking, and no other animals were present when each experimental animal was killed, which was in line with animal ethics.

Bacterial strains and reagents

All bacterial strains, including Tet(X3/X4)-positive and negative laboratory strains as well as clinical isolates, were described in detail in our prior study [19]. The examined isolates were preserved, cultivated, and described following standard microbiological procedures.

All marketed pharmaceuticals were acquired from a certified retail pharmacy. Nine tetracycline antibiotics and mefloquine (with purities confirmed to exceed 95%) were acquired from the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control and Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd, and dissolved in DMSO for in vitro experiments. For animal experiment, mefloquine or methacycline were suspended in 0.5% CMC-Na at different doses (1 mg/mL of mefloquine, 1 mg/mL of mefloquine or mixture of them). All the compounds utilized in this investigation, unless specified otherwise, were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc. (St. Louis MO, USA).

Antimicrobial susceptibilities

The synergistic antibacterial effects of mefloquine and tetracycline antibiotics against Tet(X3/X4)-positive bacterial strains were determined using checkerboard minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays in Mueller-Hinton broth, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2019 protocol [20].

Following 16–24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the synergistic efficacies of antibiotics in conjunction with mefloquine were evaluated through the determination of the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) using the following formula: FIC index = (MIC mefloquine in combination/MIC mefloquine alone) + (MIC antibiotics in combination/MIC antibiotics alone). A FICI ≤ 0.5 was considered synergistic. Furthermore, E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4)-P315A, E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4)-G233A, E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4)-F316A and E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4)-F221A, generated through site-directed mutagenesis utilizing a QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis kit (Stratagene), were employed to validate the synergistic antibacterial effects of mefloquine and methacycline.

Growth curves and time-dependent killing assay

Overnight cultures of E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) and E. coli 47EC were diluted 1:1000 and subsequently incubated at 37 °C with shaking to achieve an OD600nm of 0.3. The bacterial cultures were equally divided and supplemented with varying amounts of mefloquine (0 µg/mL, 8–64 µg/mL). The examined bacteria were cultivated for a maximum of 12 h, and bacterial proliferation was assessed by measuring the OD600 nm at one-hour intervals.

The bactericidal efficacy of mefloquine and tetracycline antibiotics was assessed using a time-dependent killing test. Various concentrations of mefloquine, methacycline/tigecycline, or their combinations were administered and incubated for microbiological plating at the specified time points. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which colony-forming units (CFUs) were enumerated [21].

Live/dead bacteria staining

E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) strains cultivated overnight were diluted, and logarithmic growth was continued. The bacteria were diluted (1:100) and subsequently exposed to varying concentrations of mefloquine, methacycline, or their combination and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C.

The bacteria were harvested and stained with green/red fluorescence via the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China), Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM assays were employed to visually examine the morphological alterations associated with the eradication of E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) by the combination of mefloquine and methacycline. E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) strains were subjected to treatment with mefloquine, methacycline, or their combination (Control (no treatment); Mefloquine, (only 32 µg/mL of mefloquine); Methacycline, (only 16 µg/mL of methacycline); Combination, (32 µg/mL of mefloquine plus 16 µg/mL of methacycline)). The bacteria were harvested via centrifugation, subsequently washed, and resuspended in sterile PBS.

The bacterial samples were treated with 0.1% polylysine and subsequently fixed in glutaraldehyde for 6 h at 4 °C. The morphological alterations of the examined microorganisms were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Enzyme inhibition assays

The purified Tet(X4) protein was incubated with NADPH, Mg2+, and O2 at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, varying doses of mefloquine were introduced into the cultures for an additional 30 min. Each well was subsequently combined with an identical volume of tetracycline.

The enzymatic activity of Tet(X4) was assessed by measuring the absorbance at an OD of 400 nm over 1 h. The inhibition of enzymatic activity was quantified as Inhibition (%) = 1−(∆OD400 nm of mefloquine/∆OD400 nm of normal control)×100% [22].

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

The operational procedures and methodologies for molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations were delineated in our prior study [19]. The PDB code (PDB ID: 7epv) acquired from the Protein Data Bank pertains to the crystal structure of Tet(X4) [22]. The preliminary configuration of the MD simulation complex of mefloquine-Tet(X4) was acquired via AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 software for conventional docking protocols.

Following the 100 ns simulation, the binding free energies (ΔGbind in kcal/mol) were computed utilizing the Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) method. Free energy decomposition can be categorized into the van der Waals contribution (∆Evdw), the solvation contribution (∆Esol), the total contribution (∆Eele), and the total contribution (∆Etotal) [23].

Western blotting analysis

Western blotting was conducted to verify the impact of mefloquine on Tet(X4) synthesis in various bacteria over time. The examined microorganisms were subjected to mefloquine treatment at 37 °C for either 4–8 h.

Bacterial samples were obtained via centrifugation to facilitate western blot analysis. The samples were fractionated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked and treated with a primary antibody targeting Tet(X4) and a goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (HRP). The membranes were ultimately visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit from Biosharp.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

The metabolite analysis of E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) was conducted using Ultra High-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry/Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) following the previously outlined technique [20]. The metabolite analysis of E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) was conducted using Ultra High-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry/Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) following the previously outlined technique [20].

The bacterial metabolites were collected and evaluated in both positive ion (POS) mode and negative ion (NEG) mode. Using the gathered data, systematic pathway annotation was conducted for all the detected metabolites, alongside differential analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.kegg.jp) analysis for all the differentially abundant metabolites.

Inner membrane (IM) and outer membrane (OM) permeability determination

The bacterial cells were harvested, washed, resuspended, and cultured with mefloquine, methacycline, or their combination at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by the addition of N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN, 10 µmol/L) for outer membrane permeability assessment or propidium iodide (PI, 10 nM) for inner membrane permeability assessment in the dark for 30 min. The integrity of the outer membrane was assessed using an excitation wavelength of 535 nm and an emission wavelength of 615 nm, whereas the integrity of the inner membrane was evaluated via an excitation wavelength of 350 nm and an emission wavelength of 420 nm.

Membrane depolarization analysis

A total of 5 × 108 CFUs/mL bacterial cells were harvested, washed, resuspended, and treated with 2 µM 3-dipropylthiadicarbocyanine iodide DiSC3 [5] (Aladdin) at 37 °C for 30 min. Ultimately, mefloquine, methacycline, or their mixtures were administered, and measurements were taken continuously at an excitation wavelength of 622 nm and an emission wavelength of 670 nm for 50 min.

ROS detection

The reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) under various treatments were quantified using 2′,7′- dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 10 µM, Sigma) following the provided protocol. The bacterial solution of E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) was treated with DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min and subsequently washed with sterile PBS buffer. Then, the fluorescence intensity of mefloquine, methacycline, or their combination was measured by a spectrofluorimeter at 488 nm/605 nm.

NAD+/NADH ratio determination

The redox reaction constitutes the chemical foundation of bacterial energy metabolism, and the NAD+/NADH ratio is critical for assessing the bacterial redox reaction. E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) was cocultured with mefloquine, methacycline, or their mixtures. The bacterial cells were harvested, rinsed, and resuspended to assess the NAD+/NADH ratio with an NAD+/NADH Assay Kit with WST-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intracellular ATP determination

Overnight cultured E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) was injected at a ratio of 1:100 in LB medium supplemented with various quantities of mefloquine, methacycline, or their combinations at 37 °C for 5 h. Bacterial cells were harvested, washed, and lysed for 2 h for ATP detection in the lysate. The intracellular ATP concentrations were subsequently measured following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Mouse infection model assays

Female BALB/c mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were procured from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. An intraperitoneal infection model in which E. coli 47EC (5 × 109 CFUs/mL) was used in the mice (n = 15 per group) was used to validate the synergistic impact of methacycline and mefloquine. The dosage given to mice is derived from the conversion of the dosage given to a child. The experiment was divided into four groups, each treated once every 12 h, control (only solvent); The mice were orally fed methacycline (20 mg/kg); The mice were orally fed mefloquine (30 mg/kg); Or the mice were orally fed combination of methacycline (20 mg/kg) and mefloquine (30 mg/kg). The quantity of living mice was documented every 12 h until 144 h post infection.

For the murine sepsis score (MSS) and bacterial loading assays, six mice per group were intraperitoneally infected with 5 × 108 CFUs/mL of E. coli 47EC and treated as previously described. The MSS was evaluated and quantified via a rigorous scoring system as previously described [24]. The mice were subsequently anesthetized and euthanized to assess the bacterial load in the lungs, hearts, livers, and spleens. Tribromoethanol (M2910,Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology) with a dose of 16 μl/g was injected intraperitoneally into mice. After coma, mice were killed by neck breaking, and no other animals were present when each experimental animal was killed, which was in line with animal ethics.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed via SPSS version 19.0, and diagrams were generated via GraphPad Prism 7.0. ANOVA tests were used for multi-group comparisons and t-test was used for the comparison of two groups. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). “**” denotes a highly significant difference (P < 0.01), and “*” signifies a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Ethics

The animal experiments received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jilin University and were conducted following the committee’s rules.

Results

Mefloquine from commercial medications was evaluated to determine the antibacterial efficacy of tetracycline antibiotics against Tet(X3/X4)-positive bacteria

The proliferation of Tet(X3/X4)-mediated gram-negative bacterial resistance to tetracyclines has significantly surpassed the advancement of novel pharmaceuticals. Consequently, the most efficient method to obtain drugs with synergistic antibacterial properties is currently to evaluate existing clinically utilized medications. We employed the modified checkerboard microdilution approach to evaluate tetracycline synergists and isolate mefloquine from clinically utilized anti-parasitic agents (Fig. 1A). The checkerboard MIC data demonstrated a synergistic impact between mefloquine and tigecycline or methacycline in the Tet(X3/X4)-positive laboratory strains (Fig. 1B and E). Moreover, the checkerboard MIC results indicated that mefloquine combined with other tetracyclines had a significant synergistic effect (FICI ≤ 0.5), whereas mefloquine had an additive effect with tetracyclines (excluding minocycline) against the Tet(X3/X4)-negative laboratory strain E. coli DH5α + pAM401 (0.5 < FICI ≤ 1) (Fig. 2A). Substantial synergies between mefloquine and methacycline were also confirmed in various Tet(X3/X4)-positive isolates (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the presence of Tet(X4) induced the resistance of the clinical isolates E. coli J53-p47EC and K. pneumoniae K12016-p47EC to tetracyclines, whereas the presence of mefloquine markedly augmented the bacteriostatic effect of tetracyclines (Fig. 2C and D).

Conversely, mefloquine can bind to the catalytic pocket of Tet(X4), hence inhibiting its biological activity in vitro

Tet(X4) can hydrolyze tetracycline, resulting in alterations in the absorbance value of tetracycline at 400 nm. Figure 4A shows that the hydrolytic activity of Tet(X4) toward tetracycline was markedly suppressed by increasing doses of mefloquine. This study employed molecular docking and molecular modeling to investigate the inhibitory effects of mefloquine on Tet(X4). Following 100,000 ps of simulation in an equilibrium state (Fig. 4D and E), the three-dimensional binding mode of Tet(X4)-mefloquine was established via molecular docking, as illustrated in Fig. 4B, with the binding site adjacent to flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). The stability of Tet(X4)-mefloquine is attributed to van der Waals forces, conventional hydrogen bonds, pi-pi interactions, and other stacking factors (Fig. 4C). The energy contribution of the binding site residues in Tet(X4)-mefloquine mostly involves PHE221, PHE316, GLY233, PRO315, PHE232, MET212, ALA222 and HIE231, which establish robust interactions with mefloquine (ΔEtotal of ≤ − 0.5 kcal/mol) (Fig. 4F).

To validate the precision of the molecular docking and molecular modeling outcomes, we developed four mutants of E. coli DH5α + pAM401-tet(X4) for synergistic testing. The results shown in Fig. 4G indicate that the synergistic effects of mefloquine and methacycline were dramatically decreased in the Tet(X4) mutants. These findings suggest that the data produced by the MD simulation of the Tet(X4)-mefloquine complex are dependable. Furthermore, the findings of the western blot analysis indicated that mefloquine did not affect the production of Tet(X4) in various bacteria over time (Fig. 4H).

Mefloquine can have synergistic antibacterial actions by influencing bacterial energy metabolism

Tet(X4) functions as a monooxygenase; thus, the inhibitory impact of mefloquine on Tet(X4) may disrupt the energy metabolism of bacteria harboring Tet(X4). Consequently, we employed metabonomics analysis utilizing both negative ion mode (NEG) and positive ion mode (POS) to validate an alternative mechanism of synergistic antibacterial action in E. coli DH5a + pAM401-tet(X4). Initially, the chemical categories of the metabolites were enumerated, and pie charts were generated to illustrate these classifications. Figure 5A and B show that the metabolites predominantly consisted of organic acids and derivatives, as well as lipids and lipid-like compounds from both negative and positive modes. In POS mode, 6 metabolites were downregulated, and 11 metabolites were elevated following mefloquine administration (Fig. 5C). In NEG mode, 12 metabolites were downregulated, and 5 metabolites were upregulated following mefloquine administration (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the differentially expressed metabolites were involved in nucleic acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and microbial metabolism across various settings in both the NEG and POS modes (Fig. 5E and F), all of which are directly associated with energy metabolism (Fig. 6A). These findings support the anti-malarial mechanism of mefloquine, which disrupts DNA and hemoglobin synthesis.

We thus verified the impact of mefloquine or mefloquine-enhanced tetracyclines on the membrane integrity and energy metabolism of E. coli DH5a + pAM401-tet(X4). Figure 6B and C show that the inner/outer membranes dramatically increased upon the addition of 16 µg/mL mefloquine, as evidenced by an increased fluorescence intensity (PI for the inner membrane, NPN for the outside membrane). Simultaneously, the alteration in the fluorescence of the redox probe DiSC3 [5] was employed to assess the bacterial membrane potential, with mefloquine treatment resulting in increased fluorescence, indicating that mefloquine significantly altered the bacterial membrane potential (∆ψ) (Fig. 6D). The production and buildup of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and alterations in intracellular ATP levels are intricately linked to bacterial energy metabolism and membrane integrity. As anticipated, the concentration of ROS in the energy metabolism pathway was markedly increased (Fig. 6E), whereas the intracellular ATP level dramatically decreased following the administration of mefloquine (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, the introduction of mefloquine resulted in a decreased NAD+/NADH ratio, signifying a disruption in the redox equilibrium within the bacteria and an alteration in energy metabolism (Fig. 6F).

The combination of mefloquine and methacycline effectively protected mice from intraperitoneal infections caused by E. coli

The interaction of mefloquine with methacycline was further demonstrated in animal models of E. coli 47EC infection. The survival rate of the mouse infection models increased from 20.00% (solvent-treated, control) to 40.00% (20 mg/kg of methacycline-treated, monotherapy), 46.67% (30 mg/kg of mefloquine-treated, monotherapy) and 80.00% (combination), respectively (Fig. 7A). The animals administered the combination demonstrated markedly lower sepsis scores at 24 h and 48 h post-treatment (Fig. 7B). Moreover, the combination therapy markedly decreased the bacterial burden in the lungs, heart, liver, and spleen relative to that of the monotherapy or control (Fig. 7C and F).

Discussion

The advent and global dissemination of mobile resistance genes ndm-1, tet(X4), and mcr-1 in gram-negative bacteria present a significant danger to human health and animal husbandry [25]. In particular, a significant proportion of co-harboring ndm-1, mcr-1, or tet(X4) strains are grouped into distinct STs, predominantly originating from Asian nations, notably China, Malaysia, and Pakistan [25–28]. Thus, prevention of the dissemination of critical antibiotic resistance genes in gram-negative bacteria, possibly facilitated by horizontal plasmid transfer, through prompt intervention measures is crucial.

Since the first identification of plasmid-mediated tigecycline resistance gene tet(X4) in E. coli, it has been identified multiple sources, including poultry gut microbiomes and Salmonella isolated from human [29, 30]. In our study, molecular dynamics simulations were used to predict the interactions between mefloquine and the binding site of Tet(X4), analyzing how these interactions influence the enzyme’s conformational changes and catalytic activity. Equilibrium simulations were conducted to elucidate the three-dimensional binding mode of Tet (X4)-mefloquine through molecular docking, with binding sites adjacent to FAD. The stability of these complexes primarily arises from stacking interactions, encompassing van der Waals forces, conventional hydrogen bonding, and π-π interactions. Notably, the energy contribution of the Tet (X4)-mefloquine binding site residue correlates with its effective interaction with mefloquine, as evidenced in result Fig. 4F. Consequently, our findings demonstrate that mefloquine effectively binds to the catalytic pocket of tetracycline (X4),, thereby inhibiting its biological activity in vitro. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for future optimization of the structure of mefloquine to increase its inhibitory efficacy, contributing to the development of novel antibiotic adjuvants. The screening of the commercial drug library revealed multiple tetracycline synergists, with mefloquine being the sole compound that enhanced tetracycline antibiotic efficacy in all in vitro synergistic antibacterial assays. This may pertain to the dual-targeting effect whereby mefloquine not only disrupts bacterial energy metabolism but also substantially influences the oxidoreductase activity of Tet(X4). Mefloquine has been previously confirmed to act against Mycobacterium avium and Mycobacterium tuberculosis infections [31, 32]. These findings suggest that mefloquine universally affects bacterial energy metabolism, potentially in therapeutic applications for various pathogenic illnesses [33].

Mefloquine hydrochloride is a widely utilized anti-malarial agent because of its good efficacy and safety profile [34]. Recent studies have revealed that it possesses multiple antibacterial mechanisms, showing significant activity particularly against Mycobacterium tuberculosis and some drug-resistant bacteria. This activity is directly related to its ability to inhibit bacterial ATP synthase. ATP synthase is a key enzyme in the energy metabolism of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, responsible for generating ATP, the cell’s primary energy currency. mefloquine can specifically inhibit the activity of ATP synthase, disrupt bacterial energy metabolism, and lead to bacterial death. Furthermore, mefloquine may inhibit efflux pumps, which are utilized by many drug-resistant pathogens to export antimicrobial agents out of cells. By inhibiting these pumps, mefloquine may enhance intracellular concentrations of both itself and other antimicrobial drugs, thereby overcoming certain forms of drug resistance [35]. Tetracyclines are important in the clinical management of bacterial infections [36]. However, there is a notable risk of side effects and structure-related toxicity. Mefloquine is contentious due to its psychological side effects, which include insomnia, abnormal dreams, anxiety, and depressed mood. Tetracyclines, such as doxycycline, are associated primarily with dyspepsia and photosensitivity [16, 37, 38]. The concurrent use of mefloquine and tetracyclines at reduced dosages may effectively mitigate negative effects in the treatment of resistant bacterial infections [38]. Mefloquine works synergistically with aminoglycoside drugs (such as geneticin G418, paromomycin, and hygromycin B) to promote the ribosome’s ability to ignore these premature termination codons and continue translating full-length functional proteins. Particularly valuable is that mefloquine demonstrates synergistic effects when combined with aminoglycoside drugs, allowing for the use of lower concentrations of aminoglycosides and thus reducing the risk of their well-known toxicity [39].

Consequently, to facilitate the prompt clinical application of this combination, additional dose regimens must be developed. Furthermore, the potential synergistic effects of mefloquine with various antibiotics and the efficacy of alternative modes of delivery beyond oral delivery need additional investigation.

In conclusion, our study revealed that mefloquine, sourced from commercial pharmaceuticals, demonstrated remarkable synergistic effects with tetracyclines against tetracycline-resistant microorganisms. This research not only identifies mefloquine as a synergist of tetracycline antibiotics but also proposes a novel, goal-oriented strategy for screening commercialized drugs to identify antibiotic synergists and elucidate the mechanisms underlying synergistic effects against drug-resistant bacteria.
