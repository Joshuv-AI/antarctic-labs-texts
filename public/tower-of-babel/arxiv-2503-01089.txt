Privacy-preserving Machine Learning in Internet of Vehicle Applications: Fundamentals, Recent Advances, and Future Directions
Nazmul Islam, Mohammad Zulkernine

Abstract
Machine learning (ML) in Internet of Vehicles (IoV) applications enhanced intelligent transportation, autonomous driving capabilities, and various connected services within a large, heterogeneous network. However, the increased connectivity and massive data exchange for ML applications introduce significant privacy challenges. Privacy-preserving machine learning (PPML) offers potential solutions to address these challenges by preserving privacy at various stages of the ML pipeline. Despite the rapid development of ML-based IoV applications and the growing data privacy concerns, there are limited comprehensive studies on the adoption of PPML within this domain. Therefore, this study provides a comprehensive review of the fundamentals, recent advancements, and the challenges of integrating PPML into IoV applications. We first review existing surveys of various PPML techniques and their integration into IoV across different scopes. We then categorize IoV applications into three key domains and analyze the privacy challenges in leveraging ML in these application domains. Building on these fundamentals, we review recent advancements in integrating various PPML techniques within IoV applications, discussing their frameworks, key features, and performance. Finally, we identify current challenges and propose future research directions to enhance privacy and reliability in IoV applications. Keywords: Autonomous driving, Deep learning, Intelligent transportation system, Internet of vehicles, Privacy preserving machine learning, Federated learning, Blockchain, Homomorphic encryption, Multi-party computations, Differential privacy



1
Introduction

The internet of vehicles (IoV) integrates advanced communication technologies and intelligent systems to establish a connected ecosystem of vehicles, infrastructure, and services. Leveraging real-time data, IoV systems can predict traffic patterns, optimize routes, and dynamically adjust traffic signals for improved transportation efficiency. For autonomous driving, these systems integrate advanced sensing and computer vision, enabling vehicles to perceive their surroundings, make informed decisions, and navigate safely without human intervention. Furthermore, IoV systems integrate with urban ecosystem infrastructure to enable smart services such as intelligent parking, dynamic electric vehicle (EV) charging, and personalized infotainment.

IoV inherently generates vast and diverse amounts of data from vehicles, infrastructure, pedestrians, and other sources in the ecosystem. Given the data-intensive nature of machine learning (ML) algorithms and their reliance on large-scale datasets for training, these data form an ideal foundation for ML applications
[
1
]
. This led to the rapid adoption of ML in IoV applications over the past decade. The integration of ML into IoV has significantly improved existing applications in the ecosystem, while also enabling new capabilities such as autonomous driving and smart EV charging. However, IoV data includes sensitive and private information such as location details, driving patterns, and personally identifiable information, raising significant privacy concerns regarding its collection, use, and potential misuse or breaches
[
1
,
2
]
.

In ML-based IoV applications, various attacks can compromise both the training and inference stages of the ML pipeline, as well as the data collection and storage processes
[
3
,
4
]
. During the training phase, attacks such as data poisoning, property inference, or Byzantine attacks, and in the inference phase, membership inference, model inversion, or model extraction attacks can undermine the privacy and integrity of both the model and its underlying data
[
4
,
5
]
. Moreover, attacks targeting communication channels, such as man-in-the-middle, can intercept or alter sensitive data, compromising data security and integrity. The involvement of third-party services in data collection, model training, or deployment introduces additional vulnerabilities, as mishandling of data or breaches at third-party entities can threaten the entire IoV ecosystem and compromise vehicle security and user privacy on a broader scale
[
6
]
.

To circumvent these privacy risks, advanced privacy-preserving ML (PPML) techniques such as federated learning (FL), homomorphic encryption (HE), secure multi-party computations (SMPC), and differential privacy (DP) are being thoroughly studied to protect user data and model integrity in the ML pipeline
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Furthermore, blockchain-based PPML (BC-PPML) enhances security and trust in distributed IoV systems while preserving privacy.

1.2
Scope and Contribution

Existing survey literature generally covered either a broad review of PPML technologies, security and privacy in IoV, or specific PPML techniques within a narrow application domain. The current study provides a comprehensive survey of PPML techniques in vast IoV applications. To achieve this, we first provide an overview of the IoV ecosystem with proposed architecture and then categorize the applications into three major domains: (1) intelligent transportation and traffic management, (2) autonomous driving and safety-critical applications, and (3) communication infrastructure and smart services. We then analyze the various data types within each domain and their associated privacy challenges. Following this, we review various PPML techniques and compare their performance and applicability. Building on these foundations, we review the recent advancements in the adoption of key PPML techniques within the three application domains focusing on their implementation and performance evaluation in terms of privacy, utility and efficiency. Finally, we identify gaps in current research and propose future directions for the integration of PPML in IoV.

The scope of this survey is privacy-preserving techniques employed within ML processes in IoV applications. Notably, our survey excludes studies adopting privacy-enhancing techniques such as data perturbation, anonymization, pseudonymization, k-anonymity, and others, unless they are specifically integrated into PPML for data privacy in IoV applications. Additionally, techniques related to authentication, authorization, and access control, aimed at securing user identities or access to data, are out of scope.

2
IoV Architecture, Applications and Privacy Challenges

This section provides the fundamentals of IoV architecture, various applications and their privacy challenges. IoV integrates real-time data acquisition, robust communication networks, intelligent data processing, and application-driven services into a cohesive framework. Various environmental and vehicular data are collected via sensors, actuators and edge devices, which are then transmitted through high-speed, low-latency networks supporting diverse vehicle-to-everything (V2X) interactions
[
30
,
31
]
. These data streams are processed through ML-based analytics, enabling real-time artificial intelligence (AI) applications for traffic management, autonomous driving or smart infrastructure and services.

2.1
IoV Architecture

The IoV architecture consists of multiple layers that work together among vehicles, infrastructure, users, cloud and other entities to enable seamless communication, data processing and applications. Based on the literature review and recent advances in IoV, we have proposed a four-layered IoV architecture illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
The hierarchical architecture of IoV in four layers.

The perception layer in the IoV architecture is responsible for collecting comprehensive data related to vehicles, the environment, and users from various sources, including vehicles, road side units (RSU), smart devices, and other connected data points within the network. The data is collected using different sensors and actuators such as global positioning system (GPS), cameras, ultrasonic sensors, light detection and ranging (LiDAR), accelerometers, radars and magnetometers. This layer is the principal source of all data within the IoV ecosystem
[
30
,
31
]
.

The communication layer is divided into V2X communication and in-vehicle communication. V2X enables vehicles to exchange information with other vehicles, infrastructure, pedestrians, and the network
[
30
]
. It comprises vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V), vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I), vehicle-to-network (V2N), and vehicle-to-pedestrian (V2P) communication. In-vehicle communication systems integrate both external (wireless) and internal (wired) networks to enable seamless data exchange. External network of V2X leverages communication standards to facilitate connectivity with systems such as the global navigation satellite system (GNSS), in-vehicle infotainment (IVI), and advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS). Internal networks enable bus-level communication among electronic control units (ECUs), sensors, controllers, and actuators within the vehicle
[
31
]
. Collectively, these communication networks ensure reliable and high-speed data transfer across diverse subsystems.

The data processing and coordination layer is responsible for efficient handling of vast amounts of data generated in IoV. This layer integrates advanced technologies including cloud computing for scalable data storage, big data analytics for extracting meaningful insights, and data management systems for ensuring data integrity and accessibility. In some IoV architectures, the data management layer functions as a centralized information management hub, facilitating the storage, processing, and analysis of data received from lower layers, other architectures employ a more distributed approach with edge and cloud layers to optimize latency, bandwidth, and scalability. Additionally, this layer incorporates communication control and management services, enforcing policies such as load balancing and quality of service (QoS) management.

The application layer leverages the vast amounts of data collected and processed by the lower layers to enable intelligent applications and services. This layer utilizes advanced ML and DL algorithms to enable a wide range of intelligent IoV applications. Various AI-based IoV applications are discussed in the following section.

2.2
IoV Application Domains

The IoV is a rapidly evolving ecosystem encompassing diverse applications that can be broadly categorized into three key domains: (1) intelligent transportation and traffic management, (2) autonomous driving and safety-critical applications, and (3) communication infrastructure and smart services. Each domain leverages advanced technologies such as AI, the Internet of Things (IoT), V2X communications, and distributed computing with edge-cloud collaboration.

2.2.1
Intelligent Transportation and Traffic Management

Intelligent transportation system (ITS) encompasses a wide range of applications designed to enhance safety, efficiency, and sustainability in transportation
[
29
]
. AI-based computer vision applications, such as object detection, play a pivotal role in ITS by accurately detecting and classifying vehicles, pedestrians, and other road users in real time
[
32
]
. This technology enables advanced collision prevention systems that predict and mitigate potential accidents, enhancing road safety. Traffic management is another critical aspect of ITS, where AI algorithms analyze real time traffic data to optimize traffic flow, adaptively adjust traffic signals, and provide accurate vehicle positioning utilizing advanced AI algorithms. Additionally, smart public transportation systems utilize AI to optimize routes, predict passenger demand, and improve overall efficiency. Furthermore, AI-driven applications, such as pothole prediction, road damage assessment, and driver misbehavior detection, contribute to enhanced road safety and infrastructure maintenance.

2.2.2
Autonomous Driving and Safety-critical Applications

Connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs) integrate advanced communication systems and AI with autonomous driving capabilities to perceive, understand, and navigate complex road environments with minimal human intervention
[
27
]
. AI algorithms process and interpret vast amounts of data in real time for tasks such as object recognition, trajectory prediction, route planning, and cooperative decision-making
[
33
]
. CAVs rely on these AI-based perception systems to detect and classify obstacles, pedestrians, and other vehicles by fusing data from multiple sensors and actuators facilitating autonomous driving, while ensuring safety and reliability. Safety-critical applications such as emergency braking or collision avoidance systems are designed to address scenarios requiring high reliability and precision to avoid catastrophic outcomes. Examples include emergency braking systems, collision avoidance mechanisms, and fail-safe operations during sensor or system malfunctions
[
32
,
33
]
. AI plays a pivotal role in these applications by enabling rapid anomaly detection, redundancy management, and failover protocols to maintain operational safety. Furthermore, reliable communication and coordination between CAVs and other entities through V2X communication are essential for enhancing situational awareness and enabling cooperative decision-making using advanced AI systems.

2.2.3
Communication Infrastructure and Smart Services

Communication infrastructure and smart services integrate physical and digital systems to enable intelligent, connected, and automated transportation solutions. A key aspect of smart infrastructure is the charging network for electric vehicles (EVs)
[
34
]
. AI algorithms optimize charging schedules, predict energy demand, and facilitate smart grid integration, ensuring efficient and sustainable charging operations. Most charging stations (CSs) are strategically located near parking spots to enhance accessibility and convenience. AI-driven parking management systems form another vital component of smart services, leveraging sensors, cameras, and real-time data analytics to guide drivers and CAVs to available parking spots, optimize parking space utilization, and streamline automated payment processes
[
35
]
. Smart services also offer personalized user experiences, such as infotainment systems to passengers
[
36
]
or personalized route planning for optimized navigation recommendations. Predictive maintenance is another critical application which leverages AI models to analyze sensor data from vehicles and infrastructure to predict potential failures or optimize maintenance schedules, thereby improving vehicle reliability and assess risk profiles for insurance services
[
37
]
.

2.3
Data Usage and Privacy Challenges

The IoV relies on diverse data types to enable AI-based downstream applications, enhancing transportation efficiency, safety, and user experience. Common data types in IoV and their privacy challenges are discussed in this section.

2.3.1
Data Usage in IoV Applications

The IoV leverages various data types to enhance vehicle functionality, user experience, and the integration of smart services. We have categorized the data types that are commonly required for IoV applications. Personal data tracks user travel patterns, frequented locations, and contact information, enabling hands-free connectivity for improved operation and convenience. Biometric data, such as facial recognition and fingerprint scanning, enhances security and personalizes vehicle access and operation
[
38
]
. Behavioral data analyzes driving habits, including speed, braking, and route choices, optimizing safety, enabling applications such as autonomous driving and predictive maintenance
[
37
]
. Vehicle operation data continuously monitors metrics like engine status and fuel efficiency, supporting reliability, autonomy and preemptive service. Environmental data on weather, road conditions, and cabin climate improve comfort and driving experience
[
39
]
. Multimedia and connectivity data store user preferences for navigation, media, and in-vehicle settings while supporting seamless hands-free operations for safety and convenience
[
36
]
. Road condition and traffic data, collected through vehicle sensors and RSUs, assess traffic flow and pavement quality, providing real-time updates for traffic management, autonomous driving, and road maintenance. Furthermore, IoV requires infrastructure and grid data for EV charging coordination, smart parking, and energy allocation, resource optimization and sustainability.

Among the three key domains, intelligent transportation and traffic management mostly leverages personal, environmental and traffic data to enable advanced navigation, real-time traffic management, hazard warnings and collision prevention
[
32
]
. Autonomous driving and safety-critical applications depend on all types of personal, behavioral, biometric, operational, road and environmental data to support autonomous driving, secure access, adaptive safety systems, advanced navigation and route optimization
[
32
,
33
]
. Meanwhile, communication infrastructure and smart services mostly utilizes multimedia, infrastructural, environmental, operational and energy data to facilitate smart parking
[
35
]
, charging coordination, predictive maintenance
[
39
]
, and personalized infotainment
[
36
]
.

2.3.2
Privacy Challenges in IoV Applications

The adoption of AI in IoV applications raises privacy concerns due to the large amounts of data collected, transmitted and stored from vehicles, passengers, infrastructure, and pedestrians
[
1
,
2
]
. A primary challenge is the exposure of Personally Identifiable Information (PII), including location, driving patterns, and biometric data. Vehicle-specific data, such as make, model, and usage, can also increase the risk of targeted attacks
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Data security is another critical challenge, as the transmission and storage of sensitive data create vulnerabilities that may lead to breaches, exposing both individual and aggregate information
[
7
,
8
,
16
]
. Furthermore, unauthorized access to models can compromise privacy, as AI algorithms may inadvertently expose personal details through inference, even when data is anonymized.

In the ML pipeline of an IoV application, privacy risks can arise during the training and inference phases to compromise the confidentiality and integrity of the system
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. In the training phase, data poisoning attacks involve adversaries manipulating training data to compromise model integrity or insert backdoors, a significant risk in IoV where data quality is crucial for safety. Property inference attacks allow malicious participants to extract statistical properties about other models/users private training data, potentially exposing vehicle patterns or user behaviors. Byzantine attacks (common in distributed learning scenarios) involve malicious vehicles or data points injecting false gradients during training, destabilizing the model impacting its efficiency and reliability.

During the inference phase, privacy risks are amplified by attacks like membership inference, model inversion, and model extraction
[
4
]
. Membership inference attacks enable adversaries to determine whether specific vehicle data was used in training, potentially revealing sensitive patterns. Model inversion attacks aim to reconstruct sensitive training data from model parameters, risking exposure to personal vehicle information, such as location histories. Model extraction attacks involve attempts to steal the functionality of the model through carefully crafted queries, potentially compromising proprietary vehicle behavior models
[
4
,
20
]
.

The involvement of third-party services for data collection, training, or model deployment further exacerbates privacy concerns. Third parties can mishandle data, expose sensitive information, or become targets of separate breaches, jeopardizing the entire ecosystem. If adversaries access third-party data or training pipelines, they could launch targeted attacks that affect all connected vehicles using the compromised system. Data breaches within third-party services could lead to the loss of proprietary vehicle models or private data, compromising user privacy and vehicle security on a large scale
[
7
,
8
,
16
]
. Additionally, man-in-the-middle or other interception attacks on communication channels between vehicles and third-party infrastructures can intercept or alter data, threatening privacy and security of IoV applications.

3
Privacy-preserving Machine Learning

Privacy-preserving machine learning (PPML) integrates various privacy-preserving techniques into ML pipelines to mitigate privacy risks in collaborative learning scenarios. As IoV systems handle vast amounts of sensitive and private data, the need for PPML solutions has become increasingly critical on both the user and server sides.

3.1
PPML Overview

The focus of this study is on three distinct types of PPML approaches integrated into IoV application domains. Firstly, the architecture-based PPML approach that represent distributed architectural solutions of federated learning (FL) and blockchain-based PPML (BC-PPML). FL enables privacy-preserving distributed learning, while BC-PPML adds an additional layer of transparency and accountability to the ML process, ensuring secure model updates and verification. Secondly, the data processing-based PPML methods that include homomorphic encryption (HE) and garbled circuits, where HE enables computation on encrypted data and garbled circuits facilitate secure multi-party computation (SMPC), protecting intermediate calculations during training. Finally, the data publishing approach of differential privacy (DP) which introduces statistical noise to protect individual privacy while maintaining useful aggregate information for training.

PPML operates across three main phases: model generation, model training, and model serving
[
4
]
. During model generation, privacy protection is implemented in the initial development and architecture design stages. Model training incorporates privacy-preserving techniques throughout the learning process, while model serving ensures privacy during deployment and inference stages, protecting both user queries and model outputs. The privacy guarantees in PPML can be categorized into two main categories
[
4
]
. Object-oriented guarantees focus on protecting specific components, including data-oriented protection for raw training data and user inputs, and model-oriented protection for model parameters and architecture. Pipeline-oriented guarantees are generic end-to-end privacy protection across the entire ML workflow. Table
2

3.2
PPML Techniques

This section discusses the fundamentals of the PPML techniques covered in the study, including their mathematical foundations and types. It also discusses how these techniques are applied in the context of ML-based IoV applications.

3.2.1
Federated Learning

FL is a PPML technique that enables training models on distributed datasets without directly sharing raw data. This method not only enhances data privacy but also addresses challenges associated with data heterogeneity and communication efficiency
[
4
]
. As illustrated in Figure
3
, FL can be categorized into two main types, centralized FL (CFL) and decentralized FL (DFL)
[
16
]
. In CFL, participants (also known as clients or nodes) train models locally using their data and send the updates (usually model gradients or parameters) to the central server. The server refines the model by aggregating the updates using various algorithms and then sends back the updated global model to clients for further training, inference, or deployment. Two popular aggregation algorithms are federated averaging (FedAvg) and federated distillation
[
16
]
. FedAvg performs weighted averaging of client updates for data aggregation at the server side using:

\theta^{(t+1)}=\sum_{v\in V}\frac{n_{v}^{(t)}}{n^{(t)}}\theta_{v}^{(t+1)}

(1)

Figure 3:
Basic illustration of centralized FL (left) and decentralized FL (right) structure in IoV.

where,
\theta_{v}^{(t+1)}
are the updated parameters from vehicle
v
, and
n_{v}^{(t)}
is the size of its local dataset. On the client side, the objective function for the local training of the
v
th vehicle can be expressed as:

f_{v}(\theta_{v})=E_{z_{v}\sim D_{v}}\left[l(\theta_{v},z_{v})\right]

(2)

where
\theta_{v}
are the model parameters,
z_{v}
is a data sample from the local dataset
D_{v}
, and
l(\theta_{v},z_{v})
is the loss function.

FedAvg falls short in real-world heterogeneous data environments, since a single global model may not suit the individual vehicles, and frequent local updates can result in divergence from the global objective. Knowledge distillation overcomes the challenges of model heterogeneity and data heterogeneity by allowing different clients to use varied model architectures (by exchanging logits instead of model parameters), and mitigating non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) issues in data respectively. In knowledge distillation the knowledge (typically in the form of logits) is transferred from a complex teacher model to simpler student models. In this process, each client trains its local model with its data and generates logit outputs, which are shared with a central server. The server aggregates these logits to form a global logit, representing the distilled knowledge from the teacher model. Clients then use this global logit to train their local models, aligning the student’s outputs with the teacher’s soft targets. The student is trained using linear combination of loss functions:

L=(1-\lambda)L_{CE}(q_{s},y)+\lambda L_{KL}\left(q_{s}^{\tau},q_{T}^{\tau}\right)

(3)

where
L_{CE}
is cross-entropy loss between the student’s predictions
q_{s}
and true labels
y
.
L_{KL}
is Kullback-Leibler divergence between the student’s soft targets
q_{s}^{\tau}
and the teacher’s soft targets
q_{T}^{\tau}
,
\lambda
is a hyperparameter that balances the two loss components.
\tau
is a temperature parameter to smooth the probability distribution produced by SoftMax.

While CFL is effective in various scenarios, it has limitations such as a single point of failure, bottlenecks at the server, and various privacy risks associated with centralized data aggregation
[
16
]
. DFL addresses these limitations by eliminating the need for a central server. Instead, each client trains its local model independently and exchanges model updates with its peers (participating nodes). This enhances fault tolerance, robustness against single points of failure and reduces trust dependencies on a central entity. In DFL, achieving consensus among participating nodes is crucial to ensure coherent model updates and reflect the contributions of all
[
16
]
. A common approach to achieve consensus involves using a mixing matrix
w_{ij}(t)
, where each element
w_{ij}
represents the weight of communication between node
i
and node
j
. The update rule for each peer’s model is:

\theta_{i}(t+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{ij}(t)\theta_{j}(t)

(4)

The number of nodes is given by
n
and
\theta(t)
represents the state of a node at time
t
. The matrix ensures that each peer’s update considers information from its neighbors, weighted by the strength of their connection. This balances contributions from different nodes and achieves consensus without a central coordinator. The communication among peers can be organized based on different network topologies, such as fully connected networks, partially connected networks, or clustered networks. The choice of topology affects the overall privacy, robustness, flexibility, fault tolerance, and communication costs of the network
[
16
]
.

3.2.2
Blockchain-based PPML

The decentralized and cryptographic nature of BC technology addresses several privacy concerns inherent in traditional, centralized ML architectures. BC provides a decentralized ledger that records transactions securely and immutably. This ledger is maintained across multiple nodes, ensuring there is no single point of failure and no single entity has control over the entire dataset, thus preserving privacy. BC also employs various cryptographic techniques to secure data. Each transaction or data entry on the BC is hashed and linked to previous entries, forming an immutable chain. This ensures that once data is recorded, it cannot be altered without consensus from the network, providing robustness against tampering and unauthorized modifications
[
26
]
.

BC consists of several key layers. The data layer stores transaction data using cryptographic methods for data integrity and confidentiality. The network layer enables peer-to-peer communication for secure data broadcasting and authentication. The consensus layer employs lightweight algorithms such as practical byzantine fault tolerance (PBFT) or directed acyclic graphs (DAG) to achieve agreement on transaction validity without energy-intensive mining processes
[
26
]
. Lastly, the application layer supports various IoV applications by utilizing smart contracts to automate processes, prevent unauthorized interventions and ensure secure operations.

BC technology can be combined with other privacy-preserving techniques to further enhance privacy. BC supports pseudonymity by allowing users to interact with the system through pseudonymous addresses rather than real identities. This feature is crucial for PPML, as it prevents the exposure of participants’ identities during data transactions and model training processes. Advanced cryptographic techniques such as zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP) and HE integrated into BC systems also significantly enhance data privacy. ZKPs allow one party to prove to another that a statement is true without revealing any information beyond the validity of the statement itself. This can be particularly useful in ML for verifying computations or model parameters without exposing underlying data. Furthermore, BC can enhance FL by providing a secure and decentralized framework for aggregating model updates from different parties. It can also introduce incentive mechanisms to encourage honest participation and fair behavior
[
26
]
.

3.2.3
Homomorphic Encryption

PPML utilizes HE to maintain data confidentiality during the training and inference phases of ML pipeline. HE allows computations to be performed on encrypted data without needing to decrypt it, preserving privacy of sensitive user data.
HE=(KeyGen,Enc,Dec,Eval)
, is composed of four probabilistic polynomial-time algorithms and they are defined as follows. Key generation (
HE_{KeyGen}
) takes a security parameter
\lambda
as input and outputs a public key (
pk
), a secret key (
sk
), and an evaluation key (
ek
), represented as
(pk,sk,ek)\leftarrow HE_{KeyGen}(\lambda)
. Encryption (
HE_{Enc}
) involves taking the public key
pk
and a plaintext message
m
as inputs to produce a ciphertext
c
, expressed as
c\leftarrow HE_{Enc}(pk,m)
. Decryption (
HE_{Dec}
) requires the secret key
sk
and the ciphertext
c
as inputs to output the decrypted message
m^{*}
, denoted as
m^{*}\leftarrow HE_{Dec}(sk,c)
. Evaluation (
HE_{Eval}
) takes the evaluation key
ek
, a function
f
, and a series of ciphertexts
c_{0},\ldots,c_{l-1}
as inputs. Here, each ciphertext
c_{i}
corresponds to a plaintext
m_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,l-1
, where
l
is the number of ciphertexts. The evaluation outputs a final ciphertext
c_{fin}
, expressed as
c_{fin}\leftarrow HE_{Eval}(ek,f,c_{0},\ldots,c_{l-1})
such that
HE_{Dec}(sk,c_{fin})=f(m_{0},\ldots,m_{l-1})
. The function
f
represents an operational circuit over the plaintext space.

HE can be broadly categorized into three types: partial HE, somewhat (or strong) homomorphic, and Fully HE (FHE)
[
40
]
. Partially HE supports unlimited operations of a single type (such as addition or multiplication). For instance, the Rivest-Shamir-Adleman (RSA) cryptosystem allows multiplication and the Paillier cryptosystem supports addition on encrypted data without decryption. The Boneh–Goh–Nissim (BGN) cryptosystem is well-known for supporting both addition and multiplication but only allows a limited number of operations, making it somewhat HE. The first FHE scheme was proposed using lattice-based cryptography, which introduced the concept of bootstrapping to refresh ciphertexts and manage noise accumulation
[
40
]
. This has led to schemes based on lattice theory like learning with errors (LWE) and ring-LWE, as well as those based on approximating the greatest common divisor
[
40
]
.

FHE is a powerful tool for PPML because it allows for arbitrary computations on encrypted data, ensuring that both training and inference processes can be conducted securely. For instance, consider a simple linear model represented by the equation
y=w^{t}x+b
where
w
is the weight vector,
x
is the input feature vector, and
b
is the bias term. In this context, FHE enables each component to be encrypted: the input as
E(x)
, the weights as
E(w)
, and the bias as
E(b)
. The model can then compute the encrypted output as
E(y)=E(w^{t}x+b)=E(w^{t}x)+E(b)
. This preserves data privacy throughout the ML computation process. Despite various advances, high computation and complexity associated with HE still persist when integrating in PPML
[
40
]
.

3.2.4
Secure Multi-party Computing

The goal of SMPC is to create a secure protocol that allows multiple participants
P_{i}
, where
i=1,\ldots,m
to jointly compute a function
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
based on their private inputs
x_{i}

[
9
]
. Each participant
P_{i}
should receive only their respective output
y_{i}
, without gaining extra knowledge. This ensures input privacy and correctness, where no information about individual participants can be inferred beyond what the output reveals, and even if some parties collude or deviate from the protocol, they cannot impose an incorrect result on honest parties
[
4
]
. SMPC can leverage several cryptographic techniques such as secret sharing and secure computation to further enhance privacy
[
9
]
. In secret sharing data is divided into shares and distributed among participants, ensuring that only specific combinations of these shares can reconstruct the original data. This ensures that no single party has access to the complete dataset, maintaining privacy across the board. In secure schemes (like HE), computations are performed on encrypted data enabling privacy throughout the lifecycle
[
9
]
.

In the context of PPML, SMPC allows for collaborative model training where multiple parties can jointly train using their private datasets as shown in Figure
4
. SMPC also supports secure inference, enabling parties to perform model inference while ensuring that neither the model owner nor the input provider learns about information more than necessary. Furthermore, SMPC can be applied to feature selection, allowing parties to collaboratively determine important features for a model without exposing raw feature values, enhancing model accuracy while preserving privacy.

3.2.5
Differential Privacy

Figure 5:
Illustration of centralized DP (left) and local DP (right) for IoV applications.

DP provides a mathematical guarantee that individual data points cannot be inferred from the output of a model
[
12
,
23
]
. It achieves this by introducing controlled randomness into the data analysis process, ensuring that the inclusion or exclusion of any single data point does not significantly affect the outcome. In distributed ML, DP helps protect user data by adding noise to the model updates before they are aggregated. DP operates on the principle that a privacy-preserving mechanism should produce outputs that are statistically indistinguishable whether or not any single client data is included
[
12
,
23
]
. A mechanism
M
satisfies
\varepsilon
-DP if, for any two neighboring datasets
d
and
d^{\prime}
and for any possible output subset
S
, the probability
\Pr
that
M
produces an output in
S
is nearly the same for both datasets:

\Pr\left[M(d)\in S\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr\left[M(d^{\prime})\in S\right]

(5)

Here,
\varepsilon
is a non-negative parameter that quantifies the privacy loss. Smaller values of
\varepsilon
indicate stronger privacy.

The noise or controlled randomness in DP is often drawn from a Laplacian distribution, which is determined by the sensitivity of the function being computed. Sensitivity, denoted as
\Delta_{p}Q
, measures how much the output can change when a client’s data is added or removed:

\Delta_{p}Q=\max_{d,d^{\prime}}\left\|Q(d)-Q(d^{\prime})\right\|_{p}

(6)

where the maximum is taken over all neighboring datasets
d
and
d^{\prime}
;
p=1
is used for the Laplace mechanism and
p=2
for the Gaussian mechanism.

Noise drawn from a Laplace distribution is typically added to the function’s output to obscure individual contributions:

M(d)=Q(d)+\mathrm{Laplace}\left(0,\frac{\Delta_{1}Q}{\varepsilon}\right)

(7)

here,
Q(d)
represents the true query result, and Laplace noise ensures that individual contributions are obscured. A relaxed version known as
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP allows for a small probability
\delta
that the strict privacy guarantee might be violated:

\Pr\left[M(d)\in S\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr\left[M(d^{\prime})\in S\right]+\delta

(8)

This relaxation provides more flexibility in designing mechanisms with privacy gurantee tradeoff. The Gaussian mechanism, which scales noise according to the L2 norm, is commonly used:

M(d)=Q(d)+N\left(0,\frac{2\ln(1.25/\delta)\left(\Delta_{2}Q\right)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

(9)

where
N(0,2\ln(1.25/\delta)(\Delta_{2}Q)^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
represents Gaussian noise with variance calibrated to satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, scaled according to sensitivity. This calibration holds for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
; larger values of
\varepsilon
require a different noise calibration.

As illustrated in Figure
5
, DP can be broadly categorized into two main approaches, centralized and local DP. These techniques can be implemented through various mechanisms in DL, including adding random noise to input samples, gradients, or functions during the training process
[
12
,
23
]
. Implementing DP involves balancing privacy and utility. More noise generally implies better privacy but degrades model accuracy. Additionally, managing cumulative privacy loss over multiple queries (the privacy budget) is crucial to maintaining robust privacy guarantees over time.

4.1.1
Federated Learning

FL is increasingly being utilized in intelligent transportation systems (ITS) to enhance privacy during downstream traffic management tasks such as mobility flow prediction (traffic or crowd), travel time estimation, traffic signal control and traffic status identification. Traditional ML models face challenges like data privacy risks, long training times, and high communication costs. FL mitigates these by enhancing privacy while improving computational and communication efficiency. FL for traffic flow prediction proposed in

]
is a potential solution to this which uses FL and GNN for traffic flow prediction that protects graph topological data privacy. Another development for traffic flow prediction is the ability to handle heterogeneous spatial characteristics across participants using vertical FL (VFL)
[

]
. The authors in

]
introduced a federated graph attention layer to preserve spatial information while capturing short-term temporal features. This allows participants with different spatial attributes to contribute to the global model training without privacy exposure.

]
, an improved FedAvg that uses random subsampling of participants has helped reduce communication costs by limiting the number of participants in each round. Additionally, clustering techniques based on geographical proximity or model similarity have been applied in

]

[

]

In addition to traffic prediction, FL is increasingly being utilized in various smart traffic management applications, such as crowd flow prediction, and traffic signal control. The authors in
[
47

proposed an enhanced FL framework that integrates clustering algorithms to manage human trajectory data. Their method improved both the efficiency and accuracy of FL-based crowd mobility predictions. ]
. This framework facilitates joint learning over vertically partitioned data from multiple clients, without compromising sensitive data.

Traffic signal control is another key area where FL has been highly effective. Optimization of traffic signal control

]
has utilized various ML techniques, however, these approaches do not account for hybrid traffic flow or vehicular control and coordination models. [

]

Accurate vehicle positioning is essential for enhancing road safety and reducing traffic congestion. The authors in

employed FL to analyze high-resolution remote sensing images to enhance traffic status identification and congestion monitoring systems while ensuring user privacy. Blockchain-based PPML

Various studies have integrated BC into ML for privacy-preserving ITS applications, including traffic management, traffic congestion control, traffic flow prediction, and destination prediction. For traffic management, authors in
[
60
]
proposed a two-level privacy protection framework incorporating smart contract (SC)-based proof-of-work (PoW) to validate data integrity and counteract data poisoning threats and LSTM-autoencoder (LSTM-AE) to transform data into a secure, encoded format, mitigating inference attacks. Authors in
[
61
]
proposed a BC-based model designed to estimate traffic congestion probabilities while preserving privacy. In
[
62
]
a BC-enabled FL framework was proposed for urban traffic flow management, enhancing security by verifying model updates through miners to prevent malicious vehicles and reduce data poisoning. It also counteracts inference attacks using local DP (LDP) in the gradients. In another study
[
63
]
, BFRT was introduced as a decentralized method for real-time traffic flow prediction. It uses permissioned BC technology to protect vehicle privacy while ensuring accurate predictions. A bi-level BC architecture was developed for secure FL-based traffic prediction in
[
64
]
. It used a distributed HE federated averaging (DHFA) approach to secure the federated process and connect decentralized model validation.

Similar to traffic flow prediction, the authors in
[
100
]
proposed a BC-integrated FL framework for destination prediction, addressing user and location privacy concerns. Besides specific IoV applications, BC-based FL can enhance the fairness and privacy of the ML frameworks. In
[
65
]
, a decentralized architecture ensured privacy and fairness through peer-to-peer (P2P) collaboration. It employs a three-layer encryption combining DP, BC, and HE to protect data and improve accuracy, along with a credibility evaluation system to promote fairness and incentivize participants.

In collaborative ML applications, establishing trust among participants and ensuring the credibility of task publishers is crucial. The authors in
[
66
]
proposed a hierarchical trust evaluation strategy for 5G-ITS, using a heterogeneous BC and ML. It assessed trustworthiness through three levels, with rewards or penalties, and reassessed trust after task completion. In
[
67
]
, many-to-one matching model (based on reputation) was proposed to enable secure and efficient assignment of tasks. Authors in
[
68
]
, proposed a two-tier BC architecture for FL to improve security and efficiency using local and global model update chains. Privacy preservation was obtained through task fragmentation, decentralized reputation management, and incentives, ensuring minimal data sharing and motivating honest participation.

To reconcile large-scale traffic-data aggregation with individual privacy, Chen et al.
[
69
]
embedded ZKPs into a consortium BC for C-V2X, letting vehicles certify the validity of their telemetry without exposing the underlying values. Addressing the coexistence of internal-combustion and electric fleets, Dong et al.
[
70
]
coupled a consortium-BC speed advisory system with Paillier homomorphic aggregation, allowing the base station to compute carbon-optimal recommendations directly on encrypted emission data submitted by vehicles. A tunable coordination factor further lets policymakers rebalance optimization weight between direct and indirect emissions as grid carbon intensity varies across regions and time. Wang et al.
[
71
]
combined asynchronous federated aggregation with a BC-based dynamic-grouping PBFT consensus for multi-vehicle trajectory prediction, so that locally trained GRU-based models can be validated and fused across RSUs without exposing raw sensor histories. The resulting framework is shown to remain markedly more robust to gradient-tampering attacks than a non-BC federated counterpart. To secure hierarchical intelligence in IoV without relying on a trusted central server,
[
72
]
designed a fully decentralized BC-FL framework in which base stations perform validation-based local selection while a two-tier microblock and keyblock structure coordinates global consensus. The scheme sustains strong recognition performance on the GTSRB benchmark even under adversarial label-flipping and partial base-station outages, addressing the single point of failure inherent to conventional BC-FL integrations.

Authors in
[
73
]
targeted the tension between immutable accident records and the "right to be forgotten" by designing a decentralized attribute-based chameleon hash that lets multiple authorities jointly authorize block- and transaction-level redactions on a permissioned ledger. Since outsourcing raw crowdsourcing task metadata to a public ledger risks severe privacy exposure, Yang et al.
[
74
]
devised a constant-length ciphertext attribute-based encryption scheme that keeps on-chain access-list storage proportional only to the number of task requesters rather than every authorization pair. A hybrid searchable-encryption matching protocol further supports keyword and range queries over encrypted tasks, closing a gap left by earlier BC crowdsourcing frameworks limited to single-keyword search. Given the semi-trusted nature of roadside units in vehicular edge networks, Li et al.
[
75
]
paired FHE with a BC-recorded offloading ledger, and introduced a joint cost metric spanning energy, delay, and privacy exposure risk to steer task placement decisions. A self-learning potential-game algorithm then lets vehicles independently converge on dependable offloading strategies without relying on a central coordinator, outperforming heuristic and centralized RL baselines.

4.1.3
Homomorphic Encryption

HE has been applied for IoV applications such as traffic flow prediction, travel time estimation, and traffic signal optimization. Study
[
76
]
used HE to secure model parameters, preventing inference attacks for traffic flow prediction. A prior study
[
77
]
introduced a privacy-preserving aggregation scheme for vehicular fog computing using homomorphic threshold cryptosystems. While these studies analyzed security against honest-but-curious fog servers and dishonest users, they did not address advanced inference attacks or evaluate the reliability of secure model updates. In
[
78
]
, BC, elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC), and FHE were used for secure, reliable model updates and privacy-preserving data transmission. Study
[
64
]
used HE for privacy preservation and DHFA for secure aggregation of encrypted local updates. It also featured a partial private key distribution protocol and a partial HE/decryption scheme for robust privacy protection. To harden parameter aggregation against reverse inference and poisoning in heterogeneous connected-vehicle fleets, another study
[
79
]
combined multi-key partially HE with secret sharing and a Gaussian noise mechanism so that the server can filter corrupted updates without accessing plaintext gradients.

To address the privacy and security concerns associated with real-time traffic and vehicle data from a large number of participants,
[
80
]
proposed a fog-based vehicle crowd sensing (FBVC) architecture using HE. It features a two-tier fog framework with static upper-tier and dynamic lower-tier fog nodes, supporting secure data fusion, traceability, and integrity. Similarly,
[
81
]
presented a privacy-preserving data sharing scheme for IoV, using modified Paillier cryptosystem to ensure location privacy and secure data aggregation. Proxy re-encryption enabled secure edge querying, providing strong privacy and resistance to collusion attacks. Additionally,
[
82
]
introduced a decentralized location privacy-preserving scheme for spatial crowdsourcing using BC and HE. It also incorporated order-preserving encryption and non-interactive ZKP to prevent location forgery.

4.1.4
Secure Multi-party Computing

IoV applications such as traffic navigation, traffic signal control, and routing services with location privacy, leverage SMPC to protect sensitive vehicular data. The authors in
[
83
]
proposed EPNS, a privacy-preserving IoV solution using SMPC and multiparty delegated computation (MPDC). It employs two non-colluding servers – one for re-encryption and another for computation – to prevent data leakage even if one is compromised. A traffic signal control framework for connected autonomous vehicles (CAV) was proposed in
[
84
]
. The framework integrates SMPC and DP to protect CAV data against three types of privacy threats: collusion attacks, control center database attacks, and inference attacks. The framework used a linear programming model and arrival rate estimator to ensure efficiency in varying traffic scenarios. A two-stage stochastic programming model mitigates DP-induced noise impacts on performance.

For cycle-based arrival profile estimation drawn from multiple competing mobility companies,
[
85
]
cast the problem as a matrix completion task in which additive secret sharing and Laplace perturbation jointly protect each company’s connected-vehicle trajectories during co-reconstruction. To enable driver-assistance systems to anticipate lane changes without exposing surrounding vehicle telemetry,
[
86
]
deployed a two-server SMPC protocol, including Beaver-triple multiplication and a secure tanh activation, around a recurrent neural network trained on the HighD dataset. Experiments show that encrypted inference reproduces plaintext accuracy almost exactly, though runtime remains bottlenecked by the least capable participating server. In the absence of a trusted broker for coordinating platoons across competing logistics carriers,
[
87
]
developed a real-time coordination framework in which distributed additive secret sharing and gossip-based averaging let carriers jointly optimize hub waiting times without disclosing itinerary information to peers or third parties.

While SMPC-based cryptographic protocols provide privacy-preserving computation, leveraging distributed edge architectures can enhance scalability and computational efficiency. The SPEED framework in
[
88
]
distributed data processing across edge nodes, reducing centralized attack risks. It also used compressed sensing, data shuffling, and split computation to minimize raw data exposure. Authors in
[
89
]
proposed a privacy-preserving decentralized routing service using SMPC and DP. It combined additive and Shamir secret sharing to protect location data and the DP Laplace mechanism to enhance privacy in sparse data scenarios, reducing risks of tracing individual trajectories.

4.1.5
Differential Privacy

In ML-based IoV applications DP has been adopted in traffic flow prediction, real-time traffic monitoring, trajectory prediction and location privacy. Study
[
90
]
combined FL and LDP for privacy-preserved traffic flow prediction. Their method applies Gaussian noise to the gradients of an LSTM model before sharing them with a central server for aggregation, preventing inference attacks. Furthermore, predicting and managing traffic flow effectively requires robust monitoring systems. Authors in
[
91
]
presented a distributed traffic monitoring system that protects individual privacy using DP. They introduce three algorithms with different trade-offs between noise and computation, with the third approach providing the most balanced performance.

Besides traffic management, location privacy is crucial for protecting personal identification and routes. Authors in
[
62
]
applied LDP to gradient updates in a BC-enabled FL framework for traffic flow prediction, protecting against location-revealing inference attacks. For EV location data in vehicle-to-grid (V2G) networks, a privacy-preserving mechanism using DP, quadtree spatial decomposition, and Bernoulli random sampling was proposed in
[
92
]
. This approach effectively protected location privacy while minimizing relative errors in data utility. Study in
[
93
]
developed a DP data streaming system for CAV networks. It addressed the challenges of data correlation and dynamic network topology by implementing group-based data compression and adaptive noise addition. Study
[
94
]
confronted the residual leakage risk that persists in federated trajectory prediction despite avoiding raw-data sharing, by injecting Gaussian differential-privacy noise into locally trained parameters before they reach the aggregation server.

For communication with roadside units for traffic management, rather than assuming a fully trusted roadside unit, study
[
95
]
proposed a location-publishing mechanism that limits how long any single vehicle can act as group leader, using directional thresholds and lifespan-based hopping to curb prolonged data collection under semi-trusted infrastructure. Study
[
96
]
noted that generative trajectory synthesis alone can still closely mirror real user movement, and responded with a two-layer pipeline in which a density-aware LSTM-GAN first substitutes real trajectories with synthetic ones before a personalized differential-privacy step perturbs points according to local density and distance from cluster cores. Comparisons against related baselines showed the added perturbation layer meaningfully reduced mutual-information leakage without sacrificing trajectory data utility.

4.2
Autonomous Driving and Safety-critical Applications

Autonomous driving and safety-critical applications require ultra-reliable and low-latency decision-making capabilities. These capabilities are powered by ML algorithms trained on vast datasets from in-vehicle communication and vehicle-to-everything (V2X) networks. PPML techniques are essential for ensuring privacy and mitigating adversarial attacks in autonomous driving systems, while also enabling efficient and reliable operation. We review the recent advances in the adoption of PPML techniques in this domain and the technical summary is given in Table

Connected autonomous vehicles rely heavily on real-time data processing and decision-making such as monitoring system to ensure safety and efficiency. HE integrated into in-vehicle monitoring and secure communication systems, enable remote data processing while preserving user and vehicle privacy. Authors in

Another study

To reconcile end-to-end confidentiality with the real-time demands of vehicular anomaly detection,
[

5.2
PPML Techniques

This section discusses the challenges and future directions of the PPML techniques in light of the throughput, adversarial, and heterogeneity challenges of realistic IoV deployment scenarios.

5.2.1
Federated Learning

Statistical heterogeneity must be resolved without a central arbiter capable of imposing a corrective view of the data. A 93-study review finds no non-IID benchmarking method generalizes across applications, and that more than half of surveyed studies still default to MNIST or CIFAR-10 regardless of their stated target, undermining the reported robustness claims
[
271
]
. IoV research showed some progress here. Early traffic-prediction FL treated heterogeneity as a secondary concern for a generic aggregation rule to absorb
[
41
,
44
]
, whereas recent studies work around it directly with personalized clustering with time-window training to absorb cross-region skew
[
58
]
, mixture-of-experts routing that synchronizes only the experts actually used
[
57
]
, and fuzzy-inference client trust scoring
[
59
]
. This is exactly the application-tailored, real-data-driven design
[
271
]
argues the wider field still lacks, but these mechanisms remain validated on curated regional datasets rather than the standardized benchmarks. Systems heterogeneity compounds the problem. A 6G-focused survey finds a common on-device task can require 2 GB of memory that many resource-constrained fleet devices cannot supply, and shows that FL’s threat surface itself shifts under the hierarchical and peer-to-peer architectures increasingly used to manage this heterogeneity
[
272
]
.

A largely separate challenge is that privacy and robustness defenses interact rather than compose. Poisoning can increase the effectiveness of gradient-inversion reconstruction, and colluding Sybil clients can bias a global model toward one participant’s data, improving a subsequent membership-inference attack
[
273
]
. This directly implicates the Byzantine detectors already reviewed in Section
4.2

[
112
,
114
,
160
]
, none of which is evaluated under simultaneous DP noise or secure aggregation. A benchmarking study spanning twelve widely used FL frameworks found that none of the frameworks implement geometric-median aggregation despite its documented robustness to poisoning
[
273
]
.

Future Direction:
Closing this gap is likely to require evaluating existing defenses jointly with the privacy mechanisms they would realistically be deployed alongside, rather than validating each in isolation, and complementing them with federated unlearning that lets a fleet operator revoke a poisoned or voluntarily withdrawn contribution without full retraining
[
271
,
272
]
. It is a capability that connects naturally to the privacy-aware federated RL formulations already emerging in the vehicular literature
[
274
]
.

5.2.2
Blockchain-based PPML

Table
2
already shows BC scoring strongly on auditability but only conditionally on confidentiality. Transaction-graph analysis can deanonymize pseudonymous participants without breaking any cryptography, and because the ledger is immutable, a deanonymization technique after deployment can retroactively unmask a participant’s entire history
[
11
]
. It is a risk that the pseudonymity and traceable-identity schemes already used in IoV
[
144
]
do not evaluate against directly. Consensus adds a second, better-quantified trade-off. No protocol jointly satisfies efficiency, security, and decentralization
[
26
]
, and one IoV integration study measures consensus success collapsing from 97% to 75% as the malicious-node share rises from 10% to 50%
[
231
]
. Recent work responds by layering verifiable cryptography onto lightweight consensus. These include chameleon-hash ledger redaction
[
73
]
, aggregated zero-knowledge telemetry proofs
[
69
]
and a two-tier microblock/keyblock structure
[
72
]
. This is exactly the direction
[
26
]
identifies as a research hotspot, though none of these schemes is load-tested near the malicious-node range where
[
231
]
shows robustness actually collapsing.

Another challenge is coordinating FL specifically. On-chain storage does not scale, off-chain trusted computation "under the chain is extremely challenging"
[
26
]
, and a DAG-based trust framework built to avoid a single ledger was found to introduce high overhead precisely because it multiplies validation points
[
231
]
. The redactable-DAG-plus-SGX design already reviewed
[
237
]
is the closest realized answer but remains a single deployment, not the general off-chain framework
[
26
]
calls for. BC security also rests on non-quantum-safe signatures, and given vehicles’ decade-plus service life, migration cannot realistically be deferred to a later update cycle the way it might be for shorter-lived consumer devices.

Future Direction:
The most immediate step is testing the verifiable-cryptography-on-lightweight-consensus direction
[
73
,
69
,
72
]
at city scale and near the malicious-node range where
[
231
]
shows robustness collapsing, rather than only at the single operating point each scheme was designed around. Alongside this, maturing TEE-secured off-chain verification beyond the single instance in
[
237
]
(potentially by drawing on the TEE primitives already surveyed for other purposes
[
275
,
276
]
) would address the general off-chain framework
[
26
]
calls for. Lattice-based post-quantum signature migration paths deserve attention now given how long vehicles built on today’s cryptographic assumptions will remain in service.

5.2.3
Homomorphic Encryption

Bootstrapping cost remains HE’s core bottleneck. Noise grows exponentially under multiplication, and even FHE’s roughly tenfold improvement over earlier bootstrapping leaves per-gate cost many orders of magnitude above a plaintext operation
[
40
]
. No IoV study reviewed here solves this. Each shrinks what gets encrypted instead such as last-layer-only encryption
[
160
]
, dynamic-precision CKKS
[
161
]
, ring-topology modular decomposition
[
162
]
. This is a pragmatic trajectory but an unevaluated one. Partial encryption leaves earlier gradients exposed to the very inversion attacks HE is meant to prevent, and no study quantifies the resulting protection gap relative to full encryption.

Multi-key FHE, the natural fit for a federated aggregation server, remains bounded to a fixed participant count and roughly doubles noise growth relative to single-key evaluation
[
40
]
(opposite of a fleet’s dynamically changing membership). Current work sidesteps this with HE/secret-sharing hybrids
[
79
]
or authentication-only MACs
[
246
]
rather than true multi-key FHE, and only few studies utilized HE in perception and object-detection tasks (Section
4.4
). This is the clearest evidence that its overhead still rules out real-time vision workloads. Independent re-analysis has also found that bit-wise security parameters claimed by many HE libraries provide lower security levels in practice
[
40
]
. This is a caution for any qualitative privacy rating, including Table
2
’s, that does not fix an implementation and parameter regime.

Future Direction:
Two directions seem most likely to move the needle. The first is characterizing how CKKS’s precision-versus-depth trade-off
[
277
]
should differ across the classification, regression, and detection heads common in IoV models, and quantifying what partial encryption schemes such as
[
160
,
161
]
actually give up relative to full encryption. This is so far adapted at runtime within a single benchmark
[
161
]
. The second is developing multi-key or threshold HE that supports an unbounded participant set and pairing hardware acceleration specifically with the convolutional and recurrent architectures that dominate IoV perception and forecasting, rather than pursuing general-purpose HE speedups. This echoes back to the adaptive, accuracy-versus-complexity balance
[
277
]
identifies as HE’s own closing priority.

5.2.4
Secure Multi-party Computing

SMPC’s synchronous, multi-party structure introduces a weakest-link latency effect that FL and HE do not share. A two-server lane-change protocol is bottlenecked by whichever server is slowest
[
86
]
, while protocols redesigned specifically for IoV comparison and activation operations report speedups of up to several hundred times over generic MPC frameworks
[
167
,
168
]
. This shows that protocol genericity, not cryptography itself, drives most of the overhead. Privacy also rests on rigid two- or three-server non-collusion assumptions
[
83
,
86
,
167
,
251
]
that fail categorically, not gracefully, if violated. IoV’s dynamic RSU topology strains this classical fixed-party assumption, addressed so far only by
[
166
]
’s dropout-tolerant packed secret sharing. It was a purpose-built response rather than a general theory applicable to the gossip-coordinated platooning and cross-carrier settings reviewed elsewhere
[
85
,
87
]
.

SMPC also protects computation, not output. It does not stop a repeatedly queried result from leaking information over time, which is why several studies pair it with DP
[
84
,
89
]
. Yet no study formally composes SMPC’s guarantee with DP’s budget accounting for a system queried continuously over an operational lifetime rather than trained once
[
87
,
169
]
.

Future Direction:
Elastic protocols that avoid the weakest-link bottleneck documented in
[
86
]
, and dynamic-membership SMPC that generalizes
[
166
]
’s dropout tolerance beyond the single RSU-aggregation setting it was designed for, are the most tractable future steps. The harder but more consequential problem is formal SMPC-DP composition for continuous querying which is relevant to the always-on coordination settings in
[
87
,
169
]
. The combination of SMPC with TEE-rooted trust
[
275
,
276
]
could help by loosening the rigid non-collusion assumption it currently depends on.

5.2.5
Differential Privacy

DP-SGD’s clip-then-noise mechanism means larger IoV models suffer proportionally larger utility loss
[
278
]
. This is consistent with significant accuracy drop (87.76% to 77.55%) reported for gradient-level noise injection in FL-based trajectory prediction
[
94
]
. The choice of composition theorem matters just as much. f-DP permits roughly 20
\times
more training epochs than Rényi DP at the same budget
[
12
]
, yet none of the FL/DP IoV studies reviewed specifies which theorem it uses. Under a worst-case adversary, empirically measured leakage has been shown to approach the full theoretical bound rather than staying safely below it
[
278
]
, undercutting any assumption that higher-utility budgets are safely conservative.

DP’s cost also falls unevenly. Gradient clipping mechanically penalizes outlier and underrepresented samples, with disparate impact documented even under mild 30–70% class imbalance and loose budgets
[
278
]
. This raises qualitative concern about sparse-region ITS bias with an actual mechanism. The DP also does not defend against poisoning, mirroring the privacy-robustness interaction already identified for FL
[
12
,
273
]
. A systematic review of 106 DP-in-ML studies confirms fairness-aware budgeting remains unresolved field-wide and names adaptive DP combined with FL in VANET/IoV specifically as a priority direction
[
279
]
.

Future Direction:
Reporting the composition theorem and the resulting round budget explicitly, rather than leaving it implicit, would be a low-cost step that makes IoV studies genuinely comparable. Beyond that, noise calibrated to data density or economic sensitivity rather than a single global budget, and the adaptive DP-plus-FL direction that
[
279
]
names directly for VANET/IoV, are where this survey sees the most promising path forward. This would extend the geospatial and personalized calibration already explored for location publishing, automotive data sharing, and battery health estimation
[
95
,
261
,
259
]
.

This section examines each application domain to identify its main privacy challenges and the directions needed to address them. Table

5.3.1
Intelligent Transportation and Traffic Management

Several of this domain’s applications share a common shape. They consume continuous, fine-grained signals from many vehicles and are only useful if the resulting model or output is accurate and timely.
Traffic flow, speed, and state prediction

[
41
,
58
,

]
,
crowd flow and congestion monitoring

[
47
,

, and
signal control and intersection management

[

,
84

all sit on this trade-off, but each strains it differently. Prediction and monitoring tasks need enough spatial and temporal resolution to be useful, so aggressive noise or aggregation directly erodes utility or accuracy. This tension is already visible in the accuracy loss reported when DP noise is added to gradients in traffic-flow FL
[
90
]
. Because traffic density varies enormously across a city, a privacy setting tuned for a dense downtown is typically too weak or too strong everywhere else. Signal control adds a harder constraint on top. Decisions happen in a sub-second control loop, and the SMPC-plus-DP scheme already proposed for connected-vehicle signal control needed a dedicated two-stage stochastic program just to keep DP-induced noise from degrading control performance
[
84
]
. This shows how much harder this constraint is to satisfy than for prediction or monitoring alone. Multiple stakeholders (a municipality, a private mobility operator, a neighbouring jurisdiction) may also need to coordinate control decisions without any one of them acting as a fully trusted central authority.

A second cluster concerns individuals rather than the road network.
Vehicle positioning and trajectory prediction

[
53
,

,

]
,
travel mode and behavior identification

[

]
, and

[

all depend on data that is quasi-identifying almost by definition. A trajectory can reveal where someone lives and works even after coordinates are anonymized, and inferring a person’s likely next location or travel mode is inferring something about their intent or habits. Publishing such data for downstream planning or research use adds a further tension, since a dataset useful enough for traffic engineers to plan around is often precise enough to re-identify the people in it. The density-aware, personalized perturbation already explored for trajectory publishing
[
96
]
and the rotating-group-leadership scheme for location publishing
[
95
]
both respond to exactly this tension, but each is tailored to one specific data type rather than the class of problem generally.

A differently shaped set of applications is

]
and

The redactable, authority-gated ledger proposed for accident forensics is one approach to balancing attribution with individual privacy
[
73

. However, this creates a tension between identifying attackers and protecting the privacy of other network users. Resource sharing among mobility operators, edge providers, and RSUs presents a similar challenge. For example, the zero-knowledge C-V2X data-sharing scheme and the gossip-coordinated platooning framework reviewed earlier

5.3.2
Autonomous Driving and Safety-critical Applications

This domain includes applications that differ in how directly they affect physical safety.
Driver and occupant monitoring

[
102
,
104
,
156
]
includes drowsiness, distraction, fatigue, and activity recognition. These applications collect highly sensitive biometric and behavioral data continuously inside a private space. However, this information must also be processed quickly to detect dangerous conditions and allow timely action. The HE- and FL-based drowsiness and fatigue detection methods reviewed earlier showed that encryption can introduce additional computation and communication overhead compared with fully local models
[
104
,
156
]
. Therefore, achieving strong privacy while maintaining very low latency is particularly challenging.
Steering, motion control, and speed or signal control

[
101
,
109
,
163
]
face a similar challenge. Privacy mechanisms used in real time control systems must not introduce delays that could affect vehicle operation. For example, the affine-masked cooperative cruise-control scheme keeps its overhead below 30 ms to meet this requirement
[
163
]
. Additionally, these safety-related decisions need sufficient records for post-incident analysis, which can conflict with minimizing stored data.

Trajectory prediction and publishing

[
117
,
112
]
and
vehicle positioning and decision-making

[
139
]
extend the privacy concerns of individual tracking into cooperative perception. Sharing a predicted trajectory with nearby vehicles can help prevent collisions, but it also reveals information about a vehicle’s location and intended movement to potentially untrusted parties. In addition, safety-related decisions need to remain auditable even when they are performed using encryption or secret sharing. This creates a trade-off between protecting information and maintaining transparency for later verification.
Perception
, including object, road, sign, and scene detection
[
123
,
136
,
108
]
, presents a particularly important challenge in this domain. It is the largest and most active application area in the reviewed literature, but most existing approaches rely on FL. This is because processing high-volume video and LiDAR data with convolutional models is difficult to combine with cryptographic PPML under strict real-time requirements. As a result, current privacy protection for perception mainly relies on system architecture, such as keeping raw sensor data on the device, as demonstrated by the federated bird’s-eye-view and adapter-based detection frameworks reviewed earlier
[
136
,
137
]
.

Security: misbehavior, intrusion, attack, and trust

[
112
,
160
,
161
]

6
Conclusion

Integration of ML into the IoV has significantly enhanced transportation efficiency and autonomous driving capabilities. However, it also introduces privacy risks due to the sensitivity of vehicular, environmental and user data. Architectural PPML techniques such as FL and BC-PPML enable privacy preserving decentralized collaboration, while computational PPML techniques such as HE, SMPC and DP protect data confidentiality and limit unauthorized inference, though robustness to adversarial and poisoning attacks remains a largely open challenge. This survey provided a comprehensive review of the recent advances in adopting PPML techniques for IoV applications. We systematically analyzed the privacy challenges inherent to ML-driven IoV systems, including sensitive data exposure, adversarial attacks, and communication vulnerabilities. We categorized IoV applications into three key domains and evaluated how PPML techniques effectively mitigate privacy risks while preserving utility. Despite various advancements, significant challenges remain, such as balancing privacy-utility trade-offs, managing computational overhead, and ensuring scalability across heterogeneous networks. To overcome these, we further discussed potential future directions such as hybrid PPML framework and lightweight encryption for edge devices, among others.
