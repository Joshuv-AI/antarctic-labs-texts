RVd and CyBorD therapies remodel B-cell maturation signaling and alter immune and clonal architecture in multiple myeloma
Authors: Zuzana Valuskova, Dana Cholujova, Gabor Beke, Milan Hucko, Maria E. Marinkovicova, Katarina Suroviakova, Lubos Klucar, Lubos Drgona, Merav Leiba, Efstathios Kastritis, David M. Dorfman, Kenneth C. Anderson, Jana Jakubikova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) features plasma cell (PC) heterogeneity and alterations in B-cell differentiation and immune regulation. Although lenalidomide/bortezomib/dexamethasone (RVd) and cyclophosphamide/bortezomib/dexamethasone (CyBorD) are clinically effective, their precise impacts on PC/B‐cell maturation remain unclear.MethodsWe performed CyTOF profiling on bone marrow samples from RVd- (n = 47) and CyBorD-treated MM patients (n = 15), each compared to untreated cohort (n = 43). Within each therapeutic arm, responders (RVd, n = 35; CyBorD, n = 5) were compared to non-responders (RVd, n = 12; CyBorD, n = 10).ResultsRVd and CyBorD therapies exerted distinct immune modulation patterns, differentially affecting PC, early B-cell stages, and naive T cells. Evaluation of transcriptional B-cell regulators in B-cell lymphopoiesis revealed that both regimens decreased IRF4, CXCR4, and FGFR3 while upregulating Pax-5 across B‐cell stages and PC. RVd uniquely upregulated MYD88 and c-Myc and decreased sXBP1; its responders further suppressed BLIMP-1 and FGFR3. In contrast, CyBorD elevated sXBP1, BLIMP-1, and Notch-1 while reducing c-Myc in the B and PC subsets. Both therapies increased the expression of the stemness factor KLF4 and variably modulated NANOG; CyBorD altered Nestin and RARα2 in responder PCs, whereas RVd suppressed OCT3/4. Shared immunophenotypic aberrations included decreased MMSET, CD200, and CD52, and increased CD47, CD81, and CD44 in B‐cell compartments. In PC, both regimens elevated CD338 while reducing CD47, CD319, and CD138. RVd responders further downregulated CD56, CD269, and CD329, and increased CD243.ConclusionsThese shared and divergent modulations elucidate the molecular underpinnings of RVd and CyBorD efficacy and inform precision regimen selection.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a complex hematological malignancy marked by the uncontrolled proliferation of clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow and elevated serum monoclonal protein levels, leading to hallmark signs of end-organ damage, including hypercalcemia, renal impairment, anemia, and osteolytic lesions. High-risk cytogenetic abnormalities, such as TP53 deletion, t(4;14), t(14;16), t(14;20), and 1q21 amplifications, are associated with poor prognosis and resistance to therapy. These genetic changes drive the overexpression of oncogenes such as FGFR3, MMSET, MAF, and MAFB, and genes such as CKS1B, which increase myeloma cell proliferation, survival, and apoptosis resistance.1,2 Additionally, the BM microenvironment, which is composed of cellular (stromal cells, immune cells, osteoblasts, osteoclasts) and non-cellular elements (extracellular matrix, cytokines, growth factors), interacts with malignant plasma cells, influencing their behavior, survival, and response to therapy,3 thus playing a critical role in MM pathogenesis and progression.

Over the years, therapeutic advancements in MM have significantly increased survival rates and quality of life, with current approaches encompassing induction therapies, autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), maintenance strategies, and innovative emerging treatments.4 Induction therapy is a critical first step in MM treatment, aiming to reduce tumor burden and achieve remission prior to ASCT or further therapies. The Revlimid (lenalidomide), Velcade (bortezomib), and dexamethasone (RVd) regimen is a standard induction therapy that results in high response rates and prolonged progression-free survival (PFS).5,6 RVd achieves overall response rates (ORR) of 80–90% in newly diagnosed MM, with very good partial response (VGPR) rates increasing from 58% after three cycles to 87% post-consolidation. Long-term outcomes include 77% PFS at 39 months and 100% overall survival (OS) in select cohorts.5 RVd is widely recommended for newly diagnosed patients and is also effective as salvage therapy in relapsed or refractory cases.6,7 For patients unable to tolerate lenalidomide, the cyclophosphamide-bortezomib-dexamethasone (CyBorD) regimen is an effective alternative.8 The CyBorD regimen achieves an overall response rate (ORR) of 88%, with 61% attaining VGPR or better, 39% reaching complete (CR) or near-complete responses (nCR) after four cycles, and three-year OS and PFS rates of 89% and 42.7%, respectively.9 It induces rapid responses, reducing serum monoclonal protein by 80% within two cycles, and serves as a post-ASCT consolidation therapy to improve outcomes in patients with residual disease.10 While RVd remains the preferred regimen due to its efficacy, CyBorD is invaluable for patients with reduced tolerability or specific clinical needs.

Both RVd and CyBorD combination therapies target myeloma cells through distinct mechanisms, including the modulation of signaling pathways and the immune microenvironment, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy.11 Lenalidomide, a key component of RVd, promotes T-cell proliferation, enhances natural killer (NK) cell activity, and shifts the immune environment toward an anti-tumor state. It reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, increases anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, and suppresses immune checkpoints such as PD-1, thereby countering immune evasion and boosting T-cell responses against myeloma cells.12 Cyclophosphamide, which is central to CyBorD, acts as an alkylating agent that induces DNA cross-linking, leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in rapidly dividing myeloma cells, while its immunomodulatory effects further enhance immune responses.13,14 Like both combinations, bortezomib, a proteasome inhibitor, induces apoptosis in MM cells by preventing the degradation of pro-apoptotic factors and inhibiting NF-κB activity, enhancing T-cell activation and sensitizing cells to other therapies.15 Although dexamethasone effectively reduces inflammation, it also exerts immunosuppressive effects on T cells and NK cells, potentially increasing the risk of infection.16

By enhancing our understanding of MM pathophysiology, we previously characterized B-cell lymphopoiesis, plasma cell (PC) differentiation, and the immune landscape of the bone marrow microenvironment across different stages of myeloma disease evolution and progression using high-dimensional mass cytometry (CyTOF). Our findings revealed distinct immunophenotypic profiles, with differential expression of key B cell signaling regulators (IRF-4, Bcl-6, c-Myc, CXCR4, MYD88, and sXBP-1), aberrant PC markers (CD319, CD269, CD200, CD117, CD56, and CD28), and stemness-associated factors (Nestin, Sox2, KLF-4, and Nanog), all of which correlated with clinical outcomes.17 In addition, we identified stage-specific immune alterations, highlighting profound remodeling of both the innate and adaptive immune subsets throughout disease progression.18

In this study, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the dynamic interactions between clonal PC subsets, B cell differentiation, and immune modulation in response to therapy, comparing the effects of the RVd versus CyBorD regimens. By integrating high-dimensional CyTOF profiling with clinical response data, we systematically compared responders and non-responders across both therapeutic regimens, evaluating immunophenotypic shifts, alterations in clonal dynamics, and molecular signaling perturbations associated with treatment efficacy. These findings offer crucial mechanistic insights into MM pathophysiology and therapeutic response, establishing a foundation for optimizing precision medicine approaches in MM management.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment and sample collection

Bone marrow (BM) aspirates were collected from 105 MM patients, including untreated patients (no Tx, n = 43) and those treated with RVd- or CyBorD-treated MM (n = 62) patients. The clinical characteristics of the patients are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Comprehensive clinical and treatment histories were recorded at the time of sampling. BM samples were obtained during routine diagnostic procedures at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (Boston, USA) and the Biomedical Research Center (Bratislava, Slovakia). Ethical approval was granted by the Biomedical Research Center’s local ethics committee (Protocol No. Myelom 001) and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (Protocol No. 10-106), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell lines and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Multiple myeloma cell lines (RPMI 8226-S, RPMI-Dox40, RPMI-MR20, and MM.1S) and additional cancer cell lines (RAJI, KG-1a, Jurkat, MOLT-4, HeLa, and NTERA-2) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Cellgro, Mediatech, VA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Harlan, Indianapolis, IN), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, except for NTERA-2, which was maintained in DMEM (Cellgro, Mediatech, VA).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMNCs) from healthy volunteers were isolated by Ficoll-Hypaque (Pharmacia, Piscataway, NJ) density gradient centrifugation and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 20% FBS, antibiotics, and L-glutamine. Informed consent was obtained following the Declaration of Helsinki. PBMNCs were stimulated with GM-CSF (50 ng/mL, 7 d), while MM.1S cells were treated with camptothecin (6 μM, 8 h).

Antibody conjugation strategy

Antibodies, including clones, providers, and concentrations, are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Using the MaxPAR antibody conjugation kit (Fluidigm Sciences), 100 μg of antibodies were conjugated with mass isotopes according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lanthanide metal solutions (2.5 mM) were polymer-loaded for 40 min at 37 °C, followed by antibody buffer exchange and partial reduction of the antibody with 4 mM TCEP for 30 min. After purification, the antibodies were conjugated to lanthanide-loaded polymers for 1 h and recovered through four washing steps.

Performance assessment of metal-conjugated antibodies

Metal-conjugated antibodies were digested in 2% HCl and 2% HNO₃ (diluted in ddH₂O) and analyzed on a CyTOF 2 mass cytometer (Fluidigm). The metal content (≥100 metal atoms/antibody) was verified using blank and tuning solutions as normalization standards, and the antibody concentrations were measured at 280 nm with a NanoDrop, adjusted to 0.5 mg/mL in PBS stabilization buffer with 0.05% sodium azide, and stored at 4 °C. Antibody efficacy was validated using titration and positive controls, such as human peripheral blood mononuclear cells or cell lines (Supplementary Table S2).

Bone marrow processing

Fresh BM aspirates were collected in sodium heparinized tubes, fixed with proteomic stabilizer buffer (1.4 mL buffer/mL BM) for 10 min at RT, and stored at –80 °C. Before analysis, the samples were thawed at 4 °C, and erythrocytes were lysed with hypotonic thaw-lyse buffer (4:1 buffer-to-sample ratio) for 10 min at RT. Leukocytes were pelleted at 600 × g for 6 min, with repeated lysis as needed. The cells were washed twice with cell staining medium (CSM: PBS with 0.5% BSA and 0.02% NaN₃) and pelleted for further analysis.

CyTOF workflow: sample preparation and data acquisition

The samples were stained with anti-human CD15-PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone HI98; BD Pharmingen) for 30 min at 4 °C. CD15⁻ cells were sorted using a FACS Aria III 5 L cell sorter (70-μm nozzle) to deplete granulocytes from the BI and BII panels (not the TIME). The cells were washed in CSM, blocked with 5 μL of Fc receptor blocking solution for 10 min at RT, and stained with specific cell surface antibody coctail in 100 μL CSM for 30 min. Aliquots of the sample were stained with either the BI or BII panel of surface antibodies (sorted or unsorted) or the TIME panel (unsorted only). After staining, the cells were washed twice in CSM and centrifuged (600 × g, 6 min, RT). For intracellular staining, BI and BII samples were permeabilized with ice-cold methanol (1 mL) and stored at −80 °C, while TIME samples were treated with FOXP3 Perm buffer (1 mL, 1 × , 30 min, RT). The cells were then washed twice in CSM and stained with intracellular antibody cocktails specific to each panel, along with 1 μL of 191/193 Iridium (Ir) DNA intercalator (Fluidigm, San Francisco, CA) in 100 μL of CSM for 1 h at RT. The cells were washed twice in CSM, centrifuged (600 × g, 6 min, RT), and subsequently washed with PBS and ddH₂O before acquisition. After washing, the cells were diluted to 0.5 × 10⁶ cells/mL in ddH₂O with 10% EQ Four Element Calibration Beads and acquired on a CyTOF 2 cytometer at 300–500 events/second.

High-dimensional data processing and SPADE analysis

The data were normalized using the Normalizer tool and EQ Four Element Calibration Beads to correct signal fluctuations, then concatenated with the Cytobank concatenation tool (Mountain View, CA). The signal intensities were arcsinh-transformed (cofactor 5), and gating strategies were applied to exclude debris, beads, and apoptotic cells. High-dimensional data were analyzed using Spanning-tree Progression Analysis of Density-normalized Events (SPADE) in Cytobank (Mountain View, CA), generating tree structures representing phenotypically similar cell clusters. Nodes in the SPADE tree were color-coded by median marker intensity (blue: low, red: high), with node size reflecting the fraction of assigned cells. SPADE clustering included 13 markers in the BI/BII panels and 33 in the TIME panel, with parameters of 1% outlier density, 10% target density, and 300/500 clusters for the BI/BII and TIME panels, respectively. Bone marrow samples from MM patients and healthy donors were analyzed together, generating unified SPADE trees for each panel. SPADE data were exported to R for further analysis, with cluster annotations based on marker expression and prior knowledge.

Interactive and advanced visualization tools

To enable interactive visualization of high-dimensional data, we developed a Shiny-based R web portal using additional packages such as shinyBS, igraph, ggplot2, Cairo, gplots, colorRamps, plotly, and DT (Supplementary Table 3) to annotate cell population clusters from SPADE analyses. Following annotation, we normalized the number of cells in each population relative to the total sample size using the formula:x=cB/cA,where x represents the normalized cell count, cB​ is the total number of cells in a given cluster, and cA​ is the total number of cells across all clusters. Median marker expression was normalized using: y=Σ(c1.e1+c2.e2+⋯+cn.en)n1/cB,where y is the normalized median expression of a marker, c represents the number of cells in a node, e is the marker's median expression, and cB​ is the total number of cells in the cluster.

For further analysis, we modified functions from the SPADEVizR package, including mergeClusters, identifyDAC, to assess marker expression across clusters. R Shiny and additional packages (e.g., shinydashboard, htmlwidgets, openxlsx, queryBuilder, rhtmlHeatmap, RColorBrewer, viridisLite, grid, and gridExtra) were used to visualize normalized SPADE results as SPADE trees, heatmaps, dot plots, and whisker/box plots. Marker regulation across multiple cell clusters was visualized using ComplexHeatmap libraries, while Venn diagrams (VennDiagram package) illustrated common marker expression changes in MM (Supplementary Table 3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Differences between two groups were assessed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann‒Whitney U test). Comparisons among three or more groups were performed via one-way ANOVA for mean values and Dunn's test after the Kruskal‒Wallis test for medians. The data distribution was evaluated via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov and Shapiro‒Wilk tests, and outliers were identified by Tukey’s test. Multiple comparisons were analyzed using the Holm‒Sidak method and Tukey's test. P-values (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001) are shown in the graphs and figure legends.

Results

Single-cell CyTOF profiling of immune and clonal dynamics in MM under therapies

To elucidate the effects of standard anti-MM therapies within the complex pathophysiology of MM, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of clonal PC subsets, B cell differentiation, and immune modulation in response to RVd and CyBorD treatment regimens using large-scale CyTOF profiling. Our study cohort included RVd-treated patients (n = 47) compared to untreated MM patients (n = 43) and CyBorD-treated patients (n = 15) compared to untreated MM patients (n = 43). Moreover, we stratified RVd responders (n = 35) versus non-responders (n = 12) and CyBorD responders (n = 5) versus non-responders (n = 10) to assess the immunophenotypic changes and molecular signaling alterations associated with treatment efficacy (Supplementary Table 1). Our analytical pipeline focused on profiling PC heterogeneity within and across patient groups, evaluating molecular disruptions of transcription factors and signaling regulators (CyTOF BI panel), as well as stemness-associated and aberrant PC markers (CyTOF BII panel); in addition, both panels profiled B-cell lymphopoiesis (Figure 1A). Furthermore, the CyTOF TIME panel was used to examine the interplay between plasma cell disease and immune status (Supplementary Table 2). Briefly, BM-isolated cells from these cohorts (either pre-sorted for BI and BII panels or unsorted for TIME panel) were stained in parallel using the three CyTOF panels, analyzed by CyTOF technology, and subjected to comprehensive bioinformatics and statistical analysis (Supplementary Table 3).

(A) The experimental design schematic shows that patient samples from the study cohort (n = 105) were stained with metal-conjugated antibodies from the CyTOF BI, BII, and TIME panels, analyzed by CyTOF, and subjected to comprehensive bioinformatics and statistical analyses. The statistically significant normalized cell frequency of immune cell clusters are depicted as boxplots for (B) MM patients treated with RVd regimen (n = 47) versus untreated MM patients (n = 43); (C) RVd responders (n = 35) versus non-responders (n = 12); (D) MM patients treated with the CyBorD regimen (n = 15) versus untreated MM patients (n = 43); and (E) CyBorD responders (n = 5) versus non-responders (n = 10). All pairwise comparisons were evaluated by the Mann–Whitney U test (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).

Immune modulation patterns in MM patients treated with RVd and CyBorD therapies

In our previous study, we used high-dimensional CyTOF profiling to characterize BM immune dynamics across MM progression, revealing adaptive immune suppression and increased innate immune infiltration.18 To further investigate these immune alterations, we analyzed changes in immune cell distribution and organization in MM patients treated with RVd and CyBorD therapies. Following RVd treatment, we observed significant decreases in plasma cell (PC) clusters (PC1-3,5), plasmablasts (PB/PC2), transitional B cells (T2), unswitched memory B cells (UM1-4), and naive helper T cells (CD4Tn), while pre-BI cells and erythroblasts (Eb) increased (Figure 1B). Among RVd responders, PC1-4 and naive cytotoxic T cells (CD8Tn1-2) were reduced, whereas immature B cells (I2), T1,3 clusters, and B cells within the tumor-immune microenvironment expanded (Figure 1C). CyBorD treatment resulted in lower frequencies of PC2,7 clusters, UM2-3, and T1-2 cells, while PB/PC1 subsets were upregulated (Figure 1D). In CyBorD responders, only T3 and naïve B cells (N) showed a reduction Compared to non-responders (Figure 1E). Neither therapy significantly affected the distribution of mature CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T cell subsets, including both central memory and effector memory cells (data not shown). These findings demonstrate distinct immune modulation patterns associated with RVd and CyBorD therapies, highlighting their differential effects on immune cell subsets, particularly plasma cells, early B cell stages, and naive T cells, in MM patients.

Distinct molecular signatures in plasmablasts and plasma cells induced by RVd and CyBorD therapies in MM patients: implications for treatment response

Comparative analysis of intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity in MM patients versus healthy-donor plasma cell (PC) and plasmablast (PB) clusters demonstrated a universal upregulation of MMSET (WHSC1), CD47, and Notch‐1. In two precursor PB/PC clusters (CD20–, CD27het, CD38+, CD45+, CD138–), we observed constitutive overexpression of CXCR4 (CD184), c-Myc, IRF-4, MYD88, and P-gp (CD243), together with aberrant MM markers such as CD52, CD56, and CD319. Among mature PCs (CD19het, CD20het, CD27het, CD38+, CD45lo, CD138+), we identified seven immunophenotypically and functionally distinct subsets, each defined by unique combinations of B-cell regulators and transcription factors (IRF-4, sXBP-1, Bcl-6, c-Myc), stem-cell-associated markers (Nanog, Nestin, Sox-2, RARα2), and aberrant myeloma antigens (MMSET, CD200, CD319, CD289, CD28).17

To evaluate signaling responses associated with RVd therapy or CyBorD compared to untreated myeloma samples, significant alterations were observed in plasmablasts and plasma cell clusters. In plasmablasts, RVd therapy induced the upregulation of CD38 (PB/PC1), CD81, and CD45 (PB/PC2) while downregulating CD200, c-Myc, CD27, sXBP-1, CD362, Oct3/4, MMSET, CD289, CD38, IRF-4, Nanog, and CD319 (PB/PC2). Within plasma cell (PC) clonal clusters, RVd therapy increased CD81 (PC1-4), CD338 (PC1,2,5), FGFR-3 (PC1-3,5), Pax-5 (PC1,2,6), CD45 (PC1,4,5), IgA, CD19 (PC4,5), KLF-4 (PC6,7), CD117, CD62L, and IgM (PC5) expression. Conversely, a significant reduction in the expression of markers such as CD200, CD138 (PC1,2,4,5), MMSET (PC4-7), CD38 (PC2,6,7), c-Myc, CD269, CD319 (PC2,4), CD27 (PC2-4), CD184 (PC2,3), CD47 (PC2,5), sXBP-1 (PC2,7), CD289 (PC2,6), CD45, CD28, Nanog, IRF-4, Oct3/4, (PC2), IgM (PC3), and CD20 (PC4) was noted across several PC clusters (Figure 2A). In RVd responders, plasmablasts presented decreased expression of CD329 (PB/PC1) and CD319 (PB/PC2), alongside increased levels of CD81 (PB/PC1-2), IgM, IgG, CD243 (PB/PC1), and CD45 (PB/PC2). Plasma cell clusters from responders showed an increase in IgA (PC1,6), IgM (PC1,7), CD81 (PC4,7), CD52 (PC1), CD19 (PC2), CD289 (PC3), CD243 (PC4), CD138 (PC6), and IgG (PC7), along with downregulation of CD200 (PC1-3), CD56 (PC1-3), IRF-4 (PC1,2,4), Nanog (PC1,2,4,6), CD38 (PC2,4), CD269 (PC4), CD319 (PC2), MMSET, FGFR-3, Blimp-1 (PC4), CD45 (PC5), and RARa2 (PC6) compared to non-responders (Figure 2B).

Comparatively, CyBorD-treated patients demonstrated upregulation of CD38 (PB/PC1) and CD45 (PB/PC2) in plasmablasts, while reductions were observed in IgA, CD319 (PB/PC1-2), IgG, and CD243 (PB/PC1). In CyBorD responders, plasmablasts showed a reduction in only CD319 (PB/PC2). In plasma cell clusters, increased expression of CD27 (PC1), CD138 (PC4), CD24 (PC5), Notch-1, CD289, and CD19 (PC6) was identified, along with reduced levels of CD184 (PC2) and Sox-2 (PC7) (Figure 2D).

Shared molecular alterations in plasmablast and plasma cell (PC) clusters induced by RVd and CyBorD therapies revealed overlapping signaling dynamics, characterized by upregulation of CD38 (PB/PC1), CD45 (PC1,4,5), CD338 (PC1,2), Pax-5 (PC1,6), FGFR3, and CD19 (PC5), alongside downregulation of CD27 (PC2,3), MMSET (PC5,7), CD289 (PC2,6), CD184 (PC3), CD28, CD47, and CD138 (PC2). Notably, a therapy-specific response was observed, in which treatment responders from both cohorts exhibited only CD319 downregulation in plasmablasts (PB/PC2) (Figure 2E). Collectively, RVd and CyBorD therapies modulate signaling in myeloma by upregulating CD81, CD38, and CD45 in plasmablasts and FGFR-3, CD338, Pax-5, and KLF-4 in plasma cells, while downregulating MMSET, CD200, c-Myc, CD319, and IRF-4. Moreover, RVd responders exhibited increased CD81 and CD52 with concomitant decreases in CD56, IRF-4, and Nanog; in contrast, CyBorD responders showed elevated Notch-1 alongside reduced CD184 and Sox-2.

Molecular alterations in B cell progenitors by RVd and CyBorD therapies in MM patients

In a previous study, we characterized the phenotypic and molecular modulations of myeloma B cell precursors/progenitors, tracing their development from hematopoietic stem cells (hsc; CD34⁺, CD38(lo), CD45(lo)) through distinct B cell maturation stages: pre-pro-B cells (CD34+ , CD38het, CD45lo), pro-B cells (CD34lo, CD38+ , CD45lo), pre-BI cells (CD34het, CD10+ , CD19lo), and pre-BII cells (CD34–, CD10lo, CD19lo, IgMlo). Across these stages, we identified the upregulation of key B-cell signaling regulators, including MYD88, MMSET, c-Myc, Notch-1, CXCR4, IRF-4, Bcl-6, and Bcl-2, as well as the ATP-binding cassette transporter CD243 and aberrant plasma cell markers (CD47, CD52, CD269, CD117, and CD25), during the transition from MGUS to smoldering MM and active MM progression.17

In hsc, RVd-treated patients showed downregulation of MMSET and upregulation of Bcl-2, CD47, CD38, CD25, and the stemness marker CD34 compared to untreated MM patients. In pre-pro-B subset, RVd therapy led to reduced expression of MMSET and aberrant markers (CD52, CD200, CD289, CD362), while increasing CD81 levels. Similarly, in pro-B cells, RVd treatment resulted in reduced MMSET, CD52, CD289, CD362, and the stemness marker Oct3/4, along with upregulated CD81 expression. In both the pre-BI and pre-BII subsets, MMSET was downregulated, while MYD88 was upregulated. Pre-BI cells also exhibited increased CD269 and CD34, whereas pre-BII cells showed elevated CD81 and reduced IRF-4 expression under RVd therapy (Figure 3A). When comparing RVd responders to non-responders, responders exhibited reduced CD200 and CD45 expression in hsc, along with increased MYD88 and CD52 levels. In pre-pro-B cells, responders showed upregulated c-Myc, Bcl-2, CD25, MYD88, and CD52 expression, accompanied by reduced Nanog expression. In pro-B cells, we observed increased c-Myc, CD243, Oct3/4, and CD52 expression, while in pre-BI cells, responders showed decreased CD45 expression. In contrast, pre-BII cells in responders exhibited increased CD45, CD52, and sXBP-1 levels, along with reduced CD47 expression (Figure 3B).

Evaluation of early B cell progenies under CyBorD therapy revealed distinct alterations, with hsc showing reduced expression of MMSET, c-Myc, CD362, CD243, and CD52, whereas CD45, CD34, CD38, and CD25 were upregulated. When comparing CyBorD responders to non-responders, responders exhibited downregulation of CD81 in both hsc and pre-pro-B cells, while pro-B cells showed no significant changes. CyBorD responders also exhibited reduced CD38 levels in pre-BII cells, while no significant modulation was observed in pre-BI cells (Figure 3D).

Shared alterations in myeloma B cell progenitors induced by RVd and CyBorD therapies included the downregulation of MMSET from hsc through pre-BII stages, and the downregulation of CD52, specifically in pre-pro-B and pro-B subsets. Moreover, hsc cells presented upregulation of CD25, CD34, and CD38, while pre-pro-B cells showed reduced expression of CD200 and CD362, and pre-BII cells demonstrated IRF-4 suppression. Notably, no common molecular alterations were identified among the responders to either therapy (Figure 3E). In summary, RVd and CyBorD therapies in MM patients induced distinct molecular changes, with MMSET downregulation across all B cell progenitors as a shared effect. RVd therapy upregulated MYD88, CD81, and Bcl-2, whereas CyBorD therapy increased Pax-5. Among responders, RVd responders exhibited increased MYD88, CD52, and c-Myc, while CyBorD responders showed reduced CD81 expression in early B cell progenitors.

Impact of RVd and CyBorD on B cell signaling maturation in MM patients

Further profiling of myeloma B cell maturation, from immature (I1-2; CD10lo, CD19+, IgM+, IgD–/lo, CD20–/+, CD27–) to transitional (T1-3; CD10–, CD19+, IgM+, IgDlo, CD20+, CD22+, CD27–) and naive (N; CD19+, IgM+, IgD+, CD20+, CD22+, CD27–) B cells, revealed the upregulation of key B cell signaling regulators, including MMSET, MYD88, c-Myc, CXCR4, and Notch-1. Additionally, the expression of the stem cell marker KLF-4, the ABC transporter CD243, and aberrant plasma cell markers (CD47 and CD52) showed progressive upregulation from immature to naive B cells.17

In immature B cells, RVd-treated MM patients, compared to untreated patients, exhibited decreased expression of CD200 (I1-2), IRF-4 (I1), MMSET, CD22, CD52, and IgD (I2), alongside increased expression of Pax-5 and CD329 (I1), as well as CD81, IgM, MYD88, KLF-4, CD10, and Notch-1 (I2). Transitional B cells subjected to RVd therapy showed upregulated expression of IgM, CD22, and CD20 (T1-3); Pax-5 (T1-2); CD38 and CD47 (T1,3); KLF-4 and IRF-4 (T1); MYD88 and c-Myc (T2); and CD44 (T3), while reducing CD200 (T1-3), CD19 (T1,3), and MMSET (T3). In naive B cells, RVd therapy led to reduced expression of CD200 and MMSET, accompanied by upregulation of Pax-5, IgM, CD22, and CD81 (Figure 4A). In RVd therapy, responders exhibited downregulation of the stemness marker Nanog (I1-2) and upregulation of CD52 and CD20 (I1), as well as IgM and IgD (I2), compared to non-responders. Transitional B cells in RVd responders showed increased expression of CD52 (T1-3), c-Myc (T1), CD81 and CD38 (T2-3), IgM (T2), and IgD (T3). Naive B cells in RVd responders were characterized by the upregulation of sXBP-1, CD81, CD52, and c-Myc, along with the downregulation of CD45 (Figure 4B).

In CyBorD-treated MM patients, compared to untreated individuals, immature B cells demonstrated increased expression of Pax-5 and CD10 (I1-2), CD45 (I1), KLF-4, Nanog, Notch-1, and CD47 (I2), while CD319 (I1) and CD22, CD200, and CD52 (I2) were downregulated. In naive B cells, CyBorD therapy induced the upregulation of Pax-5, CD20, IgM, CD81, CD45, and CD22 (Figure 4C). Among CyBorD-treated patients, responders exhibited downregulation of CD19 and CD22 (I2) in immature cells and CD52 (I2, T1) in both immature and transitional B cells, as well as reduced CD44 (T3) in transitional B cells. Additionally, increased expression of CD10, Notch-1 (I2), and CD47 (T3) was observed, while CD22, CD52, and CD200 (I2) were downregulated. Consistent with findings in progenitor populations, no shared molecular responses were identified among responders from either treatment regimen (Figure 4E). Overall, RVd and CyBorD therapies modulated B-cell maturation in MM patients by inducing distinct molecular changes. RVd downregulated MMSET and CD200 across immature, transitional, and naive B cells, while upregulating Pax-5, MYD88, KLF-4, and CD81; among responders, CD52, c-Myc, and CD81 were further elevated. CyBorD decreased CD52 and similarly upregulated Pax-5 and KLF-4. In CyBorD responders, CD52 and CD44 were further reduced in immature and transitional B cells, while MYD88 and CD319 were increased in transitional and naive B cells, respectively.

Therapeutic signaling modulation of memory B cells by RVd and CyBorD regimens in MM patients

Mapping myeloma memory B cells have previously demonstrated the upregulation of key B cell signaling regulators, including MMSET, MYD88, c-Myc, CXCR4, and Notch-1, along with the stem cell marker KLF-4, the ABC transporter CD243, and the aberrant plasma cell marker CD47. These alterations were identified in unswitched memory B cell clusters (UM1-4: CD19+, IgM+, IgDhet, CD20+, CD22+, CD27+) and switched memory B cell clusters (SM1-3: IgM+, IgD–, CD27het, IgG+, IgA+).17

In unswitched memory B cells, RVd-treated patients compared to untreated MM patients exhibited increased expression of Pax-5, MYD88, IgM (UM1-4), KLF-4, CD47 (UM2-4), IRF-4 (UM1,3), CD22 (UM1,2), CD329, c-Myc, CD44 (UM3,4), CD38 (UM1), CD24, Nanog, CD243, Notch-1, CD362 (UM3), and CD27 (UM4). Concurrently, RVd therapy led to the downregulation of MMSET (UM1), CD19 (UM1-3), CD200 (UM1,3,4), CD184 (UM3,4), CD52, and CD45 (UM3). In switched memory B cells, RVd therapy resulted in the upregulation of Pax-5 (SM1-3), CD329, FGFR3 (SM1,3), CD24 (SM1,2), MYD88, IgM (SM2,3), CD45 (SM1), KLF-4, and IgA (SM3), while downregulating CD289, CD319, CD52, and MMSET (SM1-3), CD184 and CD38 (SM1) (Figure 5A). Among RVd responders compared to non-responders, unswitched memory B cells showed increased expression of CD38 (UM1-4), IgM (UM2-4), CD23 (UM1,2,4), c-Myc (UM1,4), MYD88 (UM1), and IgD (UM2), with downregulation of Nanog (UM2,3) and CD19 (UM4). In switched memory B cells, RVd responders displayed upregulation of CD52 and CD28 (SM2), alongside downregulation of CD329 (SM3) (Figure 5B).

Discussion

During normal B-cell development, B-cell maturation into antibody-secreting plasma cells is governed by a tightly integrated network of transcription factors and signaling adaptors. IRF4 accumulates during germinal center reactions to regulate proliferation, class switching and survival, partnering with BLIMP 1 and spliced XBP1 to drive immunoglobulin secretion while repressing B cell identity genes such as PAX5 and BCL6.19 BLIMP 1 irrevocably commits cells to the plasma cell fate and maintains long-lived plasma cells by silencing germinal center programs.20 IRF4 is universally upregulated in MM, often driven by translocations, that enforces an “oncogene addiction” phenotype by repressing pro-apoptotic factors such as BIM, activating anti-apoptotic programs, and engaging in a positive feedback loop with MYC, thereby correlating with aggressive disease and poor prognosis and indicating that IRF4 is a compelling therapeutic target.21 Conversely, although PAX5 is normally silenced in plasma cells, it resurfaces in 10%–15% of CD20-positive MM through incomplete repression or post transcriptional regulation, phenocopying plasmacytoid B cell lymphomas yet paradoxically triggering apoptosis and growth arrest upon re-expression, which underscores its complex but potential therapeutic relevance.22 Assessment of transcriptional regulators across B cell ontogeny under RVd and CyBorD treatment revealed both shared and regimen-specific modulation. Both regimens downregulated IRF4 in early progenitors and PCs but upregulated PAX5 broadly throughout B-cell maturation, indicating a shift toward B-cell identity regulation that may influence differentiation and treatment response. RVd’s unique pattern, suppressing IRF4 in immature B cells but transiently elevating it in transitional and memory subsets, along with responder-specific IRF4 downregulation in PCs, suggests that dynamic remodeling is associated with effective tumor clearance (Figure 6).

Markers shown adjacent to each major B-cell stage (B cell progenitors, immature-naive [I-N], memory B cells, and plasmablast/plasma cell [PB/PC]) indicate relative changes in expression observed following RVd or CyBorD treatment. Shared changes are shown in gray–purple, whereas regimen-specific changes are depicted in pink (RVd) and green (CyBorD). Arrowheads denote the direction of change (increase or decrease).

MYD88 transduces TLR signals via the NF-κB and MAPK pathways to support germinal center expansion and plasma cell longevity during normal B-cell development.23,24 In MM, despite the absence of the MYD88 L265P oncoprotein observed in Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia and ABC DLBCL, intact wild type MYD88 signaling in malignant PC can promote survival, drive autocrine IL-6-mediated prosurvival cytokine production within the bone marrow niche, and confer resistance to proteasome inhibitors and immunomodulatory drugs.25 MYD88 was consistently upregulated in unswitched memory B cells by both therapies, implicating MYD88-driven signaling in the treatment response. Notably, RVd broadly induced MYD88 and c-Myc across early progenitors and multiple B cell subsets, indicating the activation of proliferative and survival pathways that might reflect both tumor adaptation and immune engagement. CyBorD modulated MYD88 and c-Myc in restricted compartments, suggesting divergent impact on tumor microenvironment interactions and malignant cell plasticity. In normal B cell development, c-Myc promotes early B cell proliferation and transiently fuels germinal center responses under BCL6 control before yielding to IRF4 and BLIMP-1 during terminal differentiation.26 During early B lymphopoiesis, Pax5-driven activation of CD19 and BLK cooperates with c‑Myc–mediated signaling to fuel pro‑B to pre‑B proliferation and survival, while high CXCR4 expression anchors these progenitors in the CXCL12‑rich bone marrow niche until maturation cues permit egress.27 Emerging Notch‑1 induction upon pre‑BCR engagement then amplifies progenitor expansion via Hes1 transcriptional activation, priming cells for downstream germinal center entry.28 Spliced XBP1 (sXBP1) drives endoplasmic reticulum biogenesis and reinforces IRF4/BLIMP-1-mediated transcription to sustain the high capacity immunoglobulin synthesis and secretory function of terminally differentiated plasma cells.29 In MM, hypoxia driven CXCR4 overexpression in the bone marrow niche enhances tumor cell survival and angiogenesis, while ligand induced Notch 1 activation variably promotes survival or apoptotic sensitization, and MYC translocations/amplifications drive c-Myc-dependent proliferation and apoptosis evasion via Mcl-1/Bcl-xL upregulation, with t(4;14) associated FGFR3/MMSET overexpression further activating MAPK/STAT3 to block differentiation.30,31 Concurrently, loss of spliced XBP1 correlates with high risk cytogenetics, advanced stage and proteasome inhibitor resistance, reversible upon sXBP1 restoration, whereas aberrant Blimp-1 reexpression in proliferating myeloma cells partners with Aiolos to repress pro apoptotic genes, highlighting both as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.32 Both RVd and CyBorD converged on suppressing CXCR4 and FGFR3 in PC clusters, likely disrupting homing and survival signaling critical to myeloma persistence. RVd's unique induction of NOTCH1 in unswitched memory B cells and FGFR3 in switched memory B cells, along with the selective reduction of CXCR4 and sXBP1 in plasmablasts and plasma cells, suggests targeted disruption of malignant niche interactions and the unfolded protein response. In contrast, CyBorD upregulated sXBP1 and BLIMP1 in memory and plasma cell compartments, highlighting the distinct regulation of differentiation and stress response pathways. Lenalidomide induces Blimp-1 degradation via ubiquitination, derepressing targets such as CUL4A and reducing Aiolos to promote MM cell death,32 while CRISPR Blimp-1 knockout in anti-BCMA CAR-T cells enhances memory-like traits, protein synthesis, and antitumor activity, indicating that Blimp-1 is a target to increase T-cell function and limit exhaustion.33 In RVd responders, Blimp-1 and FGFR3 are selectively downregulated in the PC4 compartment, a change that likely diminishes plasma-cell survival/differentiation and oncogenic FGFR3 signaling. Unlike healthy plasma cells, which downregulate BCL2 upon differentiation, malignant plasma cells in the bone marrow maintain high BCL2 levels, reinforced by NF κB and STAT3 signaling, to sequester pro-apoptotic proteins and evade chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Notably, roughly 10% of MM cases, particularly those harboring t(11;14), show BCL2 overexpression driven by promoter hypomethylation, loss of miR15/16, or clonal evolution, further supporting its role as a therapeutic target.34 Differential BCL2 induction by each regimen in early progenitors reflects varied BCL2 dependence and reveals a vulnerability that could be exploited, for example, with BH3 mimetics or NF-κB/STAT3 pathway inhibitors, to overcome resistance in relapsed/refractory MM. Both RVd and CyBorD reprogram B-cell transcription, promoting B-cell identity (PAX5 and BCL2 upregulation in B-cell progenitors and IRF4, CXCR4, and FGFR3 downregulation in plasma cells) while inducing regimen-specific changes in pathways (MYD88, c-Myc, NOTCH1, sXBP1, BLIMP1) that likely drive differential treatment responses and reveal therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Key stemness regulators such as OCT3/4 (POU5F1), SOX2, NANOG and KLF4, together with nestin and the retinoic-acid receptor isoform RARα2, are essential for pluripotency and self-renewal, while also forming an integrated network that orchestrates early B-cell development.35-38 During early B-cell ontogeny, low OCT3/4 cooperates with SOX2 and transient NANOG expression to maintain progenitor identity, while KLF4 and nestin peak in pro- and pre-B cells to regulate cell cycle progression, survival and cytoskeletal integrity, and RARα2-mediated retinoic-acid signaling drives germinal-center formation, affinity maturation and class switching. As cells traverse the plasmablast stage and commit to the plasma-cell lineage, expression of OCT3/4, SOX2, NANOG and nestin progressively declines, RARα2 continues to enforce lineage-specific gene programs, and elevated or experimentally induced KLF4 activity imposes cell-cycle arrest and balanced apoptosis to promote terminal plasma-cell differentiation.35,36,38-40 In MM, aberrant reactivation of this stemness network endows CD138⁺ plasma cells with enhanced self-renewal, apoptosis resistance and clonogenic potential: OCT3/4, SOX2 and NANOG rekindle pluripotency circuits; KLF4 drives prosurvival autophagy via SQSTM1/p62; nestin stimulates PI3K/AKT- and NF-κB-dependent proliferation; and RARα2 activates the Wnt and Hedgehog pathways while upregulating ABCC3 and Bcl-2 to confer chemoresistance.17,41,42 These changes drive tumor growth, relapse, drug resistance, and an immunosuppressive bone-marrow niche that promotes MM; therefore, we compared RVd versus CyBorD by profiling B-cell stemness. Both therapies uniformly upregulated KLF4 in immature, transitional, and memory B cells, as well as in PC clusters, suggesting the shared induction of a stemness-associated transcriptional program potentially involved in cellular plasticity and survival. The uniform elevation of KLF4 might contribute to a resilient subpopulation that persists under therapy, representing a target for strategies aimed at preventing minimal residual disease. In contrast, NANOG expression exhibited treatment- and subset-specific dynamics. While both regimens increased NANOG levels in unswitched memory B cells, CyBorD uniquely increased NANOG in additional compartments, including immature, switched memory B cells, and PCs, indicating broader activation of self-renewal pathways that may underpin adaptive resistance. Conversely, RVd selectively diminished NANOG in plasmablasts and PCs and notably suppressed NANOG across multiple B-cell stages among clinical responders. These findings suggest that downregulation of NANOG may be a hallmark of effective anti-myeloma activity with RVd, implicating NANOG as both a biomarker of response and a potential node to sensitize resistant cells. RVd suppressed OCT3/4 in pro-B, plasmablast and PC clusters, supporting the targeting of core pluripotency factors, though paradoxical upregulation in pro-B cells of responders suggests complex cell-state remodeling that may prime subsets for differentiation or immune clearance. CyBorD uniquely modulated nestin in PC clusters, suggesting that altered cytoskeletal/niche interactions that influence malignant fitness and drug sensitivity. The differential regulation of RARα2, which is decreased by CyBorD in pro-B and by RVd in responder PCs, also highlights retinoid signaling as a convergent pathway influenced by these regimens, offering a candidate axis for therapeutic modulation to disrupt tumor maintenance (Figure 6). By mapping these dynamic shifts, we reveal how therapeutic reprogramming of the stemness network underlies treatment responses and resistance mechanisms, and point toward targeted strategies to disrupt malignant self-renewal and restore effective anti-tumor immunity in MM.

Among malignant plasma cell-specific targets, CD319 (SLAMF7) is ubiquitously expressed on normal and malignant plasma cells, conferring superior diagnostic sensitivity and prognostic stratification.43 CD56, expressed in 65%–80% of MM cases but is absent in normal plasma cells, is linked to osteolysis and extramedullary spread. Its absence correlates with poor outcomes by increasing MMP-9-mediated invasion, whereas its overexpression enhances proteasome inhibitor-induced apoptosis.44 In 30% of MM patients, aberrant CD117 expression activates PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling to promote malignant plasma cell survival; CD117⁺ cells may reflect favorable marrow profiles, but CD117⁺CD56⁻ phenotypes are associated with aggressive, drug-resistant disease.45 MMSET, a histone methyltransferase overexpressed in t(4;14) MM (15% of cases), catalyzes H3K36 demethylation to activate oncogenes (MAF, c-MYC), represses tumor suppressors via Sin3a/HDAC1/LSD1 interactions, and upregulates cell-cycle and anti-apoptotic genes, correlating with poor prognosis.46 Finally, CD44 variants (v6, v9) are upregulated in aggressive and extramedullary MM, mediating hyaluronan-driven proteasome inhibitor resistance through γ-secretase-mediated cleavage of CD44’s intracellular domain, and elevated soluble CD44 levels are associated with poor prognosis.47

Both RVd and CyBorD therapies induced broadly overlapping immunophenotypic changes across the B cell continuum, reflecting shared mechanisms that affected myeloma biology at multiple differentiation stages. Early downregulation of MMSET, CD200, and CD362 in B progenitors likely impaired epigenetic and microenvironmental support essential for malignant propagation. Concurrent suppression of CD52 in early and immature B cells may reduce tumor-cell survival and niche retention, while upregulation of CD47 in transitional and memory B cells may increase “don’t eat me” signaling, contributing to immune evasion. Both regimens consistently increased CD81 expression in naive B cells and PC clusters, indicating that the modulation of adhesion and signaling pathways is relevant to differentiation and therapeutic responsiveness. The upregulation of CD329 (Siglec-9) and CD44 in memory B cell compartments highlighted the importance of immunomodulatory and adhesion molecules potentially critical for myeloma cell migration and immune interactions. The downregulation of MMSET and CD319 in memory B cells and PCs suggested the impairment of epigenetic regulation and tumor-promoting signaling. Importantly, both treatments also decreased CD28, CD47, and CD200 expression in plasma cells-markers associated with tumor growth and immune evasion, indicating a therapeutic impact on malignant survival and microenvironmental crosstalk. The elevation of CD338 (ABCG2) in PCs may have reflected selection of drug-resistant subclones, highlighting a potential mechanism of treatment escape (Figure 6).

Among additional plasma cell-associated markers, CD200, normally restricted to early B‐cell precursors and memory B cells, is aberrantly overexpressed approximately in 70% of MM cases, where it facilitates immune evasion, correlates with advanced disease, reduced overall survival, and the adverse rs1131199 GG genotype.48 In parallel, CD28, ubiquitously expressed on plasma cells, is upregulated in high-risk MM, where CD28-CD86 autocrine signaling activates PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways, effectively substituting for IGF-1/IL-6 signaling to promote cell survival, and engages bone marrow dendritic cells to induce IL-6 and IDO production, further sustaining tumor persistence.49 By contrast, CD81, a tetraspanin sustaining CD19 trafficking in the BCR coreceptor complex, persists on maturing plasma cells; in MM, high CD81 marks poorly differentiated CD19⁺CD81⁺ clones with distinct mutation profiles that drive chemoresistance, relapse, and rapid progression from smoldering to active disease.50 Several cell surface markers implicated in MM pathogenesis include BCMA (CD269), which is essential for plasmablast and plasma cell survival and is uniformly overexpressed on MM cells despite γ-secretase-mediated shedding that generates soluble BCMA. Membrane-bound BCMA activates NF-κB to sustain proliferation and immune suppression and serves as a key target in MM immunotherapy.51 Moreover, syndecan‐2 (CD362), a heparan sulfate proteoglycan, is upregulated in hsc and early B‐lineage cells across MGUS to relapsed MM, where it activates NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and Notch-1 to drive proliferation, survival, and drug resistance, while its sulfated glycosaminoglycans promote niche adhesion.17 Siglec-9 (CD329), enriched on switched memory B cells in MM, delivers inhibitory signals via ITIMs to recruit SHP-1/2 and dampen anti‐tumor immunity, correlating with disease progression.17,52 CD47, “don’t eat me” receptor, is upregulated from MGUS to MM, especially in cases with 17p deletions and high β2-microglobulin, to bind SIRPα on macrophages, inhibit phagocytosis, and associate with proteasome inhibitor resistance and poor survival.17,53 Finally, CD52, which normally attenuates BCR signaling and enforces tolerance in naïve and memory B cells, is largely lost on CD45⁻CD38⁺⁺ MM plasma cells; its residual expression in a minor CD45⁺ subset aligns with c-MAF/FGFR3 translocations but has not translated into clinical benefit from alemtuzumab.54

Despite these parallels, RVd and CyBorD impart distinct molecular footprints. RVd uniquely upregulated CD47 and CD81 in early B-cell progenitors and transitional B cells, along with CD269 (BCMA) in pre-BI cells, while downregulating CD289 and CD362 in early B-cell progenitors, suggesting enhanced targeting of immune-evasion and pro-survival pathways that may increase malignant-cell persistence and resistance to clearance, which could help explain its clinical efficacy in disrupting malignant niches. The responders to RVd specifically showed decreased CD200 and CD47 in early B cell progenitors, together with increased CD52 and CD243 (P-glycoprotein) expression, potentially reflecting adaptive changes in immune regulation and drug efflux that affect treatment sensitivity. During the transitional stages of B-cell maturation, RVd uniquely elevates CD81, CD329, and CD44, and downregulated CD200, changes that may reduce immune evasion, alter adhesion and homing to supportive niches, and thereby modulate microenvironmental interactions. CyBorD imparted unique modulation by decreasing CD362, CD243, and CD52 in HSCs and early progenitors, as well as CD200 and CD44 in early B-cell progenitors, while increasing CD47 and CD200 in B-cell progenitors and transitional B cells, indicating differential impacts on immune checkpoints, cell‒cell adhesion, and progenitor survival. Conversely, CyBorD specifically decreased CD319 and CD52 in immature and transitional B cells, which may alter signaling important for activation and niche retention. In the memory B cell compartment, RVd induced CD243 and CD362 in unswitched memory B cells while decreasing MMSET, and lowered CD289 in switched memory B cells, changes that may influence antigen responsiveness, epigenetic regulation, and migratory behavior. In contrast, CyBorD uniquely increased CD81 and CD44 in memory B cell populations, suggesting that enhanced adhesion and signaling could affect trafficking and cell survival. In mature plasma cells and plasmablast clusters, RVd induced CD81 and CD117 expression while suppressing BCMA (CD269), CD289, CD200, MMSET, and CD319, indicating broad suppression of survival and adhesion molecules and potential disruption of plasma-cell niches and pro-tumor signaling. The observed downregulation of CD329 also suggested a diminished immune-evasion capacity. Conversely, CyBorD increased CD329 in plasma cells but reduced CD243, CD44, CD362, and CD52, which may reflect distinct remodeling of adhesion, drug-resistance, and immune-escape pathways. Overall, both regimens remodel B cell phenotypes to impair tumor-supportive epigenetic and microenvironmental programs while leaving distinct molecular footprints in adhesion, immune-checkpoint, and drug-resistance pathways that may drive differential response and resistance (Figure 6).

Specific immunophenotypic signatures may inform first-line therapy selection between RVd and CyBorD. Both regimens reinforced B-cell identity, suppressed tumor-supporting markers (MMSET, CD200, CD362, CD52), and increased KLF4, indicating shared anti-myeloma mechanisms and potential targets to prevent relapse. RVd demonstrated broader MYD88/c-Myc activation, reduced NANOG expression, and distinctive CD47/CD81 modulation, consistent with enhanced clearance of MYD88/c-Myc–driven disease. In contrast, CyBorD activated BLIMP1 and sXBP1, implicated retinoid pathways, and remodeled adhesion and immune-evasion markers, suggesting preferential efficacy in plasma cell–dominant or stress-adapted myeloma phenotypes. Following RVd or CyBorD failure, key therapeutic vulnerabilities included persistent immune-evasion signals (CD47, CD200), drug-efflux activity (CD338/ABCG2), and altered adhesion and epigenetic regulators (CD81, CD44, MMSET). Targeting these pathways, together with BCL2, MYD88, c-Myc, FGFR3, and CXCR4 signaling, may help overcome resistance, while modulation of BCMA and CD329 supported antigen-directed strategies, and inhibition of stemness and niche factors (NANOG, OCT3/4, nestin, RARα2) could prevent relapse. Collectively, these findings provide a framework for integrating immunophenotypic profiling into therapeutic decision-making, enabling more precise selection and sequencing of myeloma treatments to improve clinical outcomes.
