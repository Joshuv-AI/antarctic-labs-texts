Equilibrium Propagation for Learning in Lagrangian Dynamical Systems
Serge Massar

Abstract
We propose a method for training dynamical systems governed by Lagrangian mechanics using Equilibrium Propagation. Our approach extends Equilibrium Propagation – initially developed for energy-based models – to dynamical trajectories by leveraging the principle of action extremization. Training is achieved by gently nudging trajectories toward desired targets and measuring how the variables conjugate to the parameters to be trained respond. This method is particularly suited to systems with periodic boundary conditions or fixed initial and final states, enabling efficient parameter updates without requiring explicit backpropagation through time. In the case of periodic boundary conditions, this approach yields the semiclassical limit of Quantum Equilibrium Propagation. Applications to systems with dissipation are also discussed.



I
Introduction

Most neural networks used in machine learning, such as feedforward networks, are static rather than dynamic. By this we mean that they
do not evolve dynamically during the inference phase. There are some exceptions such as recurrent neural networks (see e.g.
Mienye et al. 2024
for a recent review) and reservoir computing (see e.g.
Nakajima and Fischer 2021
), as well as neural differential equations (see
Oh et al. 2025
for a recent review) which themselves are connected to control theory
Weinan 2017
;
Ruiz-Balet and Zuazua 2023

The most straightforward method to train the internal parameters of such time dependent neural networks is backpropagation through time, see for instance
Hermans et al. 2015
for its use in the case of reservoir computing.
However one may wish for more efficient training mechanisms which allow all parameters to be updated at once. One may also wish for training methods adapted to the specific dynamical systems arising in classical mechanics, as these are particularly relevant for potential implementation in physical systems.
An interesting advance in this respect is
López-Pastor and Marquardt 2023
in which dynamical systems governed by Hamiltonian dynamics are trained using both a forward and backward pass through the system, exploiting the time reversal invariance of the dynamics.

Here we show how dynamical systems governed by Lagrangian dynamics can be trained using Equilibrium Propagation
Scellier and Bengio 2017
.
Compared to
López-Pastor and Marquardt 2023
, we note that Lagrangian mechanics is slightly more general, allowing for instance to incorporate some forms of dissipation. The main differences with respect to
López-Pastor and Marquardt 2023
are on the one hand that we do not need to explicitly propagate through the system backwards in time, but on the other hand our approach only applies to certain classes of trajectories, such as periodic trajectories or trajectories with fixed initial and final conditions. The use of Equilibrium Propagation for training dynamical systems with time varying inputs was introduced independently in
Scellier 2021
.

Equilibrium Propagation, as formulated in
Scellier and Bengio 2017
, uses a neural network that minimises an energy functional. Reaching the minimum energy state takes time (either real time in a physical system, or computational time). Once the equilibrium state is reached, the training of the network is highly efficient. It is obtained by nudging the output in the desired direction and measuring how the variables conjugate to the trainable parameters shift in response to the nudge.
While reaching the minimum energy state is time consuming in digital implementations, it could be highly efficient in physical implementations that relax fast to their low energy configuration.
Conceptually, one expects that the Equilibrium Propagation approach can be used for training any machine learning system that is operated in a state that extremises a functional (not necessarily the energy). This was for instance the basis for extending Equilibrium Propagation to the
quantum case
Massar and Mognetti 2025
;
Scellier 2024
;
Wanjura and Marquardt 2024
or to the thermal case
Scellier and Bengio 2017
;
Massar and Mognetti 2025
. It is also the basis for the present generalisation to dynamical systems, the “equilibrium” condition being here replaced by the condition that the equations of motion extremise the classical action.
Equilibrium Propagation was improved in
Laborieux et al. 2021
where symmetric nudging of the output that removes biases and improves performance was introduced. It was extended to vector field dynamics

Scellier et al. 2018
, to continuous time updates
Ernoult et al. 2020
, to spiking neural networks
Martin et al. 2021
, to large nudgings of the output
Laborieux and Zenke 2022
. For a comparison with other energy based models, see
Scellier et al. 2023
.

The most interesting boundary conditions for Equilibrium Propagation are probably the periodic boundary conditions, as these can most naturally arise in applications. Below we show how they give rise to the semiclassical limit of Quantum Equilibrium Propagation. We also show how to incorporate dissipation in the formalism.

In the remainder of this paper we first set up the problem and define our notations. Then we show how to apply equilibrium propagation to dynamical systems obeying Euler-Lagrange equations.

II
Setting up the problem

II.1
General formulation

Let
{L}_{0}(x,\dot{x},t,\alpha,u)
be some Lagrangian depending on position

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, velocity
\dot{x}=(\dot{x}_{1},\dot{x}_{2},\dots,\dot{x}_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, time
t
, some parameters
\alpha
, and on the input
u
.

Let
C(x,\dot{x},t,u)
be a cost density that measures how much the trajectory differs from the desired trajectory.
The total cost
{\cal C}
is the time integral of the cost density:

{\cal C}(x,\dot{x},t,u)=\int\!C(x,t,u)\,dt

(1)

The total Lagrangian is

{L}(x,\dot{x},t,\alpha,\beta,u)={L}_{0}(x,\dot{x},t,\alpha,u)+\beta{C}(x,\dot{x},t,u)

(2)

where we have multiplied the cost by a parameter
\beta
which will be used to nudge the trajectory towards (or away from) the desired trajectory.
Although it is not essential for what follows, physical systems have a Lagrangian
L
quadratic in
\dot{x}
. The quadratic terms in
\dot{x}
correspond to the kinetic energy, linear terms in
\dot{x}
can be interpreted as resulting from a magnetic field, and the term independent of
\dot{x}
is minus the potential energy.

The Euler Lagrange equations are

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial{L}}{\partial{\dot{x}_{j}}}-\frac{\partial{L}}{\partial{x_{j}}}=0\ .

(3)

Their solution is denoted with the subscript
*

x_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u)\ .

(4)

We also denote by the subscript
*
quantities evaluated on the solution, for instance
the Lagrangian evaluated on a solution is

{L}_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u)={L}(x_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u),\dot{x}_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u),t,\alpha,\beta,u)\ ,

(5)

while the cost on a solution is

{\cal C}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u)=\int\!C(x_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u),\dot{x}_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u),t,u)\,dt\ ,

(6)

and the action evaluated on a solution (the on-shell action) is

{\cal S}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u)=\int\!{L}_{*}(t,\alpha,\beta,u)\,dt\ .

(7)

The aim is, given a set of training examples, to adjust the parameters
\alpha
in order to minimize the average cost (over the inputs
u
) when
\beta=0
:

\min_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{u}\left[{\cal C}_{*}(\alpha,\beta=0,u)\right]\ .

(8)

II.2
Illustrative example

To be concrete, we consider an example. We suppose that
{L}_{0}=L_{S0}+D
is the sum of two terms. The first term
L_{S0}
describes a dynamical system unperturbed by the input:

\displaystyle{L}_{S0}(x,\dot{x},t,\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}\sum_{j}\dot{x_{j}}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}+\mu(t)\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq k}\alpha_{jk}\rho(x_{j})\rho(x_{k})+\nu(t)\sum_{j}\alpha^{\prime}_{j}\rho(x_{j})\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\mu(t)\frac{1}{2}\rho(x)\alpha\rho(x)+\nu(t)\alpha^{\prime}\rho(x)\right)

where

m
is a mass,

\mu(t)
and
\nu(t)
are time dependent functions,

\rho
is a sigmoid function such as

\rho(\cdot)=\tanh(\cdot)
.
In this example there are
d(d+3)/2
trainable parameters corresponding to the independent components of the symmetric matrix
\alpha_{ij}
and the
d
components of the vector
\alpha^{\prime}_{j}
. In the second line we have introduced the compact notation in which sums over indices are not written explicitly.

The second term in the Lagrangian, which encodes forces that depend on the inputs, is a
u
–dependent potential
D(t,x,u)
. The corresponding forces are denoted
F_{u}=-\nabla D
.
For instance if the input were images drawn from the MNIST database, the grey value of each pixel could determine the force acting on the corresponding coordinate through the potential

\displaystyle D(x,t,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t)\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in J_{\text{in}}}(x_{j}(t)-u_{j})^{2}

(10)

where

J_{\text{in}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
is
a subset of the dynamical variables and

\delta(t)
is some time dependent function, for instance a gaussian. The task in this case would be to classify the images.
Note that we can clamp the inputs (by taking
\delta
to be arbitrarily large in Eq. (
10
)), so that the variables
x_{j}
,
j\in J_{\text{in}}
, are clamped for all time to the values
u_{j}
.

As an alternative example, the input could consist in trajectories
z(t,u)
, for instance generated by another dynamical system with parameters
u
. The potential could then take the form

\displaystyle D(x,t,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t)\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in J_{\text{in}}}(x_{j}(t)-z_{j}(t,u))^{2}

(11)

and the task could for instance consist in reconstructing the parameters
u
controlling the other dynamical system.

Our aim is for the trajectory to follow a target trajectory
y(t,u)
that depends on the input
u
. To this end the
cost density
C(t,x,u)
, which measures how close the trajectory is to
y(t,u)
, is given by

C(x,t,u)=\gamma(t)\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in J_{\text{out}}}(x_{j}(t)-y_{j}(t,u))^{2}

(12)

where
J_{\text{out}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
is the subset of the dynamical variables used for readout, and
\gamma(t)
is some time dependent weight, for instance a gaussian.
The corresponding force is
F_{C}=-\nabla C
.

For classification tasks
y(t,u)
would naturally be taken to be time independent. For instance for the MNIST database, one would have
10
outputs
|J_{\text{out}}|=10
, and one would use one-hot encoding. However the output could also be a time dependent function, for instance a sine whose frequency encodes the output.

Note that if the kinetic term is neglected (large mass limit), and the time dependent functions are all taken to be constant
\mu(t)=\nu(t)=\delta(t)=\gamma(t)=1
, then we recover identically the problem of
Scellier and Bengio 2017
. The novelty is that we now have a non trivial time dependence.

III
Equilibrium Propagation

Let
\alpha_{j}
be one of the parameters entering the Lagrangian. Given a training example (input
u
and target
y(t,u)
) we wish to update the parameter
\alpha_{j}
according to

\alpha_{j}\to\alpha_{j}-\lambda\frac{d{\cal C}_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}

(13)

with
\lambda
a learning rate. Iterating this procedure will decrease the expected cost Eq. (
8
).

Let us compute the derivative
\frac{d{\cal S}_{*}}{d\beta}
of the on-shell action with respect to
\beta
:

\displaystyle\frac{d{\cal S}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u)}{d\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\left(\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}\frac{d\dot{x_{*}}}{d\beta}+\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial x}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}+\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\beta}\right)\,dt

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\left(\left(-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}+\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial x}\right)\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}+\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\beta}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}+{\cal C}_{*}

where summation over the
d
components of
x_{*}
or
\dot{x}_{*}
is understood as in Eq. (
9
).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d{\cal S}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u)}{d\alpha_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt\ .

(15)

We use the equality

\frac{d^{2}{\cal S}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u)}{d\alpha_{j}d\beta}=\frac{d^{2}{\cal S}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u)}{d\beta d\alpha_{j}}

(16)

to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d{\cal C}_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}(\alpha,\beta,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\beta}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt+\frac{d}{d\beta}\left[\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}-\frac{d}{d\alpha_{j}}\left[\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\beta}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt+\left[{B}_{*}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}

\displaystyle{B}_{*}(\alpha,\beta,u,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}^{2}}\left(\frac{d\dot{x}_{*}}{d\beta}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}-\frac{d\dot{x}_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}\right)+\left(\frac{\partial^{2}{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}\partial\beta}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}-\frac{\partial^{2}{L}_{*}}{\partial\dot{x}\partial\alpha_{j}}\frac{dx_{*}}{d\beta}\right)

(18)

where we have used the symmetry of the matrices

\frac{\partial^{2}{L}_{*}}{\partial x_{j}\partial\dot{x}_{k}}
and

\frac{dx_{*_{j}}}{d\alpha_{j}}\frac{dx_{*_{k}}}{d\alpha_{j}}
to simplify the expression of the boundary term Eq. (
18
).

Let us suppose that the boundary term
\left[{B}_{*}\right]^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}
vanishes (we discuss how to impose this condition in the next section).
Then we can estimate the derivative of the cost function by finite differences.
We compute two trajectories
x_{*}(\pm\beta)
which are nudged by the small positive and negative values of
\beta
–dependent cost term. From these trajectories, we can obtain the derivative

\frac{d{\cal C}_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}
by evaluating through integration how the variable conjugate to
\alpha_{j}
, i.e.
\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt
, changes when we change the cost. We thus obtain

\frac{d{\cal C}_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}\approx\frac{1}{2\beta}\left(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\frac{\partial{L}_{*}(+\beta)}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt-\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\frac{\partial{L}_{*}(-\beta)}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt\right)\ .

(19)

This is the analog for dynamical systems of the Equilibrium Propagation update rule proposed in
Scellier and Bengio 2017
and then improved in
Laborieux et al. 2021
. Note the economy of the method: from only two trajectories, one nudged towards the desired trajectory (
\beta
positive) and one nudged in the opposite direction (
\beta
negative) we obtain all the gradients, and hence can update all the parameters.

IV
Vanishing of boundary terms

IV.1
Causal boundary conditions do not work

Equation (
19
) is only valid if the boundary terms in Eq. (
17
) vanish. We now discuss when this is the case. This requires imposing appropriate boundary conditions.

Note that the usual boundary conditions, in which the initial position
x(t_{i})
and velocity
\dot{x}(t_{i})
are specified, do not work.
(It can even be the case that the first term in Eq. (
19
) vanishes and the gradient
d{\cal C}_{*}/{d\alpha_{j}}
is entirely given by the boundary terms. This is, for instance, the case in the example given in Figure
1
panel (a) where the right hand side of Eq. (
19
) vanishes since, due to the order in which
F_{\alpha}
and the nudging towards
y(t,u)
are applied,

\frac{\partial{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}
does not depend on
\beta
).

IV.2
Fixed initial and final position

The simplest boundary condition that ensures that the boundary terms in Eq. (
17
) vanish is to fix both the initial and final positions:

\displaystyle x_{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle x_{*}(t_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{f}\ .

(20)

Examples are depicted in Fig.
1
, panels (c) and (d).

IV.3
Fixed initial and final velocity

Another possible boundary condition is to fix both the initial and final velocity. This ensures that the first boundary term in Eq. (
17
) vanishes (since then
d\dot{x}_{*}/d\beta=d\dot{x}_{*}/d\alpha_{j}=0
on the boundary). However an additional condition must be added so that the second boundary term vanishes, namely the
dependence of Lagrangian on
\beta
and
\alpha_{j}
must vanish at the initial and final time as expressed in the following conditions

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{*}(t_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{f}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{L}}{\partial\beta\partial\dot{x}}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{L}}{\partial\beta\partial\dot{x}}(t_{f})=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{L}}{\partial\alpha_{j}\partial\dot{x}}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{L}}{\partial\alpha_{j}\partial\dot{x}}(t_{f})=0\ .

(21)

The last two conditions are easy to implement: they require either that the cost and terms in the Lagrangian that depend on the trainable parameters
\alpha
contain no dependence on the velocities, or that the cost and terms in the Lagrangian that depend on
\alpha
vanish at the intial and final time
t_{i}
and
t_{f}
.

IV.4
Periodic boundary conditions

IV.4.1
General condition

Particularly interesting are periodic boundary conditions:

\displaystyle x_{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{*}(t_{f})

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{*}(t_{f})

\displaystyle{L}(x,\dot{x},t_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{L}(x,\dot{x},t_{f})\ .

(22)

It is useful to rephrase them by extending the Lagrangian and trajectories to periodic functions, using
t_{f}=t_{i}+T
, as

\displaystyle{L}(x,\dot{x},t+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{L}(x,\dot{x},t)

(23)

\displaystyle x_{*}(t+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{*}(t)\ .

(24)

This is illustrated in Fig.
1
, panels (b).

IV.4.2
Time independent case and Hamiltonian formalism

Before analysing in more detail periodic boundary conditions, it is useful to recall the Hamiltonian formulation of the equations of motion.
The momentum is defined by

p_{i}(x,\dot{x},t)=\frac{\partial{L}(x,\dot{x},t)}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\ .

(25)

The energy is defined by

{E}(x,\dot{x},t)=\sum_{i}p_{i}(x,\dot{x},t)\dot{x}_{i}-{L}(x,\dot{x},t)\ .

(26)

The action can then be rewritten as

{\cal S}=\int\!\sum_{i}p_{i}\dot{x}_{i}-{E}\,dt\ .

(27)

Suppose that the Lagrangian does not have any explicit time dependence. Then
we can impose
x_{*}(t+T)=x_{*}(t)
, but leave
T
a free parameter, and
impose other constraints.
We consider three such constraints.

Constant solution.

We can take the solutions to be constant
\dot{x}_{*}=0
, in which case the system is at a local minimum of the energy. We then recover the original problem of
Scellier and Bengio 2017
, since the time dependence has disapeared.

Fixed energy.

We can take the solutions to be periodic with fixed energy
E
. In this case the period
T
is not fixed, and will adjust itself in order to satisfy the condition on the energy.

Fixed reduced action.

We can take the solutions to be periodic with fixed reduced action

{\cal S}_{0}=\oint\!\sum_{i}p_{i}\dot{x}_{i}\,dt=\oint\!p\,dx

(28)

In this case the period
T
is not fixed, and will adjust itself in order to satisfy the condition on
S_{0}
.

Imposing fixed reduced action
S_{0}
can be viewed as the semiclassical version of Quantum Equilibrium Propagation
Massar and Mognetti 2025
;
Scellier 2024
;
Wanjura and Marquardt 2024
. Indeed the semi-classical quantization conditions is

\oint\!p\,dx=2\pi(n+\alpha)\hbar

(29)

with
n
integer and
\alpha
the Maslov index of the trajectory
Percival 1977
.

IV.5
Damped equations of motion

If the equations of motion include damping, then they are no longer symmetric with respect to time reversal. There are several methods to introduce damping in the Lagrangian formalism, see e.g.
Riewe 1996
.
Here we consider a specific method, and consider how it affects equilibrium propagation.

To include damping, we take the original Lagrangian Eq. (
2
) and multiply it by
e^{\Gamma t}
to obtain

\tilde{L}(x,\dot{x},t,\alpha,\beta,u)=e^{\Gamma t}{L}(x,\dot{x},t,\alpha,\beta,u)\ .

(30)

The new equations of motion are the old equations of motion plus a damping term:

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial{L}}{\partial{\dot{x}_{i}}}+\Gamma\frac{\partial{L}}{\partial{\dot{x}_{i}}}-\frac{\partial{L}}{\partial{x_{i}}}=0\ .

(31)

If we apply fixed initial and final position, or fixed initial and final velocity, as in Subsections
IV.2
and
IV.3
, we can apply Equilibrium Propagation as before. The only difference is that the time dependent functions
\delta(t)
and
\gamma(t)
in Eqs. (
10
) and (
12
) are multiplied by
e^{\Gamma t}
, but this can be absorbed in a redefinition of
\delta(t)
and
\gamma(t)
.

Let us now consider periodic boundary conditions. We suppose that the original Lagrangian
{L}
is periodic in time with period
T
, see Eq. (
23
). However with damping the boundary terms no longer vanish becasue the new Lagrangian

\tilde{L}
is not periodic in time.
However, if the damping is weak, the boundary terms will be small, as we now show.
Suppose
x_{*}
is a solution of the
the damped equations of motion Eq. (
31
) with
periodic boundary conditions, Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
).

One finds that Eq. (
17
) becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\cal C}_{*}}{d\alpha_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\beta}\int_{0}^{T}\!\frac{\partial\tilde{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt+(e^{\Gamma T}-1){B}(0)

(32)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\beta}\int_{0}^{T}\!\frac{\partial\tilde{L}_{*}}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\,dt+\Gamma T{B}(0)

where
{B}(t)
is the boundary term Eq. (
18
) computed for the original Lagrangian
{L}
, and
where in Eq. (
32
) we have supposed that the damping is small
\Gamma T\ll 1
. We see that as the damping decreases, the error made if one neglects the boundary terms becomes smaller.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have generalized the framework of Equilibrium Propagation to the training of dynamical systems governed by Lagrangian mechanics, with the cost expressed as a functional that can be included in the Lagrangian. By exploiting the structure of the underlying dynamics, we derived an efficient method to compute the gradient of the cost function with respect to the trainable parameters.
This approach requires only two perturbed trajectories to estimate all parameter updates.

Importantly, we showed that successful application of this method hinges on the choice of appropriate boundary conditions: periodic trajectories or trajectories with fixed initial and final states. In particular, periodic boundary conditions are especially attractive, as they naturally connect to the semiclassical limit of quantum Equilibrium Propagation and can accommodate weak dissipation effects.

Our results show that Equilibrium Propagation can be extended beyond
static energy–based models to a broad class of dynamical systems, including those with time–dependent or dissipative effects. This opens new avenues for training physical, analog, neuromorphic computing systems, where the natural dynamics of the system can be harnessed for learning.

Future work may explore the implementation of these ideas in physical systems, the extension to stochastic dynamics, and connections with Hamiltonian-based approaches and control theory.
