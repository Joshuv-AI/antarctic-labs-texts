Short-chain fatty acids mediate interactions between immune responses and commensal bacteria in high altitude yaks
Authors: Na Guo, Nana Gou, Fuyu Shi, Wenyin Wang, Shanshan Li, Sisi Bi, Jianxin Jiao, Binyu Luo, Mei Huang, A Allan Degen, Zhanhuan Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complex interplay between host and commensal gut microbiota affects the major biological functions such as metabolism and stress adaptation, and displays pronounced seasonality in mammals. However, the seasonal dynamic patterns of immune responses and microbiota, and their interactions remain uncertain in animals inhabiting extreme environments. We analyzed monthly hormones, immunoglobulins and fecal microbiota from yaks grazing on the Tibetan plateau. Clear seasonal patterns were observed: glucocorticoid levels peaked in the cold season, while concentrations of IgA, IgG, IgM, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) increased during the warm season. Yak fecal microbiota also fluctuated seasonally, with lowest diversity in the warm season but accompanied by an enrichment of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria. Taxa such as Alistipes, Bacteroides, Romboutsia and Arthrobacter contributed to seasonal shifts in the levels of SCFAs and immunoglobulins. These results indicate that yaks synchronize peak immune activation and energy production with the nutrient-rich warm season, suggesting a role for microbiome plasticity in driving immune flexibility for high-altitude animals.Subject terms: Microbiome, Grassland ecology, Microbial ecology

Background

Complex gut microbiota confers a multitude of mutualistic functions to mammalian hosts via metabolic activities and regulation of immune cell maturation and functions1,2. This symbiotic relationship functions by complex crosstalk between the immune system, microbial metabolites, and nutritional cues3 and is sensitive to changes to both internal factors, such as hormones, and external factors, such as dietary intake, environment and pathogens2,4. Therefore, knowledge on how microbial communities shift over time is crucial in understanding the adaptations of free-ranging animals. However, most studies on mammalian microbiota dynamics have focused on non-stressed, penned animals, which display less diversity and different microbiota composition than mammals under extensive conditions4–6. Free-ranging animals increase the likelihood of exposure to a wider variety of foods, as well as to pathogenic and non-pathogenic microbes, parasites and allergens7. Although a previous study examined the fecal bacterial diversity in high-altitude free-grazing yaks in a single time-frame8, time-series changes of microbiota in yaks encountering fluctuating environments and resources and whether microbial shifts influence the host’s immune responses remain largely unknown.

Mammals harboring microbiota exhibit highly complex and individualized immunoglobulin responses that confer metabolic benefits for the host, and ensure tissue health and homeostasis9. The immune system is regulated by commensal microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by microbial fermentation of carbohydrates10,11. SCFAs, known to induce the expansion of regulatory T cells and promote gut B cell responses in mice and humans12, mediate epithelial barrier function as well as mucosal and systemic immunity via evolutionary conserved processes that involve G protein-coupled receptor signaling or histone deacetylase activity13. For example, acetate, a major gut microbial metabolite, is not only involved in intestinal IgA production but also directs selective IgA binding to certain microorganisms11. Production of acetate promotes the resolution of intestinal inflammation via the G-protein-coupled receptor GPR4314. A low level of butyrate, a SCFA, modifies the cytokine production profile of TH cells and promotes intestinal epithelial barrier integrity15, thereby limiting the exposure of the mucosal immune system to luminal microbes which prevents aberrant inflammatory responses. A previous study reported that microbiota expansion and the associated production of SCFAs in weaned mice could be time-critical events in mucosal immune maturation16. These metabolic signals are part of a broader, dynamic crosstalk that govern host-microbiota interactions. Emerging evidence suggests that the environment and diet affect IgA responses in the intestinal tract, influencing the microbiota and whole-body metabolism17–19. A better understanding of these mutualistic interactions between immunity and commensal microbiota in extreme environments could have therapeutic implications in mammals2.

Despite these advances, critical knowledge gaps exist: the specific roles of microbiota in mediating the immune system of mammals in extreme environments, as well as the underlying biological mechanisms, are largely unknown. This uncertainty stems from the lack of research on free-ranging mammals that experience seasonal and nutritional stresses, presumably to buffer seasonal energetic challenges5. The aim of this study was to fill this knowledge gap by addressing two questions: (1) are there seasonal dynamic fluctuations in concentrations of hormones and immunoglobulins, and relative abundances of fecal bacteria in yaks; and (2) do commensal bacteria affect the immune system of yaks throughout the year? By determining the seasonal patterns of hormones and bacterial composition, along with the responses of IgA, IgM, and IgG to bacterial dynamics, this research will improve our fundamental understanding of the host-microbiome relationship. The findings may also provide new insights for protecting and improving the health of human and animal populations in challenging environments.

Results

Seasonal trends of hormone, immunoglobulin and SCFA concentrations

Over a one-year period, we collected monthly fecal samples (n = 20) to determine bacteria community diversity, and concentrations of glucocorticoids, Ig (IgA, IgG and IgM), and melatonin, monthly rumen content samples (n = 10) to determine concentrations of SCFAs, and quarterly hair samples (n = 20) in February, May, August and November to measure cortisol concentrations. The hormones cortisol and glucocorticoids displayed seasonal patterns in males and females (Fig. 1); cortisol peaked in November in females but declined in May in both males and females (Fig. 1A, B), while glucocorticoids peaked in the cold season from October to March and declined in August and September in both males and females (Fig. 1C, D). IgA concentrations ranged from 0.27 to 0.65 g/L in females and 0.35 to 0.60 g/L in males, IgG from 2.18 to 3.61 g/L in females and 2.04 to 3.51 g/L in males, and IgM from 0.61 to 1.18 g/L in females and 0.70 to 1.20 g/L in males. The IgA, IgG, IgM and melatonin levels displayed seasonality, following a unimodal curve (Fig. 2). Specifically, in females, IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations were greater in January, June, July and September, and lesser in February and March (Fig. 2A); whereas, in males, the concentrations were greater in January, August and September, and lesser in February and March (Fig. 2B). Melatonin also exhibited similar seasonality, peaking in the warm season from June to September and declining in February, March and April in both females and males, with concentrations ranging from 2.80 to 5.58 pg/mL in females and 2.37 to 5.81 pg/mL in males (Fig. 2C, D). The concentrations of total volatile fatty acids (TVFAs), acetate, propionate, and butyrate were greater in the warm than cold seasons (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E). Ig, melatonin and SCFAs displayed similar patterns with peaks in the warm season and troughs in the cold seasons in both females and males.

How seasonal changes shape the structure and composition of fecal bacteria

When applying nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) to Bray-Curtis distances, the fecal bacteria of individual yaks displayed distinct clustering by month (Supplementary Fig. 2) and clear segregation among the seasons W1, W2, C1 and C2 (Fig. 3). Alpha Chao 1, observed ASV richness, and Simpson and Shannon diversity indices differed across seasons (P < 0.001; Fig. 4), being lesser in August and September, and greater in January, March, April, November and December. These alpha diversity metrics in females and males also displayed similar seasonality (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, greater alpha diversity metrics were observed in the cold season from October to April than in the warm season from May to September.

Correlation analyses revealed a close association of host phenotypes (hormone, Ig) with the altered bacteria and their metabolites (Fig. 8A), indicator bacteria Alistipes, p-2534-18B5_gut_group, UCG-009, UCG-010 and WCHB1-41 were correlated positively with glucocorticoids in C1 and C2, Romboutsia was related positively with acetate, butyrate, TVFA, melatonin and IgA in W1 and W2, and Micrococcaceae_unclassified and Arthrobacter were correlated positively with acetate, propionate, TVFA, melatonin, IgA and IgM in W2. Seasonal shifts in metabolism and homeostasis at the mucosal surface were driven by the commensal bacteria Bacteroides, Alistipes, Romboutsia and Arthrobacter, resulting in lesser concentrations of IgA, IgG, and IgM in the cold seasons (C1 and C2) and elevated concentrations in the warm seasons (W1 and W2) (Fig. 8C). These findings suggest that IgA, IgG, and IgM levels may be linked to shifts in the microbes of yaks through seasonal concentrations of SCFAs.

Discussion

Seasonal patterns of hormones, short chain fatty acids, and immune responses

Yak hormones displayed seasonal patterns, reflecting not only intrinsic physiological rhythms but also adaptive responses to environmental conditions. Differences in seasonal glucocorticoid peaks have been reported among species, including bison (Bison bison)20, yellow-pine chimunks (Tamias amoenus)21, and golden-mantled ground squirrels (Spermophilus saturatus)22, which suggest species-specific strategies in the regulation of energy homeostasis and resource allocation trade-offs in different life-history stages or seasonal activities to cope with local environmental pressures23. Seasonal glucocorticoid fluctuations are thought to be a dominant regulator of physiological and behavioral traits in animals24, and are shaped by factors such as environmental conditions, and food abundance and quality, which influence energy metabolism, resource allocation, and stress responses25,26. For example, stress-induced glucocorticoid levels were correlated negatively with body condition, body mass, and immune functions during a population decline in water voles (Arvicola scherman)27. In high-altitude yaks, cold season low metabolic rates, extended digesta turnover, high upper critical temperature, and elevated glucocorticoids represent adaptive strategies to conserve energy when forage is scarce and of poor quality. This is consistent with previous studies in bisons, another ruminant adapted to cold conditions28. Moreover, these elevated glucocorticoid levels may also suppress responses, consistent with energy being prioritized for survival rather than for defense functions during the cold season.

IgA, IgG, and IgM antibodies represent key measures of immunity at mucosal barriers and provide protection against toxins and invading pathogens29. Melatonin, an indoleamine possessing antioxidant, chronobiotic, and anti-inflammatory properties30, has been shown to reshape intestinal microflora and promote enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa, thereby enhancing mucosal immunity through SCFAs such as butyrate that activates GPR109A-mediated anti-inflammatory pathways31–34. In a weaned human child, SCFA production was reported to be a critical event in mucosal immune maturation13. In the present study, peaks in melatonin and immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM) occurred in the warm season. This seasonal immune peak was likely mediated by gut microbiota-derived SCFAs, which increased with a greater intake of nutrient-rich forage, suggesting that seasonal dietary improvement enhances microbial fermentation, strengthens mucosal defenses, and contributes to overall immune resilience. Thus, the observed seasonal variations in hormones, immune parameters and SCFAs in yaks highlight the integrated role of metabolism, stress adaptation, and gut bacteria in mediating adaptive physiological trade-offs in a challenging environment.

Seasonal dynamics in fecal bacteria in high-altitude yaks

The seasonal shifts in the bacteria community composition across seasons were probably caused by dietary shifts driven by plant phenology as well as by climatic conditions such as air temperature and rainfall35. Seasonal climatic patterns affect the forage consumed by yaks - mainly greater biomass and richer nutrition in the warm season (W1/W2) than in the cold season (C1/C2)36. A previous study in yaks reported that shifts in seasonal diets and gut bacterial composition occurred consistently8, and that the bacteria were highly plastic, responding rapidly to changes in diet and seasonal environmental variables. These shifts enable yaks to cope with seasonal fluctuations in nutrient intake and energy balance in a challenging environment. This pattern highlights the importance of microbiome in the digestion of diverse diets across seasons.

The relative abundances of bacteria taxa varied across seasons, with the phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria the most variable. In previous studies, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant gut microbiota of both wild and captive animals37,38, and, in the present study were dominant in C1, C2, and W1. These two phyla are involved in butyrate-production, nutrient absorption and epithelial cell maturation and maintenance39, that is, beneficial functions for the host. In a study on wild sifakas (Propithecus verreauxi), the relative abundance of Firmicutes, a taxa that degrades fibers40, increased in the dry season41, which was similar to the increase of Firmicutes during C1, C2 and W1 when a fiber rich diet was consumed compared to W28,36. Studies demonstrated that a low fructo-oligosaccharide and galacto-oligosaccharide (FODMAPs) diet can lead to a decrease in the relative abundance of Actinobacteria42. The abundance of Actinobacteria is associated positively with a high-fat diet but negatively with a high fiber intake43–45, which is in agreement with the results of the present study. Actinobacteria increased in W2, a period of abundant forage with relatively low fiber and high nitrogen (crude protein) and fat contents8,36. Actinobacteria can mediate inflammatory and autoimmune responses by inducing regulatory T cells46. The increase in Actinobacteria during W2 could promote immune functions and suppress glucocorticoid levels, which would align with a rich diet and non-stressful environmental conditions.

The relative abundance of most genera, including UCG-010, UCG-005, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Bacteroides, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Oscillospirales, p-2534-18B5_gut_group, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003, did not change seasonally, which suggests that most taxa followed consistent roles throughout the year. Despite high variations in microbial diversities of yaks across seasons8, we identified several indicator genera during C1/C2/W1/W2, such as Alistipes, Bacteroides, Arthrobacter, and Romboutsia. These bacteria have the potential to degrade polysaccharides and produce SCFAs47. For example, an enrichment of Alistipes in C1 can be explained by its capacity to metabolize protein and fat44. Bacteroides peaked in C1 and C2, which are characterized by limited forage and high fiber content, consistent with its capacity to degrade polysaccharides. The increase in these cellulolytic/fibrolytic taxa and high versatility of the secondary fermenters likely enable yaks to optimally extract nutrients from high-fiber forage intake during the cold season47. These patterns highlight the importance of microbiota for yaks in their complementary roles, that is, providing energy during nutrient-deficient seasons and contributing to metabolic and immune regulation in resource-rich periods. These alterations in the relative abundances of bacteria taxa were concomitant with fluctuations in body weight and immune functions. Interestingly, individual traits such as sex had no effect on microbiota in yaks.

Trade-offs in fecal bacteria changes between warm and cold seasons are proposed, where shifts that benefit one aspect of host physiology consequently leads to changes in the microbiome that are also necessary for the host. Interestingly, these bacteria display their own unique co-occurrence pattern in each season, being complex from W1 to C2, but simple in C1. The strong co-occurrence patterns between these bacteria might indicate a low level of competition, potentially due to niche partitioning, enabling them to coexist48. Indeed, we observed many positive interactions among fecal bacteria with rich food availability; however, there were few bacteria with simple co-occurrence networks with severe food scarcity and an extremely harsh environment. Diet analysis of resource utilization and food nutrient level and nitrogen and energy metabolism potential across seasons supported these findings8,49, which probably drives complex positive interactions. A diverse microbiota network could be beneficial in resisting pathogenic infections in W2 and C2. These co-occurrence patterns might reflect different combinations of microbial trophic chains and likely impact the synergistic interrelations with the host yak. In seasonal and nutritionally challenging environments, trade-offs among microbiota may be necessary for host survival and reproduction.

Effects of microbiota-derived short chain fatty acids on immune responses

Interactions between the mammalian host and commensal microbiota are enhanced through a range of immune responses that confer metabolic benefits and promote host health and homeostasis. Immunoglobulin responses are highly sensitive to changes in nutrition and diet composition, with consequences for the microbiota and whole-body metabolism18. Recent advances suggest that IgA levels display a rhythmic pattern and are entrained by the time of feeding, circadian regulation of microbial pathways of glucose metabolism, and glucose availability, both in the intestine and circulation3. In many cases, IgA binding plays a key role in modulating bacterial gene expression, suppressing bacterial flagellum expression and motility, and promoting host mutualism in mucosal-dwelling commensals. This implies that IgA may be an important immune pathway for glucose metabolism and the risk of metabolic and inflammatory diseases (i.e. obesity, type 2 diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease)17. Elevations in IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations during the warm season were most likely due to enhanced nutrient availability and increased SCFA production, consistent with the notion that seasonal nutritional shifts strongly regulate mucosal antibody responses in mammals. Previous studies reported that altering nutrient consumption or metabolite levels changed the magnitude of IgA secretion in the intestinal tract, resulting in alterations in microbial composition and abundance and in whole-body metabolism18,19. In line with these reports, the present study demonstrated that elevated food availability in the warm season acted as a key determinant in enhancing intestinal plasma cells to secrete antibodies in yaks. Shifts between warm and cold seasons could feasibly contribute to the interconnected nature of the diet, microbiota, and intestinal immunity50–52.

The present study provides further evidence that acetate and propionate influence immune regulation in mammals. Previous research in model animals, livestock and wildlife established that microbiota-derived SCFAs are not only energy sources but also potent modulators of intestinal epithelial and immune cell functions, regulating cytokine and mucin gene expression53. For instance, in mice, fiber fermentation and SCFA production promoted anti-inflammatory responses through regulatory T cells54,55, acetate amplified TLR-mediated microbial sensing in CD4⁺ T cells to regulate IgA production11, while butyrate enhanced the intestinal barrier protein occluding expression56. Similarly, in comparative studies in single-season measurements, concentrations of total SCFAs, acetate, and butyrate in mammals in challenging environments (high altitude) such as Tibetan sheep and plateau pikas were greater than in their lowland counterparts such as small-tailed Han sheep57 and Inner Daurian pikas, and the SCFAs were correlated with increased bacterial energy metabolism58. Moreover, greater concentrations of SCFA-producing bacteria were reported in the high-altitude mammals59. Similar patterns were observed in yaks, suggesting a general adaptive strategy among high-altitude animals.

The gelada (Theropithecus gelada) increased production of SCFAs during both drier and colder periods and, therefore, supplied additional energy in periods of energetically and thermoregulatory challenging periods5. However, an increase in energy production during cold periods may come at physiological costs, such as a reduction in immunity. Indeed, bacteria that promote host digestive efficiency and energy metabolism may also trigger inflammation or suppress immune function60,61. Such trade-offs between energy production and immunity have been proposed in theory, but clear evidence in mammals has been scarce. Findings in yaks in the present study provide support for the trade-off. Seasonal shifts in gut microbiota and SCFA production reflect a trade-off between immune activation and energy metabolism, whereby immune enhancement is prioritized only during periods of high nutrient availability. During the warm season, elevated SCFA levels coincided with increased IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations, whereas in the cold season, both acetate and propionate concentrations produced by key bacterial taxa (Alistipes, Bacteroides, Romboutsia and Arthrobacter) declined, along with a reduction in IgA, IgG, and IgM levels. This pattern indicates that the yaks adopted an energy-saving immune strategy, downregulating costly immune responses during the nutritionally constrained cold seasons to conserve energy for thermoregulation and survival in the high-altitude environment. Furthermore, SCFAs such as acetate and propionate signal via surface-expressed free fatty acid receptors or through the G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) GPR41 and GPR43, and by inhibiting histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity14. These pathways maintain the epithelial barrier integrity by promoting antimicrobial peptide production and regulating epithelial cell turnover, while also shaping anti-inflammatory and tolerogenic immune responses13. Although these mechanisms are well established in other mammals, their specific roles in yaks remain uncertain. Nonetheless, the seasonal synchronization of SCFA production and antibody responses in yaks highlight a plasticity and seasonally tuned microbiota-immune crosstalk, enabling them to maintain energy homeostasis and immune competence in the face of seasonal fluctuations in food availability. The interpretation of these correlations acknowledges the differences between rumen and fecal microbial communities and does not presume the latter to be a direct surrogate62. Instead, the dynamics of fecal microbiota are interpreted as a holistic, systemic response to seasonal dietary shifts—a perspective well-supported by studies in ruminants demonstrating that fecal microbiota can serve as a non-invasive proxy for rumen-dependent metabolic traits63,64.

Unlike other mammals where SCFA production may buffer energy shortages during stressful seasons (e.g., geladas during dry/cold periods)5, yaks synchronize peak SCFA production and immune activities with the warm season, when nutrient intake is greatest. This strategy likely minimizes the immunometabolic costs of maintaining high immune activation under nutrient-poor and hypoxic winter conditions. Thus, the yak model provides evidence for an adaptive mechanism in which gut microbiota-derived metabolites regulate the balance between energy allocation, immune competence, and environmental stress resilience in a high-altitude herbivore.

Conclusions

The complex interplay between hormones, immune system and gut microbiota is increasingly being recognized as central to inflammatory, metabolic and systemic regulation. This study raises the possibility of an internal seasonal clock in high-altitude yaks, establishing an endocrine set-point that aligns with seasonal energy demands. Commensal bacteria display high plasticity, and the sensing of gut microbial metabolites results in the production of IgA, IgG, and IgM, thereby enhancing immune responses during the warm season with elevated nutrient availability. This hormone-microbiota-immune crosstalk enables yaks to buffer seasonal fluctuations in nutrient intake and energy shortages, enabling them to adapt to challenging seasonal environments. The present study adds to the growing evidence that SCFA-mediated regulation of host–microbiota interactions balance immune function with microbial mutualism. These findings not only highlight the adaptive strategies of mammals inhabiting areas of extreme seasonal constraints but also provide insights for improving animal health and resilience in challenging environments, with potential translational relevance for other species including humans.

Materials and methods

All procedures on yaks in this study were approved by the Committee of Animal Ethics of Lanzhou University (Protocol number EAF2021012). We complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Experiment design

In the present study, measurements were made on yaks (Poephagus grunniens) grazing a 2000 ha alpine meadow and alpine shrub meadow grassland on the Tibetan Plateau (37°10′-37°12′N, 102°44′-102°51′E; 3000-3300 m above sea level) from August 2018 to July 2019. The mean annual rainfall at the site averaged 56 mm and ranged between 2.1 to 182 mm, with a peak in May-September, and the mean annual air temperature averaged 0.37 °C and ranged between -11.9 and 11.7 °C, with a peak in May-September. The dominant and associated plant species included Kobesia humilis, Dasiphora fruticose, K. capillifolia, Polygonum viviparum, Stipa capillata, Elymus nutans, Thalictrum alpinum, Medicago ruthenica and Artemisia smithii.

The herd of 200 yaks, composed primarily of females with 4-year-old males for breeding, grazed natural grassland all year, without any supplements. We selected five 4-year-old males and 15 non-pregnant, non-lactating females (five each aged 6-, 8-, and 12-years). To maintain consistency within the groups, individuals with similar body weights were selected for each age group. All study animals were dye-marked (Pushide, Nanjing, China) with unique, easily recognizable symbols. The year was divided into four seasons based on environmental conditions including temperature, rainfall, and forage availability8: warm season 1 from May to July (W1; n = 60), with an average air temperature of 8.6 °C and rainfall of 114 mm, warm season 2 from August to September (W2; n = 40), with an average air temperature of 8.8 °C and rainfall of 129 mm, cold season 1 from January to April (C1; n = 80), with an average air temperature of -5.5 °C and rainfall of 10.8 mm, and cold season 2 from October to December (C2; n = 57), with an average air temperature of -5.6 °C and rainfall of 11.7 mm (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Collection of samples

Yaks were the only herded herbivores at the site. Each month, the grazing yaks were observed and fresh feces were collected from the ground within 30 min after defecation. Each fecal sample (n = 240) was mixed thoroughly using sterile disposable gloves, placed in a self-sealing plastic bag, stored immediately in a liquid nitrogen container in the field, and maintained at – 80 °C until further processing. Before 07:00 on the sampling day, approximately 150 mL of rumen content (mixed solid and liquid) were collected from ten, 4-year old yaks each month using an oral stomach tube (outer diameter, 19 mm; Anscitech, Wuhan, China) connected to a suction pump. The stomach tube was washed thoroughly between samplings, and the first 50 mL were discarded to avoid saliva contamination. The rumen fluid from each yak was stored in 10 mL centrifuge tubes at -80 °C for determination of SCFAs. Hair samples (n = 80) were collected quarterly (in February, May, August, and November) from the neck, shoulder and hip of the yaks by shaving a 3 cm × 3 cm area using a pet hair trimmer (BRL146, Zuhai, China). We cleaned the clippers with a brush between collections and disassembled the clippers at the end of each shaving to remove any trapped hair. Each hair sample was stored in a paper envelope at room temperature until cortisol extraction.

Measurement of hormones and immunoglobulins

Concentrations of glucocorticoids were measured in feces using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)3,65 kit (Shanghai Yuan Mu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China). A 0.5 g sample of thawed feces was added to an appropriate amount of physiological saline, centrifuged for 20 min at 2500 × g, and the supernatant was analyzed for glucocorticoid concentration. Ig (IgA, IgG and IgM) and melatonin were assayed in feces using a radioimmunoassay (RIA) kit (Beijing Sinouk Institute of Biological Technology, Beijing, China). Five mL of 80% methanol were added to 0.5 g feces, vortexed for 1 min, incubated for 20 min at 60 °C and centrifuged for 15 min at 2500 × g20. The supernatant was analyzed for Ig and melatonin concentrations. To measure cortisol concentration, 50 mg of a mixed sample of hair from the neck, shoulder and hips were placed into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube containing phosphate buffer (pH=7.4, 1:9) and vortexed. The mixture was centrifuged for 20 min at 2500 × g and the supernatant was assayed with an ELISA kit (Shanghai Yuan Mu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China) for cortisol concentration. All measurements were done in triplicate in three parallel runs following the manufacturer’s instructions. If the CV of the triplicate values was greater than 15%, the assay was repeated. Inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation were 11% and 5%, respectively. The validities of the assays for measuring glucocorticoid, melatonin, cortisol and Ig were confirmed by demonstrating that the displacement curves of the analytes exhibited slopes consistent with the linear portion of their respective standard curves (R2 = 0.99). The concentrations of these hormones in fecal and hair samples were reported to reflect adrenocortical responses to environmental changes in non-domesticated animals66,67.

Measurement of short chain fatty acids

The measurement of ruminal SCFAs followed Zhang et al.68, with samples run in triplicate. In brief, 6 mL of ruminal fluid were placed in 10 mL plastic tubes and centrifuged at 3,000×g for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of supernatant and 0.2 mL of 25% ortho-phosphoric acid containing 2 g/L internal standard substances (2-ethyl butyraldehyde) were mixed uniformly in a 1.5 mL centrifugal tube, placed on ice water for 30 min and then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. SCFAs were analyzed by gas chromatography (Beijing Beifen-Ruili Analytical Instrument, Beijing, China) fitted with a flame ionization detector using N2 as the carrier gas. The conditions were as follows: column - 30 m × 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness; column oven: 90 oC for 1 min, first ramp 10 oC/min to 120 oC, 3 min hold, second ramp 10 oC/min to 180 oC, 18 min. Injector and detector temperatures were 220 oC and 230 oC, respectively. The standard curves of SCFAs were plotted based on this heating program and the concentrations of SCFAs were determined using calibration curves.

Bacteria sample processing and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the QIAamp® Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3/V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified. Extracted DNA was subjected to first-round 16S PCR amplification using 341 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806 R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’)69. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: an initial melt at 94 oC for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of amplification at 94 oC for 30 s, 56 oC annealing for 45 s, extension at 72 oC for 45 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplicons were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq 2500, resulting in 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads. Quality control, merging of pair ends, OTU clustering, and taxonomic assignment used QIIME270. Singletons and chimeras were discarded. The amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated by DADA2. The taxonomy of these features was seeded into SILVA rRNA gene databases. The sequences identified as archaea, chloroplasts, and mitochondria were removed.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses and visualizations used R software. A t-test tested for differences of hormones (cortisol, glucocorticoids, and melatonin) and Ig (IgG, IgA, IgM) compared to their averages across seasons. The non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test compared alpha diversity profiles. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was determined using the vegan package. Pairwise differences within and across seasonal bacterial measurements were tested using permutest with 9999 permutations and false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Seasonal differences in bacterial composition were tested using adonis and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) with 9999 permutations in vegan71. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) used vegan and the most abundant bacterial genera (relative abundance >1%) were visualized using downstream. Indicator species analyses, using the signassoc function with 9999 permutations for multiple comparisons, determined the seasonal variation contributed by the bacterial taxa72. Significant indicator bacteria were identified using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test. Indicator genera were selected when the relative abundance was > 0.5% in at least 50% of the samples73. Spearman correlation tested the relationships between phenotype and bacteria using the ‘Hmisc’ package, and significance was accepted at a false discovery rate adjusted P-values < 0.05. Microbial networks and the associative network between host and bacteria of significant co-occurrence and co-exclusion interactions, which were built separately for each season, were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.8.0) based on correlation analysis74. The exact sample size (n) for each analysis is provided in the figure legends.
