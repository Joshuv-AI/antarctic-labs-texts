Characterization of saponin biotransformation driven by intrinsic proteins in Aralia elata leaves
Authors: Ke-Wen Guo, Li-Juan Ma, Yu-Xin Kan, Jian-Jun Zhang, Feng-Xiang Zhang, Jian-Bo Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aralia elata (Miq.) Seem. a nutrient-rich wild vegetable widely consumed in Northeast Asia, is abundant in triterpenoid saponins. Methanol and water are commonly used for phytochemical extraction and food processing, respectively. Although solvent-dependent alterations in saponin composition have been reported in Panax leaves, whether similar effects occur in A. elata leaves (AEL), another member of the Araliaceae family, remain unclear. In this study, a UPLC–Q–TOF MS method was established to comprehensively profile saponins in methanolic and aqueous extracts of AEL. A total of 78 saponins were identified and 22 quantified, including one previously unreported compound. The major saponins detected in methanolic extracts were transformed in aqueous extracts into their corresponding deglucosylated forms through selective hydrolysis at the C-28 position. This conversion was mediated by intrinsic proteins during the aqueous extraction process. One of transformed product was isolated and structurally elucidated by NMR spectroscopy as a new saponin. These findings demonstrate protein-mediated, solvent-dependent saponin biotransformation during AEL processing, and provide new insights into the chemical changes occurring in edible and medicinal plant materials.

Introduction

Aralia elata (Miq.) Seem., a member of the Araliaceae family, is widely distributed across China, Korea, Japan, and the Russian Far East (Xi et al., 2009). In northeastern China, its young leaves are traditionally consumed as a seasonal wild vegetable and are also used as a medicinal food (Huang et al., 2026). Owing to this long history of dietary and ethnomedicinal use, A. elata leaves (AEL) are recognized as a valuable plant resource with both nutritional and functional significance. Phytochemical studies have demonstrated that AEL contains diverse constituents, including triterpenoid saponins, flavonoids, amino acids, volatile compounds, and trace elements (Xu et al., 2022). Among these, triterpenoid saponins represent the predominant and most pharmacologically relevant components. Structurally, these saponins are classified into four aglycone types: oleanolic acid (Type I), hederagenin (Type II), echinocystic acid (Type III), and caulophyllogenin (Type IV) (Xu et al., 2022). Correspondingly, AEL extracts have been reported to exhibit multiple biological activities, including anti-inflammatory (Li et al., 2026), antioxidative (Zhang et al., 2018), hepatoprotective (Luo et al., 2015), and cardioprotective (Gao et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2019), and stress-protective effects (Shikov et al., 2016). These properties collectively support the promising potential of AEL in functional foods, nutraceuticals, and plant-derived bioactive products.

Extraction conditions play a critical role in shaping the chemical composition of plant-based foods. Methanol is widely used in phytochemical investigations to preserve native metabolites, whereas water serves as the primary solvent in traditional food processing and decoction. Accumulating evidence indicates that solvent selection can substantially alter the chemical profiles of plant leaves (Cao et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2017). Our previous studies demonstrated that, compared with methanolic extraction, aqueous extraction of Panax notoginseng leaves (PNL), induces hydrolysis of main ginsenosides, yielding corresponding deglucosylated saponins via selective cleavage of the β-(1 → 2) glycosidic linkage at the C-3 position (Cao et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2017). Thistransformation was subsequently shown to be mediated by a chloroplast-localized β-glucosidase (PnGH1), resulting in a marked alteration of the ginsenoside profile (Ma et al., 2024). Similar solvent-dependent transformations have also been reported in the leaves of other Panax species (Ma et al., 2025) and Gynostemma pentaphyllum (Liu et al., 2026), suggesting that intrinsic plant proteins may play an active role in driving saponin biotransformation during aqueous processing. Moreover, selective hydrolysis of oleanane-type saponins has been documented in other plant systems. For example, in Medicago truncatula, a β-glucosidase specifically removed a glucosyl residue at the C-3 position and was associated with plant defense responses (Lacchini et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings suggest that structurally selective saponin hydrolysis may represent a more general biochemical phenomenon in plants rather than a species-specific event.

These findings raise a broader question as to whether such solvent-dependent, protein-mediated transformations are conserved across other members of the Araliaceae family. To address this possibility, we conducted a preliminary screening of several Araliaceae species. Notably, pronounced differences between aqueous and methanolic extracts of AEL were observed by HPLC–UV analysis, suggesting the occurrence of extraction-induced saponin modification. Importantly, AEL differs fundamentally from Panax species in its saponin composition, being predominantly enriched in oleanane-type triterpenoid saponins rather than dammarane-type saponins (Wang et al., 2022). This structural distinction provides a valuable framework to examine whether solvent-dependent transformation in AEL follow glycosidic cleavage pattern distinct from those previously reported in Panax species. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC–Q–TOF MS) enables high-resolution separation and accurate mass detection and has been widely applied to the comprehensive analysis of complex plant matrices, including herbal medicines and food materials (Huo et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025). Therefore, in the present study, triterpenoid saponins in methanolic and aqueous extracts of AEL were systematically characterized and quantitatively profiled using UPLC–Q–TOF MS. Furthermore, the underlying biotransformation process was investigated and experimentally verified to be mediated by intrinsic proteins isolated from AEL, thereby providing new mechanistic insight into solvent-dependent chemical changes during the processing of this edible plant.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Seven batches of AEL samples were collected from Liaoning Province, China. The botanical identity was authenticated by Prof. Xi-Wen Li (Institute of Chinese Materia Medica, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences), and a voucher specimen was deposited at the Institute of Chinese Medical Sciences, University of Macau. A total of 27 authentic reference standards were used in this study. Compounds 1–7, 10–11, 1a−15a, and 17a were isolated from AEL in our laboratory, whereas compounds 8 and 9 were purchased from Chengdu Alfa Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The purities of the laboratory-isolated compounds were greater than 95%, as determined by HPLC–UV analysis, while the commercially obtained standards had purities greater than 98%. Compounds 12–15 and 16a were also isolated from AEL; however, due to their relatively low purity, they were used solely for qualitative identification. Their structural assignments were further supported by MS/MS spectra (Fig. S1). The chemical structures of all compounds are shown in Fig. 1. LC–MS-grade acetonitrile and methanol were obtained from RCI Labscan Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). Deionized water was prepared using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) powder was purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail were obtained from CWBIO Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Taizhou, China). Sample preparation

Air-dried AEL samples were pulverized to a fine powder and passed through a 40-mesh sieve. Two extraction protocols were employed according to the analytical objectives. For qualitative and quantitative analysis, approximately 125 mg of AEL powder was extracted with 10 mL of either methanol or water in a 15 mL centrifuge tube. The mixture was vortexed for 20 s and subjected to ultrasonic extraction (160 W, 40 kHz) for 2 h at room temperature using an ultrasonic bath (Bransonic CPX5800H-E, USA). The resulting extracts were diluted 25-fold, centrifuged at 21,000 ×g for 15 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane before UPLC–Q–TOF MS analysis. For untargeted metabolomic analysis, an additional extraction procedure was used to minimize bias arising from solvent-dependent effects. Briefly, 125 mg of AEL powder was first extracted with 10 mL of either methanol or water under the same ultrasonic conditions. An additional 10 mL of the complementary solvent was then added to obtain a 50% methanol–water solution. The extracts were thoroughly mixed, diluted 12.5-fold, centrifuged, and filtered before analysis. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling equal volumes of all extracts and was used to monitor analytical stability throughout the analysis.

UPLC–Q–TOF MS analysis

LC–MS analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY™ UPLC system coupled to a SYNAPT™ G2-Si quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Waters Corp., Manchester, UK). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% aqueous formic acid solution (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–3 min, 5–28% B; 3–6 min, 28% B; 6–9 min, 28–30% B; 9–26 min, 30–35% B; 26–40 min, 35–47% B; 40–41 min, 47–95% B; 41–42 min, 95% B; and 42–43 min, 95–5% B. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The Q–TOF mass spectrometer was operated in the negative ion mode under the following conditions: capillary voltage, 2.0 kV; cone voltage, 40 V; source temperature, 100 °C; desolvation temperature, 350 °C; desolvation gas flow, 600 L/h; and collision energy ramped from 20 to 80 eV. Data were acquired over an m/z range of 100–1500 for both MS1 and MS2 scans. Data acquisition and processing were performed using MassLynx software (version 4.2, Waters).

Method validation

Stock solutions of authentic standards (compounds 1–11 and 1a–11a) were prepared and serially diluted with methanol to generate six or seven concentration levels for calibration curve construction. Limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were determined using serially diluted standard solutions at signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios of approximately 3 and 10, respectively. Intra-day precision was evaluated by analyzing six replicates within a single day, while inter-day precision was assessed over three consecutive days.

Crude protein extraction and enzyme incubation

To obtain crude protein extracts from AEL while minimizing interference from endogenous saponins, AEL powder was first ultrasonically extracted with 40 mL of deionized water for 30 min, and the supernatant was discarded prior to subsequent protein extraction. Approximately 200 mg of the resulting residue was then suspended in 1.5 mL of 0.01 M PBS with a stainless-steel bead and homogenized using a TissueLyser II (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) at 30 Hz for 5 min. After centrifugation at 6000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, low-molecular-weight components, including saponins, were further reduced using Amicon® Ultra 4 centrifugal filters (10 kDa molecular weight cutoff). The resulting supernatant was collected as the total protein extract. A protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail were added, followed by supplementation with 20% (v/v) glycerol. Reference standards (compounds 1–11) were individually dissolved in water and combined to prepare a mixed standard solution. The mixture was incubated with the total protein extract at 50 °C and 500 rpm for 3 h. The reaction was terminated by adding six volumes of ice-cold methanol to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 21,000 ×g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected, filtered and subjected to UPLC–Q–TOF MS analysis.

NMR analysis

For structural characterization, the isolated compound was dissolved in pyridine‑d5, and 1H, 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance 600 MHz /150 MHz NMR spectrometer (Karlsruhe, Germany). For the novel compound 8a, additional two-dimensional NMR experiments, including 1H—1H correlation spectroscopy (COSY), heteronuclear single-quantum correlation (HSQC), and heteronuclear multiple-bond correlation (HMBC), were also performed.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least three independent replicates, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Raw data from untargeted metabolomic analysis were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters, USA) for peak alignment and normalization. Multivariate statistical analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), was conducted using SIMCA software (version 14.1, Umetrics, Sweden). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For paired comparisons, a paired t-test was applied to normally distributed data, whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used when normality was not met. Statistical significance was set at *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01. Hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization were performed using the R package pheatmap (version 4.4.3).

Results and discussion

Comparison of saponins in AEL extracted with methanol and water

Previous phytochemical studies of AEL have primarily employed aqueous organic solvents, such as methanol (Kim et al., 2005) or 60%–70% ethanol (Gao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018), for the extraction of secondary metabolites. In contrast, water is the principal solvent used in traditional decoction and food processing. In a preliminary study, we observed pronounced differences in HPLC-UV profiles of methanolic and aqueous extracts of AEL, suggesting solvent-dependent alterations in saponin composition. To systematically evaluate these differences, seven independent batches of AEL were extracted with methanol (AELM) and water (AELW) and analyzed using UPLC–Q–TOF MS.

To establish a reference profile of native saponins, the chemical composition of AELM was first comprehensively characterized. As summarized in Table S1, a total of 78 triterpenoid saponins were identified and tentatively annotated based on retention time, accurate mass measurements, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns, with the aid of authentic standards or published literatures. This dataset provided a robust foundation for subsequent comparative analysis. In negative ion mode, saponins produced multiple quasi-molecular ions in MS1 spectra (Zhao et al., 2019), including deprotonated ions ([M − H]−), formate adducts ([M + HCOO]−), and doubly charged ions such as [M − H + HCOO]2− and [M + 2HCOO]2−. Structural classification in MS2 spectra relied on diagnostic aglycone fragment ions at m/z 455.3525 (Type I), 471.3474 (Types II and III), and 487.3423 (Type IV). Characteristic fragmentation involved neutral losses corresponding to hexose (162.0528 Da), pentose (132.0422 Da), and glucuronic acid (176.0321 Da) residues, as well as acetyl (42.0367 Da) and malonyl (86.0462 Da) substituents. Taking compound M24 (tR 10.18 min, m/z 989.5132, [M + HCOO]−) as an example (Fig. 2A). In the MS1 spectrum, the deprotonated molecular ion was detected at m/z 943.4893, consistent with the molecular formula C₄₇H₇₆O₁₉. A formate adduct ion ([M + HCOO]−) was observed at m/z 989.5132, and doubly charged ions appeared at and m/z 494.2440 ([M − H + HCOO]2−) and 517.2467 ([M + 2HCOO]2−). MS2 fragmentation of the deprotonated ion revealed successive losses of two hexose units, generating fragment ions at m/z 781.4351 and 619.3885, followed by the loss of a pentose unit to yield the Type IV aglycone ion at m/z 487.3386. Further neutral losses of H₂O and CO₂ from the C-28 carboxyl group produced characteristic secondary fragment ions (Fig. 2B). Based on this fragmentation behavior and comparison with reported data (Xu et al., 2022), M24 was tentatively identified as Congmunoside II, a Type IV saponin bearing a 3-O-β-d-glucopyranosyl (1 → 3)-α-L-arabinopyranosyl moiety and a 28-O-β-D- glucopyranosyl substituent (Fig. 2C).Fig. 2Structural elucidation of compound M24 by UPLC–Q–TOF MS. Comparison of the chemical profiles of AELM and AELW by UPLC–Q–TOF MS revealed pronounced differences in saponin composition (Fig. 3). The predominant constituents detected in AELM (compounds 1–17) mainly possessed a β-d-glucopyranosyl ester at the C-28 position, whereas the dominant components in AELW corresponded to their deglucosylated counterparts (compounds 1a–17a). Each compound identified in AELM had a direct analogue in AELW, differing by 162.05 Da, consistent with the loss of a glucose unit. These structural assignments were further validated using authentic reference standards. Collectively, these findings indicate that the major saponins in AEL undergo hydrolysis during aqueous extraction, yielding the corresponding C-28 deglucosylated products. This observation underscores the significant impact of extraction conditions on the saponin profile of AEL preparations and highlights the necessity of carefully considering solvent effects in future phytochemical characterization and functional assessments.Fig. 3Base peak intensity (BPI) chromatograms of triterpenoid saponins in A. elata leaves extracted with different solvents by UPLC–Q–TOF MS. Untargeted metabolomic analysis of AEL extracted with water and methanol

To comprehensively assess hydrolysis-dependent differences in saponin composition, an untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed using UPLC–Q–TOF MS. To minimize analytical bias arising from differences in extraction efficiency, both methanolic and aqueous extracts were adjusted to a uniform 50% methanol–water matrix before analysis. In the PCA score plot, QC samples clustered tightly, indicating good analytical stability and reproducibility, whereas AELM and AELW samples were clearly separated into two distinct groups, reflecting substantial metabolic divergence between the two extraction methods (Fig. S2). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained for 51.6% and 22.4% of the total variance, respectively, accounting for 74.0% of the overall variability. An OPLS-DA model was subsequently constructed to further characterize these metabolic differences (Fig. 4A). Clear separation between AELM and AELW samples was achieved. The model exhibited excellent performance, with R2X = 0.851, R2Y = 0.997, and Q2 = 0.985, indicating strong explanatory and predictive capabilities (Q2 > 0.5). Model robustness was further validated by a 200-time permutation test, in which the original R2 and Q2 values were significantly higher than those of the permuted models, effectively excluding the possibility of overfitting (Fig. 4B)(Chen et al., 2023). To identify metabolites contributing most prominently to group separation, differential features were screened by integrating variable importance in projection (VIP) values, S-plots, and volcano plot analysis (Fig. 4C–D). Using the criteria VIP > 1.5, P < 0.05, and fold-change (FC > 2 or < 0.5), 54 saponins were identified as key discriminatory metabolites between AELM and AELW. Among them, 31 were structurally annotated, including 14 unambiguously confirmed using authentic reference standards (Table S2).Fig. 4Multivariate statistical analysis of A. elata leaves extracted with methanol and water. Based on similarities in MS2 fragmentation patterns and structural features, four additional putative deglucosylated pairs were tentatively assigned. Two representative pairs (U1/U1a and U2/U2a) are shown in Fig. 5A–B, and the remaining pairs are presented in Fig. S3. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, U1 (tR 8.61 min, m/z 1267.6013, [M-H]−) and U1a (tR 17.11 min, m/z 1105.5458, [M-H]−) differed by 162.0587 Da, corresponding to the loss of a glucosyl moiety at the C-28 position. Both compounds exhibited fragment ions arising from the sequential loss of three hexose units and one pentose unit from the C-3 position, ultimately generating an aglycone ion at m/z 487.3386, the characteristic of type IV saponins. Similarly, U2 (tR 14.78 min, m/z 973.5013, [M + HCOO]−) and U2a (tR 32.32 min, m/z 811.4483, [M + HCOO]−) exhibited a mass difference of 162.0523 Da (Fig. 5B), again consistent with C-28 deglucosylation. Their MS2 spectra revealed the sequential loss of one hexose and one pentose residue, yielding an aglycone ion at m/z 471.3516, indicative of type II saponins. Accordingly, U2 and U2a were tentatively annotated as Quinoasaponin B and Collinsonidin, respectively (Xu et al., 2022). Collectively, the results suggest that solvent-dependent deglucosylation in AEL is not restricted to a few major constituents but may represent a broader transformation pattern affecting multiple oleanane-type saponins. Quantification of saponins in AEL extracted with water and methanol

To enable absolute quantification of saponins in AEL, a UPLC–Q–TOF MS method was developed for the simultaneous determination of compounds 1–11 and their corresponding deglucosylated forms (1a–11a) in seven batches of AEL extracted with methanol and water. Only saponins for which both intact and deglucosylated forms were available as authentic standards were included. To ensure analytical reliability, the method was validated in terms of linearity, sensitivity, and precision. As shown in Table 1, calibration curves for analyzed saponins exhibited excellent linearity over the tested concentration ranges, with correlation coefficients (R2) exceeding 0.9987. The LOD and LOQ ranged from 0.03 to 0.12 ng/mL and from 0.06 to 0.27 ng/mL, respectively. The contents of the 22 saponins in the seven batches are summarized in Table S3. Although individual compounds showed exhibited quantitative variation, the overall distribution patterns were highly consistent across batches. In particular, the relative proportions of compounds 1–11 and their deglucosylated counterparts (1a–11a) were stable, supporting the reproducibility of the compositional differences observed between AELM and AELW. The total concentration of the 22 quantified saponins reached 12.0 mg/g in AELM and 8.77 mg/g in AELW. To better understand these differences, the quantified saponins were first grouped according to aglycone type. Type II saponins were the most abundant in both extracts, reaching 6.28 mg/g in AELM and 4.66 mg/g in AELW. Type I and type III saponins were present at intermediate levels: type I accounted for 3.09 mg/g in AELM and 1.69 mg/g in AELW, whereas type III reached 1.62 mg/g in AELM and 1.71 mg/g in AELW. In contrast, type IV saponins were consistently detected at the lowest levels in both extracts. The saponins were further classified into two structural series: compounds 1–11 and their corresponding deglucosylated forms 1a–11a (Fig. 5C). Intact saponins (1−11) predominated in AELM, with a total content of 11.40 mg/g, whereas deglucosylated saponins (1a–11a) were dominant in AELW, reaching 8.74 mg/g. The complementary series was present only at low levels in each extract. This clear shift in composition between methanolic and aqueous extracts provides quantitative evidence of solvent-dependent saponin transformation. To visualize these differences, a hierarchical clustering heat map was constructed based on quantitative data (Fig. 5D). The data were cube-root transformed to reduce the influence of highly abundant compounds and to improve visualization of lower-abundance saponins. In the heat map, red to white indicates decreasing abundance. Complete-linkage clustering clearly separated the samples into two groups corresponding to methanol and water extracts.

Batch-to-batch variation in saponin levels may be associated with differences in cultivation environment, climatic conditions, harvest season, or plant developmental stage. These factors could influence both the biosynthesis and accumulation of saponins as well as the expression or activity of endogenous enzymes involved in their transformation. Although moderate variability among batches was observed, multivariate analysis indicates that extraction solvent remains the primary determinant shaping the phytochemical profile of AEL extracts under the tested conditions.

Protein-mediated hydrolysis and substrate specificity

The pronounced differences between AELM and AELW suggested that the deglucosylation of AEL saponins might be catalyzed by an endogenous protein. To test this hypothesis, total protein was extracted from dried AEL and incubated with an aqueous standard mixture containing compounds 1–11 (Fig. 6A–C). Following incubation, compounds 1–11 were almost completely converted into their corresponding deglucosylated saponins (1a–11a). In contrast, no conversion was observed when the protein extract was heat-inactivated by boiling for 10 min (Fig. 6D). Additional control experiments further supported the involvement of a heat-labile enzymatic process. No detectable changes in saponin profiles were observed in methanolic extracts or in aqueous extracts obtained by direct reflux at 100 °C. In contrast, a stepwise heating protocol from 25 to 100 °C resulted in partial saponin hydrolysis (Fig. S4), consistent with transient activation followed by thermal inactivation. These findings suggest that the AEL protein selectively hydrolyzes a single glucosyl ester at the C-28 position of oleanane-type saponins and does not act on substrates bearing longer oligosaccharide chains at this site. This selective hydrolysis pattern differs from previously reported Panax systems, in which hydrolysis predominantly occurs at glucosyl residues linked to the C-3 position of dammarane-type saponins. The comparison suggests that, even among closely related Araliaceae species, protein-associated saponin transformation patterns and substrate preferences may vary substantially depending on structural class. The observed selectivity may be associated with differences in substrate accessibility or steric constraints related to glycosyl chain length. However, as the present study utilized crude total protein extracts rather than purified enzymes, the precise catalytic mechanism remains to be elucidated in future work. In addition, selective deglucosylation at the C-28 position may influence the biological properties of AEL saponins. Previous studies have indicated that the glycosylation pattern of hederagenin-derived saponins, particularly the absence of glycosyl residues at the C-28 position, is associated with enhanced surfactant-like behavior and increased membrane interaction, which may lead to stronger hemolytic activity compared with the corresponding C-28 glycosylated forms (Chwalek et al., 2006). Therefore, the solvent-dependent transformation observed in AEL could potentially modify the physicochemical and functional characteristics of these saponins. However, direct evaluation of bioavailability, toxicity, or efficacy was beyond the scope of the present study and warrants further investigation.

Structural elucidation of novel compound

Given the selective deglucosylation behavior observed in the preceding experiments, structural characterization of the resulting transformation products was necessary to confirm the outcome of the protein-mediated reaction. Among the saponins investigated, compound 8 (Congmunoside X) was commercially available and was therefore selected as a representative substrate for further transformation analysis. Incubation with the AEL total protein extract yielded a deglucosylated product, designated as compound 8a. Its structure was elucidated by NMR spectroscopy and subsequently used as a reference standard for quantitative analysis.

Compound 8a was obtained as a white amorphous powder. Its molecular formula was determined to be C₅₄H₈₈O₂₃ based on the deprotonated molecular ion [M–H]− at m/z 1103.5636 observed in the MS spectrum. The 1H NMR spectrum exhibited seven characteristic singlet methyl proton signals at δ 1.31 (H-27), 1.27 (H−23), 1.08 (H-24), 1.02 (H−30), 0.99 (H-26), 0.96 (H-29), and 0.79 (H-25), consistent with an oleanane-type triterpenoid aglycone (Kuang et al., 2013). In the downfield region, four anomeric proton signals at δ 5.66 (d, J = 7.8 Hz), 5.37 (d, J = 7.9 Hz), 5.16 (d, J = 7.9 Hz), and 4.83 (d, J = 7.8 Hz) indicated the presence of four sugar residues, all adopting β-configuration. The 13C NMR spectrum exhibited characteristic olefinic carbon signals at δ 145.22 and 122.88, confirming the oleanane skeleton. Four anomeric carbon signals at δ 105.55, 105.28, 104.54, and 104.07 further supported the presence of four sugar units. Signals corresponding to sugar C-6 carbons appeared at δ 62.37, 62.91, 63.73, and 62.73, consistent with glucopyranosyl residues. A carboxyl carbon resonance at δ 180.52 confirmed the presence of a free carboxyl group at C-28, while the downfield shift of C-3 at δ 89.62 indicated glycosylation at this position. In addition, carbon signals at δ 78.83, 88.97, and 87.89 suggested the presence of one 1 → 2 glycosidic linkage and two 1 → 3 glycosidic linkages among the sugar moieties (Song et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2013). Conclusions

In summary, a robust UPLC–Q–TOF MS method was developed to comprehensively characterize and simultaneously quantify 22 saponins, including one novel compound, in AEL samples extracted with methanol and water. The results demonstrated that the major saponins in AEL were converted into their corresponding deglucosylated forms during aqueous extraction via highly selective hydrolysis of a glucosyl residue at the C-28 position. Untargeted metabolomic analysis further revealed four additional pairs of saponins involved in hydrolysis. This conversion was confirmed to be mediated by an endogenous AEL protein. In addition, compound 8a, the hydrolyzed product of Congmunoside X (8), was generated via protein-mediated transformation, and its chemical structure was elucidated by NMR spectroscopy, leading to its identification as a novel natural saponin. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that endogenous proteins can drive solvent-dependent saponin hydrolysis in AEL during aqueous extraction. This study provides new insights into the biochemical mechanisms underlying chemical transformations during herbal processing, particularly for leaf-derived medicinal materials, and highlights the importance of extraction conditions in shaping phytochemical profiles.
