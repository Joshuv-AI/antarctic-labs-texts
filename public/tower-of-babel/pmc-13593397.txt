Regional variation in within‐ and transgenerational responses to an invasive predator
Authors: Matthew S Baker, Geoffrey C Trussell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen confronted with environmental stress, individuals may enhance their fitness by altering their phenotype via within‐generation plasticity (WGP) or their offspring's phenotype via transgenerational plasticity (TGP). Despite growing appreciation of TGP in adapting to environmental change, our understanding of how it may vary geographically among populations having different eco‐evolutionary histories remains limited. In the Gulf of Maine, the carnivorous snail, Nucella lapillus, must solve the foraging‐predation risk trade‐off by reducing their vulnerability to invasive predatory green crabs while also acquiring the food necessary to survive. Nucella populations in the southern Gulf have been in contact with green crabs for ~125 years and are able to forage on an abundant supply of food. By contrast, populations in the northern Gulf have had limited contact with green crabs until ~25 years ago and must persist on a very limited food supply. In the southern Gulf, Nucella are known to exhibit TGP in response to green crab risk cues, but how these responses may vary along a spatiotemporal gradient in invasion history and food availability is unknown. We collected Nucella from six populations (three northern, three southern) and evaluated WGP and TGP in response to green crab predation risk in an 18‐month laboratory experiment. We detected regional variation in Nucella life history traits, the expression of both forms of plasticity in response to predation risk, and Nucella foraging on key basal resources. Our results support the hypothesis that low food availability in the northern Gulf may reduce the sensitivity of northern Nucella to predation risk. Hence, geographic variation in TGP in response to predation risk may be shaped by regional differences in other factors such as food availability, thereby yielding outcomes not well predicted by current TGP theory.Keywords: geographic variation, invasive species, parental effects, phenotypic plasticity, predation risk, resource availability, transgenerational phenotypic plasticity

INTRODUCTION

It is well established that environmental cues can induce within‐generation plasticity (WGP) in individual traits, but more recent work has shown that plasticity can also be induced in offspring based on the environment experienced by their parents (Agrawal et al., 1999). Broadly defined, such transgenerational plasticity (TGP) is any effect of parental environment on offspring phenotype (Harmon & Pfennig, 2021). Transgenerational plastic responses occur across a diverse range of taxa, persist across multiple generations, and sometimes exert a stronger influence on offspring phenotype than an offspring's current environment (Bonduriansky & Day, 2018; Donelan & Trussell, 2018a, 2018b; Donelson et al., 2018; Fox et al., 1997). Because TGP, like WGP, can either enhance or diminish offspring fitness under environmental change, determining the factors shaping its evolution has been an important goal in evolutionary biology over the past 30 years (Agrawal et al., 1999; Bonduriansky & Day, 2018; Donelan et al., 2020; Harmon & Pfennig, 2021; Levis & Pfennig, 2016; Pigliucci, 2007).

Comparisons of TGP in species having broad biogeographic distributions across different selective regimes may provide insight into how it evolves (Munch et al., 2021; Walsh et al., 2016). Indeed, recent work (Walsh et al., 2016) utilizing natural geographic variation in predation pressure addressed a central prediction of TGP theory by showing that selection is more likely to favor TGP over WGP or fixed responses when parental environment reliably predicts offspring environment. For example, zooplankton populations (Daphnia ambigua) experiencing historically consistent alewife predation pressure (high or low) exhibited anti‐predator TGP while those from populations that experienced seasonally variable predation pressure expressed WGP (Walsh et al., 2016). Despite recent work on the evolution of TGP across biogeographic gradients (Munch et al., 2021; Walsh et al., 2016), we have a poor understanding of how TGP in response to one stressor may be shaped by other stressors that also covary biogeographically. In this study, we examined potential biogeographic variation in predator‐induced TGP of snail (Nucella lapillus, hereafter, Nucella) populations from two regions within the Gulf of Maine (northern vs. southern) that differ in both their contact history with the invasive predatory green crab (Carcinus maenas, hereafter, green crab; Audet et al., 2003; Carlton & Cohen, 2003; Corbett & Trussell, 2025; Scattergood, 1952; Seeley, 1986; Welch, 1968) and the type and amount of food available for them to consume (Bryson et al., 2014; McClure, 2017).

After arriving in southern Massachusetts in the 1800s, the green crab invaded the southern Gulf of Maine in the early 1900s (Audet et al., 2003; Say, 1818; Welch, 1968) and promoted increased morphological defense in marine mollusks, such as Littorina obtusata and Nucella lapillus (Corbett & Trussell, 2025; Seeley, 1986; Trussell & Smith, 2000; Vermeij, 1982). Green crabs reached the northern Gulf in the 1950s, but cold winters prevented the establishment of robust breeding populations through the 1990s and thus the higher crab density and intense predation pressure on Nucella typical of the southern Gulf (Carlton & Cohen, 2003; Seeley, 1986; Welch et al., 1973). In the 2000s through the 2010s the Gulf of Maine experienced rapid warming (Pershing et al., 2015, 2021) and marine heat waves (Mills et al., 2013; Pershing et al., 2018) that likely facilitated the establishment of higher green crab density and more permanent green crab populations in the northern Gulf (Corbett & Trussell, 2025). Nevertheless, populations of Nucella and other marine mollusks in the southern Gulf have likely experienced more intense selective pressure from green crabs for considerably longer than populations in the northern Gulf (Seeley, 1986; Trussell & Smith, 2000).

In the southern Gulf, Nucella exhibit both WGP and TGP in response to green crab risk cues that alter their foraging, growth, and behavior (Donelan & Trussell, 2018a, 2018b; Trussell et al., 2003, 2006). These responses can influence the surrounding community because Nucella foraging on Mytilus edulis (hereafter, mussels) and Semibalanus balanoides (hereafter, barnacles) can strongly shape the structure and functioning of rocky intertidal communities (Bryson et al., 2014; Menge, 1976; Trussell et al., 2003, 2006).

Importantly, the Gulf of Maine is also characterized by substantial regional variation in the recruitment of resources (e.g., mussels and barnacles) that Nucella feeds upon (Bryson et al., 2014; McClure, 2017). The northern Gulf receives consistently lower recruitment of barnacles and mussels than the southern Gulf (Bryson et al., 2014; McClure, 2017) and prior work (Corbett & Trussell, 2024) suggested that low food availability in the northern Gulf may reduce the expression of within‐generation predator‐induced plasticity in northern Nucella relative to southern Nucella. Isotopic diet analyses (McClure, 2017) are also consistent with the hypothesis that reduced availability of barnacles and mussels in the northern Gulf may drive Nucella to consume other prey, such as littorinid snails and limpets. Hence, there is growing evidence that selection operating through low food availability may shape the foraging and growth of northern Nucella (Corbett & Trussell, 2024; McClure, 2017).

In this study, we evaluated both within and transgenerational predator‐induced plasticity in the offspring of Nucella from six populations (three northern, three southern) in the Gulf of Maine. We detected regional variation in the life history traits, WGP, and TGP of Nucella in response to predation risk that is consistent with the hypothesis that reduced food availability in the northern Gulf may alter the risk responses of northern Nucella. Our findings highlight that geographic variation in TGP in response to one stressor (predation risk) may be modified by prevailing differences in other selective forces (food availability) in ways not predicted by current theory.

METHODS

We conducted an 18‐month laboratory experiment to examine geographic variation in predator‐induced TGP of six Gulf of Maine Nucella populations (three northern, three southern; see Appendix S1: Figure S1, Table S1 for experimental design and site locations). We exposed parental snails to green crab predation risk (risk, no risk) and evaluated parental risk effects on offspring traits at emergence and the performance of 1‐year‐old offspring under current risk (risk, no risk). To control for regional variation in water temperature (on average summer water temperatures in the northern Gulf are 6.62°C cooler than in the southern Gulf; Corbett & Trussell, 2024), we conducted all parental exposure, breeding, offspring care, and the 1‐year‐old offspring performance trial under ambient seawater temperatures typical of the southern Gulf in the flowing seawater system of the Northeastern Marine Science Center (MSC) in Nahant, MA.

Study populations and parental risk exposure

In February 2020, prior to the onset of Nucella mating season, we collected adult Nucella from six wave‐exposed populations in the northern (N = 3) and southern (N = 3) Gulf of Maine (Appendix S1: Table S1). Because the probability of sexual maturity at a given shell length differed among regions (Appendix S1: Figure S2, Table S2), we collected parents using region‐specific size thresholds to avoid including sexually immature individuals. Indeed, at the 20 mm maturity threshold previously established in the southern Gulf (Donelan & Trussell, 2018a, 2018b), we found that 93% of southern snails were mature while only 33% of northern snails were mature (Appendix S1: Figure S2). Hence, we collected southern parents larger than 20 mm (23.17 mm ± 1.30; mean ± SD) and northern parents larger than 25 mm (27.87 mm ± 1.30; mean ± SD) to ensure all snails were sexually mature. After collection, we sexed Nucella and separated out 40 adult Nucella per population (N = 20 males, N = 20 females; 40 adults × 3 populations × 2 regions = 240 Nucella in total). We isolated each individual adult in a separate container (4.5 × 4.5 × 5.5 cm, L × W × H) and maintained them in seawater tables. Each adult Nucella was fed an ad libitum supply of blue mussels (M. edulis, hereafter mussels; mean shell length = ~13 mm) for 3 months prior to the beginning of the breeding trial. Separating each individual for 3 months prior to breeding was necessary to ensure Nucella paternity because females can store sperm from prior mating events for 3 months (Crothers, 1985). In May 2020, we randomly assigned adult Nucella to mating pairs from their respective populations (20 mating pairs per population) and then randomly assigned each breeding pair to a parental risk experience treatment (risk, no risk; Appendix S1: Figure S3). We exposed breeding pairs to risk in mesocosms (17 × 17 × 14.5 cm, L × W × H) that had one upstream compartment (12 × 12 × 10 cm, L × W × H) that held either a single adult male green crab and two Nucella for food (risk) or just had two Nucella (no risk). The mesocosms also had two separate downstream compartments (4.5 × 4.5 × 5.5 cm, L × W × H) each of which held either a single male or a female Nucella. We first fed parent Nucella mussels (N = 8 per individual) while they were exposed to their respective risk treatments (risk, no risk) for 4 days. Following this 4‐day period, we then placed the male and female Nucella together in a separate risk‐free container (8.5 × 8.5 × 10.5 cm, L × W × H) and allowed them to breed and lay egg capsules for 3 days. Allowing Nucella to lay eggs in the absence of risk cues regardless of parental risk treatment ensured that offspring within egg capsules did not experience embryonic induction. At the end of each 7‐day breeding cycle, we isolated all egg capsules laid by a breeding pair in a mesh‐lined tea infuser (5.5 × 5.5 × 6 cm, L × W × H). We then cycled parent Nucella between their risk treatment mesocosms and breeding containers for 8 weeks (i.e., 8 cycles). We also recorded the number of mussels consumed by each parent and the clutch size laid by each breeding pair throughout the breeding trial. We analyzed mussel consumption and egg capsule production by parents with ANOVAs that considered predation risk exposure (risk, no risk) and region (north, south) as fixed effects and source population as a random effect nested within region. For mussel consumption our model also included sex (male, female) as a fixed effect and breeding pair as a random effect nested within region, source population, and predation risk exposure. We conducted all analyses in JMP 19 Pro (SAS Institute, 2025).

Offspring traits at emergence

To measure offspring traits at emergence, we monitored a subset of the egg capsules laid during the breeding trial. This subset of egg capsules included four families from each population × parental risk treatment combination (3 egg capsules × 4 families × 2 parent risk treatments × 6 populations = 144 total egg capsules). We maintained each individual egg capsule in its own tea infuser and checked for emerging juveniles every 3–4 days beginning 6 weeks after laying. We recorded the date of first emergence, counted the number of emerging juveniles, and photographed a subset of emerging juveniles (N = 378) with an AZ100 Nikon macroscope and NIS Elements Basic Research microscope imaging software (v. 4.30, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). We analyzed offspring shell length and width at emergence with ANOVAs that considered parental risk exposure (risk, no risk) and region (north, south) as fixed effects, source population as a random effect nested within region, and family as a random effect nested within region, source population, and parental risk exposure. Because we measured the shell length and width of multiple individuals per egg capsule, we also included egg capsule as a random effect nested within region, source population, parental risk exposure, and family in our analyses.

We analyzed the number of days until emergence and the number of emerging juveniles with ANOVAs that considered parental risk exposure (risk, no risk) and region (north, south) as fixed effects, source population as a random effect nested within region, and family as a random effect nested within region, source population, and parental risk exposure.

Culturing of offspring

We maintained Nucella egg capsules from each parental pair in mesh lined tea infusers in the absence of risk as they developed. Six weeks after being laid, we began to monitor egg capsules every 3–4 days for hatching juveniles. Upon hatching, we fed offspring 200 juvenile mussels (~1 mm shell length). We then continued to feed Nucella a progressively larger ad libitum supply of mussels and transferred them to larger cylindrical (8.5 × 8.5 × 10.5 cm, L × W × H) containers until the offspring performance trial the following July (Donelan & Trussell, 2018a).

Offspring performance trial

In July 2021, we measured the tissue growth, shell growth, refuge use, and mussel consumption of 1‐year‐old offspring in the absence and presence of current predation risk. We selected immature Nucella for use in our offspring performance trial based on population‐specific size at sexual maturity data we collected. Importantly, due to the rapid growth of northern Nucella over the year‐long culturing period, there were systemic differences among treatment groups in the size of immature individuals used in the trial. Northern Nucella began the offspring performance trial with approximately twice the tissue and shell mass than southern Nucella. While regional differences in offspring size may have modified the risk responses of Nucella, the patterns we observed among regions are inconsistent with past work (Matassa et al., 2016) that explicitly examined the influence of size on the foraging and growth of Nucella with and without predation risk (see Discussion ). After selecting offspring and randomly assigning them to current risk exposure treatments, we estimated their initial shell and tissue mass using a non‐destructive weighing technique (Matassa & Trussell, 2014; Palmer, 1982). We exposed offspring to current risk in mesocosms (27 × 15 × 5 cm, L × W × H) that consisted of two chambers separated by a perforated barrier. The upstream “risk” chamber (11 × 15 × 5 cm, L × W × H) was stocked with either one adult male green crab and two Nucella for food (risk) or just two Nucella (no risk). The downstream “response” chamber (16 × 15 × 5 cm, L × W × H) housed a granite tile (15 × 7.5 × 1 cm, L × W × H) that rested upon polyvinyl chloride spacers (1 cm H). This approach provided Nucella a choice between a refuge habitat (beneath the tile) that contained no food and a risky habitat (the top of the tile) with food provided by 120 mussels (13.48 mm shell length [±] 0.02; mean [±] SE) to potentially consume. We stocked each response chamber with four Nucella offspring while being sure to include one individual from each family of the same parental experience × population combination. In total, we used 120 mesocosms for our offspring performance trial (5 replicate mesocosms × 2 current risk treatments × 2 parental risk treatments × 3 populations × 2 regions = 120 mesocosms; 4 snails per mesocosm × 120 mesocosms = 480 snails). For 28 days, we allowed offspring to grow and forage in mesocosms under their respective current risk treatment. We recorded the proportion of Nucella within each mesocosm in the refuge habitat every 3–4 days (Corbett & Trussell, 2024; Matassa et al., 2016) and collected mussels consumed by Nucella in each mesocosm every 7 days.

Upon termination of the offspring performance trial, we used a non‐destructive weighing technique to estimate the tissue mass growth and shell mass growth of all Nucella offspring (Matassa & Trussell, 2014; Palmer, 1982). Due to differences in the initial shell and tissue mass of northern and southern Nucella, we conducted separate analyses for each region. We evaluated TGP in tissue growth and shell growth with analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) that considered parental risk treatment (risk, no risk) and current risk exposure (risk, no risk) as fixed effects, source population as a random effect, family as a random effect nested within source population and parental risk exposure, and mesocosm as a random effect nested within source population, parental risk exposure, and current risk exposure. Initial tissue or shell mass served as the covariate in these analyses.

We calculated refuge use as the proportion of snails sheltering in the refuge habitat (beneath the tile) within each mesocosm across all behavioral checks. The data for each region were analyzed with separate ANOVAs that considered parental risk treatment (risk, no risk) and current risk exposure (risk, no risk) as fixed effects and source population as a random effect.

At the end of the offspring performance trial we measured every mussel consumed by Nucella to estimate total per capita energy acquisition using established methods (Matassa & Trussell, 2014). We focused on mean per capita consumption within each mesocosm because mussel consumption could not be accurately attributed to a single Nucella within a given mesocosm. For each region, we analyzed mussel consumption with separate ANOVAs that considered parental risk treatment (risk, no risk) and current risk exposure (risk, no risk) as fixed effects and source population as a random effect.

RESULTS

Parental risk exposure

During the breeding trial, regional effects on the number of mussels consumed by parents depended on predation risk (F (1,85) = 13.09; p = 0.0005; Figure 1A,B; Appendix S1: Table S3). In the presence of risk, northern parents consumed 24.7% fewer mussels than their counterparts in the absence of risk (least squares contrast; F (1,85) = 21.04; p < 0.0001). By contrast, southern parents exposed to risk consumed 49.5% fewer mussels than those not exposed to risk (least squares contrast; F (1,84) = 112.06; p < 0.0001). Thus, risk exposure was associated with reduced mussel consumption across both regions, but the suppressive effect of risk on Nucella foraging was greater for southern than northern parents. On average, across both predation risk treatments and regions, males consumed 12.2% fewer mussels than females (F (1,88) = 7.69; p = 0.0068; Figure 1A,B; Appendix S1: Table S3). Regional effects on clutch size also depended on predation risk (F (1,85) = 4.86; p = 0.0302; Figure 1C; Appendix S1: Table S3). Northern parents produced clutches of similar sizes across both risk treatments (least squares contrast; F (1,85) = 0.48; p = 0.4892) whereas the clutches produced by southern parents in the presence of risk were 27.3% smaller than those produced in the absence of risk (least squares contrast; F (1,84) = 6.43; p = 0.0131).

Offspring traits at emergence

Accompanying the observed regional patterns in mussel foraging and clutch sizes of parents, we detected an interaction between region and parental risk exposure on offspring length (F (1,37) = 5.49; p = 0.0245; Figure 2A; Appendix S1: Table S4) and width (F (1,37) = 8.96; p = 0.0049; Figure 2B; Appendix S1: Table S4) at emergence. The offspring of both risk‐experienced and risk‐naïve parents from the northern Gulf emerged from egg capsules with similar shell length (least squares contrast; F (1,38) = 1.51; p = 0.2265) and width (least squares contrast; F (1,38) = 3.70; p = 0.0653). By contrast, the offspring of risk‐experienced southern parents were 10.2% shorter (least squares contrast; F (1,37) = 4.46; p = 0.0417) and 10.7% narrower (least squares contrast; F (1,36) = 5.39; p = 0.0261) than those of risk‐naïve parents. Furthermore, northern offspring always emerged at a larger size than southern offspring across both parental risk treatments (least squares contrasts, p < 0.05 in all cases). Across both parental risk treatments, regional variation in offspring size was accompanied by differences in offspring number and time of hatching. Northern egg capsules had 44.5% fewer emerging offspring than southern egg capsules (F (1,4) = 13.97; p = 0.0261; Figure 2C; Appendix S1: Table S5) and northern offspring hatched 3.1% later than southern offspring (F (1,4) = 8.04; p = 0.0456; Figure 2D; Appendix S1: Table S5).

(A) Mean (±SE) shell length (in millimeters) of offspring at emergence among parental risk treatments (risk, no risk) and regions (north = blue, south = red); (B) mean (±SE) shell width (in millimeters) of offspring at emergence; (C) mean (±SE) offspring number per egg capsule; and (D) mean (±SE) days until emergence.

Offspring performance trial

After one year of growth without risk, we measured the foraging, growth, and refuge use of offspring from risk‐experienced and risk‐naïve parents after exposure to current risk treatments (risk, no risk). For northern populations, the offspring of risk‐experienced parents consumed 20.0% more energy from mussels than the offspring of risk‐naïve parents (F (1,53) = 6.41; p = 0.0144; Figure 3A; Appendix S1: Table S6). On average, across both parental risk treatments, current risk exposure reduced mussel consumption by 75.8% (F (1,53) = 289.43; p < 0.0001; Figure 3A; Appendix S1: Table S6). Parental risk exposure had no effect on refuge use (F (1,54) = 2.77; p = 0.1016; Figure 3B; Appendix S1: Table S7), but current risk exposure substantially increased refuge use by 160.8% across both parental risk treatments (F (1,54) = 271.10; p < 0.0001; Figure 3B; Appendix S1: Table S7). Interestingly, the difference in mussel consumption among parental risk treatments did not produce differences in tissue growth (F (1,24) = 1.05; p = 0.3159; Figure 3C; Appendix S1: Table S8). Instead, tissue growth was only influenced by current risk exposure which, on average, reduced tissue growth by 144.1% (i.e., tissue loss) across both parental risk treatments (F (1,55) = 259.59; p < 0.0001; Figure 3C; Appendix S1: Table S8). Similarly, shell mass growth was not affected by parental risk exposure (F (1,25) = 0.80; p = 0.3796; Figure 3D; Appendix S1: Table S8), but was significantly reduced (61.3%) by current risk exposure (F (1,54) = 76.84; p < 0.0001; Figure 3D; Appendix S1: Table S8).

(A) Mean (±SE) per capita offspring energy acquisition (in joules) from mussels among parental risk treatments (risk = triangles with dashed lines, no risk = circles with solid lines), current risk treatments, and regions (north = blue, south = red); (B) mean (±SE) proportion of offspring utilizing refuge habitat; (C) mean (±SE) tissue mass growth (in grams) of offspring; and (D) mean (±SE) shell mass growth (in grams) of offspring.

For southern populations, the effect of parental risk exposure on mussel consumption depended on current risk exposure (F (1,53) = 5.10; p = 0.0280; Figure 3A; Appendix S1: Table S6). In the absence of current risk, the offspring of risk‐experienced parents consumed 14.1% more mussel energy (least squares contrast; F (1,53) = 9.72; p = 0.0029) than the offspring of risk‐naïve parents. In the presence of current predation risk, mussel consumption by the offspring of both risk‐experienced parents and risk‐naïve parents was similar (least squares contrast; F (1,53) = 0.01; p = 0.9165). In addition, the offspring of risk‐experienced southern parents spent 11.9% less time in refuges than the offspring of risk‐naïve parents (F (1,53) = 6.86; p = 0.0115; Figure 3B; Appendix S1: Table S7). On average, current risk exposure increased refuge use by 188.7% across both parental risk treatments (F (1,53) = 407.07; p < 0.0001; Figure 3B; Appendix S1: Table S7). We did not detect an effect of parental risk exposure on tissue mass growth (F (1,35) = 0.36; p = 0.5521; Figure 3C; Table S8), but current risk exposure reduced tissue growth by 98.9% (F (1,59) = 495.14; p < 0.0001; Figure 3C; Appendix S1: Table S8). Similarly, we detected no effect of parental risk exposure on shell mass growth (F (1,37) = 0.36; p = 0.5540; Figure 3D; Appendix S1: Table S8), but current risk reduced shell mass growth by 68.2% (F (1,54) = 313.28; p < 0.0001; Figure 3D; Appendix S1: Table S8).

DISCUSSION

Our study revealed regional variation in Nucella life history traits and both WGP and TGP in response to predation risk. Regional variation in Nucella life history traits was consistent with a size versus number of offspring trade‐off (Smith & Fretwell, 1974): egg capsules produced by northern parents yielded few, large offspring while those from southern parents yielded many, small offspring. This pattern likely reflects, in part, regional differences in selection pressures shaping Nucella life history variation such as green crab predation, resource scarcity, and water temperature. For example, classic work with Trinidadian guppies has shown that populations evolving with predators produce many, small offspring whereas those evolving without predators produce few, large offspring (Reznick et al., 1990). Because of the historically higher green crab densities in the southern Gulf (Bryson et al., 2014; Corbett & Trussell, 2025; Welch et al., 1973), we suggest that selection has favored the production of many, small offspring to dampen the adverse effects of more intense predation in this region. In addition, we know that the availability of Nucella's preferred food (barnacles and mussels) is substantially lower in the northern Gulf (Bryson et al., 2014; Corbett & Trussell, 2024; McClure, 2017). Hence, selection imposed by food limitation and potential starvation risk may favor the production of fewer, larger offspring in the northern Gulf. This hypothesis is consistent with a recent synthesis (Pettersen et al., 2022) suggesting that large offspring use proportionally less energy relative to their size than small offspring, which may be favorable in resource‐poor environments. In addition to these bio‐energetic benefits when food is scarce, a larger size at emergence may allow northern offspring to consume larger prey, thus offsetting the adverse consequences of less frequent prey encounters (McClure, 2017; Palmer, 1990). For example, Palmer (1990) found that larger offspring produced by Nucella emarginata were able to successfully drill and consume a larger size range of barnacles. The production of fewer, larger offspring by northern Nucella may also be driven by the cooler summer water temperatures that limit the duration of the Nucella growing season in the northern Gulf. This shorter favorable window for foraging and growth may further exacerbate the risk of starvation imposed by reduced resource availability, thus promoting the production of fewer, larger offspring.

Regional variation in food availability, rather than invasion history, may also explain differences in within‐generation plasticity exhibited by parents during the breeding trial. Regional differences in the mussel consumption of parents revealed that the suppressive effects of risk exposure were much stronger for southern parents than northern parents. This result contrasts with theory (Clark & Harvell, 1992; Levins, 1968; Tollrian & Harvell, 1999) predicting that populations having shorter, more variable contact histories with invasive predators should exhibit a greater degree of anti‐predator plasticity than populations having longer, more consistent contact histories (Nunes et al., 2014; Trussell & Smith, 2000). These patterns are expected, in part, because populations in recently invaded areas (1) typically experience temporally variable predation pressure owing to initially patchy predator densities, and (2) likely do not have the capacity and/or sufficient time to evolve constitutive anti‐predator phenotypes (Clark & Harvell, 1992; Levins, 1968; Tollrian & Harvell, 1999). Yet recent work (Corbett & Trussell, 2024) suggested that the effects of resource availability may interact with predation risk to shape the anti‐predator responses of prey. They found that juvenile Nucella from the southern Gulf reduced their foraging and growth more substantially under green crab predation risk than northern juveniles, both at a common southern thermal regime and at their respective native water temperatures in the field (Corbett & Trussell, 2024). Hence, reduced food availability in the northern Gulf may impose selection on northern Nucella from “the bottom up” to limit the effects of predation risk on their foraging and growth (Corbett & Trussell, 2024). If this hypothesis is correct, then maintaining high foraging activity in the face of predation risk may be key to survival in resource poor environments and thus may explain regional variation in the within‐generation plasticity of parents and the cascading transgenerational effects we observed in offspring size at emergence.

We also found that southern parents exposed to risk consumed fewer mussels and likely had less energy available to allocate to reproduction than those not exposed to risk. Such reduced energy stores may explain why southern parents exposed to risk laid smaller clutches that yielded smaller offspring than those produced by southern parents in the absence of risk. While emerging at a smaller size may entail substantial risks for offspring, we suggest that the abundant supply of food in the southern Gulf may allow offspring to grow quickly and minimize these risks. Similarly, northern parents exposed to risk also consumed fewer mussels and likely experienced attendant reductions in energy reserves relative to northern parents raised in the absence of risk. Yet despite their reduced mussel consumption, northern parents exposed to risk still produced clutches and offspring that were similar in size to those produced by northern parents in the absence of risk. We suggest that low resource availability in the northern Gulf may exacerbate the fitness costs of emerging at a smaller size, resulting in selection favoring a transgenerational plastic response that maintains offspring size in spite of reductions in parent foraging. This larger size at emergence may allow northern offspring to consume a broader size range of food, which is key when other preferred prey items (barnacles, mussels) are less abundant, as well as achieve a size refuge from predation more rapidly. Although the precise mechanism underlying this transgenerational response in northern Nucella remains unclear, previous work with Nucella suggests that achieving a larger size at emergence may be facilitated by reductions in embryonic metabolic rates (Donelan & Trussell, 2018b).

Based on spatiotemporal variation in green crab predation intensity on Nucella, theory predicts a different pattern of TGP than we observed (Bonduriansky & Day, 2018; Harmon & Pfennig, 2021). Current theory holds that adaptive TGP should evolve in variable environments when parental environment reliably predicts offspring environment (Bonduriansky & Day, 2018; Harmon & Pfennig, 2021). Given the prolonged contact of southern Nucella with green crabs (Audet et al., 2003; Carlton & Cohen, 2003; Scattergood, 1952; Welch, 1968) and historically higher green crab densities in the southern Gulf (Bryson et al., 2014; Corbett & Trussell, 2024; Welch et al., 1973), fluctuations in parental risk environment may be a more reliable predictor of fluctuations in offspring risk environment in the southern versus northern Gulf (Bonduriansky & Day, 2018; Harmon & Pfennig, 2021). Hence, one would expect that adaptive TGP should be more strongly favored by selection in the southern Gulf, assuming parental risk environment more reliably predicts offspring risk environment. We observed a different pattern: under predation risk, southern parents exhibited a strong within‐generation response that corresponded to potentially negative transgenerational effects (i.e., smaller size), whereas northern parents exhibited a dampened within‐generation response that corresponded to a potentially positive transgenerational response that maintains offspring size despite reduced parental foraging. We suggest that consideration of the broader selective landscape (i.e., multiple selective agents) will improve our ability to predict the conditions under which WGP and TGP should evolve.

After one year of growth without risk, we continued to observe regional differences in the foraging of offspring from risk‐experienced and risk‐naïve parents. We found that the offspring of risk‐experienced northern parents consumed more mussels than the offspring of risk‐naïve parents across both current risk treatments, whereas the offspring of risk‐experienced southern parents consumed more mussels than the offspring of risk‐naïve parents in the absence of current risk, but not in the presence of current risk. These results suggest that TGP in mussel foraging is contingent on the current risk environment for southern but not northern Nucella, perhaps because of increased sensitivity of southern offspring to current risk relative to that of northern offspring (Corbett & Trussell, 2024).

The regional patterns of TGP in mussel foraging we observed may have implications for rocky intertidal community dynamics in the Gulf of Maine (Bryson et al., 2014; Menge, 1976; Trussell et al., 2003, 2006). We know that Nucella foraging on mussels and barnacles strongly influences the structure and successional dynamics of rocky shore communities (Bryson et al., 2014; Menge, 1976; Trussell et al., 2003, 2006). For example, parental risk experience may enhance consumer control of basal resources by southern Nucella only in the absence of current risk, but such control by northern Nucella may operate regardless of the current risk environment. If our results apply to the field, then enhanced foraging driven by parental risk exposure may be especially impactful on communities in the northern Gulf because of the already limited supply of basal resources. Thus, our results suggest that parental risk experience may modify how northern and southern Nucella manage the foraging‐predation risk trade‐off in unique ways and potentially link TGP to community level processes.

The regional differences in WGP and TGP we observed in the 1‐year‐old offspring performance trial may reflect regional differences in the initial size of offspring. Northern offspring were considerably larger than southern offspring in our assays, which may have modified the predation risk responses of Nucella. Previous work (Matassa et al., 2016) examining how size influences the risk responses of Nucella found that larger snails foraged and grew more in the absence of current risk than smaller snails. However, our results indicate that size alone was not driving the regional effects we observed. For example, during our offspring performance trial, larger northern snails foraged and grew less than smaller southern snails in the absence of current risk. This pattern is the opposite of what we would expect if the responses of northern and southern Nucella were based solely on size differences.

It is important to note that ocean temperature may have contributed to the regional patterns of WGP and TGP in response to predation risk that we observed (Corbett & Trussell, 2024). We assayed for regional variation in TGP at southern ocean temperatures, but it is unclear how TGP may vary regionally when populations are reared under their native temperature profiles. Previous work (Corbett & Trussell, 2024) found that juvenile Nucella from the northern and southern Gulf raised in the laboratory at southern ocean temperatures both reduced their foraging and growth when exposed to risk, but these responses were stronger for southern than for northern juveniles. However, when raised under risk at their native ocean temperatures in the field, southern Nucella reduced their foraging and growth whereas northern snails displayed no change in their foraging or growth (Corbett & Trussell, 2024). Hence, the risk responses of northern and southern juvenile Nucella diverged more when populations were exposed to risk under their native thermal regimes than under a common southern thermal regime. These results suggest that the regional variation in TGP we observed at southern ocean temperatures in this study may underestimate regional variation in TGP exhibited by Nucella under their native thermal regimes. Moreover, the Gulf of Maine has recently experienced a period of rapid warming (Pershing et al., 2015, 2021) and marine heat waves (Mills et al., 2013; Pershing et al., 2018) and if this trend continues, then the transgenerational responses we observed in northern Nucella at southern ocean temperatures and their cascading effects on basal resources may become increasingly relevant for northern populations in the field.

Over the past three decades there has been increased interest in the role that TGP may play in population persistence and community dynamics (Agrawal et al., 1999; Donelan et al., 2020; Harmon & Pfennig, 2021). Recent studies have utilized biogeographic gradients to inform our understanding of the conditions under which TGP may evolve (Munch et al., 2021; Walsh et al., 2016), while other studies have linked TGP to community and ecosystem level processes (Donelan & Trussell, 2018a; Sommer et al., 2025). Our study expands current theory (Bonduriansky & Day, 2018; Donelan et al., 2020; Harmon & Pfennig, 2021) by showing that regional variation in TGP may be modified by other selective forces that covary along biogeographic gradients and produce outcomes that are not fully captured by theory focused on a single inducing agent. Increased attention to how multiple inducers of plasticity shape the selective landscape across biogeographic scales will likely improve our ability to predict the conditions under which TGP evolves and its potential to influence community dynamics.
