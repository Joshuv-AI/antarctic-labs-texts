On Approximate Representation of Fractional Brownian Motion
Konstantin A. Rybakov

Abstract




Abstract.
This paper considers the orthogonal expansion of the fractional Brownian motion relative to the Legendre polynomials. It has not only theoretical but also practical interest, since it can be applied to approximate and simulate the fractional Brownian motion in continuous time. The relations for the mean square approximation error are presented, and a comparison with the previously obtained result is carried out.

Keywords:
fractional Brownian motion, approximation, simulation, orthogonal expansion, spectral representation, Legendre polynomials

1.
Introduction

The analysis of probability models for various objects often involves not only obtaining theoretical results but also the statistical simulation. It can be used to verify theoretical results or, if such results are missing, it can be the only source of information about objects under consideration. For statistical simulation, the key is the ability to obtain realizations of scalar and vector random variables as well as random processes
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we study a one-parameter family of Gaussian random processes, namely the fractional Brownian motion with a parameter called the Hurst index. The aim of this paper is to present a method for its approximation and simulation in continuous time using the orthogonal expansion of this random process
[
3
]
.

The simplest method for simulation of a Gaussian random process given by a mathematical expectation and covariance function is reduced to simulation of a Gaussian random vector of increments corresponding to the nodes of a uniform time grid
[
2
,
4
]
. This method is universal, i.e., suitable for any Gaussian random process. It guarantees an exact result at the grid nodes, i.e., the simulation result corresponds to the given distribution law, but it provides the approximation of a random process in discrete time only.

The fractional Brownian motion simulation can be performed using different approaches, many of which are also universal, i.e., suitable for any Gaussian random process. Their efficiency is evaluated using various criteria by which a comparison can be carried out: accuracy, computational complexity, computational speed, amount of memory used, etc. We recommend that the reader refer to frequently cited reviews of methods for the fractional Brownian motion simulation
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Despite quite some time passing since their publication, they have not lost their relevance. Methods described in these reviews are either applied without modification
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, or they are used as the basis for more advanced methods to solve specific problems
[
12
]
.

A brief overview of various natural and applied problems whose mathematical models include the fractional Brownian motion is given in
[
13
]
. This overview is useful because it not only lists specific applications with references to published results but also specifies the range for the Hurst index. The fractional Brownian motion simulation is important not only in specific applications but also for verifying theoretical results that are related to different functionals depending on the fractional Brownian motion
[
14
,
15
]
.

The paper
[
3
]
proposes to use the orthogonal expansion of the fractional Brownian motion relative to the Legendre polynomials for approximation and simulation of this random process. The main result from
[
3
]
is based on two components. The first one is the integral representation of the fractional Brownian motion proposed in
[
16
]
. The second one is the spectral form of mathematical description of control systems
[
18
,
17
]
. The paper
[
3
]
gives the expansion of the kernel, corresponding to the integral representation of the fractional Brownian motion, into orthogonal series with respect to the Legendre polynomials. The matrix of expansion coefficients is formed as the product of four matrices: two matrices corresponding to multiplication operators with power functions as multipliers and two matrices corresponding to fractional integration operators.

In this paper, simpler relations for expansion coefficients are obtained. Their use reduces the computational complexity and increases the accuracy for the approximate representation of the fractional Brownian motion. The method proposed here does not replace but complements the previously obtained results. The method for finding expansion coefficients from
[
3
]
ensures computational stability when using machine arithmetic operations. The approach considered in this paper requires the use of libraries that provide floating point calculations with any given accuracy or the use of symbolic calculations, but it allows one to calculate exactly the mean square approximation error when applying both methods. We emphasize that both methods provide the fractional Brownian motion approximation in continuous time. They can be used to represent and approximate random processes that are defined by the fractional Brownian motion (a generalization of the method proposed in
[
19
,
20
]
), e.g., the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and the fractional Brownian bridge
[
21
]
. The obtained results can be further applied to represent and simulate iterated stochastic integrals over the fractional Brownian motion or a combination of independent fractional Brownian motions (a generalization of the method described in
[
22
]
).

The rest of this paper has the following structure. Section
2
provides the definition of the fractional Brownian motion and formulates the problem statement. Section
3
contains the relations for integral and spectral representations of the fractional Brownian motion to the extent necessary to obtain the new results and carry out a comparative analysis. The main result of the paper is presented in Section
4
. Section
5
is devoted to the fractional Brownian motion approximation, in which the relations for the mean square approximation error are obtained. Brief conclusions are given in Section
6
.

2.
The Problem Statement

The paper considers the fractional Brownian motion
B_{H}(\cdot)

[
23
,
24
,
25
]
, i.e., a centered Gaussian random process with the covariance function

R_{H}(t,\tau)=\frac{t^{2H}+\tau^{2H}-|t-\tau|^{2H}}{2},\ \ \ t,\tau\geqslant 0,

where
H\in(0,1)
is a parameter called the Hurst index.

The random process
B_{H}(\cdot)
can be represented as a stochastic integral over the Brownian motion
B(\cdot)=B_{1/2}(\cdot)
, i.e., the standard Wiener process. Such a representation was first introduced in
[
26
]
, where
B_{H}(t)
is defined as a stochastic integral on the set
(-\infty,t]
. Another representation was proposed in
[
16
]
, and it underlies the problem being solved:

B_{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}k_{H}(t,\tau)dB(\tau).

(1)

On the one hand, the integral representation defined by Equation (
1
) is convenient, since
B_{H}(t)
is defined as a stochastic integral on the finite set
[0,t]
. On the other hand, the kernel
k_{H}(\cdot)
corresponding to Equation (
1
) is not expressed in a simple way through elementary functions
[
24
]
:

k_{H}(t,\tau)=a_{H}(t-\tau)^{H-1/2}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl(\frac{1}{2}-H,H-\frac{1}{2},H+\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{t}{\tau}\biggr)1(t-\tau),

where the constant
a_{H}
is defined as

a_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{2H\Gamma(H+1/2)\Gamma(3/2-H)}{\Gamma(2-2H)}},\ \ \ \text{or for $H\neq 1/2$}\ \ \ a_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi H(1-2H)}{\Gamma(2-2H)\cos\pi H}},

and the following functions are used:
{}_{2}F_{1}(\cdot)
is the hypergeometric function,
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function,
1(\cdot)
is the Heaviside function, i.e.,
1(\eta)=1
if
\eta>0
, and
1(\eta)=0
if
\eta\leqslant 0
. For
H=1/2
, we have
a_{H}=1
and
k_{H}(t,\tau)=1(t-\tau)
.

The problem to be solved consists of finding expansion coefficients of the function
k_{H}(\cdot)
relative to the orthonormal basis of
{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}
(the space of square integrable functions defined on
\mathds{T}^{2}
, where
\mathds{T}=[0,T]
). It is assumed that the basis is formed by all possible products of functions that form the orthonormal basis of
{L_{2}(\mathds{T})}
(the space of square integrable functions defined on
\mathds{T}
)
[
27
]
. The Legendre polynomials
[
18
]
are chosen as such a basis:

\hat{P}(i,t)=\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{i}{l_{ik}\,\frac{t^{k}}{T^{k}}},\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots,

(2)

where

l_{ik}=(-1)^{i-k}C^{i}_{i+k}C^{i-k}_{i}=(-1)^{i-k}\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(i-m+1)(i+m)}{m^{2}}.

(3)

The proof that the function
k_{H}(\cdot)
belongs to
{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}
follows, e.g., from the representation of the covariance function
R_{H}(\cdot)
:

R_{H}(t,\tau)=\int_{\mathds{T}}{k_{H}(t,\theta)k_{H}(\tau,\theta)d\theta},

which defines a trace class operator in
{L_{2}(\mathds{T})}

[
28
]
. The Itô stochastic integral properties
[
29
]
, in particular the Itô isometry, allow one to confirm that

\mathrm{E}\int_{\mathds{T}}B_{H}^{2}(t)dt=\int_{\mathds{T}}R_{H}(t,t)dt=\|k_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2},

where
\mathrm{E}
denotes the mathematical expectation, consequently,

\|k_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}=\int_{\mathds{T}}t^{2H}dt=\frac{T^{2H+1}}{2H+1}.

(4)

Thus, it is required to find values

K_{ij}^{H}=\int_{\mathds{T}^{2}}\hat{P}(i,t)\hat{P}(j,\tau)k_{H}(t,\tau)dtd\tau=\int_{\mathds{T}}\hat{P}(i,t)\biggl[\int_{0}^{t}k_{H}(t,\tau)\hat{P}(j,\tau)d\tau\biggr]dt,\ \ \ i,j=0,1,2,\dots

(5)

The solution to this problem provides a representation of the fractional Brownian motion as the orthogonal expansion, on the basis of which it is possible to obtain an algorithm for the approximate simulation of its paths. Such an algorithm has two important advantages. First, the fractional Brownian motion approximation in continuous time is constructed, and second, the mean square approximation error can be calculated exactly.

The relations similar to Equation (
5
) can be formally written for an arbitrary orthonormal basis, but it is quite easy to derive analytical expressions for expansion coefficients of the function
k_{H}(\cdot)
when choosing the Legendre polynomials. In addition, the Legendre polynomials were previously used to represent the Wiener process, which is a special case of the fractional Brownian motion, and such a representation turned out to be quite effective
[
30
,
31
]
.

3.
Preliminary Results

The function
k_{H}(\cdot)
defines the Hilbert–Schmidt operator
[
27
]
in
{L_{2}(\mathds{T})}
, namely

\mathcal{K}_{H}\varphi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}k_{H}(t,\tau)\varphi(\tau)d\tau,\ \ \ \varphi(\cdot)\in{L_{2}(\mathds{T})},

(6)

and according to
[
16
]
this operator is represented as follows:

\mathcal{K}_{H}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{H}\,\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{2H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1/2-H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}&\text{for}~H<1/2\\
a_{H}\,\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{H-1/2}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}&\text{for}~H>1/2,\end{array}\right.

(7)

where
\mathcal{A}_{f_{\alpha}}
and
\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{\beta}
are the multiplication operator with multiplier
f_{\alpha}(t)=t^{\alpha}
,
\alpha>-1/2
, and the left-sided Riemann–Liouville integration operator of fractional order
\beta>0
, respectively, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}_{f_{\alpha}}\varphi(t)=t^{\alpha}\varphi(t),\ \ \ \mathcal{J}_{0+}^{\beta}\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}{\frac{\varphi(\tau)d\tau}{(t-\tau)^{1-\beta}}},\ \ \ t>0.

For
H=1/2
, the operator
\mathcal{K}_{H}
coincides with the first-order integration operator
\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}
.

The representation of the fractional Brownian motion as the expansion into orthogonal series with respect to the orthonormal basis of
{L_{2}(\mathds{T})}
is given in
[
3
]
. Such a representation relative to the Legendre polynomials (
2
) is given below:

B_{H}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{i}^{H}\hat{P}(i,t),\ \ \ t\in\mathds{T},

(8)

and here

\mathcal{B}_{i}^{H}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}K_{ij}^{H}\mathcal{V}_{j},\ \ \ \mathcal{V}_{i}=\int_{\mathds{T}}{\hat{P}(i,t)dB(t)},\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots,

(9)

where values
K_{ij}^{H}
are determined by Equation (
5
), and random variables
\mathcal{V}_{i}
are independent and have a standard normal distribution.

Using matrix notation, we obtain
\mathcal{B}^{H}=K^{H}\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{B}^{H}
and
\mathcal{V}
are infinite random column matrices with elements
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{H}
and
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, respectively, and
K^{H}
is the infinite matrix with elements
K_{ij}^{H}
;
K^{H}
is the matrix representation of the operator
\mathcal{K}_{H}
.

Another result from
[
3
]
is the representation of the matrix
K^{H}
in the form

K^{H}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{H}\,P^{-2H}A^{1/2-H}P^{-(1/2-H)}A^{H-1/2}&\text{for}~H<1/2\\
a_{H}\,P^{-1}A^{H-1/2}P^{-(H-1/2)}A^{1/2-H}&\text{for}~H>1/2,\end{array}\right.

(10)

where
A^{\alpha}
and
P^{-\beta}
are infinite matrices with elements
A_{ij}^{\alpha}
and
P_{ij}^{-\beta}
, expressions for which are given below;
A^{\alpha}
and
P^{-\beta}
are matrix representations of operators
\mathcal{A}_{f_{\alpha}}
and
\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{\beta}
, respectively. For
H=1/2
, the matrix
K^{H}
coincides with the matrix
P^{-1}
corresponding to the operator
\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}
.

The terminology associated with the spectral form of mathematical description of control systems
[
18
]
is used in
[
3
]
. For instance, infinite column matrices are called spectral characteristics of functions or random processes, including generalized ones; infinite matrices are called two-dimensional spectral characteristics of functions or spectral characteristics of linear operators. Thus, spectral representations of functions, random processes, and linear operators (signals and systems) are considered
[
3
,
32
]
. The representation of the matrix
K^{H}
as a product of four matrices corresponds to a sequential signal transformation (in this case, the input signal is white noise) by linear blocks: two proportional blocks and two fractional-order integral blocks. This approach corresponds to the concept of the spectral form of mathematical description: the use of matrix representations of linear operators corresponding to elementary blocks of control systems to transform expansion coefficients of the input signal
[
18
]
. In this paper, the specified terminology will not be used for a more concise presentation of the results.

The constructiveness of Equation (
10
) is ensured by analytical expressions for elements of matrices
A^{\alpha}
and
P^{-\beta}
derived in
[
3
]
.

Below are expressions for elements of matrices
A^{\alpha}
and
P^{-\beta}
that use expansion coefficients of the power function
f_{\alpha}(t)=t^{\alpha}
,
\alpha>-1/2
, relative to the Legendre polynomials (
2
):

\begin{gathered}F_{i}^{\alpha}=\int_{\mathds{T}}{t^{\alpha}\hat{P}(i,t)dt}=\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{i}{l_{ik}\,\frac{T^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+k+1}}=T^{\alpha}\sqrt{T}\,\frac{\sqrt{2i+1}\,\alpha^{\underline{i}}}{(\alpha+1)^{\overline{i+1}}},\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots,\end{gathered}

(11)

where
\alpha^{\underline{i}}
and
(\alpha+1)^{\overline{i+1}}
are lower and upper factorials, respectively. Further, we use the notation
F^{\alpha}
for the infinite column matrix with elements
F_{i}^{\alpha}
. The lower (falling) and upper (rising) factorials are defined as follows:
\alpha^{\underline{i}}=\alpha(\alpha-1)\ldots(\alpha-i+1)
and
\alpha^{\overline{i}}=\alpha(\alpha+1)\ldots(\alpha+i-1)
.

In addition to Equation (
11
), two recurrent relations are obtained
[
3

, namely by index
i
and by degree
\alpha
. The latter allows one to obtain the following relation:

F_{i}^{\alpha+k}=\frac{T^{k}[(\alpha+1)^{\overline{k}}]^{2}}{(\alpha-i+1)^{\overline{k}}(\alpha+i+2)^{\overline{k}}}\,F_{i}^{\alpha},

(12)

where
(\alpha+1)^{\overline{k}}
,
(\alpha-i+1)^{\overline{k}}
, and
(\alpha+i+2)^{\overline{k}}
are upper factorials, and
k
is the natural number.

For
j
th columns of matrices
A^{\alpha}
and
P^{-\beta}
the following equations hold:

A_{*j}^{\alpha}=\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}}{T^{k}}\,F^{\alpha+k},\ \ \ P_{*j}^{-\beta}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}k!}{T^{k}(\beta+1)^{\overline{k}}}\,F^{\beta+k},\ \ \ j=0,1,2,\dots,

(13)

where
(\beta+1)^{\overline{k}}
is the upper factorial.

The derivation of these equations is not difficult, but their use entails computational instability due to peculiarities of machine arithmetic for floating point numbers. In the paper
[
3
]
, computationally stable algorithms based on symmetry properties are proposed.

The matrix representation of the multiplication operator relative to an arbitrary orthonormal basis corresponds to the infinite symmetric matrix

A_{ij}^{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\,F_{i}^{\alpha}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}\biggl(\frac{\alpha+m}{m}\biggr)^{2}\frac{j-m+1}{\alpha-i+m}\,\frac{j+m}{\alpha+i+m+1}&\text{for}~i\geqslant j\\
A_{ji}^{\alpha}&\text{for}~i<j,\end{array}\right.

(14)

or

A_{ij}^{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\,F_{i}^{\alpha}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\Pi_{k}^{(ij)}&\text{for}~i\geqslant j\\
A_{ji}^{\alpha}&\text{for}~i<j,\end{array}\right.

(15)

where

\Pi_{0}^{(ij)}=1,\ \ \ \Pi_{k}^{(ij)}=\biggl(\frac{\alpha+k}{k}\biggr)^{2}\frac{j-k+1}{\alpha-i+k}\,\frac{j+k}{\alpha+i+k+1}\,\Pi_{k-1}^{(ij)},\ \ \ k=1,2,\dots

To represent the matrix

as the sum of symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices, it is sufficient to choose elements

(-1)^{i+j}P_{ji}^{-\beta}&\text{for}~i<j,\end{array}\right.

(16)

P_{ij}^{-\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\,F_{i}^{\beta}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\Pi_{m}^{(ij)}&\text{for}~i\geqslant j\\

\Pi_{0}^{(ij)}=1,\ \ \ \Pi_{k}^{(ij)}=\biggl(\frac{\alpha+k}{k}\biggr)^{2}\frac{j-k+1}{\alpha-i+k}\,\frac{j+k}{\alpha+i+k+1}\,\Pi_{k-1}^{(ij)},\ \ \ k=1,2,\dots

4.
The Main Result

The expression similar to Equation (
13
) can be easily derived for the
j
th column of the matrix
K^{H}
by Equation (
7
):

K_{*j}^{H}=a_{H}\Gamma(3/2-H)\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}(3/2-H)^{\overline{k}}}{T^{k}(H+1/2+k)k!}\,F^{H+1/2+k},\ \ \ j=0,1,2,\dots,

(18)

where
(3/2-H)^{\overline{k}}
is the upper factorial.

Indeed, expansion coefficients
K_{ij}^{H}
are determined by Equation (
5
). According to Equation (
6
), it can be rewritten as

\displaystyle K_{ij}^{H}=\int_{\mathds{T}}\hat{P}(i,t)\mathcal{K}_{H}P(j,t)dt=\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\int_{\mathds{T}}{\hat{P}(i,t)\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}{l_{jk}\,\frac{\mathcal{K}_{H}t^{k}}{T^{k}}}\,dt},\ \ \ i,j=0,1,2,\dots,

where the operator
\mathcal{K}_{H}
is defined by Equation (
7
).

First, we consider the case
H<1/2
:

K_{ij}^{H}=a_{H}\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\int_{\mathds{T}}{\hat{P}(i,t)\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}{l_{jk}\,\frac{\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{2H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1/2-H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}t^{k}}{T^{k}}}\,dt}.

Using the well-known relation
[
33
]

\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{\beta}t^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}{\frac{\tau^{\alpha}d\tau}{(t-\tau)^{1-\beta}}}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)}\,t^{\alpha+\beta},

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{2H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1/2-H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}t^{k}=\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{2H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1/2-H}t^{H-1/2+k}

\displaystyle\ \ \ =\frac{\Gamma(H+1/2+k)}{\Gamma(k+1)}\,\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{2H}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}t^{k}=\frac{\Gamma(H+1/2+k)}{k!}\,\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{2H}\circ t^{1/2-H+k}

\displaystyle\ \ \ =\frac{\Gamma(H+1/2+k)\Gamma(3/2-H+k)}{\Gamma(H+3/2+k)k!}\,t^{H+1/2+k}=\frac{\Gamma(3/2-H+k)}{(H+1/2+k)k!}\,t^{H+1/2+k},

hence,

\displaystyle K_{ij}^{H}

\displaystyle=a_{H}\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}}{T^{k}}\,\frac{\Gamma(3/2-H+k)}{(H+1/2+k)k!}\int_{\mathds{T}}{\hat{P}(i,t)t^{H+1/2+k}dt}

\displaystyle=a_{H}\Gamma(3/2-H)\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}}{T^{k}}\,\frac{(1/2-H+1)\ldots(1/2-H+k)}{(H+1/2+k)k!}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2+k}

\displaystyle=a_{H}\Gamma(3/2-H)\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}(3/2-H)^{\overline{k}}}{T^{k}(H+1/2+k)k!}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2+k},\ \ \ i,j=0,1,2,\dots,

where
F_{i}^{H+1/2+k}
are expansion coefficients of the power function
f_{H+1/2+k}(t)=t^{H+1/2+k}
relative to the Legendre polynomials (
2
). This implies Equation (
18
).

Next, we consider the case
H>1/2
:

K_{ij}^{H}=a_{H}\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\int_{\mathds{T}}{\hat{P}(i,t)\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}{l_{jk}\,\frac{\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{H-1/2}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}t^{k}}{T^{k}}}\,dt}.

Here, we again obtain Equation (
18
), since

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{H-1/2}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{1/2-H}}t^{k}=\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{H-1/2}t^{1/2-H+k}

\displaystyle\ \ \ =\frac{\Gamma(3/2-H+k)}{\Gamma(k+1)}\,\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}\circ\mathcal{A}_{f_{H-1/2}}t^{k}=\frac{\Gamma(3/2-H+k)}{k!}\,\mathcal{J}_{0+}^{1}t^{H-1/2+k}=\frac{\Gamma(3/2-H+k)}{(H+1/2+k)k!}\,t^{H+1/2+k}.

The case
H=1/2
is trivial. The proof is left to the reader.

Equation (
18
) implies the following relation:

K_{ij}^{H}=a_{H}\Gamma(3/2-H)\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{l_{jk}(3/2-H)^{\overline{k}}}{T^{k}(H+1/2+k)k!}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2+k},\ \ \ i,j=0,1,2,\dots,

in which according to Equations (
3
) and (
12
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{l_{jk}(3/2-H)^{\overline{k}}}{T^{k}(H+1/2+k)k!}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2+k}

\displaystyle\ \ \ =(-1)^{j-k}\frac{(j-k+1)\ldots j(j+1)\ldots(j+k)(1/2-H+1)\ldots(1/2-H+k)}{(k!)^{3}(H+1/2+k)}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ {}\times\frac{(H+1/2+1)^{2}\dots(H+1/2+k)^{2}}{(H+1/2-i+1)\dots(H+1/2-i+k)(H+1/2+i+2)\dots(H+1/2+i+k+1)}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ =\frac{(-1)^{j-k}F_{i}^{H+1/2}}{H+1/2+k}\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(H+1/2+m)^{2}(1/2-H+m)}{m^{3}}\,\frac{j-m+1}{H+1/2-i+m}\,\frac{j+m}{H+1/2+i+m+1}.

Using the gamma function properties, namely
\Gamma(3/2-H)=(1/2-H)\Gamma(1/2-H)
, and also taking into account the equality

\displaystyle(1/2-H)\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}(1/2-H+m)=(1/2-H)\biggl(\,\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k-1}(1/2-H+m)\biggr)(1/2-H+k)

\displaystyle=(1/2-H+k)\prod\limits_{m=0}^{k-1}(1/2-H+m)=(1/2-H+k)\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}(m-1/2-H),

we obtain

\displaystyle K_{ij}^{H}

\displaystyle=a_{H}\Gamma(1/2-H)\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\,\frac{1/2-H+k}{H+1/2+k}

\displaystyle\ \ \ {}\times\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(H+1/2+m)^{2}(m-1/2-H)}{m^{3}}\,\frac{j-m+1}{H+1/2-i+m}\,\frac{j+m}{H+1/2+i+m+1},

or

K_{ij}^{H}=a_{H}\Gamma(1/2-H)\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{T}}\,F_{i}^{H+1/2}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\,\frac{1/2-H+k}{H+1/2+k}\,\Pi_{k}^{(ij)},

where

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}^{(ij)}=1,\ \ \ \Pi_{k}^{(ij)}=\frac{(H+1/2+k)^{2}(k-1/2-H)}{k^{3}}\,\frac{j-k+1}{H+1/2-i+k}\,\frac{j+k}{H+1/2+i+k+1}\,\Pi_{k-1}^{(ij)},

\displaystyle k=1,2,\dots

For
H=1/2
, it is sufficient to use the following result
[
18
]
:

K_{ij}^{H}=P_{ij}^{-1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}&\text{for}~i=j=0\\
\displaystyle\frac{T}{2\sqrt{4i^{2}-1}}&\text{for}~i=j+1\\
\displaystyle-\frac{T}{2\sqrt{4j^{2}-1}}&\text{for}~j=i+1\\
\displaystyle 0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(19)

According to Equation (
11
) all expansion coefficients
F_{i}^{H+1/2}
include the factor
T^{H+1}
, and values
F_{i}^{H+1/2}/T^{H+1}
are independent on
T
. Consequently, all expansion coefficients
K_{ij}^{H}
include the factor
T^{H+1/2}
, and values
K_{ij}^{H}/T^{H+1/2}
are also independent on
T
. This means that it is sufficient to have the matrix representation of the operator
\mathcal{K}_{H}
under the condition
\mathds{T}=[0,1]
, then the matrix representation under the condition
\mathds{T}=[0,T]
can be obtained by the scaling factor
T^{H+1/2}
. Obviously, this property can be proven if we analyze the dependence of elements
A_{ij}^{\alpha}
and
P_{ij}^{-\beta}
on
T
and apply Equation (
10
).

The specified property reflects the self-similarity of the fractional Brownian motion. The factor
1/\sqrt{T}
in all the functions
\hat{P}(i,t)
according to Equation (
2
) leads to the factor
T^{H}
on the right-hand side of Equation (
8
). Recall that for the self-similar random process with the Hurst index
H
, a change in the time scale
t\mapsto at
is equivalent to a change in the phase scale
x\mapsto a^{H}x
, i.e.,
\mathrm{Law}\bigl(B_{H}(at),t\geqslant 0\bigr)=\mathrm{Law}\bigl(a^{H}B_{H}(t),t\geqslant 0\bigr)
,
a>0
, where
\mathrm{Law}(\cdot)
means the distribution law
[
23
]
.

5.
Approximate Representation of Fractional Brownian Motion

To approximate the fractional Brownian motion, we replace series in Equations (
8
) and (
9
) with their partial sums:

B_{H}(t)\approx\tilde{B}_{H}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{L-1}\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{H}\hat{P}(i,t),\ \ \ t\in\mathds{T},

(20)

where

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{H}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{L-1}K_{ij}^{H}\mathcal{V}_{j},\ \ \ i=0,1,\dots,L-1.

Using matrix notation, we have
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{H}=\bar{K}^{H}\bar{\mathcal{V}}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{H}
and
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
are random column matrices of size
L
with elements
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{H}
and
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, respectively, and
\bar{K}^{H}
is the square matrix of size
L\times L
with elements
K_{ij}^{H}
. Independent normally distributed random variables
\mathcal{V}_{i}
were defined earlier (see Equation (
9
)).

The notation
\bar{M}
corresponds to the truncation of the infinite column matrix or infinite matrix
M
, and the notation
\tilde{M}
means that, in addition to the truncation, other elements of
M
contain some errors, in particular
\bar{\mathcal{B}}^{H}\neq\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{H}
and
\bar{K}^{H}\neq\tilde{K}^{H}
, where the matrix
\tilde{K}^{H}
is defined as follows
[
3
]
:

\tilde{K}^{H}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{H}\,\bar{P}^{-2H}\bar{A}^{1/2-H}\bar{P}^{-(1/2-H)}\bar{A}^{H-1/2}&\text{for}~H<1/2\\
a_{H}\,\bar{P}^{-1}\bar{A}^{H-1/2}\bar{P}^{-(H-1/2)}\bar{A}^{1/2-H}&\text{for}~H>1/2,\end{array}\right.

(21)

and
\bar{A}^{H-1/2},\bar{A}^{1/2-H},\bar{P}^{-2H},\bar{P}^{-1},\bar{P}^{-(1/2-H)}
, and
\bar{P}^{-(H-1/2)}
are square matrices of size
L\times L
, obtained by the truncation of infinite matrices
A^{H-1/2},A^{1/2-H},P^{-2H},P^{-1},P^{-(1/2-H)}
, and
P^{-(H-1/2)}
, respectively. The inequality
\bar{K}^{H}\neq\tilde{K}^{H}
holds under the condition
H\neq 1/2
, and
\tilde{K}^{H}=\bar{K}^{H}=\bar{P}^{-1}
otherwise.

The random process
\tilde{B}_{H}(\cdot)
is represented as

\tilde{B}_{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bar{k}_{H}(t,\tau)dB(\tau),\ \ \ \bar{k}_{H}(t,\tau)=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{L-1}K_{ij}^{H}\hat{P}(i,t)\hat{P}(j,\tau),

hence,

B_{H}(t)-\tilde{B}_{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(k_{H}(t,\tau)-\bar{k}_{H}(t,\tau)\bigr)dB(\tau)

and the relation

\varepsilon=\mathrm{E}\int_{\mathds{T}}\bigl(B_{H}(t)-\tilde{B}_{H}(t)\bigr)^{2}dt=\|k_{H}(\cdot)-\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}=\|k_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}-\|\bar{K}^{H}\|^{2}

defines the mean square error of the fractional Brownian motion approximation. For this relation, Equation (
4
) gives the squared norm of the function
k_{H}(\cdot)
, and the notation
\|\cdot\|
means the Euclidean norm of the matrix. The last equality follows from properties of orthogonal expansions:

\|k_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{\infty}(K_{ij}^{H})^{2},\ \ \ \|\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}=\|\bar{K}^{H}\|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{L-1}(K_{ij}^{H})^{2}.

The paper
[
3
]
proposes the approximate representation of the fractional Brownian motion given by Equation (
20
) but under the condition

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{H}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{L-1}\tilde{K}_{ij}^{H}\mathcal{V}_{j},\ \ \ i=0,1,\dots,L-1,

i.e.,
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{H}=\tilde{K}^{H}\bar{\mathcal{V}}
, where
\tilde{K}^{H}
is the square matrix of size
L\times L
with elements
\tilde{K}_{ij}^{H}
corresponding to Equation (
21
).

In this case,

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{k}_{H}(t,\tau)dB(\tau),\ \ \ \tilde{k}_{H}(t,\tau)=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{L-1}\tilde{K}_{ij}^{H}\hat{P}(i,t)\hat{P}(j,\tau),

\displaystyle B_{H}(t)-\tilde{B}_{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(k_{H}(t,\tau)-\tilde{k}_{H}(t,\tau)\bigr)dB(\tau),

and the mean square error of the fractional Brownian motion approximation is expressed as

\varepsilon^{*}=\mathrm{E}\int_{\mathds{T}}\bigl(B_{H}(t)-\tilde{B}_{H}(t)\bigr)^{2}dt=\|k_{H}(\cdot)-\tilde{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}>\varepsilon,

where

\displaystyle\|k_{H}(\cdot)-\tilde{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|k_{H}(\cdot)-\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}+\|\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)-\tilde{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|k_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}-\|\bar{K}^{H}\|^{2}+\|\bar{K}^{H}-\tilde{K}^{H}\|^{2},

since

\|\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)-\tilde{k}_{H}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}(\mathds{T}^{2})}^{2}=\|\bar{K}^{H}-\tilde{K}^{H}\|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{L-1}(\bar{K}_{ij}^{H}-\tilde{K}_{ij}^{H})^{2},

also the orthogonality of functions
k_{H}(\cdot)-\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)
and
\bar{k}_{H}(\cdot)-\tilde{k}_{H}(\cdot)
is used here.

Thus, the mean square approximation error
\varepsilon
can be calculated exactly for an arbitrary
L
. In addition, the result obtained in this paper allows one to calculate the mean square approximation error
\varepsilon^{*}
exactly. Note that in
[
3
]
, the norm of the difference between covariance functions of random processes
B_{H}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{B}_{H}(\cdot)
was chosen as the accuracy measure of the approximation instead of the mean square approximation error of the random process (Equation (
20
) ensures that the mathematical expectation of the random process
\tilde{B}_{H}(\cdot)
is equal to zero for an arbitrary
L
, but its covariance function differs from
R_{H}(\cdot)
).

6.
Conclusions

The paper considers the orthogonal expansion of the fractional Brownian motion relative to the Legendre polynomials. This expansion underlies the method for approximation and simulation of this random process in continuous time, namely the fractional Brownian motion is approximately represented by the polynomial with random coefficients. The relations for expansion coefficients of the kernel corresponding to the integral representation of the fractional Brownian motion and relations for the mean square approximation error are obtained. The proposed method can be used not only to simulate the paths of the considered random process but also other random processes as well as functionals depending on the fractional Brownian motion.
