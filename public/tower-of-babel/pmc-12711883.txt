Quality and accuracy of scoliosis-related short videos on TikTok reviewed in China in August 2025
Authors: Qinxin Zhou, Jianzeng Shen, Dongdong Cao, Weijie Yu, Jixin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractScoliosis is a common spinal disorder that affects 2–4% of adolescents worldwide. With the rise of short-video platforms like TikTok, they have increasingly played a significant role in health information dissemination. However, the quality and accuracy of scoliosis-related video content on these platforms have not been thoroughly studied. The objective of this study was to assess the accuracy and quality of scoliosis-related videos on TikTok. Using a cross-sectional design and a newly created TikTok account with cleared cache to minimize bias, we retrieved the platform’s top 100 scoliosis-related short videos via its default sorting algorithm on August 17, 2025. Two independent reviewers with backgrounds in orthopedic surgery and health-information assessment extracted basic data and evaluated each video’s quality and accuracy with the Global Quality Score (GQS, 1–5), modified DISCERN (0–5), and JAMA (0–4). Bivariate associations used Spearman’s rank correlation; multivariable associations used a proportional-odds ordinal logistic model with GQS as the outcome. A total of 95 videos were included in the analysis. 5 videos were excluded due to language mismatch, redundancy, or commercial content. Video duration was median 60 s (IQR 45–87). The median number of views was 8109 (IQR 2112–33,856). Professional individuals accounted for 63.8% of the uploaded videos, while non-professional individuals contributed 33.0%. The median GQS for professional videos was 3, while for non-professional videos it was 2. The video content primarily focused on treatment (68.1%), with less emphasis on diagnosis (16%) and prevention (8.5%). The videos exhibited moderate overall quality, with median scores of 3 for GQS and mDISCERN, and 2 for JAMA, with IQRs of 2–4, 2–3, and 1–2, respectively. Videos uploaded by professional individuals had higher quality and accuracy scores. Fans correlated with mDISCERN and JAMA scores (r = 0.241, P = 0.017 and r = 0.275, P = 0.005, respectively). In the multivariable model, collections (OR = 4.287, 95% CI 1.313–13.989) and duration (OR = 2.664, 95% CI 1.370–5.182) were associated with higher GQS, whereas patient as the uploader identity (OR = 0.064, 95% CI 0.007–0.621) and prognosis content (OR = 0.052, 95% CI 0.005–0.555) were associated with lower GQS. The quality and accuracy of scoliosis-related short videos on TikTok are generally low, with lower quality and accuracy scores for non-professionals.. The main problems are lack of professional review, misleading information, and emphasis on treatment rather than prevention. It is recommended that the public exercise caution when browsing health information on short-video platforms, and that medical professionals provide higher-quality educational content. Introduction

Scoliosis is a three-dimensional structural disorder of the spine characterized by lateral curvature and vertebral rotation, which can lead to impaired posture, pain, cardiopulmonary dysfunction, and reduced quality of life in severe cases1. The estimated prevalence of scoliosis among adolescents ranges from 2 to 4%, making it one of the most common spinal deformities worldwide2. Early recognition and appropriate management are critical for preventing progression and improving long-term outcomes. However, public awareness of scoliosis, including its symptoms, treatment options, and preventive strategies, remains limited in many populations3,4.

In recent years, social media has become a popular source of health information. Platforms such as TikTok, which focus on short-form videos, have gained widespread popularity, particularly among younger audiences. TikTok’s algorithm-driven content dissemination provides users with rapid and visually engaging information, making it a potentially valuable tool for health education. At the same time, the lack of professional oversight raises concerns about the accuracy and quality of medical information shared on the platform5. Previous studies have shown that health-related videos on social media often contain misleading, incomplete, or even harmful information, which may negatively influence public health behaviors and clinical decision-making6,7.

Given that scoliosis primarily affects adolescents and young adults—the same demographic that constitutes the core user base of TikTok—evaluating the quality of scoliosis-related videos on this platform is of particular importance. While a number of studies have assessed the accuracy of online health information across conditions such as orthopedics, dermatology, and infectious diseases, limited evidence is available regarding scoliosis content on TikTok8,9. A systematic examination of the quality and accuracy of such videos can provide insights into the current landscape of scoliosis-related health communication on social media, as well as highlight the potential risks and opportunities for public health education.

Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the quality and accuracy of short videos about scoliosis on TikTok using validated evaluation tools, including the global quality scale (GQS), modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), and JAMA benchmarks, which are widely recognized for their ability to measure essential aspects of health-related content, such as accuracy, clarity, and completeness. GQS is a five-point Likert-type scale that assesses overall quality, organization/flow, and usefulness (1 = very poor; 5 = excellent). Higher scores indicate better quality, and scores ≥ 4 are commonly interpreted as good to excellent9–11. The mDISCERN instrument consists of five dichotomous items (total 0–5), adapted from the original DISCERN tool, to evaluate reliability—clarity of aims, sourcing, balance, referencing, and acknowledgment of uncertainty—with higher scores (e.g., 4–5) denoting greater reliability12,13. The JAMA benchmark criteria assess transparency across four domains—authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency—awarding one point for each criterion met (total 0–4)14,15. Accordingly, we tested the null hypothesis that there is no difference in GQS, mDISCERN, or JAMA ratings between videos uploaded by physicians/affiliated institutions and those uploaded by lay users. By systematically analyzing video characteristics, authorship, content types, and user engagement metrics, this study sought to provide evidence on the credibility of scoliosis-related information available to the public on TikTok, thereby informing clinicians, educators, and policymakers about the implications of social media in health communication.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a cross-sectional design to evaluate the quality and accuracy of scoliosis-related short videos on TikTok. The study was conducted in accordance with established methodologies for social media content analysis and followed the principles of transparency and reproducibility6,7.

Data collection

Video data were collected on August 17, 2025 from the Chinese-region TikTok platform; searches were performed in Simplified Chinese using the domestic keyword “脊柱侧弯” to ensure all retrieved videos were locally uploaded and language-consistent. To minimize bias introduced by personalized recommendation algorithms, a newly created account was employed, and all browsing history and cache were cleared prior to the search.

The search results were displayed using TikTok’s default sorting algorithm. The first 100 eligible videos were included in the analysis, as these are most likely to be encountered by general users and thus represent the content with the greatest visibility and potential influence. Selecting the top 100 videos follows the established methodology in previous TikTok content analyses, ensuring the inclusion of the most viewed and impactful videos typically encountered by general users. A total of 100 videos were initially selected. We then applied the screening criteria to exclude videos that did not meet the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria

Videos must be related to scoliosis or spinal disorders. Videos must be available on the Chinese-region TikTok platform. Only publicly accessible videos were included (videos that are not restricted or private). Videos must be in Simplified Chinese or have Chinese subtitles. The videos must have English or Chinese titles for easier identification and categorization.

Exclusion criteria

Videos that were commercial advertisements or irrelevant to scoliosis (e.g., promotional content). Duplicate content (videos from the same uploader with identical content). Videos where the language was not Simplified Chinese (e.g., content in other languages). Videos that were too short or too long (less than 30 s or more than 15 min). Videos containing inaccurate medical information or misleading content that violated platform guidelines.

Variables and data extraction

For each included video, information was systematically extracted and entered into a structured database. The variables recorded included the author’s name and identity, which was classified as professional individual, professional organization, non-professional individual, or non-professional organization. The Professional Individual category includes individuals with recognized credentials or expertise in the medical field, such as doctors, therapists, or health professionals, identified through their profiles or video content. The Professional Organization category consists of official organizations related to healthcare or scoliosis, such as hospitals, medical societies, or health institutions. The Non-Professional Individual category includes individuals without recognized medical credentials, sharing personal experiences or opinions on scoliosis. Finally, the Non-Professional Organization category refers to groups or organizations that are not officially recognized in the medical field, such as non-profits or community groups, without direct medical authority6–8. The content of each video was categorized into one of the following themes: epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, or prognosis. Additional characteristics such as video format and duration in seconds were documented, together with engagement metrics including the number of likes, comments, shares, saves, and the date of publication. If any disagreements arose during the screening process, a third independent reviewer was consulted to resolve the discrepancy. This third reviewer was also trained in the same evaluation tools and had expertise in health information assessment, with backgrounds in orthopedic surgery and health-information assessment. When a video addressed more than one theme, two reviewers independently determined the dominant topic from spoken content, captions, and relative screen time. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. If consensus could not be reached, the theme occupying the longest initial segment was recorded.

Quality and accuracy assessment

The quality and accuracy of the included videos were assessed using three validated instruments that have been widely applied in social media health research. JAMA benchmark criteria were used to evaluate authorship, attribution, currency, and disclosure, with a total score ranging from 0 to 414. Scores of 3–4 indicate high credibility. GQS was applied as a five-point Likert scale to assess the overall quality, comprehensibility, and flow of the information, where higher scores indicate better quality10. The GQS assesses quality on a 1–5 scale based on information quality, flow, and usefulness. Scores of 4–5 indicate high quality. In addition, the mDISCERN instrument, consisting of five items addressing clarity, relevance, traceability, robustness, and impartiality, was used to provide an overall score between 0 and 512,13. Scores of 4–5 indicate high reliability. The original wording and complete scoring rubrics for all three instruments are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Furthermore, we tracked inter-reviewer accuracy by calculating the Cohen’s Kappa coefficient for each of the quality scales (JAMA, GQS, and mDISCERN). The inter-reviewer accuracy for all scoring instruments was found to be high (Cohen’s κ = 0.78), indicating substantial agreement between the two reviewers. If any disagreements arose during the screening process, a third independent reviewer was consulted to resolve the discrepancy. This third reviewer was also trained in the same evaluation tools and had expertise in health information assessment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the videos, including frequencies, percentages, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR). Means and standard deviations (SD) were used only for normally distributed variables. Group comparisons, such as those between professional and non-professional authors, were conducted using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test or independent t-test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Subgroup comparisons across professional categories were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-parametric data. When significant, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to identify specific group differences. Categories with fewer than five observations were excluded from subgroup analyses to avoid unstable estimates. Comparisons among the four content categories were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Correlations between engagement metrics and quality scores were examined using Ordinal logistic regression was used to identify predictors of GQS, given the ordinal and non-normal distribution of quality scores (Shapiro–Wilk test, P < 0.05). Spearman’s rank correlation was used for univariate associations. Medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were reported for non-normally distributed variables, as confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk tests. For categorical predictors, we used one-hot (dummy) coding with drop-first. To improve model stability, dummy columns with very low frequency (column sum < 5) were removed and their levels were absorbed into the reference set. Reference sets were Nonprofessional individuals (Uploaders), Fitness coach (Profession), and Diagnosis (Content). We did not use stepwise procedures. We report odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided P values (α = 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Analysis of basic features of videos

A total of 95 short scoliosis-related videos uploaded by Chinese authors were retrieved and analyzed. The median fans were 79,080.0 (IQR: 8898–330,865). Median likes were 8109 (interquartile range 2 112–33 856), median comments 346 (107–1 068), median collections 5 287 (1 149–21 812) and median shares 3 843 (836–15 421). The median duration was 45 s (IQR: 30–60 s), with 53.2% of videos shorter than 60 s, 42.6% between 60 and 300 s and only 5.3% exceeding 300 s (Tables 1, 2).

Overall quality was modest. Median GQS was 3/5 (IQR: 3–4), mDISCERN was 3/5 (IQR: 2–3), and JAMA was 2/4 (IQR: 2–2) (Table 1). Distribution of GQS showed 23.4% at 1–2 points, 50.0% at 3 points and 27.7% at 4 points, with no video reaching 5 (Table 2). For mDISCERN, 42.6% scored 0–2, 50.0% scored 3 and only 8.5% scored 4–5. JAMA ratings were 1 point in 19.1%, 2 points in 67.0% and 3 points in 14.9%, with no 4-point ratings (Table 2).

Video quality analysis

Comparisons across uploader types revealed no significant differences in fans, interaction metrics or duration between professional and non-professional individuals (Table 3). Although GQS were generally higher for professional individuals, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.379). The mDISCERN scores differed markedly, with professional individuals achieving a median of 3 versus 2 for non-professional individuals (z = 35.95, P < 0.001) (Table 3). JAMA ratings also favoured professional individuals (median 2 versus 2, z = 33.64, P < 0.001) (Table 3). Among the four JAMA criteria, disclosure and attribution were most frequently absent (present in only 18% and 24% of videos, respectively). Authorship was identified in 71% of cases, while currency was present in 64%. Using the conventional threshold of GQS ≥ 4/5 to denote high/excellent quality, 24 videos (27.7%) met this criterion. Among the mDISCERN reliability criteria, attainment varied considerably across items. Clarity, conciseness, and understandability were fulfilled by 94 videos (98.9%). Balanced and unbiased presentation was observed in 76 videos (80.0%). Valid sources cited was met by only 3 videos (3.2%). Additional sources listed for patient reference appeared in 20 videos (21.1%). Uncertainty acknowledged was noted in 53 videos (55.8%) (Fig. 2).

Video numerical correlation and regression analysis

Spearman correlation analysis (Table 6) revealed weak but significant positive correlations between follower count and both mDISCERN score (r = 0.24, P < 0.05) and JAMA rating (r = 0.28, P < 0.01). In contrast, likes, comments, collections, and shares were not significantly correlated with any of the quality measures (GQS, mDISCERN, or JAMA; all P > 0.05). Although these engagement metrics were highly intercorrelated with one another (r = 0.65–0.95, all P < 0.01), such strong internal relationships did not translate into associations with video quality. Video duration showed a weak positive correlation with follower count (r = 0.23, P < 0.05), but it was not significantly associated with engagement or quality scores. Days since upload were moderately and positively associated with likes, collections, and shares (r = 0.39–0.47, all P < 0.01), yet were unrelated to any quality measure. Construct validity among the three quality assessment instruments was supported by significant positive correlations between GQS and mDISCERN (r = 0.31, P < 0.01), GQS and JAMA (r = 0.29, P < 0.01), and mDISCERN and JAMA (r = 0.58, P < 0.01). These relationships indicate that the three quality scoring systems evaluate overlapping but not identical aspects of video reliability and information quality. We fit a proportional-odds model (logit link) with the Global Quality Score (GQS; 1–4) as the ordinal outcome. Categorical predictors were dummy-coded with the following reference sets: Nonprofessional individuals (Uploaders), Fitness coach (Profession), and Diagnosis (Content); sparse levels were removed and absorbed into the corresponding reference sets. Numeric predictors (fans, likes, comments, collections, shares, duration, days uploaded) were z-standardized. mDISCERN and JAMA were excluded from the predictors. Among 83 videos with complete data, collections (OR = 4.287, 95% CI 1.313–13.989, P = 0.016) and duration (OR = 2.664, 95% CI 1.370–5.182, P = 0.004) were independently associated with higher GQS, whereas patient as the professional identity (vs. reference) (OR = 0.064, 95% CI 0.007–0.621, P = 0.018) and prognosis as the content theme (vs. reference) (OR = 0.052, 95% CI 0.005–0.555, P = 0.014) were associated with lower GQS (Table 7).

Discussion

Key findings and comparison with existing literature

The findings from Table 2 reveal a significant imbalance in the coverage of scoliosis-related topics on TikTok. While treatment and diagnosis are crucial, it is alarming that only 2.1% of the videos address Symptoms and only 1.1% discuss Aetiology. This gap highlights a critical educational shortcoming in the information available to users. Many viewers are likely to be unaware of the early signs and causes of scoliosis, which are essential for early detection and management. The lack of content on Symptoms and Aetiology means that viewers may not have the full picture of scoliosis, which could hinder early detection and proper understanding of the condition. This gap in information is an important area where healthcare professionals can make a significant impact by creating content that educates the public on these essential aspects of scoliosis. We suggest that healthcare professionals, particularly orthopedists, prioritize creating videos that cover the full spectrum of scoliosis, including early symptoms and causes, in order to provide a more balanced and comprehensive understanding of the condition. In this study, we observed that accounts with larger audiences tended to produce content rated as more accurate and authoritative. However, we hypothesize that this relationship may be bidirectional: more accurate content may attract larger audiences, rather than the reverse. This suggests that the quality of content plays an essential role in shaping its reach. Accurate, trustworthy, and educational content might not only be more highly rated but could also encourage higher engagement, leading to increased viewership and a larger audience over time. In this study, some analyses were conducted on categories with small sample sizes. While we aimed to provide insights into differences between various video types, it is important to note that small sample sizes may limit the robustness and generalizability of the statistical findings. The analysis of small sample categories should be interpreted with caution, as the results may not be fully representative of the broader video population. Future studies with larger sample sizes may help mitigate these limitations and provide more accurate conclusions.

The analysis of different content creator groups (e.g., professional individuals, patients, and non-professional sources) revealed distinct differences in the GQS and other quality indicators. Professional individuals (e.g., orthopedic doctors, rehabilitation therapists) generally produced higher-quality videos compared to non-professional individuals or patients, as evidenced by higher GQS, mDISCERN, and JAMA scores. These findings underscore the importance of professional input in health-related content, particularly for topics like scoliosis where accuracy and clarity are crucial for effective patient education. Notably, patient-generated content also performed relatively well on the GQS, suggesting that audiences might value authentic, peer-driven experiences over purely professional narratives. However, the low quality of content from non-professionals and some professional organizations indicates that there is still a significant gap in accurate scoliosis education available on social media platforms like TikTok. This highlights the need for more rigorous oversight and collaboration between health professionals and content platforms to ensure that accurate and evidence-based information reaches broader audiences. The subgroup comparisons in the Results section provide insight into how different types of content creators influence the perceived quality of health-related content. Understanding these differences is crucial for healthcare professionals and educators aiming to improve the quality of information shared on social media. We suggest that future interventions focus on enhancing the visibility of high-quality content and provide support for non-professional creators to ensure that they can deliver trustworthy information.

The analysis reveals that professional individuals, particularly orthopaedic doctors and rehabilitation therapists, were the primary contributors to scoliosis-related content. This mirrors trends seen in other health domains where medical professionals are the primary sources of health information16,17. However, the overall quality of this content was relatively modest. Only 27.7% of the videos achieved a GQS ≥ 4, and 8.5% met the threshold for high quality according to the mDISCERN scale. These proportions are notably lower than the high-quality content found in other health areas on similar platforms, suggesting a gap in the quality of educational content available on scoliosis. While physicians are experts in their field, they may not fully understand the constraints of the TikTok format. TikTok videos are limited to a short duration, which makes it challenging for doctors to provide in-depth medical explanations without oversimplifying or omitting important details. In the medical field, accuracy is paramount, and providing detailed, nuanced information often requires longer formats. Physicians may struggle to balance providing accurate information with the brevity required by TikTok’s format. In some cases, doctors may feel that simplifying complex medical topics might lead to a loss of important details, leading them to choose less detailed explanations to fit the format. TikTok’s algorithm rewards content that engages viewers, often favoring entertainment over educational value. This shift in focus may result in content creators (even doctors) prioritizing engagement and entertainment over delivering high-quality, detailed medical information, thus impacting the overall quality of their videos. Videos from non-professional sources and patients scored significantly lower on the mDISCERN and JAMA scales, emphasizing the importance of professional credentials in ensuring content quality.

Video quality and accuracy

Video quality appeared to reach a suboptimal plateau rather than a true ceiling effect, suggesting that while some improvement exists among professional sources, the overall quality of scoliosis-related TikTok videos remains below optimal standards. In the regression analysis, videos with more ‘Likes’ tended to have slightly lower GQS scores, indicating that high engagement does not necessarily reflect better informational quality. Higher numbers of ‘Collections’ were associated with higher GQS scores, whereas videos uploaded by patients were associated with lower GQS values, indicating that patient-generated content may enhance authenticity but often lacks professional accuracy. This aligns with the trend that likes may more accurately reflect user entertainment preferences rather than the true educational value or informational quality of the content19,20. Non-professional institutional uploads were also negatively associated with GQS, possibly due to their reliance on more informal or entertainment-driven content, which may prioritize audience engagement over educational depth. This phenomenon raises concerns about the quality of health information on platforms where content is influenced more by user engagement metrics (such as likes) than by professional or scientific rigor21,22. As non-professional creators-such as patients or entertainment-driven accounts—tend to emphasize emotional appeal, the quality of information may be compromised. This, in turn, could lead to fragmented narratives that may trivialize the condition’s progression or overstate the efficacy of certain interventions. The observed deficit in symptom and prognosis-related content further exacerbates this issue, as the lack of accurate information about the disease’s course could mislead viewers. This reliance on engagement-driven content could cause misinformed health decisions, potentially leading to delays in appropriate treatment or inappropriate care. The findings underscore the importance of integrating credible professional sources into social media platforms to ensure that content not only engages users but also provides scientifically accurate and comprehensive health information. Doctors should focus on providing scientifically backed information, ensuring that the content is supported by peer-reviewed studies or guidelines from reputable health organizations. Given the short nature of TikTok videos, it’s important to deliver information in a simple, easy-to-understand manner. Avoid jargon and focus on key messages that are relevant to the viewer. Since TikTok is a visual platform, healthcare professionals can use animations, diagrams, or brief demonstrations to improve the clarity of their explanations. Doctors should encourage viewers to ask questions in the comments section and engage in meaningful conversations, helping to clarify any misconceptions. By teaming up with credible health organizations or using established health platforms, doctors can improve the visibility and accuracy of their content. These findings are consistent with prior systematic reviews demonstrating that conflict-of-interest disclosure and peer-review mechanisms correlate with higher DISCERN scores across musculoskeletal content23. When professional standards such as these are applied, content quality significantly improves, supporting the need for more stringent regulations and better content governance on social media platforms.

Correlation between engagement and quality metrics

Although engagement indicators such as likes, comments, and shares reflect popularity, they were not significantly correlated with video quality scores. Only the number of fans showed a weak positive association with quality, suggesting that popularity and informational quality are largely independent constructs on social media platforms. While we previously mentioned that construct validity among the quality instruments was supported by positive correlations between the tools, we now clarify that this should be understood as an example of construct validity or hypothesis testing validity. Construct validity, by definition, refers to the interrelation of items within a single instrument, which was not the case here. In our study, the positive correlations between different tools, such as the GQS, mDISCERN, and JAMA rating, provide evidence of their construct validity—that is, their ability to measure the underlying concept of content quality from different perspectives. These correlations suggest that the tools are assessing similar dimensions of video quality. In our study, we observed that engagement metrics such as Likes were negatively associated with GQS, indicating that higher engagement does not necessarily reflect higher content quality. This finding suggests that video quality, as measured by GQS, is influenced by factors beyond engagement metrics, such as the type of content and the professionalism of the uploader. Additionally, we found that videos with more Collections and content uploaded by patients were associated with higher GQS scores, highlighting the importance of content type and uploader credentials in determining perceived quality. This decoupling highlights the significant risk that high visibility does not equate to high accuracy, a trend commonly observed in the spread of health information on social media platforms. The negative regression coefficient for likes and the positive coefficient for collections align with previous studies on conditions such as gallstone disease and hepatocellular carcinoma11,24. This underscores the algorithmic tendency to prioritize sensational content over educational material. The amplification of emotionally charged content, rather than scientifically grounded information, poses a significant challenge in health communication on social media. However, it is important to recognize that platforms like TikTok were not originally designed to prioritize medical content but to offer entertainment. While it may be unrealistic to expect social media platforms to shift their entire mission, there are alternative ways to enhance the quality of health information. One approach is to make medical content more engaging and entertaining without compromising accuracy. Additionally, platform developers can collaborate with health experts to monitor and flag inappropriate content, ensuring better curation. Public health organizations can also provide educational campaigns to help users distinguish between credible health information and entertainment, fostering digital literacy. Misinformation on TikTok, especially regarding medical conditions like scoliosis, can lead to misguided treatment decisions, delays in seeking proper care, and a general lack of awareness of critical early symptoms and prevention methods. We suggest that professional guidelines for content creation should be developed for health-related videos, and professional supervision should be encouraged to ensure the accuracy and reliability of content. Additionally, increasing user education about how to critically evaluate online content will help mitigate the impact of unreliable information.

Strengths and implications

This study is the first to conduct a cross-platform evaluation of scoliosis-related short videos, integrating quality instruments with uploader characterization. Our findings highlight the need for evidence-based interventions to improve health information on social media, including verification badges for licensed spine specialists, algorithmic changes to prioritize longer, citation-rich content, and co-production models that pair clinicians with digital storytellers. These approaches can help reduce misinformation and ensure that health content is both accurate and educational. Policymakers can use these findings to refine platform governance and ensure the public receives accurate health information. Additionally, short-video platforms have the potential to be powerful tools for public health education. However, this potential must be accompanied by responsible content regulation, focusing on transparency, scientifically sound videos, and monitoring user engagement to address misleading or harmful content.

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides valuable insights into the quality of scoliosis-related videos on short-video platforms, it has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design captures only a snapshot of the current content landscape, and the rapid evolution of platform algorithms and content policies could result in significant changes over time. Second, the analysis is limited to Chinese-language videos, restricting the generalizability of the findings to non-Chinese-speaking audiences. Third, despite substantial inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.78), the inherent subjectivity of DISCERN and JAMA scoring introduces potential bias in quality assessments.

Future research should address these limitations by incorporating real-time monitoring tools such as natural language processing and computer vision to conduct large-scale quality surveillance. Our study suggests that increasing medical professional involvement in content creation could significantly improve the quality of scoliosis-related videos. Platforms like TikTok could consider collaborating with healthcare organizations to establish guidelines for content creators, ensuring videos are both accurate and educational. Additionally, public health campaigns should focus on educating non-professional users about the importance of sourcing information from trusted medical professionals. Additionally, longitudinal studies could track the impacts of interventions over time, providing insights into the effectiveness of regulatory measures and content quality improvements. Furthermore, expanding the analysis to include videos from non-Chinese-language platforms, such as Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, and TikTok’s international variants, would provide a broader perspective on the quality and impact of health-related videos on a global scale. This would be particularly valuable for policymakers and public health officials who seek to better understand the global trends in health information dissemination through social media.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while short-video platforms offer a promising avenue for health education, the current quality of scoliosis content is inconsistent. Collaborative efforts among platforms, healthcare providers, and users are essential to improve information quality and mitigate misinformation risks. Future research should explore longitudinal trends and cross-cultural comparisons to refine health communication strategies further.
