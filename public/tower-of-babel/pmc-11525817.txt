The CRISPR-dCas9 interference system suppresses inhA gene expression in Mycobacterium smegmatis
Authors: Nuntita Singpanomchai, Panan Ratthawongjirakul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
CRISPR-dead Cas9 interference (CRISPRi) has become a valuable tool for precise gene regulation. In this study, CRISPRi was designed to target the inhA gene of Mycobacterium smegmatis (Msm), a gene necessary for mycolic acid synthesis. Our findings revealed that sgRNA2 induced with 100 ng/ml aTc achieved over 90% downregulation of inhA gene expression and inhibited bacterial viability by approximately 1,000-fold. Furthermore, CRISPRi enhanced the susceptibility of M. smegmatis to isoniazid and rifampicin, which are both 50% and 90% lower than those of the wild-type strain or other strains, respectively. This study highlights the ability of CRISPRi to silence the inhA gene, which impacts bacterial viability and drug susceptibility. The findings provide valuable insights into the utility of CRISPRi as an alternative tool for gene regulation. CRISPRi might be further assessed for its synergistic effect with current anti-tuberculosis drugs and its possible implications for combating mycobacterial infections, especially drug-resistant tuberculosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77442-2.

Introduction

Cluster Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeat (CRISPR) and CRISPR-associated proteins (Cas proteins) are adaptive immune responses against foreign nucleic acid in bacteria and archaea1,2. The unique short exogenous DNA from invasive genomes is incorporated between direct repeats of the CRISPR locus to store memory3. Upon reinfection, CRISPR‒Cas proteins use short complementary RNAs to target and specifically cleave invading sequences through RNA-guided endonucleases, and a single guide RNA (sgRNA), an engineered RNA molecule, can be designed and replaced in this natural system4,5. The CRISPR-Cas system has been widely utilized as a powerful genome engineering tool that has the potential to revolutionize many fields by targeting humans or other organisms6,7. In a related method, CRISPR-Cas is available for genome modification and genome regulation by Cas9 modification to generate an inactivated dead Cas9 (dCas9) that has lost nuclease activity. After sgRNA at the complementary target site and the dCas9 molecule form the complex, it blocks the transcriptional process. This related technology is called “CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)”8. CRISPRi can be used to turn off specific genes in bacteria, including those involved in virulence, antibiotic resistance, or other factors9. For example, Wang et al. demonstrated that CRISPRi targeting the mecA gene of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) could repress 77% of gene transcription and could lower the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of oxacillin10. Li et al. also utilized the CRISPRi system to suppress the mobile class 1 integron in Escherichia coli. Their results showed that CRISPRi not only downregulated target genes but also reduced bacterial growth. Furthermore, this system significantly repressed the rates of antibiotic resistance and horizontal gene transfer. They also suggested that the CRISPRi system might be applied for combating multidrug-resistant pathogens by targeting virulence or biofilm-associated genes11.

Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It has become a global health crisis with high morbidity and mortality rates. The first-line regimen for TB treatment consists of four drugs: isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide. Isoniazid and rifampicin are the two most potent against TB infection. Late TB diagnosis and inappropriate treatment, especially a multidrug-resistant TB that resists both isoniazid and rifampicin, are factors interrupting TB control12. TB also has virulence factors that are associated with natural resistance to antibiotics. One factor is mycolic acid, a significant component of the outer membrane of the mycobacterial cell wall. Mycolic acid is a hydrophobic long-chain fatty acid that is crucial for mycobacterial survival and maintaining a rigid cell shape. This component leads to low permeability of cell membranes, blocking small hydrophilic molecules, such as antibiotics, from passing through13. It also plays a critical role in a first-line defence that is advantageous for mycobacterial viability under host-derived stresses14 and lowers the level of chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines during a host immune response15,16. Due to its importance to current antibiotic resistance in TB, mycolic acids are interesting targets for novel drug development17,18. Various genes are involved in mycolic acid synthesis and are therefore potential drug targets19. For instance, the inhA gene encodes NADH-dependent enoyl-ACP reductase, an essential protein responsible for the final step of the fatty acid synthase II elongation system of mycolic acid synthesis20.

Recently, CRISPRi has been implemented in mycobacterial research to investigate essential gene functions, validate drug targets, and restore drug susceptibility of resistant strains21–23. Re-sensitized rifampicin resistance was achieved using CRISPRi targeting non-essential genes involved in rifampicin resistance24. A previous study showed that the repression of peptidoglycan-related genes in mycobacterial cells by CRISPRi could synergize with beta-lactam treatment25. However, comprehensive knowledge needs to be improved in some aspects. Hence, this study aimed to investigate the effect of CRISPRi (dCas9) targeting the expression of the inhA gene of Mycobacterium smegmatis. The laboratory-engineered mutant of M. smegmatis (Msm), Msm mc2 155, was chosen as a surrogate model in this study to overcome the pathogenic nature and slow colony formation of M. tuberculosis. This mutant shares genome similarities with M. tuberculosis, including genes associated with mycolic acid synthesis, and has proven to be a reliable substitute for M. tuberculosis and other slow-growing mycobacteria in studies, especially in molecular biology26,27. A consequence of inhA suppression was evaluated through bacterial vitality, morphology, and drug susceptibility. Our findings provide another line of evidence showing the benefit of establishing CRISPRi-based inhA gene silencing as an alternative strategy or complementary to existing antimycobacterial agents in combating and controlling TB.

Results

Selection of sgRNAs

To construct the model for this study, three different sgRNAs targeting the inhA gene (MSMEG_3151) of M. smegmatis (Msm) mc2 155 were designed, separately inserted into the dCas9 vector PLJR962, and subsequently transformed into Msm mc2 155. The most suitable sgRNA for inhA gene suppression was evaluated using reverse transcriptase quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) and dot-spotting phenotypic methods. Initially, the expression of dCas9 mRNA was significantly increased in all strains (Msm with vector control, Msm with different sgRNAs, and Msm with a positive sgRNA, mmpL3) upon exposure to 100 ng/ml of anhydrotetracycline (aTc) compared to the absence of aTc at 0 h. This trend continued for 24 h (Fig. 1a and e). This result indicated that adding aTc could relieve transcriptional blocking and lead to transcription of the dCas9 gene. The expression level of the mmpL3 positive control gene did not decrease in Msm with the control vector, regardless of exposure to aTc at 0 and 24 h (Fig. 1f), indicating that the control vector had no effect on the inserted gene. This finding from the vector control was in concordance with inhA gene expression (Fig. 1h). However, Msm with the mmpL3 positive control gene showed a significant reduction after induction by aTc at 24 h (Fig. 1g), indicating that the dCas9 gene effectively bound to the inserted sgRNA targeting the mmpL3 gene and led to transcriptional interruption. In addition to the sgRNAs targeting the inhA gene, in the presence of sgRNA and aTc for 24 h, the inhA gene was significantly downregulated compared to that in the absence of aTc (Fig. 1i and k). When comparing these three sgRNAs, only sgRNA2 and sgRNA3 showed significant differences compared to the vector control. However, there was no substantial difference in the expression levels of these two sgRNAs (Fig. 1l).

The effect of aTc concentration on CRISPRi system expression

The expression of the CRISPRi system can be titratable depending on the aTc concentration. The optimal aTc concentration was determined through RT‒qPCR or dot-spotting phenotypic assays. At 0 h, the dCas9 gene expression in Msm with the vector control, Msm with sgRNA2 and Msm with the positive sgRNA mmpL3 was significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner with aTc values of 50, 100, and 200 ng/ml compared to that in the uninduced groups. However, after exposure to 100 ng/ml of aTc for 24 h, the dCas9 gene was highly activated, with significant differences compared to that in the other groups (Fig. 3a and c). The positive control showed a reduction in mmpL3 gene expression, with fold changes of 0.059 ± 0.011%, 0.072 ± 0.026%, and 0.068 ± 0.044% for 50, 100, and 200 ng/ml, respectively. Nevertheless, no significant differences were observed among these three concentrations (Fig. 3e). The expression of the inhA gene decreased with a fold change of 0.048 ± 0.019, 0.048 ± 0.023, and 0.043 ± 0.020 for 50, 100, and 200 ng/ml, respectively, without any statistically significant differences among the groups (Fig. 3g). Target gene expression and growth in Msm with a CRISPRi vector containing positive sgRNA or sgRNA2 were affected by aTc, with over 90% repression. However, nonsignificant effects on gene expression or bacterial growth were observed among the three different aTc concentrations (Figs. 2d and e and 3).

Inhibitory concentrations at 50% (IC50) and 90% (IC90) according to the resazurin microtiter assay (REMA)

The impact of inhibiting the inhA gene of Msm on resistance to isoniazid and rifampicin, the two most potent anti-tuberculosis agents, was investigated using REMA. The colorimetric REMA, which relies on an oxidation-reduction reaction to induce a blue-to-pink color change in the presence of live bacterium, was endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) for drug sensitivity testing of M. tuberculosis28. We assessed the resistance level of isoniazid and rifampicin through the IC90 and IC50 as the interpretative guidelines of MIC for both agents are unavailable. In this recent study, Msm mc2 155, a wild-type control, was used as the baseline for evaluating the resistance of the test strains to both drugs. The results revealed that the IC90 and IC50 (the concentration of drug required for 90% and 50% inhibition, respectively) of Msm with sgRNA2 with 100 ng/ml of aTc activation were lower than those of the wild-type control or Msm with the vector control, irrespective of exposure to aTc (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

The effect of CRISPRi targeting sgRNA2 on bacterial morphological changes was visualized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and compared to that of Msm mc2 155 wild-type and Msm vector control cells under aTc activation. Altered morphology, including slight cell shortening and a rough cell surface (Fig. 5e), was observed in aTc-activated Msm cells with sgRNA2, while the other cell types exhibited no morphological changes (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The CRISPRi system has become a valuable tool for precise gene regulation in prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. By modulating the expression of specific genes, CRISPRi allows researchers to study the function of essential genes, regulatory networks, genetic pathways, metabolic engineering, and therapeutic applications, particularly in human systems29,30.

CRISPRi has shown promising results in various mycobacterial research. For example, in one previous study, mmpL3 repression through the CRISPRi system in combination with sub-MIC of isoniazid or rifampicin significantly reduced mycobacterial viability31. In another study, CRISPRi has demonstrated the potential to downregulate the arr gene associated with rifampicin resistance. While suppressing the arr gene did not affect bacterial growth, it did decrease the MIC of rifampicin by 8 to 16 times compared to the wild-type control, highlighting that CRISPRi can effectively restore drug sensitivity23. In addition, genes responsible for mycobacterial peptidoglycan modifications that promote beta-lactam resistance were examined. Some of the sgRNAs could inhibit bacterial growth. Additionally, some of them could decrease the MIC of several beta-lactam antibiotics25. These findings supported the concept of improving or restoring the efficacy of existing antimycobacterial agents using CRISPRi technology, which may be beneficial for TB treatment and TB control. Furthermore, other evidence showed that CRISPRi targeted a necessary gene for M. tuberculosis. The suppression of M. tuberculosis DNA gyrase prohibited both extracellular and intracellular bacterial proliferation, suggesting that gyrase depletion made M. tuberculosis unbearable against host immunity32.

Here, we investigated the effectiveness of CRISPRi against inhA gene expression in Msm mc2 155. The CRISPOR, a free web-based tool, was used to predict the sequences of sgRNAs next to PAM sites, providing in silico specificity of each sgRNA. The higher specificity yields a better outcome for the CRISPRi system by preventing an undesirable off-target activity, when CRISPRi might act at other similar sequences besides the target site33. Hence, off-targets at 0–4 mismatches next to PAM sites were also predicted, and the sgRNAs without any off-targets in silico were selected for the experiment34. Three sgRNAs that work with CRISPRi were constructed and screened to identify those that explicitly target the inhA gene. The mmpL3 gene encodes mycobacterial membrane protein large 3, an essential mycolic acid and lipid transporter required for growth and cell viability35. A previous study showed that CRISPRi targeting mmpL3 severely affected cell growth. Thus, the mmpL3 gene served as a positive control in this study to confirm the successful construction of CRISPRi and gene interference36. The dCas9 gene and specific-inserted sgRNA in the PLJR962 vector are expressed by TetR-regulated promoters, which bind to Tet operators (TetO). These inducible promoters require activators such as aTc, a tetracycline derivative that does not express antimicrobial activity. However, aTc is a more active inducer than tetracycline and is commonly used as an activator for controlling Tet-regulated promoters37. Upon the aTc activation, aTc binds with TetR, causing conformational changes that release TetR from TetO and subsequently allow transcription to proceed38. Following aTc activation, the expression of the dCas9 gene increased in all strains and continued to rise over 24 h. The formation of a complex between the dCas9 transcript and the sgRNA targeting the inhA gene bound to the complementary site on the target gene induced CRISPRi and disrupted inhA transcription, leading to a reduction in inhA expression. CRISPRi can block transcription initiation by targeting either template or nontemplate strands of the promoter region39,40 or prevent transcription elongation by targeting nontemplate strands of the 5’UTR and ORF8,41,42. The variation in repression efficiency may be attributed to the design of the sgRNAs, as the PAM sites of sgRNA2 and sgRNA3 were located within the ORF of the nontarget strand. The transcription start sites (TSSs) of Msm of these two sgRNAs were at positions + 327 and + 406 downstream of the MSMEG_3151 gene. The PAM site of sgRNA1 was located − 18 upstream of the TSS. These results contrast with previous work from Gilbert et al., who reported that a strongly effective sgRNA for CRISPRi in K562 cell lines was designed to target − 50 to + 300 base pairs at the TSS43. However, these findings paralleled the results of Rock et al.44 suggesting that sgRNAs that were designed far from the TSS of M. tuberculosis and M. smegmatis yielded high-level gene repression. In addition, specific strands of the PAM site should be considered much more than the position from the TSS. Likewise, the positive sgRNA targeting the mmpL3 gene resulted in significant repression of its expression.

The phenotypic dot-spotting assay provided further evidence that strains harboring sgRNAs targeting inhA or mmpL3 could reduce viability by up to ~ 1,000-fold. However, the vector control did not affect inhA or mmpL3 repression, according to either gene expression or dot-spotting phenotypic assays, since no specific sgRNA targeting was present. The inducible promoters that express dCas9 and sgRNA are titratable, affecting the control of gene repression23,45. A wide range of concentrations of aTc that induces the Tet operator has been reported25,46. However, a high concentration of aTc could inhibit mycobacterial viability. The growth of M. abscessus was suppressed by aTc concentrations ≥ 100 ng/ml in a previous study47. In this study, after exposure to aTc for 24 h, the dCas9 gene was significantly upregulated in all the strains and at all the different aTc concentrations compared to that in the inactivated strains. As a result of the expression of the dCas9 gene, mmpL3 (a positive control) and the inhA gene were significantly repressed at all concentrations. Treatment with 100 ng/ml of aTc did not result in the greatest growth inhibition of Msm with sgRNA2, although it was a suitable aTc concentration for suppressing the growth of Msm with the positive sgRNA mmpL3. However, no statistically significant differences were detected for mmpL3 or inhA gene repression. Rock et al. developed an orthogonal CRISPRi platform for transcriptional repression in mycobacteria. Successful significant repression has been demonstrated using the dCas9sth1 system (PLJR962 vector) with 100 mg/ml of aTc44. Other studies have also used 100 ng/ml of aTc for the induction of CRISPRi targeting Msm25,44,48.

As a result, inhA suppression affected the viability of the Msm cells with sgRNA2 after aTc induction compared to other Msm strains. A previous study revealed that inhA inactivation led to a similar outcome as isoniazid treatment49. Hence, the inhA repression may reduce the level of drug resistance. Isoniazid and rifampicin are the two most potent first-line anti-tuberculosis agents. The IC90 and IC50 of isoniazid of Msm with sgRNA2 tended to increase in response to isoniazid (up to ~ 4-fold and ~ 14-fold for the IC90 and IC50, respectively). Isoniazid interferes with mycolic acids, the main unique component of the mycobacterial cell wall50,51. Isoniazid-treated M. smegmatis cells exhibited the accumulation of a long chain of saturated fatty acids and failure of mycolic acid synthesis. The morphology of these cells tends to bleb, shorten, widen, and finally lead to cell lysis49,52,53. The effect of inhA inactivation was comparable to that of isoniazid treatment. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) analysis showed that either inhA depletion or isoniazid treatment resulted in the accumulation of α′-mycolates and the inhibition of mycolic acid synthesis49,52. As previously stated, CRISPRi could inhibit the expression of the inhA gene, which may cause cell death as an isoniazid treatment and could lower the IC90 and IC50 compared with those of the controls. Similarly, the rifampicin susceptibility of Msm with sgRNA2 decreased (by up to ~ 4-fold and ~ 14-fold for the IC90 and IC50, respectively). This result was in accordance with a previous study using other vectors (pRH2502 and pRH2521) for dCas9 expression from Streptococcus pyogenes and sgRNA expression, demonstrating an increased MIC of rifampicin when the target gene of isoniazid was suppressed54. This result could be explained by inhA downregulation in response to isoniazid treatment. We conducted a pilot study to investigate the effect of CRISPRi targeting the inhA gene on bacterial drug susceptibility (Supplementary data 2). We performed a dot-spotting assay on Middlebrook 7H11 supplemented with isoniazid and rifampicin. The growth of Msm with sgRNA2 was followed at every 24-hour time point from 0 to 72 h after aTc activation. Msm with sgRNA2 lost their viability on both drug-supplemented plates after aTc induction (~ 10 to ~ 100-fold compared to the Msm control strains). The impaired growth was continually observed until 72 h. These findings support the potential of the CRISPRi system to effectively silence the inhA gene, which impacts bacterial drug susceptibility.

The long-term stability of inhA suppression has yet to be directly studied in this research. However, during our pilot study, the suppression of the inhA gene was observed in Msm with vector control and with sgRNA1, sgRNA2, and sgRNA3 at 0, 24, and 48 h by both qRT-PCR and dot-spotting phenotypic method, with no significant difference between each time point. Moreover, a pilot study investigating the effect of CRISPRi targeting the inhA gene on bacterial drug susceptibility (Supplementary data 2) revealed a similar trend of inhA suppression observed through drug susceptibility testing until 72 h without additional aTc during the experiment.

Silencing of the inhA gene affected the morphology of mycobacterial cells. The cells were shortened, bulged, and lysed49,55. In the present study, cell morphological changes were detected in some of the Msm strains generated with sgRNA2 along with aTc induction. Porous cells could be found after three days of treatment with isoniazid53. Hence, morphological images should be further investigated for a more extended period for CRISPRi-mediated methods. The inhA gene is highly involved in mycolic acid synthesis for maintaining the mycobacterial cell shape19. Thus, to fully understand how CRISPRi targeting the inhA gene impacts the morphology of mycobacterial cells, it should be further examined whether inhA gene silencing could affect mycobacteria’s mycolic acid synthesis. Free mycolic acids are associated with the formation of biofilms by M. smegmatis and M. tuberculosis56,57. Here, we further conducted a pilot study to investigate the effect of CRISPRi targeting the inhA gene on bacterial biofilm formation (Supplementary data 2).

This recent study’s limitations include the lack of an M. tuberculosis model to represent the more closely related findings. Here, we used Msm mc2 155 as a surrogate for M. tuberculosis to avoid the pathogenic nature and slow colony formation of M. tuberculosis, which made it difficult to work within our laboratory. Msm mc2 155 encodes thousands of conserved mycobacterial gene orthologs and has cell architecture and physiology similar to M. tuberculosis26. Msm mc2 155 has contributed to various applications, such as discovering or developing antimycobacterial agents, studying host-pathogen interaction, or studying mycobacterial gene and genome characteristics through a novel molecular tool. However, the M. tuberculosis model should be used in a future experiment to complete the research’s comprehensive knowledge. Furthermore, an in vivo animal model study and in vivo delivery study of the CRISPR-dCas9 interference system should be conducted to provide important insights into potential therapeutic applications.

In summary, these findings support the potential of CRISPRi as a powerful tool for gene regulation. By targeting the inhA gene in Msm, the CRISPRi system effectively reduced gene expression. Bacterial viability was significantly inhibited, presumably due to the downregulation of the inhA gene. This highlights the ability to manipulate gene activity and control bacterial growth precisely. Additionally, the CRISPRi system increased the susceptibility of bacteria to commonly used antibiotics, suggesting its potential for enhancing existing drugs’ efficacy, especially in combating drug-resistant strains. These results might have implications for future research on the development of novel therapeutic strategies against mycobacterial infections by synergistic or additive CRISPRi technology with current anti-tuberculosis regimens. Nonetheless, the potential of CRISPRi should be further assessed by exploiting M. tuberculosis as a model which might reflect a more reasonable outcome of tuberculosis treatment. In addition, the effect of CRISPRi should be further examined in vivo. Another challenge that needs elucidation is CRISPRi delivery to a specifically targeted host cell to achieve a comprehensive knowledge of implementing CRISPRi in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and culture conditions

Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) were grown on Luria–Bertani (LB) media (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India). Mycobacterium smegmatis mc2 155 (Msm mc2 155, ATCC™ 700084) was cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (Sigma‒Aldrich, Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) and on Middlebrook 7H11 agar (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) supplemented with 0.2% glycerol, 0.05% Tween 80, and 10% ODAC. The PLJR962 CRISPRi vector expressing Streptococcus thermophilus dCas9 (SthdCas9) with a specific site for inserted sgRNAs (Addgene plasmid #115162) was kindly provided by Dr. Sarah Fortune (Department of Immunology and Infectious Diseases, Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, USA). E. coli DH5α and Msm containing the PLJR962 plasmid were cultured in the presence of kanamycin (50 µg/ml), a selective marker for the vector.

In each experiment, a primary culture of Msm was prepared by inoculating a single colony of each strain from log phase cultures into 2 ml of Middlebrook 7H9 broth and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h with constant shaking at 200 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.8-1.0 before subsequent experiments.

Msm model construction

Construction of PLJR962 harboring sgRNAs of interest

Several target sequences adjacent to the PAM site of SthdCas9 (5’-NNAGAAW-3’) were identified from the inhA (MSMEG_3151) gene encoding [NADH] enoyl-[acyl-carrier-protein] reductase of the reference Msm mc2 155 DNA sequence (GenBank accession no. NC_008596.1) by the free web-based tool CRISPOR (Table 1)34. At the 5’ end, all forward oligonucleotides had additional sequences of “ggga”, whereas reverse oligonucleotides had “aaac” to ligate with the PLJR962 vector. Each pair of oligonucleotides was annealed following Rock et al.42 with slight modifications. Briefly, 25 µl of each F’ and R’ mixture at a final concentration of 20 µM, 1X T4 ligase buffer, and nuclease-free water were added, followed by heating at 95°C for 5 minutes, cooling to 0.1°C per second to 25°C and incubation at 25°C for 10 minutes. The PLJR962 CRISPRi vector was digested with BsmBI enzyme (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and ligated with annealed oligos simultaneously by incubating the reaction mixture composed of BsmBI enzyme (5 units), the PLJR962 vector (25 ng), T4 DNA ligase (200 units), annealed oligos (1 µM), and nuclease-free water with the following conditions: 30 cycles of 42°C for 5 minutes, 16°C for 5 minutes, and 60°C for 5 minutes. The ligation products were transformed into E. coli DH5α cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. The ligation plasmids and E. coli DH5α cells were mixed, incubated on ice for 30 minutes, heated at 42°C for 30 seconds, and cooled on ice for 5 minutes. SOC medium was added, and the cultures were incubated at 37°C with vigorous shaking for 1 hour. Finally, the cultures were spread on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/ml). After overnight incubation, colonies that grew on the selection plate were selected for the insertion of sgRNA by colony PCR using the sgRNA-specific R’ primer coupled with the pBR322ori F’ primer (Table 2). The successful integration plasmids and primer 5’-ttcctgtgaagagccattgataatg-3’ were sent to Bionics Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea, to perform sequence analysis via conventional Sanger DNA sequencing using a 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). sgRNA targeting the mmpL3 gene was used as a positive control for cloning and gene interference, as recommended by Rock et al.42.

Transformation of construct plasmids into Msm mc2 155

Msm mc2 155 electrocompetent cells were prepared according to the following protocol. The primary culture prepared as described above was diluted to an OD600 of 0.025 in 50 ml of Middlebrook 7H9 broth and incubated with constant shaking for 1–2 days until it reached an OD600 of ~ 0.8-1.0. The cell pellet was collected by centrifugation at 4,000×g and 4 °C for 10 min and gently resuspended in 1 ml of ice-cold sterile 10% glycerol. Then, another 24 ml of ice-cold sterile 10% glycerol was added. The washing procedure was repeated three times before the cells were resuspended in 1 ml of ice-cold sterile 10% glycerol, and 100 µl of cells was aliquoted into each 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube. To preserve the cells for later use, the cells were quickly frozen in a dry ice ethanol bath. Each verified plasmid from DH5α competent E. coli cells was electroporated into Msm mc2 155 electrocompetent cells by pipetting at least 100 ng of plasmid DNA into Msm mc2 155 electrocompetent cells, gently mixed, incubated on ice for 1 min, and transferred to a prechilled 0.2 cm electrode gap cuvette. The transformation was performed by an EC100 Electroporator at 2,800 V. Then, Middlebrook 7H9 broth was added, the culture was incubated at 37 °C with constant shaking for approximately 2 hours, and the culture was plated on Middlebrook 7H11 agar supplemented with 20 µg/ml of kanamycin to select the transformant. After incubation for 2–4 days, the colonies were screened, and successful integration was confirmed by colony PCR (Table 2.) and DNA sequencing.

Effect of CRISPRi constructs on mycobacteria

Selection of sgRNA

The sgRNA that most highly repressed the mRNA expression of the inhA gene or affected bacterial growth to the greatest extent was selected using reverse transcriptase quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) and dot-spotting phenotyping methods. A primary culture of each Msm cell that carried the vector control, different sgRNAs, and the positive control sgRNA, mmpL3, was diluted in Middlebrook 7H9 broth to an OD600 of 0.001–0.002 and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and constant shaking (200 rpm). When the OD600 reached 0.1–0.2, each culture was equally divided into two tubes. The first tube was not exposed to anhydrotetracycline (aTc), while the other culture was supplemented with 100 ng/ml of aTc to activate the expression of dCas9 and sgRNA.

After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h, the bacterial cells were sedimented at 4,000×g and 4 °C for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellets were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen™, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were determined using a NanoDrop™ 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA samples (1 µg) were treated with RNase-free DNase I enzyme (1 unit) (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 100 ng of the DNase I-treated RNA samples were converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) with a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

The synthesized cDNA was used as a template to determine the transcription levels of each target gene (Table 2) by RT‒qPCR with the following steps: initial activation at 95 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s; and annealing/extension and plate reading at 60 °C for 30 s. Each target sequence was independently tested in triplicate. The relative mRNA levels were obtained by normalization to those of sigA, a housekeeping gene, and quantified by the 2−ΔΔct method. Moreover, the growth of each culture with or without aTc activation at 0 and 24 h was also determined by 10-fold serial dilution and spotted on Middlebrook 7H11 agar media supplemented with 20 µg/ml of kanamycin. The bacterial growth was determined after incubation at 37 °C for three days. Each target sequence was independently tested in triplicate.

Titration of aTc-activating CRISPRi

Different concentrations of aTc (50, 100, and 200 ng/ml) were added to the culture of Msm cells harboring the vector control, the selected sgRNA, or the positive control sgRNA mmpL3. The maximal induction of dCas9 and the sgRNA that repressed inhA gene expression was assessed by qRT‒PCR and phenotypic methods following previously described protocols. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test by resazurin microtiter assay (REMA)

A broth microdilution assay using resazurin dye was applied to examine the effect of CRISPRi targeting the inhA gene on the susceptibility to isoniazid and rifampicin. Isoniazid and rifampicin were diluted 2-fold in 100 µl of Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 0.2% glycerol and 10% OADC without Tween 80 (7H9C) to give final concentrations ranging from 0.39 to 100 µg/ml (for isoniazid) and from 0.39 to 50 µg/ml (for rifampicin). The wild-type control, Msm mc2 155, Msm with the vector control, and Msm with the selected sgRNA were prepared as a bacterial suspension with a concentration of approximately 1 × 104 CFU/ml and were inoculated in 100 µl per well. Media and bacteria only were included as media controls (M) and bacteria controls (B). The outer perimeter was filled with sterile water to prevent dehydration of the test wells. The plate was sealed with parafilm and incubated at 37 °C. After two days of incubation, 30 µl of 0.01% resazurin was added and the plate was reincubated at 37 °C for 18 h to observe the color change from blue to pink, indicating bacterial growth in the bacterial control wells. Fluorescence units (FUs) were determined by a BioTek™ Synergy™ microplate reader (BioTek® Instruments, Inc., Vermont, USA) with excitation at 530 nm and emission at 590 nm. All experiments were performed in triplicate on three separate occasions. The percent inhibition was calculated from the following formula: 1-(test well FU/mean FU of triplicate B wells) × 100. The inhibitory concentrations (IC90 and IC50) of isoniazid and rifampicin were defined as the lowest drug concentrations that can inhibit ≥ 90% or ≥ 50% of bacterial growth, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The wild-type control, Msm mc2 155, Msm with the vector control, and Msm with the selected sgRNA were prepared for morphological change inspection by SEM. All cultures were prepared as previously described in the selection of the sgRNA section. All bacterial cells were collected after centrifugation at 4,000×g and 4 °C for 10 min and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) for at least 1 h. The samples were washed twice with phosphate buffer, followed by distilled water. Serial dehydration was performed with 30%, 50%, 70%, 95%, and 3 × 100% ethanol for 5 min each. All the samples were dried at a critical point, mounted onto a stub and sputter-coated with gold. All the samples were examined, and images were recorded with a JSM-IT500 HR (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). SEM was performed by the Scientific and Technological Research Equipment Center at Chulalongkorn University, Thailand.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Differences between two groups or more were determined by nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test) or one-way nonparametric ANOVA (Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test), respectively, using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). A P-value of *p < 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01 and ***p ≤ 0.001 were considered statistically significant.
