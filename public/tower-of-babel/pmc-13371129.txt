U-CBAMNet: an attention-guided deep learning model for accurate and explainable prediction of HER2 expression from breast ultrasound cine videos
Authors: Zhiwen Zhang, Chongxuan Tian, Linlin Shi, Liguang Zhou, Kaining Zhang, Jinshu Pang, Qian Wang, Hong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate assessment of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression is essential for guiding targeted therapy in breast cancer. Conventional immunohistochemistry and fluorescence in situ hybridization remain the diagnostic standard but are invasive, costly, and limited by sampling bias.PurposeTo develop and internally evaluate an explainable deep learning model based on an improved Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) integrated with EfficientNet-B3 (termed U-CBAMNet) for non-invasive prediction of HER2 expression from breast ultrasound cine videos.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 149 patients with pathologically confirmed HER2 status was used. Ultrasound cine videos were divided by patient ID into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. For each lesion, dynamic cine sequences were processed frame-wise using U-CBAMNet, and frame-level features were aggregated via temporal average pooling to obtain video-level predictions. The proposed model incorporated a refined CBAM with adaptive weighted pooling and spatial attention to emphasize diagnostically informative regions. Performance was compared against ResNet50, DenseNet121, Swin-Transformer, and baseline EfficientNet-B3 using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC. Ablation analysis confirmed the complementary contributions of channel and spatial attention mechanisms. Visual attention maps highlighted lesion-centric regions consistent with radiologist-identified areas, demonstrating strong biological plausibility.ConclusionThe proposed U-CBAMNet model enables accurate and interpretable non-invasive prediction of HER2 expression directly from routine cine ultrasound imaging. This approach may serve as a cost-effective adjunct to molecular testing, facilitating preoperative risk stratification and personalized treatment planning in breast cancer management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02383-3.Keywords: Breast cancer, Ultrasound imaging, HER2, Deep learning, Attention mechanism

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to represent a major global health burden as the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1]. The remarkable biological and molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer underscores the necessity of precision oncology, wherein treatment strategies are tailored to the tumor’s intrinsic molecular subtype. Among these, the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–enriched subtype constitutes approximately 15–20% of cases and is characterized by high proliferative activity, aggressive clinical behavior, and historically poor prognosis [2]. The advent of targeted anti-HER2 therapies-most notably trastuzumab and its subsequent generations-has revolutionized disease management, dramatically improving patient survival. Consequently, accurate and reproducible assessment of HER2 expression has become a fundamental prerequisite for therapeutic decision-making and prognostic evaluation [3].

Currently, HER2 status is determined through invasive tissue sampling and subsequent immunohistochemistry (IHC) or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing, following the latest ASCO guidelines [4]. Although these histopathological standards are definitive, they are limited by procedural invasiveness, high cost, and delayed turnaround time. More importantly, the well-recognized intratumoral heterogeneity of HER2 expression can lead to sampling bias, where a single core biopsy may not accurately reflect the molecular landscape of the entire tumor [5]. Furthermore, in clinical practice, dynamic ultrasound cine clips—which comprehensively capture tumor morphology—are often not routinely preserved, resulting in the loss of potentially valuable data for thorough analysis. This diagnostic limitation highlights an urgent clinical need for developing non-invasive, comprehensive, and reproducible strategies capable of accurately characterizing HER2 status across the whole lesion.

Ultrasound (US) is a non-ionizing, widely available, non-invasive, and cost-efficient imaging modality that plays an essential role in breast cancer screening, diagnosis, and local staging. Beyond its anatomical visualization capacity, growing evidence suggests that ultrasound phenotypes may encode critical information related to tumor biology and molecular subtypes [6, 7]. Distinct sonographic patterns-such as irregular margins, posterior acoustic shadowing, and heterogeneous echotexture-have been linked to specific genomic and histopathological signatures. This correlation supports the concept of “imaging genomics,” wherein non-invasive image-derived biomarkers may serve as surrogates for molecular characteristics. Deep learning (DL), with its unparalleled ability to extract high-dimensional, sub-visual features from medical images, offers a promising avenue for decoding these latent “imaging signatures” of HER2 expression directly from routine ultrasound scans.

In recent years, DL-based approaches have shown great promise in breast imaging, primarily in differentiating benign from malignant lesions [8] or identifying broad molecular subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer [9]. However, fine-grained prediction of HER2 expression levels-categorizing cases into negative, low, and positive groups-remains largely underexplored. Existing studies are often constrained by small datasets, binary task simplifications that overlook the clinically significant “low” category, or insufficient model interpretability that limits clinical translation [10]. Most conventional studies have relied on single-frame ultrasound images, whereas clinicians base their diagnostic decisions on real-time dynamic videos in which variations in probe scanning trajectory, angle, and applied pressure provide additional diagnostic information. Earlier methods predominantly adopted a ‘frame-level feature extraction combined with RNN/LSTM’ architecture [11]; more recent approaches increasingly employ 3D CNNs [12], spatiotemporal attention mechanisms [13], and video Transformers [14, 15], enabling direct modeling of both local and global spatiotemporal information.

To address these gaps, we propose a novel attention-guided deep learning framework, termed U-CBAMNet (Ultrasound-CBAMNet; “U” denotes ultrasound-specific enhancement), for the non-invasive, multi-class prediction of HER2 expression status from standard breast ultrasound cine videos. The model integrates an EfficientNet backbone pre-trained on ImageNet with an enhanced convolutional block attention mechanism (CBAM), enabling adaptive focus on pathologically informative regions of the tumor and its microenvironment. Using a well-annotated retrospective cohort, we systematically evaluated the model’s performance and interpretability. HER2 status determination and classification criteria

HER2 status was extracted from the official pathology reports of surgical resection or core needle biopsy specimens. The HER2 expression status for all enrolled patients was rigorously determined according to the latest American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists (ASCO/CAP) guideline update [3]. The diagnostic workflow and specific thresholds for each category are as follows(supplement Table 1):

HER2-positive (HER2-P)

Defined as an immunohistochemistry (IHC) score of 3+, or an IHC score of 2 + with a confirmatory fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay result that is positive. A positive FISH result requires a HER2/CEP17 ratio of ≥ 2.0 with an average HER2 gene copy number of ≥ 4.0 signals per cell.

HER2-low (HER2-L)

Defined as an IHC score of 1+, or an IHC score of 2 + with a negative confirmatory FISH assay. A negative FISH result is defined as a HER2/CEP17 ratio of < 2.0 with an average HER2 gene copy number of < 4.0 signals per cell.

HER2-zero (HER2-Z)

Defined as an IHC score of 0.

All pathological reports, including IHC slides and FISH images when applicable, were retrospectively reviewed and validated by two dedicated breast pathologists to ensure diagnostic consistency and adherence to the aforementioned criteria.”

Ultrasound equipment and acquisition protocol

The ultrasound cine videos and images used in this study were acquired from multiple clinical ultrasound systems to improve the generalizability of the developed model. A standardized scanning protocol was followed for all examinations. Patients were positioned supine with the ipsilateral arm raised. Each lesion was scanned dynamically in radial and anti-radial as well as orthogonal planes using a freehand technique to include the entire lesion and adjacent parenchyma, with particular emphasis on obtaining the largest cross-sectional view. Real-time cine clips were recorded throughout the examination, and representative static images were saved from the most informative frames. Concurrently, representative static DICOM-format images were saved from the most illustrative frames. All image data were subsequently converted and preprocessed into a uniform JPEG format for model input. The inclusion of imaging data acquired from multiple ultrasound vendors introduced variability in image texture and contrast within the internal cohort. This heterogeneity may reduce overfitting to a single device setting. however, it should be regarded as internal data diversity rather than external validation.

Data preprocessing

A standardized preprocessing pipeline was rigorously implemented to ensure data consistency and model robustness. For each identified tumor, an experienced breast radiologist with over 10 years of experience selected the ultrasound cine video that best captured the largest cross-sectional area of the lesion and its surrounding tissue during dynamic scanning. Multiple tumors in the same patient were treated as independent samples. Two blinded radiologists with more than 10 years of experience in breast ultrasound independently annotated tumor regions on representative frames within each cine sequence using bounding boxes. Inter-observer agreement was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement), and agreement was considered excellent when ICC > 0.75. Any discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by a senior radiologist with more than 15 years of experience in breast ultrasound. The bounding box annotated on a representative frame was then propagated to the remaining frames of the same cine clip by reusing the same coordinates with a small padding margin to accommodate minor in-plane motion during the short acquisition. No additional object detection or tracking algorithm was applied for this propagation. Any disagreements between the two radiologists were adjudicated by a third senior radiologist to establish the ground truth regions of interest (ROI). For each cine video, frames within the annotated ROI were cropped with a minimal margin, resized to 224 × 224 pixels via bicubic interpolation, and normalized to the [0,1] range. To control computational cost, reduce inter-frame redundancy, and standardize model input across cine clips of variable duration, we uniformly sampled a fixed number of frames (denoted as T) from each cine sequence. In the present study, T was set to 8 as a pragmatic compromise between preserving representative temporal information and maintaining computational efficiency, given that adjacent ultrasound frames were often highly similar. Using all frames from every cine clip would have substantially increased redundancy and sequence-length variability without proportionally improving input diversity. During training, real-time data augmentation-including random rotation (± 15°), horizontal flipping, and brightness/contrast adjustments (± 10%) was applied in a clip-consistent manner: one set of augmentation parameters was sampled per cine clip and applied identically to all sampled frames within that clip, thereby preserving temporal coherence while still improving model generalization. All experiments reported in this study used this clip-consistent augmentation strategy. U-CBAMNet was applied frame-wise to the preprocessed sequences, where the same EfficientNet-B3 backbone was reused for all frames with shared weights. The resulting frame-level feature vectors were aggregated using temporal average pooling to form a compact video-level representation, which was subsequently fed into the classification head to predict the HER2 expression category for each cine video.

U-CBAMNet

The proposed framework is a three-class convolutional neural network based on the EfficientNet-B3 backbone pre-trained on ImageNet, enhanced with a CBAM to adaptively emphasize informative features in ultrasound cine videos. The overall architecture is designed to capture both global semantic representations and local discriminative details from video frames, achieving robust classification performance on subtle visual differences among the three target categories. Training configuration and hyperparameters

The network was trained end-to-end for three-class classification using the categorical cross-entropy loss. We used the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10− 4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10− 4. The learning rate followed a cosine annealing schedule with a 5-epoch linear warm-up. The batch size was set to 64, and the model was trained for 120 epochs. For each ultrasound cine clip, we uniformly sampled T = 8 frames as input, as described in the preprocessing pipeline. This fixed-length sampling strategy was adopted to provide standardized model input across clips with variable duration while limiting redundancy among highly similar adjacent frames. As described in the preprocessing pipeline, data augmentation during training was applied in a clip-consistent manner, with one shared set of augmentation parameters used across all sampled frames within the same clip. The fusion weights w1 and w2 in Eq. (2) were learnable scalars (w1 + w2 = 1), initialized as (0.5, 0.5) and optimized jointly with all network parameters via backpropagation (implemented by applying a softmax normalization over two unconstrained parameters).

Experimental setup

In this study, all experiments were conducted using a strict patient-level data split to prevent potential data leakage. Specifically, the dataset was divided according to patient IDs into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%). This ensured that videos from the same patient did not appear across different subsets, thereby preserving evaluation independence and improving the reliability of the reported results. For statistical analysis of baseline clinicopathological variables, categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5), whereas continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and then compared using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data or the Kruskal-Wallis test otherwise. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate model performance, multiple classification metrics were calculated on the independent test cohort, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, each reported at the class level as well as in macro-averaged and micro-averaged forms [16]. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for key performance metrics were estimated using patient-level nonparametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples on the held-out test set. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to further characterize model discrimination. Because the task involved three-class classification, ROC curves were derived in a one-vs-rest manner, and the overall AUC values reported in this study were expressed as macro-averaged AUCs. Pairwise comparisons of AUC values between models on the same independent test cohort were performed using DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves. The resulting two-sided P values were used to assess whether differences in AUC between models were statistically significant. In addition to quantitative comparisons, qualitative interpretability analysis was also performed [17]. The Grad-CAM technique and the model’s own attention maps were used to generate heatmaps highlighting the most discriminative regions attended to by the network. These visualizations were compared with physicians’prior diagnostic knowledge to examine whether the model’s focus aligned with clinically relevant areas, thereby improving the transparency and interpretability of the proposed framework.

The distribution of baseline characteristics in this cohort closely reflected that of typical patients with hormone receptor–positive breast tumors reported in previous clinical studies [18–20]. Approximately 18.1% of patients were younger than 40 years, 36.9% were 40–49 years old, and 44.9% were aged 50 years or older. These findings suggest that the study cohort was clinically representative and that the observed variations in HER2 expression reflected biologically plausible patterns rather than sampling bias.

Ablation study

To quantify the contribution of each component, we conducted a step-wise ablation starting from the full model and then removing CBAM as a whole, channel attention, spatial attention, or the weighted pooling fusion in the channel branch. Table 3 reports accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC (with 95% confidence intervals(CI)) on the test set. The full model achieved 87.32% accuracy(95%CI: 77.6–97.0), 88.91% precision (95% CI: 79.7–98.1), 87.64% recall (95% CI: 78.0-97.3), 88.12% F1 (95% CI: 78.7–97.6), and 0.88 AUC(95% CI: 0.79–0.97). Removing the entire CBAM led to the largest performance drop across all metrics, confirming that attention-based refinement is the main driver of the observed gains(83.47% accuracy, 95% CI: 72.6–94.3; 0.80 AUC, 95% CI: 0.68–0.92). Isolating the two attention branches shows complementary effects. Eliminating channel attention decreased accuracy to 85.26% (95% CI: 74.9–95.6)and AUC to 0.83(95% CI: 0.72–0.94). The largest relative losses were in precision and F1, indicating that channel-wise reweighting helps suppress irrelevant or noisy filters and stabilizes the decision boundary-particularly important for reducing false positives in speckle-rich ultrasound. In contrast, removing spatial attention reduced accuracy to 84.91% (95% CI: 74.5–95.4) and produced the largest AUC degradation 0.79 (95% CI: 0.67–0.91), implying that spatial saliency is critical for class separability. This aligns with the imaging characteristics of ultrasound, where lesion boundaries are weak and local texture is subtle; spatial masks guide the network to focus on diagnostically meaningful regions and thereby improve ranking quality. Finally, disabling the weighted pooling fusion in the channel branch yielded 84.63% accuracy (95% CI: 74.1–95.2) with a modest AUC drop to 0.86 (95% CI: 0.76–0.96), but a clear reduction in recall and F1. This pattern suggests that adaptive weighting between GAP and GMP contributes mainly to sensitivity-it balances intensity-driven and texture-driven responses, helping the model detect more true positives in the low-contrast, small-lesion regime.

Compared with the baseline CBAM, the improved module generally produced more compact and spatially coherent activations with less diffuse background response. In some cases, however, the highlighted regions extended beyond the annotated bounding box or covered only part of the visually apparent lesion. This is expected because the heatmaps were generated on lesion-centered ROI crops rather than hard-masked lesion-only inputs, and both Grad-CAM-like and attention-based visualizations provide coarse localization after upsampling from lower-resolution feature maps. Therefore, activations outside the bounding box but within the cropped patch may reflect the contribution of adjacent perilesional tissue as well as the limited spatial precision of saliency mapping. Overall, the improved CBAM yielded more consistent case-specific attention patterns, suggesting improved focus on diagnostically relevant regions.

Comparison among different models

Figure 4 presents the patient-level confusion matrices of the four compared models (ResNet50, Swin-Transformer, EfficientNet-B3, and the proposed U-CBAMNet) on the independent test cohort(n = 45). Each matrix shows how predictions are distributed across the three HER2 expression categories (HER2-Z, HER2-L, and HER2-P), and the accuracy values reported below are computed directly from these matrices. Overall, ResNet50 achieved 34/45 correct (75.6%). It performed reasonably on HER2-Z (14/16 correct) but showed substantial confusion involving the HER2-L and HER2-P categories: 3 HER2-L cases were predicted as HER2-Z, and 3 HER2-P cases were predicted as HER2-L, indicating limited ability to separate borderline patterns between low and positive expression. Swin-Transformer improved the diagonal dominance to 37/45 correct (82.2%), maintaining strong recognition for HER2-Z (15/16 correct) and HER2-P (12/15 correct), while the main remaining error mode was HER2-L misclassified as HER2-P (3 cases), suggesting that low-expression lesions remain challenging. EfficientNet-B3 showed 33/45 correct (73.3%), with the largest confusion occurring in the HER2-P group (9/15 correct), where misclassifications were mainly toward HER2-L (4 cases) and HER2-Z (2 cases), consistent with difficulty in distinguishing positive cases under speckle noise and weak boundaries. In contrast, the proposed U-CBAMNet demonstrated the most consistent classification with 39/45 correct (86.7%) and the clearest diagonal structure. Misclassifications were limited and concentrated in the clinically challenging HER2-L category (3 HER2-L cases predicted as HER2-P), while HER2-Z and HER2-P were recognized robustly (15/16 and 13/15 correct, respectively). These results support that the improved attention design helps the network suppress background speckle and focus on lesion-relevant patterns, improving separability especially around the HER2-low boundary.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and internally evaluated an ultrasound-tailored deep learning framework, U-CBAMNet, for automatic classification of HER2 expression from breast ultrasound cine videos. Built on an EfficientNet-B3 backbone and a modified CBAM, the proposed network achieved an accuracy of 87.32% on the independent patient-level test set and a macro-average AUC of 0.88, showing a consistent trend toward improved performance compared with conventional architectures such as ResNet50, Swin-Transformer, and the baseline EfficientNet-B3. Notably, however, the absolute differences among models were modest on this dataset and may correspond to only a small number of patients; therefore, these results should be interpreted as suggestive rather than definitive evidence of superiority, pending larger-scale validation and formal statistical comparison. Beyond overall performance, the design of U-CBAMNet introduces several methodological choices that are well matched to ultrasound cine data. First, the channel attention branch adaptively balances global average and max pooling responses rather than treating them equally, which may help integrate complementary intensity- and texture-related information in speckle-rich ultrasound images. Second, the spatial attention branch emphasizes lesion-focused regions through refined spatial weighting, potentially suppress irrelevant background artifacts and ultrasound-specific speckle noise. Together with a lightweight temporal aggregation of frame-wise features, these design choices allow U-CBAMNet to leverage information from cine videos while maintaining computational efficiency and reducing the risk of overfitting in a relatively small single-center dataset. Biologically, HER2-positive breast cancers are often associated with increased cellularity, irregular margins, and heterogeneous echotexture, reflecting their aggressive growth and invasive behavior [21]. By jointly modeling channel and spatial information, attention mechanisms may facilitate highlighting sonographic patterns associated with HER2 expression levels.

Our findings align with prior studies investigating deep learning and radiomics-based prediction of HER2 status using various imaging modalities. For example, Cao et al. [22] reportedthat multi-parametric MRI radiomic features incorporating I both intratumoral and peritumoral regions could predict HER2 status with an AUC of approximately 0.82 in an external test cohort (n = 67). In mammography, Zeng et al. [23] developed a CBAM ResNet-18 model for biomarker prediction, reporting a HER2 test-set AUC of 0.708 (95% CI: 0.609–0.808) in a multi-center setting. Compared with MRI and mammography, ultrasound is more accessible, portable, and cost-effective, butmolecular phenotype prediction from ultrasound remains challenging due to speckle noise, operator dependence, and weak echogenic differences-particularly for HER2-low lesions. While a few ultrasound-based approaches have emerged (e.g., an ultrasound/US + MRI combined model reported an AUC of 0.842 in a test cohort of 48 patients [24]). In this context, our results suggest that an ultrasound-oriented attention design may improve feature discrimination for cine data and yield more stable error patterns, as also supported by the lesion-focused attention heatmaps. Nevertheless, direct comparisons across studies should be made cautiously because of differences in cohorts, reference standards, and evaluation protocols.

From a clinical perspective, this work highlights the potential of ultrasound-based deep learning as a non-invasive adjunct to standard pathology. Although the current evidence is limited to internal testing, if validated externally, U-CBAMNet could support preoperative HER2 assessment and potentially assist in triaging cases for further confirmatory testing, particularly when immunohistochemistry results are equivocal (IHC 2+). The attention-based visualizations provide intuitive interpretability that may help clinicians understand model focus and facilitate adoption. Beyond diagnosis, such models could be explored for treatment response monitoring, as changes in ultrasound appearance and attention distributions may reflect evolving tumor heterogeneity during targeted therapy [25]. These clinical implications, however, remain prospective and require evaluation in larger, multi-center studies.

While this study focused on breast cancer, the target biomarker, HER2, is a well-characterized oncogenic driver in several other solid tumors, notably gastric/gastroesophageal junction (GC/GEJ) cancer, colorectal cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where its assessment is critical for guiding targeted therapies [26, 27]. The methodological framework of U-CBAMNet—leveraging dynamic ultrasound cine videos for non-invasive, attention-guided prediction of HER2 status—is inherently adaptable to these contexts. For instance, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) is routinely used for staging and characterization of GC/GEJ and pancreatic lesions; our approach could be refined using EUS video sequences to predict HER2 status, potentially supplementing or guiding biopsy site selection. Similarly, for superficial lesions in NSCLC or head and neck cancers, high-resolution ultrasound cine clips could serve as the input modality. The key technical adaptations would involve fine-tuning the model on annotated datasets from the target organ and potentially adjusting the architecture to account for organ-specific sonographic patterns (e.g., layering in the gut wall). Future work will explore these directions, aiming to develop a versatile, image-based HER2 phenotyping tool that could reduce reliance on invasive tissue sampling across multiple oncology disciplines. In addition, the proposed framework is not organ-specific: it learns from image-derived spatiotemporal patterns and uses attention to emphasize diagnostically relevant regions, which could be replicated in other settings by (i) collecting video or multi-view imaging data with a consistent pathology reference standard, (ii) defining clinically meaningful label groupings for the target disease, and (iii) fine-tuning the backbone and attention module to account for domain shift introduced by different organs, scanners, and imaging modalities. For non-ultrasound modalities, the same frame-wise feature extraction plus temporal aggregation strategy can be retained, while the ROI definition and preprocessing should be adapted to the anatomy and acquisition protocol. Large, multi-center datasets and external validation will be essential to confirm whether imaging correlates of HER2 status generalize across diseases.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and the model was evaluated only on an internal independent test cohort. Although the dataset included scans acquired from multiple ultrasound vendors, this intra-center heterogeneity does not replace external validation. Therefore, the generalizability of the proposed model across institutions, operators, acquisition protocols, and patient populations remains uncertain and should be assessed in future larger-sample, multi-center studies. In addition, our cohort included only pathology-confirmed cancers with clearly visible lesions on ultrasound, and cine loops with insufficient image quality or poor lesion conspicuity were excluded. While this design is reasonable for evaluating HER2 prediction in a ruling-in setting, it may limit applicability to real-world scenarios in which lesions are subtle, partially obscured, incompletely captured due to suboptimal planes, or even occult on ultrasound. For each lesion, only one representative lesion-targeted cine clip was selected and a fixed number of sampled frames were used for analysis. Although this design facilitated standardized model input, it may not fully capture the full heterogeneity of lesion appearance across all scanning planes and throughout the entire real-time examination. For such cases, an image-only model may yield uncertain or unreliable predictions; future work should incorporate automated quality-control and lesion-conspicuity assessment, uncertainty-aware outputs, and multimodal strategies to improve robustness. Second, lesion localization relied on manual bounding-box annotation on a representative frame with propagation to the remaining frames of each cine clip. This annotation strategy was feasible for the present retrospective proof-of-concept study, but it does not fully represent routine clinical breast ultrasound workflow, in which image acquisition is dynamic and lesion appearance may vary across frames and scanning planes. Accordingly, the current results should be interpreted as being obtained under a research-oriented ROI setting. Future work should focus on automated lesion localization and tracking to enable a more clinically realistic end-to-end pipeline. Third, although cine videos were analyzed, temporal information was modeled using frame-wise feature extraction followed by temporal average pooling. More advanced spatiotemporal architectures may better capture motion patterns and subtle temporal changes, albeit at increased computational cost. Fourth, we did not perform formal statistical comparisons between models; given the modest absolute differences observed, future studies should incorporate paired statistical testing and uncertainty quantification to assess whether performance differences are significant. Finally, future research may benefit from multimodal integration of ultrasound with clinical and molecular variables, as well as radiomic-deep learning fusion strategies, to better characterize spatiotemporal and molecular heterogeneity associated with HER2 signaling. With rigorous external validation and robust uncertainty reporting, U-CBAMNet may evolve toward a clinically explainable, non-invasive, and real-time tool for HER2 evaluation that bridgesimaging phenotypes and molecular biology in precision breast oncology.

Conclusion

This study presents a preliminary deep learning approach, U-CBAMNet, for non-invasive prediction of HER2 expression in breast cancer using ultrasound cine videos. By integrating an ultrasound-tailored attention module with lightweight temporal aggregation, the model achieved promising performance on the internal test cohort and produced interpretable attention maps. These findings suggest that ultrasound-based deep learning may serve as a useful adjunct for preoperative HER2 assessment. Further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts and multimodal studies is needed before broader clinical application.
