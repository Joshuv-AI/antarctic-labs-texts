Phosphorus Regulates Coordination Number and Electronegativity of Cobalt Atomic Sites Triggering Efficient Photocatalytic Water Splitting
Authors: Yuqi Zhao, Xi Wu, Hengliang Wang, Ming Ma, Jian Tian, Xin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing the local electronic structure of a single-atom catalyst (SAC) is crucial for efficient photocatalytic hydrogen evolution reactions. This study synthesized a Co-P4/g-C3N4 heterostructure by selective phosphidation of the Co metal–organic framework/graphitic carbon nitride (Co-MOF/g-C3N4), converting the Co-O6 configuration into a highly electronegative, coordinatively unsaturated Co-P4 configuration anchored to a carbon matrix. P-doping induces strong charge redistribution, shifting the d-band center toward the Fermi level, transforming the Co sites from an electron-deficient state to an electron-rich state, and resulting in a significant reduction in the free energy barrier for HER to −0.08 eV. The Co-P4/g-C3N4 heterostructure demonstrated a HER rate of 13.51 mmol g–1 h–1, approximately 4.82–8.35 times greater than those of photocatalysts loaded with noble metals. The apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) was 28.45% at 380 nm. The synergistic effect of the low coordination number and high electronegativity metal sites significantly enhances the photocatalytic HER performance.Keywords: coordination environment, electronegativity, selective phosphidation, Co single atoms, photocatalytic water splitting

Solar water splitting for hydrogen as an alternative energy solution is gaining prominence amid environmental and energy crises.1−3 Over the past half century, significant efforts have focused on developing efficient photocatalysts for hydrogen production. However, most high-performance photocatalysts require precious metals as cocatalysts, and the high cost and scarcity limit their large-scale commercial applications.4,5 Single-atom catalysts (SAC) are attracting increasing attention owing to their maximum atom utilization efficiency, high selectivity, well-defined active sites, and unique electronic structure.6−9 The unsaturated coordination number (CN) and electronic structure of the metal center sites are crucial for enhancing the performance of SAC.10−12 As the catalyst transitions from the nanoscale to the atomic-level scale, the metal–support interaction strengthens, facilitating charge from the support to catalytically active metal centers, thereby enhancing catalytic activity.13,14 However, owing to atomic-level dispersion, the ultrahigh specific surface energy can lead to atomic aggregation tendencies, negatively impacting the catalytic performance of SAC.15−17 Furthermore, the lack of reliable preparation strategies to precise regulation of the electronic structure of SAC remains a significant challenge. Therefore, the development of SAC with a tunable electronic structure and high stability is crucial for improving their photocatalytic activity.

The local coordination environment of SAC significantly affects the activity and stability of photocatalysts. In general, the coordination environment includes the metal center atom, CN, and coordination atomic species. Therefore, in this study, we analyzed the effect of the CN and coordination atoms on the electronegativity of the metal center atoms. SAC metal sites with low CN possess extraordinary electronic structures.18−20 Isolated metal atomic sites with low CN provide a flexible and tunable electronic environment to activate the active sites of catalysts and enhance their catalytic performance.21 For instance, Li et al. synthesized a series of atomically dispersed Co catalysts with varying nitrogen CN.22 Research has indicated that metal sites with low CN significantly enhance the CO2 electroreduction catalytic performance. Zhang et al. significantly accelerated the kinetics of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) by synthesizing Fe–Nx catalyst with low CN.23 However, significantly low CN of SAC can weaken the strong metal–support interactions, compromising their stability.

The change in the electronegativity of the metal center atom due to the coordination atomic species in SAC has a significant impact on the catalytic activity. The strong coordination bonds between the metal species and coordinating atoms with lone-pair electron pairs, such as N, O, and S, plays a crucial role in preventing atomic aggregation and achieving atomic-level dispersion of the catalytic structures.24 The charge transfer and redistribution between the metal and coordinating atoms may cause a shift in the d-band center of an individual metal site relative to the Fermi level owing to electronegativity differences, thereby enhancing the catalytic activity.20 However, coordination atoms with high electronegativities may lead to overoxidation, poisoning or deactivating the single-atom active sites, weakening the metal–support interactions, and decreasing the stability.25 Therefore, the use of coordinating atoms with low electronegativities is essential to construct central metal atoms with high electronegativities. For instance, Wang et al. synthesized a catalyst with a FeN4Cl1 configuration.26 The introduction of low- electronegativity Cl increased the charge density of Fe in FeN4Cl1, enhanced its electronegativity, and resulted in significantly enhanced ORR activity. Li et al. introduced low-electronegativity P atoms to replace the C atoms in C3N4, thereby enhancing the electron density of Cu in Cu1N3@PCN and significantly improving the catalytic efficiency of CO2 conversion to CO.27

However, studies on the synergistic effect of the CN and electronegativity to coregulate the local coordination environment of Co-SAC and enhance its catalytic activity for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) are limited. Therefore, in this study, we developed a Co-SAC with a low CN and high electronegativity using a selective phosphidation strategy. During the thermal decomposition, the coordination environment of the Co atoms can shift from the Co-O6 sites to the coordination-unsaturated Co-P4 sites, which are subsequently anchored on the MOF-derived C matrices to form the Co-P4/g-C3N4 heterostructure via strong metal–support interactions. Co sites can transition from electron-deficient to electron-rich states, significantly reducing the free energy barrier for the photocatalytic HER. The HER rate of Co-P4/g-C3N4 reached 13.51 mmol g–1 h–1, which is approximately 4.82–8.35 times higher than that of noble metals (Pt, Au, Ag, and Rh). The apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) at 380 nm was 28.45%, with its performance surpassing those of most previously reported phosphide photocatalysts.

Atomically dispersed Co sites on g-C3N4 were prepared via solvothermal and low-temperature phosphorization (Scheme S1 and Figure 1a). First, 2D Co-MOF nanosheets were synthesized via a solvothermal method as reported procedure.28 The synthesized crystal structure revealed that Co atoms coordinated octahedrally with six oxygen atoms. Subsequently, ultrasonic exfoliation was employed to blend Co-MOF with g-C3N4, forming a low-layer Co-MOF/g-C3N4 structure (Figure S1a). Subsequently, Co-MOF/g-C3N4 was thermally decomposed under N2, resulting in the transformation of the Co-O6 configuration into Co-C4 and generating Co-C4/g-C3N4 (Figure S1b). For comparison, Co-MOF/g-C3N4 was phosphorized using NaH2PO2 as the phosphorus source under N2. Consequently, the Co-O6 configuration transitions to an unsaturated Co-P4 configuration, forming of Co-P4/g-C3N4 (Figure S1c). Thermogravimetric analysis revealed g-C3N4 remained stable below 520 °C, while the pyrolysis temperature of Co-MOF/g-C3N4 was 350 °C (Figure S2). Inductively coupled plasma emission spectroscopy revealed that the prepared Co-P4/g-C3N4 contained 5.51 wt % Co. X-ray diffraction was employed to analyze the crystalline structures of the catalysts (Figure S3), with the peaks at 27.61° and 13.33° attributed to the (002) and (100) planes of g-C3N4 with heptazine structures and periodic stacking, respectively. The analysis reveals that the characteristic peak of Co-MOF disappears during the pyrolysis process, while the crystal structure of g-C3N4 remains stable. Furthermore, no crystal diffraction peaks corresponding to Co were detected, indicating the atomic dispersion of Co on the surface of g-C3N4. According to the N2 adsorption desorption isotherms of Co-MOF/g-C3N4 and Co-P4/g-C3N4, all photocatalysts exhibited typical Langmuir IV isotherms and H3 hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of well-defined mesopores.29 However, the specific surface area of Co-MOF/g-C3N4 was 31.09 m2g–1, whereas that of Co-P4/g-C3N4 significantly decreased to 14.77 m2g–1. Additionally, the distinctive 7.41 nm pore structure observed in Co-MOF was absent in Co-P4/g-C3N4, indicating framework collapse during the phosphorization process (Figure S4). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed that phosphorus was not doped into the framework of g-C3N4 during phosphorization (Figure S5).

The morphology and microstructure of Co-P4/g-C3N4 were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and spherical aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited a characteristic 2D nanosheet structure with smooth surfaces (Figure 1b). The atomic state of Co in the Co-P4/g-C3N4 sample was further examined via HAADF-STEM (Figure 1c). In Figure 1d, small bright spots marked with red circles represent the atomically dispersed Co on g-C3N4. Elemental mapping also confirmed the uniform distribution of Co atoms in g-C3N4 (Figure 1e).

To further elucidate the chemical state of Co-P4/g-C3N4 and the coordination environment of single-atom Co, we conducted XPS and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) studies. In the C 1s spectrum, the binding energies at 284.80, 286.55, and 288.16 eV were attributed to graphitic carbon (C–C/C=C), C–(N)3, and sp2-hybridized carbon (N–C=N), respectively.30−32 A new peak attributed to the C–Co bond was observed at 283.20 eV in the spectrum of Co-C4/g-C3N4,33 indicating the collapse of Co-MOF under high-temperature conditions to form carbon-anchored Co atoms. However, the characteristic peak of C–Co was not detected for Co-P4/g-C3N4. A new peak at 285.63 eV was attributed to the C–P bond.34 In the Co 2p spectrum, Co–P peaks were observed at 793.24 and 778.38 eV, whereas the Co–C peak in Co-C4/g-C3N4 was located at 778.73 eV. This observation indicates that alterations in the coordination environment led to the redistribution of charges at the Co site, enhancing the electron density.35−37 Additionally, peaks at 781.24 and 797.23 eV were attributed to Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2, with corresponding satellite peaks at 785.81 and 802.91 eV.38,39 The P 2p spectrum can be divided into two peaks: the P–C peak at 132.63 eV and the P–Co peak at 129.11 eV (Figures 2a-c and S6).27,40

In the Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Figure 2d), the K-edge absorption edge of Co-MOF/g-C3N4 closely resembled that of CoO, indicating a valence state close to +2 for the Co sites in Co-MOF/g-C3N4. The absorption edge of Co-C4/g-C3N4 was close to and lower than that of CoPc, suggesting the presence of Co–C coordination, which is in agreement with the XPS results. The absorption edge of Co-P4/g-C3N4 was between CoO and Co foil, indicating a valence state range of 0 to +2 for the Co sites in Co-P4/g-C3N4. Furthermore, the absorption thresholds of Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4, and Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited the following trend of Co-MOF/g-C3N4 > Co-C4/g-C3N4 > Co-P4/g-C3N4, this trend is consistent with the electronegativity of the coordinating atoms. In the reciprocal space Fourier transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectra (Figure 2e), Co-MOF/g-C3N4 exhibited a predominant Co–O peak at 1.61 Å, which is similar to that for Co–O bonds in standard CoO, with the peak ranging from 1.60 to 1.70 Å.41 However, Co–C4/g-C3N4 exhibited a main Co–C peak at 1.58 Å,42 whereas Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited a major Co–P peak at 1.79 Å.43 No additional peaks were observed in the 2.10–3.00 Å range for all samples, in contrast to the Co–O and Co–Co peaks in Co foil and CoO samples.44,45 These results confirmed the unique coordination environment of Co in all the samples. The specific CN and fine structure around the Co centers were studied by fitting the EXAFS data of the first coordination shell in the samples (Figures 2f, S7–S8). In Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4, and Co-P4/g-C3N4, the Co atoms were coordinated by six O atoms (Co-O6), four C atoms (Co-C4), and four phosphorus atoms (Co-P4), with average bond lengths of approximately 2.07 Å, 2.03 Å, and 2.25 Å, respectively (Table S1). In the wavelet transform (WT) contour plots of Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4, and Co-P4/g-C3N4 (Figure 2g), peak values were detected at approximately 5.11, 4.83, and 6.38 Å–1 (horizontal axis), respectively. The main peaks were located at approximately 1.61, 1.58, and 1.79 Å (vertical axis), corresponding to Co–O, Co–C, and Co–P coordination, respectively, in the first coordination shell. Moreover, no significant contribution from Co–Co bonding was observed in the second shell, with a maximum intensity of approximately 2.91 Å, providing additional evidence for the absence of Co–Co bonds across all samples (Figure S9).

Pure g-C3N4 exhibited a significantly low HER activity owing to the significantly low efficiency of the photogenerated charge carrier separation. In contrast, the HER rate of Co-P4/g-C3N4 is significantly enhanced (Figure 3a). Moreover, its activity was considerably higher compared to those of supported noble metals (Rh, Au, Ag and Pt) (Figure 3b). No significant improvements were observed in the HER rates of Co-MOF/g-C3N4 and Co-C4/g-C3N4, suggesting that phosphorus atoms facilitate charge redistribution, increase the electronegativity difference between the metal sites and coordinating atoms, promote charge transfer from g-C3N4 to the Co-P4 side, and enhance HER activity. The phosphorus CN was optimized for different phosphide durations to investigate its effect on the HER. Figures S10 and S11 examine the relationship between phosphating time, Co-MOF loading, and the HER rate. The complete substitution of C atoms by less-electronegative P atoms caused electronic structure rearrangement, altering the electron density around the Co sites and increasing the electronegativity difference, which were crucial in enhancing the HER rate. In comparison to other photocatalysts, it demonstrated exceptional HER performance (Figure 3c and Tables S2 and S3). Figure 3d shows the AQE curves at various wavelengths corresponding to the UV–visible absorption spectra. The AQE of Co-P4/g-C3N4 peaks at 28.45% at 380 nm and drops to 0 at 550 nm.

The photoelectrochemical performance of the synthesized catalysts were evaluated to investigate their electronic and optical properties. The UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectra indicated that compared to g-C3N4, Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited the highest light absorption intensity (Figure 4a). The absorption edge shifted from 460 to 517 nm, demonstrating a significantly enhanced visible-light harvesting capability. The Kubelka–Munk function was employed to calculate the bandgaps (Eg) of g-C3N4, Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4, and Co-P4/g-C3N4, which were 2.76, 2.72, 2.65, and 2.51 eV, respectively (Figure S12). Mott–Schottky tests were conducted to determine the energy band structures of g-C3N4, Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4 and Co-P4/g-C3N4, revealing that all the samples exhibited n-type semiconductor characteristics (Figures 4b and S13).46 The flat band potentials (Efb) of g-C3N4, Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4 and Co-P4/g-C3N4 were −0.55, −0.77, −0.72, and −0.86 V (vs Ag/AgCl at pH 7), and −0.35, −0.57, −0.52, and −0.66 V vs the hydrogen reference electrode (RHE at pH 7), respectively. The conduction band edge (ECB) of the n-type semiconductors was approximately 0.1 V lower than their flat band potential (Efb).47 Thus, the conduction band edges (ECB) of g-C3N4, Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4, and Co-P4/g-C3N4 can be approximated as −0.45, −0.67, −0.62, and −0.76 V (vs RHE at pH 7), respectively, whereas the valence band positions can be calculated as 2.31, 2.05, 2.03, and 1.75 eV, respectively, using the formula ECB = EVB – Eg (Figure 4c and Table S4).48 The incorporation of phosphorus decreased the HER overpotential (Figure 4d). At identical current densities, Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited a lower overpotential than the other synthesized catalysts, correlating with its enhanced catalytic activity.49,50 Tafel slope analysis revealed that Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited the shallowest slope, indicating a notable improvement in HER kinetics (Figure 4e). Figure 4f shows the cyclic on/off photocurrent responses of g-C3N4, Co-MOF/g-C3N4, Co-C4/g-C3N4, and Co-P4/g-C3N4. Pure g-C3N4 exhibited the lowest photocurrent intensity, whereas that of Co-P4/g-C3N4 was significantly enhanced. Consequently, the incorporation of phosphorus atoms with lower electronegativity strengthens the bonding between Co atoms and reactants, thereby serving as an effective electron medium for charge separation. A smaller semicircle radius in electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) indicates a higher electron transfer rate.51 Co-P4/g-C3N4 demonstrates the highest electron transfer rate among all catalysts. Under illumination, electrons are excited and rapidly transferred from g-C3N4 to Co-P4, thereby enhancing the separation and transport of photogenerated charges (Figures 4g and S14). In the photoluminescence (PL) spectrum (Figure 4h), the PL peak of Co-P4/g-C3N4 was significantly quenched compared to g-C3N4, which is attributed to enhanced carrier excitation and transfer processes.52 The introduction of Co-P4 sites achieves the spatial separation of electrons and holes, effectively suppressing the recombination of photo generated electron hole pairs. In addition, the shorter PL lifetime of Co-P4/ g-C3N4 (Figure 4i) indicates that more photo generated electrons and holes in g-C3N4 are rapidly extracted and captured, further suppressing recombination.53 The Co-P4 configuration significantly enhances the separation and transfer of photogenerated charges, thereby improving the photocatalytic performance.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted using first-principles calculations to elucidate the effect of the Co coordination structure on the HER performance. Based on the results obtained from the HAADF-STEM and XAFS analyses, the coordination structures of Co in the three different samples were determined (Figure S15). The differential charge density of the samples was calculated based on the optimized models, the electron accumulation regions are displayed in yellow and the electron depletion regions are presented in cyan. The overall charge redistribution analysis revealed that the introduction of P significantly transferred electrons from g-C3N4 to the metal sites (Figures 5a-c and S16), and enhancing the electron transfer kinetics and charge separation efficiency.54 To investigate the relationship between the changes in the local coordination environment and charge distribution, local differential charge density and Bader charge analyses were conducted. A change in the coordination environment of Co resulted in charge redistribution, where charges were transferred from the less electronegative P atoms to the Co atoms owing to differences in electronegativity (Figures 5d-f and S17).

Bader charge analysis revealed that alterations in the coordination environment prompt Co atoms to transition from electron-deficient to electron-rich states, resulting in an increased electronegativity difference between Co sites and coordinating atoms. The introduction of P atoms increased the charge density of the Co single atoms (Figure 5j).55,56 Partial density of states calculations revealed that the d-band center shifted from 2.73 eV in Co-MOF/g-C3N4 and 1.72 eV in Co-C4/g-C3N4 to 1.37 eV in Co-P4/g-C3N4, approaching the Fermi level (Figures 5k and S18). Therefore, the introduction of P atoms increased the electron density states near the Fermi level in Co-P4/g-C3N4.27,57 To explore the intrinsic thermodynamic performance of HER, DFT calculations were employed to investigate the key reaction steps. The hydrogen adsorption Gibbs free energy (ΔGH*) serves as the primary indicator to describe the catalytic HER activity. Figure S19 shows the structures after hydrogen adsorption at the corresponding catalytic site. The calculated ΔGH* values for Co-MOF/g-C3N4 and Co-C4/g-C3N4 were 0.63 and 0.85 eV, respectively, indicating unfavorable H adsorption and desorption.58 However, Co-P4/g-C3N4 exhibited a ΔGH* value of −0.08 eV, which is closer to the HER potential of 0 eV, facilitating selective H adsorption with a lower hydrogen desorption energy, thus presenting the lowest ΔGH* value (Figure 5l and Table S5).59,60 Therefore, altering the coordination environment enhanced the electronegativity difference between the active centers of Co coordinated with P atoms. The larger electronegativity difference resulted in strong electron synergy, promoting charge separation and transfer, effectively reducing the free energy barrier for HER, facilitating the formation of H intermediates, and accelerating the progression of the reaction.

In summary, Co-SAC with a low CN and high electronegativity were prepared via the selective phosphidation of 2D Co-MOF/g-C3N4. During phosphidation, the Co-MOF thermally decomposed to form C matrices and anchor Co, followed by P-doping to create an unsaturated coordination configuration of Co-P4. Theoretical and experimental results indicated that the introduction of P caused Co atoms to transition from an electron deficient state to an electron rich state, resulting in an increased electronegativity difference between the Co sites and coordinating atoms, with the center of the d-band gradually moving toward the Fermi level. This shift in the d-band significantly reduced the HER potential to −0.08 eV, enabling the material to demonstrate excellent photocatalytic activity. This study provides new insights into the design of atomically dispersed photocatalysts through the synergistic effects of low CN and high electronegativity, promoting the rational design of single-atom metal catalysts for solar energy conversion.
