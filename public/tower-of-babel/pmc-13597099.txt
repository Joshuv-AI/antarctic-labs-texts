Nurses' Perspectives on the Integration of Telehealth in Palliative Care: A Qualitative Descriptive Study
Authors: Hartiah Haroen, Finny Fauziah Hidayat, Sri Hartati Pratiwi, Citra Windani Mambang Sari, Sheizi Pritasari, Neti Juniarti, Nadirawati, Peni Cahyati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to explore palliative care nurses' perspectives on integrating telehealth into palliative care in Indonesia.DesignA qualitative descriptive design was employed. Sixteen palliative care nurses were recruited through purposive sampling based on their involvement in palliative care practice. Data were collected through semi‐structured interviews and analysed inductively following a five‐phase process of qualitative data analysis. The resulting themes were subsequently interpreted through the Non‐Adoption, Abandonment, Scale‐up, Spread, and Sustainability (NASSS) framework. Methodological rigour was ensured through structured peer debriefing, an audit trail, and detailed procedural documentation to enhance credibility and trustworthiness.ResultThe analysis generated four major themes: (1) structural and regulatory foundations of palliative care; (2) post‐discharge care needs and deficiencies; (3) inter‐facility coordination gaps affecting continuity of palliative care; and (4) nurses' perceived benefits, barriers, and functional expectations of telehealth in palliative care. The findings indicate that although foundational structures for palliative care have been established, significant gaps remain in care continuity, coordination, and system integration.ConclusionThis study underscores the need for a more integrated palliative care system in Indonesia. Nurses perceived telehealth as a potentially valuable complementary modality for supporting continuity of care, although this study did not evaluate its implementation feasibility, clinical effectiveness, or scalability. Strengthening regulatory frameworks, improving healthcare workforce capacity, and increasing public and patient readiness for digital health were identified by participants as prerequisites for the future development of telehealth‐based palliative care services.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareThe findings indicate that nurses require clearer regulatory guidance, structured inter‐facility coordination mechanisms, and dedicated workforce capacity before telehealth can be formally and safely integrated into palliative care pathways. Nurse leaders and policymakers can draw on these findings to prioritise the formalisation of referral and communication protocols, the development of telehealth‐specific competencies among palliative care nurses, and the preparation of patients and families for digitally supported home‐based care.ImpactThese findings are relevant to palliative care nurses, nurse leaders, health policymakers, and researchers in Indonesia and other resource‐constrained health systems seeking to inform the future regulation, workforce preparation, and system‐level integration of telehealth‐based palliative care.Reporting MethodThis study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline.No Patient or Public ContributionPatients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis, or reporting of this study. Participants were palliative care nurses who contributed their perspectives through semi‐structured interviews.

Introduction

Palliative care is a holistic approach that aims to improve the quality of life (QoL) of patients and families facing life‐threatening illnesses (WHO 2026). The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that more than 56.8 million people require palliative care annually. However, only approximately 14% of patients currently receive it (WHO 2026). This inequality is further reflected in global mapping data, which show that only 30 of 198 countries have achieved the highest levels of palliative care development, all of which are located in Europe (Clark et al. 2020). At the regional level, the recently published Atlas of Palliative Care in Asia Pacific 2025, the first standardised, WHO‐indicator‐based mapping of palliative care development across 41 countries in the region, reveals substantial and persistent disparities in policy implementation, service integration, education, and access to essential medicines, with development remaining markedly uneven even among neighbouring Southeast Asian countries (Monzón Llamas et al. 2025). Even in comparatively more advanced Southeast Asian settings, substantial urban–rural disparities in the availability, distance, and cost of accessing palliative care persist, underscoring that regional policy progress has not been matched by equitable service delivery (Ho et al. 2024; Lau and Pickersgill 2019; Segarmurthy et al. 2024). This regional unevenness reflects broader structural challenges common to Southeast Asian health systems, including fragmented care delivery models and a limited evidence base to guide implementation (Tseng 2023). However, in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including Indonesia, implementation remains constrained by multiple systemic barriers.

In Indonesia palliative care is still in its early stages of development and continue to face significant challenges in terms of access and service equity (Agustina, Romadhon, and Lin 2025; Wilson et al. 2024; Putranto et al. 2017; Haroen et al. 2024). The government has formally established the Indonesian National Palliative Care Policy as the legal framework for palliative care service delivery (Agustina, Romadhon, and Lin 2025), supported by subsequent policies such as the 2015 national guidelines for cancer palliative care and the revision of the national formulary through the Decree of the Minister of Health of the Republic of Indonesia No. HK.02.02/Menkes/137/2016 to improve access to essential palliative medications (The ministry of health indonesia 2016) and Law Number 17 of 2023 concerning health officially lists palliative care as an integral component of comprehensive health services, along with promotive, preventive, curative, and rehabilitative efforts (President of the Republic of Indonesia 2023). Despite this recognition, the provision of palliative care remains limited and largely concentrated in major urban centres, such as Jakarta, Yogyakarta, Surabaya, Denpasar, and Makassar, with Surabaya as the first palliative care city in Indonesia (Agustina, Romadhon, and Lin 2025).

Several barriers to effective palliative care delivery have been identified in Indonesia, including shortages of trained healthcare professionals, limited integration of palliative care across health services, and limited public awareness (Effendy and Yodang 2024; Arisanti et al. 2019; Rochmawati et al. 2016). These challenges indicate that the development of palliative care in Indonesia is not solely a matter of expanding service availability, but also of strengthening coordination, workforce capacity, and continuity across healthcare settings (Putranto et al. 2017; Agustina, Purba, et al. 2025). Importantly, these existing system‐level gaps raise questions about the feasibility and prerequisites for introducing additional models of care, including telehealth, within the current palliative care infrastructure.

Nurses play a central role in palliative care, acting as key intermediaries between patients, families, and physicians, particularly in supporting decision making for individuals with life‐limiting conditions (Adams et al. 2011). However, limited access to specialised training and continuing professional development in Indonesia constrains nurses' capacity to deliver comprehensive palliative care (Effendy and Yodang 2024; Rochmawati et al. 2016; Abdullah et al. 2021). Established professional nursing organisations have been proposed as a strategy to address these gaps by strengthening knowledge development, improving service quality, and advocating supportive policies (Agustina, Romadhon, and Lin 2025; Effendy and Yodang 2024).

In Indonesia, telehealth has developed as part of the broader digital transformation of the healthcare system. Telemedicine has been formally regulated, while digital health platforms such as SATUSEHAT have supported the development of an increasingly connected health information ecosystem (Ministry of Health of Republic Indonesia 2021). Telehealth services have been used for remote consultation, communication, monitoring, and home‐based care, including emerging applications in palliative care (Kosasih et al. 2026; Afiyanti et al. 2024). However, in Indonesia, telehealth implementation remains heterogeneous, and its integration into palliative care is still limited and insufficiently characterised. This context highlights the need to understand how telehealth can be appropriately integrated within the existing palliative care system in Indonesia.

Against this background, telehealth has emerged as a potential modality for extending palliative care beyond conventional face‐to‐face encounters and supporting communication across healthcare settings. However, telehealth should not be considered a standalone solution to the existing structural and organisational limitations of palliative care. Its integration requires an understanding of existing care infrastructure, coordination mechanisms, workforce capacity, technological readiness, and the extent to which telehealth can address rather than amplify existing gaps in continuity of care (Ghazal et al. 2024; Nwosu 2023; Imam et al. 2024). Successful implementation also requires adequate readiness among healthcare providers, particularly nurses (Yu et al. 2026; Guo et al. 2023). Although telehealth‐based palliative care has been introduced in limited contexts, its application in Indonesia remains at an early stage. The limited available evidence has highlighted challenges related to infrastructure, policy, digital literacy, and patient and family experiences (Esterilita et al. 2024; Dasat and Anggraini 2023; Nasution and Waren 2024). These findings suggest that the question is not simply whether telehealth can be introduced, but under what conditions it can be appropriately integrated into an already developing and fragmented palliative care system.

This context highlights an important knowledge gap concerning how telehealth might be appropriately integrated into palliative care within existing health‐system constraints. Previous studies in Indonesia have primarily examined service experiences, program implementation, or technological barriers, while limited attention has been given to how nurses perceive the feasibility, prerequisites, potential roles, and barriers associated with telehealth integration across different palliative care settings (Esterilita et al. 2024; Dasat and Anggraini 2023; Nasution and Waren 2024). A recent systematic review synthesised evidence from seven primary quantitative studies on telehealth interventions in community‐based palliative care, reporting consistent improvements in functional status and reductions in hospitalisation and psychological distress among patients (Haroen, Juniarti, Sari, Sari, et al. 2025). However, that review addressed patient‐ and caregiver‐level clinical outcomes across predominantly high‐income settings and did not examine the perspectives of nurses, nor the organisational and system‐level conditions required for telehealth integration within a specific low‐ and middle‐income health system such as Indonesia's. The present study addresses this distinct gap by qualitatively exploring how palliative care nurses in Indonesia perceive the structural, organisational, and practice‐level conditions surrounding telehealth integration. Exploring their perspectives can therefore clarify whether telehealth is perceived as feasible within the existing system, what conditions are required for its safe and sustainable integration, and which existing gaps it may realistically address.

The findings of this study are interpreted through the Non‐Adoption, Abandonment, Scale‐up, Spread, and Sustainability (NASSS) framework, which explains the implementation of complex health technologies through the interaction of multiple domains, namely the condition, the technology, its value proposition, the intended adopter system, the organisation, and the wider system, together with how these evolve over time (Greenhalgh et al. 2017). This framework was applied as a post hoc interpretive lens because it captures the organisational and systemic complexity of telehealth implementation in fragmented, resource‐constrained health systems, allowing nurses' inductively derived perspectives to be interpreted not merely as individual attitudes toward technology, but as situated reflections of the structural and system‐level conditions that determine whether telehealth can be feasibly adopted and sustained within palliative care. Accordingly, this study aimed to explore palliative care nurses' perspectives on the integration of telehealth into palliative care in Indonesia, with particular attention to perceived needs, potential benefits, barriers, and functional expectations.

Methods

Research Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive (QD) design, a distinct and well‐established methodological approach in health sciences research, rather than a generic label for unstructured qualitative inquiry (Sandelowski 2000; Neergaard et al. 2009; Colorafi and Evans 2016; Sandelowski 2010). Unlike phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, or case study designs, which are anchored in specific theoretical or philosophical traditions and aim to generate interpretive theory, uncover essence of lived experience, describe cultural patterns, or bound analysis within a single case, QD is grounded in naturalistic inquiry and is specifically suited to producing a comprehensive, low‐inference summary of participants' experiences and perspectives, presented close to the surface of the data and in their own terms (Colorafi and Evans 2016). This design was selected because the aim of the present study was not to generate new theory, uncover the essential structure of a lived phenomenon, or produce an ethnographic account of cultural practice, but rather to systematically describe nurses' perspectives on telehealth integration in a manner that is directly translatable into clinical practice and policy recommendations (Sandelowski 2000, 2010; Neergaard et al. 2009; Colorafi and Evans 2016).

The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), a 32‐item reporting guideline designed to enhance the transparency and completeness of qualitative studies involving interviews and focus groups. COREQ was used to guide reporting across key methodological domains, including the research team and reflexivity, study design, participant selection, data collection, and data analysis and reporting.

Setting and Participants

The study population consisted of palliative care nurses working in hospitals, home care services, primary healthcare centres, and non‐governmental organisations (NGOs) in Bandung City, Indonesia. Sixteen palliative care nurses were recruited using purposive sampling based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were eligible if they were palliative care nurses working in hospitals, health centres, home care services, or NGOs in Bandung City; had at least 2–3 years of experience caring for patients with terminal or complex illnesses; held at least a Diploma in Nursing as their highest educational qualification; had received formal training in palliative care; and were actively involved in a palliative care team within their respective institutions. Both permanent and contract‐based nurses were eligible to participate. Nurses who did not meet the eligibility criteria or were unwilling to participate were excluded.

Recruitment was facilitated through the Himpunan Perawat Paliatif Indonesia [Indonesian Palliative Nurses Association] (HPPI), a professional organisation for palliative care nurses in Indonesia. At the time of recruitment, the first author held the position of chair of HPPI. To minimise the potential for perceived obligation arising from this position of organisational authority, dissemination of study invitations and initial screening of eligible nurses were conducted by another member of the research team, independently of the first author; the first author was not involved in identifying, contacting, or screening potential participants and had no knowledge of which HPPI members received the invitation, expressed disinterest, or declined to participate. The invitation included information about the study purpose, participation requirements, interview procedures, and the voluntary nature of participation, and explicitly stated that the decision to participate or not would have no bearing on the nurse's standing or activities within HPPI. Nurses who expressed interest were subsequently contacted by the responsible research team member to confirm their eligibility and arrange the interview; only nurses who had already agreed to participate were then referred for interview. Participation was voluntary, and recruitment through HPPI did not constitute an obligation to participate.

Bandung City is a large urban centre in West Java, Indonesia, with a population of approximately 2.6 million people in 2026. Its healthcare delivery system includes both public and private providers, with services delivered through primary healthcare centres, private primary clinics, general and specialised hospitals, and community‐based services. This relatively diverse healthcare structure provides access to multiple levels of care but also requires coordination across providers and healthcare settings. In Indonesia, palliative care services are typically not provided in dedicated palliative wards. Instead, patients requiring palliative care are managed across various hospital units according to their primary diagnosis or condition, including medical, surgical, and oncology wards. When palliative care is required, the hospital palliative care team provides consultation services upon request from the treating unit.

Sample size in this study was guided by the principle of data saturation rather than being fixed a priori, consistent with recommendations for interview‐based qualitative health research indicating that saturation is typically achieved within 12–20 participants (Vasileiou et al. 2018). Data collection and analysis were conducted iteratively rather than sequentially: each interview was transcribed and analysed before the subsequent interview was conducted, allowing the research team to continuously assess whether new codes, sub‐themes, or substantive variations emerged relative to the accumulating dataset. Saturation was considered to have been provisionally reached once no new information or codes emerged from an interview; to confirm this judgement and guard against premature closure, one additional participant was subsequently interviewed, and the resulting data were reviewed to verify the absence of new themes. This determination was not made unilaterally but was confirmed through peer debriefing discussions within the research team. On this basis, data saturation was confirmed at the 16th participant, and data collection was concluded accordingly. This sample size was considered adequate to provide a rich and comprehensive understanding of palliative nurses' experiences and perspectives regarding the implementation of palliative care in Bandung.

Data Collection

Data were collected from 2 September to 8 November 2024 through face‐to‐face and online semi‐structured interviews. This study employed a semi‐structured interview method, with each participant interviewed once for approximately 1 h (Dicicco‐bloom and Crabtree 2006). The interview guide was developed based on relevant literature and was further informed by the first author's doctoral study entitled “An Exploration of the Concept of Integrated Palliative Cancer Care in the Indonesian Context” (Haroen 2022). This study provides a conceptual framework that guides the domains explored in the present research. The guide was centred on the core question: ‘How do you perceive the implementation and future potential of palliative care, particularly in relation to continuity of care and the role of telehealth, in your clinical practice?’

To address this central question, the interview guide included 15 open‐ended questions covering key topics such as the implementation of palliative care at both the national and national levels, policies and service delivery processes, facilitating and inhibiting factors, transition and continuity of care, inter‐facility collaboration, and the opportunities and challenges of telehealth. Probing questions were used to further explore the participants' experiences, challenges, and recommendations within each domain.

Data collection proceeded in three sequential stages. In the preparatory stage, nurses identified as eligible through HPPI were contacted individually to reconfirm eligibility, provided with written study information, and scheduled for interview at a time and in a modality (face‐to‐face or online) of their own choosing; written informed consent was obtained and permission for audio recording was reconfirmed immediately before the interview commenced. In the interview stage, each interview opened with rapport‐building and clarification of the participant's professional role and practice setting, followed by the central question and the 15 open‐ended guide questions, with probes used to pursue accounts that were salient, ambiguous, or divergent. In the closing stage, the interviewer summarised the principal points raised and invited the participant to correct, qualify, or extend them, after which recording was terminated, the audio file was checked for completeness, and the interview was transcribed verbatim before the subsequent interview was scheduled. Analytic impressions arising during data collection were discussed within the research team following each interview to inform subsequent interviews and to monitor emerging saturation. This staged procedure is consistent with established methodological guidance for the design and conduct of semi‐structured interviews in health research (DeJonckheere and Vaughn 2019; Kallio et al. 2016).

The interviews were conducted using an interview guide and audio recorders. Data collection was carried out by two interviewers: the first author, a professor of nursing specialising in palliative care in Indonesia and concurrently chair of HPPI at the time of the study, with extensive prior experience conducting qualitative research in this field, including a related doctoral study on integrated palliative care; and the second author, an undergraduate nursing student with academic training and demonstrated capability in qualitative research methods. Although the first author held a formal leadership position within HPPI, neither interviewer had a prior direct clinical, supervisory, or personal relationship with individual participants. The first author had no role in identifying, contacting, or screening potential participants and was blinded to the identities of nurses who declined to participate. This organisational position is nonetheless acknowledged as a potential source of perceived obligation among prospective participants and is addressed further in the Reflexivity section below. Prior to data collection, both interviewers underwent a preparatory briefing, facilitated by the first author, to ensure a shared understanding of the interview guide, consistency in probing techniques, and adherence to ethical conduct when interviewing palliative care nurses; this briefing was conducted as an orientation session rather than a formal, structured training programme.

The interview guide was not separately pretested prior to use, as its content and structure were directly informed by the conceptual framework previously developed and empirically validated through the first author's doctoral research on integrated palliative cancer care in the Indonesian context. The broader seven‐member research team, comprising lecturers and students, contributed to study design, data analysis, and peer debriefing, but did not conduct interviews directly. All interviews were conducted, audio‐recorded, and transcribed verbatim in Bahasa Indonesia, the participants' native language, to preserve the nuance and authenticity of their accounts.

Data coding and thematic analysis were likewise conducted in Bahasa Indonesia to minimise the loss of meaning associated with premature translation. Only the final selected illustrative quotations presented in the Results section were translated into English, following a forward‐translation and back‐translation procedure: quotations were first translated into English by a bilingual member of the research team, then independently back‐translated into Bahasa Indonesia by a second bilingual research team member who had not seen the original quotations, and the back‐translated version was compared against the original Indonesian transcript to verify semantic equivalence. Discrepancies identified during this process were resolved through discussion within the research team to ensure that the translated quotations accurately preserved participants' intended meaning.

Data Analysis

Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently in an iterative cycle, whereby each transcript was analysed prior to the subsequent interview to inform ongoing data collection and to monitor emerging saturation. The interview data were analysed inductively, following a five‐phase process of qualitative data analysis comprising data organisation, immersive reading for familiarisation, initial coding, categorisation, and the identification of overarching themes (Bingham 2023). Codes were generated directly from participants' accounts, without reference to a predetermined theoretical framework, and were iteratively compared, refined, and grouped through discussion within the research team until four major themes were established: (1) structural and regulatory foundations of palliative care as preconditions for telehealth integration; (2) post‐discharge care deficiencies as a catalyst for telehealth adoption; (3) inter‐facility coordination failures undermining continuity of palliative care; and (4) nurses' perceived benefits, barriers, and functional expectations of telehealth in palliative care.

Following completion of the inductive analysis, the Non‐Adoption, Abandonment, Scale‐up, Spread, and Sustainability (NASSS) framework was applied post hoc as an interpretive lens to situate the four inductively derived themes within a recognised model of complex health technology implementation, without altering the content, boundaries, or structure of the original themes (Greenhalgh et al. 2017). Specifically, Theme 1 was aligned with the organisation and wider system domains, reflecting the regulatory and institutional foundations of palliative care; Theme 2 with the condition and value proposition domains, reflecting patients' post‐discharge needs and the anticipated relevance of telehealth in addressing them; Theme 3 with the organisation domain, reflecting inter‐facility coordination structures and failures; and Theme 4 with the adopter system and technology domains, reflecting nurses' readiness, perceived benefits, barriers, and functional expectations of telehealth. This post hoc application was intended not to redefine the empirical findings but to enhance their theoretical situatedness and to facilitate interpretation of the identified barriers and enablers within an established implementation‐science framework.

The stages of the data analysis were as follows: (1) listening to the recorded interviews and transcribing them verbatim; (2) rereading the transcripts three to five times to achieve data familiarisation; (3) conducting initial coding by identifying meaningful units and significant statements; (4) developing themes, sub‐themes, and sub‐sub‐themes based on the interview guide, relevant literature, and emerging patterns from the data; (5) organising the coded data into a structured data matrix based on participant codes; (6) identifying similarities and differences across participants; (7) reviewing and refining the themes to ensure coherence and consistency; (8) compiling the results into a table containing themes, sub‐themes, sub‐sub‐themes, key statements, and participant codes; and (9) interpreting and comparing the findings with those of previous studies in the discussion section.

The number of quotes presented for each subtheme was not predetermined, but varied according to the richness, clarity, and representativeness of the participants' responses. Subthemes with more diverse or detailed narratives were supported with multiple quotes, whereas those expressed more uniformly across participants were illustrated with fewer representative quotes to avoid redundancy. Trustworthiness and Reflexivity

Trustworthiness is essential to ensure the rigour of qualitative research and encompasses credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Lincoln and Guba 1985). In this study, credibility was enhanced through structured peer debriefing sessions conducted after every four interviews/transcripts, involving four members of the research team (HH, FFH, SHP, and CWMS). During each session, coded transcripts and emerging categories were collectively reviewed, and structured discussions were undertaken to reach consensus on the naming, boundaries, and interrelationships of themes and sub‐themes, thereby reducing the potential influence of individual researcher interpretations on the findings. Transferability was supported through detailed descriptions of the research context, participant criteria, and data collection procedures. Dependability was enhanced through systematic documentation of the research process, including records of analytic decisions made during peer debriefing. Confirmability was supported through verbatim transcription of the interview recordings, the maintenance of an audit trail documenting coding and interpretive decisions, and structured peer debriefing within the research team, ensuring that the findings were grounded in participants' actual accounts.

Reflexivity was considered throughout data collection and analysis to acknowledge the potential influence of the researchers on the research process and interpretation of participants' accounts. A particular reflexive consideration concerned the first author's dual role as principal interviewer/researcher and chair of HPPI, the professional organisation through which participants were recruited. This dual positioning carried an inherent risk that prospective participants might feel a sense of professional obligation to participate or might moderate their responses during interviews in light of the first author's leadership status within their professional community. Several safeguards were implemented to mitigate this risk: dissemination of study invitations and screening of eligible nurses were conducted by another research team member, independently of the first author; the first author had no involvement in and no knowledge of which nurses were approached, expressed disinterest, or declined participation; and prospective participants were explicitly informed that their decision to participate or not would have no bearing on their standing or activities within HPPI. During the interviews themselves, the first author maintained a neutral, non‐evaluative interview style, refrained from invoking their organisational role, and explicitly encouraged participants to speak candidly, including about critical or unfavourable perspectives on existing services and policies. Nonetheless, the possibility that participants moderated their accounts in light of the first author's professional standing cannot be entirely excluded and is acknowledged as a limitation of this study. Two interviewers were involved in data collection and received an orientation briefing before conducting the interviews.

During the interviews, the interviewers maintained a neutral and non‐judgmental approach and encouraged participants to describe their experiences and perspectives in their own terms. The research team subsequently discussed emerging interpretations during the analytic process, while peer debriefing provided opportunities to critically examine interpretations and identify potential researcher assumptions, including possible influence stemming from the first author's leadership role within HPPI. These processes helped maintain the analysis close to participants' accounts and reduce the potential influence of individual researcher perspectives on the interpretation of the findings. Collectively, these strategies strengthened the rigour, transparency, and trustworthiness of the study.

Ethical Consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the University of Padjadjaran (no. 595/UN6. KEP/EC/2024). All participants were informed of the study's purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their participation in the study. Participants' anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained and participation was voluntary throughout the research process. The 16‐participant interview dataset reported in this manuscript is original and has not been previously reported, in whole or in part, in the first author's doctoral thesis, any other publication, or any other concurrent submission.

Analysis of Findings

This study examined nurses' perspectives regarding the integration of telehealth into palliative care services in Indonesia. The findings are presented as four themes, derived inductively and reported here in descriptive form, close to participants' own accounts; the theoretical interpretation of these themes through the NASSS framework is presented separately in the Discussion section. The themes identified were: (1) structural and regulatory foundations of palliative care; (2) post‐discharge care needs and deficiencies; (3) inter‐facility coordination gaps affecting continuity of palliative care; and (4) nurses' perceived benefits, barriers, and functional expectations of telehealth in palliative care. These four themes are interconnected, collectively describing the current structural and functional conditions of palliative care delivery within which nurses perceive telehealth as a potential response.

Theme 1: Structural and Regulatory Foundations of Palliative Care

This theme describes participants' accounts of the existing institutional and regulatory infrastructure underpinning palliative care in Indonesia.

Policy Infrastructure: Formal Mandate Without System‐Level Operationalisation

Participants consistently affirmed that palliative care services operate under formal regulatory authority grounded in institutional decrees and national ministerial guidelines. This provides a critical enabling condition: the presence of a formal mandate confers institutional legitimacy and clarifies the roles within the care team. However, the findings also indicate that these policy frameworks are largely structural in nature, defining organisational responsibility without detailing operational protocols, care standards across settings, or provisions for the use of.The establishment of palliative care in our hospital is formally based on the Director's Decree, which aligns with national regulations from the Ministry of Health. (P1) There is also a Mayor's Decree that regulates the formation of the palliative care team, ensuring that the hospital has an official structure for managing these patients. (P2) The presence of a mayoral decree reflects engagement across multiple levels of governance. However, participants indicated that these policies have been extended to regulate the use of telehealth. As discussed in Theme 4, this regulatory gap emerges as one of the most significant barriers to formal adoption of telehealth.

This team‐based model represents a structural asset that can serve as the organisational backbone for telehealth‐enabled coordination. Theme 2: Post‐Discharge Care Needs and Deficiencies

This theme addresses one of the most consistently reported issues in participants' accounts: the lack of adequate support for patients and families after hospital discharge. Participants' accounts revealed a consistent pattern of post‐discharge vulnerability marked by caregiver burden, limited knowledge, psychosocial distress, and financial strain.

Discharge Preparation: Structured Processes With Limited Effectiveness

The participants described discharge preparation as a structured component of palliative care practice, including family meetings, patient and caregiver education, and communication of care plans. These processes are intended for caregiving at home. Theme 4: Nurses' Perceived Benefits, Barriers, and Functional Expectations of Telehealth in Palliative Care

This theme constitutes the analytical core of the study, providing a direct account of nurses' perspectives on telehealth as a response to the care gaps identified in Themes 1–3. The analysis was organised around three interrelated dimensions: nurses' perceived utility of and openness to telehealth, the multilevel barriers constraining its implementation, and their normative expectations regarding the roles within a reconfigured palliative care system. Taken together, these dimensions illuminate the structural conditions, persistent constraints, and professional expectations that shape the trajectory of telehealth integration in Indonesian palliative care.

Discussion

Interpreted through the lens of the NASSS framework, the findings suggest that the potential integration of telehealth into palliative care in Indonesia is influenced not only by nurses' attitudes toward the technology but also by conditions within the organisational and wider system domains. Gaps in inter‐facility coordination and post‐discharge continuity indicate challenges within these domains, while unmet and evolving patient needs following discharge highlight considerations related to the condition and value proposition domains. Meanwhile, nurses' generally favourable yet cautious perspectives on telehealth suggest that acceptance of the technology may already be present, but its broader integration remains dependent on the surrounding organisational and system conditions. This pattern is consistent with the NASSS framework, which emphasises that sustainable implementation of health technologies depends on the interaction and alignment of multiple domains rather than on the readiness of any single domain. In the Indonesian context, unresolved organisational, regulatory, and coordination challenges may therefore influence whether telehealth develops beyond informal or fragmented use toward more systematic integration within palliative care.

The findings indicate that palliative care services in Bandung City have established a formal structural foundation through institutional policies and interdisciplinary teams; however, these structures have not yet translated into effective system‐level integration across healthcare settings. This gap between policy‐level formalisation and service‐level implementation was evident in fragmented care pathways and weak continuity across hospitals, primary healthcare centres, and community‐based services. Thus, the existence of formal policies and organisational structures alone appears insufficient to ensure integrated palliative care when mechanisms for inter‐facility coordination, referral, communication, and shared follow‐up remain underdeveloped. This finding is consistent with the organisation and wider system domains of the NASSS framework, which emphasise that successful implementation of complex health technologies depends on the alignment of organisational structures, inter‐organisational processes, and the broader health‐system environment. Similar fragmentation has been reported in Indonesia and other developing‐country settings, where palliative care remains insufficiently integrated across primary and referral services (Sandelowski 2010; Vasileiou et al. 2018). At the governance level, participants identified inadequate policies, shortages of health workers in health centres, complex patient conditions, negative perceptions of palliative care among communities and cadres, and loss of patient contact as additional barriers to integrated care. Although palliative care has been formally recognised within Indonesia's national legal framework (Clelland et al. 2020), the absence of a clearly designated authority responsible for overseeing its implementation may weaken accountability and hinder coordination across healthcare facilities (Clelland et al. 2020).

Inter‐facility communication and coordination represent another important challenge to continuity of palliative care. In the present study, nurses described limited systematic collaboration between hospitals and other health centres, suggesting that information and responsibility may not be consistently transferred across care settings. Effective coordination is essential for supporting patients during transitions from hospital to community or home‐based care (Kirsebom et al. 2013), and previous evidence has similarly identified substantial gaps in continuity and coordination across health systems (Khatri et al. 2023). A previous review reported that, among 56 countries included in the analysis, only eight had implemented continuity of services and only 14 demonstrated adequate coordination between health facilities (Khatri et al. 2023). Key barriers included the absence of specific regulations governing referral and transition processes, shortages of trained human resources in primary care, and the complexity of patient conditions, which may exceed the perceived capacity of basic‐level services. Social factors, including stigma and feelings of shame among patients and families, may further hinder communication with healthcare providers after patients return home (Tseng 2023; Kallio et al. 2016; Bingham 2023; Lincoln and Guba 1985). These findings suggest that improving continuity requires not only formal policy but also operational mechanisms that facilitate communication, referral, handover, and shared responsibility across levels of care.

These system‐level gaps have important consequences for patients and families after discharge. In Bandung City, the limited availability of trained palliative care providers may leave patients and families with insufficient professional support when care transitions from hospital to home. Participants' accounts indicate that patients receiving palliative care may experience complex and fluctuating needs, including pain, dyspnoea, wounds, loneliness, stress, and depression, which can complicate care delivery outside healthcare facilities. Effective pain management is a critical component of home‐based palliative care services (Liu et al. 2021), while patients receiving palliative care may also experience psychological and cognitive symptoms, including sleep disturbances, anxiety, agitation, confusion, feelings of worthlessness, and depression (Michel et al. 2023). At the same time, discharge to home may place substantial responsibilities on family caregivers, highlighting the continued need for accessible professional support. The burden experienced by family caregivers in chronic and palliative care contexts further reinforces the importance of supportive interventions that can be sustained beyond facility‐based encounters (Haroen, Juniarti, Sari, Prista Sari, et al. 2025). Thus, the post‐discharge period represents an important point of vulnerability in the continuity of palliative care and provides a rationale for considering additional mechanisms of professional support.

Against this background, nurses in the present study generally perceived telehealth‐based palliative care as a potentially valuable mechanism for extending professional support beyond healthcare facilities and supporting continuity of care, consistent with perceptions reported among healthcare providers in other settings (Walton et al. 2023). It should be noted that these findings reflect nurses' perceptions rather than an evaluation of telehealth's actual feasibility, clinical effectiveness, or impact on continuity of care, none of which were assessed in this study. Participants described practical applications involving photographs, videos, and video calls to communicate patient conditions and support remote consultation. These practices are consistent with previous findings highlighting the value of photographs and videos for visual documentation and video calls for remote consultation in home‐based palliative care (Schröder et al. 2024). Technology‐enabled palliative care may also encompass video conferencing, remote patient monitoring, mobile applications for information sharing, and integrated online documentation systems that can be accessed across healthcare facilities (Finucane et al. 2021; Disalvo et al. 2021). Beyond supporting patient–provider communication, these technologies may facilitate communication among healthcare professionals and contribute to more continuous information exchange across care settings.

Evidence from other, outcome‐focused studies distinct in design and scope from the present perception‐based study further suggests the potential of telehealth to enhance access, service quality, and patient satisfaction, especially among patients living in rural or underserved areas. Consistent with this, a systematic review of quantitative studies found that telehealth interventions in community‐based palliative care were associated with improved functional status and reduced hospitalisation and psychological distress among patients (Haroen, Juniarti, Sari, Sari, et al. 2025). However, unlike the present study, that review evaluated patient‐level clinical outcomes rather than the perspectives of nurses or the organisational preconditions for telehealth integration, underscoring the complementary nature of the two bodies of evidence.

A scoping review found that telehealth can strengthen continuity of care, support symptom management, and facilitate communication among healthcare providers, patients, and families (Ghazal et al. 2024). In Indonesia, tele‐palliative programmes have successfully reached patients with cancer in remote areas (Nasution and Waren 2024) and provided support for children with cancer (Esterilita et al. 2024). Similar applications have been reported in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where telehealth has been used for remote consultation, family education, and symptom monitoring, potentially reducing geographical and financial barriers to care (Ghazal et al. 2024; Abu‐Odah et al. 2020). These findings support the potential value of telehealth as a complementary approach to extending palliative care beyond healthcare facilities. However, the potential benefits of telehealth should be interpreted alongside the implementation barriers identified in the present study, including limited digital literacy among patients and families, inadequate technological infrastructure, unclear regulatory arrangements, limited time and capacity among healthcare providers, and continued preferences for face‐to‐face interactions (Dasat and Anggraini 2023). These barriers indicate that telehealth adoption cannot be considered solely a technological issue but must be addressed within the broader organisational and health‐system context.

The successful integration of telehealth into palliative care will therefore require coordinated efforts to strengthen the conditions that support implementation. Relevant priorities include clarifying telehealth policies and regulatory arrangements, strengthening healthcare professionals' competencies and workforce capacity, improving technological infrastructure, and ensuring that patients and families are adequately prepared to engage with digital health technologies. Existing progress in service development, interdisciplinary collaboration, and awareness among healthcare providers provides a foundation for further development; however, these enabling factors need to be accompanied by clear responsibilities and operational mechanisms across levels of care. From the perspective of the NASSS framework, sustainable implementation requires alignment across the technology, adopter system, organisation, and wider system rather than technological adoption in isolation. Telehealth should therefore be conceptualised as a complementary modality that can support continuity, communication, and access, while its effectiveness remains dependent on the strength of the underlying palliative care system. A systemic approach that simultaneously addresses fragmentation, strengthens inter‐facility coordination, develops workforce capacity, and improves digital readiness may provide a more feasible pathway for integrating telehealth into palliative care in Indonesia.

Prior to the development of telehealth‐based palliative care in Indonesia, several fundamental aspects must be addressed, including enhancing public awareness, strengthening the competencies of healthcare professionals, and aligning relevant policies. Initial progress is evident through ongoing service development, interdisciplinary collaboration, and increased awareness among healthcare providers. However, significant challenges remain, such as unclear regulatory frameworks, limited time and capacity among healthcare providers, and varying levels of technological literacy among patients and their families. Therefore, the successful implementation of telehealth in palliative care will require strategic efforts to address these barriers while reinforcing existing support. This includes clarifying telehealth policies, improving workforce capacity and training, and ensuring that families are adequately prepared to engage in digital health technologies. Overall, the development of telehealth‐based palliative care should be guided by a systemic approach that strengthens the enabling factors while mitigating potential obstacles.

Implications for Nursing Practice

Based on nurses' perspectives, telehealth‐based palliative care may offer a potential pathway for strengthening the delivery of palliative care services in Indonesia, particularly in Bandung, although this potential remains to be evaluated through dedicated implementation and outcome studies. The complex and multifaceted needs of patients with life‐limiting conditions, as described by participants, underscore the importance of further exploring this approach. The ongoing expansion of palliative care in Bandung provides a supportive foundation for integrating telehealth into routine practice. However, the successful development and implementation of such services require coordinated support from multiple stakeholders. The findings of this study may inform policymakers in establishing clear and comprehensive regulations, including guidelines, standards, financing mechanisms, and competency frameworks for telehealth healthcare professionals. Simultaneously, healthcare providers must strengthen their competencies in delivering telehealth‐based services. Public awareness is also essential as communities need to understand the value of telehealth in supporting patients with life‐limiting illnesses. Ultimately, the effective integration of palliative care across all levels of the healthcare system depends on shared commitment among stakeholders to improve both the access to and quality of palliative care services.

Although this study was conducted in Bandung City, Indonesia, its findings may have broader relevance for other developing countries facing similar challenges in palliative care delivery. The limited availability of trained healthcare professionals, fragmented coordination between facilities, and unequal access due to geographical barriers are common issues across many low‐ and middle‐income countries. The Indonesian context illustrates how telehealth is perceived by nurses as a potentially useful strategy for addressing these gaps in resource‐constrained settings, although its actual feasibility and scalability were not evaluated in this study and would require dedicated implementation research. By highlighting both the opportunities and barriers identified by nurses, this study offers insights that may inform health policy development, workforce training, and future research on the integration of technology into palliative care in comparable global contexts.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study is strengthened by the depth of exploration of the perspectives of nurses directly involved in palliative care and by the inclusion of participants from diverse healthcare settings. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, interviewers received an orientation briefing rather than formal, structured interviewer training, and the interview guide was not formally pretested before data collection. Although the guide was developed based on an established conceptual framework, the absence of a pilot phase may have limited opportunities to refine question wording and identify potential issues before the interviews. Second, the study was geographically restricted to Bandung City, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions of Indonesia with different healthcare systems, resources, and palliative care contexts. Third, the exclusive focus on nurses' perspectives may have limited a more comprehensive understanding of telehealth implementation across the broader healthcare system, as the perspectives of patients, family caregivers, physicians, healthcare managers, and policymakers were not explored. Fourth, although several safeguards were implemented to minimise the risk of perceived obligation arising from the first author's concurrent role as chair of HPPI, including independent, blinded screening of participants by another research team member, the possibility that this dual role influenced nurses' willingness to participate or the candour of their responses cannot be entirely excluded.

The characteristics of the participants also represent a consideration for transferability. More than half of the participants (56.25%) had more than 15 years of professional experience, which may have resulted in limited representation of perspectives from early‐career nurses. In addition, the relatively homogeneous religious background of the participants may have limited the diversity of perspectives regarding the role of spirituality and religious beliefs in palliative care. The under‐representation of younger nurses may also have limited the range of perspectives on telehealth, particularly regarding experiences and expectations related to digital technologies. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously when considering their transferability to younger nurses, professionals from different religious or cultural backgrounds, and healthcare settings with different demographic and professional characteristics.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the implementation of palliative care in Bandung City is constrained not primarily by the absence of structural and regulatory foundations, but by their limited translation into integrated and coordinated practice. Although foundational policies and service structures for palliative care are in place, telehealth has not yet been formally incorporated into existing governance and service frameworks. Rather, telehealth practices have developed informally as provider‐driven responses to persistent gaps in post‐discharge care and weak coordination across healthcare settings. These gaps contribute to systemic fragmentation, particularly during transitions between hospital‐based and community‐based services, while creating the conditions for telehealth to emerge as an informal means of maintaining communication, follow‐up, and continuity of care.

Within this context, nurses' perceptions of the benefits, barriers, and functional roles of telehealth suggest that it has the potential to serve not merely as a supportive communication tool but as a strategic mechanism for addressing discontinuities in care delivery. However, realising this potential requires the formalisation of telehealth within palliative care pathways, supported by appropriate regulatory frameworks, strengthened healthcare workforce capacity, and improved digital readiness among stakeholders. These findings contribute to the growing evidence from low‐ and middle‐income countries by highlighting that the effective use of telehealth in palliative care, should it be implemented, is likely to depend not only on technological availability but also on its systematic incorporation into health service delivery and broader health system reforms aimed at strengthening continuity, coordination, and patient‐centred care.
