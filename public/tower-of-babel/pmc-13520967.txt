Safety and Efficacy of Optimal Keratoplasty in Reduction of Hyperopia and Hyperopic Presbyopia: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Kenneth Jonathan Rodgers, Harry G Glen, Michael J Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the safety and efficacy of the VIS Optimal Keratoplasty device and procedure for treating eyes with hyperopia and hyperopic presbyopia to achieve uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) and uncorrected near visual acuity (UNVA) improvements.SettingBahamas Vision Centre, Nassau, New Providence, The Bahamas.Methods114 eyes of 57 patients with low to moderate hyperopic presbyopia (MRSE = 0.88 to 3.25 D; mean add ± standard deviation (SD) = 2.08 ± 0.43 D) received primary treatments (Txs) by the VIS NTK Optimal Keratoplasty device; 44 and 22 patients also received secondary and ternary Txs, respectively. Patients were treated to produce best UDVA and best UNVA in both eyes using a multifocal vision protocol. Safety and efficacy measures with follow-up (f/u) extending to 24 months post-Tx were analyzed.ResultsSafety – There were no clinically significant adverse events or complications. Efficacy – Geometric mean (gm) ± SD binocular UDVA improvements corresponded to 3.6 ± 3.9 lines gained at 1 day post-primary Tx (p < 0.01), with gradual reduction of improvement to 1.7 ± 2.1 lines gained at 24m′ post-secondary Tx. Gm binocular UNVA improvements peaked at 4.8 ± 2.9 lines gained at 1 day post-primary Tx (p < 0.01), with gradual reduction to 1.1 ± 2.6 lines gained at 24m′ post-secondary Tx. The mean durations of “functional” (>20/40) binocular UDVA and UNVA were over 2 years and ca. 6 months post-Tx, respectively.ConclusionsOptimal keratoplasty appears to be safe and efficacious for improving both UDVA and UNVA in eyes with hyperopic presbyopia, The procedure is noninvasive, simple, rapid, comfortable and repeatable. UDVA improvement is long lasting (2 years or more), although UNVA improvement is temporary. Additional optimal keratoplasty Txs can be repeated whenever needed to maintain functional UNVA.Keywords: safety, efficacy, hyperopia, presbyopia, visual acuity

Introduction

Presbyopia (aka age-related focus dysfunction) is a leading cause of vision impairment worldwide, adversely affecting over a billion people.1 Presbyopia is a progressive condition, typically starting at ca. age 40, and leading to ever increasing loss of uncorrected near visual acuity (UNVA) until lens flexibility is completely lost.2 Emmetropic presbyopes typically have good uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) but hyperopic presbyopes need both UDVA and UNVA improvements.

Numerous surgical and pharmacological approaches have been developed for the treatment of presbyopia.3 All of these approaches are limited in efficacy and in some cases have safety problems, but VIS Optimal Keratoplasty treatment has a very safe and efficacious clinical profile.4 The VIS device and procedure are being evaluated in an US FDA Clinical Trial, currently starting Phase 3 (pivotal, definitive) study; through Phase 2, treatment outcomes for only 10 eyes with hyperopic presbyopia have been obtained.5 Optimal keratoplasty treatment outcomes for a small number of hyperopic presbyopic patients in pilot studies have also been obtained.6,7

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the VIS Optimal Keratoplasty device and procedure to overcome hyperopic presbyopia by providing both UDVA and UNVA improvements.

Patients and Methods

This retrospective observational cohort study (registered as NCT06702020 at ClinicalTrials.gov) was completed in conformance with ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Bahamas Ministry of Health. Optimal keratoplasty treatment (Tx) was provided to 114 eyes of 57 patients (44♀, 13♂; mean age: 53.8 ± 6.9 y, range 38 to 71 y) with hyperopic presbyopia (HP). Inclusion criteria included age ≥ 40 y; manifest refraction, spherical equivalent (MRSE) = 0.88 to +3.25 diopter (D); mean ± SD: +1.67 ± 0.57 D, with ≤ 0.75 D cylinder; and corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) ≥ 20/30. Exclusion criteria included nystagmus; uncontrolled uveitis, severe blepharitis, lagophthalmos; moderate to severe dry eye disease; corneal pathology and/or shape disorders such as keratoconus; previous ocular surgery; autoimmune disease; immunocompromised status; chronic allergic reactions; glaucoma; and diabetes. Pre-Tx, all eyes had presbyopia with mean add of +2.08 ± 0.43 D (range: 0 to +2.75 D) and with corrected near visual acuity (CNVA) ≥ J1 (0.1 logMAR, 0.8 decimal, 20/25 Snellen).

Examinations were performed pre-Tx and at post-Tx times (1d, 1w, 1m, 3m, 6m, 12m, and 24m) for both primary and non-primary Txs. Examination measurements included uncorrected (U) and corrected (C) distance and near visual acuities (UDVA, UNVA, CDVA, CNVA, binocular UDVA, binocular UNVA) – all measured using projected letter charts for distance VA and a Rosenbaum chart for near VA (Jaeger values were converted to logMAR, decimal and Snellen values for reporting); subjective manifest refraction; corneal topography (using an Atlas Model 991 corneal topographer; Zeiss Humphrey Systems, Dublin, CA); intraocular pressure (by Goldmann applanation tonometry); pachymetry (using Model DGH-500 ultrasonic pachymeter; DGH Technology, Eaton, PA); slit-lamp biomicroscopic evaluation (including Na fluorescein staining to visualize epithelial damage); subjective discomfort assessment; and subjective satisfaction with binocular UDVA/UNVA.

All eyes were treated with a prototype VIS Optimal Keratoplasty device.8, The device included a continuous wave (cw) laser source operating at 1.93 µm wavelength, an optical delivery system comprising 16 optical fibers in a handpiece with a fixed Tx pattern, and a sapphire applanation window/suction ring that is mounted on the cornea. Figure 1 shows the primary Tx pattern which consisted of two rings (6.0 and 7.2 mm centerline diameters; in some cases, two rings of 5.5 and 6.7 mm or 5.0 and 6.2 mm centerline diameters were used – there was no significant difference in outcomes for the three sets of Tx parameters so only combined data are reported) of Tx spots (0.5 mm diameter) arranged in symmetrical octagons with spots on radials; for secondary Tx, the Tx pattern was rotated ca. 22° from the primary Tx orientation shown in Figure 1. For ternary and quartary Txs, the Tx pattern was rotated back to the primary and secondary positions, respectively. The same Tx patterns were used for 88, 16 and 4 eyes receiving secondary, ternary and quartary Txs, respectively. Two eyes of one patient received 10 Txs over a five-year period. The procedure evolved over the course of the clinical study in terms of suction ring designs; pre-Tx instillation fluid – the final choice was solute-free water; Tx nomogram (based on varying the Tx energy density); and post-Tx medication – the final choice was one drop of Xibrom™ (bromfenac ophthalmic solution 0.09%; Ista Pharmaceuticals, Irvine, CA), a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), instilled onto each treated eye immediately post-Tx and at 12 hour periods thereafter for the first 24 hours. No other medications were routinely used.

The first 12 patients were treated differently in their dominant eyes (for distance vision) compared to their nondominant eyes (for near vision) using a monovision protocol. After it was discovered that post-Tx UDVA for the nondominant eyes was maintained or improved, all eyes were treated using a multifocal vision protocol in which both eyes were treated for “best” post-Tx UNVA. Non-primary Txs were typically given when needed for UNVA improvement following regression of primary Tx UNVA values. Most patients received bilateral Txs; eyes of patients not reported herein had pre-Tx emmetropic presbyopia and are part of a separate cohort.

Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) was used for data entry and general statistical analysis. Mean ± standard deviation values are listed for outcomes. Statistical significance (SS) of outcomes (post- vs pre-primary Tx) was evaluated using Wilcoxon paired sample signed-rank two-sided tests; single (*) and double (**) asterisks indicate SS at the p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 levels, respectively.

Results

Post-secondary Tx time is identified by a prime; for example, a single prime as in 12m′ indicates twelve months post-secondary Tx time.

Safety

There were no clinically significant adverse events or complications in any eye at any post-Tx time. There were no cases of a temporary loss of more than 2 lines of corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) at any post-primary or post-secondary Tx time. The maximum induced astigmatism was 1.5 D and it was temporary. Mean cylinder changes for all eyes at all post-Tx times were in the range of −0.26 to +0.06 D for primary Txs and −0.21 to +0.19 D for secondary Txs.

Tables 1 and 2 list mean intraocular pressure (IOP) and mean central cornea thickness (CCT) respectively for subsets of eyes. There were small reductions in mean IOP for both primary and secondary Txs but reductions were not statistically nor clinically significant. No eye experienced IOP increase greater than 3 mm Hg; no eye had IOP > 19 mm Hg at any examination time. There were small changes in mean CCT for both primary and secondary Txs but changes were not statistically nor clinically significant. No eye experienced CCT change greater than 43 µm.

Epithelial Protection

Superficial punctate keratitis (SPK) was observed (by slit-lamp biomicroscopic examination using Na fluorescein staining) in some Tx spots. Epithelial damage was infrequent immediately post-Tx, was more frequent at the 1d post-primary Tx exam and was negligible or absent at the 1w and later f/u exams. Secondary Txs typically had a lower incidence of SPK compared to primary Txs, partly due to reduced Tx energy density. The procedure evolved over the course of the clinical study to increase epithelial protection and reduce discomfort. It was found that instilling solutions such as preservative-free Systane® (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX) and balanced saline solution (BSS) onto the eye increased the incidence of SPK, so solute-free water was selected as the instillation fluid. As the study progressed, SPK was found to be negligible for Tx energy density of 48 mJ/spot or less.

Manifest Refraction, Spherical Equivalent (Delta MRSE)

Mean ± SD manifest refraction, spherical equivalent (MRSE) for 114 eyes was +1.67 ± 0.57 D pre-Tx and +0.49 ± 0.63 D at 1 week post-primary Tx, corresponding to a MRSE change (myopic shift) of −1.18 ± 0.55 D, and then gradually returned to a maximum of +1.08 D at 24m post-Tx. Corneal Topography (CT)

Corneal topography (CT) maps showed mean ± SD central steepening of 1.40 ± 0.45 D from pre-Tx to 1 week post-primary Tx, in good agreement with the mean ± SD MRSE change (myopic shift) of −1.18 ± 0.55 D. At 6m post-primary Tx, the CT steepening was 0.65 ± 0.31 D, somewhat larger than the MRSE change of −0.60 ± 0.33 D.

Comfort

Subjective patient self-assessments of discomfort due to pain, tearing, photophobia and foreign body sensation (fbs) were each obtained on a scale of 0 (none), 1 (trace), 2 (mild), 3 (moderate) and 4 (severe). Maximum discomfort occurred on 1d post-primary Tx, with means ± standard deviations of 0.46 ± 1.13 (pain), 0.64 ± 1.23 (tearing), 0.93 ± 1.47 (photophobia) and 0.95 ± 1.28 (fbs) for all eyes. Patients who received Xibrom™ immediately post-Tx and at 12 hour intervals for 24 hours post-Tx had little or no discomfort. Other procedure improvements may also have contributed to comfort increase – the last 14 eyes that received primary Txs had mean discomforts of 0 (pain), 0 (tearing), 0 (photophobia) and 0.21 (fbs) on 1d post-primary Tx. Comfort was typically better for secondary Txs compared to primary Txs due, in part, to reduced Tx energy density. Comfort was usually excellent at 1w, 1w′ and later post-Tx times.

Recovery Time

Most patients had little or no post-Tx recovery time. As evidenced by visual acuity and subjective comfort outcomes, the majority of patients were fully functional at 1 day post-Tx.

Patient Satisfaction with Binocular Distance and Near Vision

Subjective self-assessments of patient satisfaction with binocular distance vision and binocular near vision were made on a 5-point scale in which a rating of 5 corresponded to high satisfaction and other ratings were 4 – satisfaction, 3 – neither satisfaction nor dissatisfaction, 2 – dissatisfaction, and 1 – high dissatisfaction. Baseline (pre-primary Tx) mean ± SD satisfaction with distance vision was moderately low: 3.08 ± 1.14 while mean ± SD satisfaction with near vision was very low: 1.70 ± 0.84.

Post-Tx satisfaction with distance vision: Both post-primary and post-secondary Tx values indicated greater satisfaction than at baseline at all f/u times; mean satisfaction values were in the range 4.00 to 4.50 and were statistically significant (p<0.05) for cohorts with n = 12 or more patients.

Post-Tx satisfaction with near vision: Both post-primary and post-secondary Tx values indicated much greater satisfaction than at baseline. Post-primary mean values at 1d (4.27 ± 0.85), 1w (3.10 ± 1.01), 1m (2.67 ± 0.97), 3m (2.89 ± 1.09) and 6m (3.04 ± 1.08) were all statistically significant improvements relative to baseline. Post-secondary Tx values also indicated greater satisfaction than at baseline; values at 1d′ (4.04 ± 0.96), 1w′ (3.74 ± 0.80), 1m′ (3.23 ± 0.74), 3m′ (2.91 ± 0.97) and 6m′ (2.79 ± 0.52) were statistically significant improvements relative to baseline. Other post-primary and post-secondary Tx value improvements in mean satisfaction with near vision were not statistically significant, partly due to small numbers of patient responses at those f/u times.

Discussion

Visual Acuities

Uncorrected Distance VA (both UDVA and Bi UDVA; for both primary and secondary Txs) was improved significantly from baseline at all post-Tx times. The mean ± SD duration of “functional” Bi UDVA effect (with mean value 20/40 or better) was 24 months or longer for patients with primary Txs only, and for patients with both primary and secondary Txs, but 44 of 57 (77.2%) patients required secondary Txs for Bi UNVA, not Bi UDVA improvements. Several of these patients received ternary and later quaternary Txs to boost Bi UNVA outcomes. 29 of 57 (50.9%) of patients had pre-Tx Bi UDVA of 20/40 or better, so they received Txs primarily to improve near visual acuity. The remaining 28 of 57 (49.1%) of patients had pre-Tx UDVA of 20/50 or worse, so they received Txs primarily to improve distance visual acuity. Separate analyses of the two cohorts (20/40 or better; 2050 or worse) indicate that post-primary Tx mean Bi UDVA values are nearly the same for both cohorts at 1d, 1w and 6m post-Tx, are a few letters less for 20/50 or worse at 1m and 3m, and are several letters better at 12m and 24m for 20/50or worse, although there is only data for three patients at 12m and 24m so the differences are not significant.

Uncorrected Near VA (both UNVA and Bi UNVA; for both primary and secondary Txs) improvements were large and statistically significant at early f/u times (up to 6m post-Tx) but regressed so that additional Txs were typically needed to boost improvement. The mean ± SD duration of “functional” Bi UNVA effect (with mean value 20/40 or better) was ca. 6 months for patients with primary Txs only. The mean ± SD duration of “functional” Bi UNVA effect was ca. 9 months for patients with both primary and secondary Txs. Regression of duration of effect timescales was confirmed by MRSE and CT measurements; both showed that initial refractive change and corneal steepening decreased as f/u time increased.

Both binocular UDVA and binocular UNVA outcomes were considerably better than monocular outcomes, due to binocular interaction.6

Presbyopia is a progressive condition, typically requiring presbyopic patients to change eyewear every 2 to 3 years to improve near vision. The duration of treatment (Tx) effect is less than needed to match the full timescale of presbyopia progression; however, the procedure is so safe, noninvasive, comfortable and repeatable that retreatment is a fine option. In addition, in contrast to many other procedures for presbyopia correction that involve monovision,3 Optimal Keratoplasty Txs are typically given bilaterally, thereby retaining stereoacuity, contrast sensitivity and freedom from dysphotopsias such as glare and halo.

Optimal Keratoplasty visual acuity outcomes presented herein are consistent with previous clinical study outcomes.7,8 Outcomes for a different thermal procedure for uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) improvement for hyperopic presbyopes (HPs), conductive keratoplasty (CK), have been reported.9,10 The CK outcomes are not directly comparable to the present Optimal Keratoplasty outcomes since CK Txs were all unilateral in a monovision protocol. Monovision was adopted for hyperopic presbyopes probably due to the high percentages of CK-treated eyes that lost significant UDVA − 77% and 60% had UDVA of 20/25 or worse at 3 months and 1 year post-Tx, respectively; 33% had UDVA of 20/50 or worse (dysfunctional) at 3 months post-Tx10 Figure 28. As emphasized herein, Optimal Keratoplasty Tx improves both UDVA and Bi UDVA markedly.

Outcomes from another thermal procedure for UDVA improvement of UDVA and Bi UDVA for hyperopes, noncontact holmium YAG laser thermal keratoplasty (LTK), have also been reported11 Table 5. The percentage of treated eyes in the US FDA Clinical Trial that had UDVA = 20/40 or better ranged between 65.4% (1m) to 68.1% (24m) in comparison to the present Nassau Clinical Study values of UDVA = 20/40 or better that ranged between 86.4% (1m) to 90.9% (24m). The Nassau patients had a similar MRSE = 1.67 ± 0.57 D (low to moderate hyperopia) compared to the FDA patients with MRSE = 1.69 ± 0.45 D; however, Nassau outcomes benefited from a better pre-Tx USDA distribution as well as from reduction of the cohort with primary Txs by transfer of patients with non-primary Txs to a separate cohort. Bi UDVA outcomes from the FDA study were not reported; only a subset of patients received bilateral treatments. Other drawbacks of the FDA compared to the Nassau study included greater induced astigmatism11 Table 10 and reduced patient satisfaction11 Table 12. Short-term (1m or less) discomfort such as pain was not reported in the FDA but was greater than for the Nassau study.

Hyperopic refractive errors are often corrected by other laser-based procedures (LASIK, PRK and Refractive Lens Exchange) with good outcomes in many cases; however, these procedures often have significant complications such as corneal haze and dysphotopsias.12 The Optimal Keratoplasty outcomes reported herein appear to often an alternative hyperopia correction procedure without significant complications.

Study Limitations

The present clinical study is limited in terms of the number of patients (57), follow-up time (2 years or less), and lack of other measurements (specular microscopy, reading speed, contrast sensitivity, stereoacuity, aberrometry, histology, etc.) that would contribute to understanding the mechanism of action (corneal shape change leading to refractive change) and full outcomes of Optimal Keratoplasty treatment. In addition, centration accuracy has been improved in a later study,5 leading to larger UDVA improvements than in the present study. The pupil center was used as the centration reference in the present study, but visual acuity outcomes for hyperopic patients may be improved in magnitude (and possibly in duration) by using the coaxially sighted corneal light reflex as the centration reference.13

A related paper presents Optimal Keratoplasty treatment outcomes for patients with emmetropic presbyopia.14

A concern is that the authors may have interpretation bias due to proprietary and other conflicts of interest. As indicated above, a totally independent study on hyperopic presbyopes using the same Optimal Keratoplasty device and procedure7 yielded very similar outcomes and interpretations compared to those presented in this MS.

Conclusions

Optimal Keratoplasty appears to be safe and efficacious in providing significant and long duration improvement of monocular and binocular UDVA to patients with hyperopic presbyopia. Bi UDVA improvement to a functional decimal level of 0.50 (0.30 logMAR, 20/40 Snellen) has a duration of more than two years post-secondary Tx for at least half the patients with hyperopic presbyopia. However, the near visual acuity improvement is less (both in magnitude and duration of effect), but Txs are repeatable and can be applied whenever needed to improve near visual acuity. The Optimal Keratoplasty Tx procedure is minimally invasive and rapid and is simple and convenient for both physician and patient.
