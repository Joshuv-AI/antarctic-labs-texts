A Positivity-Preserving Finite Element Framework for Accurate Dose Computation in Proton Therapy
Ben S. Ashby, Abdalaziz Hamdan, Tristan Pryer

Abstract
. We present a stabilised finite element method for modelling proton transport in tissue, incorporating both inelastic energy loss and elastic angular scattering. A key innovation is a positivity-preserving formulation that guarantees non-negative fluence and dose, even on coarse meshes. This enables reliable computation of clinically relevant quantities for treatment planning. We derive a priori error estimates demonstrating optimal convergence rates and validate the method through numerical benchmarks. The proposed framework provides a robust, accurate and efficient tool for advancing proton beam therapy.



1.
Introduction

Proton Beam Therapy (PBT) has emerged as a promising modality for
treating cancers where conventional radiotherapy fails to sufficiently
spare surrounding healthy tissue. These include pediatric cases,
skull-base tumours and complex head and neck malignancies.

A proton deposits energy as it traverses matter, with energy
deposition increasing with depth and peaking at the end of its path, a
phenomenon known as the Bragg peak, illustrated in
Figure
1
. This dose localisation underpins the
appeal of PBT, it enables precise tumour targeting while reducing
irradiation to critical structures.

Figure 1
.

A 2D slice of dose from a 62 MeV proton beam. The sharp Bragg
peak near the end of the track highlights the potential of proton
beams to localise dose. This deterministic simulation includes
inelastic and Coulomb scattering but neglects nuclear
interactions.

The potential for improved dose distributions has been recognised for
decades
[
LGA03
]
, and by 2021, over 275,000
patients had received PBT worldwide

[
Par22
]
. Nonetheless,
conclusive evidence of its superiority over photon-based treatments
remains limited
[
Che+23
]
.

A persistent challenge in PBT is inter-fractional anatomical
variation, such as changes in patient hydration or positioning. These
perturbations introduce uncertainties in the delivered dose, often
mitigated by enlarging treatment margins, compromising the very
advantage of protons. Improving robustness thus hinges on predictive,
high-fidelity models of proton transport.

From a computational perspective, accurately modelling proton
transport and dose deposition is essential to addressing these
challenges. Protons interact with matter through inelastic collisions
(leading to continuous energy loss), elastic scattering (which
deflects particle direction), and non-elastic nuclear reactions
(producing secondaries); see Figure
2
. These processes
make high-fidelity simulation computationally intensive, creating a
bottleneck for routine clinical use.

In this work, we develop a deterministic finite element model for
proton transport, designed to ensure numerical accuracy and preserve
key physical properties. Deterministic solvers provide efficient
alternatives to Monte Carlo methods, but must contend with issues such
as nonphysical negativity and the stiffness of kinetic-type equations.

Our model incorporates both inelastic and elastic scattering into a
computationally efficient PDE-based framework. Continuous energy loss
is treated using the transport model of
[
Ash+25
]
,
while angular broadening is handled via a Laplace–Beltrami diffusion
operator on the sphere
[
Dzi88
,
DMS13
]
. A
key innovation is a positivity-preserving discretisation that enforces
nonnegativity at Lagrange nodes using a variational inequality
formulation
[
ABP24
,
Bar+24
,
AHP25
]
. This
addresses a long-standing issue in deterministic transport, where
standard methods produce unphysical negative fluences or
doses
[
Sta+24
]
.

We also propose three approaches for dose computation, Galerkin
projection, piecewise constant projection and a variational
inequality, which trade off between accuracy, simplicity and
positivity preservation. This makes our framework suitable for use in
downstream optimisation and inverse problems. Our techniques
generalise to other observables such as LET and biological response
models
[
Ash+25
]
.

We prove optimal convergence rates for both fluence and dose in the
SUPG and positivity-preserving schemes. These theoretical results are
validated through numerical benchmarks, including comparisons with
Monte Carlo and semi-analytic solutions. The results demonstrate that
our method yields stable, accurate and physically meaningful
approximations, making it a practical tool for treatment planning and
future optimisation in proton therapy.

Monte Carlo methods have traditionally served as the gold standard for
modelling proton transport due to their ability to accurately capture
individual proton
interactions
[
Sal13
,
JS14
]
. However, their
high computational cost often limits their practicality in clinical
contexts, particularly for real-time treatment planning and dose
delivery verification
[
Cox+24
]
. In
contrast, deterministic modelling of proton transport remains
relatively underexplored when compared to the extensive literature on
neutron transport and linear Boltzmann transport more broadly. Recent
efforts, such as
[
Sta+24
]
, have begun to
address this gap by developing deterministic solvers that balance
computational efficiency with physical fidelity. These approaches
borrow heavily from the numerical and analytical theory developed for
the linear Boltzmann equation, which models transport in space, angle,
energy and time.

For instance,
[
Hou+24
]
investigates fully
discontinuous Galerkin (dG) discretisations of linear Boltzmann
transport, treating the angular variable as an independent
coordinate. This leads to a high-dimensional problem: in spatial
dimension
d
, the PDE spans
d
spatial,
d-1
angular and one energy
variable. The resulting computational burden has motivated the
development of iterative and acceleration techniques, such as the
improved source iteration in
[
HHR24
]
,
quadrature-free methods in
[
RHH23
]
and efficient
sweep-based solvers in
[
Cal+24
]
. These works aim to
reduce the cost of solving kinetic equations without sacrificing
accuracy or stability.

In deterministic settings, elastic scattering is often modelled using
simplified diffusion operators or approximate angular kernels. The
Laplace–Beltrami operator provides a geometrically natural model for
angular diffusion and has been applied within the context of
stochastic differential equation modelling for proton transport

[
Cro+25
]
. Inelastic scattering, which describes
continuous energy loss, is typically encoded via stopping power models
such as Bethe–Bloch. More recently,
[
Ash+25
]

introduced a PDE-based transport framework that captures continuous
slowing down efficiently, providing an alternative to stochastic
particle simulations.

Ensuring physical fidelity in deterministic models requires the
development of positivity-preserving schemes. Without such mechanisms,
standard discretisations may produce unphysical negative values for
fluence or dose, especially when working with coarse meshes. This
issue has been extensively studied in related elliptic and
convection-dominated problems. For
instance,
[
Bar+24
]
proposes a variational
inequality framework that enforces positivity at nodal degrees of
freedom. The discrete solution lies in a convex set defined by bounds
on the nodal values, ensuring physical admissibility. These ideas
provide the foundation for our treatment of positivity in proton
transport.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In
§
2
, we introduce the mathematical model for charged
particle transport, including the representation of elastic and
inelastic scattering processes and the formulation of the governing
partial differential equation. §
3
develops the
streamline-upwind Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG) discretisation, including
the positivity-preserving variational inequality scheme and associated
error analysis. In §
4
, we consider numerical
approximation of the absorbed dose, propose several projection
strategies and derive bounds for the induced dose error. Numerical
results are presented in §
5
, where we validate our
method against analytic benchmarks and high-fidelity Boltzmann
solutions and demonstrate the accuracy and stability of the proposed
formulations. Finally, §
6
summarises the main
findings and outlines directions for future research.

For fixed unitary angle
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we treat
direction as a parameter rather than an independent variable, thereby
reducing the angular domain to a point. In the full Boltzmann
framework,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
ranges over the unit sphere and the
Laplace–Beltrami operator acts on the angular variable. In this
setting, however, we consider
\boldsymbol{\omega}
fixed and examine the
implications for modelling angular diffusion.

The operator
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami
operator on the sphere, which formally takes the form

(3)

\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cdot(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u),

where the angular gradient is defined using the projection operator

(4)

\mathcal{P}=\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega},

which projects vectors onto the tangent space of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
at

\boldsymbol{\omega}
. Specifically, for a function
u
, the projected
gradient is

(5)

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u=\mathcal{P}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u=(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u-\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}.

2.1 Remark

(2D projection identity)
.

For
d=2
, the projection of the gradient orthogonal to a unit
vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
can be written compactly using the
perpendicular vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp}
, defined as

(6)

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp}=\begin{pmatrix}-\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{1}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}\\
\omega_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

(7)

(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega})\nabla u=(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla u)\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp},

expressing the orthogonal projection of
\nabla u
as a scalar
multiple of a single direction.

For
d=3
, the orthogonal complement of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is
two-dimensional, so no unique analogue of
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp}

exists. The projection must instead be expressed in terms of a full
basis for the plane orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

This representation assumes
u
is smooth enough for all
quantities to be well defined. Fixing
\boldsymbol{\omega}
eliminates the
angular domain and replaces the Laplace–Beltrami operator with an
artificial angular diffusion term involving an implicit boundary
condition. Since
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is a compact manifold without
boundary, any notion of inflow or Dirichlet data on the angular domain
is induced entirely by this parametrisation.

Given
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, we introduce the degenerate elliptic proton
transport problem: find the fluence
\psi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such
that

(8)

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},E)-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial E}\left(\mathscr{S}(E)\psi(\boldsymbol{x},E)\right)-\epsilon\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},E)&=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
\psi&=g\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{-}.\end{split}

The inflow boundary
\Gamma_{-}\subset\partial\Omega
is given by

\Gamma_{-}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}:\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}<0\}\cup\{\partial\Omega_{E}=E_{\max}\},

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
denotes the outward unit normal vector to

\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. The problem domain is illustrated in

The analysis of this degenerate elliptic problem is challenging due to
the partial smoothing effects of the angular diffusion term, combined
with the advective behaviour. For example, while angular diffusion
regularises the solution in directions orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, the advection
term can propagate discontinuities along streamlines.

2.2 Remark

(Physical interpretation)
.

The proton transport equation models the evolution of fluence in
both space and energy, incorporating the two dominant physical
effects from Figure
2
:

(9)

\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi}_{\text{Transport}}-\underbrace{\nabla_{E}\left(\mathscr{S}(E)\psi\right)}_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0.2,1,1}Inelastic Coulomb scattering}}}-\underbrace{\epsilon\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi}_{\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}Elastic Coulomb scattering}}}=f\quad\text{in }\Omega.

The first term represents directional transport, describing how
protons travel in straight lines through the medium along a fixed
direction.

The second term accounts for energy loss due to inelastic Coulomb
interactions, where protons transfer energy to bound electrons in
the material. We model this dissipation using the Bragg–Kleeman
stopping power formulation, a simple representation of the
continuous slowing down of protons. Other models, such as
Bethe–Bloch, are compatible with the framework but are not
considered further here.

The third term represents elastic Coulomb scattering, corresponding
to angular deflections caused by interactions with atomic nuclei. This
produces a diffusion-like effect in the angular variable, capturing
the gradual broadening of the beam from small-angle scattering.

The source term allows for an external input of protons within the
domain, while the inflow boundary condition accounts for incident
protons entering the system, either from a physical boundary or a
prescribed beam source.

2.2.
Weak formulation

We work in the space
\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and impose the inflow boundary
condition weakly through the bilinear and linear forms. This space
allows a variational formulation of problem
(
8
): Find
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

(10)

\mathcal{A}(\psi,v)+\mathcal{B}(\psi,v)=l(v)\quad\>\forall\>v\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega),

where

(11)

\mathcal{A}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}v\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}E,

(12)

\mathcal{B}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u-\nabla_{E}\!\left({\mathscr{S}(E)u}\right)}\right)v\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}E-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)uv\,\mathrm{d}s,

and

(13)

l(v)=\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}E-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)gv\,\mathrm{d}s.

2.3 Remark

(Boundary conditions and projected diffusion)
.

The inflow boundary condition is imposed weakly through the bilinear
form
\mathcal{B}
and linear form
l
. This inflow condition allows
us to account for prescribed incident particles entering the domain,
typically from a beam source.

The diffusion operator acts only in directions orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
. Since no boundary condition is imposed explicitly in those
directions, we must assume a homogeneous Neumann condition

(14)

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\subset\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}},

where

(15)

\Gamma^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}:\;\mathcal{P}\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\right\}\quad\text{with }\mathcal{P}=\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}.

This identifies the portion of the boundary where the outward normal
has a nonzero component orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and thus where
transverse diffusion may induce a nontrivial normal flux.

Physically, this corresponds to assuming that elastic Coulomb
scattering, which induces transverse broadening of the beam, does
not result in any loss of particles through the boundary in the
directions orthogonal to transport. That is, particles scatter
within the medium but do not escape through the sides. This is
consistent with the interpretation of the domain as a confined
spatial region in which the beam evolves.

For
d=2
, this condition reduces to checking whether
\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp}\neq 0
. For example, on a square
spatial domain if
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(1,0)
, then
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\perp}=(0,1)
, and the Neumann condition becomes
\partial_{x_{2}}\psi=0

on the top and bottom edges of the domain, where the outer normal
has nonzero vertical component.

2.4 Remark

(Regularising effects and hypocoercivity)
.

The angular diffusion term contributes to improved regularity of
solutions by penalising high-frequency oscillations orthogonal to
the transport direction. When combined with the advection term, the
system exhibits a property known as
hypocoercivity
, a
mechanism by which the interplay between transport and dissipation
leads to decay to equilibrium and enhanced regularity under suitable
conditions
[
Vil09
]
. This effect is
particularly relevant in degenerate elliptic problems, where
coercivity may be lacking in some directions, but regularity is
recovered through the coupling of transport and diffusion

[
Geo21
,
PZ17
]
. See also

[
PPT24
]
for a discussion of hypocoercivity in the
context of charged particle transport.

2.5 Remark

(Problems satisfying a priori bounds)
.

For some choices of
\mathscr{S}
and
\epsilon=0
, problem
(
8
) can be solved analytically using the
method of characteristics
[
Ash+25
]
. When
\epsilon>0
, the angular diffusion term provides a regularising effect that
suppresses transverse oscillations, but the problem is no longer
analytically tractable in general. In either case, the structure of
the equation allows for the derivation of useful
a priori

bounds on
\psi
, depending on the data
f
and
g
.

2.6 Remark

(Maximum principle)
.

The solution to problem (
8
) satisfies a
maximum principle. Specifically, if
f=0
and
g\geq 0
, then the
solution is nonnegative and bounded above by the incoming boundary
data

0\leq\psi(\boldsymbol{x},E)\leq\sup_{\Gamma_{-}}g,\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{x},E)\in\Omega.

This follows from the directional structure of the equation: the
advection term transports information along characteristics from

\Gamma_{-}
and the diffusion operator
\epsilon\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
acts only in directions orthogonal to transport, without
introducing any additional source of negativity. In this setting,

\boldsymbol{\omega}
is treated as a fixed parameter and no boundary
condition is imposed in the directions where diffusion acts.

This property is important to ensure nonnegativity of the fluence
and is essential for the physical interpretability of the model;
see, e.g.,
[
RR06
]
. It also forms the core
principle motivating the design of our numerical scheme in the
following section.

3.
SUPG discretisation

We now introduce a stabilised finite element method for the
variational problem (
10
). The key idea is
to retain the diffusion term in its standard Galerkin form, while
modifying the treatment of the advective terms using a Streamline
Upwind Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG) approach. This stabilisation introduces
a consistent perturbation to the test space along the characteristic
direction of transport, enhancing stability and suppressing
nonphysical oscillations in the numerical solution. The method
preserves the variational structure of the problem and is compatible
with the weak enforcement of boundary conditions described previously.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a regular subdivision of
\Omega
into disjoint
simplicial or box-type (quadrilateral/hexahedral) elements
K
, with

\bar{\Omega}=\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\bar{K}
. We assume

\mathcal{T}
is shape-regular and that the elemental faces are
straight planar segments. Let
h_{K}=\text{diam}(K)
, and define the
piecewise constant meshsize function
h
by
h|_{K}=h_{K}
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the continuous finite element space

(16)

\mathbb{V}:=\left\{v_{h}\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega):\;v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(K)\quad\>\forall\>K\in\mathcal{T}\right\}\cap\operatorname{C}^{0}(\Omega),

and denote the set of Lagrange nodes by
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{V}}
.

3.0.
Stabilised formulation

For
u_{h},v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
, define the transport operator

(17)

\mathscr{L}(u_{h}):=\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u_{h}-\nabla_{E}\!\left({\mathscr{S}(E)u_{h}}\right),

and consider the stabilised bilinear form

(18)

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})&:=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\delta_{K}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\mathscr{L}(v_{h})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u_{h}-\nabla_{E}(\mathscr{S}(E)u_{h})}\right)v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\delta_{K}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u_{h}-\nabla_{E}(\mathscr{S}(E)u_{h})}\right)\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}v_{h}-\nabla_{E}(\mathscr{S}(E)v_{h})}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E.\end{split}

Here,
\delta_{K}
is a stabilisation parameter chosen based on the
local mesh size and characteristic transport speed to be specified
below.

The stabilised finite element method then reads: find
\psi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
such that

(19)

\mathcal{A}(\psi_{h},v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi_{h},v_{h})=l(v_{h})\quad\>\forall\>v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}.

3.1 Remark

(Positivity in deterministic radiation transport simulations)
.

Even in simplified settings, standard finite element solvers may
fail to produce physically meaningful, nonnegative solutions

[
AHP25
]
. This issue becomes more pronounced in
realistic configurations
[
Sta+24
]
; see also
the example in §
5
. In the next section, we
describe a modification of the SUPG scheme (
19
) that
enforces positivity at the degrees of freedom, using techniques
developed in
[
AHP25
]
.

3.1.
A positivity-preserving scheme

Let
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the Lagrange nodes of
\mathbb{V}
. We
define the convex subset
K_{h}\subset\mathbb{V}
consisting of all
functions whose nodal values lie within the physical bounds:

(20)

K_{h}:=\left\{v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}:\;v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\in[0,\sup_{\Gamma_{-}}g]\quad\>\forall\>i=1,\dots,N\right\}.

3.2 Remark

(Preservation of bounds at the degrees of freedom)
.

For polynomial degree 1, functions in
K_{h}
are guaranteed to
satisfy the upper and lower bounds pointwise, as they are linearly
interpolated between nodal values. For polynomial degree 2 or
higher, this is no longer the case: functions in
K_{h}
are only

nodally
bound-preserving. This notion was studied in

[
Bar+24
]
for second-order elliptic problems,
where nodal bound preservation was achieved via a nonlinear
stabilised method. There, the bound-preserving discrete solution was
shown to coincide with the solution of a discrete variational
inequality.

We now seek a solution
\psi_{h}^{+}\in K_{h}
satisfying the inequality

(21)

\mathcal{A}(\psi_{h}^{+},v_{h}-\psi_{h}^{+})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi_{h}^{+},v_{h}-\psi_{h}^{+})\geq l(v_{h}-\psi_{h}^{+})\quad\quad\>\forall\>v_{h}\in K_{h}.

By construction,
\psi_{h}^{+}\in K_{h}
satisfies a nodal analogue of the
maximum principle, preserving nonnegativity and an upper bound
determined by the inflow data.

We proceed to analyse the method from an
a priori
perspective.

3.3 Lemma

(Consistency)
.

Let
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)
solve (
10
) and
satisfy
\psi=g
pointwise on
\Gamma_{-}
. Then

(22)

\mathcal{A}(\psi,v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi,v_{h})=l(v_{h})\quad\>\forall\>v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}.

Proof.

Since
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\mathcal{P}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we may integrate by parts in the angular diffusion term:

(23)

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\psi,v_{h})&=\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}E\\
&=-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi\,v_{h}\,\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\,\mathrm{d}E+\epsilon\int_{\Gamma^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\omega})}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\,v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

The integration by parts identity follows from standard divergence
theory applied to projected gradients; see, e.g.,

[
EG04
, §1.9]
.

The boundary term vanishes due to the homogeneous Neumann condition

(24)

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),

as discussed in Remark
2.3
above. Thus,

(25)

\mathcal{A}(\psi,v_{h})=-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi\,v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E.

For the transport terms, since
\psi
solves the PDE in the strong
form, the residual of
\mathscr{L}(\psi)
vanishes pointwise. Therefore, the
SUPG stabilisation term gives no contribution,

(26)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\delta_{K}\mathscr{L}(\psi)\mathscr{L}(v_{h})=0.

For the transport terms, since
\psi
solves the PDE in the strong
form, the residual of
\mathscr{L}(\psi)
vanishes pointwise. Therefore, the
SUPG stabilisation term gives no contribution

(27)

\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi,v_{h})=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}(\psi)v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\delta_{K}\mathscr{L}(\psi)\mathscr{L}(v_{h})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}(\psi)v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E.

Finally, since
\psi
satisfies the variational formulation
(
10
), we conclude that

(28)

\mathcal{A}(\psi,v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi,v_{h})=l(v_{h})\quad\quad\>\forall\>v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}.

∎

A natural notion of error when considering the problem
(
19
) is the energy norm, defined by

(29)

\begin{split}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}&:=\epsilon\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\mu\left\|u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{+}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Note that this is indeed a norm since
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}\geq 0
on
\Gamma^{+}
.

3.4 Lemma

(Continuity and coercivity)
.

Let
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
. Then

(30)

\mathcal{A}(u_{h},u_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})\geq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

Furthermore, let

(31)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}^{2}:={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\delta_{K}^{-1}\left\|u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}

Then, for
u_{h},v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}

(32)

\mathcal{A}(u_{h},v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})\leq C_{B}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

Proof.

By definition we have

(33)

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})&=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})u_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}.\end{split}

We expand the first term using the product rule

(34)

\nabla_{E}(\mathscr{S}(E)u_{h})=\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E)u_{h}+\mathscr{S}(E)\nabla_{E}u_{h}.

Then,

(35)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})u_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E&=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}u_{h}\,u_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E-\int_{\Omega}\!\left({\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E)u_{h}+\mathscr{S}(E)\nabla_{E}u_{h}}\right)u_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\,u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}\,u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E.\end{split}

Therefore,

(36)

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})&=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{+}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E.\end{split}

The term involving
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E)
is strictly negative and we recall
that
\mu:=-\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E_{\min})>0
. Therefore, using the
monotonicity of
\mathscr{S}
, we have

(37)

\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(E)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E\leq-\mu\left\|u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

The angular diffusion term contributes

(38)

\mathcal{A}(u_{h},u_{h})=\epsilon\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Combining all terms, we find

(39)

\mathcal{A}(u_{h},u_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})\geq\epsilon\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\mu\left\|u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum_{K}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}+\int_{\Gamma^{+}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

showing coercivity.

For continuity, each term is estimated separately. For the angular
diffusion term,

(40)

\mathcal{A}(u_{h},v_{h})=\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u_{h}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}E\leq\epsilon\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}v_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

For the transport term, we use Cauchy–Schwarz on each element

(41)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})v_{h}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})v_{h}&\leq\sum_{K}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\delta_{K}^{-1}\left\|v_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}.\\
&\leq\!\left({\sum_{K}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\!\left({\sum_{K}\delta_{K}^{-1}\left\|v_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

The stabilisation term satisfies

(42)

\sum_{K}\int_{K}\delta_{K}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\mathscr{L}(v_{h})\leq\!\left({\sum_{K}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(u_{h})\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\!\left({\sum_{K}\delta_{K}^{-1}\left\|v_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.

For the inflow boundary term, observe that the weight
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}
is non-negative and
uniformly bounded above on
\Gamma_{-}
by

(43)

\left|\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}\right|\leq 1+\left\|\mathscr{S}(E)\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Then, by a trace inverse inequality on shape-regular meshes (e.g.,

[
EG04
]
), we obtain

(44)

\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{\operatorname{tr}}\!\left({1+\left\|\mathscr{S}(E)\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}}\right)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\delta_{K}^{-1/2}\left\|u_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\delta_{K}^{1/2}\left\|v_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz over
K\in\mathcal{T}
, this yields

(45)

\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mathscr{S}(E)n_{E}}\right)u_{h}v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{\operatorname{tr}}\!\left({1+\left\|\mathscr{S}(E)\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

which, upon combining with the previous estimates, completes the
proof.
∎

3.5 Remark

(Continuity constant and
\delta_{K}
dependence)
.

The continuity constant
C_{B}
in Lemma
3.4
is given
by

(46)

C_{B}^{2}=\epsilon+\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\delta_{K}+C_{\operatorname{tr}}^{2}\!\left({1+\left\|\mathscr{S}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega_{E})}}\right)^{2}.

In the case of the Bragg–Kleeman model (
1
), the
stopping power satisfies

(47)

\mathscr{S}(E)=\frac{1}{\alpha p}E^{1-p},\quad\left\|\mathscr{S}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega_{E})}\sim E_{\min}^{1-p},

so
C_{B}
may still become large as
E_{\min}\to 0
when
p>1
,
reflecting the degeneracy of the model at low energy. We take

E_{\min}>0
fixed throughout this work.

3.6 Theorem

(Quasi-optimality)
.

Let
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)
be the solution to the continuous
problem (
10
), and let
\psi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}

solve the discrete problem (
19
). Then

(48)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-\psi_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\!\left({1+C_{B}}\right)\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}

where
C_{B}>0
is the continuity constant from
Lemma
3.4
.

Proof.

Let
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
be arbitrary and define the error
e_{h}=\psi_{h}-v_{h}
. By consistency of the method (Lemma
3.3
) and
Galerkin orthogonality,

(49)

\mathcal{A}(\psi_{h}-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi_{h}-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h})=\mathcal{A}(\psi-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h}).

Applying the coercivity estimate from Lemma
3.4
,

(50)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi_{h}-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leq\mathcal{A}(\psi_{h}-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi_{h}-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h}).

Applying the continuity bound from Lemma
3.4
,

(51)

\mathcal{A}(\psi-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h})+\mathcal{B}_{h}(\psi-v_{h},\psi_{h}-v_{h})\leq C_{B}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi_{h}-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

Dividing both sides, we obtain

(52)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi_{h}-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C_{B}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}.

Since this holds for all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
, we conclude

(53)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-\psi_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi_{h}-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\!\left({1+C_{B}}\right)\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}.

The result follows after absorbing the prefactor into the constant.
∎

3.7 Corollary

(Best approximation)
.

Let
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
for some
s\geq 1
, and let
\psi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
solve the discrete problem (
19
). Suppose the stabilisation parameter
\delta_{K}
is chosen according to

(54)

\delta_{K}=\frac{h_{K}}{2\!\left({\left|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right|+\left|\Pi_{0}\mathscr{S}(E)\right|}\right)},

where
\Pi_{0}
denotes the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
projection onto elementwise
constants. Then the following a priori estimate holds

(55)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-\psi_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\!\left({C_{1}\epsilon^{1/2}h^{\min\!\left({p,s}\right)}+C_{2}h^{\min\!\left({p+1/2,s}\right)}}\right)\left|\psi\right|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)},

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are constants independent of
h
.

Proof.

Let
I_{h}\psi\in\mathbb{V}
denote the Lagrange interpolant of

\psi
. Applying Theorem
3.6
yields

(56)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-\psi_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\!\left({1+C_{B}}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-I_{h}\psi\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}.

To estimate each component of the error, we apply standard
interpolation estimates for
I_{h}\psi
assuming
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
. First, for the angular diffusion term

(57)

\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\psi-I_{h}\psi)\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{\min(k,s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}.

Next, for the stabilisation term involving the transport operator

(58)

\!\left({\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\left\|\delta_{K}^{1/2}\mathscr{L}(\psi-I_{h}\psi)\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\leq Ch^{\min(k+1/2,s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}.

This rate reflects the half-order gain due to the use of local

\delta_{K}\sim h_{K}
stabilisation.

For the boundary term we apply a trace inequality

(59)

\left\|\psi-I_{h}\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Gamma^{+})}^{2}\leq Ch^{2\min(k+1/2,s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Finally, for the dual norm contribution

(60)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\delta_{K}^{-1}\left\|\psi-I_{h}\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}^{2}\leq C\sum_{K}h_{K}^{-1}h_{K}^{2\min(k+1,s+1)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(K)}^{2}\leq Ch^{2\min(k+1/2,s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Collecting all terms, we find

(61)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\psi-\psi_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}h^{\min(k,s)}\left|\psi\right|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}+Ch^{\min(k+1/2,s)}\left|\psi\right|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)},

as claimed.
∎

3.8 Remark

(Error rates and the role of
\epsilon
)
.

Notice, if
\epsilon=0
, corresponding to the absence of angular
diffusion, the bilinear form remains coercive with respect to

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
. However, the problem becomes purely hyperbolic and
a stronger result is obtained, that is, the error rate improves to

\operatorname{O}(h^{p+1/2})
.

4.
Computation of Absorbed Dose

The absorbed dose
\mathscr{D}(\boldsymbol{x})
represents the energy deposited per unit
mass at a given spatial position
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. It is
computed as

(62)

\mathscr{D}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{E_{\text{min}}}^{E_{\text{max}}}\frac{S(E)}{\rho(\boldsymbol{x})}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},E)\,\mathrm{d}E,

where
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})
is the mass density of the medium at position

\boldsymbol{x}
.

The absorbed dose is an important output in proton beam therapy. The
nature of proton interactions with matter leads to the Bragg peak,
where protons deposit the majority of their energy near the end of
their range. Accurate computation of dose ensures that the tumour
receives the prescribed energy deposition while minimising exposure to
healthy tissues.

To approximate the absorbed dose numerically, we consider the discrete
fluence
\psi_{h}
and define the approximate dose
\mathscr{D}_{h}
via quadrature
in energy. Several approaches are possible depending on how the result
is projected into a spatial finite element space. Let
\{\xi_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q_{E}}

and
\{w_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q_{E}}
be quadrature nodes and weights in energy.

4.0.
Galerkin projection

A natural approach is to define
\mathscr{D}_{h}
by spatial projection of the
integrand in (
62
) onto the finite element space

\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Given the discrete fluence
\psi_{h}
, we define
\mathscr{D}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
by the variational formulation

(63)

\int_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{E}}w_{q}\frac{S(\xi_{q})}{\rho(\boldsymbol{x})}\psi_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\xi_{q})v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\quad\>\forall\>v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}.

That is
\mathscr{D}_{h}
is the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-projection of the energy-integrated
quantity onto the spatial finite element space
\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

4.1 Lemma

(Error estimate for Galerkin dose projection)
.

Let
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
for some
s\geq 1
, and let
\psi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
be the finite element approximation. Let
\mathscr{D}
denote the
exact dose defined by (
62
), and let
\mathscr{D}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
be the Galerkin projection defined in
(
63
) using a quadrature rule of order
q_{E}
in
energy with step size
h_{E}
. Then

(64)

\left\|\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{D}_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\leq C_{1}h_{E}^{\min(q_{E},s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}+C_{2}h^{\min(k,s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Define the auxiliary quadrature-integrated exact dose as

(65)

\mathscr{D}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{E}}w_{q}\frac{S(\xi_{q})}{\rho(\boldsymbol{x})}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\xi_{q}).

Now decompose the error as

(66)

\left\|\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{D}_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\leq\left\|\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{D}^{*}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}+\left\|\mathscr{D}^{*}-\mathscr{D}_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}.

The first term is the quadrature error. Since
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
, the map
E\mapsto\psi(\boldsymbol{x},E)
is smooth for
each fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
and standard quadrature theory yields

(67)

\left\|\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{D}^{*}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\leq C_{1}h_{E}^{\min(q_{E},s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}.

The second term is the projection error in space. Since
\psi
is
smooth
\mathscr{D}^{*}
is smooth in
\boldsymbol{x}
and

(68)

\left\|\mathscr{D}^{*}-\mathscr{D}_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\leq Ch^{\min(k,s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)}.

Combining both gives the stated bound.
∎

4.2 Remark

(Failure of positivity)
.

Although the Galerkin projection is variationally consistent and
convergent, it does not preserve nonnegativity. Even if
\psi_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},E)\geq 0
for all
(\boldsymbol{x},E)\in\Omega
, and
S(E),\rho(\boldsymbol{x})>0
, the projection onto
\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
may produce
\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})<0
at some nodes due to sign changes in the basis functions.

In particular, even when
\psi_{h}
is computed using a nodally bound
preserving method, the dose
\mathscr{D}_{h}
does not inherit this property
unless additional constraints are enforced. This limitation makes
the Galerkin approach unsuitable in contexts where pointwise
nonnegativity of the dose is required, for example, as part of a
constraint in inverse problems or optimisation routines.

4.2.
Elementwise constant projection

A robust and positivity-preserving alternative is to compute
\mathscr{D}_{h}
as
a piecewise constant function. For each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, define

(69)

\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})|_{K}:=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{E}}w_{q}\frac{S(\xi_{q})}{\rho(\boldsymbol{x})}\psi_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\xi_{q})\,\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

4.3 Lemma

(Error estimate for elementwise constant projection)
.

Let
\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)
and let
\mathscr{D}
be the exact dose
(
62
). Let
\mathscr{D}_{h}
be the elementwise constant
approximation defined above, using a quadrature rule of order
q_{E}\geq 1
in energy. Then

(70)

\left\|\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{D}_{h}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\leq Ch\left\|\psi\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)},

where
C
depends on bounds for
S
and
\rho
but is independent of

h
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as Lemma
4.1
noting
that the piecewise constant structure limits accuracy.
∎

4.4 Remark

(Positivity and stability)
.

The elementwise constant projection preserves positivity at the
level of elements. If
\psi_{h}\geq 0
, given
S,\rho>0
, then

\mathscr{D}_{h}\geq 0
on each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. This follows
directly from the positivity of the integrand and weights in the
quadrature rule. In addition, the projection is stable in
\operatorname{L}^{2}
.

While it lacks higher-order accuracy in space, it is robust and easy
to implement and maybe sufficient for many visualisation and dose
aggregation tasks where smoothness is not critical.

4.4.
Variational inequality projection

To remain consistent with the nodal positivity framework, a natural
approach amenable to higher order approximations is to define the
discrete dose as the solution of a constrained variational inequality
that preserves nonnegativity at nodal degrees of freedom. Let

(71)

K_{\boldsymbol{x}}:=\left\{v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}:\;v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\geq 0\text{ for all nodes }\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}.

Then the variational inequality formulation reads: Find
\mathscr{D}_{h}\in K_{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that

(72)

\int_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})(v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\int_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\sum_{q=1}^{Q_{E}}w_{q}\frac{S(\xi_{q})}{\rho(\boldsymbol{x})}\psi_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\xi_{q})(v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\quad\>\forall\>v_{h}\in K_{\boldsymbol{x}}.

This formulation ensures that the discrete dose satisfies the same
nodal bound-preserving structure as the underlying fluence

\psi_{h}
. In particular, assuming the quadrature is chosen to have
positive weights, the resulting dose
\mathscr{D}_{h}
satisfies
\mathscr{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\geq 0
at all nodes, regardless of the sign of the basis
functions.

integration of Coulomb scattering is examined to quantify its impact
on fluence distributions. Beyond accuracy considerations, we analyse

Example 3: Angular Scattering and Laplace–Beltrami Diffusion

We now investigate the effect of angular scattering on proton
transport by incorporating a nonzero Laplace–Beltrami coefficient

\epsilon>0
into the model. The underlying operator arises from the
linear Boltzmann transport equation in phase space
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\omega},E)\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\Omega_{E}
, with
angular scattering described by

(78)

\mathscr{L}(\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\omega},E))=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime},E)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}-\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\omega},E),

where
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})
is a normalised phase
function. A common choice is the Henyey–Greenstein kernel,

(79)

\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})=\frac{1-g^{2}}{\!\left({1+g^{2}-2g(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})}\right)^{3/2}},

which becomes sharply forward-peaked as
g\to 1^{-}
. In this regime,
the operator admits the angular diffusion (Fokker–Planck)
approximation

(80)

\mathscr{L}(\psi)\approx\epsilon\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi,\qquad\epsilon=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-g),

where
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
the sphere.

We solve the angular diffusion model using the SUPG method described
in §
3
, with adaptive mesh
refinement. Figure

angular diffusion strengths
\epsilon\in\{0,0.005,0.01,0.1\}
. As

\epsilon
increases, angular scattering broadens the fluence and
smooths the Bragg peak, leading to spatially diffuse dose
distributions. These results illustrate the role of angular diffusion
in regulating beam directionality and spatial dose spread.

6.
Conclusion

We have presented a deterministic finite element framework for
modelling proton transport that accounts for both inelastic energy
loss and angular scattering. A key contribution of this work is the
development of a positivity-preserving variational inequality
formulation, which ensures non-negative fluence and dose even on
coarse meshes. This formulation is compatible with streamline-upwind
Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG) stabilisation and enables accurate computation
of physically meaningful observables such as absorbed dose.

The proposed method was validated against analytic benchmarks, with
numerical experiments demonstrating optimal convergence rates,
stability under adaptive mesh refinement and robustness in the
presence of sharp material interfaces. In particular, we showed that
the variational inequality scheme eliminates spurious oscillations
that commonly arise in standard discretisations and is capable of
resolving the Bragg peak and steep energy gradients with high
fidelity. Extensions to angular diffusion models and heterogeneous
geometries confirm the versatility of the method in clinically
relevant scenarios.

This work provides a foundation for future developments in treatment
planning and robustness analysis. Immediate extensions include
dose-weighted quantities such as LET, biological models based on
survival fraction and sensitivity analysis under anatomical
uncertainty. The structure-preserving properties of the scheme also
suggest its potential compatibility with inverse planning and
optimisation frameworks.
