Blood Host-Response mRNA Assays in Adult Acute Care: Diagnostic Performance, Translational Evidence, and the Clinical Implementation Gap
Authors: Xinzhuo Li, Xinyue Tu, Zihao Yan, Yi Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlood host-response messenger RNA (mRNA) assays provide a complementary approach to evaluating suspected acute infection by measuring the patient’s transcriptional response. Fixed multigene assays can distinguish infection from noninfectious inflammation and estimate bacterial or viral likelihood, and several rapid platforms have demonstrated clinically relevant diagnostic performance in emergency and critical-care populations. This critical narrative review synthesizes representative evidence from fixed blood host-response mRNA assays in adult acute care. Structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science Core Collection were completed through August 12, 2026, and were supplemented by citation tracking and assay-specific update searches through September 2, 2026. We examine evidence by assay generation, patient-source independence, prespecified operating points, and workflow characteristics, with particular attention to the distinction between diagnostic performance and clinical utility. Current evidence is strongest for clinical validity and implementation feasibility, whereas evidence that test-guided use safely improves antimicrobial management or patient outcomes remains limited. We therefore propose a staged conceptual framework linking fixed-assay validation and prespecified operating points to representative recruitment, end-to-end workflow, clinician-visible reporting, test-guided clinical evaluation, and prospectively measured safety, patient, and resource outcomes. This framework is intended to organize translational evidence and identify the steps still required before routine clinical utility can be established.Keywords: host response, messenger RNA, acute infection, sepsis, antimicrobial stewardship, clinical utility

Introduction

Adults with acute infection often present before the cause of illness is clear. Fever, leukocyte abnormalities and organ dysfunction can accompany infection, but similar findings occur after surgery, trauma, tissue injury or inflammatory disease. Sepsis adds a further distinction: it describes life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection, not infection itself.1 Clinicians must therefore judge both whether infection is present and how urgently it threatens the patient. In those at high risk, treatment cannot always wait for diagnostic certainty. Current sepsis guidance reflects that urgency while also emphasizing reassessment as new information becomes available.2

Microbiology remains indispensable because it can identify a pathogen and, in many cases, guide susceptibility-based treatment. Yet culture depends on the site and timing of sampling, organism burden, specimen quality and prior antimicrobial exposure. Molecular pathogen tests shorten some of these pathways, but the clinical meaning of a detected organism still depends on the specimen and syndrome. A negative test is equally conditional: it excludes only the targets and sites adequately sampled. Host-response diagnostics address another part of the problem. Rather than asking whether microbial material is present, they ask whether circulating blood cells show a transcriptional pattern associated with infection or its likely cause.3

That approach is attractive in adult acute care. A host-response result may add information when cultures are negative, when the relevant anatomical site is difficult to sample, or when pathogen detection alone does not settle causation. Fixed blood mRNA classifiers have been developed for infection versus noninfectious inflammation and for bacterial or viral classification. Some remain research scores. Others have progressed to targeted RT-qPCR or rapid cartridge and multiplex platforms.3,4 In the United States, SeptiCyte RAPID and TriVerity have FDA 510(k) clearance for distinct intended populations and adjunctive uses.5,6 These clearances establish regulatory authorization for the specified devices; clinical outcome effects require separate study.

Several features complicate interpretation. A familiar assay name may cover generations with different transcripts, algorithms or report formats. Public transcriptomic cohorts can be reused across classifier studies, and a single clinical cohort may support more than one publication. Cross-dataset comparisons have also shown that performance can change when a signature is transported to new populations.7 A long publication list may therefore contain less independent patient evidence than it first appears to contain.

The metric being reported matters just as much. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) summarizes discrimination across thresholds, whereas clinical use depends on a particular cutoff or report band. A low boundary chosen for rule-out and a high boundary chosen for rule-in answer different questions. Their sensitivity and specificity are not a pair. The distinction becomes especially important when a test is proposed to influence antibacterial treatment, because false reassurance and unnecessary exposure carry different clinical costs.

Antimicrobial stewardship provides the clinical context for this translation. The global burden of antimicrobial resistance creates a strong need for better-targeted treatment decisions.8 A clinically useful host-response result must arrive within the decision window, be understood by the treating team, and support a management choice that can be evaluated for safety and benefit. Implementation also depends on assay and instrument costs, laboratory staffing, and whether testing reduces downstream diagnostic or treatment resource use; comparative economic evidence remains limited. Host-response biology and diagnostic performance are already well described.3,4 The central review question is how much independent, generation-specific, clinically actionable evidence supports these assays beyond favorable diagnostic discrimination. We address this question by separating evidence by assay generation and patient source and by interpreting performance at clinically intended operating points rather than by AUROC alone. The review is intended for clinicians, laboratory and diagnostic researchers, and investigators involved in implementation and clinical translation. These strands are brought together in a staged conceptual framework linking analytical and clinical validation to workflow, clinician-visible decisions, and downstream patient and resource outcomes.

Literature Approach

This critical narrative review used a structured literature-identification and study-selection process for fixed multigene blood host-response mRNA assays in adult acute care. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science Core Collection were searched from database inception through August 12, 2026, without language restrictions. After deduplication, two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer. The evidence base was updated through September 2, 2026, using assay-specific searches and backward/forward citation tracking, including targeted checks for new validation, implementation, regulatory, and interventional evidence. Search concepts covered acute infection or sepsis, host-response mRNA or gene expression, blood-derived testing, diagnostic classification, and relevant assay names; full database-specific strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The review focused on predefined multigene blood host-response mRNA assays evaluated in adult emergency, inpatient, perioperative, or critical-care settings. Diagnostic targets were treated separately: infection versus noninfectious inflammation, sepsis-related classification when explicitly defined by the primary study, bacterial infection, viral infection, and bacterial-versus-viral or bacterial/viral/noninfected classification. Evidence was interpreted against the reference standard used in each primary report, including expert adjudication, final clinical diagnosis, and microbiological-clinical composite procedures. Pure discovery-stage signatures, pediatric-only studies, nonblood assays, protein-only tests, and mechanistic studies without direct relevance to assay development, validation, or implementation were outside the principal scope.

Evidence was organized by the exact assay generation evaluated, including transcript composition, platform, algorithm or calibration, and report format. Reports using shared or substantially overlapping patient sources were not treated as independent patient-level confirmation. Two reviewers independently checked key quantitative results against the primary reports, including the analysis population, reference standard, operating threshold or report band, and validation setting. The synthesis remained narrative, with no pooled diagnostic-accuracy estimation, and emphasized generation-specific evidence, clinically relevant operating points, workflow feasibility, and the distinction between clinical validity and clinical utility.

From Host Transcriptional Response to a Clinically Reportable Assay

Biological Basis

Acute infection changes transcription across circulating immune and blood-cell populations. The pattern is not a direct fingerprint of a microorganism. It reflects how the host senses and responds to illness, and selected transcripts can be combined into a score associated with infection, bacterial disease or viral disease. This biological layer may contain information that conventional microbiology cannot provide, particularly when the pathogen is not recovered.3 It also introduces sources of variation. Severity, immune suppression, comorbidity and treatment can alter the measured response, as can the timing of sampling.

Clinical targets differ across assays. SeptiCyte and several research scores were developed around infection or sepsis versus noninfectious systemic inflammation.9,10 Other classifiers estimate bacterial and viral likelihoods or assign a patient to bacterial, viral or noninfected states.11,12 These are related uses of host biology, not equivalent outcomes. A test validated for sepsis discrimination cannot be assumed to classify etiology, and a bacterial-versus-viral score does not by itself exclude infection.

Host response should consequently be viewed as complementary information. It may change the probability of a diagnosis, but its meaning still depends on the syndrome, pretest probability and available microbiological evidence. The value of the result lies in whether it resolves a real decision for the patient in front of the clinician.

From Discovery Signal to Fixed Classifier

A clinically validated assay requires a fixed transcript set, measurement platform, algorithm, calibration strategy, and reportable output. The Sepsis MetaScore, for example, is an 11-gene research score developed across several cohorts, while the Sweeney bacterial-versus-viral classifier uses seven genes; other programs use 29 mRNAs or 45 transcripts.10–13 These architectures should be described by their actual components and platform because the same broad label can conceal materially different tests.

Fixing the transcript set is only part of freezing a model. Preprocessing, coefficients, calibration, and output structure can also affect the result. Reselection, refitting, or outcome-informed recalibration during evaluation constitutes further development, even when the study is described as validation.14

Platform transfer is another empirical step. Moving a frozen score from stored RNA or a research expression platform to targeted RT-qPCR, a cartridge, or a rapid multiplex system changes measurement and workflow and may require recalibration. Portable and rapid host-response systems show how technical deployment can improve usability while still requiring direct evaluation of the implemented assay.15,16

Scores, Probabilities and Report Bands

Fixed assays produce several kinds of output. A continuous score preserves ordering across patients. A probability gives a more familiar scale, although its calibration may shift when disease prevalence and case mix change. Some tests use categories such as very low, low, moderate, high and very high. In an emergency-department pilot, 66 adults were enrolled and 60 had evaluable assay results. Samples reached the investigational Myrna system within one hour of collection. The integrated assay then generated three scores and five interpretation bands in approximately 30 minutes. Patients were managed according to standard care.17 The study established integrated technical feasibility in that setting; clinical impact requires clinician-visible, test-guided evaluation.

The operating point sits between the statistical output and the clinical interpretation. AUROC describes discrimination over many possible cutoffs. It does not tell a clinician what happens at the cutoff used to withhold, start or continue treatment. Prediction-model methodology likewise separates discrimination from calibration and clinical usefulness.18 Likelihood ratios can help translate a tested category from pretest to post-test probability, but only when they correspond to the actual boundary and population.19

Banded tests make the asymmetry visible. A low-probability boundary may be set to retain sensitivity and support rule-out, while a high-probability boundary favors specificity and supports rule-in. The middle of the distribution preserves uncertainty. IMX-BVN-1 was evaluated with separate bacterial and viral scores and distinct rule-in and rule-out regions.20 Later rapid systems also use category-based reporting.17,21 These outputs may fit clinical reasoning better than a forced binary result, provided each band has a prespecified meaning and is not recombined into a performance measure the study never tested.

Assay Lineage and Generation

Development programs can retain a common biological aim while changing the test itself. An assay lineage captures that developmental continuity, whereas an assay generation is the specific transcript set, measurement platform, algorithm or calibration, and report format evaluated together. SeptiCyte illustrates the distinction. SeptiCyte LAB is a four-gene laboratory RT-qPCR assay developed and validated in critically ill patients; SeptiCyte RAPID uses two host mRNAs on a cartridge-based RT-qPCR platform and reports a rapid SeptiScore.5,9,22–24 The names are related, but the tested architectures are materially different.

The 29-mRNA program has similar versioning. IMX-BVN-1 was developed as a bacterial, viral and noninfected research classifier, followed by versioned 29-mRNA implementations in acute-care cohorts.11,25,26 These studies span research expression platforms and evolving output formats. The 2025 pivotal report notes that TriVerity was formerly called HostDx or InSep. The cleared TriVerity system is the later locked Myrna device implementation, with its own measurement process, fine-tuned classifiers, three clinical scores and banded output.6,21

A separate respiratory program moved from an 87-transcript RT-PCR classifier, validated for bacterial, viral and noninfectious respiratory illness, to a later 45-transcript rapid implementation with its own clinical evaluation.12,27,28 These examples show why the clinically meaningful unit is the exact generation that produced the result: its transcripts, platform, algorithm, report format and intended population.

Current Clinical Evidence

Infection versus Noninfectious Inflammation

The distinction between infection and sterile inflammation remains the main clinical use for several fixed host-response assays. The four-gene SeptiCyte LAB classifier was developed in critically ill adults and then evaluated in independent and multicenter ICU cohorts.9,22 In the 447-patient multicenter evaluation, consensus-adjudicated analysis included 410 patients and produced an AUROC of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.81–0.89).22 Further studies examined adults with acute respiratory failure and patients after high-risk gastrointestinal surgery, settings in which noninfectious inflammation can closely resemble infection.30,31 These studies support a reproducible association between the SeptiScore and adjudicated infection status. They also show why the reference diagnosis and the selected operating point matter. A score that separates groups reasonably well may still leave a wide trade-off between missed infection and false-positive classification at a particular cutoff.

SeptiCyte RAPID shortened and simplified testing in the two-gene generation. A multicenter study reported validation against noninfectious systemic inflammation, and more recent prospective evaluations examined its performance in patients considered septic or critically ill at presentation.23,24,32 These publications extend generation-specific clinical experience with rapid testing. Their principal contribution is clinical validity; the effect of releasing the result on treatment remains a separate clinical question.

Research classifiers provide a broader view of transportability. The 11-gene Sepsis MetaScore was derived from several transcriptomic cohorts and has since been tested in a prospective severe-trauma cohort, where sterile inflammation is a meaningful alternative diagnosis.10,29 Previously specified scores have also been compared head to head in critically ill adults.33 FAIM3:PLAC8 addressed a narrower ICU question involving community-acquired pneumonia and sepsis.34 The range of populations is scientifically useful. It also prevents a simple ranking of assays by reported AUROC, because each result reflects a different patient spectrum, platform and definition of the target condition.

Bacterial and Viral Classification

Etiologic classification has generated a second major group of fixed assays. The 29-mRNA IMX-BVN-1 model was developed across multiple cohorts and then assessed without retraining in new data.11 Prospective emergency-department studies evaluated related 29-mRNA implementations for bacterial and viral likelihoods, including independent acute-care and multisite suspected-influenza cohorts.20,25,26,35 These reports consistently treat bacterial and viral outputs as separate scores, with different regions for high-sensitivity and high-specificity interpretation.

TriVerity represents a later rapid implementation. SEPSIS-SHIELD enrolled 1441 adults across 22 emergency departments; 1222 had valid TriVerity results, of whom 729 with consensus Yes/No adjudications constituted the primary diagnostic analysis population. In this population, the bacterial and viral scores achieved AUROCs of 0.83 (80% CI, 0.81–0.85) and 0.91 (80% CI, 0.89–0.93), respectively. The Very High bands provided specificities of 95.5% for bacterial and 98.6% for viral infection, whereas the Very Low bands provided sensitivities of 97.2% and 95.5%, respectively.21 A post hoc analysis of prospectively collected Acutelines biobank samples included 467 adults; the locked scores were calculated from RNA sequencing rather than real-time testing on the Myrna device.36 Together, these studies show progression from device-generation validation to broader case-mix assessment. SEPSIS-SHIELD management remained standard care and its antibiotic-effect analysis was post hoc, so test-guided clinical utility remains unestablished.

Other programs illustrate how etiologic classifiers can be translated to faster platforms when implementation-specific evidence is generated. A 45-transcript host-response test was developed and independently evaluated on a rapid system with an analytical output reported at about 45 minutes.12 Prospective multicenter work later evaluated four likelihood groups in febrile acute respiratory illness, although that cohort included children as well as adults.28 Direct comparison of two rapid host-response tests in adults with acute respiratory infection further demonstrates that tests aimed at the same question may behave differently.16

Smaller and software-based classifiers remain relevant, especially as sources of biological and methodological insight. Sweeney7 is a fixed seven-gene bacterial-versus-viral research score, and BoVI uses eight genes across diverse populations.13,37 InfectDiagno applies a rank-based ensemble to infection and bacterial or viral classification and remains a software-based implementation without routine rapid-platform deployment.38 Disease-specific classifiers have also distinguished septicemic melioidosis from other bacterial sepsis and enteric fever from other febrile illnesses.39,40 Their evidence remains specific to the disease comparisons evaluated, with limited direct transfer to the broader emergency-department differential.

Independent Clinical Validation

Independent clinical validation asks whether a locked assay retains performance in patients who contributed nothing to transcript selection, model fitting or calibration. The specification should be fixed before outcomes in the new cohort are examined. A held-out subset remains useful, but a genuinely external setting adds new recruitment, workflow and case mix. Cross-dataset comparisons of bacterial and viral signatures show the value of direct transport testing across new populations.7

Publication count is most informative when the underlying patient sources are also clear. Public datasets enable reproducible benchmarking and head-to-head comparison, while repeated use of the same samples contributes additional analyses rather than additional patient-level validation.41 A platform study or secondary analysis can still answer a legitimate question; its contribution is clearest when the reader can identify what changed: population, platform, outcome or analysis.

Clinical spectrum provides another form of independence. Validation in severe trauma, immunocompromised hosts, organ transplantation or COVID-19 tests fixed classifiers in distinct biological contexts.29,42–44 These studies show where an assay has actually been challenged rather than supplying a universal estimate of performance. Dedicated validation is especially important in immunocompromised populations. Prior antimicrobial exposure is common in acute care, but generation-specific effects on host-response performance remain incompletely characterized.3

A persuasive external validation therefore combines a locked model, a clearly identified assay generation and patients who add genuinely new clinical information. Recruitment setting and case mix help determine how far the result can travel. Changes in platform, calibration or category boundaries define a new validation question rather than additional confirmation of an unchanged test.

Interpreting Diagnostic Performance for Clinical Use

Independent Evidence and Publication Volume

Publication volume can give a misleading sense of replication. A successful host-response signature may be reported first as a discovery model, then evaluated on a targeted platform, examined in a subgroup and compared with other scores. Each article can add knowledge, even when the participants overlap. The key distinction is between additional analysis of an existing cohort and genuinely new patient-level validation.

Public transcriptomic cohorts make this issue particularly visible. They allow a fixed score to be tested by investigators outside the original group and support direct comparisons under common conditions.7,41 At the same time, several papers may depend on the same patients. A secondary analysis can demonstrate reproducibility of code or reveal a useful subgroup pattern, but it does not expand the number of independent clinical populations.

For a narrative interpretation, the relevant question is simple: what is the patient source behind the result? Trial identifiers, accession numbers, recruitment dates and baseline characteristics often provide the answer. Evidence becomes more persuasive when a frozen assay retains performance across genuinely separate populations, particularly when recruitment and reference-standard procedures also differ.

Assay Generation and Platform Transfer

Assay evolution is clinically desirable because reducing a signature, automating extraction or moving to a cartridge may make urgent testing practical. Those changes can alter measurement scale, preprocessing, calibration, analytical error and report output. Transcript abundance may be measured differently, and the same biological target may be translated into a new categorical report. The interpretive consequence is simple: evidence supports the generation that was actually evaluated.

SeptiCyte LAB and SeptiCyte RAPID illustrate the point directly.5,9 They address a related diagnostic problem and belong to one broad lineage, but they use different transcript sets and platforms. Evidence from the four-gene laboratory assay therefore supports that generation, while the two-gene rapid assay requires its own data. The 87-transcript and 45-transcript respiratory classifiers provide another example of a shared objective followed by a material change in architecture.12,27

Portable-system evaluations show that platform transfer can succeed and can improve practical usability.15 Performance should not be transferred automatically across materially different assay generations unless analytical or clinical equivalence has been demonstrated. A development lineage helps explain technological progression; evidence remains attached to the implementation actually tested.

From AUROC to Clinically Relevant Operating Points

For clinical interpretation, AUROC is most useful when reported beside the performance and diagnostic yield of the intended cutoff or report band. The operating point shows the trade-off at the boundary where a decision is made.

Continuous-score evaluations of SeptiCyte LAB established discrimination in critically ill populations, while clinical interpretation depended on the chosen SeptiScore boundary.22,30 Banded 29-mRNA assays make the decision structure more explicit because the same underlying score can support several intended interpretations.20,21

A 2026 secondary analysis pooled prospectively collected SeptiCyte RAPID data from 889 critically ill patients recruited across 19 hospitals and characterized band-specific likelihood ratios. Median LR+ was 6.97 in Band 4, whereas median LR− was 0.085 in Band 1.45 Because the analysis combined existing clinical cohorts, it refines interpretation of SeptiScore operating regions rather than adding independent patient-level validation.

A clinically useful account therefore pairs global discrimination with performance at the intended operating point. Sensitivity and specificity should come from the same boundary and analysis population. Predictive values belong to the prevalence and setting in which they were observed, and probabilistic models also require attention to calibration.18

Rule-in, Rule-Out, and Threshold Provenance

Rule-in specificity and rule-out sensitivity from different boundaries are not a paired operating point. In the IMX-BVN-1 literature, high-score regions supported specific rule-in interpretation and low-score regions supported sensitive rule-out interpretation.20 Independent acute-care evaluation preserved the same separation.26 TriVerity later expressed related probability information in five bands.17,21 Combining metrics across boundaries would describe a threshold that was never tested.

Threshold provenance adds another layer. A cutoff chosen before the validation outcomes are known provides a direct test of a proposed clinical rule. A cutoff selected from the same dataset, perhaps to maximize the Youden index, remains useful for development but is more vulnerable to optimism. The eight-gene BoVI study, for example, included study-derived Youden cutoffs; these should not be presented as prespecified clinical operating points.37 Exploratory cutoffs remain useful during development but require prospective confirmation.

Target Definitions, Reference Standards, and Comparator Spectrum

The reference diagnosis for acute infection is rarely perfect. Microbiological confirmation is specific in many settings but may select patients with higher organism burden or accessible infection sites. Expert adjudication can incorporate imaging, clinical course and culture-negative infection, yet it introduces judgment. The strongest studies describe the information available to adjudicators, whether they were blinded to the index test and how disagreements or uncertain cases were handled.46,47

Labels also require care. Infection and sepsis are not synonyms.1 Bacterial-versus-viral classification differs from bacterial-versus-all-other illness, especially when coinfection, uncertain diagnoses and noninfectious controls are present. A three-class model may distribute error differently from two separate one-versus-rest scores. These choices define the denominator and the clinical question before any performance statistic is calculated.

Comparator spectrum can alter the apparent result. Severe bacterial infection versus healthy controls is a useful biological contrast but a poor model of most emergency decisions. Adults with postoperative inflammation, trauma, immunosuppression or organ failure present harder and more relevant alternatives. Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity have been shown to vary with prevalence and case mix across settings, reflecting spectrum and related effects rather than a universal property of a test.48 Performance should therefore travel with its population and reference standard.

Usable Results, Denominators, and Transparent Reporting

Clinical usefulness begins with a usable result. RNA quality failure, inadequate sampling, processing delay or instrument error may prevent a report, while an intermediate band communicates uncertainty rather than technical failure. An intermediate category can still inform care, but it has a different meaning from analytical failure or a definitive low or high band. All remain part of the population offered testing and contribute to real-world diagnostic yield.

Transparent reporting keeps the clinical pathway visible. STARD recommends describing participant flow, thresholds, indeterminate results and estimates with uncertainty.46,47 For a named operating point, readers need the total tested population, the valid-result denominator and the target definition. Sensitivity and specificity should refer to one boundary, and true-positive, false-positive, false-negative and true-negative counts make precision and small-sample effects easier to judge.

These details serve a bedside question: if the assay is ordered for the next patient, how often will an interpretable result be available in time and what will that category mean? Denominators, threshold provenance, invalid results and intermediate categories place the AUROC in that practical context.46,47

From Diagnostic Accuracy to Clinical Workflow

End-to-End Turnaround

In acute care, speed is clinically meaningful when a result returns before a decision closes. Analytical run time starts after a specimen enters the assay process. End-to-end turnaround begins with blood collection and includes transport, accessioning, sample preparation, instrument access, quality control, result verification and release to the clinical record. A rapid analytical run and a timely clinical result are therefore related but distinct measures, and both should be matched to the intended decision window.

Recent platforms have shortened the analytical phase substantially. A rapid 45-transcript bacterial or viral test was developed for an automated molecular platform, the two-gene SeptiCyte RAPID cartridge produces a result on a different rapid system, and newer 29-mRNA devices have been evaluated in emergency-department workflows.5,12,17 These studies establish technical feasibility for the implemented platforms. Their reported times belong to the generation and workflow actually tested, including the extraction process, instrument and report structure used in that study.

For acute care, the most informative measure is often the proportion of verified results available before a prespecified decision point. Median sample-to-result time can conceal delays during nights, weekends or periods of high laboratory demand, so the distribution of turnaround is often more informative than a single summary value. Timing should therefore be linked to the intended use, such as the initial antibacterial decision, early reassessment or de-escalation. The Acutelines analysis broadened evidence across a quasi-unselected emergency-department case mix; real-time device deployment and clinician-visible reporting remain separate workflow endpoints for prospective study.36

Pre-Analytical and Operational Barriers

Blood host-response testing begins before the tube reaches the analyzer. The transcriptional signal may vary with the timing of sampling, prior treatment, illness duration, immune state, and concurrent inflammatory conditions. Specimen volume, RNA preservation, transport temperature, processing requirements, and failed quality-control checks can further affect whether a result is obtained. Reviews of the field have consequently emphasized that platform development and clinical implementation are connected but separate problems.3

Retrospective studies using archived blood remain valuable for classifier development and clinical validation, often under controlled processing conditions. Samples may be selected after the clinical episode, tested in batches and handled by staff who are not working to a treatment deadline. Routine emergency and intensive-care workflows add queues, repeat testing, maintenance, after-hours staffing, verification and result transmission. A cartridge system can remove several laboratory steps, while local workflow still determines whether a technically rapid assay becomes clinically timely.

Routine feasibility also depends on diagnostic yield. Patients with an inadequate specimen, analytical failure, missing output or intermediate report remain part of the population offered testing. Their experience affects both availability and real-world turnaround. STARD treats indeterminate results, missing data, thresholds and participant flow as essential elements of transparent diagnostic reporting.46,47 Implementation studies can extend this with repeat-test frequency, time lost to repeats, instrument downtime and the percentage of patients receiving an interpretable result within the intended window.

Clinician-Visible Results

Clinical effect begins after assay completion. Between a generated result and a management action lie authorization, transmission to the electronic record, alerting and interpretation by a clinician who can still respond. A result can therefore be analytically complete yet clinically late. These workflow steps are distinct from the diagnostic discrimination captured by AUROC and need their own implementation endpoints.

The report itself also matters. Probability bands or categorical outputs can make a score easier to use during a crowded shift when each boundary has a stated meaning and is linked to the evaluated population. Clinicians need to know whether a low bacterial score supports rule-out, whether a high score provides rule-in evidence and how intermediate findings are handled. The same numeric result can carry different weight when severity, microbiology and pretest probability differ. A comparison with clinician judgment establishes relative classification performance; clinician-visible studies test whether that information changes care.51

Experience with rapid pathogen diagnostics offers a useful implementation lesson. Benefits are more likely when laboratories and antimicrobial-stewardship teams design the testing pathway together, define who is tested and provide interpretation at the time of reporting.52,53 Host-response implementation can use the same principles for ordering, display, responsibility and follow-up. Studies can record when the result became visible, who viewed it, whether interpretation support was available, whether the intended meaning was understood and whether a relevant management choice was still pending.

From Feasibility to Clinical Utility

Analytical validity asks whether the molecular signal is measured reliably. Clinical validity asks whether the fixed assay distinguishes the stated diagnostic groups. Workflow evaluation then asks whether routine patients receive an interpretable result in time. Clinical utility begins only when access to that result is linked to an observed change in care or to a downstream outcome.

The remaining translational question is whether a timely, interpretable result changes care and downstream outcomes. Diagnostic-test trials that follow this complete test-treatment pathway remain uncommon, and descriptions of how results lead to treatment are often incomplete.49,50 Figure 1 therefore ends with prospective evaluation of actions and outcomes.

Conventional protein biomarkers remain important comparators. C-reactive protein is widely available but is nonspecific for infection, while procalcitonin has a substantially larger interventional evidence base for antibiotic-guided care.54 In one prospective respiratory cohort, the rapid HR-B/V test showed higher diagnostic discrimination than procalcitonin, but that study-level comparison does not establish superior clinical utility across settings.28 Some mRNA assays provide separate bacterial and viral likelihoods, but require dedicated molecular platforms and currently lack comparable test-guided outcome evidence.2,3,21 Published head-to-head economic evaluations comparing mRNA assays with CRP, procalcitonin, or rapid pathogen tests remain sparse, so cost-effectiveness cannot be inferred from diagnostic performance alone.

Potential Implications for Antimicrobial Decision-Making and Diagnostic Stewardship

Potential Relevance to Early Antibacterial Decisions

The first antibacterial decision is made under unequal risks. Delayed treatment is hazardous in severe bacterial infection, while broad empirical use exposes many patients without bacterial disease to unnecessary therapy. Antimicrobial stewardship aims to optimize appropriate initiation, selection, and duration of treatment while protecting patient safety. Sepsis guidance prioritizes prompt antimicrobials for patients with septic shock or probable or definite sepsis, while allowing a time-limited rapid investigation when sepsis is possible without shock and infection remains uncertain.2 The 2026 guidance also concluded that evidence is insufficient to make a recommendation regarding novel rapid host-response diagnostics.2 The global burden of antimicrobial resistance adds a strong reason to improve targeting, but it does not lower the safety standard for withholding treatment.8

At presentation, a host-response assay provides a different type of diagnostic information. It estimates whether the patient’s transcriptional response is compatible with bacterial infection, viral infection or another state rather than detecting an organism directly. Prospective studies of fixed 29-mRNA classifiers in emergency-department patients demonstrate bacterial and viral likelihood reporting where early decisions occur.25,55 Its clinical role is adjunctive to bedside assessment and microbiology.

The proposed action determines the required performance. A high-probability bacterial category may strengthen a rule-in judgment, whereas withholding antibiotics demands a sensitive rule-out boundary and careful follow-up. Clinical severity, infection site, immune status, and previous treatment remain important even when a score is available. In a patient with shock or rapidly progressive organ dysfunction, an unevaluated assay-guided delay would be difficult to justify. Early use should therefore be embedded in a pathway that states who is eligible, when blood is drawn, how discordant findings are handled, and who retains responsibility for treatment.

Potential Relevance to Reassessment and de-Escalation

Reassessment after empirical therapy may provide a particularly practical decision window. Once treatment has begun, clinicians revisit the working diagnosis as cultures, imaging, bedside trajectory and other laboratory results become available. A host-response result delivered during this interval may contribute additional diagnostic information, but whether it can safely support narrowing or discontinuation of antibacterial therapy remains a question for prospective test-guided evaluation. Any such strategy requires prespecified eligibility criteria, integration with microbiology and clinical trajectory, and an explicit rescue pathway when uncertainty or deterioration persists.

Low-probability report categories are attractive for this purpose, but the score must be interpreted at its own boundary. Validation studies of IMX-BVN-1 and related fixed classifiers report distinct bacterial and viral likelihood regions rather than a single universal cutoff.20,26 Sensitivity at a low bacterial boundary cannot be paired with specificity from a separate high bacterial boundary to describe one operating point. The prevalence and clinical spectrum also matter. Accuracy can change across settings because the patients, disease severity, and competing diagnoses differ.48

Reassessment is contextual. A low bacterial likelihood accompanied by negative microbiology and clinical stability may carry different weight from the same result in a deteriorating immunocompromised patient. Coinfection, prior antimicrobial exposure, immune suppression, and noninfectious inflammatory states may also alter the interpretation of a host-response signal. Dedicated validation in immunocompromised hosts illustrates the value of case-mix-specific evidence.44 Prospective test-guided studies should explicitly measure false-negative classifications, inappropriate withholding or discontinuation, antibiotic reinitiation, delayed appropriate therapy, clinical deterioration, and other safety outcomes.

Combining Host-Response and Pathogen-Directed Testing

Host-response and pathogen-directed assays answer different questions. Microbiology seeks the organism and may provide susceptibility or resistance information. Host transcription describes the patient’s biological response. A negative culture can follow prior antibiotics, low organism burden, or sampling from the wrong site. Conversely, detection of microbial nucleic acid does not always establish that the organism is causing the current syndrome.

Used together, the two approaches may resolve uncertainty that either leaves behind. A bacterial-compatible host response could increase concern when pathogen testing is unrevealing, while a low bacterial likelihood might prompt closer examination of a positive molecular result that could reflect colonization or prolonged shedding. Their information is therefore complementary rather than redundant. This integrated interpretation remains probabilistic. Host-response classifiers describe host state; species identification, susceptibility and infection-control investigation remain functions of microbiology and clinical assessment.

Diagnostic stewardship provides the appropriate frame. Testing should be selected for a defined patient and timed to a decision, with interpretation anchored to the clinical question and other available evidence.53 Studies of integrated strategies can compare host-response information added to usual care and report discordant host-pathogen patterns, which are likely to reveal both the value and the limits of the combined pathway.

From Diagnostic Information to Stewardship Benefit

Interventional evaluation is now beginning. SEPSIS-SHIELD established observational device-generation diagnostic evidence, and the Acutelines analysis broadened case-mix evidence for the locked classifier using post hoc RNA-seq-derived scores.21,36 TIMED (NCT06637904) is prospectively evaluating clinician-visible TriVerity-guided management in emergency-department patients, including antimicrobial and other management outcomes.56 Because the study is still recruiting, clinical utility remains to be established. This progression from observational accuracy to test-guided evaluation marks the next evidence milestone for the field.

Experience from other diagnostic fields provides a trial-design precedent. Procalcitonin-guided algorithms have been evaluated prospectively with antibiotic exposure and safety outcomes, while rapid bloodstream-infection diagnostics have shown greater value when coupled with active stewardship support.54,57 They provide useful precedents for evaluating result delivery, stewardship response and safety, without supplying efficacy evidence for mRNA assays.

A future host-response trial should start with one prespecified decision and follow the result through interpretation, management, safety, and downstream outcomes.

A Staged Framework for Clinical Translation

Based on recurring gaps in assay validation, operating-point reporting, workflow, and clinical impact, we propose a staged conceptual framework for clinical translation (Figure 2). It is intended as a domain-specific synthesis, not a validated instrument. STARD and TRIPOD inform the reporting of diagnostic accuracy and multivariable prediction, while test-treatment pathway and implementation-outcomes frameworks emphasize links between results, management, patient outcomes, feasibility, adoption, implementation cost, and sustainability.14,46,49,50,58 The stages below adapt these principles to fixed host-response mRNA assays rather than replace the source frameworks.

Intended Use and Representative Populations

Intended use should be specified before recruitment, including the clinical setting, target population, diagnostic question, sampling time, report interpretation, and the decision the test is expected to inform. These elements determine the relevant operating point, comparator, timing requirement, and safety endpoints; a rule-out application for withholding antibiotics, for example, carries a different error cost from a high-specificity rule-in use.

Validation is most informative when recruitment reflects the uncertainty encountered in practice. Emergency and critical-care populations include overlapping alternatives, variable severity, prior antimicrobial exposure, immune dysfunction, and patients who remain microbiologically or clinically indeterminate. Prospective consecutive enrollment, transparent exclusions, and independent adjudication therefore provide a stronger test of transportability than highly separated case-control contrasts, and distinct care settings should not be treated as interchangeable adult populations.48

Fixed Assays, Operating Points, and Transparent Reporting

External validation is meaningful only when the assay being tested is fixed in advance: transcript set, platform, preprocessing, coefficients or algorithm, calibration, output format, and intended boundaries. Reselection, retraining, or outcome-informed recalibration creates a modified classifier that requires another independent evaluation. TRIPOD and STARD provide complementary reporting foundations for the model and the clinical accuracy study.14,46

Each intended boundary should likewise be prespecified and reported with confidence intervals, diagnostic-cell counts, and a denominator that retains invalid or intermediate results where appropriate.18,19,47 Reporting the assay generation alongside these estimates keeps performance linked to the transcript composition, platform, calibration, and report format actually tested.5,9

Workflow and Clinician Visibility

Workflow evaluation should measure collection-to-visibility time and the proportion of results delivered within the intended decision window. Specimen failures, repeats, after-hours access, staffing, instrument availability, and information-system transmission can create delays that analytical run time does not capture. The Myrna pilot demonstrated integrated on-device feasibility in 60 evaluable participants from 66 enrolled,17 whereas Acutelines broadened case-mix evidence using post hoc RNA-seq-derived scores.36 Full clinician-visible workflow remains a separate implementation endpoint.

It also matters who receives the result, when it is seen, and whether a relevant decision is still open. Intermediate categories may need different presentation or support from clear low-probability results, and clinician uptake can distinguish late or unused results from deliberate disagreement with the test. Diagnostic and antimicrobial stewardship teams can help shape these pathways.52 Figure 2 therefore places clinician visibility between clinical validation and test-guided evaluation.

Test-Guided Decisions and Downstream Outcomes

Once results can reach clinicians on time, comparative studies should test whether adding host-response information to usual care changes a prespecified management decision within the intended window. Depending on maturity and setting, this may require a randomized design or a pragmatic controlled implementation, with clinician uptake recorded so that nonuse, late use, and deliberate disagreement can be distinguished.

The primary endpoint should follow the intended use. Early-decision trials might evaluate initiation or withholding of antibacterial therapy, whereas reassessment studies might examine narrowing, discontinuation, additional diagnostics, or escalation of care. These management endpoints should be paired with prespecified safety outcomes such as missed serious bacterial infection, delayed rescue therapy, and clinical deterioration. Downstream evaluation can then consider antibiotic exposure, adverse drug events, length of stay, readmission, mortality, and resource use; economic analyses should include assay and implementation costs and downstream utilization.50

Scope and Limitations of This Review

This critical narrative review focuses on clinically consequential features of fixed blood host-response mRNA assays rather than exhaustive study mapping. Formal risk-of-bias judgments and certainty-of-evidence grading are not presented, and no pooled diagnostic-accuracy analysis was performed. Comparative conclusions should therefore be read in light of study design, population, reference standard, operating point, validation setting, and the limitations summarized in the text and tables. The adult acute-care focus excludes pediatric, nonblood, protein-only, and most discovery-stage signatures except where they clarify assay development or transportability. Formal database searches ended August 12, 2026; targeted assay-specific, citation, regulatory, and emerging-study updates continued through September 2, 2026. Later clearances, trial results, or platform changes may alter the evidence landscape.

Conclusions

The field has progressed from transcriptomic discovery to fixed and increasingly rapid blood mRNA platforms for adult acute care. Generation-specific studies support clinical validity for several infection-versus-inflammation and bacterial or viral classification tasks, and rapid platforms increasingly support implementation feasibility. Evidence for clinical utility remains substantially less mature: current studies do not yet establish that clinician-visible, test-guided use safely improves antimicrobial management or patient outcomes.

The proposed staged conceptual framework separates analytical validity, clinical validity, implementation feasibility, test-guided clinical utility, and downstream patient or resource outcomes. It links these stages to clear intended use, representative enrollment, prespecified operating points, transparent diagnostic yield, end-to-end workflow and clinician-visible reporting. Prospective comparative studies are now needed to determine whether host-response information changes antimicrobial decisions safely and whether those changes translate into meaningful patient or resource benefits.
