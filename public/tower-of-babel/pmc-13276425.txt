Metabolic profile association with white matter hyperintensity in patients with mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Armin Ariaei, Raziyeh Zamiri, Amirabbas Ghasemi, Bahareh Rezayani, Shayesteh Rezayani, Aylar Ezatpour, Narges Farshidi, Elham Asghari Sheikhi, Ali Roghanian, for the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, for the Alzheimer's Disease Metabolomics Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain vasculopathy, including cerebral white matter hyperintensity (WMH), is highly associated with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology. Metabolite levels provide clues to illuminate the mechanism of WMH in MCI. Therefore, the current study investigated the association between metabolite profile and WMH in MCI. Metabolite profiles and WMH data of MCI patients with three consecutive years of follow-up were retrospectively extracted from the ADNI database. Multivariate logistic regression and mixed model regression adjusted for age, gender, education, and ADAS-Cog13 were used to evaluate the association between metabolite levels and WMH. A total number of 189 subjects were included in this study, allocated into three groups of healthy individuals (n = 67), mild cognitive impairment (n = 112), and AD (n = 10). Based on the multivariate logistic regression, methionine, deoxycholic acid, trimethylamine N-oxide, glutamic acid, and alanine, palmitoylethanolamide, and arachidonic acid positively correlated with WMH (OR:4.014 [95% CI: 1.329–12.128]; OR: 1.272 [95% CI: 1.076–1.504]; OR: 1.839 [95% CI: 1.007–1.390]; OR: 8.474 [95% CI: 2.730–26.300]; OR: 3.532 [95% CI: 1.544–8.076]; OR: 2.486 [95% CI: 1.152–5.364]; OR:2.110 [95% CI: 1.003–4.440], respectively), while tyrosine, proline, histidine, and beta-aminobutyric acid negatively correlated with WMH (OR:0.287 [95% CI:0.092–0.894]; OR:0.381 [95% CI:0.162–0.894]; OR:0.240 [95% CI:0.059–0.982]; OR: 0.598 [95% CI: 0.382–0.939], respectively). The association of metabolite levels with WMH in the MCI group is complex. One of the suggested mechanisms is amino acid overconsumption in the MCI group, resulting in decreasing their detected level in the serum while accumulation of their metabolites in the brain increases the risk of WMH.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Metabolite profile, White matter hyperintensity, Liquid chromatography

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most common cause of dementia (Usefi et al., 2024, Rustamzadeh et al., 2024), is characterized by gradual memory loss disrupting the person's daily life (Ariaei and Ramezani, 2023, Eratne et al., 2018, Mohammadi et al., 2024, Shah-Abadi et al., 2023). The prevalence of AD and other dementias is predicted to be 152 million by 2050, indicating the socioeconomic burden of rehabilitation and care programs (Nichols et al., 2022).

A comprehensive autopsy study indicated that 80% of individuals diagnosed with Alzheimer's Disease (AD) exhibited evidence of vascular pathology within the brain. This included cortical infarcts, lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, multiple microinfarcts, and dysfunction of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), all of which are suggestive of small vessel disease (SVD), intracranial atherosclerosis, arteriolosclerosis, perivascular spacing, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) (Govindpani et al., 2019, Toledo et al., 2013). Cerebral white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are characterized by diffuse areas exhibiting high signal intensity on T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, which are recognized as a defining feature of human cerebral SVD (Gao et al., 2022). Moreover, demyelination, loss of oligodendrocytes, and axonal damage could be classified as WMH (Dean et al., 2017, Gouw et al., 2011). In addition, a significant association between WMH and AD severity has been reported, especially for parietal, temporal, and occipital WMH (Garnier-Crussard et al., 2022). Correspondingly, there is a shred of new evidence suggesting that posterior WMH, particularly splenium of the corpus callosum (S-CC) WMH, are the core features of AD occurring relatively early in the disease progression (Garnier-Crussard, 2022).

Metabolomics is a technique used to analyze biological samples containing metabolites, including amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleotides, etc., to provide a bridge to correlate biological processes with genomics and proteomics (Nasab et al., 2023). Some diseases related to metabolite dysregulation, for instance, type 2 diabetes, increase the risk of AD, which may suggest a significant role of metabolites in disease onset and progression (Horgusluoglu et al., 2022). Correspondingly, a lower concentration of valine, glutamate, aspartate, phenylalanine, and D-glutamate has been described in AD (Chang et al., 2021, Corso et al., 2017, González-Domínguez et al., 2015, Li et al., 2018, Polis and Samson, 2020, Siddik et al., 2022, Toledo et al., 2017). Besides, dysregulation of lipids, especially cholesterol metabolism, higher levels of ceramide and saturated fatty acid were associated with AD incidence (Fonteh et al., 2020, Janicki and Schupf, 2010, Kao et al., 2020, Kousparou et al., 2023, Reveglia et al., 2023, Sánchez-Alegría and Arias, 2023, Torrens-Mas et al., 2020, Yang et al., 2011). Eventually, microbial metabolites detected in the blood, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), are another important factor, and their elevation is correlated to cognitive decline (Connell et al., 2022, Kalecký et al., 2022, Vogt et al., 2018).

Among the multiple biomarkers suggested in the diagnosis of AD, neuroimaging techniques were highlighted as a valuable diagnostic marker, including fluorine 18 (18F) fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET), Aβ and tau PET, conventional MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, and 1H-MR spectroscopy (Ariaei et al., 2024; Mohammadi et al., 2024). The association of the metabolite level with neuroimaging findings was mentioned in the previous studies. One of the studies mentioned the combination of T1-weighted images and metabolite levels measured by MR spectroscopy as an efficient model in the classification of AD (Wang et al., 2020). Moreover, the application of MRI-based deuterium (2H) metabolic imaging (DMI) specified an oxidative glucose metabolism impairment in the AD group (Khan et al., 2024). Another study highlighted the association of phosphatidylcholines with SVD features and WMH in AD (Li et al., 2019).

Despite the previous report of metabolite levels association with neuroimaging findings in AD patients, since in the previous studies the analyses were limited to a few metabolites without longitudinal data, the relation between metabolite profiles and WMH in cognitive impairment was not extensively elaborated. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the correlation between the blood metabolite profile and WMH in MCI patients over time.

Methods

Subjects

This is a retrospective study with a longitudinal design. Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer’s disease (AD).

The participant selection method is summarized in Fig. 1. A total number of 1524 and 1822 participants from ADNI−1, ADNI-GO, and ADNI−2 underwent metabolite profile assessment and whole-brain MRI, respectively. Among these, 842 individuals completed both metabolite profile and MRI assessment. Since the patients’ metabolite levels and the value of WMH were not the same over time, we excluded participants whose the difference between the date of metabolite profile assessment and MRI was higher than one-year (n = 210). In addition, since our study aimed to evaluate patients over time, those participants who took metabolite profile and MRI for less than three consecutive years or without follow-up data were excluded (n = 443). Therefore, a total number of 189 participants who underwent metabolite profile assessment and MRI in three consecutive years were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Measurement of white matter hyperintensity (WMH)

The methodology employed for quantifying WMH in this study was based on a Bayesian segmentation approach applied to high-resolution 3D T1 and FLAIR sequences. In summary, before measurement, an automated atlas-based method was employed to exclude non-brain structures from 3D T1-weighted images. The FLAIR image was subjected to an affine transformation to the 3D T1 image using the FLIRT method available in the FSL toolbox. Error estimation was performed using the correlation ratio method. To correct potential inhomogeneities in the 3D T1 image, interleaved bias estimation and B-Spline deformation with a template were employed (Fletcher et al., 2012). This approach involves an iterative process to refine the alignment of a B-spline-based deformation model, adjusting for variations in the intensity between an unbiased template image and the subject image. This bias field modulation employs interpolation of a spatially smooth thin-plate spline rooted in the intensity means of the local image patch ratio derived from both the deformed template and subject images.

To correct FLAIR inhomogeneities after co-registration of the FLAIR to the 3D T1 image, a previously published normalization technique of the local histogram was used (DeCarli et al., 1996). Before calculating the WMH, each 3D T1 image was subjected to a nonlinear alignment process to align it with a standardized template atlas. Moreover, all accompanying images underwent a transformation process onto the same atlas, utilizing the same set of transformation parameters. The estimation of WMH was carried out using a modified Bayesian probability employed approach, which relied on a method of histogram fitting previously described in the literature (DeCarli et al., 1999). To generate previous probability maps for WMH, a cohort of over 700 individuals was employed. Likelihood estimates of the original image are determined by employing histogram segmentation along with thresholding.

The entire segmentation process is first conducted in a standardized space, yielding probability likelihood values for WMH at each voxel within the white matter region. Following that, these probabilities are subjected to a threshold of 3.5 standard deviations above the mean, resulting in the creation of a binary WMH mask. To calculate tissue volume, these segmented WMH masks underwent a back transformation into the native space. As image segmentation was conducted in a standardized template space, group statistics can be applied by utilizing established methods previously reported (Carmichael et al., 2010, DeCarli et al., 2005, Yoshita et al., 2006).

Blood sample preparation

The MxP Quant 500 kit (Biogrates Innsbruck, Austria) was utilized to prepare the samples using their detailed protocol. The kit contains an internal standard that is applied to each well of the 96-well extraction plate. The appropriate wells contained ten microliters of each blank, calibration standard, Biogrates QC, Global Reference QG, serum sample, and SPQC. A gentle stream of nitrogen was used to dry the plate for 30 min. After derivatizing with phenyl isothiocyanate, the samples were eluted with 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol. For the UPLC analysis, samples were diluted either 1:1 with flowing solvent (a specialized mixture given by Biocrates) or 50:1 with flow injection analysis (an 80:20 water: methanol mixture). The SPQC created a pool of human serum samples analyzed on the first two plates, with 152 samples for ADNI−1 and 150 samples for ADNI-G0/2. The pooled sample was made in triplicate and analyzed in the same manner as the study samples. To assess the assay's performance across the sample cohort, this sample was injected three times: before, during, and after analyzing the study samples. To evaluate potential batch effects, analyses of this pool can be utilized.

Blood sample analysis

An Exion AD liquid chromatograph (Sciex, Framingham, MA) was used for Ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) separation, along with a guard column and proprietary analytical column that was included with the MP Quant 500 kit (Biocrates AG, Innsbruck, Austria). By using a gradient, the analytes were separated from 0.2% formic acid in water to 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile. Total UPC lasted approximately 6 min per sample, per injection. Samples for UPLC analysis were immediately injected into a QTRAP 6500 + (Sciex) operating in the Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode using two injections, one in electrospray ionization positive mode (ESI+) and the other in negative mode (ESI-). MRM transitions (compound-specific precursor to product ion transitions) were gathered over the appropriate retention time for each analyte and internal standard. Sciex application MultiQuant™ was used to import the UPLC mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) data and perform peak integration, calibration, and concentration calculations. Acylcarnitines, monosaccharides (hexose), diglycerides, triglycerides, lysophosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylcholines, sphingomyelins, ceramides, and cholesteryl esters were subjected to three distinct flow injection analysis-tandem mass spectrometry (FIA-MS/MS) methods. The electrospray ionization in positive mode (ESI+) was utilized on a Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters). The total analysis time per injection was approximately 3.8 min. Sample introduction was conducted using Acquit UPLC (Waters). For each analyte and internal standard, a precursor to product ion transitions specific to the compound was gathered. The software Biocrates MetIDOTM was used to analyze the FIA-MS/MS data.

Metabolite profiles and MRI datasets are available from the ADNI repository (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/). The complete list of metabolites used in this study is available in the supplementary files (106 metabolites) (Supplementary file 1).

Statistical analysis

Participants were allocated into three groups (normal, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD) based on the physician’s decision report in the ADNI database. Baseline characteristic normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Moreover, baseline demographic data of the patients consisting of Clinical Dementia Rating Global Score (CDR-GS), Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Box (CDR-SB), Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS-Cog 13 score), gender, age, and education in three groups were compared using chi-square (χ2) and Kruskal-Wallis tests. To analyze the longitudinal data evaluating the association of metabolite profile and WMH, a mixed-model regression was utilized, considering WMH as the output. In addition to defining the odds ratio of the metabolite profiles in correlation with WMH, multivariate logistic regression was used.

In the subgroup analysis, to further evaluate the relation between metabolite profile and cognitive impairment severity, data were analyzed both in the cross-sectional design using an ordinal logistic regression model at the baseline and longitudinal design using the Cox proportional hazards regression, determining the hazard ratio (HR). Moreover, to investigate the possible correlation between WMH and cognitive impairment severity over time, repeated-measure ANOVA using the Greenhouse-Geisser test, calculating Eta squared (η2), and observed power was applied. However, in the repeated-measure ANOVA test, due to low observed power in the AD group, only the result of the comparison of the normal and MCI groups was reported. All the analyses were adjusted for baseline gender and education, and time-varying age and ADAS-Cog 13. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0.

The demographic information of the participants did not differ remarkably, except for age, which specified a significant difference (p < 0.001). Association of serum metabolite profile with WMH

The result of the mixed model regression revealed tyrosine (Estimate= −1.891, 95% CI: −2.744 to −1.039, p < 0.001), glycine (Estimate= −1.207, 95% CI: −1.900 to −0.514, p = 0.001), beta-aminobutyric acid (Estimate= −0.862, 95% CI: −1.375 to −0.349, p = 0.001), histidine (Estimate= −1.691, 95% CI: −2.994 to −0.388, p = 0.011), and proline (Estimate= −0.691, 95% CI: −1.252 to −0.129, p = 0.016) as protective metabolites against WMH. Nevertheless, there was a positive correlation between WMH and methionine (Estimate=1.819, 95% CI: 0.761–2.877, p = 0.001), dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) (Estimate=0.685, 95% CI: 0.187–1.183, p = 0.007), betaine (Estimate=0.548, 95% CI: 0.150–0.947, p = 0.007), glutamic acid (Estimate=1.601, 95% CI: 0.424–2.778, p = 0.008), symmetric dimethylarginine (Estimate= 0.088, 95% CI: 0.006–0.170, p = 0.035), and dichloroacetic acid (Estimate=0.171, 95% CI: 0.005–0.337, p = 0.043). Among the metabolites, tyrosine and methionine had the strongest correlation with WMH (Table 2).

Multivariate logistic regression revealed 12 metabolites with a strong correlation with WMH. Seven serum metabolites had a significant association with WMH. The strongest positive correlation with the Wald value of 13.67 was observed for glutamic acid (OR = 8.474, 95% CI: 2.730–26.300, p < 0.01). Moreover, there were two amino acids with remarkable correlation, including methionine (OR = 4.014, 95% CI: 1.329–12.128, p = 0.014) and alanine (OR = 3.532, 95% CI: 1.544–8.076, p < 0.01). In addition, there was a significant correlation between levels of PEA (OR = 2.486, 95% CI: 1.152–5.364, p = 0.02) and WMH. We also found a weak but statistically significant correlation between trimethylamine N-oxide (OR = 1.183, 95% CI: 1.007–1.390, p = 0.04) and WMH. Finally, the only bile acid with a significant correlation with WMH was deoxycholic acid (OR = 1.272, 95% CI: 1.076–1.504, p < 0.01). Besides, there were three amino acids with a significant inverse association with WMH, namely histidine (OR = 0.240, 95% CI: 0.059–0.982, p = 0.047), proline (OR = 0.381, 95% CI: 0.162–0.894, p = 0.027), and tyrosine (OR = 0.287, 95% CI: 0.092–0.894, p = 0.031). In addition, a significant negative correlation was observed between beta-aminobutyric acid (OR = 0.598, 95% CI: 0.382–0.939, p = 0.025) and WMH. In the fatty acids subgroup, there was a significant correlation between arachidonic acid (OR = 2.110, 95% CI: 1.003–4.440, p = 0.049) and WMH. (Table 3).

Association of WMH with disease severity

The repeated measures ANOVA results highlighted a significant increase in the value of the WMH over three years in the MCI group compared to that in the control group. This result was measured using the Greenhouse-Geisser test with a p-value of 0.048 (η2=0.018 and observed power=0.575). Moreover, the mean difference between the second and third years (p = 0.008), as well as the first and third years in the MCI group (p = 0.006), was significant. There were no remarkable differences between the different years in the normal group. As it was depicted in the plot, the WMH is a slow progressive procedure that accelerated in MCI patients. Therefore, the low power for this analysis was hypothesized to be due to short follow-ups (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between WMH and the metabolic profiles of patients with MCI in three consecutive years. Changes in the levels of alanine, glutamic acid, methionine, arachidonic acid, deoxycholic acid, trimethylamine n-oxide, betaine, DOPA, PEA, symmetric dimethylarginine, and dichloroacetic acid were positively associated with WMH. In contrast, beta-aminobutyric acid, tyrosine, glycine, proline, and histidine levels were negatively associated with WMH.

Alanine was stated to be positively correlated with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tau level in AD (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) in a study published by Aquilani et al. (2020). This correlation was deemed to be a result of a reduction in the alanine aminotransferase in converting α-ketoglutarate to glutamate and a reduction in glutamate uptake by astrocytes (Aquilani et al., 2020, Schousboe et al., 2014). Correspondingly, Aquilani et al., two years later in 2022, measured the CSF and blood level of amino acids, which reported a significant reduction in the level of alanine in both CSF and blood samples of AD subjects (p = 0.0008 and p = 0.004). These results were explained by brain amino acid overutilization as evidenced by elevated urea levels in the CSF and tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA). Therefore, hyperammonia resulted in a decrease in glutamine synthetase enzymes in astrocytes (Aquilani et al., 2022). Our study revealed α-alanine as a protective metabolite in which an increase in the serum concentration was associated with lower AD risk (HR 0.240; 95% CI 0.058–0.989), whereas there was a positive association with WMH (OR = 3.532, 95% CI: 1.544–8.076, p < 0.01). These results suggested a complex influence of alanine on MCI, in which the sum effect of alanine is protective on MCI. This controversy results in the group of patients with a high level of α-alanine and WMH without remarkable cognitive impairment, which was highlighted in the previously published article, mentioning that in some patients with high WMH burden, the cognitive impairment was not significant. The article specified differences in gut microbiota for this issue; nevertheless, the controversy in WMH and cognitive function has yet to be discovered (Xu et al., 2026).

Glutamic acid is a non-essential amino acid that turns into glutamate in the body and acts as a neurotransmitter. We found that levels of glutamic acid were positively correlated with WMH, consistent with previous studies that linked glutamic acid to increased atherosclerosis risk via its effects on type 2 diabetes, adiposity, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance (Palmer et al., 2015, Takashina et al., 2016). According to some recent studies, cholesterol levels play a significant role in the β-amylogenesis of AD (Rudajev and Novotny, 2023, Wu et al., 2022). Thus, another explanation for the positive association of glutamic acid and alanine with WMH could be due to the relation between the levels of these two amino acids and cholesterol mentioned in a recently published study by Gu et al. (2023). In addition, glutamate was reported to up-regulate the CYP46A1 enzyme in a post-synaptic neuron, which enhances the production of 24S-hydroxycholesterol from cholesterol, increasing the risk of Aβ plaque formation (Zhang et al., 2020).

Histidine and β-Alanine are required to build carnosine, which is beneficial in controlling oxidative and nitrosative stress in AD (Solana-Manrique et al., 2022). Moreover, carnosine reduces Aβ aggregation (Shen et al., 2022). In our study, histidine levels were negatively correlated to WMH with an OR of 0.24 (95% CI: 0.059–0.982). Glutamine was another protective serum metabolite with an HR of 0.192 (95% CI: 0.079–0.463). In a large, randomized study, glutamine was correlated with a lower risk of developing AD with an OR of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.71–0.92). In the Nuzzo et al. study, serum glutamine levels were compared between healthy controls and patients with AD. The results stated that glutamine levels increased in the AD group, whereas there was a negative correlation between glutamine and the mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score. This difference could be due to the patient selection, since in the MCI group, there were no differences in the level of glutamine (Nuzzo et al., 2021). Moreover, in another study, no differences were reported in the blood level of glutamine. Nevertheless, in the CSF samples analysis, a meaningful reduction was mentioned in the AD group (p < 0.0001) (Aquilani et al., 2022). Besides, our results revealed a protective effect of threonine with an HR of 0.152 (95% CI: 0.034 – 0.679). This result was mentioned in the previous study analyzing the CSF level of AD patients as the remarkable reduction in threonine level compared to the control group (p = 0.001). Another study using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) instead of UPLC reported an increase in the concentration of threonine in the AD group with a fold change of 1.1, while the association with the risk of AD was not defined (Figueira et al., 2019).

Methionine is an essential amino acid with an important role in DNA regulation and cell functions. In addition, it is a precursor of succinyl-CoA, cysteine, creatine, and carnitine (Martínez et al., 2017). Our results showed that methionine had a significant positive correlation with WMH, in agreement with prior research that reported associations between high levels of methionine and total homocysteine and brain radiological changes, which resolved after normalization of methionine levels (Sasai et al., 2015, Vatanavicharn et al., 2008). Conversely, in another study, a higher methionine to homocysteine ratio was related to a lower risk of dementia and AD (Hooshmand et al., 2019). Methionine transfers to methionine-sulfoxide to absorb reactive oxygen species (ROS). This reaction can be reversed by methionine sulfoxide reductase. In the case of an excess amount of ROS, methionine-sulfoxide transfers to methionine-sulfone, in which the reaction is irreversible. Aggregation of methionine-sulfone led to protein carbonylation or dysregulation in the metabolism of cell proteins, including aggregation of amyloid-beta (Ali et al., 2005, Maupin-Furlow, 2018). Unfortunately, in our analysis, the homocysteine level was not included due to a large number of missing values. Based on the previously published article, betaine was suggested to be involved in the homocysteine to methionine conversion. In addition, an excess level of homocysteine was associated with WMH (Hathout et al., 2015). In the current study, the increased level of betaine and methionine was positively associated with WMH. Since the level of homocysteine was not assessed in our study, the interaction between methionine, homocysteine, and betaine in WMH could not be concluded correctly. Besides, PEA was suggested to increase WMH with an OR of 2.486 (95% CI: 1.152–5.364), as well as the cognitive impairment risk with HR of 3.887 (95% CI: 1.224 – 12.343). PEA’s protective influence on AD pathophysiology was mentioned before (Bronzuoli et al., 2018, Scuderi et al., 2018). One of the explanations for the contradictory results could be mentioned as the difference in the methodology since the previously published articles were conducted on in-vitro and in-vivo mouse models of AD. Moreover, in a review article, the negative effect of Aβ on oligodendrocyte function and complexity, along with the effect of PEA on increasing oligodendrocyte differentiation, led to the proposal that PEA could ameliorate WMH. Nevertheless, as was evident in the previously published article, the association of PEA with WMH has yet to be defined (Valenza et al., 2022).

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) is a type of amine oxide produced through the metabolic processes of gut microbiota. TMAO was elevated in the CSF of the AD patients associated with phosphorylated tau and Aβ42 (Vogt et al., 2018). Besides, TMAO was reported to be associated with WMH by triggering the ROS/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in the oligodendrocytes, resulting in pyroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction (Ji et al., 2022). In addition, TMAO was mentioned to increase WMH by enhancing cerebral SVDs, including acute lacunar infarction with an OR of 1.67 (95% CI, 1.05–2.66) (Kijpaisalratana et al., 2023). This result was confirmed by a systematic review evaluating the OR of TMAO with cerebral infarction incidence (Wang et al., 2023). Hence, two independent mechanisms were proposed to be involved in WMH triggered by TMAO.

Deoxycholic acid (DCA) is a secondary bile acid that facilitates the absorption of fats in the intestine (Weng et al., 2022). In the current study, DCA depicted a significant positive correlation with WMH. This result was in line with the former studies, indicating a higher ratio of DCA (secondary bile acid) and cholic acid (primary bile acid) as a deteriorating factor for cognition (MahmoudianDehkordi et al., 2019, Nho et al., 2019).

Arachidonic acid is one of the omega−6 fatty acids and one of the most abundant fatty acids in the brain (Imig, 2018, Wang et al., 2021). In the current study, arachidonic acid was associated with increased WMH volume, with an OR of 2.11 (95% CI: 1.003–4.440). In another study, the impact of arachidonic acid on WMH was determined through a ratio of eicosapentaenoic acid to arachidonic acid, in which a low ratio was associated with a higher risk of WMH and AD progression (Suwa et al., 2015). Nevertheless, in another study, there was no remarkable association between arachidonic acid and WMH (Shinto et al., 2020).

Furthermore, in our study, the protective aspect of proline on cognitive impairment severity and WMH was revealed (OR: 0.381, 95% CI: 0.162–0.894). In a systematic review, arginine and proline metabolism were involved in both aging and AD, without defining a comparison between their plasma level (Xie et al., 2021). In another study published in 2023, a decrease in D-proline levels was reported in AD patients (Liu et al., 2023). Proline was mentioned in glutamate and arginine metabolism (Köhler et al., 2008), while L-arginine was reported to participate in reducing nitro-oxidative stress (Barros et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the exact role of proline in decreasing or enhancing WMH was not mentioned before.

Besides, in the Wang et al. 2020 research, a negative correlation was observed in linoleic acid (ρ = − 0.25) and deoxycholate/cholate (ρ = − 0.21) with the global cognitive function score (Wang et al., 2020). In the current study, linoleic acid (HR: 1.889, 95% CI: 1.223 – 2.917) and glycocholic acid (HR: 1.712, 95% CI: 1.080 – 2.713) increased the risk of cognitive impairment progression. Moreover, a previous study reported an increase in the level of oleic acid with a fold change of 1.28 in AD (González-Domínguez et al., 2015). Nevertheless, in a recent article, without measuring the level of blood concentration of oleic acid, a remarkable correlation between oleic acid and Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) was observed, in which consumption of oleic acid attenuated cognitive decline (Sakurai et al., 2021). Accordingly, our study showed a protective role of oleic acid with an HR of 0.035 (95% CI: 0.186 – 0.683).

Symptom severity and WMH increased over the years in the MCI group. Previous studies have revealed an association between WMH volume and AD (Eloyan et al., 2023, Hong et al., 2024, Shirzadi et al., 2023). Similarly, our study showed a significant longitudinal correlation between WMH and MCI (p = 0.048). Gut microbiota have a tangible effect on the concentration of the metabolites in the blood and are correlated with WMH, cerebral small vessel disease, and cognitive impairment. Increase in WMH and its association with microbiota, metabolite levels, glymphatic system dysfunction, and cognitive impairment highlighted the complex interactions that lead to AD progression (Song et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2026). The association between microbiota, metabolite levels, and cognitive impairment was previously mentioned in another study on the gut-brain axis. The study specified the higher level of Arachidonic acid in the fecal metabolites in the AD compared to the normal group (Zhao et al., 2025). Moreover, in the current study, the Arachidonic acid level was mentioned as a risk factor for WMH. These findings highlighted the gut-brain axis mediated by metabolites.

To summarize, the association of metabolite profile with WMH and AD sheds light on the covert aspect of signaling cascades involved in deteriorating or ameliorating AD symptoms. The aggregation of amyloid-beta plaques, as one of the pathological signs associated with AD and one of the risk factors of WMH (i.e., CAA), could be enhanced by glutamate excitotoxicity and methionine-sulfone accumulation as a result of increasing ROS. In addition, overutilization of amino acids in the brain causes hyperammonemia, which decreases the viability of neurons and glia. Finally, other causes of WMH could be ascribed to pyroptosis, demyelination, and gut microbiota, highlighting the important role of oligodendrocytes in WMH of MCI patients (Fig. 3).

One of our study limitations was the small number of AD participants, in which the results of this study could be interpreted for the subjects with early and mid-stage memory impairment, mainly categorized in the MCI group. Another limitation was missing data on some of the metabolites due to their concentration, for which the device sensitivity was not enough to determine their levels.

Conclusion

WMH etiology comprises a wide range of factors. In the current study, serum metabolite profiles (amino acids, amine oxides, bile acids, biogenic amines, carboxylic acids, fatty acids, hormones, indole derivatives, nucleobases related, and vitamins & cofactors) were analyzed in patients with MCI. The WMH in the MCI group was associated with changes in the level of metabolites. Since the grade of WMH correlated with the disease severity, it could be presumed that dysregulation in the level of metabolites has a synergistic effect with the WMH and MCI pathology.
