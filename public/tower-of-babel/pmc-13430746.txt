Access to pulmonary rehabilitation in real-world territorial care: the APTA friendly network as a structural and organizational model

Introduction

Pulmonary rehabilitation is one of the most robustly supported non-pharmacological interventions in chronic respiratory diseases, with consistent evidence demonstrating improvements in exercise tolerance, symptom burden, health-related quality of life and, in selected populations, survival [1–3]. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and other chronic respiratory conditions, rehabilitation represents a cornerstone of comprehensive care, with an effect size that is clinically meaningful and, in some contexts, comparable to or exceeding that of established pharmacological strategies [4]. Despite this strong background, access to pulmonary rehabilitation remains limited, heterogeneous and frequently delayed, particularly in real-world territorial settings [4–6].

This discrepancy between evidence and implementation has been repeatedly acknowledged in international guidelines and position statements, while persisting across healthcare systems with different organizational models [4]. The reasons for this gap are multifactorial and extend well beyond the availability of rehabilitation programs per se [7]. Fragmentation of care pathways, insufficient integration between hospital-based and community-based services, lack of standardized referral criteria, variability in professional competencies and administrative barriers all contribute to a scenario in which pulmonary rehabilitation is underutilized, inconsistently delivered or reduced to a nominal intervention with limited clinical impact [8–10].

The problem is particularly evident in the management of chronic respiratory diseases, where continuity of care represents a critical determinant of outcomes. Hospital-centered models are intrinsically ill-suited to address long-term disease trajectories characterized by progressive functional decline, recurrent exacerbations, and complex comorbidity patterns [1, 4]. In this context, territorial respiratory care, and specifically the role of the community-based pulmonologist, becomes central. However, in many healthcare systems, this role remains weakly defined, poorly supported and structurally isolated, resulting in a paradox whereby specialists who are closest to everyday clinical needs of patients operate with fewer tools, fewer formal pathways and limited institutional recognition [5, 6].

Pulmonary rehabilitation exemplifies this paradox. While it is widely endorsed as an essential component of chronic respiratory disease management, its practical delivery at the territorial level often depends on informal networks, individual professional initiative, or ad hoc solutions rather than on structured, institutionalized pathways [8–10]. The transition from acute hospital care to post-acute and maintenance phases is frequently uncoordinated, exposing patients to therapeutic discontinuity precisely at the time when rehabilitation would be most effective [1, 4]. Moreover, domiciliary rehabilitation, which could theoretically address accessibility barriers, is frequently implemented without adequate specialization, equipment or outcome monitoring, raising concerns about both effectiveness and appropriateness [11, 12].

Regional healthcare organization further amplifies these issues. In decentralized systems, variability between districts and local health authorities can translate into profound inequities in access to rehabilitation services, even within the same geographic area [4, 6]. Differences in administrative procedures, availability of trained personnel, interpretation of eligibility criteria and resource allocation lead to highly heterogeneous patient experiences. As a result, pulmonary rehabilitation risks becoming a postcode-dependent intervention rather than a standard of care grounded in clinical need [6, 8]. In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to innovative solutions, including telemedicine and home-based rehabilitation models, as potential strategies to overcome logistical and organizational barriers [11, 13]. While these approaches hold promise, their implementation has often been driven by technological availability rather than by a coherent clinical and organizational framework. Without clear integration into existing care pathways, appropriate patient selection and defined professional responsibilities, such solutions may fail to deliver their expected benefits and may even contribute to further fragmentation of care [13].

Against this background, there is a growing need to move beyond descriptive accounts of access limitations and to systematically analyze the structural and organizational barriers that hinder effective pulmonary rehabilitation delivery in real-world settings [5, 6]. Importantly, such analyses should be grounded in the direct experience of clinicians operating within territorial respiratory care, who are uniquely positioned to identify critical gaps, unintended consequences of current models and feasible, context-sensitive solutions [6, 14].

The present work arises from a structured expert initiative involving pulmonologists and stakeholders actively engaged in community-based respiratory care within a regional healthcare system. Using a combination of expert discussion, shared experiential analysis and targeted survey data, this paper aims to identify the principal barriers to pulmonary rehabilitation access and integration at the territorial level, and to propose an operational framework designed to enhance continuity, equity and clinical effectiveness. While the analysis is rooted in a specific regional context, the challenges identified and the proposed strategic directions are intended to be transferable to other healthcare systems facing similar implementation gaps. By focusing on organizational dynamics, professional roles and care pathway integration, rather than on disease-specific efficacy alone, this work seeks to contribute to the broader discussion on how evidence-based interventions can be effectively translated into everyday clinical practice. In doing so, it positions pulmonary rehabilitation not as an isolated therapeutic option, but as a test case for the capacity of healthcare systems to deliver integrated, patient-centered care for chronic respiratory diseases.

Methods

Study design and conceptual framework

This work was designed as a mixed-methods, expert-based analysis aimed at identifying organizational barriers and implementation gaps in pulmonary rehabilitation delivery within territorial respiratory care. The methodological approach combined structured expert discussion, experiential qualitative analysis and survey-based data collection to capture both systemic patterns and context-specific criticalities. The study did not aim to evaluate clinical efficacy, which is already well established in the literature [4], but rather to explore real-world barriers to access, continuity and quality of pulmonary rehabilitation across different care settings.

The conceptual framework was informed by principles of health services research and implementation science, with a specific focus on care pathway integration, professional roles and organizational determinants of access. The process was intentionally grounded in clinical practice, privileging insights derived from direct involvement in territorial respiratory care over abstract or purely theoretical models.

Expert panel composition and setting

The expert initiative was conducted within the framework of the Associazione Pneumologi Territoriali Ambulatoriali (APTA), a professional network bringing together pulmonologists operating in community-based and outpatient respiratory care across the Campania Region. The panel consisted of specialists with direct and sustained clinical involvement in the management of chronic respiratory diseases within territorial settings, including referral, activation, and follow-up of pulmonary rehabilitation programs. The study intentionally focused on the territorial perspective, without the inclusion of hospital-based respiratory physicians, in order to capture the operational and organizational realities encountered by the clinicians who are most directly responsible for the long-term management of patients with chronic respiratory diseases outside the hospital setting. Two investigators (M.M. and C.C.), based at a tertiary referral centre, contributed to the conceptual design of the study and to the coordination of the network-based activities, while the survey component was reserved to pulmonologists directly engaged in territorial respiratory care, in order to preserve the distinctive territorial perspective of the data collected. Participation in the network-based activities was based on active clinical engagement in territorial respiratory care rather than on formal academic criteria, with the explicit aim of capturing operational challenges, organizational variability, and implementation barriers encountered in everyday practice.

The initiative was conducted within the regional healthcare system of Campania, Italy, which is characterized by decentralized governance and district-level autonomy. This organizational context allowed exploration of inter-district heterogeneity in access pathways, administrative processes and service availability within a single regional framework. Although the analysis originated from a specific regional setting, the diversity of professional backgrounds represented within the panel was intended to enhance the interpretability and potential transferability of the findings to other healthcare systems facing similar challenges in integrating pulmonary rehabilitation into territorial respiratory care.

Structured expert meeting and thematic analysis

A dedicated in-person expert meeting was convened in Pompei on 12 December 2025 and involved 38 pulmonologists operating in territorial respiratory care across the five provinces of the Campania Region, all of whom had also completed the APTA survey. The meeting lasted approximately six hours and followed a predefined thematic agenda addressing referral pathways, waiting times, domiciliary versus center-based rehabilitation, continuity between hospital and territorial care, professional competencies, and administrative constraints. Discussion was facilitated by two investigators with specific expertise in pulmonary rehabilitation (M.M. and C.C.), who did not participate in the survey component, in order to keep the roles of facilitation and data collection separate. Contributions were documented contemporaneously through structured written notes taken by a designated rapporteur, reviewed and consolidated immediately after the meeting, and subsequently shared with the investigator team for collegial verification.

Rather than applying a formal consensus technique, such as a Delphi process, the meeting was designed to encourage open, experience-driven discussion, allowing divergent perspectives to emerge and be critically examined. Thematic analysis was conducted by two investigators (P.A. and C.C.), who independently reviewed the consolidated meeting notes and identified recurring content clusters, points of convergence, and areas of marked disagreement. Themes were generated inductively from the discussion record and were subsequently organized into macro-domains reflecting structural, organizational, professional, and systemic barriers. Discrepancies in coding were resolved collegially, and the final thematic structure was reviewed by the full investigator team to ensure internal validity and coherence with the survey domains. These domains were iteratively refined through comparison with survey findings to ensure convergence between qualitative insights and quantitative trends.

Survey development and data collection

To complement expert discussion and provide a structured overview of real-world barriers to pulmonary rehabilitation, a targeted survey was developed within the APTA network and administered to pulmonologists operating in territorial and outpatient respiratory care across the Campania Region.

The questionnaire was developed through a three-step process. First, an initial item pool was generated by three investigators (M.M., P.A., C.C.) on the basis of the key domains addressed in the ATS/ERS policy statement on pulmonary rehabilitation implementation [5, 15], of the principal organizational barriers identified in the recent implementation literature [6, 7, 14], and of the direct clinical experience of the investigators in the management of chronic respiratory diseases. Second, the draft questionnaire was reviewed internally by the three territorial pulmonologists among the authors (A.D.S., G.C., E.S.), each practicing in a different province of the Campania Region, who assessed item clarity, domain relevance, and coverage of the main stages of the rehabilitation pathway. Their feedback was used to refine item wording, eliminate redundancies, and reorganize response options. Third, the revised instrument was circulated to the APTA members for completion. The questionnaire was not intended as a validated psychometric scale and did not undergo formal pilot testing on an independent external sample, an approach that would have been difficult to implement within the limited and highly specialized population of territorial pulmonologists in the region. Rather, it was designed as an exploratory domain-based instrument aimed at capturing shared organizational criticalities from the perspective of clinicians directly engaged in territorial respiratory care.

The thematic domains addressed by the questionnaire reflected the main stages of the rehabilitation pathway and included barriers to access and waiting times, availability and timeliness of rehabilitation services, quality and appropriateness of domiciliary rehabilitation, administrative and bureaucratic constraints, integration between hospital-based and territorial care, professional training and availability of respiratory-specific rehabilitation expertise, and perceived priorities for system improvement (Table 1). The final version of the questionnaire was distributed electronically between 15 October and 1 December 2025 to all 39 pulmonologists actively affiliated with the APTA network in the Campania Region at the time of the study, including three of the authors (A.D.S., G.C., E.S.) operating as territorial pulmonologists in the Provinces of Caserta, Salerno, and Avellino, respectively. A total of 38 complete responses were received, corresponding to a response rate of 97.4%.

Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings

Findings from expert discussion and survey data were integrated through a triangulation process aimed at reinforcing the robustness of identified barriers and proposed solutions. Convergent themes were prioritized as indicators of systemic relevance, while divergent observations were retained to highlight contextual variability and potential areas for tailored interventions.

This integrative approach allowed for the development of a coherent analytical narrative linking individual clinical experiences to broader organizational patterns, thereby strengthening the external plausibility and transferability of the proposed framework.

Ethical considerations

The initiative was conducted as a professional service evaluation and organizational analysis among healthcare professionals, without patient involvement or collection of personal health data, and therefore did not require formal ethical approval according to institutional and national regulations. The IRCCS Maugeri (Telese Terme Institute) operates under the jurisdiction of the Comitato Etico Territoriale (CET) Campania 1, and all procedures complied with applicable data protection regulations.

Results

The integrated analysis of structured expert discussion and survey responses conducted within the APTA network, referring to territorial respiratory care across the Campania Region, points to a consistent pattern of organizational barriers affecting access to and delivery of pulmonary rehabilitation. Across survey domains, numerical trends were concordant with the qualitative insights derived from the expert meeting, supporting the interpretation of these findings as systemic rather than anecdotal within the context examined.

Of the 39 pulmonologists invited to complete the APTA survey, 38 returned a complete response, corresponding to a response rate of 97.4%. The only non-respondent was a territorial pulmonologist practicing in the metropolitan area of Naples. Based on the Local Health Authority (ASL) of affiliation within the APTA network, the provincial distribution of respondents was as follows: 17 respondents (44.7%) from the metropolitan area of Naples (ASL Napoli 1 Centro, Napoli 2 Nord, and Napoli 3 Sud), 8 (21.1%) from the Province of Caserta, 7 (18.4%) from the Province of Salerno, 5 (13.2%) from the Province of Avellino, and 1 (2.6%) from the Province of Benevento. All five provinces of the Campania Region were therefore represented. A descriptive summary of the main survey domains and their associated quantitative findings is provided in Table 1, highlighting the relative frequency of access-related barriers, organizational constraints and perceived limitations of current rehabilitation models.

Access to pulmonary rehabilitation and waiting times

Difficulties in accessing pulmonary rehabilitation emerged as one of the most prevalent barriers. Approximately two thirds of respondents reported frequent or persistent problems related to long waiting lists and limited short-term availability of rehabilitation services. Delays were particularly pronounced in center-based and ambulatory settings, where waiting times commonly exceeded clinically meaningful post-acute windows. Several clinicians explicitly reported advising patients to seek rehabilitation outside their local district or province because of excessive delays, a practice reflecting both system-level insufficiency and inequity of access.

Organizational and bureaucratic barriers

Organizational and administrative constraints were identified as major limiting factors by a substantial proportion of respondents. Around 50% of participants reported significant bureaucratic obstacles, including complex authorization procedures, unclear referral responsibilities and fragmented communication between services. Although approximately half of these respondents indicated that such barriers could occasionally be overcome through individual effort, this was consistently associated with delays in rehabilitation activation and increased professional workload, rather than with structurally efficient solutions.

Heterogeneity across districts and services

Marked inter-district heterogeneity was consistently reported across multiple survey items. Respondents described substantial variability in eligibility criteria, referral pathways and service availability across local health authorities within the Campania Region. This heterogeneity translated into unequal access to pulmonary rehabilitation independent of clinical severity, reinforcing the perception that organizational context, rather than patient need, was the dominant determinant of rehabilitation uptake.

Domiciliary pulmonary rehabilitation

Responses related to domiciliary pulmonary rehabilitation highlighted a discrepancy between theoretical accessibility and perceived effectiveness. Although home-based rehabilitation was reported as more readily activated than center-based programs, a large proportion of respondents questioned its quality. Insufficient respiratory-specific training of personnel, inadequate session frequency and lack of structured outcome monitoring were recurrently cited. These limitations led many clinicians to consider domiciliary rehabilitation as a suboptimal or provisional option rather than a true alternative to structured rehabilitation pathways.

Professional training and expertise

Deficits in respiratory-specific rehabilitation expertise emerged clearly from the survey. Approximately one quarter of respondents reported inadequate knowledge or awareness of pulmonary rehabilitation principles among referring physicians, while concerns regarding insufficient training of physiotherapists in respiratory rehabilitation were even more prevalent. Limited availability of dedicated rehabilitation personnel was frequently reported as a critical bottleneck, particularly in domiciliary and integrated home-care (Assistenza Domiciliare Integrata, ADI) settings, where time per session and number of weekly sessions were considered insufficient to achieve meaningful clinical benefit.

Perceived alignment between clinicians and patients

When asked whether patients and physicians shared similar expectations regarding pulmonary rehabilitation, approximately 70% of respondents indicated concordance, while nearly 30% perceived a mismatch between professional recommendations and patient expectations. Qualitative comments suggested that this discrepancy often related to unrealistic expectations regarding disease modification or cure, potentially contributing to reduced adherence or delayed acceptance of rehabilitation programs.

Perceptions of telemedicine and telerehabilitation

Perceptions of telemedicine-supported pulmonary rehabilitation were heterogeneous. More than half of respondents expressed uncertainty or skepticism regarding its effectiveness if implemented independently of structured programs. Importantly, this skepticism was not directed toward telemedicine per se, but rather toward its application in the absence of adequate technological infrastructure, training and standardized outcome monitoring. Conversely, respondents broadly agreed that telerehabilitation could play a complementary role within integrated care pathways, particularly in maintenance phases, provided that appropriate governance and quality indicators were established.

Proposed priorities for system improvement

Across open-ended survey items, several priorities for improving pulmonary rehabilitation delivery emerged with high consistency. These priorities reflect the perspectives of clinicians on system-level needs and are derived from both survey responses and structured expert discussion. Discussion

This expert-based analysis provides a structured interpretation of the barriers limiting access to and effective delivery of pulmonary rehabilitation within territorial respiratory care, using the Campania Region as a real-world case study of a broader systemic challenge [5, 15]. Within the limits of an exploratory expert-based design, the findings suggest that the underutilization of pulmonary rehabilitation in this setting is not primarily driven by uncertainty regarding its clinical value, which was widely acknowledged among the clinicians surveyed, but rather by organizational fragmentation, weak integration across levels of care, and misalignment between institutional structures and the longitudinal needs of patients with chronic respiratory disease [4, 5].

In Campania, a highly populated region characterized by decentralized healthcare governance and marked inter-district variability, pulmonary rehabilitation emerges as a paradigmatic example of the gap between strong evidence and fragile implementation [5]. The heterogeneity observed across local health authorities reflects not isolated inefficiencies, but the absence of a shared organizational framework capable of translating evidence-based recommendations into coherent and accessible territorial pathways [5, 15]. This observation is consistent with international reports describing pulmonary rehabilitation as a persistently underdelivered intervention despite its inclusion in all major respiratory guidelines [4, 15, 16].

One of the most relevant insights emerging from this analysis concerns the structural isolation of the community-based pulmonologist [5, 14]. Unlike hospital-based settings, where multidisciplinary collaboration is embedded in routine practice, territorial care frequently relies on informal professional relationships and individual initiative [5]. This structural isolation not only delays rehabilitation activation but also undermines continuity and accountability, particularly during the transition from acute hospitalization to post-acute and maintenance phases [4, 15].

The discontinuity between hospital discharge and territorial follow-up identified in this study is further reinforced by the quantitative findings [15]. Approximately two thirds of surveyed clinicians reported frequent or persistent difficulties related to waiting lists and limited short-term availability of pulmonary rehabilitation services, especially in center-based and ambulatory settings [5]. These delays were consistently perceived as clinically meaningful, particularly in the post-acute phase, when the therapeutic window for pulmonary rehabilitation is most favorable [1, 4]. The recurrent need to redirect patients outside their local district or province highlights how organizational insufficiency translates into inequitable access rather than simply reduced service volume [5, 6].

Within this context, administrative and bureaucratic barriers, a well-documented obstacle to pulmonary rehabilitation uptake [15], emerged as a second major determinant of limited access in the present analysis. Around half of respondents identified authorization procedures, unclear referral responsibilities and fragmented communication between services as key obstacles to timely rehabilitation activation [5]. Although some clinicians reported being able to overcome these barriers through individual effort, this was consistently described as inefficient and unsustainable, suggesting that professional commitment alone cannot compensate for structural deficiencies in territorial settings [14, 15].

Marked heterogeneity across districts within the Campania Region further reinforces the interpretation that organizational context, rather than clinical need, drives access to pulmonary rehabilitation [5, 6]. Such heterogeneity mirrors observations reported in other decentralized healthcare systems and underscores the absence of shared operational frameworks capable of ensuring minimum standards of access and continuity of care [14, 15]. Within this fragmented landscape, domiciliary pulmonary rehabilitation represents a particularly critical area [4, 12]. Although home-based programs were often easier to activate, their perceived effectiveness was frequently questioned due to insufficient respiratory-specific training of personnel, inadequate session intensity and lack of structured outcome monitoring [11, 12].

Deficits in professional training and expertise further compound these challenges [5, 15]. Approximately one quarter of respondents reported inadequate awareness of pulmonary rehabilitation principles among referring physicians, while concerns regarding respiratory-specific physiotherapy training were even more prevalent [5]. This gap points to a disconnect between guideline recommendations and real-world professional preparedness, particularly in territorial and domiciliary settings, and indicates that educational initiatives alone are unlikely to be sufficient in the absence of formal recognition and organizational integration of respiratory rehabilitation competencies [14, 15]. The analysis also captures how the clinicians surveyed perceive their alignment with patients in terms of expectations about pulmonary rehabilitation. Although most respondents reported concordant expectations, nearly one third described a perceived mismatch, which they commonly attributed to unrealistic patient expectations regarding disease modification or cure [4]. It should be emphasized that this finding reflects the clinicians’ perspective only, since the patient voice was not directly sampled in the present study. Such perceived misalignment may nonetheless contribute to delayed acceptance or suboptimal adherence to rehabilitation programs and underscores the importance of structured patient education and shared decision-making within rehabilitation pathways [1, 4].

Perceptions of telemedicine and telerehabilitation further illustrate the complexity of implementation [11, 13]. More than half of respondents expressed skepticism regarding their effectiveness when applied in isolation. Clinicians consistently emphasized that telemedicine could represent a valuable adjunct, particularly in maintenance phases, only if embedded within structured programs supported by adequate infrastructure, professional training and standardized outcome monitoring [12, 17]. Limited uptake of pulmonary rehabilitation appears driven less by lack of clinical awareness than by fragmentation of care pathways, insufficient coordination between hospital and territorial services and weak formalization of professional roles [5, 15].

Although grounded in a specific regional context, the patterns identified are likely relevant to other healthcare systems characterized by decentralized governance and resource constraints [5, 6]. By integrating numerical trends with expert-derived interpretation, this study contributes to implementation-oriented research aimed at bridging the persistent gap between evidence and routine care, positioning pulmonary rehabilitation not only as an effective therapeutic intervention but also as a sensitive indicator of system performance in chronic respiratory disease management [4, 15, 16].

Limitations

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the expert-based design and the focus on a single regional healthcare system may limit statistical generalizability. However, the aim of the study was not to provide population-level estimates, but to explore organizational and implementation barriers as they emerge in real-world territorial respiratory care. In this respect, the Campania Region represents a particularly informative setting, given its decentralized governance and pronounced inter-district variability, which allowed observation of a wide spectrum of organizational models within the same regulatory framework [5].

Second, the absence of patient-level clinical outcomes precludes direct assessment of the impact of pulmonary rehabilitation pathways on functional or prognostic endpoints. This choice reflects the exploratory and system-oriented nature of the analysis, which intentionally focused on structural and process-related determinants of care rather than on efficacy, already extensively documented in the literature [4]. Future studies integrating patient-reported outcomes and healthcare utilization data will be essential to quantify the clinical and economic implications of the organizational gaps identified.

Third, although the survey component was not designed as a representative epidemiological instrument, the convergence between survey findings and qualitative insights derived from structured expert discussion strengthens the internal coherence and plausibility of the results. The consistency of reported barriers across different districts and professional settings supports the interpretation of these findings as systemic rather than anecdotal [15].

A specific limitation concerns the absence of direct patient input. The study was intentionally designed to capture the perspective of pulmonologists operating in territorial respiratory care and did not include a patient-level component. Observations on the alignment between clinicians and patients should therefore be read as clinicians’ perceptions of concordance rather than as a direct assessment of patient expectations or experience. Future work integrating patient-reported outcomes, structured patient interviews, and shared decision-making indicators will be essential to complete the picture and to verify whether the perceived misalignment reported by clinicians corresponds to actual divergences in patient priorities.

Finally, the emphasis on real-world practice, while limiting experimental control, represents a key strength of the study. By grounding the analysis in everyday clinical and organizational experience, the proposed framework is particularly suited to implementation-oriented research and policy development, where feasibility, contextual adaptability and professional engagement are critical determinants of success [5, 15].

Conclusions

This expert-based analysis suggests that limited uptake of pulmonary rehabilitation in territorial respiratory care, at least within the Campania Region, is primarily shaped by organizational fragmentation rather than by uncertainty regarding clinical efficacy. These findings should be interpreted as implementation-informed insights from a specific professional and regional context, rather than as definitive evidence of regional system performance. The persistent gap between evidence and practice reflects structural weaknesses in care integration, insufficient formalization of professional roles and lack of coordinated pathways linking hospital, territorial and rehabilitation services.

The Campania Region provides a representative case study of how decentralized healthcare governance, in the absence of shared operational frameworks, can amplify inter-district variability and generate inequities in access to evidence-based interventions. Within this context, pulmonary rehabilitation emerges as a sensitive indicator of system performance, revealing how fragmented referral processes, delayed transitions of care and inconsistent service quality can undermine interventions with otherwise well-established benefits.

These findings underscore the need to move beyond incremental or isolated solutions, such as increasing service availability without addressing coordination mechanisms. Sustainable improvement requires organizational coherence, including integrated respiratory care networks, standardized access criteria and clearly defined interfaces between acute, post-acute and long-term care settings. Strengthening the role of the community-based pulmonologist as a central coordinator of chronic respiratory care represents a key element of this redesign.

Importantly, the proposed framework does not advocate for uniformity at the expense of local adaptability. Rather, it emphasizes the establishment of shared principles and minimum standards capable of supporting contextualized implementation while preserving equity and clinical appropriateness. Although derived from a regional experience, the challenges identified and the strategic directions outlined are relevant to other healthcare systems facing similar difficulties in translating non-pharmacological evidence into routine practice.

In this perspective, pulmonary rehabilitation should be regarded not as an ancillary service, but as an integral component of chronic respiratory disease management and a benchmark for evaluating the effectiveness of territorial care models. Addressing the systemic barriers highlighted in this study represents a critical step toward delivering respiratory care that is equitable, effective and aligned with the long-term needs of patients with chronic respiratory diseases.
