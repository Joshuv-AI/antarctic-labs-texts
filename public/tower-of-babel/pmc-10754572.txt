Comparison of the antiemetic efficacy of a combination of midazolam with ramosetron and midazolam with palonosetron for postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis in laparoscopic cholecystectomy
Authors: Jung A Lim, Sungbin Jo, Eun Kyung Choi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:A multimodal therapeutic strategy for preventing postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) benefits moderate- and high-risk surgical patients. We compared the efficacy of a combination of midazolam and ramosetron and a combination of midazolam and palonosetron for PONV prophylaxis in patients scheduled for laparoscopic cholecystectomy.Methods:We enrolled 68 patients aged 20 to 65 years undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Patients were randomly allocated to the midazolam 0.05 mg/kg with ramosetron 0.3 mg (MR) or midazolam 0.05 mg/kg with palonosetron 0.075 mg (MP) groups. The incidence of PONV, severity of nausea, use of rescue antiemetics, and pain severity were evaluated at 2, 24, and 48 hours after surgery.Results:The incidence (38.2% vs 5.9%) and severity of postoperative nausea were significantly lower in the MP group at 2 hours after surgery (P < .05). There were no significant differences in the incidence of vomiting, use of rescue antiemetics, or pain severity between the 2 groups.Conclusion:The combination of midazolam with palonosetron significantly decreased the incidence and severity of postoperative nausea compared with midazolam combined with ramosetron, especially in the early postoperative phase (0–2 hours) in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

1. Introduction

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is a common complication of general anesthesia. The incidence of PONV is between 10% and 80%, especially in high-risk patients[1,2] and it affects the recovery and satisfaction of patients after surgery. Females, nonsmokers, previous history of PONV or motion sickness, laparoscopic surgery, volatile anesthesia, and the use of opioids for postoperative analgesia are known risk factors for PONV.[3] In patients with more than one risk factor, a multimodal prophylactic approach has recently been recommended to reduce inappropriate prophylaxis.[4]

Several studies have introduced drugs and methods to prevent PONV, and 5-hydroxytryptamine type 3 (5-HT3) receptor antagonists have been widely used. Ramosetron and palonosetron are the most commonly used 5-HT3 receptor antagonists for preventing PONV. Ramosetron has a slow dissociation rate with high receptor affinity compared to previous 5-HT3 antagonists, resulting in a longer duration of approximately 48 hours.[5] Palonosetron’s characteristics differ from ramosetron, which has a higher receptor binding affinity and a plasma half-life exceeding 40 hours compared with the half-life of other 5-HT3 antagonists, which range from 5 to 12 hours.[6] Moreover, palonosetron acts as an allosteric antagonist in a positive, cooperative manner, causing receptor internalization via conformational changes. These alterations result in prolonged receptor function inhibition.[7]

Midazolam is a short-acting benzodiazepine administered to reduce anxiety and sedation before surgery. It is also effective in preventing PONV[8–10] and has been reported effective when administered with other drugs, such as antiemetic agents.[11,12] Based on this, it was assumed that the co-administration of midazolam with 5-HT3 receptor antagonists would also be effective, particularly in patients with moderate and high-risk PONV.

This study compared the effects of administering a combination of midazolam and ramosetron or palonosetron on preventing PONV in moderate- and high-risk patients.

2. Method

This study was approved by the institutional ethics review committee of Daegu Catholic University Medical Center (CR-22-159-L) and registered at Clinical Research Information System (no. KCT0008767). 68 female patients aged 20 to 65 years with the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status 1 to 2 scheduled for laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Pregnant or breastfeeding patients, smokers, patients with a history of chronic opioid abuse, hypersensitivity to 5-HT3 receptor antagonists or analgesics, and antiemetic or opioid treatment within 24 hours before surgery were excluded from the study.

Patients were randomly allocated to receive either midazolam 0.05 mg/kg with ramosetron 0.3 mg (group MR) or midazolam 0.05 mg/kg with palonosetron 0.075 mg (group MP) using an online randomizer program (http://www.randomizer.org). Opaque and sealed envelopes containing random numbers were opened immediately before surgery by an anesthesiologist who was not involved in any part of this study (including drug preparation). Patients were unaware of group assignment.

The patient arrived in the operating room without premedication. After anesthetic monitoring was performed using noninvasive blood pressure monitoring, pulse oximetry, electrocardiogram, neuromuscular monitoring, and Bispectral Index, 0.05 mg/kg midazolam was administered intravenously. Group MP received an injection of palonosetron 0.075 mg immediately before anesthesia, and Group MR received 0.3 mg ramosetron 10 minutes before the end of the surgery. Anesthesia was induced with propofol 1.5 mg/kg, remifentanil 1.0 μg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg. General anesthesia was maintained with 0.1 to 0.2 μg/kg/min remifentanil intravenous infusion and sevoflurane 1.5% to 2.5% in 50% oxygen/air. The tidal volume and respiratory rate were regulated to maintain normocapnia (end-tidal CO2 concentration 35 ± 5 mm Hg), and the anesthetic depth was maintained using a Bispectral Index within a range of 40 to 60. At the end of surgery, the remifentanil infusion was stopped, and intravenous fentanyl 50 μg was given to all patients to reduce postoperative pain. Neuromuscular block was antagonized by a sugammadex. Extubation was performed when the patient was fully awake with a train-of-four ratio > 0.9. All anesthetic procedures were performed by anesthesiologists blinded to group assignment.

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of PONV between the 2 groups. Secondary endpoints were the severity of postoperative nausea, need for rescue antiemetics, postoperative pain, and analgesic requirements. These variables were assessed 0 to 2, 2 to 24, and 24 to 48 hours after surgery by an evaluator blinded to the combination type of the antiemetic agent. The severity of nausea was graded on a 4-point scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe) after asking patients,[13] and metoclopramide 10 mg was administered during vomiting or when the patient requested an antiemetic drug.

The degree of postoperative pain was assessed using a numerical analog scale.[14] This method allows the patient to indicate the pain level numerically, where 0 indicates no pain at all and, 10 indicates extreme pain that the patient cannot imagine. Suppose the patients complained of pain (numerical analog scale ≥ 5) or required rescue analgesics; fentanyl 50 to 100 μg was given intravenously in postanesthetic care unit. Ketorolac 30 mg was administered every 8 hours after surgery.

2.1. Statistics

The sample size was determined using PASS 15 (Power Analysis and Sample Size Software [2017]. NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, UT, ncss.com/software/pass). The pilot study results showed a PONV incidence of 56% and 20% in the MR and MP groups, respectively. Twenty-eight patients per group were required to achieve 80% statistical power for detecting a difference of 36% in the incidence of PONV between the 2 groups, allowing for an alpha error of 0.05. Considering an expected dropout rate of 20%, 35 patients were required per group.

Data are presented as median and interquartile range. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. The chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 19.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with a statistical significance of P < .05.

3. Results

Seventy-six patients were assessed for eligibility, and 6 were excluded due to refusal to participate in the study and dissatisfaction with the inclusion criteria. Seventy patients were enrolled in this study, of which 2 were excluded during the follow-up period due to the use of other types of analgesics (acetaminophen) and combined surgery (Fig. 1). The 2 groups had no statistically significant differences in patient characteristics or operative and anesthetic data (Table 1). The incidence of nausea was significantly lower 0 to 2 hours after surgery in the MP group than in the MR group (P < .05); however, there was no difference between the 2 groups 2 to 24 and 24 to 48 hours after surgery. The incidence of vomiting and the use of rescue antiemetics were also not significantly different between the 2 groups (Table 2). The severity of nausea, as measured on the 4-point scale, was significantly reduced at 0 to 2 hours in the MP group (P = .013) than in the MR group, and there was no difference in the severity of nausea between the 2 groups 2 to 24 and 24 to 48 hours after surgery (Table 3). There was no significant difference in the visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for postoperative pain or postoperative fentanyl use between the 2 groups (Table 4). The incidence of side effects, including headache and dizziness (group MR: 1/34, 2.9% vs group MP: 2/34, 5.9%), was similar in both groups.

4. Discussion

This study showed that the combined administration of midazolam and palonosetron provided superior antiemetic efficacy compared to the combination of midazolam and ramosetron regarding the incidence and severity of nausea 0 to 2 hours after laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

In our study, all the patients had risk factors for nonsmoking and postoperative opioid use. Moreover, surgical and anesthetic factors such as laparoscopic surgery and inhalation anesthesia also increase the incidence of PONV.[15] Apfel et al[16] recommended that additional effects can be obtained by co-administering antiemetic agents with different sites of action. Guidelines for PONV management published by the Society of Ambulatory Anesthesiology in 2020 recommended that PONV in high-risk groups should be actively prevented using a combination of at least 2 drugs.[4]

The vomiting center is located in the lateral reticular formation of the medulla and coordinates nausea and vomiting. This center receives various afferent inputs from the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ), cerebral cortex, nucleus solitarius, vestibular system, gastrointestinal tract, and peripheral pain receptors. Several neurotransmitters and receptors exists, such as serotonin, dopamine, acetylcholine, and neurokinin, in the vomiting center and CTZ.[2,17] Especially in laparoscopic surgery, mechanical stimuli such as CO2 pneumoperitoneum are associated with transient bowel ischemia and the secretion of emetogenic substances such as serotonin.[18] Serotonin can induce vomiting and increase intestinal motility by stimulating primary vagal afferent nerves via 5-HT3 receptors.[19] The most recently developed 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, ramosetron and palonosetron, have a high affinity for the receptor, are more effective, and have a longer duration of action than previous drugs.[20,21]

Midazolam is a commonly used anxiolytic agent and is also known to be effective in preventing PONV.[22] Midazolam’s mechanism of action as an antiemetic agent remains unclear. However, mechanisms that have been identified so far are as follows: midazolam reduces dopamine release in the CTZ by adenosine, and adenosine also reduces dopaminergic neuronal activity and 5-HT3 release by binding to the gamma-aminobutyric acid receptor.[23,24] One current meta-analysis reported that the incidence of PONV was significantly reduced when midazolam was administered with a known antiemetic agent than using the sole antiemetic, especially within 24 hours postoperatively.[8] Therefore, we assumed that a combination of midazolam and 5-HT3 antagonists would effectively prevent PONV, given the abovementioned properties of midazolam and 5-HT3 antagonists.

Previous studies have investigated the efficacy of midazolam in combination with 5-HT3 receptor antagonists for PONV prophylaxis. Park et al[25] showed that the proportion of patients without nausea during the 48-hours postoperative period was higher in the MR group than in the ramosetron alone group when high-risk patients underwent laparoscopic gynecological surgery. Kim et al[12] also showed that the severity of nausea was significantly reduced in the ramosetron (0.3 mg) and midazolam (75 μg/kg) combination group than in the ramosetron or midazolam group, especially in the early postoperative phase after thyroidectomy. In a study comparing palonosetron 0.075 mg and palonosetron with midazolam 0.05 mg/kg in female patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the incidence, and severity of PONV in the combination group were lower than those in the palonosetron alone group at 0 to 2 hours after surgery.[26] The above studies show that combination therapy with midazolam and 5-HT3 receptor antagonists is more effective than 5-HT3 receptor antagonist administration alone for PONV prophylaxis. However, comparing the effects of combination prophylaxis between the results of these studies is limited because each study had a different protocol and surgery type. Furthermore, the postoperative administration of opioids increases PONV in a dose-dependent manner,[27] and the use of opioid-containing intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (not applied in our study) can be another risk factor for PONV. Therefore, it should be noted that the prophylactic effect and duration of drugs can be interpreted differently depending on whether or not patient-controlled analgesia is applied after surgery in each study.

A recent study has shown that 5-HT3 receptor antagonists are effective for not only preventing PONV, but also for pain control. When intraperitoneal ondansetron was administered to patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy, PONV incidence and VAS score were significantly reduced 8 to 24 hours after surgery compared to patients from a placebo group.[28] It is believed that 5-HT3 receptor antagonists exert an analgesic effect by interfering with the peripheral effect of serotonin on pain sensation. In addition, it is known that the analgesic effects of drugs used for preventing PONV not only reduce opioid consumption, but also lead to a virtuous cycle which reduces opioid-induced nausea and vomiting.[29] Our study showed that there were no differences in VAS scores between the MR and MP groups, and the analgesic effects of different 5-HT3 receptor antagonists of the same class were considered similar when co-administered with midazolam. The analgesic effects of 5-HT3 receptor antagonists may vary depending on the route of drug administration and type of added rescue drugs.

In our study, we compared the combined treatment of midazolam with ramosetron and midazolam with palonosetron, and there was no statistical difference between the 2 groups in the incidence and severity of nausea, except during the postoperative 0 to 2 hours. Considering midazolam’s short half-life (1.5–2.5 hours), midazolam would be more effective in the early phase, especially during the postoperative 0 to 2 hours. In addition, the abovementioned unique properties and mechanism of action of palonosetron and midazolam showed a synergistic effect when administered in combination, suggesting that they are more effective in preventing PONV. In the late phase, the 5-HT3 antagonist, which has a relatively longer duration of action than midazolam, exhibited a main effect on the antiemetic mechanism. This could mean that combination treatment effectively prevents PONV regardless of the type of antiemetic drug combined with midazolam in the late phase. The results of several studies comparing PONV prophylaxis with ramosetron and palonosetron are varied and conflicting.[30–32] Moreover, a recent meta-analysis showed no difference in efficacy between the 2 groups.[33]

Various studies have shown that the optimal time for midazolam administration for PONV prevention is at the induction[34] or the end of surgery.[35] However, other studies have reported that the effect is similar at any point before surgery, at induction, or the end of surgery.[8,25] In our study, we administered midazolam (0.05 mg/kg) at induction, considering the surgery time of 30 to 40 minutes and the possibility of excessive postoperative sedation or delayed emergence. Splinter et al[36] recommended the effective antiemetic dose of midazolam as 35 to 75 μg/kg; however, there was no difference in reducing PONV between administering the lower dose (<0.05 mg/kg) and the higher dose (≥0.075 mg/kg) in the current meta-analysis.[8] Therefore, PONV can be effectively prevented at sub-hypnotic doses (<0.05 mg/kg) without undesirable side effects, such as delayed recovery or cognitive delay after surgery.

Our study had some limitations. The first limitation of our study was the small sample size. Second, effective doses of ramosetron or palonosetron for preventing PONV when combined with other antiemetic drugs are unknown. In addition, equipotent doses of ramosetron and palonosetron are unknown, and recommended doses for preventing nausea and vomiting after surgery were used to compare the efficacy of the 2 drugs. Further studies are required to determine the optimal dose of antiemetic agents, when combined, for PONV prophylaxis.

In conclusion, the combination of midazolam and palonosetron for PONV prophylaxis is more effective than the combination of midazolam and ramosetron for PONV prophylaxis in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy, especially in the early postoperative phase.
