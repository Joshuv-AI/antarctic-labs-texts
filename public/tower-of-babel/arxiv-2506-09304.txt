Fullshape power spectrum for the Symmetron modified gravity model
Jorge L. Cervantes-Cota

Abstract
We make use of the perturbation theory for modified gravity models that we developed in previous works and apply it to construct the fullshape galaxy power spectrum for the Symmetron modified gravity model. First, we study the growth rate, that is a scale dependent quantity, and compare our results with those of the n = 1 n=1 Hu-Sawcki (HS) model, finding that the Symmetron has a growth quite similar to the HS F6 in the wavenumber interval 0.01 ≤ k ≤ 0.1 0.01\leq k\leq 0.1 and for redshifts where Symmetron model is viable. We also propose a growth parametrization that turns to be a good approximation for the HS and Symmetron models, with a deviation less than 0.6 % 0.6\% . To compute the RSD multipoles we employ an expansion of the velocity moments generating function that is suitable for general modified gravity models. Later, we apply the fk-Perturbation Theory (fkPT) approximation to reduce the computation time of nonlinear kernels, to find the fullshape galaxy power spectrum for the Symmetron, and study the differences with HS model. The RSD multipoles of the Symmetron result similar to those of the HS F6 model. Next, we integrate this theory to an MCMC sampler and validate our results by fitting our parameters to EZMocks to recover the parameters that bring the model to GR. We found a similar agreement in the model validation between Symmetron and F6 model, recovering the simulation cosmological parameters, and concluding that our pipeline is ready to make cosmological parameters’ inference with real data.

It assumes that the way to understand the Universe on large scales is through the gravitational model based on general relativity (GR). Scale dependent growth rate

To see the effect of MG we can start with the differential equation for the density contrast:

\displaystyle\ddot{\delta}+2H\dot{\delta}-\frac{3}{2}\Omega_{m}H^{2}\delta=0,

4
Nonlinear MG contributions

The MG theories studied are determined by the modified Poisson equation
[
26
,
42
,
29
]
,

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\nabla^{2}\Phi=4\pi G\bar{\rho}\delta-\frac{1}{2a^{2}}\nabla^{2}\varphi,

(4.1)

where the bar above
\rho
stands for the dark matter energy density of the background,
\delta
its perturbation, and
\Phi
the gravitational potential, cf. eq. (
2.3
). The scalar field (
\varphi
) is coupled to the dark matter energy density through the Klein-Gordon equation, and when the quasi-static approximation is taken and considering the case of small-scale mode interactions, it can be written as
[
27
,
26
]

(3+2w_{\text{BD}})\frac{1}{a^{2}}\nabla^{2}\varphi=-8\pi G\bar{\rho}+\text{NL},

(4.2)

with NL being the possible non-linearities. Introducing eq. (
4.2
) into the Poisson equation, and expanded in Fourier space one obtains
[
36
]
,

-\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}\Phi=A(k,t)\delta(\vec{k})+S(\vec{k}),

(4.3)

where the function
A(k,t)
determines the linear model, whereas
S(\vec{k})
represents the nonlinear contribution. One identifies the function
A(k,t)
in the usual way by the function
A(k,t)=4\pi G\overline{\rho}\mu(k,t)
, where the function
\mu(k,t)
is more widely employed to parameterize the linear effects of different MG models. The origin of the
S(\vec{k})
function in the case of MG models is from non-linearities of the Klein-Gordon equation and it is responsible for the screening mechanism that enables to recover General Relativity at small scales. One can express
S(\vec{k})
in a Taylor series in Fourier space as shown in
[
36
]
,

S(\vec{k})=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\vec{k}_{12}=\vec{k}}\mathcal{S}^{(2)}(\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k}_{2})\delta(\vec{k}_{1})\delta(\vec{k}_{2})+\frac{1}{6}\int\limits_{\vec{k}_{123}=\vec{k}}\mathcal{S}^{(3)}(\vec{k}_{1},\vec{k}_{2},\vec{k}_{3})\delta(\vec{k}_{1})\delta(\vec{k}_{2})\delta(\vec{k}_{3})+\cdots,

(4.4)

with each
\mathcal{S}^{(n)}
term being determined once we specify the theory.

Inserting eq. (
4.3
) into the Euler and continuity equations, these are, in Fourier space given by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H}\frac{\partial\delta(\bm{k})}{\partial t}-f_{0}\theta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=f_{0}\underset{\bm{k}_{12}=\bm{k}}{\int}\alpha(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\theta(\bm{k}_{1})\delta(\bm{k}_{2}),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H}\frac{\partial f_{0}\theta(\bm{k})}{\partial t}+\left(2+\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\right)f_{0}\theta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle-\frac{A(k)}{H^{2}}\delta(\bm{k})-\frac{S(\bm{k})}{H^{2}}=f_{0}^{2}\underset{\bm{k}_{12}=\bm{k}}{\int}\beta(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\theta(\bm{k}_{1})\theta(\bm{k}_{2}),

(4.6)

where the
\theta(\bm{k})
function is the velocity field and
\delta(\bm{k})
is the matter field. Identifying the functions

\alpha(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})=1+\frac{\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2}}{k_{1}^{2}},\qquad\beta(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})=\frac{k_{12}^{2}(\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2})}{2k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}}.

(4.7)

Given the analytical expressions for
A(k,t)
and
S(\vec{k})
we can find perturbative solutions of
n
-orders using eqs. (
4.4
-
4.6
).

4.1
Hu-Sawicky f(R)

For Hu-Sawicky
f(R)
model the
A(k,t)
and
S(\bm{k})
functions are well known. Then, from the calculation of eq. (
4.4
) we can rewrite
S(\vec{k})
as follow,

\displaystyle S(\vec{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{6}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+a^{2}m^{2}(a)}{\delta\cal I},

(4.8)

where
m\equiv\sqrt{M_{1}/3}
,
M_{1}(k)
function encodes the gravitational pull at linear level and can be absorbed by the function
A(k,t)
and
{\delta\cal I}
only contains
M_{2,3}
functions responsible for the screening behavior and is given by:

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{I}(\delta f_{R})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta_{D}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{12})M_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\delta f_{R}(\bm{k}_{1})\delta f_{R}(\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{6}\int\frac{d^{3}k_{1}d^{3}k_{2}d^{3}k_{3}}{(2\pi)^{6}}\delta_{D}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{123})M_{3}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},\bm{k}_{3})\delta f_{R}(\bm{k}_{1})\delta f_{R}(\bm{k}_{2})\delta f_{R}(\bm{k}_{3})+\cdots.

(4.9)

The
M_{2,3}
functions are second and third order functions that are responsible for the screening mechanism and for this modified gravity model are given by:

\displaystyle M_{2}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{9}{4}\frac{H_{0}^{2}}{|f_{R0}|^{2}}\frac{(\Omega_{m}a^{-3}+4\Omega_{\Lambda})^{5}}{(\Omega_{m}+4\Omega_{\Lambda})^{4}},

(4.10)

\displaystyle M_{3}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{45}{8}\frac{H_{0}^{2}}{|f_{R0}|^{3}}\frac{(\Omega_{m}a^{-3}+4\Omega_{\Lambda})^{7}}{(\Omega_{m}+4\Omega_{\Lambda})^{6}}.

(4.11)

We note that these functions are only background quantities dependent, but this is a special feature of the Hu-Sawicki model.

4.2
Symmetron

In the Symmetron case we can obtain
A(k,t)
from the
\mu(k,t)
given in equation (
2.18
).

A(k,t)=4\pi G\bar{\rho}\left(1+\frac{2\beta^{2}(a)}{A(\phi)}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+a^{2}m^{2}(a)}\right).

(4.12)

It is important to mention that Symmetron model also features a screening and anti-screening mechanism
[
23
]
. Similarly as in the
f(R)
model we can compute the
M_{2,3}
functions responsible for the screenings, but unlike
f(R)
in the Symmetron model these functions depend on both scale and time (for more details on the following functions, see
[
23
]
). We will omit the time argument for simplicity visualization in the equations:

\displaystyle M_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2A\beta_{2}A_{0}}{\beta^{2}}\mathcal{K}_{\chi^{\delta}}^{(1)}(\bm{k}_{1})-\frac{A^{2}\kappa_{3}}{4\beta^{3}},

(4.13)

\displaystyle M_{3}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},\bm{k}_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3A\beta_{2}A_{0}}{\beta^{2}}\mathcal{K}_{\chi^{\delta}}^{(2)}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})-\frac{3A^{2}\beta_{3}A_{0}}{2\beta^{3}}\mathcal{K}_{\chi^{\delta}}^{(1)}(\bm{k}_{1})+\frac{A^{3}\kappa_{4}}{8\beta^{4}},

(4.14)

where
\mathcal{K}_{\chi^{\delta}}^{(1,2)}
are kernels given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\chi^{\delta}}^{(1)}(\bm{k}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A}{6a^{2}\beta^{2}}(k_{1}^{2}+m^{2}a^{2}),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\chi^{\delta}}^{(2)}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2A_{0}}M_{2}^{\text{FL}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2}).

(4.16)

The frame-lagging function
M_{2}^{\text{FL}}
is

M_{2}^{\text{FL}}=M_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})+\frac{9A}{4\beta^{2}A_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{K}_{\text{FL}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\Pi(\bm{k}_{1})\Pi(\bm{k}_{2}),

(4.17)

\mathcal{K}_{\text{FL}}
is the frame-lagging kernel of second order that depends on
A(k,t)
function;
\Pi(k_{i})\equiv\frac{1}{3a^{2}}(3k_{i}^{2}+M_{1}a^{2})
; and functions
\beta_{n},\kappa_{n}
defined in
[
55
]
:

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=M^{n}_{pl}~\frac{d^{n}A(\phi)}{d\phi},

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=M^{n-2}_{pl}\left(\frac{d^{n}V(\phi)}{d\phi}+\bar{\rho}~\frac{d^{n}A(\phi)}{d\phi}\right).

The
M_{2,3}
functions encode the screening and anti-screening mechanisms
[
23
]
and they are necessary to compute numerically the growth functions for the construction of the power spectrum up to 1-loop. The frame-lagging term arises when the transformation of the Klein-Gordon equation from Standard Eulerian Perturbation Theory (SPT) to Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (LPT) is performed and is necessary to recover
\Lambda
CDM on large scales
[
27
]
.

4.3
Full one-loop power spectrum

In the study of the PT when one considers a general MG theory, it is necessary to take into account the scale dependence in the growth rate even in the linear regime, as we showed in Section
3
.
In general, the PS for PT up to 1-loop is given by
[
36
]
:

P^{1-loop}_{ab}(k)=P^{L}_{ab}(k)+P^{22}_{ab}(k)+P^{13}_{ab}(k),

(4.18)

where
a,b
refer to matter or velocity fields and
P^{L}_{ab}(k)
is the linear power spectrum

P^{L}_{\delta\delta}(k)\equiv P_{L}(k),\quad P^{L}_{\delta\theta}(k)=\frac{f(k)}{f_{0}}P_{L}(k),\quad P^{L}_{\theta\theta}(k)=\left(\frac{f(k)}{f_{0}}\right)^{2}P_{L}(k),

(4.19)

whit
f_{0}=f(k=0,t)
and
P^{22}_{ab}(k)
and
P^{13}_{ab}
are the pure 1-loop contributions
[
36
,
29
]

\displaystyle P_{\delta\delta}^{22}(k)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\bm{p}}\big[F_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{k}-\bm{p})\big]^{2}P_{L}(p)P_{L}(|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|),

(4.20)

\displaystyle P_{\delta\theta}^{22}(k)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\bm{p}}F_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{k}-\bm{p})G_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{k}-\bm{p})P_{L}(p)P_{L}(|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|),

(4.21)

\displaystyle P_{\theta\theta}^{22}(k)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\bm{p}}\big[G_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{k}-\bm{p})\big]^{2}P_{L}(p)P_{L}(|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|),

(4.22)

\displaystyle P_{\delta\delta}^{13}(k)

\displaystyle=6P_{L}(k)\int_{\bm{p}}F_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})P_{L}(p),

(4.23)

\displaystyle P_{\delta\theta}^{13}(k)

\displaystyle=3P_{L}(k)\int_{\bm{p}}\big[F_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})G_{1}(\bm{k})+G_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})\big]P_{L}(p),

(4.24)

\displaystyle P_{\theta\theta}^{13}(k)

\displaystyle=6P_{L}(k)\int_{\bm{p}}G_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})G_{1}(\bm{k})P_{L}(p).

(4.25)

There are two approaches to obtain the SPT
F_{n}
and
G_{n}
kernels, the first is to calculate them using Euler and continuity equations and find
n
-order solutions by solving iteratively. The second way is to compute the LPT kernels, then doing the translation into the SPT kernels. Actually, the later approach was developed in
[
29
,
56
]
where they do the map from LPT to SPT kernels. In fact, the use of LPT is more convenient in the study of baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) features and the correlation function around the acoustic peak. Furthermore, unlike SPT, the PS of LPT can be transformed to Fourier space to obtain the correlation function. However, SPT follows better the broadband form of the PS and, in the case of LPT, it is observed that at small scales the PS decays quickly causing a deficit in the formation of structures at these scales. Both approaches have pros and cons.

Up to second order the
F_{2}
and
G_{2}
kernels are given by
[
47
,
36
,
22
]
:

\displaystyle F_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{14}\mathcal{A}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{14}\mathcal{B}\right)\frac{(\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2})^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}}+\frac{\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2}}{2k_{1}k_{2}}\left(\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}+\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right),

(4.26)

\displaystyle G_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{3\mathcal{A}(f_{1}+f_{2})+3\dot{\mathcal{A}}/H}{14f_{0}}+\left(\frac{f_{1}+f_{2}}{2f_{0}}-\frac{3\mathcal{B}(f_{1}+f_{2})+3\dot{\mathcal{B}}/H}{14f_{0}}\right)\frac{(\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2})^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2}}{2k_{1}k_{2}}\left(\frac{f_{2}}{f_{0}}\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}+\frac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right),

(4.27)

where
f_{1,2}=f(\bm{k}_{1,2})
. We can see the presence of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
functions that are time and scale dependent

\mathcal{A}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},t)=\frac{7D^{(2)}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},t)}{3D_{+}(k_{1},t)D_{+}(k_{2},t)},\qquad\mathcal{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},t)=\frac{7D^{(2)}_{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},t)}{3D_{+}(k_{1},t)D_{+}(k_{2},t)},

(4.28)

these functions arise because the relation
f^{2}=\Omega_{m}(t)
does not hold, with
D^{(2)}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},t)
and
D^{(2)}_{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2},t)
the second order growth functions being solution of the linear second order differential equations
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{\hat{\mathcal{T}}}-A(k)\big)D^{(2)}_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[A(k)+(A(k)-A(k_{1}))\frac{\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2}}{k_{2}^{2}}+(A(k)-A(k_{2}))\frac{\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{k}_{2}}{k_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{S}^{(2)}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\Bigg]D_{+}(k_{1})D_{+}(k_{2}),

(4.29)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{\hat{\mathcal{T}}}-A(k)\big)D^{(2)}_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\Big[A(k_{1})+A(k_{2})-A(k)\Big]D_{+}(k_{1})D_{+}(k_{2}).

(4.30)

For third order kernels the computation is more cumbersome in SPT and it is more convenient to do the map from the known LPT kernels, they are given by
[
47
,
36
,
22
]
:

\displaystyle F_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Gamma_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})-\frac{1}{6}\frac{(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{p})^{2}}{p^{4}}+\frac{1}{7}\frac{(\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{k}-\bm{p}))(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{p})}{|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|^{2}p^{2}}\Gamma_{2}(\bm{k},-\bm{p}),

(4.31)

\displaystyle G_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma^{f}_{3}(\bm{k},-\bm{p},\bm{p})+\frac{2}{7}\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{p}}{p^{2}}\Gamma^{f}_{2}(\bm{k},-\bm{p})+\frac{1}{7}\frac{f(p)}{f_{0}}\Gamma_{2}(\bm{k},-\bm{p})\frac{\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{k}-\bm{p})}{|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{7}\left[2\Gamma_{2}^{f}(\bm{k},-\bm{p})+\frac{f(p)}{f_{0}}\Gamma_{2}(\bm{k},-\bm{p})\right]\left[1-\frac{(\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{k}-\bm{p}))^{2}}{p^{2}|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{6}\frac{(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{p})^{2}}{p^{4}}\frac{f(k)}{f_{0}},

(4.32)

where

\Gamma^{f}_{n}(\bm{k}_{1},\cdots,\bm{k}_{n},t)\equiv\Gamma_{n}(\bm{k}_{1},\cdots,\bm{k}_{n},t)\frac{f(k_{1})+\cdots+f(k_{n})}{nf_{0}}+\frac{1}{nf_{0}H}\dot{\Gamma}_{n}(\bm{k}_{1},\cdots,\bm{k}_{n},t).

(4.33)

The
F_{n}
and
G_{n}
PT kernels are the way to compute the exact 1-loop contributions. However, the use of them increases the computational cost for the scale-dependent MG case, which is amplified when we estimate cosmological parameters using an MCMC code since differential equations have to be solved to find them.

On the other hand, to compute the galaxy PS from the dark matter PS, one introduces redshift space distortions (RSD), that account for the bias and the galaxy peculiar velocity. This behavior can be described by a velocity moments generating function
[
57
,
58
]
that can be expanded in many different ways to yield different approaches to RSD modeling
[
57
]
. We use this approach and further expand the perturbative approach with the use of the functions
I_{n}^{m}(k)

[
36
]
, to obtain

\displaystyle P_{s}^{\text{ME}}(k,\mu)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\mu^{2n}f_{0}^{m}I_{n}^{m}(k).

(4.34)

Furthermore, when we take account the EFT corrections and the shot-noise terms, we can write the full 1-loop expression for the PS, that is,

P_{s}(k,\mu)=P_{s}^{\text{ME}}(k,\mu)+P_{s}^{\text{EFT}}(k,\mu)+P_{s}^{\text{shot}}(k,\mu),

(4.35)

where the first term can be obtained by expanding eq. (
4.34
) with
n=2
, this results in

P_{s}^{\text{ME}}(k,\mu)=P_{\delta\delta}(k)+2f_{0}\mu^{2}P_{\delta\theta}(k)+f_{0}^{2}\mu^{4}P_{\theta\theta}(k)+A^{\text{TNS}}(k,\mu)+D(k,\mu).

(4.36)

The function
A^{\text{TNS}}
corresponding to the corrections to the non-linear Kaiser model
[
59
]
is

\displaystyle A(k,\mu)=2\mu kf_{0}\int_{\bm{p}}\frac{\bm{p}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}}{p^{2}}B_{\sigma}(\bm{p},-\bm{k},\bm{k}-\bm{p})\,,

(4.37)

where
B_{\sigma}
is the bispectra contributions given in
[
36
]
. The rest of the velocity moments contributions are placed in
D
function

\displaystyle D(k,\mu)=B(k,\mu)+C(k,\mu)-(\mu kf_{0}\sigma_{v})^{2}P^{K}_{s}(k,\mu).

(4.38)

wherein
[
59
]

\displaystyle B(k,\mu)

\displaystyle=(k\mu f_{0})^{2}\int_{\bm{p}}F(\bm{p})F(\bm{k}-\bm{p}),

(4.39)

\displaystyle F(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{p}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}}{p^{2}}\Bigg[P_{\delta\theta}(p)+f_{0}\frac{(\bm{p}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}})^{2}}{p^{2}}P_{\theta\theta}(p)\Bigg],

(4.40)

and

C(k,\mu)=(k\mu f_{0})^{2}\int_{\bm{p}}\frac{(\bm{p}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}})^{2}}{p^{4}}P_{\theta\theta}(p)P^{K}_{s}(|\bm{k}-\bm{p}|,\mu_{\bm{k}-\bm{p}}).

(4.41)

The second and third terms of eq. (
4.35
) are given by,

\displaystyle P_{s}^{\text{EFT}}(k,\mu)=\big(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{2}\mu^{2}+\alpha_{4}\mu^{4}+\alpha_{6}\mu^{6}\big)k^{2}P_{L}(k)+\tilde{c}\big(\mu kf_{0}\big)^{4}P^{K}_{s}(k,\mu),

(4.42)

\displaystyle P_{s}^{\text{shot}}(k,\mu)=\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\big(\alpha_{0}^{\text{shot}}+k^{2}\mu^{2}\alpha^{\text{shot}}_{2}\big),

(4.43)

with
\bar{n}
the average number density of galaxies. For the case of a Poisson distribution,
\alpha_{0}^{\text{shot}}=1
and
\alpha^{\text{shot}}_{2}=0
.

The above modeling of the PS is very successful in the study of the broadband. However, this is not useful for the Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) modeling because the damping effects due to long-wavelength displacements fields are not perturbative in a SPT scheme. To take account the BAO oscillations correctly, one employs infrared resummations (IR-resummations)
[
60
]
. The aim is to split the linear PS in a part without oscillations and other one with them, i.e., the wiggles removed piece,
P_{nw}
and the wiggles piece,
P_{w}
. Then the linear PS is
P_{L}=P_{nw}+P_{w}
. Thus, the one-loop IR-resummed PS is
[
61
]

\displaystyle P_{s}^{\text{IR}}(k,\mu)

\displaystyle=e^{-k^{2}\Sigma^{2}_{\text{tot}}(k,\mu)}P_{s}(k,\mu)+\big(1-e^{-k^{2}\Sigma^{2}_{\text{tot}}(k,\mu)}\big)P_{s,nw}(k,\mu)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-k^{2}\Sigma^{2}_{\text{tot}}(k,\mu)}P_{w}(k)k^{2}\Sigma^{2}_{\text{tot}}(k,\mu),

(4.44)

where

\Sigma^{2}_{\text{tot}}(k,\mu)=\big[1+\mu^{2}f\big(2+f\big)\big]\Sigma^{2}+\mu^{2}f^{2}(\mu^{2}-1)\delta\Sigma^{2},

(4.45)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{k_{s}}dp\,P_{nw}(p)\left[1-j_{0}\left(p\,\ell_{\text{BAO}}\right)+2j_{2}\left(p\,\ell_{\text{BAO}}\right)\right],

(4.46)

\displaystyle\delta\Sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{k_{s}}dp\,P_{nw}(p)j_{2}\left(p\,\ell_{\text{BAO}}\right).

(4.47)

where
j_{n}
is the spherical Bessel function of order
n
. eq.(
4.3
) is the final expression that was used to compare simulated halo data in
[
36
]
. In fact, the BAO scale is well determined and is given by
\ell_{\text{BAO}}\simeq 105\,h^{-1}\text{Mpc}
. Moreover, to fit data one uses the multipoles given by
[
22
]
:

P_{\ell}(k)=\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu\,P_{s}^{\text{IR}}(k,\mu)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu),

(4.48)

with
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
being the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
.

4.4
fk-kernels approximation

In the previous subsections, the complete theory for calculating the 1-loop power spectrum is presented, using an IR-resummed scheme. However, the calculation of the 1-loop contributions depends on the kernels
F_{n}
and
G_{n}
, which slow down the numerical computation.

Furthermore, when using the MCMC method, the calculation is further delayed. To optimize this process, the fkPT approach
[
47
]
was developed, where an approximation of the kernels is derived by considering only the
k
-dependence through the growth rate, that in turn is computed from eq. (
3.7
). This approximation is implemented in the codes, FOLPS-nu
3
3

3

https://github.com/henoriega/FOLPS-nu.git

[
37
]
for neutrinos and fkpt
4
4

4

https://github.com/alejandroaviles/fkpt.git

[
22
]
for HS-
f(R)
.

In the particular case of the full kernels for 1-loop contribution eqs. (
4.26
,
4.27
) and eqs. (
4.31
,
4.32
) was demonstrated in
[
47
]
that we can evaluate them on the limit in large scales (LS). Then, the fk-kernels are defined by taking
k=0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{2}^{\texttt{fk}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\Big|_{\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}^{\text{LS}}},

(4.49)

\displaystyle G_{2}^{\texttt{fk}}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=G_{2}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\Big|_{\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{\text{LS}}},

(4.50)

the values
\mathcal{A}^{\text{LS}}
,
\mathcal{B}^{\text{LS}}
are obtained from eqs. (
4.28
) for the second order kernels and
the same for
F_{3}
and
G_{3}
kernels with the corresponding
\mathcal{A}^{\text{LS}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\text{LS}}
expressions. Therefore, using the fk-approach for the Symmetron, we only need to calculate the scale-dependent growth rate
f(k,t)
and the limit of perturbative kernels at large scales. Also, using FFTLog method in FOLPS-nu one can speed up the calculation of loop integrals and hence, accelerate the numerical calculation in the analysis using an MCMC method.

Model validation: EZMocks

With the implementation of MG models in FOLPS-nu, using the fkPT approximation as we explained above, we want to make fittings with simulations. One might naively think of using some of the simulations of Symmetron model
[
68

. However, current simulations for this model are not sufficiently precise to constrain the MG parameters. In
[
69
,
17
]
, the authors compared the quasi-static approximation (QSA) against the non quasi-static finding that QSA has implications on the matter distribution via the fifth force. In
[
68
]
, three MG models are studied where Symmetron is one of them. In this work the aim is to construct an efficient and robust simulation code. Nevertheless, they only studied the linear matter PS and therefore that simulation is not suitable for testing the 1-loop RSD nor the IR-resummation scheme. For that reason we only tested our MG models with GR mocks, EZMocks
[
40
]
, to see if we recover GR from the Symmetron pipeline, as was done in
[
22
]
for
f(R)
, where the authors used NSERIES GR simulations that consist of 7 realizations for
z=0.5
and the same EZMocks for the computations of the covariances.

As part of the methodology, we need to calculate the covariance matrix. The cov matrix of the PS multipoles of one realization is computed by,

C_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}=\left\langle\left(\bar{P}_{\ell}(k_{i})-\hat{P}_{\ell}(k_{i})\right)\left(\bar{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}(k_{j})-\hat{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}(k_{j})\right)\right\rangle,

(5.4)

where the PS of a single realization at
k_{i}
is
\hat{P}(k_{i})
and
\bar{P}(k_{i})=\langle\hat{P}(k_{i})\rangle
is the mean over the ensemble of realizations at
k_{i}
.

For the covariance matrix construction, we use the 1000 EZMocks catalogs, also the covariance matrix is rescaled like
C_{N}=\frac{1}{N}C_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k_{i},k_{j})
with
N=7
, since we fit over the mean of 7 mocks. In addition, we apply the Hartlap factor
[
70
]
to the cov matrix.

6
Conclusions

The large-scale structure of the universe offers a unique arena for studying gravitational interactions through the growth rate and two-point statistics. It is of special interest nowadays when a hint of dark energy/modified gravity has been implied in the recent analysis of the DESI
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
and DES
[
8
]
collaborations, among other analyses. In the present work, we discuss the observational consequences of two MG models: the HS-
f(R)
model and the Symmetron, and compare our results with those of the standard
\Lambda
CDM model. Results for the HS model have been previously reported in the literature; however, we computed them here to compare them directly with those of the Symmetron.

At linear PT level, the gravitational modifications stem from two terms, the expansion rate and the source of the Poisson equation (
\mu(k,t)
term), and both effects are encoded in the equation for the dark matter density contrast or in the growth rate, see Section
3
. The growth rate for Symmetron, HS-
f(R)
, and
\Lambda
CDM models are presented in Figs.
2
and
3
, where in the former figure we plot it against redshift in two
k-
bins (
0.01\leq k\leq 0.05
and
0.06\leq k\leq 0.1

h~\text{Mpc}^{-1}
) and in the later against the wavenumber for three redshits (
0.8,0.5,0
) to evaluate its effect after the Symmetron phase transition occurrence at
z_{\text{ssb}}=1.0
. From these plots we conclude that the Symmetron has a similar effect in the growth as that of the HS F6 model. The HS F4 and F5 models have a greater gravitational effect in the growth, but they are almost ruled out by observations
[
46
]
, thus Symmetron remains viable to be tested. Both MG models can be parameterized through an analytical function, eq. (
3.9
), that turns out to be a good approximation for
k
-dependency, with an error less than
0.6\%
, cf. Fig.
4
. This formula could be of potential interest when using an MCMC code to infer cosmological parameters, since its analyticity can save computational time.

When we consider non-linear contributions in both models, HS-
f(R)
and Symmetron, there is an important difference between them in the scale dependence of
M_{i}
functions that appear in the nonlinear term,
S(\vec{k})
, in the Poisson equation (
4.3
). Whereas for HS model
M_{2,3}
are only time dependent, for the Symmetron both functions present
k
-dependence explicitly. Regardless of the specific
M_{i}
functions that characterize the MG model, the formalism presented in Section
4.3
remains applicable. However, the calculation of the 1-loop contributions requires numerical solutions of differential equations, which depend on the kernels
F_{n}
and
G_{n}
. This process can significantly slow down the MCMC-based inference of cosmological parameters. To alleviate this delay, we adapted the fkPT approximation scheme to the Symmetron. Further, we corrected the growth function that enters in the resummation scheme of Section
4.3
, correcting
f_{0}(t)
for
f(k,t)
, as is meant in the resummation procedure for MG. Then, our results for the HS-
f(R)
are new, and corrected those presented in Ref.
[
36
]
.

Next, we computed the RSD multipoles for the Symmetron, HS-
f(R)
, and
\Lambda
CDM models, and displayed our results in Figs.
5
and
6

for redshifts
z=0.5
and
1
. We find that the monopole and the quadrupole for both Symmetron and the HS F6 model are quite similar, corresponding to its similarity in their growth functions, discussed above. Finally, we continued to validate our pipeline with simulated data. However, we found no proper simulated data for the Symmetron model. Instead, we tested our pipeline with GR EZMocks data
[
40
]
to find out if Symmetron recovers GR results, i.e. as
\beta\rightarrow 0
, similar as it was done
[
22
]
for
f(R)
. Our results show that indeed the GR parameters are obtained. At present, the fullshape covariances suitable for the DESI DR1
[
72
]
or DR2 are not yet available to compute a proper parameter inference using real data, leaving this task for a future work.
