Successful Emergent Surgical Repair of Post–Myocardial Infarction Ventricular Septal Rupture With Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Michel Martins Braga Lima, Wanderson Maia da Silva, Julia Fernandes Aguiar, Nilson Tavares Poppi, Leonardo Vedovato Vilela de Salis, Júlio Zoé de Medeiros Brito, Elinthon Tavares Veronese, Antônio Fernando Diniz Freire, Paulo Rogério Soares, Thiago Luis Scudeler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-myocardial infarction (MI) ventricular septal rupture (VSR) is a rare mechanical complication associated with very high mortality, particularly when complicated by cardiogenic shock.Case SummaryA 58-year-old woman presented with anterior ST-segment elevation MI after failed thrombolysis, rapidly progressing to cardiogenic shock and cardiac arrest. Coronary angiography revealed proximal left anterior descending artery occlusion and a large mid-septal VSR. Despite refractory shock requiring vasopressors and intra-aortic balloon pump support, emergent surgical repair with bovine pericardial patch closure and concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting was performed. The patient experienced hemodynamic recovery, later requiring cardiac resynchronization therapy pacemaker for advanced atrioventricular block, and was discharged in stable condition.DiscussionEmergent surgical repair of post-MI VSR may be lifesaving even in extreme clinical scenarios when guided by rapid diagnosis and coordinated multidisciplinary care.Take-Home MessageEarly recognition and prompt multidisciplinary intervention are critical for survival in catastrophic post-MI VSR.

A 58-year-old woman presented to a secondary care hospital with 7 hours of severe chest pain and dyspnea. Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) showed anterior ST-segment elevation (V1-V6) with sinus rhythm and right bundle branch block (Figure 1A). Reperfusion failed, and the patient developed cardiogenic shock (CS) requiring norepinephrine and dobutamine, prompting transfer to our tertiary center.

On arrival, she was hypotensive despite vasoactive support (norepinephrine at 0.30 μg/kg/min and dobutamine at 15 μg/kg/min). Physical examination showed hypoperfusion with hypotension (86/54 mm Hg), and a heart rate of 86 beats/min. Cardiac auscultation identified a harsh holosystolic murmur along the left parasternal border, raising suspicion for mechanical complication.

ECG demonstrated persistent anterior ST-segment elevation with new left bundle branch block and atrioventricular (AV) dissociation (Figure 1B). High-sensitivity troponin was markedly elevated (47,979 ng/L; upper limit of normal: ≤16 ng/L). Bedside point-of-care ultrasound suggested a ventricular septal rupture (VSR), and the patient was transferred emergently to the catheterization laboratory.

Past Medical History

The patient had type 2 diabetes mellitus with poor glycemic control (hemoglobin A1c: 9.6%) and dyslipidemia, with elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (217 mg/dL). She had no history of coronary artery disease, prior cardiovascular events, or established microvascular or macrovascular diabetic complications.

Differential Diagnosis

The presentation was consistent with CS due to anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) with electrical and mechanical complications. The differential diagnosis included severe acute mitral regurgitation due to papillary muscle or chordal rupture; and left ventricular free-wall rupture or pseudoaneurysm, all representing catastrophic mechanical complications of myocardial infarction (MI).

Investigations

Coronary angiography demonstrated proximal left anterior descending occlusion and a moderate lesion in the proximal first obtuse marginal branch (Figure 2, Videos 1 and 2). Drug-eluting stent percutaneous coronary intervention of the left anterior descending was performed (Figure 3, Videos 3 and 4) with restoration of TIMI flow grade 3. The patient developed hemodynamic collapse and cardiac arrest, requiring resuscitation and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after approximately 6 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

Management

Despite successful revascularization, refractory CS persisted due to the large VSR. Advanced mechanical circulatory support was carefully considered. However, Impella was not available at our institution at the time of presentation. Furthermore, the anticipated delay required to initiate extracorporeal support, together with vascular access and logistical challenges in a profoundly unstable patient, led the heart team to proceed directly to emergent surgical intervention as the most time-sensitive and definitive strategy.

Emergent surgical repair was pursued because transcatheter closure was precluded by the large defect, friable necrotic tissue, and inadequate septal rims, and the patient's ongoing myocardial necrosis and profound hemodynamic instability mandated immediate definitive intervention.

A cardiothoracic surgeon was urgently consulted, and emergent surgical repair was performed 4 hours after percutaneous coronary intervention. Intraoperative inspection revealed a 30-mm full-thickness mid-septal rupture without valvular involvement (Figure 6). The defect was repaired with a bovine pericardial patch using interrupted polypropylene sutures, with additional reinforcement of the left ventricular free wall. Outcome and Follow-Up

Despite the high predicted operative risk, the patient demonstrated progressive clinical recovery. She was extubated on postoperative day 1. On postoperative day 3, she inadvertently removed the IABP without hemodynamic deterioration. Over the following days, vasoactive support was gradually discontinued.

Serial TTEs confirmed durable VSR repair, with slight improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction from 30% to 35% and persistent mild RV dysfunction. The patient was discharged after 24 days in stable condition on guideline-directed heart failure therapy. At 1-month follow-up, she reported only mild exertional dyspnea with higher physical activity.

Discussion

VSR remains one of the most catastrophic mechanical complications of acute MI and remains associated with poor outcomes despite modern therapy. This case illustrates that even in the setting of refractory CS complicated by cardiac arrest, post-MI VSR may still be amenable to definitive surgical repair when early recognition is followed by rapid, coordinated multidisciplinary intervention.

Post-MI ventricular septal rupture remains a rare but catastrophic complication with high mortality, particularly in CS.1,2 Contemporary incidence has declined3; however, outcomes remain poor, especially in patients with failed reperfusion and extensive anterior MI, as in this case.

Although VSR was classically described 3 to 5 days after infarction, early rupture is well recognized after extensive anterior STEMI and failed reperfusion.2,3 Established risk factors—including female sex, anterior STEMI, and unsuccessful reperfusion3—were present and should prompt heightened clinical vigilance. The sudden appearance of a harsh holosystolic murmur in a hemodynamically unstable patient should immediately raise suspicion for a mechanical complication.

Once suspected, rapid diagnostic confirmation is essential. Point-of-care TTE enables immediate bedside diagnosis and hemodynamic assessment,4 particularly in unstable patients in whom advanced imaging is impractical and should not delay definitive therapy.

Despite advances in surgical and percutaneous approaches, post-MI VSR remains associated with poor outcomes, with high in-hospital and operative mortality,4,5 particularly in CS.6 Although delayed surgical repair may benefit selected stable patients by allowing infarct maturation, this strategy is rarely feasible in shock, where urgent intervention is unavoidable despite prohibitive operative risk.7 In selected cases, transcatheter closure may serve as an alternative or bridge to surgery, providing temporary hemodynamic stabilization and shunt reduction.8,9 However, success depends on anatomic and clinical factors, including defect size, location, tissue integrity, and adequate septal rims for device anchoring. Large defects, friable necrotic myocardium, and complex mid-septal or basal ruptures are associated with lower success rates and higher risk of residual shunt or device instability.

In this case, transcatheter closure was not feasible due to the large defect size (≈30 mm), friable necrotic tissue, and mid-septal location with inadequate rims. Furthermore, the patient's rapidly progressive CS and ongoing myocardial necrosis favored immediate surgical repair as the only definitive and time-sensitive strategy.

Accordingly, management of post-MI VSR requires a coordinated heart team approach. In this case, emergent surgical repair was performed despite recent cardiac arrest and refractory shock, reflecting a deliberate balance between balancing prohibitive operative risk against the poor prognosis associated with conservative management. Concomitant surgical revascularization was performed to address residual ischemia, a strategy supported by observational data suggesting reduced early mortality and potential long-term ventricular benefit.5,10

Finally, this case highlights important electrophysiological considerations. Device selection required individualized clinical judgment. Although current guidelines recommend reassessment of left ventricular function after the early post-MI period, this patient developed persistent high-grade AV block and left bundle branch block with an anticipated near-continuous ventricular pacing requirement, implying a substantial RV pacing burden. In this context, conventional RV pacing carried a high risk of worsening electrical dyssynchrony and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy; therefore, cardiac resynchronization therapy with a pacemaker was selected.

Cardiac magnetic resonance was not performed during the index hospitalization. However, 4 TTEs were performed during hospitalization, including a follow-up study 18 days after the acute event, all demonstrating persistent left ventricular ejection fraction and unchanged structural abnormalities, suggesting a low likelihood of meaningful ventricular recovery. This assessment was further supported by the prolonged ischemic time, extensive anterior-septal infarction involving the conduction system, and persistent conduction disease.

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation was deferred because no sustained ventricular arrhythmias occurred, and randomized trials have not demonstrated a survival benefit from prophylactic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation in the early post-MI period.

Conclusions

Post-MI VSR requires rapid recognition and coordinated multidisciplinary management. In patients with refractory CS, emergent surgical repair remains the definitive and potentially lifesaving therapy.
