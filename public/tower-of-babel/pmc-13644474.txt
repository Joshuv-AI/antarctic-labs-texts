Extraterrestrial amino acids evolve differently in asteroid Bennu and Ryugu: Possible mechanisms and future perspectives
Authors: Yamei Li, Norio Kitadai, Jinfei Yu, Pengfei Zhang
Published: 2026

The origin of life on early Earth remains unsolved. One of the fundamental questions is the sources of life’s building blocks. Amino acids, the backbone of biological proteins, must have been supplied at some point. In addition to diverse endogenous pathways for synthesizing amino acids through geochemical processes on the early Earth, extraterrestrial delivery of amino acids from carbonaceous chondrites (CCs) or comets has also been considered a contributor to the amino acid inventory. So far, 14 types of proteinogenic amino acids have been detected in primitive CCs [1]. Comets also contain glycine and alanine [2]. More importantly, only these extraterrestrial meteoritic materials were found to contain amino acids with L-enantiomeric excess that can reach up to ∼20% [3,4]. Since biological proteins only use left-handed α-amino acids (hereafter named proteinogenic amino acids), it has long been proposed that extraterrestrial amino acids could have contributed to the origin of life’s homochirality.

Despite isotopic, isomeric, and enantiomeric data supporting the indigeneity of amino acids in CCs, concerns remain about the sources and extent of terrestrial contamination. In this regard, obtaining pristine samples from carbonaceous asteroids, which are considered the parent bodies of CCs, is important for eliminating terrestrial contamination. Asteroids are the fragments from collisions involving planetesimals. In 2023, NASA’s OSIRIS-Rex mission brought the surface material of the asteroid Bennu (thereafter denoted as Bennu sample) back to Earth. Bennu samples were carefully stored in a clean, deoxygenated atmosphere, which has been shown to be critical for preserving organics against terrestrial contamination [1]. The recent paper by Glavin et al., reported the first analysis of organic compounds in Bennu sample [1]. Bennu samples were found to contain 14 types of proteinogenic amino acids and all 5 canonical nucleobases, confirming the potential roles of such extraterrestrial materials in seeding life’s building blocks on early Earth. Since JAXA’s Hayabusa 2 mission also brought samples from another organic- and water-rich asteroid, Ryugu, just 3 years ago, comparing these two precious samples can help deepen our understanding of the diversity and evolution of amino acids on primitive asteroids.

Comparison of amino acid distributions between Bennu, Ryugu, and previously reported chondrites

Bennu contains more proteinogenic amino acids, nearly twice the number found in Ryugu (8 types) [5] (Table 1). Bennu samples carry glycine (Gly), alanine (Ala), β-alanine (β-Ala), aspartic acid (Asp), and glutamic acid (Glu) as the dominating species [1]. In contrast, Ryugu samples contain non-proteinogenic amino acids as the dominating species, including primarily β-Ala and γ-aminobutyric acid (γ-ABA) [5]. The total amino acid abundance in Bennu (70 nmol/g) is about five times higher than Ryugu (15 nmol/g) [1].

These new findings have raised several questions. The effects of aqueous alteration on amino acid distribution in these two asteroid samples deviate from predictions derived from CCs. CCs with petrographic types 1–3 feature different degrees of aqueous alteration, with a higher aqueous alteration degree corresponding to a lower petrographic grade. The characterization of organic-rich CCs and classification into different groups has improved understanding of the origin of organics in the parent planetesimals. Generally, type 2 CCs with moderate degrees of alteration contain the highest abundances of proteinogenic amino acids and exhibit greater chemical diversity [4,6]. With increasing degrees of aqueous alteration, amino acid abundances tend to decrease, accompanied by simplified distributions that favor non-α amino acid isomers.

The higher relative abundance of α-amino acids in Bennu than in Ryugu suggests Bennu experienced a lower degree of aqueous alteration than Ryugu. However, both Ryugu and Bennu were found to be heavily aqueously altered, as reflected in their modal mineralogy, dominated by phyllosilicates, with other secondary minerals (e.g., carbonates, sulfides, magnetite) expected to have formed by aqueous alteration of primordial minerals. Moreover, the elemental and oxygen isotope compositions of both samples are similar to those of CI chondrites, reflecting a primitive, Sun-like composition [7]. Spectral features at similar wavelengths in the spectra of Polana, Bennu, and Ryugu support the hypothesis that both asteroids originated in the Polana family [8]. Resolving the apparent divergences in the evolutionary pathways of minerals and organic matter between these two asteroid samples remains a fundamental challenge.

The parent planetesimals of both Ryugu and Bennu likely formed in the outer solar system, as suggested by the observation of CO2-bearing inclusions in Ryugu and the high abundances of ammonia and carbonate in Bennu. After accretion, the parent bodies of Ryugu and Bennu underwent aqueous alteration due to ice melting, driven by internal heat (e.g., radioactive decay of 26Al). Once the ice has melted, amino acids can be synthesized in aqueous solution using released primordial molecules as precursors, such as hydrogen cyanide, ammonia, and formaldehyde, which were originally condensed in ice in the outer solar system. These synthetic pathways mainly generate α-amino acids as the dominating species. Aqueous generation of non-proteinogenic amino acids, particularly for β-Ala and γ-ABA, is limited to formose-type reactions, with lower abundances than proteinogenic amino acids. Therefore, these synthetic pathways alone cannot account for the relatively higher concentrations of β-Ala and γ-ABA in Ryugu.

Glavin et al. attributed the distinct amino acid distributions between Bennu and Ryugu samples to the relatively lower temperatures on Bennu compared to Ryugu [1]. Notably, both Bennu and Ryugu feature relatively low alteration temperatures (<50 °C). In Bennu, high concentrations of ammonium salts could have formed liquid brines at very low temperatures (the NH3—H2O eutectic at −97 °C), thus allowing aqueous organic chemistry to continue. At such low temperatures, reaction rates are expected to decrease; however, studies on amino acid synthesis via HCN polymerization at −20 and −80 °C have only reported the formation of proteinogenic amino acids [9], which thus cannot explain the amino acid differences. The remaining question is, why Bennu and Ryugu are so different in amino acid compositions despite similar upper temperature limits (∼50 °C) and aqueous alteration degrees?

Amino acid molecular descriptors that dictate evolutionary processes

To explore the underlying mechanism that dictates amino acid evolution, a quantitative descriptor is necessary to characterize the chemical nature of amino acid distribution patterns. Glavin et al. have employed the [β-Ala]/[Gly] abundance ratio as a descriptor, with markedly higher values in Ryugu samples and CI chondrites than in Bennu samples [1]. However, because the chemical pathways linking these two molecules are unclear, these ratios serve primarily as empirical descriptors. Recently, an alternative molecular descriptor, namely the abundance ratios [β-Ala]/[Asp] and [γ-ABA]/[Glu], has been proposed to track the effects of aqueous alteration [10]. β-Ala and γ-ABA were products generated by decarboxylative breakdown of Asp and Glu, respectively: C4H7NO4 (Asp) → C3H7NO2 (β-Ala) + CO2 and C5H9NO4 (Glu) → C4H9NO2 (γ-ABA) + CO2.

The breakdown chemistry of dicarboxylic amino acids (Asp and Glu) provides chemical linkages among these four amino acids. By analyzing the relative abundances of β-Ala and γ-ABA versus their dicarboxylic amino acid precursors, the relative importance of both synthetic and breakdown chemistry can be assessed. Notably, the Bennu sample exhibits relatively low ratios of [β-Ala]/[Asp] and [γ-ABA]/[Glu], similar to those reported in all 19 CMs (CM1, CM2.5-2.9) and less altered CR chondrites (CR 2.7-2.8). Importantly, these ratios are close to the reported values generated by hydrothermal synthesis experiments (FS_90 and FS_150) and the product generated by the UV-irradiated interstellar ice analog (ISM_UV) (Fig. 1). Therefore, the low ratios in these CCs and Bennu suggest that these proteinogenic amino acids (Asp and Glu) were well preserved during the aqueous alteration period, and the recorded amino acid distribution patterns were governed by hydrothermal syntheses and/or pre-accretionary interstellar reactions.

In contrast, remarkable enrichments of β-Ala and γ-ABA relative to Asp and Glu were observed in 2 Ryugu samples (Ryugu_1–2), 3 CI1 (Orgueil_1–3), and 2 heavily aqueously altered CR chondrites (Fig. 1). These enrichments suggest that decomposition of amino acids has occurred via decarboxylation and dominantly shaped the amino acid distribution. Based on these statistically significant features, the decomposition-dominated chemistry in Ryugu samples and CI chondrites strongly contrasts with the synthesis-dominated processes in Bennu samples. Since they share similarly high degrees of aqueous alteration, the extent of aqueous alteration should not be the key factor that results in the chemical difference. Although the decomposition reactions of Asp and Glu have been reported via hydrothermal reactions, they typically occur at elevated temperatures (>120 °C) [[11], [12], [13]]. Moreover, hydrothermal decomposition of Glu does not generate γ-ABA; rather, pyroglutamate, α-hydroxyglutarate, α-ketoglutarate, and succinic acid are the main products, formed via dehydration, cyclization, and deamination [13]. Therefore, hydrothermal decomposition of Asp and Glu cannot account for the occurrence of β-Ala and γ-ABA with high abundances at low temperatures. To the best of our knowledge, electrochemical decomposition catalyzed by iron sulfide and nickel sulfide is, so far, the only mechanism that shows the simultaneous conversion of Asp-to-β-Ala and Glu-to-γ-ABA at a relatively low temperature (25 °C), close to the alteration temperatures of Ryugu and Bennu [10].

Possible core-mantle differentiation mechanisms that account for the amino acid distributions

How did such an electrochemical process happen? Icy planetesimals with sufficiently large sizes (e.g., >200 km) likely experienced physical water/rock differentiation, similar to Ceres, resulting in the formation of a rock-rich core (with a low water/rock (W/R) mass ratio, <1) and a watery mantle (with a high W/R ratio, >1). Here we hypothesize that Bennu and Ryugu might originate from parent bodies with similar rock composition; however, they may originated from different locations or bodies with different sizes. Similar initial rock compositions resulted in similar modal mineralogy after aqueous alteration; nevertheless, differences in W/R ratios and locations led to different H2 fugacities, which affected amino acid stability and distribution. Specifically, Ryugu might originate from the outer region of a water/rock-differentiated parent body (Fig. 2B). Through water/rock differentiation, the rock-rich core can facilitate the efficient generation of H2 gas by reducing water with Fe2+ in the primordial anhydrous silicates. In contrast, a high W/R ratio condition, as in the water-rich mantle, would suppress the H2-generation efficiency attributable to dilution of Fe2+. The steep chemical gradient of fH2 between the core and mantle provided electrochemical potential differences. In the rock-rich core, oxidation of H2 generated electrons, which can be transferred through electrically conductive minerals (e.g., sulfides, magnetite, metals) to the mantle fluid/mineral interface, thereby driving the Asp-to-β-Ala and Glu-to-γ-ABA conversions in the mantle, as experimentally demonstrated [10]. Therefore, Ryugu’s high [β-Ala]/[Asp] and [γ-ABA]/[Glu] ratios likely suggest that it came from the mantle material of a water/rock differentiated body. After the water activity ceased, the primordial parent body retained the gradient structure and then fragmented to generate secondary asteroids with different materials sourced from the mantle and core material (Fig. 2C, 2D).

On the other hand, Bennu may have originated from the core material of a water/rock differentiated body. The lower W/R ratio and higher pressure in the core enhance the generation and concentration of H2 gas, which has been demonstrated to stabilize amino acids [13]. The rocky core area may sustain a longer aqueous phase due to the thermal gradient and continued heat supply from radiogenic 26Al in the rock. The longer duration of aqueous activity would have promoted amino acid synthesis, which is consistent with the higher abundances and diversity of amino acids observed in Bennu samples [1]. Thus, it is possible that Bennu originated mainly from the core material of the progenitor planetesimal where Asp and Glu are effectively synthesized and well preserved.

Such core-mantle differentiation scenarios can also help explain Bennu’s salt composition. Bennu contains large amounts of evaporite minerals (e.g., Ca- and Mg-carbonates, Na-carbonates, halides, and sulfates) [14], which are present only in trace amounts or are absent in Ryugu. Assuming Bennu originated from the rocky core region, two mechanisms can facilitate the salt generation. First, the higher rate of serpentinization under high-pressure, high-temperature conditions can promote the leaching of mobile elements from the primordial minerals. Second, the thermal gradient would further concentrate these mobile ions in the core relative to the mantle due to higher solubility in the hotter region, as suggested by the enrichment of fluid-mobile elements in some particles of Bennu samples [7]. These mobile ions will ultimately become salts after sublimation/evaporation of ice/water.

Another possibility is that Bennu’s parent body has not experienced physical differentiation. In this case, the chemical gradient of fH2 won’t form, so electrochemical decomposition of Asp and Glu won’t occur. This is possible if the planetesimal is small and has low gravity (e.g., <100 km). This possibility is high, given that Bennu has large-scale carbonate veins, suggesting kilometer-scale fluid activity and a high water/rock ratio [15]. However, such a scenario requires an explanation for the high abundance of salts, which may be associated with primordial sources, as detected in comets [16].

Chirality of amino acids in asteroidal samples

Another important finding that contradicts the expected trend in CCs is that all chiral amino acids, isovaline in particular, do not show enantiomeric excess in both Ryugu and Bennu. In CCs, an empirical trend has been observed: the enantiomeric excess of L-isovaline increases with a greater degree of aqueous alteration [3,4]. Thus, in light of the high aqueous alteration degrees of Ryugu and Bennu, a different mechanism independent of aqueous alteration is needed to explain such discrepancy. Post-alteration via space weathering could be a possible reason, as cosmic-ray radiation has been shown to racemize amino acids [17]. The space weathering process has been proposed to explain the textural and spectral slope features [7] and water contents in asteroids. Although Ryugu exhibits alteration levels similar to those of Type-1 chondrites, analyses of its samples indicate it experienced some degree of dehydration or has limited water content [18]. This indicates that during later evolutionary processes, it underwent prolonged dehydration, thermal processing, and/or space weathering. Notably, laboratory experiments simulating space weathering processes have reported alterations in organic matter, including the degradative generation of aromatic species [19] and an overall decrease in organic content [20]. Therefore, the similar degrees of alteration and evidence of space weathering in both Ryugu and Bennu suggest they may have shared a common post-alteration processing history.

Other possible mechanisms that can be tested by future missions to small bodies

Other possibilities for the sources of amino acids should also be discussed. Carbonaceous asteroids may have accreted icy, volatile-rich components by two mechanisms. The first is the accretion in the outer solar system, and the other is the impact of carbonaceous materials after the body moved to the inner solar system. So far, we have only discussed the first possibility. Supposing that the latter mechanism is also possible, the different amino acid compositions between Ryugu and Bennu samples may reflect the different compositions of exogenous carbonaceous materials that were accreted during their inward migration and/or collision with other bodies. This possibility, however, lacks definitive evidence yet. Furthermore, the impact of silicate-rich materials may also result in the alteration of organic inventory due to the high temperature and high pressure conditions generated during impact process, as demonstrated in recent studies [21]. Nevertheless, obtaining carbonaceous components with different amino acid compositions remains as a possibility, which can be teste by future space exploration missions to asteroids and comets, including the ongoing Tianwen-2 mission that will target asteroid 2016 HO3 and comet 311P/PanSTARRS, the DESTINY⁺ mission that will target asteroid 3200 Phaethon and the Lucy mission toward Trojan asteroids, together with the upcoming MMX mission to Phobos.

Outlook on future research

Taken together, Bennu may represent a very precious source of interior samples that were heavily aqueously altered in a water/rock differentiated parent body. Alternatively, Bennu may originate from a non-differentiated parent planetesimal of relatively small size. Each scenario needs to be reconciled with more geochemical and mineralogical observations. For example, the breakdown chemistry of Asp and Glu into β-Ala and γ-ABA under simulated parent-body conditions with different W/R ratios and mineral compositions should be investigated in more detail. The impacts of mineral assemblages derived from different W/R conditions on the S- and N-incorporation into soluble organic matter would also provide critical insights into trends in the diversity and complexity of soluble organic compounds, as have been found to be distinct between Ryugu and Bennu [1]. Additionally, the analysis of insoluble organic matter, the major fraction of carbonaceous materials, would also provide important insights into the divergent evolution of these two asteroid samples. The difference in amino acid distributions between Bennu and Ryugu also suggests that the contributions of carbonaceous chondrites to life’s emergence on the early Earth are not the same and depend heavily on their evolutionary histories on their parent bodies. In particular, the real contributions of CCs to seeding and amplifying the enantiomeric excess of protein amino acids remain to be clarified.
