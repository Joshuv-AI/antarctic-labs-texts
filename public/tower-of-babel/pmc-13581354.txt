Trends in Intrauterine Device Use Before and After National Eligibility Guideline Changes
Authors: Kandice A. Kapinos, Gabriela Alvarado, Kortney Floyd James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Updates to national clinical guidelines expanding intrauterine device (IUD) eligibility to nulliparous women were associated with increased IUD use, whereas use among parous women plateaued after 2014.

Long-acting reversible contraceptives, which include intrauterine devices (IUDs) and etonogestrel implants, are among the most effective nonpermanent contraceptive methods, with failure rates of 0.8% and 0.05%, respectively.1 Despite their effectiveness, IUD use in the United States has fluctuated over the decades, declining to less than 1% in the mid-1990s.2 Recent estimates indicate a resurgence, with IUD and implant use at 8.4% and 2%, respectively.3

The relatively low rates of IUD use, particularly among younger populations, may be partly attributed to historical guidelines that discouraged the use of IUDs in nulliparous women—those who have not previously given birth—until 2010. Although the World Health Organization published the evidence-based Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use in 1996 affirming that IUDs were safe and effective for most women,4 U.S. clinical guidelines remained age and parity based until 2010. Guidelines in the United States cited concerns about expulsion in nulliparous women and the risk of sexually transmitted infections among adolescents despite a growing body of evidence demonstrating low expulsion rates and no increased risk of infection for nulliparous and younger women.5 Formal changes emerged between 2010 and 2014 from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC),6 the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) in 2011,7 and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).8 By 2014, U.S. guidelines explicitly endorsed IUDs “for almost all women,” including adolescents and nulliparous women.6–8

Evidence from clinician surveys and claims data suggests that, although IUD use increased in the postguideline period, adoption was uneven and incomplete. The literature has separately examined clinician and patient response, with clinician-level evidence suggesting a slow change in clinical concordance with guidelines: Between 12% and 33% of clinicians reported IUDs as safe and appropriate for nulliparous women following the guideline changes.9,10 Among patients, population-level uptake increased, but effect sizes varied widely across settings, payers, and populations ranging from nearly 0 to 10 percentage points.11,12 Critically, these studies share a common limitation, isolating the effect of the guideline changes from concurrent trends, including increases due to secular and policy changes such as the passage of the Affordable Care Act in 2010, remains difficult regardless of study design.11,12

The objective of this study was to examine whether the national guideline updates between 2010 and 2014 expanding IUD eligibility to nulliparous women and adolescents were associated with differential changes in IUD use by parity, using an interrupted time series analysis of nationally representative survey data and a parous comparison group to account for concurrent trends in contraceptive use.

METHODS

Cross-sectional data from the 2002 through 2022–2023 waves of the National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG) were used to examine changes in contraceptive use among sexually active females. The NSFG is the primary U.S. federal data source for national estimates of contraceptive use and reproductive health, fielded continuously by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) among the civilian, noninstitutionalized population 15–44 years of age using a multistage, stratified area probability sampling design with oversampling of teenagers, non-Hispanic Black individuals, and Hispanic individuals to improve subgroup precision.13 Data are collected through in-person interviews; sensitive items, including contraceptive and sexual behavior, are administered through audio computer-assisted self-interview, which has been shown to reduce social desirability bias compared with interviewer-administered questions.14 Before 2006, the NSFG was fielded in discrete cycles (including 2002); beginning with the 2006–2010 cycle, data collection became continuous, enabling analysis of year-to-year trends. Contraceptive use is captured through self-report of current or most recently used method, classified with standardized NCHS algorithms. This study used publicly available, deidentified data and did not constitute human subjects research as defined under 45 CFR 46.102(e); IRB review was therefore not required.

The sample was restricted to individuals who had no missing data on contraceptive use or key covariates (Appendix 1, available online at https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985). The NSFG uses “female” to describe study participants based on self-reported sex assigned at birth; we follow this convention throughout, using women and adolescents in interpretive sections where age- or life stage–specific language aids clarity.

Our main outcome was a mutually exclusive categorization of the most effective (lowest failure rate) contraceptive method most recently used (following NSFG algorithms).15 We examined trends in each method. The primary exposure measure was parity. In the main analyses, we aggregated the data to age group and parity group by year, which allowed us to compare nulliparous (those with no previous live births) with those with two or more previous live births. We excluded those with only one previous live birth for two reasons. First, under the pre-2010 U.S. guidelines, these individuals would have been considered eligible for IUD use (ie, they would not have been “treated”).6 Second, women with exactly one prior birth have the highest rates of stated desire for additional children among parous women,15 and clinicians have historically cited expected future fertility (regardless of formal guidelines) as a reason to counsel against IUDs. 16–18 Thus, excluding these individuals from the comparator group minimizes contamination risk. However, we included these individuals in sensitivity analyses, and results are qualitatively similar (Appendices 2 and 3, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985). Age groups included 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, and 40–44 years.

We calculated rates of contraceptive use for each age and parity group in each interview year and estimated interrupted time series models testing for both an immediate level change and a change in the slope after the guideline dates (2010–2014; see below) with an interaction term capturing postperiod trend divergence. We tested for first-order autocorrelation using the Cumby–Huizinga test19,20 and adjusted for autocorrelation when present using the Prais–Winsten estimator.21

The guideline updates from 2010 to 2014 unfolded in a sequence that involved distinct clinical channels. Although the AAP issued its long-acting reversible contraceptive policy statement after the CDC and ACOG (in 2014), this is of particular relevance given that adolescents—the age group most likely to be nulliparous—receive the majority of their primary care from pediatricians,22 who play a critical role in contraceptive counseling and referral even when insertion occurs with a specialist.23 We therefore used 2014 as our primary intervention date to capture the cumulative effect of the full guideline sequence. We used 2010 and 2011 as well (Appendices 4 and 5, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985). Because the guidelines were issued in close succession and operate through overlapping but distinct clinical mechanisms, precise attribution to any single source is not possible.

In interrupted time series analysis, each age–parity group of sexually active women serves as its own control group: the preperiod level and slope are estimated separately and extrapolated to create that group's counterfactual, reflecting expected trends absent the guideline change. This framework does not require parallel preperiod trends across groups.24,25 The primary inference, therefore, rests on whether the within-group trajectory among nulliparous women accelerated beyond what their own preperiod trend would predict. The parous comparison provides supplementary evidence that any observed acceleration was not driven by secular trends affecting all women equally. However, secular trends that differentially affect nulliparous and parous women cannot be ruled out. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the primary model stratified by insurance status (insured vs uninsured) to assess whether Affordable Care Act–related coverage gains differentially confounded the comparison by parity (Appendix 6, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985).

All analyses were conducted with Stata MP 18.5. This study used deidentified publicly available data and was thus exempt from IB oversight. The study adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guideline for observational studies (Appendix 7, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985).

RESULTS

The analytic sample included 31,050 sexually active women who were not pregnant. When we aggregate these women to the age by parity and year level, we have 993 age–parity–year observations in our main analysis. In Table 1, we show the mean percent of sexually active women using each type of contraceptive for each parity group over the full sample. The three parity groups have significantly different distributions of use. Both nulliparous individuals and those with only one previous live birth used other hormonal methods most frequently but at different rates (49.9% vs 31.4%). Permanent methods were the most commonly used among women with two or more previous live births (50.6%).

Figure 1 depicts the interrupted time series results for IUD use. Among the sexually active nulliparous women, rates of IUD use were initially very low, nearly 0% in 2002, and increased slowly in the pre-period with a slope of 0.4% points per year (95% CI, −0.003% to 0.7%). There was an immediate level increase of IUD use by 6.1% points in 2014, which was followed by an annual subsequent increase of 1.32%/y (95% CI, 0.8–1.8%/y) reaching 17.3% (95% CI: 13.0-21.5%) by 2023. Because preperiod trends differed for sexually active parous women (two or more previous births), consistent with distinct contraceptive histories of nulliparous and parous women, we present parous results as a partial rather than strict comparator. In 2014, there was a 3.3 percentage point drop in IUD use among this group, followed by little change thereafter; this distinct pattern is inconsistent with a shared secular trend driving the post-2014 increase among nulliparous women.

Of note, we did not find similar patterns in use of other contraceptives (Table 2). Condom use without other methods (also shown in Fig. 2) among sexually active nulliparous women was initially 20.33% (95% CI, 17.2–23.5%) and stayed relatively flat before 2014. However, use declined by nearly 6% (95% CI, −11.6% to 0.2%) immediately after the guideline change and then by 0.56% (95% CI, −1.1% to −0.06%) per year in the postperiod. Among the sexually active parous counterparts, condom use was significantly lower initially at 13.5% (95% CI, 10.1–16.8%), but there was also no change in that rate over time. Use of other hormonal contraceptives (not including IUDs) was nearly half (49.3%, 95% CI, 43.6–55.0%) of sexually active nulliparous women initially and was relatively stable over time without a significant level or slope change (Appendix 8, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985). Rates of use of other hormonal methods among parous women were about half the rate of nulliparous women, declining slightly over time. Patterns in use of less effective methods did not differ significantly between parity groups (Appendix 8, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985). Reports of no contraceptive use appeared to increase during the postperiod (slope change 0.68%, 95% CI, 0.06–1.29%) among parous women while barely changing among nulliparous women (Appendix 8, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985).

As sensitivity check, we also estimated these models using 2010 (CDC) and 2011 (ACOG) as the “treatment” years. The IUD results were qualitatively similar with a slope change of 1.33% (95% CI, 1.03–1.63%) and 1.37% (95% CI, 1.03–1.7%) after the CDC and ACOG change, respectively. The immediate or level changes were smaller and statistically insignificant in the earlier years, which reflects the dynamic nature of the behavioral responses (Appendices 4 and 5, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985). Similarly, there was little difference in these postperiod trends among the parous women regardless of intervention date used.

As a second sensitivity check, we also included parous women with only one prior birth, and the postperiod slope for IUD use was similar, increasing 1.5% (95% CI, 0.8–2.2%) annually (Appendix 3, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985).

As a third sensitivity check, we stratified the primary analysis by insurance status. The differential post-guideline trend increase for nulliparous compared with parous women was present among both insured (1.1 percentage points/y, 95% CI, 0.5–1.7%) and uninsured (+1.6 percentage points/y, 95% CI, 0.03–2.9%) women, with no significant difference between groups (P=.624; Appendix 6, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E985).

DISCUSSION

National guideline updates between 2010 and 2014 that expanded IUD eligibility to adolescents and nulliparous women were associated with a differential increase in IUD use among nulliparous women. Use of IUDs rose from nearly 0 in 2002 to 17.3% by 2023, with the steepest annual gains occurring after the 2014 AAP statement. In contrast, IUD use among parous women—whose eligibility was unchanged—increased steadily through 2014 and then plateaued. The divergence in post-2014 trends across parity groups was statistically significant. Condom-only use declined among nulliparous women in the post-period but not among parous women, consistent with population-level compositional shifts toward more effective methods. Reports of no contraceptive use increased among parous women while remaining stable among nulliparous women, yielding a statistically significant differential slope.

Our study advances the prior literature in three ways. First, unlike existing studies that relied on a single-state Medicaid or commercial claims dataset, we used the nationally representative NSFG, allowing our findings to generalize beyond a particular state or insurance status population. Second, our quasi-experimental design, an interrupted time series analysis, accounts for secular trends in contraceptive use and reduces (but cannot fully eliminate) confounding from the concurrent changes such as the Affordable Care Act. Third, prior work examined only the 2014 AAP guideline, whereas we examined the full sequence of updates, testing 2010 as an alternative intervention date.

Results were consistent across all three guideline dates (2010–2014), and the quasi-experimental design with a within-data comparison group reduces (though cannot eliminate) confounding from concurrent policy changes, improves upon the descriptive pre–post designs that characterize prior work.

The observed annual increase of 1.32% is more modest than prior estimates but consistent with the broader literature that shows the heterogeneous effects of guideline changes. Although the gains from guideline changes were significant, other issues such as clinician hesitancy documented in prior surveys9,10 continue to limit practice change.

The role of the Affordable Care Act warrants consideration. The contraceptive coverage mandate of the Affordable Care Act took effect concurrently with the guideline changes, and prior studies have found mixed effects of the Affordable Care Act on IUD use, depending on the population and payer examined.26–30 Our comparison to parous women, who faced similar insurance landscape changes, partially accounts for this confounding, to the extent that Affordable Care Act–related changes did not differentially affect nulliparous women.

Three Affordable Care Act provisions are most relevant. The contraceptive coverage mandate required private insurers to cover contraceptives without cost sharing regardless of parity and should be largely absorbed by the parous comparator. The dependent coverage expansion, which allowed young adults to remain on parental insurance until 26 years of age, disproportionately benefited younger, nulliparous women who were more likely to remain dependents; parous women younger than 26 years of age, having established independent households, were less likely to benefit. Medicaid expansion was rolling and varied across states, operating through broad coverage gains rather than parity-specific channels, and cannot be fully addressed without state-level geocoded data.

In a sensitivity analysis stratified by insurance status, the differential postguideline trend increase was present and statistically significant among both insured and uninsured respondents, with no significant difference between groups. This finding suggests differential insurance coverage gains do not explain our main results. The differential postguideline increase in IUD use among nulliparous women (relative to parous women), which persisted across insurance groups, suggests that formal changes to clinical eligibility criteria can shift contraceptive behavior at the population level, but the effect is partial and likely mediated by clinician adoption. Although we do not observe clinician behavior in the NSFG, the continued increase in IUD use among nulliparous women through 2023—nearly a decade after the last guideline update—is consistent with the idea that changes in clinical practice take time and that clinicians across specialties, including primary care and pediatric clinicians, may still be in the process of incorporating updated guidance into practice. 31

For clinicians, these findings reinforce the importance of offering IUDs as a routine option to all eligible patients, including adolescents and nulliparous women. The data suggest that uptake has increased but remains incomplete, pointing to continued opportunity for clinician education and practice change. Although tiered-effectiveness counseling can help patients understand method efficacy,32 historical legacies of reproductive coercion, including involuntary sterilization33,34 and the unequal promotion of certain contraceptive methods based on race, ethnicity, income, or education,35,36 have contributed to mistrust in the health care system. Patient-centered counseling, which includes review of method efficacy, along with other patient-driven priorities, remains critical to avoid coercion37 and aligning contraceptive care with individual preferences and values. Furthermore, the adoption of recent recommendations for pain management protocols during IUD insertion25 should be prioritized.

For policy makers, although these findings do not directly measure the effects of specific policies, they point to structural barriers that clinical guidelines alone cannot address over time. Structural barriers—including cost without insurance coverage,38 appointment availability, and Medicaid restrictions on IUD removal in some states39—limit the reach of clinical eligibility changes and are unlikely to resolve without targeted policy intervention. Expanding same-day access to IUDs in publicly funded settings and ensuring reimbursement for counseling and insertion would likely do more to advance uptake than guideline dissemination alone.40 In addition, only 24 states and Washington, DC, give minors a right to consent to contraceptive care,41 further complicating access for adolescents.

This analysis cannot distinguish between new IUD initiations and continuations, nor can it identify whether observed changes reflect shifts in clinician counseling, patient demand, or both. Individual-level longitudinal data would be necessary to disentangle these mechanisms. The sample size of the NSFG also limits our ability to examine heterogeneity by race and ethnicity or geography, factors that likely shape both clinician behavior and patient access in ways that aggregate national estimates cannot capture.

An additional limitation is that preperiod trends differed across groups, as expected given their distinct contraceptive histories. This does not violate the interrupted time series identifying assumption, which requires only that each group's own preperiod trend serve as a valid counterfactual, and the parous comparison is presented as supplementary rather than definitive evidence against secular trends. State-level variation in Medicaid expansion timing remains a potential residual confounder that cannot be addressed without restricted geocoded NSFG data. Because the NSFG captures current contraceptive use at a single point per respondent rather than incident uptake, observed prevalence increases may reflect greater initiation, longer continuation, or both, although the slope-dominant pattern and concentration of differential gains in the newly eligible group are more consistent with a guideline-driven initiation effect. Finally, we cannot account for clinician-level factors, local policy environments, or changes in IUD availability and cost over the study period.

National guideline updates expanding IUD eligibility were associated with a sustained and differential increase in IUD use among nulliparous women, with no comparable change among parous women whose eligibility was unchanged. These findings are consistent with a guideline-driven effect, although the magnitude was modest and likely constrained by incomplete clinician adoption, structural access barriers, and competing policy influences. Clinical eligibility is a necessary but not sufficient condition for equitable contraceptive access. Achieving broader uptake will require coordinated efforts across clinicians, payers, and policy makers to address the structural and experiential barriers that clinical guidelines alone cannot overcome.
