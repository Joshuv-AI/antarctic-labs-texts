Geometry-Aware Decentralized Sinkhorn for Wasserstein Barycenters
Ali Baheri, David Millard, Alireza Vahid

Abstract
Distributed systems require fusing heterogeneous local probability distributions into a global summary over sparse and unreliable communication networks. Traditional consensus algorithms, which average distributions in Euclidean space, ignore their inherent geometric structure, leading to misleading results. Wasserstein barycenters offer a geometry-aware alternative by minimizing optimal transport costs, but their entropic approximations via the Sinkhorn algorithm typically require centralized coordination. This paper proposes a fully decentralized Sinkhorn algorithm that reformulates the centralized geometric mean as an arithmetic average in the log-domain, enabling approximation through local gossip protocols. Agents exchange log-messages with neighbors, interleaving consensus phases with local updates to mimic centralized iterations without a coordinator. To optimize bandwidth, we integrate event-triggered transmissions and b-bit quantization, providing tunable trade-offs between accuracy and communication while accommodating asynchrony and packet loss. Under mild assumptions, we prove convergence to a neighborhood of the centralized entropic barycenter, with bias linearly dependent on consensus tolerance, trigger threshold, and quantization error. Complexity scales near-linearly with network size. Simulations confirm near-centralized accuracy with significantly fewer messages, across various topologies and conditions.



I
INTRODUCTION

Distributed systems increasingly require fusing heterogeneous local probability distributions into network-wide summaries while communicating over sparse, unreliable links
[
1
,
2
]
. Consider autonomous robots that share beliefs about target locations, sensor networks that aggregate environmental measurements, or edge devices that collaborate on machine learning models without sharing raw data. In each case, individual nodes maintain probability distributions that must be combined to obtain a meaningful consensus.

Classical distributed consensus algorithms treat probability distributions as vectors in Euclidean space and compute arithmetic averages
[
3
]
. Although simple and well-studied, this approach fundamentally ignores the geometric structure of probability measures. Naive averaging can produce misleading results: when two robots believe a target is at opposite locations, the arithmetic average suggests it lies at the midpoint, potentially an impossible location like the center of a wall. Similarly, averaging pollution distributions across spatial regions without considering transport mechanisms can create artificial patterns that mislead decision-making algorithms.

The core issue is that probability distributions live on a curved manifold, not in a flat Euclidean space. The cost of transforming one distribution into another depends on the underlying geometry, whether spatial (sensor networks), temporal (time series), or feature-based (machine learning). Optimal transport provides a framework for this geometry-aware comparison by measuring distances between distributions using the minimum cost of moving probability mass according to a ground cost function
[
4
]
.

Wasserstein barycenters extend this concept to averaging multiple distributions while preserving their geometric structure
[
5
]
. Unlike Euclidean averaging, Wasserstein barycenters maintain distribution shapes, respect spatial or feature relationships, and provide robustness against outliers. These properties have driven their adoption in computer vision
[
6
]
, computational biology
[
7
]
, machine learning
[
8
]
, and robotics applications
[
9
]
where distribution geometry matters.

The computational challenge lies in efficiently computing these barycenters. Exact computation is prohibitively expensive, but entropic regularization enables practical approximations through the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
10
]
. This iterative method alternates between local scaling operations at each agent and a global geometric mean computation. However, the geometric mean step requires collecting information from all agents at every iteration, creating a fundamental centralization bottleneck that defeats the purpose of distributed computation.

Existing decentralized approaches suffer from critical limitations. ADMM and primal-dual methods
[
11
,
12
]
exchange full coupling matrices or dual variables, creating a massive communication overhead that scales quadratically with the dimension of the problem. Push-sum variants
[
13
]
handle directed graphs but ignore Sinkhorn’s multiplicative structure and demand exact arithmetic precision. Federated approaches
[
14
,
15
]
assume star topologies with central parameter servers, contradicting peer-to-peer requirements. None provide explicit mechanisms for trading accuracy against bandwidth or handling unreliable communication.

Our key idea is that the geometric mean operation can be reformulated as an arithmetic average in a logarithmic space. This transformation changes the communication pattern from requiring central coordination to enabling fully distributed computation via neighbor-to-neighbor consensus. Each agent computes its contribution locally in log-space, then uses standard gossip algorithms
[
3
]
to reach agreement on the average. To address bandwidth constraints, we introduce event-triggered transmission
[
16
]
, where agents communicate only when their local states change significantly, and adaptive quantization
[
17
]
, which reduces message precision based on available bandwidth. These mechanisms provide explicit controls for trading accuracy against communication cost while supporting asynchrony and packet loss.

Contributions.
This paper advances decentralized optimal transport in three directions.
Algorithmically
, we develop a fully decentralized Sinkhorn algorithm that eliminates central coordination by reformulating geometric mean operations as distributed arithmetic consensus in log-space, maintaining numerical stability where traditional methods fail.
Communication-wise
, we design event-triggered and quantized protocols that reduce bandwidth by orders of magnitude while preserving convergence guarantees, providing explicit parameters for tuning accuracy-communication trade-offs.
Theoretically
, we establish convergence to the same solution as centralized versions with explicit bounds on how consensus tolerance, trigger thresholds, and quantization precision affect accuracy.

Paper organization.
Section II reviews related work. Section III presents preliminaries and the centralized entropic barycenter. Section IV develops the decentralized log-gossip Sinkhorn algorithm with event-triggered, quantized communication. Section V establishes convergence guarantees and complexity bounds. Section VI reports experiments on accuracy-bandwidth trade-offs and scalability. Section VII concludes with limitations and future directions.

Clipping makes the analysis self-consistent and removes pathological range blow-ups at small

VII
CONCLUSIONS

We proposed a geometry-aware decentralized Sinkhorn algorithm for computing entropic Wasserstein barycenters over sparse, unreliable networks. By recasting the centralized geometric mean as an arithmetic average in the log-domain and approximating it via local gossip consensus, our method eliminates the need for a coordinator while preserving the multiplicative structure of iterative Bregman projections. We further enhanced communication efficiency through event-triggered transmissions and quantization, providing explicit, tunable trade-offs between accuracy and bandwidth that tolerate asynchrony, packet drops, and delays. Theoretical analysis established convergence to a neighborhood of the centralized entropic barycenter, with bias bounds scaling linearly in consensus tolerance, trigger threshold, and quantization step. Complexity characterizations demonstrated near-linear scaling with network size in common topologies, and empirical evaluations confirmed centralized-level accuracy with substantially reduced message volumes across varying graphs, supports, and stress conditions. Although our approach advances decentralized OT solvers, limitations include assumptions of common supports and fixed regularization, as well as simulation-based validation on modest scales. Future directions may explore heterogeneous support, adaptive quantization schemes, integration with sliced or convolutional OT for structured data, and real-world deployments in distributed learning or sensor fusion scenarios.
