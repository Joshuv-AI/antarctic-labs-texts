Plasmon-enhanced reductive defluorination of PFAS in water under ambient conditions
Authors: Ozce Durak, Seung Soo S Lee, Lydia MacFarlane Watt, Susanna K Maisto, Fabian Menges, Changwoo Kim, James M Mayer, John D Fortner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhoto-driven, plasmon-enhanced catalysis enables polyfluoroalkyl and perfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) degradation in water under ambient conditions. Here we show that ultraviolet (UV) irradiation-induced localized surface plasmon resonance drives reductive defluorination of perfluorooctanoic acid and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid, two legacy contaminants. Stable, quantum-sized palladium and platinum nanocatalysts supported on aminated mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt) transform perfluorooctanoic acid and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid with stoichiometric, or near-stoichiometric, fluoride release under low-intensity UVC or UVA irradiation at room temperature. Time-resolved 19F nuclear magnetic resonance, high-resolution mass spectrometry and complementary analyses indicate a stepwise reductive hydrodefluorination pathway in which oxidized, short-chain products do not measurably accumulate under the conditions studied. Scavenger and control experiments are consistent with hydrated electrons and in situ hydrogen generation as key reactive species. Taken together, the results indicate that synergistic interfacial PFAS sorption, plasmon-induced hydrogen evolution and hydrated electron formation drive PFAS defluorination under mild reaction conditions.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Environmental chemistry, Environmental impact

Main

Polyfluoroalkyl and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) represent a broad class of synthetic organic chemicals that contain a hydrophobic fluorinated carbon chain and a polar end group. Due to their amphiphilic properties and high stability, PFAS have been extensively used as surface coatings, and aqueous film-forming foams for fire suppression, among other widespread applications1, resulting in extensive environmental contamination2. Based on the highly recalcitrant nature of PFAS attributed to the strength of C–F covalent bonds (~485 kJ mol−1) and a large number of potential PFAS structures (~15,000) occurring at low environmental concentrations with documented human health risks, effective PFAS remediation remains an important and urgent global health need3,4, which is reflected in recent regulatory actions5.

The treatment of PFAS contamination using conventional processes poses several challenges due to their highly stable chemical structure(s)6. The various technologies currently available to transform PFAS can be generally categorized as chemical/catalytic, plasma-based, thermal and bio-based6,7. Among these, chemical/catalytic transformation processes have proven effective for PFAS degradation via chemical oxidation and reduction, photocatalysis, electrocatalysis and sonolysis-based pathways6. Catalytic transformations have been demonstrated for a library of PFAS compounds with relatively lower energy/economic requirements compared with traditional methods, such as thermal degradation, and are characterized by rapid processes, unlike the slow (or limited) nature of biologically driven transformations8,9.

From a sustainability perspective, photo-driven catalytic processes offer inherent advantages by reducing energy demands and chemical inputs. Recent advances in ultraviolet (UV) reactor design and light-emitting diode (LED) technology have significantly improved the scalability and energy efficiency of UV-based treatment systems, making them increasingly viable for scaled water treatment and environmental remediation10,11. Techno-economic analyses have further highlighted the potential cost-effectiveness of UV-driven approaches, particularly for decentralized or point-of-entry treatment scenarios compared with other emerging technologies12,13. Among photocatalytic strategies, plasmon-enhanced photocatalysis, a growing field focused on enabling/enhancing chemical reactions through specific light–matter interactions, is a powerful strategy for driving thermodynamically challenging reactions under ambient conditions14. This approach exploits localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), which arises from the photoexcitation of free electrons in metal nanostructures, typically noble metal nanoparticles (for example, Au, Ag, Pd and Pt), in the 0.5–8 nm size range15. When incident light matches the plasmon resonance frequency, collective oscillation of surface electrons is induced, generating highly energetic charge carriers (‘hot’ electrons and holes), localized heating and/or strong electromagnetic fields at the nanoparticle interface16. Such light-induced processes have been harnessed to catalyse a range of chemical transformations, including hydrogen evolution via water splitting, selective hydrogenation reactions and degradation of environmental pollutants17–21. However, while clear potential exists, this approach has not been demonstrated for effective PFAS treatment, despite supportive computational reports22,23.

In this study, plasmon-induced, photocatalytic defluorination of PFAS is demonstrated through an efficient, reductive mechanism, with minimal inputs at room temperature (RT). For this, stable LSPR-active palladium (Pd) and platinum (Pt) catalysts supported on mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) were developed and demonstrated under UV irradiation (both UVC and UVA). For optimized materials and conditions, we observe near-stochiometric (∼100%) defluorination for both perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), with (similarly) high reaction kinetics and no external hydrogen required. Reaction pathway(s) are explored and described through control experiments and advanced, time-resolved spectroscopic analyses.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of plasmonic photocatalysts

MSN holds significant potential as a material platform(s) for environmental applications based on facile/scalable synthesis, (surface) functional and geometric tunability, and stability. As demonstrated by our lab and others, such properties also make MSN an ideal candidate for supporting/stabilizing metal nanoparticle deposition and, thus, a catalyst(s) support for reaction-based applications24. In this study, synthesized MSN support particles were highly porous, monodisperse, spherical structures with an average particle size of 77.7 ± 6.1 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1) with high surface area (496.3 m2 g−1) and an average pore diameter of 3.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To facilitate metal nanoparticle deposition, MSN was surface functionalized with primary amine groups (MSN–NH2), and subsequent metal (Pd and Pt) nanoparticle synthesis/deposition, was carried out under solvothermal conditions as detailed in Supplementary Information. The average particle sizes of monodispersed Pd and Pt nanoparticles on MSN-NH2 were determined to be 0.7 ± 0.1 nm and 1.5 ± 0.9 nm (Fig. 1a,b), which is consistent with small-angle X-ray scattering measurements presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. For materials evaluated, metal nanoparticle concentrations on MSN–NH2, were 4.0 wt% for Pd and 3.7 wt% for Pt. Additional characterization of both plasmonic photocatalysts including X-ray diffraction (crystal structure) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (elemental oxidation states and relative ratios) can be found in our previous study25.

Plasmonic nanoparticles (PNPs), including Pd and Pt, can catalyse various reactions through three primary energy dissipation mechanisms: heat generation, enhancement of the electric field, and/or hot electron formation26. For this, the energy of the photon is transferred into the oscillating surface free electrons and subsequently excites a resonance mode that can be identified with response(s) in the reflected light intensity via absorption-sensitive diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS)27. Consistent with previous studies, MSN–NH2 support material has relatively low optical absorption in the UV–visible (UV–vis) region, between the wavelengths of 800 nm and 200 nm, allowing LSPR absorption bands of associated Pd and Pt PNPs (termed MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt) to be observed in the 370–250 nm range27 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Accordingly, aqueous reactions were conducted under constant UVC or UVA irradiation as discussed below. We note that PNP stability was also evaluated after reaction (up to 96 h) with Pd/Pt PNPs remaining highly stable throughout, with no evidence surface-based particle aggregation (via time-resolved transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis) or particle dissolution (inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis).

Reductive PFAS transformation

As-synthesized PNPs supported on MSN–NH2 (MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt) were evaluated for PFAS transformation, under a matrix of reaction conditions. For this, we focused on two environmentally relevant, commonly found (and studied) eight-carbon chain PFAS compounds, PFOA and PFOS, representing two PFAS classes (carboxylic acid and sulfonic acid). The experimental design was structured to control key reaction parameters, including pH, light irradiation conditions, and potential radicals/reactive species (thus appropriate scavengers). Both MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt completely degraded PFOA and PFOS, as shown in Fig. 2a,b, under constant UVC irradiation (light intensity of 2.6 mW cm−2), in the presence of a hydroxyl scavenger (MeOH) at a working pH of 4.2–4.5 (achieved by CO2 sparging/headspace). Within 24 h, MSN–NH2–Pd (MSN–NH2–Pt) materials (400 ppm) degraded 99.1% (99%) and 98.7% (99.9%) of PFOA and PFOS (10 ppm initial concentrations), with first-order reaction rates of 0.34 (0.25) h−1 and 0.27 (0.37) h−1, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). In no-catalyst controls under UVC irradiation, no measurable fluoride formation was observed over time, indicating that direct photolysis does not promote reductive defluorination of either PFOA or PFOS under the conditions studied. Under the same reaction conditions, defluorination was verified via F− mass balance using ion chromatography (IC), with near 100% defluorination efficiency for both PFOA and PFOS within 72 h (Fig. 2c,d), which was further confirmed by 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The parent PFAS degradation rate was significantly faster than the total defluorination rate as products/fragments continue to react, consistent with previous studies28,29. Enhanced reaction rates and degradation extent in the presence of hydroxyl radical (●OH) scavenger suggest a major degradation mechanism occurs through a reductive reaction pathway (Supplementary Fig. 4). Observed defluorination efficiencies are comparable to those reported for several reductive PFAS defluorination systems in the literature, which span a broad range depending on catalyst composition and reaction conditions28–35.

PFAS defluorination via advanced reductive processes (ARP) offers advantages over oxidative pathways, which often produce shorter-chain PFAS (for example, PFHpA, PFHxA, PFPeA and PFBA) that are generally more hydrophilic, less well understood and subject to evolving regulatory requirements28,29,34–38. Furthermore, common aqueous-based oxidative species (for example, hydroxyl radicals and superoxide radicals) do not efficiently react with C—F bonds unless used in some combination with additional conditions/reactive species, such as sulfate radical-based oxidation (SO4●−)39,40. It has also been shown theoretically that reductive degradation requires comparatively lower energy (compared with other treatment processes), making it a more cost-effective and scalable solution41,42.

Assessing major reaction variables

Reaction parameters, including pH, reactive species and incident light were explored through a matrix of control experiments, which are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. For reactions evaluating pH and reactive species, MSN–NH2–Pd was used as the plasmonic photocatalyst and PFOA as the target PFAS. The role of pH was assessed by applying CO2 headspace to maintain mildly acidic conditions (pH 4.2–4.5), deionized (DI) water for circumneutral pH (pH 6.8–7.2) and a bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.2) for mildly basic conditions. For these, we observe higher performance under acidic conditions (continuous UVC irradiation with MeOH) as shown in Fig. 4a, which aligns with a surface-sorption model for more (effectively) positively charged surfaces as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 6, and by others43. Batch PFAS sorption isotherm measurements conducted within this pH range, presented in Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 7, show favourable PFAS sorption under evaluated conditions. Catalyst suspensions kept in the dark (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8) showed no measurable fluoride formation over time, confirming that irradiation is required.

Reactivity was also evaluated under UVA irradiation (λ = 368 nm, light intensity of 7.3 mW cm−2). For these experiments, PFOA degradation efficiency decreased from 99.1% to 60.7% for MSN–NH2–Pd while MSN–NH2–Pt decreased from 99% to 69.8% (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8) for a 24-h reaction period. Similar efficiency trends are observed for PFOS degradation (Supplementary Fig. 9). While slower than UVC-based reactions, MSN–NH2–Pd also catalyse reductive defluorination of both PFOA and PFOS upon UVA irradiation, with corresponding fluoride evolution observed (∼80% fluoride evolution for both PFOA and PFOS over a 96-h reaction time; Supplementary Fig. 10). By contrast, MSN–NH2–Pt did not achieve similar defluorination outcomes either PFAS. To compare (photo)efficiency of different UV wavelengths (that is, UVA versus UVC), photon flux normalized reaction rates are presented in Supplementary Table 5. For PFOA, MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt show the highest per-photon efficiency (1.1 × 10−20 h−1 and 0.8 × 10−20 h−1 per applied photon, respectively) under UVC. UVA-based reactions under identical conditions were comparatively less efficient, with normalized rates of 6 × 10−22 h−1 and 2 × 10−22 h−1 per applied photon, respectively. Considering PFOS defluorination, MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt under UVC also exhibit similar normalized rates (0.8 × 10−20 h−1 and 1.1 × 10−20 h−1 per applied photon, respectively). UVA-based PFOS defluorination reactions, were observed to decrease similar to PFOA (8 × 10−22 h−1 and 2 × 10−22 h−1 per applied photon for MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt, respectively). Collectively, UVC offers a clear advantage in per-photon efficiency (~10–75×) for all materials and PFAS evaluated, despite the higher incident photon flux under UVA. This is consistent with higher photon energy and more favourable absorption within the UVC range (for example, 254 nm) for both Pd and Pt metals (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, UVA-based defluorination processes do have the distinct advantage of being compatible with solar or near-visible LED sources that are typically more cost-effective and longer-lived than traditional UVC lamps44. Furthermore, while proof of concept of UVA-based reductive defluorination is achieved here for MSN–NH2–Pd, material improvements towards more plasmonic-based efficient (for example, UVA, solar and visible light) reactions are likely to be achieved in the future45,46.

Identifying major reactive species

As briefly discussed, the presence of ●OH scavenger increased reaction kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 4), while the degradation efficiency of PFOA (within 24 h) decreased to 65.3% (from 99.1% with MeOH under identical experimental conditions) in the absence of a hydroxyl scavenger43 (here MeOH, tert-butanol (TBA) or isopropanol (IPA) were evaluated; Supplementary Fig. 4). This suggests that an oxidative pathway probably competes with the reductive pathway47,48, which is further supported by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis, whereby chain-shortened PFCA (for PFOA degradation) by-products are observed when no scavenger is present (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Reactive species involved in the reductive pathway were further explored using appropriate scavengers in excess. For this, AgNO3 was used to scavenge reducing species30,49,50, such as hydrated electrons (eaq−), whereas superoxide dismutase (SOD) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were used to scavenge, superoxide radical (O2●−), and holes (h+), respectively. Formation reactions of reactive species are given below, in equations (1)–(4)51,52. It is noteworthy that, given equation (2) and the reported pKₐ of the H●/eaq− redox couple, Haq● is not expected to be present under our experimental conditions.

Considering PFOA degradation, data presented in Fig. 4c indicate that O2●− and h+ play low(er) or negligible roles in the observed reaction process. By contrast, eaq− appears to be a primary reactive species, as demonstrated by the dramatic decrease in degradation performance with AgNO3 (from 99.1% to 25%, under constant UVC irradiation at 4.2–4.5 pH for 24 h) under identical reaction conditions. In the presence of AgNO3, degradation performance significantly decreased, consistent with scavenging of hydrated electrons by Ag+ and NO3− (k = 2.8 × 109 and 9.7 × 109 M−1 s−1, respectively, for reaction with eaq−)30,49,50,52. Although some contribution from surface effects cannot be excluded, this result, together with wavelength dependence, fluoride evolution and product distributions, supports hydrated electrons as the dominant reductive intermediate under the conditions studied. ICP-MS analysis of post-reaction solutions showed no measurable Pd or Pt dissolution, further indicating that the loss of reactivity is unlikely to arise from catalyst instability. While the precise nature of hydrated electrons remains a topic of discussion, their subsequent reaction mechanism is conventionally defined as outer sphere one-electron transfer (reduction), capable of tunnelling over long distances without requiring direct coordination53. The negative charge of the formed/available hot electrons polarize nearby neutral water molecules (meta)stabilizing regions that an electron can briefly occupy, also referred to as the cavity model54. Although hot-carrier relaxation occurs on femtosecond-to-picosecond timescales, these carriers can initiate electron ejection into water, generating hydrated electrons that persist long enough to participate in solution-phase reduction chemistry14,55. This phenomenon enables effective interaction with electronically stabilized substrates such as fluorine-saturated carbon chains54.

LSPR-driven hydrogen generation

Conventional reductive defluorination strategies typically rely on exogenous hydrogen sources and metal catalysts to activate C–F bonds at reactive surfaces56. By contrast, plasmon-enhanced systems offer a fundamentally distinct approach wherein LSPR excitation can drive in situ hydrogen generation directly at the catalyst–solution interface57,58. This mechanism not only reduces the need for external reductants but also enables interfacial co-localization of reactants. For the plasmonic nanocatalysts evaluated, gas chromatography analysis confirmed the generation of hydrogen gas upon UV irradiation, in both the presence and the absence of PFAS upon UV irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 12). Proposed pathways for such hydrogen formation are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 13 and discussed in detail in Supplementary Information. To assess whether hydrogen availability limits PFAS reaction kinetics, control experiments with external hydrogen gas addition were conducted, which showed nearly identical kinetics (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 3), indicating a self-sustaining pathway where hydrogen is not rate-limiting under the tested conditions (see the Supplementary Information for details). Importantly, in the absence of UV irradiation with external hydrogen present (sparged), significant PFAS degradation was not observed, further confirming an LSPR-driven reaction process (Supplementary Fig. 14).

PFAS reductive degradation products and pathway(s)

A detailed, time-resolved analysis of PFOA and PFOS transformation products was performed using LC–high-resolution (HR) tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) under conditions optimized for degradation performance30,42 (UVC irradiation (λ = 254 nm), 400 ppm of MSN–NH2–Pd catalyst, 10 ppm PFAS, 20 ppm MeOH (as a hydroxyl radical scavenger) and continuous CO2 sparging to maintain mildly acidic conditions; Figs. 2 and 4). For these conditions, the primary observed products were hydrodefluorinated analogues of PFOA and PFOS that maintained their corresponding head group functionalities. This is consistent with a dominant hydrogenation-driven defluorination mechanism that does not involve significant early-stage decarboxylation or desulfonation. Considering major intermediate species, the initial carboxylate/sulfonate reaction product has one F replaced by H, C7F14HCOO− (m/z 394.97; Supplementary Figs. 15–20 and Supplementary Table 6) and C8F16HSO3− (m/z 480.93; Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22 and Supplementary Table 7), termed PFOA(1H) or PFOS(1H). These species were transformed within the first 12 h of irradiation, and a series of more highly defluorinated products appeared over time, up to C7F1H14COO− (14H; Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19) and C8H17SO3− (17H; Supplementary Fig. 21), in line with a stepwise hydrodefluorination pathway and consistent with ARP mechanism, as discussed below, and corroborated by scavenger experiments (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 4). Although occurring at significantly lower abundance, we also observed decarboxylated and desulfonated products, including the perfluoroalkyl species C6F13CF2− (m/z 368.97) and shorter-chain sulfonated compounds (Supplementary Figs. 15–17 and 22, and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). The formation of these minor products indicates that alternative and simultaneous degradation pathways exist, which are probably influenced by structural differences between PFAS compounds, such as variations in head groups or surface interaction sterics and/or sites. However, these pathways appear less favourable, either kinetically or thermodynamically, under the reaction conditions used in this study.

To further explore major product structures and fragmentation behaviour, MS/MS analysis was performed to differentiate resulting isomers based on bond stability and fragmentation pathways (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 22). As the reaction proceeds, nH products (where n indicates the number of fluorine-to-hydrogen substitutions) emerge sequentially, with species exhibiting decreasing (maximum) MS signal intensities with increasing n. This reflects the evolution of a complex and increasingly dilute product mixture. LC traces revealed significant isomeric diversity within each nH class. For example, PFOA(1H) eluted in at least four major chromatographic fractions (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18), each representing one or more positional isomers. If fluorine loss can occur from any backbone carbon, as suggested by the MS/MS data, a 2H species could produce up to 7 unique regioisomers (plus diastereomers), and at least 13 distinct 1H/2H species could coexist. As defluorination continues past 2H species, the number of possible structural isomers increases exponentially in chemical diversity, consistent with the observation of reduced occurrence of individual intermediate species. These results are in contrast to the common reports that the α-CF2 adjacent to the carboxylate (central −CF2− segments in the case of sulfonated PFAS) is the most reactive for the first H/F exchange29,42. Prior computational work has suggested that the initial dissociation site can be variable rather than restricted to a single preferred position, because the excess electron is initially delocalized over the PFAS molecule59. While bond dissociation energies have been proposed to assess and predict C–F bond cleavage selectivity42, additional factors probably contribute, including PFAS association dynamics, related steric interactions and solvation of departing F− anions.

Notably, the intermediate products observed during PFOA and PFOS degradation did not show measurable accumulation of chain-shortened carboxylate products, such as PFHpA (C6F13COO−) and PFHxA (C5F11COO−), by either quantitative LC–MS or LC–HR-MS/MS under the conditions studied. Shorter-chain PFCA products are typically observed via oxidative pathways, especially through a decarboxylation–hydroxylation–elimination–hydrolysis (DHEH)-based mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 23), which includes decarboxylation or desulfonation, hydroxylation, elimination and subsequent hydrolysis. In this work, the absence (or quenching) of an available oxidative reactant (for example, hydroxyl radicals) limits the hydroxylation step of DHEH process, thus inhibiting the generation of PFCA products.

ARP is well established as a pathway for PFAS transformation and is typically initiated by electron donors such as hydrated electrons, zero-valent metals or molecular hydrogen42. While several recent studies have explored hybrid or combined oxidative—reductive systems for PFAS treatment28,40, relatively few have focused exclusively on ARP-based pathways operating under ambient or mild conditions and even fewer have achieved near-complete defluorination under such conditions27,28,50,51,60. Here, based on our experimental findings, we propose that the primary degradation pathway in this LSPR-driven system proceeds through an ARP mechanism. Specifically, LSPR excitation promotes hydrated-electron generation, which drives reductive PFAS defluorination. The process is initiated by electron transfer to the PFAS lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), which has substantial contributions from C–F antibonding (σ*) orbitals61. Such electron addition initiates F− dissociation, leaving a fluorocarbon radical that is subsequently reduced and protonated to hydrofluorocarboxylates or hydrofluorosulfonates. This cycle is repeated to generate subsequent C7F15-nHnCOO− or C8F16-nHnSO3− species, as observed (this mechanism is depicted in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 15–22). While our findings provide clear evidence for a classic ARP mechanism, future studies are needed to resolve subtle process details. For example, given the small (~3.6 nm) pores of MSN–NH2, PFAS molecules may associate in close proximity to Pd or Pt nanoparticles (0.7–1.0 nm) with varying orientations. In such confined environments, the initial reduction step could involve hydrated electrons and/or H atoms associated with the Pd (or Pt) PNPs, either in driving C–F bond cleavage and/or in subsequent H addition to the carbon radical. Addressing these and other next-step mechanistic questions will enable deeper understanding towards optimizing light-driven, plasmonic processes for PFAS transformation.

Proposed advanced reduction reaction pathway (ARP) for eaq−-mediated reductive transformation of PFAS.

Upon UV activation, hydrated electrons initiate C–F bond cleavage, forming a carbon-centred radical. Subsequent proton and electron additions result in stepwise H/F exchange reactions. This mechanism is consistent with ARP-type hydrohalogenation pathways and supported by HR-MS detection of partially defluorinated intermediates (C7F15-nHnCOO− and C8F16-nHnSO3−) as well as radical scavenger experiments. Fluoride elimination can occur from various carbon sites, generating structural isomers, with products capable of re-entering the cycle for continued H/F substitutions.

Conclusion

This study presents a plasmon-enhanced approach for the reductive defluorination of PFOA and PFOS in aqueous media under ambient conditions. By leveraging quantum-sized Pd and Pt nanoparticles precisely dispersed on amine-functionalized mesoporous silica, we demonstrate rapid PFAS degradation kinetics and at- or near-stoichiometric defluorination upon simple UV irradiation, without the need for external hydrogen addition. Mechanistic experiments and LC–HR-MS/MS analyses of intermediate species reveal sequential H/F exchange driven primarily by hydrated electrons generated through plasmon-enabled processes.

Beyond the observed reactivity, the system offers several practical features relevant to future process development. Material synthesis is straightforward and conducted under ambient conditions using readily available precursors. The estimated raw material cost is approximately US$3.80 per gram of catalyst (Supplementary Table 8), although this value reflects raw material inputs only and excludes operating and irradiation costs, which should be incorporated in a full techno-economic assessment. The noble metals are present at lower loadings (4.0 wt% Pd and 3.7 wt% Pt on MSN–NH2) within the composite catalyst and operate catalytically rather than stoichiometrically. ICP-MS analysis of post-reaction samples showed no measurable Pd or Pt leaching under the tested conditions, indicating catalyst stability. Reducing noble-metal content and evaluating earth-abundant alternatives will be important for future process development. The process was also effective under both UVC and UVA irradiation, suggesting compatibility with alternative light sources in future studies. Taken together, these findings establish a plasmon-enabled reductive pathway for PFAS defluorination under ambient conditions. Continued mechanistic investigation, development of visible-light-responsive variants and integration into continuous-flow systems will be important next steps towards process optimization and application.

Methods

Materials

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, >99.0%), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 98%) triethanolamine (TEA, >99.0%), (aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES, 99%), chloroplatinic acid hydrate (H2PtCl4·xH2O, >99.9%), sodium tetrachloropalladate (Na2PdCl4, 99.99%), trisodium citrate dihydrate (US Pharmacopeia grade), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 99%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, >99.0%), PFOA (>95.0%), superoxide dismutase (SOD, >95.0%), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, >99.0%), tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, anhydrous, ≥99.5%), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, ≥99.5%), methanol (MeOH, 99.95%) and ethanol (EtOH, 200 proof, anhydrous, 99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PFOS (>99.0%) was purchased from TCI. All experiments were conducted in ultrapure Milli-Q water (MQ water, 18.2 MΩ cm) using quartz reactors.

Synthesis of MSN supports

CTAB (5.72 g, 15.69 mmol) was dissolved in EtOH (18 g, 0.39 mol) and water (129.6 g, 7.2 mol) under stirring (750 rpm) at RT for 30 min. In the CTAB micelle-containing solution, TEA (0.568 g, 3.81 mmol) was added at RT and agitated for an additional 30 min. The final pH was recorded as approximately 9.0 ± 0.5 at RT. The mixture was then heated to 70 °C (ramp rate of 10 °C min−1) under vigorous agitation (at 750 rpm). Then, TEOS (66.28 mmol) was injected into the solution at the injection rate of 1,000 µl min−1, and the whole mixture was continuously stirred at 750 rpm and 70 °C for 2 h under N2 flow. The resulting cloudy solution was further purified using HCl-containing EtOH (1.0 wt.% HCl in EtOH) and separated by centrifugation (at 13,750g for 25 min). The purified MSN was dried and kept under vacuum overnight. The as-synthesized MSN was further purified under acidic conditions at a high temperature above 70 °C to remove CTAB and TEA within the MSN pore space. Then, 4,000 mg of MSN was homogenously dispersed in HCl-containing EtOH (120 ml) at pH below 1.0 (typically 2 wt% HCl in EtOH) and agitated at 70 °C for 3 h. The purified MSN was cooled and separated by centrifugation (13,750g for 40 min). The CTAB removal process was repeated three times62. As synthesized, MSN exhibits a significantly larger surface area compared to nanoscale silica prepared by the Stöber method, a conventional approach, which has a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of 34.9 m2 g−1 for particle sizes of approximately 200 nm, as reported elsewhere63.

MSN amine surface modification (MSN–NH2)

Primary amine functionalization on MSN was achieved via post-synthesis surface modification with APTES. Before the reaction, MSN samples were purified under acidic conditions to remove CTAB and TEA as described above. APTES (6 ml, 25.64 mmol) was added to the purified MSN (1,000 mg) containing EtOH/water (120 ml/60 ml) co-solvent at RT under vigorous agitation (at 750 rpm) and heated to 70 °C (ramp rate of 10 °C min−1). The reaction mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 2 h under N2 flow. The resulting cloudy solution was purified using EtOH and separated by centrifugation (at 13,750g for 25 min) and stored in an amber bottle.

Palladium nanoparticles on MSN (MSN–NH2–Pd)

Palladium nanoparticles were nucleated and grown on primary amine functionalized MSN (MSN–NH2) in aqueous media at 83 °C. All synthetic conditions were processed using palladium and citrate stock solutions; the palladium stock solution was 0.59 mol l−1, and the citrate stock solution was 0.105 mol l−1 (in water). The stock solutions were prepared 1 day before the reaction and stored in the refrigerator (3–5 °C). Typically, to synthesize 5 wt% Pd-loaded MSN–NH2–Pd, 400 mg of MSN–NH2 was dispersed in DI water (100 ml) at RT and stirred at 750 rpm under N2 flow for 30 min. Homogeneously mixed MSN–NH2 solution was then agitated with palladium stock solution (0.377 ml, 19.30 mg of Pd, 0.181 mmol) and sodium citrate stock solution (8.8 ml, 0.924 mmol) at RT. The temperature of the reaction was then heated to 83 °C (ramp rate of 10 °C min−1) and kept at that temperature for the desired time (25, 60 and 120 min) under N2 flow. After the reaction occurred at 83 °C, the reaction vessel was cooled immediately in an ice bath to stop the growth of Pd nanoparticles. The resulting brown precipitates (or grey/black, depending on Pd nanoparticle size and/or concentration) were purified using EtOH and water mixture, then separated by centrifugation at 13,750g for 25 min. The purified MSN–NH2–Pd particles were vacuum-dried overnight and kept in an amber vial.

Platinum nanoparticles on MSN (MSN–NH2–Pt)

Platinum nanoparticles were nucleated and grown on MSN–NH2 in aqueous media at 90 °C. All synthetic conditions were processed using platinum and citrate stock solutions; the platinum stock solution was 0.50 mol l−1, and the citrate stock solution was 0.105 mol l−1 (in water). The stock solutions were made 1 day before the reaction and stored in a refrigerator (3–5 °C). Typically, to make 3.7 wt% Pt-loaded MSN–Pt, 400 mg of MSN–NH2 was dispersed in DI water (100 ml) at RT and stirred at 750 rpm under N2 flow for 30 min. Homogeneously mixed MSN–NH2 solution was then agitated with platinum stock solution (0.715 ml, 31.00 mg of Pt, 0.159 mmol) and sodium citrate stock solution (6.6 ml, 0.693 mmol) at RT. The reaction temperature of the reaction was then heated to 95 °C (ramp rate of 10 °C min−1) and kept at that temperature for the desired time (25, 60 and 120 min) under N2 flow. After the reaction at 90 °C, the reaction vessel was cooled immediately in an ice bath to stop the growth of Pt nanoparticles. The resulting yellow precipitates (or grey/black, depending on Pt nanoparticle size and/or concentration) were purified using EtOH and water, then separated by centrifugation at 13,750g for 25 min. The purified MSN–NH2–Pt was vacuum-dried overnight and kept in an amber vial.

Characterization

As-synthesized materials were characterized through established and standard methods including TEM, ICP-MS, UV–vis DRS and BET surface area analyser. The size and shape of the engineered nanomaterials was characterized through FEI Tecnai Osiris 200 KV TEM. TEM samples (10 mg of sample in 10 ml of EtOH) were bath-sonicated for several minutes, and a drop of the dispersed sample was subsequently placed onto a TEM grid and left to dry overnight at RT under ambient air. The elemental composition analysis of the nanomaterials was examined by ICP-MS (NexION 5000, PerkinElmer) to determine the metal loading and nanoparticle integrity/dissolution (throughout 0–72 h experimental time frame) of the plasmonic photocatalysts. The analytical samples were digested in the boiling acid solution (typically 3:1 v/v ratio of HCl:HNO3 at 100 °C for 4 h). UV–vis DRS measurements of each sample were conducted by using Shimadzu UV-3600 Plus equipped with Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer IRTracer-100. BET specific surface area of the engineered nanomaterials was analysed using BET spectrometer (Micromeritics ASAP 2460 Surface Area and Porosity Analyzer) by N2 adsorption isotherm at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K). Before BET measurement, the sample was degassed at 110 °C over 15 h under nitrogen flow using the sample degas system (Micrometritics FlowPrep 060).

Sorption experiments

Batch adsorption experiments were performed to assess the PFAS sorption performance of the plasmonic photocatalysts. Fifteen-millilitre tubes were prepared each with approximately 20 mg particles (MSN–NH2–Pd and MSN–NH2–Pt) for the sorption experiment. Ten-millilitre solutions of 1–500 ppm PFAS were prepared, with 1,000 ppm stock solutions and DI water bubbled with CO2 to control pH, then added to each tube. Batch experiments were mixed on a rotating shaker for 24 h (in the dark) to achieve equilibrium; solution pH was measured at the start and end of the experiment. After each 24 h, 1.5-ml samples were taken and centrifuged. The supernatant was then sampled, diluted to achieve the relevant instrument concentration range, and analysed with a Shimadzu Scientific Instruments 8060 Triple-Quad LC–MS system. Duplicate experiments were conducted, including blanks without plasmonic photocatalyst particles, for comparison and sorption capacity calculations at each equilibrium concentration. Equilibrium data (in aqueous and solid phases) were plotted, and Langmuir and Freundlich sorption models were assessed for each isotherm; for Freundlich isotherms, model fits were performed for the range below the maximum sorption capacity (approximated using the average of sorption capacity values at a maximum steady state). Photocatalytic reactions

Photocatalytic experiments were conducted under constant UV irradiation at 254 nm (UVC, Phillips, low-pressure mercury lamps, light intensity of 2.6 mW cm−2) or 368 nm (UVA, USHIO, phosphor-coated low-pressure mercury lamps, light intensity of 7.3 mW cm−2) in a 100-ml quartz vial. The reaction suspension consisted of 400 mg l−1 (ppm) of catalyst, 10 mg l−1 (ppm) of PFOA and 2.5 ml of MeOH. Before UV irradiation, the quartz vial containing reaction suspension was sealed and bubbled with CO2 (and H2, if necessary) for 30 min. The working pH was maintained in the range of 4.2–4.5 with the use of CO2 bubbling before each experiment. Samples were collected at different reaction times (hourly for the first 4 h and then every 2 h until the eighth hour) and analysed accordingly for PFAS and F⁻ concentration. In the case of long-term experiments (up to 5 days) under constant UVA irradiation, sampling started at 24 h and was performed at 12-h intervals thereafter. The concentration of PFAS and corresponding by-products were measured using LC–MS (Shimadzu LCMS TQ 8060) while the F⁻ concentration was determined by IC. The concentrations of PFOA and catalyst were kept constant in each scavenger experiment. Carbonate–bicarbonate buffer was used as the weak basic buffer in the pH study. For scavenger experiments, 2.5 ml TBA, 2.5 ml IPA, 400 mg AgNO3, 100 mg SOD and 100 mg EDTA were used. Reaction constants associated with stated scavengers can be given as kAgNO3,eaq−=2.8×109M−1s−1,kAgNO3,OH=1.0×1010M−1s−1 (ref. 52), kSOD,O2∙−=∼2×109M−1s−1,kSOD,OH=∼1.0×1010M−1s−1) (ref. 64) and kEDTA,h+=8.6×109M−1s−1 (ref. 65). Photon flux, the number of photons incident per unit area per unit time, was calculated based on light intensity and wavelength to evaluate the availability of photons for catalytic processes. The resulting photon flux values were used to normalize reaction rates for direct comparison between different light irradiation (UVA and UVC) efficiencies; full equations and calculation details are provided in the Results and Discussion section of the SI.

19F NMR analysis

NMR samples were prepared by sampling the reaction at delineated times, transferring 500 µl aliquots to new NMR tubes, and adding 10% D2O to the NMR tube (for NMR deuterium lock). NMR spectra were recorded on an Agilent DD2 400 MHz spectrometer equipped with a OneNMR probe at 23 °C. Standard Agilent pulse sequences were employed. 19F NMR spectra are reported in ppm. A spectral window with a range of -50 to -250 ppm was used. Following preliminary NMR scans, the 72 h samples were acidified (10 µl of 0.1 M HNO3 were added to each NMR tube) to transform aqueous F⁻ into HF (pKa ≈ 3.2). The parameters of NMR analysis are outlined in Supplementary Table 1. All 19F NMR spectra processing was completed using MestreNova software. A detailed description of the methodology for referencing 19F NMR spectra is provided in the ‘Results’ and ‘Discussion’ sections of Supplementary Information, and identified chemical shifts are tabulated in Supplementary Table 2.

Quantitative LC–MS measurements

Mass spectrometric measurements were performed with a Shimadzu Scientific Instruments 8060 Triple-Quad LC–MS system, equipped with a Nexera LC-40D xs UHPLC, consisting of a CBM-40 Lite system controller, a DGU-405 Degasser Unit, two LC-40D XS UHPLC pumps, a SIL-40C XS autosampler and a Column Oven CTO-40S. Mass spectra were subsequently recorded with the triple quadrupole (TQ) 8060 mass spectrometer. The samples were held at 20 °C in the autosampler compartment after ultrasonification. One microlitre of each sample was injected into a sample loop and separated on a Shimadzu Velox PFPP column, 1.8 μl, 2.1 × 100 mm, equilibrated at 40 °C in a column oven. A binary gradient was used during the measurements, respectively, solvent A (Water, HPLC grade Chromasolv, with 0.1% Formic Acid) and solvent B (MeOH, HPLC grade Chromasolv).

HR-MS analysis

HR-MS measurements were conducted using a Shimadzu Scientific Instruments QToF 9030 LC–MS system, equipped with a Nexera LC-40D xs UHPLC set-up identical to the one used in the quantitative LC–MS analysis. For mass spectrum recording, the quadrupole time-of-flight (QToF) 9030 mass spectrometer was employed. Samples were maintained at 4 °C within the autosampler compartment. A 1-µl aliquot of each sample was injected into a sample loop and separated using a Shimadzu Velox PFPP column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm), equilibrated at 40 °C in a column oven. A binary gradient was applied with solvent A being water (HPLC grade Chromasolv) with 0.1% formic acid and solvent B being acetonitrile (HPLC grade Chromasolv) with no additives. The flow rate was set at 0.250 ml min−1, and the gradient composition varied as follows: from 0 to 2 min, 60% A and 40% B; from 2 to 6.5 min, 15% A and 85% B; from 6.5 to 7.5 min, 15% A and 85% B; from 7.5 to 7.51 min, 60% A and 40% B; and from 7.51 to 10 min, 60% A and 40% B. The ionization source operated in electrospray ionization mode with the electrospray needle at −3.0 kV. Nebulizer gas was set at 2 l min−1, heating gas flow at 10 l min−1 and the interface at 300 °C. Dry gas was maintained at 10 l min−1, with the desolvation line at 250 °C and the heating block at 400 °C. Mass spectra were acquired in the range of 50–1,000 m/z in negative ion mode, while data-dependent MS/MS fragmentation was performed in the range of 25–500 m/z with a collision energy spread of 40 ± 20 V (CE spread). Measurements and data post-processing were managed using LabSolutions 5.97 Realtime Analysis and PostRun software.
