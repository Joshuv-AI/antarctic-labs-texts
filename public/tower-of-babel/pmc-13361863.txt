Innovations in liver disease assessment: clinical insights from spleen stiffness measurement in chronic liver diseases
Authors: Zhi-Guo Wu, Long Yang, Meng-Bo Wu, Wen-Na Xi, Ke Sun, Mao-Xing Ai, Bao-Gang Xie, Rong-Yan Yu, Shui-Lin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic liver diseases are a major global health burden, with cirrhosis and portal hypertension driving morbidity and mortality. Non-invasive assessment of portal hypertension is critical for risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Spleen stiffness measurement (SSM) has emerged as a novel elastography-based biomarker reflecting the hemodynamic consequences of portal hypertension.Main bodyThis review summarizes current evidence on SSM across major chronic liver disease etiologies, including viral hepatitis, metabolic-associated fatty liver disease, and autoimmune liver diseases. We outline the principles and technical evolution of SSM across elastography platforms, including transient elastography, shear-wave elastography, and magnetic resonance elastography. Emerging data indicate that SSM provides incremental value beyond established non-invasive markers such as liver stiffness measurement and platelet count, particularly in refining risk stratification for clinically significant portal hypertension and high-risk varices. SSM may reduce unnecessary endoscopic screening and improve patient selection for therapeutic interventions. This review is based on a structured literature search of PubMed and Web of Science.ConclusionsSSM is a promising non-invasive biomarker with increasing translational relevance in the management of chronic liver diseases. With further standardization and prospective validation, SSM has the potential to support precision risk stratification and guide individualized clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Liver diseases encompass a broad spectrum of conditions, including viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), autoimmune liver diseases (AILD), and cirrhosis. As the end stage of many chronic liver disorders, cirrhosis is frequently accompanied by portal hypertension, which may lead to serious complications such as ascites and esophageal variceal bleeding [1, 2]. Traditional approaches for evaluating liver disease, particularly liver biopsy, are associated with several limitations and potential risks [3, 4]. Therefore, the development of reliable non-invasive assessment methods is of considerable importance for improving disease evaluation and optimizing patient management.

Importantly, spleen stiffness reflects liver disease severity primarily through its close pathophysiological association with portal hypertension and splenic congestion secondary to chronic liver disease. Progressive increases in portal venous pressure impair splenic venous outflow, leading to splenic enlargement, elevated intrasplenic pressure, and structural remodeling of the splenic parenchyma. These hemodynamic and microstructural changes contribute to increased spleen stiffness, which therefore serves as an indirect but clinically meaningful surrogate marker of portal hypertension and its related complications, such as gastroesophageal varices and hepatic decompensation. Consequently, spleen stiffness measurement provides complementary information to liver stiffness measurement by capturing downstream portal-systemic alterations rather than parenchymal liver changes alone.

Early identification of patients with clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) is crucial for preventing complications such as variceal bleeding. Although measurement of the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) remains the gold standard for assessing portal hypertension, its invasive nature and technical complexity limit its widespread use in routine clinical practice [5]. Similarly, endoscopy, while considered an accurate method for detecting gastroesophageal varices, is also invasive and may cause discomfort, including severe coughing or vomiting during the procedure, which could potentially increase the risk of variceal rupture [6] .

In recent years, SSM has emerged as a promising non-invasive diagnostic approach, providing new insights into the evaluation and management of chronic liver diseases. SSM has been proposed as a complementary tool to liver stiffness measurement (LSM) for the non-invasive assessment of CSPH. Several studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between spleen stiffness and the severity of portal hypertension [7–9]. Moreover, SSM has shown good reproducibility and feasibility in clinical practice, offering a dynamic parameter for the non-invasive evaluation of portal hypertension [10] .Following the introduction of the Baveno VII consensus, the clinical value of SSM in ruling out high-risk varices in patients with cirrhosis has been increasingly recognized, highlighting its potential utility in routine clinical practice. In this review, we summarize current evidence regarding the role of SSM in major chronic liver diseases. We discuss its underlying principles, technological developments, and clinical applications, with particular emphasis on its role within the Baveno VII framework. In addition, we highlight current limitations and future research directions, including the potential integration of SSM with other non-invasive diagnostic modalities. Through this review, we aim to provide clinicians and researchers with a comprehensive overview of this emerging technique and its potential role in improving the diagnosis and management of chronic liver diseases.

Although spleen stiffness measurement has been investigated across a wide spectrum of chronic liver diseases, the majority of currently available evidence is derived from studies involving viral hepatitis, NAFLD)/MAFLD, and autoimmune liver diseases. These etiologies represent major global causes of chronic liver disease and have been the primary focus of most clinical investigations evaluating the diagnostic and prognostic value of SSM. In contrast, evidence regarding other etiologies, particularly alcohol-associated liver disease and less common causes of cirrhosis, remains relatively limited and heterogeneous. Therefore, in this review we primarily focus on these three well-studied disease categories, while also briefly discussing emerging evidence and future research needs for other etiologies of chronic liver disease.

Principles and techniques of SSM

SSM is a non-invasive technique used to evaluate hemodynamic alterations associated with portal hypertension.This approach is based on ultrasound elastography, which quantifies the stiffness of splenic tissue as an indirect indicator of portal venous pressure. As portal hypertension progresses, impaired splenic venous outflow leads to passive splenic congestion and splenomegaly, resulting in increased intrasplenic pressure and tissue stiffness. Consequently, spleen stiffness reflects the hemodynamic consequences of portal hypertension and may provide a useful non-invasive marker for assessing disease severity.

Currently, several elastography-based techniques are used to measure spleen stiffness, including transient elastography (TE), point shear wave elastography (p-SWE), two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE), acoustic radiation force impulse imaging (ARFI), and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) [11]. These techniques generate and detect shear waves using different physical principles to quantify tissue stiffness.

FibroScan-based LSM, derived from the principles of TE, is currently the most widely used and well-validated technique in clinical practice. In patients with chronic liver diseases, TE-derived LSM values can be stratified into five risk categories (5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 kPa) to reflect the progressively increasing risk of hepatic decompensation and liver-related mortality [12]. Compared with LSM, SSM using TE has been shown to correlate more strongly with HVPG, CSPH, and the presence of high-risk esophagogastric varices prone to bleeding [13] .

SWE is an advanced ultrasound-based elastography technique that includes two main modalities: p-SWE and 2D-SWE. Both approaches combine conventional ultrasound imaging with elastography, allowing precise placement of the region of interest under real-time B-mode ultrasound guidance. SWE has been integrated into high-end ultrasound platforms and is capable of generating shear waves in deeper tissues. This feature enables reliable assessment of tissue stiffness, even in patients with ascites [13].

Acoustic radiation force impulse (ARFI) imaging is an ultrasound-based elastography technique that generates localized mechanical excitation within the tissue using focused acoustic pulses. The induced shear waves propagate outward from the excitation site, allowing quantitative measurement of shear wave velocity and thereby enabling assessment of tissue stiffness [14, 15] .

In recent years, MRE has attracted increasing attention because of its ability to evaluate the viscoelastic properties of biological tissues. Several studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between MRE-derived shear wave velocity and HVPG, highlighting its potential value in the assessment of portal hypertension [16, 17]. However, the high cost of MRE, limited availability, and the requirement for specialized acquisition and interpretation by experienced radiologists restrict its widespread use in routine clinical practice.

Despite its clinical potential, SSM has several limitations that should be considered in clinical practice and may perform suboptimally under certain conditions. For example, in patients with splenic infarction, ischemic necrotic areas within the spleen may lead to measurements that do not accurately represent overall splenic stiffness, potentially resulting in misleading interpretations. Similarly, in cases of splenic vein thrombosis, thrombus-related obstruction of venous outflow may alter splenic hemodynamics, thereby affecting the reliability of SSM and complicating the interpretation of stiffness measurements.

Technical considerations and standardization of SSM

Although SSM has emerged as a promising non-invasive tool for assessing portal hypertension and related complications, several technical considerations and methodological factors may influence measurement accuracy and reproducibility. Standardization of measurement procedures is therefore essential for ensuring reliable results across different clinical settings.

Patient positioning and measurement protocol

Patient positioning may affect the quality of SSM acquisition. In most ultrasound-based elastography techniques, measurements are typically performed with the patient in the supine or slight right lateral decubitus position with the left arm elevated to widen the intercostal space. The transducer is usually placed in the left intercostal space to obtain optimal acoustic access to the spleen. Breath holding during acquisition may further improve measurement stability and reduce motion-related artifacts.

Region of interest selection

Appropriate selection of the region of interest (ROI) is critical for accurate spleen stiffness assessment. The ROI should be placed within homogeneous splenic parenchyma and should avoid large vessels, the splenic hilum, and areas close to the capsule. Placement of the ROI too close to vascular structures may introduce measurement variability due to pulsatile blood flow. Ultrasound guidance is therefore recommended to ensure accurate localization of the measurement area.

Measurement number and quality criteria

To improve reliability, multiple measurements are generally recommended during SSM acquisition. Similar to liver stiffness measurement, several studies suggest that at least 5–10 valid measurements should be obtained, with the median value used for analysis. Quality control parameters such as the interquartile range to median ratio (IQR/median) may also be applied to assess measurement reliability, although standardized criteria for SSM are still being refined.

Feasibility and technical limitations

The feasibility of spleen stiffness measurement may be influenced by patient-related factors. Obesity, narrow intercostal spaces, and limited acoustic windows can reduce measurement success rates. In addition, the relatively small size and variable anatomical position of the spleen compared with the liver may further complicate measurement acquisition. These factors highlight the importance of operator experience and appropriate patient selection when performing SSM.

Potential confounding factors

Several conditions unrelated to chronic liver disease may influence spleen stiffness values and should be considered when interpreting SSM results. Splenic infarction and splenic vein thrombosis can significantly alter splenic tissue elasticity and lead to misleading measurements. Acute systemic inflammation, congestive heart failure, or other causes of splenic congestion may also affect spleen stiffness independently of portal hypertension. Careful clinical evaluation is therefore necessary to avoid misinterpretation of SSM findings.

Viral hepatitis

Currently, SSM has been primarily applied in patients with viral hepatitis to assess liver fibrosis, portal hypertension, and related complications. Previous studies have shown that approximately 15–40% of untreated patients with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection may progress to cirrhosis, liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma [29]. Therefore, rapid, dynamic, and non-invasive monitoring of disease progression and complications in patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is of considerable clinical importance. FibroScan, which is based on transient elastography, has been recommended by several national and international guidelines for the assessment of liver fibrosis [30, 31]. However, LSM obtained using FibroScan may be influenced by several factors, including serum total bilirubin (TBIL) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, which may lead to an overestimation of fibrosis severity.

Owing to the unique anatomical structure and microcirculatory characteristics of the spleen, numerous studies have highlighted the diagnostic and predictive value of SSM in evaluating liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and related complications [18, 32, 33]. Feng et al. [20] demonstrated that spleen stiffness is an effective non-invasive tool for assessing liver fibrosis, particularly in situations where LSM results are unstable or when advanced fibrosis is present. These findings suggest that SSM may serve as a valuable adjunct to LSM in the evaluation of hepatic fibrosis. Lu et al. [21] further reported that combining LSM with SSM using TE significantly improves the diagnostic accuracy for staging liver fibrosis in patients with CHB. In addition, a recent study conducted by Chen Jinjun and colleagues at Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University, published in the Journal of Hepatology, demonstrated that FibroScan-based SSM shows excellent diagnostic performance for ruling out gastroesophageal varices in patients with high-risk HBV-related cirrhosis [19]. This study supports the Baveno VII consensus algorithm, which recommends that in cirrhotic patients who do not meet the Baveno VI criteria, an SSM value ≤ 40 kPa can reliably exclude high-risk varices (HRV), thereby avoiding unnecessary endoscopic screening, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Such an approach may substantially reduce the burden of endoscopic procedures and associated healthcare costs. Moreover, SSM has also shown clinical utility in hepatitis C virus (HCV)-related liver disease. Studies comparing SSM among patients with cirrhosis of different etiologies, including HCV infection, have demonstrated that spleen stiffness serves as a reliable marker for predicting portal hypertension and its related complications. Interestingly, increases in spleen stiffness may precede changes in liver stiffness, even in cases with minimal hepatic fibrosis [34]. Furthermore, Ivica et al. [22] reported that real-time 2D-SWE can effectively stratify stages of liver fibrosis, distinguish advanced fibrosis from cirrhosis, and differentiate compensated from decompensated cirrhosis.

In recent years, SSM has demonstrated substantial clinical value in assessing portal hypertension and its complications in patients with chronic liver disease. Studies suggest that SSM serves as a dynamic and non-invasive indicator of portal hypertension, reflecting the spectrum of pathophysiological changes from early to advanced stages of cirrhosis. It is particularly useful for monitoring the severity of portal hypertension and evaluating treatment response. SSM has shown good accuracy in predicting CSPH and has emerged as a reliable non-invasive diagnostic tool. In certain settings, its predictive performance may even exceed that of LSM. In addition, SSM has shown promise in reducing the need for endoscopic screening by enabling non-invasive evaluation of HRV, thereby improving clinical management [26, 34]. Liu et al. [35] demonstrated that spleen stiffness measured using ultrasound-based SWE exhibits high diagnostic performance for detecting esophageal varices in patients with cirrhosis. These findings suggest that spleen stiffness may serve as a useful imaging biomarker for assessing esophageal varices in clinical practice. Other studies have reported that although liver stiffness shows higher specificity for predicting esophageal varices, spleen stiffness is generally more sensitive and may provide superior overall diagnostic performance [36] .Furthermore, 2D-SWE measurements of both liver and spleen stiffness have been shown to effectively predict the severity of esophageal varices in cirrhotic patients, and the combination of these parameters further improves diagnostic accuracy [36] .A meta-analysis by Hu et al. [28] further confirmed the clinical value of SSM as a monitoring tool in chronic liver disease. Specifically, SSM may help rule out high-risk esophageal varices and thereby reduce unnecessary endoscopic examinations. The clinical utility of SSM has also been explored in treatment monitoring. For example, changes in spleen stiffness may predict the response of patients with high-risk varices to non-selective beta-blocker (NSBB) therapy, providing a useful indicator for evaluating treatment efficacy [26]. In addition, SSM has been reported to serve as a non-invasive predictor of postoperative outcomes, including stent dysfunction and clinical prognosis, in patients undergoing transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) placement [26] .

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

With global economic development and lifestyle changes, NAFLD has become one of the most prevalent liver diseases worldwide, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 25% [37, 38]. NAFLD is a chronic liver disorder associated with metabolic dysfunction and encompasses a spectrum of conditions, including simple steatosis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), NASH-related cirrhosis, and, in rare cases, hepatocellular carcinoma. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are therefore crucial for slowing disease progression [39]. Because liver biopsy is invasive and subject to sampling error and inter-observer variability, increasing attention has been directed toward the development of non-invasive diagnostic approaches, including SSM. Imaging-based diagnostic techniques, valued for their non-invasiveness and reproducibility, have been increasingly used to evaluate liver fibrosis in patients with NAFLD. However, conventional imaging modalities such as ultrasound, CT, and MRI often fail to detect early fibrotic changes associated with NAFLD, thereby limiting their value in early-stage diagnosis. In contrast, SSM has emerged as a promising non-invasive technique for patients with NAFLD and may provide improved accuracy in the assessment of liver fibrosis and portal hypertension [40]. Dong et al. [23] demonstrated that both LSM and SSM assessed by 2D-SWE can serve as useful non-invasive parameters for diagnosing portal hypertension in patients with NAFLD, with diagnostic performance superior to that of conventional composite scoring systems. Similarly, Odriozola et al. [24] validated the value of SSM in diagnosing clinically CSPH in patients with metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), and further showed that incorporating SSM into the Baveno VII criteria may improve diagnostic accuracy.

One study reported that SSM is an independent predictor of high-risk varices, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.987, indicating excellent diagnostic performance [25]. In addition, combining SSM with other non-invasive parameters, such as LSM and the hepatic steatosis index, may further improve the assessment of disease severity in patients with NAFLD. Evidence also suggests that the combined use of LSM and SSM provides better diagnostic performance than either parameter alone for identifying moderate-to-severe NAFLD [25] .

Notably, SSM has shown considerable potential in the management of NAFLD, particularly for assessing portal hypertension and predicting disease progression. However, evidence in this field remains limited and continues to evolve. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate these preliminary findings and to clarify the optimal clinical role of SSM in the management of patients with NAFLD.

Autoimmune liver disease

Autoimmune liver diseases (AILDs) comprise a group of chronic liver disorders mediated by dysregulated immune responses, including autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC). These conditions are characterized by persistent hepatic inflammation, which may progress to cirrhosis and liver failure, resulting in severe complications. As the largest peripheral lymphoid organ, the spleen plays a key role in immune regulation, and alterations in splenic function may reflect dynamic changes in the systemic immune response. In this context, SSM, as an emerging non-invasive diagnostic technique, has demonstrated potential clinical value in several studies for assessing disease progression, predicting complications, and monitoring treatment response in patients with AILDs.

The application of SSM in autoimmune liver diseases primarily focuses on the non-invasive assessment of PH. Because AIH can progress to cirrhosis and PH, SSM may serve as a useful tool for indirectly reflecting portal pressure by quantifying splenic stiffness, thereby aiding in the evaluation of disease severity and the risk of complications. In a prospective cohort study involving patients with PBC, SSM was shown to accurately predict portal hypertension and help exclude high-risk esophageal varices [26]. In that study, 114 patients with PBC underwent both liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and SSM. The results indicated that among patients with SSM > 40 kPa, 21% had high-risk esophageal varices, whereas no high-risk varices were detected in those with SSM ≤ 40 kPa. Furthermore, patients with both LSM ≥ 10 kPa and SSM > 40 kPa had a significantly higher risk of liver decompensation. During follow-up, increases in spleen stiffness were also associated with a higher likelihood of hepatic decompensation. These findings suggest that SSM may serve not only as a non-invasive tool for assessing portal hypertension in patients with PBC but also as a useful aid in clinical decision-making. For example, SSM may help determine whether endoscopic evaluation is necessary for assessing the risk of esophageal varices, thereby potentially reducing unnecessary invasive procedures and improving patient management. Similarly, Roccarina et al. [27] demonstrated that SWE-based measurements of liver and spleen stiffness in patients with PSC represent a reliable and reproducible approach for the non-invasive assessment of liver disease severity. This technique may enable effective risk stratification for liver-related outcomes. Notably, SSM may also help identify patients at higher risk of portal hypertension and esophageal varices, thereby facilitating appropriate triage for endoscopic evaluation.

It should be noted that the clinical performance of SSM may be influenced by conditions such as splenic infarction or splenic vein thrombosis, which can compromise measurement accuracy. Therefore, patient-specific factors and potential confounding variables should be carefully considered when interpreting SSM results in clinical practice. The application of SSM in AILDs remains an area of ongoing investigation. Although current evidence is limited, available studies suggest that SSM may provide useful information for assessing portal hypertension in patients with AILDs, particularly in evaluating disease progression and the risk of complications.

Alcohol-associated liver disease and other etiologies

Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) is one of the leading causes of chronic liver disease and cirrhosis worldwide. Similar to other chronic liver diseases, progressive hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis in ALD may lead to the development of portal hypertension and its related complications. In theory, SSM may provide valuable information for assessing portal hypertension in patients with ALD, given the shared hemodynamic mechanisms underlying portal hypertension across different etiologies.

However, compared with viral hepatitis and metabolic-associated liver disease, the clinical evidence supporting the use of SSM in alcohol-associated liver disease remains relatively limited. Existing studies are often small in sample size and heterogeneous in design, and standardized diagnostic thresholds have not yet been clearly established for this population. Moreover, alcohol-related inflammation and acute alcoholic hepatitis may potentially influence elastography-based measurements, which could affect the interpretation of spleen stiffness values.

Beyond ALD, SSM has also been explored in other less common causes of chronic liver disease, including genetic or metabolic disorders. However, available data remain sparse, and further prospective studies are required to determine the diagnostic accuracy, optimal cut-off values, and prognostic significance of SSM across these diverse disease etiologies.

Taken together, while SSM shows promise as a non-invasive tool for evaluating portal hypertension across various chronic liver diseases, additional large-scale and etiology-specific studies are needed to clarify its clinical utility in alcohol-associated liver disease and other less frequently studied conditions.

It should not be applied to individuals with decompensated cirrhosis.

Underlying potential mechanisms

Hemodynamic mechanisms: splenic congestion secondary to portal hypertension

Portal hypertension is a hallmark of advanced chronic liver disease and plays a central role in the development of splenic alterations. Elevated portal venous pressure impairs splenic venous outflow, leading to progressive splenic congestion and splenomegaly. This hemodynamic disturbance increases intrasplenic pressure and contributes to structural changes within the splenic parenchyma. Accumulating evidence indicates that spleen stiffness largely reflects these hemodynamic consequences of portal hypertension [41, 42]. Therefore, spleen stiffness measurement is considered an indirect but clinically meaningful indicator of portal hypertension severity and its associated complications, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Structural remodeling of the spleen

Persistent splenic congestion may lead to structural remodeling of the splenic tissue. Experimental and pathological studies suggest that prolonged elevation of intrasplenic pressure can promote extracellular matrix deposition and fibrous tissue proliferation within the spleen. These structural alterations may increase tissue stiffness and contribute to the elevated spleen stiffness values detected by elastography-based techniques. Thus, spleen stiffness may reflect not only hemodynamic changes but also structural remodeling of splenic tissue in the setting of chronic liver disease.

Hypothesized inflammatory and immune mechanisms

In addition to hemodynamic and structural changes, inflammatory and immune-related mechanisms have been proposed but remain incompletely understood. Splenic congestion may alter the local immune microenvironment and promote activation of inflammatory pathways. Some studies have suggested that inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) may participate in extracellular matrix remodeling and splenic fibrogenesis. However, the current evidence supporting these mechanisms remains limited, and most observations are derived from experimental models or indirect pathological findings. Further mechanistic studies are required to clarify the role of immune and inflammatory pathways in spleen stiffness alterations.

Conclusion

SSM has emerged as a promising non-invasive tool for the evaluation and management of chronic liver diseases. Accumulating evidence suggests that SSM may provide valuable information for assessing portal hypertension, predicting disease progression, and guiding clinical decision-making. With ongoing technological advances and further clinical validation, SSM is expected to play an increasingly important role in the non-invasive diagnosis and management of liver diseases, ultimately contributing to improved patient care and clinical outcomes.

Challenges and future directions

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting SSM as a promising non-invasive tool for the assessment of CLDs, several important challenges continue to limit its widespread clinical implementation. First, the lack of standardized measurement protocols—including variations in technical parameters, cutoff values, and device-specific differences—hinders cross-study comparisons and broader clinical adoption. Second, operator dependency and variability in measurement quality, particularly in patients with difficult acoustic windows or severe obesity, may affect the reliability and reproducibility of SSM across different clinical settings. Third, the heterogeneity of chronic liver diseases—such as viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver diseases, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease—complicates the establishment of universally applicable diagnostic thresholds and predictive models.

In addition, several limitations in the current evidence base should be acknowledged. Most available studies are observational and conducted in relatively small or single-center cohorts, which may limit the generalizability of their findings. Differences among elastography devices and measurement techniques may also introduce methodological heterogeneity across studies. Moreover, certain clinical conditions—such as splenic infarction, splenic vein thrombosis, or systemic inflammatory states—may influence spleen stiffness independently of portal hypertension, potentially leading to misclassification or overestimation of disease severity. These factors highlight the need for cautious interpretation of SSM results in clinical practice.

Furthermore, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying alterations in spleen stiffness remain incompletely understood. Although SSM has been strongly associated with portal hypertension, fibrosis severity, and variceal risk, the complex interactions among splenic hemodynamics, immune cell infiltration, extracellular matrix remodeling, and local fibrogenesis require further investigation. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms may provide novel insights into potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets beyond pressure-related changes alone.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal and multicenter studies to better define the prognostic value and dynamic monitoring potential of SSM across diverse populations and clinical settings. Particular attention should be given to the relationship between changes in SSM and responses to antiviral therapy, especially in patients with CHB. As strategies for achieving a clinical cure continue to evolve, understanding how SSM reflects the immunological and virological effects of pegylated interferon (PEG-IFN) may help improve patient stratification and treatment optimization. For instance, SSM may serve as a complementary biomarker for identifying CHB patients who are more likely to achieve a sustained virological response or functional cure with PEG-IFN–based therapies.

In summary, SSM represents a valuable addition to the current repertoire of non-invasive tools for the assessment of CLDs. However, addressing existing technical and methodological limitations, as well as gaining deeper mechanistic insights, will be essential for translating this promising technology into a widely adopted clinical decision-support tool in hepatology.
