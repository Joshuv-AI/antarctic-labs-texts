Helicity controls the direction of fluxes in rotating turbulence Preprint: APS/123-QED
Sébastien Gomé, Anna Frishman

Abstract
Turbulence sustains out-of-equilibrium fluxes that are shaped by conservation laws. Three-dimensional flows conserve energy and sign-indefinite helicity, both being transferred to small scales. Here, we uncover a dual organization of energy fluxes in 3D rotating flows, shaped by helicity. When sufficiently-fast inertial waves interact with a large-scale 2D flow, they conserve their helicity separately by sign. This causes an inverse energy transfer, from 3D to 2D motions, which promotes self-organization and spectral condensation. In contrast, slower modes exchange helicity with modes of opposite helicity sign, similarly to non-rotating 3D turbulence. This generates a forward energy transfer, from the large-scale 2D flow to small 3D scales, coexisting with the inverse transfer. We determine analytically these bi-directional energy transfers to the 2D mean flow via a quasi-linear wave-kinetic theory. The theory captures the main Reynolds number and rotation rate dependence of the mean-flow amplitude in Navier-Stokes simulations from zero to infinite rotation rates.



Inviscid invariants are expected to control out-of-equilibrium fluxes in turbulent systems.
For example, in two dimensions, an inviscid fluid conserves energy and enstrophy (vorticity squared). These
two sign-definite scale-related quantities
are constrained to flow in opposite directions: energy to large scales and enstrophy to small scales
(
21
;
29
)
. This leads to self-organization of turbulence into large-scale structures.
In contrast, three-dimensional turbulence
conserves energy and sign-indefinite helicity (the scalar product between velocity and vorticity)

(
30
)
.
This leaves the spectral fluxes unconstrained, with a simultaneous transfer of both energy and helicity to small scales
(
11
;
17
;
4
)
.

Defying this classification, 3D fluids in the presence of
external constraints, such as geometry, magnetic field or rotation,
can exhibit a dual organization of energy, with simultaneous transfers to large and small scales
(
1
;
9
;
49
;
19
;
41
;
15
;
6
;
51
;
45
;
47
)
.
Rotation, a primary ingredient in geophysical or astrophysical flows, with a crucial role in climate and weather modeling
(
50
;
24
)
,
provides a striking example:
under rotation,
three-dimensional fluctuations take the form of waves and self-organize into large-scale 2D structures
(
25
;
45
)
,
although the invariants are identical to those in 3D turbulence.

Meanwhile, when rotation is not too fast, 3D modes can also extract energy from large-scale 2D structures
43
;
13
, expected from, e.g., spontaneous generation of instabilities
(
48
;
8
)
.
Flows in the Earth’s oceans

(
5
)
and atmosphere
(
34
;
14
)
often lie in this regime.
These bi-directional energy pathways in rotating 3D turbulence lack a general understanding:
why are large scales energized in the absence of a sign-definite invariant?
How can upscale and downscale energy fluxes coexist?

Here we show that, in the presence of rotation, helicity crucially dictates the partition of energy.
For moderate rotation rates, after a large-scale 2D flow spontaneously forms, two types of modes exist:
fast waves, dominated by rotation, and slow waves, dominated by the 2D shear.
For the former, 2D-3D interactions conserve helicity by sign so energy is transferred
to
the 2D flow, while the latter break this conservation law and extract energy
from
the 2D flow.
We capture this behavior analytically via a mean-wave kinetic theory. Our work suggests a general mechanism for the generation of bi-directional fluxes in wave-dominated systems
(
2
;
31
)
.

We consider the 3D Navier-Stokes equations (3DNSE) describing an incompressible fluid in a rotating frame, i.e. subject to a Coriolis force
-2\Omega\mathbf{e}_{z}\times\bm{v}
, in a domain of dimensions

(L_{x},L_{y},L_{z})
.
The flow is forced by a random three-dimensional isotropic forcing
\bm{f}

where
\langle\bm{f}_{\bm{p}}(t)\bm{f}_{\bm{q}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2\epsilon\chi_{\bm{p}}\delta(\bm{p}+\bm{q})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,

\chi_{p}
is restricted to a shell around
k_{f}=2\pi/l_{f}
and energy is injected at rate
\epsilon
. The Reynolds number,
Re=\epsilon^{1/3}k_{f}^{-4/3}/\nu
, where
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity,
and
the Rossby number,
Ro=\epsilon^{1/3}k_{f}^{2/3}/(2\Omega)
, jointly determine the resulting state. In the following we will also use the combined parameter
Ro_{\epsilon}=Ro\times\sqrt{Re}
. We use GHOST
(
38
)
to solve this system numerically, taking
l_{f}=L_{y}/10
and
L_{x}=L_{z}=L_{y}/2=\pi

(and including hyperviscosity).

For low enough Ro, a large-scale 2D mean flow, termed a condensate, spontaneously emerges. This occurs after a transient where an inverse energy cascade among 2D modes develops once they are energized
(
1
)
.
For our choice of domain geometry, the condensate takes the form of jets, see Fig.
1
(a)-(b). We consider the statistical steady state with

\bm{U}=\langle\bm{v}\rangle=U(y)\mathbf{e}_{x}
the jet mean flow. Typically, most of its energy is contained in the lowest mode
k_{y}=2\pi/L_{y}
, so we approximate
U(y)={U^{\prime}}\sqrt{2}/k_{y}~\sin(k_{y}y)
,
where
{U^{\prime}}\equiv\sqrt{\int(\partial_{y}U)^{2}(dy/L_{y})}
denotes the rms shear rate.
Perhaps the most basic question is: what value does the condensate amplitude
U^{\prime}
take as a function of
\text{Ro},\text{Re}
and the box geometry? Here we answer this question in a mixed regime, where not all interactions with the condensate are wave-dominated, thereby completing
(
26
)
.

The condensate is fueled by 3D waves
(
26
)
, called inertial waves. For each 3D mode
\bm{p}
(
p_{z}\neq 0
) there are two wave polarizations
\bm{h}_{\bm{p}}^{s}
, such that
i\bm{p}\times\bm{h}_{\bm{p}}^{s}=sp\bm{h}_{\bm{p}}^{s}
, with chiralities
s=\pm 1
, dispersion relation
\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}=s2\Omega p_{z}/p
, and with a given helicity sign
s=\pm 1
.

U^{\prime}
is set by the energy transfer between the 3D fluctuations and the condensate,
T_{\rm 3D-2D}
, via the global energy balance:

\displaystyle\nu{U^{\prime}}^{2}=\int\langle uv\rangle\partial_{y}U\frac{dy}{L_{y}}=T_{\rm 3D-2D}

(1)

where
\bm{u}=\bm{v}-U
,
u=\bm{u}_{x},v=\bm{u}_{y}
, and
\langle uv\rangle
is the Reynolds stress. Our goal is thus to determine
T_{\rm 3D-2D}
as a function of
U^{\prime}
and
\Omega
, allowing to close Eq. (
1
).

Quasi-Linear Kinetic Theory —

As a result of energy condensation, mean-wave interactions dominate over wave-wave interactions (
\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}\ll\bm{U}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}
)
for most scales (below a wavenumber denoted by
k_{U}
).
Fluctuations are therefore governed by quasi-linear (QL) dynamics.
Decomposing the fluctuations field into helical waves,
\bm{u}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{p},s}a_{\bm{p}}^{s}\bm{h}_{\bm{p}}^{s}e^{i\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}t+i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{x}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\partial_{t}a_{\bm{p}}^{s}

\displaystyle=\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{q},\bm{k}=\pm\frac{2\pi}{L_{y}},\\
\tilde{s}=\pm s\end{subarray}}V_{\bm{p}\bm{k}\bm{q}}^{s\tilde{s}}U_{\bm{k}}^{*}a_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}*}e^{-i(\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}+\omega_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}})t}\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{p}\bm{q}}+f_{\bm{p}}^{s}e^{-i\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}t},

(2)

with
\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{p}\bm{q}}=\delta_{\bm{k}+\bm{p}+\bm{q},0}

and

V_{\bm{p}\bm{k}\bm{q}}^{s_{1},s_{2}}=-ip_{x}~(\bm{h}_{\bm{p}}^{-s_{1}}\cdot\bm{h}_{\bm{q}}^{-s_{2}})+ik_{y}~(h_{\bm{p},x}^{-s_{1}}~h_{\bm{q},y}^{-s_{2}})
the coupling coefficients with the 2D mode.

A 3D mode
a_{\bm{p}}^{s}
interacts with the condensate and with a companion mode
a_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}}
via Eq.
2
.
The interaction involves waves of either the same helicity sign
\tilde{s}=s
(
homochiral-wave interaction
), or of opposite helicity signs
\tilde{s}=-s
(
heterochiral-wave interaction
).
In addition, the helicity exchange between the large-scale 2D flow and the fluctuations is negligible due to scale separation
l_{f}/L_{y}\ll 1
, so the helicity of the fluctuations is conserved separately — see
(
26
)
and Supplementary Material (SM). Species with
opposite helicites can therefore exchange energy (and helicity)
only through heterochiral-wave interactions with the condensate.

In classical 3D turbulence,
the exchange of energy and helicity between opposite helicity modes
(
30
)

results in the transfer of both invariants to smaller scales
(
11
;
4
)
.
As shown in Fig.
1
(d), and explained throughout this Letter,
introducing rotation
progressively suppresses heterochiral-wave interactions,
with a profound impact on the energy fluxes.
Measuring the energy contribution from waves of either the same helicity sign (hollow circles), or of opposite helicity signs (colored circles), we observe that the condensate is fueled by the former, while the latter extract energy from it.

Rotation acts to select particular interactions, which contribute
only if they satisfy the near-resonance condition

\displaystyle|\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}+\omega_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}}|<{U^{\prime}},~~~\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}=s2\Omega\frac{p_{z}}{p},

(3)

for which the
frequency sum
\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}+\omega_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}}
is sufficiently small so that the interaction does not average-out over the slow time scale
t=o(1/{U^{\prime}})
. This can be shown by following wave kinetic theory
(
52
;
39
;
26
)
, considering the
dynamics at the lowest order in time-scale separation
{U^{\prime}}/\Omega

(
26
)
.
The larger the rotation, the more restrictive condition (
3
) becomes, decreasing the number of possible interactions.

Condition (
3
) is more easily satisfied by homochiral-wave interactions (
\tilde{s}=s
), for which it reads
|\omega_{p}^{s}+\omega_{q}^{s}|\approx|k_{y}\partial_{p}\omega_{p}^{s}|<U^{\prime}
assuming scale separation
l_{f}/L_{y}\ll 1
, than for heterochiral-wave interactions, where it requires
\omega_{\bm{p}}^{+}+\omega_{\bm{q}}^{-}\approx 2\omega_{\bm{p}}^{+}<U^{\prime}
.
Here we work in the regime where homochiral-wave interactions
fulfill resonant condition (
3
),

Ro_{\epsilon}>l_{f}/(2L_{y})
(see
26
).

For
\tilde{s}=-s
, a mode
\bm{p}
is involved only in homochiral-wave interactions if it violates the resonance condition (
3
), and participates in both types of interactions if it satisfies it. Mode
\bm{p}
therefore belongs to one of two sectors:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\text{Sector H: }2\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}>{U^{\prime}}\text{ (homochiral interactions)}\\
\text{Sector A: }2\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}\leq{U^{\prime}}\text{ (all interactions),}\end{cases}

(4)

illustrated in Fig.
2
(a).
Following (
4
),
modes in sector H have fast frequencies, corresponding to a wave-dominated behavior.
In contrast, the dynamics in sector A is shear-dominated and essentially unaffected by rotation.
As the frequency
\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}
decreases with
p
, a transition from sector H to sector A occurs when
p>p^{*}\equiv\frac{4\Omega|p_{z}|}{{U^{\prime}}}
.
The rotation rate, via
U^{\prime}/\Omega
, therefore controls the number of modes in sectors
A
and
H
.

The mean flow
U(y)\mathbf{e}_{x}
tends to shear the waves, moving their energy to increasing values of
|p_{y}|
, while leaving
p_{x}
and
p_{z}
unchanged.
This generates a positive energy flux towards large
|p_{y}|
, denoted by
\Pi^{s}_{\bm{p}}
for waves of helicity sign
s
, whose magnitude at small scales remains to be determined.
Energy injected in Sector H thus flows into Sector A (
p>p^{*})
, see Fig.
2
(b).
In the following we will
compute the energy flux
\Pi^{s}_{\bm{p}}
in sectors H and A separately.
This flux will, in turn, determine the energy transfer to the condensate from modes with given (
p_{x},p_{z},s
), equal to the difference between the energy injected into these modes,
\epsilon^{s}_{p_{x},p_{z}}
, and the flux to arbitrarily small scales:
\epsilon^{s}_{p_{x},p_{z}}-\Pi^{s}_{|p_{y}|\to\infty}
.

The spectral energy flux
\Pi_{\bm{p}}^{s}
is constrained by the conservation of single-sign helicity
H_{\bm{p}}^{s}=sp\langle|a_{\bm{p}}^{s}|^{2}\rangle/2
, occurring in sector H but not in A.
This can be seen from the spectral balance for the wave helicity, obtained using scale separation in the QL system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H_{\bm{p}}^{s}+\partial_{p_{y}}(sp\Pi^{s}_{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=-sp~\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{2}}\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}+sp\epsilon\chi_{\bm{p}}^{s},

(5)

where
\Pi^{s}_{\bm{p}}=-{U^{\prime}}(\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}+\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle+\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}-\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle)/(2\sqrt{2})
and
\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}=\Re\left[h_{\bm{p},x}^{s}h_{-\bm{p},y}^{-s}\left(\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}+\bm{k}}^{-s}\rangle+\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}-\bm{k}}^{-s}\rangle\right)\right]
is the Reynolds stress related to waves of opposite chiralities.
The transfer of helicity (and energy) between species
s
and
-s
is driven by the opposite-chirality correlators
\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}+\bm{k}}^{-s}\rangle
entering
\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}
in Eq. (
5
).

Energy flux in sector H —

In sector H, species
s
and
-s
are uncoupled and
\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}=0
.
Equation
5
therefore becomes a continuity equation for
H_{\bm{p}}^{s}
: helicity in sector H is conserved
by sign
, which is equivalent to the conservation of wave action
\langle|a_{\bm{p}}^{s}|^{2}\rangle/\omega_{\bm{p}}^{s}
for each species
s
.
Due to this additional sign-definite invariant, the steady-state energy flux solving (
5
) decays with
p_{y}
:

\Pi^{s}_{\bm{p}}=\epsilon^{s}_{p_{x},p_{z}}k_{f}/p
for
p>k_{f}
.
As
\epsilon^{s}_{p_{x},p_{z}}-\Pi^{s}_{|p_{y}|\to\infty}>0
, this implies that energy is transferred to the large-scale 2D flow, as depicted in Fig.
2
.

At large-enough rotation,
all modes are effectively in sector
H
. In this regime, all waves interacting with the large-scale 2D flow conserve their single-sign helicity:
rotation has turned sign-definite an otherwise sign-indefinite invariant.
This regime is obtained for
Ro_{\epsilon}\lesssim 0.1

in Fig.
1
(d), for which the transfer of energy is purely inverse, going

from
the waves
to
the large-scale 2D flow
(
26
;
28
)
.
In contrast, for
Ro_{\epsilon}\gtrsim 0.1
, heterochiral interactions are possible and generate a finite flux of energy to small scales, as we now explain.

Energy flux in sector A —

Modes in sector A are shear-dominated, and their energy balance is typical for a non-rotating flow. There, modes of opposite helicities interact,
helicity is no longer conserved by sign,
and energy is extracted from the shear flow.
Using scale separation
l_{f}/L_{y}\ll 1
, the dynamics effectively reduces to that for a linear shear flow, which is analytically solvable when changing variables to fluctuating cross-flow velocity
v
and vorticity
\eta
, see Eq. (
12
) and
(
20
)
.
The condensate generates vorticity from
z
-varying
v
,
which tilts the mean shear (via the RHS of Eq. (
12b
)),
and converts mean-flow energy into turbulent kinetic energy (via Eq. (
12c
)).

Assuming an incoming flux of
\Pi^{*}(p_{x},p_{z})
at

into sector A, the flux
\Pi_{\bm{p}}\equiv\Pi^{+}_{\bm{p}}+\Pi^{-}_{\bm{p}}
can be exactly computed (see End Matter), reading

\displaystyle\Pi_{\bm{p}}

\displaystyle\underset{p_{y}\gg p^{*}_{y}}{=}\frac{\Pi^{*}}{2}\Bigg[1+\frac{p^{*2}}{p_{0}^{2}}\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan\Big(\frac{p_{y}^{*}}{p_{0}}\Big)\Big)^{2}\Bigg],

(6)

\displaystyle\text{with }p^{*}=\frac{4|p_{z}|\Omega}{{U^{\prime}}},~~~p_{0}=\sqrt{p_{x}^{2}+p_{z}^{2}},~~~p_{y}^{*}=(p^{*2}-p_{0}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

for large
p_{y}
. The small-scale flux is finite (independent of

) and can be larger than the incoming energy
\Pi^{*}
, corresponding to an energy extraction from the mean flow. This is most efficient for small

(long interaction time with the mean flow) and large

(large tilting rate).
Note that Sector A can also be directly energized from the forcing shell

, and the resulting energy flux slightly differs (see SM).

Total energy transfer and closure —
To combine the results for sector H and A we, take the incoming flux
\Pi^{*}=\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}(p_{x},p_{z})k_{f}/p^{*}
.
The energy transfer to the 2D condensate as a function of the wavenumber
\bm{p}
of inertial waves,
{U^{\prime}}\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}

(see Eq. (

Waves in sector

{U^{\prime}}\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}<0

The total energy transfer to the 2D flow,
\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}-\Pi_{|p_{y}|\to\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\rm 3D-2D}}{\epsilon}

From Eq. (

From 2D condensates to strong eddy turbulence —

When, instead, fixing Re and increasing Ro, scaling
the
U^{\prime}\sim\Omega

\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}<1/{U^{\prime}}
, which breaks the QL approximation.
As a consequence, the homochiral sector H, which is the source of energy for the condensate, is cutoff at
p=k_{\Omega}
(sector A being cutoff at

where eddy-eddy interactions dominate).
As rotation is decreased,
k_{\Omega}
diminishes and
the energy input to the condensate is reduced,
until no modes exist in sector H when
Ro=0.5

k_{\Omega}(p_{z}=k_{f})=k_{f}
.
This marks the disappearance of condensates to a strong turbulence of 3D eddies.
Introducing the wavenumber cutoff into our QL theory and integrating the resulting Reynolds stress numerically, we reproduce the drop in
energy transfer and condensate amplitude
observed in DNS, see the solid lines in Fig.

While jets are found for most

The former may be due to time-scale competition between modes of different scales, consistent with the metastability of such states

Conclusion —

We have untangled the
organization of kinetic energy fluxes in rotating turbulence, showing it is controlled by helicity.
Large-scale 2D structures are energized by fast-enough inertial waves via wave-dominated interactions which conserve helicity by sign.
In contrast, the 2D flow loses energy to slow waves through shear-dominated interactions which break helicity-by-sign conservation,
the waves carrying
energy to small scales similarly to non-rotating 3D turbulence.
We have determined these bi-directional energy transfers analytically across
rotation regimes via a quasi-linear wave-kinetic theory, which reproduces the
Ro
and
Re
behavior of 2D condensates in Navier-Stokes simulations, as summarized in Fig.
4
.

Our results show that large-scale self-organization can be regulated by a sign-indefinite invariant in the presence of waves
(
7
;
46
)
, and thus arise even in systems that do not conserve multiple sign-definite quantities.
The mean-turbulent
framework developed here provides a route to analyzing such behavior in other settings
– such as thin layers
(
6
)
, chiral flows
(
15
)
, stratified flows

(
12
;
44
;
46
;
31
)
,
magnetohydrodynamics
(
40
;
22
)

plasmas
(
16
)
or geophysical flows
(
27
)

— and to elucidating the energy sinks and feedback mechanisms governing their self-organization.

The theory in §

(i)

Supplementary information

In this supplementary information, we detail the derivation of the mean-wave quasi-linear kinetic theory describing the interactions between a large-scale 2D flow and 3D inertial waves in rotating turbulence.
We first establish the near-resonant conditions that select particular interaction types with increased rotation (§
B
).
Wave-dominated interactions due to fast waves (in the sector called H), which conserve helicity by sign, are described in §
C
.
Shear-dominated interactions due to slow waves
(in the sector called A), which do not conserve helicity by sign, are described in §
D
.
We derive analytical expressions for the wave energy flux through scales in this system
in §
E
, and the resulting total energy transfer between the waves and the mean flow.
Finally, we introduce a wavenumber cutoff above which wave-wave or eddy-eddy dominate over mean-fluctuation interactions, which controls the energy balance at low rotation and the transition to a regime of eddy turbulence (§
F
).

We consider the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations (3DNSE) written in the rotating frame,

(23)

Here we will generally consider interactions between waves of opposite chiralities due to their coupling with the condensate mode
\bm{k}

.

\langle f_{\bm{p}}^{s}f_{\bm{q}}^{-s}\rangle=0

An additional equation is therefore needed to close the system, given by the evolution of the correlation between opposite polarity modes
\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s*}a_{\bm{p}}^{-s}\rangle

\displaystyle\frac{\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s*}a_{\bm{p}}^{-s}\rangle(t)-\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s*}a_{\bm{p}}^{-s}\rangle(0)}{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{q},\bm{k},s}\Bigg[U_{\bm{k}}^{*}V_{\bm{p}\bm{k}\bm{q}}^{-s,\tilde{s}}\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}}\rangle^{*}\Delta^{-s,\tilde{s}}+U_{\bm{k}}V_{\bm{p}\bm{k}\bm{q}}^{s\tilde{s}*}\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{-s}a_{\bm{q}}^{\tilde{s}}\rangle\Delta^{s\tilde{s}*}\Bigg]\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{p}\bm{q}}

Similarly to

Note also that there is a lower bound for the time

s=-\tilde{s}
. Hence, such an expansion makes sense for

U^{\prime}/4\Omega

We now consider the lowest complex-conjugate modes

\displaystyle=-ip_{x}-2ik_{y}V_{\bm{p}}^{ss}+O\left(\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle V_{\bm{p}}^{ss}\equiv-is\frac{p_{z}}{p}\left(1+\frac{2p_{x}^{2}}{p_{\perp}^{2}}\right)+\frac{p_{x}p_{y}}{p^{2}},~~~~~~~~~~~V_{\bm{p}}^{s,-s}\equiv\frac{p_{x}p_{y}}{p^{2}}\Big(1+\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{\perp}^{2}}\Big)+is\frac{p_{z}}{p}\Big(1-\frac{2p_{x}^{2}}{p_{\perp}^{2}}\Big).

When considering condition (

with correlators

\begin{split}\Phi_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s}&\equiv\frac{\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}+\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle+\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}-\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle}{2}=\frac{\langle a_{\bm{p}}^{s}a_{-\bm{p}+\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle+c.c}{2}+O\left(\frac{k_{y}}{p}\right)\end{split}

Now focusing on helicity, multiplying (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(spe_{\bm{p}}^{s})+\partial_{p_{y}}(sp\Pi^{s})=-\mathbb{1}_{\bm{p}\in A}sp~\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{2}}\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}+sp\epsilon\chi_{\bm{p}}^{s}.

\bm{k}
into its two chiralities, the helicity transfer with the waves is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k}}=k(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{+}-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{-})

at most,

\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}=\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{-s,s}

(52)

\text{sign}(s)

p

s

must therefore increase with

s

From Eq. (
50

\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{2}}\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}^{s,-s}<0

Therefore, in the presence of cross-helicity interactions, modes of positive and negative helicity extract energy from the mean flow symmetrically, to compensate for the increase of helicity of a particular sign due to the
p_{y}
energy flux.

94

S/4\ll 1
:

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\rm 3D-2D}^{\rm II}}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-\left(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4}\right)^{2}}}dp_{x}\Bigg[\underbrace{\frac{1}{k_{f}}\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{S}{4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}}_{\mathcal{I},\text{ energy input from H}}~~\underbrace{-\frac{S}{8}\frac{\log\Big(\frac{2\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}{Sk_{f}/4}\Big)}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}-\frac{S}{8}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}_{\mathcal{D},\text{ energy extraction from A}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+O\Big(\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\Big(\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{3}\Big)+\mathcal{C}_{{\rm disc},p_{z}}

(95)

with corrections in
O(L_{x}(S/4)^{3})
arising due to the expansion in
S/4
. In the following we estimate each term in (
95
) individually.

We start with the leading-order term in
L_{x}/l_{f}
, which comes from the last term in the integral in (
95
).
This term is not integrable at
p_{x}=0
, and its
p_{x}
-integral must therefore be interpreted carefully.
With finite size
L_{x}
, our development actually concerns a discrete sum in (
95
), whose lower bound is the same as the step
dp_{x}=2\pi/L_{x}
.
We therefore want to estimate

\displaystyle r_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{x}=mr_{x},m\in\mathbb{N}\\
r_{x}\leq p_{x}\leq\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}},

(96)

with
r_{x}\equiv 2\pi/L_{x}
being both the grid spacing and a cutoff at the first grid point.
If the lower bound in the sum was fixed (e.g
p_{x}=1
), we would use the Riemann result to obtain an integral of the continuous variable
p_{x}\in\mathbb{R}
, which yields a good estimate of the sum.
However, here the lower bound evolves with the discretization parameter.
A corrected estimate can be obtained by the Euler-Maclaurin series

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{x}=r_{x}}^{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-\left(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4}\right)^{2}}}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{x}}\int_{r_{x}}^{k_{f}}f(p_{x})+\frac{f\left(\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}}\right)+f(r_{x})}{2}+\frac{r_{x}}{24}\left[f^{\prime}\left(\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-\left(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4}\right)^{2}}\right)-f^{\prime}(r_{x})\right]

(97)

denoting
f(p_{x})=\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}/p_{x}^{2}
a function of the continuous variable
p_{x}\in[k_{x},k_{f}]
.
Here we have truncated the Euler-Maclaurin series at the second order, assuming the next orders be small due to their diminishing prefactors. However, these next order terms are not necessarily small in the parameter
r_{x}/k_{f}=l_{f}/L_{x}
.
More explicitly, using that
\int dp_{x}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}=-\frac{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}{p_{x}}-\arctan\Big(\frac{p_{x}}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}\Big)
, this leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{x}=r_{x}}^{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}}}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{x}}\Bigg[\frac{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-r_{x}^{2}}}{r_{x}}+\arctan\left(\frac{r_{x}}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-r_{x}^{2}}}\right)-\frac{\pi}{2}+O\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{3}\Bigg]+\frac{1}{2r_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-r_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\frac{r_{x}}{24}\Big[f^{\prime}\left(\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-\left(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4}\right)^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{r_{x}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-r_{x}^{2}}}+\frac{2}{r_{x}^{3}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-r_{x}^{2}}\Big].

(98)

The problem raised by estimating the sum with only the integral can be appreciated as
f(r_{x})\sim 1/r_{x}^{2}
leads to a term of the same order as the integral.
Note that

f^{\prime}(\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}})\simeq\frac{4}{Sk_{f}^{2}}+\frac{S}{2k_{f}^{2}}
so that its contribution is negligible compared to
2k_{f}/r_{x}^{3}
when
r_{x}/k_{f}\to 0
(also recall that this term is multiplied by
S/4
in equation (
89
)).

All in all, we obtain the leading order asymptotics

\displaystyle r_{x}\sum_{p_{x}=r_{x}}^{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}}}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{19}{12}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}-\frac{\pi}{2}+O\Big(\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}\Big)+O\left(\Big(\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\frac{S}{4}\Big)\right)^{3}

(99)

with
l_{f}=2\pi/k_{f}
.

All terms in (
95
) other than the last one can be estimated with their continuous integrals at the leading order.

For the third term in (
95
), we use that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log(2\sqrt{1-x^{2}})}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=0

(100)

so that

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}}}\frac{S}{8}\frac{\log\Big(\frac{2\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}{Sk_{f}/4}\Big)}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}=-\frac{S}{8}\log\frac{S}{4}+O\Big(\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}\Big)+O\Big(\Big(\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}\log\frac{S}{4}\Big)

(101)

where the last term comes from
the upper bound of the integral,

\int_{1-\epsilon}^{1}\frac{\log(2\sqrt{1-x^{2}})}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\simeq\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\frac{\log\sqrt{2u}}{\sqrt{2u}}\simeq-\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\log\epsilon
, with
\epsilon=S/4
.
Deviations from the discrete sum are here also in
O\left(\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}}\right)
.

For the energy input term in (
95
),
using that

\int dp_{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}=\arctan\Big(\frac{p_{x}}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}\Big)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{I}}{\epsilon}=\frac{2}{\pi}r_{x}\sum\left(1-\frac{S}{4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{S}{4}+O\left(\frac{S}{4}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}}\right)+O\left(\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}\right)

(102)

using the Mac Laurin expansion for the correction term due to discretization errors in
p_{x}
, coming from the term in
S/4
.

We finally obtain at the leading order in
L_{x}/l_{f}
and
S/4
:

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\rm 3D-2D}^{\rm II}}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{D}

(103)

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{S}{4}\Bigg(\frac{19}{12}\frac{L_{x}}{\pi l_{f}}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{S}{4}\Bigg)

(104)

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{S}{4}\frac{19}{12\pi}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}+O\left(\frac{S}{4}\right),~~~~~S=\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\Omega},

(105)

Note here that we have considered that that the term
S/4\log S/4
, which produces a steeper-than-linear behavior when
S\to 0
, is bounded by
O(S/4)
when
S/4\ll 1
.
Corrections to (
105
) other than those determined so far might involve combinations of functions of
S
and
L_{x}/l_{f},L_{z}/l_{f}
, which we determine in §
E.3
.

The energy transfer between the waves and the 2D condensate in (
105
) decreases with decreasing rotation, via increasing
S={U^{\prime}}/\Omega
, due to the growing number of modes in sector A that break conservation of
H_{\bm{p}}^{\pm}
and extract mean-flow energy.
This energy extraction is larger as
L_{x}/l_{f}
increases, due to vortex-tilting preferentially energizing modes with large
p_{z}/p_{x}\sim L_{x}/l_{f}
, via the term in
p_{z}/p_{x}
in (
87
).

The energy extraction in Layers I should follow the same logic in terms of
L_{x}/l_{f}
dependence, with a production of
kinetic energy at small scales in
p_{z}^{2}/p_{x}^{2}
, similarly to that in Layers II (Eq. (
87
).
However, as Layers I are constrained by
p_{z}<Sk_{f}/4\ll k_{f}
, there are fewer modes in Layers I within our approximation (
88
), so the production of kinetic energy should be bounded by
p_{z}^{2}/p_{x}^{2}\sim(\frac{S}{4})^{2}/p_{x}^{2}
, hence an order
(S/4)^{2}
lower than that in layers II, and to the left-most term in Eq. (
18
).
Therefore, we expect Layers I to dominantly behave as 2D-3C modes
p_{z}=0
, transferring energy to small scales at rate
\Pi^{I}\sim\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}/2
at leading order in
S
.
(The factor
1/2
comes from the vertical component
w
producing small-scale energy.)

These qualitative arguments on Layers I will be confirmed in §
E.2
and
E.3.2
, where we compute the energy flux due to these layers, and confirm it is of subleading order compared to (
105
).
We will also detail why layers I can be fully neglected compared to layers II with the parameter choice in our DNS (
L_{x}k_{f}=5
), even at
O(S/4)
.

E.2
Exact energy transfer

In the following, we compute all the contributions to the Reynolds stress, including Layers I.
We also
introduce an arbitrary cutoff
K(p_{z})
over which the Reynolds stress is integrated. Modes
p>K

Introducing such a cutoff has a particular physical importance at low rotation, which we discuss in §
F
.

We further introduce two intersection points of the surface
\Gamma

\displaystyle p_{z}^{0}\equiv\frac{|p_{x}|}{(\frac{16}{S^{2}}-1)^{1/2}}:\qquad p_{y}^{*}(p_{x},p_{z}^{0})=0,

Second,

\displaystyle p_{z}^{c}=\frac{SK}{4}

(107)

is he value of
p_{z}
at which the cutoff-curve

intersects
\Gamma
(see Fig.
6

For modes in layers I, the geometry of the curve
\Gamma
imposes two possible sub-cases:

Ia

–
The
p_{z}
-layer is below the parabola
\Gamma
(
|p_{z}|<p_{z}^{0}
and
p_{y}^{*}=0

Ib

Sector A is forced, but a parabolic section below the forcing shell lies in Sector H (
p^{1}_{z}>|p_{z}|>p_{z}^{0}

p_{y}>-p_{y}^{*}

Note that introducing a cutoff at

–

Layers IIa – (

The energy flux emerging from Sector H is transferred to Sector A, and is eventually cutoff. This case is solved by (

–

Layers IIb – (

The energy flux emerging from Sector H is cutoff before it can enter sector A (

This case is solved by (
57

We now detail the computation of the Reynolds stresses in layers Ib (see Fig.
6

There, all stresses are integrated from

In the

The corresponding velocity and vorticity correlators for

&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~p_{y}>p_{y}^{f}\end{aligned}\right.

Overall, this leads to the Reynolds stress in layers Ib:

\displaystyle\langle uv\rangle_{\bm{p}\bm{k}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}k_{f}^{2}}{\sqrt{2}U^{\prime}}\Bigg[\frac{p_{y}}{p^{4}}-\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big(\arctan\Big(\frac{p_{y}}{p_{0}}\Big)+\arctan\Big(\frac{p_{y}^{f}}{p_{0}}\Big)\Big)\Bigg],~~~~~-p_{y}^{f}<p_{y}<-p_{y}^{*}\\

E.3
Subleading corrections to the energy transfer

We now derive subleading corrections to solution (
105
).
These corrections will perfect the agreement with our DNS data and are also important for consistency and completeness.
In particular, we will establish why (
105

E.3.1
Layers II

First, note that we willingly dropped the term in
O\left(\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{3}\frac{k_{f}(p_{x}^{2}+p_{z}^{2})}{p_{z}p_{x}^{2}}\right)
in Eq. (
89
), coming from the expansion in
S/4\ll 1
.
Over integration, this term generates a correction to the total energy transfer
in
(\frac{S}{4})^{3}\Big[\log(\frac{S}{4})+L_{x}k_{f}\Big]=O\Big((\frac{S}{4})^{3}L_{x}k_{f}\Big)

The important source of corrections now arises due to discretization errors, in particular those due to the
p_{z}
-integration, denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{{\rm disc},p_{z}}
in (
95
).
These can be estimated via the Euler-Maclaurin formula as follows (we denote
r_{z}=2\pi/L_{z}
and
\alpha=\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{{\rm disc},p_{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{z}}{2}\left(\eqref{eq:uv_leading_1}|_{p_{z}=\alpha-r_{z}}+\eqref{eq:uv_leading_1}|_{p_{z}=\frac{Sk_{f}}{4}}\right)

(116)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\frac{r_{z}}{k_{f}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r_{z}\alpha}}\Bigg[1-\frac{Sk_{f}}{8\alpha}(1+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}})\Bigg]+\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\frac{r_{z}}{k_{f}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}}}\Bigg[1-\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}\Big)\Bigg]

(117)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\frac{r_{z}}{k_{f}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r_{z}k_{f}}}\Bigg[1-\frac{S}{8}(1+\frac{k_{f}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}})\Bigg]+\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\frac{r_{z}}{k_{f}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigg[1-\frac{1}{2p_{x}^{2}}(\frac{Sk_{f}}{4})^{2}\Big)\Bigg],

(118)

when considering only modes
p_{x}\ll k_{f}
in the first sum, which give the dominant contribution in

:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{{\rm disc},p_{z}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}\Bigg[1-\frac{S}{8}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\Bigg]+O\Big(\frac{l_{f}}{L_{z}}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\Big)=O\Big(\frac{S}{4}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{L_{z}}}\Big)

(119)

Note that we exclude the thin layer case
L_{z}k_{f}\sim 1
in this paper, where our
p_{z}-
integration does not hold. However, a situation where
L_{x}\gg L_{z}
could arise where this term is relevant.
Furthermore, with our parameter choice, these terms are not negligible compared to
O\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)
.

The leading-order discretization errors due to the
p_{x}
-integrations are already included in solution (
105
), as they contribute at
O(\frac{S}{4}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}})
.
Other discretization errors, due to the
p_{x}-
integrable terms in (

O(S\sqrt{L_{x}/l_{f}})
, like the one arising in (

All in all we obtain the corrected energy transfer in layers II:

\displaystyle T_{\rm 3D-2D}^{\rm II}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{\prime}\Bigg[1+\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}-\frac{S}{4}\Bigg(\frac{19}{12}\frac{L_{x}}{\pi l_{f}}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{S}{4}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}\Bigg)

(120)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}}\right)+O\left({\frac{l_{f}}{L_{x}}}\right)+O\Big(\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\Big(\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{3}\Big)+O\Big(\Big(\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}\log\frac{S}{4}\Big)\Bigg]

with
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/(1+\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}})
the energy injection rate corrected to include discretization errors.

E.3.2
Layers I

The energy transfer in layers I (
112
) is written as

\displaystyle\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{2}}\langle uv\rangle_{p_{x},p_{z}}^{\rm I}=\frac{\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}}{2}\Bigg[\underbrace{1}_{\mathcal{I},\text{ input from H}}\underbrace{-\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}\frac{k_{f}^{2}}{p_{0}^{2}}\Bigg[\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(\frac{p_{y}^{*}}{p_{0}})\Big)^{2}+\Big(\arctan(\frac{p_{y}^{f}}{p_{0}})-\arctan(\frac{p_{y}^{*}}{p_{0}})\Big)^{2}\Bigg]}_{\mathcal{D},\text{ extraction from A}}\Bigg]

when

Note here the prefactor
1/2
, which governs the injection of energy for
p_{z}\to 0
:
quasi-2D layers conserve horizontal enstrophy, and exhibit a forward cascade of the vertical component
w
, which produces small-scale energy. Energy is therefore equally distributed in the two dynamical variables,
w^{2}
and
||u_{\perp}||^{2}

The energy input to the condensate from layers I is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\int_{-k_{f}}^{-2\pi/L_{x}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{z}}}^{p_{z}^{1}}\frac{\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}}{2}dp_{z}dp_{x}=\frac{2\epsilon}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\frac{1}{2k_{f}}\Bigg[\arctan\left(\frac{p_{z}}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}}\right)\Bigg]_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{z}}}^{\frac{Sk_{f}}{4}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\epsilon}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\Bigg(\frac{S}{8}-\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}\Bigg)\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}=\frac{2\epsilon}{\pi}\Big(\frac{S}{8}-\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}\Big)\Bigg[\arctan\left(\frac{p_{x}}{\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}}}\right)\Bigg]_{0}^{k_{f}}=\epsilon\Big(\frac{S}{8}-\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}\Big),

(122)

where we have put the IR cutoff in
p_{x}
to zero in the last step, since the integral converges for small
p_{x}
.
Note that 2D modes
p_{z}=0
are excluded, and that this contribution vanishes when
S/8<\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}

To obtain an estimate of the production of kinetic energy from Layers I (denoted by
\mathcal{D}
), for which
0<p_{y}^{*}<p_{y}^{f}
,
we can consider two limiting behaviors in Eq. (
112

p_{0}\ll p_{y}^{*}
and
p_{y}^{*}\ll p_{0}
. These two limiting behaviors, which control the values of the
\arctan

p_{z}/p_{x}
.
Indeed,

p_{0}\ll p_{y}^{*}\Leftrightarrow p_{z}^{2}((\frac{4}{S})^{2}-2)2p_{x}^{2}

p_{z}^{2}>2p_{x}^{2}(\frac{S}{4})^{2}

Therefore, cases
p_{0}\ll p_{y}^{*}
and
p_{y}^{*}\ll p_{0}
correspond to different values of the ratio
\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{2p_{x}^{2}}(\frac{4}{S})^{2}
.
We now split the integral of the term in
\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}
in (
112
), hereby denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}
, as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\equiv\int\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}\simeq\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{z}}}^{\sqrt{2}\frac{p_{x}S}{4}}\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}+\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}\int_{\sqrt{2}\frac{p_{x}S}{4}}^{\frac{k_{f}S}{4}}\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}

(123)

Each integral is denoted, respectively, as
\int_{1}\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}
and

\int_{2}\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}
.

If
p_{0}\ll p_{y}^{*}\Leftrightarrow p_{z}>\sqrt{2}p_{x}(\frac{S}{4})

p_{0}\ll p_{y}^{f}
(as
p_{y}^{*}<p_{y}^{f})
, and therefore
p_{y}^{f}\simeq k_{f}
.
Similarly,
p_{0}\ll p_{y}^{*}\Rightarrow p_{y}^{*}\sim p^{*}

then the arctan terms can be estimated like in Layer II, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}}{2}\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}\frac{k_{f}^{2}}{p_{0}^{2}}\Bigg(\left(\frac{p_{0}}{p^{*}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{p_{0}}{p_{y}^{f}}-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{p_{0}}{p_{y}^{*}}\right)^{2}\Bigg)

(124)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}}{{U^{\prime}}}\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}k_{f}^{2}\Bigg(\left(\frac{S}{4p_{z}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{S}{4p_{z}}-\frac{1}{k_{f}}\right)^{2}\Bigg)

(125)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}}{2}\frac{k_{f}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}\Bigg(\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{S}{4}-\frac{p_{z}}{k_{f}}\right)^{2}\Bigg)

(126)

Note that here we do not make any assumption on
p_{y}^{*}/p_{y}^{f}
.
Upon integration in this geometrical sector, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{2}\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}=-\frac{2\epsilon}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\int_{\sqrt{2}\frac{p_{x}S}{4}}^{\frac{k_{f}S}{4}}dp_{z}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\Bigg(\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{S}{4}-\frac{p_{z}}{k_{f}}\right)^{2}\Bigg)

(127)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\Bigg[\Big(\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{2}\Big(\frac{k_{f}S}{4}-\frac{\sqrt{2}p_{x}S}{4}\Big)-\frac{k_{f}}{3}\Big[\Big(\frac{S}{4}-\frac{p_{z}}{k_{f}}\Big)^{3}\Big]_{\sqrt{2}\frac{p_{x}S}{4}}^{\frac{k_{f}S}{4}}\Bigg]

(128)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\Bigg[\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{k_{f}L_{x}}{2\pi}-1\right)-\sqrt{2}\frac{S}{4}\log\left(\frac{k_{f}L_{x}}{2\pi}\right)\Bigg]+\frac{k_{f}}{3}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}\frac{1}{p_{x}^{2}}\Big(\frac{S}{4}-\sqrt{2}\frac{p_{x}}{k_{f}}\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{3}

(129)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\Bigg[\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{k_{f}L_{x}}{2\pi}-1\right)-\sqrt{2}\frac{S}{4}\log(\frac{k_{f}L_{x}}{2\pi})\Bigg]+\frac{k_{f}}{3}\Bigg[-\frac{L_{x}}{2\pi}(\frac{S}{4})^{3}-3\sqrt{2}(\frac{S}{4})^{3}\log\left(\frac{k_{f}}{k_{x}}\right)\Bigg]

(130)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\frac{2}{3}\frac{L_{x}k_{f}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{3}+O\Big(\left(\frac{S}{4}\right)^{3}\log\left(\frac{L_{x}k_{f}}{2\pi}\right)\Big)

Now, if
p_{y}^{*}\ll p_{0}

(132)

with in the .

\displaystyle\int_{1}\mathcal{D}_{p_{x},p_{z}}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}}^{k_{f}}dp_{x}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{L_{z}}}^{\sqrt{2}\frac{p_{x}S}{4}}dp_{z}\frac{\epsilon}{k_{f}^{2}}\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}}\frac{k_{f}^{2}}{p_{x}^{2}+p_{z}^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}

where in the last line we extend

Surprisingly, this term does not dominate in

\displaystyle T_{\rm 3D-2D}^{\rm I}\simeq\epsilon\Bigg(\frac{S}{8}-\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}-\frac{2}{3\pi}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\Big(\frac{S}{4}\Big)^{3}\Bigg),

We see from (

\displaystyle T_{\rm 3D-2D}\approx T_{\rm 3D-2D}^{\rm II}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{\prime}\Bigg[1+\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}-\frac{S}{4}\Bigg(\frac{19}{12}\frac{L_{x}}{\pi l_{f}}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{S}{4}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{L_{x}}{l_{f}}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}\Bigg)\Bigg]

The function (

) is shown in Figure

With this estimate of the energy transfer, we obtain a flux-loop state

.

E.4
Numerical integration without cutoff

We now integrate numerically the Reynolds stress in Eq. (
113
) over a discrete set of wavenumbers
p_{z}=2\pi m_{z}/L_{z}
and
p_{x}=2\pi m_{x}/L_{x}
, where
m_{z},m_{x}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This amounts to making a semi-continuous approximation only along
p_{y}
, which is still treated as a continuous variable.
Note that we use the value of
\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}

corresponding to the same discrete forcing as in the DNS, such that
\sum_{p_{y}}\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}=\epsilon
.
In this section, we let the wavenumber cutoff
K\to\infty
.

Figure
7
shows the total energy transfer
T_{\rm 3D-2D}[S]
in (
113
), without homochiral-wave filter (
F_{k_{f}}=1
, light blue), resulting from summing over discrete
p_{x},p_{z}
and with the parameters corresponding to our DNS (
L_{x}/l_{f}=L_{z}/l_{f}=5
).
The asymptotic scaling (
104

7
.
It reproduces the dominant behavior of the numerical integration of

(light blue line).
This validates our leading-order expansion, and the fact that layers I can be fully neglected with our parameter choice.
We have also verified that (
104

L_{x}/l_{f}
and

, which we do not show here.

We now consider a filter
F_{k_{f}}
for the homochiral waves, and show the corresponding transfer
T_{\rm 3D-2D}(S)
in Fig.
7
as a solid blue line.
We chose a Lorentzian form for the homochiral-wave filter,

\displaystyle F_{k_{f}}=\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\frac{1}{S}Q\frac{4\pi p_{z}\sqrt{k_{f}^{2}-p_{x}^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}}{k_{f}^{3}}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}},

(140)

When including this filter, the theory captures the decoupling between the waves and the 2D flow occurring at large rotation (
S<l_{f}/L_{y}
), when resonances are more stringent.
It leads to the decay of
T_{\rm 3D-2D}
when
S\to 0
.
With such a smooth filter, the function
T_{\rm 3D-2D}
exhibits a smooth transition from the decoupling to the heterochiral regime, around
S\simeq 0.2
.
A sharper filter for such resonant interactions, like the Heaviside function (
34
), would result in a continuous but sharp transition between the two regimes.

Note, finally, that we have neglected 2D-2D interactions in this computation.
With isotropic forcing, these should lead to an energy input
T_{\rm 2D-2D}\simeq l_{f}/(4L_{z})\ll 1
(see
(
26
)
), which is negligible for sufficiently large values of
Ro_{\epsilon}
, but not too large.
With this hypothesis, the energy injection rate
\epsilon
corresponds to the energy input in 3D modes only.
At low rotation, considering 2D-2D interactions goes beyond our QL theory, as they can disappear due to instabilities, which are not included in our framework.

Adding Layers I (Eq. (
122

\displaystyle T_{\rm 3D-2D}

\displaystyle\underset{K\gg k_{f}}{\approx}\epsilon\Bigg[1-\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}-\frac{S}{4}\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{19}{12}\frac{L_{x}}{\pi l_{f}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{S}{4}\Big)\Bigg]

(141)

where the constant term,
\epsilon(1-\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}})\equiv\epsilon_{\rm 3D}
is the fraction of energy injected in 3D modes
p_{z}\neq 0
when forcing is isotropic.

(146)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}}\approx\frac{1}{8}\left(1-\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{l_{f}}{2L_{z}}}\right),

) is written as

with

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=S^{*}\Bigg[1+c\frac{\log S^{*}}{b+\frac{S^{*}}{2Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}\Bigg]

(150)

\displaystyle=2bRo_{\epsilon}^{2}\Bigg(-1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{b^{2}Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}\Bigg)\Bigg[1+\frac{c}{b}\frac{\log\Bigg(2bRo_{\epsilon}^{2}\Bigg(-1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{b^{2}Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}\Bigg)\Bigg)}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{b^{2}Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}}\Bigg]

(151)

148
), is shown in the main text Fig.
3
(b) with

We show in Fig.

(a) the corresponding energy transfer from the waves, obtained from (

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\rm 3D-2D}[Ro_{\epsilon}]}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{S^{2}}{4Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}=\frac{b}{2Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}\Bigg(-1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{b^{2}Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}\Bigg)\Bigg[1+\frac{c}{b}\frac{\log\Bigg(2bRo_{\epsilon}^{2}\Bigg(-1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{b^{2}Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}\Bigg)\Bigg)}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{b^{2}Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}}}\Bigg]

(152)

Appendix F
Cutting-off wave-wave and eddy-eddy interactions

F.1
Choice of cutoff

We now discuss the introduction of a wavenumber cutoff
K
, which, as we will see, provides a validity range for both quasi-linear hypothesis and wave kinetics.
We first introduce various relevant timescales:

\displaystyle\tau_{\Omega}=\frac{p}{\Omega p_{z}},~~\text{twice the wave period}

(153)

\displaystyle\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}=\epsilon^{-1/3}p^{-2/3}\text{, the eddy-eddy interaction time}

(154)

\displaystyle\tau_{nl}^{\rm wave}=\Omega^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/2}p_{z}^{1/2}p_{\perp}^{-3/2}\text{, the wave-wave interaction time}

(155)

The eddy turnover time
\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}
is determined from the Kolmgorov scaling, while the wave-wave interaction time scale
\tau_{nl}^{\rm wave}
is predicted from wave-turbulent theory
(
23
)
.

These time scales lead to the definition of various
p_{z}
-dependent wavenumbers:

\displaystyle k_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\Omega^{3/5}\epsilon^{-1/5}p_{z}^{3/5},~~~\text{the crossover from waves to eddies}

(156)

\displaystyle k_{U}^{\rm wave}

\displaystyle=(\Omega p_{z})^{1/3}S^{2/3}\epsilon^{-1/3},~~~\text{the crossover from condensate- to wave-dominated interactions}

(157)

\displaystyle k_{U}^{\rm eddy}

\displaystyle=(\Omega S)^{3/2}\epsilon^{-3/2},~~~\text{the crossover from condensate- to eddy-dominated interactions,}

(158)

with
S\equiv\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\Omega}
.
These various wavenumbers are explained in the following.

Wavenumber
k_{\Omega}
in (
156
) denotes the crossover wavenumber delimiting wave-like (
p<k_{\Omega}\Longleftrightarrow\tau_{\Omega}<\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}
) from eddy-like (
p>k_{\Omega}\Longleftrightarrow\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}<\tau_{\Omega}
) behaviors, called the Zeman wavenumber.
Here we define by “waves” rotation-dominated modes, as opposed to “eddies”.

Nonlinear interactions between fluctuation modes occur over a nonlinear time scale
\tau_{nl}
, whose estimation depends on whether mode
\bm{p}
is wave-dominated (
p<k_{\Omega}
) or eddy-dominated (
p>k_{\Omega}
).
When modes are wave-dominated,
they interact with other waves over a slow nonlinear timescale
\tau_{nl}^{\rm wave}
in (
155
), which is
\gg\tau_{\Omega}
.
In contrast, when modes are eddy-dominated, they interact with each other over the Kolmogorov time scale
\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}
in (
154
).

The quasi-linear assumption is valid when mean-fluctuation interactions dominate over nonlinear interactions within fluctuations, i.e when
1/{U^{\prime}}<\tau_{nl}
.
Depending on whether modes are eddy or wave-dominated, this leads to two definitions for the crossover wavenumber
k_{U}
,
k_{U}^{\rm wave}
in (
157
) and
k_{U}^{\rm eddy}
in (
158
), above which interactions with the mean flow are negligible.

Wave-dominated modes (
p<k_{\Omega}
), if
\tau_{nl}^{\rm wave}<1/U^{\prime}
, are outside the QL framework.
Therefore, modes above
k_{U}^{\rm wave}
(
157
)
must be cutoff from the QL computation.
Waves obeying the QL assumption, i.e such that
p<k_{U}^{\rm wave}
, can lie either in Sector H or in Sector A, depending if
\tau_{\Omega}
is above or below
1/U^{\prime}
.
When rotation is sufficiently large, most modes behave as waves, and only the cutoff
k_{U}^{\rm wave}
needs to be considered.
This high-rotation case is illustrated in Fig.
8
a.

Eddy-dominated modes (
p>k_{\Omega}
), which appear at low rotation, obey a different time-scale hierarchy, illustrated in Fig.
8
b.
When eddies
(\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}<\tau_{\Omega})
dominantly interact with the mean flow
(1/{U^{\prime}}<\tau_{nl}^{\rm eddy}
), they are such that

1/{U^{\prime}}<\tau_{\Omega}
, hence, from condition (
37
), they necessarily belong to the heterochiral Sector A.
This is a consequence of the fact that eddies
cannot be homochiral-only when interacting with the condensate
, as expected from them being unaffected by rotation hence not subject to resonant constraints.
As a consequence, the homochiral Sector H only concerns wave-like modes and not eddies and must cutoff at
k_{\Omega}
.
In contrast, Sector A can sustain both waves and eddies: the former being cutoff at
k_{U}^{\rm wave}
, the latter at
k_{U}^{\rm eddy}
.
Modes in Sector A are therefore necessarily cutoff at
\min(k_{U}^{\rm eddy},k_{U}^{\rm wt})
to obey the QL condition.

All in all, we define the cutoff wavenumber as

\displaystyle K(p_{z},\Omega,{U^{\prime}})=\begin{cases}k_{U}^{\rm wave}~~~\text{ if }k_{U}^{\rm wave}<k_{\Omega}\text{ and }k_{U}^{\rm wave}<k_{U}^{\rm eddy}\vskip 10.00002pt\\
\begin{cases}k_{\Omega}\text{ if }\tau_{\Omega}<\frac{1}{{U^{\prime}}}~~(\text{Sector H})\\
\min(k_{U}^{\rm eddy},k_{U}^{\rm wave})\text{ if }\tau_{\Omega}\geq\frac{1}{{U^{\prime}}}~~(\text{Sector A})\end{cases}\end{cases}

(159)

to cover both wave-dominated and eddy-dominated cases.

The dominant effect of this cutoff appears at low rotation, where both
k_{\Omega}
and
k_{U}^{\rm eddy}\ll k_{U}^{\rm waves}
.
Here, Sector H is cutoff at
k_{\Omega}
and Sector A at
k_{U}^{\rm eddy}
.
The presence of a cutoff in Sector H at
k_{\Omega}
strongly affects the energy input to the condensate from Sector H.
As
Ro\to 0.5
,
k_{\Omega}(p_{z}=k_{f})\to k_{f}
, and the contribution from sector H progressively vanishes, as illustrated in Fig.
8
(c).
This causes the condensate to vanish at
Ro\simeq 0.5
to a state of pure eddy turbulence.

F.2
Numerical integration of the Reynolds stress with cutoff

We now describe how to solve the quasi-linear system numerically, and how its solution depends on the parameters of the system, in particular cutoff
K
.
The condensate amplitude
{U^{\prime}}/\Omega
solves in steady state the mean-flow energy balance

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4Ro_{\epsilon}^{2}}\frac{{U^{\prime}}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}=\frac{T_{\rm 3D-2D}}{\epsilon}\Big[\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\Omega},Ro\Big],

(160)

where
T_{\rm 2D}\Big[\frac{{U^{\prime}}}{\Omega},Ro\Big]
is given explicitly in Eq. (
113

Ro

K
defined in (
159

In order to compare the QL theory with our DNS of rotating 3DNSE, we numerically integrate the Reynolds stress in Eq. (
113
) over a discrete set of wavenumbers
p_{z}=2\pi m_{z}/L_{z}
and
p_{x}=2\pi m_{x}/L_{x}
, where
m_{z},m_{x}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This amounts to making a semi-continuous approximation only along
p_{y}
, which is still treated as a continuous variable.
Note that we use the value of
\epsilon_{p_{x},p_{z}}

corresponding to the same discrete forcing as in the DNS, such that

113

and
