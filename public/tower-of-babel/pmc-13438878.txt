Rethinking Isometric Handgrip Exercise Prescription: A Commentary on Duration, Fatigue, and Blood Pressure Management
Authors: Stjepan Škudar
Published: 2026

Hypertension remains one of the most important modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease and a central target of prevention in primary care. Contemporary hypertension guidance continues to emphasize lifestyle modification, patient‐centered implementation, and long‐term cardiovascular risk reduction alongside pharmacological therapy when indicated [1, 2]. Within this framework, isometric handgrip exercise has attracted increasing attention as a simple, low‐cost, and accessible intervention that may complement usual blood pressure management. Its practical appeal is evident: it requires little space, minimal equipment, and may be feasible for patients who cannot easily engage in conventional aerobic or dynamic resistance exercise.

However, the translation of isometric handgrip exercise into routine clinical practice remains limited by heterogeneity in exercise protocols and inconsistent cardiovascular outcomes. Some studies and reviews suggest meaningful reductions in blood pressure, whereas others report smaller or nonsignificant effects [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. This variability raises a clinically important question: should isometric handgrip exercise be prescribed as a general intervention, or should clinicians and researchers move toward more individualized prescriptions based on the physiological response to exercise?

The study by Wang et al. [8] provides a timely contribution to this question. Rather than asking only whether isometric handgrip exercise can lower blood pressure, the authors examined which elements of the exercise prescription may matter most. In a crossover study involving 16 healthy young adults, they compared four protocols that varied by intensity, duration, and engaged muscle mass. The standard protocol consisted of unilateral handgrip exercise at 30% maximal voluntary contraction for four 2‐min bouts. The three variations increased intensity to 45% maximal voluntary contraction, increased bout duration to 3 min, or used bilateral rather than unilateral exercise. Surface electromyography was used to quantify neuromuscular fatigue in forearm muscles, while systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and heart rate were assessed after exercise.

The most clinically relevant message from this study is that “more intense” does not necessarily mean “more effective.” The higher‐intensity protocol produced greater neuromuscular fatigue, but it did not produce the most favorable blood pressure response. In contrast, the longer‐duration protocol appeared to produce the strongest post‐exercise reduction in blood pressure. This distinction is important because exercise prescription is often framed around intensity. Wang et al. [8] suggest a more nuanced interpretation: the blood pressure response to isometric handgrip exercise may depend not only on how hard the muscle contracts, but also on how long the contraction is sustained, how fatigue develops, and whether the cardiovascular system receives an adequate but tolerable stimulus.

This interpretation aligns with contemporary exercise physiology frameworks. During isometric contraction, intramuscular pressure can compress local blood vessels and restrict perfusion, particularly as contraction intensity increases. The resulting local ischemia and metabolite accumulation activate group III and IV afferent fibers, contributing to the exercise pressor reflex and metaboreflex‐mediated sympathetic activation [9, 10]. During recovery, changes in vascular conductance, autonomic balance, endothelial function, and total peripheral resistance may contribute to post‐exercise blood pressure reduction [11, 12]. A moderate‐intensity contraction sustained for a longer duration may therefore provide a sufficient local metabolic and vascular stimulus without the excessive vascular occlusion and discomfort that may accompany higher‐intensity protocols.

This has direct implications for clinical translation. A protocol that maximizes local fatigue may not be the best protocol for a patient with hypertension, multimorbidity, low exercise tolerance, or limited confidence with exercise. The goal should not be to make isometric handgrip exercise harder, but to make it physiologically effective, safe, acceptable, and repeatable. Recent evidence also supports the broader safety, efficacy, and dosage considerations of isometric resistance training for blood pressure management, while emphasizing the need for careful delivery and patient selection [12, 13]. In addition, the emerging evidence base is moving beyond healthy populations. For example, acute isometric handgrip exercise has been shown to enhance cardiac baroreflex sensitivity and reduce systolic blood pressure in patients with chronic kidney disease, a clinically vulnerable group at high cardiovascular risk [14]. Such findings strengthen the case for studying isometric interventions in populations where simple and accessible exercise strategies may have particular value.

Wang et al. [8] also highlight neuromuscular fatigue as a potentially useful mechanistic marker. The observed associations between fatigue in specific forearm muscles, particularly the extensor carpi radialis and flexor digitorum superficialis, and post‐exercise cardiovascular responses suggest that not all local muscular responses are equally informative. This finding should be considered exploratory, given the small sample size, but it points toward a future in which isometric exercise prescription may be guided by more than maximal voluntary contraction alone.

A patient‐centered approach could combine several acute markers. Neuromuscular fatigue patterns may help identify whether the local muscle stimulus is adequate, while acute cardiovascular reactivity may help predict long‐term blood pressure response. A recent review with meta‐analysis examined acute and chronic blood pressure responses to dynamic and isometric resistance exercise and highlighted the potential relevance of acute cardiovascular responses for understanding later blood pressure adaptations [15]. Similarly, Yamagata and Sako [16] found that greater cardiovascular reactivity and muscle strength attenuated the hypotensive effect of an 8‐week isometric handgrip training program in young women. Although the evidence remains incomplete, these findings support the idea that acute physiological responses may help identify likely responders and nonresponders.

Tolerability should also be central to future research. Even the typical isometric handgrip protocol of four 2‐min bouts at 30% maximal voluntary contraction can be perceived as demanding by normotensive and hypertensive individuals [17]. Patient‐reported outcomes such as perceived exertion, discomfort, confidence, and willingness to continue should therefore be considered essential rather than secondary. A protocol that produces an attractive acute blood pressure response but is unpleasant, difficult to perform correctly, or poorly adhered to is unlikely to succeed in real‐world primary care.

Several limitations of the Wang et al. [8] study should temper interpretation. The study included only 16 participants and was conducted in healthy young adults without hypertension. Its crossover design strengthens within‐participant comparisons, but the results remain hypothesis‐generating. The study also evaluated acute responses rather than long‐term training effects. Acute post‐exercise blood pressure changes are physiologically informative, but they do not automatically translate into sustained reductions in resting, home, or ambulatory blood pressure. Future studies should therefore test whether the apparent advantage of longer‐duration moderate‐intensity protocols is preserved in older adults, patients with hypertension, patients taking antihypertensive medication, and individuals with chronic conditions such as chronic kidney disease.

Future trials should also compare standard 2‐min and longer 3‐min protocols over several weeks or months, include home and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, assess adherence and adverse effects, and evaluate patient‐reported tolerability. Where feasible, studies should incorporate both neuromuscular and cardiovascular markers to determine whether acute fatigue, perceived exertion, and blood pressure reactivity can predict chronic response. This would move the field closer to individualized exercise prescription rather than uniform protocol delivery.

In conclusion, Wang et al. [8] provide valuable mechanistic evidence suggesting that duration, not intensity alone, may be an important determinant of the blood pressure response to isometric handgrip exercise. Their findings encourage a more refined approach to exercise prescription, one that integrates neuromuscular fatigue, vascular physiology, cardiovascular reactivity, and patient tolerability. For clinicians, particularly in primary care, the practical message is clear: isometric handgrip exercise should not simply be made harder; it should be prescribed more intelligently.
