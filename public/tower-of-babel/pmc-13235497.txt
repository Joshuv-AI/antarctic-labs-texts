Association of socioeconomic status and lifestyle factors with incident diabetes among individuals with prediabetes: a prospective cohort study from UK Biobank
Authors: Xinyu Liu, Xixian Ruan, Qizhen Long, Fangmin Jing, Wenzhao Xie, Jie Chen, Feilong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine how socioeconomic status (SES) affects diabetes incidence in prediabetic adults, quantifying the mediating role of modifiable lifestyle factors in SES-related disparities to guide interventions.MethodsA prospective cohort of 34,669 adults with prediabetes from UK Biobank (2006–2010) with follow-up through 31 October 2022. From 27 SES indicators based on the social determinants of health framework, education, employment, and income were selected for latent class analysis. Lifestyle was assessed using a composite score (0–5) including smoking, diet, sleep duration, body mass index (BMI), and sedentary behavior. Cox models estimated associations; mediation analysis quantified the proportion mediated by lifestyle, and population-attributable fractions estimated preventable burden.ResultsOver a median 13.22-year follow-up, 6904 incident diabetes cases were identified. Compared with the high SES group, the hazard ratios for incident diabetes were 1.36 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.25, 1.48) and 1.91 (95% CI: 1.75, 2.09) for medium and low SES, respectively. Lifestyle factors mediated 33.71% and 31.44% of the SES-diabetes association in the medium and low SES, respectively. The population-attributable fractions were 24.05% for lifestyle and 34.50% for SES.ConclusionsLower SES is associated with elevated diabetes progression risk, with about one-third mediated by lifestyle factors, particularly BMI.Keywords: Prediabetes, Socioeconomic status, Diabetes, Lifestyle, Prospective study

Introduction

Over the past three decades, the global incidence of diabetes has increased nearly fourfold (Zheng et al., 2018), fueled by the rapid expansion of the prediabetic population, which is expected to encompass 638 million adults worldwide by 2045 (Rooney et al., 2023). Without intervention, about 5–10% of individuals with prediabetes progress to diabetes each year, and as many as 70% eventually develop the disease (Rooney et al., 2023). Importantly, this trajectory is not inevitable, as lifestyle interventions have been shown to substantially delay and, in some cases, prevent the onset of diabetes (Echouffo-Tcheugui et al., 2023).

While lifestyle interventions are highly effective at reducing diabetes risk, the capacity to adopt and maintain these preventive behaviors is strongly patterned by socioeconomic inequalities. Adults with lower socioeconomic status (SES) face a 40–60% higher incidence of diabetes than their higher-SES counterparts (Kolb and Martin, 2017). Beyond diabetes onset, this socioeconomic disadvantage continues to exert a detrimental impact, with large-scale nationwide registries and systematic reviews consistently demonstrating its independent association with an exacerbated burden of cardiovascular complications, as well as elevated rates of premature mortality (Hill-Briggs et al., 2020; Sattar et al., 2023). Despite these profound implications for long-term morbidity and healthcare burden, less is known about how socioeconomic disadvantage specifically drives the progression from prediabetes to diabetes.

This disparity is driven by multifaceted social determinants of health, encompassing economic instability, limited educational attainment, inadequate healthcare access, and inadequate neighborhood access (Haire-Joshu and Hill-Briggs, 2019; World Health Organization, 2024c). Yet most epidemiological studies rely on single SES indicators, which may fail to capture the cumulative burden of socioeconomic disadvantage. Lifestyle factors such as body mass index (BMI), sedentary behavior, and diet are likely important pathways linking SES to diabetes risk. However, few studies have examined whether a composite of lifestyle behaviors mediates the association between multidimensional SES and incident diabetes among individuals with prediabetes, leaving this relationship insufficiently understood (Mirmiran et al., 2021).

Using data from the UK Biobank, we conducted a prospective cohort study to investigate the associations of multidimensional SES and modifiable lifestyle behaviors with incident diabetes among individuals with prediabetes. We further evaluated the extent to which lifestyle mediates the SES-diabetes relationship.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective observational cohort study focuses on individuals with prediabetes in the UK Biobank (2006–2010), which comprises over 500,000 adults aged 40–69 from 22 assessment centers. Participants provided data at recruitment on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and biological samples, with follow-up conducted through linked electronic health records (Sudlow et al., 2015). Ethical approval for the UK Biobank was granted by the North West-Haydock Research Ethics Committee (reference:21/NW/0157).

We identified 66,818 individuals meeting the American Diabetes Association criteria for prediabetes, defined as a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level of 5.70%–6.40% (39.00–47.00 mmol/mol) (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee, 2025a). Exclusion criteria included: (1) Prevalent diabetes was defined as any diagnosis of diabetes or its complications at baseline, ascertained from UK Biobank inpatient hospital records, primary-care records, self-reported medical history, or death-registry data; (2) Undiagnosed diabetes: individuals with fasting glucose ≥7.00 mmol/L or random blood glucose ≥11.10 mmol/L according to American Diabetes Association criteria (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee, 2025a); (3) Missing data: participants lacking complete SES or lifestyle factors assessments. After exclusions, the final analytical cohort comprised 34,669 prediabetic adults (Fig. 1).

Measures

Based on the Healthy People 2030 framework and drawing on prior research, we incorporated 27 extensive social determinants of health factors, encompassing education access and quality, economic stability, social and community context, neighborhood and built environment, and health care access and quality (Zhong et al., 2024). Baseline data were collected using standard questionnaires administered by well-trained researchers at recruitment (Supplementary Table 1 A).

SES, as a key component of the social determinants of health, is usually measured at the individual level through education, income and employment (Haire-Joshu and Hill-Briggs, 2019). Based on previous studies, we used latent class analysis to divide the participants into high, medium and low levels to assess the overall SES of the participants (Supplementary Table 2) (Zhong et al., 2024).

Based on previous research, we constructed a comprehensive lifestyle score that incorporated five modifiable factors: smoking status, sleep quality, diet, sedentary behavior, and BMI. These factors were selected after evaluating collinear variables (sedentary behavior and physical activity; BMI, waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio) (Supplementary Table 1 A) (Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2021; Song et al., 2021), in line with the guidelines of the World Health Organization and the American Diabetes Association (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee, 2025b; World Health Organization, 2024b). The lifestyle scores (range: 0–5) assign 1 point to each of the following: never smoking; healthy diet (score ≥ 4); better sleep quality (score ≥ 4); BMI between 18.50 and 25.00 kg/m2; sedentary time < 4 h/day. However, the impact of each lifestyle on the onset of diabetes may not be the same. In the sensitivity analysis, we also calculated a weighted score based on the relative risk contribution of each behavior (Han et al., 2022). The covariates were obtained from the baseline questionnaire at the assessment center, including sex, ethnicity and age.

Diagnostic information was obtained from the hospital inpatient records, primary care data, death registries, and self-reported diagnoses. Incident diabetes cases were ascertained by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (ICD-9: 250; ICD-10: E10-E14) (Supplementary Table 1B) (Fu et al., 2024). The follow-up time was calculated from the date of recruitment until the earliest occurrence of a diagnosis of diabetes, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up (England: 2022-10-31, Scotland: 2022-08-31, Wales: 2022-03-31).

Statistical analysis

We employed Cox proportional hazards models to examine the associations between extensive SES, composite SES, and lifestyle factors with the incidence of diabetes, and we developed both an unadjusted crude model and a fully adjusted model, accounting for potential confounding factors, including age, sex and ethnicity. The model for composite SES further incorporated additional adjustments for lifestyle scores. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (all P-values >0.05). And polygenic risk score (PRS) for diabetes was obtained from the UK Biobank “Standard PRS Release” (Category 301), which provides genome-wide scores for 53 traits constructed exclusively from external genome-wide association study summary statistics and processed via Bayesian meta-analysis, ancestry-centering, and within-ancestry standardization. In addition, we calculated the population-attributable fractions for SES, key lifestyle factors, and these standardized PRS to estimate the theoretical maximum proportion of incident diabetes cases that could be prevented if each class of risk factors were entirely eliminated (Lee et al., 2022). Meanwhile, to elucidate the interaction between SES and lifestyle factors, we utilized the R package mediation (with 1000 simulations) to perform a mediation analysis and quantify the proportion of effects mediated by lifestyle factors (Tönnies et al., 2023). We also conducted an exploratory mediation using the same approach to estimate how much of the SES-diabetes link is mediated by our extensive social determinants of health-based SES construct.

We then conducted a joint association analysis to assess the associations among the onset of diabetes, SES, and healthy lifestyle factors. Participants were cross-classified by composite SES (low/medium/high) and lifestyle scores (0–1/2–3/4–5). Using the group with high SES and a lifestyle score of 4–5 as the reference, we estimated the hazard ratios (HRs) for the nine combination groups (Kim et al., 2024). We also repeated the analysis using the entire high SES group (all lifestyle scores) as the reference to compare the seven combination levels. We further conducted stratified subgroup analyses based on several key factors to assess potential effect modification: sex, ethnicity (white/others), age (< 60 years and ≥ 60 years, defined as the elderly by the World Health Organization), BMI, and sedentary behavior (both showed a relatively high proportion of mediation). Other subgroup analyses included regular use of antidiabetic drugs, grip strength (frailty status: < 16.00 kg for women; < 27.00 kg for men), HbA1c (5.70%–6.00% (39.00–42.00 mmol/mol) and 6.00%–6.50% (42.00–47.00 mmol/mol)), and skeletal muscle mass (sex-standardized tertiles (Dan et al., 2024; Kucukdagli and Bahat, 2019; Peila and Rohan, 2020; World Health Organization, 2024a)).

We also conducted several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings: (1) Replacing the composite SES with three adjusted individual indicators: education, employment and income; (2) Substituting composite SES with the area-level Townsend deprivation index; (3) Adopting a weighted lifestyle score based on the relative risk of each behavior; (4) Replacing BMI with waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio in the lifestyle scores; (5) To reduce the influence of reverse causality, diabetes events that occurred within the first two years of follow-up were excluded.

All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.0, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

The study included 34,669 participants with prediabetes (mean age 59.38 ± 7.02 years, 48.12% male) from UK Biobank. Participants were stratified into three SES groups: high (13.19%, n = 4574), medium (56.68%, n = 19,650), and low (30.13%, n = 10,445). Baseline characteristics revealed significant SES-based disparities (Table 1). Compared with the high-SES group, individuals with low SES were older (61.32 years, compared with 56.28 years), had a much lower proportion with higher educational attainment (12.02% compared with 78.42%), and were more likely to report unemployment (18.64% compared with 5.01%). Lifestyle behaviors also varied substantially by SES: low-SES participants had higher rates of current smoking (20.80% compared with 8.96%), sedentary behavior (≥4 h/day: 49.64% compared with 13.12%), and obesity (BMI ≥ 30.00 kg/m2: 39.46% compared with 32.29%).

Associations of SES and lifestyle factors with incident diabetes

Over a median follow-up of 13.22 years, 6904 incident diabetes cases were identified. In Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity, nearly all individual social determinants of health factors were significantly associated with diabetes risk (Supplementary Fig. 1). When these were distilled into a composite SES index, lower SES was strongly associated with increased diabetes incidence (Table 2). Compared to the high-SES reference group, the HR was 1.36 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.25, 1.48) for medium SES and 1.91 (95% CI: 1.75, 2.09) for low SES. Further adjustment for the composite lifestyle score attenuated these associations, but significant gradients persisted (medium SES: HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.33; low SES: HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.43, 1.71), suggesting that lifestyle factors partially, but not fully, explain SES-related disparities in diabetes risk.

All lifestyle factors (BMI, sedentary behavior, smoking, diet, and sleep quality) were significantly associated with diabetes incidence (Supplementary Fig. 2). BMI demonstrated the strongest association (HR = 4.27, 95% CI: 3.99, 4.58; for obesity compared with normal weight). For context, this effect size exceeded that of other adiposity measures, including waist circumference (HR = 2.79, 95% CI: 2.63, 2.95) or waist-to-hip ratio (HR = 2.45, 95% CI: 2.33, 2.57). Sedentary behavior (≥ 4 h/day) was also a potent risk factor (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.45, 1.57), with a stronger effect than physical inactivity (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.32, 1.43).

Population-attributable fraction estimates quantified the preventable burden of diabetes attributable to key risk factors (Fig. 2A). Modifiable lifestyle behaviors accounted for 24.05% of incident cases, while composite SES explained 21.27%. When SES was expanded to include extensive social determinants of health factors, its attributable fraction increased to 34.50%, surpassing the contribution of PRS (24.50%).

Mediation analyses indicated that lifestyle factors collectively explained 33.71% (95% CI: 25.96%, 46.58%) of the SES-diabetes association in the medium SES group and 31.44% (95% CI: 27.07%, 37.58%) in the low SES group (Fig. 2B). BMI was the dominant mediator, accounting for 23.17% and 19.86% of the excess risk in medium and low SES groups, respectively. Other factors, including sedentary behavior (19.35%), sleep quality (3.85%), diet (3.54%), and smoking status (3.16%), had smaller but nontrivial mediating effects in the medium-SES group. In the low SES group, these same factors mediated 19.45%, 4.44%, 3.09% and 2.93%, respectively. In contrast, mediation analysis of the extensive social determinants of health-derived SES construct revealed a minimal indirect effect (Supplementary Table 3 A).

Joint, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses

Participants were cross-classified by SES and lifestyle scores (0–5) to assess synergistic effects (Fig. 2C). Using high SES individuals with optimal lifestyles (score 4–5) as the reference, diabetes risk escalated sharply with worsening SES and poorer lifestyles. Low SES individuals with the least favorable lifestyles (score 0–1) faced a 4.68-fold higher risk (95% CI: 3.77, 5.80). Notably, even low SES individuals with healthy lifestyles (score 4–5) had no significantly elevated risk compared to the overall high-SES group (HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.77, 1.10), underscoring the potential for lifestyle modifications to mitigate socioeconomic disparities (Fig. 2D).

Stratified analyses suggested that the low versus high SES association with diabetes was generally consistent across subgroups, with somewhat stronger associations observed in females and normal-weight participants (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings. Replacing composite SES with individual indicators (education, income, employment) or the Townsend deprivation index yielded consistent associations (Supplementary Table 3B). Similarly, alternative lifestyle scores formulations, including weighted scores and waist circumference substitutions for BMI, did not materially alter the results (Supplementary Table 3C). Exclusion of incident diabetes cases within the first two years of follow-up further mitigated concerns about reverse causality, reinforcing the temporal relationship between SES, lifestyle, and diabetes development (Supplementary Table 3D).

Discussion

In this prospective study of prediabetic individuals from the UK Biobank, lower SES was associated with a higher incidence of diabetes, with HRs of 1.36 and 1.91 for medium and low SES groups, respectively. Population-attributable fraction analyses showed that 24.05% of incident cases were attributable to modifiable lifestyle behaviors, while SES disparities accounted for 34.50%, exceeding the contribution of PRS (24.50%). Mediation analyses further indicated that lifestyle factors, particularly BMI, collectively explained approximately one-third of this association. Notably, individuals with low SES and an unfavorable lifestyle faced a 4.68-fold higher diabetes risk than those with high SES and a favorable lifestyle. These findings suggest that social and behavioral determinants may exert a greater population-level influence on diabetes progression than genetic predisposition.

Population-attributable fractions analysis indicated that when expanded to encompass broader social determinants, socioeconomic factors contributed more to incident diabetes than either lifestyle factors or polygenic risk. This underscores the vital role of structural inequalities. Socioeconomic position does not merely correlate with health outcomes but fundamentally shapes the capacity to adopt and maintain preventive behaviors (Haire-Joshu and Hill-Briggs, 2019). Individuals with lower SES often face reduced access to safe recreational environments, nutritious food, and health education, which can constrain lifestyle choices beyond individual intention (Hill-Briggs et al., 2020).

Unlike immutable genetic architecture, lifestyle behaviors are modifiable yet constrained by socioeconomic disadvantage; hence, addressing this substantial preventable burden requires a coordinated dual-level strategy. At the macro level, mitigating socioeconomic disadvantage requires structural policy interventions aimed at improving the broader social and material conditions that shape health, such as access to healthy food environments and community resources (World Health Organization, 2025). Concurrently at the individual level, clinical interventions can focus on empowering socioeconomically disadvantaged populations to optimize modifiable lifestyle behaviors. Integrating personalized behavioral counseling with these broader policy-driven structural changes may represent a promising framework for reducing the diabetes burden (Hill-Briggs et al., 2020).

Mediation analyses indicated that lifestyle factors partially mediated the association between SES and incident diabetes, consistent with evidence that behavioral pathways contribute to socioeconomic inequalities in diabetes risk and glycemic outcomes (Liu et al., 2023; Sebastian-Valles et al., 2025; Sebastian-Valles et al., 2026; Tiwari et al., 2022). BMI emerged as the primary mediator, likely reflecting differential exposure to obesogenic environments shaped by socioeconomic conditions. Other factors, including sedentary behavior, sleep quality, diet, and smoking, contributed additional effects, together accounting for approximately one-third of the association. While these behavioral factors are significant, a substantial proportion of the SES-diabetes association remained unexplained. This residual effect likely reflects both measurement imprecision inherent in self-reported lifestyle variables and the contribution of additional unmeasured pathways. Prior evidence indicates that psychosocial stress, limited health literacy (Tajdar et al., 2021) adverse neighborhood environments (Bilal et al., 2018), and inadequate healthcare access (Kurani et al., 2021), profoundly influence diabetes risk beyond these conventional behaviors.

To our best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation in a prediabetic population to systematically incorporate social determinants of health indicators, thereby reinforcing the validity of education, employment, and income as core measures. By using latent class analysis to derive latent classes, this study effectively overcomes the limitations of relying solely on a single indicator. Leveraging the UK Biobank's extensive national cohort provides additional methodological rigor, as its scale enhances the statistical power and reliability of our findings.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational analysis, causal relationships between SES, lifestyle factors, and incident diabetes cannot be established, and residual confounding or reverse causality cannot be fully excluded. Second, SES and lifestyle variables were assessed at a single baseline time point, which may introduce measurement error and fail to capture changes over time; moreover, the UK Biobank baseline (2006–2010) may not reflect current socioeconomic conditions. Third, the absence of direct measures of health literacy may have limited the precision of the lifestyle score, despite sensitivity analyses using alternative specifications. Finally, potential selection bias in the UK Biobank cohort, particularly the underrepresentation of individuals with the most disadvantaged SES, may limit generalizability (Foster et al., 2018).

Conclusions

These findings support multifaceted strategies to reduce diabetes disparities. Although BMI-focused lifestyle intervention remains important across socioeconomic strata, the residual SES effect indicates that individual counseling alone is insufficient. Prevention efforts should therefore combine behavioral support with policies that reduce socioeconomic inequalities and improve access to preventive resources.

In conclusion, lower SES is a major driver of diabetes progression in prediabetic adults, with about one-third of this association mediated by lifestyle factors. Effective diabetes prevention will require addressing both lifestyle and social determinants of health.
