When the ECG Speaks First
Authors: Estelle Torbey
Published: 2026

The electrocardiogram (ECG) is often viewed as a simple screening tool, yet its true value lies in capturing real-time myocardial electrical activity, changes that frequently precede structural abnormalities, biomarker elevation, or clinical deterioration. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, 3 studies across distinct disease states underscore a unifying principle: early electrical disturbances can serve as sentinel markers of myocardial vulnerability, particularly in the context of immune-mediated injury, infiltrative processes, and targeted pharmacologic therapy.

Progressive Conduction Disease in Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Myocarditis

Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)–associated myocarditis (especially when combined with the myasthenia-myositis syndrome) remains one of the most feared complications of PD1-PDL-1 and CTL4 immunotherapy. Although relatively rare, it is characterized by a high incidence of malignant arrhythmias, conduction disease, and early mortality. The paper in this issue by Nelson et al highlights the progressive conduction system disease in ICI myocarditis and underscores an important and underappreciated signal: electrical disturbances may precede or outweigh traditional markers of myocardial injury and warrant urgent initiation of anti-inflammatory therapy.1

Immune-mediated inflammation appears to have first a predilection for the conduction system, resulting in PR prolongation that is the earliest indicator, bundle branch block, and advanced atrioventricular block even in the absence of severe systolic dysfunction. These changes should not be dismissed as secondary or nonspecific. Rather, they may represent direct immune injury to specialized conduction tissue, thereby conferring both diagnostic and prognostic significance.

From a clinical standpoint, this observation reinforces several practical lessons. First, serial ECG assessment is essential in patients receiving ICIs, particularly when nonspecific symptoms such as fatigue, dyspnea, or presyncope arise. Second, evolving conduction abnormalities warrant heightened suspicion for myocarditis and should prompt expedited evaluation and multidisciplinary discussion. Finally, early ECG changes may identify patients at risk for sudden deterioration, thus informing decisions about immunosuppression, rhythm monitoring, and pacing strategies.

ECG Clues to Cardiac Amyloidosis in Patients With Monoclonal Gammopathy of Undetermined Significance

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, present in roughly 5% of adults aged >50 years, is a precursor to plasma cell disorders but is typically asymptomatic, and routine surveillance rarely includes cardiac imaging. Progression to amyloid light-chain amyloidosis (AL amyloidosis) with cardiac involvement may manifest through heart failure symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide, left ventricular hypertrophy, pericardial effusion, or restrictive physiology. Cardiac magnetic resonance and scintigraphy remain diagnostic mainstays, reflecting increased extracellular volume from fibril deposition. As highlighted by Piwowarski et al in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, the ECG is an inexpensive, underused tool for detecting cardiac involvement. Classic findings—low voltage, pseudoinfarct patterns, and conduction disease—are well described but are often recognized only after suspicion is raised, whereas this work reframes the ECG as a proactive screening adjunct to identify patients with monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance who warrant advanced imaging. Because cardiac involvement is the strongest prognostic determinant in AL amyloidosis, early recognition is essential to initiate timely therapy.2

Brugada Phenocopy Unmasked by Targeted Therapy and Fever

Targeted oncologic therapies can unmask characteristic ECG patterns, including coved ST-segment elevation in the right precordial leads, particularly when combined with systemic stressors such as fever. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Nelson et al describe a compelling case of a Brugada phenocopy triggered by fever in a patient receiving avapritinib, a platelet-derived growth factor receptor and tyrosine kinase inhibitor. This presentation underscores the ECG’s value in identifying transient, exposure-related sodium channel dysfunction rather than a fixed, inherited channelopathy. The case highlights ongoing debate about whether Brugada syndrome arises solely from sodium 1.5v abnormalities or whether perturbations in outward potassium currents contribute to the syndrome. If increased, phase 2 Ito-mediated transmural dispersion plays a role, then tyrosine kinase inhibitors—through effects on hERG and related channels—may plausibly induce a Brugada phenocopy. Although reversible, such findings are clinically meaningful. They signal heightened arrhythmic vulnerability and prompt important considerations regarding drug continuation, rechallenge, and preventive measures such as aggressive fever management or telemetry monitoring. In the absence of cardiogenic syncope, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement is not indicated for primary prevention of sudden cardiac death.3
