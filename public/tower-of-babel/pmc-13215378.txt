The books on my patients’ bedside tables
Authors: Emma Zattarin
Published: 2026

Before I became an oncologist, novels taught me how to sit with complexity and pain. I discovered literature in my teenage years, and it has accompanied me ever since. From the very beginning, it gave me a sense of belonging and taught me to better understand not only myself, but also others and the small and large events that shape our lives.

When I began my medical residency in oncology, I soon realized that I would have less time to read, yet that books could still serve as silent companions throughout my days in the hospital. I started noticing the books on my patients’ bedside tables. I eventually developed a small personal ritual: during ward rounds, after discussing symptoms and treatment adjustments, I would ask patients how the book they were reading was going. It was a simple question, but one that could subtly change the atmosphere of the room. Even though we never said it explicitly, the awareness that patients, too, found a sense of belonging and even relief in novels brought us onto common ground.

One story from the early years of my residency has stayed with me. A colleague once recounted a conversation between a terminally ill patient and a palliative care oncologist at our hospital. The physician explained the initiation of home-based palliative care and the planned therapeutic adjustments. The patient said she accepted the proposed plan, but asked for a favor in return: she wanted the physician to read a book she had brought with her. The book was “The Year of the Death of Ricardo Reis” by José Saramago. I never had the opportunity to ask either the patient or the palliative care oncologist why she had chosen to recommend that particular book. What struck me most, however, was not the book itself, but the nature of her request: at the threshold of death, the patient wished to share with her physician a novel that mattered deeply to her.

Since I completed my oncology residency, my work has become primarily outpatient. No longer are there patients’ bedside tables to observe, but sometimes I notice the books patients are reading in the waiting room or carrying in their bags or jacket pockets during their appointments. Given the tight schedule of outpatient visits, there is often no time to talk about books; sometimes there is only time for a fleeting glance at the cover. I have come to understand that some patients feel more at ease when their daily hospital routine allows space for novels, not necessarily to talk about them, but simply to keep them close and to feel their presence.

In my first year as a specialist oncologist, I worked at a hospital an hour away from home by train. Life as a commuter was very demanding, but the train gave me time to read. At the time, I shared an office with a colleague who is an avid reader. A small ritual developed between us: after our commutes, we would leave our books on our respective desks, silently updating each other on what we were reading. Checking the books on his desk became one of the small joys of my day.

One day, another colleague at the same hospital mentioned that, ever since he began his research career, he had stopped reading novels. The intellectual demands and dedication required by research, on top of clinical duties, had absorbed all his energy and had become his main passion. Later, I found a note with this message before another train journey: “In Tereza's eyes, books were the emblems of a secret brotherhood. For she had but a single weapon against the world of crudity surrounding her: the novels.”1 The note had been left by my colleague who had stopped reading novels, and I still keep it. Perhaps one never truly stops being a reader, nor loses that sense of belonging and comfort that novels provide.

As oncologists, we spend much of our professional lives confronting suffering. We speak daily of progression, prognosis, toxicity, and mortality. In doing so, it can become dangerously easy to see patients primarily through the lens of disease. Yet the people we care for continually remind us that, even in serious illness, life does not cease to contain beauty, curiosity, passion, and meaning.

Novels have long taught me the same lesson. The stories I have loved most are those that reveal how, even in the face of suffering, human beings continue to seek love, purpose, beauty, and connection.

My patients and the books they carry remind me of this every day: that life may remain beautiful even in the midst of pain, and that one of our responsibilities as oncologists is not only to prolong life when possible, but also to preserve the humanity that gives life meaning.
