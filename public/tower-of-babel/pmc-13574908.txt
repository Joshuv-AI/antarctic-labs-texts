Towards federated learning for face recognition: a comprehensive review of secure and privacy-preserving techniques and prospective research
Authors: Ajnas Muhammed, João Marcos, Nuno Gonçalves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Federated learning is a popular decentralized machine learning paradigm that enables multiple devices or entities to collectively train a common model without sending data to a central server. The distributed data availability improves privacy and security for each participating entity by keeping their data localized. Face recognition, a rapidly evolving field, uses deep learning techniques to achieve remarkable advances, frequently outperforming human performance on specific public datasets. However, the sensitive nature of facial data, as faces belong to personally identifiable information, raises serious privacy and security concerns on training data sharing. Federated learning has emerged as a promising solution to the privacy and security issues confronting the face recognition community. This paper provides a systematic, PRISMA-guided review of 50 publications released between 2020 and 2026, identified through searches of IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, arXiv, Google Scholar, and DBLP, on face recognition frameworks that use federated learning. The reviewed techniques are systematically classified into six categories, with an emphasis on their contributions and relevance to the larger domain of federated learning based face recognition. This work aims to summarize and analyze various federated facial recognition methods, including their underlying techniques, results, and goals. This survey also compares against twelve existing face recognition surveys to clarify its distinct and unique contributions. Furthermore, it provides a high-level detailed overview of how various federated learning functionalities and design principles have been used in face recognition applications. This review identifies key challenges while highlighting promising research directions for future advancements in the field.

Introduction

Federated Learning (FL) is an innovative decentralized machine learning training framework, introduced by McMahan et al. [1], that allows multiple participants to train a single model on distributed local datasets. FL is especially useful in different fields where data privacy, security, and regulatory compliance are critical, such as healthcare, finance, biometrics, and many more. In FL, by storing data locally and sharing only model updates, the framework reduces the risk of data breaches, improves privacy and security, and ensures compliance with regulations such as the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (EU-GDPR) [2], California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and its extension California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA) [3], and others. Adherence to the principles supported by various regulations, such as the EU-GDPR and CCPA, is critical for any technology that handles personal data, including FL systems. Several studies have investigated how FL can be designed to meet different regulatory requirements [4]. Furthermore, as FL keeps the data local, it reduces the need for conventional large-scale data transfers, which saves bandwidth and computing resources. Due to these numerous benefits, FL has become a popular topic for academic research and industry applications [5].Fig. 1Block diagram representing CL vs. FL

Block diagram representing CL vs. FL

Figure 1 depicts the centralized Learning (CL) versus FL. In CL, the initial step is to collect the training data from all participating entities (or clients). The next step involves transfer of all the collected data to the centralized location for model training. Later, the trained model is passed to the clients based on the design framework and/or authentic request. Whereas in FL, the framework works differently. Here, an initial model created by the server is distributed to participating clients. Each client performs local training with the local data and only the model updates are passed to the central server. Upon getting the local model updates, server aggregates to built a global model, and this process continues until the model converges.

Face Recognition (FR) systems detect and verify individuals by examining their distinctive facial characteristics (or features). Over the years, FR has experienced remarkable enhancements in performance due to advances in machine learning algorithms, the accessibility of extensive datasets, and the augmented computational capabilities of contemporary hardware [6]. Recently, especially after COVID-19 pandemic, FR has emerged as a core element in modern security applications [7], social media platforms [8], border management, and numerous other crucial and critical applications [9]. Despite this popularity, FR systems pose substantial security and privacy concerns. Conventional FR methods rely on centralized repositories to gather and analyze sensitive facial data, increasing exposure to single-point attacks and data breaches [10]. As faces are part of human anatomy and face revocation is highly challenging and costly, privacy preservation in FR is of paramount importance. Unauthorized access or misuse may result in severe consequences such as surveillance overreach, identity theft, and abuses of individual freedom [11]. Consequently, there is an increasing demand for methods capable of effectively safeguarding privacy while ensuring the accuracy and dependability of FR systems [10].

Although FR systems have attained notable accuracy and extensive applications, they encounter substantial challenges concerning data privacy, security, and regulatory adherence. Recently, the FR community has adopted FL as a promising approach to mitigate urgent privacy issues. FL enables decentralized data processing and model training, guaranteeing that sensitive facial data stays at local devices instead of being transmitted to a central server. This method not only preserve privacy but also enhance security by substantially decreasing the likelihood of data breaches. Furthermore, FL facilitates collaboration among diverse datasets, promoting the development of more robust and inclusive FR systems. By maintaining data localized and sharing solely model updates, FL adheres to rigorous privacy regulations, rendering it a feasible framework for developing ethical and compliant FR technologies. The scope, FL coverage, key gaps of existing surveys on related field

Existing FR surveys consist of twelve related studies: [10–20, 22]. References [14] and [15] are surveys of deep learning-based FR, discussing architecture, loss functions and training pipelines. However, they remain centered on centralized training settings. Zeng et al. [12] discusses the technique focuses on privacy-preserving biometric identification and authentication, leveraging Homomorphic Encryption (HE) and Multi-Party Computation (MPC). Wang et al. [13] classifies privacy-preserving FR techniques into four paradigms: appearance-guided, identity-guided, reversible, and privacy-preserving FR systems. Surveys [10] and [11] discusses privacy leakage and presentation-level protection techniques on FR domain. Leyva et al. [16] and Roux et al. [17] discusses FR security through a broad attack-vector taxonomy and a focused review of backdoor attacks and defenses. Surveys [18] and [19] discusses democratic and racial fairness in FR. Hahn and Marcel [20] reviews biometric template protection techniques, and [22] conducts a systematic review of Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architectures, databases, and performance metrics used in FR. Table 1 summarizes the scope, FL coverage, and key gaps of these twelve surveys. Five of twelve surveys touch FL to some degree. References [10, 16], and [17] discuss FL only tangentially amid broader security concerns. Zeng et al. [12] and Wang et al. [13] mention it explicitly without focusing it as a main theme. The remaining seven do not discuss it at all. Hence, none of these surveys discussed FL as the primary axis. Therefore, this paper presents a comprehensive survey to systematically organize, and analyze the scope of FL on FR domain. With this review, our objective is to discuss the principal contributions, challenges and future works on this domain. This survey will function as a significant resource for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers engaged in the field of FL and FR. Review methodology

This paper follows a structured, PRISMA-style protocol with three search pattern: an initial database search (Round 1), a DBLP supplementary search (Round 2), and an additional records identified outside the formal database queries (Round 3). Figure 2 summarizes the complete flow.Fig. 2PRISMA-style flow diagram of the study selection process across all three identification streams. Total included: n = 22 (Round 1) +\ 26 (Round 2) +\ 2 (Round 3) = 50

PRISMA-style flow diagram of the study selection process across all three identification streams. Total included: n = 22 (Round 1) +\ 26 (Round 2) +\ 2 (Round 3) = 50

Databases searched

Round 1 searched IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, arXiv, and Google Scholar. Round 2 (y) queried DBLP (dblp.org) directly by screening results manually one at a time. Round 3 consists of a small number of records identified through other means (e.g., citation snowballing, author familiarity with the field).

Search strategy and keywords

Round 1 search strings combined terms from two concept groups, e.g.: (“federated learning” OR “FL”) AND (“face recognition” OR “facial recognition” OR “biometric recognition”), refined with secondary terms such as “privacy-preserving,” “secure aggregation,” “differential privacy,” “non-IID,” “fairness,” “domain adaptation,” and “edge/IoT deployment”, identifying 187 records. Round 2 ran two literal DBLP queries: (i) “privacy face recognition” and (ii) “federated ...face” (a DBLP proximity-style query matching “federated” and “face” co-occurring in a title), returning 182 raw records combined.

Federated face recognition

This section classifies 50 studies of Federated Face Recognition (FFR) methodologies into six categories: (1) privacy-preserving techniques (Sect. 3.1), (2) algorithmic innovations (Sect. 3.2), (3) fairness and explainability (Sect. 3.3), (4) domain adaptation and transfer learning (Sect. 3.4), (5) efficiency and scalability (Sect. 3.5), and (6) security—presentation attack detection and forgery detection (Sect. 3.6) as shown in Fig. 3. This classification is based on the primary area of focus, research direction and contribution. Privacy-preserving techniques include methods and frameworks exclusively aimed at improving data confidentiality within FFR systems. Algorithmic innovations emphasize innovative algorithms and optimization techniques designed to enhance the performance and robustness of FFR models. The fairness and explainability section examines methodologies that address ethical issues, bias reduction, and transparency within FFR systems. Domain adaptation and transfer learning emphasize methods that allow FR models to generalize efficiently across various domains and datasets. The efficiency and scalability section addresses methodologies for optimizing communication, minimizing latency, and improving computational performance in FFR systems. Finally, the security category examines methodologies for detecting presentation attacks and synthetic-media forgery within FFR systems, addressing threats directed at the recognition target itself rather than at the training process.Fig. 3FFR taxonomy diagram with all six categories

FFR taxonomy diagram with all six categories

Due to the fact that FFR models are trained locally on distributed devices and only model updates, rather than raw facial data, are shared, a direct comparison of the performance of two FFR techniques presents a challenge. The efficacy of FFR is contingent upon local data distribution, device characteristics, and the employed aggregation and communication protocols. The disparate operating environments, encompassing varying data privacy regulations, device specifications, and network parameters, inherent to each federated system render direct performance comparisons unfeasible. In a similar vein, the absence of a standardized benchmark or centralized dataset for evaluation complicates the comparison of different FFR systems. The FFR systems exhibit notable performance trade-offs relative to centralized (non-FFR) systems. Centralized, extensive datasets contribute to enhanced recognition performance. FFR facilitates facial data localization to enhance privacy and security; however, restricted facial data diversity and potential biases within local datasets may compromise performance. Given the inherent complexities and associated challenges, a fully controlled performance comparison between different FFR systems is not attempted in this study. However, for the subset of reviewed studies that report results on shared, widely-used benchmarks (LFW, IJB-B, IJB-C), Sect. 3.7 consolidates these already-published figures into indicative cross-method comparison tables, subject to the limitations regarding non-standardized training data, backbones, and client partitioning.

Privacy-preserving techniques

Privacy preservation methods in FFR focus on protecting sensitive facial data while maintaining optimal model performance. A method for preserving privacy of transferable facial images utilizing FL and ensemble models is described in [29]. This method establishes an FL model based on a distributed dataset. On the client side, a locally trained model based on the local data is provided as input to PcadvGAN [72]. On the server side, the discriminators of PcadvGAN and the FL model are combined through ensembling. Last, the global optimal aggregation parameter matrix of the GAN is computed. This method is capable of generating new, private facial images within a defined design framework. The experiments and results indicate that the generated facial images improve transferability and practicality. FedGP [70] trains a federated GAN with FedAvg to synthesize artificial data that can replace clients’ real data used for training. With this, no client ever shares their raw data that maps directly back to any individual. Beyond a differential-average-case-privacy analysis, the paper specifically stress-tests the approach against the model inversion attack on the CelebA [73] dataset. Reconstructed images are passed through OpenFace to measure how much identity information survives the attack. Table 4 shows that FedGP sharply reduces both detection and recognition success on reconstructed faces compared to a non-private baseline model trained directly on real images.

PrivacyFace, a framework developed to improve FFR by enabling the exchange of auxiliary and privacy-preserving information among clients, is presented in [31]. PrivacyFace incorporates an effective Differentially Private Local Clustering (DPLC) mechanism to derive sanitized clusters from local class centers, complemented by a consensus-aware recognition loss that improves the discriminative efficacy of the outcomes. PrivacyFace mathematically demonstrates that the technique introduces minimal overhead while delivering significant performance enhancements. An alternative FFR approach that integrates FL with both supervised and unsupervised FR classifiers, while preserving user privacy, is described in [35, 36]. In [35, 36], the authors introduce FL framework with and without aggregators, within the context of both supervised and unsupervised systems. Here, each local model is either transmitted to a secure aggregator (if available) or directly to the central server. A GAN model is utilized on edge devices to produce synthetic data. This process reduces the transmission load on the available bandwidth and reveals potential vulnerabilities through the disclosure of specific information regarding the local user. Subsequently, the secure aggregator or central server aggregates these individual models to form a comprehensive global model, which is then communicated back to the edge devices. The experimental findings on the CelebA [73] dataset demonstrate that FL offers substantial benefits in both supervised and unsupervised FR tasks. Experimental results demonstrate that implementing FFR system offers benefits such as improved privacy, since the original data remains on edge devices, and the aggregated model achieves performance nearly comparable to that of individual models, particularly when the FL model does not utilize a secure aggregator. In [48], a privacy-secure FFR training method called PriSecFedFR is presented. The technique solves two independent problems: sharing non-IID data between clients and preventing biometric privacy leakage. To solve with the former problem, the server pre-trains a global model on CASIA-WebFace [76] dataset. For each round, it selects negative samples from the dataset for each client based on Euclidean distance in the feature embedding space. In this way, a client with only one local name can still figure out both positive and negative loss terms. The local model’s drift related to the global model is limited even more by a contrastive regularization loss. In order to protect privacy, random projection (RP) is used to transform client feature embeddings before local training. RP is non-invertable and fulfills the properties of ideal biometric template protection (irreversibility, revocability, and unlinkability). RP also keeps pairwise distances almost similar. In [21], a federated cancelable-template protection framework for FR called DeepShieldFed is presented. A 512-D face embedding is extracted from each client using the InsightFace buffalo_l model. The first federated unlearning framework for FR models, called FORGET ME (F2GU), is presented in [55]. When a client asks for data removal under the “right to be forgotten," F2GU modifies that client’s generative trajectory (the path from an initial noise/latent vector to the final generated image) toward a target endpoint built using a Mirroring-guided Trajectory Optimization strategy, which avoids the quality loss seen with naive random-trajectory redirection. The goal of unlearning is driven by a trajectory-consistency loss, and the effect on the quality of generation for retained clients is limited by a multi-client regularization loss. One important difference between unlearning and retraining-based methods is that unlearning only needs one round of communication. There are three datasets that show that the method works on both GAN (EG3D/StyleGAN2) and Diffusion Model (Stable Diffusion v1.4) architectures. Algorithmic innovations

This section discusses various FFR techniques in relation to algorithmic innovations. The algorithmic innovation category is selected as FFR is a swiftly advancing domain, with numerous works presenting novel algorithms to enhance model performance, robustness, and adaptability. Algorithmic advancements in FFR seek to enhance performance and convergence.

Gradient correction is a vital methodology in FL designed to improve privacy preservation while sustaining model efficacy. In this study, the authors investigate the concept of gradient correction within the context of backward propagation and introduce a softmax-based regularizer to adjust the gradients of class embeddings by accurately incorporating a cross-client gradient component. The method theoretically demonstrates that the FedGC functions as a loss function analogous to the conventional softmax. FedFV [62] addresses the extreme FL scenario: each client possesses images of just one identity, and class embeddings are not transferable among clients. This undermines traditional softmax-based feature representation losses, as the negative loss component requires embeddings from other classes. In the absence of additional classes, the softmax loss diminishes to zero, preventing a client from calculating the necessary term to maintain the distinctiveness of its embedding. Two previous strategies tackle this issue: FedAwS, which incorporates a server-side regularization term to disperse class embeddings, and FedUV, which allocates a codeword from an error-correcting code to each client, so ensuring embeddings are inherently separated; both approaches only partially mitigate the disparity. FedFV introduces a equivalent class embedding: the server computes the average and re-normalizes a random pair of non-participating clients’ embeddings for that round, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{w} = (w_i+w_j)/\Vert w_i+w_j\Vert $$\end{document}, and transmits a batch of n such vectors to each participating client, along with the global model and the client’s individual embedding. The client considers these as fixed negative-class anchors for the conventional softmax loss during local training, subsequently returning only its updated embedding and model weights, while the corresponding embeddings are never transmitted back. The paper demonstrates that a client cannot retrieve the original \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_i, w_j$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{w}$$\end{document}, as an infinite number of embedding pairs on the pertinent semi-hypersphere could have generated it; an optional variant, FedFV(k), amalgamates k>2 real embeddings per equivalent vector for enhanced security at a minor accuracy expense, with k = 3 suggested as the optimal balance of privacy, accuracy, and computational efficiency. Trained on CASIA-WebFace [76] (1,000 identities, one client each) and assessed on LFW [87], CFP-FP [88], and AgeDB-30 [89], FedFV-100 (100 equivalent embeddings per round) surpasses both FedAwS and FedUV across all three benchmarks, reducing the disparity with centralized training; the paper attributes this to FedFV’s learned class-embedding similarity distribution being significantly closer to that of the centralized model compared to FedAwS or FedUV. Fairness and explainability

This section offers an overview of papers that address explainability, fairness considerations, and biases in FFR models. Fairness and explainability techniques ensure that the FFR systems are equitable and transparent. To reduce bias in model predictions, approaches such as adaptive FL with actor-critic selection (Privacy Safeguarding for Human-Computer Interaction) are employed. Model decisions are made interpretable through the incorporation of explainable AI (XAI) methodologies, which are essential for applications in healthcare and law enforcement. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of data among clients and the challenges associated with auditing decentralized models have hindered the attainment of fairness in FFR. A selection of key techniques that elucidate fairness and explainability are as follows.

In [26], a technique is introduced to develop system capable of ensuring trustworthiness by providing privacy, fairness, and explainability through intentional design. The technique demonstrates the potential for concurrently acquiring data while maintaining privacy. To ensure the privacy of the individual, HE has been implemented. The technique attains fairness and interpretability without compromising the accuracy of the final models. The FairFace [90] dataset is utilized to substantiate the authors’ claims. F3 [61] addresses Face Attribute Classification (FAC, e.g., gender classification) under federated data heterogeneity. Here, each client’s local data belongs to only a single demographic group for some sensitive attribute (e.g., age), rather than a representative mix of groups—for instance, a client might hold only “≤ 30” samples or only “>30” samples, never both. The paper proves, in its Proposition 1, that this setting breaks existing fair-FL methods such as the Lagrangian Multiplier Method. A client’s local fairness-violation term requires samples from every demographic group to compute. Under this heterogeneity, that term diverges to infinity, so fairness cannot be enforced during local training at all. F3’s response is to move fairness enforcement to the aggregation step instead. Clients train purely for accuracy. In [68], another FFR technique is introduced. Domain adaptation and transfer learning

FFR models are essential for generalizing across diverse domains, including different lighting environments and demographic groups. In this section, techniques focus on domain adaptation and transfer learning as strategies to improve model performance across diverse environments. Domain adaptation and transfer learning methodologies enhance the effectiveness of FFR models in novel environments.

FedPFR [47] addresses FFR when clients use different model architectures and hold entirely different sets of identities. Conventional FL and previous prototype-sharing techniques are inadequate in this scenario, as the aggregation of identity prototypes from separate clients undermines their significance. Local training integrates a conventional classification loss with an innovative prototype-anchor contrastive loss that attracts embeddings towards their respective prototypes while repelling them from others. Across four distinct backbone architectures, FedPFR surpasses previous heterogeneous FL techniques on IJB-C verification at all thresholds. Efficiency and scalability

The computational and communication challenges associated with FFR are explained in efficiency and scalability section. In environments with limited resources, such as edge devices and IoT systems, these techniques are crucial for the development of real-time FR applications.

Kim et al. [23] is a short position paper that proposes strategies for FFR model training, and claims to be the first publication to do so. It employs the ArcFace loss within MobileFaceNets and recognizes what it terms the “continuous" characteristic of FR: a singular identity produces novel facial data as time progresses, while new identities are introduced over time, resulting in an ever-increasing number of tasks. Four approaches tackle this issue. A labeling approach deduces positive labels en masse when the majority of frames within a window are positive. A data management approach mitigates catastrophic forgetting within mobile memory constraints by preserving solely the subset of embedding vectors that optimizes inter-vector distance, instead than maintaining all previous images. An assessment methodology divides devices into training and evaluation functions at a predetermined ratio (e.g., 80:20), enabling the validation of an updated global model on-device while maintaining the confidentiality of private data, with the option to revert to the most effective previous model if updates are rejected over consecutive iterations. An update buffer technique permits devices to train whenever they are accessible and stores their weight modifications, facilitating asynchronous involvement rather than server-directed device selection for each round. The document presents no experiments, datasets, or quantitative findings; it is provided as an initial conceptual contribution rather than as an empirical outcome.

Aggarwal et al. [25] introduces an FFR framework referred to as FedFace. FedFace utilizes face images collected from multiple clients to develop a robust and accurate FR model. The facial images stored on each client are not disclosed to other clients or the central server, and each client is a mobile device that contains the facial images of its owner (one identity per client). The experiments substantiate the effectiveness of FedFace in enhancing the verification performance of pre-trained FR systems across a range of face verification benchmarks. Federated Active Authentication (FAA) [69] tackles the identical one-identity-per-device implementation context as FedFace [25], although focuses on continuous or active authentication instead of singular verification, demonstrating that standard FedAvg is inadequately equipped for this. The technique initially measures this: inside the FAA framework, each device retains information from precisely one user (the paper’s specific \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_{IID} = 0$$\end{document}, the most extreme non-IID scenario), and the detection accuracy of FedAvg deteriorates to random-guess levels when data deviates from IID. To tackle this issue, the suggested approach pre-trains an FR model using a public dataset, divides it into a feature extractor and a classifier, and transmits solely the static feature extractor to devices. Each device subsequently transmits only the average and covariance of its respective user’s characteristics ("user impressions"), avoiding the transmission of raw images or complete model updates; the server generates synthetic features from these individual user Gaussians to train a multi-class classifier, which is then returned to the devices along with the feature extractor. This necessitates merely one instance of client–server interaction, in contrast to Split Learning, which demands several. Security: attacks on the training process and recognition target

FFR systems are particularly prone to two groups of attacks: the first group targets the training process: A malicious or Sybil client submit poisoned model updates to corrupt the global model. Whereas the second group targets the recognition target itself: presentation attacks, in which an attacker impersonates a live face using a printed image, recorded video, or a 3D/silicone mask, and synthetic-media forgery, where deepfake technology creates an artificial face that has never existed. This section explains several existing works in both this groups.

Process-level security: robustness against malicious clients

Conclusion

This survey reviewed 50 studies (2020–2026) at the intersection of FL and FR, categorized into six categories, and validated through a comparison of twelve surveys (Table 1) that no previous research prioritizes FL as its central framework. A singular tension dominates various categories as the core concept of the field: privacy, accuracy, and fairness are often in conflict, with nearly every study addressing multiple dimensions reporting a quantifiable trade-off (e.g., the 35-point variation in DP accuracy [43], and the accepted gap between centralized and federated approaches in FedForgery [59]), and no standard framework currently encompasses all three aspects. The security category demonstrates the significance of shared benchmarks: the presentation-attack sub-cluster, having established a single protocol, improved from 32% to below 5% average HTER over five years, whereas forgery detection, devoid of a comparable standard, continues to yield disparate, non-comparable outcomes—a weakness in infrastructure rather than in methodological quality. Open problems worth dedicated future work include vertical federated learning for face recognition, rigorous cryptographic guarantees, multi-modal biometric fusion under federation, joint process-level and target-level security, and unified federated unlearning with cancelable-template revocation. Taken together, these findings position federated learning as a promising but unevenly developed foundation for privacy-preserving face recognition, and this survey aims to serve as a structured reference for what is solved, partially solved, and still open in this fast-moving field.
