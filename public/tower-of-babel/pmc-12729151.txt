Premenstrual syndrome and traumatic childbirth perception: the role of childhood abuse, neglect history and taking reproductive health course: a cross-sectional study in Turkey
Authors: Hacer Yalnız Di̇lcen, Gamze Uykucu Armutçuoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPremenstrual syndrome (PMS) and the perception of childbirth as traumatic are significant phenomena that can negatively impact women’s psychological well-being, particularly during adolescence—a period marked by heightened emotional and physiological sensitivity. Emerging evidence suggests that early adverse experiences, especially childhood trauma, neglect, and various forms of abuse, may shape both the severity of PMS symptoms and the way childbirth is cognitively and emotionally perceived. In light of this, the present study aims to examine the mediating role of childhood trauma—including the type and nature of neglect and abuse—in the relationship between PMS and traumatic childbirth perception among adolescent women.MethodThis study was designed as quantitative, cross-sectional research employing the relational survey model, a subtype of the general survey model, to examine the relationships among variables. Data were collected from 410 women aged 18 to 25 who voluntarily participated in the study in Bartın University, Türkiye. The data collection tools included the Participant Information Form, the Premenstrual Syndrome Scale, the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, and the Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale. Data were gathered online via Google Forms between June and December 2022. The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24, and statistical procedures including correlation analysis, simple linear regression, mediation, and moderation analyses were conducted to test the research hypotheses.ResultsA significant relationship was found between premenstrual syndrome, traumatic birth perception, and childhood traumas. Furthermore, childhood traumas—particularly emotional and physical neglect—were found to have a mediating role in this relationship (p < 0.05). The participants’ status of taking courses related to reproductive health has a moderating effect on the relationship between premenstrual syndrome and traumatic birth perception (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study highlights the significant role of childhood trauma in influencing premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and traumatic childbirth perception (TCP) among young women. It also emphasizes the protective effect of reproductive health education. Our findings fill a gap in the literature by exploring these interconnected factors and their impact on women’s emotional well-being and maternal outcomes. The results suggest the importance of integrating trauma-informed approaches into nursing and midwifery education and providing ongoing training for healthcare professionals to better support women during reproductive periods. This research contributes to advancing preventive and supportive interventions aimed at improving maternal mental health.

Introduction

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) is a condition experienced by millions of women of reproductive age and is characterized by recurrent, moderate to severe physical, behavioral, and emotional symptoms that emerge during the late luteal phase of the menstrual cycle and subside shortly after menstruation begins [1]. The severity and manifestation of PMS symptoms vary among individuals, and these symptoms can negatively affect daily life by impairing physical, psychological, and social functioning. While some women experience mild symptoms, others report symptoms severe enough to significantly disrupt daily activities [2]. In a study [3], the most frequently reported PMS symptoms included depressive mood and hopelessness (90%), self-blame (90%), mood swings (89%), anger (81%), physical complaints (78%), fatigue (78%), anxiety (67%), and loss of interest (63%).

Research involving student populations has shown that experiencing moderate to severe PMS symptoms can lead to school absenteeism, reduced academic performance, difficulty with concentration and attention, and acute psychological distress [4, 5]. Although studies examining the genetic or environmental predispositions to PMS are limited, occupational health perspectives highlight that PMS symptoms can significantly affect women’s functioning in the workplace, underlining the importance of supportive strategies for symptom management [6]. In line with these findings, it has been reported that emotional and physical abuse experienced early in life increases the risk of PMS during the middle and late reproductive periods [7].

Childhood abuse and neglect, perpetrated by caregivers or individuals responsible for the child’s welfare, can take various forms, including emotional, physical, and sexual abuse, as well as neglect involving the failure to meet basic emotional and physical needs such as affection, nutrition, education, safety, and healthcare [8, 9]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that adverse childhood experiences are significant predictors of long-term health outcomes and psychological disorders [10]. Similarly, findings suggest that a higher number of negative childhood experiences is associated with increased PMS symptoms [11]. Specifically, childhood sexual abuse and emotional neglect have shown significant associations with the frequency and severity of PMS symptoms [12]. Supporting this, additional studies have reported a significant positive relationship between PMS and sexual abuse in childhood [7, 13, 14].

Furthermore, recent research in Turkish adolescent samples has identified significant associations between childhood trauma, premenstrual syndrome, and mental state, suggesting that early life experiences may play a critical role in the emergence of later reproductive health outcomes such as PMS [15].

Recent research has further elucidated the biological and psychosocial mechanisms linking childhood trauma to PMS severity. Additionally, coping strategies have been shown to mediate the relationship between fear of childbirth and psychological birth trauma, highlighting the potential of interventions targeting coping styles to reduce adverse outcomes [16]. Furthermore, studies emphasized that social contexts significantly modulate the relationship between childhood adversity and PMS in diverse populations [17].

Moreover, symptoms of PTSD have been shown to increase the risk of PMS. In a study examining the relationship between PTSD symptoms and PMS, women aged 27–44 with PTSD symptoms were found to be at greater risk of experiencing PMS compared to those without such symptoms [18]. However, how different types of childhood trauma specifically influence the prevalence and intensity of PMS remains largely unclear.

Recent research has also explored the relationship between PMS and perceptions of childbirth. Premenstrual symptoms have been found to increase fear of childbirth [19, 20], and this fear—rooted in concerns about harm to the baby or mother, pain, loss of control, or distrust in healthcare providers—can lead to the perception of childbirth as a traumatic event [21, 22]. Traumatic childbirth is defined as perceiving the birth process as a threat of death or injury to oneself or the baby [23]. A significant relationship has been identified between PMS and the perception of childbirth as traumatic [24]. In a study involving 258 postpartum women, those with a history of childhood sexual abuse were more than five times more likely to perceive childbirth as traumatic [25]. These women reported intense fear, prolonged labor, and greater likelihood of interpreting the experience as traumatic [26].

Additionally, evidence suggests that psychosocial characteristics such as gender perceptions and emotional health status during pregnancy may also affect both premenstrual and birth-related psychological processes [27]. Menstruation and reproductive experiences are deeply interwoven with women’s self-perception. From menarche onward, societal alienation from bodily processes may contribute to a sense of disconnection from childbirth as a powerful reproductive experience [28].

In this context, the present study aims to examine the relationship between premenstrual syndrome and traumatic childbirth perception among adolescent women, and to explore the mediating role of childhood abuse and neglect (type and presence) in this relationship. Additionally, the potential moderating effect of reproductive health education will be evaluated.

The study hypothesized that premenstrual syndrome is associated with both childhood trauma (including abuse and neglect) and the perception of childbirth as traumatic. It was also proposed that childhood trauma mediates the relationship between premenstrual syndrome and traumatic childbirth perception, with a specific focus on the mediating roles of emotional and physical neglect. Furthermore, the moderating role of having received education on reproductive health in this relationship was also explored.

Methods

Design

This study, designed as cross-sectional research, employed the relational survey model, a subtype of the general survey model. The relational survey model is a research paradigm that investigates the presence and degree of association between two or more variables. According to the indirect effects of the relationship between the dependent variable and the independent variables, the third variables in the relationship model are called mediators and moderators. Relationship models established with the help of mediating and moderating variables are referred to as mediating and moderating impact models [29]. The moderating variable can increase or decrease the power of the relationship between the independent and dependent variable or change the direction of this relationship [30].

Sample and settings

The study sample consisted of 410 women aged 18 to 25 residing in Bartın, Türkiye. Participants were recruited using purposive and convenience sampling methods via online platforms. Although the majority were university students—primarily due to the distribution of the survey through academic and student-focused digital networks—participation was not limited to student populations. Inclusion criteria were identifying as female, being aged between 18 and 25, and providing informed consent. No exclusion criteria were applied based on educational or occupational status, allowing for a heterogeneous sample with varying backgrounds.

The minimum required sample size was calculated as 385 using a 95% confidence interval, 3% margin of error, and an expected frequency of 10%. To account for potential data loss and ensure sufficient statistical power, a total of 472 responses were collected. After excluding incomplete or invalid submissions, data from 410 participants were included in the final analysis.

Data collection took place between June and December 2022 using a closed-format online questionnaire administered via Google Forms. The usability and technical functionality of the survey were tested prior to data collection. The survey link was shared via university-affiliated communication channels, including faculty mailing lists, official student groups, and social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Telegram) commonly used by young adults in academic environments. Additionally, the researchers directly contacted potential participants through personal and institutional networks to broaden outreach. These methods were employed to facilitate voluntary participation while maintaining accessibility and anonymity. All participants accessed the survey through the same secure Google Forms link, which was accompanied by a brief explanation of the study purpose, inclusion criteria, and informed consent details. No incentives were provided for participation.

The survey consisted of five pages with 20 items per page and took approximately 20 min to complete. Each item included a non-response option such as “not applicable” or “prefer not to say,” and forced-response settings ensured completeness. Once submitted, participants were unable to revise their responses, and the survey could not be accessed again.

Ethical approval and institutional permission were obtained prior to data collection. An informed consent form was presented at the beginning of the survey, and only participants who consented electronically were allowed to proceed.

Data sources and measurements

The study data were collected with the Participant Information Form, Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale, Childhood Trauma Questionnaire and Premenstrual Syndrome Scale created as a result of the literature review.

Participant information form

It consists of questions about the sociodemographic characteristics of women (educational status, age, social security, marital status), obstetric history (woman’s first menstrual age, menstrual cycle duration, pregnancy, birth, number of children), family history of adverse events and trauma history.

The Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale (STCP)

was developed by Yalnız et al., 2016 to assess psychological trauma associated with childbirth [31]. The scale comprises 13 items evaluating women’s perceptions and expectations of childbirth, both during pregnancy and in the postpartum period. Responses are scored on a scale from 0 (positive perception) to 10 (negative perception), with higher total scores indicating a more traumatic perception of childbirth. Based on the summed mean scores, perception levels are categorized as low (0–52), moderate (53–78), and high (79–130). In the original development study, the internal consistency of the scale was reported as Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89 [31]. The scale was originally developed in Turkish, and its validity and reliability were established through psychometric analyses conducted at Bartın University. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the STCP was found to be 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency.

The Premenstrual Syndrome Scale (PMSS)

was developed by Gençdoğan (2006) to assess the presence and severity of premenstrual syndrome (PMS) symptoms. The scale includes 44 items designed to measure physical, emotional, and behavioral symptoms experienced over the past three months, using a 5-point Likert-type response format ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Higher total scores reflect greater severity of PMS symptoms. Women scoring above 50% of the total score are classified as PMS-positive [32]. In the original study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.75. The PMSS was originally developed in Turkish, and its validity and reliability were confirmed through comprehensive psychometric evaluation, including factor analysis and internal consistency testing [32]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the PMSS was found to be Cronbach’s alpha = 0.97, indicating excellent reliability for the current sample.

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ-28)

was originally developed by Bernstein and Fink (1998) to retrospectively assess experiences of childhood maltreatment. The instrument consists of 28 self-report items and covers five subscales: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. The original version demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95 for the overall scale, and values ranging from 0.71 to 0.92 for the subscales [33]. The Turkish adaptation was conducted by Şar, Öztürk, and İkikardeş (2012), who reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 for the total scale, indicating strong reliability in the Turkish sample [34]. Furthermore, the psychometric properties of the Turkish version were supported in a subsequent validation study by Tekin and Kırlıoğlu (2020), who confirmed the construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and reported satisfactory sampling adequacy via the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test. In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the CTQ-28 was calculated as 0.87 [35].

Data analysis

The data obtained from the participants were tested with IBM SPSS Statistics 24 program and correlation, simple linear regression, mediation, and moderating analysis were performed for the results obtained and only completed questionnaires analyzed. The “PROCESS 4.0” program with SPSS was used for mediation and moderating analysis. The relationship between the variables was examined with Pearson product of moments correlation coefficients. Simple linear regression analysis was performed to determine the direction of the relationship between premenstrual syndrome, childhood traumas (neglect, abuse) and traumatic childbirth perception. The mediation and moderating effects that may exist in the relationship between the relevant variables were examined with the fourth model developed by Hayes [36], for mediation analysis and the first model for moderating analysis. When testing the significance of the mediation relationship, 95% confidencentervals were calculated using 5000 resampling (bootstrapping) techniques. Before the mediation analysis, the skewness and kurtosis values of the variables were examined and the values of the relevant variables were found to be between − 1.5 and + 1.5, and the data showed normal distribution.

Mediating analyses

The present study examined the mediating role of childhood traumas—specifically abuse and neglect—in the relationship between premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and the perception of traumatic childbirth. Results from the mediation analysis indicated that PMS significantly predicted traumatic childbirth perception (β = 0.41, b = 1.09, SE = 0.12, t = 9.20, p <.01, LLCI = 0.8594, ULCI = 1.3262). PMS also significantly predicted childhood trauma, the proposed mediator (b = 0.16, SE = 0.02, t = 6.50, p <.01, LLCI = 0.1107, ULCI = 0.2068). In turn, childhood trauma significantly predicted traumatic childbirth perception (b = 0.49, SE = 0.24, t = 2.05, p =.04, LLCI = 0.0196, ULCI = 0.9612). When childhood trauma was included in the model, the direct effect of PMS on traumatic childbirth perception slightly decreased (β = 0.38, b = 1.01, SE = 0.12, t = 2.04, p <.01, LLCI = 0.7707, ULCI = 1.2591), indicating a partial mediation effect. These findings suggest that exposure to childhood trauma partially explains the link between PMS and traumatic childbirth perception. That is, individuals with more severe PMS symptoms who have also experienced childhood trauma are more likely to perceive childbirth as traumatic (see Fig. 1). Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the relationship between premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and the perception of traumatic childbirth (TCP) in adolescent women, with a focus on the mediating role of childhood abuse and neglect, and the moderating role of reproductive health education.

Consistent with previous literature, PMS was found to be a significant issue not only affecting women’s mental health but also impacting family dynamics and broader community well-being, with consequences such as reduced labor productivity and diminished quality of life [37]. Moreover, premenstrual symptoms have been linked to students’ perceptions of childbirth, with studies reporting a positive correlation between PMS and traumatic childbirth perception among student midwives [19, 24].

Our findings revealed that adolescents with PMS exhibited elevated scores on both the PMS scale (136.28 ± 35.31) and the Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale (STCP: 76.5 ± 27.5), alongside moderately high levels of childhood psychological trauma (76.45 ± 27.49). These results align with prior research reporting similar PMS and STCP scores among adolescent populations [24, 38]. The near-universal experience of at least one premenstrual symptom (99.5%) within this group [19] supports the premise that adolescents experiencing PMS may also experience heightened stress and negative perceptions of their health.

Significant positive correlations were identified between PMS, STCP, and various types of childhood trauma, including physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, as well as neglect and overprotection. These findings are congruent with existing evidence indicating that women with histories of abuse are at increased risk for PMS [12, 39, 40]. Notably, exposure to childhood emotional abuse has been strongly associated with subsequent development of PMS, with affected women having 2.6 times higher odds compared to those without such histories [7]. Similarly, severe childhood physical abuse has been linked to a 2.1-fold increased risk of PMS [7]. The convergence of PMS and traumatic childbirth perception with histories of childhood abuse underscores the potential role of early trauma in shaping reproductive mental health outcomes [41].

Childhood trauma emerged as a partial mediator in the relationship between PMS and traumatic childbirth perception. Among the different trauma types, physical neglect and sexual abuse exerted the strongest influence on traumatic childbirth perceptions. These findings suggest that traumatic experiences and neglect during childhood may impair women’s ability to cope with the physical and emotional changes preceding menstruation, thereby intensifying PMS symptoms [12, 39]. Furthermore, childhood adversity disrupts emotional regulation, leading to maladaptive coping mechanisms characterized by insecurity, poor impulse control, and difficulty managing emotions [42]. Such deficits may diminish adult women’s capacity to adapt to premenstrual changes, potentially exacerbating distress and dysfunction [43], and contribute to heightened perceptions of childbirth trauma.

Supporting evidence indicates that women with PMS have higher prevalence of childhood trauma compared to controls, including emotional abuse, neglect, physical violence, and sexual abuse [40, 44]. Moreover, childhood stress exposure is associated with impaired ovarian function, reduced fertility, and menstrual irregularities, all of which negatively impact reproductive health [45]. Child sexual abuse, in particular, is critically relevant to pregnancy and childbirth, as it is linked to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [25, 26]. Women with such histories are more likely to report traumatic birth experiences and have unique healthcare needs that require sensitivity and awareness from midwives, nurses, and other professionals [26, 46].

Pregnancies in women with histories of childhood trauma tend to be more complicated, accompanied by increased anxiety and heightened sensitivity to medical procedures [46, 47]. These findings highlight the importance of early identification of trauma histories by healthcare providers to optimize prenatal care and support.

The observed moderating effect of reproductive health education further emphasizes its importance; absence of such education intensified the association between high PMS levels and traumatic childbirth perception, whereas those with low PMS and no reproductive health education had reduced negative childbirth perceptions. This underscores the critical role of reproductive health education during adolescence in shaping attitudes and mitigating negative reproductive experiences [48, 49].

This study offers important strengths, including the use of validated instruments and a sufficiently large sample size, enhancing reliability and generalizability. The focus on late adolescent and young adult women addresses an understudied demographic in reproductive mental health research. Robust statistical analyses allowed nuanced examination of mediation and moderation effects.

Nevertheless, limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inferences, reliance on self-report data subject to bias, and a predominantly university student sample that may restrict generalizability. Additionally, the online survey format may have excluded individuals with limited internet access.

In conclusion, this study contributes valuable insights into the complex interplay between PMS, childhood trauma, reproductive health education, and traumatic childbirth perception. These findings highlight the need for trauma-informed care and targeted educational interventions in nursing and midwifery to support women’s reproductive mental health.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it contributes to the growing body of literature on the psychological factors influencing childbirth perceptions by highlighting the mediating role of childhood trauma and the moderating role of reproductive health education in the relationship between premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and traumatic childbirth perception. The study’s use of validated and widely accepted measurement tools (e.g., Premenstrual Syndrome Scale, Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale) ensures the reliability and content validity of the data collected. In addition, the relatively large sample size (N = 410), which was determined through appropriate statistical calculations, enhances the study’s statistical power and the generalizability of its findings within the target population.

Furthermore, the study focuses on late adolescent and young adult women (aged 18–25), a group that is often underrepresented in reproductive mental health research, despite being at a critical developmental stage. The application of mediation and moderation analyses using robust statistical procedures also allows for a more nuanced understanding of the complex relationships among the variables examined.

However, the study is not without limitations. The use of a cross-sectional design prevents any inference of causality between the variables. Additionally, data were collected through self-report questionnaires, which may introduce common method bias and are subject to participants’ introspective ability and social desirability tendencies. Another limitation lies in the sampling strategy: although efforts were made to include a heterogeneous sample, the majority of participants were university students recruited via academic platforms, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations.

Lastly, the online nature of the survey might have excluded individuals with limited internet access or digital literacy, potentially introducing a sampling bias. Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable insights and establish a foundation for future longitudinal or experimental studies in this field.

Conclusion

Our study adds to the growing research into the long-term negative effects of childhood trauma on mental health in later life. To the best of our knowledge, there is a limited body of literature specifically addressing the potential impact of childhood trauma on premenstrual syndrome and the perception of childbirth as traumatic, particularly among women. Existing research tends to overlook the interplay between these variables and their possible association with adverse emotional states and psychosocial outcomes that may affect maternal well-being. Therefore, this study is expected to raise awareness in women who have experienced childhood trauma, by revealing the relationship and mediation effect between PMS and TCP, and to reduce the perception of traumatic birth during pregnancy and delivery. It is important to protect mother and child health, especially in the fields of nursing and midwifery. As a result, it is thought that it is important for midwifery and nursing students, who are the healthcare personnel of the future, to receive training in this context during their education. In addition, it is thought that the education programs of nursing and midwifery students should be reviewed and relevant topics should be included in the light of current current information and needs.
