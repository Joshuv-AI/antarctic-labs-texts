A European collaborative initiative to systematically map cancer risk by migration background: the Cancer RADAR project
Authors: Catharina Johanna Alberts, Damien Georges, Freddie Bray, Andrea Bordoni, Marià Carulla Aresté, Maria Dolores Chirlaque López, Joanna Didkowska, Jaume Galceran, Koen Van Herck, Enrica Migliore, Laura Ortelli, Lorenzo Richiardi, Antonia Sánchez Gil, Freija Verdoodt, Otto Visser, Urszula Wojciechowska, Andrea Gini, Irene Man, Stefano Rosso, Iacopo Baussano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe World Health Organization (WHO) Action Plan for Refugee and Migrant Health highlights the need for strengthened migration health governance and data-informed policies. A major barrier in Europe has been the lack of systematically collected and comparable cancer data stratified by migration background.MethodsThis study assesses the availability of routine data to map and examines differences of cancer risk among those with a migration background in Europe. The cancer types selected (breast, colorectal, cervical, stomach, liver, and lung) are either infection-related or amenable to detection through screening. In collaboration with pilot population-based cancer registries, a framework was developed to collect cancer data stratified by country of birth.ResultsFeasibility was high: 48 of 80 registries (60%) are able and willing to share data and eight (10%) intend to retrieve and share data, together from 22 countries. Reasons for data sharing included the prospects of reducing cancer inequalities and enhancing registry visibility, while barriers included time constraints, lack of resources, and the need for ethical and legal approval, particularly for data linkage. A protocol and analysis tool, co-developed with pilot registries, supports data collection of comparable effect measures while ensuring protection through anonymisation and aggregation. The data collection enables analysis of cancer estimates in inequality and trends, including major events such as the war in Ukraine.ConclusionCancer RADAR provides a framework for the systematic and comparable collection of cancer data by migration background, collaboratively, across Europe. This work represents a necessary enabling step toward standardised international comparisons and supports data-driven policies to address cancer inequalities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02899-8.

Introduction

International migration to and within Europe has increased and diversified markedly, with nearly 87 million migrant residents recorded by 2020, an increase of around 16% since 2015 [1]. The proportion of the population with a migration background (i.e. born in another country than the person is residing) varies substantially across European countries, ranging from 3% to 55% [2].

Individuals with a migration background often have distinct health needs compared to the general population in the host country [3, 4]. They also face considerable barriers to access appropriate healthcare [4]. Evidence from a systematic review, offering qualitative insights, indicated that migrant populations more frequently had a lower risk of cancers common in high-income countries (such as colorectal and breast cancer), and are at higher risk of infection-related cancers [5]. In addition, a recent systematic review suggests that migrants are usually diagnosed with cancer at a later stage than the host population [6].

Despite these observed disparities, there is a lack of systematically collected, comparable cancer data stratified by migration background across the world [7]. Cancer statistics are typically derived from nationwide analyses that aggregate data of all residents, regardless of migration background. While many European population-based cancer registries do collect information on migration background, these data are rarely analysed, and the extent of missing or incomplete information is poorly understood. Where sub-analyses exist, they often focus on the largest migrant groups, apply broad regional categories, cover limited time periods, and restrict comparisons to the host population [5, 8].

This fragmentation of evidence is not only the result of technical or knowledge limitations [9, 10]. Yet, it also reflects the influence of “political factors at local, national, and European levels, which have hindered the implementation of existing knowledge and guidelines” as stated by Bozorgmehr et al.9,10

Indeed, the Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan acknowledges that significant health inequalities persist across Europe [11]. In response, the European Cancer Inequalities Registry was established to monitor and compare disparities in cancer outcomes [12]. This registry disaggregates data by country, sex, education, income, urbanisation, age, disability and employment. However, a key dimension remains absent from this registry, which is migration background. This absence reflects a combination of factors, including lack of standardized definitions of migration background across Europe, legal and privacy constraints on data collection, lack of innovative methodology to analyse by country of birth, and competing priorities under limited resources to disaggregate cancer data by migration status.

To address these challenges, we have established Cancer RADAR, a collaborative initiative involving cancer registries across Europe. The project aim is to examine differences in cancer risk (measured as age-standardized incidence rates) between individuals with a migration background and host populations, by country of birth and cancer type. We hypothesize that cancer incidence differs between migrant and host populations, varies by country of birth and cancer type, and is context-dependent, reflecting both origin-related risk profiles and host country healthcare systems, including screening practices. For example, infection-related cancers (e.g., cervical, stomach, liver) may reflect exposures in the country of birth, whereas cancers such as colorectal cancer may be more influenced by host country factors such as lifestyle and screening participation.

Cancer RADAR selected cancers based on their actionability, focusing on those that are infection-related, detectable through established screening programmes, or amenable to detection through (targeted) screening, namely breast, colorectal, cervical, stomach, liver, and lung.

Here, we describe (1) the availability and feasibility of collecting and sharing cancer data stratified by migration background, and (2) the framework necessary for collecting cancer data stratified by migration background, across European cancer registries. Although this study is restricted to European population-based cancer registries, the Cancer RADAR initiative is intended as a proof of concept, with the long-term goal of expanding this approach to other regions worldwide where migration is also a major demographic driver.

Methods

Availability and feasibility of collecting cancer data stratified by migration background across European cancer registries

To assess the availability and feasibility of collecting cancer data by migration background, we conducted a survey among European cancer registries. The structured questionnaire (Annex 1), was developed in consultation with seven pilot registries from Murcia and Tarragona from Spain, Turin from Italy, Ticino from Switzerland, and national registries from Belgium, the Netherlands, and Poland. The questionnaire included questions on: (1) availability of cancer data to estimate effect measures on incidence by country of birth and data source; (2) willingness to share such data, measured on a 6-point Likert scale; and (3) perceived facilitators and barriers to data collection and sharing, using both closed and open-ended formats. European population-based cancer registries were approached through established networks of the International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization (IARC-WHO), the International Association of Cancer Registries (IACR), European Network of Cancer Registries (ENCR) and Group for Cancer Epidemiology and Registration in Latin Language Countries (GRELL).

Standardized protocol for cancer data collection stratified by migration background across cancer registries in Europe

A standardised data collection protocol was developed in collaboration with seven pilot cancer registries. See Annex 2.1 for the Cancer RADAR protocol.

This protocol will be used to collect data from population-based cancer registries across Europe, stratified by country of birth. Country of birth is used as an indicator of migration background, capturing first-generation migrants. It was selected as the preferred variable because it is routinely collected in many administrative systems, and it is an objective variable that does not change over time. For countries that have undergone boundary changes, individuals are classified based on present-day countries of birth (i.e., according to current geographical classifications rather than historical entities). This can, for example, be achieved by using the city of birth as an initial proxy to determine the corresponding current geographical region. If this information is not available, cases are redistributed to the respective countries.

The protocol was developed through an iterative process, leading to several key decisions, including the requirement that all data shared must be highly aggregated and anonymous. By highly aggregated, we mean that the data are grouped at a broad level (e.g. by age group, sex, or migration background) without any individual-level information. Registries are also given the flexibility to choose whether to stratify data by country of birth or to provide more aggregated groupings, depending on data availability and national data protection regulations.

Collection process of cancer data stratified by migration background

Pilot cancer registries have already shared data and cancer registries identified as potential participants through the Cancer RADAR survey on data availability have been invited to contribute data for which data collection is currently ongoing. The data collection process consists of four steps:

Step 1: Data collection

Data on the number of cancer cases for selected cancer types, stratified by country of birth are currently being extracted by cancer registries identified as potential participants through the Cancer RADAR survey. Collection of population-at-risk data, defined as the underlying population from which cancer cases arise (e.g. all women in the case of cervical cancer), stratified by country of birth is encouraged but optional. Registries may report data on one or more of the standard five-year periods defined by the Cancer Incidence in Five Continents (CI5): 2003–2007, 2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2022 [13]. Inclusion of multiple time periods will allow the analysis of trends in cancer disparities over time.

Step 2: Data entry

To ensure consistency, a standardised data input template has been provided (see Annex 2.2). Country of birth will be coded using ISO 3166 numeric-3 codes, in accordance with UN classifications [14].

Step 3: Data processing

Data processing is conducted locally at each cancer registry. The completed input file will be imported into R (R Core Team), an open-source statistical computing environment. The Cancer RADAR R package (cancerradarr), which has been developed for the purpose of this initiative, is locally used to automatically generate predefined and highly aggregated risk measures (see Annex 2.3 for data dictionary on the generated output data).

Step 4: Data sharing

The output file generated in Step 3 is uploaded to REDCap, after which it will be transferred to a secure platform. The Cancer RADAR data are stored at IARC-WHO in Lyon, France. The Cancer RADAR data remain the property of the contributing registries, whose consent is required before they can be used for purposes other than those originally envisaged in the Cancer RADAR protocol.

Throughout Steps 1–3, all data remain securely within each registry. Only in Step 4, the locally generated highly aggregated effect measures are shared.

Inclusion criteria

Participating cancer registries must collect, or have access to, information on country of birth for cancer cases. The selected cancer types include breast (C50), colorectal (C18-20), cervical (C53), stomach (C16), liver (C22.0), and lung (C33-34) cancers using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) [13]. Ideally, data should be available from 2003 onwards, aligning with the CI5 reporting periods [13]. An intention to collect these data in future periods is also encouraged, to facilitate ongoing monitoring of cancer inequalities over time.

Data quality and consistency

The data used are derived from datasets prepared for the CI5 submissions, which undergo standardized quality control procedures. To further assess data quality and the completeness of country-of-birth information, we will evaluate several indicators, including the proportion of death certificate only (DCO) cases and microscopically verified (MV) cases stratified by birth country. In addition, we ask registries to report the proportion with an unknown birth country (i.e., known to be foreign-born but without a specified country) and cancer cases with missing birth country information.

Statistical analysis

The analysis conducted in Step 3 of data processing includes crude and age-standardised incidence rates by country of birth, using the world standard population (Segi-Doll distribution [15]), as well as comparisons between migrant and reference populations based on several measures: incidence rate ratio (IRR), incidence rate difference (IRD), standardised incidence ratio (SIR), and proportional incidence ratio (PIR) [16], all with 95% confidence intervals. When the age distribution of the migrant population is similar to that of the reference population, IRR and SIR are expected to yield comparable results. The IRR directly compares incidence rates, while the SIR estimates expected cases if migrants shared the reference population’s age-specific risks. The PIR relies solely on cancer case data and does not require information on the population at risk. Instead, it compares the proportion of a specific cancer among all cancer cases (C00-97, excluding C44) in the study population with the corresponding proportion in the reference population. Further methodological details are provided in Annex 2.1.

Cancer risk among migrant populations will be compared with several reference groups, including the registry’s general population, country-specific populations reported by the Cancer Incidence in Five Continents (CI5), and European populations (EU27 and Europe as defined by UN). Results will be stratified by UN region of birth, Human Development Index (HDI) category, global quartiles of country-specific cancer risk, and, where possible, by individual country of birth.

Ethical considerations

Cancer RADAR has been reviewed and approved by the IARC Ethics Committee (IEC 23–38). As only highly aggregated, anonymised data are collected, no further ethical approval is required at the international coordination level. However, local or national ethic and legal approvals may be required depending on country-specific regulations, such as ethical review, data linkage, data protection, General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance, or other contextual factors. In such cases, participating cancer registries will be responsible for securing the necessary permission from the relevant ethics committees, data protection authorities, or other designated bodies.

Results

Feasibility of cancer data collection stratified by country of birth

Cancer registries from 33 European countries, comprising 104 registries, were invited to complete a questionnaire on the availability of country of birth data within their registry (Annex 3). In total, 83 of the 104 (80%) cancer registries from 29 (88%) countries responded. Of these, 7 registries from 6 countries had been involved from the start as pilot sites to support the development of the data collection protocol and questionnaire.

Among the 80 registries that provided consent to analyse their response, 52 out of 80 (65%) reported having country of birth data available at the registry level, 27 (34%) did not, and one registry reported to be able to use nationality as a proxy (Table 1). Data on population-at-risk stratified by country of birth was available in about half of the registries (36/80; 45%).

Among the 52 registries with available country of birth data, 48 out of 52 (92%) were neutral or likely to share these data (Table 1). The most commonly reported sources were national statistical agencies and hospital records. Key motivations for sharing data included the perceived importance of the topic, increased visibility of the registry, and sufficient staff time. Reported barriers included limited time, lack of financial resources for data preparation, and complex data-sharing procedures. A minority (6%) reported not considering the topic to be of sufficient importance.

Of the 27 registries lacking country of birth data locally at their registry, 11 out of 27 (41%) indicated that linkage with external data sources (such as statistical agencies or national census data) could be used to retrieve the data (Table 1). Among these, 8 out of 11 (72%) were neutral or likely to pursue such linkage. Facilitators for retrieval were similar to those cited by registries already in possession of the data. However, additional barriers were reported, including ethical, logistical and legal constraints, limited capacity, and costs associated with data access and integration.

Combining availability and self-reported willingness to share or retrieve data, 48 (60%) registries indicated they were likely or neutral towards sharing data stratified by country of birth, and 8 (10%) indicated a willingness or neutrality toward pursuing data linkage (Table 1). These registries combined (n = 56) are situated in Austria, Belgium, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Iceland, Italy, Luxembourg, Malta, Montenegro, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, United Kingdom.

Framework of cancer data collection

In collaboration with pilot registries, a standardised protocol was developed to guide the cancer data collection process (Annex 2.1). This protocol was designed to facilitate harmonised, decentralised, and anonymised data processing across registries. Details of the data collection approach are provided in the Methods section and all code for data processing is openly available on gitlab (Annex 2.1).

Several considerations guided the development of the protocol, e.g. concerns raised by registries regarding small case numbers, collection of methodologically correct and relevant data and limited staff time availability. Therefore, a series of safeguards and methodological decisions were incorporated to ensure that all shared data are highly aggregated, non-identifiable, easy to process, and epidemiologically relevant:

Aggregation: Stratified data are assessed for potential privacy risks by considering both the number of cases and the size of the underlying population. For example, where the combination of a small numerator and a small denominator could risk identifiability, data cells are merged with adjacent categories to meet a minimum threshold. For cervical cancer, data are included from age 20 years and older. These measures were designed to assure a balance between data relevance and privacy protection, thereby enabling broader participation among registries, especially those with small number of cancer cases or missing denominator data among migrant subgroups. The protocol was developed to be adaptable to local constraints while maintaining comparability across participating registries.

Discussion

The Cancer RADAR initiative shows that the integration of migration background into routine cancer surveillance is feasible and offers a model for international collaboration. Coordinated data collection across population-based cancer registries, supported by standardized tools, accommodates local variability, reduces fragmentation, and informs equity-focused cancer control at the European level. Included cancers concern infection-related cancers and cancers amenable to screening, namely breast, cervical, colorectal, lung, liver, and stomach. This initiative provides a crucial step to inform evidence-based public health responses, including the potential need of culturally tailored screening and prevention strategies, with the ultimate aim of reducing cancer disparities across diverse populations. The Cancer RADAR initiative currently focuses on Europe and aims, in the long term, to expand this approach to other regions worldwide where migration is also a major demographic driver [17].

Within Cancer RADAR, cancers were selected based on their actionability namely, their potential for prevention through infection control, detection through established population-based screening programmes, or amenable to detection through (targeted) screening. Migrants may face context-specific risks due to different prior exposure to infectious agents, differences in healthcare availability, and barriers to accessing healthcare in both their country of birth and host country. For example, hepatitis B virus and Helicobacter pylori infections, linked to liver and stomach cancers respectively, are often acquired in early life, frequently before migration. Such early-life exposures may lead to elevated cancer risks later in adulthood [8, 5, 18]. Conversely, migrants from low-HDI countries are often found to have a lower or similar risk of being diagnosed with colon and breast cancers compared with the host population, potentially reflecting differences in lifestyle-related risk factors [19]. Cervical cancer, caused by human papillomavirus (HPV), is an infection-related cancer where elevated risk among individuals with a migration background may stem from limited access or participation to screening, either due to a lack of organised programmes in their country of origin/birth, or lower participation when moving to the new host country independent of their birth countries HDI [20–22]. In future, HPV vaccination, while overall reducing cervical cancer incidence, may increase disparities. Understanding the reasons behind this changing risk may offer valuable insights into modifiable factors in cancer prevention.

To support long-term monitoring, Cancer RADAR aligns its data collection with the CI5 reporting periods, laying the foundation for an ongoing, sustainable initiative. This approach enables the systematic assessment of how cancer disparities by migration background evolve over time. While the impact of cancer control policies often becomes evident only over the long term, a continuous surveillance framework is essential to detect trends and evaluate policy effectiveness. In addition, Cancer RADAR also allows for the timely monitoring of acute disruptions. For example, the war in Ukraine highlights the value of cancer registries in tracking changes in geographic distribution of cancer cases following large-scale population displacement to neighbouring countries. The national Polish cancer registry, involved in Cancer RADAR, is supporting the quantification of cross-border cancer burden shifts linked to crisis-related migration.

Recent global and regional initiatives underscore the increasing need for attention to migrant health [7, 23, 24]. In October 2023, WHO launched its first-ever global research agenda on health, migration, and displacement, highlighting the need of evidence-based approaches in addressing health inequities [7]. In parallel, efforts to develop regional and global action plans continue to provide policy frameworks to improve the health of refugees and migrants [23, 24]. A consistent message across these initiatives is the urgent need for robust data to inform effective responses. As the WHO notes, “the lack of evidence and data has hindered the development of policies and programmes needed to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for migrants” [7]. The Cancer RADAR initiative directly responds to this call by building an infrastructure for the systematic collection of migrant-specific cancer data, thereby contributing to more equitable and data-driven public health policies.

The survey data obtained through Cancer RADAR demonstrates the ability and a strong willingness among cancer registries to retrieve and contribute data, despite limited resources and competing demands. However, the complexity of data sharing was frequently cited as a key barrier. By building on data already collected and validated for the CI5 series and providing the Cancer RADAR R package for automated processing, this challenge is partially addressed. The Cancer RADAR R package is open-source, enabling cancer registries that are unable to participate within the Cancer RADAR collection initiative, to process and analyse their data separately from this initiative, while generating comparable data across Europe or other regions worldwide. This also allows them to share insights with local policymakers when feasible. This initiative offers valuable insights into the challenges and opportunities of establishing a standardized cancer registry system that includes migration data. By understanding the barriers to data sharing and identifying feasible solutions, Cancer RADAR aims to create a scalable infrastructure that can improve the surveillance of cancer in migrants, including to other regions in the world.

As shown in our results, approximately half of the registries reported lacking denominator data. However, in follow-up conversations, where we explained how and where this data can be obtained, several registries indicated that they will attempt or were already able to access this information. Once data collection is firmly established across Europe, we plan to expand this framework to other regions, where stratified population data is difficult to obtain such as low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). To ensure this we are collecting both PIR and SIR data from regions/countries that have both available. This expansion is essential given that the majority of global migration happens within LMICs [25]. In addition, one registry reported having nationality data. Unfortunately, this cannot currently be used as a proxy for migration background, as it reflects citizenship status rather than migration history, which may change over time and introduce regulatory and indirect selection biases that affect the accurate estimation of e.g. the at-risk population.

We consider the Cancer RADAR aggregation tool a key strength of the initiative, as it enables cancer registries to contribute data in a highly aggregated format. This approach has facilitated broad participation across registries, given that it addresses data protection concerns and reduces the burden of sharing sensitive information. However, this same feature may also be perceived as a limitation, as the resulting dataset does not support individual-level analyses needed to explore causal relationships or adjust for confounders such as socioeconomic status. While we acknowledge that more granular analyses are essential for a deeper understanding of cancer disparities, arguments for perfect data have too often stalled progress. We believe that placing migrant-specific cancer risk on the European agenda (through a coordinated and standardised approach) is an important enabling step toward more detailed future investigations. Moreover, even if socioeconomic status is a potential confounder, population-level observations of consistently higher or lower cancer risks among migrant groups remain relevant for public health action. These patterns, visible at the aggregated level, can guide policymakers in identifying priority areas for prevention and further research, particularly in contexts where individual-level data are not readily available. In addition, the Cancer RADAR initiative focuses exclusively on cancer incidence data. In a next phase, we plan to also collect screening uptake and mortality data for these cancers. This is particularly important to understand how screening uptake influences incidence and mortality. When interpreting data among migrant populations, several important biases must be considered. The “healthy migrant effect,” whereby healthier individuals are more likely to emigrate, resulting in a systematically healthier migrant population at baseline, that may lead to lower observed cancer risk among migrants [26]. Additionally, the “salmon bias,” the tendency of migrants to return to their country of origin at end of life, is relevant to take into account when interpreting mortality data, as deaths occurring outside the host country may not be captured [27, 28].

Forced displacement –whether due to war, climate change, political persecution, economic instability, or natural disaster, among other causes— may contribute to a delay in access to healthcare and thus cancer presentation in a more advanced stage at diagnosis [29, 30]. Health system responses to such displacement should, among other priorities, strengthen health information systems to monitor and forecast the resources required to scale up essential care [31]. In Poland, the rapid expansion of existing public health services for Ukrainian refugees addressed immediate needs but strained already limited oncological capacity [29, 32, 33]. Local assessments at the National Cancer Institute in Krakow found breast, colon, lung, and cervical cancers to be the most frequently diagnosed among Ukrainians [32]. Comprehensive monitoring of cancer incidence among Ukrainian populations across Europe, however, has remained challenging [33]. In 2022, in response to rising migration, including refugees from Ukraine, the ENCR recommended that only legal residents of a registry’s catchment should be included [34]. In Poland, all Ukrainians residing for more than 30 days are eligible for a national identification number, which enables access to free cancer care and allows the Polish national registry to monitor cross-border cancer burden shifts linked to crisis-related migration [29].

The evidence generated through Cancer RADAR should serve as a foundation for guiding policy development. However, it is important to recognize that these data are neither entirely value-free nor neutral, as stated by Bozorgmehr et al. [9]. Bozorgmehr et al. suggest that to ensure that impact aligns with equity-oriented policymaking the evidence must support the SDGs and uphold the principle that health is a fundamental human right [9]. To do this, effective communication of the created evidence is critical, including the use of non-stigmatizing language and a clear presentation of findings [9]. To attain this goal, Cancer RADAR will proactively disseminate findings with policymakers and the media, ensuring that the results are accurately interpreted and effectively communicated [9].

Conclusion

In conclusion, European cancer registries indicate availability of migrant-specific data at their registries and the majority of cancer registries indicated to be either willing or neutral to share this data. Financial and time barriers may prohibit further data collection and sharing. A standardized protocol for quantifying and monitoring cancer risks among migrants is developed enabling decentralized analyses and preserving anonymity and avoiding small cell counts. Coordinated international analyses will allow more meaningful conclusions than those from individual registries or countries alone.
