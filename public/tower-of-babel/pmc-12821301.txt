Optimal sleep duration for cardiovascular prevention in Eastern Mediterranean populations: insights from a prospective cohort study
Authors: Danial Habibi, Maryam Kazemi Naeini, Shaghyegh Haghjooy Javanmard, Noushin Mohamadifard, Razieh Hassannejad, Taha Rafiei, Jamshid Najafian, Mahdi Akbarzadeh, Hamid Reza Marateb, Marjan Mansourian, Miguel Ángel Mañanas, Nizal Sarrafzadegan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere is a complex relationship between sleep duration and the risk of developing cardiovascular diseases. This study aimed to identify sleep duration trajectories in a healthy population and examine their associations with incident cardiovascular disease (CVDs) in the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR).MethodsThis prospective population-based cohort study included 5,432 participants free of cardiovascular disease at baseline (2001–2013). The associations between sleep duration trajectories and CVD risk were analyzed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Cubic and restricted cubic spline models were applied to identify the optimal sleep duration associated with the lowest CVD risk and to examine the shape of the relationship between total daily sleep duration and CVD hazard ratios.ResultsWe observed a U-shaped association between total daily sleep duration and incident CVD after adjusting for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health status. Compared with individuals sleeping below 5 h, adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for CVD were as follows: 5–6 h (HR = 0.930, 95%CI: 0.70–1.22, p-value = 0.61), 6–7 h (HR = 0.772, 95%CI: 0.59–0.99, p-value = 0.04), 7–8 h (HR = 0.764, 95%CI: 0.60–0.97, p-value = 0.03), 8–9 h (HR = 0.701, 95%CI: 0.53–0.91, p-value = 0.01), 9–10 h (HR = 0.755, 95%CI: 0.54–1.05, p-value = 0.10], and > 10 h (HR = 0.816, 95%CI: 0.54–1.22, p-value = 0.01). According to the results of the cubic spline curves, the lowest risk was identified at a sleep duration of 8.38 h.ConclusionsThe internationally recommended sleep duration may not be ideal for Middle Eastern populations. In this Iranian cohort, approximately 8.4 h of total daily sleep was associated with the lowest CVD risk.

Introduction

Despite active management of known risk factors, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the world’s leading cause of death worldwide. Therefore, novel strategies are required to improve CVD outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests a potential link between sleep duration and CVD events, as well as all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. This area has been the extensively investigated in recent years [1–3].

Although several studies have reported links between both short and long sleep durations and an increased risk of CVD [2, 4, 5], other investigations have failed to confirm this relationship [6–9]. Some studies suggest a more complex pattern with a U-shaped or a J-shaped association between sleep duration and CVD risk [3, 8].This inconsistency underscores the need for further research to clarify therelationship between sleep and cardiovascular risk. Such discrepancies may arise from geographic, cultural, or methodological differences, particularly regarding how sleep duration cut-offs for CVD risk are defined.

Moreover, the sleep duration cut-off used to determine CVD risk varies across countries [10–13]. To date, no study has examined the relationship between sleep duration, its critical cut-off point, and the risk of incident CVD in Iranian populations [14, 15]. Although several invistigations in Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) have explored associations between lifestyle factors and CVD, research specifically focusing on sleep duration remains scarce, particularly studies identifying sleep patterns that contribute to increased CVD risk [16, 17]. One large multicenter study involving 116,632 participants from 21 countries investigated the association between total daily sleep duration and CVD using cubic spline modeling, revealing that an estimated sleep duration of 6–8 h was associated with the lowest CVD risk [8].

Despite the growing global body of evidence [18–21], a significant knowledge gap persists in the EMR, particularly in Iran. Most existing studies have been conducted in Western (North American and European) or East Asian populations. Given the unique genetic, cultural, and lifestyle characteristics of the EMR, the relationship between sleep duration and CVD risk in this region remains largely unexplored. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to investigate the association between self-reported total daily sleep duration on CVD incidence, and to characterize this relationship in a middle-aged, representative population from the EMR.

Methods

Population

The present analysis was derived from the prospective, population-based Isfahan Cohort Study (ICS), initiated in 2001. The baseline cohort included 6,504 adults aged 35–75 years, recruited as permanent residents from three purposively selected counties in central Iran (Isfahan, Arak, and Najafabad) to ensure both demographic and geographic representativeness. Participants were chosen through a multi-stage random sampling procedure. First, clusters were randomly selected from each county in proportion to population size. Next, households within these clusters were systematically sampled using local health center registries. Finally, one eligible individual per household—free of any prior physician-diagnosed cardiovascular disease—was randomly selected using the “most recent birthday” method. The upper age limit of 75 years was chosen to minimize the influence of frailty and competing mortality risks, thereby focusing the study on incident CVD events. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Isfahan Cardiovascular Research Center, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Further details of the study design, participant selection, and methodology have been published previously [22]. Participants with a history of myocardial infraction, stroke, or heart failure at baseline, as well as those older than 75 years, were excluded from the present analysis.

Time zero for follow-up was defined as the date of the baseline survey completion for each participant, which was conducted between January 2 and September 28, 2001. Follow-up of the participants was conducted every two years after the baseline survey.

The study documented the following main cardiovascular events: Fatal Myocardial Infarction (MI), defined as death caused by MI; non-fatal MI, diagnosed based on the presence of at least two of three criteria-(i) typical chest pain lasting more than 30 min, (ii) ST-segment elevation greater than 0.1 mV in at least 2 adjacent ECG leads, or (iii) an elevation in cardiac biomarkers; unstable angina (UA), defined as typical chest discomfort lasting more than 20 min within 24 h preceding hospitalization, representing a change in the usual pattern of angina, accompanied by dynamic ST-segment or T-wave changes; fatal stroke: defined as death caused by a stroke; non-fatal stroke: defined as a rapidly developing focal neurological disorder persisting for at least 24 h with a probable vascular origin according to the WHO definition; sudden cardiac death: defined as death accuring within one hour of symptom onset, a witnessed cardiac arrest, or an abrupt collapse not preceded by more than one hour of symptoms; Ischemic heart disease (IHD) was a composite endpoint comprising definite or probable MI, UA, and sudden cardiac death, whereas cardiovascular disease (CVD) was a composite endpoint of IHD and stroke.

To ensure the highest accuracy in endpoint adjudication, the study employed a rigorous, multi-source verification process. potential cardiovascular events initially identified through interviews were cross-referenced with hospital records and a dedicated registry database. For out-of-hospital deaths, verbal autopsies were conducted with family members, and all cases were sunsequently reviewed by independent panels of specialist cardiologists and neurologists, who made final diagnoses based on a comprehensive assessments of medical documentation, questionnaires, and death certificates.

Although the study experienced a cumulative attrition rate of 15% after the first follow-up, extensive retention strategies were implemented, including at least five telephone contact attempts and subsequent home visits for non-responders. Most of the early loss to follow-up was attributed to changes in contact information, an occurrence considered random and non-systematic. Importantly, analysis revealed no significant differences in baseline characteristics or CVD risk factor prevalence between those retained and those lost to follow-up, indicating that attrition was non-differential. The cohort was followed for a median of 4.8 years (IQR: 4.6–5.0), accumulating 24,379 person-years of observation.

Measurements

The primary outcome of this analysis was cardiovascular disease. The CVD endpoint was defined as either ischaemic heart disease (IHD) events including fatal or non-fatal myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death or stroke. Although hospital physician diagnoses were considered, all outcome determinations were made independently by an adjudication panel. The main covariate of interest was total average sleep duration, measured by the question, “How many hours of sleep did you have in any 24 hours?” (calculated as the sum of nighttime sleep and daytime nap hours). Information on demographic factors, smoking, disease history, physical activity, and socioeconomic status (SES) was collected through interviews conducted by trained health professionals using validated questionnaires. Smoking status is defined as ever having smoked. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), based on self-reported data. Three domains of socioeconomic status (SES), namely education, employment, and income, were evaluated. A composite SES z-score was created to summarize each participant’s socioeconomic position. Education level was coded as an ordinal variable according to the highest level attained (1: illiterate; 2: primary school; 3: high school diploma; 4: university degree). Employment status was categorized as either binary (0: unemployed, 1: employed) or ordinal (0: unemployed, 1: manual labor, 2: skilled labor, 3: professional). Monthly household income was treated as a continuous variable (local currency). Each of the three SES variables was standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 to generate z-scores, allowing variables measured on different scales to be combined. The overall composite SES z-score for each participant was calculated as the sum of these individual z-scores. Physical activity was assessed using the validated Baecke questionnaire. Participants were followed up every two years, and outcome status was determined at each follow-up via structured telephone interviews or home visits after the baseline survey in 2001. Further details on study methodology are available in “The Isfahan Cohort Study: Rationale, Methods, and Main Findings.” [23].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarized according to event occurrence categories. The frequency of categorical risk factors and the mean with standard deviation for continuous risk factors, were calculated across different sleep duration groups. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for total CVD incidence in relation to sleep duration. Data completeness exceeded 98% for all variables; Therefore, a complete-case analysis was performed.

Sleep duration was considered the main covariate of interest. The estimates were adjusted for sex, age, smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, high body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status (SES) score, and physical activity using Cox proportional hazards models. Since this study aimed to determine a specific cut-off point for sleep duration, the categories of total sleep duration were selected in shorter and narrower intervals.

Sleep duration was categorized into seven mutually exclusive groups to avoid ambiguity: ≤5 h, > 5–6 h, > 6–7 h, > 7–8 h, > 8–9 h, > 9–10 h, and > 10 h. The ≤ 5 h category served as the reference group. Model 1 was adjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was fully adjusted for age, sex, smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, BMI, SES, and physical activity. The category of ≤ 5 h of sleep was used as the reference group in all models. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox models was verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

To explore potential non-linear associations between sleep duration and CVD risk, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted using restricted cubic splines (RCS), with sleep duration treated as a continuous variable. We used spline curves to determine potential cut-off points based on previous studies [23]. RCS allow flexible modelling of non-linear dose-response relationships without assuming a pre-defined functional form [24]. We placed 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the sleep duration distribution, a standard approach that maintans flexibility while minimizing overfitting [25].

The optimal sleep duration associated with the lowest CVD risk was identified as the point on the fitted spline curve where the hazard ratio reached its minimum. To determine the best-fitting model objectively, we compared models with varying degrees of complexities (three, four, and five knots) using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The model with the lowest AIC value, corresponding to four knots, was selected as the optimal fit [26]. The minimum point of this best-fitting spline function was then calculated analytically, yielding an optimal sleep duration of hours [8]. All statistical analyses were performed using the survival package in R software (version 3.5.0). A two–tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Of the total 6,504 subjects recruited, 5,432 (83.7%) completed the follow-ups. The mean age of all recruited subjects was 50.7 ± 11.6 years, and 2,648 subjects (48.74%) were men. In terms of baseline characteristics and the prevalence of CVD risk factors, no significant differences were observed between participants who completed follow-ups and those lost to follow-up. All eligible participants were included in the analysis. After 59,208.8 person-years of follow-up, with a median follow-up time of 10.9 years, 751 incident cases of CVD (405 in men and 346 in women) were documented. The results revealed a statistically significant association between sleep duration and CVD risk. In the initial (unadjusted) analysis, sleep durations of 6–7 h, 7–8 h, and 8–9 h were associated with a significantly lower risk of CVD compared with the reference group (< 5 h). Associations for other categories (5–6 h, 9–10 h, and > 10 h) were not statistically significant in this unadjusted model. However, after adjustment for age and sex (Model 2), the overall pattern of associations changed. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3)—which accounted for age, sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, BMI, SES, and physical activity—several sleep duration categories were significantly associated with lower CVD risk compared with the reference group (< 5 h). The strongest protective association was observed for 8–9 h of sleep (HR = 0.701, 95% CI: 0.53–0.91, p = 0.01). Significant risk reductions were also found for 7–8 h (HR = 0.764, 95% CI: 0.60–0.97, p = 0.03) and 6–7 h (HR = 0.772, 95% CI: 0.59–0.99, p = 0.04). The associations for 5–6 h (HR = 0.930, 95% CI: 0.70–1.22, p = 0.61), 9–10 h (HR = 0.755, 95% CI: 0.54–1.05, p = 0.10), and > 10 h (HR = 0.816, 95% CI: 0.54–1.22, p = 0.32) were not statistically significant. A non-significant p-value (p > 0.05) found for the global test and for each variable.

Unlike previous studies that used the typical midpoint sleep duration as a reference, this study used the shortest sleep category (< 5 h) as the baseline. This choice allowed clearer visualization of the relationship between increasing sleep duration and CVD risk, particularly between the 6–9 h range. To determine the sleep duration cut-off associated with the lowest CVD risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were fitted using a Cox model with sleep duration as a continuous variable. Comparisons of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values for spline models with different degrees of freedom indicated that the model with four knots provided the best fit. Based on Model 3 (fully adjusted), the optimal cut-off point was located within the 8–9 h sleep category, corresponding to a total daily sleep duration of 8 h and 38 min. As shown in Figs. 1 and 2, the risk of CVD incidence among individuals with shorter total daily sleep durations was higher than those with longer total daily sleep duration.

To further confirm the U-shaped pattern, a secondary analysis using the 7–8-hour category as the reference demonstrated increased CVD risk at both extremes of sleep duration. Compared with 7–8 h, hazard ratios were significantly higher for < 5 h (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.03–1.66, p = 0.03) and > 10 h (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.01–1.60, p = 0.04).Gradual increases in risk were observed for other sub-optimal durations (5–6 h: HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.95–1.55, p = 0.12; 6–7 h: HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.81–1.26, p = 0.92; 8–9 h: HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.73–1.16, p = 0.47; and 9–10 h: HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.79–1.49, p = 0.62).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, the protective effect of adequate sleep duration (6–9 h) was more pronounced than that of longer sleep durations (>9 h), although both were associated with a lower risk than very short sleep (< 5 h). These results are consistent with the findings of Amagai et al., who investigated sleep duration and the incidence of cardiovascular events in a Japanese population, where the minimum incidence risk was also observed in 8–9 h of sleep [27]. Similarly, In the MORGEN study, a prospective cohort conducted in the Netherlands, after adjustment for lifestyle confounders, short sleep duration was associated with with increased CVD incidence, whereas no significant association was observed for sleep durations exceeding eight hours [28]. Furthermore, a 2017 meta-analysis reported that both short and long sleep durations were associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events, with the lowest risk at 7 h of sleep per day [3].

consistent with these studies, our findings demonstrate that the reduction in CVD risk associated with adequate sleep duration (6–9 h) was stronger than the reduction associated with longer sleep (>9 h) when compared to the reference group of very short sleepers (< 5 h). The results also indicated that shorter sleep duration was associated with a higher prevalence of CVD, whereas extended sleep duration did not significantly affect disease prevalence. The lowest prevalence of myocardial infraction (MI) was observed in the range of 8–9 h [15].

Two meta-analyses examining the association between sleep duration and health outcomes found that sleeping less than six hours was associated with significantly increased risk of mortality and adverse outcomes such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, and obesity. Together, these findings support the “U-shaped” relationship between sleep duration and multiple adverse health outcomes, although these prior studies did not directly test the curve shape statistically [4, 5].

A large study involving 116,632 participants investigated the association between sleep duration and CVD using spline curves [8]. Their findings identified a J-shaped relationship, where shorter sleep duration had a weaker effect on CVD risk than longer sleep. Although our sleep duration categories were similar, we used narrower one-hour intervals within the 6–8-hour range (commonly considered the “normal” range) to pinpoint the exact interval associated with the lowest CVD risk. This approach resulted in a slightly different optimal duration (8.4 h) compared with the previous findings. The discrepancy may be attributable to greater heterogeneity in the earlier study, which included participants from multiple races and ethnicities.

Several factors may contribute to inconsistencies across studies, especially regarding short sleep duration. First, Differences in whether total or nighttime sleep was assessed could influence observed associations; some studies included daytime naps in total sleep duration, whereas others did not. Second, the definitions of “cardiovascular events” varied across studies, resulted in differences in outcomes. Third, inconsistencies in how sleep duration categories were defined (e.g., the thresholds used for short and long sleep) could also account for variation in results.

Furthermore, many CVD risk factors vary by race and ethnicity. Epidemiological evidence suggests that racial and ethnic differences may exist in the association between sleep duration and CVD, as well as in other CVD-related risk factors. In addition, genetic variation has been linked to differences in sleep duration and its health effects [29].

A cross-sectional study shown a link between short sleep duration and low vitamin D levels, and vitamin D deficiency is itself associated with an increased CVD risk [30]. This raises the possibility that, in geographically and racially distinct regions such as the Middle East (including Iran), the combined effects of vitamin D deficiency and short sleep duration in the Iranian population could have an even greater impact on CVD risk [31].

Supporting this hypothesis, a cross-sectional study conducted among participants of the present cohort found a positive association between sleeping less than 5 h and hypertension risk, as well as a negative association between sleeping more than 9 h and hypertension risk in individuals younger than 60 years [32].

Therefore, it can be concluded that one reason for the inconsistency in reported results may be attributed to genetic and ethnic variability, which influences the cut-off points of sleep duration associated with health outcomes. In our population, short sleep duration appears to increase both the risk CVD and incidence of CVD [33].

The results of both categorical Cox regression and restricted cubic spline analyses complement each other, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the non-linear relationship between sleep and CVD risk. The categorical model, which used the high-risk group of very short sleepers (< 5 h) as the reference, demonstrated a significant reduction in risk (HR < 1) for sleep durations between 6 and 9 h. This effectively captures the descending limb of the U-shaped curve. The spline analysis, which treated sleep duration as a continuous variable, identified the precise point of lowest risk at 8.38 h and stablished this duration as the optimal reference (HR = 1.0).

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, sleep duration was self-reported using questionnaires rather than measured objectively. While polysomnography provides accurate sleep assessment, its use in large population-based studies is impractical due to cost and logistical constraints. Second, individual sleep duration may vary over time for various reasons and may not remain constant throughout the study period. Third, sleep disorders such as sleep apnea and sleep quality, which can influence cardiovascular outcomes, were not assessed. fourth, the study could not account for additional potential confounders, including alcohol consumption, clinical hyperlipidemia, use of sleep medications, employment status, and shift work. Finally, we did not analyze nighttime and daytime sleep seperately. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the identified optimal total sleep duration of 8.38 h represents primarily nocturnal sleep or a combination of nocturnal sleep and daytime naps.

Conclusion

In this population-based study from an Iranian cohort, the association between total daily sleep duration and CVD risk of CVD followed a U-shaped pattern. The optimal sleep duration associated with the lowest CVD risk was 8.38 h, whereas sleeping less than 5 h was linked to the highest risk. These findings suggest that the internationally recommended sleep duration of approximately 8.4 h appears to offer the most favorable balance for cardiovascular health.
