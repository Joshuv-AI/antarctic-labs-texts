A parametrized ♢ \diamondsuit for the Laver property and nontrivial automorphisms of 𝒫 ⁡ ( ω ) / Fin \mathcal{P}(\omega)/\mathrm{Fin} Thanks: The first author is supported in part by NSF grant DMS-2154229
Will Brian, Alan Dow

Abstract
. We introduce a new parametrized diamond principle denoted ♢ ⁡ ( 𝖫𝖯 ) \diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP}) . This principle is akin to the parametrized diamonds of Moore, Hrušák, and Džamonja, each of which corresponds to some cardinal invariant of the continuum, and gives a ♢ \diamondsuit -like guessing principle implying the corresponding invariant is ℵ 1 \aleph_{1} . Our principle ♢ ⁡ ( 𝖫𝖯 ) \diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP}) is a ♢ \diamondsuit -like guessing principle implying the Laver property holds over a given inner model, such as the ground model in a forcing extension. We show ♢ ⁡ ( 𝖫𝖯 ) \diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP}) holds in many familiar models of 𝖹𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{ZFC} obtained by forcing, namely those obtained from a model of 𝖢𝖧 \mathsf{CH} by a length- ω 2 \omega_{2} countable support iteration of proper Borel posets with the Laver property. This is true for essentially the same reason that the usual parametrized diamonds hold in similarly described forcing extensions where their corresponding cardinal invariant is ℵ 1 \aleph_{1} . We also prove that if ♢ ⁡ ( 𝖫𝖯 ) \diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP}) holds over an inner model of 𝖢𝖧 \mathsf{CH} then there are nontrivial automorphisms of 𝒫 ⁡ ( ω ) / Fin \mathcal{P}(\omega)/\mathrm{Fin} ; in fact we get particularly nice automorphisms extending nontrivial involutions built around P P -points in the ground model. Additionally, we show that, like the Sacks model, all automorphisms of 𝒫 ⁡ ( ω ) / Fin \mathcal{P}(\omega)/\mathrm{Fin} are somewhere trivial in the Mathias model. This puts a limitation on the kinds of automorphisms obtainable from ♢ ⁡ ( 𝖫𝖯 ) \diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP}) .



1.
Introduction

An automorphism of the Boolean algebra
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is called
trivial
if it is induced by a function on
\omega
.
Walter Rudin proved 70 years ago in
[
18
]
that
\mathsf{CH}
implies there are a total of
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. As there are only
\mathfrak{c}
functions
\omega\to\omega
, hence only
\mathfrak{c}
trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, this means
\mathsf{CH}
implies there are nontrivial automorphisms.

Years later, Shelah proved in
[
19
, Chapter 4]
that it is consistent with
\mathsf{ZFC}
that all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are trivial.
Building on Shelah’s work, Shelah and Steprāns showed in
[
21
]
that
\mathsf{PFA}
implies all automorphisms are trivial. Veličković showed in
[
27
]
that
\mathsf{OCA+MA}
suffices, and that it is consistent with
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}
to have nontrivial automorphisms. (Here
\mathsf{OCA}
denotes Todorčević’s Open Coloring Axiom, defined in
[
25
]
, sometimes denoted
\mathsf{OCA_{T}}
or
\mathsf{OGA}
.)
Building on work of Moore in
[
16
]
, De Bondt, Farah, and Vignati showed recently in
[
2
]
that
\mathsf{OCA}
alone implies all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are trivial.
Further models in which all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are trivial, models not satisfying
\mathsf{OCA}
, have also been constructed. These include models with
\mathfrak{c}>\aleph_{2}
in
[
7
]
or with
\mathfrak{d}=\aleph_{1}
in
[
11
]
. (Recall that
\mathsf{OCA}
implies
\mathfrak{d}=\mathfrak{c}=\aleph_{2}
).

On the other hand, Veličković outlines an argument in
[
26
]
, which he attributes to Baumgartner, showing that the existence of a
P
-point with character
\aleph_{1}
implies the existence of a nontrivial automorphism (see also the second half of
[
22
]
). In particular, the existence of nontrivial automorphisms is consistent with
\neg
\mathsf{CH}
.
Furthermore, Shelah and Steprāns prove in
[
22
]
that there are nontrivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
in the
\aleph_{2}
-Cohen model. In fact, their argument shows that after forcing over a model of
\mathsf{CH}
to add
\aleph_{2}
Cohen reals, every automorphism from the ground model extends to an automorphism in the forcing extension.
The same authors also show in
[
24
]
that there are nontrivial automorphisms if certain cardinal invariants related to
\mathfrak{d}
are equal to
\aleph_{1}
.

All this raises a few questions:

\circ

What familiar set-theoretic axioms imply that all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are trivial?

\circ

Or, to ask essentially the same question in a different way, in which of the familiar models of
\mathsf{ZFC}
are all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
trivial?

\circ

Furthermore, what kinds of nontrivial automorphisms can be constructed from familiar axioms? What kinds of nontrivial automorphisms exist in familiar models?

There are several ways to interpret the third question. Here we focus on basic topological properties of automorphisms (are they of finite order? somewhere trivial? what does the set of fixed points look like?)
Another interesting interpretation comes from considering properties defined by the action of an automorphism on the trivial automorphisms (see
[
3
,
4
]
).

The objective of this paper is to contribute to the first and third of these questions.
It is worth pointing out from the start that, strictly speaking, this paper does not contribute any new answers to the second question.
All the nontrivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
constructed here are constructed from a new combinatorial principle introduced below, denoted
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
.
We prove that
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
holds in models obtained from models of
\mathsf{CH}
by a length-
\omega_{2}
countable support iteration of proper Borel posets with the Laver property (e.g., the Laver, Mathias, Miller, Sacks, and Silver models).
These are currently the only known nontrivial models in which
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
holds.
Shelah and Steprāns proved already in
[
24
]
that there are nontrivial automorphisms in such models. (They define some new cardinal characteristics, which are
\aleph_{1}
in these models, and they prove that when these cardinals are
\aleph_{1}
then there are nontrivial automorphisms.) The main purposes of the paper are:

\circ

The introduction of the parametrized
\diamondsuit
principle
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
, which is interesting in its own right. Aside from defining the principle and using it to construct nontrivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, we also introduce two weaker forms of
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
and articulate several open questions concerning these three new principles.

\circ

The simplicity of the automorphisms built from
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
.
We mentioned earlier the Baumgartner-Veličković construction that builds a nontrivial automorphism from a
P
-point of character
\aleph_{1}
.
Their automorphisms are involutions that fix precisely one ultrafilter (the
P
-point), and are trivial on every set in the dual ideal of that ultrafilter.
Our construction generalizes this one, using
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
to build a nontrivial involution that fixes a nowhere dense
P
-filter of character
\aleph_{1}
, and is trivial on every set in the dual ideal of that filter.

In addition to these two main objectives, in the final section of the paper we show that all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are somewhere trivial in the Mathias model.
The same is already known for the Sacks model (see
[
23
]
).
Because
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
holds in the Mathias and Sacks models, this puts a limitation on the kinds of automorphisms that can be obtained from
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
: one cannot build a nowhere trivial automorphism from
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
.
While of course this fact can be deduced from the Sacks model alone, we have included a proof of the analogous fact in the Mathias model because it seems a worthwhile addition to the third question above.

2.
Bowties of functions

The aim of this section is to present a general topological structure from which one can build a nontrivial automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. The argument directly generalizes the Baumgartner-Veličković construction in
[
26
]
building an automorphism from a
P
-point of character
\aleph_{1}
. But the version presented here applies more broadly: we show that such structures (and their accompanying automorphisms) follow from
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
, which holds in the Laver model (where there are no
P
-points of character
\aleph_{1}
) and the Silver model (where there are no
P
-points at all
[
5
]
).

The power set of
\omega
,
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
, when ordered by inclusion, is a complete Boolean algebra.
The Boolean algebra
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}

is the quotient of this Boolean algebra by the ideal of finite sets.
For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we write
A=^{*}B
to mean that
A
and
B
differ by finitely many elements, i.e.
\left\lvert(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)\right\rvert<\aleph_{0}
.
The members of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are equivalence classes of the
=^{*}
relation, and we denote the equivalence class of
A\subseteq\omega
by
[A]_{\text{Fin}}
.
The Boolean-algebraic relation
[A]_{\text{Fin}}\leq[B]_{\text{Fin}}
is denoted on the level of representatives by writing
A\subseteq^{*}B
: that is,
A\subseteq^{*}B
means
[A]_{\text{Fin}}\leq[B]_{\text{Fin}}
, or (equivalently)
B
contains all but finitely many members of
A
.

The Stone space of
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
is
\beta\omega
, the Čech-Stone compactification of the countable discrete space
\omega
. The Stone space of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is
\omega^{*}=\beta\omega\setminus\omega
.

An
almost permutation
on
\omega
is a bijection between cofinite subsets of
\omega
.
Every permutation of
\omega
is an almost permutation, but not every almost permutation is a permutation or even the restriction of one (e.g. consider the successor function
n\mapsto n+1
).
Every almost permutation
f
of
\omega
induces an automorphism
\alpha_{f}
of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, defined by the formula

\alpha_{f}\big([A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\big)\,=\,\big[f[A]\big]_{\mathrm{Fin}}.

An automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
that is induced in this way by an almost permutation of
\omega
is called
trivial
.
Note that if
f
is not an almost permutation of
\omega
then the formula above does not define an automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
(though it still defines a homomorphism).
A
trivial autohomeomorphism
of
\omega^{*}
means the Stone dual of a trivial automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. Specifically, if
f
is an almost permutation of
\omega
, it induces the trivial autohomeomorphism
f^{*}
defined by taking

f^{*}(u)
to be the ultrafilter generated by
\left\{f^{-1}[A]\colon A\in u\right\}
.

If
A,B
are infinite subsets of
\omega
, then
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(A)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
and
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(B)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are both isomorphic to
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
As with automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, an isomorphism
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(A)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}\to\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(B)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is
trivial
if it is induced by the action of an almost bijection
A\to B
.
An automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is
trivial on
A\subseteq\omega
if its restriction to
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(A)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is trivial in this sense.
An automorphism is
somewhere trivial
if it is trivial on some infinite
A\subseteq\omega
.

Extending the
=^{*}
relation from subsets of
\omega
to functions,

f=^{*}g
means
\mathrm{dom}(f)=^{*}\mathrm{dom}(g)=^{*}\left\{x\in\mathrm{dom}(f)\cap\mathrm{dom}(g)\colon f(x)=g(x)\right\}
.
Similarly, if
f
and
g
are functions into
\omega
then

f\leq g
means
\mathrm{dom}(f)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(g)
and
f(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
, and

f\leq^{*}g
means
\mathrm{dom}(f)\subseteq^{*}\mathrm{dom}(g)
and
f(x)\leq g(x)
for all but finitely many
x\in\mathrm{dom}(f)\cap\mathrm{dom}(g)
.

We now introduce the main object of this section, bowties of functions. In addition to generalizing the Baumgartner-Veličković argument mentioned above, these bowties are related to the bowtie points studied by the second author and Shelah in
[
8
]
(also known as tie-points or butterfly points). We show in this section that the existence of a bowtie of functions implies there is a (particularly nice) nontrivial automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. In the next section we show that
\diamondsuit(\mathsf{LP})
implies the existence of a bowtie of functions.

For each
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, let
D_{\alpha}\subseteq\omega
and let
f_{\alpha}
be a permutation
D_{\alpha}\to D_{\alpha}
with no fixed points.
The sequence
\left\langle f_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\omega_{1}\right\rangle
is a
bowtie of functions
if

\circ

D_{\alpha}\subseteq^{*}D_{\beta}
and
D_{\beta}\setminus D_{\alpha}
is infinite whenever
\alpha<\beta<\omega_{1}
,

\circ

f_{\alpha}=^{*}f_{\beta}\!\restriction\!D_{\alpha}
whenever
\alpha\leq\beta<\omega_{1}
,

\circ

for every
A\subseteq\omega
there is some
\delta_{A}<\omega_{1}
such that if
\delta_{A}\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}
then
(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A
and

(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\setminus A

are almost
f_{\alpha}
-invariant sets, by which we mean that

f_{\alpha}[(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A]=^{*}(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A

and

f_{\alpha}[(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\setminus A]=^{*}(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\setminus A
.

If furthermore each
f_{\alpha}
is an involution (meaning
f_{\alpha}^{2}=\mathrm{id}
), then
\left\langle f_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\omega_{1}\right\rangle
is a
bowtie of involutions
.

Theorem 2.1
.

If there is a bowtie of functions then there is a nontrivial automorphism
F
of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
Furthermore, there is a closed
P
-set in
\omega^{*}
with character
\aleph_{1}
, namely
K=\bigcap_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}(\omega\setminus D_{\alpha})^{*}
, such that
F
is trivial on
A
whenever
A^{*}
does not meet the boundary of
K
.
Moreover, if the bowtie of functions is a bowtie of involutions, then
F
is an involution of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.

Proof.

Fix a bowtie of functions
\left\langle f_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\omega_{1}\right\rangle
.
For each
A\subseteq\omega
, let
\delta_{A}
denote the least ordinal having the property in the definition of “bowtie of functions” above.
Define
F:\mathcal{P}(\omega)\to\mathcal{P}(\omega)
by

F(A)\,=\,f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}}).

Note that if
A=^{*}B
then
\delta_{A}=\delta_{B}
. Consequently,
F
induces a mapping on
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, which we denote
\tilde{F}
, namely

\tilde{F}\big([A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\big)\,=\,\big[F[A]\big]_{\mathrm{Fin}}.

We aim to show that this mapping is a nontrivial automorphism with the properties described in the theorem.

Fix
A\subseteq\omega
, and fix
\delta
with
\delta_{A}\leq\delta<\omega_{1}
.
Because
\delta_{A}\leq\delta
we have
f_{\delta_{A}}=^{*}f_{\delta}\!\restriction\!D_{\delta_{A}}
, hence
f_{\delta}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]=^{*}f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]
. Furthermore, our choice of
\delta_{A}
ensures that
f_{\delta}\big[(D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A\big]=^{*}(D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A
. Consequently,

\displaystyle F(A)

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup\big((D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A\big)\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}\big[(A\cap D_{\delta_{A}})\cup\big((D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\cap A\big)\big]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta}[A\cap D_{\delta}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta}).

In other words, removing the subscripted
A
’s in the definition of
F(A)
only changes the definition modulo
\mathrm{Fin}
.

Consider some
A,B\subseteq\omega
, and let
\delta=\max\{\delta_{A},\delta_{B},\delta_{A\cup B}\}
. Then

\displaystyle F(A)\cup F(B)

\displaystyle\,=\,\big(f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\big)\cup\big(f_{\delta_{B}}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big)

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,\big(f_{\delta}[A\cap D_{\delta}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta})\big)\cup\big(f_{\delta}[B\cap D_{\delta}]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta})\big)

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta}[(A\cup B)\cap D_{\delta}]\cup\big((A\cup B)\setminus D_{\delta}\big)

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta_{A\cup B}}[(A\cup B)\cap D_{\delta_{A\cup B}}]\cup\big((A\cup B)\setminus D_{\delta_{A\cup B}}\big)

\displaystyle\,=\,F(A\cup B).

A similar computation shows that
F(A\cap B)=^{*}F(A)\cap F(B)
, and it follows from the definition of
F
that
F(\omega)=\omega
and
F(\emptyset)=\emptyset
.
This shows that
\tilde{F}
is a homomorphism
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}\to\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.

To show that this homomorphism is an automorphism, it remains to show
\tilde{F}
is a bijection.
It follows straightaway from the definition of
F
that if
A
is infinite then
F(A)
is also infinite, hence the kernel of
\tilde{F}
is
\{[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\}
, which implies
\tilde{F}
is injective.

For surjectivity, fix
B\subseteq\omega
, and let
A=f_{\delta_{B}}^{-1}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})
. If
\delta
is such that
\max\{\delta_{A},\delta_{B}\}\leq\delta<\omega_{1}
, then

\displaystyle F(A)

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}[A\cap D_{\delta}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta}\big[\big(h_{\delta_{B}}^{-1}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big)\cap D_{\delta}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cup\big(\big(f_{\delta_{B}}^{-1}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big)\setminus D_{\delta}\big)

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}\big[\big(f_{\delta_{B}}^{-1}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big)\cap D_{\delta}\big]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta}\big[\big(f_{\delta_{B}}^{-1}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\cup\big(B\cap(D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big)\big]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta}\big[\big(f_{\delta_{B}}^{-1}[B\cap D_{\delta_{B}}]\big]\cup f_{\delta}\big[B\cap(D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big]\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=^{*}\,(B\cap D_{\delta_{B}})\cup\big(B\cap(D_{\delta}\setminus D_{\delta_{B}})\big)\cup(B\setminus D_{\delta})

\displaystyle\,=\,B.

As
B\subseteq\omega
was arbitrary, this shows
\tilde{F}
is surjective, and completes the proof that
\tilde{F}
is an automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.

It remains to show that
\tilde{F}
is not a trivial automorphism, and to prove the “furthermore” and “moreover” assertions of the theorem. To see that
F
is nontrivial, recall that Frolík proved in
[
12
]
that
if
F:\omega^{*}\to\omega^{*}
is a trivial autohomeomorphism of
\omega^{*}
, then
\mathrm{Fix}(F)=\left\{u\in\omega^{*}\colon F(u)=u\right\}
is clopen.

To apply Frolík’s result, we work temporarily in the topological category. Let
\bar{F}:\omega^{*}\to\omega^{*}
denote Stone dual of
\tilde{F}
, i.e., the function

\bar{F}(u)\,=\,\textstyle\bigcup\left\{\tilde{F}^{-1}\big([A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\big)\colon A\in u\right\}.

Let
K=\bigcap_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}(\omega\setminus D_{\alpha})^{*}
.
Suppose
u\in K
and let
A\in u
. Then

F(A)\,=\,f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}})\,\supseteq\,A\setminus D_{\delta_{A}}\in u.

Hence
F
maps sets in
u
to sets in
u
, which implies
\bar{F}(u)=u
.

Now suppose
u\notin K
. Then
D_{\alpha}\in u
for some
\alpha<\omega_{1}
. Using the three set lemma (see
[
10
]
) and the fact that
f_{\delta_{D_{\alpha}}}
has no fixed points,
D_{\alpha}
can be partitioned into three sets,
A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}
, such that
f_{\delta_{D_{\alpha}}}[A_{i}]\cap A_{i}=\emptyset
for each
i
. Suppose without loss of generality
A_{0}\in u
. If
\delta\geq\max\{\alpha,\delta_{D_{\alpha}},\delta_{A_{0}}\}
, then

F(A_{0})\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}[A_{0}\cap D_{\delta}]\cup(A_{0}\setminus D_{\delta})\,=^{*}\,f_{\delta}[A_{0}]

(because
A_{0}\subseteq D_{\alpha}\subseteq^{*}D_{\delta}
), so
F
maps a set in
u
to a set not in
u
. This shows
u
is not a fixed point of
\bar{F}
.

Therefore
K
is precisely the set of fixed point of
\bar{F}
. To see that
K
is not clopen, fix
C\subseteq\omega
such that
C^{*}\subseteq P
.
Then
D_{\alpha}\subseteq^{*}\omega\setminus C
, for all
\alpha
, and
if
\alpha<\beta<\omega_{1}
then
(C\cup D_{\beta})\setminus(C\cup D_{\alpha})
is infinite.
Hence
\left\langle\big(\omega\setminus(C\cup D_{\alpha})\big)^{*}\colon\alpha<\omega_{1}\right\rangle
is a decreasing sequence of nonempty clopen sets in
\omega^{*}
. By compactness,
K\setminus C^{*}=\bigcap_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}\big(\omega\setminus(C\cup D_{\alpha})\big)^{*}\neq\emptyset
. Consequently,
C^{*}\neq K
. As
C^{*}
was an arbitrary clopen subset of
K
, this shows that
K
is not clopen. Applying the aforementioned result of Frolík, it follows that
\bar{F}
is nontrivial (hence
F
is too).

To prove the “furthermore” part of the theorem, suppose that
A\subseteq\omega
and
A^{*}
does not meet the boundary of
K
. This implies there is a partition of
A
into two sets
B,C
such that
B^{*}\subseteq\mathrm{Int}(K)
and
C^{*}\cap K=\emptyset
.
The fact that
B^{*}\subseteq K
means
B\cap D_{\alpha}=^{*}\emptyset
for all
\alpha<\omega_{1}
. This implies
\delta_{B}=0
and

F(B)\,=\,f_{0}[B\cap D_{0}]\cup(B\setminus D_{0})\,=^{*}\,B.

Hence
F\!\restriction\!\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(B)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is the identity map, and in particular it is trivial.
As for
C
, first note that using a compactness argument as in the previous paragraph, the fact that
C^{*}\cap K=\emptyset
implies
C\subseteq^{*}D_{\delta_{0}}
for some particular
\delta_{0}<\omega_{1}
.
Increasing
\delta_{0}
if needed, we may (and do) assume
\delta_{0}\geq\delta_{C}
.
If
X\subseteq C
and
\delta_{0}\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}
, then

(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{0}})\cap X
and
(D_{\alpha}\setminus D_{\delta_{0}})\setminus X
are both finite, since
X\subseteq C\subseteq^{*}D_{\delta_{0}}
.
It follows that
\delta_{X}\leq\delta_{0}
.
Consequently, if
X\subseteq C

F(X)\,=\,f_{\delta_{0}}[X\cap D_{\delta_{0}}]\cup(X\setminus D_{\delta_{0}})\,=\,f_{\delta_{0}}[X\cap D_{\delta_{0}}].

is induced by

(or by
f_{\delta}\!\restriction\!C

); in particular this map is trivial.

Finally, to prove the “moreover” part of the theorem, suppose each

is an involution. Note that
\delta_{A}=\delta_{\omega\setminus A}
for every

\displaystyle F\big(F(A)\big)

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta_{\omega\setminus A}}\big[f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\big]\cup(A\setminus D_{\delta_{\omega\setminus A}})

\displaystyle\,=\,f_{\delta_{A}}\big[f_{\delta_{A}}[A\cap D_{\delta_{A}}]\big]\cup(A\setminus D_{A})

\displaystyle\,=\,(A\cap D_{\delta_{A}})\cup(A\setminus D_{A})\,=\,A.

Thus if each

is an involution, so is

.

\subseteq^{*}
-decreasing.

\circ

\displaystyle J^{0}_{\alpha}\cap I_{j}

\displaystyle=J_{\xi_{n+1}}\cap I_{j}\qquad\qquad\hskip-11.95013pt\text{ if }\ j\in\big[s_{\xi_{n}}(k_{n}),s_{\xi_{n+1}}(k_{n+1})\big),

For each

.

.

.

Now fix

4.
Automorphisms in the Mathias model

Shelah and Steprāns proved in
[
23
]
that all automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
in the Sacks model are somewhere trivial. In this section we prove the same for the Mathias model.

Let
\mathbb{M}
denote the Mathias forcing poset:

\mathbb{M}\,=\,\left\{(a,A)\colon a\in[\omega]^{<\omega},\,A\in[\omega]^{\omega},\text{ and }\max(a)<\min(A)\right\},

with the order defined by setting
(b,B)\leq(a,A)
if and only if
b\supseteq a
,
B\subseteq A
, and
b\subseteq a\cup A
.
The
Mathias model
refers to any model obtained by forcing with a length-
\omega_{2}
countable support iteration of
\mathbb{M}
over a model of
\mathsf{CH}
.
The main theorem of this section is:

Theorem 4.1
.

In the Mathias model, every automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is somewhere trivial.

We prove this theorem mostly by piecing together facts from the literature. To begin, recall that
a nonprincipal ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on
\omega
is
Ramsey
if for every infinite partition
\left\{A_{n}\colon n\in\omega\right\}
of
\omega
, either there is some
n
with
A_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
, or else there is some
S\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\left\lvert S\cap A_{n}\right\rvert\leq 1
for all
n
.
More generally, if
V
and
W
are two models of set theory with
V\subseteq W
, a filter
\mathcal{U}\in W
is
Ramsey over
V
if for every infinite partition
\left\{A_{n}\colon n\in\omega\right\}
of
\omega
with
\left\{A_{n}\colon n\in\omega\right\}\in V
, either there is some
n
with
A_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
, or else there is some
S\in\mathcal{U}\cap V
such that
\left\lvert S\cap A_{n}\right\rvert\leq 1
for all
n
. In other words,
\mathcal{U}
is Ramsey over
V
if
\mathcal{U}\cap V
satisfies the definition of a Ramsey ultrafilter within
V
(but we do not require
\mathcal{U}\cap V\in V
).

Suppose again that
V
and
W
are two models of set theory with
V\subseteq W
.
If
X\subseteq\omega
and
X\in W
, then

\left\{A\in V\colon X\subseteq^{*}A\subseteq\omega\right\}

is a filter, which we call the filter
induced
by
X
on
V
.
If this filter is Ramsey over
V
, then we say that
X

induces a Ramsey ultrafilter
on
V
.
Viewing things the other way around, note that an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}\in V
is induced by a set
X\in W
if and only if
X
is a pseudo-intersection for
\mathcal{U}
in
W
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
\omega
, then

\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{U})\,=\,\left\{(a,A)\in\mathbb{M}\colon A\in\mathcal{U}\right\}.

This is a variant of the Mathias forcing, sometimes referred to as the Mathias forcing “guided” by the ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
.
If
G
is an
\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{U})
-generic filter over
V
, then
S=\textstyle\bigcup\left\{a\colon(a,A)\in G\text{ for some }A\right\}

is a pseudo-intersection for
\mathcal{U}
in
V[G]
. In particular, if
\mathcal{U}
is Ramsey in
V
, then
\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{U})
adds a subset of
\omega
that induces a Ramsey ultrafilter on
V
, namely
\mathcal{U}
.

Recall that forcing with
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
adds a Ramsey ultrafilter on
\omega
. Specifically, if
G
is a
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
-generic filter over
V
, then

\mathcal{U}=\left\{A\subseteq\omega\colon[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in G\right\}

is a Ramsey ultrafilter on
\omega
in
V[G]
(i.e.,
G
itself is a Ramsey ultrafilter, modulo the natural transfer from
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
to
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
).
The following fact was proved by Mathias in
[
15
]
:

Lemma 4.2

(Mathias)
.

The poset
\mathbb{M}
is forcing equivalent to the
2
-step iteration
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}*\mathbb{M}(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
, where
\dot{\mathcal{U}}
is a name for the generic Ramsey ultrafilter added by forcing with
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, as described above.

In what follows, the
ultrafilter added by forcing with
\mathbb{M}
refers specifically to the Ramsey ultrafilter added by the first stage of this decomposition.
Mathias’ proof of this lemma shows that if
G
is an
\mathbb{M}
-generic filter over
V
, then the ultrafilter added by forcing with
\mathbb{M}
is definable in
V[G]
as

\mathcal{U}\,=\,\left\{B\subseteq\omega\colon\text{there is some }(a,A)\in G\text{ with }A\subseteq^{*}B\right\}.

Equivalently, this is the filter
\mathcal{U}_{X}
induced on
V
by the
\mathbb{M}
-generic real

X\,=\,\textstyle\bigcup\left\{a\in[\omega]^{<\omega}\colon\text{there is some $A$ with }(a,A)\in G\right\}.

In particular, the ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
added by forcing with
\mathbb{M}
has a pseudo-intersection in

, and

is induced on

by this pseudo-intersection.

Recall that two filters

(Shelah-Spinas)
.

Let

For the remainder of this section, we abuse notation slightly by letting
F(A)

\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}

under

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
F
is an automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, and let
\dot{\mathcal{U}}
be the canonical name for the generic ultrafilter added by forcing with
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. Then
\Vdash_{\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}}F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
is Ramsey.

Proof.

This proof is essentially identical to the usual proof that
\Vdash_{\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}}\dot{\mathcal{U}}
is Ramsey.
Let
G
be
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
-generic over
V
, and let
\mathcal{U}=\left\{A\subseteq\omega\colon[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in G\right\}
denote the generic ultrafilter added by forcing with
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
Because
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is a countably closed forcing, it adds no new reals, and in particular it adds no new partitions of
\omega
.

Working in the ground model, let
\left\{A_{n}\colon n\in\omega\right\}
be an infinite partition of
\omega
and fix a condition
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
If there is some

[A\cap F^{-1}(A_{n})]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\leq[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
and
[A\cap F^{-1}(A_{n})]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\Vdash A_{n}\in F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
.
On the other hand, if
F(A)\cap A_{n}
is finite for all
n
, then there is an infinite

such that
|S\cap A_{n}|\leq 1
for all

; then
[F^{-1}(S)]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\leq[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
and
[F^{-1}(S)]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\Vdash S\in F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
.
Either way, there is an extension of

forcing “either there is some

A_{n}\in F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
, or else there is some

such that
\left\lvert S\cap A_{n}\right\rvert\leq 1
for all

.”

Hence, in the extension, either there is some
n
with
A_{n}\in F(\mathcal{U})
, or else there is some
S\in F(\mathcal{U})
such that
\left\lvert S\cap A_{n}\right\rvert\leq 1
for all

. As this is true for an arbitrary partition

in

, and because every partition of
\omega
in

is already in

, this shows

is Ramsey in

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose

is an automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, and

, and

.
If

Assuming

is not induced by

, there is some infinite

If

is infinite, then let

.
If

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
F
is a nowhere trivial automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, and let
\dot{\mathcal{U}}
be a name for the generic ultrafilter added by forcing with
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. Then
\Vdash_{\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}}

\,\dot{\mathcal{U}}
and
F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
are not RK-equivalent.

Proof.

Let
G
be
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
-generic over
V
, and let
\mathcal{U}=\left\{A\subseteq\omega\colon[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in G\right\}
denote the generic ultrafilter added by forcing with
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
Because
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is a countably closed forcing, it adds no new reals, and in particular it adds no new bijections
\omega\to\omega
, so if
h\in V[G]
is a bijection
\omega\to\omega
then
h\in V
.
Thus, in order to show
\mathcal{U}
and
F(\mathcal{U})
are not RK-equivalent in
V[G]
, it suffices to show they are not RK-equivalent via a bijection
h\in V
.

Working in the ground model, fix a bijection
h:\omega\to\omega
and fix a condition
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
As we are assuming
F
is nowhere trivial,
F\!\restriction\!\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(A)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is not induced by
h\!\restriction\!A
.
By Lemma
4.6
, there is an infinite
B\subseteq A
such that
F(B)\cap h[B]
is finite. So
[B]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\leq[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, and

[B]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\,\Vdash\,B\in\dot{\mathcal{U}},\,F(B)\in F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})\text{, and consequently, }h[B]\notin F(\dot{\mathcal{U}}).

Thus an arbitrary condition
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(\omega)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
has an extension forcing
\dot{\mathcal{U}}
and
F(\dot{\mathcal{U}})
are not RK-equivalent via
h
.

Hence
\mathcal{U}
and
F(\mathcal{U})
are not equivalent via
h
in
V[G]
. As
h
was arbitrary bijection in
V
, and every bijection in
V[G]
is already in
V
, this shows
\mathcal{U}
and
F(\mathcal{U})
are not RK-equivalent in
V[G]
.
∎
