Non-SOS Positivstellensätze for semi-algebraic sets defined by polynomial matrix inequalities Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Feng Guo

Abstract
This paper establishes new Positivstellensätze for polynomials that are positive on sets defined by polynomial matrix inequalities (PMIs). We extend the classical Handelman and Krivine–Stengle theorems from the scalar inequality setting to the matrix context, deriving explicit certificate forms that do not rely on sums-of-squares (SOS). Specifically, we show that under certain conditions, any polynomial positive on a PMI-defined semialgebraic set admits a representation using Kronecker powers of the defining matrix (or its dilated form) with positive semidefinite coefficient matrices. Under correlative sparsity pattern, we further prove more efficient, sparse representations that significantly reduce computational complexity. By applying these results to polynomial optimization with PMI constraints, we construct a hierarchy of semidefinite programming relaxations whose size depends only on the dimension of the constraint matrix, and not on the number of variables. Consequently, our relaxations may remain computationally feasible for problems with large number of variables and low-dimensional matrix constraints, offering a practical alternative where the traditional SOS-based relaxations become intractable.

Introduction

Positivstellensätze are fundamental results in real algebraic geometry,
offering constructive certificates for the positivity of a polynomial over a semialgebraic set

[
5
,
26
,

,

]
.
These powerful results have widespread applications in areas like optimization, algebraic geometry, control theory, and more where polynomial positivity is a key concern
[
4
,
12
,

,

,

]
.

Typically, Positivstellensätze characterize a polynomial

f(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]:={\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that is positive on a basic semialgebraic set

\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid g_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 0,j=1,\ldots,s\},

(1)

where
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
.
Many such results achieve this by expressing the polynomial using
sums of squares (SOS) structures. For instance,
under the assumption that the set (
1
) is compact,
Schmüdgen
[
40
]
showed that any polynomial
f
positive on the set (
1
)
belongs to the preordering generated by
g_{j}
’s–that is, it can be written as an SOS-weighted
combination of cross-products of
g_{j}
’s.
Putinar’s Positivstellensatz
[
33
]
provides an alternative representation that avoids
the need for cross-products of
g_{j}
’s under the Archimedean condition (slightly stronger than compactness).
Building on Putinar’s Positivstellensatz, Lasserre
[
20
,
23
,
24
]

introduced the well-known moment-SOS hierarchy of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations
for polynomial optimization.
Reznick
[
36
]
proved that after be multiplying by some power of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
, any
positive definite form is a sum of even
powers of linear forms. Putinar and Vasilescu
[
34
]
extended Reznick’s result to the constrained case where
f
and
g_{j}
’s are homogeneous polynomial of even degree.

There also exist several non-SOS structured Positivstellensätze.
Pólya
[
31
]
demonstrated that if
f
is homogeneous and positive on

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, then
multiplying
f
by some power of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
, one obtains a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients.
Dickinson and Povh
[
8
]
generalized Pólya’s Positivstellensatz for homogeneous polynomials
being positive on the intersection of

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and the set (
1
), where
g_{j}
’s are assumed to be also homogeneous.
By replacing SOS polynomials by sums of non-negative circuit polynomials
and sums of arithmetic-geometric mean exponential functions,
non-SOS Schmüdgen-type and Putinar-type Positivstellensätze have been derived in

[
6
,
10
,
37
]
.
When the set (
1
) is a compact polyhedron with non-empty interior, Handelman’s Positivstellensatz

[
11
]
states
that any polynomial
f
positive on the set (
1
) can be expressed as

f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}}\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots g_{s}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\alpha_{s}},

(2)

for some (finitely many) nonnegative scalars
\{\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}
. In general,
assuming that
0\leq g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq 1
on the set (
1
) for every

\{1,g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
generates
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
, Krivine-Stengle’s Positivstellensatz

[
19
,
41
]
(see also
[
43
]
)
asserts that a positive polynomial
f
on the set (
1
)
admits a representation of the form

f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\alpha_{1}}(1-g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}))^{\beta_{1}}\cdots g_{s}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\alpha_{s}}(1-g_{s}({\boldsymbol{x}}))^{\beta_{s}},

(3)

for some (finitely many) nonnegative scalars
\{\delta_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\}

As an alternative to the moment-SOS relaxations for polynomial optimization,
Lasserre
[
21
,
22
]
proposed the linear programming (LP) relaxations
by leveraging the representations (
2
) and (

Most Positivstellensätze could be generalized to the matrix setting.
A matrix version of Handelman’s Positivstellensatz was proposed in

Building on this, Dinh et al.

generalized the classical Schmüdgen,
Putinar-Vasilescu, and Dickinson–Povh Positivstellensätze to the polynomial matrix setting.
Given the projections of two PMI-defined semialgebraic sets, Klep and Nie
[

a matrix Positivstellensatz with lifting polynomials to determine whether one is contained in the other.
These generalizations have a wide range of applications, particularly in areas
such as optimal control, systems theory

(iii) Assume that

3
Non-SOS Positivstellensätze for PMI constraints

In this section, we characterize polynomials positive on the PMI-defined set
\mathbf{K}

by constructing a novel polynomial cone using Kronecker powers of the defining matrix
G({\boldsymbol{x}})

(or its dilated form) with PSD coefficient matrices.
Our results extend the classical Positivstellensätze of Handelman (
2
)
and Krivine-Stengle (
3
) from scalar polynomial inequalities to
the matrix-valued setting.
Our proof strategy, inspired by
[
43
]
, involves adapting the scalar framework to
the matrix setting and integrating our novel cone construction.

3.1
The linear case

In this part, we assume that the defining matrix
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
of
\mathbf{K}
is linear in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
i.e., there exist matrices
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}
, such that

\mathbf{K}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \Big|\ G({\boldsymbol{x}})=A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}x_{i}\succeq 0\right\}.

(8)

Recall the polar of the convex set
\mathbf{K}
defined as follows

\mathbf{K}^{\circ}=\{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq 1,\ \forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbf{K}\}.

Proposition 4
.

Assume that
\mathbf{0}
is an interior of
\mathbf{K}
, then

\mathbf{K}^{\circ}=\{-(\langle U,A_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle U,A_{n}\rangle)\mid U\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}_{+},\ \langle U,A_{0}\rangle\leq 1\}.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{0}
is an interior of
\mathbf{K}
, we have
A_{0}\succeq 0
and the affine hull of
\mathbf{K}
is

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then, the conclusion follows from
[
35
, Theorem 2]
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
U\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}
,
denote by
U^{\otimes k}
the
k
-times Kronecker product of
U
, i.e.,

U^{\otimes k}=\underbrace{U\otimes U\otimes\cdots\otimes U}_{k\ \text{times}}.

Assumption 5
.

The set
\mathbf{K}
is compact and has nonempty interior.

Proposition 6
.

Suppose that Assumption
5
holds and
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is an interior of
\mathbf{K}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\beta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}x_{i}
,
\beta_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(i)

If
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
, then there exist
U\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}_{+}
and
0\leq\gamma\leq{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{u}})
such that

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\langle U,G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle+\gamma
;

(ii)

There exists
U\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}_{++}
such that
1=\langle U,G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle
;

(iii)

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
U\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{k}}_{++}
such that

1=\left\langle U,G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle
;

(iv)

For each
i\in[n]
, there exists
U_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}
such that
x_{i}=\langle U_{i},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle
.

Proof.

(i) Without loss of generality, we may assume that
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbf{K}
, we have
\beta_{0}\geq 0
. If
\beta_{0}=0
, then
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
would be identically
zero since
\mathbf{0}
is an interior of
\mathbf{K}
, and there would be

\beta_{0}>0

4
, there exists

such that

\beta_{i}/\beta_{0}=\langle U^{\prime},A_{i}\rangle
,

, and

\langle U^{\prime},A_{0}\rangle\leq 1
. Thus,

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\beta_{0}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle U^{\prime},A_{i}\rangle x_{i}\right)=\langle\beta_{0}U^{\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle+\beta_{0}(1-\langle U^{\prime},A_{0}\rangle).

The conclusion follows by letting
U=\beta_{0}U^{\prime}
and
\gamma=\beta_{0}(1-\langle U^{\prime},A_{0}\rangle)
.

(ii) Since
\mathbf{K}
is compact, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

1-\varepsilon\langle I_{q},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle\geq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
. By (i), there

U^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}_{+}
and
0\leq\gamma\leq 1-\varepsilon\langle I_{q},G({\boldsymbol{u}})\rangle
such that

1-\varepsilon\langle I_{q},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle=\langle U^{\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle+\gamma.

Then, it holds
1-\gamma=\langle\varepsilon I_{q}+U^{\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is an interior of
\mathbf{K}
, we must have
\langle I_{q},G({\boldsymbol{u}})\rangle>0
and
thus
\gamma<1
. Then, the conclusion follows by letting
U=(\varepsilon I_{q}+U^{\prime})/(1-\gamma)
.

(iii) By (ii), there exists
U^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}_{++}
such that
1=\langle U^{\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

, then

and

\displaystyle\left\langle U,G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}\left(U^{\prime\otimes k}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}\left(\left(U^{\prime\otimes k-1}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k-1}\right)\otimes(U^{\prime}G({\boldsymbol{x}}))\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}\left((U^{\prime\otimes k-1}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k-1}\right)\mbox{tr}\left(U^{\prime}G({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle U^{\prime\otimes k-1},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k-1}\right\rangle\left\langle U^{\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle U^{\prime\otimes k-1},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k-1}\right\rangle

Then, the conclusion follows by induction on

(iv) Fix an
i\in[n]
. Since
\mathbf{K}
is compact, there exists positive

M_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
x_{i}\geq-M_{i}
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbf{K}
. Then, by (i),
there exists
U^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}_{+}
and
0\leq\gamma\leq M_{i}
such that

x_{i}+M_{i}=\langle U^{\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle+\gamma
. By (ii), there exists
U^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}
such that

\gamma-M_{i}=\langle U^{\prime\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle
. Thus,

x_{i}=(x_{i}+M_{i})-M_{i}=\langle U^{\prime}+U^{\prime\prime},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle.

The conclusion follows by letting
U=U^{\prime}+U^{\prime\prime}
.

Let

\mathcal{H}(G):=\left\{\lambda_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Lambda_{k},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\ \Big|\ m\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\ \Lambda_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{k}}_{+},\ k\in[m]\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}].

Clearly, if
f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
, then
f({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
.

Proposition 7
.

The cone
\mathcal{H}(G)
is closed under addition and multiplication.

Proof.

It is obvious that
f+g\in\mathcal{H}(G)
if
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(G)
. To see
f\cdot g\in\mathcal{H}(G)
,
notice that for any
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda_{k},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}\left(\Lambda_{k}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right)\mbox{tr}\left(\Lambda_{\ell}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}\left(\left(\Lambda_{k}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right)\otimes\left(\Lambda_{\ell}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}\left(\left(\Lambda_{k}\otimes\Lambda_{\ell}\right)\left(G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\otimes G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Lambda_{k}\otimes\Lambda_{\ell},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k+\ell}\right\rangle,

and
\Lambda_{k}\otimes\Lambda_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{k+\ell}}_{+}
for any
\Lambda_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{k}}_{+}

and
\Lambda_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{\ell}}_{+}
.

Moreover, we have

Proposition 8
.

If Assumption
5
holds, then for any

h\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
, there exist
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
such that

h=h_{1}-h_{2}
; that is
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
.

Proof.

Now suppose that Assumption
5
holds. To prove
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
,
without loss of generality, it suffices to prove that
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)

for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
By Proposition
6
(iv), there exists
U_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}
such
that
x_{i}=\langle U_{i},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle
. Then, for each
i\in[n]
and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,

x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}=\left\langle U_{i}^{\otimes{\alpha_{i}}},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes{\alpha_{i}}}\right\rangle,

and

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}=\left\langle U_{1}^{\otimes{\alpha_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}^{\otimes{\alpha_{n}}},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}\right\rangle.

Decompose
U_{1}^{\otimes{\alpha_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}^{\otimes{\alpha_{n}}}=U^{+}-V^{+}
,
where
U^{+},V^{+}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{q^{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}
. Then,

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\left\langle U^{+},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}\right\rangle-\left\langle V^{+},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G).

The conclusion follows.

Definition 9
.

Let
V
be a vector space and let
A\subset V
be a convex set. We
say that
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in A
lies in the algebraic interior of
A
if for any
straight line
\ell
passing through
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, the point
{\boldsymbol{v}}
lies in
the interior of the intersection
A\cap\ell
.

Theorem 10
.

[
3
, III, Corollary 1.7]

Let
V
be a vector space and let
A,B\subset V
be non-empty convex sets such that
A\cap B=\emptyset
. Suppose that
A
has
a non-empty algebraic interior. Then,
A
and
B
can be separated
by an affine hyperplane.

Proposition 11
.

Suppose that Assumption
5
holds.
Then,
1
lies in the algebraic interior of the convex set

\mathcal{H}(G)
.

Proof.

By definition, we need to prove that for any
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
, there
exists
\varepsilon_{f}>0
such that
1+\lambda f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
for all

|\lambda|<\varepsilon_{f}
. Without loss of generality,
it suffices to assume that
f
is a monomial.

Fixing an
f={\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
we prove that there exists positive integer
a
such that

a\pm{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
As proved in Proposition
8
,
there exists
U_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q}
,
i\in[n]
, such that

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\left\langle U_{1}^{\otimes{\alpha_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}^{\otimes{\alpha_{n}}},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}\right\rangle.

By Proposition
6
(iii), there exists
U\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}_{++}
such that

1=\left\langle U,G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}\right\rangle
.
As
U
is positive definite, there exists a positive integer
a
such that

aU\pm U_{1}^{\otimes{\alpha_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}^{\otimes{\alpha_{n}}}\succeq 0
.
Hence,

a\cdot 1\pm{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\left\langle aU\pm U_{1}^{\otimes{\alpha_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}^{\otimes{\alpha_{n}}},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(G).

Then,
1+\lambda{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
for all

|\lambda|<1/a
. By letting
\varepsilon_{f}=1/a
, the proof is complete.

Denote by
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
the set of all linear functionals
L:{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
L(1)=1
and
L(f)\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Clearly,
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
is a convex set.
We can identify each
L\in\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
with the point
(L({\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}))_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
.
Hence, we regard

\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
as a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
, which we endow with the
product topology, i.e., the topology of pointwise convergence. Then,
we have

Lemma 12
.

If
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
,
then
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
is compact.

Proof.

Suppose that
\{L_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
and

\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{k}=L_{\infty}
. Then, by the pointwise convergence,
we have
L_{\infty}(1)=1
and
L_{\infty}(f)\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Then,
L_{\infty}\in\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
and thus
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
is closed.
Since
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
, for any
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
there exists a positive integer
N_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
such that
N_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\pm{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Hence,
|L({\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})|\leq N_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
for any
L\in\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
Thus,
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
is pointwise bounded and hence compact
by Tychonoff’s Theorem.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
and
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
.
If a linear functional
L:{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies that
L(1)=0
and
L(h)\geq 0

for all
h\in\mathcal{H}(G)
, then
L=0
.

Proof.

As
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
,
we only need to prove
L=0
on
\mathcal{H}(G)
. Fix an arbitrary
f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Then
L(f)\geq 0
.
Since
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
,
there exists a positive integer
N_{f}
such that
N_{f}-f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
. Hence,

0\leq L(N_{f}-f)=N_{f}L(1)-L(f)=-L(f),

which shows that
L(f)=0
.

Proposition 14
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
and
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Then, for any extreme point
L
of
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
, there exists

{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{K}
such that
L(f)=f({\boldsymbol{u}})
for all
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
.

Proof.

We first prove that
L
is multiplicative on
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
. That is,
L(fg)=L(f)L(g)

for any
f,g\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
. As
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
,
it suffices to assume that
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Fixing arbitrary
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(G)
, let us prove
L(fg)=L(f)L(g)
.
Since
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
,
there exists a positive integer
N_{f}
such that
N_{f}-f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
Thus,
0\leq L(f)\leq L(N_{f})=N_{f}
.

If
0<L(f)<N_{f}
, define two linear functionals
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
as follows

L_{1}(h)=\frac{L(fh)}{L(f)},\quad L_{2}(h)=\frac{L((N_{f}-f)h)}{N_{f}-L(f)},\ \ \forall h\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}].

As
N_{f}-f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
, we have
L_{1},L_{2}\in\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
. Observe that

L=\frac{L(f)}{N_{f}}L_{1}+\frac{N_{f}-L(f)}{N_{f}}L_{2},\quad\frac{L(f)}{N_{f}}+\frac{N_{f}-L(f)}{N_{f}}=1.

As
L
is an extreme point of
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
, we must have

L=L_{1}
. Therefore, we have

L(g)=L_{1}(g)=\frac{L(fg)}{L(f)},

which implies
L(fg)=L(f)L(g)
.

If
L(f)=0
, let
\widetilde{L}
be the linear functional on
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
such that
\widetilde{L}(h)=L(fh)
for all
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
.
Then,
\widetilde{L}(1)=0
and
\widetilde{L}(h)\geq 0
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.
By Lemma
13
, we have
\widetilde{L}=0
and hence

L(fg)=\widetilde{L}(g)=0=L(f)L(g).

If
L(f)=N_{f}
, let
\widetilde{L}
be the linear functional on
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
such that
\widetilde{L}(h)=L((N_{f}-f)h)
for all
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
. Then, repeating the above arguments shows that

\widetilde{L}=0
and hence,

N_{f}L(g)-L(fg)=L((N_{f}-f)g)=\widetilde{L}(g)=0=(N_{f}-L(f))L(g)=N_{f}L(g)-L(f)L(g),

which implies
L(fg)=L(f)L(g)
.

Letting
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(L(x_{1}),\ldots,L(x_{n}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, as we have shown that

L
is multiplicative on
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
,
it holds
L(f)=f({\boldsymbol{u}})
for all
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
. It remains to show that
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{K}
.
Fix an arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
. As

{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\intercal}G({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{v}}=\langle{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\intercal},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(G)
,
we have

{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\intercal}G({\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{v}}=\langle{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\intercal},G({\boldsymbol{u}})\rangle=L(\langle{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\intercal},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle)\geq 0.

Thus,
G({\boldsymbol{u}})\succeq 0
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{K}
.

Now we are ready to prove the following result,
which extends the Handelman’s Positivstellensatz
[
11
]
from the scalar inequality
setting to the matrix context.

Theorem 15
.

Suppose that Assumption
5
holds. If a polynomial

f({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
is positive on
\mathbf{K}
, then
f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.

Proof.

As Assumption
5
holds, by Propositions
8
and
11
,
we have
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
and
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
.

Suppose to the contrary that
f\not\in\mathcal{H}(G)
. Then, by Proposition
11
and Theorem
10
,
there exists a nonzero linear functional
L:{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
L(f)\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}(G)}L(h)
. As
\mathcal{H}(G)
is a cone,
we must have
\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}(G)}L(h)=0
, which implies that

L(f)\leq 0
and
L(h)\geq 0
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}(G)
. By Lemma
13
, we have
L(1)>0
and hence
L/L(1)\in\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
.
By applying the Krein-Milman theorem to the convex compact set
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
,
we can conclude that
L/L(1)
lies in the closed convex hull of
extreme points of
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
. Hence, we have

L/L(1)=\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{k}
(pointwise convergence),
where each
L_{k}
belongs to the convex hull
of extreme points of
\pi(\mathcal{H}(G))
. By Proposition
14
, for each

k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist points
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\ell_{k}}\in\mathbf{K}
for some
\ell_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and positive real numbers
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell_{k}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{k}}\lambda_{i}=1
such
that

L_{k}(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{k}}\lambda_{i}h({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\ \ \forall h\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}].

As
\mathbf{K}
is compact, there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that
f({\boldsymbol{x}})\geq\varepsilon
on
\mathbf{K}
. Therefore,

L_{k}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{k}}\lambda_{i}f({\boldsymbol{u}}_{k})\geq\varepsilon
for all

k\in\mathbb{N}
. By the pointwise convergence,

L(f)=L(1)\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{k}(f)\geq L(1)\varepsilon>0,

a contradiction.

Remark 16
.

From the proof, we observe that the key to establishing Theorem
15

lies in the properties: (i)
\mathcal{H}(G)
is closed under addition and multiplication;
(ii)
{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)
; and (iii)
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
.

3.2
The nonlinear case

In this part, let
G({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{q}
be a nonlinear polynomial matrix.
If the set
\mathbf{K}
defined by the inequality

is compact,
by rescaling if necessary, we may assume that
I_{q}-G({\boldsymbol{x}})\succeq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
.
We now introduce the dilated form of
G({\boldsymbol{x}})

\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\diag(G({\boldsymbol{x}}),I_{q}-G({\boldsymbol{x}}))\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{2q}.

Clearly, the inequality
\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\succeq 0
defines the same set
\mathbf{K}
.

Let

\mathcal{H}(G):=\left\{\lambda_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Lambda_{k},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\ \Big|\ m\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\ \Lambda_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{(2q)^{k}}_{+},\ k\in[m]\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}].

As with Proposition
7
, it is readily seen that the cone

\mathcal{H}(G)
is closed under both addition and multiplication.

Assumption 17
.

(i)

\mathbf{K}
is compact and
I_{q}-G({\boldsymbol{x}})\succeq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
;

(ii)

{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\mathcal{H}(G)-\mathcal{H}(G)

Under Assumption

, it is clear that

on
\mathbf{K}
if

.

Remark 18
.

Assumption
17
(ii) holds if and only if for any
f({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
,
there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and symmetric matrices

\Gamma_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{(2q)^{k}}
,

, such that

In this case, we say that

can be generated by
\{I_{q},G({\boldsymbol{x}})\}

If Assumption

(ii) does not hold, it can be restored by augmenting

. Then, it follows directly that Assumption

is satisfied when

is replaced by
\diag(G({\boldsymbol{x}}),x_{1}-b_{1},\ldots,x_{n}-b_{n})
.

Proposition 19
.

Suppose that Assumption
17
holds.
Then,
1
lies in the algebraic interior of the convex set

\mathcal{H}(G)
.

Proof.

To prove that
1
is an algebraic interior point of
\mathcal{H}(G)
,
it suffices to prove that for any monomial
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
there exists positive
\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
1\pm t{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
for all

0\leq t\leq\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
.

As Assumption
17
(ii) holds, there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and symmetric matrices

\Gamma_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{(2q)^{k}}
,
k\in[m]
, such that

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Gamma_{k},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle.

Write
\gamma=\gamma^{+}-\gamma^{-}
and
\Gamma_{k}=\Gamma_{k}^{+}-\Gamma_{k}^{-}
,
k\in[m]
, where

\gamma^{\pm}>0
,
\Gamma_{k}^{\pm}\succeq 0
,
k\in[m]
.

Letting
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
be positive such that

0<t\leq\left(\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mbox{tr}\left(\Gamma_{k}^{+}\right)\right)^{-1}.

(9)

We first show that
1-t{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
.

We have

\displaystyle 1-t{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=1-\left(t\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle t\Gamma_{k}^{+},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\right)+\left(t\gamma^{-}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle t\Gamma_{k}^{-},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-t\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle-t\Gamma_{k}^{+},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\right)+\left(t\gamma^{-}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle t\Gamma_{k}^{-},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\right).

Observe that

\displaystyle 1-t\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle-t\Gamma_{k}^{+},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=1-t\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle-t\Gamma_{k}^{+},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}-I_{(2q)^{k}}+I_{(2q)^{k}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=1-t\left(\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mbox{tr}\left(\Gamma_{k}^{+}\right)\right)+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Gamma_{k}^{+},I_{(2q)^{k}}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle.

By (
9
), to show
1-t{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
, it remains to prove that for each
k\in[m]
,

\left\langle\Gamma_{k}^{+},I_{(2q)^{k}}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(G).

Consider the case when
k=1
. Observe that
I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\diag(I_{q}-G({\boldsymbol{x}}),G({\boldsymbol{x}}))
.
Equally partition
\Gamma_{1}^{+}
as a
2\times 2
block matrix
[(\Gamma_{1}^{k})_{ij}]_{i,j\in[2]}
, where

(\Gamma_{1}^{+})_{ij}
denotes its
(i,j)
-th block. Let
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}^{+}=\diag((\Gamma_{1}^{+})_{22},(\Gamma_{1}^{+})_{11})
.
Then, we have
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}^{+}\succeq 0
and

\left\langle\Gamma_{1}^{+},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle=\left\langle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}^{+},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(G).

Now fix a
k\in[m]
with
k\geq 2
. We define matrices
\Lambda_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{(2q)^{\ell}}
,

\ell\in[k]
, in the following way: (i) Let
\Lambda_{k}=\Gamma_{k}^{+}
;
(ii) For
\ell=k,\ldots,2
,
equally partition
\Lambda_{\ell}
as a
2q\times 2q
block matrix

[(\Lambda_{\ell})_{ij}]_{i,j\in[2q]}
, where

(\Lambda_{\ell})_{ij}
denotes its
(i,j)
-th block; (iii) Let

\Lambda_{\ell-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{2q}(\Lambda_{\ell})_{ii}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{(2q)^{\ell-1}}
.
One can check that the following properties hold:

(i)

\Lambda_{\ell}\succeq 0
for all
\ell\in[k]
;

(ii)

\left\langle\Lambda_{1},I_{2q}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Lambda_{k},I_{(2q)^{k}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Gamma_{k}^{+},I_{(2q)^{k}}\right\rangle
;

(iii)

For each
\ell=2,\ldots,k
,

\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},I_{2q}\otimes\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell-1}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell-1},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell-1}\right\rangle.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda_{1},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle+\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},\left(I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\otimes\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell-1}\right\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda_{1},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle+\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\left(\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},I_{2q}\otimes\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell-1}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda_{1},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle+\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\left(\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell-1},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell-1}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda_{1},I_{2q}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda_{k},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Lambda_{k},I_{2q}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda_{k},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Gamma_{k}^{+},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle.

For each
\ell\in[k]
, equally partition
\Lambda_{\ell}

as a
2\times 2
block matrix
[(\Lambda_{\ell})_{ij}]_{i,j\in[2]},
where

(\Lambda_{\ell})_{ij}
denotes its
(i,j)
-th block, and let

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\ell}=\diag((\Lambda_{\ell})_{22},(\Lambda_{\ell})_{11})
. We have

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\ell}\succeq 0
for all
\ell\in[k]
, and

\left\langle\Lambda_{1},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle=\left\langle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{1},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle,\ \ \left\langle\Lambda_{\ell},\left(I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\otimes\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell-1}\right\rangle=\left\langle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\ell},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right\rangle,

for all
\ell=2,\ldots,k.

Therefore, by (
10
), it holds that

\left\langle\Gamma_{k}^{+},I_{2q}-\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left\langle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\ell},\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\ell}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}(G).

Now we have shown that
1-t{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
for all

satisfying (

).

By similar arguments, we can prove that

for all

satisfying

Then, by letting

\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\min\left\{\left(\gamma^{+}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mbox{tr}\left(\Gamma_{k}^{+}\right)\right)^{-1},\left(\gamma^{-}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mbox{tr}\left(\Gamma_{k}^{-}\right)\right)^{-1}\right\},

the proof is complete.

3.3
Using sparse structures

In this part, we assume that the matrix
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
presents
block-diagonal structure, i.e.,
G({\boldsymbol{x}})=\diag(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
for some
G_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{q_{i}}
,

q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in[t]
.
We first give an efficient representation of the cone
\mathcal{H}(G)

by exploiting
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
’s block-diagonal structure.
Under the additional assumption that each block
G_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
depends on overlapping variable subsets,
we then derive sparse versions of Positivstellensätze Theorems
15
and
20
.

Denote by
\mathcal{W}(t)
the set of words
{\boldsymbol{w}}({\boldsymbol{X}})
generated by
t

noncommuting letters
{\boldsymbol{X}}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})
.
For example, the set of words in 2 noncommuting letters of length 3 is

\{X_{1}^{3},\ X_{1}^{2}X_{2},\ X_{1}X_{2}X_{1},\ X_{1}X_{2}^{2},\ X_{2}X_{1}^{2},\ X_{2}X_{1}X_{2},\ X_{2}^{2}X_{1},\ X_{2}^{3}\}.

For a word
{\boldsymbol{w}}({\boldsymbol{X}})
, its evaluation
{\boldsymbol{w}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
on the tuple of matrices

{\boldsymbol{A}}:=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t})

is defined as the matrix obtained by substituting
the letter
X_{i}
by
A_{i}
,
i\in[t]
,
and forming the corresponding Kronecker product. For example, the evaluations
of words in 2 noncommuting letters of length 3 on
(A_{1},A_{2})
are the matrices

\left\{A_{1}^{\otimes 3},\ A_{1}^{\otimes 2}\otimes A_{2},\ A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\otimes A_{1},\ A_{1}\otimes A_{2}^{\otimes 2},\ A_{2}\otimes A_{1}^{\otimes 2},\ A_{2}\otimes A_{1}\otimes A_{2},\ A_{2}^{\otimes 2}\otimes A_{1},\ A_{2}^{\otimes 3}\right\}.

For a word
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W}(t)
, denote by
w_{i}
,
i\in[t]
, the frequency of
the letter
X_{i}
appearing in
{\boldsymbol{w}}({\boldsymbol{X}})
.
Denote the length of the word
{\boldsymbol{w}}({\boldsymbol{X}})
by
|{\boldsymbol{w}}|
. Thus, it holds
|{\boldsymbol{w}}|=w_{1}+\cdots+w_{t}
.

In the following, with a slight abuse of notation, let
{\boldsymbol{w}}(G({\boldsymbol{x}}))={\boldsymbol{w}}(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))

for
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W}(t)
and

{\boldsymbol{w}}(\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}}))={\boldsymbol{w}}(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),I_{q_{1}}-G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,I_{q_{t}}-G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))

for
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W}(2t)
.

Proposition 21
.

Suppose that
G({\boldsymbol{x}})=\diag(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
for some
G_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{q_{i}}
,

q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in[t]
. If
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear, it holds

\mathcal{H}(G)=\left\{\lambda_{0}+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W}(t),|{\boldsymbol{w}}|\in[m]}\left\langle\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}},{{\boldsymbol{w}}}(G({\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rangle\ \Bigg|\ \begin{aligned} &m\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\ \Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{{d({\boldsymbol{w}})}},\\
&d({\boldsymbol{w}})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}q_{i}^{w_{i}},\ |{\boldsymbol{w}}|\in[m]\end{aligned}\right\}.

If
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is nonlinear, it holds

\mathcal{H}(G)=\left\{\lambda_{0}+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W}(2t),|{\boldsymbol{w}}|\in[m]}\left\langle\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}},{{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rangle\ \Bigg|\ \begin{aligned} &m\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\ \Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{{e({\boldsymbol{w}})}},\\
&e({\boldsymbol{w}})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}q_{i}^{w_{2i-1}+w_{2i}},\ |{\boldsymbol{w}}|\in[m]\end{aligned}\right\}.

Proof.

We only prove the linear case and the nonlinear case follows similarly.
Let
h({\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathcal{H}(G)
. By definition, there exist
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Lambda_{k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{k}}_{+}
,
q=q_{1}+\cdots+q_{t}
,
k\in[m]
, such that

h({\boldsymbol{x}})=\lambda_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Lambda_{k},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle.

Note that for each
k\in[m]
,
G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}
is permutation-similar to a block-diagonal
matrix whose blocks consist of

\left\{{\boldsymbol{w}}(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})),\ {\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W},\ |{\boldsymbol{w}}|=k\right\}.

For each
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
with
|{\boldsymbol{w}}|=k
, let
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
be the submatrix
of
\Lambda_{k}
which corresponds to the block
{\boldsymbol{w}}(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
in
G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}
.
Then,
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\succeq 0
and

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{W},|{\boldsymbol{w}}|=k}\left\langle\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{w}}},{\boldsymbol{w}}(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rangle=\left\langle\Lambda_{k},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle.

Hence, the conclusion follows.

Remark 22
.

Proposition
21
establishes that when

is block-diagonal, the PSD
matrices representing polynomials in
\mathcal{H}(G)
can be decomposed into smaller matrices.
In Section
4
, when we apply Theorems
15
and

20
to PMI-constrained
polynomial optimization, this decomposition accelerates SDP solvers for the resulting relaxations.

When scalar polynomial blocks appear in

’s block-diagonal structure,
the commutativity of scalar multiplication enables reduction in the number
of PSD matrices required to represent polynomials in

. Take the linear case for example,

Corollary 23
.

Suppose that
G({\boldsymbol{x}})=\diag(g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,g_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
for some linear

,

,

\mathcal{H}(G)=\left\{\lambda_{0}+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{N}^{t}_{m}}\left\langle\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}},g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots g_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\gamma_{t-1}}G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes\gamma_{t}}\right\rangle\ \Bigg|\ \begin{aligned} &m\in\mathbb{N},\ \lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\\
&\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{q^{\gamma_{t}}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{N}_{m}^{t}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Proof.

The conclusion follows from Proposition
21
.

Next, we further assume that the block-diagonal matrix

exhibits the correlative sparsity. In other words,

can be grouped into overlapping subsets

in

depending only on variables from a single subset. Precisely, we consider the following assumption.

Assumption 24

The index set

can be decomposed as

such that

(ii)

.

For each

, let

\mathbf{K}_{i}:=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|I_{i}|}\mid G_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\succeq 0\}.

(11)

Assumption 25

If

is linear, each

is compact with nonempty interior. Otherwise, for each

,

is compact,

and give the following sparse version of

and

.

Theorem 26
.

Suppose that Assumptions

and

with some

,

. If

Proof.

As

\mathbf{K}

such that

on
\mathbf{K}
.
By Assumption

4
Applications to polynomial optimization with matrix inequality constraints

Consider the problem of minimizing a polynomial function over a semialgebraic set
defined by a polynomial matrix inequality:

(
\mathbb{P}
)

\displaystyle f^{\star}:=\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbf{K}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\ \ \text{s.t. }\ \mathbf{K}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid G({\boldsymbol{x}})\succeq 0\},

where
f({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
and
G({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{q}
.

In the definition of
\mathcal{H}(G)
, by fixing the number
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
we obtain the
m
-th truncation of
\mathcal{H}(G)
and denote it by
\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)
.
Then, we have
\mathcal{H}(G)=\cup_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)
.
For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the problem

(
\mathbb{H}_{m}
)

\displaystyle f^{\star}_{m}:=\sup_{r\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ r\ \ \text{s.t. }\ f({\boldsymbol{x}})-r\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(G).

By the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)
, the problem (
\mathbb{H}_{m}
) is a semidefinite program.

Remark 28
.

When
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is diagonal, the SDP relaxation (
\mathbb{H}_{m}
) retrieves the LP-relaxation
for scalar polynomial optimization problems obtained by Lasserre in
[
21
,
22
]
.

4.1
Asymptotic behavior of the SDP relaxations
\{(\mathbb{H}_{m})\}

We establish the asymptotic convergence of the optimal values of the SDP relaxations
\{(\mathbb{H}_{m})\}

as
m\to\infty
.

Theorem 29
.

Suppose that
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear (resp., nonlinear) and Assumption
5

(resp.,
17
) holds. Then,
f_{m}^{\star}\uparrow f^{\star}
as
m\to\infty
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{m+1}(G)
, the sequence
\{f_{m}^{\star}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is nondecreasing.
By definition, all elements in
\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)
are nonnegative on
\mathbf{K}
. Then,

f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f^{\star}_{m}\geq 0
on
\mathbf{K}
and hence
f^{\star}\geq f^{\star}_{m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f^{\star}+\varepsilon>0
on
\mathbf{K}
.
Since
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear (resp., nonlinear) and Assumption
5

(resp., Assumption
17
)
holds, Theorem
15
(resp., Theorem
20
) implies that

f^{\star}_{m}\geq f^{\star}-\varepsilon
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, it holds

f_{m}^{\star}\uparrow f^{\star}
as
m\to\infty
.

Let
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be a number such that the SDP relaxation (

m\geq m_{0}

As with the LP-relaxation for scalar polynomial optimization
[

we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the optimal solutions of

as
m\to\infty

in the linear case. The result extends to the nonlinear case via analogous arguments
and we omit the details for simplicity.

Proposition 30
.

Suppose that
G({\boldsymbol{x}})

{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star}

\mathbb{P}
). Then,

0=f({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})-f^{\star}\geq\lambda_{\star,0}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left\langle\Lambda_{\star,k},G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle.

As
\lambda_{\star,0}\geq 0
and
\Lambda_{\star,k}\succeq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
we must have
\lambda_{\star,0}=0
and

\left\langle\Lambda_{\star,k},G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
This completes the proof of (i) and (ii).

4.2
Exactness of the SDP relaxations
\{(\mathbb{H}_{m})\}

We say that the relaxations {(
\mathbb{H}_{m}
)} are exact if
f_{m}^{\star}=f^{\star}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Similar to the LP-relaxation for scalar polynomial optimization
[
22
, Proposition 3.2]
,
we will show that the SDP relaxations
\{(\mathbb{H}_{m})\}
can not be exact in general.

Lemma 31
.

For
A,B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s\times t}
, if the null space
\text{Null}(A)\subseteq\text{Null}(B)
, then

\text{Null}(A^{\otimes k})\subseteq\text{Null}(B^{\otimes k})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have (see
[
7
, 14.8]
)

\displaystyle\text{Null}(A^{\otimes k})

\displaystyle=\text{Null}(A)\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}+{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes\text{Null}(A)\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}+{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes\cdots\otimes\text{Null}(A),

\displaystyle\text{Null}(B^{\otimes k})

\displaystyle=\text{Null}(B)\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}+{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes\text{Null}(B)\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}+{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\otimes\cdots\otimes\text{Null}(B).

As
\text{Null}(A)\subseteq\text{Null}(B)
, it is clear that
\text{Null}(A^{\otimes k})\subseteq\text{Null}(B^{\otimes k})
.

Proposition 32
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star}
be a minimizer of
(\mathbb{P})
and
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
be linear (resp., nonlinear).
If there exists a nonoptimal point
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{K}
of such that

\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star}))\subseteq\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{u}}))
(resp.,
\text{Null}(\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star}))\subseteq\text{Null}(\widetilde{G}({\boldsymbol{u}}))
),
then no relaxation
(\mathbb{H}_{m})
is exact.
In particular, if
G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})\succ 0
(resp.,
G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})\succ 0
and
I_{q}-G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})\succ 0
),
then no relaxation
(\mathbb{H}_{m})
is exact.

Proof.

We only need to consider the case when
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear.
Suppose to the contrary that the
m
-th relaxation {(
\mathbb{H}_{m}
)} is exact, i.e., the
representation

f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f^{\star}=\lambda_{m,0}+\sum_{k\in[m]}\left\langle\Lambda_{m,k},G({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle,

holds for some
\lambda_{m,0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\Lambda_{m,k}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{q^{k}}_{+}
,
k\in[m]
. Then,

0=f({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})-f^{\star}=\lambda_{m,0}+\sum_{k\in[m]}\left\langle\Lambda_{m,k},G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle,

which implies that
\lambda_{m,0}=0
and
\left\langle\Lambda_{m,k},G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle

for all
k\in[m]
. For any decomposition

\Lambda_{m,k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s_{k}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(k,\ell)}({\boldsymbol{v}}^{(k,\ell)})^{\intercal}
with

{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(k,\ell)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q^{k}}
, it holds that

G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})^{\otimes k}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(k,\ell)}=0
for all
\ell\in[s_{k}]
,
k\in[m]
.
Since
\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star}))\subseteq\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{u}}))
, by Lemma
31
, we have

\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\star})^{\otimes k})\subseteq\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{u}})^{\otimes k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus,
G({\boldsymbol{u}})^{\otimes k}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(k,\ell)}=0
for all
\ell\in[s_{k}]
,
k\in[m]
, and hence

\left\langle\Lambda_{m,k},G({\boldsymbol{u}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle=0

for all
k\in[m]
. Therefore, as
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{K}
is nonoptimal, we have

0<f({\boldsymbol{u}})-f^{\star}=\lambda_{m,0}+\sum_{k\in[m]}\left\langle\Lambda_{m,k},G({\boldsymbol{u}})^{\otimes k}\right\rangle=0,

a contradiction.

Remark 33
.

When
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear, the null space
\text{Null}(G({\boldsymbol{x}}))
is constant over the relative interior
of any face of
\mathbf{K}
(
[
35
, Corollary 1]
).
Thus when
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear, Proposition
32
implies that the only case where the relaxations

{
(
\mathbb{H}_{m}
)
}
may be exact is if every global minimizer of (
\mathbb{P}
) is a

0
-dimensional face of
\mathbf{K}
or if
f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f^{\star}
vanishes on every face of
\mathbf{K}
that
contains a global minimizer of (
\mathbb{P}
) as a relative interior.

In the following examples, we implement the relaxations {(
\mathbb{H}_{m}
)} using
Yalmip

[
28
]

and solve the resulting SDPs by
Mosek

[
2

.
To formulate (
\mathbb{H}_{m}

we need to express

symbolically to get the coefficients with respect to the monomial basis could be
computationally prohibitive. Instead, if

is linear (resp., nonlinear),

with 8-Core Intel i7 3.8 GHz CPUs and 8 GB RAM.

4.3
Sparse SDP relaxations

If the matrix
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
exhibits a block-diagonal structure, i.e., it holds that

G({\boldsymbol{x}})=\diag(G_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ldots,G_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
for some
G_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{q_{i}}
,
q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,

i\in[t]
, one can leverage the characterization of
\mathcal{H}(G)
provided in Proposition

21
. Applying this to the relaxations
(\mathbb{H}_{m})

allows the PSD matrices in the associated SDP problems
to be decomposed into smaller matrices, which reduces the computational burden.

Furthermore, if each monomial in
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
and each block in
G({\boldsymbol{x}})

depends on overlapping variable subsets, then one can construct the sparse relaxations

(
\mathbb{H}^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}_{m}
)

\displaystyle f^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}_{m}:=\sup_{r\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ r\ \ \text{s.t. }\ f({\boldsymbol{x}})-r\in\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{H}_{m}(G_{i}).

The asymptotic convergence of the optimal values
\{f^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}_{m}\}
as
m\to\infty

can be established using Theorem
26
.

Theorem 36
.

Suppose that Assumptions
24
and
25
hold. Let

f=\sum_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]
with some
f_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}(I_{i})]
,
i\in[t]
.
Then,
f_{m}^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}\uparrow f^{\star}
as
m\to\infty
.

The asymptotic and exactness properties obtained for
(\mathbb{H}_{m})
extend analogously
to
(\mathbb{H}^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}_{m})
. We omit the details for simplicity.

As mentioned in Remark
27
, the resulting SDP problem for
(\mathbb{H}^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}_{m})

is of a much smaller size than that of
(\mathbb{H}_{m})
.
This key advantage allows
(\mathbb{H}^{\mbox{\tiny sps}}_{m})
to be applied to problems of
a significantly larger scale that possess the requisite sparse structure.

4.4
Comparing with SOS-based SDP relaxations

Applying the SOS-based Positivstellensatz in Theorem
3

to the problem (
\mathbb{P}
),
one can construct the following
m
-th SDP relaxation for (
\mathbb{P}
)

(
\mathbb{P}_{m}
)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} f^{\mbox{\tiny sos}}_{m}:=\sup_{r,\sigma,\Sigma}&\ r\\
\text{s.t.}&\ f({\boldsymbol{x}})-r=\sigma({\boldsymbol{x}})+\left\langle\Sigma({\boldsymbol{x}}),G({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle,\\
&\ r\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]\ \text{and}\ \Sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}[{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{q}\text{ are SOS},\\
&\ \deg(\sigma),\deg(\left\langle\Sigma({\boldsymbol{x}}),G({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle)\leq 2m.\end{aligned}\right.

Under Assumption
2
, by Theorem
3
, we have

f^{\mbox{\tiny sos}}_{m}\uparrow f^{\star}
as
m\to\infty
.

Now we
compare the size of the SDP relaxations of

(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
and (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
),
which use the same degree bound
2m
in the representation of
f({\boldsymbol{x}})-r
.
By equating two polynomials of degree at most
2m
,
both
(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
and (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
) have the same number
\binom{n+2m}{n}
of
equality constraints on their respective variables.
The variables in the relaxation
(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
are the nonnegative number
\lambda_{0}

and
2m
PSD matrices
\Lambda_{k}
,
k\in[2m]
. For each
k\in[2m]
,
the size of
\Lambda_{k}
is
q^{k}
when
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is linear and

(2q)^{k}
when
G({\boldsymbol{x}})
is nonlinear. In comparison, the
variables in the relaxation (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
) involve two PSD matrices of size

\binom{n+m}{n}
and
q\binom{n+m}{n}
, associated with the SOS polynomial
\sigma({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
the SOS polynomial matrix
\Sigma({\boldsymbol{x}})
, respectively.

The size of PSD matrices in both
(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
and (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
)
grows exponentially, becoming prohibitively large as the order
m
increases.
Although the exact convergence rate of
f_{2m}^{\star}
to
f^{\star}

remains unknown, we anticipate–based on the slow convergence observed in LP relaxations

[
21
,
22
]
for scalar polynomial optimization derived from the
Positivstellensätze of Handelman (
2
) and Krivine–Stengle (
3
)
–that the convergence
f_{2m}^{\star}\uparrow f^{\star}
will also be slow.
Moreover, Huang and Nie
[
15
]
recently established the
finite convergence of (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
) under certain nondegeneracy and optimality
conditions.
This suggests that SOS-based SDP relaxations (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
) are, in principle,
superior to the relaxations
(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
.
However, it is important to observe that the size of PSD matrices
in
(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
depends only on the powers of
q
and remains independent of
the number
n
of variables in (
\mathbb{P}
). In contrast, the sizes of two PSD
matrices in (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
) approximately equal
n^{m}
and
qn^{m}
.
Consequently,
for the problems (
\mathbb{P}
) with very large
n
and relatively small
q

that are significantly beyond the capability of the SDP relaxation (
\mathbb{P}_{m}
),
solving the alternatives
(\mathbb{H}_{2m})
may still yield meaningful lower bounds of

f^{\star}
in a reasonable time.

Conclusions

In this paper, we have established new non-SOS Positivstellensätze for polynomials positive on
PMI-defined semialgebraic sets. By extending the classical theorems of Handelman and Krivine–Stengle
to the matrix setting, we provided explicit representation forms for such polynomials without using
sums of squares.
Specifically, we showed that if is strictly positive on a compact PMI-defined set,
then it can be written as a linear combination of Kronecker powers of the defining matrix
(or its dilated form ) with PSD coefficient matrices.
When the polynomial and the constraint matrix exhibit the correlative sparsity,
we proved that significantly simpler representations exist,
drastically reducing the number and size of required PSD matrices.
By applying these algebraic certificates to polynomial optimization problems with PMI constraints,
we constructed a new hierarchy of semidefinite programming relaxations.
Although not exact in general, these relaxations remain computationally tractable even when
the number of variables is large, provided the constaint matrix dimension is modest.
For such problems, this offers a practical alternative to the traditional SOS-based hierarchies of
SDP relaxations.

Several interesting questions remain for future work.
It would be a primary challenge to derive degree bounds for the representations proposed
in Theorems
15
and
20
.
Extending the present results to non-compact semialgebraic sets could broaden their applicability.
It would also be valuable to exploit additional sparsity structures
(e.g., chordal sparsity
[
45
]
) in the polynomial matrix.
To tighten the proposed SDP relaxations in practice, one may incorporate bounded-degree SOS terms

[
25
,
44
]
, leading to a hybrid hierarchy.
Using computationally cheaper cones (e.g., diagonally dominant or scaled diagonally dominant matrices

[
1
]
)
instead of full PSD constraints in the proposed SDP relaxations to improve scalability while balancing
solution quality.
