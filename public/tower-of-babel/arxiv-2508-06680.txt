New unlikely intersections on elliptic surfaces
Douglas Ulmer, José Felipe Voloch

Abstract
. Consider a Jacobian elliptic surface ℰ → 𝒞 \mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C} with a section P P of infinite order. Previous work of the first author and Urzúa over the complex numbers gives a bound on the number of tangencies between P P and a torsion section of ℰ \mathcal{E} (an “unlikely intersection”), and more precisely, an exact formula for the weighted number of tangencies between P P and elements of the “Betti foliation”. This work used analytic techniques that apparently do not generalize to positive characteristic. In this paper, we extend their work to characteristic p p , and we develop a second approach to tangency properties of algebraic curves on a complex elliptic surface, yielding a new family of unlikely intersections with a strong connection to a famous homomorphism of Manin. We also correct inaccuracies in the literature about this homomorphism.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

We study unlikely intersections in the form of tangencies of curves on
elliptic surfaces. In
[
8
]
, the first author and
Urzúa worked over the complex numbers and gave a bound on the number
of tangencies between a section of infinite order and
n
-torsion
(multi-)sections for varying
n
. Such tangencies may be viewed as
“unlikely intersections.” More generally, they introduced a
“Betti” foliation (considered independently by several other
authors) of a dense open subset of the elliptic surface by
multisections generalizing the
n
-torsion multisections, and they
showed that there is an exact formula for the weighted number of
tangencies between the given section of infinite order and the leaves
of the Betti foliation. These leaves are generally of infinite degree
over the base, so non-algebraic, and the bound on tangencies uses a
certain non-holomorphic (but real-analytic) 1-form. It is thus not
evident whether or how these bounds might be generalized to base
fields of positive characteristic.

In this paper, we extend their work to positive characteristic. Our
results are somewhat similar but there are some differences as
well. In doing so, we noticed that there is a second way of looking at
tangency properties of algebraic curves on a complex elliptic surface,
which we develop. Our results in characteristic
p
are somehow
intermediate between the results of
[
8
]
and this
second approach to tangencies in characteristic zero.

For our purposes, an elliptic surface is a smooth projective algebraic
surface
\mathcal{E}/k
, where
k
is an algebraically closed field, equipped
with an elliptic fibration
\pi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}/k
is a smooth
projective algebraic curve, admitting a zero section
O
. The generic
fiber of
\pi
is thus an elliptic curve
E/K
, where
K=k(\mathcal{C})
is
the function field of
\mathcal{C}
. We take a point
P\in E(K)
of infinite
order and also denote by
P
the corresponding section of
\pi
.

Our work depends crucially on a homomorphism of groups

\mu:E(K)\to K
which was introduced in characteristic zero by Manin

[
3
]
and generalized to positive characteristic by the
second author
[
9
]
. (This map was also studied
cohomologically in
[
7
]
and made very explicit in

[
1
]
.) The value
\mu(P)
is expressed as a rational
function of the coordinates of
P
and their derivatives. Our results
on tangencies with
P
are obtained by a careful study of the local
and global properties of
\mu
. We sharpen the local analysis of the
values of
\mu
done by previous authors and relate them with the
tangency properties were are interested in. Interpreting the values
of
\mu
as sections of an appropriate bundle on
\mathcal{C}
leads to a
bound on the number of tangencies.

To give the flavor of our results, we state one result in
characteristic
p
and one over
\mathbb{C}
.

First, let
k
be algebraically closed of characteristic
p>3
, let

N>3
be an integer prime to
p
, and let
\mathcal{C}=\Ig_{1}(N)
be the
(Igusa) curve parameterizing elliptic curves with a point of order
N

and an Igusa structure of order
p
. Let
K=k(\mathcal{C})
, let
E/K
be the
universal curve, and let
P\in E(K)
be a point of infinite order. We
define a local intersection invariant
I(P,t)
for
t\in\mathcal{C}
which
measures the order of contact between
P
and local sections which are
divisible by
p
. (See Definition
3.4
for details.) We
consider the set
T
(“tangencies”) of points

where

I(P,t)>1
, and we divide it into subsets
T_{o}
and
T_{s}
where the
elliptic curve corresponding to
t
is ordinary or supersingular
respectively. Our result is then a bound

\displaystyle|T|\leq|T_{o}|+p|T_{s}|

\displaystyle\leq p(2g-2-d)+(p-1)\delta,

\displaystyle=p(p-1)\sigma-\delta,

where

\mathcal{C}
,

\omega

Now assume that
k=\mathbb{C}
. We define “complex Betti leaves” which are
parameterized by two complex numbers modulo 1 and are such that on a
dense open subset of
\mathcal{E}
, there is a complex Betti leaf through a
given point with a given tangent direction. Thus, an unlikely
intersection is when
P
meets some complex Betti leaf to order at
least 3. We define an exceptional set

(essentially
where
\mathcal{E}
has bad reduction or the period map is not an immersion)

In both characteristic zero and

, our arguments have a local aspect
(relating intersection numbers to orders of vanishing of a Manin map)
and a global aspect (interpreting the image of the Manin map as a
section of a suitable line bundle and deducing a bound from degree
considerations). In both the complex and characteristic

[

3.
A more general class of tangencies

One of the main lessons of

4.
Complex Betti leaves and the Manin map

In this section, we work over the complex numbers. It was already
noted in
[

that the Manin
map can be used to bound tangencies with the Betti foliation, although
the connection there is rather loose and does not directly give
finiteness. Here, we define a new class of tangencies via

and deduce a strong upper bound.

In order to compute examples, we need an explicit algebraic version of
the Manin map, and we note, as did the authors of

[
2
]
, that published versions
of this map are incorrect, so we give a detailed correction.

Throughout this section,
\pi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}
is an elliptic
fibration with zero section

and another section

of infinite
order. Except in Remark

, we assume that
\mathcal{E}
is
non-isotrivial.

4.1.

[

]

use the notation of that paper freely here. In particular, let

\mathcal{C}^{0}
be the open over which

Let

be a point of bad reduction. We say that a

Lemma 4.2
.

(1)

A complex Betti leaf
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}
extends to a section of
\pi

in a neighborhood of

if and only if it is an invariant leaf.
In this case, it meets the closed fiber over
t
in a point at which

\pi
is smooth.

(2)

If
\mathcal{E}
has reduction of type

at

(

)
, then the invariant leaves
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}

are those where

, and every smooth point of the
fiber
\pi^{-1}(t)
is the limit of an invariant leaf.

(3)

If
\mathcal{E}
has additive reduction at
t
, then there are
only finitely many invariant leaves at
t
, and their images
in the special fiber are exactly the torsion points of the
fiber.

(4)

A section is a complex Betti leaf if and only if it is
torsion.

(In the additive case, the order of the torsion points in the special
fiber is the same as the order of the component group, namely 1, 2, 3,
or 4.)

Remark 4.3
.

If

is isotrivial, then the period function

at least 2. In general, we have

I(P,t)=\begin{cases}\mu(h)&\text{if $\mu(h)\neq\mu(\tau)$}\\
\geq\mu(h)+1&\text{if $\mu(h)=\mu(\tau)$}\end{cases}

Note that in this case
I(P,t)\geq 1
and
I(P,t)\geq 2
if
\tau^{\prime}(t)\neq 0
.

If
E
has multiplicative reduction at
t
, then the invariant leaves
are parameterized by
b\in\mathbb{C}
, there is a unique leaf meeting
P
(the
one corresponding to
b=h(0)
), and we have

I(P,t)=\ord_{z=0}\left(h-h(0)\right)=\mu(h)\geq 1.

If
E
has additive reduction at
t
, the only invariant leaf is the
zero section, and we have

I(P,t)=\ord_{z=0}\left(h(z)\right)\geq 0.

4.5.
The Manin map

Let
K=\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{C})
and let
E/K
be the generic fiber of
\pi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}
.
Then the set of sections of
\pi
can be identified with the
Mordell-Weil group
E(K)
. In
[
3
]
, Manin defines a
homomorphism

M:E(K)\to K

which depends on the choice of an invariant differential on
E
and on
a “Picard-Fuchs operator”
L
, i.e., a second-order differential
operator which kills the periods of the fibers of
\pi
. (We will
make this more explicit in one example below.) In terms of the
uniformization of Section
4.1
and a Weierstrass equation

y^{2}=f(x)
, we have

M(P)=\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}

(4.3)

where the integral is a function on
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}^{0}}
given by
integrating from
O
to
P(t)
along a path in the fiber

\pi^{-1}(t)
. The values of the integral are only well defined up to
periods, and these are killed by the second-order derivative, so

M(P)
defines a meromorphic function on
\mathcal{C}
. As explained in

[
4
]
, one may make this independent of choices by dividing
by the symbol of
d^{2}/d\tau^{2}
(i.e., multiplying by the
quadratic differential
(d\tau)^{\otimes 2}
) and dividing by the
section
dx/y
of
\omega
. This yields a map

P\mapsto\mathcal{M}(P):=\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}\right)\frac{(d\tau)^{2}}{dx/y}

(4.4)

from
E(K)
to
meromorphic sections of the line bundle

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}.

We will quantify the possible poles of
\mathcal{M}(P)
in
Proposition
4.7
below.

An alternative point of view is afforded by the exact
sequence (
4.2
). If
P
lies in
\mathcal{E}^{id}
, computing the
integral
\int_{O}^{P}dx/y
amounts to lifting
P
to a section of

\omega^{-1}
. A simple calculation shows that taking the second
derivative with respect to
\tau
yields a (rational) section of

\omega^{3}
, and the Kodaira-Spencer map

\omega\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\omega^{-1}
then gives a section of

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}
.

Define
J(P,t)
as the order of
\mathcal{M}(P)
:

J(P,t):=\ord_{t}\mathcal{M}(P),

where the order is computed as a section of

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}
.

Define a set
S
of points of
\mathcal{C}
by

S=\left\{t\in\mathcal{C}\mid\text{$\mathcal{E}$ has bad reduction at $t$}\right\}\cup\left\{t\in\mathcal{C}\mid\text{$j(t)\neq 0,1728$ and $\ord_{t}(dj)>0$}\right\}\\
\cup\left\{t\in\mathcal{C}\mid\text{$j(t)=0$ and $\ord_{t}(dj)>2$}\right\}\cup\left\{t\in\mathcal{C}\mid\text{$j(t)=1728$ and $\ord_{t}(dj)>1$}\right\}.

The points in
S
are where either
\mathcal{E}
has bad reduction or a
branch of the period
\tau
is not a uniformizer:

Lemma 4.6
.

If
t\in\mathcal{C}\setminus S
and
\tilde{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}
lies over
t
, then

\tau^{\prime}(\tilde{t})\neq 0
.

Proof.

This follows from the well-known fact that
j
, considered as a map
from the upper half plane to
\mathbb{C}
, is unramified away from
j=0
or

1728
, and it is ramified of order 3 over
j=0
and of order 2 over

j=1728
.
∎

The following is the key connection between intersection numbers and the
Manin map.

Proposition 4.7
.

If
t\not\in S
,

J(P,t)=I(P,t)-2\geq 0.

If
t\in S
,

J(P,t)\geq-1.

Proof.

Suppose that
t\not\in S
and choose a Weierstrass equation
y^{2}=f(x)

for
E
which is regular minimal at
t
. Since
\tau
is a
uniformizer at
t
,
(d\tau)^{2}/(dx/y)
is a generating section of

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}
near
t
.
Comparing (
4.4
) and (
4.3
), we see that

J(P,t)=\ord_{t}\mathcal{M}(P)
is the usual ord at
t
of the function
M(P)
:

J(P,t)=\ord_{t}M(P).

Expanding the integral
\int_{O}^{P}dx/y
as a series in
\tau
near
t
,
say

\int_{O}^{P}dx/y=a_{0}+a_{1}\tau+a_{I}\tau^{I}+\cdots

(where
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
might be zero), we find

\ord_{t}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}\right)=I(P,T)-2\geq 0.

Now suppose that
t\in S
, and choose a uniformizer
z
at
t
. We
rewrite
d^{2}/d\tau^{2}
in terms of
z
:

\frac{d}{d\tau}=\left(\frac{d\tau}{dz}\right)^{-1}\frac{d}{dz}\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}=\left(\frac{d\tau}{dz}\right)^{-2}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-\left(\frac{d\tau}{dz}\right)^{-3}\frac{d^{2}\tau}{dz^{2}}\frac{d}{dz}.

Computing the right hand side of (
4.4
) divided by the
generating section
(dz)^{2}/(dx/y)
of

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}
near
t
,
we obtain

H=\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}-\left(\frac{d\tau}{dz}\right)^{-1}\frac{d^{2}\tau}{dz^{2}}\frac{d}{dz}\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y},

so
J(P,t)=\ord_{t}(H)
. The derivatives of the integrals are regular,
but the factor

d\log\left(\frac{d\tau}{dz}\right)=\left(\frac{d\tau}{dz}\right)^{-1}\frac{d^{2}\tau}{dz^{2}}

could have a simple pole (and does except when
\tau
is a
uniformizer at
t
and this might happen if
\mathcal{E}
has reduction
type
I_{0}^{*}
there). Thus

J(P,t)\geq-1
for
t\in S
.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

The proof shows why we cannot bound
I(P,t)
above in terms of

J(P,t)
at points
t\in S
: If
0<m<n
,

\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}=a_{0}+a_{m}z^{m}+a_{n}z^{n}+O(z^{n+1}),

and

\tau=b_{0}+b_{m}z^{m}+O(z^{n+1}),

then for generic choices of the coefficients,
I(P,t)=n
whereas

J(P,t)=m-2
, so
J(P,t)<I(P,t)-(n-m+1)
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let

T_{\mathbb{C}}=\left\{t\in\mathcal{C}\mid\text{$t\not\in S$ and $I(P,t)>2$}\right\}.

Then

|T_{\mathbb{C}}|\leq 4g-4-d+|S|.

Proof.

Indeed,

|T_{\mathbb{C}}|\leq\sum_{t\not\in S}\left(I(P,t)-2\right)=\sum_{t\not\in S}J(P,t)

and the latter is the sum of all but finitely many ords of a
regular section of

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}(S)
.
∎

4.10.
Corrected algebraic Manin map

As above, let
K=\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{C})
and let
E
over
K
be given by a
Weierstrass equation
y^{2}=f(x)
. It is not difficult to express the
Manin map for this situation in algebraic coordinates, i.e., in terms
of the function field
K
. However, there are at least two
incorrect statements of the formula in the literature, so we give a
careful derivation here which we will use below in examples.

Choose a non-trivial
\mathbb{C}
-derivation
\delta
of
K
and let
\eta
be
the meromorphic differential on
\mathbb{C}
dual to
\delta
. Extend

\delta
to
K
by setting
\delta x=0
. Extend
\delta
to
differentials of
E
by setting
\delta(h\,dx)=(\delta h)\,dx
. We use
a prime (
′
) to denote the action of
\delta
on elements of
K
to
distinguish this action from the lifts.

Suppose that
A,B,C\in K
are such that

L=A\delta^{2}+B\delta+C

is a second order differential operator with

L\left(\frac{dx}{y}\right)=dF

with
F\in K(E)
, i.e.,
L(dx/y)
is exact. Then we get a
well-defined Manin map

M(P):=L\int_{0}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}

and its invariant version

\mathcal{M}(P)=M(P)\frac{\eta^{2}}{A(dx/y)}

which is a meromorphic section of

\left(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega^{-1}

independent of the choices of
\delta
and the model of
E
.

The Manin map kills 2-torsion points (indeed, any torsion points), so
it suffices to give a formula for it which is valid when
y(P)\neq 0
.

Proposition 4.11
.

We have

M(P)=F(P)-F(O)+A\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}(-\delta f)(P)}{2y(P)^{3}}+\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right)^{\prime}\right)+B\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right).

Proof.

The tricky point is to evaluate the terms coming from the fact
that the limits of integration vary on
\mathcal{C}
. The model
y^{2}=f(x)

presents
E
as a double cover of the Riemann sphere. Making branch
cuts (say from 0 to 1 and from
t
to
\infty
and choosing a branch
of
y=f^{1/2}
we have

\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}=\int_{\infty}^{x(P)}\frac{1}{y}\,dx=\int_{\infty}^{x(P)}f^{-1/2}\,dx

where the second integral is along a path in the Riemann sphere
avoiding the branch cuts. We have

\delta\int_{\infty}^{x(P)}\frac{1}{y}\,dx=\int_{\infty}^{x(P)}\delta\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\,dx+\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)},

and since
\delta(1/y)=(-\delta y)/y^{2}=(-\delta f)/(2y^{3})
,

\delta^{2}\int_{\infty}^{x(P)}\frac{1}{y}\,dx=\int_{\infty}^{x(P)}\delta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\,dx+\frac{x(P)^{\prime}(-\delta f)(P)}{2y(P)^{3}}+\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right)^{\prime}.

(The second term on the right is the source of trouble in other
versions. The correct order is to apply the
\delta
to the
integrand first and then evaluate.)
Putting everything together,

\displaystyle L\int_{O}^{P}\frac{dx}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{O}^{P}L\frac{dx}{y}+A\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}(-\delta f)(P)}{2y(P)^{3}}+\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right)^{\prime}\right)+B\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right)

\displaystyle=F(P)-F(O)+A\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}(-\delta f)(P)}{2y(P)^{3}}+\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right)^{\prime}\right)+B\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right).

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

4.12.
Examples

4.12.1.
Manin for Legendre

Let
K=\mathbb{C}(t)
with
\delta=d/dt
, and let
E
be given by

E:\quad y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-t).

Then it is well known (and easy to check by hand) that

\left(t(1-t)\delta^{2}+(1-2t)\delta-\frac{1}{4}\right)\left(\frac{dx}{y}\right)=d\left(\frac{y}{2(x-t)^{2}}\right).

Applying the proposition, we find that

M(P)=\frac{y(P)}{2(x(P)-t)^{2}}+t(1-t)\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}x(P)(x(P)-1)}{2y(P)^{3}}+\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right)^{\prime}\right)+(1-2t)\left(\frac{x(P)^{\prime}}{y(P)}\right).

4.12.2.
Tangencies on Legendre 1

Now let
L/K
be the quadratic extension
L=\mathbb{C}(s)
where

t=2-s^{2}/2
and consider the point
P=(2,s)
of
E(L)
.
Applying the formula above, we find that

\mathcal{M}(P)=\frac{-8}{s(s^{2}-4)(s^{2}-2)}\frac{(ds)^{2}}{(dx/y)},

with divisor

\dvsr(\mathcal{M}(P))=2(\infty)-(s(s^{2}-4)(s^{2}-2)).

Since the support of the divisor of
\mathcal{M}(P)
is contained in the
exceptional set
S
, Corollary
4.9
shows that there
are no higher order tangencies between
P
and the complex Betti
foliation away from
S
, i.e.,

T_{\mathbb{C}}=\emptyset.

4.12.3.
Tangencies on Legendre 2

Similar results apply for the point
(a,s)
where

and

,

\mathcal{M}(P)=\frac{-2a^{2}(a-1)^{2}}{s\left(s^{2}-a^{2}(a-1)\right)\left(s^{2}-a(a-1)^{2}\right)}\frac{(ds)^{2}}{(dx/y)},

with divisor

Again, the support of the divisor of
\mathcal{M}(P)

exceptional set
S
, so Corollary
4.9
shows that there
are no higher order tangencies between
P
and the complex Betti
foliation away from
S
, i.e.,

T_{\mathbb{C}}=\emptyset.

4.12.4.
Tangencies on Legendre 3

Now let
L

generated by
