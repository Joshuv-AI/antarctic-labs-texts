Cardiovascular preventive role of thiazolidinedione in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Jinkwon Kim, Jimin Jeon, Joonsang Yoo, Minyoul Baik, Seok‐Jae Heo, Ji Woong Roh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThiazolidinediones (TZD) are a class of oral antidiabetic medications that improve glycemic control and insulin sensitivity. We evaluated the cardiovascular outcomes according to thiazolidinedione (TZD) use in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsUsing the national health claims database in South Korea, we constructed a cohort of type 2 diabetes mellitus patients who underwent PCI between 2015 and 2022. Patients were classified as TZD users or nonusers and matched at a 1:3 ratio using propensity score matching. Stratified Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the risk for the primary composite outcome, which included myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, and all‐cause death.ResultsAmong 169,747 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus who underwent PCI, 7.4% were TZD users. During a mean follow‐up of 3.63 ± 2.30 years, 31,813 patients (18.7%) experienced the primary outcome. In the stratified Cox regression analysis, which consisted of 12,597 TZD users and 37,791 nonusers, TZD treatment was associated with a lower risk of primary outcome (HR 0.87, 95% CI: 0.83–0.92, P < 0.001). No difference was observed in the risk of heart failure admission between TZD nonusers and users.ConclusionsTZD treatment in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients undergoing PCI was associated with a reduced risk of adverse cardiovascular events.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) are at increased risk for the development of major cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including stroke and coronary artery disease (CAD). 1 , 2 , 3 CAD is a leading cause of death in patients with diabetes; percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a nonsurgical, invasive procedure to treat significantly narrowed coronary arteries, improving symptoms and clinical outcomes. 4 The presence of DM is an independent risk factor for poor prognosis after PCI. 5 Therefore, effective preventive strategies are critical to prevent future clinical events in patients with DM undergoing PCI.

Antidiabetic medications not only affect glycemic control but may also prevent future cardiovascular events in high‐risk diabetic patients. 6 , 7 Several studies have investigated the efficacy of different classes of antidiabetic drugs in preventing cardiovascular complications, based on their different mechanisms of action with pleiotropic effects. 8 Thiazolidinediones (TZDs), a class of oral antidiabetic medications, play a crucial role in activating peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR)‐γ, affecting lipid and energy metabolism and improving insulin sensitivity. 9 TZDs have been found to possess multiple pleiotropic effects, particularly anti‐atherosclerotic properties. 10 Meta‐analyses of randomized clinical trials have shown that treatment with pioglitazone, the most widely used TZD, reduces the risk of major cardiovascular events, particularly in patients with established CVD. 11 Lobeglitazone, a newly developed TZD with higher affinity for PPAR‐γ, was also effective in secondary cardiovascular prevention in patients with stroke, according to a recent cohort study. 12 However, real‐world evidence regarding cardiovascular outcomes and safety of TZD, especially in high‐risk populations such as patients with DM undergoing PCI, remains insufficient. Therefore, we investigated the long‐term cardiovascular outcomes and safety according to TZD use in patients with DM undergoing PCI using the nationwide health claims database in Korea.

METHODS

Source of data

South Korea's National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) provides public health insurance for all citizens. The Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) oversees its review and quality control. After submitting a research plan and obtaining approval from the HIRA, we accessed the anonymized nationwide claims database. The database contains information on hospital visits, diagnoses based on the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD‐10), medical procedures, prescription records, and demographics of individual participants.

Study participants

We identified participants aged 18 years or older who underwent PCI, including balloon angioplasty and bare metal or drug‐eluting stent insertion (claim codes of ‘M6551–4’, ‘M6561–7’, or ‘M6571–2’) between January 2015 and December 2022. The index date was defined as the date of the claim for PCI. We excluded those who underwent PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) before the index date or during the washout period of 2014. Among them, we selected patients with type 2 DM, defined by the presence of claims for antidiabetic medications, along with a related diagnostic code (‘E11’, ‘E12’, ‘E13’, or ‘E14’) before or on the index date. Study participants were followed up until the first occurrence of the study outcome, the end of the study period (June 2023), or the last date of an observable HIRA claim record. Patients who did not receive antiplatelet therapy following PCI were excluded, as they were unlikely to receive standard treatment. In addition, patients hospitalized for more than 30 days for the index PCI, or those experiencing early events or short follow‐up (≤30 days), were excluded to ensure the stability of medication classification and to prevent the inclusion of outcomes potentially related to the procedure itself rather than the pharmacological intervention. Propensity score matching

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to minimize potential bias from differences in characteristics between TZD users and nonusers. For each participant in the study cohort, the propensity score was calculated to estimate the likelihood of TZD use, based on a binary logistic regression model with the collected covariates except TZD use (variables are listed in Figure 1). Patients treated with TZD (TZD group) were matched in a 1:3 ratio to those not treated with TZDs (non‐TZD group) using the nearest‐neighbor method without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.10. To assess the balance of characteristics, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated between the groups, with an absolute SMD <0.1 considered to indicate appropriate balance.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was defined as the composite development of myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, and all‐cause death after the 30‐day landmark period following the index PCI, whichever occurred first. Through a review of the health claims, development of MI was determined as admission with the primary diagnosis of ‘I21‐22’ accompanied by the claim codes of fibrinolytic treatment or coronary revascularization (PCI or CABG) for acute MI (‘M6553’ or ‘M6565’). 13 Development of stroke was identified as admission with the primary diagnosis of ‘I60‐63’ (I60‐62: hemorrhagic stroke, I63: ischemic stroke) accompanied by brain computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. 14 The diagnostic accuracy of stroke and MI based on health claims data in Korea is known to be high and has been widely used in epidemiologic studies. 15 Mortality data were available in linkage with the national statistical office in Korea. As secondary outcomes, we also evaluated the development of the individual outcomes of MI, stroke, and all‐cause mortality.

Safety outcome

Increasing the risk of heart failure (HF) is a concern with TZD treatment. Therefore, we evaluated the risk of admission for HF according to TZD treatment as a safety outcome measure. We compared the occurrence of admission for HF throughout the study follow‐up, which was identified as admission with a primary diagnostic code for HF (‘I11.0’, ‘I50’, or ‘I97.1’). 16

Use of TZD and other medications

Antidiabetic medications were classified into TZDs, sulfonylureas or meglitinides (insulin stimulators), biguanides, dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors, SGLT2 (sodium–glucose cotransporter 2) inhibitors, alpha‐glucosidase inhibitors, GLP‐1 (glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor) agonists, and insulin. During the study period, two TZDs (pioglitazone and lobeglitazone) were available in South Korea. 17 For each study patient, we determined the use of TZDs, other classes of antidiabetic medications, aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors, calcium channel blockers, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors, beta blockers, spironolactone, loop diuretics, and statins after PCI based on the prescription records. This determination was made by the presence of at least one exposure to the medications within the 30‐day period following PCI, which served as the landmark period for evaluating medication usage and ensuring that exposure assessment preceded the observation of study outcomes.

Covariates

Data on sex, age, and the presence of comorbidities, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, HF, atrial fibrillation, peripheral artery disease, prior stroke, renal disease, and malignancy, were collected. Baseline characteristics and comorbidities were identified based on the claims data from 2014 to the index PCI date. Detailed definitions of comorbidities are available in Table S1. The public health care service in South Korea has a two‐tiered system, with the lowest‐income population supported by the Medical Aid program while the rest is covered by the National Health Insurance. 18 Whether PCI was performed for the treatment of acute MI was determined based on the presence of a diagnostic code for acute MI (‘I21’ or ‘I22’) at the index admission or the presence of claims codes of PCI for acute MI (‘M6553’ or ‘M6565’). The type of PCI was classified into: (1) angioplasty only; (2) bare metal stent insertion (‘J5231’, ‘J5232’, or ‘J8231’) with or without angioplasty; and (3) drug‐eluting stent insertion (‘J5083’, ‘J8083’, or ‘J5084’) with or without angioplasty. 19 Patients who received both drug‐eluting and bare metal stents were classified as receiving a drug‐eluting stent. According to the number of coronary artery stents used, participants were subdivided into those who received only angioplasty or one coronary stent (0–1), and multiple coronary stents (≥2).

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of study participants are presented as number (%) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for age. To assess the relationship between TZD use and prognosis, we performed time‐to‐event analysis (survival analysis) with the TZD and non‐TZD groups after 1:3 ratio PSM. Cumulative incidence curves for the primary and safety outcomes were illustrated, and the curves of the TZD and non‐TZD groups were compared by a stratified log‐rank test. To estimate the risk for study outcomes between the two groups, we calculated the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) based on the stratified Cox proportional hazard regression model. The proportional hazard assumption for TZD in the stratified Cox regression model was verified by calculating the Schoenfeld residuals, which were found to be satisfied.

To evaluate the effect of TZD on the secondary outcomes, separate cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed for stroke, MI, and all‐cause death. In the competing risks framework, each event type was treated as the cause of interest in turn, while the remaining events served as competing risks. Patients who experienced a competing event prior to the occurrence of the event of interest were censored at the time of that competing event, thereby allowing each cause‐specific hazard to be estimated independently. If MI and death occurred on the same day, the outcome for the patient was defined as MI; if stroke and death occurred on the same day, the outcome was defined as stroke, ensuring that no event was left ambiguously attributed. To address the potential interaction between the effect of TZD and specific subgroups, we performed a subgroup analysis based on sex, age (≤65 and >65), PCI for acute MI, PCI stent type (drug‐eluting stent or not), prior stroke, hypertension, HF, renal disease, treatment with SGLT2 inhibitor, GLP‐1 agonist, and insulin use. For the subgroup analysis with PSM, we subdivided the total study population into the relevant subgroups. Within each subgroup, we re‐constructed a 1:3 PSM dataset, and then compared the risk between the TZD and non‐TZD groups using stratified Cox regression with the PSM samples. 20

For sensitivity analyses, we further subdivided the TZD group (at least one exposure to the medications within the 30‐day period following PCI) into ‘prevalent TZD users’ defined as patients who had at least one TZD exposure between 180 and 30 days before the index PCI and ‘non‐prevalent TZD users’ defined as those without TZD exposure between 180 and 30 days before the index PCI. Then, separate analyses were performed for each subgroup against the matched non‐TZD group to ensure consistency of the treatment effect. To describe changes in antidiabetic medication use during follow‐up, we additionally evaluated the use of each class of antidiabetic medication, including TZD, in both the baseline TZD and non‐TZD groups.

In addition, we performed a time‐varying Cox regression analysis incorporating antidiabetic medications as time‐varying covariates in the entire eligible study cohort before PSM, using all available patients in the database who met the study eligibility criteria. In the time‐varying analysis, medication exposure status was updated daily according to prescription coverage, ensuring alignment between exposure status and the corresponding risk period.

Data manipulation and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Two‐sided P‐values <0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Study participants and characteristics

A total of 489,152 patients received PCI between 2015 and 2022. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we constructed a cohort of 169,747 patients with type 2 DM undergoing index PCI (Figure 1). The characteristics of the study cohort before PSM are given in Table 1. The cohort comprised 69.6% men, with a mean age of 66.9 years. PCI for acute MI was performed in 34.9% of patients and 94.0% of patients underwent PCI with a drug‐eluting stent. The proportion of patients who received TZD was 7.4%. After 1:3 ratio PSM (Figure 1), 50,388 patients were included in the final analysis, consisting of 12,597 in the TZD group and 37,791 in the non‐TZD group. Baseline characteristics were well‐balanced between the groups (Table 1).

Risk for primary outcome

Before PSM, the total study population had a mean follow‐up period of 3.63 ± 2.30 years. The primary outcome occurred in 31,813 patients (18.7%), comprising 6,265 cases of MI, 6,353 cases of stroke, and 19,195 cases of all‐cause death. After 1:3 PSM, the mean follow‐up durations were 3.68 ± 2.26 and 3.66 ± 2.30 years for the TZD and non‐TZD groups, respectively. In the matched cohort, 9,212 patients experienced a primary outcome, of which 2,123 patients (16.9%) came from the TZD group while 7,089 patients (18.8%) came from the non‐TZD group. Cumulative incidence curves of the matched cohorts showed lower risk for the primary outcome after PCI in the TZD group compared with the non‐TZD group (P < 0.001, Figure 2A). In the stratified Cox regression analysis with the matched cohort (Table 2), the TZD group had significantly lower risk for the primary outcome compared with the non‐TZD group (HR 0.87, 95% CI: 0.83–0.92, P < 0.001). When the TZD group was further divided into pioglitazone and lobeglitazone, both pioglitazone (HR 0.87, 95% CI: 0.81–0.92, P < 0.001) and lobeglitazone (HR 0.89, 95% CI: 0.80–0.99, P = 0.039) were associated with fewer primary outcome events compared with the non‐TZD group.

Risk for secondary outcome

When we conducted separate cause‐specific Cox regression analyses for individual secondary outcomes with the matched cohort (Table 2), the TZD group had significantly reduced risks of MI (HR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.75–0.96, P = 0.009), stroke (HR 0.84, 95% CI: 0.75–0.95, P = 0.005), and all‐cause death (HR 0.89, 95% CI: 0.83–0.96, P = 0.001) compared with the non‐TZD group.

Safety analysis

In the matched cohort, 2,386 patients were admitted for HF, of which 574 patients (4.6%) came from the TZD group and 1,812 patients (4.8%) from the non‐TZD group. Cumulative incidence curves for HF admission in the matched cohort did not differ between the two groups (Figure 2B, P = 0.091). In the stratified Cox regression analysis, TZD treatment was not associated with an increased risk of admission for HF (HR 0.91, 95% CI: 0.83–1.01, P = 0.090). Compared with the non‐TZD group, use of pioglitazone (HR 0.89, 95% CI: 0.79–1.00, P = 0.057) and lobeglitazone (HR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.81–1.21, P = 0.919) showed no difference in the risk of HF admission.

Subgroup analysis

We performed subgroup analyses to explore whether TZD exerted consistent effects across different patient groups in terms of sex, age, PCI for acute MI, PCI stent type, hypertension, prior stroke, HF, renal disease, treatment with SGLT2 inhibitor, GLP‐1 agonist, and insulin use (Figure 3). Overall, the findings of the lower risk of primary outcome occurrence with TZD treatment were consistent across the subgroups, and no significant interaction was observed. For the safety outcome of admission for HF, no subgroup demonstrated a significantly increased risk with TZD, and no significant interaction was observed.

Sensitivity analyses

To further address potential interactions related to prior TZD use before PCI, the TZD group (defined as those with at least one exposure within 30 days post‐PCI; N = 12,597) was stratified into ‘prevalent TZD users’ (N = 9,917) and ‘non‐prevalent TZD users’ (N = 2,680) based on their exposure between 180 and 30 days before the index PCI. This sensitivity analysis (Table S2) yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis; both prevalent and nonprevalent TZD users exhibited a lower risk of the primary outcome, with no significant change in the risk of HF admission compared with non‐TZD users.

We also evaluated the use of antidiabetic medications including TZD throughout the follow‐up period in both the baseline TZD and non‐TZD groups (Table S3). Among the baseline TZD group (N = 12,597), the proportion of patients treated with TZD decreased to 53.5% at 180 days and 48.3% at 1 year, and then gradually declined to 30.4% at 5 years. Conversely, among the baseline non‐TZD group (N = 37,791), TZD use during follow‐up gradually increased from 1.6% at 180 days to 6.5% at 5 years. Use of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 receptor agonists also showed gradual increases over time in both groups, suggesting dynamic changes in glucose‐lowering therapy during follow‐up. These findings support the need for additional analyses accounting for dynamic changes in antidiabetic medications during follow‐up.

Accordingly, the time‐varying Cox regression analysis was conducted in the entire cohort of type 2 diabetes mellitus patients who underwent PCI (N = 169,747; indicated as ‘Study cohort’ in Figure 1). In this analysis, antidiabetic medication use was treated as a time‐varying covariate to account for treatment changes during follow‐up. TZD use during the follow‐up period after PCI was significantly associated with a lower risk for the primary outcome, but was not associated with admission for HF (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this large population‐based cohort study, TZD treatment was associated with a lower risk of future cardiovascular events in type 2 DM patients undergoing PCI. This cardiovascular preventive effect was consistent in those treated with pioglitazone and lobeglitazone. From a safety perspective, no increase in admission for HF was observed. In the subgroup analysis, no significant interaction was observed between the beneficial effect of TZD and sex, age, clinical presentation, and type of revascularization. The results of this study suggest that TZDs, originally developed as antidiabetic agents for type 2 DM, may have a potential benefit in preventing future cardiovascular events in patients who have undergone PCI without increasing the risk of HF admission.

Intensive glycemic control in patients with type 2 DM has traditionally been associated with a reduction in microvascular complications, but its impact on macrovascular events such as CVD remains less certain. Major trials such as ACCORD and ADVANCE failed to demonstrate a significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality with intensive glycemic control, and in some cases, showed an increase in mortality and admission, raising concerns about the cardiovascular safety of aggressive glucose‐lowering strategies, particularly in patients with type 2 DM. 21 , 22 Recently, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 agonists have shown clear benefits for macrovascular events without the concern for hypoglycemia, leading to a substantial increase in use. However, SGLT2 inhibitors may cause urinary tract infections in women and severe dehydration with worsening kidney function in older patients, while GLP‐1 agonists may affect mood and gastrointestinal problems associated with slow gastric emptying due to their incretin effects. 23 , 24 , 25

Even before the introduction of SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 agonists, TZDs were used to potentially improve cardiovascular outcomes without the risk of hypoglycemia. TZDs are synthetic agonists of PPAR‐γ, a transcription factor primarily responsible for the regulation of lipid/glucose metabolism and adipogenesis. 26 PPAR‐γ activation is implicated in whole‐body energy homeostasis and insulin sensitivity, acting on several tissues including adipose cells, muscles, liver, heart, macrophages, lymphocytes, vascular endothelium, and pancreatic β‐cells. 27 , 28 , 29 In addition to their metabolic effects, TZDs have shown pleiotropic effects that could reduce the progression of atherosclerosis by improving endothelial function, reducing intimal hyperplasia and arterial stiffness, suppressing inflammatory pathways, reducing oxidative stress, and stabilizing atherosclerotic plaques. 30 , 31 , 32 In addition to experimental data on the benefits of TZDs, clinical evidence also suggests the potential protective role of TZDs in atherosclerosis‐related vascular events, including coronary, cerebral, and peripheral artery disease. 10 , 12 Studies in patients with CAD have documented a decrease in coronary artery inflammation, stabilization of coronary plaque, and reduction in coronary atheroma volume with pioglitazone treatment through intravascular coronary artery imaging. 33 , 34 A meta‐analysis in patients with diabetes and coronary atherosclerosis showed that TZD treatment resulted in an overall increase in adiponectin and a decrease in plaque volume, suggesting a protective effect on the vascular endothelium. 35 In addition, significant reductions in neointimal proliferation, in‐stent restenosis, and target lesion revascularization after PCI with stent implantation have been observed in patients with type 2 DM treated with TZD. 36 , 37

Despite these promising findings, TZD use has been limited in clinical practice due to concerns about potential side effects, particularly the risk of HF. 38 , 39 Indeed, rosiglitazone, another TZD drug, was withdrawn from the market due to reports of increased cardiovascular risk and mortality, specifically related to HF. 40 , 41 However, current evidence from studies on pioglitazone shows the risk of HF is primarily due to fluid retention without an increase in cardiovascular mortality or worsening cardiac function. 42 Furthermore, TZDs including pioglitazone can positively modulate several cardiac and vascular functions and risk factors, primarily through activation of PPAR‐γ. 10 , 43 Further research is needed on the cardiovascular protective effects and potential risk associated with TZDs, and to identify the patient groups that can maximally benefit from TZD use while minimizing potential harmful effects.

Consistent with previous research, the current study provides additional evidence that TZD treatment offers a favorable cardiovascular prognosis for high‐risk patients with type 2 DM undergoing PCI without an increase in HF admission. In an aging society with increasing incidence of CAD and PCI in patients with type 2 DM, the ability of TZDs to improve insulin sensitivity and provide cardiovascular protection makes them a viable option for patients who cannot tolerate other therapies due to their side effects or have previously used SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 agonists and have not achieved their HbA1c goal. 40 TZDs may be particularly beneficial as an alternative to SGLT2 inhibitors in women with urinary tract problems and in the older or dehydration‐prone patients. TZDs do not promote diuresis and do not increase the risk of dehydration or acute kidney injury, making them a safer option for patients who are older or with impaired renal function. In addition, for patients who experience significant gastrointestinal side effects or weight loss with GLP‐1 agonists, TZDs offer an alternative that does not affect appetite and can provide stable glycemic control without the associated risks of nausea or vomiting.

This study used a landmark design in which medication exposure was defined according to the use of each medication during the first 30 days after PCI. Subgroup analyses confirmed that the preventive benefits of TZD remained consistent across various baseline medication profiles, including those of SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP‐1 agonists, and insulin. While this approach reflects the initial therapeutic strategy and reduces potential immortal time bias, it does not fully account for subsequent medication switching or discontinuation during the long‐term follow‐up, which was common in real‐world practice.

In the current study, we observed a relatively high rate of TZD discontinuation during the early period after PCI. Although the specific reasons for TZD discontinuation could not be directly ascertained from the claims database, this pattern should be interpreted in the clinical context of a post‐PCI cohort. First, TZDs are commonly discontinued in routine practice because of concerns regarding fluid retention and HF, which may be particularly relevant in patients after coronary revascularization who are vulnerable to left ventricular dysfunction. Second, during the study period, glucose‐lowering strategies for patients with diabetes and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease increasingly emphasized agents with favorable cardiovascular profiles, such as SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 agonists. Third, in the Korean shared‐care setting, antidiabetic medications are often re‐evaluated after PCI in consultation with diabetes care providers. Therefore, the observed discontinuation of TZD may reflect clinically motivated treatment re‐evaluation. To further address the potential influence of treatment changes during follow‐up, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a time‐varying Cox regression model, which yielded results consistent with the main analysis, showing a beneficial association of TZD with the primary outcome and no significant increase in the risk of HF admission.

The current study has several strengths and limitations. Using nationwide claims data, we were able to collect a large number of patients with type 2 DM undergoing PCI in real‐world practice. Due to the nature of claims‐based research, events without hospital visit may be missed. Nevertheless, all‐cause death was precisely captured through linkage with national death records from Statistics Korea. The lower mortality risk seen in TZD users reinforces the robust protective effects of TZD following PCI. This population‐based data source enhanced the representativeness and accuracy of the results. Moreover, several subgroup analyses were conducted to further validate the findings across patient subgroups. However, a retrospective study based on a pre‐existing health claims database also has limitations. Because the claims database was not originally intended for research purposes, clinical data on traditional risk factors such as smoking, physical activity, blood pressure, laboratory results including lipid profiles or inflammatory markers, severity of CAD, echocardiography, duration of type 2 DM, body weight, and hemoglobin A1c levels are lacking. Although we attempted to minimize potential bias due to differences in characteristics through PSM, unmeasured confounders could still introduce bias into the results. Furthermore, this study was conducted only in Korean patients with type 2 DM undergoing PCI. Racial and ethnic differences may exist. Finally, medication use was determined from prescription records, and discrepancies between prescription data and actual medication use by patients may occur. The clinical reasons for TZD discontinuation during follow‐up could not be ascertained from the claims database. Notably, this study could not account for treatment duration or cumulative dose, which should be considered an additional limitation. Future research incorporating these dynamic treatment parameters would provide additional insights into the long‐term safety and efficacy of TZD use.

In conclusion, treatment with a TZD was associated with a reduced risk of future cardiovascular outcomes, including MI, stroke, and all‐cause mortality in patients with type 2 DM undergoing PCI. The risk of HF admission was not increased with TZD use. Further large, randomized trials are needed to determine the cardioprotective effects and safety of TZDs.
