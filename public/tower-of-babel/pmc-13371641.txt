Cross sectional examination of public perceptions around substance misuse using state behavioral risk factor surveillance system data
Authors: Elise V. Bailey, Evan V. Goldstein, Papungkorn Kitcharoenkarnkul, Erik J. Hansen, Laura Summers, Fernando A. Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to explore public sympathy toward individuals with substance misuse, including relationships between that phenomenon and demographic, socioeconomic, and health factors in a single U.S. state.MethodsWeighted summary statistics and logistic regression analyses were conducted using data from the 2021 Utah Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. The outcome measure was derived from a unique question which asked about sympathy toward those misusing substances, which may reflect behaviors indicative of or preceding substance use disorder (SUD). Other measures included standard demographics; education; religious affiliation; veteran status; LGBTQ+ status; history of cigarette, alcohol, and marijuana use; and health status.ResultsOlder age, Hispanic race/ethnicity, affiliation with several Christian religions, residence in rural counties, and lack of health insurance were negatively related to sympathy toward individuals misusing substances. Those with past-month marijuana use, higher education, and current poor mental health were significantly more likely than their counterparts to express this sympathy.ConclusionsThis state-level measure of sympathy toward those misusing substances allowed for detection of both previously known and potentially newly identified predictors of sympathy around substance misuse and, potentially, disorders. Efforts should be made to measure SUD sympathy or stigma in nationally-representative datasets. The knowledge derived from such data could be used to increase SUD sympathy and reduce SUD stigma. In addition, further research in this area should focus on education level and religion.

Background

Substance misuse—the use of substances including alcohol or drugs in a way that can cause harm—is one of the costliest health problems in the U.S., with some estimates showing costs upwards of $400 billion annually in crime, health, and lost productivity [1, 2]. Some proportion of individuals who misuse substances will subsequently develop a clinically diagnosable substance use disorder (SUDs). SUDs are treatable but highly stigmatized chronic diseases in which cognitive and behavioral symptoms can lead to continued substance use despite the harmful consequences. In this way, substance misuse may also be indicative of a SUD [2]. In 2020, over 40 million Americans aged 12 or older had an SUD in the past year [1–3].

Although negative public perceptions and stigmatizing beliefs around drug use can be protective against initial experimentation among non-users, they also tend to negatively impact individuals already misusing drugs by acting as a barrier to treatment initiation, treatment continuation, connection with treatment, and treatment adherence [4–10]. Public perceptions toward those misusing substances or who have SUDs also likely informs policy decisions about this population. Public policy theory posits that policy will benefit and burden groups differently based on how the groups are perceived, including whether that perception is positive or negative [11]. Research has shown that this theory bears out in reality—negative attitudes about SUDs are associated with support for punishments over public health-oriented policies [12–14]. For these reasons, attitudes toward individuals with substance misuse may have important population health, policy, and economic consequences.

Despite this, there are considerable gaps in understanding attitudes toward individuals who misuse substances or have SUDs in the U.S. Few studies have systematically examined the factors that may predict such attitudes. The research that does exist provides some evidence that sociodemographic, physical health, and mental health factors may be related to an individual’s perceptions of those who misuse substance or have SUDs [15–23]. There is also evidence that such attitudes may be impacted by a person’s history with other substance use—for example, those who have used marijuana in the past are less likely to stigmatize users of other substances [4, 16]. These potential predictors are not often examined jointly. Furthermore, these studies have not examined potential predictors of attitudes beyond basic sociodemographic factors like age, sex, race/ethnicity, and education. In addition, most sample sizes are relatively small. Finally, there is little population-representative research examining attitudes toward individuals misusing substances at the national or even the state level, despite the involvement of many state and local governments in promoting anti-stigma initiatives [24–26].

We used restricted-access data from the Utah Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) to investigate sympathetic attitudes toward those misusing substances and address this knowledge gap. As of 2021, Utah had more than 3.3 million residents, most of whom live in urban areas [27]. It is the 30th most populous state, but the third fastest growing in the nation—driven by the highest per capita birth rate in the U.S [28]. It also has the youngest median age in the U.S., a higher proportion of religious adherents than any other state, and is among the highest of the states in terms of life expectancy [29, 30]. Utah residents are mostly non-Hispanic White, but nearly 25% self-identify as a racial or ethnic minority, and this share is growing [29]. The proportion of Utahns with a college degree is similar to that in the entire U.S., and its economy tends to outperform the nation’s [31, 32]. The state’s rates of substance and alcohol use disorders are lower than the nation’s. However, opioid use disorder, misuse of prescription pain relievers, and heroin use rates in Utah are about the same as U.S. rates [33].

The objective of this study was to understand the usefulness of population-representative data for research on perceptions of individuals with substance misuse. To do so, we measured the proportion of Utah residents who self-report sympathy toward individuals misusing substances, and examined relationships between those perceptions and other factors. We hypothesized that younger age, higher education, worse physical and mental health, urban residence, and substance use would be positively related to such sympathy in our data.

Methods

Study data

The data used in this study came from the 2021 BRFSS. The BRFSS is a nationally representative health survey that collects information from more than 400,000 non-institutionalized U.S. adults (aged 18 and older) annually. It is administered via telephone by state health departments, with support from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Participants are not compensated. The survey has a standardized core questionnaire administered everywhere in the U.S. alongside optional modules and state-added questions [34]. The data can be weighted to be representative at the state level using weights provided by the CDC. Data for the core questionnaire and optional modules can be obtained from the CDC. Data from state-added questions must be obtained from the relevant state health departments, some of whom (including Utah) restrict access to that data and impose their own reporting requirements.

Variables

Our outcome variable describes sympathy toward people who misuse substances or experience substance-related problems, which may reflect behaviors indicative of or preceding SUD. This variable comes from a question added to the survey by the state of Utah for 2021’s survey administration. The question asks, “How would you respond to the following statement? I am generally caring and sympathetic towards people who abuse substances. Would you say you strongly agree, agree, disagree, or strongly disagree?” It also includes a definitional note: “Substance abuse refers to using a legal or illegal substance that causes the user significant problems or distress.” We dichotomized this outcome variable by combining responses into “agreement” and “disagreement”.

The other BRFSS variables used in our analysis describe the survey respondents’ age, sex at birth, race/ethnicity, marital status, religion, veteran status, self-identification as a sexual or gender minority (LGBTQ+), the urbanicity of the county of residence, education, employment status, general health status, past-month days of poor mental health, depression diagnosis history, primary health insurance, past-month marijuana use, history of cigarette use, past-month of alcohol use, and county of residence. Each of these categorical variables is described in Table 1. More information about these variables can be found in Appendix 1. Supplementary mapping data were obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau. Materials and analysis code for this study are not available.

Unweighted and survey-weighted sample characteristics, stratified by sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances

Analytic approach

The data were weighted using weights and instructions provided by the CDC. The CDC generates these weights based on geographic area, number of landline and cellular phones in a household, number of adults using those phones, and a correction to address overlapping landline and cellphone sampling frames. In all our analyses, we applied the CDC’s weights as instructed by the agency. After weighting the data, we performed three major types of analyses. First, we conducted survey-weighted summary analyses stratified by our outcome variable. Chi-square tests were performed to evaluate the univariate relationships between each variable and our binary indicator of substance misuse sympathy. Second, we estimated a survey-weighted logistic regression model to understand the relationships between sympathy toward people misusing substances and all other BRFSS variables in our data, excluding county of residence. Results for our summary analyses and logistic regression were considered statistically significant if the relevant p values were less than 0.05. Third, we estimated the weighted proportion of individuals who expressed substance misuse-related sympathy in each county and mapped those estimates using state and county boundaries. Throughout all our analyses, individuals with missing data for any variable were omitted from our analyses. In our reporting of results, any results drawn from groups or areas with fewer than 10 responses were suppressed to comply with Utah’s restricted BRFSS data use requirements. All analyses were performed in RStudio.

This study did not meet the definition of human subjects research. As a result, University of Utah IRB approval was not required by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.

Results

Our unweighted sample contained responses from 8,003 Utahns. After weighting the sample to be representative at the state-level, an estimated 80.7% of Utahns reported feeling sympathetic toward individuals with substance misuse. Examination of the un-dichotomized version of this variable shows that an estimated 23.3% of Utahns strongly agreed, 57.4% agreed, 14.4% neither agreed nor disagreed, 5.0% disagreed, and 0% strongly disagreed with the statement that they felt sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances (after weighting). Table 1 contains unweighted and weighted summary statistics for all BRFSS variables, stratified by sympathy toward individuals misusing substances. These summary analyses show differences in the proportion of population groups who are sympathetic towards people with substance misuse by age, sex at birth, race/ethnicity, religious affiliation, veteran status, self-identification as a sexual or gender minority (LGBTQ+), the urbanicity of the county of residence, education, past-month days of poor mental health, depression diagnosis history, primary health insurance, past-month marijuana use, and history of cigarette use.

Table 2 shows the results from our model predicting sympathy, which included all the potential predictors we selected from the BRFSS data. These results show that age, race/ethnicity, religious affiliation, urbanicity, education, uninsurance, poor mental health, and past-month marijuana use are significantly related to feeling sympathetic toward individuals who misuse substances in Utah.

Utahns aged 65 or older were less likely than those ages 18–24 to express sympathy toward individuals with substance misuse (aOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.44–0.97, p = 0.033). No other age groups were significant in our multivariable model. Hispanic Utahns were less likely than non-Hispanic White Utahns to express this sympathy (aOR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.54–0.90, p = 0.006). No other race/ethnicity groups were significant in the model. Protestants and Catholics had 0.63 (95% CI: 0.42–0.94, p = 0.023) and 0.61 (95% CI: 0.44–0.86, p = 0.005) times the odds of feeling sympathetic toward people who misuse substances, respectively, compared to those with no religion. Utahns who self-identified as Episcopalian, Lutheran, Methodist, or Presbyterian (considered together) had 0.26 times the odds of expressing sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances compared to those with no religion (95% CI: 0.13–0.54, p < 0.001). Utahns who indicated they were Jehovah’s Witnesses were 9.41 times more likely than those with no religion to feel sympathetic toward those misusing substances, although this large odds ratio is likely unreliable and may have been measured because few Jehovah’s Witnesses were sampled (95% CI: 1.60-55.24, p = 0.013, n = 18). There were no other significant differences between religious groups in our multivariable model. Residents of rural counties were less likely than urban county residents to express sympathy toward those misusing substances (aOR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.53–0.89, p = 0.004). Utahns who had experienced 14 or more days of bad mental health in the past month had 1.34 times the odds of expressing this sympathy, compared to Utahns with no past-month bad mental health days (95% CI: 1.03–1.76, p = 0.032). People with no health insurance were estimated to have 0.69 times the odds of expressing sympathy toward those with substance misuse compared to those with employer-based insurance in our multivariable model (95% CI: 0.52–0.92, p = 0.010). Finally, in our multivariable model, individuals who had used marijuana in the past month had 1.52 times the odds of expressing sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances in their survey responses compared to those who had not (95% CI: 1.06–2.20, p = 0.024).

Our regression results also show a dose-response relationship between education and sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances. Compared to those with less than a high school education, those with more education were significantly more likely to express this sympathy, and the likelihood rises with increasing education. High school graduates, individuals who attended some college or technical school, and college or technical school graduates were 1.99 (95% CI: 1.42–2.79, p < 0.001), 2.91 (95% CI: 2.07–4.08, p < 0.001), and 3.42 (95% CI: 2.42–4.83, p < 0.001) times more likely to express sympathy towards those with substance misuse, respectively.

No variable in our regression model had variance inflation factor (VIF) above 10. Three had VIF greater than 5: age (5.16), religion (6.13), and primary health insurance (6.80). VIF is a post-hoc diagnostic for multicollinearity that can be applied to regression models. Larger values indicate a higher degree of dependence between the selected variable and other predictors in the model. Cutoff values are debated, but values higher than 10 are often interpreted as indicating serious multicollinearity [35, 36].

As noted above, an estimated 80.7% of Utahns feel sympathetic toward individuals with substance misuse. When examining the proportion of residents expressing sympathy toward those misusing substances by county, the proportion of residents who are sympathetic is lower than the state level in all but Davis, Morgan, Salt Lake, Summit, and Utah Counties—excluding counties whose results were suppressed to comply with Utah BRFSS data reporting requirements (see Fig. 1).

Discussion

We undertook this study to explore substance misuse sympathy measurement and prediction in population-representative data. We measured the proportion of residents in one state who self-reported sympathy toward individuals misusing substances, and tested relationships between those perceptions and other factors. As this is an infrequently measured phenomenon, especially in population-representative data, the overarching contribution of this study is demonstrating how population-representative data can be used to investigate substance misuse or SUD-related perceptions in ways that may not possible with national datasets. We found that most Utahns experience sympathy toward people who misuse substances (80.7%). Our results also show that (1) Utah Protestants; Catholics; and Episcopalians, Lutherans, Methodists, or Presbyterians (considered together) were significantly less likely to feel sympathetic than Utahns without a religious affiliation; (2) residents of rural counties were significantly less likely to feel sympathetic than Utahns in urban counties; (3) those without health insurance were significantly less likely than those with employer-based health insurance to feel sympathetic, (4) Utahns aged 65 or older were less likely than those ages 18–24 to express sympathy, and (5) Hispanic Utahns were less likely than non-Hispanic White Utahns to express sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances. Those who (1) had used marijuana in the past month were significantly more likely to express sympathy than those who had used none, and (2) those with 14 + days of bad mental health in the past month were more likely to express sympathy than those who had none. Increasing levels of education also appeared to be related to an increasing likelihood of feeling sympathetic toward those who misuse substances. Some of these factors had been shown to be related to substance misuse or SUD sympathy before, while others have not been detected elsewhere in the literature.

We believe that our study is among the first to have detected potential relationships between this outcome and rural residence, uninsurance, or poor mental health. Our finding that rural residents are less likely to be sympathetic toward individuals misusing substances is consistent with recent research which suggests rural residents may need different interventions to reduce SUD stigma than urban residents and that stigma in rural populations might be more difficult to change than that in urban populations [37]. Additionally, previous studies have suggested that uninsured individuals may be more likely than privately insured individuals to have SUDs [38, 39]. There does not, however, appear to be similar research focused on Utah. Initiatives to increase sympathy around substance misuse and SUDs may be critical for improving the acceptability of SUD treatment and opportunities for accessing SUD care among uninsured and publicly insured individuals. Finally, poor mental health, measured as 14 or more days of bad mental health in the past month, was a significant predictor of substance misuse sympathy in our data even when adjusting for history of depression. To our knowledge, there is no other literature examining this relationship. Individuals with poor mental health may use substances as a coping strategy to deal with their symptoms [40, 41]. Those who are sympathetic toward individuals with substance misuse may be more likely to engage in this behavior and subsequently develop a SUD themselves.

Findings on the relationship between age and perceptions of substance misuse or SUDs are mixed in the literature. Our finding that older adults are less likely than their younger peers to express substance misuse sympathy is consistent with the findings of at least two earlier studies [17, 23]. At least two other studies find the opposite result [18, 21]. Two studies agree with ours as to the findings around Hispanic race/ethnicity. One found that U.S. Hispanic panel survey respondents had higher SUD stigma than non-Hispanic respondents with regard to four substances (opioids, methamphetamines, crack cocaine, and alcohol), even when controlling for political party affiliation, racial attitudes, history of criminal legal involvement, and demographics [21]. The second found that Hispanic participants had higher mean alcohol stigma score than non-Hispanic White participants [19]. A third small study, focusing on specific models of belief about addiction, found that Hispanic survey respondents were less likely than non-Hispanic White respondents to indicate that they felt addiction was initiated and sustained by psychological or disease factors [17]. Our finding may be related to variables that are not present in our study, potentially including birth country or household income. Other studies have either not examined race/ethnicity [18], found no difference between race/ethnicity groups in substance use perceptions [16, 22], or found that other groups had statistically significant relationships with such outcomes [17, 19–21].

Although research has examined attitudes around individuals with SUD among religious communities [42], there appears to be very little research examining whether individuals who express specific religious affiliations are more or less likely to hold sympathetic views about people with SUDs compared to those who do not have a religious affiliation. Our data show that individuals in Utah who self-identify as Protestant; Catholic; or Episcopalian, Lutheran, Methodist, or Presbyterian (considered jointly) are less likely than those who are not religious to express sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances. Spiritual and religious social support has been found to be related to better outcomes for those with SUDs, and some individuals with SUDs view spiritual support as an important part of recovery [43, 44]. Therefore, religious groups may be a key source of social support for individuals with SUDs. Approximately 14% of Utahns belong to a Protestant faith, 5% are Catholic, less than 4% identify as Episcopalian, Lutheran, Methodist, or Presbyterian (considered jointly), and less than 1% identify as a Jehovah’s Witness [45]. Although each is a minority faith in the state, combined they represent nearly 25% of Utahns. Campaigns to increase sympathy towards individuals with SUDs among Protestant, Catholic, Episcopalian, Lutheran, Methodist, or Presbyterian congregations may be beneficial to Utahns with SUDs.

Previous studies have also shown mixed outcomes regarding the relationship between education and sympathy around SUDs. At least three studies have found that lower education was associated with more stigma towards alcohol use disorders, which is consistent with our results [19, 20, 22]. However, a more recent study found that college graduates were more likely to report stigmatizing attitudes towards individuals who experience alcohol use and substance use disorders [16]. Our results, which show that increasing education is associated with increasing sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances in Utah, provide further evidence for the idea that higher education may be associated with more positive attitudes. However, more research is needed to definitively conclude whether this is the case.

Finally, we showed that the proportion of residents expressing sympathy toward individuals with substance misuse is higher than the state level in five counties (Davis, Morgan, Salt Lake, Summit, and Utah Counties). For reference, the state’s capitol is in Salt Lake County, and Salt Lake and Summit Counties are home to most of the state’s ski industry, which includes a significant population of high socio-economic status and seasonal residents who may have different perspectives than other regions within Utah [46, 47]. These counties are also geographically adjacent to one another. Our county-level results are likely related to our findings in the individual-level regression analysis. Salt Lake and Davis Counties are the state’s two most populous counties, potentially making insights from these counties relevant to other U.S. metropolitan areas [27]. In addition, all five of these counties have higher proportions of residents who completed a college education than the state level. This is particularly pronounced in Summit and Utah Counties, which are number one and three in the state on this metric, with 51.8% and 43.8% of residents having a bachelor’s degree or higher. The proportion of residents in the state with these degrees is 36.9% [48]. Utah County also has a lower proportion of residents living below 138% of the federal poverty line who are uninsured than the proportion measured at the state level (15.1% vs. 19.6%). That said, the proportion in Davis County is very similar to the state proportion, and the proportions in Salt Lake and Summit Counties are much higher than the state-level proportion (23.8% and 29.2%, respectively) [49], which does not seem concordant with our individual-level results but may be explained if uninsurance is a less important factor in substance misuse sympathy than other factors such as education or living in a rural county.

Significantly more research on perceptions and attitudes around substance misuse and SUD is needed. In particular, further research should be done to understand the relationships between these perceptions and demographics, health conditions, and history of substance use. States or the CDC should consider the addition of such questions to the BRFSS in more areas, perhaps as part of the pre-existing optional module on Marijuana Use. Alternatively, such questions could be added to the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, which already asks respondents about their substance use and health. If such efforts are made, care should be given to using non-stigmatizing language in the survey questions; the question from which our outcome was derived did use such stigmatizing language. Researchers seeking to understand what factors predict negative views toward individuals with SUDs should particularly focus on education level, which has mixed outcomes in the literature, and religion, which is poorly understood. It may also be desirable to examine sympathy specific to misuse of individuals substances or different kinds of SUDs—which our data did not allow. Our findings may also have implications for research around SUD stigma. Stigma is a social phenomenon that occurs when individuals or groups are believed to be different than the general population in meaningful ways. Stigma involves four components: (1) a difference between the group in question and the general public, (2) use of negatively stereotyping language, (3) labelling of individuals as members of the group in question, and (4) a difference in social power between those that hold these views and those who are the subject of the view [50]. Most individuals who misuse drugs or have SUDs may experience some kind of stigmatization—whether it arises from the general public, health care providers, or themselves [5, 51, 52]. Although our work does not directly examine stigmatizing views, past research has suggested that sympathetic and empathetic attitudes may be inversely related to stigmatizing views [53–55].

Finally, our results have specific implications for Utah policymakers. Again, negative perceptions and stigmatizing views around substance misuse and SUDs may represent barriers to treatment. They may also increase the likelihood of risky use among current substance users [56]. There is evidence that some kinds of anti-stigma campaigns—including educational leaflets, motivational interviews, and social media campaigns—can effectively reduce public stigma around SUDs [57, 58]. To combat negative perceptions of people with SUDs, Utah’s policymakers should target the populations in which those negative perceptions are most likely to occur. In the past, state and local governments have been involved in multiple Utah anti-stigma campaigns related to SUD [59, 60]. It is unclear whether these campaigns specifically sought to reach Utah’s Protestant, Catholic, Episcopalian, Lutheran, Methodist, Presbyterian, and rural communities, individuals with lower education levels, older adults, Hispanic Utahns, or those without health insurance. Future efforts to decrease negative perceptions around SUD in the state should involve campaigns targeted toward these groups. It may also be useful to target future campaigns to other counties than Davis, Morgan, Salt Lake, Summit, and Utah Counties, given that our results show all counties except these have rates of sympathy lower than the state level.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is that the results may not be generalizable beyond the Utah population. Notably, Utah has similar rates of opioid, heroin, and prescription pain reliever misuse as the nation [33], though Utah is also younger, healthier, more religious, and less diverse than the rest of the nation [29, 30]. That some of our results are concordant with the previous literature suggests that the study’s results may be at least somewhat generalizable. While the use of data from a single state may limit generalizability, the internal validity of the analysis using distinctive variables available through the Utah BRFSS is strong, providing confidence in the observed associations and offering a compelling argument for questions about SUD perceptions or stigma to be fielded at the national level, which is currently not done. Another limitation is that our outcome is derived from a single self-reported measure. It is possible sympathy toward individuals who misuse substances is overestimated in our results if survey responses were distorted by social desirability bias [61]. Additionally, by using the term ‘abuse’, the survey question we derived our outcome from uses language which is itself stigmatizing and may have influenced responses [62] We hypothesize that, if so, it would have depressed the number of respondents indicating sympathy around substance misuse compared to what would have been measured otherwise—potentially making our estimates of sympathy conservative. Further, we did not evaluate how sympathetic feelings were impacted by social contact with individuals who have SUDs. In addition, BRFSS is fielded only via telephone and its administration during this study period may have been impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. However, there is evidence that the pandemic may not have impacted BRFSS response rates in a major way [63]. Utah’s full BRFSS sample sizes were about 12,129 in 2019 and 10,843 in 2021 [64, 65]. Finally, our religion variable only measures self-identified religious affiliation and does not account for internal or external religiosity. Despite these limitations, we believe that the relative dearth of studies on this issue and the limitations of those that do exist indicate that this research is important. Our specific results are also likely to be relevant to policymakers in Utah.

Conclusions

Our results show that population-representative data—even at the state level—can be used to explore public perceptions of individuals who misuse substances. Older age, Hispanic race/ethnicity, affiliation with several Christian religions, residence in rural counties, and lack of health insurance were negatively related to sympathy toward individuals misusing substances. Those with past-month marijuana use, higher education, and current poor mental health were significantly more likely than their counterparts to express such sympathy. Questions on SUD sympathy or stigma should be added to nationally-representative surveys. Further, given that negative attitudes around SUD are related to lower public support for public health-oriented policy and SUD stigma—which may be inversely related to sympathetic views—is a meaningful barrier to treatment, Utah’s public programs aiming to improve perceptions of individuals with SUDs should focus on these population groups.
