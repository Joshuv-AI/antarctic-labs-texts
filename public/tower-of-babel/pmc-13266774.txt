Predicting pulmonary nodule growth from a single time point: a fusion model of radiomics and deep learning to optimize follow-up strategies
Authors: Zhimin Xu, Xiang Wang, Zirui Cao, Xianpan Pan, Jiayi Zhang, Fukai Li, Lei Chen, Yi Xiao, Shiyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growth of pulmonary nodules is an important predictor of malignancy. Their varied size makes them difficult to detect and monitor, and improper management may result in a high risk of malignancy. This study explores a non-invasive method for analyzing growth progression in pulmonary nodules between baseline and follow-up computed tomography (CT) scans. The approach integrates radiomics and deep features extracted from baseline imaging data. These features led to the development of a nodule growth prediction model to accurately differentiate growth status, thus providing an objective, quantitative basis for optimizing clinical follow-up strategies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with pulmonary nodules who underwent ≥2 chest CT examinations between 2021 and 2024, comprising 1,387 nodules (1,109 for internal training and 278 for internal testing) from baseline and follow-up CT scans. Nodules were classified into growth (volume increase ≥25%) and non-growth groups based on 1-year follow-up volumetric changes. From CT images, 2,264 radiomics features were extracted. Feature selection was conducted via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. For deep feature extraction, we utilized a Swin Transformer architecture for nodule classification, extracting features from its penultimate layer which similarly underwent PCA-based dimensionality reduction. The extracted radiomics and deep features were integrated to develop logistic regression (LR) and random forest predictive models. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and additional metrics. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves further evaluated clinical utility.ResultsThe combined model demonstrated strong predictive performance in predicting nodule growth in the Internal training set (n=1,109 nodules). It achieved an AUC of 0.902 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.876–0.929], with a sensitivity of 0.815 and a specificity of 0.837, indicating a robust balance in diagnostic performance. The model significantly surpassed both radiomics-based (AUC =0.829; P<0.001) and deep learning-based (AUC =0.875; P<0.01) benchmarks. Furthermore, decision curve and calibration analyses consistently validated its clinical superiority and prediction accuracy.ConclusionsThe integrated radiomics and deep learning model effectively stratifies pulmonary nodule growth risk. This approach provides clinicians with a quantitative tool for personalizing follow-up and intervention strategies, demonstrating significant potential for clinical translation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study developed a fusion model integrating radiomics and Swin Transformer-based deep learning features to predict pulmonary nodule growth from baseline computed tomography scans.

• In the internal training set (n=1,109 nodules), the combined model achieved an area under the curve of 0.902, significantly outperforming individual radiomics or deep learning models, especially for mixed ground-glass and solid nodules.

What is known and what is new?

• Nodule growth indicates malignancy, but current size-based guidelines lack precision. Previous artificial intelligence studies often relied on small datasets or single nodule types.

• This study presents a novel approach fusing interpretable radiomics features with rich semantic deep learning features. It robustly predicts growth across diverse nodule morphologies using only a single time-point scan, validated on both internal and external datasets.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The proposed model provides a non-invasive, quantitative tool for early risk stratification.

• Clinicians can utilize this model to personalize follow-up strategies: reducing unnecessary radiation and anxiety for patients with stable nodules while prompting timely intervention for those with high growth potential.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of global cancer incidence and mortality (1,2). In 2022, it was the most frequently diagnosed cancer, accounting for 12.4% of all new cancer cases, and the primary driver of cancer-related deaths, representing 18.7% of total cancer mortality (3). A critical factor in assessing the likelihood of malignancy in pulmonary nodules, which are a common radiographic finding, is their growth rate (4). Different pathological types and histological subtypes of nodules exhibit distinct growth kinetics, which reflect their malignant potential (5). Evidence indicates that rapidly growing nodules are associated with a higher risk of malignancy, whereas slow-growing nodules often correspond to benign lesions or indolent tumors (6).

Clinical management of pulmonary nodules mainly depends on their size and growth behavior, as recommended by major guidelines such as the Fleischner Society and the 2022 Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) (7,8). For example, subsolid nodules smaller than 6 mm generally require no follow-up, whereas those with a solid component larger than 8 mm warrant further evaluation with positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) or surgery (8,9). However, among all pulmonary nodules, the growth patterns of solitary pulmonary nodules (SPNs) are particularly crucial in early malignancy prediction, as their growth trend can reflect malignant potential. Inconsistent management strategies and subjective interpretations have raised concerns about overdiagnosis and overtreatment (10,11). Although guidelines such as Lung-RADS (12) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) (13) address screening-detected nodules, while the Fleischner Society guidelines focus on incidental nodules, and others like British Thoracic Society/American College of Chest Physicians (BTS/ACCP) advocate a unified approach (14), their collective inability to provide personalized risk assessment tools remains a major obstacle to optimizing patient care and reducing overdiagnosis (15). Therefore, accurately predicting pulmonary nodule growth based on imaging data is essential to balance timely intervention against unnecessary procedures and to support evidence-based personalized management.

With the continuous advancement of radiomics, the assessment of pulmonary nodule growth patterns has become more personalized. By transforming computed tomography (CT) images into high-dimensional quantitative features, radiomics provides clinicians with a more precise prediction of nodule growth trends, thereby supporting personalized treatment decisions. Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility and potential of radiomics in predicting pulmonary nodule growth, particularly by integrating imaging data with clinical features for more personalized prediction, showing high accuracy and effectiveness (16-20). Despite these promising results, several limitations exist in the current literature. Many studies rely on small sample sizes, which limit the generalizability of the models. Furthermore, most studies focus on a single type of nodule, overlooking the heterogeneity of different nodule types, such as subsolid nodules and ground-glass opacities (21,22).

Recent advancements in deep learning have significantly driven the development of pulmonary nodule growth prediction. By leveraging neural networks, deep learning models can automatically extract complex imaging features, reducing the reliance on manual annotations and improving prediction accuracy (23-27). Several studies have demonstrated the potential of deep learning in predicting pulmonary nodule growth, particularly in surpassing traditional methods in terms of growth assessment precision (21). However, despite the tremendous potential of deep learning technology, models in existing studies still face several challenges. Some studies rely on single-type nodule data, lacking adaptability to various nodule types (28). Additionally, deep learning models often require long training times and have poor interpretability, which, to some extent, affects their acceptance in clinical applications.

Although recent studies have advanced pulmonary nodule growth prediction, most methods still rely solely on handcrafted radiomics features or data-driven deep learning representations. Radiomics models are interpretable but lack generalizability, while deep learning models capture richer semantics yet remain “black boxes” with inconsistent performance (29). Integrating these complementary features could yield more robust and clinically explainable predictions, but such approaches remain rare (30). This study aimed to identify imaging features predictive of longitudinal changes in pulmonary nodules and to develop a growth prediction model that combines radiomics and deep learning features. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0298/rc).

Methods

Patient selection

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai Changzheng Hospital (approval No. 2024SL005). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the anonymization of the data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Consecutive patients with pulmonary nodules who underwent chest CT at Shanghai Changzheng Hospital between January 2021 and June 2024 were retrospectively identified. The inclusion criteria: (I) pulmonary nodules detected on chest CT with a maximum diameter ≤30 mm; (II) at least two chest CT examinations with an interval of approximately one year between scans, without loss to follow-up or missing data; (III) availability of complete clinical history and imaging reports. The exclusion criteria: (I) had undergone any surgical procedures, percutaneous biopsies, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or other invasive diagnostic or anti-cancer treatments prior to the CT examination; (II) CT images exhibiting severe artifacts, inadequate resolution, or other pulmonary pathologies that prevent accurate nodule observation and segmentation; (III) incomplete Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format CT images or insufficient clinical documentation. After screening, 402 patients with 1,387 nodules were included in the internal cohort from Shanghai Changzheng Hospital (Figure 1). In addition, an external validation cohort comprising 224 patients with 224 nodules was obtained from the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) dataset (31) (Figure 1). Consistent with BTS guidelines (32), nodules were classified into growth or non-growth groups based on a ≥25% volume increase on follow-up scans. The internal cohort was randomly divided into an internal training set (n=1,109 nodules) and an internal testing set (n=278 nodules) at an 8:2 ratio. Model performance was subsequently evaluated on the external NLST dataset (n=224 nodules). The patient selection flowchart is presented in Figure 1.

CT images analyzed in this study were obtained from two sources: the Changzheng dataset and the NLST dataset. For the Changzheng dataset, imaging was performed on five multidetector CT scanners (TOSHIBA Aquilion, two Philips Ingenuity units, GE LightSpeed VCT, and Philips iCT256). Participants were scanned in the supine position at full inspiration, with the scan range extending from the adrenal glands to the thoracic inlet. The acquisition parameters were standardized as follows: 120 kV, 50–150 mA, 512×512 matrix, and 0.5-second rotation time. Images were reconstructed with 0.625–1.0 mm slice thickness and exported in DICOM format for further processing. The NLST dataset consisted of low-dose helical CT scans obtained under a harmonized acquisition protocol. All scans were acquired at 120 kV and ≤80 mAs with a pitch of approximately 1.35, rotation time of 0.5 s, and slice thickness of 1.0–2.5 mm reconstructed at 1.0–2.5 mm intervals. The estimated CT dose index volume (CTDIvol) was 2–3 mGy, consistent with the low-dose design of the trial. All NLST images were provided in DICOM.

Nodule segmentation and matching

In this study, all pulmonary nodule segmentations were performed using the uAI Research Porta (uRP), an intelligent research platform developed by Shanghai United Imaging Intelligence Co., Ltd. (http://urp.united-imaging.com) (33). For each patient, precise three-dimensional volumes of interest (VOIs) of the target pulmonary nodules were delineated on both baseline and follow-up CT images, and nodule matching across different time points was performed to ensure accurate correspondence of the segmented regions. This process involved outlining the entire contour of each nodule. Subsequently, the artificial intelligence (AI)-generated segmentation results were manually reviewed and corrected by experienced radiologists. Particular attention was paid to assessing the completeness of the nodule contours, the accuracy of boundary delineation relative to surrounding tissues, and the inclusion of any small adjacent structures that might have been missed. In the segmentation component, a cascaded detection-segmentation network architecture was designed (34). First, detection of pulmonary nodule regions was performed across the entire image. Once detected, each nodule was cropped and resampled to a resolution of 96×96×96 voxels, centered on the nodule. Subsequently, segmentation of the nodule contours was carried out on the higher-resolution cropped images to obtain more precise segmentation masks, the average dice for the segmented nodules was 91.5%. For the matching of nodules across multiple follow-up time points, a hierarchical segmentation approach was adopted, proceeding from coarse to fine structures, including airways, pulmonary lobes, and pulmonary vessels. Whole-lung registration was first achieved at the semantic level by aligning masks of the segmented airways and lobes. Subsequently, fine-grained registration at the texture level was performed by aligning pulmonary vessels across different time points (35). Finally, for pulmonary nodules at different time points, matching was accomplished by calculating positional differences.

Image interpretation by radiologists

The lung nodules were assessed by 9 radiologists based on thin-section, non-contrast CT images. In cases of disagreement, a radiologist (X.W., with 5 years of thoracic imaging experience) served as the arbitrator. All CT images and masks were formatted for the uRP research platform (https://urp.united-imaging.com/). The boundaries of the VOI were manually adjusted slice by slice, if necessary, to exclude normal vessels, adjacent nodules, or when the nodule margins were ill-defined. To ensure segmentation accuracy, a second review of all images was conducted by radiologist F.L.

After uploading all paired lung nodule CT images and masks to the uRP research platform, the radiologists reviewed the volume and density data of the nodules to ensure they met the growth criteria for lung nodules. This included confirming that the changes in volume and density were consistent with the expected features of malignant growth.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

The extraction of radiomics features from the first time point of pulmonary nodule images in multi-time-point follow-up data was performed using the uRP platform. Radiomics features were divided into the following categories: (I) first-order features, (II) shape-based features, (III) higher-order features, including gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM), gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM), and neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM). The radiomics feature values were standardized using the z-score method. Subsequently, feature selection was performed using both the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method and principal component analysis (PCA).

Machine learning (ML) model construction

A predictive model for pulmonary nodule growth was developed using radiomics features. The selected features informed the construction of two ML models, leveraging algorithms commonly used in biomedical research: logistic regression (LR) and random forest (RF). The average area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated for both the training and testing sets using a probability threshold of 0.5. Model performance was further evaluated with calibration curves, ROC curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Construction of deep learning classification models and deep feature extraction

In this study, a Swin Transformer-based deep learning architecture was employed to predict pulmonary nodule growth (36). The model incorporates a Transformer-based encoder in place of conventional convolution operations to effectively capture long-range dependencies within the images. To address the high computational complexity of traditional attention mechanisms, the model applies a sliding window operation to compute local attention, significantly reducing memory consumption and computational overhead. Specifically, the input images are first partitioned into fixed-size patches using the patch partition module, followed by channel dimension expansion through a linear projection layer. The encoder consists of four repeated down sampling modules, each comprising a Swin Transformer module and a merging module. The Swin Transformer module efficiently computes local attention using a sliding window mechanism, while the Merging module progressively reduces the feature map size and increases the channel dimension through down sampling operations. Finally, a fully connected layer maps the encoder output to classification prediction probabilities.

The feature extraction process was performed using the pretrained Swin Transformer model. Images were first batch-loaded into the model via a data loader, with the model set to evaluation mode to ensure the stability of feature extraction. Within the model, the images sequentially passed through multiple down-sampling modules to enable multi-scale feature extraction. Ultimately, the feature map from the penultimate layer was extracted, effectively integrating multi-scale semantic information ranging from fine-grained textures to macrostructural patterns. The resulting deep features were used for subsequent analysis. An LR model was constructed using these deep features to predict pulmonary nodule growth.

Construction of predictive models integrating radiomics and deep features

Among the two individual models, LR demonstrated superior performance over RF in predicting pulmonary nodule growth trends and was ultimately selected as the optimal model. The selected radiomics and deep learning features were combined to construct an integrated model, for which LR was employed for modeling. The LR prediction model was developed by integrating radiomics and deep features. After training on the training set and internal validation, the model was subsequently applied to the external test set to assess its performance in predicting pulmonary nodule growth and non-growth. Key performance metrics, including AUC and sensitivity, were calculated. Calibration curves, ROC curves, and DCA were used to evaluate the calibration and clinical utility of probability predictions for growth and non-growth outcomes. Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0. Continuous data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data were represented as percentages. For continuous variables, comparisons were made using independent-sample t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests, while categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-squared test. The performance of the models was evaluated through metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. ROC curves were generated to assess diagnostic performance, and the ROC was computed. To compare AUC values between groups, the DeLong test was applied. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the models. A P value of less than 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Data overview

The study identified patients with pulmonary nodules who underwent multiple chest CT scans. The imaging data were processed using the uRP platform to perform segmentation and matching of nodules across different examination time points. Paired images were selected based on an interval of 305–405 days between examinations, and pairs in which the long and short diameters of the nodule at the first scan ranged between 5 mm and 3 cm were included in the study. By calculating the nodule volumes from the two time points in each pair, a 25% increase in volume was used as the threshold for grouping (32). The internal dataset ultimately comprised 1,387 nodules, including 182 in the growth group and 1,205 in the non-growth group. The external test set contained 224 nodules, with 129 growth and 95 non-growth nodules. Of these, 1,109 nodules were used for feature extraction and model training, 278 nodules were reserved for internal testing, and 224 nodules were reserved for external testing.

Feature selection

In constructing the radiomics model 2,264 radiomics features were extracted from each segmented pulmonary nodule VOI. To reduce dimensionality and select the most predictive features, the feature values were first standardized using z-score normalization. Feature selection was then performed using the LASSO resulting in the retention of 180 features. PCA was subsequently applied to these features for further dimensionality reduction. Following experimental comparisons of different dimensionalities, the top 30 principal components accounting for the highest cumulative variance were ultimately retained.

Deep learning produced a high-dimensional feature vector of 20,736 dimensions due to the considerable network depth and the large number of channels in the underlying feature space. To adequately retain the spatial and semantic information inherent in medical images, PCA was first applied to reduce the dimensionality to 2,264 dimensions, to match the initial dimensionality of the radiomics features. Subsequently, the LASSO was employed to identify and retain the features most strongly associated with the prediction outcome. This process resulted in the selection of 50 predictive features. Finally, a second PCA was applied to further reduce dimensionality, retaining the top 30 principal components explaining the highest cumulative variance as guided by the same experimental experience.

After independently processing the radiomics and deep learning features, each set of features was used to construct individual models. Subsequently, a channel concatenation strategy was employed to integrate the two feature types into a single unified model. Specifically, the 30-dimensional radiomics principal components were concatenated with the 30-dimensional deep learning features, resulting in a 60-dimensional combined feature vector. To reduce computational redundancy and mitigate the risk of overfitting associated with high-dimensional data, PCA was then applied to the concatenated features. The top 30 principal components, which accounted for the highest cumulative variance, were retained, ensuring optimal feature representation in the final model.

Construction and evaluation of the integrated model

This study developed a predictive model for pulmonary nodule growth by integrating deep learning and radiomics features. Three modeling approaches—radiomics, deep learning, and a combined method—were evaluated using LR as the modeling framework. The combined model demonstrated balanced performance and superior results on the test set, confirming its effectiveness.

On the training set, the radiomics model achieved an AUC of 0.829 (0.795–0.862), with a sensitivity of 0.781, specificity of 0.74, and accuracy of 0.746. The deep learning model showed superior performance, attaining an AUC of 0.875 (0.842–0.908), sensitivity of 0.808, and accuracy of 0.795. The combined model, integrating multimodal features, performed best overall, with an AUC of 0.902 (0.876–0.929) and an accuracy of 0.834, along with coordinated improvements across other key metrics (Table 1; Figure 3A-3C).

Discussion

Key findings

The core finding of this study is that the fusion model, which combines radiomics and deep learning features, successfully predicted the growth of pulmonary nodules. Compared to the individual use of radiomics and deep learning models, the fusion model demonstrated significantly better predictive accuracy and clinical utility. In the training set of 1,109 nodules, the fusion model achieved an AUC of 0.902 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.876–0.929], with a sensitivity of 0.815, specificity of 0.837, and accuracy of 0.834, showing a good balance and robustness. This result suggests that the fusion strategy, integrating radiomics and deep learning features, can effectively improve the prediction accuracy of pulmonary nodule growth, overcoming the limitations of single methods and providing more reliable support for clinical decision-making (37,38).

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several notable strengths. First, the internal cohort included 1,387 nodules and the inclusion of both domestic and international cohorts NLST enhance the reliability of our findings. Second, the fusion of handcrafted radiomics features and deep learning semantic features via the Swin Transformer provides a more comprehensive representation of nodule heterogeneity. Furthermore, the detailed subgroup analysis across GGO, mixed GGO, and solid nodules demonstrates the model’s robustness and clinical applicability.

Although this study yielded promising results, there are still some limitations. First, the data for the growth and non-growth groups is imbalanced, with a significantly larger sample size in the non-growth group. This may affect the accuracy of the model in predicting growing nodules. Future studies should aim to balance the dataset by increasing the number of growing nodules, which would improve the model’s ability to predict growing nodules. Second, the data used in this study were sourced from Shanghai Changzheng Hospital and the NLST database. While these datasets are representative, differences across populations in terms of ethnicity, region, and other factors may affect the generalizability of the model. Third, the combined model showed a noticeable decline in performance in the external validation cohort compared with the internal training and testing cohorts. This decrease may partly reflect residual overfitting, despite the use of PCA and LASSO for dimensionality reduction and feature selection. More importantly, it may also be associated with limited generalizability caused by differences between the internal Shanghai Changzheng Hospital cohort and the external NLST cohort, including potential differences in patient characteristics, class distribution, and CT acquisition protocols. These inter-cohort differences may have introduced domain shift and thereby reduced model robustness in the external setting. Future studies should include larger multicenter datasets and further external validation to improve model generalizability. Therefore, future studies need to validate the model in more diverse and broad samples to ensure its applicability in different clinical settings and populations.

Comparison with similar research

In comparison with existing research, recent studies have shown that both radiomics and deep learning offer promising methods for modeling pulmonary nodule growth. Traditional exponential models often oversimplify tumor progression, which is now known to be influenced by complex interactions within the tumor microenvironment (39). Historically, radiologists have relied on subjective visual assessments of tumor images, which can vary depending on factors such as individual expertise and image resolution. This highlights the need for more objective and comprehensive approaches to evaluating tumor growth. Radiomics, based on CT imaging, offers a promising pathway for predicting pulmonary nodule growth. For instance, Tan et al. (40) developed a combined radiomics-radiological model using CT images, achieving an AUC of 0.78 for growth prediction. Similarly, Lu et al. (41) analyzed 150 pulmonary nodules from 114 lung cancer patients in the NLST and showed that a multi-window radiomics-clinical feature model could effectively predict indolent lung cancer, with an AUC of 0.846. In 2022, Liao et al. (24) employed deep learning to predict subsolid nodule growth, achieving AUC of 0.858 and 0.760 on internal and external datasets, respectively, further validating the feasibility of automated learning frameworks. These studies collectively highlight the evolution from handcrafted feature-based analysis toward data-driven approaches. However, most models remain limited by their focus on single nodule types or lack integration of complementary imaging and semantic representations, underscoring the need for more comprehensive and generalizable predictive frameworks.

Explanations of findings

As shown in Table 3, this study independently evaluated the performance of the model on different types of pulmonary nodules (GGO, mixed GGO, and solid nodules), with results indicating a significant impact of nodule type on model performance, because this analysis included nodules from both the training and internal testing sets, the results should be interpreted as exploratory rather than independent validation performance. For GGO, nodules, the model achieved an AUC of 0.829 (95% CI: 0.789–0.869), with relatively low sensitivity (0.702) but high specificity (0.813) and accuracy (0.8), demonstrating a robust performance in predicting growth for this type of nodule. GGO nodules typically have less distinct imaging features, which increases the difficulty of prediction; however, the model, utilizing a fusion of radiomics and deep learning features, effectively differentiated their growth status. For mixed GGO nodules, the model showed outstanding performance, with an AUC of 0.906 (95% CI: 0.868–0.943), sensitivity reaching 0.885, and accuracy of 0.845, significantly outperforming GGO nodules. This result indicates the importance of predicting growth for mixed GGO nodules, given their complex imaging characteristics and potential malignancy, with the model effectively capturing their growth trends. For solid nodules, the model demonstrated the best performance with an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.916–0.944), sensitivity of 0.859, and accuracy of 0.835. Solid nodules typically exhibit more distinct imaging features, and the model’s high predictive accuracy further underscores the advantages of the fusion of radiomics and deep learning features. Overall, the combined model demonstrated superior performance for mixed GGO and solid nodules, highlighting the importance of personalized predictions based on nodule type to enhance the clinical applicability of the model. Future research could further optimize the model, particularly by improving the classification accuracy and feature extraction capabilities for GGO nodules, thereby enhancing its applicability across a broader range of clinical settings. These advantages may be attributed to the complementary nature of radiomics and deep learning features. Radiomics offers quantitative insights into nodule heterogeneity and structural complexity through handcrafted features, including first-order statistics, shape descriptors, and high-order texture metrics such as GLCM and GLSZM (42,43). In contrast, the Swin Transformer utilizes a hierarchical shifted-window attention mechanism to automatically capture multiscale spatial dependencies within CT images. By integrating these modalities through LR, the combined model preserves fine-grained lesion information while improving its capacity to identify complex temporal and morphological growth patterns (36). DCA and calibration plots further confirmed that the integrated model provides higher net clinical benefit and better predictive consistency compared to either individual approach (44).

Implications and actions needed

The fusion model employed in this study successfully combined radiomics and deep learning features, leveraging the strengths of both approaches. Radiomics provides a detailed description of tumor morphology and texture, while deep learning excels at capturing higher-level complex semantic information. By fusing these features, the model gains a more comprehensive understanding of nodule growth patterns, thus enhancing prediction accuracy. The effectiveness of this strategy was validated through DCA and calibration curves, further demonstrating the potential clinical value of the fusion model in aiding clinical decision-making. Moreover, the high accuracy of the fusion model in predicting pulmonary nodule growth showcases its strong clinical applicability. By providing a more objective and quantifiable basis, the model helps optimize follow-up strategies, reducing overdiagnosis and unnecessary treatments. For instance, for nodules with slow or no growth, the model can suggest minimizing unnecessary exams and treatments, thereby alleviating the patient’s medical burden. When applied in clinical practice, this model could effectively improve early cancer detection rates, reduce false positives, and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study presents a fusion model combining radiomics and deep learning features for predicting pulmonary nodule growth, demonstrating superior accuracy and clinical utility compared to individual models. The model provides a quantitative basis for personalized follow-up and treatment strategies, with significant clinical potential. Future research should optimize the model, particularly for recognizing complex nodule types and integrating multi-modal data to improve prediction accuracy and interpretability.
