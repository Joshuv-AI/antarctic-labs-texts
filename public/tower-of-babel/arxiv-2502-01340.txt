Human-Agent Interaction in Synthetic Social Networks: A Framework for Studying Online Polarization
Tim Donkers, Jürgen Ziegler

Abstract
Online political polarization operates across interconnected dimensions—beliefs radicalize, networks fragment, discourse transforms—yet we lack understanding of how these processes causally interact and whether interventions targeting one dimension inadvertently affect others. This paper introduces a framework enabling controlled causal investigation by integrating formal opinion dynamics models with LLM-generated communication, allowing computational agents and humans to interact in shared social environments while maintaining experimental control over psychological, structural, and algorithmic factors. Systematic computational experiments reveal counterintuitive dynamics that challenge conventional theories: complete network segregation buffers against extreme polarization while moderate integration creates optimal conditions for extremism, contradicting echo chamber explanations. Opinion radicalization and network segregation can decouple, with algorithmic interventions showing sharp threshold effects where identical approaches succeed preventatively but fail remedially. Polarization manifests as a systematic communicative syndrome—simultaneously increased emotionality, heightened group identity salience, and reduced uncertainty. Human validation with 122 participants confirms computational environments produce psychologically meaningful effects with large effect sizes while revealing that discourse climate influences opinion change through ambient processing that bypasses deliberate evaluation. This framework provides infrastructure for evidence-based polarization theory and intervention design that neither purely computational nor purely empirical approaches could achieve alone.

Introduction

Online political polarization operates across multiple interconnected dimensions—beliefs radicalize, networks fragment, and discourse transforms—yet we lack systematic understanding of how these processes causally interact. Do echo chambers cause belief extremization, or does belief polarization drive network sorting
Sasahara et al. (2021)
? Can algorithmic interventions reduce opinion polarization while inadvertently increasing structural segregation? Does discourse toxicity reflect or drive attitude change? Understanding these causal relationships requires frameworks that can isolate specific mechanisms while maintaining communicative realism and validating computational predictions against actual human behavior
Lazer et al. (2009)
;
Epstein (1999)
. Without such frameworks, polarization theory rests on correlational observational patterns and untested computational assumptions, constraining both theoretical understanding and evidence-based intervention design.

This validation gap stems from fundamental trade-offs across existing research approaches. Observational studies leverage large-scale social media data to identify macro-level patterns
Karjus and Cuskley (2024)
;
Bond and Sweitzer (2022)
;
Al Amin et al. (2017)
, providing invaluable ecological insights, but cannot manipulate key variables or establish causal relationships between specific mechanisms and polarization outcomes. Traditional opinion dynamics models enable controlled experimentation with mathematically precise belief update mechanisms
Hegselmann and Krause (2002)
;
DeGroot (1974)
;
Sasahara et al. (2021)
, yet rely on simplified interaction rules that sacrifice communicative realism and prevent direct validation against human responses. Recent LLM-based simulations achieve unprecedented communicative authenticity
Chuang et al. (2024)
;
Breum et al. (2024)
;
Ohagi (2024)
, but operate as ”black boxes” lacking the explicit, formal mechanisms necessary for systematic theoretical testing. Each approach has distinct strengths, yet none achieves the combination of formal mechanisms, realistic communication, and human validation necessary for causal investigation of polarization dynamics.

This paper introduces a framework that bridges this gap by enabling seamless interaction between humans and computational agents in shared social environments. Our approach combines the analytical rigor of formal opinion dynamics models with the communicative authenticity of LLM-based agents: agents’ beliefs evolve according to mathematical equations grounded in Social Judgment Theory
Sherif et al. (1965)
, while their communication occurs through natural language, allowing direct interaction with human participants. Agents and humans interact in simulated social media environments where they encounter posts, react with likes or comments, and experience algorithmically-curated content streams. The framework provides systematic control over psychological, structural, and algorithmic factors, enabling investigation of how these dimensions interact to drive polarization.

Our work makes three primary contributions to polarization research.

First, we provide novel
theoretical insights
that reveal patterns challenging structural echo chamber explanations
Sunstein (2017)
while aligning with theoretical perspectives conceptualizing polarization as an inherent communicative order rather than a pathological deviation from democratic norms
Kumkar (2025)
. Our computational experiments demonstrate that complete network segregation actually buffers against extreme polarization while moderately integrated networks create optimal conditions for extremism—consistent with political identity formation requiring friction with opposing viewpoints and recent computational work showing that digital media drive affective polarization through partisan sorting rather than isolation
Törnberg (2022)
;
Törnberg et al. (2021)
. These findings suggest polarization may function as the inherent logic of political discourse, with profound implications for intervention design: structural fixes such as breaking echo chambers or maximizing cross-cutting exposure may intensify rather than reduce polarization by increasing the friction necessary for identity crystallization. We reveal an ”engagement valley” where moderate disagreement produces the lowest interaction levels, suggesting fundamental instability in the measured discourse often considered essential for democracy. Our co-evolutionary analysis demonstrates that structural network segregation can occur with only moderate belief polarization, indicating that network-based studies may systematically overestimate ideological extremization by conflating social sorting with radical opinion change. By analyzing LLM-generated discourse, we show that polarization manifests as a systematic ”communicative syndrome”—discourse with simultaneously higher emotionality, greater group salience, and reduced uncertainty—with message interpretation shaped by individual personality traits and interaction history in ways that create path dependencies extending beyond initial polarization triggers.

Second, our
empirical validation study
with 122 participants demonstrates that computational environments produce psychologically meaningful effects while revealing critical gaps in current models. Participants accurately perceived the distinct communicative differences our computational analysis identified, with large effect sizes (emotionality
g=1.53
, group salience
g=0.73
, uncertainty
g=-1.26
). Critically, human validation revealed that environmental polarization influenced opinion change through pathways our computational model did not capture—discourse climate affects attitudes through rapid, ambient contextual processing
Petty and Cacioppo (1986)
that bypasses conscious evaluation of specific message features. This divergence between computational predictions and human mechanisms points toward refinements needed in opinion dynamics models, demonstrating the value of systematic human-in-the-loop validation for advancing theoretical understanding.

Third, we introduce a
methodological framework
that enables systematic validation of computational polarization models against human behavior. By integrating computational modeling and human experimentation
Lazer et al. (2009)
, we provide replicable infrastructure for evidence-based model development that neither approach could achieve alone, enabling systematic investigation of social dynamics at scales and levels of control impossible with traditional approaches.

Section 5 validates computational predictions through human-subject experimentation investigating how individuals perceive and respond to simulated environments. Section 6 synthesizes computational and empirical findings to advance polarization theory, addressing the echo chamber paradox, algorithmic intervention dynamics, and communicative transformation while discussing methodological contributions and limitations. Section 7 concludes with contributions and future directions.

Related Work

Research on social polarization in online networks spans multiple disciplines and methodological approaches. The existing literature reveals three main streams: (1) observational studies analyzing large-scale empirical data, (2) theoretical models simulating opinion dynamics, and (3) experimental studies with human participants. While observational and theoretical approaches are well-developed, human experiments providing direct causal insights into individual-level effects remain comparatively scarce.

Observational and Theoretical Approaches

Observational studies leveraging social media data employ sophisticated analytical techniques to identify macro-level polarization patterns. Sentiment analysis approaches track emotional polarization and linguistic divergence over time
Karjus and Cuskley (2024)
;
Alsinet et al. (2021)
;
Buder et al. (2021)
, while network clustering methods reveal ideological segregation and echo chamber formation
Treuillier et al. (2024)
;
Bond and Sweitzer (2022)
;
Al Amin et al. (2017)

Conover et al. (2011)
;
Bakshy et al. (2015)
;
Xing et al. (2022)
;
Yarchi et al. (2021)
. Topic modeling techniques analyze how polarization manifests across different subjects and contexts
Kim et al. (2019)
;
Chen et al. (2021)
, and specialized approaches explore the roles of specific users in shaping information flow
Jiang et al. (2020)
;
Recuero et al. (2019)
.

Complementing these empirical findings, theoretical research has developed increasingly sophisticated mathematical models of opinion dynamics. Classical models established foundational mechanisms like consensus through averaging
DeGroot (1974)
;
Friedkin and Johnsen (1990)
, clustering via bounded confidence thresholds
Deffuant et al. (2000)
;
Hegselmann and Krause (2002)
, imitation
Holley and Liggett (1975)
, social validation
Sznajd-Weron and Sznajd (2000)
, or cultural trait dissemination
Axelrod (2006)
. Modern extensions enhance realism by incorporating cognitive biases like confirmation bias
Del Vicario et al. (2017)
;
Allahverdyan and Galstyan (2014)
and reactance
Flache et al. (2017)
;
Cornacchia et al. (2020)
, modeling network co-evolution
Baumann et al. (2020)
;
Sasahara et al. (2021)
, accounting for varying influence
Amelkin et al. (2017)
;
Chen et al. (2016)
, and including algorithmic amplification effects
Donkers and Ziegler (2023)
;
Donkers and Ziegler (2021)
;
Geschke et al. (2019)
. However, these mathematical models, while analytically powerful, typically abstract complex human communication into simplified numerical or rule-based interactions, often sacrificing linguistic richness and contextual nuance vital to understanding online discourse.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a different simulation paradigm focused on communicative realism. LLM-based agents can simulate polarization dynamics
Ohagi (2024)
, replicate emergent network phenomena like diffusion through text
Gao et al. (2023b)
, and allow exploration of linguistic influence strategies
Breum et al. (2024)
and agent biases
Cisneros-Velarde (2024)

Ghaffarzadegan et al. (2024)
;
Gurcan (2024)
;
Junprung (2023)
. Surveys highlight the growing potential and challenges of these methods
Gao et al. (2023a)
. While capturing linguistic interaction effectively, these LLM simulations often lack the explicit, interpretable belief update mechanisms and formal grounding of traditional opinion dynamics models
Chuang et al. (2024)
, creating a methodological disconnect between communicative realism and analytical precision.

Experimental Human Studies

Experimental studies with human participants, though scarce

, provide crucial causal insights unavailable from observation or pure simulation alone, drawing on foundations in social psychology regarding social influence and group dynamics

, affective dynamics
Schieferdecker et al. (2024)
, identity cues
Wuestenenk et al. (2023)
, or network influence
Stewart et al. (2019)
, but often use simplified stimuli or abstract away from complex platform environments.
Model calibration studies
use controlled tasks, often involving numeric judgments
Chacoma and Zanette (2015)
;
Vande Kerckhove et al. (2016)

Das et al. (2014)

(

, but these constrained interactions rarely capture the richness of online discourse.

Summarized, a significant methodological challenge remains in creating an experimental framework that integrates the analytical rigor of formal models with the ecological validity of realistic, LLM-driven communication and direct human interaction. The following section details our approach to addressing this challenge.

3
Simulation Framework

To investigate online polarization, we developed a computational framework that integrates opinion dynamics principles with the communicative realism afforded by Large Language Model (LLM)-based artificial agents. This hybrid approach bridges the analytical precision of formal models with the ecological validity of natural language simulations. The framework comprises four core components: an adaptive agent architecture, a psychologically-grounded model of opinion dynamics, an explicit model of communicative actions, and a co-evolving social network. This section outlines these components, while the full mathematical specification is provided in
Section

8
.

3.1
Adaptive Agent Architecture

At the core of our framework are adaptive agents (
A_{i}
) that possess rich, evolving internal states: a continuous
opinion score
(
o_{i}(t)\in[-1,1]
), a static
persona
(
P_{i}
) defining personality traits, and a dynamic
interaction history
(
H_{i}(t)
) that serves as memory. A fundamental principle of our architecture is the distinction between these hidden internal states and the
manifest communication
they produce. We model a realistic social process where individuals cannot directly access others’ private thoughts—they only observe public expressions, i.e., the messages people post.

A key aspect of our framework is how these messages are generated: an LLM dynamically crafts text by conditioning on the agent’s
entire
internal state. This produces nuanced communication where message content reflects not just the agent’s core opinion, but also their personality and recent interactions (
Tables

1
and
2
). Equally important is the framework’s
interpretive layer
. Unlike traditional models where an author’s opinion score is directly transmitted, an LLM-driven evaluation function,
\pi
, simulates subjective perception—it reads the textual content of an encountered message (
m
) to estimate its perceived stance (
o_{m}
). Crucially, all subsequent opinion updates and reactions are based on this
perception
of the message, not on privileged access to the author’s true beliefs. This explicit interpretation step introduces realistic potential for misunderstanding, context-dependent perception, and bias.

Importantly, this design choice enables agents to interact seamlessly with human users without requiring explicit opinion measurements. While traditional opinion dynamics models depend on direct access to other agents’ opinion values (e.g., to apply averaging mechanisms), our agents derive perceived opinions purely from textual content evaluation. This eliminates the need to elicit numerical opinion scores from humans, who may themselves be uncertain about their precise positions, and allows agents to update their beliefs based on natural language communication just as humans do in real-world interactions.

Opinion Dynamics

Our framework models opinion evolution based on principles from Social Judgment Theory
Sherif et al. (1965)
, which suggests that individuals evaluate new information through the lens of their existing attitudes. Agent opinions evolve through a process of assimilation and repulsion, governed by their personal latitudes of acceptance and rejection, as visualized in

When an agent encounters a message, the opinion update process begins with the message evaluation function
\pi
, which determines the perceived opinion
o_{m}
expressed in the message content. The agent then calculates the opinion difference
d=o_{i}(t)-o_{m}
and compares this difference to their current ”latitude of acceptance”—the range of opinion differences they can tolerate before shifting from attraction to repulsion.

The core opinion shift is governed by a cubic polynomial function:

\displaystyle\omega_{i}(m)=d\cdot(d^{2}-\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}(o_{i},o_{m}))

(1)

where
d
is the opinion difference and
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
represents the effective squared width of the attraction zone. This function creates distinct behavioral regimes: when the opinion difference falls within the effective width (
|d|^{2}<\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
), the agent is attracted toward the message; when outside this range (
|d|^{2}>\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
), the agent is repelled away from the message. The final opinion shift is bounded using the hyperbolic tangent function:
\Delta o_{i}(m)=\tanh(\omega_{i}(m))
.

A distinctive feature of our model is that the attraction width is not fixed but dynamically adjusted based on the agent’s current conviction and the relationship to the encountered message. This models motivated reasoning
Kunda (1990)
, where an agent’s existing beliefs influence how they process new information.

The effective attraction width is calculated as:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}(o_{i},o_{m})=\left(\sigma_{\text{base}}\cdot f_{\text{scale}}(o_{i},o_{m})\right)^{2}

(2)

where
\sigma_{\text{base}}
represents an agent’s
tolerance for disagreement
—technically, the baseline width of their ”latitude of acceptance”
Sherif et al. (1965)
. Since agents operate on opinions in
[-1,1]
, this parameter ranges from 0 to 2:
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0
creates agents who reject any deviation from their exact position, while
\sigma_{\text{base}}=2
creates agents who assimilate across the entire opinion spectrum. Opinions falling within this tolerance range are assimilated; those outside trigger reactance.
f_{\text{scale}}
is a scaling factor that modulates this width based on the agent’s conviction and the concordance between agent and message opinions.

The scaling factor significantly narrows the attraction width for highly convicted agents when they encounter ideologically aligned but more moderate messages. This mechanism models psychological resistance to moderating influences from one’s own side—a highly convicted liberal, for instance, might reject a more moderate liberal message as insufficiently committed. By shrinking the effective attraction width in these scenarios, the model makes repulsion more likely even for relatively similar opinions, preventing agents with extreme views from being easily moderated by their own side.

When opinions are discordant (opposite sides of the spectrum), the effective width remains close to the baseline, allowing normal assimilation-repulsion dynamics to operate. This asymmetric treatment captures the psychological reality that people are often more resistant to moderation from allies than to opposition from adversaries. 3.3
Communicative Actions

Distinct from internal opinion evolution, our framework explicitly models observable communicative behaviors. Drawing on theories of social interaction, we assume engagement is driven by two competing motivations: the desire to affirm in-group identity (
homophily
) and the desire to challenge out-group views (
antagonism
).

These motivations are captured by a
concordance trigger
(
P_{\text{con}}
) that activates on opinion alignment, and a
discordance trigger
whose base probability (
P_{\text{dis}}
) is scaled by an agent’s intrinsic
propensity for discordant reaction (
p_{\text{dis}}
)
. This dual-trigger model proves highly flexible (see
Fig.

1
b):
likes
can be modeled as low-effort agreement with
p_{\text{dis}}\approx 0
(making the discordance term vanish and leaving only the homophilic trigger), while argumentative
comments
involve both concordance and antagonism components with non-zero
p_{\text{dis}}
.

Assuming these triggers are independent, the total probability of interaction is based on their union. This is calculated by summing the two trigger probabilities and then subtracting their joint probability to avoid double-counting the (extremely rare) cases where both triggers might activate simultaneously, yielding the final formula:

\displaystyle P_{\text{int}}(A_{i},m)=p_{\text{base}}\left(P_{\text{con}}+p_{\text{dis}}\cdot P_{\text{dis}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-p_{\text{dis}}\cdot P_{\text{con}}\cdot P_{\text{dis}}\right)

(3)

This allows us to simulate not just whether agents engage, but the nature and function of that engagement (see
Section

8.3
for the full derivation of
P_{\text{con}}
and
P_{\text{dis}}
).

3.4
Network Structure and Information Flow

Agent interactions unfold within a co-evolving directed social network,
G(t)
, that governs information flow. We initialize the network using a hybrid model that blends principles from
Stochastic Block Models (SBMs)
and
Preferential Attachment
. This creates realistic initial conditions with both homophilous community clustering (based on opinion) and heterogeneous ”hub” structures, reflecting the ”rich-get-richer” dynamics common in online networks.

The network then evolves as agents rewire their connections based on opinion similarity, modeling the process of social sorting that can lead to echo chambers. Information propagation is managed by a recommendation system that simulates algorithmic curation, balancing exposure to content from an agent’s existing network (the ”filter bubble”) with content discovered from outside (controlled by a discovery rate,
\delta_{\text{rec}}
). This key parameter allows systematic exploration of how algorithmic design choices affect polarization dynamics (see
Section

8.4
).

3.5
Simulation Workflow

The simulation progresses through an iterative workflow over discrete time steps, creating interconnected feedback loops (see
Algorithm

1
in
Section

8.5
for detailed pseudocode). Each time step represents a full cycle of social interaction. The process begins with
Content Generation
, where a subset of agents post new messages reflecting their current states. Next, the
Recommendation System
curates a personalized feed for every agent, determining their information exposure for that step. Agents then
Interact
with this content, performing actions like liking or commenting based on the probabilistic models. Following these interactions, agents undergo
Opinion Updates
, where their internal beliefs shift in response to the messages they perceived. Finally, agents may perform
Network Updates
, rewiring their follow connections based on recent encounters.

This complete cycle—where opinions shape communication, which in turn shapes opinions and network ties—repeats, allowing the framework to model the emergent, multi-level dynamics of phenomena like polarization.

4
Computational Experiments

Having established our simulation framework, we now demonstrate its capabilities through systematic computational experiments investigating the drivers of online polarization. Each experiment involves 50 agents interacting over 100 time steps, with specific parameters varied to isolate different mechanisms. All results represent averages across 5 simulation runs per parameter constellation to ensure statistical robustness, with each run employing freshly generated network structures and randomized initial conditions to ensure observed patterns reflect genuine dynamics rather than artifacts of specific realizations.

Polarization is measured using the Esteban-Ray polarization index:
P=K\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{n^{2+\alpha}}|o_{i}-o_{j}|
, where
o_{i}
and
o_{j}
are agent opinions,
n
is population size,
\alpha=1.6
, and
K=1
is a normalization constant. This index captures opinion distance between agents weighted by population frequency, appropriately measuring polarization in contexts with multimodal distributions including the trimodal outcomes observed in our experiments
Esteban and Ray (1994)
.

4.3
). Finally, we investigate how polarized environments shape different forms of social engagement, from supportive interactions to antagonistic exchanges (
Section

4.4
). Detailed experimental setups and additional results are provided in

4.1
Polarization Drivers: Psychology, Diversity, and Network Structure

Understanding when and how societies become polarized requires examining the complex interplay between individual psychology, social structure, and initial conditions. Our simulations reveal that collective opinion outcomes depend critically on three specific factors: agents’ tolerance for disagreement (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
), initial opinion diversity, and network connectivity between opposing camps. Timeline analyses with confidence intervals for all experiments are provided in
Section

9
.

4.1.1
Initial Opinion Diversity

We first explored how agents’ psychological tolerance for disagreement—their ”latitude of acceptance”
Sherif et al. (1965)
—interacts with the initial spread of opinions in the population. The parameter
\sigma_{\text{base}}
governs this fundamental balance: low values create agents who reject nearly any differing opinion, while high values create agents who assimilate broadly. To isolate these psychological dynamics, we held network structure constant at moderate integration (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0.5
), representing a realistic balance between community clustering and cross-cutting ties that allows both within-group and between-group interactions to influence opinion dynamics. The systematic effects of varying this network parameter are examined in
Section

4.1.2
.

The relationship between tolerance and polarization proves highly nonlinear and depends critically on initial diversity (
Fig.

2
a). To understand this complex landscape, we examine three prototypical cases representing the natural parameter range (0-2) established in our methods that capture distinct edge conditions. Under pure repulsion (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0
), polarization occurs only when sufficient initial diversity exists (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}\geq 0.4
, confirmed across multiple simulation runs), producing characteristic trimodal distributions—two extreme peaks flanking a persistent moderate center (
Fig.

3
a). This trimodal structure emerges from the interaction between pure repulsion and biased network connectivity: moderate agents are predominantly exposed to extreme content from their own side, causing repulsion toward the center. However, when they cross the neutral line (opinion = 0), they become exposed to extremists from the opposite side, again causing repulsion back toward the center. This creates stable oscillations around neutrality, sustaining a substantial moderate camp. This center-repulsion mechanism aligns with theoretical accounts of polarization as an inherent communicative order where moderates may be alienated by the dogmatic discourse of their own ideological camp
Kumkar (2025)
.

Under these conditions, pre-existing extreme factions provide stable attractors: intermediate tolerance enables selective attraction to similar agents within the same broad ideological camp while still repelling opposing extremists, allowing agents to consolidate around opposing poles rather than exhibiting the center-oscillation dynamics observed under pure repulsion.

Crucially, the same intermediate tolerance parameters produce fundamentally different outcomes under lower initial diversity conditions. With moderate or low initial diversity (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}\leq 0.7

Without sufficient pre-existing extreme factions, the same tolerance parameters drive toward consensus as moderate agents find common ground. This bifurcation reveals the conditional nature of intermediate tolerance: outcomes depend critically on whether the initial distribution contains extreme factions that can serve as ideological attractors.

Conversely, high tolerance (
\sigma_{\text{base}}\geq 1.4
) consistently drives consensus across all initial diversity conditions (
Fig.

3

, combined with pre-existing extreme factions that can serve as ideological attractors for moderate agents.

4.1.3
The Role of Extremist Influencers

The presence of extremist actors further demonstrates how structural context shapes polarization dynamics. We examined scenarios where extremist influencers (
\pm 0.8
opinions) attempt to influence a predominantly moderate population (
\pm 0.1
opinions) across different network mixing conditions. Our parameter ranges reflect empirical patterns: we varied influencer presence from 0-10 per faction (0-40% of the population), encompassing estimates that extreme political activists comprise 10-15% of politically engaged populations
Pew Research Center (2021)
, while dedicated political extremists represent 5-10%
Enders and Uscinski (2021)
. This range covers both minimal extremist presence observed in most democratic societies and higher concentrations found in polarized contexts where extremist voices can be algorithmically amplified beyond their demographic proportions
Tucker et al. (2018)
.

Extremist influencers achieve maximum impact in a ”Goldilocks zone” of moderate network mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}\approx 0.3-0.7
) that provides optimal conditions for both faction consolidation and cross-cutting friction (
Fig.

2
c). The heatmap reveals that polarization peaks at intermediate mixing levels, particularly when extremist presence is substantial, declining toward both complete segregation and full integration. Under complete segregation (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0
), influencers’ reach is constrained to their own communities, limiting their ability to create society-wide polarization. Under full integration (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=1.0
), extremist voices become diluted within diverse information streams, reducing their relative influence on moderate agents.

The moderate mixing zone represents the structural configuration most conducive to polarization. Here, influencers retain sufficient connectivity to reach across community boundaries while maintaining enough clustering to build cohesive opposing factions. This enables them to simultaneously leverage in-group dynamics (through community consolidation) and inter-group dynamics (through cross-cutting antagonism), maximizing their polarizing potential. The ”Goldilocks zone” effect intensifies with the number of influencers, demonstrating that moderate network structures show highest susceptibility to extremist influence.

This finding reveals nonlinear relationships between network structure and polarization outcomes. Neither complete fragmentation nor total integration produces the highest extremist effectiveness; instead, moderately integrated networks—typically presumed to promote balanced discourse—demonstrate greatest susceptibility to polarization dynamics. This pattern suggests that structural parameters may have counterintuitive effects on polarization, where intermediate connectivity levels optimize conditions for extremist influence.

4.4
From Beliefs to Behavior: The Functions of Social Engagement

Our framework enables investigation of observable behaviors and their different social functions beyond latent beliefs. We analyzed agent engagement patterns to understand not just what agents believe, but how and why they interact. Our findings reveal how distinct forms of communication—from simple affirmation to direct antagonism—emerge from the interplay of the social environment and agent psychology. All engagement analyses use our unified interaction model with different parameter configurations to approximate distinct interaction types, following the established experimental protocol of 5 simulation runs per condition (detailed experimental setups in
Sections

9.8
,
9.9
and
9.10
).

First, we examined a simplified behavioral model based on homophilic agreement with no discordant interaction (
p_{\text{dis}}^{\text{(react)}}=0
), approximating supportive interactions like ”likes” where agents predominantly engage with ideologically aligned content, consistent with empirical patterns showing likes are typically homophilic
Himelboim et al. (2013)
;
Colleoni et al. (2014)
(detailed in
Section

9.8
). This model shows a clear limitation: in polarized environments, it predicts complete communicative separation. As shown in
Fig.

12
, agents in polarized conditions form rigid
”reaction bubbles,”
with cross-stance interaction ratios remaining at zero regardless of algorithmic discovery rates or network mixing. While the same algorithmic interventions successfully promote cross-cutting interaction in unpolarized populations, they show limited effectiveness once opinions become polarized. This highlights that a purely homophilic model cannot account for the cross-cutting—albeit often hostile—discourse commonly observed in real online spaces
Bail et al. (2018)
;
Gruzd and Roy (2014)
.

To build a more realistic model, we then introduced a mechanism to explain this phenomenon: antagonistic engagement. We activated the agent’s intrinsic
propensity for discordant reaction
(
p_{\text{dis}}^{\text{(react)}}
), where higher values approximate argumentative interactions like comments that occur across ideological divides (
Section

9.9
)
siegel_digital_2021
. As shown in
Fig.

13
, this psychological factor provides a mechanism for understanding how communicative bubbles are pierced. In polarized environments, a high propensity for discordant interaction generates significant cross-ideological engagement where algorithmic changes alone could not. Notably, this communicative bridging does not imply ideological moderation. On the contrary, the function of such antagonistic engagement may be to reinforce in-group convictions and perform identity, explaining how cross-cutting exchanges can coexist with, or even deepen, underlying polarization.

Finally, we analyzed these dynamics under the most realistic conditions, where opinions and engagement patterns co-evolve with maximum propensity for discordant interaction (
p_{\text{dis}}^{\text{(react)}}=1.0
;
Section

9.10
). The results reveal a counterintuitive relationship between polarization and engagement (
Fig.

14
). While both consensus conditions (low initial diversity) and highly polarized outcomes (high initial diversity) generate substantial interaction, moderate polarization creates an ”engagement valley” with the lowest interaction levels. By comparing engagement patterns with final polarization outcomes (
Fig.

14
c), we observe that this interaction minimum corresponds precisely to conditions producing moderate disagreement. This suggests that in our computational model, lukewarm conflict is less engaging than either consensus-building interactions or passionate antagonistic exchanges. While these findings require careful interpretation given the computational nature of our approach, they point toward potentially important dynamics where moderate disagreement—often considered healthy for democratic discourse—may be inherently unstable in engagement-driven environments. The persistence of cross-cutting engagement under high polarization conditions demonstrates that complete communicative separation is not inevitable when opinions remain dynamic, though the quality and consequences of such engagement warrant careful consideration.

5
Empirical Validation with Human Participants

The preceding computational experiments demonstrated our framework’s capacity to generate complex structural, communicative, and behavioral dynamics. A critical question, however, is whether these agent-generated environments are psychologically meaningful to real users. To bridge the gap between simulation and human experience, we conducted an empirical experiment to validate the core assumptions of our model and to test whether human responses align with our computational findings. This section details the methodology and results of that validation study, placing them in direct conversation with the simulation results from
Section

4
. The full study details are available in
Section

11
.

5.1
Methodology

To validate our framework, we employed a
2\times 3
between-subjects factorial design, manipulating two key environmental factors that mirror our computational experiments: (1)
Polarization Degree
(Moderate vs. Polarized agent discourse) and (2)
Recommendation Bias
(pro-UBI, contra-UBI, or balanced). The discussion topic was Universal Basic Income (UBI).

We recruited 122 experienced social media users via Prolific. Participants were instructed to engage naturally for 10 minutes with a simulated microblogging platform populated by 30 pre-programmed agents reflecting their assigned condition (
Fig.

15
). During this period, we measured perceptual variables using multi-item Likert scales and logged all user interactions (likes, comments, reposts, follows). Pre- and post-interaction questionnaires captured opinion change on the topic. A full description of the procedure, participant demographics, and measures is provided in
Section

11
.

5.2
Results: Aligning Human Data with Computational Models

Our experimental results provide strong validation for the framework’s ability to generate psychologically salient environments and reveal key areas of both alignment and divergence between simulated and human behavior.

Our computational content analysis (

This pattern can be understood through the lens of our participant sample characteristics: the majority held moderate attitudes toward UBI, creating two distinct interaction scenarios. In the moderate condition, these participants encountered agents expressing nuanced viewpoints similar to their own, facilitating engagement through commenting. In the polarized condition, the same moderate participants faced highly extreme positions distant from their own perspectives, reducing their willingness to engage through high-effort interactions like commenting. This pattern resembles our computational findings where agents with low propensity for discordant engagement showed reduced cross-cutting interaction in polarized environments while maintaining activity in consensual settings. The human data thus suggests that discourse climate interacts with individual positioning to shape engagement propensity.

Recommendation Bias
significantly influenced total engagement volume (
p=.042

Given that participants began with neutral-to-negative UBI attitudes, exposure to polarized arguments against UBI seemed to provide psychological justification for maintaining their existing skepticism. Participants’ opinions moved toward the extreme anti-UBI position, suggesting these arguments resonated with their predispositions. Notably, exposure to polarized pro-UBI arguments did not produce comparable positive opinion shifts, potentially because endorsing major societal change carries higher psychological costs than maintaining the current state.

While some interaction patterns showed trends rather than statistical significance, the observed effect sizes (Cohen’s
d\approx 0.3-0.5

This suggests that environmental factors influenced how much participants’ opinions changed without operating through their conscious evaluation of specific message characteristics.

This finding suggests that humans may respond to discourse climate through processes that bypass deliberate content analysis. The polarization degree of the environment directly affected opinion volatility, indicating that contextual factors—the overall ”feel” of the discussion—can influence attitude change independently of participants’ explicit recognition of specific persuasive features.

Our computational framework models opinion updates through a polynomial function based on opinion distance (

The human pattern shows some alignment with this computational approach, as both involve rapid responses to environmental characteristics rather than deliberate feature-by-feature evaluation. However, key differences remain in the specific pathways through which environmental factors operate, pointing toward opportunities to refine our understanding of how discourse climate shapes opinion dynamics in both computational and human contexts.

6
Discussion

Our integrated computational-empirical approach reveals polarization as a multi-dimensional phenomenon where structural, psychological, and communicative processes systematically decouple in ways that challenge unidimensional explanations. Through controlled computational experiments measuring network structure, belief distributions, and discourse characteristics simultaneously, combined with human validation testing whether these patterns translate to actual psychological responses, we demonstrate that dimensions assumed to move in lockstep can evolve independently. Network segregation can occur without belief radicalization. Discourse can transform independently of opinion change. Interventions targeting one dimension can fail or intensify dynamics at another. These findings fundamentally challenge assumptions underlying both theoretical models and intervention strategies, from structural explanations emphasizing echo chambers to psychological models assuming homogeneous opinion dynamics to communicative approaches treating discourse quality as primary. We structure this discussion in three parts: computational insights revealing dimensional decoupling and counterintuitive dynamics (
Section

6.1
), human validation confirming psychological relevance while uncovering mechanisms computational models miss (
Section

6.2
), and methodological contributions establishing infrastructure for systematic human-in-the-loop investigation (
Section

6.3
).

6.1
Computational Insights: Multi-Level Polarization Dynamics

Our computational experiments revealed polarization as involving complex interactions between psychological, structural, and algorithmic factors that challenge simpler models. Rather than identifying single causal mechanisms, our systematic exploration demonstrated how these forces combine through counterintuitive dynamics with important implications for both theory and intervention design.

6.1.1
The Echo Chamber Paradox: Polarization as Communicative Order

Our network connectivity analysis (
Section

4.1
) reveals this paradox: segregation buffers while integration polarizes. In the presence of extremist influencers, peak polarization emerges at moderate network mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}\approx 0.3-0.7
), though full integration also produces high polarization under pure repulsion conditions. This finds strong empirical support in recent observational studies where cross-cutting interactions, rather than echo chambers, drove the most polarized exchanges
Dubois and Blank (2018)
;
Bail et al. (2018)
, with computational models demonstrating how digital media drive affective polarization through partisan sorting rather than isolation
Törnberg (2022)
;
Törnberg et al. (2021)
. Our computational experiments provide a mechanistic explanation: contact between opposing camps enables rather than mitigates polarization dynamics.

These findings align with what Kumkar terms polarization as ”communicative order”; the inherent modus operandi of political discourse rather than a pathological deviation from democratic norms
Kumkar (2025)
. Our computational model operationalizes this theoretical perspective through friction-based mechanisms grounded in Social Judgment Theory: agents develop conviction resistance to disagreement, evaluate messages through interpretive lenses shaped by interaction history, and form political identities through differentiation from opposing positions. The model’s behavior under different parameter regimes provides crucial insight: polarization emerges reliably under conditions representative of real political discourse—moderate tolerance for disagreement, network integration enabling contact with opposing views, and presence of committed voices that anchor opposing positions. Conversely, consensus emerges only under conditions atypical of political contexts: unrealistically high tolerance for disagreement that eliminates conviction resistance, complete network segregation that removes the constitutive other necessary for political identity formation, or absence of committed voices that would provide recognizable poles for orientation.

Critically, even our trimodal outcomes, which superficially might appear to show moderate consensus, actually demonstrate polarization dynamics consistent with Kumkar’s framework. Using his magnetic field metaphor: polarization manifests not primarily as clustering at the poles but as orientation toward poles. Even when moderate agents constitute the numerical majority, if they define their positions by differentiating from two extremes rather than through autonomous principles, the communicative situation remains polarized. Our trimodal distributions exhibit precisely this pattern: the central cluster does not represent an independent centrist ideology but rather agents caught between and oriented toward opposing poles, their moderate positions intelligible only through reference to the extremes they reject.

Why does friction drive polarization? Following Schmitt’s concept of the political as defined by the friend-enemy distinction
Schmitt (2008)
, political identity formation requires the presence of a constitutive
other
against which group identity crystallizes. Complete segregation eliminates this constitutive tension; without contact with opposing perspectives, there is no clear boundary against which to define one’s political position. Laclau and Mouffe’s theory of hegemonic articulation through antagonism
Laclau and Mouffe (2014)
clarifies this dynamic: political identities are not pre-existing essences but emerge through differentiation from opposing positions, with antagonism constitutive of politics itself; political subjects define themselves through opposition.

The temporal dynamics through which this friction generates polarization can be understood through what Bateson termed
complementary schismogenesis
; progressive differentiation through interaction that amplifies opposing characteristics
Bateson (1958)
. Our simulations demonstrate this process: when agents from opposing camps encounter each other in moderately mixed networks, their interactions create a feedback loop where exposure to disagreement strengthens conviction in one’s own position while simultaneously pushing opponents further in the opposite direction. Moderate integration provides optimal conditions for this dynamic: sufficient cross-cutting exposure to fuel the differentiation process (enabling identity crystallization), sufficient clustering to maintain the group boundaries being differentiated. Pure segregation prevents the interaction necessary for schismogenesis, while complete mixing dilutes the community structure necessary for group identity formation.

These convergent theoretical perspectives and empirical patterns suggest that once minimal inter-group contact exists—a condition nearly universal in real-world networks
Guess et al. (2018)
;
Barberá et al. (2015)
—
tolerance for disagreement
becomes the decisive factor shaping polarization outcomes rather than network structure per se. Our experiments demonstrate that psychological parameters governing how agents process disagreement exert stronger influence on final polarization than structural parameters once networks contain even modest cross-cutting ties.

This reconceptualization of polarization as communicative order rather than structural breakdown has profound implications for intervention design. Attempts to eliminate polarization through structural means—breaking echo chambers, maximizing cross-cutting exposure between opposing camps—may be fighting against the intrinsic logic of political identity formation. Our findings suggest that such interventions may intensify rather than reduce polarization by increasing the friction necessary for identity crystallization. The question shifts from ”how do we eliminate polarization?” to ”how do we structure productive discourse within the friction-based dynamics inherent to political pluralism?” This reframing suggests interventions should focus on cultivating psychological capacities for engaging with disagreement constructively rather than attempting to eliminate disagreement through structural engineering. Our empirical validation provides further evidence for this communicative order perspective, as detailed in
Section

6.2
, where participants’ engagement patterns reflected the friction-based dynamics predicted by our computational model.

6.1.2
Algorithmic Interventions: Temporal Asymmetries and Decoupling

Our co-evolutionary analysis (
Section

4.2
) reveals that the relationship between opinion polarization and network structure is more complex than traditional homophilic rewiring models suggest
Sasahara et al. (2021)
;
Centola et al. (2007)
. A particularly important finding emerges from our examination of influencer-driven dynamics: moderate influencer presence (3-7 per side) can drive substantial structural segregation while producing only moderate opinion polarization (indices 0.6-0.8 rather than extreme values approaching 1.5). This intermediate regime challenges assumptions that visible community sorting necessarily indicates belief radicalization. Rather than the binary of consensus versus extreme polarization typically assumed in computational models, real-world polarization may often stabilize at moderate belief levels where sufficient opinion differences exist to drive homophilic clustering yet beliefs remain moderate enough to avoid the conviction resistance and repulsion mechanisms that characterize extreme polarization. This decoupling suggests that structural sorting and belief extremization may operate as partially independent processes
DellaPosta et al. (2015)
, with different sensitivity thresholds that enable early-stage polarization to create stable, moderately differentiated communities that resist further belief extremization.

Our analysis of algorithmic discovery rates revealed a second, distinct mechanism through which opinion and network dynamics decouple. Sharp threshold effects
Granovetter (1978)
emerge where identical algorithmic interventions produce dramatically different outcomes depending on the reach of extreme influencers in the population. When extreme influencer presence remains minimal (0-2 influencers per side), polarization stays low regardless of whether recommendation algorithms prioritize network-based content or enable discovery of new connections. However, once extreme influencer presence reaches intermediate levels (3-6 influencers), discovery rates begin to strongly mediate outcomes. Algorithmic recommendations that help moderate agents discover like-minded moderates—rather than forcing cross-cutting exposure—offer genuine protection against belief radicalization when extreme influencer reach remains below these critical thresholds. The intervention works as intended: moderates form connections that dilute extreme influence by expanding their moderate networks.

Critically, however, these same interventions show limited effectiveness when applied reactively to populations where extremist presence has already exceeded threshold levels. Under high extremist presence (7+ influencers), high discovery rates provide only temporal mitigation, delaying polarization progression and reducing its ultimate severity, but not preventing substantial opinion shift. This reveals a fundamental temporal asymmetry in algorithmic intervention effectiveness: increasing exposure diversity functions preventatively but not remedially. Early intervention, when extremist voices constitute a small fraction of discourse, allows algorithmic diversity mechanisms to connect moderates and dilute extremist influence within broader information streams. Reactive measures applied after extremist presence becomes substantial face a different informational environment where extremist content constitutes a significant portion of recommended content, rendering discovery mechanisms far less effective at countering polarization dynamics already in motion.

6.1.3
The Engagement Valley: Instability of Moderate Discourse

Our analysis of engagement dynamics (
Section

4.4
) reveals a counterintuitive pattern with troubling implications for democratic discourse: moderate disagreement produces the lowest engagement levels—an ”engagement valley”—while both consensus and high polarization generate substantial interaction. This finding directly challenges assumptions about moderate discourse as inherently stable and engaging for democratic deliberation
Wojcieszak (2011)
;
Mouffe (1999)
. The engagement valley suggests that moderately polarized discourse may be inherently unstable in attention-driven social environments, where neither the collaborative satisfaction of consensus-building nor the emotional arousal of passionate disagreement
Fischer and Lelkes (2023)
provides sufficient motivation for sustained interaction.

This inherent engagement deficit of moderate disagreement may render it particularly vulnerable in algorithmic systems that optimize for engagement metrics
Stray (2022)
. If platforms maximize user activity—a reasonable business objective that correlates with advertising revenue—algorithmic recommendation systems face implicit pressure against surfacing moderate discourse. Content generating lukewarm responses gets deprioritized relative to both consensus-affirming content (which generates supportive engagement through likes and shares) and polarizing content (which generates passionate engagement through comments and antagonistic interactions). This creates systematic disadvantages for the measured disagreement often considered essential for healthy democratic discourse. Our findings suggest that even without intentional suppression of moderate voices, engagement optimization creates structural incentives that inadvertently favor the extremes.

6.1.4
Polarization as Communicative Transformation

The convergent evidence from computational content analysis (
Section

4.3
) and human validation (
Section

5
) reveals polarization as fundamentally transforming communicative practice itself. Rather than manifesting as isolated textual features, polarization produces a systematic
communicative syndrome
: the simultaneous activation of increased emotionality, heightened group identity salience, and reduced epistemic uncertainty that operates as an integrated discourse transformation. Our computational analysis demonstrated these patterns in agent-generated content, and crucially, human participants accurately perceived these differences with large effect sizes (detailed in
Section

6.2
), confirming that computational patterns translate into recognizable communicative environments.

This syndrome aligns with multiple theoretical frameworks. The dramatic increase in group identity salience validates Social Identity model of Deindividuation Effects (SIDE) theory
Reicher et al. (1995)
: when individual identities become less salient in online contexts, group-based identities fill the void, transforming discussions from person-to-person dialogue into faction-versus-faction confrontation. The concurrent rise in emotionality supports intergroup emotions theory
Mackie et al. (2000)
, where group-based cognition triggers emotional responses—anger toward out-groups, pride in in-groups—that differ qualitatively from interpersonal emotions. The reduction in expressed uncertainty aligns with uncertainty-identity theory
Hogg (2007)
: when political identity becomes central to self-concept, admitting uncertainty threatens that identity, creating pressure toward confident assertion.

Crucially, our framework reveals message interpretation as an
active, historically-situated process
rather than passive information reception. Individual differences in interpretation—mediated by both stable personality traits and dynamic interaction histories—create persistent interpretive biases that operate independently of message content. Our computational experiments demonstrated that agents with negative interaction histories systematically interpret subsequent neutral messages as more negative, emotional, and group-salient, even when message content remains constant. This demonstrates how
negativity bias

Rozin and Royzman (2001)
creates interpretive frames that persist beyond triggering events, functioning as lenses through which all subsequent communication gets filtered.

This mechanism provides a novel explanation for polarization persistence that extends beyond attitude-based accounts. Communication systems develop hysteresis
Arthur (1994)
;
Pierson (2000)
where past interactions create interpretive lenses that may lock in polarization dynamics even after initial triggers disappear. Unlike opinion-based models that assume attitudes drive persistence—requiring continued exposure to polarizing content to maintain extreme views—our findings suggest that accumulated interpretive biases embedded in communication patterns may prove more durable than attitude shifts themselves. Once interpretive frames become established, they color perception of new information in ways that reinforce existing polarization, potentially explaining why polarization can persist despite changing circumstances, moderated content, or attempts at depolarization through exposure to balanced information.

6.2
Human Validation: Convergences and Divergences

The human validation study (
Section

5
) tests our computational theory against actual human psychology, revealing both strong convergences that confirm the framework’s psychological relevance and illuminating divergences that point toward previously unrecognized mechanisms of human social cognition. These patterns provide critical insights for both validating computational models and advancing theoretical understanding of polarization processes. Our findings align with recent theoretical work conceptualizing polarization as an inherent communicative order
Kumkar (2025)
, while revealing specific mechanisms through which discourse climate shapes human psychology.

Strong Convergences

The human validation provides crucial confirmation that our computational manipulations translate into psychologically meaningful experiences. Participants’ ability to accurately perceive the polarization-induced communicative syndrome—increased emotionality (
g=1.53
), heightened group salience (
g=0.73
), and reduced uncertainty (
g=-1.26
)—demonstrates that the systematic linguistic and rhetorical differences between polarized and moderate discourse create recognizable communicative environments that humans detect automatically, validating our framework’s capacity to generate authentic discourse variations.

The structural equation modeling reveals psychological pathways underlying these perceptions: perceived group salience strongly mediates the effect of emotionality on perceived polarization, indicating that emotional content primarily activates group-based thinking rather than conveying information. This provides human-level validation for our computational insights about polarization as simultaneous in-group attraction and out-group differentiation.

Engagement patterns show meaningful alignment between human and computational behavior. Participants with moderate UBI attitudes showed systematically different engagement patterns across conditions: reduced commenting in polarized environments (
M=0.28
vs.
M=0.77
in moderate conditions) while maintaining activity through low-effort interactions. This pattern reflects ideological distance effects: moderate participants encountered nuanced viewpoints similar to their own in moderate conditions, facilitating engagement through commenting, while polarized conditions presented extreme positions distant from their perspectives, reducing willingness for high-effort interactions. This behavioral adaptation echoes our computational findings where agents with low propensity for discordant engagement showed reduced cross-cutting interaction in polarized environments while remaining active in consensual settings.

Illuminating Divergences: Beyond Opinion Distance Models

While convergences validate core framework assumptions, the divergences prove equally valuable for advancing theoretical understanding and point toward next-generation computational models. The most significant divergence concerns opinion formation mechanisms and suggests that current opinion dynamics models may miss crucial environmental effects.

Our computational model assumes that environmental conditions influence opinion change primarily through message content interpretation—specifically, how perceived opinion positions within messages are evaluated and processed through formal opinion dynamics equations based on opinion distance calculations. However, our Structural Equation Model analysis revealed that environmental polarization directly influenced the magnitude of opinion change without operating through participants’ conscious recognition of specific persuasive features or mediation through measured perceptual variables such as emotionality, bias, or group salience.

This finding suggests that discourse climate operates as an
ambient influence
—shaping cognitive and behavioral responses through environmental properties that bypass deliberate content evaluation. Environmental polarization influences opinion volatility through rapid contextual processing that creates what we might conceptualize as a general ”receptivity state” that affects how readily opinions shift, operating alongside but distinct from deliberate message evaluation processes. This pattern aligns with dual-process theories of persuasion, particularly the Elaboration Likelihood Model’s distinction between central and peripheral routes to attitude change
Petty and Cacioppo (1986)
. While our computational model implements central route processing—systematic evaluation of message content based on opinion distances—the human data suggest peripheral route processing where environmental cues influence attitude change through heuristic mechanisms that bypass detailed scrutiny. This interpretation also resonates with theoretical perspectives suggesting that polarization functions through the form and structure of discourse itself, not merely through explicit ideological content
Kumkar (2025)
.

This divergence points toward future opinion dynamics models that incorporate environmental discourse parameters as direct influences on opinion update rates, independent of specific message content. Rather than relying solely on opinion distance calculations, future computational models might include discourse climate variables that modulate attitude changeability at the environmental level—capturing how the overall communicative context shapes psychological responsiveness.

The engagement analysis revealed theoretically meaningful pathways that warrant investigation in larger samples. A ”Status Quo Reinforcement” pathway appeared in polarized contra-UBI conditions, where arguments against societal change triggered the highest engagement levels while reinforcing existing beliefs—consistent with loss aversion and status quo bias
Kahneman et al. (1991)
. Conversely, a ”Deliberative Engagement” pathway emerged in moderate pro-UBI conditions, where cooperative discourse invited consideration without immediate opinion shifts. Additionally, pro-UBI recommendation bias unexpectedly decreased opinion change magnitude, suggesting asymmetric resistance where humans more readily maintain skepticism toward change proposals than adopt them; a directional asymmetry that opinion dynamics models typically overlook. These findings indicate that future computational models should incorporate both environmental discourse effects and directional biases to better capture the complex ways humans respond to polarized communication environments.

6.3
A Framework for Systematic Discovery

Beyond its specific theoretical and empirical insights, this work demonstrates a methodological approach that addresses fundamental challenges in social dynamics research. Current approaches force constrained choices: mathematical models sacrifice communicative realism for analytical precision
Flache et al. (2017)
;
Vande Kerckhove et al. (2016)
, observational studies limit researchers to passive inference from correlational data
Bakshy et al. (2015)
, and conventional experiments restrict scope and scale
Bail et al. (2018)
;
Banks et al. (2021)
. By integrating LLM-generated communication with formal opinion dynamics and systematic human validation, our framework enables systematic investigation across these traditional boundaries.

To our knowledge, this represents the first opinion dynamics framework integrating formal belief update mechanisms, LLM-generated communication, multi-modal interaction behaviors (likes, comments, reposts), algorithmic recommendation systems, and dynamic network co-evolution within a single unified architecture validated against human behavior. While prior work has combined subsets of these components—such as belief dynamics with network co-evolution
Sasahara et al. (2021)
;
Baumann et al. (2020)
or LLM-based communication with agent interactions
Chuang et al. (2024)
;
Breum et al. (2024)
—the complete integration enables investigation of cross-dimensional dynamics impossible with partial models. This unification proves essential for our core findings: the decoupling of opinion and network polarization emerges precisely because we model both processes and their algorithmic mediation; the communicative syndrome becomes observable only through LLM-generated discourse analyzed alongside formal opinion states; the engagement valley manifests from separating belief formation from behavioral interaction.

The framework’s capabilities emerge from this integration. LLM-based agents generate semantically rich, contextually appropriate discourse while maintaining experimental control over psychological, structural, and algorithmic variables. Researchers can systematically vary specific factors and directly test how humans respond to the resulting environments. Computational experiments explore vast parameter spaces to identify interesting dynamics—such as the intermediate regime where structural sorting occurs without belief radicalization, or temporal asymmetries in algorithmic intervention effectiveness—which can then be validated against human behavior. Human validation, in turn, reveals mechanisms not captured by computational models—such as ambient environmental influences on opinion volatility—guiding theoretical refinement. This iterative cycle enables cumulative knowledge building impossible with either approach alone, enabling systematic investigation of communication processes, memory effects, and interpretive biases with levels of control and realism previously unattainable.

The methodology provides replicable infrastructure that other researchers can adopt and extend beyond polarization to other social phenomena involving communication, influence, and collective dynamics. Future work can build on this foundation by incorporating more sophisticated agent architectures, enhanced opinion dynamics models with multiple belief dimensions, and more realistic platform mechanisms. The framework’s value lies not just in the specific findings it generated for this study, but in establishing a general template for human-in-the-loop validation of computational social science models.

6.4
Limitations and Future Directions

Several important limitations constrain current findings and suggest development priorities. First, it is essential to distinguish the function of LLM-based agents from human cognition. Despite their impressive capabilities, these agents lack genuine beliefs, emotions, and deep reasoning processes. Their primary value lies in generating controlled, stylistically appropriate textual stimuli that constitute a manipulable social environment for human participants, rather than perfectly replicating human actors. Future work should continue validating agent behavior against more fine-grained human interaction data and exploring more sophisticated agent architectures with explicitly modeled memory decay, adaptive strategies, or heterogeneous personality traits.

Second, our simulation necessarily simplifies complex real-world dynamics. The network structures, recommendation algorithms, and interaction mechanisms represent abstracted versions of the sophisticated systems governing actual platforms. The single recommendation algorithm we implement, while providing controlled experimental conditions, cannot capture the full complexity of deep learning-based recommendation systems used by major platforms. Further refinement of these components—particularly incorporating more realistic recommendation algorithms and platform mechanisms—would enhance ecological validity.

Third, our validation study focused on short-term exposure effects without a zero-treatment control group. The 10-minute interaction period serves as an initial validation of the framework’s core principles rather than a comprehensive examination of long-term polarization. This brief exposure captures immediate heuristic processing and reactions to content but may not reflect the dynamics of identity entrenchment, social proof
Cialdini (2021)
;
Festinger (1954)
, and parasocial relationship development
Horton and Wohl (1956)
;
Rubin et al. (2000)
that unfold over extended periods. While this design effectively compares the relative impact of different polarized environments, it cannot establish absolute effects against a non-exposed baseline. For example, while moderate environments initially fostered more commenting, extended exposure to polarized content might overcome initial inhibitions through habituation effects.

Fourth, the single-topic focus (Universal Basic Income) and predominantly moderate participant sample limit generalizability across different issues and populations. Political polarization manifests differently across topics, with some issues generating more intense affective responses than others. Similarly, our sample’s demographic characteristics may not represent populations most susceptible to polarization or those with stronger prior political commitments.

Fifth, it is important to distinguish between
model-specific quantitative predictions
and
generalizable qualitative insights
. Precise parameter thresholds (e.g.,
\sigma_{\text{base}}\approx 0.8
-
1.2
for peak polarization) depend on our specific mathematical formulations and should be interpreted as demonstrating the existence of threshold effects rather than exact values applicable across contexts. Similarly, the specific numbers of influencers required for regime transitions reflect our model parameterization, though the ranges were selected to align with empirical estimates of extremist prevalence in real-world discourse
Pew Research Center (2021)
;
Enders and Uscinski (2021)
. However, the
qualitative patterns
—that segregation can buffer against polarization, that extremist influence peaks at moderate network mixing, that opinion and structural polarization can decouple, that polarization produces a multi-dimensional communicative syndrome—represent theoretical insights that emerge from the model’s structure rather than arbitrary parameter choices.

These limitations point toward several productive research directions. Longitudinal studies tracking attitude evolution over weeks or months would illuminate how polarization dynamics develop and potentially crystallize over time. Cross-topic validation studies would establish whether our findings generalize beyond economic policy to other domains such as social issues, science topics, or international conflicts. Investigations of how different demographic groups, including those with stronger initial political commitments, respond to polarized environments would enhance external validity. Technical extensions should focus on more sophisticated agent architectures, enhanced opinion dynamics models incorporating multiple belief dimensions, and more realistic platform mechanisms that better approximate real-world algorithmic systems.

7
Conclusion

This study reveals online political polarization as a multi-dimensional phenomenon where opinion radicalization, network segregation, and discourse transformation systematically decouple in ways that challenge unidimensional theories and interventions. Through integrated computational experiments and human validation with 122 participants, we demonstrate that dimensions assumed to move in lockstep can evolve independently: structural sorting can occur without belief extremization, algorithmic interventions can reduce opinion polarization while increasing network segregation, and discourse can transform through ambient environmental effects that bypass deliberate message evaluation. These findings fundamentally reframe polarization from a problem of isolated dimensions—whether echo chambers, extreme beliefs, or toxic discourse—into a friction-based communicative order requiring multi-dimensional understanding and intervention.

Our computational experiments revealed counterintuitive dynamics that challenge conventional assumptions. Complete network segregation buffers against extreme polarization while moderate integration creates optimal conditions for extremism, contradicting echo chamber theories. Algorithmic interventions show sharp threshold effects, succeeding preventatively but failing remedially once extremist presence exceeds critical levels. Moderate disagreement occupies an unstable ”engagement valley” in attention-driven environments. Polarization manifests as a systematic communicative syndrome—simultaneously increased emotionality, heightened group identity salience, and reduced uncertainty—operating through friction inherent to political discourse itself. Human validation confirmed that participants accurately perceived these computational patterns, yet their opinion changes operated through ambient environmental influences rather than deliberate evaluation, revealing mechanisms current models miss.

The framework’s broader contribution lies in establishing replicable infrastructure for investigating social phenomena at the intersection of communication, influence, and collective behavior. By enabling systematic variation of psychological, structural, and algorithmic factors while maintaining communicative realism, this methodology creates what we term a ”digital laboratory” for causal inquiry into dynamics that traditional approaches cannot access—from threshold effects in algorithmic intervention to memory effects in interpretive bias.

Important limitations constrain current findings. The 10-minute exposure captures immediate reactions but not long-term identity crystallization. The single-topic focus (Universal Basic Income) and predominantly moderate sample limit generalizability. The LLM-based agents generate realistic stimuli but lack genuine cognitive processes. Future research can extend these findings through longitudinal studies, cross-topic validation, investigation of populations with stronger political commitments, and technical enhancements incorporating more sophisticated agent architectures and platform mechanisms. By demonstrating how computational rigor and human realism can be productively integrated, this work provides both specific theoretical insights and methodological infrastructure for advancing understanding of our evolving digital public sphere.

Simulation Model

Our computational framework integrates opinion dynamics principles with LLM-based agents to investigate polarization in social networks. Unlike traditional opinion dynamics models that often focus on simplified representations, our approach makes four key contributions:

First, we deeply integrate LLM-based message generation with an opinion dynamics model, enabling agents to adapt content based on individual opinion strengths, personal backgrounds, and interaction histories (Contribution A). Second, we develop a sophisticated opinion dynamics pipeline that captures both homophily and backfire effects, accounting for various initial opinion distributions, community structures, and the influence of extremist actors (Contribution B). Third, we model communication processes (liking, reposting, commenting) as independent from opinion evolution, allowing for more realistic representation of social media interactions (Contribution C). Fourth, we implement dynamic network evolution through following/unfollowing behaviors and personalized recommendation systems that extend beyond simple neighborhood-based approaches (Contribution D).

The system comprises three primary components: (1) an agent-based architecture where individuals possess distinct opinion values, personality traits, and interaction histories; (2) an LLM infrastructure that enables content generation, stance analysis, and interaction evaluation; and (3) a social network structure that governs information dissemination through a recommendation mechanism. Operating through discrete time steps, each iteration encompasses content generation, agent interactions, and network evolution processes.

The core innovation highlighted in this section (Contribution A) lies in the agent architecture’s deep integration of dynamic internal states with sophisticated, LLM-driven communication capabilities. 8.1.3
LLM-Powered Adaptive Communication

A central element of Contribution A is the mechanism by which agents generate communicative content. Rather than using predefined messages, agents leverage an LLM to produce text dynamically. This generation process is adaptive, being conditioned on multiple facets of the agent’s current state and context. An agent’s internal opinion state
o_{i}
directly informs the generated message’s stance and intensity, which in turn influences linguistic style and argument strength, guided by the specifications detailed in
Table

1
and
Table

2
. Simultaneously, the agent’s unique persona
P_{i}
guides the LLM in adopting a consistent and distinct communicative style. The process also incorporates the agent’s interaction history
H_{i}
, allowing the LLM to generate contextually relevant messages that reflect past interactions or experiences, contributing to more coherent conversational behavior. The generated content’s relevance to the ongoing discussion is maintained by conditioning on the simulation’s defined topic term and description. In reactive communication, such as comments or reposts, the generation is additionally conditioned on the specific content and stance of the message being responded to, facilitating direct engagement. This multi-faceted conditioning allows the simulation to capture a richer, more realistic form of online communication where messages are dynamic linguistic acts sensitive to the sender’s state, identity, history, and the immediate situation. This adaptive capability is fundamental to exploring the interplay between nuanced communication, opinion dynamics (detailed in
Section

8.2
), and interaction patterns (
Section

8.3
).

8.2
Opinion Dynamics: Homophily and Reactance (Contribution B)

Building upon the adaptive agent architecture, our framework incorporates a sophisticated opinion dynamics pipeline (Contribution B) designed to capture nuanced psychological phenomena such as homophilous assimilation and reactance (backfire effects). This mechanism governs how agent opinions evolve in response to information exposure, aiming for greater realism compared to simpler linear or bounded confidence models.

8.2.1
Modeling Opinion Shifts

An agent
A_{i}
’s opinion
o_{i}(t)
is updated at each time step
t
based on the set of messages
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
it is exposed to through the recommendation system (detailed in
Section

8.4.3
). A critical prerequisite for calculating the opinion shift induced by a message
m\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
is determining the perceived opinion expressed within that message. Our model achieves this through a dedicated message evaluation function,
\pi
. This function takes the agent
A_{i}
and the message
m
as input and returns a perceived opinion value,
o_{m}
, situated on the standard opinion scale:

\displaystyle o_{m}=\pi(A_{i},m)\mapsto[-1,1]

(9)

This function
\pi
leverages an LLM tasked with analyzing the textual content of message
m
within the context of the simulation’s defined topic. This evaluation ensures that the subsequent opinion update mechanism responds directly to the semantic and pragmatic content of the communication encountered, rather than solely relying on the generating agent’s internal opinion state.

Once the perceived message opinion
o_{m}=\pi(A_{i},m)
has been determined, the calculation of the potential opinion shift
\Delta o_{i}(m)
induced by message
m
for agent
A_{i}
proceeds using a polynomial function modulated by agent conviction and opinion alignment. This approach captures both assimilation towards similar opinions and potential repulsion from dissimilar ones, with a specific mechanism for resistance among highly opinionated agents.

First, the difference
d
between the agent’s current opinion
o_{i}(t)
and the perceived message opinion
o_{m}
is calculated:

\displaystyle d=o_{i}(t)-o_{m}

(10)

The sign of
d
indicates whether the agent’s opinion is more positive (
d>0
) or negative (
d<0
) than the message’s perceived opinion. If
d=0
, no shift occurs for this message.

The core of the shift mechanism relies on comparing this difference
d
to an effective attraction zone, characterized by a squared width
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
. This squared width dynamically adapts based on the agent’s conviction
|o_{i}|
and whether the agent and message opinions share the same sign (
o_{i}\cdot o_{m}>0
). The calculation begins with a fundamental parameter, the
baseline attraction width
,
\sigma_{\text{base}}
. This parameter,
\sigma_{\text{base}}>0
, defines the intrinsic scale governing the balance between assimilation and repulsion. It is modulated by factors incorporating agent conviction.

The resistance effect is primarily active when the agent holds a strong opinion (
|o_{i}|
close to 1) and encounters a message on the same side of the opinion spectrum (
o_{i}\cdot o_{m}>0
). The potential for resistance increases as the agent’s opinion becomes more extreme. This is modeled via a
conviction modulation factor
,
\chi(o_{i})
:

\displaystyle\chi(o_{i})=1-o_{i}^{2}

(11)

This factor,
\chi(o_{i})
, quantifies an agent’s susceptibility to moderating influences based on its current conviction. It approaches 0 for highly convicted agents (
|o_{i}|\to 1
), signifying reduced susceptibility, and is 1 for neutral agents (
o_{i}=0
). This mechanism models the phenomenon where individuals with strong convictions are particularly resistant to information that might moderate their stance, even if that information originates from their own side of the opinion spectrum.

To determine how conviction modulates the attraction width, a smooth switch function,
w_{\text{con}}
, quantifies the concordance between the agent and message opinions based on their product:

\displaystyle w_{\text{con}}(o_{i},o_{m})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh(o_{i}\cdot o_{m})\right)

(12)

This function transitions smoothly from
w_{\text{con}}\approx 0
for discordant opinions (opposite signs) to
w_{\text{con}}\approx 1
for concordant opinions (same sign).

An effective scaling factor for the attraction width,
f_{\text{scale}}
, is calculated based on the concordance and the conviction modulation factor
\chi(o_{i})
:

\displaystyle f_{\text{scale}}(o_{i},o_{m})=1+w_{\text{con}}(o_{i},o_{m})\cdot(\chi(o_{i})-1)

(13)

This factor is approximately 1 for discordant messages (
w_{\text{con}}\approx 0
) and approaches
\chi(o_{i})
for concordant messages (
w_{\text{con}}\approx 1
). Thus, for concordant messages,
f_{\text{scale}}
decreases as agent conviction increases (as
\chi(o_{i})
decreases).

The dynamically adjusted squared attraction width
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
is then computed:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}(o_{i},o_{m})=\left(\sigma_{\text{base}}\cdot f_{\text{scale}}(o_{i},o_{m})\right)^{2}

(14)

This formulation ensures that if opinions are discordant,
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}\approx\sigma_{\text{base}}^{2}
. If opinions are concordant,
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
is scaled down towards
(\sigma_{\text{base}}\cdot\chi(o_{i}))^{2}
. For agents with extreme opinions (
|o_{i}|\to 1
),
\chi(o_{i})\to 0
, causing
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
to shrink significantly when encountering concordant messages. This shrinking activates the resistance mechanism by narrowing the opinion range within which assimilation occurs, making repulsion more likely or reducing the magnitude of assimilation towards less extreme, same-sign opinions.

The raw opinion shift influence
\omega_{i}(m)
is then computed using a cubic polynomial function involving the difference
d
and the effective squared width
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\omega_{i}(m)=d\cdot(d^{2}-\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}(o_{i},o_{m}))

(15)

The behavior of this function depends on the relationship between the opinion difference magnitude
|d|
and the effective width
\sigma_{\text{eff}}=\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}}
:

•

Attraction:
If
|d|^{2}<\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
, then
(d^{2}-\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2})
is negative. The overall shift
\omega_{i}(m)
has the opposite sign to
d=o_{i}-o_{m}
, causing
o_{i}
to shift
towards

o_{m}
.

•

Repulsion:
If
|d|^{2}>\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
, then
(d^{2}-\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2})
is positive. The overall shift
\omega_{i}(m)
has the
same
sign as
d=o_{i}-o_{m}
, causing
o_{i}
to shift
away
from
o_{m}
.

The resistance mechanism, by shrinking
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
for extreme agents receiving same-sign messages, increases the likelihood that
|d|^{2}>\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
, thus promoting repulsion or significantly reducing attraction even for messages that are relatively close but represent a moderation of the agent’s extreme stance.

To ensure the shift induced by a single message remains bounded and its magnitude controllable, the raw influence
\omega_{i}(m)
is passed through the hyperbolic tangent function:

\displaystyle\Delta o_{i}(m)=\tanh\left(\omega_{i}(m)\right)

(16)

This function maps the potentially large raw shift value to the interval
(-1,1)
.

The total opinion change
\Delta O_{i}
for agent
A_{i}
within a time step
t
results from aggregating the individual shifts induced by all messages encountered
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
, scaled by an overall learning rate
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\Delta O_{i}=\lambda\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)}\Delta o_{i}(m)

(17)

The learning rate
\lambda
governs the overall pace of opinion evolution within the simulation. The agent’s opinion
o_{i}(t+1)
is then updated by adding this aggregated shift
\Delta O_{i}
to the current opinion
o_{i}(t)
, and subsequently clipped to remain within the
[-1,1]
range:

\displaystyle o_{i}(t+1)=\text{clip}(o_{i}(t)+\Delta O_{i},-1,1)

(18)

This formulation provides a flexible mechanism for opinion dynamics. Agents are attracted to messages within an effective opinion distance determined by
\sigma_{\text{base}}
and modulated by concordance and conviction (via
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
). They are repelled by messages outside this distance. Crucially, agents exhibit resistance to moderating influences from their own side when their convictions are strong. The key parameter
\sigma_{\text{base}}
controls the baseline balance between attraction and repulsion, allowing the model to simulate various polarization phenomena depending on its configuration, driven by the semantic content of messages as evaluated by the
\pi
function.

8.3
Modeling Communicative Actions (Contribution C)

Distinct from the process of opinion evolution (
Section

8.2
), our framework explicitly models the communicative actions agents perform in response to messages they encounter (Contribution C). These actions, such as liking, reposting, or commenting, are prevalent in online social networks and serve various functions beyond direct persuasion, including social signaling, identity expression, and engagement management. Modeling these actions as a separate probabilistic process allows for a richer simulation of online social behavior.

8.3.1
Interaction Probability Model

When an agent
A_{i}
encounters a message
m
(with perceived stance
o_{m}=\pi(A_{i},m)
), the probability
P_{\text{int}}(A_{i},m)
of performing a communicative action is modeled based on two potential triggers: one driven by opinion concordance (similarity) and another by opinion discordance (oppositeness).

Concordance Trigger Probability (
P_{\text{con}}
)

This component captures interaction driven by agreement or similarity. It depends on the direct opinion difference
d_{\text{con}}=o_{i}-o_{m}
. We model
P_{\text{con}}
using a Gaussian-like function centered at zero difference, reflecting higher probability for interactions with ideologically closer messages:

\displaystyle P_{\text{con}}(o_{i},o_{m})=\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\text{con}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{con}}^{2}}\right)

(19)

The parameter
\sigma_{\text{con}}>0
is the characteristic width controlling tolerance for difference; smaller
\sigma_{\text{con}}
implies interactions are triggered only by very similar opinions (strong homophily).

Discordance Trigger Probability (
P_{\text{dis}}
)

This component captures interaction driven by disagreement or opposition. It depends first on the ”oppositeness distance”:

\displaystyle d_{\text{dis}}(o_{i},o_{m})=\max(0,-\text{sgn}(o_{m})\cdot o_{i})

(20)

This measures how much the agent’s opinion
o_{i}
is on the opposite side of neutral (0) compared to the message
o_{m}
. The sensitivity to this distance is governed by a dynamically adapting width
\sigma_{\text{dis}}(o_{m})
, which narrows for more extreme messages, making the trigger more sensitive to opposition. This dynamic width is defined relative to a
baseline discordance width

\sigma_{\text{dis,base}}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{dis}}(o_{m})=\sigma_{\text{dis,base}}\cdot\exp(-|o_{m}|)

(21)

The probability of a discordance trigger activating, which we denote as
P_{\text{dis}}
, increases with
d_{\text{dis}}
relative to
\sigma_{\text{dis}}(o_{m})
. We model this using a complementary exponential function:

\displaystyle P_{\text{dis}}(o_{i},o_{m})=1-\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\text{dis}}(o_{i},o_{m})^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{dis}}(o_{m})^{2}}\right)

(22)

Combined Interaction Probability

The agent interacts if
either
the concordance or the discordance trigger is activated. The discordance trigger itself is scaled by a key psychological parameter, the
propensity for discordant reaction (
p_{\text{dis}}\in[0,1]
)
, which represents an agent’s general tendency to engage antagonistically.

Assuming the triggers are independent events, the probability of at least one activating is given by the standard formula for the union of two events:
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)
. In our model, this translates to:

\displaystyle P(\text{trigger})=P_{\text{con}}+(p_{\text{dis}}\cdot P_{\text{dis}})-P_{\text{con}}\cdot(p_{\text{dis}}\cdot P_{\text{dis}})

(23)

This reflects that the total probability of a discordance-driven interaction is the base probability
P_{\text{dis}}
scaled by the agent’s propensity
p_{\text{dis}}
.

Finally, this combined trigger probability is scaled by a
base interaction probability (
p_{\text{base}}\in[0,1]
)
, which represents the overall activity level for a given interaction type (e.g., likes are more frequent than comments). This yields the final interaction probability:

\displaystyle P_{\text{int}}(A_{i},m)=p_{\text{base}}\left(P_{\text{con}}+p_{\text{dis}}\cdot P_{\text{dis}}-p_{\text{dis}}\cdot P_{\text{con}}\cdot P_{\text{dis}}\right)

(24)

where
P_{\text{con}}
and
P_{\text{dis}}
are shorthand for their full functional forms dependent on
o_{i}
and
o_{m}
. This model produces interaction likelihoods that peak for highly similar opinions (via
P_{\text{con}}
) but can also increase for highly opposing opinions if
p_{\text{dis}}>0
. It thus captures both homophilic and antagonistic engagement within a single, unified formula.

Modeling Different Interaction Types

The flexibility of the parameters (
p_{\text{base}}
,
\sigma_{\text{con}}
,
p_{\text{dis}}
,
\sigma_{\text{dis,base}}
) allows this model to represent different types of communicative actions:

•

Likes:
Often represent low-effort agreement or endorsement. One might model this with a relatively high base probability (
p_{\text{base}}
), a strong concordance trigger (small
\sigma_{\text{con}}
to primarily like similar content), and a very low or zero discordance propensity (
p_{\text{dis}}\approx 0
).

•

Comments:
Can serve diverse functions, including agreement, clarification, or disagreement. Modeling comments might involve a lower base probability (
p_{\text{base}}
, higher effort than liking), potentially a wider concordance width (
\sigma_{\text{con}}
, allowing comments on slightly different views), and crucially, a non-zero discordance propensity (
p_{\text{dis}}>0
) to capture argumentative replies. The sensitivity parameters (
\sigma_{\text{con}},\sigma_{\text{dis, base}}
) would tune whether comments are primarily supportive or critical.

•

Reposts (without comment):
Typically strong endorsements. Could be modeled similarly to likes but perhaps with an even stronger concordance trigger (very small
\sigma_{\text{con}}
) and/or a lower base probability (
p_{\text{base}}
) reflecting lower frequency.

By setting distinct parameter sets for each action type, the simulation can capture differentiated engagement patterns reflecting the varied functions of interactions on social platforms.

8.3.2
Contextual Response Generation

If the probabilistic determination results in an agent deciding to perform a communicative action that involves generating new content (specifically, commenting or reposting with commentary), the simulation leverages the LLM-based message generation capability described earlier (
Section

8.1.3
). Crucially, for these reactive message types, the generation process is provided with additional context: the content and perceived stance (
o_{m}
) of the original message
m
being reacted to. The LLM is instructed to generate a response that directly addresses, agrees with, or disagrees with the original message, ensuring conversational coherence and relevance. This context-aware generation allows the simulation to produce more realistic dialogue sequences compared to models where reactions are merely abstract events.

8.4
Co-evolving Social Network Structure (Contribution D)

A key aspect of our simulation framework is the explicit modeling of the social network structure and its dynamic evolution alongside agent opinions (Contribution D). This allows us to investigate the interplay between network topology and polarization processes. The network is represented as a directed graph
G(t)=(V,E(t))
, where
V
is the set of
N
agents, and
E(t)
is the set of directed edges
(A_{i},A_{j})
at time
t
, signifying that agent
A_{i}
follows agent
A_{j}
. This section details the initialization of the network at
t=0
and its subsequent evolution.

8.4.1
Network Representation and Initialization

The simulation begins by constructing an initial social graph
G(0)
that reflects characteristics often observed in online social networks, namely community structure based on shared attributes (here, opinion) and heterogeneous degree distributions (presence of hubs/influencers). The generation process aims to create a network with a specified overall edge density
\rho_{e}
, tunable community mixing
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
, and a degree distribution influenced by preferential attachment, shaped by an exponent parameter
\gamma_{\text{pl}}
.

The process initializes an empty directed graph with all
N
agents as nodes. It then iteratively adds directed edges until a target number of edges,
M\approx\rho_{e}N(N-1)
, is reached. The formation of each potential edge
(A_{i},A_{j})
involves several considerations.

First, the model incorporates
Community Structure and Mixing
. Agents are implicitly grouped into communities based on their initial opinion sign (e.g.,
o_{i}>0
vs.
o_{i}\leq 0
). The parameter
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}\in[0,1]
controls the probability of forming cross-community ties. When selecting a potential followee
A_{j}
for a follower
A_{i}
, if
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
belong to different opinion communities, the connection is considered only with probability
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
. A value of
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0
yields completely segregated communities in the initial graph, while
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=1
makes the connection choice blind to community membership. Intermediate values allow for varying degrees of homophilous structure.

Second, connection formation is driven by
Preferential Attachment
. The likelihood of an agent
A_{j}
being chosen as a followee is proportional to their current ”attractiveness” or popularity, typically related to their in-degree (number of followers) accumulated during the generation process. Let
\alpha_{j}(k)
represent the attractiveness of agent
A_{j}
after
k
edges have been added (initialized with a small random value and incremented each time
A_{j}
gains a follower). The probability of agent
A_{i}
choosing to follow an
allowed
(by community mixing rules) agent
A_{j}
is proportional to
\alpha_{j}(k)
raised to a power related to the preferential attachment exponent
\gamma_{\text{pl}}
:

\displaystyle P(A_{i}\text{ follows }A_{j}|A_{j}\text{ allowed})\propto\alpha_{j}(k)^{1/\gamma_{\text{pl}}}

(25)

The parameter
\gamma_{\text{pl}}
(typically between 1 and 3) controls the strength of the preferential attachment. Lower values lead to a stronger ”rich-get-richer” effect, resulting in more pronounced hubs and a heavier tail in the degree distribution, characteristic of scale-free networks.

In summary, the edge generation process iteratively selects a potential follower
A_{i}
, identifies potential followees
A_{j}
, filters these candidates based on the community mixing rule (probability
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
for cross-community ties), and then selects the final followee from the allowed candidates based on the preferential attachment mechanism (
Eq.

25
). Following edge addition
(A_{i},A_{j})
, the attractiveness
\alpha_{j}
is increased. A final step ensures network connectivity by adding minimal outgoing edges for any potentially isolated nodes, guided by homophily and attractiveness.

This initialization procedure can be understood as generating a network structure that blends principles from
Stochastic Block Models (SBMs)
and
Preferential Attachment Models
. Like SBMs, it explicitly models community structures and controls the density of connections within and between these communities (via
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
). However, unlike standard SBMs which often assume uniform connection probabilities within blocks, our approach incorporates a preferential attachment mechanism (via
\alpha_{j}
and
\gamma_{\text{pl}}
). This hybrid approach aims to generate initial networks that exhibit both plausible community structures based on agent attributes (opinion homophily) and the heterogeneous, scale-free-like degree distributions commonly observed in real-world social networks. The resulting graph
G(0)
provides the initial substrate for information diffusion and subsequent network evolution.

8.4.2
Dynamic Connection Formation and Dissolution

Beyond the initial structure, the social network
G(t)
evolves over time as agents dynamically adjust their connections, reflecting processes like discovering new users and ceasing to follow others. This dynamic connection process is a core component of Contribution D, enabling the study of the feedback loop between opinion formation and network structure.

In each time step, agents have the opportunity to revise their outgoing connections (i.e., who they follow) based on the other users they encounter, primarily through the messages recommended to them (
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
). For an agent
A_{i}
, the set of potential new users to follow consists of the authors
A_{j}
of these recommended messages, provided
A_{i}
is not already following
A_{j}
and
A_{j}\neq A_{i}
.

The decision for agent
A_{i}
to initiate a follow relationship with a potential candidate
A_{j}
is probabilistic. The probability,
P_{\text{follow}}(A_{i},A_{j})
, is determined using a mechanism structurally similar to the general interaction probability model described in
Section

8.3.1
(
Eq.

24
). However, instead of evaluating alignment based on the
message’s
perceived opinion
\pi(A_{i},m)
, the calculation relies directly on the difference between the agent’s own opinion
o_{i}
and the
potential followee’s
(author’s) opinion
o_{j}
.

A key aspect of the implemented dynamic is the constraint imposed on network structure changes. To maintain stability or simulate user attention limits, the model may enforce that an agent’s out-degree (number of users they follow) remains constant or changes slowly. In the current mechanism, for each
new
follow connection
(A_{i},A_{j})
that is successfully established based on the probability above, agent
A_{i}
simultaneously
removes
one randomly selected existing outgoing connection
(A_{i},A_{k})
, where
A_{k}
was previously followed by
A_{i}
. This ”rewiring” approach ensures that the total number of outgoing links for agent
A_{i}
remains constant throughout the update step, effectively modeling a process where new follows replace older ones.

The outcome of this process across all agents is an updated set of edges
E(t+1)
, reflecting both the formation of new social ties influenced by opinion similarity and the dissolution of previous ones. This evolving network structure directly impacts subsequent information propagation and agent interactions in the following time steps.

8.4.3
Recommendation-Driven Information Propagation

The flow of information within the simulated social network is governed by a recommendation system that determines message visibility for each agent
A_{i}
at each time step
t
. This system defines the set of messages
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
that agent
A_{i}
is exposed to, forming the basis for potential interactions and opinion updates. The design aims to balance exposure to content from an agent’s existing social ties with opportunities for discovering content from beyond their immediate neighborhood, while allowing control over the ideological diversity of discovered content.

The recommendation process for agent
A_{i}
begins by identifying the pool of eligible messages
\mathcal{M}_{\text{elig},i}(t)
. This pool consists of all messages currently available in the system, excluding those authored by agent
A_{i}
itself and any messages with which
A_{i}
has previously interacted, thus preventing redundant exposure. Messages within this eligible pool are then conceptually categorized based on their source relative to agent
A_{i}
: messages originating from the agent’s direct network neighborhood (authors
A_{j}
such that
(A_{i},A_{j})\in E(t)
) constitute the
Network Source
, while messages from all other authors constitute the
Discovery Source
.

The composition of the final recommended set
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
, which typically aims for a fixed size
N
, is controlled by a key parameter, the
discovery rate

\delta_{\text{rec}}\in[0,1]
. This parameter specifies the target proportion of recommendations intended to originate from the Discovery Source. Consequently, the target number of recommendations from the Discovery Source is
N_{\text{disc}}=N\cdot\delta_{\text{rec}}
, and the target from the Network Source is
N_{\text{net}}=N\cdot(1-\delta_{\text{rec}})
.

Furthermore, the ideological diversity of content drawn from the Discovery Source is regulated by the
discovery community mixing
parameter
\mu_{c,\text{disc}}\in[0,1]
. This parameter controls the balance between recommending discovery messages from authors sharing the same opinion sign as agent
A_{i}
versus authors with the opposite opinion sign. Let
f_{\text{same}}(\mu_{c,\text{disc}})
be the target fraction of discovery messages to be drawn from same-opinion authors (a function potentially decreasing from 1 to 0.5 as
\mu_{c,\text{disc}}
goes from 0 to 1, depending on the desired interpretation). The number of targeted same-opinion discovery messages is then
N_{\text{disc, same}}=N_{\text{disc}}\cdot f_{\text{same}}(\mu_{c,\text{disc}})
, and the number of targeted different-opinion discovery messages is
N_{\text{disc, diff}}=N_{\text{disc}}-N_{\text{disc, same}}
. A value of
\mu_{c,\text{disc}}=0
(leading to
f_{\text{same}}=1
) creates an echo chamber within discovery, while higher values increase cross-ideological exposure.

The selection process involves randomly sampling the required number of messages from each category (Network, Discovery-Same-Opinion, Discovery-Different-Opinion) up to the calculated target for that category (
N_{\text{net}}
,
N_{\text{disc, same}}
,
N_{\text{disc, diff}}
) or the number of available eligible messages in that category, whichever is smaller. Configuration settings can determine whether the system strictly adheres to these targets, possibly providing fewer than
N
recommendations if a source is exhausted (
strict weights
), or whether it attempts to fill remaining slots using messages from other available sources (
flexible weights
).

A significant feature is the potential for
adaptive mixing
. Instead of using a fixed
\mu_{c,\text{disc}}
, this parameter can be dynamically computed each time step based on the current global network structure
G(t)
. This involves calculating the observed ratio of inter-community edges (
E_{\text{inter}}
) to intra-community edges (
E_{\text{intra}}
) in the graph,
\rho_{\text{obs}}=E_{\text{inter}}/E_{\text{intra}}
, and comparing it to the ratio expected in a random graph with equivalent community sizes,
\rho_{\text{exp}}
. The adaptive mixing parameter can then be set as a function of this comparison, for example, proportional to the normalized ratio
\min(1,\rho_{\text{obs}}/\rho_{\text{exp}})
, linking the diversity of discovered information to the emergent structural integration or segregation of the network.

Finally, the selected messages from all sources are combined and shuffled to form the final recommendation set
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
presented to agent
A_{i}
. This recommendation mechanism provides a flexible means to explore how different balances of network-based filtering versus controlled discovery influence opinion dynamics and polarization.

8.5
Simulation Workflow

The simulation progresses through two main phases: initialization and iterative execution over discrete time steps. This workflow orchestrates the interactions between the different model components described previously, as detailed in
Algorithm

1
.

The
Initialization Phase
sets up the simulation environment. This involves creating the population
V
of
N
agents, assigning each agent
A_{i}
its initial state including opinion
o_{i}(0)
drawn from a selected distribution, and generating its unique persona
P_{i}
using the LLM (
Section

8.1.2
). Concurrently, the initial social network graph
G(0)
is constructed based on specified parameters governing density, community mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
), and preferential attachment (
\gamma_{\text{pl}}

The
Iterative Simulation Phase
proceeds through a configured number of discrete time steps,
t=1,2,...
. Each time step represents a period of activity on the simulated social network. Within each step, first, agents engage in
Content Generation
. Each agent
A_{i}
stochastically decides whether to create a new message based on its posting probability
p_{\text{post},i}

8.1.3

P_{i}

) and ideological context. These new messages augment the system’s content pool. Following evaluation, the
Information Recommendation
system generates a personalized set of messages
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rec},i}(t)
for each agent
A_{i}

\delta_{\text{rec}}
and
\mu_{c,\text{disc}}

Section

8.4.3
). Next, agents undertake
Interaction

A_{i}

P_{\text{react}}(A_{i},m,\text{type})
(
Eq.

24

Section

8.3.2
), and all interactions are recorded. After interactions,
Opinion Updates
are calculated. The total opinion shift
\Delta O_{i}
for each agent is determined by aggregating individual shifts
\Delta o_{i}(m)
(
Eq.

16

\lambda

o_{i}(t+1)
(
Eq.

17
). Parallel to or following opinion updates, the
Network Update

Section

8.4.2
), resulting in the updated graph
G(t+1)
. Finally, relevant simulation
Metrics
are calculated and recorded for the completed time step.

This sequence creates interconnected feedback loops. Opinions influence communication (message generation, reactions) and network choices (following). Communication influences opinions (updates) and network choices. Network structure shapes information exposure (recommendations), which in turn influences opinions and future network changes. Executing this workflow over multiple time steps allows for the study of emergent phenomena like polarization, echo chamber formation, and the co-evolution of opinions and network structure.

Detailed Computational Experiments

This appendix provides detailed descriptions of the computational experiments conducted to assess the simulation framework’s capabilities, explore the mechanisms driving opinion and network dynamics related to social polarization, and provide empirical support for the analyses presented in the main text. The general methodology involved systematic variation of key model parameters while observing the resultant changes in emergent phenomena such as agent opinion distributions, social network structure, and/or agent interaction patterns over time.

To isolate core mechanisms governing opinion shifts (Contribution B) and network evolution (Contribution D), many of these experiments employed a simplified agent logic. In these simulations, advanced functionalities leveraging LLMs for nuanced agent behavior (Contributions A and C aspects like persona-driven communication, complex message evaluation, and reactive content generation) were typically deactivated. Unless stated otherwise, agents in these runs utilized streamlined logic where message content followed templates, perceived message opinion (
o_{m}
) equaled the author’s opinion (
o_{j}
), and explicit reactive interactions (i.e., likes, reposts, and comments) were inactive. Experiments focusing specifically on reaction dynamics (B.7-B.9) activated the relevant interaction models (Contribution C) as described in their respective setups.

The experiments detailed herein cover a range of investigations designed to build understanding from fundamental mechanisms to more complex, coupled dynamics. These include: the fundamental behavior of the opinion dynamics model under varying base attraction width (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
) and initial opinion fragmentation (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}
) (Experiment B.1); base attraction width (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
) and the influence of initial community mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
) (Experiment B.2); the impact of extremist influencers (
N_{\text{inf}}
) and initial community mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
) (Experiment B.3); the isolated dynamics of network evolution driven by agent opinions when opinions themselves are fixed (Experiment B.4); the co-evolutionary feedback between opinions and network topology, considering the roles of influencers (
N_{\text{inf}}
), initial mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
), and algorithmic discovery rates (
\delta_{\text{rec}}
) (Experiments B.5, B.6); and the patterns of agent reaction dynamics under various structural, algorithmic, and psychological parameters, with both fixed and evolving opinions (Experiments B.7, B.8, B.9).

Each experiment description below follows a consistent structure: a brief introduction outlining its specific objective, a description of the experimental setup detailing the varied parameters and constant settings, and a presentation and interpretation of the results, often including visualizations like heatmaps, line graphs, or kernel density estimates. Parameter configurations generally adhere to a common baseline (defined in Table

), with specific parameter variations detailed within each experiment description.

9.1
Experiment 1: Initial Opinion Landscape and Base Attraction Width

This first experiment investigates the fundamental interplay between the initial configuration of opinions within the population (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}
) and the core parameter of the opinion update mechanism, the base attraction width (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
). The primary objective is to understand how initial opinion diversity influences the system’s trajectory towards consensus or polarization, depending on the baseline range of opinion differences agents tolerate before shifting from assimilation (attraction) to repulsion (backfire). This establishes the fundamental behavior of the opinion dynamics model (Contribution B) under varying initial conditions and assimilation/repulsion thresholds, without network evolution or specific structural features.

9.1.1
Experimental Setup

We systematically varied two key parameters:

1.

Initial Opinion Range Limit (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}
):
Agent opinions initialized from
U[-o_{\text{max}}^{(0)},o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}]
, varied over
[0.1,...,1.0]
. Higher values signify greater initial diversity.

2.

Base Attraction Width (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
):
This parameter (
8.2.1
) sets the baseline threshold for opinion difference (
|d|
) separating assimilation from repulsion. Agents assimilate opinions where
|d|^{2}<\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
(and
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}
is primarily determined by
\sigma_{\text{base}}^{2}
when conviction/concordance effects are minimal). We varied
\sigma_{\text{base}}
over
[0.0,0.2,...,2.0]
. A
larger

\sigma_{\text{base}}
defines a wider range for assimilation, meaning agents are attracted towards more dissimilar opinions before repulsion occurs. A
smaller

\sigma_{\text{base}}
defines a narrower assimilation range, triggering repulsion for smaller opinion differences.
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0
implies repulsion for any non-zero difference.

All other parameters followed the baseline configuration (Table
3
), notably a static network (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0.5
, network evolution off) and no influencers (
N_{\text{inf}}=0
). Five simulation runs were performed over
T=100
steps for each (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}
,
\sigma_{\text{base}}
) combination. The final mean opinion polarization (Esteban-Ray index) was the primary outcome measure.

9.2
Experiment 2: Structural Segregation and Base Attraction Width

This second experiment explores the interplay between the initial structural segregation of the social network and the sensitivity of the opinion update mechanism. Specifically, we investigate how the level of initial community mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
), which determines the prevalence of cross-group ties, interacts with the base attraction width (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
) to shape the final polarization outcome. The analysis aims to understand whether structural features like echo chambers (low
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
) invariably lead to higher polarization, or if this relationship is modulated by the agents’ propensity for assimilation versus repulsion (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
), keeping opinions dynamic but the network static.

9.2.1
Experimental Setup

We systematically varied two parameters:

1.

Initial Community Mixing (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}

Governing the probability of forming cross-community ties during initial network generation (
8.4.1
),
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}
was varied across the full range
[0.0,0.1,...,1.0]
.
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0.0
represents completely segregated communities based on initial opinion sign, while
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=1.0
represents a network structure generated without regard to opinion (fully mixed).

2.

Base Attraction Width (
\sigma_{\text{base}}
):
As in Experiment 1, this parameter was varied over the range
[0.0,0.2,...,2.0]
.

All other parameters were held constant according to the baseline configuration (Table
3
). Crucially, the initial opinion range limit was set to a rather high initial diversity (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}=0.7
) to ensure sufficient initial diversity for polarization dynamics to potentially unfold, based on the findings of Experiment 1. The network remained static throughout the simulation (
T=100
). The final mean polarization index (Esteban-Ray) averaged over five runs was the primary outcome measure.

to determine the global outcome: pure repulsion drives bipolarization, wide attraction drives consensus, and intermediate attraction results in a trimodal state under these specific diversity initial conditions.

9.2.3
Synthesis: Understanding Parameter Interactions Between Tolerance, Diversity, and Network Structure

The apparent discrepancies between Experiments B.1 and B.2 reveal crucial insights about how tolerance, initial diversity, and network structure interact to produce different polarization patterns. Understanding these interactions is essential for interpreting the main text results.

Why moderate tolerance produces different outcomes

In Experiment B.1, moderate tolerance (
\sigma_{\text{base}}\approx 1.0
) combined with
high initial diversity
(
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}\geq 0.9
) produced the highest polarization levels through clear bipolarization. This occurs because sufficient extremist voices exist at the outset to drive dual attraction-repulsion dynamics: moderate agents are simultaneously attracted to their own side’s extremists while being repelled by the opposing extreme, rapidly emptying the political center.

However, in Experiment B.2 with
moderate initial diversity
(
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}=0.7
) and full integration, the same tolerance parameters produce trimodal distributions. The key difference is the initial extremist presence: without strong pre-existing extreme factions (as in the
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}=0.7
case), there is insufficient ”pull” to draw all moderate agents fully toward the poles. Instead, a persistent moderate center coexists with emerging extremes, creating the trimodal pattern.

Why pure repulsion produces different outcomes

Pure repulsion (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0
) shows even more dramatic sensitivity to network structure:

In Experiment B.1 with
moderate network integration
(
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0.5
), pure repulsion produces trimodal distributions. Agents initially positioned near neutrality are exposed primarily to extremists from their own side (due to limited cross-cutting ties). This causes them to moderate
inward
, away from their own side’s extremists, creating a persistent central peak alongside the two extreme peaks.

In Experiment B.2 with
full integration
(
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=1.0
), the same pure repulsion produces bipolarization. Here, near-neutral agents are exposed
equally
to extremists from both sides. This symmetric exposure, combined with their slight initial lean toward one side, creates asymmetric repulsion: agents move further from the opposing camp than from their own faction. This neutralizes the inward moderation effect seen under limited integration, allowing cleaner bipolarization.

Under
complete segregation
(
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}=0.0
) in Experiment B.2, pure repulsion again produces trimodal distributions, but through a different mechanism: agents can only interact with their own community, so moderate agents are pushed toward the center by same-side extremists without any cross-cutting influence to counteract this effect.

Critical parameters for bipolarization

These experiments reveal that strong bipolarization requires specific parameter combinations:

•

High initial diversity + moderate tolerance
: Works robustly across network structures (Experiment B.1)

•

Moderate diversity + pure repulsion + full integration
: Creates bipolarization through asymmetric repulsion (Experiment B.2)

•

Moderate diversity + moderate tolerance
: Produces trimodal distributions regardless of network structure due to insufficient extremist pull

The network structure thus acts as a critical mediator: it determines whether agents experience symmetric or asymmetric exposure to opposing views, which in turn determines whether psychological mechanisms (tolerance/repulsion) produce bipolar or trimodal outcomes.

In summary, this experiment demonstrates complex interactions between network structure and the assimilation/repulsion mechanism. While complete segregation stabilizes moderate polarization irrespective of
\sigma_{\text{base}}
(except for
\sigma_{\text{base}}=2
), interaction across groups (
\mu_{\text{mix}}^{(0)}>0
) allows
\sigma_{\text{base}}
to shape the outcome. Pure repulsion (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0
) or intermediate attraction (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=1
) can both lead to high polarization indices when mixing occurs, but via different underlying distributions (bipolar vs. trimodal under full mixing). Wide attraction (
\sigma_{\text{base}}\geq 1.4
) consistently promotes consensus.

9.4
Parameter Realism and Model Applicability

While the experiments systematically explore parameter extremes to establish model boundaries, understanding real-world applicability requires mapping these parameters to observable social phenomena and assessing their empirical plausibility.

Psychological tolerance for disagreement
(
\sigma_{\text{base}}
) varies substantially across contexts and individuals. Pure repulsion (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0
) may characterize highly dogmatic individuals or discussions of deeply held moral convictions where any deviation triggers rejection. Moderate tolerance (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=1.0
) likely reflects typical political discourse where individuals exhibit some openness to similar viewpoints while resisting strongly opposing positions. Full assimilation (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=2.0
) represents idealized ”rational discourse” where individuals remain genuinely open to persuasion from any position—potentially characterizing academic debates, deliberative forums, or discussions of technical issues where personal identity is less threatened. Most real-world scenarios likely fall in the moderate range (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0.5
–
1.5
), as complete openness or complete closure are psychologically and socially unsustainable.

Initial opinion diversity
(
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}
) reflects how individuals enter discussions. High initial diversity (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}\geq 0.9
) may characterize highly polarized topics where strong factions already exist—such as abortion rights, gun control, or partisan political identities where prior socialization has created distinct camps. Moderate diversity (
o_{\text{max}}^{(0)}=0.7
) may reflect emerging issues or topics where extreme positions exist but have not yet captured large populations—such as new technology policies, climate change solutions, or evolving social norms where most individuals hold intermediate positions. Low diversity scenarios are less theoretically interesting but may reflect topics with strong social consensus or limited prior exposure.

Network integration
(
\mu_{\text{mix}}
) depends on structural and algorithmic factors. Complete segregation (
\mu_{\text{mix}}=0.0
) approximates echo chambers in highly curated social media environments or ideologically homogeneous communities. Full integration (
\mu_{\text{mix}}=1.0
) may occur in diverse public forums, traditional mass media exposure, or algorithmic systems designed to maximize cross-cutting contact. Most realistic scenarios involve moderate integration (
\mu_{\text{mix}}=0.3
–
0.7
), reflecting the permeable clustering typical of social networks where homophily creates tendencies toward like-minded connections while maintaining some cross-cutting ties through shared institutions, geographic proximity, or algorithmic recommendation.

Extremist influencer presence
reflects the number of highly committed actors attempting to shift moderate populations. Low influencer presence (0-2 per side) may characterize grassroots movements or emerging controversies where few dedicated activists exist. Moderate presence (3-6 per side) could reflect established political campaigns, advocacy organizations, or social movements with some but limited organizational capacity. High presence (7+ per side) might characterize highly mobilized contexts such as major political campaigns, culture wars, or topics where multiple well-funded organizations compete for influence. The ”Goldilocks zone” effect observed in our results suggests that moderate influencer presence may be most effective for polarization, as insufficient numbers fail to create momentum while excessive numbers may trigger counter-mobilization or audience fatigue.

The model’s predictive power emerges from identifying which parameter combinations characterize specific real-world contexts. Topics involving moral foundations or partisan identities may combine moderate-to-low tolerance with high initial diversity and low-to-moderate integration, predicting persistent polarization. Technical or policy debates in diverse forums may combine higher tolerance with moderate diversity and integration, suggesting more complex, potentially trimodal outcomes. The framework thus provides a systematic approach to anticipating polarization dynamics by mapping observable social and psychological characteristics onto specific parameter regimes.

These findings demonstrate that tolerance for disagreement becomes the decisive psychological factor once sufficient inter-group contact exists, but its effects are fundamentally mediated by both initial conditions and network structure. No single parameter determines polarization outcomes—rather, specific combinations create distinct regimes through different underlying mechanisms. This suggests that structural measures alone may be insufficient predictors of polarization without considering the psychological dispositions that govern how individuals process cross-cutting exposure and the initial distribution of extreme voices within the population.

11
Detailed User Study Methodology and Findings

To supplement the discussion in
Section

5
, this appendix serves as a detailed account of the methodology and results from the user study, which was conducted to assess the framework’s empirical utility. The study had human participants interact with a simulated social media environment where conditions of polarization and recommendation bias were experimentally manipulated. The following sections cover the complete details of the experimental design, the implementation of the prototype platform and its recommendation algorithm, the agent pre-simulation, participant recruitment and characteristics, the validation of measurement scales, the full experimental procedure, and in-depth statistical analyses, all of which provide the necessary background for the key results highlighted in the main text.

11.1
Experimental Design

A
2\times 3
between-subjects factorial design was utilized to investigate the dynamics of opinion formation and the perception of polarization within online discussions. In this design, two key dimensions were manipulated: the
Polarization Degree
within the artificial agent population and a systematic
Recommendation Bias
. Universal Basic Income (UBI) was held as the constant discussion topic throughout the experiment.

The first manipulated dimension placed highly polarized discussions in contrast with moderate ones by altering the behavior of the artificial agents. For the polarized condition, agents were set to express extreme viewpoints and use confrontational discourse, characterized by emotional language, forceful assertions, and minimal acknowledgment of opposing perspectives. In contrast, the moderate condition was defined by more nuanced discussions and cooperative interaction styles, with agents programmed to express uncertainty, recognize limitations in their knowledge, and engage with opposing views constructively.

The second dimension involved the introduction of a systematic bias into the platform’s recommendation system. This was implemented across three distinct levels: a ’neutral’ setting (featuring 50% pro-UBI and 50% contra-UBI content), a ’pro-bias’ setting (70% pro-UBI, 30% contra-UBI), and a ’contra-bias’ setting (30% pro-UBI, 70% contra-UBI). This manipulation was designed to allow for an investigation into how algorithmic content curation can influence both the formation of opinions and the perception of a debate’s polarization.

The decision to select UBI as the focal topic was guided by several strategic factors. It represents an emerging policy proposal where public opinion is still relatively malleable, unlike heavily polarized topics where individuals’ positions are often entrenched, making it an ideal context for studying opinion formation. Although it may evoke fewer preconceived notions, UBI is a sufficiently concrete and consequential subject to fuel meaningful discourse, with a complexity that touches upon economic, social, and technological domains. Support for its suitability is found in recent polling data, which indicates a balanced distribution of opinions among Europeans (approx. 51.2% support,
Vlandas (2019)
) and Americans (approx. 48% support,
Hamilton et al. (2022)
). Furthermore, because of UBI’s limited real-world implementation, participants’ opinions are more likely to be formed based on the arguments presented in the experiment rather than on direct experience or pre-existing partisan loyalties. This allows for a clearer examination of how social media dynamics can shape opinion before entrenched polarization develops.

11.2
System Prototype

The implementation of the prototype is a web application that simulates a social media platform, stylistically reminiscent of X (formerly Twitter), for the purpose of studying social polarization dynamics. The user interface, shown in Figure
15
, was designed with a familiar layout to promote intuitive user engagement and interaction.

11.2.1
User Interface

The application’s main interface is structured into three primary components: a navigation sidebar on the left, a central Newsfeed, and a recommendation panel on the right. Essential functionalities such as the user’s profile, a general user overview, and a logout option are accessible via the navigation sidebar. The primary space for interaction is the central Newsfeed, where users can view and engage with content posted by others. At the top of this feed, a text input area prompts users to share their own thoughts, mimicking the spontaneous nature of social media communication.

Displayed within the Newsfeed is a series of posts, each one accompanied by standard social media elements like user avatars, usernames, timestamps, and interaction metrics (
likes
,
comments
, and
reposts
). The purpose of this design is to encourage user engagement by providing visual cues related to the popularity and impact of each post. On the right side of the interface, the recommendation panel suggests other users to follow, which can potentially influence the expansion of a user’s network and their exposure to diverse viewpoints.

Dynamically generated user profiles display each user’s posts, their follower relationships, and other relevant metadata such as their handle and biography (for details on the agent model, see
Section

8.1
and
Section

8.4
). The system also permits users to follow and unfollow the artificial agents.

11.3
Newsfeed Recommendations

An adaptive recommendation system for content presentation that evolves with user activity is implemented in the web application. This system makes use of two distinct algorithmic approaches: a ’default’ variant for new users and a ’collaborative’ variant that is activated once a user has established an interaction history.

The default variant utilizes a popularity-based scoring mechanism that takes into account multiple forms of engagement to establish content visibility. For any given message
m
, a composite popularity score is calculated by the system using the following formula:

\displaystyle S_{p}(m)=l_{m}+2c_{m}+2r_{m}

(26)

where
l_{m},c_{m},\text{ and }r_{m}
correspond to the number of
likes
,
comments
, and
reposts
for that message, respectively. The coefficients are weighted to reflect the higher importance assigned to more active forms of engagement.

After a user begins to interact with the platform, the system shifts to a collaborative variant that incorporates not only popularity metrics but also ideological proximity and a stochastic element to ensure recommendation diversity. This enhanced scoring function combines these elements into a composite score:

\displaystyle S_{c}(m)=\omega_{p}\cdot\frac{S_{p}(m)}{S_{max}}+\omega_{i}\cdot\frac{2-|o_{u}-o_{a}|}{2}+\omega_{r}\cdot\epsilon

(27)

In this equation,
S_{p}(m)
is the popularity score normalized by the maximum observed score
S_{max}
;
o_{u}
and
o_{a}
are the opinion scores of the active user and the message author;
\epsilon
is a uniform random variable in
[0,1]
; and
\omega_{p},\omega_{i},\text{ and }\omega_{r}
are weighting parameters that sum to one. For the current implementation, these weights are configured to
\omega_{p}=0.6,\omega_{i}=0.2,\text{ and }\omega_{r}=0.2
, which balances the influence of popularity, ideological similarity, and randomization.

To maintain temporal relevance, both variants present a user’s most recent contributions at the top of their feed upon page load. This design ensures users remain aware of their own contributions while still experiencing the broader content landscape delivered by the scoring-based recommendation system.

This dual-variant system design allows the platform to provide meaningful content recommendations even without initial user interaction data, while also enabling a smooth transition to more personalized recommendations as users become active. By incorporating popularity metrics, mild ideological factors, and controlled randomization, the system generates diverse recommendations that create the impression of a dynamic network environment, all while operating within the conditional
Recommendation Bias
regime. The inclusion of a stochastic element is particularly helpful in preventing recommendation stagnation and ensuring dynamic content delivery.

The initial state of the platform, including a history of posts, comments, reposts, likes, and follow relationships, was generated by first running agent-only simulations for 10 iterations with agent opinions held fixed.

2.16
posts,
8.07
likes,
5.47
comments, and
2.90
reposts each. In contrast, the moderate condition exhibited similar posting patterns (
2.07
posts per agent) but reduced commenting (
1.60
) and reposting (
1.57
) activity. Complete statistics can be found in Appendix B, Table
9
.

Participants received instructions to engage with the platform naturally, as they would with their regular social media, based on the following text:

”You will now interact with a social media platform discussing
Universal Basic Income
. Please use the platform as you normally would use social media. You can read posts, like them, comment on them, or create your own posts. Your goal is to form an opinion on the topic. You will have 10 minutes for this task.”

Interactions from this 10-minute phase (including likes, comments, reposts, and follows) were recorded for later analysis. A crucial aspect of the design was that while participants were interacting, the artificial agents did not generate any new content. This was a deliberate choice to guarantee exposure to a controlled initial environment. The participant’s newsfeed, however, remained dynamic, as it was updated by the recommendation algorithm, which presented content from the pre-generated pool based on their individual interaction behavior.

Post-interaction Phase

In the final phase, after the interaction period concluded, participants were asked to complete a post-test questionnaire. This questionnaire included measures of their perception of the key constructs listed below, as well as an evaluation of the platform’s realism and effectiveness.

11.5
Measures

All key constructs were quantified using four-item scales rated on 5-point Likert-type scales, ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). The full item list and corresponding factor loadings can be found in this appendix, Table
8
. The key constructs measured in the study are enumerated below:

•

Opinion Change
: This captured the shift in a participant’s opinion on UBI between the pre- and post-interaction phases. It was operationalized as the difference between the average scores of the opinion items before and after the interaction.

•

Perceived Polarization
: This construct assessed how participants perceived the extremity and ideological division of the discourse, focusing on the perceived distance between opposing viewpoints.

•

Perceived Group Salience
: This evaluated the degree to which participants saw the discussion as being driven by group identities as opposed to individual perspectives. It should be noted that, following psychometric analysis, this scale was refined to retain only two of its four initial items (details in Appendix
11
).

•

Perceived Emotionality
: This measure captured participants’ judgment of the discussion’s emotional intensity and affective tone, assessing the perceived level of emotional versus rational communication.

•

Perceived Uncertainty
: This was designed to capture the extent to which participants noticed expressions of doubt or acknowledgments of knowledge limitations from the agents.

•

Perceived Bias
: This measured participants’ evaluation of the discussion’s viewpoint balance and fairness of representation.

11.7
Preliminary Analysis

An evaluation of the platform’s ecological validity was conducted. Participants provided ratings on several aspects using 7-point scales (1 = Not at all, 7 = Extremely). M=3.12,SD=0.90

M=2.99,SD=0.99

Additionally, an examination of the psychometric properties of our key measures was performed (see Table

The two remaining items exhibited modest to acceptable loadings (
.585

11.10
Extended Discussion: Aligning Human-Subject Data with Computational Models

This discussion provides a detailed interpretation of the validation study’s findings, placing them in direct conversation with the computational experiments presented in the main text. By synthesizing human-subject data with agent-based modeling results, we can more deeply inform the framework’s ecological validity, identify areas of alignment and divergence, and chart paths for future research.

A central achievement of the validation study is its confirmation of the framework’s ability to generate psychologically meaningful discourse. Participants proved highly attuned to the manipulated
Polarization Degree
, perceiving the polarized environment as more emotional, more biased, and higher in group salience (Hedges’
g=1.53
for emotionality). This provides crucial empirical validation for our LLM-based content analysis (Section
), which demonstrated the model’s capacity to generate text with these specific communicative features. Critically, the strong inverse relationship observed between polarization and perceived uncertainty (
g=-1.26
) resonates with theoretical work on epistemic humility in discourse
(
Holtz and Miller, 2001
;
Holtz and Nihiser, 2008
;
Winter and Feixas, 2019
)
and confirms that humans are sensitive to the very same textual cues that inform the belief-update mechanisms of our formal opinion dynamics model.

The SEM analysis of human data reveals a more nuanced perceptual pathway than our computational models assume. It suggests that the effect of emotionality on perceived polarization is primarily mediated by group salience (
\beta=.417
), a finding consistent with theories of affective polarization
(
Iyengar et al., 2012
)
. This implies that for humans, emotional content may function as a key trigger for activating group-based antagonisms, echoing political theories that frame conflict in terms of friend-enemy distinctions
(
Schmitt, 2008
;
Laclau and Mouffe, 2014
)
. This insight complements our computational findings, which identified structural factors like network mixing as primary drivers of polarization. The user study thus suggests an interplay where structural conditions may determine the potential for division, while affective communication acts as the proximate trigger for its perception.

Regarding opinion formation, the study highlights a divergence between the formal model and human behavior. While the experimental conditions directly influenced the
magnitude
of participants’ opinion change, the SEM found this effect was not mediated by any of the measured perceptual variables. This contrasts with our agent update rule (Eq.
18
), which models opinion shifts as a direct function of perceived content. This divergence strongly suggests that human opinion adjustment in these contexts may involve non-deliberative processing routes
(
Petty and Cacioppo, 1986
;
Chaiken, 2014
)
or a direct response to the ”environmental press”
(
Lewin, 2013
)
, pathways that our current formal model does not capture. This finding is not a limitation but a key insight, providing a clear directive for future work to enhance the psychological realism of agent-based opinion dynamics models.

Turning to user engagement, participants’ preference for low-effort interactions like
likes
over
comments
mirrors typical social media behavior. Interestingly, the finding that participants commented more in moderate environments
(
Koudenburg and Kashima, 2022
;
Yousafzai, 2022
)
aligns with our extended agent reaction simulations (
Section

9
), where agents in consensual environments also exhibited more variable, less homophilic engagement patterns. The cautious interpretation of this finding due to potential participant-environment mismatch
(
Simchon et al., 2022
)
remains valid, but the alignment with agent behavior suggests the framework is capturing a genuine dynamic. The strong effect of contra-bias recommendations on engagement, especially in polarized conditions, further underscores the power of exposing users to challenging arguments, a finding that connects to literature on loss aversion and status quo bias
(
Kahneman et al., 1991
)
.

In conclusion, the validation study confirms that our hybrid framework can produce controlled, manipulable environments that elicit complex and psychologically meaningful responses from human users. The strong alignment on perceptual dimensions validates the core of our content generation model. The divergences observed in behavioral and opinion-update pathways do not invalidate the framework; rather, they highlight its utility as a tool for discovery, pointing toward specific, data-driven avenues for refining the next generation of opinion dynamics models. The study thus confirms the value of an integrated approach that places computational modeling and human-subject experimentation in a continuous, productive dialogue.
