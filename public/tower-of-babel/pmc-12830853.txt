Glioblastoma zebrafish Avatars guide therapeutic decisions in a patient with gliosarcoma: a case report
Authors: Márcia Fontes, Daniela Garcez, Joana Ruivo, Catarina Albuquerque, Catarina Viegas, Miguel da Cruz-Ribeiro, Jorge Lima, Manuela Mafra, José Bravo Marques, Rita Fior
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma is the most common and lethal brain tumor. Therapeutic options after recurrence are scarce and with no agreement as to which treatment to offer. Also, no functional tests are currently in use to guide clinical decisions. As a result, patients often undergo multiple treatments, being exposed to toxicities and wasting valuable time.MethodsHere we report a case of a 37-year-old man with a left temporal glioblastoma (gliosarcoma subtype), who underwent several surgeries and treatments. Tumor DNA sequencing revealed no actionable mutations. At the second recurrence, brain MRI showed three new left-hemisphere nodular lesions, leading to a third surgery. However, twenty-days post-surgery the patient relapsed. Therapeutic options available for this patient were no longer well defined and 13 possible off-label options emerged. Fresh tumor samples were used to generate patient-derived zebrafish xenografts (zAvatars) to test the 13 options in combination with radiotherapy.ResultsPemetrexed, and Pemetrexed with Doxorubicin together with Radiotherapy, were both effective in inducing apoptosis. Due to toxicity concerns, the physician opted for the combination of radiotherapy and pemetrexed, with doxorubicin introduced later. The patient recovered and was stable for two months, consistent with the zAvatar prediction.ConclusionsThe rapid establishment of zAvatars from glioblastoma tumor samples allows for the testing of drug combinations and the tailoring of treatment in a personalized and timely manner. A future pipeline of tumor sequencing together with zAvatars testing, since the first surgery, may help manage patients more effectively, potentially improving their Progression free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).Subject terms: Cancer, CNS cancer

Introduction

Glioblastoma Multiforme (GBM) is an extremely aggressive and the most common form of primary brain cancer, accounting for approximately 45% of all central nervous system (CNS) malignancies1. GBM is characterized by the World Health Organization as a grade 4 glioma and may originate from neural stem cells and glial cells2. GBM is associated with poor prognosis, with an average survival rate of 12–18 months, particularly due to resistance to therapy and incomplete surgical resection3,4. Despite several efforts, GBM management has shown little improvement as the therapeutical options remain very limited3.

The standard approach for GBM tumors starts by maximal surgical resection. Upon surgery, the most common approach consists of a radiotherapy scheme, usually delivering 50–60 Gy in 1.8–2 Gy daily fractions over a period of 6 weeks, concomitant with Temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy, 75 mg/m2 daily and followed by additional 6 cycles of TMZ alone (150–200 mg/m2 for 5 days, every 28 days)4,5. In 2009, FDA approved Bevacizumab (Beva), an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody, as a salvage treatment for GBM patients6. While Bevacizumab as a monotherapy has failed to show results in increasing the overall survival (OS), it has demonstrated to improve quality of life by controlling the symptoms7.

Targeted therapy for high grade gliomas has emerged as a promising treatment option. Genetic biomarkers, such as BRAFV600E, H3K27M mutations or NTRK fusions, and others, have allowed clinicians to guide the prescription of more personalized treatments (MEK inhibitors, dordaviprone, Larotrectinib respectively). However, many glioblastomas lack clinically actionable mutations, and even when present, these tumors often do not respond, probably due to their heterogeneity and compensatory mechanisms of resistance8.

Here, we report a case study of 37-year-old man with a glioblastoma of the rare gliosarcoma subtype. At the second relapse, third surgery, a tumor sample was used to generate zebrafish patient-derived xenografts (zAvatars) to aid clinical decision. We present the histopathological and genomic characterization of this patient as well as the development of the zAvatar model and how it helped the treatment management of this patient.

Materials and methods

Zebrafish care, handling & Ethics

In vivo experiments were performed using zebrafish (Danio rerio), nacre, casper and Tg(Fli1:eGFP), which were handled according to European animal welfare Legislation, Directive 2010/63/EU (European Commission, 2016). This study was performed under a project approved by the Champalimaud IRB-ORBEA and the Portuguese Direcção Geral de Veterinaria (DGAV) DGAV/ORBEA 004166/0421/000/000/2020. Tg(Fli1:eGFP) allows the visualization of blood and lymphatic vessels, through the expression of eGFP linked to fli1 promoter.

Cell culture

U87MG were kindly provided by Marta Pojo (IPO Lisbon). Cell line was authenticated through short tandem repeat profile analysis and tested for mycoplasma. U87MG cell line was cultured and expanded to 80% confluence in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium High Glucose (DMEM, Biowest) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Sigma) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (P/S) 10,000 Units/mL (Hyclone).

U87 Cell labeling prior to injection

Cells were labeled with Deep Red Cell Tracker (1 μL/ml, 1 mM stock, Thermo Fisher Scientific) fluorescent lipophilic dye, diluted in DPBS 1x and protected from light.

Human tissue processing

Surgical resected brain tumor tissue was obtained from the brain surgery at CUF Tejo Hospital within the project 231114/Zevatars&TS, approved by the Champalimaud Foundation Ethics Committee. The patient gave written informed consent, including publication of information making them identifiable.

On the day of the surgery, tissue was received and cryopreserved until injection (90% (v/v) FBS and 10% (v/v) DMSO). For microinjection, samples were thawed and centrifuged in DMEM-F12 (250 x g, 5 min). After centrifugation, tissue was minced on a cold-surface in Mix1 (Table S1), passed through a 70 μm strainer and labeled with Deep Red CellTracker (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1:1000 dilution (stock 1 mM) for 5 min on ice. Staining was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cell suspension was centrifuged twice (250 x g, 5 min). Cell pellet was washed every hour in 1 mL of Mix1 to remove dead cells.

Zebrafish patient-derived xenograft microinjection

Tumor cells were microinjected into the hindbrain of anesthetized 2 days post fertilization (dpf) (Supplementary Fig. 1) zebrafish embryos (anesthetized with 0.6 mM tricaine-MS-222 for the injection procedure) and maintained at 34 °C until the end of the experiment. At 1-day post-injection (dpi), zAvatars were screened regarding the presence or absence of a tumoral mass. zAvatars with severe edema, cell debris or non-injected zebrafish embryos were discarded. At 3dpi or 4dpi, zAvatars and U87MG xenografts, were sacrificed by overdose of anesthesia 15 mM tricaine-MS-222, fixed with 4% (v/v) formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C overnight, and then preserved at −20 °C in 100% (v/v) methanol (VWR Chemicals). Zebrafish xenografts irradiation and drug administration

At 1dpi zAvatars and U87MG xenografts were randomly distributed in the treatment groups: control (E3 medium) or selected chemotherapy, replaced daily until the end of the assay. A single dose of 25 Gy was delivered to zebrafish xenografts, only at 1dpi, before administering drug treatments in the fish water, as described in ref. 9. The maximum tolerated concentration (MTC) of anti-cancer drugs was determined using as a reference the reported maximum patient’s plasma concentration10 and testing different doses in non-injected embryos (Supplementary Fig. 2). The highest dose without toxic effects was chosen for each drug (Table S2). Chemotherapies were replaced daily until the end of the assay.

Zebrafish xenograft antibody treatments

Besides the daily addition to the E3 medium, Bevacizumab (100 ng/mL) antibody was added to the cell suspension prior to injection, i.e. cells were injected with the Bevacizumab antibody and then the antibody was also added in the water of the fish for 2 or 3 consecutive days (see Table S1).

Zebrafish xenograft whole-mount immunofluorescence

Primary antibodies: anti-Activated Caspase3 (rabbit, Cell signaling, 1:100, cat#9661), anti-Human mitochondria (mouse, Merck Millipore, 1:50, cat#MAB1273). Secondary antibodies: Alexa goat anti-rabbit 488 (Molecular probes, 1:400), anti-mouse 488 (Molecular probes, 1:400), and anti-mouse 647 (Molecular probes 1:400) were applied simultaneously with DAPI. zAvatars were mounted with Mowiol. Whole-mount immunofluorescence was performed as previously described11,12.

Imaging and quantification

zAvatars and U87 xenografts tumors were acquired in Zeiss LSM 710 fluorescence confocal microscope or in the ANDOR BC43 spinning disc with 5 μm interval z-stacks with an average of 12 slices per stack. Images were analyzed using Fiji software13, using the Cell Counter plugin. Percentage of activated caspase 3 was quantified manually, counting all cells in every slice of the tumor. In zAvatars, the tumor size was quantified by the sum of tumor areas, determined by drawing the tumor area (ROI-free hand tool) in every-other-slice of the tumor. In U87 xenografts the tumor size was determined by counting the total number tumor cells using the DAPI staining, where tumor size = AVG (3 slices Zfirst, Zmidle, Zlast) x total n° slices/1.5 (as described in refs. 11,12).

Genomic profiling

The tumor was sequenced using FoundationOne CDX test, using DNA extracted from FFPE tumor samples. 50–1000 ng of DNA underwent whole-genome shotgun library construction and hybridization-based capture of all coding exons from 309 cancer-related genes, one promoter region, one non-coding (ncRNA), and select intronic regions from 34 commonly rearranged genes, 21 of which also include the coding exons. The assay uses an Illumina® HiSeq platform, with hybrid capture–selected libraries sequenced to high uniform depth (targeting >500X median coverage with >99% of exons at coverage >100X). Sequence data was processed using a customized analysis pipeline designed to detect all classes of genomic alterations, including base substitutions, indels, focal copy number amplifications, homozygous gene deletions, selected genomic rearrangements, as well as genomic signatures including loss of heterozygosity (LOH), microsatellite instability (MSI) and tumor mutational burden (TMB). The sequence data was mapped to the human genome, Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37 (GRCh37), also known as hg19. The software version was MR Suite Version (RG) 8.9.0; MR Reporting Config Version 77; Analysis Pipeline Version v3.41.1.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. All data sets were challenged with D’Agostino & Pearson and the Shapiro-Wilk normality tests. All data sets are checked for any outliers using the GraphPad outlier calculator – which are then excluded from the analysis. Data with Gaussian distribution were analyzed by parametric unpaired two-tailed t-test and data sets that did not pass the normality tests were analyzed by nonparametric unpaired Mann–Whitney test. We also performed ANOVA, but pairwise comparisons were performed per figure panel, comparing controls with each treatment group, results are indicated in the figure legends. Implantation data sets were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. All were two-sided tests with a confidence interval of 95%. Differences were considered significant at P <.05 and statistical output was represented by stars as non-significant (NS) > 0.05, *≤0.05, **≤0.01, ***≤0.001. All graphs presented the results as AVG ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Case presentation

A 37-year-old man was diagnosed with a left temporal lesion after developing olfactory epileptic seizures (Fig. 1a–d). Surgery was performed under an initial histological diagnosis compatible with grade III pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma. The patient was treated with radiotherapy and subsequent chemotherapy with Lomustine-Procarbazine-Vincristine (PCV). After five cycles of PCV, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested a relapse (Fig. 1e-g). A second opinion at Champalimaud Foundation was requested.

A histological review resulted in a definitive diagnosis of glioblastoma-gliosarcoma subtype (Fig. 1h–k). Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) staining showed highly cellular neoplasia with glial and spindled tumor cells (Fig. 1h, i). The spindle cell component displays herringbone architecture in some areas. The tumor had extensive areas of geographical and pseudopalisading necrosis (Fig. 1h), with severe atypia and abundant atypical mitosis (Fig. 1i, black arrow heads). Immunohistochemistry showed p53 positivity in both glial and sarcomatous regions (Fig. 1j) and a ki67 high proliferation index of approximately 80% (Fig. 1k).

Patient was re-operated 14 months later and this surgical material was used to perform a comprehensive genetic profile (CGP) using the 324-gene NGS panel from Foundation Medicine (FoundationOne CDx). The CGP profile revealed the c.−124C > T hotspot mutation in the TERT promoter region, alongside with the loss of CDKN2A, CDKN2B and MTAP genes, as well as the TP53 p.(Ile195Phe) mutation. The tumor was microsatellite stable (MSS) and the mutational burden was 2 mutations per megabase (Fig. 1l). All the genetic alterations are typical of high grade astrocytomas, namely glioblastomas.

Since the CGP revealed no high-rank clinically actionable mutations, treatment with metronomic TMZ and bevacizumab every 2 weeks (a total of 4 administrations) was initiated. A follow-up brain MRI two months after the second surgery, revealed a thicker extra-axial component on the margin of the surgical cavity, favoring tumor infiltration (Fig. 1m). Re-irradiation was performed and only metronomic TMZ was maintained.

Five months after the second surgery, the patient presented severe headaches, aphasia and pronation of the right hand. A brain MRI showed dural infiltration with three new nodular lesions in the left frontal-temporal-parietal region, which caused edema and mass effect (Fig. 1n, o). A third surgery was performed, with subtotal resection of the dural lesions and prompt clinical improvement. However, twenty days later, the patient relapsed again with aphasia and loss of strength.

The subsequent therapeutic choices for this patient were no longer well defined in the guidelines, and 13 potential chemotherapy options were considered.

The analysis of the pathogenic genetic alterations did not show a therapeutic target that is considered top-ranked in the different actionability scales (eg. NTRK fusions, BRAF V600E or high tumor mutation burden). However, the tumor harbored a loss of exons 2-8 in the MTAP gene, presumably resulting in loss of activity of the S-methyl-5’-thioadenosine phosphorylase enzyme. MTAP loss introduces a metabolic vulnerability in purine and DNA damage repair pathways, supporting the inclusion of antifolates like pemetrexed and cytarabine, and DNA-damaging agents, such as etoposide. Preclinical and clinical evidence shows that MTAP-deficient tumors are synthetically lethal to de novo purine synthesis inhibition, particularly by antifolate agents like pemetrexed14–16, thus we reasoned that this could be a viable therapeutic approach.

Bevacizumab, lomustine, temozolomide, carboplatin, and irinotecan were chosen due to their frequent clinical use in recurrent glioblastoma and established CNS activity6,7,17–21. Given the sarcomatous component of gliosarcoma, agents that have demonstrated efficacy in soft tissue sarcomas—such as doxorubicin, docetaxel, gemcitabine, dacarbazine, vinorelbine, and paclitaxel—were also included22–24. Additionally, olaparib was selected based on emerging evidence that PARP inhibition may exploit this vulnerability via synthetic lethality in MTAP-deficient tumor contexts25.

Therefore, in an attempt to guide the choice of the best therapeutical option, gliosarcoma zebrafish patient derived xenografts were generated to screen the 13 possible options of treatment.

Glioblastoma-patient derived zAvatars show sensitivity to combination of radiotherapy with Pemetrexed and Doxorubicin

In the last years, we have been developing a fast in vivo functional test with unprecedented cellular resolution: the zebrafish-patient-derived xenograft model or zAvatar-test9,11,26–28. This assay relies on the injection of tumor cells into zebrafish embryos to generate the zAvatars, where several treatment options can be tested and final results can be obtained within 10 days; a time frame compatible with oncological clinical decisions11. Previous studies, including our own, have shown that zAvatars can predict patient clinical outcomes with outstanding predictive values. We have performed several observational clinical studies to determine the predictive value of the zAvatar model, where the patient’s clinical response to therapy was directly compared to their matching zAvatar-test. Our data demonstrates a predictive accuracy of 90% in colorectal cancer (N = 55 patients)27 and 100% in breast cancer (N = 18 patients)28.

Before generating the gliosarcoma patient’s zAvatars, we first optimized the glioblastoma model using an established cancer cell line and tested the standard therapeutic options (Supplementary Fig. 3a–p). U87 glioblastoma fluorescently labeled cells were injected in the hindbrain ventricle of 2 days post-fertilization zebrafish embryos. 24 h post-injection (hpi) U87 xenografts were subjected to treatment with the main therapeutic options recommended in GBM guidelines. After 3 days of treatment, U87 zebrafish xenografts were analyzed by confocal imaging to evaluate the impact of treatments on tumor implantation (Supplementary Fig. 3b), cell death by apoptosis and tumor size (Supplementary Fig. 3c–p). U87 glioblastoma zebrafish xenografts were sensitive to all treatments tested, with Temozolomide (TMZ) and Radiotherapy (RT) combination (RT + TMZ) demonstrating a higher efficacy in inducing apoptosis and tumor shrinkage (RT + TMZ: ****P < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 3m, p). All treatments were compared to untreated controls.

Next, we generated zAvatars from the gliosarcoma patient using tissue that was resected from the third surgery to test the 13 available options for that patient, all in combination with radiotherapy (Fig. 2a, b).

Tumor tissue was dissociated, and the tumor cell suspension was fluorescently labeled for hindbrain injection (see Methods). At 1 dpi, zAvatars were subjected to the different treatments. To determine the sensitivity or resistance to treatment, tumor implantation and apoptosis induction were analyzed as previously. Implantation was defined as incidence of zAvatars that present a tumor mass in the site of injection at 3 dpi. We could detect a decrease in implantation (~10%) compared to control, in four different therapeutic schemes, Etoposide, Pemetrexed (Peme), Pemetrexed + Doxorubicin (Peme+Doxo) and Olaparib; all in combination with Radiotherapy (RT). Statistical significance was only observed in the treatments involving Pemetrexed chemotherapy: RT+Peme (**P = 0.0076) and RT+Peme+Doxo (**P = 0.0065) (Fig. 2c–h). To further assess treatment response, we analyzed these prioritized conditions by single cell confocal microscopy and quantified apoptosis induction (activated caspase 3). A statistically significant increase in apoptosis was observed in RT+Peme (**P = 0.0018) and RT+Peme+Doxo (****P < 0.0001), whereas no significant effect was detected with Olaparib or Ectoposide (Fig. 2c–i, Supplementary Fig. 4 for absolute and percentage of activated Caspase3 quantification). All treatments were compared to untreated controls.

Our zAvatar-test results indicated that two combinations—radiotherapy with Pemetrexed, and Pemetrexed with Doxorubicin—were both effective in inducing apoptosis. The attending physician opted for the combination of radiotherapy and Pemetrexed, with Doxorubicin introduced 3 weeks later, due to concerns about potential myelosuppression and cardiotoxicity.

The patient recovered from aphasia and regained arm strength, being stable for 2 months, suggesting that the treatment was clinically effective during this period (Fig. 3a, b). At this time, the MRI showed improvement of the lesions treated by radiotherapy (Fig. 3c, blue), however new dural lesions emerged outside the radiotherapy field (Fig. 3d, red). Again, this raised the question of which therapy to combine next with focal radiotherapy. Given that the patient had previously been quite stable and controlled under Beva+TMZ, we wondered whether we could still detect sensitivity to this regimen in the zAvatars, and therefore this could still be a valid option for this patient.

Patient cells were still sensitive to Beva + TMZ chemotherapy but resistant to PCV

Next, we challenged the zAvatars with RT+Beva+TMZ, while also testing an earlier treatment (RT + PCV), a therapy under which the patient progressed and thus we expected to observe resistance to treatment and could be considered a negative control (Fig. 4a–g). Our data show that, as expected, induction of apoptosis was only registered in the combination of RT+Beva+TMZ (*P = 0.0286), suggesting that the tumor was still sensitive to BEVA + TMZ combination and resistant to the RT + PCV (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 5 for absolute numbers and percentage of activated caspase quantification).

Based on this, the patient restarted metronomic BEVA + TMZ and showed intracranial stable response for the following two months. Unfortunately, by this time (23 months since the diagnosis) a brain MRI revealed progression of the tumoral lesions in non-radiated regions (Fig. 4h) and a thoracic CT scan revealed lung metastases (Fig. 4i), consistent with the gliosarcoma behavior. Due to persistent fatigue, oncologic treatment was withdrawn, and the patient received best supportive care and deceased two weeks later.

In summary, our results suggest that the zAvatars were able to recapitulate the patient’s past clinical history, i.e., resistance to RT + PCV and relative sensitivity to RT + BEVA + TMZ and RT+Peme+Doxo. These results highlight the importance of having a functional predictive assay to guide oncological treatments decisions.

Discussion

Gliosarcoma is a rare type of glioblastoma, estimated to comprise only ~2,1% of all glioblastomas and is characterized by a combination of gliomatous and sarcomatous components. Although most gliomas rarely metastasize outside the CNS, gliosarcomas have the capacity to spread to the blood, lungs but also to the liver and lymph nodes29. The survival period of gliosarcoma is reported to range from 6 to 18.5 months30. Currently, first line available therapeutic options are scarce. Furthermore, upon recurrence, treatment decisions are made in the absence of reliable biomarkers. Here we report for the first time the use of the zAvatars-test to guide treatment choices in a gliosarcoma patient.

The patient was a 37-year-old male with a relapsing gliosarcoma, who had experienced multiple cycles of response/resistance to previous treatments.

We successfully established zAvatars from a gliosarcoma surgical sample, obtained from the third surgery (second relapse) and screened the 13 options that were available for that patient in that time-point.

Our results showed that the combinatorial treatment of Radiotherapy + Pemetrexed or Radiotherapy + Pemetrexed & Doxorubicin induced higher cytotoxicity than the other tested treatments. Based on these findings, the patient was treated accordingly, and the treatment was clinically effective: the symptoms of aphasia and loss of strength reduced, and the patient was stable for two months. However, it is worth noting that the limited penetration of these drugs across the blood-brain barrier may also have restricted the full extent of their efficacy. After the appearance of a new lesion, the attending physician wondered whether the tumor was still sensitive to RT+Beva+TMZ combination, as the patient had previously demonstrated disease control under this regimen.

To investigate this, new zAvatars were generated to test the patient’s prior therapies. As expected, resistance to RT + PCV was observed. However, the zAvatars remained sensitive to RT+Beva+TMZ, suggesting that the model effectively captured therapy resistance while also revealing a subset of tumor cells still responsive to RT+Beva+TMZ.

In accordance, the patient was subsequently treated with RT+Beva+TMZ combination, achieving an intracranial stable response for the next two months. Regrettably, but expected, given the gliosarcoma’s aggressive nature and behavior, the patient developed lung metastases, atypical in glioblastoma but common and characteristic of the rare gliosarcomas29. Consequently, oncologic interventions were discontinued, and the patient transitioned to optimal supportive care. The patient passed away two weeks later. Notably, the OS of this patient was ~24 months, surpassing already the expected median OS for gliosarcoma patients (median OS 6–14/18 m)30.

Our findings underscore the critical significance of employing a functional predictive assay and a strict follow-up to help tailor oncological interventions effectively and to optimize the management of this shattering disease.

The primary limitation of the zAvatar-test is the requirement for fresh tumor tissue, which constrains the number of therapies that can be evaluated from a given sample. In glioblastoma, however, this is generally less problematic: when testing is coordinated with surgery, tissue availability is typically sufficient, unlike in several other tumor types where material is more limited. In fact, provided there is enough fresh sample, such in glioblastoma, a major strength of the zAvatar platform is its capacity to generate large numbers of xenografts from a single patient sample. In the present study, we generated a total of 920 PDXs distributed across 14 treatment conditions (minimum of 43 PDXs per condition) and quantified by confocal analysis 166 PDXs across 10 conditions, since the patient sample was not limiting. Such throughput is rarely achievable in other in vivo or in vitro functional models and enables statistically robust comparisons while capturing the intrinsic heterogeneity of patient tumors. Variability across xenografts likely reflects this biological diversity, which we consider biologically meaningful rather than a limitation, consistent with the model’s high predictive accuracy27,28.

Importantly, results from the zAvatar-test can generally be obtained within 10 days—well within the window when treatment decisions are made—thereby enabling the early assessment of therapeutic options before the initiation of therapy. In contrast, conventional clinical and molecular profiling approaches often require longer turnaround times and do not directly measure treatment response.

However, while confocal-based quantification of apoptosis is highly predictive9,12,27,28, it is also time-consuming. In this particular case study, the patient’s condition was time-sensitive and required rapid guidance for therapy selection. Given the large number of potential therapies to evaluate, we used tumor implantation as a pre-screening step to prioritize which conditions to analyze by confocal microscopy. To overcome time constraints for future patients, we are actively developing methods to accelerate this step and deliver results more quickly without compromising accuracy.

Although we did not perform a predictive clinical study in glioblastoma, such validation could be valuable to assess applicability in this aggressive tumor type. Our immediate next step, focuses on a randomized clinical trial (EU CT 2023-509598-22) in ovarian and breast cancer, which we hope will provide robust evidence of the clinical utility of the zAvatar-test.

Importantly, in this case study we also show how functional testing can and should be used in conjunction with medical judgment and comprehensive knowledge of the patient’s underlying conditions. The zAvatar-test showed that Pemetrexed or Pemetrexed with Doxorubicin in combination with radiotherapy were both effective in inducing apoptosis. The oncologist opted for the combination of radiotherapy and Pemetrexed first and only introduce Doxorubicin later due to toxicity concerns.

Finally, if the tumor is sequenced in parallel and actionable mutations are found, the efficacy of the corresponding compounds can also be tested. Thus, a pipeline combining genomic sequencing with the zAvatar functional test and stringent follow-up with clinical judgment may improve the management of these patients, in a truly personalized way, ultimately improving their PFS, OS and quality of life.
