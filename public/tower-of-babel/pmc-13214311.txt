Rapid improvement in glycaemic control is associated with increased circulating osteocalcin in type 2 diabetes: a concise observational comparative study
Authors: Dured Dardari, Francois-Xavier Laborne, Claire Thomas, Caroline Tourte, Elodie Henry, Philippe Lopes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes is associated with low bone turnover and skeletal fragility. Osteocalcin has been linked to glucose homeostasis, but its short-term response to rapid improvement in chronic hyperglycaemia in adults remains insufficiently described.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre observational comparative study in adults with type 2 diabetes. Group 1 had chronic poor control (HbA1c > 8.5% for at least 6 months) and underwent individualised treatment optimisation over 3 months as part of routine care. Group 2 had stable good control (HbA1c < 7% for at least 6 months) and served as a reference group for temporal stability. Total serum osteocalcin was measured at baseline (T0) and at 3 months (T3) and is reported in ng/mL. Body weight was retrieved retrospectively from the medical records. Analyses were exploratory.ResultsSixty participants were included (mean age 52 +/- 9 years; 50% women). Baseline osteocalcin concentrations were similar in Group 1 and Group 2 (12.4 +/- 4.3 vs. 12.5 +/- 3.9 ng/mL; between-group mean difference − 0.1 ng/mL, 95% CI -2.2 to 2.0). In Group 1, osteocalcin increased from 12.4 +/- 4.3 to 20.4 +/- 4.8 ng/mL (absolute mean change + 8.0 ng/mL; p < 0.0001), whereas Group 2 remained stable (12.5 +/- 3.9 to 12.8 +/- 3.7 ng/mL; absolute mean change + 0.3 ng/mL; p = 0.94). At 3 months, Group 1 osteocalcin exceeded Group 2 by 7.6 ng/mL (95% CI 5.4 to 9.8). Overall body weight increased descriptively from 79 +/- 3 kg at baseline to 81 +/- 2.4 kg at 3 months.ConclusionsRapid improvement in glycaemic control was associated with increased circulating total osteocalcin over 3 months in chronically poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, while osteocalcin remained stable in a well-controlled reference group. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is associated with increased fragility fracture risk despite normal or increased bone mineral density, suggesting impaired bone quality and low bone turnover [1, 2]. Osteocalcin is a non-collagenous protein produced by osteoblasts and has been linked to glucose homeostasis, particularly in its undercarboxylated form [3–6]. Chronic hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress and advanced glycation end-products may suppress osteoblast function and contribute to reduced bone remodelling in diabetes [3–5].

Although cross-sectional associations between osteocalcin and glycaemic status have been reported, short-term longitudinal data during rapid correction of chronic hyperglycaemia in adults with type 2 diabetes remain limited, and concerns have been raised that rapid correction may affect bone remodelling [7, 8]. We therefore investigated whether rapid improvement in glycaemic control over 3 months was associated with a change in circulating total osteocalcin in a single-centre cohort, using a stable well-controlled group as a reference for temporal stability.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-centre observational comparative study conducted in the Department of Diabetes, Hopital Sud Francilien (Corbeil-Essonnes, France). The present report is an ancillary osteocalcin analysis within the prospective RANKL-GLYC parent study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04893135; protocol version 1.1 dated 18 May 2021). The parent study was designed to evaluate change in RANKL; accordingly, the number of participants available for the present ancillary analysis was inherited from that protocol rather than determined specifically for osteocalcin. No formal sample-size calculation was therefore performed for osteocalcin, and all eligible participants with paired samples were included.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18–70 years with type 2 diabetes for at least 1 year and stable glucose-lowering therapy before enrolment. Group 1 (poor control) had HbA1c > 8.5% for at least 6 months despite therapy and underwent treatment optimisation during routine care. Group 2 (well-controlled reference group) had HbA1c < 7% for at least 6 months with stable control and was included to assess short-term stability of osteocalcin rather than to serve as a causal control group. A persistently uncontrolled non-improving comparator group was not included because improving HbA1c was the clinical objective.

Exclusion criteria were initiation of insulin therapy or modification of glucose-lowering therapy within 6 months before enrolment, systemic corticosteroid use during follow-up, anti-RANKL treatment, Charcot neuroarthropathy, or symptomatic autonomic neuropathy.

Clinical management during follow-up

Over 3 months, participants in Group 1 underwent rapid optimisation of glycaemic control as part of routine clinical care. Management was individualised by the treating diabetologist and could include reinforcement of dietary and physical activity recommendations and adjustment or addition of glucose-lowering medications. Participants in Group 2 continued usual care without planned intensification. Detailed follow-up information on medication classes, insulin exposure or dose, and lifestyle changes was not systematically captured.

Measurements

We collected age, sex, diabetes duration, microvascular complications, body mass index, serum creatinine and HbA1c. Total serum osteocalcin was measured at baseline (T0) and after 3 months (T3) in the same clinical laboratory and is reported in ng/mL throughout. Total osteocalcin, rather than undercarboxylated osteocalcin, was measured because only the total osteocalcin assay was routinely available in the clinical laboratory at the time of the study. Body weight at T0 and T3 was retrieved retrospectively from medical records after chart review.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as number (percentage). Within-group changes were assessed using paired t-tests. Between-group contrasts at baseline and at 3 months are reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. Because the sample size for this ancillary analysis was inherited from the parent study, and because interventions during follow-up were heterogeneous and incompletely captured, with important confounders unavailable, we did not perform a multivariable adjusted model or a post hoc correlation/regression analysis of HbA1c change against osteocalcin change, as such analyses would have been vulnerable to overinterpretation. Analyses were therefore considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Participants were enrolled under the parent RANKL-GLYC protocol, which received a favourable opinion from the French Ethics Committee for the Protection of Persons, South Mediterranean III (Comite de Protection des Personnes Sud Mediterranee III) (reference no. 2021.05.01 bis_21.03.15.39441; protocol version 1.1 dated 18 May 2021; effective 30 June 2021; opinion letter dated 1 July 2021). The present report is an ancillary osteocalcin analysis of that cohort. All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Baseline osteocalcin concentrations were similar between groups: 12.4 +/- 4.3 ng/mL in Group 1 and 12.5 +/- 3.9 ng/mL in Group 2 (between-group mean difference − 0.1 ng/mL, 95% CI -2.2 to 2.0). In the total cohort, mean osteocalcin increased from 12.5 +/- 4.1 ng/mL at T0 to 16.4 +/- 5.7 ng/mL at T3.

Group 1 showed a marked increase in osteocalcin from 12.4 +/- 4.3 to 20.4 +/- 4.8 ng/mL (absolute mean change + 8.0 ng/mL; paired p < 0.0001), whereas Group 2 showed no meaningful change over time, from 12.5 +/- 3.9 to 12.8 +/- 3.7 ng/mL (absolute mean change + 0.3 ng/mL; paired p = 0.94). At 3 months, osteocalcin levels were higher in Group 1 than in Group 2 by 7.6 ng/mL (95% CI 5.4 to 9.8). A retrospective review of body-weight data showed a descriptive increase in the overall cohort from 79 +/- 3 kg at baseline to 81 +/- 2.4 kg at 3 months.

Discussion

In this concise observational study, rapid improvement in chronic hyperglycaemia was accompanied by a marked increase in circulating total osteocalcin over 3 months in adults with chronically poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, whereas osteocalcin remained stable in a well-controlled reference group. The findings support an association between metabolic improvement and a short-term change in a bone-derived marker, but they do not establish that glucose lowering itself caused the osteocalcin increase.The absence of a baseline osteocalcin difference despite markedly different HbA1c levels is noteworthy. Total osteocalcin is influenced by multiple determinants besides glycaemia, including interindividual variation in age, sex, adiposity, renal function, medication exposure and unmeasured skeletal status. In this context, the longitudinal change signal may be more informative than the cross-sectional baseline comparison. Because only total osteocalcin was measured, for pragmatic reasons of assay availability, the study cannot determine whether the metabolically active undercarboxylated fraction changed in parallel.

Several limitations temper interpretation. This was a small single-centre study without a persistently uncontrolled non-improving comparator group. The ancillary sample size was inherited from the parent RANKL-GLYC study, which sought a significant reduction in RANKL rather than a prespecified osteocalcin endpoint. Intensified management was individualised, and detailed data on insulin exposure, medication classes and lifestyle changes were not systematically recorded, creating major potential for confounding. Body weight, retrieved retrospectively, increased descriptively over follow-up, and unmeasured changes in physical activity or weight-bearing exercise may also have contributed to osteocalcin variation. The absence of a direct analysis relating HbA1c change to osteocalcin change further limits mechanistic interpretation. No adjusted multivariable model was performed. We also lacked additional bone turnover markers (such as P1NP and CTX), bone mineral density or fracture data, menopausal status, and longer-term follow-up; therefore, the clinical significance of the observed short-term osteocalcin increase for bone or metabolic health remains uncertain. Although no a priori osteocalcin-specific sample-size calculation was performed, the observed 3-month between-group osteocalcin difference was large (standardized mean difference approximately 1.8), which likely explains why a clear signal was detectable despite the modest sample size. This should not be interpreted as evidence that the study was powered for correlation analyses or adjusted modelling. Detailed assay platform characteristics were not available for reporting in this ancillary analysis.

Despite these limitations, the study has practical strengths: paired biomarker measurements, clear separation of glycaemic trajectories between groups, and a clinically relevant question. The findings are best viewed as hypothesis-generating and supportive of larger prospective studies with standardised treatment capture, prespecified weight and physical activity assessment, measurement of undercarboxylated osteocalcin and other bone turnover markers, and longer follow-up.

Conclusions

Rapid improvement in glycaemic control over 3 months was associated with increased circulating total osteocalcin in adults with chronically poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, whereas osteocalcin remained stable in a well-controlled reference group. Because important treatment, weight, lifestyle and skeletal confounders were incompletely captured, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Whether this short-term osteocalcin increase reflects improved bone remodelling, a marker of metabolic recovery, or a neutral epiphenomenon remains unknown.
