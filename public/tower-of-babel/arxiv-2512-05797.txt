Floer sections in multisymplectic geometry
Ronen Brilleslijper, Oliver Fabert

Abstract
In symplectic geometry, Floer theory is the most important tool to prove the existence of time-periodic solutions in Hamiltonian mechanics. The core observation is that the L 2 L^{2} -gradient lines of the symplectic action functional are pseudo-holomorphic curves, enabling the use of elliptic PDE methods. Multisymplectic geometry is the geometric framework underlying Hamiltonian field theory, where the time line is replaced by higher-dimensional manifolds. In the case of two dimensions and using complex structures, we introduce a novel multisymplectic framework that is fit for the generalization of the elliptic methods from symplectic geometry. Besides proving a Darboux theorem, we show that the L 2 L^{2} -gradient lines of our multisymplectic action functional are now pseudo-Fueter curves defined using a compatible almost hyperkähler structure.



In symplectic geometry, one studies solutions
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S^{1}\to M
to Hamilton’s equation

\displaystyle\omega(\cdot,\dot{u}(t))=dH_{t}(u(t))

(1)

on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
with periodic Hamiltonian
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S^{1}\times M\to\mathbb{R}
. One of the most powerful tools to study the existence of these 1-dimensional objects is Floer theory. The idea is that solutions to
equation

1
can be seen as critical points of the action functional. One can therefore study negative gradient lines of the action functional and use ideas from Morse theory to find solutions to Hamiltons equation. These negative gradient lines are referred to as Floer curves and are given by maps
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}\to M
satisfying the Floer equation

\displaystyle\partial_{s}u+J\partial_{t}u=\nabla H(u),

(2)

where
J
is an almost complex structure compatible with
\omega
and the gradient is taken with respect to the induced metric
\omega(J\cdot,\cdot)
. Floer theory fundamentally uses the fact that
equation

2
is an elliptic PDE.

When moving from Hamiltonian mechanics to field theory, 1-dimensional time is replaced by higher-dimensional manifolds. Multisymplectic geometry is a generalization of symplectic geometry to the setting where we study maps from
d
-dimensional manifolds. In
section

1
we will dive more into the framework of multisymplectic geometry. For more background on multisymplectic and related geometries see
[
DLSV15
,
FG13
,
MS99
]
. A natural question is whether we can study the existence of these higher-dimensional objects using techniques similar to Floer theory on symplectic manifolds. So far, there is no generalization of Floer theory to the multisymplectic setting. The reason is that one of the most fundamental equations in multisymplectic geometry is not elliptic. These equations, called the De Donder-Weyl equations, locally have the following form

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\partial_{1}p^{a}_{1}-\partial_{2}p^{a}_{2}&=\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}}\\
\partial_{1}q^{a}&=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{1}^{a}}\\
\partial_{2}q^{a}&=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{2}^{a}},\end{split}

(3)

for
d=2
and local coordinates
q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a}
on the multisymplectic manifold. It can easily be seen that the differential operator on the left-hand side of these equations has an infinite-dimensional kernel (see
[
BF24
]
). This makes the use of Floer-theoretic methods impossible.

If the underlying 2-dimensional manifold is a torus, the multisymplectic framework simplifies drastically to what we call polysymplectic geometry in the sense of
[
Gün87
]
.
i
i

i

The authors have been made aware that the term polysymplectic nowadays can have a slightly different meaning.
In polysymplectic geometry, the same problem arises as mentioned above, namely that the underlying equations are not elliptic. However, in
[
BF24
]
a formalism was developed in which the existence of 2-dimensional objects can be studied, in analogy with the 1-dimensional objects, that can be studied in symplectic geometry. The resulting theory, which we called complex-regularized polysymplectic (CRPS) geometry, provides a subclass of polysymplectic manifolds on which the underlying equations are in fact elliptic.

In CRPS geometry, one starts with a complex manifold
(W,I)
. A closed
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued 2-form
\Omega=\omega_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\omega_{2}\otimes\partial_{2}
on
W
is called CRPS if
\ker\Omega^{\flat}=0
and
\omega_{2}=-\omega_{1}(\cdot,I\cdot)
. In
[
BF24
]
it was shown, that this implies that both
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are in fact symplectic forms. The main example of a CRPS manifold, is the cotangent bundle of a complex manifold
Q
with its induced complex structure
I
. An easy check shows that
\omega_{1}=d\theta
and
\omega_{2}=-d(\theta\circ I)
yield a CRPS form, where
\theta=pdq
is the canonical Liouvile 1-form. Locally, all CRPS manifolds are isomorphic.

Given a CRPS manifold
(W,I,\Omega=\omega_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\omega_{2}\otimes\partial_{2})
, there is an action functional on the space of maps
\mathbb{T}^{2}\to W
. This action functional arises from the multisymplectic form
\widetilde{\Omega}=\omega_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-\omega_{2}\wedge dt_{1}
on
W\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
, that is obtained by contracting
\Omega
with the volume form
dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Assume that
\omega_{j}=d\theta_{j}
. Then we can define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(Z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\widetilde{Z}^{*}\widetilde{\Theta}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\theta_{1}(\partial_{1}Z)+\theta_{2}(\partial_{2}Z))dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2},

where
\widetilde{Z}=Z\times\textup{Id}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\to W\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
\widetilde{\Theta}
is the primitive of
\widetilde{\Omega}
. Given a Hamiltonian
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\mathbb{R}
, we may also define
\mathcal{A}_{H}(Z)=\mathcal{A}(Z)-\int_{T^{2}}H(Z)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}
. Critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
are given by the equation

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(\cdot,\partial_{1}Z)+\omega_{2}(\cdot,\partial_{2}Z)=dH.

(4)

Now, as shown in
[
BF24
]
, there exist anti-commuting almost complex structures
J
and
K
on
W
, such that
\omega_{1}=g(\cdot,J\cdot)
and
\omega_{2}=g(\cdot,K\cdot)
, for some Riemannian metric
g
. Taking the gradient of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-metric induced by
g
, yields
\textup{grad}\,\mathcal{A}_{H}(Z)=J\partial_{1}Z+K\partial_{2}Z-\nabla H(Z)
. In
[
BF24
]
, negative gradient lines of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
were used to determine a lower bound on the number of solutions of
equation

4
, using a similar reasoning as in Floer theory. A crucial fact there was that for
H=0
, these negative gradient lines are given by the Fueter equation, that has been studied by
[
HNS09
,
Wal17
,
Sal13
]
. See also the work by Doan-Rezchikov and Kontsevich-Soibelman in
[
DR23
,
KS24
]
towards defining holomorphic Floer theory.

For the construction above it is crucial that
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is parallelizable, since the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued form
\Omega
can then be seen as taking values in the tangent bundle of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. In this article, we would like to present a formalism in which we can extend the above discussion to maps from other Riemann surfaces
\Sigma
. As discussed above, we need to leave the world of polysymplectic geometry and use the multisymplectic framework to study maps on different surfaces. The idea is that the multisymplectic 3-form
\widetilde{\Omega}
on
W\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
actually generalizes well to other surfaces. In the next section, we will start by reviewing the setup of multisymplectic geometry, before specifying a subclass of manifolds, which we call complex-regularized multisymplectic (CRMS) manifolds, that are suitable for studying Floer curves like explained above. Even though the motivation of this article is to generalize the framework of
[
BF24
]
to surfaces other than
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, this paper is self-contained so no results from
[
BF24
]
are needed in order to follow the exposition.

This paper is organized as follows. In
section

1
we give an introduction to multisymplectic geometry by discussing the extended multimomentum bundle that generalizes the cotangent bundle from symplectic geometry. Subsequently, we incorporate the complex structures that are needed to produce elliptic equations in
section

2
.
Section

3
relates the construction from
section

2
to the CRPS setting from
[
BF24
]
in the case where
\Sigma=\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Next, we study the structures on the extended multimomentum bundle when
\Sigma\neq\mathbb{T}^{2}
in
section

4
that motivate the general definition of CRMS forms in
section

5
. A version of the Darboux theorem for CRMS forms is proven in
section

6
. Finally, in
section

7
, we show that the
L^{2}
-gradient lines of the multisymplectic action functional on CRMS bundles satisfy the perturbed Fueter equation, which is the analogue of the perturbed Cauchy-Riemann equation from symplectic geometry. This allows for the generalization of the elliptic methods that led to the success of symplectic geometry since the 1980s to multisymplectic geometry, which was the motivation that led to this article.

1
Multisymplectic geometry

We will start by reviewing the setup of multisymplectic geometry. For clarity of the concepts and to see what the resulting equations look like, we will indicate local coordinates for the objects introduced. This section is based on chapter 15 of
[
DLSV15
]
.

Fields are defined to be sections of a fiber bundle
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\to\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is a surface. Eventually, we want to study maps
\Sigma\to Q
, so that we are interested only in the trivial fiber bundle
Y=\Sigma\times Q
, but since the setup of the theory makes sense for general fiber bundles, we will not yet restrict ourselves here. Locally, we say that
\Sigma
has coordinates
t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(t_{1},t_{2})
and the fiber
Y_{t}
of
Y
over
t
has coordinates
q=(q^{1},\ldots,q^{n})
. Let
\Lambda^{2}(Y)\to Y
be the bundle of
2
-forms over Y. We can define a subbundle by

\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}(Y)\mid u\lrcorner(v\lrcorner\lambda)=0\text{ for all }u,v\in\ker d\pi\},

called the
extended multimomentum bundle
. We denote
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)\to Y
to be the bundle map. Given the local coordinate expressions for
Y
and
\Sigma
, we get induced coordinates
(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a},p)
on
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
, such that an element of
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)_{y}
is given by

pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}\left(p_{1}^{a}dq^{a}\wedge dt_{2}-p_{2}^{a}dq^{a}\wedge dt_{1}\right),

where
y=(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a})
.

Definition 1.1
.

The
tautological form

\Theta
on
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
is defined by

\Theta_{\lambda}=\kappa^{*}\lambda

for
\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
. The canonical multisymplectic form on the extended multimomentum bundle is
\Omega=d\Theta
.

In local coordinates we have that
\Theta
and
\Omega
are given by

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}\left(p_{1}^{a}dq^{a}\wedge dt_{2}-p_{2}^{a}dq^{a}\wedge dt_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=dp\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}\left(dp_{1}^{a}\wedge dq^{a}\wedge dt_{2}-dp_{2}^{a}\wedge dq^{a}\wedge dt_{1}\right).

Note that
\Omega
is a non-degenerate
3
-form on
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
in the sense that
X\mapsto X\lrcorner\Omega
is an injective map on
T_{\lambda}\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
for every
\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
.

Inside
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
, we have the subbundle
\pi^{*}\Lambda^{2}(\Sigma)
of 2-forms on the base. We define the
restricted multimomentum bundle
to be the quotient

\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}=\frac{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}{\pi^{*}\Lambda^{2}(\Sigma)}

and let
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}
be the canonical projection. Local coordinates on
\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}
are given by
(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a})
.

Definition 1.2
.

A
Hamiltonian section
is a section
h
of
\mu
. It induces the 2-form
\Theta^{h}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h^{*}\Theta
and the 3-form
\Omega^{h}=h^{*}\Omega=d\Theta^{h}
on the restricted multimomentum bundle.

A section
Z
of
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}\to\Sigma
is called a solution of
h
, if

\displaystyle Z^{*}(X\lrcorner\Omega^{h})=0

for all
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)})
.

Locally a Hamiltonian section is given by

h(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a})=(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a},-H(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a})),

where
H
is a smooth local function on
\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}
. Thus, in this local expression

\Omega^{h}=-dH\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}\left(dp_{1}^{a}\wedge dq^{a}\wedge dt_{2}-dp_{2}^{a}\wedge dq^{a}\wedge dt_{1}\right).

Let
Z
be a section of
\sigma
, locally given by
(t_{1},t_{2})\mapsto(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a},p_{1}^{a},p_{2}^{a})
. We see that

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}}+\partial_{1}p^{a}_{1}+\partial_{2}p^{a}_{2}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{1}^{a}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{1}^{a}}-\partial_{1}q^{a}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{2}^{a}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{2}^{a}}-\partial_{2}q^{a}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}.

Here,
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
denote the derivatives with respect to
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Therefore,
Z
is a solution of
h
if and only if in local coordinates it satisfies the De Donder-Weyl
equation

3
. A straightforward check shows that the equations obtained by contracting
\Omega^{h}
with
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
merely express the total derivative of
H
in terms of its partial derivatives as

\frac{d(H\circ Z)}{dt_{i}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial t_{i}}+\sum_{a}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}}\partial_{i}q^{a}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{a}_{1}}\partial_{i}p^{a}_{1}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{a}_{2}}\partial_{i}p^{a}_{2}\right).

Alternatively, when
\Sigma
is a closed surface, an easy calculation shows that
Z
is a solution of
h
if and only if it is a critical point of the action functional
\mathcal{A}_{h}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{h}(Z)=\int_{\Sigma}Z^{*}\Theta^{h}.

(5)

This action functional is not suitable to study Floer theory, for similar reasons as in the polysymplectic framework (see
[
BF24
]
). Thus, we restrict ourselves to a smaller class.

Incorporating complex structures

Instead of starting with a general fiber bundle, like in
section

1
, we now consider a holomorphic fiber bundle
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(Y,i)\to(\Sigma,j)
. I.e.
(Y,i)
is a complex manifold,
(\Sigma,j)
a Riemann surface, the bundle map satisfies
d\pi\circ i=j\circ d\pi
and locally
Y
is biholomorphic to the trivial bundle.
ii
ii

ii

Again, we are eventually only interested in the trivial bundle, but provide a more general setup of the theory.

Using the complex structure
i
, we may define a subbundle
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
of the extended multimomentum bundle by

\Lambda^{C}(Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)\mid\lambda(i\cdot,i\cdot)=\lambda\}.

Note that this definition makes sense, since
\lambda
is an element of
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
if and only if
\lambda(i\cdot,i\cdot)
is an element of
\Lambda_{2}^{2}(Y)
.
The tautological form on
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
restricts to a 2-form on
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
, which we still denote by
\Theta
. The 3-form
\Omega=d\Theta
on
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
is called the
complex-regularized multisymplectic form
on
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
.

We note that
\pi^{*}\Lambda^{2}(\Sigma)\subseteq\Lambda^{C}(Y)
, since
j
preserves 2-dimensional volume (as can be checked in local coordinates). Therefore, we may still define the quotient

\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}{\pi^{*}\Lambda^{2}(\Sigma)}.

The projection is denoted by
\mu^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda^{C}(Y)\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
complex-regularized Hamiltonian section
is defined to be a section
h
of
\mu^{C}
. As before it induces the forms
\Theta^{h}=h^{*}\Theta
and
\Omega^{h}=h^{*}\Omega
.

A section
Z
of
\sigma^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\to\Sigma
is called a solution of
h
if

\displaystyle Z^{*}(X\lrcorner\Omega^{h})=0

for all
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)})
.

We want to show that
Z
is a solution of
h
if and only if it locally satisfies the Bridges equations from
[
BF24
]
. We may choose local coordinates
(t_{1},t_{2})
on
\Sigma
and
(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2})
on
Y
, such that

\displaystyle j\partial_{1}=\partial_{2}

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{1}^{a}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{2}^{a}}.

They induce local coordinates
(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2},p^{a}_{1,1},p^{a}_{1,2},p^{a}_{2,1},p^{a}_{2,2},p)
on
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
such that an element in
\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)_{y}
is given by

\lambda=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}\left(p^{a}_{1,1}dq^{a}_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-p^{a}_{1,2}dq^{a}_{1}\wedge dt_{1}+p^{a}_{2,1}dq^{a}_{2}\wedge dt_{2}-p^{a}_{2,2}dq^{a}_{2}\wedge dt_{1}\right)

for
y=(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2})
. In these coordinates, we get that

\displaystyle\lambda(i\cdot,i\cdot)=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}\left(p^{a}_{2,2}dq^{a}_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+p^{a}_{2,1}dq^{a}_{1}\wedge dt_{1}-p^{a}_{1,2}dq^{a}_{2}\wedge dt_{2}-p^{a}_{1,1}dq^{a}_{2}\wedge dt_{1}\right).

Thus,
\lambda
is an element of
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
if and only if

\displaystyle p^{a}_{1,1}=p^{a}_{2,2}

\displaystyle p^{a}_{1,2}=-p^{a}_{2,1}

for all
a
. We may define
P^{a}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p^{a}_{1,1}=p^{a}_{2,2}
and
P^{a}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-p^{a}_{1,2}=p^{a}_{2,1}
. Then
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
has coordinates
(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2},P^{a}_{1},P^{a}_{2},p)
, where elements in the fiber are given by

\lambda=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}(P^{a}_{1}dq^{a}_{1}+P^{a}_{2}dq^{a}_{2})\wedge dt_{2}-\sum_{a}(P^{a}_{1}dq^{a}_{2}-P^{a}_{2}dq^{a}_{1})\wedge dt_{1}.

Therefore, we compute
\Omega
as

\Omega=dp\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}(dP^{a}_{1}\wedge dq^{a}_{1}+dP^{a}_{2}\wedge dq^{a}_{2})\wedge dt_{2}-\sum_{a}(dP^{a}_{1}\wedge dq^{a}_{2}-dP^{a}_{2}\wedge dq^{a}_{1})\wedge dt_{1},

which is indeed pointwise non-degenerate.

Now
(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2},P^{a}_{1},P^{a}_{2})
define local coordinates on
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
and a Hamiltonian section is locally given by
p=-H(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2},P^{a}_{1},P^{a}_{2})
for some function
H
as above.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
be a section that is given by
(t_{1},t_{2})\mapsto(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2},P^{a}_{1},P^{a}_{2})
in the local coordinates defined above. Then
Z
is a solution of the Hamiltonian section
h
given locally by
p=-H(t_{1},t_{2},q^{a}_{1},q^{a}_{2},P^{a}_{1},P^{a}_{2})
if and only if in local coordinates
Z
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}_{1}}&=-\partial_{1}P^{a}_{1}+\partial_{2}P^{a}_{2}\\
\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}_{2}}&=-\partial_{1}P^{a}_{2}-\partial_{2}P^{a}_{1}\\
\frac{\partial H}{\partial P^{a}_{1}}&=\phantom{-}\partial_{1}q^{a}_{1}+\partial_{2}q^{a}_{2}\\
\frac{\partial H}{\partial P^{a}_{2}}&=\phantom{-}\partial_{1}q^{a}_{2}-\partial_{2}q^{a}_{1}.\end{split}

(6)

Proof.

In the coordinates indicated above, we see that

\Omega^{h}=-dH\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\sum_{a}(dP^{a}_{1}\wedge dq^{a}_{1}+dP^{a}_{2}\wedge dq^{a}_{2})\wedge dt_{2}-\sum_{a}(dP^{a}_{1}\wedge dq^{a}_{2}-dP^{a}_{2}\wedge dq^{a}_{1})\wedge dt_{1}.

Thus contracting
\Omega^{h}
with different basis vectors gives

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}_{1}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}_{1}}+\partial_{1}P^{a}_{1}-\partial_{2}P^{a}_{2}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}_{2}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}_{2}}+\partial_{1}P^{a}_{2}+\partial_{2}P^{a}_{1}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P^{a}_{1}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P^{a}_{1}}-\partial_{1}q^{a}_{1}-\partial_{2}q^{a}_{2}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle Z^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P^{a}_{2}}\lrcorner\Omega^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P^{a}_{2}}-\partial_{1}q^{a}_{2}+\partial_{2}q^{a}_{1}\right)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}.

Indeed, we see that
Z
is a solution precisely when
equation

6
is satisfied. Just like before, the contraction of
\Omega^{h}
with
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
doesn’t give any additional information, but merely expresses the chain rule for the derivatives of
H\circ Z
.

Example 2.3
.

For
Y=\Sigma\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\Sigma

6
, just like in

]

\mu^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda^{C}(Y)\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}

\Sigma

\rho

\Sigma

(t_{1},t_{2},q_{1}^{a},q_{2}^{a},P_{1}^{a},P_{2}^{a})\mapsto\frac{1}{2}|P|^{2}+\lambda(t)V(q)

|S|^{2}=|P|^{2}|\partial_{t}\psi|^{-2}
. Thus
|S|^{2}dw_{1}\wedge dw_{2}=|P|^{2}dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}
and we see that
h
defines a global section of
\mu^{C}
. By filling in the third and fourth equalities of
equation

6
into the first and second, we find that solutions to this Hamiltonian section are given by

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{\rho(t)}\partial_{t}\partial_{\bar{t}}q^{a}_{j}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial q^{a}_{j}}.

Note that
\frac{4}{\rho(t)}\partial_{t}\partial_{\bar{t}}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Sigma
.

3
Complex-regularized polysymplectic geometry

Recall that the main example in CRPS geometry was the cotangent bundle of a complex manifold
(Q,i^{\prime})
. The tautological 1-form on
T^{*}Q
is defined by
(\theta_{1})_{\nu}=\nu(\text{pr}_{*}\cdot)
for
\nu\in T^{*}Q
and
\text{pr}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T^{*}Q\to Q
the projection. Using the complex structure, we can naturally define a second 1-form given by
(\theta_{2})_{\nu}=\nu(i^{\prime}\circ\text{pr}_{*}\cdot)
. Recall that the CRPS forms are defined by
\omega_{1}=d\theta_{1}
and
\omega_{2}=-d\theta_{2}
. We want to show that when
\Sigma=\mathbb{C}
and
Y=\mathbb{C}\times Q
is the trivial bundle with the product complex structure, the CRMS construction above recovers these two forms on
T^{*}Q
.

Let
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y=\mathbb{C}\times Q\to\mathbb{C}
be the trivial bundle. We denote by
j
the standard complex structure on
\mathbb{C}
and by
i=j\oplus i^{\prime}
the product complex structure on
Y
. Also denote by
\pi_{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\to Q
the canonical projection and by
\iota_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\to Y
the map
q\mapsto(t,q)
. As is proven in
[
DLSV15
]
, there is a diffeomorphism
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times\left(T^{*}Q\oplus T^{*}Q\right)
, given by

\Psi(\lambda_{(t,q)})=(t,\lambda_{(t,q)}(\partial_{1},\partial_{2}),\nu_{1}(\lambda)_{q},\nu_{2}(\lambda)_{q}),

where

\displaystyle\nu_{1}(\lambda)_{q}(V)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{(t,q)}((\iota_{t})_{*}V,\partial_{2})

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(\lambda)_{q}(V)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{(t,q)}(\partial_{1},(\iota_{t})_{*}V),

for
V\in T_{q}Q
. The inverse is given by

\Psi^{-1}(t,p,\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+(\pi_{Q})^{*}\nu_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-(\pi_{Q})^{*}\nu_{2}\wedge dt_{1}.

We can restrict
\Psi
to
\Lambda^{C}(Y)\subseteq\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
. For
\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)
, denote
\lambda^{i}=\lambda(i\cdot,i\cdot)
. Given
\lambda\in\Lambda^{C}(Y)
, we know
\Psi(\lambda)=\Psi(\lambda^{i})
, which yields

\displaystyle\nu_{1}(\lambda)(V)=\nu_{1}(\lambda^{i})(V)=\lambda(\partial_{1},i(\iota_{t})_{*}V)=\lambda(\partial_{1},(\iota_{t})_{*}(i^{\prime}V))=\nu_{2}(\lambda)(i^{\prime}V),

so that
\nu_{1}(\lambda)=\nu_{2}(\lambda)(i^{\prime}\cdot)
or equivalently
\nu_{2}(\lambda)=-\nu_{1}(\lambda)(i^{\prime}\cdot)
. Vice versa, given
\nu_{2}=-\nu_{1}(i^{\prime}\cdot)\in T^{*}Q
, we get

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}(t,p,\nu_{1},\nu_{2})(i\cdot,i\cdot)

\displaystyle=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+(\pi_{Q})^{*}(\nu_{1}(i^{\prime}\cdot))\wedge dt_{1}+(\pi_{Q})^{*}(\nu_{2}(i^{\prime}\cdot))\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-(\pi_{Q})^{*}\nu_{2}\wedge dt_{1}+(\pi_{Q})^{*}\nu_{1}\wedge dt_{2}

\displaystyle=\Psi^{-1}(t,p,\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

We conclude that the image of
\Psi
restricted to
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
is
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\in T^{*}Q\oplus T^{*}Q\mid\nu_{2}=-\nu_{1}(i^{\prime}\cdot)\}
. Note that the last term is diffeomorphic to
T^{*}Q
. We get a diffeomorphism
\Psi^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda^{C}(Y)\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}Q
given by

\displaystyle\Psi^{C}(\lambda_{(t,q)})

\displaystyle=(t,\lambda_{(t,q)}(\partial_{1},\partial_{2}),\nu_{1}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=(t,\lambda_{(t,q)}(\partial_{1},\partial_{2}),\nu_{2}(\lambda)(i^{\prime}\cdot))

\displaystyle(\Psi^{C})^{-1}(t,p,\nu)

\displaystyle=pdt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+(\pi_{Q})^{*}\nu\wedge dt_{2}+(\pi_{Q})^{*}(\nu(i^{\prime}\cdot))\wedge dt_{1}

Using this diffeomorphism, we can transfer the 2-form
\Theta
from
\Lambda^{C}(Y)
to
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}Q
. We also notice that
\Lambda^{C}(Y)\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\cong\mathbb{C}\times T^{*}Q
is now the trivial bundle, so we can pullback
\Theta
to
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
along the zero section. This yields a 2-form
\Theta^{0}
on
\mathbb{C}\times T^{*}Q
. Using the formulas for
\Psi^{C}
above, a straightforward but lengthy diagram chase shows that

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\lrcorner(\partial_{2}\lrcorner\Theta^{0})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(\tau_{t})^{*}(\partial_{1}\lrcorner\Theta^{0})

\displaystyle=-\theta_{2}

\displaystyle(\tau_{t})^{*}(\partial_{2}\lrcorner\Theta^{0})

\displaystyle=-\theta_{1},

where
\tau_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T^{*}Q\to\mathbb{C}\times T^{*}Q
is the map
\nu\mapsto(t,\nu)
. Thus,
d\Theta^{0}=\omega_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-\omega_{2}\wedge dt_{1}
on
\mathbb{C}\times T^{*}Q
, which can be identified with the contraction of the polysymplectic form
\omega_{1}\otimes\partial_{1}+\omega_{2}\otimes\partial_{2}
with the volume form
dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}
on
\mathbb{C}
.

Note that the form
\Theta^{0}
is precisely the 2-form that was integrated in
[
BF24
]
to define the action functional. Thus the action functional for sections of
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\to\Sigma
defined in
equation

5
coincides with the action functional in the CRPS framework when
Y=\mathbb{C}\times Q
is the trivial bundle over
\mathbb{C}
. Since
\Lambda^{C}(Y)\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
is now a trivial bundle, Hamiltonian sections
h
correspond globally to Hamiltonian functions
H
and
\Omega^{h}=-dH\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}+\omega_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-\omega_{2}\wedge dt_{1}
. One can compare this to the 2-form
-dH\wedge dt+\omega
on the mapping torus of a symplectic manifold.

4
Complex-regularized multisymplectic geometry

Recall that we are mainly interested in the case where
Y=\Sigma\times Q
is the trivial bundle, since we want to study maps
\Sigma\to Q
. In this case, we want to show some properties of
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
that will lead us to the general definition of a CRMS form on a bundle. We start by proving that
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
is a complex manifold.

Let
(\Sigma,j)
a Riemann surface,
(Q,i^{\prime})
a complex manifold, and define
Y=\Sigma\times Q
with the product complex structure. We cover
\Sigma
with charts
\psi_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}U_{\alpha}\to\psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, such that all transition maps
\psi_{\alpha\beta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\psi_{\beta}\circ\psi_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta})\to\psi_{\beta}(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta})
are biholomorphic. This means that if
t=t_{1}+it_{2}
denotes a local coordinate on
\psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})
, that
\bar{\partial}_{t}\psi_{\alpha\beta}=0
, where
\bar{\partial}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t_{1}}+i\partial_{t_{2}})
is the antiholomorphic derivative. Also, locally
j
is given by
j\partial_{t_{1}}=\partial_{t_{2}}
.
Similarly, we cover
Q
with charts
\varphi_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V_{\gamma}\to\varphi_{\gamma}(V_{\gamma})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, such that transition maps are biholomorphic. This induces product charts
\rho_{\alpha,\gamma}=\psi_{\alpha}\times\varphi_{\gamma}
on
Y
.

Pick an auxiliary metric
h
on
\Sigma
within the conformal class determined by
j
. This means that there are positive functions
\rho_{\alpha}
such that on
\psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})
the pull-back of the metric is given by

h_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\psi_{\alpha}^{-1})^{*}h=\rho_{\alpha}(t)dt\otimes d\bar{t}=\rho_{\alpha}(t)(dt_{1}^{2}+dt_{2}^{2}).

This means that on overlapping charts
\rho_{\alpha}(t)=\rho_{\beta}(\psi_{\alpha\beta}(t))|\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha\beta}(t)|^{2}
. The induced volume form
dV
on
\Sigma
is locally given by
\rho_{\alpha}(t)dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}=\frac{i}{2}\rho_{\alpha}(t)dt\wedge d\bar{t}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The manifold
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(Y)
carries the structure of a complex manifold, such that the projection
\sigma^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(Y)\to\Sigma
is holomorphic.

Proof.

The charts of
Y
induce charts
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(U_{\alpha}\times V_{\gamma})\to\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(\rho_{\alpha,\gamma}(U_{\alpha}\times V_{\gamma}))\subseteq\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{*}
for
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(Y)
whose elements are given by

\sum_{a}\left((P_{1}^{a}dq_{1}^{a}+P_{2}^{a}dq_{2}^{a})\wedge\rho_{\alpha}(t)dt_{2}-(P_{1}^{a}dq_{2}^{a}-P_{2}^{a}dq_{1}^{a})\wedge\rho_{\alpha}(t)dt_{1}\right)

with respect to local coordinates
t=t_{1}+it_{2}
on
\Sigma
and
q^{a}=q^{a}_{1}+iq^{a}_{2}
on
Q
. To determine whether
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(Y)
is a complex manifold, we need to calculate the transition functions.

Let
w=w_{1}+iw_{2}
denote a local coordinate on
\Sigma
in the chart
U_{\beta}
and
r^{a}=r^{a}_{1}+ir^{a}_{2}
in the chart
V_{\delta}
of
Q
. We denote elements of
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(U_{\beta}\times V_{\delta})
by

\sum_{a}\left((S_{1}^{a}dr_{1}^{a}+S_{2}^{a}dr_{2}^{a})\wedge\rho_{\beta}(w)dw_{2}-(S_{1}^{a}dr_{2}^{a}-S_{2}^{a}dr_{1}^{a})\wedge\rho_{\beta}(w)dw_{1}\right).

A straightforward computation, using the fact that
\psi_{\alpha\beta}
and
\varphi_{\gamma\delta}
are holomorphic, yields that

S_{1}^{a}-iS_{2}^{a}=\frac{\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial_{q^{a}}\varphi_{\gamma\delta}}(P_{1}^{a}-iP_{2}^{a}).

As this is a holomorphic map, we see that the transition function is a holomorphic map between subsets of
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{*}
, where we recall that
i^{*}=-i
. This proves that
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}}(Y)
is a complex manifold.
∎

For the bundle
\sigma^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\to\Sigma
, we denote
V^{C}=\ker d\sigma^{C}
the vertical bundle.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any complex-regularized Hamiltonian section
h
, the resulting CRMS form
\Omega^{h}
on
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
satisfies

i)

d\Omega^{h}=0
,

ii)

\Omega^{h}(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=0
for any
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V^{C}_{\lambda}
and
\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
,

iii)

for any
\xi\in T_{\lambda}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
with
d\sigma^{C}(\xi)\neq 0
, we have that
\Omega^{h}(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)
restricts to a non-degenerate bilinear form on
V^{C}_{\lambda}
,

iv)

for any
\xi\in T_{\lambda}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in V^{C}_{\lambda}
we have that

\Omega^{h}(I\xi,v_{1},v_{2})=-\Omega^{h}(\xi,v_{1},Iv_{2}),

where
I
is the complex structure on
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
.

Proof.

The first property follows trivially from
\Omega^{h}=d\Theta^{h}
. The other properties can be easily checked in local coordinates. Note that in the local coordinates introduced in
proposition

4.1
,
V^{C}_{\lambda}
is spanned by
\{\partial_{q_{1}^{a}},\partial_{q_{2}^{a}}\partial_{P_{1}^{a}},\partial_{P_{2}^{a}}\}
, where
I\partial_{q_{1}^{a}}=\partial_{q_{2}^{a}}
and
I\partial_{P_{1}^{a}}=-\partial_{P_{2}^{a}}
. Take
\xi\in T_{\lambda}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
given by

\xi=\frac{\xi_{t_{1}}}{\rho_{\alpha}(t)}\partial_{t_{1}}+\frac{\xi_{t_{2}}}{\rho_{\alpha}(t)}\partial_{t_{2}}+\sum_{a}\left(\xi_{q_{1}^{a}}\partial_{q_{1}^{a}}+\xi_{q_{2}^{a}}\partial_{q_{2}^{a}}+\xi_{P_{1}^{a}}\partial_{P_{1}^{a}}+\xi_{P_{2}^{a}}\partial_{P_{2}^{a}}\right),

and define
\omega^{\xi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Omega^{h}(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)|_{V^{C}_{\lambda}}
as a 2-form on
V^{C}_{\lambda}
.
Then

\displaystyle\omega^{\xi}=\xi_{t_{2}}\sum_{a}\left(dP_{1}^{a}\wedge dq_{1}^{a}+dP_{2}^{a}\wedge dq_{2}^{a}\right)-\xi_{t_{1}}\sum_{a}\left(dP_{1}^{a}\wedge dq_{2}^{a}-dP_{2}^{a}\wedge dq_{1}^{a}\right).

First of all, if
\xi\in V^{C}_{\lambda}
, then
\xi_{t_{1}}=\xi_{t_{2}}=0
, which proves ii). We write
\omega^{\xi}=\xi_{t_{2}}\omega_{1}-\xi_{t_{1}}\omega_{2}
, for 2-forms
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
on
V^{C}_{\lambda}
. Then an easy check shows
\omega_{2}=-\omega_{1}(\cdot,I\cdot)
. Combined with
\omega^{I\xi}=\xi_{t_{1}}\omega_{1}+\xi_{t_{2}}\omega_{2}
, this gives iv). Also, this implies

\omega^{\xi}=\omega_{1}(\cdot,(\xi_{t_{2}}\textup{Id}+\xi_{t_{1}}I)\cdot),

and since
\xi_{t_{2}}\textup{Id}+\xi_{t_{1}}I
is a non-degenerate matrix whenever
(\xi_{t_{1}},\xi_{t_{2}})\neq(0,0)
, we see that indeed
\omega^{\xi}
is non-degenerate on
V^{C}_{\lambda}
whenever
d\sigma^{C}(\xi)\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We note that
\sigma^{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\to\Sigma
is in general not a trivial bundle, even when
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\to\Sigma
is trivial. For example, let
Q=\mathbb{C}
. Let

\displaystyle\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{\Lambda^{2}_{2}(Y)}

\displaystyle\to Q\times(T^{*}\Sigma)^{\oplus 2}

\displaystyle(t,q,\lambda)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(q,t,\lambda\left(\partial_{q_{1}},\cdot\right),\lambda\left(\partial_{q_{2}},\cdot\right)\right)

with inverse
(q,t,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\mapsto(t,q,\eta_{1}\wedge dq_{1}+\eta_{2}\wedge dq_{2})
. This is a bundle isomorphism. It restricts to an isomorphism
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\overset{\sim}{\to}Q\times T^{*}\Sigma
, where
T^{*}\Sigma
sits in
(T^{*}\Sigma)^{\oplus 2}
by
(t,\eta)\mapsto(t,\eta\circ j,\eta)
. So
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}\to\Sigma
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}\times T^{*}\Sigma\to\Sigma
, which is a non-trivial bundle if
\Sigma\neq\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

5
A general definition

In this paragraph, we will define the concept of
complex-regularized multisymplectic
(CRMS) bundles, inspired by the discussion in the previous paragraphs. The spaces
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
for
Y=\Sigma\times Q
will form the main examples of CRMS bundles and we will prove a Darboux-type theorem.

For a fiber bundle
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\Sigma
, we denote
V=\ker d\sigma\subseteq TW
the vertical bundle and
V_{w}
the fiber over
w\in W
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\Sigma
be a fiber bundle, where
(W,I)
is a complex manifold,
(\Sigma,j)
is a Riemann surface, and
\sigma
is holomorphic. A 3-form
\Omega
on
W
is called
CRMS
if it satisfies the following conditions.

i)

d\Omega=0
;

ii)

\Omega
is 1-horizontal, meaning that
\Omega(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=0
for any
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V_{w}
, for all
w
;

iii)

\Omega
is fiberwise non-degenerate, meaning that for any
\xi\in T_{w}W
with
d\sigma(\xi)\neq 0
, we have that
\Omega(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)
is a non-degenerate bilinear form when restricted to
V_{w}
;

iv)

\Omega
is
I
-compatible, in the sense that

\Omega(I\xi,v_{1},v_{2})=-\Omega(\xi,v_{1},Iv_{2})

for all
\xi\in T_{w}W
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{w}
.

Note that CRMS forms are in particular multisymplectic, in the sense that they are closed and 1-nondegenerate (see
[
DLSV15
]
).

Remark 5.2
.

a.

We do not require
W\to\Sigma
to be a holomorphic fiber bundle. That is, even locally, the complex structure on
W
does not have to be the product of the complex structures on
\Sigma
and the fiber. Instead, in a local trivialization
S\times F
, where
S\subseteq\Sigma
and
F
the fiber,
I
looks like

\displaystyle I=\begin{pmatrix}j&0\\
A&I^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

for a complex structure
I^{\prime}
on
F
and a map
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}TS\to TF
such that
A\circ j=I^{\prime}\circ A
.

b.

For
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in T_{w}W
with
d\sigma(\xi)=d\sigma(\xi^{\prime})
, we have
\Omega(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)|_{V_{w}}=\Omega(\xi^{\prime},\cdot,\cdot)|_{V_{w}}
, by condition
ii)
. Thus, the
I
-compatibility condition does not restrict the part of
I
denoted by
A
in remark
a.

We will now proceed to formulate a Darboux-type theorem for CRMS bundles. Note that we cannot expect a full Darboux theorem. The definition states that
\Omega
is fiberwise non-degenerate, but does not restrict the action of
\Omega
on only one vertical vector. For example, the forms
\Omega^{h}
on
\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
defined in
section

2
, locally look like

\Omega^{h}=\omega_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-\omega_{2}\wedge dt_{1}-dH\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2},

where
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are the standard CRPS forms on the fibers. Even locally, we cannot get rid of the
dH\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}
term. What is true however, is that the action of
\Omega
on at least two vertical vectors is locally standard. This is the best we can hope for. One should compare this situation to the mapping tori from symplectic geometry. They are described by bundles
M\to S^{1}
, equipped with a 2-form
\omega_{M}=\omega-dH\wedge dt
, where
\omega
is symplectic on the fibers. Also here, we cannot hope to get local coordinates in which
\omega_{M}
is standard, but we can however find local coordinates in which the action of
\omega_{M}
on the fibers is standard.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W\to\Sigma
as in
definition

5.1
with CRMS form
\Omega\in\Omega^{3}(W)
. Then around every point in
W
we can find an open
\mathbb{U}\subseteq W
and diffeomorphisms
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{U}\to S\times U
and
\chi=(t_{1},t_{2})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{S}=\sigma(\mathbb{U})\to S
, where
S\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2n}
open, such that

•

\text{pr}\circ\psi=\chi\circ\sigma
, where
\text{pr}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{2n}\to\mathbb{C}
the projection,

•

(\psi^{-1})^{*}\Omega=\Omega_{std}-dH_{t}\wedge dt_{1}\wedge dt_{2}
for some function
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\times U\to\mathbb{R}
,

•

\chi
is holomorphic,

•

d\psi\circ I|_{V}=i\circ d\psi|_{V}
, for
i
the standard complex structure on
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{2n}
.

Here,
\Omega_{std}=\omega_{1}\wedge dt_{2}-\omega_{2}\wedge dt_{1}
, where
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are the standard CRPS forms on
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
.

Before we prove
theorem

5.3
, we start with a linear version of the Darboux theorem.

Lemma 5.4
.

Given is a short exact sequence of complex vector spaces

0\to V\to(W,I)\overset{\sigma}{\to}(T,j)\to 0,

where
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}T=1
. Let
\Omega
an alternating multilinear 3-form on
W
that satisfies

i)

\Omega(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=0
for all
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V
,

ii)

\Omega(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)
is non-degenerate on
V
for all
\xi\in W
with
\sigma(\xi)\neq 0
,

iii)

\Omega(I\xi,v_{1},v_{2})=-\Omega(\xi,v_{1},Iv_{2})
for all
\xi\in W
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
.

Then there exists a real basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},a_{1}^{k},a_{2}^{k},b_{1}^{k},b_{2}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
of
W
, such that

•

T=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\sigma(e_{1}),\sigma(e_{2})\}
and
V=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{a_{1}^{k},a_{2}^{k},b_{1}^{k},b_{2}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
,

•

Ie_{1}=e_{2}
,
Ia_{1}^{k}=a_{2}^{k}
,
Ib_{1}^{k}=-b_{2}^{k}
,

•

for
\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\alpha_{1}^{k},\alpha_{2}^{k},\beta_{1}^{k},\beta_{2}^{k}\}
the dual basis, we have

\Omega=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left((\beta_{1}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{1}^{k}+\beta_{2}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{2}^{k})\wedge\epsilon_{2}-(\beta_{1}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{2}^{k}-\beta_{2}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{1}^{k})\wedge\epsilon_{1}\right)+\nu\wedge\epsilon_{1}\wedge\epsilon_{2},

for some 1-form
\nu
on
V
.

Proof.

Take a complex-linear splitting
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to W
of the short exact sequence and a real basis
\{\widetilde{e_{1}},\widetilde{e_{2}}=j\widetilde{e_{1}}\}
of
T
. Define
e_{1}=\rho(\widetilde{e_{1}})
and
e_{2}=\rho(\widetilde{e_{2}})
and
\omega_{1}=\Omega(e_{2},\cdot,\cdot)|_{V}
and
\omega_{2}=-\Omega(e_{1},\cdot,\cdot)|_{V}
. Then

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(\cdot,I\cdot)

\displaystyle=\Omega(e_{2},\cdot,I\cdot)|_{V}=-\Omega(Ie_{2},\cdot,\cdot)|_{V}=-\omega_{2}.

So
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
define a linear CRPS structure on
(V,I)
. By the Darboux theorem for linear CRPS forms, there exists a basis
\{a_{1}^{k},a_{2}^{k},b_{1}^{k},b_{2}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
of
V
such that
Ia_{1}^{k}=a_{2}^{k}
,
Ib_{1}^{k}=-b_{2}^{k}
and

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\beta_{1}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{1}^{k}+\beta_{2}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{2}^{k}\right),

\displaystyle\omega_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\beta_{1}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{2}^{k}-\beta_{2}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{1}^{k}\right).

7
Floer sections

Now that we have defined the class of manifolds for which the Hamiltonian equations locally look like the ones that we find in CRPS geometry, we want to setup the stage for defining Floer theory. In order to do that we have to define Floer curves as the gradient of the action functional with respect to a suitable metric. Just like in symplectic geometry, the metric has to somehow relate the multisymplectic form, to almost complex structures. For a CRMS form
\Omega
on
W
and
\xi\in T_{w}W
, we define
\omega^{\xi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Omega(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)|_{V_{w}}
. By definition of CRMS forms, this is a linear symplectic form on
V_{w}
, whenever
\xi
is not purely vertical.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(W,I,\Omega)\to(\Sigma,j)
be a CRMS bundle and fix a metric
h
on
\Sigma
within the conformal class of
j
. There exists an inner product
g
on the fibers of V and a bundle map
\mathfrak{J}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sigma^{*}T\Sigma\backslash\{0\}\to\text{End}(V)
, such that for all
\xi\in T_{w}W
with
\rho=d\sigma(\xi)\neq 0
, we have

(i)

\mathfrak{J}(\rho)^{2}=-|\rho|^{2}_{h}\textup{Id}_{V_{w}}
,

(ii)

\omega^{\xi}=g(\cdot,\mathfrak{J}(\rho)\cdot)
,

(iii)

\mathfrak{J}(j\rho)=I\mathfrak{J}(\rho)
.

Remark 7.2
.

In the CRPS framework, the analogue of
\mathfrak{J}
would be
Jdt_{2}-Kdt_{1}
. Conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
then translate to the fact that any linear combination of unit norm of

and
K
is an almost complex structure compatible with the corresponding linear combination of
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. Condition
(iii)
says that

.

]
. Just like there, the essence of the proposition lies in the local version. The global statement then follows from the fact that after an initial choice of inner product on V, the rest of the construction is canonical.

Thus, we fix

and an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
on the vector space

compatible with

. All transposes will be taken with respect to this inner product. Also fix

such that

is non-zero. By non-degeneracy of

we get a skew-symmetric linear isomorphism

, where

only depends on
\rho
by

.

In fact,

for any

so that

anticommutes with

.

Let

. From the skew-symmetry of

is skew-symmetric and

for

\Sigma
.

Now, in a positive local orthonormal frame
\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}=j\rho_{1}\}
of
T\Sigma
, the integrand in
equation

7
is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(\dot{Z},dZ(\rho_{1}),dZ(\rho_{2}))d\mathcal{V}_{h},

and

\displaystyle\Omega(\dot{Z},dZ(\rho_{1}),dZ(\rho_{2}))

\displaystyle=\omega^{\rho_{2}}(\dot{Z},\nabla_{\rho_{1}}Z)-\omega^{\rho_{1}}(\dot{Z},\nabla_{\rho_{2}}Z)+\Omega(\dot{Z},\rho_{1},\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=g\left(\dot{Z},\mathfrak{J}(\rho_{2})\nabla_{\rho_{1}}Z-\mathfrak{J}(\rho_{1})\nabla_{\rho_{2}}Z\right)-g(\dot{Z},\alpha(Z)).

Here
\alpha(Z)\in V_{Z}
is defined by
g(\dot{Z},\alpha(Z))=-\Omega(\dot{Z},\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
for
\dot{Z}
vertical. Note that
\alpha(Z)
does not depend on the derivatives of
Z
.
We denote

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathfrak{J}(\rho)\left(-I\nabla_{\rho}-\nabla_{j\rho}\right)Z.

(8)

Thus, the gradient of
\mathcal{A}(Z)
is locally given by
\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z(\rho_{1})-\alpha(Z)
. We see that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z(j\rho)=-\mathfrak{J}(\rho)I(-I\nabla_{j\rho}+\nabla_{\rho})Z=\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z(\rho),

for any non-vanishing local vector field
\rho
. Note that
\{\rho,j\rho\}
locally spans
T\Sigma
, so that
\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z(\rho)
is only dependent on
|\rho|_{h}^{2}
. Thus, we may define
\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z(\rho_{1})
, which is independent of the chosen local orthonormal frame. We see that
\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}
is actually a globally well-defined operator on
\Gamma(\sigma)
, which is locally given by
equation

8
for any unit vector field
\rho
. Then globally, the gradient of
\mathcal{A}
is given by

\textup{grad}\,\mathcal{A}(Z)=\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z-\alpha(Z),

where the zero-order term
\alpha(Z)
has to be globally well-defined, since all other terms in the equation are.

As always, we may define Floer curves to be the negative gradient lines of
\mathcal{A}
. Denote
\text{pr}_{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma\to\Sigma
to be the projection. Then, Floer curves are given by sections of
\text{pr}_{\Sigma}^{*}W
satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla_{\partial_{s}}Z+\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z=\alpha(Z),

or equivalently

\displaystyle I\nabla_{\partial_{s}}Z+I\mathfrak{J}_{\nabla}Z=I\alpha(Z).

(9)

We show that this is a perturbed Fueter equation. Let
M=\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
with metric
ds^{2}+h
, for
s
the coordinate on
\mathbb{R}
. For
(\rho_{s},\rho)\in STM
, we define
\widetilde{I}(\rho_{s},\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\rho_{s}I+\mathfrak{J}(\rho)
. Then an easy check shows
\widetilde{I}(\rho_{s},\rho)
is an almost complex structure on the fiber of
\text{pr}^{*}_{\Sigma}W
and that
equation

9
is given by

\sum_{l=1}^{3}\widetilde{I}(v_{l})\nabla_{v_{l}}Z=I\alpha(Z),

in any local orthonormal frame
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}=\{\partial_{s},\rho,j\rho\}
of
M
. This is the Fueter equation as described in
[
Wal17
]
and is an elliptic equation.

The discussion above allows us to employ the elliptic methods that made symplectic geometry flourish since the 1980’s, in the setting of multisymplectic geometry. Indeed, in symplectic geometry pseudo-holomorphic curves and Floer theory are the main tools to prove results about symplectic manifolds and Hamiltonian flows on them.

In
[
Wal17
]
, the compactness theory for solutions to the Fueter equation is worked out and should guide the development of Floer theory for sections of
\sigma
, at least in the case where
\mathfrak{J}(\rho)
and
\mathfrak{J}(j\rho)
are integrable.

This line of reasoning was used in
[
BF24
]
to prove a cuplength result for perturbed harmonic maps
\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2n}
, when
\Sigma=\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
W=\Sigma\times T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{2n}
. Similar arguments should work for
W=\widetilde{\Lambda^{C}(Y)}
, where
Y=\mathbb{T}^{2n}\times\Sigma
and
\Sigma
is a general Riemann surface. We decided to leave the formal proof out of this paper in order to focus the reader’s attention on the additional geometric structures that arise when moving away from the torus to arbitrary Riemann surfaces.
