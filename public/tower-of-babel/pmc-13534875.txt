From 15,000 to near zero: Cuyahoga County's eight‐year effort to address lawfully “owed” DNA
Authors: Rachel E. Lovell, Mary C. Weston, Duoduo Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the final outcomes of an eight‐year, countywide initiative to address “lawfully owed” DNA (LODNA) among individuals who should have had a DNA sample in the Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) following a qualifying arrest or conviction but did not. Building on prior research in this jurisdiction, this study evaluates the effectiveness of policies and practices in identifying, collecting, and uploading LODNA samples to CODIS. Using a comprehensive list of 14,920 individuals confirmed to have LODNA (“census”), we assessed outcomes, lessons learned, and remaining issues in the DNA sample collection process. By the end of the funded initiative, 25.5% (n = 3802) of “census members” (the 14,920 individuals on our census) were confirmed to be in CODIS, including 641 collected directly through an enacted prosecutor‐initiated arraignment policy. DNA sample collection improved substantially over time. Results indicated that missed collections were reduced from an estimated 20–50% prior to the initiative to 2.2% in 2021 (1 in 45) and to 0.2% in 2023 (1 in 455) for probationers—a near‐elimination of missed DNA samples. Remaining challenges include deceased individuals and those residing out of state (an estimated 21.5% of those who “owed”), and collection efforts from census members who are registered sex offenders. Findings also document how this jurisdiction achieved such a dramatic reduction in missed DNA samples, highlight lessons learned and recommendations in implementing large‐scale reforms, and provide a framework for jurisdictions seeking to strengthen LODNA sample collection practices.

INTRODUCTION

The FBI's (U.S.) Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) is a software platform developed and maintained to match DNA profiles from all 50 states in the National DNA Index System, including those from crime scenes, convicted offenders, arrestees, and missing persons in US jurisdictions. With approximately 26 million DNA profiles, it is a powerful law enforcement tool, as profiles remain in the database beyond the specific crime to assist in solving past and current cases and to potentially prevent future crimes [1]. Besides helping solve crimes by identifying potential suspects (known as cold hits), CODIS hits play a vital role in conviction integrity by verifying the identities of individuals who have already been adjudicated or named as suspects (the latter known as warm hits) and/or eliminating suspects [2].

Missed DNA samples or “lawfully owed” DNA (LODNA) samples are DNA samples from individuals that “owe” DNA, meaning by law, should have been collected and entered into CODIS based on a qualifying offense, but were not. LODNA samples have been identified as a problem across forensic laboratories and law enforcement for decades [3]. Efforts to address large‐scale collection issues for LODNA have been carried out in several states, primarily in prisons, including Michigan [4] and Ohio [5]. All 50 US states participate in CODIS [6]. However, there are substantial variations among states' DNA collection laws and procedures [7], and the extent to which states ensure DNA is collected in accordance with state law influences the size of their DNA databases, which in turn affects the size of the federal DNA database. LODNA sample collections have significant public safety consequences. When LODNA DNA samples are not collected and/or entered into CODIS, crimes remain unsolved, and offenders remain free. Case solvability improves when there are more offender profiles in CODIS [8]. Variation in DNA collection laws among states also underscores the complexity of obtaining DNA samples from individuals who should have a DNA sample in CODIS based on a qualifying arrest or conviction [8, 9]. Greater legal complexity increases the likelihood of DNA sample collection issues, or “misses,” where individuals required to provide DNA did not [8].

For nearly 10 years, one federal program has worked to tackle LODNA issues, the Bureau of Justice Assistance's Sexual Assault Kit Initiative (SAKI), part of the US Department of Justice. Since 2015, SAKI has allocated hundreds of millions of dollars (and counting) to jurisdictions to resolve their unsubmitted or untested SAKs (often referred to as “backlogged”). A SAK comprises items used by medical professionals to gather and preserve evidence, such as vaginal swabs and fingernail clippings, from sexual assault victims. In 2016, SAKI established the LODNA purpose area, recognizing that uploading more LODNA profiles strengthens the probative value of CODIS, thereby increasing the value of testing SAKs by increasing the likelihood of DNA matches [8, 9]. When LODNA samples are not collected and/or uploaded, this results in missed opportunities to add potentially hundreds of thousands of profiles linked to individuals to CODIS, some of which could connect to the now‐tested SAKs [9, 10, 11]. In 2016, the Sexual Assault Kit (SAK) Task Force (Task Force), led by the Cuyahoga County Prosecutor's Office (CCPO), received the first LODNA grant from SAKI to address LODNA collection issues after discovering significant gaps in DNA collections from suspected sexual assault offenders associated with the now tested SAKs who should have been in CODIS but were not. For more information on these notable misses, see [10].

As Cuyahoga County was SAKI's first LODNA grantee, there was little existing guidance on the expected outcomes of a LODNA initiative and on how to engender and sustain lasting change in large‐scale DNA collection issues outside the prison system. As detailed in a previous publication on LODNA in this jurisdiction, the Task Force collaborated with a team of researchers to detail the Task Force's processes in: (a) conducting a census (a list of individuals who lawfully should have had a DNA sample collected due to a qualifying offense but were not in CODIS); (b) collecting DNA samples from those identified in the census and submitting them for testing; and (c) following up on any resulting CODIS offender hits (DNA sample‐to‐known‐person matches that provide an investigated lead that is tied to an individual) [10]. Additionally, the previous publication detailed the outcomes of the above‐mentioned process as of 2022, namely, the number of individuals on the census; the number of swabs collected and submitted; and the number, type, and investigative outcomes of the resulting offender hits [10].

The Task Force was awarded LODNA funds again in 2019. As part of the 2019 award, CCPO and the collaborating research team proposed conducting a follow‐up evaluation to assess current DNA collection practices in the jurisdiction. The current study is derived from the 2019 award deliverables, expanding beyond the outcomes of collected LODNA samples from census members (individuals who were confirmed to lawfully owe DNA samples), to assess how well the jurisdiction was doing in addressing the issue of LODNA, ensuring LODNA samples were collected for new arrestees in accordance with state law, and how well policies enacted to address LODNA were working. Using eight years of data (2016–2024) from Cuyahoga County, this study provides a comprehensive summary of the completion of our funded initiative to address LODNA and builds on our prior publication [10] by evaluating how large‐scale LODNA reform can be sustained over time and institutionalized within routine criminal justice practice. More specifically, we assess final outcomes, identify mechanisms of successful policy implementation, and document remaining structural barriers. In doing so, this study provides the first longitudinal evaluation of a county‐led, non‐prison‐based response to LODNA.

BACKGROUND

LODNA efforts in other jurisdictions

This county is not unique in this large‐scale failure to collect DNA samples. LODNA is a widespread problem [3, 11, 12, 13]. As of 2024, SAKI's LODNA initiative alone has identified approximately 220,000 individuals who owe DNA samples [9]. Through SAKI LODNA funding, the Texas Department of Public Safety, Texas Ranger Division, identified more than 43,000 individuals who owed DNA samples between 1995 and 2020. After four years of collecting LODNA samples, approximately 12% of those owed had a DNA sample in CODIS [11]. In 2021, Alaska reported 22,000 LODNA samples. After one year of collecting LODNA samples, 425 of those owed had a DNA sample in CODIS [13]. As was the case in this study's jurisdiction [5, 10], in Texas, one reason for missed DNA sample collections was that law enforcement agencies assumed other agencies had collected the DNA and had limited ability to confirm whether it had been collected [11]. For example, prison systems did not know, or were unable to confirm, whether DNA sample collection occurred earlier (at arrest, pre‐trial, or sentencing) [3, 10]. In Idaho, a recent survey of police and sheriff's agencies found that many respondents reported confusion about which agencies were responsible for collecting DNA samples. Police departments reported believing that sheriff's offices were collecting DNA samples, but few county‐level sheriff's offices were doing so, likely resulting in many missed DNA sample collections [14]. The report authors conclude based on the survey results that Idaho has a “fragmented and unguided approach to collecting owed DNA. There does not appear to be an agreed‐upon, publicized recommendation or best practice for when and who is collecting owed DNA in the state (p. 9)” [14]. Understanding the state's statutory framework is essential to evaluating why LODNA accumulated in this county and which reforms were legally feasible. Below, we detail Ohio's statutory framework for DNA sample collection.

LODNA efforts in Cuyahoga County

Under Ohio Senate Bill 5 (effective 1996), the state began collecting DNA samples from individuals convicted of felonies for inclusion in CODIS. This law was significantly expanded in 2010 by Senate Bill 77, which mandated the collection of DNA samples from those arrested for felonies. Starting July 1, 2011, Ohio's DNA collection statute (§2901.07) requires DNA samples from individuals arrested for or convicted of any felony, as well as convicted of certain misdemeanors (the latter, mainly related to sexual offenses against children) [15]. For individuals aged 18 or older (if a DNA sample was not already collected), DNA samples can be taken at booking following an arrest, during jail or prison intake, at sentencing, at post‐conviction hearings, or while under community supervision (probation, parole). However, §2901.07 does not permit the collection of DNA samples from individuals not currently under the criminal justice system's supervision [15, 16]. This means that if a DNA sample was not lawfully collected while an individual was under the supervision of the criminal justice system, it cannot be collected unless and until the individual is again under supervision. In the subsequent sections, we summarize the LODNA initiative in this jurisdiction and the policies enacted in its wake. This policy is important for understanding how we assessed large‐scale reform in DNA sample collections and the jurisdiction's current practices.

Regarding the scope of the issue in this jurisdiction, an estimated 20–50% of felony arrestees from the two largest law enforcement agencies in Cuyahoga County (the Cleveland Police Department or Sheriff's Department) did not have a DNA sample collected in the 7 years prior to the start of the LODNA initiative, resulting in a census that consisted of 15,370 unique individuals who as of the end of 2016 were either arrested for and/or convicted of felonies in the Cuyahoga County's Court of Common Pleas, and thus should have a DNA sample in CODIS but did not. The census was later adjusted to 14,920 after duplicate individuals were identified and removed [10]. Early in the initiative, while conducting the census and noted in the prior study [10], 623 DNA samples were actively collected—the result of the Task Force requesting “sweeps” of jail inmates (n = 77) and county probationers (n = 546) who were still under court supervision. These sweeps were conducted to collect DNA samples from those who owed and would most likely still be under supervision.

Cuyahoga County Prosecutor's Office's policy regarding DNA sample collection of arrestees for DNA submission to CODIS

Given the large number of individuals who owed a DNA sample in the jurisdiction, as a direct result of this LODNA initiative, the felony‐only Cuyahoga County Prosecutor's Office (CCPO) developed and implemented an internal policy in 2017 that requires prosecutors to perform several tasks regarding DNA sample collections while defendants are under the supervision of the court.

At arraignment (most often a defendant's first court appearance, where the court ensures a defendant has an attorney, determines bail or keeps the previous bail, and a judge is assigned to the case), prosecutors must ensure that a DNA sample is obtained from defendants who had not had a DNA sample collected but were lawfully required to provide one, and to confirm that the DNA samples were entered into CODIS. Prosecutors are also required to check a defendant's criminal history to determine whether the defendant already has a DNA sample in the Ohio DNA Databank via the “DNA statement” in the Ohio Law Enforcement Gateway (OHLEG), and if not, request the Court order a DNA sample be collected by the Sheriff's Department as a condition of bond/jail. Ohio's DNA statement, displayed next to an individual's name in their computerized criminal history (CCH) in OHLEG, reads: “A DNA profile is in the Ohio DNA Offender Databank (CODIS)” or “A DNA profile is not in the Ohio DNA Offender Databank (CODIS).” The Ohio DNA statement applies to any qualifying DNA sample from an arrestee or convicted offender collected in Ohio that was successfully processed, provided it is associated with a valid state ID number (a unique ID associated with a person's CCH in Ohio).

During pre‐trial, prior to obtaining approval for a plea offer from a supervisor and acceptance of a plea, prosecutors must determine whether a DNA sample had already been ordered to be collected and if the defendant's DNA is in CODIS. If not, prosecutors must request that the court order the defendant to submit to a DNA sample collection procedure within 24 h.

At sentencing for felonies (and certain misdemeanors), prosecutors must request that the judge determine whether the defendant has submitted a DNA sample, and if not, order its collection.

At post‐conviction/sentencing or probation violation hearing, prosecutors must determine whether the defendant's DNA is in CODIS. If it is not, prosecutors must motion the court for an order to obtain a DNA sample before any court determination.

The policy also requires additional tasks of the office's professional staff and applicable prosecutors outside the courtroom: (a) intake personnel are to determine whether a defendant has a DNA sample in CODIS or owes DNA, and to note this in CCPO's electronic case management system; and (b) prosecutors are to note in CCPO's electronic case management system whether the defendant's DNA is in CODIS prior to closing the case.

METHODS

Determining whether census members had DNA in CODIS and were registered sex offenders

As the state's CODIS laboratory is managed/supervised by Ohio Attorney General's Office (Ohio AGO), starting in mid‐2018, the Ohio AGO provided the Task Force, led by CCPO (which handles only felony‐level cases), with quarterly updates on whether census members had a DNA sample in CODIS because of a new charge or because a criminal justice entity in Ohio lawfully collected a DNA sample. This process entailed the Task Force providing the Ohio AGO a list of individuals associated with felonies, extracted from its electronic case management system. This list included identifying information (name, date of birth, Social Security Number (SSN), and state ID number) for the individuals. The Ohio AGO then provided the Task Force with information indicating whether a DNA sample was confirmed to be in CODIS. Those who were not successful merged based on the combination of identifying information were labeled as “unverified.” CCPO integrated the ability to denote and update defendants' DNA statuses into their electronic case management system, which could trigger alert notifications for assigned prosecutors when “DNA was owed” for a defendant in their case. These quarterly updates served as the basis for census members confirmed to be in CODIS. The majority of census members confirmed in CODIS as a result of this initiative were identified via these updates.

A similar process was followed to determine whether census members were currently or formerly on the sex offender registry. The Task Force provided the Ohio AGO with a list of all census members, which was merged with the Ohio sex offender registry (managed by the Ohio AGO). The Ohio AGO provided the Task Force with information indicating whether census members were currently or formerly on the registry. Those not successfully merged based on the combination of identifying information were identified as unverified.

Arraignment emails and observations

The number of census members with DNA samples collected as a direct result of CCPO policy regarding DNA sample collection at a defendant's first court appearance (most often an arraignment hearing) was determined using the following processes. The Task Force forwarded nearly daily emails from the arraignment prosecutor to the researchers, requesting DNA sample collections from individuals without a DNA sample in CODIS who were scheduled to appear at an upcoming arraignment hearing, most often the following day. The emails included the defendants' names and case numbers. The research team entered this information into the database. Many had to be manually searched in the online docket to determine whether the names listed in the arraignment emails matched those in the census. The process produced the “arraignment email list.” For context, in 2022, Cuyahoga County had approximately 12,000 felony cases, averaging more than 40 cases set for arraignment per day [17].

To further assess the effectiveness of the arraignment hearing DNA sample collection policy, the lead researcher on the team (first author) and the prosecutor supervising this initiative on this Task Force observed DNA sample collection procedures in the arraignment courtroom and at booking in the county jail in July 2023. At the time of the observations, the Sheriff's Department was responsible for the city and county jails and thus responsible for DNA sample collection at the arraignment hearings and at intake at the county jail. We observed approximately 15 arraignments. The arraignment process for each case lasted approximately 2–3 min. All felony arraignments were conducted in the same courtroom.

“Most serious” offending census members

To more actively obtain DNA samples from census members, the Task Force also investigated census members who were: (a) included on the census in connection with Felony 1 offenses and/or sexually based offenses (approximately 6.7% of the census members or 1000 individuals) and (b), as of 2022, owed a DNA sample (approximately 12,000 individuals). 801 census members met these two criteria. The logic for examining census members linked to the most serious and/or sexually‐based offenses was two‐fold. First, we sought to determine whether census members who committed the “most serious offenses” could have a DNA sample collected as a result of a new felony offense. Second, these census members potentially posed the greatest risk to the community and/or the greatest potential to link to a sexual assault kit and/or sexual offenses. We collected data on whether they: (a) had a current charge, (b) were deceased, and (c) had a last known address out of state.

Probation audits

To assess improvements in DNA sample collections, specifically the effectiveness of CCPO's DNA sample collection protocol, we conducted two probation audits. Probation audits were lists of those on probation via the felony court and, therefore, presumably should have had a DNA sample collected. In the vast majority of cases, a person's presence on the probation list indicated a missed DNA sample collection at multiple points in the criminal justice system (e.g., arrest, arraignment, pretrial, sentencing). The audits were conducted to: (a) determine if DNA sample collection was occurring and, if not, why, and (b) follow up with prosecutors and/or their supervisors in the office to ensure DNA sample collection occurred before the defendant's case was adjudicated; and (c) spur conversations about ensuring prosecutors' fidelity to the policy. Because it can take some time for a DNA sample to appear in CODIS, in both audits, we checked how many individuals from the probation audit list had a DNA sample in CODIS in the following quarter. In the first audit, we collected information on which early stage(s) in the criminal justice process the individual experienced (arrested, arraignment, arrested and arraigned, solely arraigned, or solely arrested), whether a DNA sample had been ordered at arraignment, and information on the cases' offense level, crime type, and outcomes. In the second audit, we also assessed the stage(s) in the criminal justice process, focusing on whether individuals were still able to have a DNA sample collected (awaiting competency, pretrial) and, if not, why (i.e., had already been sentenced, or the defendant had failed to appear and a warrant had been issued). At the Task Force's request, these lists were provided by the Cuyahoga County Probation Department. The first audit was in September 2021. The second was in November 2023.

DNA recollection requests

To assess the efficacy of DNA sample collection by evaluating DNA recollection requests, the Task Force requested information from the Ohio AGO regarding DNA sample collections at arraignment or intake into jail by the Sheriff's Department, but for various reasons, they were unable to be entered into CODIS. For c. 18 months (January 2023 through June 2024; dates based on when the request for recollection was sent via email), we collected data from the Ohio AGO's email requests to the Sheriff's Department to resubmit DNA samples. We collected data from the state‐provided DNA sample collection paperwork that is required to be submitted in connection with an arrestee's DNA sample, such as the date of arrest, DNA sample collection, and data recollection requested; name of the deputy/officer who collected the DNA sample; demographics of the arrestee; qualifying offense; and the reason why a recollection was being requested.

RESULTS

Outcomes of the initiative

Census members who eventually had a DNA sample in CODIS

Regarding the first research question—the outcomes of this jurisdiction's LODNA initiative—at the end of the second grant award period (August 2024), 3802 (25.5%) of the 14,920 census members were confirmed to have a DNA sample in CODIS. This is likely an undercount of census members with a DNA sample in CODIS, as 1545 (10.4%) were unverified, meaning their identifying information (name, date of birth, SSN, and state ID number) could not be merged across the CCPO and Ohio AGO databases. This implied that 9572 (64.2%) still owed a DNA sample (missing = 1). Since Ohio law does not permit DNA sample collection from individuals who are not currently under supervision, most census members who were confirmed to have a DNA sample in CODIS by the end of the award period re‐entered the criminal justice system and had a DNA sample collected for the new offense (“new arrestee”).

Outcomes of the DNA sample collected at bail/arraignment from census members

From July 2017 through June 2024 (2545 days), a total of 746 census members were identified as eligible for sample collection at arraignment hearings for a new felony charge, not the charge that led to their inclusion on the census. They were eligible for DNA sample collection if they had an arraignment hearing for a new felony in the county and, as of the day(s) leading up to the hearing, their DNA sample was not in CODIS. In other words, by tracking arraignment emails over approximately 7 years, we found that at least 5% of census members acquired a new felony charge in the same county, providing a new opportunity for sample collection.

However, a request to appear at a hearing does not necessarily imply that a DNA sample was collected, the hearing occurred as planned, or the defendant appeared at the hearing as required. Of the 746 census members, 86% (n = 641) were confirmed to have a DNA sample in CODIS as of June 2024, 13% (n = 94) did not have a DNA sample in CODIS, and 1% (n = 11) did not have a confirmed DNA sample status. These 641 DNA samples were the direct result of the policy implemented in response to the LODNA initiative. The remaining census members were confirmed to be in CODIS for reasons other than this. As the initiative progressed, we determined that some were already in CODIS, but, due to errors in their identifying information (name, DOB, SSN, state ID number), did not appear to have a DNA sample in CODIS. Census members also had a DNA sample in CODIS for reasons we were unable to determine (e.g., a new charge in another Ohio county).

Additionally, as mentioned in the prior study, once confirmed in CODIS, the Ohio AGO provided the Task Force with the offender hits generated by the DNA sample's upload [10]. When offender hits were received, the Task Force contacted the assigned detective on the case, offering to take the lead in investigating the hit or to provide assistance. For investigations not led by the initiative, Task Force investigators followed up with the assigned detective to gather information on the status of pending cases. As previously reported, approximately 4% of newly collected DNA samples yielded an offender hit (a DNA sample‐to‐known‐person match that provided an investigative lead tied to an individual), and a quarter of those cases led to prosecution [10]. The Task Force did not lead any new investigations based on the offender hits received since the 2022 study because, as the initiative progressed, the number of “cold” or investigatory offender hits declined. Receiving fewer offender hits as the initiative progressed was a positive outcome, implying fewer misses by census members in new arrests. DNA samples were being collected, and the agency of record was investigating offender hits more contemporaneously, not a quarter to two later, when the Task Force requested information on the offender hits.

The Initiative's progress in collecting DNA samples

“Most serious” offending census members

Regarding the second research question, we assessed the lessons learned from the initiative's progress in DNA sample collection in four ways. The first was by examining the “most serious offenses” census members. Of the 801 offenders, 17 (2.1%) owed a DNA sample and had pending cases in the county. The majority of these individuals were capias (9 of 17; 52.9%), 3 (of 17; 17.6%) were awaiting competency determinations, 1 2 (of 17; 11.8%) had unknown statuses, 2 (of 17; 11.8%) had been true billed, and 1 (of 17; 5.9%) had a case in pretrial. Only 1 of the 17 was in custody at the time of data collection. By August 2024, of the 801 “most serious offenses” census members and/or those connected with sexually based offenses, 59 (7.4%) resulted in a DNA sample being entered into CODIS, 505 (63.0%) did not result in a DNA sample being entered into CODIS, and 237 (29.6%) were unverified.

Probation audits

The second way we assessed this research question was by conducting two probation audits, as detailed below. Probation audits enabled evaluation of the jurisdiction's DNA sample collection efforts, as the list included those who had progressed to adjudication for a new offense and were currently on probation.

First probation audit

The September 2021 probation audit was the first assessment of this jurisdiction's implementation of CCPO DNA sample collection policy. The first probation audit listed 6995 individuals—355 of whom did not have a DNA sample in CODIS but were on probation in the county. Of 355, 186 (52.4%) had a DNA sample in CODIS the following quarter, and 154 (43.3%) still owed a DNA sample the following quarter (15 had an unknown/not applicable DNA status). This implied that 2.2% (154 of 6995) of probationers, or 1 in 45, had missed DNA sample collections (Table 1).

Regarding the previously mentioned internal CCPO policy on DNA sample collection and missed collections at various stages in the criminal justice system, DNA was ordered at arraignment in 47% of cases (54 with missing/unknown information). According to the CCPO policy, prosecutors were to file motions for DNA sample collection if a defendant did not have a DNA sample in CODIS. Given that some of these defendants' DNA status changed to “in CODIS” during the pretrial process, motions for DNA sample collection were not necessary for all defendants without a DNA sample in CODIS; however, motions to collect DNA were filed in only 5% of cases (45 with missing/unknown information). Since fewer than half of this sample had a DNA sample in CODIS in the following quarter, this statistic suggests that prosecutors were not in the habit of regularly filing these motions at that point in the initiative, resulting in additional missed DNA sample collections.

Regarding case outcomes for these 355 individuals, at the time of data collection, 54.4% had their cases result in pleas, trials, or guilty verdicts; 3.1% had their cases dismissed (missed opportunities to collect DNA in both instances); 24.2% had their cases in pretrial; 3.7% had their cases in pretrial diversion; 2.3% had an “other” status such as awaiting arraignment or competency evaluation, found incompetent to stand trial, intervention in lieu of conviction, court supervised release (pretrial supervision of felony defendants on a financial or personal bond), etc.; and 12.4% had an unknown disposition status. Thus, 30.1% could have potentially had a DNA sample collected. Those in pretrial diversion, intervention in lieu of conviction, and court‐supervised release were not considered DNA misses because their cases had not been fully adjudicated and were not traditional probationers.

Regarding the highest‐level felony offense for these 355 individuals, around 65% of third‐ and fourth‐degree felonies had DNA collected at some point. Felonies of the first and second degree had DNA sample collection rates of around 50%. Finally, the fifth‐degree felonies had a DNA sample collection rate of 43%. Thus, it did not appear that those charged with higher‐level felonies were more consistently having their DNA samples collected than those charged with lower‐level felonies.

Lastly, regarding the crime type for these 355 individuals, Table 1 presents the top five most common crime types in this sample. Burglaries and carrying concealed weapons had the highest DNA sample collection rates, ranging from 64 to 71%, while felonious assault and drug possession had the lowest DNA sample collection rates at 50%. Therefore, crimes which were almost always felonies were more frequently resulting in DNA swab collection compared to crimes that could be misdemeanors or felonies, depending on evidence supporting the degree of harm and drug type or possession amounts.

Second probation audit

Table 1 provides details on the outcomes of the second probation audit conducted in November 2023. At the second probation audit, 1.3% (n = 85) of probationers were not in CODIS (compared to 5% in the first audit). However, by the time their case statuses were reviewed, which was approximately a quarter later, 54% (46 of 85) were in CODIS. This implies that 0.6% of the entire sample of probationers from the second audit (n = 39 of 6334) were without a CODIS DNA sample.

Given the much smaller number of those not in CODIS (and thus fewer potential misses), we did not collect as much information about these potential misses as we did in the first probation audit. We did collect data on the status of their pending case in the county. The results indicated that 25 of the 39 were not eligible for DNA sample collection due to the following reasons: (a) 22 of the 39 were either in pretrial (therefore not traditional probationers who might have already had DNA collected but their sample was not yet in CODIS) or court‐supervised release for misdemeanors (not eligible for DNA sample collection); and (b) 3 of the 39 were waiting competency determinations (eligible for DNA sample collection). Therefore, 14 of the 39 were missed DNA sample collections: 8 were on probation, 4 were already sentenced, and 2 were capias. Taken together, this meant that 0.2% of the entire sample of probationers had missed DNA sample collections by the subsequent quarter. This equated to approximately 1 in 455 eligible individuals on probation who had missed DNA sample collections (compared to 1 in 45 in the first audit). The results of the second probation audit indicated improvement in DNA sample collection compared with the first probation audit and a drastic improvement from before the initiative began (20%–50%) (Figure 1). In fact, these findings demonstrate a near‐elimination of missed DNA sample collections among felony probationers.

Observations of DNA sample procedures

A third way we assessed the initiative was through observing DNA sample collection procedures in the arraignment room and at booking in the county jail. We also observed the booking process upon intake at the county jail, which included fingerprinting and DNA sample collection. The purpose of the observations was to help identify potential gaps in the collection processes, with a specific focus on atypical arraignment processes, including whether DNA sample collection was conducted for those currently in custody at the jail compared to those arraigned on felony‐level charges but not in custody.

Some defendants were in the county jail (under the Sheriff's Department's supervision). Others were not in custody but were issued summonses to appear for arraignment following a felony indictment. When a defendant did not have a DNA sample in CODIS, the court ordered them to provide one. Upon the completion of the hearing, defendants were instructed to see the Sheriff's deputies for a DNA sample collection. Along with the prosecutor supervising the initiative, the first author followed the defendants into a very small holding room connected to the arraignment courtroom (“DNA sample collection room”). The first author asked deputies what would happen if someone refused to provide a DNA sample. Deputies responded that, after following certain procedures, they were allowed to force a felony arrestee to submit to DNA sample collection; however, in practice, they said they had not personally had this issue or heard of any refusals. The first author did not observe any defendant who refused. While in the “DNA sample collection room,” she observed the DNA sample collection procedures along with the more detailed paperwork and fingerprinting that accompanied the DNA sample collection, pursuant to Ohio Administrative Code Rule 109:5‐5‐02 (“Arrestee and convicted offender DNA sample collection and submission procedures”) [16].

Overall, she did not observe significant issues with the DNA sample collection process, a finding supported by the recollection data (discussed below). DNA sample collection processes appeared to be routine at this point in the initiative. However, she did identify two potential issues that guided the subsequent assessment of the initiative (also detailed below). First, the first author noted potential issues upon completion of the DNA sample collection paperwork, such as an insufficient DNA sample or incomplete/insufficient completion of the paperwork, particularly the defendant's fingerprint requirement to verify identity. Additionally, while all defendants in the arraignment room were charged with felonies because the Court of Common Pleas is a felony‐only court, the same cannot be said for jail. The county jail holds those arrested/charged with felonies and misdemeanors. However, for DNA sample collections to occur during booking at the county jail, intake deputies would need to know whether the charge was a misdemeanor or a felony. This distinction is not always clear at intake, as some crimes can be misdemeanors or felonies. For example, an arrest for domestic violence could be a misdemeanor or felony depending on whether the individual had prior convictions for similar offenses or whether the victim was pregnant at the time of the offense [18]. The observed intake paperwork included indicators of crime‐offense level, but when asked whether deputies could determine whether the charge was a misdemeanor or a felony, Sheriff's Department leadership said yes, without elaborating on how or under what circumstances these decisions were made. Lastly, while this practice was not necessarily observed, an important component of this process was that, if DNA sample collections occurred at the time of booking, the DNA samples did not have enough time to be processed and uploaded to CODIS at the arraignment hearings, indicating duplicative DNA sample collection efforts by the criminal justice process.

Recollection requests

The fourth way we assessed lessons learned from the initiative's progress was by examining DNA sample collection requests, which were a direct result of observations made during the hearings. Over approximately 18 months, the DNA of 33 individuals who were swabbed in the arraignment courtroom or during intake at the Sheriff's Department, for various reasons, could not be entered into CODIS. As a result, the Ohio AGO requested DNA samples to be recollected. For context, during the same period, 5963 requests were made for DNA sample collection from individuals who did not have a DNA sample in CODIS at the time of their arraignment hearing (not necessarily on the census) for an indictment in a new case in the county. This number represents unique requests to collect a DNA sample from a defendant, not unique individuals, because a defendant could have a DNA sample requested multiple times during the observation period.

The original DNA samples failed to meet CODIS entry standards for several reasons (Table 2). The most common reasons were insufficient DNA in the sample (48.5%), a missing fingerprint (15.2%) or an insufficiently inked fingerprint (9.1%) on the required paperwork, and no name provided on the DNA sample (9.1%). Additionally, of the 33 failed samples, four separate deputies were mentioned more than once. These results suggest that recollection requests were uncommon and when they did occur, most often concerned deputy errors in collection procedures or paperwork completion.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Missed DNA sample collection has been identified as a problem among forensic laboratories and law enforcement for decades [3, 12]. This jurisdiction was the first to receive Sexual Assault Kit Initiative (SAKI) funding from the Bureau of Justice Assistance in 2016 to address LODNA. Over the course of two SAKI grant awards spanning eight years, this initiative tackled county‐wide missed DNA sample collections, led by the Cuyahoga County Prosecutor's Office. When this jurisdiction began its LODNA initiative, there was little guidance on how to address such a large‐scale issue for individuals outside of the prison systems. A prior study on LODNA in this jurisdiction focused on describing DNA sample collection issues prior to the start of the initiative, how the census was conducted, how DNA samples were obtained, and the initiative's initial outcomes, including the resulting offender hits [10]. The current study was a follow‐up to the previous study, which outlined the final results of this eight‐year, funded initiative, including the collection, submission, and tracking of DNA samples, as well as an evaluation of how effectively the policies implemented through the LODNA initiative addressed missed DNA sample collections. To our knowledge, this is the first publication to present the outcomes of a study conducted upon completion of the LODNA initiative and to assess how well the implemented policies engendered large‐scale LODNA reform. Additionally, this study highlights lessons learned from addressing LODNA and ongoing issues with DNA sample collection. This study fills an important knowledge gap and provides a framework for other jurisdictions seeking to address their collection issues.

Regarding the first research question—outcomes of the initiative—the results indicate the incredible progress this jurisdiction has made in collecting LODNA. Over approximately eight years (as of August 2024), 25.5% (3802) of census members had a DNA sample in CODIS, and 64.2% did not. This is likely an undercount because 10.4% of the census members' identities could not be confirmed using identifying information. Of the 3802 census members in CODIS, 623 were actively collected via jail and probation sweeps, and 641 DNA samples from census members were entered into CODIS as a direct result of looking up defendants' DNA status in OHLEG on the day(s) preceding their arraignment hearings in the county for new charges.

Regarding the second research question, we assessed the initiative's progress in DNA sample collection in four ways. First, the initiative's proactive pursuit of DNA samples from census members associated with the “most serious offenses” yielded limited results. Approximately 6.7% of census members met the criteria for additional review to potentially collect DNA more actively, but only a small percentage of these had owed DNA and pending cases in the county, and even fewer ultimately contributed new DNA samples to CODIS by the end of the grant period. The results of this effort indicated limited value in actively obtaining DNA swabs in this jurisdiction, as only a handful had new charges requiring DNA collection and were not already being swabbed as part of the traditional DNA sample collection process.

Second, we conducted two probation audits. This policy directly led to the request for 746 DNA samples from census members on new charges in the county and, more importantly, to DNA sample collection becoming widespread and routine. This meant that for those with new charges, DNA sample collection would occur for the vast majority in accordance with state law. The first probation audit indicated that 2.2% of probationers, or approximately 1 in 45, had missed DNA sample collection by the succeeding quarter. The second probation audit showed improvement—0.2% of probationers, or approximately 1 in 455, had missed DNA sample collections by the succeeding quarter. Before the initiative began, we estimated that between 20% and 50% of felony arrestees had missed DNA sample collections [10]. This substantial progress was primarily due to CCPO's DNA sample collection policies, enacted as a result of this initiative, particularly the requirement that DNA samples be collected at arraignment hearings. These findings demonstrate a near‐elimination of missed DNA sample collections among felony probationers. This meant that moving forward, individuals who owed DNA and were not in the census would (with a few exceptions) have their DNA sample collected in accordance with state law. Third, observations of the DNA sample collection procedure during these hearings by July 2023 indicated the routineness of the DNA sample collection process; however, potential collection issues were observed regarding completion of DNA sample collection paperwork and deputies knowing whether DNA sample collection should be included in the booking process in jail, depending on whether the crime was a misdemeanor or felony. Fourth, based on observations of the arraignment hearings, we obtained information over approximately 18 months regarding the number and nature of DNA recollection requests made by the Ohio AGO to the Sheriff's Department. Over this time period, 33 recollection requests were made—a relatively rare occurrence. However, when they were made, the most common reasons for the request were (in order of most to least frequent): insufficient DNA from the sample, missing or insufficiently inked fingerprints on the paperwork, and the absence of the arrestee's name on the DNA sample. Additionally, it appears that four separate deputies were mentioned more than once in these recollection requests.

Regarding the third and last research question, we examined remaining issues in DNA sample collection in three ways by determining how many census members were unlikely to be eligible for DNA sample collection. First, we found a declining number of census members each quarter who had a DNA sample entered into CODIS, indicating that fewer census members were re‐entering the criminal justice system over time and fewer census members would have DNA samples entered into CODIS, providing further support for the need to collect DNA samples lawfully the first time they were under supervision and at the earliest point in the criminal justice process the law allows. Second, we found that 21.5% of census members would likely be ineligible for DNA sample collection because they were deceased or lived out of state. Finally, we determined that while Ohio law does not mandate DNA sample collection as a condition of the sexual offender registry, only a very small percentage of the census members were currently or formerly registered sexual offenders in the state, and an even smaller percentage were currently on the registry and owed DNA.

Recommendations and lessons learned

The LODNA initiative provided several lessons learned and practice recommendations, detailed below. First, CCPO implemented a policy to assist with active DNA sample collection, which proved extremely successful. This policy directly led to confirmed DNA sample collection for 623 census members, but likely tens of thousands more as DNA collection processes became routine. In only approximately 18 months (January 2023 to June 2024), almost 6000 requests were made during arraignment hearings for DNA sample collection from those whose DNA was not in CODIS at the time of their arraignment hearing. Observations of the arraignment courtroom indicated that by 2023, the practice of confirming DNA sample collection when owed was customary.

Collecting DNA samples is complicated. These results demonstrated that multiple systems must buy in and then collaborate, coordinate, and regularly share data (e.g., the statewide CODIS administrator, the prosecutor's office, and the sheriff's department) for DNA sample collection processes to become routine in a “swab‐at‐felony‐arrest state.” Since not all defendants were arrested, collecting at an arraignment hearing proved an efficient and effective way to ensure that DNA samples were collected from those who lawfully owed. While this was not the most effective way to guarantee DNA sample collection (as not all those arrested are arraigned), at least in these data, the vast majority were. Additionally, due to the extended duration of criminal justice system adjudications, prosecutors often had extended interactions with defendants and therefore multiple opportunities to motion the court to collect DNA when owed. Requests for DNA sample collections also allowed prosecutors in this jurisdiction to indirectly educate judges and other court personnel about the need to ensure that DNA was collected before defendants left the criminal justice system. The results suggest that other states should consider allowing DNA sample collection at multiple stages of the criminal justice process and, where state law permits, other prosecutors' offices should consider implementing a similar policy.

Second, to fully implement this policy, CCPO needed: (a) a system by which an individual's DNA status could be checked at any point, which the Ohio AGO had already implemented prior to this initiative via a DNA statement in their CCH in OHLEG; and (b) regular updates (in this case, quarterly) on all defendants' DNA status, which allowed for CCPO to create a “flag” in their electronic case management system, alerting the prosecutor assigned to the case that a defendant did not have a DNA sample in CODIS but should have. This required close collaboration (in the form of memoranda of understanding).

Third, once the practice of determining whether it was legally required to submit a DNA sample at the arraignment hearing became routine, these results indicated that for the few with missed DNA sample collections, those missed were likely due to the individuals who took a non‐traditional path through the criminal justice system, such as awaiting competency hearings or a quickly dispositioned case. This further supports the need for regular updates on defendants' DNA statuses and routine checks for owed DNA throughout criminal proceedings.

Fourth, the results indicate that over approximately eight years, approximately 25% census members ended up with DNA samples in CODIS, the majority of which resulted from subsequent contact with the criminal justice system, thereby providing another opportunity to lawfully collect DNA. However, the declining number of census members in CODIS over time and the high percentage who were deceased or living out of state further support the need to ensure DNA is collected at the earliest point in the process, as success rates for DNA collection decline over time.

Fifth, several tasks undertaken by the initiative were less fruitful. Task Force members expended a great deal of time individually checking the status of census members, including the “most serious offenses,” and removing census members who were likely to have no DNA samples collected because of death or living out of state. We attempted to automate this process using Social Security and the Bureau of Motor Vehicles records, but to no avail. After we began checking census members individually, colleagues from other SAKI LODNA sites recommended that we work with credit bureaus to obtain this information. Other jurisdictions could consider obtaining updates on census members (such as addresses, deaths, etc.) from credit bureaus and similar entities. With the exception of the early jail and probation sweeps discussed in [10], these findings suggest that, given the limitations of DNA sample collection according to the state statute, the practical gains of actively collecting DNA samples were very modest, underscoring the ongoing challenges of obtaining LODNA.

Sixth, the results indicated that the vast majority of census members were not currently or previously registered sex offenders. However, since a relatively small number of registered non‐incarcerated sex offenders are on the registry in the state, this finding is not surprising. However, DNA submission is not a condition of the registry. Due to the required ongoing address verification process, there is potential for sheriff's offices to collect DNA from those who owe, given continued contact with those on the registry, if state law allowed.

Lastly, law enforcement personnel can search an arrestee's DNA status in the OHLEG (Ohio Law Enforcement Gateway), a database that was instrumental in implementing the prosecutor's DNA sample collection policy. Texas Department of Public Safety also implemented a similar system in the state's CCH database [11]. Given that approximately 15% of census members had unverified DNA statuses, this highlights the difficulty of tracking individuals through the criminal justice system, ensuring they have the correct identifying information (such as name, date of birth, SSN, and state ID number). The study's findings indicate that states should have a system that allows all law enforcement to have real‐time access to a person's DNA status in a statewide, electronic law enforcement database and to avoid duplicate samples, as some crime laboratories report receiving thousands of duplicates. Texas implemented a color‐coded flagging system in its statewide law enforcement database when an individual's DNA is owed [11]. This would allow law enforcement, as standard practice, to check the DNA status of every person. Current technological advances in DNA, such as rapid DNA, which can generate DNA samples in as little as 90 min, may also mitigate many collection issues and prevent duplicative DNA sample collections [23].

Limitations and future directions

The census did not include everyone who owes DNA; it primarily focused on individuals confirmed to owe within the two main law enforcement agencies during the observation period. Furthermore, as a case study, the results described here cannot be applied universally to all jurisdictions and would vary based on the specific characteristics of each state's DNA sample collection laws, the degree of access law enforcement and court personnel have to know whether an arrestee has a DNA sample in CODIS, whether LODNA sample collection efforts are statewide or county(ish)wide, and DNA sample collection practices within the jurisdiction. Given the widespread prevalence of DNA sample collections across jurisdictions, all are encouraged to assess and devise a DNA sample collection process, as well as a process to assess and address missed DNA collections. As this is a case study, more research is needed to assess the generalizability of the findings, monitor and evaluate efforts to address LODNA, and establish best practices, given the patchwork of state laws governing DNA sample collection.
