Epidemiological analysis of African swine fever in the European Union during 2025
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Karl Ståhl, Anette Boklund, Tomasz Podgórski, Timothée Vergne, Lina Mur, Roxani Aminalragia‐Giamini, Alessia Rusinà, Eliana Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring 2025, the number of EU Member States affected by African swine fever (ASF) increased from 13 to 14, following the detection of African swine fever virus (ASFV) in wild boar in Spain. The number of ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs in the EU increased from 333 in 2024 to 585 in 2025, largely driven by Romania, accounting for 81% of outbreaks in 2025. Most outbreaks (91%) occurred in establishments with more than 100 pigs, while 26 outbreaks were reported in establishments with 1000–10,000 pigs, and 11 in establishments with more than 10,000 pigs. As in previous years, ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs showed a summer seasonality. Most outbreaks were detected through passive surveillance based on clinical suspicions (84%). Among outbreaks in farms with more than 1000 pigs (n = 37), 65% (n = 24) were identified via systematic testing of two dead pigs per week. Overall, 518,088 samples from domestic pigs were analysed in the EU in 2025. In wild boar, the number of outbreaks notified in the EU increased from 7677 in 2024 to 11,036 in 2025, marking a change from the relatively stable situation of 2022–2024. Poland and Germany accounted for 31% and 18% of the total, respectively. Similarly to previous years, a winter seasonality in wild boar was observed in several Member States. Overall, 28% of the 44,578 wild boar carcasses tested positive for ASFV by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), representing 71% of wild boar outbreaks notified in the EU. In contrast, 1% of the 531,832 hunted wild boar tested positive by PCR, representing 27% of wild boar outbreaks. Despite the higher number of ASF outbreaks, the average size of the area under restriction in the EU due to outbreaks in domestic pigs increased only slightly in 2025 (+2%), while the average size of the area under restriction due to outbreaks in domestic pigs and in wild boar remained at a similar level as in 2024.Keywords: ASF, epidemiology, monitoring, pigs, surveillance, wild boar

SUMMARY

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has a mandate from the European Commission to generate annual epidemiological analyses of the spread and impact of African swine fever (ASF) caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV) genotype II in the European Union (EU) and neighbouring affected countries. In this context, EU Member States and non‐EU countries and territories that notified ASF during 2025 in the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS) were invited to submit laboratory test results from ASF surveillance activities and domestic pig population data to EFSA. These data were analysed together with other information sources, including official ASF outbreak notifications in wild boar and domestic pigs (ADIS), national wild boar hunting bags, wild boar abundance estimates and data on ASF restricted zones.

In 2025, the number of EU Member States affected by ASF increased from 13 to 14, following the notification of ASF outbreaks in wild boar in Spain, the first ones since the eradication of the ASFV in that country in 1995 (reviewed by Mur et al., 2012). In 2025, no Member States reported ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs for the first time; however, Estonia reported outbreaks again after reporting none in 2024.

The number of ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs in the EU increased from 333 in 2024 to 585 in 2025, representing a 76% increase; still, these figures remain below the levels observed during 2018–2023 with the exception of 2022. With 476 notified outbreaks, Romania accounted for 81% of the total number of domestic pig outbreaks in the EU. Most outbreaks (91%) occurred in establishments with fewer than 100 pigs, an increase from 78% in 2024. Nevertheless, outbreaks were also reported in 26 establishments with 1000–10,000 pigs (4% of the outbreaks) and in 11 establishments (2% of the outbreaks) with more than 10,000 pigs. Outbreaks in large farms with more than 10,000 pigs occurred mostly in Romania (n = 7), although outbreaks in this type of farms were also reported in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Slovakia. As observed in the past, ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs showed a seasonal pattern, with a summer peak that was however less pronounced comparing to previous years.

During 2025, detection of ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs relied predominantly on passive surveillance based on clinical suspicions, which accounted for 84% of detected outbreaks. Enhanced passive surveillance based on systematic testing of dead pigs led to the detection of 9% of outbreaks (n = 50), while tracing activities following outbreak detection accounted for 7%. No outbreaks in domestic pigs were detected through active surveillance targeting apparently healthy pigs. Enhanced passive surveillance continued to play an important role in outbreak detection, especially in larger establishments. Among outbreaks in farms with more than 1000 pigs (n = 37), 65% (n = 24) were identified by systematic testing of at least two dead pigs per week. Among the 50 outbreaks identified through enhanced passive surveillance, 48% occurred in farms housing 1000–10,000 animals, while 4% occurred in farms with more than 10,000 animals.

In wild boar, the number of ASF outbreaks notified in the EU increased from 7677 in 2024 to 11,036 in 2025, representing a 44% increase and the highest number since 2021. Poland notified the largest share of wild boar outbreaks (31%), followed by Germany (18%). A winter peak in ASF outbreaks in wild boar was observed in several Member States, including Hungary, Italy, Poland, Romania and Slovakia, while no clear seasonal patterns were evident in other countries.

Samples from hunted wild boar represented approximately 90% of all wild boar samples analysed in the EU during 2025, with less than 1% testing positive by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Despite the low proportion of positives, these samples accounted for around 27% of notified wild boar outbreaks. In contrast, samples from wild boar found dead represented about 7% of analysed samples, but 28% tested positive by PCR, leading to the detection of more than 70% of wild boar outbreaks in the EU. Road‐killed wild boar accounted for a small proportion of samples and notified outbreaks. Overall, the total number of wild boar samples analysed increased substantially in 2025, possibly reflecting intensified surveillance efforts in affected countries. The overall number of serological tests used in hunted wild boar in the EU decreased by 40%, from 119,843 in 2024 to 71,648 in 2025. However, the total number of PCR tests increased by 23%, from 452,262 in 2024 to 593,127 in 2025.

Two long‐distance translocation events were identified during 2025, one in Germany and one in Spain, both involving introductions into wild boar populations. In Spain, genetic analyses did not identify the origin of the isolate involved but suggested that the event was compatible with human‐mediated translocation. In Germany, genetic analyses revealed that the newly introduced isolate was distinct from those previously circulating in the country. Although the genetic analyses indicated a higher similarity to the isolates circulating in Calabria (South of Italy), the origin of the introduction remains unknown. Since the introduction in June 2025, further positive cases have been detected in that region of Germany.

In this report, restricted zones III represent areas affected by ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs, while combined restricted zones II and III represent areas affected by ASF outbreaks in either wild boar or domestic pigs. Despite the marked increase in ASF outbreaks in both domestic pigs and wild boar, the average size of restricted zone III in the EU increased only slightly in 2025 (+2%), while the combined size of restricted zones II and III remained at similar level as in 2024. The ASF incidence in affected NUTS 3 regions remained stable in most Member States, with a decrease observed in Italy.

Overall, the number of wild boars lost to ASF (found dead or killed) in the EU increased by 58% compared with 2024, reflecting the higher number of outbreaks in wild boar. However, substantial variation was observed between countries, with decreases in Czechia and increases in most of the other affected Member States.

National wild boar hunting bag data were analysed as a proxy for wild boar population size in each country. However, it is acknowledged that hunting bag figures also reflect variation in hunting effort, reporting practices and national management policies. The analysis of hunting bag data revealed differing patterns among ASF‐affected countries. In the Baltic States, after a rebound of wild boar populations observed between 2019 and 2024, no clear trend was observed in 2025. A stabilising trend following a decline after ASF introduction was observed in Bulgaria and Romania. Decreasing trends following ASF introduction were observed in several other countries, consistent with broader European patterns.

In non‐EU countries, no new country reported ASF for the first time in 2025. Overall, a declining trend was observed in both domestic pigs and wild boar, with the number of notified outbreaks decreasing from 431 to 359 in domestic pigs and from 215 to 158 in wild boar compared with 2024. Serbia remained the most heavily affected non‐EU country, accounting for 233 (65%) of the 359 outbreaks notified in domestic pigs.

INTRODUCTION

Since the Genotype II of African swine fever virus (ASFV) was detected in eastern Europe in 2007, ASFV has spread to numerous countries in Europe and far beyond (Asia, the Americas, Oceania). In the European Union (EU), Genotype II of ASFV was detected for the first time in 2014 in Eurasian wild boar (Sus scrofa) in Poland and the Baltic countries. Since then, African swine fever (ASF) has been notified in several EU countries, affecting kept and wild porcine animals (as defined in Article 4 of Regulation 2016/429), here referred to as domestic pigs and wild boar.

The control of the disease in the EU follows a regionalisation approach. This includes implementation of preventive biosecurity measures, restrictions of movement of domestic pigs, wild boar and their products, culling of domestic pigs at affected establishments and the management of wild boar populations. Therefore, the collection of samples and analysis of the surveillance data are crucial for evaluating the disease evolution, monitoring the effect of the control measures and adapting the control measures accordingly.

The current report focuses on the epidemiological assessment of ASF from 1 January to 31 December 2025 in the EU Member States and European neighbouring countries that notified ASF outbreaks among domestic pigs or wild boar in 2025 to the Animal Diseases Information System (ADIS), hereafter referred to as ‘affected countries’.

In 2025, 14 EU Member States were affected by ASF. While Bulgaria, Czechia, Germany, Hungary and Spain notified ASF outbreaks in wild boar only; Croatia, Estonia, Greece, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania and Slovakia notified ASF outbreaks both in wild boar and in domestic pigs.

In non‐EU countries, ASF was notified in ADIS in five countries in 2025: Bosnia and Herzegovina, Moldova, North Macedonia, Serbia and Ukraine.

From 2016, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has been producing annual epidemiological reports summarising the evolution of ASF (focusing on ASFV Genotype II), analysing epidemiological trends and studying the risk factors involved in the occurrence of the disease, its spread and persistence.

This report presents an analysis of the disease epidemiology and ASF surveillance in domestic pigs and wild boar, an overview of translocation events that took place in 2025 and evaluates impacts on restricted zones, affected animals and establishments.

ASSESSMENT

Disease epidemiology and ASF surveillance in domestic pigs

HIGHLIGHTS ON ASF IN DOMESTIC PIGS IN 2025

In 2025, no outbreaks of ASF were confirmed in domestic pigs in previously unaffected Member States. In total, 13 countries, including 9 Member States, notified ASF in domestic pigs. All of them also notified ASF in wild boar in 2025.

The number of outbreaks in domestic pigs in the EU increased from 333 in 2024 to 585 in 2025 but remains lower than the levels observed during 2018–2023 with the exception of 2022. Similarly to 2024, most of the outbreaks (91%) notified in 2025 occurred in establishments with fewer than 100 pigs. Nevertheless, outbreaks were also reported in 26 establishments with 1000–10,000 pigs (4% of the outbreaks) and in 11 establishments (2% of the outbreaks) with more than 10,000 pigs. Outbreaks in farms with more than 10,000 pigs occurred mostly in Romania (n = 7), although outbreaks in this type of farms were also reported in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Slovakia.

As in previous years, Romania accounted for most of the outbreaks notified in the EU (81% of the outbreaks). Compared to last year, fewer outbreaks were notified in Germany, Italy and Poland. In contrast, the numbers of notifications increased in Romania, Croatia, Estonia and Latvia (although only slightly in the latter). After the notification of only two outbreaks in the period 2022–2024, Estonia reported 11 outbreaks in domestic pigs during the summer of 2025. The epidemic in domestic pigs has not substantially expanded geographically in 2025, with 99.7% of outbreaks being notified in NUTS3 areas previously affected by ASF.

The seasonality of outbreaks in domestic pigs during the summer months was still present in 2025, although less pronounced than during previous years. The seasonal pattern seen in 2025 was mostly driven by the outbreaks in smaller establishments in Romania. A similar seasonal pattern was observed in non‐EU countries.

Most of the outbreaks in domestic pigs were detected through passive surveillance based on testing clinical suspicions (84%), followed by enhanced passive surveillance, involving systematic testing of two dead pigs per week (9%), and sampling of pigs in establishments identified following contact tracing from affected establishments (7%). In farms with more than 1000 pigs (n = 37), enhanced passive surveillance detected 65% of the outbreaks (n = 24). Overall, 518,088 samples from domestic pigs were analysed in the EU in 2025; of these, 71% (367,402 samples) were tested as part of the passive or enhanced passive surveillance 2 and 29% (150,686 samples) as part of the active surveillance programme. Most samples (97%) were tested using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), while approximately 3% of samples were tested using serological methods (%).

Spatial distribution among domestic pigs

In 2025, ASF outbreaks were confirmed and notified to ADIS among domestic pigs by 9 Member States (Croatia, Estonia, Greece, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania and Slovakia) and 4 non‐EU countries (Bosnia and Herzegovina, Moldova, Serbia and Ukraine). All of these countries have already notified ASF in domestic pigs in the past, and they also notified outbreaks among wild boar during 2025. Compared with 2024, the number of outbreaks in domestic pigs in the EU increased from 333 to 585.

In 2023 and 2024, Romania notified 736 and 220 outbreaks in domestic pig farms, respectively. Croatia, Latvia and Estonia also saw an increase in the number of outbreaks. Of note, Estonia notified 11 outbreaks, the largest number since 2015, contrasting with the absence of notified outbreaks in 2024 and 2022 and the presence of only one affected establishment in 2023. In contrast, the situation improved in Germany (with no notified outbreaks), Italy (with only one notified outbreak in the northern cluster), and Poland (with fewer outbreaks in the central/western part of the country) compared to 2024.

Monthly seasonality in domestic pigs

For domestic pigs, the seasonality in the EU was described by number of outbreaks notified per month per year (Figure 6A). Compared to previous years, the summer seasonality in 2025 was less pronounced with the peak of outbreaks occurring over a longer period (Figure 6A). In 2025, 46% of outbreaks in the EU were notified between July and September, compared with 79% in 2024. When considering a longer period between June and October, 62% of outbreaks were notified in 2025, compared with 92% in 2024 during the same period. The seasonal pattern observed in 2025 was driven by outbreaks in smaller establishments (Figure 6B) and was much less evident in larger establishments (Figure 6C). The observed pattern was mostly influenced by Romania, where ASF outbreaks among domestic pigs occurred over a longer period in 2025 compared with 2024, which is consistent with the overall pattern observed in other EU countries. However, in Estonia, all the domestic pig outbreaks were concentrated between late June and mid‐September. Epidemiological investigations did not identify a specific source of ASFV introduction into the affected farms, been the circulation of ASFV in wild boar considered the most likely source of infection.

ASF surveillance in domestic pigs

Passive surveillance (i.e. the investigation of clinical suspicions, including testing dead pigs and pigs with clinical signs) is considered the basis for the early detection of ASF among domestic pigs. As an additional component to support timely detection, an enhanced passive surveillance can be implemented on establishments, based on the weekly testing of at least two dead post‐weaning pigs (older than 60 days) as described by the EFSA AHAW Panel (2021) and in recommended for restricted zones in accordance with the Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/594. 5 Note that for small establishments where fewer than two dead pigs are found per week, it is recommended to test every single pig found dead. Such enhanced passive surveillance 6 is also used by the competent authorities of EU Member States to confirm freedom of disease status at domestic pig establishments prior to authorising animal movements in restricted areas, as prescribed by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/594. Active surveillance activities that target apparently healthy pigs are not included in the current legislation and are not implemented unless considered necessary by the competent authority.

Surveillance practices

Information on surveillance activities in domestic pigs and wild boar was collected through an online questionnaire, to which 13 of 14 affected Member States responded (Appendix A).

All Member States that answered the questionnaire reported implementing passive surveillance based on clinical suspicions, i.e. testing dead pigs and live pigs with clinical signs.

Seven Member States reported that enhanced passive surveillance6 activities took place in the whole country, either in all farms (Estonia, Italy, Romania and Slovakia) or in commercial farms only (Croatia, which applied in all establishments in the country with more than 100 pigs, Latvia and Lithuania). Other six MSs implemented it in restricted zones only, either in all farms (Germany, Greece, Hungary, Poland and Spain) or in a selection of farms (in Czechia, in farms that move domestic pigs).

Seven Member States reported implementing active surveillance by testing apparently healthy pigs before movements. This measure was applied in restricted zones in Czechia, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Poland and Slovakia. and. In Estonia, active surveillance was implemented via the testing of pigs prior to movement when the farm of origin did not have a high biosecurity level. In Spain, an active surveillance system has been in place since 2011 based on inspection at slaughter by trained official veterinarians in selected slaughterhouses combined with annual on farm random sampling and serological testing in almost 1200 holdings selected based on risk criteria such as biosecurity conditions, herd size and animal movements.

For non‐EU countries, enhanced passive surveillance was applied in all commercial pig establishments in North Macedonia (i.e. farms with more than 10 fattening pigs), while in Serbia it was implemented in large establishments located in restricted zones.

Outbreak detection by type of surveillance

Regarding the surveillance pathways leading to outbreak detection in the EU in 2025, Member States reported that 494 of the 585 notified outbreaks (84%) were detected through passive surveillance based on the testing of clinical suspicions, 50 outbreaks (9%) were detected through enhanced passive surveillance based on the weekly testing of at least two dead pigs, and 41 outbreaks (7%) were detected through sampling in at‐risk farms identified following contact tracing from affected establishments. Among the 50 outbreaks detected through the enhanced passive surveillance, 9 (18%) occurred in farms with 10 pigs or less, 13 (26%) in farms with 11–100 animals, 4 (8%) in farms with 101–1000 pigs, 22 (44%) in farms with 1000–10,000 animals and 2 (4%) in farms with more than 10,000 animals. For outbreaks in farms with more than 1000 pigs (n = 37), this surveillance component contributed to the detection of 64% of outbreaks (n = 24).

Surveillance laboratory tests

A total of 517,479 samples were analysed in the EU for the detection of ASF during 2025. Approximately 96% of samples originating from domestic pigs during 2025 were analysed by PCR only (498,333 samples), whereas 2.8% of the samples were analysed by enzyme‐linked immunoassay (ELISA) tests only (14,715 samples) and 0.9% of samples (4652) were tested by PCR and ELISA in parallel. In the previous reporting year, 97% of samples were analysed by PCR vs. 4.6% by ELISA. In 2025, other tests, such as the indirect immunoperoxidase test (IPT) or direct fluorescence antibody test were used on a limited number of samples (488 samples, < 0.001% of tests), similarly to what was observed in 2024.

In Romania, a substantial number of healthy pigs (11,433) were tested by antibody identification methods (with ELISA or immunoperoxidase). Of these, 95% (10,903) of tests were requested by the establishment owner and were not part of the national surveillance programme. The number of tests carried out under active surveillance in Romania are still much lower than the figures reported in 2019–2021, which resulted from testing carried out to lift restrictions under legislation in force at the time. In Estonia, an increase in sampling under active and passive surveillance activities was linked with samplings done after repopulation of establishments, epidemiological samplings done in outbreak sites, as well as additional samplings due to restrictions.

In 2025, none of the non‐EU countries submitted data on ASFV laboratory test results on domestic pigs (Table 2).

Disease epidemiology and ASF surveillance in wild boar

HIGHLIGHTS ON ASF IN WILD BOAR IN 2025

In 2025, ASF outbreaks in wild boar were notified for the first time in Spain since the eradication of ASF in the country in 1994.

A total of 19 countries, including 14 Member States, reported ASF outbreaks in wild boar populations.

Two notable long‐distance translocation events were identified during 2025: in Spain (Catalonia) in November 2025, and in a new region of Germany (North Rhine‐Westphalia).

In the EU, the number of notified ASF outbreaks in wild boar increased from 7677 in 2024 to 11,036 in 2025, making it the highest number since 2021. In 2025, an increase in the numbers of notifications in wild boar was observed from all countries except Italy and Czechia. Around 31% of ASF outbreaks in wild boar were notified in Poland and 18% in Germany, mainly in the more recently affected western regions. In the non‐EU countries, the number of notified ASF outbreaks in wild boar decreased from 215 in 2024 to 158 in 2025.

In 2025, there were several successful regional campaigns to control the disease. In Czechia and in southern Italy (Campania, Calabria and Lazio), no signs of active circulation of ASFV were detected during 2025 and restricted zones were lifted. The epidemiological situation improved substantially in eastern areas of Germany (Brandenburg and Saxony).

Most of the outbreaks in wild boar (97.6%) were notified in NUTS3 regions that already notified ASF outbreaks during previous years. The newly affected NUTS3 areas were in Germany, Italy, Poland, Greece, Croatia, Hungary and Spain.

A marked seasonality with a peak in winter and lower numbers of outbreaks in summer was observed in Hungary, Italy, Poland, Romania and Slovakia. No clear trends were observed in other Member States.

A total of 618,464 wild boar samples were analysed in the EU for the detection of ASF during 2025. Samples taken from hunted wild boar represented 90% of the samples analysed. Only 0.8% of them tested positive by PCR, leading to the detection of 27% of the wild boar outbreaks. In contrast, samples taken from found dead wild boar represented only 7% of the samples analysed, with 28% of them testing positive by PCR, leading to the detection of 71% of the wild boar outbreaks in the EU. The remaining samples were reported as originating from road‐killed wild boar (3% of the total analysed), which led to the detection of 2% of positive wild boar.

Spatial distribution among wild boar

During 2025, ASF outbreaks among wild boar were notified by 14 Member States (Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Estonia, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, Slovakia and Spain) and 5 non‐EU countries (Bosnia and Herzegovina, Moldova, North Macedonia, Serbia and Ukraine).

Similarly to the situation in domestic pigs, an increase in outbreaks was observed in wild boar in the EU, with the number of outbreaks rising from 7677 in 2024 to 11,036 in 2025. The geographical distribution of ASF outbreaks among wild boar in 2025 in the EU was similar to the previous year with one notable change: Spain notified, in November 2025, outbreaks in wild boar for the first time during the current epidemic in EU 9 (see Section 3.3 on translocation events for information). As in the previous year, Poland notified the highest number of ASF outbreaks in wild boar, with 3429 outbreaks notified nationwide, accounting for 31% of all wild boar outbreaks in the EU (Figure 8). In addition to Spain, a translocation event was also notified in Germany (see Section 3.3).

Regarding the geographical distribution of the disease, there were fewer outbreaks in the eastern parts of Germany and only one positive wild boar in Czechia (Figure 8). In Italy, no outbreaks were notified from Lazio and Campania, and only one positive case was notified in Calabria, which corresponded to old dry remains of wild boar assumed to originate from the outbreaks in 2023. During 2025, ASF spread in wild boar in the Italian northern cluster towards southeast and west. Compared with the previous year, fewer outbreaks were confirmed by serology in the EU, most of them from Bulgaria and a few from Romania (Figure 9).

Serbia was the non‐EU country with the highest number of ASF outbreaks among wild boar with 62 outbreaks notified across the territory, a decrease from the 101 outbreaks in 2024, followed by Ukraine with 34 outbreaks, Bosnia and Herzegovina (28) and Moldova (26) (Figure 9).

Successful ASF control or eradication in 2025

In 2025, three EU Member States (Czechia, Italy and Germany) successfully controlled or eradicated ASF in all or parts of their territories.

Czechia

Czechia

Czechia reported only one ASF‐positive wild boar case, linked to old carcass remains dating back to August 2024, indicating successful eradication. Key measures included systematic carcass searches, strong stakeholder collaboration (including students), drone and camera monitoring of wild boar, driven hunts and strict hunting biosecurity. Depopulation efforts, supported by police, faced limited acceptance among the public and hunters.

b Italy

Italy

Italy resolved three infection clusters in 2025: Lazio (January), Calabria (October) and Campania/Basilicata (November).

Lazio: ASF was first detected in May 2022 on a wild boar within Rome's urban and peri‐urban natural park. Control success was driven by active carcass searches, long‐term coordinated management, and containment using a highway barrier with fencing and targeted trapping. Depopulation through hunting and culling reduced wild boar density and contact rates, keeping the outbreak confined. The territory was established as ASF free in January 2025.

Lazio: ASF was first detected in May 2022 on a wild boar within Rome's urban and peri‐urban natural park. Control success was driven by active carcass searches, long‐term coordinated management, and containment using a highway barrier with fencing and targeted trapping. Depopulation through hunting and culling reduced wild boar density and contact rates, keeping the outbreak confined. The territory was established as ASF free in January 2025.

Calabria: ASF emerged in May 2023 in wild boar and later in pig farms. After limited surveillance in 2024, intensified monitoring in 2025 identified a final case in April. The region was declared ASF‐free in October 2025. Its mountainous, dry environment likely limited virus persistence.

Calabria: ASF emerged in May 2023 in wild boar and later in pig farms. After limited surveillance in 2024, intensified monitoring in 2025 identified a final case in April. The region was declared ASF‐free in October 2025. Its mountainous, dry environment likely limited virus persistence.

Campania/Basilicata: ASF was first detected in May 2023 in a national park with rugged terrain. Control measures included intensive carcass searches, natural and geographical containment, and population reduction through trapping, selective hunting and culling. The last case occurred in September 2024, and ASF‐free status was regained in November 2025.

Campania/Basilicata: ASF was first detected in May 2023 in a national park with rugged terrain. Control measures included intensive carcass searches, natural and geographical containment, and population reduction through trapping, selective hunting and culling. The last case occurred in September 2024, and ASF‐free status was regained in November 2025.

c Germany

Germany

In eastern Germany (Brandenburg and Saxony), the last fresh wild boar cases were detected in November 2024. Intensive surveillance activities indicated no virus circulation for over 12 months. Owing, among other factors, to intensive carcass searches – mainly conducted with dogs and drones – and flexible fencing, the disease is now considered eliminated in this area. Although the disease is considered eliminated in this area, it continues circulating in neighbouring regions posing a permanent risk of reintroduction. 11 High biosecurity standards and awareness campaigns continue. Restriction zones have been significantly reduced, with further adjustments planned.

Translocation events and genetic analyses

Translocation events

In 2025, two long‐distance viral translocation events occurred and are worth describing, one in Germany and one in Spain.

In June 2025, a dead subadult wild boar was sampled in southern Germany (North Rhine‐Westphalia) and tested positive for ASFV, around 200 km away from the nearest known case. In the following days, additional positive carcasses were detected in close proximity to the index case. Genomic analysis revealed that the virus represents a distinct strain from those previously circulating in Germany. Although the analyses indicated the highest similarity to viruses circulating in Calabria based on the current available data, the origin of the introduction remains unknown. Since the introduction in June 2025, further positive cases have been detected. Despite the continued occurrence of cases, the affected area has largely remained confined to the initially affected region. Intensive control and surveillance measures are in place within this area.

In Spain, the translocation event corresponded to the detection of ASF in wild boar in Northeast Spain (Catalonia), in November 2025. The nearest detected outbreak was in northern Italy, around 700 km away. The oldest carcasses found in the area suggest that the virus was probably introduced between late September and early October into the region's wild boar populations. Epidemiological investigations conducted by the Official Veterinary Services ruled out the hypothesis of a laboratory leak from a nearby facility handling ASF viruses, however, the definitive source of virus introduction was not determined (Ministerio de Agricultura, Pesca y Alimentación, 2026). Genomic characterisation of the ASFV isolate identified a distinct genotype II variant, defined by a unique multigene profile and a large deletion within the left variable region (LVR) of the genome, with no close genetic relationship to any known strains circulating in the EU.

As observed in previous long‐distance translocation events, none of the genetic analyses carried were able to identify the origin of the viruses introduced into new regions despite the genotyping efforts performed.

Genetic analysis reported by Member States

Reporting countries were asked about the genotyping analysis performed by them or by the EURL on their isolates during 2025. In total, four Member States (Croatia, Estonia, Italy and Poland) reported performing multi‐gene sequencing approach (Gallardo et al., 2023), while four reported performing whole genome sequencing (WGS) in some of their isolates (Czechia, Estonia, Germany and Italy). Even though genetic analyses carried out seldom allow the identification of the origin of a translocation event, such analyses still can provide important information, such as supporting or excluding epidemiological links between outbreaks (Schulz et al., 2026).

Differences in the application of the methodologies (i.e. analysis of four instead of the six recommended regions for the multi‐gene approach and different approaches for the WGS) also make the interpretation and analysis of the data difficult. Harmonising methodologies and interpretation of genetic data, optimisation of new generation sequencing for ASFV and sharing information is essential for transparency and creating databases with enough comparable data to allow future discrimination between the isolates.

Impact of the disease

HIGHLIGHTS FROM THE IMPACT OF THE DISEASE

In 2025, the total size of restricted zone III in the EU slightly increased by 2.2% (+5818 km2), while the size of restricted zones II + III remained stable. However, large variations between countries were observed, with increased sizes of restriction zone III in Estonia, Lithuania, Latvia, Greece and Croatia, and decreased sizes of restriction zone III in Italy and Germany, Slovakia and Poland.

The incidence rate in affected NUTS 3 regions remained stable in most Member States, except in Italy where a decrease in incidence was observed.

In the EU, a total of 11,036 outbreaks were notified in wild boar in 2025, corresponding to 16,007 lost individuals. This wild boar loss represents an increase of 58% compared to 2024 (2025: 16,007, 2024: 10,155; 2023: 10,160). There were marked increases in absolute numbers of wild boar losses in Poland, Germany and Romania. In non‐ EU‐ affected countries, the total number of reported wild boar lost to ASF in 2025 was 605, which was 21% less than in 2024.

The analysis of the annual hunting bags at country level showed a decrease in Lithuania and Latvia following the post‐ASF rebound that lasted from 2019 to 2024, while the re‐bound continued in Estonia. It also showed a stabilising trend in Bulgaria, Romania and Poland (following a decline after ASF introduction) and a decreasing trend in North Macedonia, Hungary and Slovakia (following ASF introduction). Those trends are consistent with the overall pattern observed in Europe.

Estimating the impact associated with animal diseases is very complex, as besides the direct costs associated with the death of the animals, many other aspects are affected, including trade, welfare of the animals and society (e.g. disruption in outdoor activities including hunting, sport events, tourism). In this report, three main aspects were analysed as indicators of the impact of ASF in affected countries: (i) the restriction zones due to ASF; (ii) the outbreak characteristics in domestic pigs including outbreak size, incidence, animals lost; and (iii) the observed disease‐induced mortality in wild boar and the evolution of wild boar abundance over time.

As Spain reported outbreaks exclusively in wild boar, no restricted zone III was implemented (Figure 17).

The sum of restricted zones II and III, approximating the restricted zones due to the occurrence of ASF outbreaks either in wild boar or domestic pigs respectively, is shown in Figure 16. In 2025, five Member States (Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Romania) had over 90% of their territory covered by restricted zones II + III. In Poland, these restricted zones constituted approximately 50% of the territory, in Slovakia, Hungary between 30 and 40%, in Greece 14%, in Croatia and Italy 6%–7% and in Czechia and Germany, less than 2% of the country area. In Spain following the outbreaks in wild boar detected in November 2025, a restriction zone (type II) of approximately 1600 km2 was set up (equivalent to 0.3% of national territory).

It is important to note that although in some countries the area affected by ASF is not large (e.g. Italy), the spread of the disease to newly affected areas might have a wide impact. For that purpose, the percentage of pig establishments and pigs located in restricted zones (III and II; as well as in restricted zone I where ASF was not notified but restrictions are in place) in the affected Member States was estimated for those countries that submitted pig population data (see Table 1). As seen in Table 4, the percentage of the pig population affected varied considerably between countries, depending on the location of the pig production areas.

Of note, in Croatia, only 12.6% of the territory was under restriction (I + II + III); but more than half of the censed pigs in the country are impacted by those restrictions. The same trend was observed in Italy, although the proportions of affected pigs and restricted areas are smaller. In contrast, in Slovakia, the largest commercial production is not in the areas affected, which is reflected in the smaller percentage of restricted pigs versus restricted establishments. In the remaining countries, the proportion of the restricted area in the country is roughly similar to the proportion of pigs affected.

However, even when restriction zones (I, II, or III) affect low‐density areas or only a limited share of the sector, their impact can still be substantial if they include high‐value production systems, particularly those oriented towards exports to third countries.

It is important to note that the estimated losses in the domestic pig sector do not include the losses indirectly caused by the disease such as preventive culling or trade restrictions. An outbreak, if even outside the restricted zone, may affect trade outside the EU.

In comparison with previous reporting year the number of outbreaks in domestic pigs increased in Romania (+260), Croatia (+47), Estonia (+11), Latvia (+5) and Lithuania (+1) while it decreased in Italy (−30), Poland (−26), Germany (−10), Bulgaria (−1) and Greece (−1). The number of pigs lost due to ASF increased markedly in Estonia, Croatia, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania and Slovakia. In 2025, losses reached approximately 45% of the national pig population in Estonia and 17% in Slovakia. By contrast, pig losses declined in Italy, which reported only one outbreak and lost less than 0.001% of its pig population, and in Greece and Germany, which reported no outbreaks in domestic pigs and consequently lost no pigs directly due to ASF (Table 5).

Over the past 5 years, an overall decreasing trend was observed in the number of domestic pig establishments registered with at least one pig in the EU. Between 2024 and 2025, the mean number of establishments with fewer than 100 pigs and with more than 100 pigs decreased by 4% and 2%, respectively (Table B.1).

In most Member States (all except Romania, Estonia and Slovakia) the number of establishments with less than 100 pigs decreased in 2025 compared to 2024. The largest reductions were recorded in Croatia (−41%), Greece (−18%) and Lithuania (−17%) (Table 5). Small establishments may open and close frequently; they can easily close in newly affected areas, when restrictions and stricter biosecurity requirements are implemented in the area or prices of pigs, feed or energy vary. Impact caused by ASF in wild boar

As previously described in the temporal dynamics, the number of ASF outbreaks in wild boar in the EU increased by 44% in 2025, while the total size of restricted zones II + III in the EU increased by 0.8%. Fifteen NUTS 3 regions had ASF notifications for the first time. In the EU, a total of 11,036 outbreaks were notified in wild boar in 2025, corresponding to 16,007 lost individual wild boar (defined as the cumulative number of animals reported as dead or killed in ADIS).

The total number of wild boar losses due to ASF increased by 58% compared with the previous year (Table 6). However, trends differed by country. Reported ASF‐related losses fell sharply in Czechia (−96%, 26 fewer lost wild boar) and decreased in Italy (−43%, 517 fewer wild boars lost). In contrast, increases of wild boar losses were recorded in Latvia (+4.7%, 67 more), Slovakia (+27%, 62 more), Bulgaria (+29%, 322 more), Lithuania (+46%, 398 more), Poland (+70%, 2359 more), Hungary (+74%, 403 more), Germany (+101%, 1010 more), Greece (+240%, 59 more), Romania (+370%, 968 more), Estonia (+700%, 303 more) and Croatia (+1100%, 423 more). Consequently, the average proportion of losses relative to wild boar population estimated abundance increased from 0.51% in 2024 to 0.85% in 2025, with notable increases in Croatia, Estonia, Romania, and a maximum of 3.13% in Latvia (Table 6). In the non‐EU affected countries, the total number of reported wild boar lost due to ASF in 2025 was 605, 21% less than previous reporting year.

The low overall proportions of wild boar losses attributable to ASF (Table 6) in relation to wild boar abundance among affected countries are likely underestimated due to (i) under‐detection of carcasses (potentially varying between countries), (ii) use of wild boar abundance estimates reflecting pre‐ASF population levels (therefore likely overestimating wild boar abundance in 2025) and (iii) additional or increased wild boar harvest as an ASF control measure (i.e. reduction of the population density). While an increased harvest does not directly result from the virus, it is indirectly related to ASF presence and, as such, could be added to ASF‐induced mortality.

Seven EU countries reported the implementation of wild boar depopulation measures applied, which can be considered as an indirect impact of ASF on wild boar populations. However, the information available from the Member States does not allow for a quantitative estimate of the scale of surplus mortality.

Several countries reported targeted wild boar depopulation or population‐control measures in ASF‐restricted or high‐risk areas, but the scope and intensity varied substantially. Czechia, Croatia, Estonia, Hungary, Germany, Greece, Italy, Poland and Spain applied some form of depopulation or control of the wild boar populations, typically concentrated in restricted zones, border areas, or designated buffer zones. Among these, the strictest measures were reported in Croatia, with a zero‐population target in four counties and a 10% target elsewhere, and in Estonia, with a zero‐population target in newly infected areas. Spain pursued depopulation in lower‐risk parts of infected areas to create a ‘White Zone’, and Italy authorised both culling and trapping in selected areas. Lithuania, Latvia, Romania and Slovakia reported no targeted depopulation measures in 2025, with hunting allowed nationwide.

CONCLUSIONS

During 2025, the number of affected Member States increased from 13 to 14 following the detection of ASF in wild boar in Spain, while no new Member State reported ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs during 2025.

In the EU, the number of ASF outbreaks notified in wild boar increased from 7677 in 2024 to 11,036 in 2025, representing a change compared with the relatively stable situation observed between 2022 and 2024. In domestic pigs, the number of outbreaks increased from 333 in 2024 to 585 in 2025, although this level remained below those reported in the period 2018–2021 and in 2023. Romania continued to be the most affected Member State, accounting for 81% of all outbreaks in domestic pigs in 2025.

In neighbouring non‐EU countries, the number of ASF outbreaks decreased further in 2025, both in domestic pigs and in wild boar.

As in previous years, most ASF outbreaks notified in the EU (97.6%) occurred in NUTS 3 regions with previous presence of the disease, indicating continued local persistence and limited expansion. New NUTS 3 regions were nevertheless affected in several Member States (two due to domestic pigs and 15 due to outbreaks among wild boar), and two long distance translocation events were identified, in Western Germany and Spain, highlighting the continued risk of human‐mediated spread.

In the EU, most ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs (91%) occurred in establishments with fewer than 100 pigs. However, outbreaks were also reported in medium and large establishments, including 26 establishments with 1000–10,000 pigs and 11 in very large establishments with more than 10,000 pigs. Among domestic pigs, 83% of outbreaks were detected through passive surveillance based on clinical suspicion, followed by enhanced passive surveillance through systematic testing of dead pigs (9%) and tracing activities (7%). For outbreaks in farms with more than 1000 pigs (n = 37), this surveillance component contributed to the detection of 64% of outbreaks (n = 24). No outbreaks were detected through active surveillance of apparently healthy pigs.

Overall, 28% of the wild boar carcasses found through passive surveillance tested positive for ASF virus by PCR, accounting for 70.7% of all wild boar outbreaks in the EU. In contrast, 0.9% of hunted wild boar tested positive, representing 27.4% of detected outbreaks. As in previous years, the use of PCR testing remained central to ASF detection in wild boar, while serological testing continued to play a limited role.

ASF outbreaks in domestic pigs in 2025 retained a seasonal pattern, although with a summer peak that was less pronounced than in 2024. In wild boar, seasonality remained heterogeneous, with winter peaks observed in some Member States but no consistent seasonal pattern across the EU.

Despite the increase in the number of outbreaks in both domestic pigs and wild boar, the average size of restricted zones III increased only slightly in 2025, while the combined restricted zones II and III remained broadly stable.

RECOMMENDATIONS

The epidemiological investigations/findings from 2025 did not reveal basis for new recommendations. However, the observed increase of outbreaks underlines the need of continued awareness.

Clinical suspicion remains the main method for detecting ASF in domestic pig establishments. Therefore, all countries in Europe are encouraged to continue running awareness campaigns targeting farmers and veterinarians.

For the detection of ASF in domestic pigs, it is advised to focus surveillance efforts on passive surveillance and thereby reduce the efforts dedicated to active surveillance of healthy pigs at slaughter, before movement or randomly selected at pig establishments.

Based on previous experience, in areas and times considered to be at risk and in restricted zones, the systematic sampling of dead pigs (enhanced passive surveillance) should continue, particularly at establishments with more than 1000 pigs.

For the detection of ASF in wild boar, surveillance efforts should prioritise passive surveillance, including the search and testing of wild boar carcasses, rather than active surveillance (testing hunted wild boar).

Collection of harmonised and complete data, e.g. on laboratory results, host populations (pig and wild boar) and surveillance efforts (e.g. carcass search), is encouraged to ease the assessment of the epidemiological situation at the European level.

Collection of reliable hunting data and timely submission to ENETWILD is highly recommended to be able to monitor the evolution of wild boar populations.

The stimulating cooperation of affected countries with EFSA, including the timely submission of epidemiological data as described in the ASF guidelines, is paramount and should continue in the coming years to ensure the accurate and precise assessment of the epidemiological situation and the formulation of tailored recommendations.

Improving knowledge of the genetic characteristics of representative, circulating ASF viral isolates and ensuring data sharing is essential for transparency and for supporting epidemiological investigations.
