Isolation of novel phages from a resistant Dorea longicatena strain reveals genes associated with phage resistance
Authors: Wenqian Wang, Zhuoran Li, Grace Renata Gondowardojo, Lu Tan, Chao Wu, Ruixin Liu, Minfeng Xiao, Cong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gut bacterium Dorea longicatena has been reported to be closely associated with metabolic disorders and has emerged as a promising target for microbiome interventions aiming at improving metabolic diseases. While phages serve as effective tools for precise modulation of gut bacteria, phage resources against this and other obligate anaerobic gut bacteria remain extremely limited, hindering both therapeutic development and studies of phage–host interactions. To address this gap and the challenge of rapid resistance emergence, we performed sequential screening by first generating phage-resistant D. longicatena mutants using a previously reported set of D. longicatena phages and then using these resistant mutants as hosts to isolate additional phages from environmental samples. This approach yielded two novel lytic phages, CPB1657 and CPB1660, which remained active against 27 of the 30 tested D. longicatena strains, including the wild-type strain and resistant derivatives. Genomic characterization confirmed their lytic properties and absence of virulence or lysogeny genes. In addition, resistance to the initial phage was strongly associated with mutations in the major tail protein-like gene MAG_TPA_asm. Several mutants resistant to all tested phages shared an identical set of seven missense mutations in this gene. Together, these findings expand the currently limited phage resources available for D. longicatena and provide new insight into phage–host interactions in anaerobic gut bacteria.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

The human gut microbiota constitutes a complex ecosystem, and its homeostasis is crucial for maintaining host immunity, metabolism, and neurological functions. A healthy gut microbiota is typically characterized by high microbial diversity, functional stability, and a balanced community structure1–4, whereas dysbiosis is closely associated with metabolic disorders and diseases such as obesity5,6. Recent metagenomic studies have identified several key microbial taxa associated with obesity, among which the obligate anaerobic bacterium Dorea longicatena has shown a persistent positive correlation with obesity indicators such as waist circumference and Body Mass Index (BMI), as well as with comorbidities like type 2 diabetes and dysregulation of the immune system7–11. This suggests that D. longicatena may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases, thereby highlighting it as a potential intervention target. Currently, specific regulatory strategies targeting this bacterium are limited. Phages, as the predominant members of the gut virome, possess the unique ability to specifically infect host bacteria, offering a distinct advantage in modulating microbial community structure and function12–14. However, due to technical challenges, particularly the isolation and characterization of phages specific to obligate anaerobic bacteria, only a few relevant phages have been documented in existing databases15,16. The resources related to phages targeting D. longicatena are particularly scarce, and the mechanisms underlying their interactions with the host remain largely unexplored17,18.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to expand the collection of phages targeting D. longicatena and to provide preliminary insights into their interaction with hosts. We first generated resistant bacterial strains using a previously reported panel of D. longicatena phages18 and subsequently succeeded in isolating two novel virulent phages capable of infecting these resistant strains from environmental samples, CPB1657 and CPB1660. We conducted a systematic characterization of the biological and genomic features of these two novel phages and further identified a MAG_TPA_asm gene that was strongly associated with resistance in the host, in which recurrent mutations were observed in resistant derivatives. This study not only enriches the phage resources available for D. longicatena but also provides initial insights into the interactions between this bacterium and phages, establishing a foundation for future phage-based precision microbiome modulation strategies.

Results

Isolation of phages against the D. longicatena phage-resistant strains

Building upon a pre-existing collection of 7 lytic phages isolated using wild-type D. longicatena strains 3917 and 0288 as hosts18, we first generated 29 phage-resistant mutants by exposing the parental strains to individual phages or to a phage cocktail under selective pressure. We then performed a second round of phage screening using these resistant mutants as hosts. In total, 26 samples, including sewage, fecal samples, and bovine rumen contents, were screened using a three-round repetitive enrichment approach. From this screening effort, CPB1657 and CPB1660 were ultimately obtained from sewage and bovine rumen samples, respectively, using D. longicatena 0288-1, a mutant resistant to the entire seven-phage cocktail, as the primary host. Both phages produced clear plaques on the isolation host (Fig. 1a, b).

Biological characteristics of CPB1657 and CPB1660

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis showed that both CPB1657 and CPB1660 exhibited an icosahedral capsid and a short, non-contractile tail. The capsid diameter was 49.9 ± 4.2 nm for CPB1657 and 51.0 ± 4.3 nm for CPB1660, while the tail length was 8.0 ± 1.3 nm and 8.0 ± 1.8 nm, respectively, consistent with a podovirus-like morphology within the class Caudoviricetes (Fig. 1c, d).

To characterize their replication cycles, one-step growth curve analysis was performed (Fig. 2a). Both phages exhibited relatively long latent periods, estimated at ~75–80 min for CPB1657 and 70 min for CPB1660. The estimated burst sizes were ~29 PFU/cell for CPB1657 and 27 PFU/cell for CPB1660 (Table 1). These results suggest that CPB1657 and CPB1660 undergo delayed progeny release with moderate burst sizes. Compared with the well-adapted gut phage ΦCrAss00119, which has a longer latent period of 120 min and a lower burst size of 2.5 PFU/cell, CPB1657 and CPB1660 show similarly delayed, although more productive, lytic replication.

Comparative analysis of phage replication parameters

Killing-curve assays were performed by monitoring bacterial growth after phage addition (Fig. 2b). The two phages displayed distinct lysis dynamics. CPB1657 induced a rapid decline in host culture density and achieved near-complete culture clearance within 8 h, with a final OD of 0.16. In contrast, CPB1660 showed a slower and more gradual reduction in bacterial growth, requiring ~16 h to reach a comparable lytic outcome and yielding a final OD of 0.206. Together, these results show that CPB1657 exerted a faster bacteriolytic effect than CPB1660 under the tested conditions.

Stability of phages under different pH levels

We next assessed the stability of phages CPB1657 and CPB1660 across a pH range from 2 to 10 (Fig. 3). Following a 1-h incubation at 37 °C at each pH, both phages maintained high titers (≥1010 PFU/mL) within the near-neutral range (pH 6–7). Phage activity decreased under acidic (pH 2–4) and alkaline (pH 8–10) conditions, although substantial infectivity was retained, with titers generally remaining above 107 PFU/mL. Overall, similar pH response trends were observed for both phages.

Phage genome characterization

Phages CPB1657 and CPB1660 possess double-stranded DNA genomes of 24,979 bp and 33,875 bp, with GC contents of 32% and 33%, respectively (Fig. 4). Genome annotation predicted 54 and 77 open reading frames (ORFs) for CPB1657 and CPB1660, respectively. In both genomes, predicted gene products were assigned to functional categories including DNA/RNA and nucleotide metabolism, head and packaging, connector, tail, lysis, and unknown or other functions, as indicated in the figure. The GC-skew patterns differed between the two genomes, with CPB1657 showing both positive and negative skew regions, whereas CPB1660 exhibited a predominantly negative skew pattern. Neither genome contained genes associated with lysogeny, virulence, or antimicrobial resistance. A single tRNA gene was identified in CPB1657 but not in CPB1660.

Phage comparative genomics and phylogenetic analysis

To assess the genomic similarity, we performed an average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis on all nine phages, including the two newly isolated phages from phage-resistant mutant hosts (CPB1657 and CPB1660) and the seven previously phages reported from the wild-type host. The results showed that they could be divided into two main subgroups, with an overall high ANI similarity (Fig. 5a). CPB1660 grouped with CPB0990, CPB1091, and CPB1092, whereas CPB1657 was positioned closer to the subgroup containing CPB0533, CPB1093, CPB0534, and CPB0989. These results indicate that the two newly isolated phages are incorporated into the genomic diversity of the previously reported D. longicatena phages rather than forming a separate lineage. However, the ANI values for some phage pairs were approximately 92%, indicating a degree of genomic divergence.

Whole-genome alignment further showed that, despite the differences described above, the overall genome architecture and synteny of the nine phages were largely conserved (Fig. 5b). This conserved organization included the back-to-back arrangement of the DNA packaging and holin genes, a feature previously identified in the seven wild-type-host phages18. Notably, this arrangement was duplicated with identical sequence in CPB1657, CPB1660, and the previously characterized phage CPB1091. In addition, CPB1657 and CPB1660 encoded multiple copies of structural genes, including major capsid and tail protein genes, which were predominantly present as single copies in the previously reported wild-type-host phages. Both phages also carried genes not broadly shared across the other isolates, including a carboxypeptidase A homolog and several hypothetical proteins.

To further assess their broader genomic relationships, we conducted a whole-genome analysis using vConTACT2. All nine D. longicatena phages formed a tight and distinct cluster that was clearly separated from reference phage genomes in the network (Supplementary Fig. 1). This clustering supports their classification as a closely related group and suggests the existence of a previously uncharacterized phage family. Together with the ANI and whole-genome alignment results, these data indicate that the nine phages retain high similarity in their core genome structure despite internal genomic divergence in specific genomic regions.

Phage–bacteria interactions

To evaluate the lytic spectrum of CPB1657 and CPB1660, we assessed their activity against a collection of 30 D. longicatena strains (Fig. 6a), comprising 29 resistant strains and one wild-type strain (Supplementary Table 2). Both phages showed activity against 27 out of 30 tested strains (90%), including the wild-type strain and the majority of resistant derivatives. However, great heterogeneity in resistance patterns was observed. Strains 0533-5, 0533-8, and 0533-9, all of which are resistant mutants induced by phage CPB0533, exhibited complete resistance to both CPB1657 and CPB1660.

To elucidate the genetic basis of resistance in these mutants, we performed whole-genome sequencing and analyzed mutation frequencies across functional gene categories (Fig. 6b). Mutation profiling identified the MAG_TPA_asm, annotated as a major tail protein-like gene, as the gene with the highest number of mutation events (n = 53), followed by the cell membrane-related gene DbpA (n = 31). The strain-level distribution of these mutation events is provided in Supplementary Table 3. The preferential accumulation of mutations in the MAG_TPA_asm gene highlighted it as a primary candidate gene associated with phage resistance.

Building on these findings, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on the MAG_TPA_asm sequences and integrated it with the lytic spectrum heatmap to assess the genotype-phenotype relationship (Fig. 6a). The analysis revealed a distinct, tightly clustered sub-clade formed by strains 0533-5, 0533-8, and 0533-9. A detailed analysis of missense mutations within MAG_TPA_asm (Supplementary Table 1) showed that these three strains shared an identical set of seven missense mutations. By contrast, strain 0534-2, which branched separately from the 0533 sub-clade, carried nine missense mutations in the same gene, only five of which overlapped with the 0533-derived strains. The remaining resistant mutants showed different mutation patterns and did not group into a similarly tight sub-clade. Furthermore, strains that remained susceptible to CPB1657 and/or CPB1660 were phylogenetically dispersed rather than concentrated in a single lineage. Together, these results indicate that the 0533-derived strains resistant to both CPB1657 and CPB1660 shared a signature mutation profile in MAG_TPA_asm.

Given that MAG_TPA_asm was the most frequently mutated gene, we further examined its genomic context and confirmed that it is not located within any predicted prophage regions. Notably, strains 0533-5, 0533-8, and 0533-9, all selected under CPB0533 pressure, were resistant to all tested phages and harbored the same unique set of seven missense mutations in MAG_TPA_asm, whereas this mutation pattern was not observed in the other analyzed strains. Taken together, these observations highlight MAG_TPA_asm as the most prominent resistance-associated gene identified in the analyzed mutants.

Discussion

In this study, we isolated and characterized two novel lytic phages, CPB1657 and CPB1660, using phage-resistant Dorea longicatena mutants as screening hosts. This resistance-informed isolation strategy enabled the recovery of phages that remained active against the wild-type strain as well as the majority of resistant derivatives, thereby expanding the currently limited phage resources available for D. longicatena. In addition, comparative genomic and mutant analyses identified a host gene, MAG_TPA_asm, that was strongly associated with resistance, providing new insight into phage–host interactions in this difficult-to-culture gut anaerobe.

This study demonstrates that the strategic use of resistant bacterial derivatives as hosts for additional phage isolation is an effective approach for D. longicatena, a poorly characterized obligate anaerobe for which cultured phage resources remain limited. While this strategy is not conceptually unprecedented20,21, its application here proved essential in expanding the Gut Phage Biobank (GPB) and filling critical gaps in the representation of gut anaerobic viruses. The recovery of CPB1657 and CPB1660 not only enriches the available phage resource for this host but also further expands the known genomic diversity of this phage group. Our ANI analysis and vConTACT2 clustering collectively indicate that despite internal genomic heterogeneity, these nine D. longicatena phages share highly conserved genomic characteristics and form a cohesive cluster clearly separated from reference genomes. These findings suggest that this group may represent a previously uncharacterized phage family within the human gut virome18.

In addition to the shared genomic structure, the different functions of these phages appear to be related to specific genetic changes within each lineage. Notably, CPB1657 and CPB1660 exhibited different infection dynamics: the former induced rapid host lysis, whereas the latter displayed a more gradual bacteriolytic effect. This phenotypic divergence may be related to the accessory genetic features, such as the zinc-dependent carboxypeptidase A homolog identified in the newly isolated phages. Given the established role of such peptidases in peptidoglycan remodeling and cell wall degradation, this homolog could potentially facilitate the circumvention of host resistance barriers22–24. Furthermore, the conserved back-to-back arrangement of the DNA packaging and holin genes across the entire set suggests that this uncommon arrangement may reflect the use of a shared promoter region. This arrangement could help to compact the genome organization and facilitate temporal coordination between DNA packaging and late-stage lysis25,26.

Increased pressure from phage predation leads to the evolution of host defenses, often resulting in an “arms race”. We observed a clear pattern of convergent evolution, where different phages selected for resistant mutants that shared identical missense mutations in the MAG_TPA_asm gene. Specifically, the mutants selected by CPB0533 (strains 0533-5, 0533-8, and 0533-9) exhibited broad resistance to all tested phages and carried a consistent set of seven missense mutations in this specific gene. This genetic convergence, combined with the tight clustering of these strains, suggests that MAG_TPA_asm is a key target for mutations that provide resistance to all tested phages, similar to patterns seen in other bacteria–phage coevolution studies27. Interestingly, these CPB0533-selected mutants showed altered growth behavior during routine liquid cultivation, characterized by slower growth rates and spontaneous sedimentation. While we did not quantify this change in this study, the phenotype is consistent with a physiological trade-off, where the cost of gaining phage resistance is a reduction in bacterial fitness27,28. These observations point to a genetic link between MAG_TPA_asm and phage susceptibility. One possible hypothesis is that sequence changes in MAG_TPA_asm may affect interactions with host surface-associated components, thereby changing the accessibility of structures required for phage adsorption. This idea is conceptually related to superinfection exclusion systems, in which prophage-encoded proteins can modify or mask cell-surface receptors and prevent infection by related phages29–31. Although CPB1657 and CPB1660 are lytic phages and MAG_TPA_asm was not located within a predicted prophage region in our analysis, similar receptor-accessibility logic may help explain why mutations in a tail protein-like gene are associated with altered phage susceptibility. The molecular basis of this association remains to be experimentally defined.

Despite these findings, several limitations of this study should be noted. First, our experiments were performed entirely in vitro under controlled anaerobic conditions. These setups do not capture the ecological complexity of the gut environment, where microbial interactions, spatial structure, and host-associated factors may substantially influence phage performance. Second, while we focused on D. longicatena, it remains unclear whether the genomic and resistance patterns observed here are representative of other gut anaerobes. Finally, the molecular mechanisms underlying our observations require further validation. Specifically, the phage genetic features that enable CPB1657 and CPB1660 to infect resistant derivatives remain unresolved. Similarly, the role of MAG_TPA_asm in phage susceptibility was inferred from comparative genomics and mutation profiling; direct functional evidence, such as from adsorption assays or gene complementation experiments, is still needed to confirm these associations.

Overall, this study expands the currently limited collection of cultured D. longicatena phages by adding two novel lytic isolates that remained active against the wild-type strain and most resistant derivatives tested. Beyond enlarging the available phage resource for this poorly characterized gut anaerobe, our results show that resistant-host-guided isolation can be effective for recovering additional phages active against resistant host derivatives. At the same time, the recurrent mutation pattern observed in MAG_TPA_asm provides a useful entry point for dissecting host genetic changes associated with phage resistance. Together, these findings establish a practical framework for studying phage–host interactions in poorly characterized gut anaerobes and provide a basis for future mechanistic and phage-based microbiome modulation studies.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The obligate anaerobic gut bacterium Dorea longicatena was used in this study. All strains were cultivated in an anaerobic workstation (Whitley A45, Meintrup DWS, Germany) under an atmosphere of 5% H₂, 5% CO₂, and 90% N₂ at 37 °C.

To meet the nutritional and stringent anaerobic requirements of D. longicatena, a modified Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) medium, designated BHI-YH, was used18. The BHI-YH liquid medium was formulated by supplementing standard BHI broth with 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract and 5 mg/L hemoglobin to provide a richer nutrient base. Additionally, 0.5% (w/v) L-cysteine and 0.025% (w/v) sodium sulfide (Na₂S·9H₂O) were added as reducing agents to establish and maintain a strict anaerobic environment. For solid media, 1.5% (w/v) agar was added to the BHI-YH formulation. For the double-layer agar assays used in phage isolation and titration, the bottom layer contained 1.5% agar, and the top (soft) layer contained 0.7% agar.

To initiate active cultures, 10 µL from a glycerol stock was streaked onto a BHI-YH agar plate and incubated for 1–2 days. A single colony was then inoculated into 1 mL of BHI-YH liquid medium and incubated overnight to generate a high-density bacterial culture for subsequent experiments.

Isolation of phage-resistant D. longicatena mutants

Wild-type bacteria D. longicatena 3917 and wild-type D. longicatena 0288 were cultured overnight anaerobically at 37 °C in BHI-YH broth. To isolate phage-resistant mutants, we exposed the wild-type bacteria to the individual lysates of the 7 isolated phages or the mixture of previous 7 phages18 (each at a concentration adjusted to ~10⁸–10⁹ PFU/ml) and then spread them on BHI-YH agar plates. After drying, ten-fold diluted overnight bacterial culture was overlaid. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C overnight. Approximately ten putative resistant colonies were picked from each plate and inoculated into fresh liquid medium to establish a primary screening library.

To further enrich and confirm resistance, a multi-round co-culture and validation strategy was employed. Wild-type phage lysate was mixed with log-phase bacterial culture and co-cultured overnight. The culture was then centrifuged, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended, serially diluted, and plated. About twenty colonies from these plates were cultured individually. Resistance phenotypes were confirmed using a double-layer agar spot assay with both the original wild-type phage lysate and induced supernatants. Strains exhibiting stable resistance in both confirmation assays were preserved at −80 °C in glycerol stocks. The species identity of all resistant mutants was confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Phage isolation

Phages were isolated from two sources: sewage collected from the Jiangsu River system and bovine rumen contents purchased from a slaughterhouse in Shenzhen. For sewage samples, 50 mL of material was centrifuged (8000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. For bovine rumen contents samples, homogenization was performed in distilled water (2:3, w/v), followed by centrifugation (8000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C) and filtration (0.45 μm). The filtered samples from both sources were then subjected to a three-round enrichment protocol. For enrichment, 1 mL of the filtered sample was inoculated into 9 mL of BHI-YH broth supplemented with 1 mM CaCl₂ and MgCl₂. Late log-phase bacterial host strains were added at a 1:100 ratio, and the culture was incubated anaerobically at 37 °C overnight. The mixture was then centrifuged (5000 × g, 15 min), and the supernatant was passed through a 0.45 μm filter. From this filtrate, 1 mL was transferred into 9 mL of fresh supplemented broth, re-inoculated with host bacteria at the same ratio, and the incubation and harvesting steps were repeated. This serial enrichment cycle was performed for a total of three rounds. After the third enrichment, the cultures from all rounds were pooled. Phages were concentrated from the pooled culture by adding a precipitant solution (1 M NaCl and 10% (w/v) PEG-8000) and incubating at 4 °C overnight. The mixture was then centrifuged (12,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of SM buffer to obtain the concentrated phage solution.

To identify isolated phages, spot assays were performed using the concentrated phage solution. Resulting plaques were picked, subjected to further purification, and the final isolated phages were stored at 4 °C.

Phage purification

For phage purification, a 10 μL aliquot of phage solution (SM buffer containing a single plaque) was applied to a double-layer plate seeded with the host bacterium in BHI-YH medium. After overnight incubation at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions, a single well-isolated plaque was picked from the bacterial lawn and transferred into 1 mL of SM buffer. The recovered phage suspension was then re-plated on fresh double-layer plates prepared with the same BHI-YH medium under the same anaerobic conditions for further purification. This single-plaque purification step was repeated for 3–5 rounds to obtain a homogeneous phage solution.

Phage enrichment and titration

Purified phages were enriched via spot assay. Specifically, 10–20 μL of the pure phage solution was spotted and incubated overnight to form a large plaque. The resulting plaque was then added to a 50 mL culture of host bacteria in the early log phase for overnight incubation. The culture was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane to obtain the phage lysate, which was stored at 4 °C. To determine the phage concentration, the lysate was subjected to a 1:10 dilution with SM buffer, and spot assays were conducted.

Transmission electron microscopy

High-purity phage samples for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were prepared using a sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation method for concentration and preliminary purification32. A 20% (w/v) sucrose solution (10 mL) was carefully underlaid beneath the phage lysate to form a density cushion. The sample was then centrifuged at 59,057 × g for 3 h at 4 °C, allowing phage particles to pellet through the sucrose layer. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was gently resuspended in 1 mL of SM buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 100 mM NaCl; 8 mM MgSO₄) to minimize damage to phage structures. To remove residual sucrose, salts, and other small molecules, the resuspended sample was transferred to a dialysis bag with a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) and dialyzed against 1 L of SM buffer at 4 °C for 4 h. The buffer was replaced with another 1 L of fresh SM buffer, and dialysis continued overnight. The final purified samples were sent to Beijing Geo Analysis Technology Co., Ltd. for TEM imaging.

One-step growth curve analysis

The reproductive characteristics of the phages were determined using a one-step growth curve protocol. Mid-logarithmic phase D. longicatena cultures were infected with phage at an MOI of 0.1 to ensure synchronous infection. After 15 min of adsorption at 37 °C, the culture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to remove unadsorbed phages. The pellet was resuspended in 50 mL of fresh pre-warmed BHI-YH broth and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C. Samples were collected every 5 min for up to 120 min. Each sample was immediately centrifuged at 7000 × g for 30 s, and the supernatant was serially diluted for plaque assay determination. The latent period was defined as the time interval from infection to the first detectable increase in phage titer. The burst size was calculated as the ratio of the final phage titer at the plateau phase to the initial number of infected bacterial cells.

Phage killing curve

To assess the killing curves, the log-phase D. longicatena culture was prepared by diluting 15 μL fresh overnight D. longicatena culture in 150 μL BHI-YH broth (pH ~7.2) supplemented with 1 mM CaCl2 and MgCl2 in the 96-well plates. The bacterial culture was allowed to grow under anaerobic conditions until OD600nm 0.3, then was mixed with 15 μL of phage. Subsequently, this mixture was incubated anaerobically at 37 °C with orbital shaking in the microplate reader. Turbidity measurements at OD600nm were taken inside the anaerobic chamber every 10 min to monitor bacterial growth for 22 h, which was then plotted on a dotted graph. For control variables, bacterial hosts were mixed with BHI-YH broth serving as the phage-free control, while pure BHI-YH liquid broth became the blank control. Each experiment group was done in triplicate.

pH stability assay

For the pH stability assay, 100 μL of phage (1010 PFU/mL) was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in 100 μL of pH solution. A 1 h incubation was used in accordance with conditions commonly adopted in previous phage-stability studies and to maintain comparability with the temperature-stability assay33.The pH solutions were prepared from SM buffer mixed with 0.1 mol/L NaOH or 0.1 mol/L HCl to create different even pH values ranging from pH 2 to 10. The phage titer was then assessed by the spot test method.

Lytic spectrum determination

The lytic spectrum of the isolated phages was determined against 30 D. longicatena strains (including wild-type and phage-resistant mutants) using spot tests. 5 μL of each phage lysate (≈10⁸ PFU/mL) was spotted onto a soft agar overlay containing the test bacterial strain. The plates were examined for plaque formation or zone of clearance after overnight anaerobic incubation at 37 °C. A strain was considered sensitive if a clear lytic spot was observed. The spot-assay results were interpreted here as lytic spectrum rather than definitive host range34,35.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using the Tianamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen Biochemical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Phage genomic DNA was extracted from enriched lysates as follows: DNase I and RNase A were added to 50 mL of phage lysate (obtained as described in the phage enrichment step) to a final concentration of 10 μg/mL each, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. A second round of nucleases was added, followed by incubation for an additional 30 min. The nucleases were inactivated at 65 °C for 20 min. A precipitant solution was then added to achieve a final concentration of 1 M NaCl and 10% (w/v) PEG-8000. After thorough mixing, the solution was stored at 4 °C overnight. The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C, 12,000 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of SM buffer. Subsequently, 0.5% SDS and 5 μL of proteinase K were added, and the resuspension was incubated at 56 °C for 1 h. A second addition of proteinase K was followed by a further 1‑h incubation. Proteinase K was inactivated at 65 °C for 20 min. The phage DNA was finally purified using the GeneClean® Turbo Kit (MP Biomedicals, #111102400) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

For both bacterial and phage DNA, single-end libraries were prepared using the MGI Easy Universal DNA Library Prep Kit. Bacterial libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ platform (MGI, BGI-Shenzhen, China), while phage genomic DNA was sequenced on the CycloneSEQ platform (MGI, BGI-Shenzhen, China). For each sequenced phage or bacterial genome, ~2 Gb of raw sequencing data were generated.

Genome assembly and quality assessment

De novo assembly of both phage and bacterial genomes was performed. For the two phage genomes, assembly was conducted using Unicycler v0.5.136, and contig completeness was evaluated with CheckV v1.0.137. For bacterial genomes, the wild-type D. longicatena 3917 strain and all 29 phage-resistant mutants were independently assembled using SPAdes v4.3.038. The quality of each bacterial assembly was assessed using CheckM2 v1.0.239, confirming high completeness (>99%) and low contamination (<1%).

Genome annotation and comparative genomics

Prophage regions in the wild-type D. longicatena 3917 genome were predicted using PHASTER40 and PhageBoost41. Both phage and bacterial genome assemblies were annotated with Pharokka v1.8.042 to obtain a baseline set of gene predictions. For phage genomes specifically, coding sequences (CDS) were predicted with PHANOTATE43, tRNAs were predicted with tRNAscan-SE 2.044, tmRNAs were predicted with Aragorn45, and CRISPRs were predicted with CRT43,46. Functional annotation was generated by matching each CDS to the PHROGs47, VFDB48, and CARD49 databases using MMseqs250 and PyHMMER51. Contigs were matched to their closest hit in the INPHARED database52 using mash53. Circular phage genome maps were created with the phold plot54.

For comparative analysis, Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) among the nine phage genomes was calculated using fastANI v1.3.455, and the resulting distance matrix was visualized as a heatmap in R (v4.5.1). Genome structures were compared using Easyfig v3.456. A whole-genome-based taxonomic analysis of all nine phages was conducted using vConTACT257 to assess their phylogenetic placement.

Mutant bacteria genome analysis

The annotated assembly of the wild-type D. longicatena 3917 strain served as the reference genome. Genetic variants (SNPs and INDELs) in the mutant isolates were identified by aligning their assembled genomes against this reference using Snippy v4.6.058. The functional impacts of variants were annotated and filtered for moderate-to-high impact mutations using SnpEff & SnpSift v5.0.259,60 with a custom-built D. longicatena 3917 database. Evolutionary analysis focused on the major tail protein gene, which exhibited the highest mutation frequency. Homologous sequences were retrieved via tBLASTn (BLAST+ v2.17.0)61, aligned with MAFFT v7.52562, and used to construct a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (1000 ultrafast bootstraps)63. The tree was rooted with the wild-type strain as the outgroup and visualized in iTOL v7.2.064.

Statistics

Data processing and figure generation were performed using R (version 4.5.1) and OriginPro 2025b (Learning Edition). For the quantitative analyses presented in this study, the data points on the curves represent the mean values of three independent biological replicates, and the y-error bars indicate the standard deviation among replicates.
