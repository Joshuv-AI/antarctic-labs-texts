Evaluating Korean Lung-RADS Category 2b: Diagnostic Performance and Interreader Agreement in Lung Cancer Screening
Authors: You Na Kim, Seulgi You, Joo Sung Sun, Kyung Joo Park, Seohyeon Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKorean Lung Computed Tomography Screening Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) category 2b was introduced to reduce false positives in a tuberculosis-endemic setting. Although its clinical validity has been partly demonstrated, interreader agreement in applying category 2b remains unclear. The aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance and interreader agreement of the modified classification.MethodsThis retrospective study included 1,371 participants who underwent low-dose chest computed tomography (CT) scans as part of the Korean national lung cancer screening program between September 2019 and January 2024 at a single tertiary hospital. Korean Lung-RADS incorporates category 2b for nodules that are likely benign—such as tuberculous granulomas or intrapulmonary lymph nodes—even if their size corresponds to category 3 or 4. All CT scans were interpreted using Korean Lung-RADS (version 1.0 until 2021, version 1.1 from 2022 through 2023, and version 2022 implemented in 2024). To compare diagnostic performance, all category 2b nodules were reclassified into original Lung-RADS categories based on nodule size and type, and diagnostic metrics were evaluated for both systems. To assess interreader agreement, three thoracic radiologists independently reviewed nodules initially categorized as 2b to determine whether they would also assign category 2b. Diagnostic performance was compared using McNemar and Park’s permutation tests, whereas interreader agreement was analyzed using the Fleiss κ statistic.ResultsCategory 2b nodules were found in 29 of the 1,371 participants (2.1%). Among them, one (3.5%) was diagnosed with lung cancer, whereas the remaining 28 (96.6%) were benign. Interreader agreement for 2b categorization was fair (Fleiss κ = 0.35; 95% confidence interval, 0.17–0.56). In terms of diagnostic performance, Korean Lung-RADS demonstrated significantly higher specificity (92.5% vs. 90.4%, P < 0.001) and comparable sensitivity (89.3% vs. 92.9%, P = 1.00) compared with the original Lung-RADS.ConclusionKorean Lung-RADS category 2b contributes to reducing false positives in lung cancer screening. However, its subjective nature results in fair interreader agreement, underscoring the need for greater standardization to improve consistency in its application.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer screening (LCS) using low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) is an effective method to reduce lung cancer mortality in high-risk populations.1234 As LCS programs have been implemented worldwide, the Lung Computed Tomography Screening Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) has been widely adopted to standardize computed tomography (CT) interpretation and guide follow-up management of pulmonary nodules.5

In South Korea, a nationwide LCS program using LDCT has been implemented since 2019 for high-risk current smokers aged 54–74 years with a smoking history of ≥ 30 pack-years.6 To reduce false-positive interpretations during LCS, South Korea has adopted the Korean Lung-RADS to LCS, a modified version of the original Lung-RADS proposed by the Korean Society of Thoracic Radiology.7 Korean Lung-RADS introduces a new category, 2b, which applies to nodules that are highly likely to be benign—such as tuberculous granulomas and intrapulmonary lymph nodes—even if their size corresponds to category 3 or 4.78

This local modification was adopted because of the high incidence of pulmonary tuberculosis in South Korea, which reached 39 per 100,000 population in 2022—ranking second among countries in the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development.9 Because post-infectious granulomas are more frequently encountered here than in Western populations, false-positive interpretations during LCS are more common, with tuberculous granulomas being a major contributing factor.10 Additionally, intrapulmonary lymph nodes, although typically benign, also contribute to false-positive interpretations during LCS.11121314 These nodules are frequently encountered in screening practice, with a reported prevalence of 22.8–28.0%,1112 and typically appear as juxtapleural or perifissural nodules with a lentiform, oval, or triangular shape, sharp margins, and diameters of < 10 mm.15 Thus, nodules suspected to represent these entities are assigned to category 2b to reduce false-positive interpretations in LCS, regardless of their size. Patients with category 2b nodules are recommended to continue annual LDCT screening at 12-month intervals, consistent with the follow-up recommendation for Lung-RADS category 2.7

A previous study reported that Korean Lung-RADS improves specificity compared with the original system.16 However, this study assessed outcomes based on lung cancer diagnosed within 1 year after LCS, highlighting the need for analyses incorporating longer follow-up data. In addition, interreader agreement in the classification of category 2b nodules has not yet been evaluated. As Korean Lung-RADS category 2b is currently implemented in the Korean national LCS, understanding its diagnostic performance and interreader consistency is necessary for optimizing screening guidelines and policy decisions. Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the diagnostic performance of Korean Lung-RADS over a longer follow-up period and assess interreader agreement in classifying category 2b nodules to identify potential variability and factors influencing practical application of the modified classification system.

METHODS

Participants and study design

For this study, participants who underwent baseline LDCT examinations at a single tertiary hospital between September 2019 and January 2024 as part of the national LCS program (n = 2,931) were included. In South Korea’s LCS program, eligible participants are current smokers aged 54–74 years with a smoking history exceeding 30 pack-years.7

Final diagnoses were established for all participants using a reference standard (Supplementary Data 1). From the initial cohort, 1,560 participants were excluded owing to indeterminate diagnoses (n = 1,540), a prior history of lung or bronchial malignancies (n = 4), metastases from other primary tumors (n = 5), or true interval cancer (n = 11). True interval cancer was defined as cancer that newly developed during follow-up at a site where no visible nodule was present on the baseline CT, even on retrospective review. Although these true interval cancers arose from previously normal-appearing parenchyma, some participants were initially classified into category 2 or 3 because of other unrelated nodules. After exclusion, a total of 1,371 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

CT acquisition and image reconstruction

Non-contrast-enhanced CT scans were performed using one of two scanners: Ingenuity (Phillips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands) or Somatom Drive (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany). Images were acquired at end-inspiration with a tube voltage of 120 kV and tube reference current of 24 mAs, using automatic tube current modulation. The volume CT dose index was maintained below 3.0 mGy. All images were reconstructed using filtered back projection and iterative reconstruction with slice thicknesses of 3 mm and 1 mm in the axial plane, respectively, and 3 mm in the coronal plane.

Chest CT interpretation and data collection

Chest CT scans were randomly assigned and interpreted by one of three thoracic radiologists as part of their routine clinical practice (reader 1, reader 2 and reader 3, with 37, 22, and 7 years of post-training clinical experience, respectively). Of the total 1,371 CT scans, reader 1 interpreted 31.2% (428/1,371), reader 2 interpreted 37.9% (519/1,371), and reader 3 interpreted 30.9% (424/1,371). All CT images were interpreted according to Korean Lung-RADS: version 1.0 until 2021, version 1.1 from 2022 to 2023, and version 2022 starting in 2024. Although Lung-RADS 1.1 and version 2022 were released in 2019 and 2022, respectively, their implementation in South Korea was delayed until 2022 and 2024 owing to the time required for national regulatory approval and related updates to health insurance policies.

A separate thoracic radiologist (reader 4, with 2 years of post-training clinical experience), who was not involved in the initial interpretation, retrospectively reviewed the chest CT reports and electronic medical records to minimize potential bias. Collected data included patient characteristics (age, sex, and smoking history including pack-years, if available), pathology results (if biopsy or surgical resection was performed), and information from CT reports (final Lung-RADS category, recorded nodule size, type, location, and nodule-specific categorization). For lung cancer cases, the time to diagnosis was also recorded, defined as the number of days between the baseline LDCT and the date of histopathologic confirmation.

Retrospective analysis of category 2b nodules

Category 2b was defined as nodules considered highly likely to be benign, such as tuberculous granulomas or juxtapleural/perifissural nodules, even if their size corresponded to category 3 or 4. For comparison between Korean Lung-RADS and the original Lung-RADS, all nodules initially assigned to category 2b were re-evaluated by one radiologist who did not participate in the initial assessment (reader 4). Each nodule was reassigned to the appropriate Lung-RADS category based on nodule type and size, and the Lung-RADS version corresponding to the date of the CT examination was applied. To explore interpretation patterns by reader experience, the radiologists who originally assigned category 2b were retrospectively identified for descriptive analysis. In addition, the radiologist identified the underlying reason for each original category 2b assignment, classifying them as either tuberculous granulomas or juxtapleural/perifissural nodules.

Interreader agreement for category 2b nodules

Considering that category 2b is a subjective classification without defined criteria, interreader agreement for category 2b was analyzed. Evaluation of category 2b nodules was performed by three thoracic radiologists (reader 2, reader 3, and reader 4) after a minimum 1-month washout period following initial CT interpretation or data review to reduce potential recall bias. All readers were provided with the full CT scans along with information on the image number of 2b nodule and location, and were informed that each nodule had previously been classified as category 2b. Although blinded to follow-up outcomes, the observers were asked to assess whether they would also categorize each nodule as: a) category 2b, b) not category 2b and instead assign a category based on size, or c) equivocal.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The diagnostic performance of Korean Lung-RADS and Lung-RADS was assessed by calculating the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Performance was calculated based on dichotomized results, where categories 3 and 4 were considered positive screenings and categories 1 and 2 were considered negative screenings. Category 2b was considered negative in the Korean Lung-RADS system for performance analysis. All diagnostic performance metrics were calculated on a per-participant basis. Sensitivity and specificity were compared using McNemar’s test with continuity correction, whereas accuracy, PPV, and NPV were compared using Park’s permutation test.17 Interreader agreement for the reassessment of category 2b nodules among the three readers was evaluated using the multirater Fleiss κ statistic. In addition, linear weighted Cohen κ statistics were used to measure agreement in each pairing of readers, and pairwise κ values were averaged across reader pairs. The κ values were classified as follows: < 0.20, slight; 0.20–0.39, fair; 0.40–0.59, moderate; 0.60–0.79, substantial; ≥ 0.80, almost perfect.18 All statistical analysis was performed using MedCalc version 22 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium) and R software version 4.2.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Ajou University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB), and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB No. AJOUIRB-DB-2024-399).

RESULTS

Characteristics of participants

A total of 1,371 participants were included in this study, comprising 1,340 men (97.7%) and 31 women (2.3%), with a median age of 61 years (IQR, 58–65 years). Among participants with available smoking history (n = 1,133), the median smoking burden was 35 pack-years (IQR, 30–40 pack-years). The median duration of follow-up was 829 days (IQR, 651–1,337 days). A detailed summary of participant characteristics is provided in Table 1. Positive screening results (categories 3 and 4) were 9.7% (126/1,371) based on Korean Lung-RADS and 11.3% (155/1,371) based on Lung-RADS, respectively. Lung cancer was diagnosed in 28 participants (2.0%), with the remaining 1,343 participants (98.0%) having either benign or no detectable nodules.

Characteristics and Korean Lung-RADS categorization of lung cancer

Among the 28 diagnosed cases of lung cancer, the initial Korean Lung-RADS categorization was as follows: one case (3.6%) was classified as category 1, one (3.6%) as category 2, one (3.6%) as category 2b, one (3.6%) as category 3, seven (25.0%) as category 4A, nine (32.1%) as category 4B, and eight (28.6%) as category 4X. Adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma were the most frequently observed histologic subtypes, each accounting for 10 cases (35.7%) of the 28 lung cancers (Table 2). The time to lung cancer diagnosis was 224 days, 1,279 days, 785 days, 857 days, 134 days (IQR, 109–242 days), 38 days (IQR, 24–73 days), and 37 days (IQR, 31–41 days) for category 1, 2, 2b, 3, 4A, 4B, and 4X, respectively. The category 1 case was likely a missed cancer at the baseline, initially interpreted as non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection because of multiple cavitary and non-cavitary centrilobular nodules and bronchiectasis. The follow-up CT at six months revealed enlargement of a single cavitary nodule among the multiple lesions, which was subsequently diagnosed as non-small cell lung cancer. The category 2 case was a ground-glass nodule that remained stable for the first 1.5 years of follow-up. However, on a CT scan obtained 2 years later (3.5 years after the baseline), the nodule had doubled in size and was confirmed as minimally invasive adenocarcinoma.

Comparison of diagnostic performance between Korean Lung-RADS and Lung-RADS

The diagnostic performance metrics are summarized in Table 3. The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of Korean Lung-RADS were 89.3% (95% confidence interval [CI], 71.8–97.7), 92.5% (95% CI, 90.9–93.8), and 92.4% (95% CI, 90.9–93.8), respectively, whereas those of Lung-RADS were 92.9% (95% CI, 76.5–99.1), 90.4% (95% CI, 88.7–92), and 90.4% (95% CI, 88.8–91.6), respectively. Although sensitivity did not significantly differ between the two systems (P = 1.00), Korean Lung-RADS demonstrated significantly higher specificity and accuracy than Lung-RADS (both P < 0.001 by McNemar’s test).

Characteristics of category 2b nodules

Among the 29 nodules, nine (31.0%) were initially interpreted by reader 1, 17 (58.6%) by reader 2, and 3 (10.3%) by reader 3. Based on the Lung-RADS criteria, 15, 9, and 5 of these nodules corresponded to categories 3, 4A, and 4B, respectively. Of the 29 nodules classified as category 2b, 28 (96.6%) were confirmed as benign and one (3.4%) was diagnosed as malignant during follow-up. This malignant nodule was not reassigned to category 2b by any of the three readers during reassessment (Fig. 2). The primary reasons for assigning nodules to category 2b included tuberculous granulomas (n = 18, 62.1%), perifissural nodules (n = 7, 24.1%), and juxtapleural nodules (n = 4, 13.8%). Interreader agreement for category 2b nodules

In the reassessment, the most experienced reader (reader 2) classified 26 of the 29 nodules as category 2b, whereas the other two readers classified 14 and 17 nodules as category 2b, respectively. Interreader agreement for Korean Lung-RADS category 2b nodules was fair (Fleiss κ = 0.35, 95% CI, 0.17–0.56) (Table 4). Pairwise agreement ranged from slight to moderate, with higher consistency observed between the less experienced radiologists. Fig. 4 illustrates a representative case in which only the most experienced reader assigned category 2b, whereas the others did not, highlighting the variability in applying this category.

DISCUSSION

The diagnostic performance of Korean Lung-RADS was compared to that of the original Lung-RADS in a large, single-institution screening cohort, and the consistency of category 2b classification was assessed among multiple readers. Korean Lung-RADS demonstrated improved diagnostic performance, primarily by reducing false-positive interpretations without compromising sensitivity. This suggests that the introduced modifications enhance suitability for the Korean LCS population. However, despite these advantages, the fair level of interreader agreement for category 2b indicates ongoing challenges in achieving consistent application of this category, even among thoracic radiologists, underscoring the need for cautious application and reader training.

Consistent with a recent study demonstrating that Korean Lung-RADS improves diagnostic performance by increasing specificity,16 our study confirmed that the introduction of category 2b effectively reduces false-positive results. Moreover, unlike previous studies that assessed outcomes based on lung cancer diagnoses made within 1 year, our study incorporated a longer follow-up period, allowing for the inclusion of cancers diagnosed beyond the first year. We also applied a reference standard to classify all included cases as benign or malignant. Although this approach enhances diagnostic certainty for the analyzed cases, the exclusion of indeterminate cases may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Intrapulmonary lymph nodes are benign entities that may appear on any pleural surface, including the fissures, costal, mediastinal, and diaphragmatic pleura. Characteristically, they present as solid nodules with sharp margins, an oval, lentiform, or triangular shape, and a diameter of < 10 mm.12 Prior studies have shown that these nodules are highly unlikely to be malignant and can be safely managed with annual follow-up, even when newly detected during LCS.1314 Accordingly, Lung-RADS version 1.1 incorporated perifissural nodules with typical morphology and a maximum diameter < 10 mm into category 2 to reduce false-positive results. Lung-RADS 2022 further extended this classification to similarly shaped nodules located on other pleural surfaces, referring to them as juxtapleural nodules. Notably, the Korean Lung-RADS system adopted this approach earlier through the introduction of category 2b. In this study, 11 of 29 nodules (37.9%) categorized as 2b were perifissural or juxtapleural nodules. These perifissural and juxtapleural nodules categorized as 2b were assigned before the implementation of Lung-RADS version 1.1 (2022) and version 2022 (2024), respectively. By incorporating these nodules into category 2b prior to their formal inclusion in Lung-RADS, Korean Lung-RADS may have helped reduce false-positive interpretations in LCS.

Despite its clinical utility, the fair interreader agreement for category 2b raises concerns regarding its subjectivity and potential variability in clinical applications. In particular, the classification of tuberculous granulomas often depends on ancillary imaging features—such as adjacent fibrotic changes, satellite nodules, or apical distribution—rather than clearly defined criteria, making interpretation more subjective.192021 The fair interreader agreement for category 2b (κ = 0.35; 95% CI, 0.17–0.56) may reflect the inherently subjective nature of this category and the lack of standardized definitions. In addition, radiologists with greater experience tended to assign category 2b more confidently, likely because of their greater familiarity with benign features and findings suggestive of tuberculosis. Therefore, clearer and more standardized definitions, along with continued education and training, may ensure a more consistent application of category 2b.

Among the 29 nodules initially assigned to category 2b, one was ultimately diagnosed as malignant. Notably, this nodule was not assigned to category 2b by any of the readers during reassessment. Although the inclusion of category 2b in Korean Lung-RADS is intended to reduce false positives, interreader variability may contribute to false-negative interpretations. This finding suggests that its application requires caution, particularly in ambiguous cases. Regular feedback and targeted educational reinforcement may help mitigate these risks.

This study has some limitations. First, this retrospective single-center study included a limited number of category 2b nodules and showed a pronounced gender imbalance (female, 2.3%),22 which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the exclusion of indeterminate diagnoses, accounting for more than half of the initial cohort (1,560 of 2,931 participants), may have introduced selection bias and potential overestimation of the specificity and accuracy. Future studies including indeterminate nodules would provide a more comprehensive assessment of diagnostic performance. Third, interreader agreement was assessed only in nodules initially classified as category 2b, which may introduce selection bias. Additionally, the readers’ awareness of prior 2b classification during reassessment may have introduced interpretation bias. Fourth, all readers in this study were thoracic radiologists, whereas many examinations in the South Korea national LCS program are interpretated by general radiologists without formal thoracic subspecialty training. This discrepancy in reader expertise may reduce reproducibility and limit the applicability of our findings to broader clinical settings. Lastly, since the implementation of Lung-RADS versions 1.1 and 2022, which incorporated perifissural and juxtapleural nodules with typical morphology of intrapulmonary lymph nodes into category 2, such nodules < 10 mm are no longer classified as category 2b. Therefore, the frequency and clinical relevance of category 2b nodules in this study may not fully reflect current practice.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that Korean Lung-RADS improves specificity and overall diagnostic accuracy compared to the original system, while maintaining comparable sensitivity. The addition of category 2b effectively reduces false-positive results, particularly in tuberculosis-endemic settings. Nevertheless, the observed interreader variability in 2b assignment suggests the need for more clearly defined criteria to support consistent application.
