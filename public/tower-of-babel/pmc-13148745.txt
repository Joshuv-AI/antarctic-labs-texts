Clinical Symptoms of Jugular Venous Outflow Obstruction and Their Modification After Targeted Surgical Treatment
Authors: Connor R. Margraf, Jackson P. Midtlien, Ferdinand K. Hui, Molly R. Ehrig, Carol A. Kittel, Adnan H. Siddiqui, Kyle M. Fargen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Cerebral venous outflow disorders (CVD) are conditions characterized by impairment of cerebral venous drainage. Associated symptoms have been poorly defined, as no standardized diagnostic criteria exist for this class of conditions; most studies to date have attempted to report on symptoms solely based on suggestive noninvasive imaging findings, which is prone to selection bias. The aim of this study was to characterize symptoms that may be present in the setting of suspected internal jugular vein outflow stenosis.METHODS:A survey was conducted among patients with suspected CVD who had previously undergone diagnostic dynamic cerebral venography and subsequent internal jugular vein stenting or styloidectomy from 2019 to 2025.RESULTS:Following distribution to 133 patients, 60 (45.1%) responded. Fifty (83.3%) of the 60 respondents indicated an improvement in symptoms following intervention. Brain fog (49, 98.0%), headache (48, 96.0%), and fatigue/low energy (46, 92.0%) were the most frequently reported symptoms pre-procedure. Uncontrollable seizures or shaking spells, headache, and eye pain were the most responsive to intervention, with improvement rates of 87.5%, 81.3%, and 81.0%, respectively. Nineteen of 50 respondents endorsed a diagnosis of Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome; 8 of 19 reported improvement in Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome symptoms after their procedure.CONCLUSION:There is a wide range of symptoms that may be present in patients with CVD. This survey illustrates the most common complaints among suspected patients with CVD and elicits which symptoms are likely due to cerebral venous outflow impairment, based on responsiveness to intervention.

Cerebral venous outflow disorders (CVD) are a spectrum of conditions characterized by head, neck, or chest-derived impairment in cerebral venous return. The most widely accepted and studied condition on this spectrum is idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), which has objective criteria and findings making diagnosis largely straightforward. Other related conditions, such as internal jugular vein (IJV) stenosis, remain poorly understood. There have been an array of suggested symptoms associated with these pathologies, most notably characteristic symptoms of pressure headache, cognitive dysfunction, visual complaints, tinnitus, and dizziness, along with protean and often strange symptoms that are much harder to classify.1 Some of the patients suffering from this condition seem to share a connective tissue disorder phenotype.2 Apart from IIH, there are no widely accepted diagnostic criteria for CVD; therefore, studies attempting to report on symptoms solely based on suggestive imaging findings, such as IJV stenosis on noninvasive imaging, are prone to selection bias. For instance, IJV stenosis is often present in unselected or asymptomatic patients.3 Symptom attribution is challenging in the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria, creating circularity in efforts to define the clinical phenotype and fueling controversy as to whether these conditions actually exist.4

Two experimental treatments, IJV stenting and open jugular decompression with styloidectomy, hold potential for treating CVD by increasing patency of the IJV component of the extracranial outflow pathway. Published evidence supporting these interventions remains limited.5-7 However, patients having undergone these procedures serve as an ideal sample to understand symptom changes after intervention, as these patients can share which symptoms improved following an intervention that targets the underlying pathology by augmenting cerebral venous outflow. The goal of this study was not to advocate for the efficacy of these procedures or durability of their results, but rather to elicit which symptoms respond to intervention and are therefore likely caused by the underlying pathology that these interventions target.

METHODS

An Institutional Review Board–approved study was conducted by distributing an electronic survey to patients with suspected CVD who underwent IJV stenting and/or open jugular decompression with styloidectomy from 2019 to 2025 at a single center. Roughly 17% of patients invited to participate carried a diagnosis of IIH, based on the modified Dandy Criteria. Patients had previously provided informed consent for their clinical procedures independent of this research study. The survey was created using Research Electronic Data Capture; Vanderbilt University, a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act–compliant, web-based survey platform. It was electronically distributed to patients through our institution's MyChart portal. Patients granted consent to supply their nonidentifiable data with completion of the survey. All data were collected and analyzed within Research Electronic Data Capture; Vanderbilt University. No compensation was provided to respondents. Given that respondents were anonymous and patients did not provide identifiers, demographic data and procedural data were not collected.

Patient Selection for Surgical Intervention

Patients are selected for surgery based on fulfillment of all 4 of the following criteria: (1) clinical symptoms highly suggestive of CVD; (2) noninvasive imaging suggesting cerebral outflow stenosis; (3) severe, medically, and standard-of-care surgical therapy-refractory symptoms; and (4) confirmatory dynamic diagnostic cerebral venography and manometry highly suggestive of IJV stenosis producing symptoms. A generalized protocol for surgical candidacy has been presented previously.6

Surgical Procedures: Open Jugular Decompression (“Styloidectomy”)

Patients offered open jugular decompression surgery8 undergo unilateral neck dissection with an incision 2 fingerbreadths below the angle of the mandible. Neuro-monitoring to aid in identifying and preserving cranial nerves VII, IX, X, XI, and XII is used. The IJV is identified and dissected from attachments and then dissection is carried rostrally to above the C1 tubercle. The styloid process is detached and removed above the level of C1. In cases where the C1 tubercle is prominent and can be accessed, the C1 tubercle is shaved with an ultrasonic aspirator. Patients are usually discharged home same day and provided brief courses of postoperative pain medications and anti-inflammatory agents. Patients may restart antiplatelet agents immediately after the procedure.

Surgical Procedures: IJV Stenting

Patients offered stenting undergo unilateral IJV stenting under conscious sedation and heparinization through a transfemoral vein approach. Patients are initiated on aspirin and clopidogrel 1 week before the procedure. Dynamic testing is performed in rotation and chin flexion to measure dynamic gradients prestenting. Next, stents sized appropriately to the vein are placed in the IJV at the sites of compression; technical details can be found in prior work.9 After stenting, dynamic testing is repeated to confirm resolution of the stenosis and dynamic gradients. The sheath is removed, pressure is held at the access site for 10 minutes, and then, patients are discharged home 1 hour later. Patients are maintained on aspirin and clopidogrel for at least 6 weeks.

Survey

The survey used for data collection is included in Supplemental Digital Content 2 (Survey, http://links.lww.com/NS9/A106). Patients were asked to identify whether they have undergone one or more procedures including right IJV stenting, left IJV stenting, right styloidectomy, and/or left styloidectomy. Next, respondents were asked if they experienced any improvement in symptoms as a result of their procedure(s). If “yes” was selected, the rest of the survey became available for completion. If “no” was selected, the survey terminated. If respondents underwent multiple procedures, they were asked to complete the remainder of the survey based on their most impactful procedure.

Patients were asked to complete preintervention and postintervention Cerebral Venous Disorder Severity Scale instruments,1 an unvalidated tool used to quantify symptom severity that has been used and published previously.10 The Cerebral Venous Disorders Severity Scale (CVDSS) assigns scores for each symptom before and after the procedure based on severity. The first 5 symptoms (headache, cognitive dysfunction, tinnitus, dizziness, and visual symptoms) have a scale from 0 to 3 based on escalating severity. Involuntary motor episodes have a score of 0, 2, or 4 based on increasing frequency, and patients identifying as being disabled due to symptoms yields a score of 4 (not disabled is 0). Next, an array of symptoms was listed, and respondents were asked to indicate which symptoms were present before their procedure and how each present symptom responded to treatment 6 weeks to 3 months after their procedure. Some control symptoms—such as fever/chills and diarrhea—were included. Finally, the history of postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) and additional symptoms was elicited.

Statistical Analysis

A linear mixed effects model with total CVDSS score as the outcome and time (preprocedure vs postprocedure) as the predictor was performed. A random intercept for subject was included to account for within-person correlation. A likelihood ratio test was used to determine whether the procedural group (IJV stenting vs styloidectomy) improves model fit. To evaluate how the individual domains of headache, cognitive dysfunction, tinnitus, dizziness, and visual symptoms change over time, generalized estimating equation methodology was used to fit models for the repeated ordinal scale CVDSS components. Quasi-likelihood under the Independent Model Criterion was used to evaluate whether procedural group improves model fit. The proportional odds assumption was assessed with a score test. Categorical variables are presented as count (percent). All statistical analyses were conducted using R: A language and environment for statistical computing (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, version 4.4.1) and RStudio: Integrated Development for R (version 2024.04.2, Posit Software, PBC).

RESULTS

The survey was sent to 133 patients: 60 responded (response rate 45.1%). Respondents indicated that they had undergone the following procedures: right styloidectomy (n = 31, 51.7%), left styloidectomy (n = 31, 51.7%), right IJV stent (n = 26, 43.3%), and/or left IJV stent (n = 21, 35.0%). Thirty-seven patients (61.7%) underwent only styloidectomy or only IJV stenting, compared with 23 (38.3%) who had undergone both styloidectomy and IJV stenting. Nineteen patients (31.7%) underwent only styloidectomy, whereas 18 patients (30.0%) underwent only IJV stenting. For the 23 patients who underwent both styloidectomy and IJV stenting, 13 reported that an IJV stenting had a greater impact on their symptoms compared with 5 who reported a styloidectomy had a greater impact. Five patients did not report which procedure had a greater impact.

Fifty (83.3%) respondents reported improvement in symptoms because of their procedure(s) and completed the remainder of the survey.

Change in additional symptoms.

Nineteen of 50 (38.0%) respondents endorsed a history of POTS. Of these 19, 8 (42.0%) reported an improvement/change in symptoms after their procedure.

Some respondents reported other symptoms that improved after intervention: face and neck swelling, lack of dreams, feelings of strangulation, metallic taste, tongue pain, throat spasms, face and neck flushing, aphasia, paranoia, barometric pressure hypersensitivity, morning erections, velopharyngeal reflux, extremity tingling, dexterity issues, gastroparesis, clavicular pain, and first bite syndrome.

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to report on symptom improvement after surgical IJV augmentation as a means of identifying and cataloguing symptoms that are likely due to impairments in cerebral venous outflow in patients with severe, medically refractory disease. The most common symptoms improving after surgical intervention were head pressure or headache; brain fog or difficulty thinking; eye pain; blurry, gray, or cloudy vision; vertigo or room spinning; difficulty speaking; and double vision. Other symptoms, such as pseudoseizures and unresponsive episodes, were less prevalent among respondents but very responsive to intervention.

The most well-recognized entity on the CVD spectrum is IIH, which possesses relatively well-defined diagnostic criteria.11,12 By contrast, many other conditions of cerebral venous outflow impairment, particularly those involving the IJVs or that are otherwise extracranially-derived, remain poorly defined and have no diagnostic criteria. Interest in these conditions has recently intensified, primarily driven by the effectiveness of venous sinus stenting for IIH. This has led to increasing realization of clinical symptoms which mimic many aspects of IIH in patients without demonstrable intracranial venous outflow obstruction, and increased attention on extracranial venous outflow tracks including the internal jugular, vertebral, and epidural venous systems. Postural changes leading to differential utility of one path vs another are well known.13-18 Examining patients with IIH-like symptoms without intracranial venous disease has led researchers to identify demonstrable structural impediments such as an enlarged styloid process causing compression of the IJV between the styloid process and C1 lateral mass, or other physical osseus or nonosseus impediments within the head, neck, and upper chest. Angiographic assessment may define the areas of greatest concern and provide evidence of physiological outflow obstruction at these sites (in the form of trans-stenosis pressure gradients).

A common methodological issue in some of the existing literature is selection bias; some studies draw conclusions about symptoms by only evaluating patients who have imaging demonstrating IJV stenosis.19-21 This tends to overestimate associations because anatomic IJV narrowing is highly prevalent even in the general population.3 Imaging in neutral head positioning fails to account for any fluctuant changes in venous outflow. Recent work has demonstrated that head rotation leads to dynamic IJV stenosis and immediate increases in global sinus pressures and even opening pressure on lumbar puncture.22-24 These findings support the notion that physiological obstruction is probably episodic and positional and often more severe than what imaging in neutral posture captures. Thus, reliance on neutral computed tomography angiography/magnetic resonance venography alone likely misclassifies cases. That is, there are likely many instances in which patients have baseline neutral IJV stenosis without functional significance as well as cases where stenosis and subsequent pressure gradient develop only with change in head position. The lack of widely accepted diagnostic criteria presents an inherent challenge in defining this disease entity. Previous approaches to this challenge have been unable to establish causality or physiological significance. Noninvasive imaging findings or symptom profiles alone are insufficient to assess the whole, dynamic presentation of patients with this pathology. In this study, those included had appropriate clinical symptoms and noninvasive imaging sufficient to warrant confirmatory, invasive diagnostic dynamic venography with manometry and then underwent an isolated, unilateral jugular revascularization procedure. By design of the survey, which terminated after an indication of nonresponse to intervention, patients who were incorrectly diagnosed and therefore would not be expected to experience symptom improvement were excluded from analysis. By contrast, true cases should demonstrate improvement specifically in symptoms related to the underlying disease process as jugular stenting or open jugular decompression would not be expected to result in symptomatic improvement for unrelated symptoms outside of a placebo effect. Unfortunately, placebo effect is very difficult to control for, specifically in retrospective series of this nature, and remains a major limitation of the study. Regardless, through evaluation of postoperative changes in patient-reported symptoms within this stringently selected cohort, we aim to more accurately characterize symptoms directly attributable to jugular flow impairment, and to date, this study represents the most rigorous attempt to do so. It is worthwhile to note that this study does introduce a selection bias of its own in that it selects for patients with refractory symptoms severe enough to warrant surgical intervention, rather than all individuals with symptomatic jugular stenosis, although this sample does provide advantages in that symptom improvement is more likely to be detectable when severity is worse.

Our study reinforces the idea that CVD often present with some of the following core symptoms: headache, cognitive dysfunction, tinnitus, dizziness, visual disturbances, and involuntary motor episodes. These symptoms all displayed moderate to high rates of response to intervention. Importantly, we also observed improvement in lesser-known symptoms: dysautonomia, neck and face symptoms, and pseudoseizures. These atypical symptoms have been occasionally observed in clinical practice and only sporadically reported in prior literature.2,25-27 With only a 32.0% prevalence among respondents, uncontrollable seizures or shaking spells was not among the top-reported symptoms. However, it had a remarkable 87.5% improvement rate after intervention. There may be a specific subset of CVD in which these seizures/shaking spells manifest, often anecdotally in patients with confirmed or suspected connective tissue disorders.2 Interestingly, revascularization appears to be particularly effective for these symptoms. Also of note, POTS, which has some overlapping symptomatology with CVD,28 was reported by 19 of 50 respondents. The relationship between these conditions requires further investigation, but it is interesting that almost half (42.1%) of respondents with a diagnosis of POTS felt an improvement/change in symptoms after their procedure, suggesting a potential primary neurological etiology to these cardiac symptoms. Finally, brain fog demonstrated similar prevalence to and was nearly as responsive to intervention as headache. This demonstrates that cognitive impairment may be more prevalent in CVD than previously thought and underscores the need for better cognitive assessment tools.29

IJV surgical interventions have demonstrated some promise in small published series but remain largely experimental and mysterious. Improvement or resolution of symptoms after styloidectomy in small retrospective series ranges from 65% to 90%,8,30,31 although the quality of these studies varies. A 2021 narrative review suggested a 79% clinical success rate.7 IJV stenting is a relatively new procedure with little data available, and while symptomatic improvement in a series of 29 patients was noted in 75%, only one-third of patients reported durable mid to long-term results.6 In addition, a second small series of IJV stenting for IIH showed excellent outcomes.32 However, severe complications, including cranial neuropathy and intracardiac stent migration, have been reported.6 While these procedures seek to promote patency of the IJVs and enhance jugular outflow to improve global cerebral venous drainage and symptoms of congestion,33 more data must be collected before any real conclusions can be drawn regarding their safety, efficacy, surgical candidacy, and long-term durability.

Limitations

There are a number of important limitations to this study. The sample size of 50 patients limits the power of the study. A 45.1% response rate introduces nonresponse bias; it is possible that nonresponders had a grossly different response to intervention compared with our sample. Of note, all symptoms listed had at least 6 “yes” responses; some respondents may have selected “yes” for all questions. Patients with a history of multiple procedures were directed to complete the survey based on the procedure they felt was most impactful, which is subjective and subject to significant recall bias. Furthermore, individuals who underwent their procedure toward the beginning of the allotted period may be subject to a greater degree of recall bias than those with a more recent procedure. It is impossible, based on this survey, to determine the correlation between degree of gradient and symptom severity as well as the interplay between the specific surgical approaches or preoperative symptoms onto outcomes given the anonymous nature of the survey. This study is not designed to comment on surgical selection, surgical technique, or its indications or value, but instead to capture symptoms that changed following these procedures as a means of more validly identifying symptoms caused by impairments in cerebral venous outflow. Reported improvements may have been partially due to a placebo effect, ongoing medical or additional surgical therapy after their surgery, or due to social desirability bias, which cannot be ruled out.

CONCLUSIONS

This study reports changes after surgical IJV augmentation to more accurately identify symptoms related specifically to IJV stenosis. Patients having undergone treatment for suspected CVD with jugular surgical interventions with symptomatic improvement afterwards report improvements in the core symptoms of headache, cognitive dysfunction, tinnitus, dizziness, and visual disturbances, as well as other protean symptoms including dysautonomia, neck and face symptoms, and pseudoseizures.
