Acute Leaflet Dysfunction in Transcatheter Pulmonary Valves
Authors: Asad Qadir, Lindsey Van Lente, Yasser Al-Khatib, Gruschen R. Veldtman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute leaflet dysfunction after transcatheter pulmonary valve (TPV) implantation is a rare but clinically important complication that remains poorly characterized.Case SummaryWe report 2 cases of acute stuck leaflet causing significant pulmonary regurgitation immediately postimplantation, the first such cases confirmed with intracardiac echocardiography in the pulmonary position. Case 1 involved a 23-mm Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia valve in a 39-year-old woman with repaired tetralogy of Fallot, requiring emergent valve-in-valve implantation with a 22-mm Melody TPV. Case 2 involved a 25-mm Harmony TPV in a 54-year-old woman, who was managed conservatively and is pending repeat catheterization. Standard rescue maneuvers including balloon dilation and catheter manipulation proved ineffective in both cases.DiscussionThis report highlights the importance of maintaining high clinical suspicion for acute leaflet dysfunction, the utility of intracardiac echocardiography for early diagnosis, and the need for valve-in-valve preparedness during TPV procedures.

Transcatheter pulmonary valve (TPV) technologies have revolutionized treatment for right ventricular (RV) outflow tract dysfunction.1,2 Although hypoattenuated leaflet thickening is a recognized intermediate-term complication, acute leaflet dysfunction immediately postimplantation remains poorly documented. We report the first cases of acute stuck leaflet causing significant regurgitation after TPV implantation.Take-Home Messages•Acute stuck leaflet associated pulmonary regurgitation is a rare complication of transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation that can affect both balloon-expandable and self-expanding devices; intracardiac echocardiography is useful for prompt diagnosis when unexpected postimplantation regurgitation is encountered.•Standard bailout maneuvers are unlikely to succeed, and operators should maintain a low threshold for emergent valve-in-valve rescue based on the hemodynamic severity of the resultant regurgitation and the patient's clinical status.

Case 1

A 39-year-old woman with repaired tetralogy of Fallot and prior 23-mm Trifecta valve had symptomatic severe pulmonary regurgitation (RV end-diastolic volume indexed: 144 mL/m2, RV-to–left ventricular ratio: 2.4, RV ejection fraction: 41%). After implantation of a 23-mm Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia valve (Edwards Lifesciences), immediate postprocedural angiography demonstrated moderate-severe regurgitation. Intracardiac echocardiography identified an acutely stuck left lateral leaflet causing eccentric regurgitation (see Video 1). Multiple rescue maneuvers failed: wedge catheter manipulation, diagnostic catheter engagement, and balloon dilation (22-mm Atlas Gold). Emergent valve-in-valve implantation with a 22-mm Melody TPV (Medtronic) successfully restored competence.

Case 2

A 54-year-old woman (body mass index: 42 kg/m2) with prior transannular patch repair had severe pulmonary regurgitation (regurgitant fraction: 40%), RV end-diastolic volume indexed to ideal body weight of 174 mL/m2, RV-to–left ventricular ratio of 1.9:1, and RV ejection fraction of 45%. Preprocedural fit analysis demonstrated acceptable suitability for the Harmony TPV 25 device (Medtronic) (see Figure 1). At implantation, initial valve loading failed because of inability to advance the device into the loader and required a second attempt, which was successful. Subsequent deployment of the 25-mm Harmony TPV was complicated by higher fluoroscopic frame rates caused by inadequate fluoroscopic visibility attributable to the patient's body habitus. This led to a longer procedure time, increased contrast use, and more extensive catheter manipulation to achieve satisfactory implant position. Intracardiac echocardiography revealed moderate regurgitation from an entrapped left lateral leaflet (see Videos 2 and 3). Balloon dilation (24-mm balloon-in-balloon) was unsuccessful. Given procedural duration and the moderate severity of the regurgitation, further intervention was deferred. Transesophageal echocardiography at 3 weeks confirmed persistent leaflet dysfunction and mild to moderate pulmonary regurgitation. At 3 months postprocedure, the patient is being rescheduled for repeat cardiac catheterization and pulmonary valve-in-valve implantation.Figure 1Preprocedural Fit Analysis for Case 2Analysis demonstrating suitability of the patient for the Harmony TPV 25 implant.

Early recognition requires a combination of angiography and intracardiac echocardiography for any postimplant regurgitation. Standard bailout techniques (balloon dilation, catheter manipulation) proved ineffective in our experience. Spontaneous resolution appears unreliable, with persistent dysfunction at 2 to 3 weeks in case 2. Critical decision-making involves whether immediate valve-in-valve placement is necessary. This should be determined by the hemodynamic impact of the regurgitation and the clinical status of the patient. Given increasing TPV use and the rare but serious nature of this complication, operators should maintain a level of high suspicion for acute leaflet dysfunction and be prepared for valve-in-valve rescue.
