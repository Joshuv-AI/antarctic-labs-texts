Biological relationship between Parkinson's disease and gallstone disease
Authors: Chao Jiang, Jingying Ma, Jingyu Mu, Yuanyuan Fu, Yan Zhao, Yiming You, Zhiqiang Cui, Chuang Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompelling evidence indicates a significant connection between dysfunction of the gastrointestinal tract, the gut microbiome, and Parkinson's disease (PD), which aligns with the notion of the “gut–brain axis”. While the exact mechanisms involved in gut–brain interactions are still not fully understood, the high incidence of gastrointestinal symptoms during the early stages of PD aids in the development of diagnostic biomarkers and prospective disease‐modifying therapies. Importantly, a number of studies have revealed a possible association between gallstone disease (GD) and PD; nevertheless, the exploration of diverse risk factors and the theory suggesting that the onset of PD might be associated with GD requires further investigation. This review aims to explore the evidence that connects alterations in GD with PD, emphasizing mechanisms that promote gut dysbiosis, the gut–brain connection, changes in lipid profiles, genetic and dietary influences, as well as neuroinflammation. Furthermore, we assess the potential implementation of innovative therapeutic strategies, including probiotic treatments and gut microbiota transplantation, in patients with PD. Although the evidence supports an association between PD and GD, causality remains to be established. Prospective cohort studies are needed to determine whether gallstones represent a prodromal marker or a causal risk factor for PD, and to validate these pathways as novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets for PD.

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative condition marked by the gradual deterioration of dopaminergic neurons located in the substantia nigra, alongside the development of Lewy bodies (LBs). 1 As the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer's disease, PD affects over 7 million people worldwide, with projections suggesting this will rise to 12 million by 2040, posing a significant global public health burden. 2 Gallstone disease (GD), another prevalent chronic digestive disorder, involves the deposition of cholesterol, bile pigments, or calcium salts to form crystals in the gallbladder. According to the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), GD affects approximately 20% of Europeans, 3 while in the United States, its associated healthcare costs exceed $62 billion annually. 4 Importantly, epidemiological studies reveal significant comorbidity between PD and GD. Notably, beyond lower gastrointestinal symptoms, epidemiological studies also reveal a significant comorbid association between PD and GD. 5 This intriguing link raises fundamental questions about a potential shared pathogenesis originating in the hepatobiliary‐gut axis. A critical, unresolved question is the temporal sequence of these disorders: does GD predispose to PD, does PD drive gallstone formation, or are both manifestations of a common, underlying dysbiotic process?

Clinically, motor symptoms are central to PD diagnosis, including resting tremor, bradykinesia, postural instability, and rigidity affecting the neck, trunk, and limbs. 6 However, PD is increasingly recognized as a multi‐system disorder, where neuroinflammation and immune dysfunction drive non‐motor symptoms including sleep disturbances, autonomic dysfunction, olfactory dysfunction, and gastrointestinal (GI) dysfunction. 7 , 8 , 9 As the disease advances, both motor and non‐motor symptoms of PD generally become more severe, hindering patients' ability to carry out daily tasks and leading to considerable social and medical challenges. Notably, GI manifestations often precede motor symptoms by years. 10 Recent research highlights the gut microbiome as a core regulator of systemic homeostasis, participating in immune modulation, neuroendocrine signaling, and neural transmission. 11 In PD patients, gut microbiota dysbiosis significantly disrupts metabolic pathways including complex polysaccharide degradation, amino acid metabolism, and xenobiotic biodegradation potentially influencing brain function via immune, neuroendocrine, and neural routes. 12 , 13 Similarly, GD‐associated microbial disturbances can also disrupt the homeostasis of the gut–brain axis by regulating the secretion of gut‐derived hormones, interfering with intestinal motility and neurotransmitter release. 14 This process can activate the proinflammatory pathways, induce the expression of caspase‐3, result in damage to nigral dopaminergic neurons, and accelerate the neurodegenerative process of PD. 15

Collectively, PD and GD likely share intersecting pathological mechanisms centered on “gut dysbiosis gut/biliary brain axis dysregulation,” with intestinal microbiota acting as a pivotal mediator. This review explores the evidence connecting GD and PD by examining their biological relationship through the lens of intestinal dysbiosis, gut–brain axis, lipid metabolism, genetic and dietary factors, and neuroinflammation. We further analyze emerging therapies (e.g., probiotics, microbiota transplantation) and their potential diagnostic/therapeutic implications. This review will therefore critically synthesize the evidence linking GD and PD, moving beyond mere epidemiological correlation to dissect the shared biological pathways. We will focus on the gut microbiome as a potential central orchestrator of this dialogue, examining its role in mediating interactions through intestinal dysbiosis, gut–brain axis, lipid metabolism, genetic susceptibilities, dietary factors, and neuroinflammation. Finally, we will assess the translational implications of this nexus, exploring the promise of microbiota‐targeted therapeutic interventions, such as probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), for mitigating the risk or progression of both conditions.

INTESTINAL DYSBIOSIS

PD and intestinal dysbiosis

PD is characterized by GI dysfunction, including constipation (prevalence 28%–80%), delayed gastric emptying, and gastroparesis, often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, early satiety, bloating, and anorectal dysfunction. These symptoms typically precede motor manifestations by over 15 years and progressively worsen with disease advancement. 9 , 16 , 17 The underlying pathology is closely linked to aberrant α‐synuclein accumulation in the enteric nervous system and mucosal inflammation, where gut dysbiosis acts as a key driver by compromising intestinal barrier integrity and activating immune responses. 9 Notably, colonic transit in patients with PD is slowed to approximately half that of healthy controls, which associates with dopaminergic neuron damage, oxidative stress, local inflammation, and increased intestinal permeability. 18 Furthermore, delayed gastric emptying may impair levodopa absorption, thereby exacerbating motor symptoms in patients with PD. 19 The hypothesis proposed by Braak et al. 20 suggests that the pathology of α‐synuclein propagates predictably along a caudo‐rostral axis in the brain, with the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV) being an unavoidable point in this propagation process. This is supported by Kim et al.'s 21 mouse model, where duodenal injection of pathological α‐synuclein fibrils led to propagation to the substantia nigra and motor/non‐motor symptoms, which were prevented by vagotomy. However, Lionnet et al. 22 noted limitations in current animal models and postmortem analyses, emphasizing that evidence for the gut as the primary origin of PD remains inconclusive.

The gut microbiota, shaped by genetics, diet, and antibiotics, comprises major phyla including Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia. 23 , 24 Although Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes predominate the gut microbiota, their relative abundance varies across intestinal regions 25 ; specifically, facultative anaerobes (e.g., Lactobacillaceae) dominate the small intestine, whereas fermentative anaerobes (e.g., Bacteroidaceae and Clostridiaceae in humans) prevail in the large intestine. 26 Patients with PD exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), reaching up to 25%, compared to healthy populations. 27 Furthermore, analysis of the colonic mucosal microbiota reveals significantly reduced abundances of butyrate‐producing commensals including Bacteroides spp., Prevotella spp., Faecalibacterium/Roseburia (Ruminococcaceae), and Clostridium cluster XIVa, alongside an elevated proportion of facultative anaerobes such as Enterococcus spp. and Enterobacteriaceae, collectively establishing a proinflammatory microbial phenotype. 28 , 29 , 30 Specifically, mucin‐degrading bacteria, particularly Bacteroides acidifaciens, B. fragilis, and Akkermansia muciniphila, function as primary colonizers of the colonic mucosal surface. These bacteria maintain mucosal homeostasis through continuous degradation of mucin oligosaccharides via secreted glycosidases and neuraminidases. 25 , 29 , 31 Critically, dysbiosis‐driven depletion of these mucin degraders suppresses Prevotella growth and impairs host mucin2 (MUC2) synthesis signaling, this cascade ultimately reduces mucin thickness, disrupts tight junctions, and exacerbates intestinal permeability. 28 , 29 , 32

This disruption of the intestinal barrier facilitates the translocation of endotoxins like lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into the systemic circulation, triggering a systemic inflammatory response and promoting the pathological accumulation of α‐synuclein within the central nervous system. 18 , 33 Consistently, fecal calprotectin levels, which serve as a key marker of intestinal inflammation, are significantly elevated in PD patients relative to healthy controls 34 , 35 ; concurrently, proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐2, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) exhibit persistent upregulation in the brain, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood. 33 Notably, specific microbial alterations directly exacerbate inflammation and neurodegeneration: anti‐inflammatory, butyrate‐producing bacteria (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Coprococcus_2) are significantly reduced, whereas increased relative abundances of Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium may further drive inflammatory cascades 36 ; higher Healthy Eating Index (HEI) scores and increased dietary fiber intake promote the proliferation of these beneficial butyrogenic bacteria, 37 but pathogenic infections (e.g., Helicobacter pylori) compromise barrier function, 38 promoting α‐synuclein misfolding and damaging dopaminergic neurons. Notwithstanding these established associations, causal relationships between individual bacterial taxa and PD pathogenesis require further validation, while modulatory effects of age and sex on microbiome‐PD interactions remain underexplored.

Collectively, a self‐reinforcing cycle initiated by gut dysbiosis, which leads to intestinal barrier disruption, subsequently triggering immune activation, facilitating the propagation of pathological proteins (notably α‐synuclein), and culminating in neuroinflammation, constitutes the central framework of GI pathology in PD; consequently, this pathogenic cascade must be prioritized in the assessment of non‐motor symptoms.

GD and intestinal dysbiosis

The pathogenesis of GD is complex, involving multiple factors such as gallbladder motility dysfunction and disturbances in the metabolism and secretion of cholesterol and bile acids. 39 As early as the 1960s, Maki et al. 40 first proposed the critical role of bacteria in the pathogenesis of brown pigment stones, a theory later validated by Vitetta and others. 41 , 42 In 1995, Swidsinski 43 and colleagues further elucidated the close relationship between gut bacteria and gallstones in their study in Germany.

Modern studies confirm that gut microbial dysregulation drives GD through three interconnected pathways: First, microbial composition shifts in GD patients are characterized by a pro‐lithogenic profile. In a murine model fed a cholesterol‐rich, gallstone‐inducing diet, Wang et al. 44 observed a significant increase in Firmicutes abundance (p < 0.05) alongside reduced levels of short‐chain fatty acid (SCFA)‐producing bacteria (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Lactobacillaceae), mirroring findings in human GD cohorts. 45 Clinical analyses confirm elevated Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcaceae in gallstone patients' fecal microbiota, with concurrent depletion of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species. Lactobacillus levels vary across studies, with most clinical reports showing depletion. Additionally, butyrate‐producing bacteria (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Roseburia) show reduced abundance. This dysbiosis enhances bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity through Lactobacillus proliferation, increasing free bile acid levels that accelerate cholesterol conversion to bile acids. The consequent disruption of biliary cholesterol solubility promotes crystal nucleation, while the metabolic shift ultimately lowers serum cholesterol levels. 45 , 46 Second, intestinal barrier disruption facilitates bacterial translocation and systemic inflammation. Under normal circumstances, most intestinal bacteria reside in the intestinal lumen and the outer mucus layer. In contrast, under pathological conditions, bacteria may translocate by damaging the intestinal epithelium, entering the portal vein, and migrating to the liver, a phenomenon known as intestinal bacterial translocation. 47 A study by Ma et al. 48 found that this translocation can trigger autoimmune cholangitis via Toll‐like receptor 2 (TLR2) signaling, impairing hepatic bile acid synthesis and gallbladder motility. Additionally, dysbiosis‐induced loss of tight junction integrity permits endotoxins (e.g., LPS) to enter the circulation. This process activates TLR4‐mediated immune responses and disrupts hepatic bile acid synthesis along with gallbladder contractility. 49 Concurrently, biliary microbiota (e.g., Klebsiella, Pseudomonas) persist within the gallbladder, secreting β‐glucuronidase and phospholipases that induce chronic mucosal inflammation and mucin hypersecretion, creating a nidus for stone formation. 50

In summary, GD is not merely caused by an imbalance in bile composition, but is closely linked to gut dysbiosis through multiple mechanisms: pro‐lithogenic shifts in microbial composition disrupt bile acid metabolism; intestinal barrier dysfunction promotes bacterial translocation and systemic inflammation; and biliary microbiota directly drive stone nucleation via enzymatic and inflammatory pathways. Central to these processes is the imbalance between pathogenic taxa and protective commensals, highlighting the gut microbiota as a core regulator of biliary homeostasis. Strategies such as restoring intestinal microbial homeostasis, increasing SCFA levels, and blocking bacterial translocation are expected to become novel approaches for primary prevention of GD, and lay a microbiological foundation for subsequent exploration of its shared pathological mechanisms with PD.

Dysregulation of gut microbiota in PD and GD

The epidemiological association between PD and GD, first highlighted by Modaine et al., 5 implies a possible shared pathophysiological substrate. While this comorbidity has been supported by subsequent studies, the fundamental question of directionality remains unresolved (as will be discussed in detail in Section 7). The gut microbiome, with its profound influence on both neurological and biliary health, emerges as a prime candidate for mediating this relationship, potentially serving as the “shared soil” from which both diseases grow (Figure 1).

The epidemiological link and the temporal question

The initial clinical observation of comorbidity has been supported by subsequent studies. However, the evidence remains largely cross‐sectional, failing to definitively establish whether GD typically precedes motor symptoms of PD or vice versa. Autonomic dysfunction in early PD, leading to gallbladder stasis, could theoretically promote stone formation. 2 , 51

Conversely, chronic, subclinical biliary inflammation and altered bile acid metabolism resulting from GD could exacerbate or even initiate processes that drive neuroinflammation and proteinopathy. 46 , 52 , 53 Large‐scale, prospective cohort studies tracking GD incidence before PD diagnosis are urgently needed to clarify this temporal relationship. The answer would have significant implications for whether GD could serve as a clinical biomarker for PD risk and whether its management could modify PD progression.

Shared dysbiotic signatures: A common microbial fingerprint

Both PD and GD patients exhibit consistent changes in gut microbiota structure and function. 36 , 44 , 54 A key shared characteristic is the reduction of SCFA‐producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Lachnospiraceae, and Coprococcus, which has been observed in both conditions 36 , 44 and leads to impaired intestinal barrier function along with reduced anti‐inflammatory capacity. 55 , 56 Additionally, there is a frequent elevation of pathobionts including Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia, Shigella) and Enterococcaceae (Enterococcus), 53 , 54 which promotes endotoxin translocation such as LPS—systemic inflammation, and bile acid dysmetabolism. 18 , 50 A mucolytic imbalance is also evident, with a reduction of mucin‐degrading Prevotella in PD 36 and a decrease in Bacteroides in GD, 54 collectively compromising mucosal integrity. Furthermore, a high prevalence of SIBO is noted in approximately 25%–30% of PD patients and a significant proportion of GD patients, 27 exacerbating nutrient malabsorption, bile acid deconjugation, and systemic inflammation. These shared taxonomic shifts are accompanied by functional deficits, particularly in SCFA synthesis and bile acid metabolism, which together create a proinflammatory and pro‐lithogenic milieu. 46 , 54

Convergent mechanistic pathways

The dysbiotic landscape detailed above activates several convergent mechanistic pathways that link the gut to the brain and the hepatobiliary system. First, SCFA deficiency and barrier disruption represent a fundamental metabolic defect; butyrate is essential for maintaining the integrity of both the colonic epithelium and the blood‐brain barrier (BBB), and its deficiency in PD and GD exacerbates intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”) and BBB dysfunction (see Section 3.1). 57 , 58 This facilitates the translocation of neurotoxic agents (e.g., LPS and inflammatory cytokines) in PD and enables the entry of lithogenic substances and bacteria into the biliary system in GD (see Section 2.2). 47 , 50 Second, gut dysbiosis disrupts the finely tuned metabolism of bile acids through altered activity of microbial enzymes (BSH and 7α‐dehydroxylase), leading to an increase in deconjugated and secondary bile acids that exhibit higher hydrophobicity and cytotoxicity. In GD, this directly promotes cholesterol crystallization, 45 , 46 while in PD, these aberrant bile acids can exacerbate α‐synuclein aggregation and mitochondrial dysfunction, 59 additionally influencing the enteric nervous system and vagal signaling. Thirdly, the proinflammatory state induced by dysbiosis serves as a critical nexus between both diseases. In GD, local inflammation in the gallbladder epithelium, which is driven by LPS and characterized by TNF‐α secretion, promotes mucin overexpression (e.g., MUC5AC) and stone nucleation. 60 , 61 In PD, the same inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐1β) can access the brain through a compromised BBB, activating microglia, amplifying oxidative stress, and ultimately leading to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. 33 , 62 These mechanisms establish TNF‐α as a pivotal cytokine in the cross‐talk between the two diseases (see Section 6). Finally, dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism, often driven by genetic factors such as glucocerebrosidase gene (GBA) mutations in PD, results in ceramide accumulation. This potent signaling molecule promotes α‐synuclein oligomerization and neuroinflammation in the brain, 63 , 64 while in the biliary system, it influences cholesterol transporter activity (ATP‐binding cassette sub‐family G member 5/8 [ABCG5/8]) and facilitates cholesterol crystal formation. 65 This exemplifies how a shared molecular disturbance can manifest across different organ systems (see Section 3.2).

Conclusion: Implications for a “Gut‐First” paradigm and therapy

The collective evidence suggests that PD and GD are likely parallel outcomes of a shared, microbiome‐mediated pathological process, rather than being directly causal of one another. A persistent state of gut dysbiosis, potentially initiated or exacerbated by genetic risk (Section 4) and dietary factors (Section 5), sets the stage for systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Depending on individual susceptibility, this manifests as cholesterol stone formation in the biliary system, neurodegeneration in the substantia nigra, or both.

This framework positions the gut microbiome as a prime therapeutic target. Interventions aimed at restoring eubiosis, such as probiotics (e.g., butyrate‐producing strains), prebiotics, FMT, and dietary modulation (as detailed in Section 5), hold promise for simultaneously mitigating risks of both conditions. 24 , 66 Furthermore, targeting downstream shared pathways, such as TNF‐α signaling or ceramide synthesis, 62 , 65 could offer novel cross‐disease therapeutic strategies. The ongoing challenges and future research directions needed to definitively establish this paradigm and validate these interventions are explored in Section 7. LIPID METABOLIC DYSFUNCTION: A SHARED PATHWAY IN PD AND GD

Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of lipid metabolism dysregulation, mediated by gut microbiota‐derived metabolites, in the pathogenesis of both PD and GD. 45 , 59 , 84 The following sections dissect the contributions of two key lipid classes: SCFAs and sphingolipids.

The role of SCFAs

SCFAs, principally acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are derived from microbial fermentation of dietary fiber and are integral to maintaining systemic metabolic homeostasis. 55 , 85 In PD, a deficiency in SCFAs constitutes a hallmark feature of intestinal dysbiosis (see Section 2.1). 36 This reduction, particularly in butyrate and propionate, compromises intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, facilitating the translocation of proinflammatory agents such as LPS and cytokines into the systemic circulation. 59 , 86 These inflammatory mediators subsequently increase BBB permeability, permitting the influx of neurotoxic substances into the central nervous system, which exacerbates neuroinflammation and promotes the aggregation of α‐synuclein. 83 Butyrate further exerts neuroprotective effects by enhancing the expression of tight junction proteins at the BBB, thereby stabilizing it. 58 Clinically, PD patients exhibit decreased serum concentrations of propionate, butyrate, and hexanoate, concomitant with a reduction in propionate‐producing Prevotella species. 87 Although propionate supplementation ameliorates motor deficits and dopaminergic neuronal loss in murine PD models via activation of the gut‐expressed free fatty acid receptor 3 (FFAR3), 88 it also exerts direct neuroprotective effects in vitro, attenuating toxin‐induced neuronal damage and stimulating neurite outgrowth. 89 Moreover, functioning as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, propionate may mitigate PD‐related pathology by suppressing neuroinflammatory cascades and preserving BBB integrity. 90

In GD, depletion of SCFAs similarly disrupts bile acid metabolism and cholesterol homeostasis. 45 Activation of the intestinal farnesoid X receptor‐fibroblast growth factor 15 (FXR‐FGF15) signaling pathway by butyrate inhibits cholesterol absorption and reduces biliary cholesterol saturation—a critical step in preventing cholesterol crystallization and subsequent gallstone formation. 91 Administration of sodium butyrate (NaB) has been demonstrated to suppress bile acid synthesis and inhibit cholesterol reabsorption, thereby attenuating the risk of cholesterol stone pathogenesis. 91 Furthermore, SCFAs are indispensable for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and regulating permeability. Butyrate serves as the primary energy substrate for colonocytes and fosters the expansion of beneficial bacterial communities, which collectively inhibit inflammatory processes and de novo fatty acid synthesis. 56 A deficiency in SCFAs, particularly resulting from a decline in SCFA‐producing microbiota, can increase intestinal permeability, exacerbate intestinal inflammation, and detrimentally impact systemic health. 56 Thus, SCFA insufficiency represents a common metabolic aberration that propagates neuroinflammation in PD and fosters a lithogenic biliary environment in GD.

Peripheral important lipid molecules: Sphingolipids

Sphingolipids are a class of bioactive lipids essential for cellular membrane architecture and signal transduction, whose dysregulation exerts profound effects on both neurological and biliary pathophysiology. 92 In PD, mutations in the GBA gene, which encodes the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase (GCase), constitute a significant genetic risk factor. 63 GCase deficiency precipitates the accumulation of glucosylceramide (GlcCer) and related sphingolipids, which impairs lysosomal function and promotes the pathological aggregation of α‐synuclein. 63 , 64 , 67 This pathogenic cascade is further aggravated by concomitant mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, which are cardinal features of PD pathology. 93 , 94 The central sphingolipid metabolite ceramide activates microglia, instigating chronic neuroinflammatory responses that accelerate the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. 95 Additionally, sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P) has been implicated in the modulation of neuroinflammatory pathways, although its precise role in PD warrants further investigation. 96

In GD, dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism is intricately linked to aberrant cholesterol homeostasis. 65 , 97 Levels of ceramide and S1P are elevated within supersaturated bile, where they promote cholesterol crystallization and stone growth by modulating the activity of ABCG5/8 transporters and inducing inflammatory responses in the gallbladder epithelium. 65 , 70 Experimental inhibition of ceramide synthesis utilizing compounds such as myriocin or chitosan effectively reduces gallstone formation in animal models. 65 , 71 For instance, myriocin administration not only inhibited gallstone formation but also downregulated mRNA expression of ABCG5/8 transporters, suggesting that ceramide may act as a regulatory factor for these transporters and facilitate cholesterol crystallization. 71 These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting sphingolipid metabolism in GD.

Interplay and therapeutic implications

The interplay between SCFAs and sphingolipids delineates a complex metabolic network through which gut microbiota modulate host lipid homeostasis. SCFA deficiency exacerbates sphingolipid‐mediated pathologies by impairing barrier function and amplifying systemic inflammation. Conversely, sphingolipid dysregulation further perturbs cholesterol homeostasis and inflammatory signaling. This vicious cycle, fueled by gut dysbiosis, establishes a shared pathological foundation for both GD and PD.

Therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring microbial eubiosis and rectifying these lipid imbalances hold significant promise. As will be discussed in subsequent sections (Sections 6 and 7), interventions such as dietary modulation, probiotics, or FMT to elevate SCFA levels, as well as approaches to target sphingolipid metabolism, could yield novel mechanism‐based treatments for both conditions. Future research should prioritize elucidating the precise molecular crosstalk between these pathways and validating these interventions in comorbid patient populations.

GENETIC FACTORS

Apolipoprotein E (APOE), a key regulator of lipid metabolism, has emerged as a critical genetic factor potentially mediating the comorbidity between PD and GD. APOE encodes a lipid transport protein whose isoforms differentially influence lipid homeostasis in both the central nervous system and the hepatobiliary system. 75 , 98 , 99 Dysregulation of lipid metabolism is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases, including PD and Alzheimer's disease, with APOE playing a central role in modulating intracellular lipid dynamics. 72 , 98 Specifically, the APOE4 isoform disrupts lipid homeostasis in human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived glial cells, leading to increased fatty acid unsaturation and aberrant lipid droplet accumulation. 98 This lipid dysregulation exhibits systemic implications, influencing both cerebral α‐synuclein pathology and biliary cholesterol secretion. 94 , 95

The APOE ε4 allele demonstrates dual pathological effects in PD and GD. Meta‐analyses in PD populations have revealed that APOE ε2 carriers exhibit an elevated risk of PD, which is associated with reduced serum total cholesterol levels. 51 , 68 , 69 The APOE ε4 allele is associated with an increased risk of PD, a younger age at onset, and poorer cognitive performance in affected individuals. 72 , 73 Evidence suggests that carrying at least one APOE ε4 allele confers a more than twofold greater risk of PD. Furthermore, a synergistic interaction between the LDL Receptor Related Protein Associated Protein 1 (LRPAP1) I allele and APOE ε4 elevates PD susceptibility nearly fourfold. 100 Both genes play crucial roles in lipid metabolism, and mutations or dysfunction in either can disrupt cerebral lipid metabolism. This disruption is implicated in neurodegenerative pathogenesis, as lipid homeostasis is essential for normal neuronal function. 100 Additionally, the APOE rs405509 T allele is associated with increased PD susceptibility in the Chinese population. 101 Murine models corroborate these findings, demonstrating that APOE ε4 exacerbates α‐synuclein toxicity, whereas APOEε2 confers neuroprotection by suppressing pathogenic aggregation. 74 In the context of GD, APOE plays a pivotal role in regulating biliary cholesterol secretion and diet‐induced cholesterol gallstone formation. 76 The ε4 allele is associated with elevated hepatic and biliary cholesterol concentrations, promoting gallstone formation and correlating significantly with increased stone cholesterol content. 75 , 77 These findings suggest that APOE ε4 contributes to gallstone pathogenesis by enhancing cholesterol saturation in bile, thereby facilitating crystal nucleation and stone growth.

Collectively, these genetic insights indicate that APOE‐mediated dysregulation of lipid metabolic networks may constitute a common molecular basis for PD‐GD comorbidity. The allele‐specific effects of APOE provide a mechanistic framework to explain the epidemiological linkage between these two disorders, highlighting a shared genetic susceptibility that operates through lipid‐centric pathways. Future studies should explore whether APOE genotyping could serve as a biomarker for identifying individuals at high risk for both conditions, thereby enabling targeted preventive strategies.

DIETARY FACTORS

Dietary composition serves as a pivotal environmental modulator of the gut microbiota and systemic metabolism, exerting profound influences on the pathogenesis of both PD and GD. 14 Western‐style diets (WDs), which are characterized by high intake of saturated fats, refined sugars, and low fiber, are implicated in disrupting gut microbial homeostasis. This dietary pattern further promotes systemic inflammation and alters lipid metabolism, which are all key processes underlying both conditions. 14 , 102 , 103

High dietary fat consumption, particularly of saturated fatty acids, elevates serum cholesterol levels and induces hepatic cholesterol hypersecretion. This physiological change leads to the formation of cholesterol‐supersaturated bile, representing a critical step in gallstone nucleation. 3 , 4 , 81 , 82 Concurrently, excessive dietary lipids may facilitate α‐synuclein aggregation in the substantia nigra through mechanisms that involve promoting oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. 78 , 79 , 80 Moreover, WDs profoundly alter gut microbial composition by typically reducing the abundance of SCFA‐producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium and Roseburia, while simultaneously enriching LPS‐producing gram‐negative taxa like Enterobacteriaceae. 103 This dysbiotic shift compromises intestinal barrier integrity, increases circulating LPS, and activates systemic and neuro‐inflammatory pathways. These changes collectively accelerate PD progression and enhance gallstone risk. 104 , 105

Notably, interactions between specific dietary components may further modulate disease susceptibility. For instance, high dietary cholesterol intake synergizes with elevated iron consumption to increase PD risk in males, whereas a combination of low saturated fat and high iron intake elevates risk in both sexes. 106 This sexual dimorphism may be partly attributed to estrogen‐mediated lipid modulation, which attenuates cholesterol‐driven pathologies in premenopausal females. 107

Conversely, dietary patterns rich in fiber, such as the Mediterranean diet, promote microbial eubiosis by enhancing SCFA production, strengthening epithelial barrier function, and exerting anti‐inflammatory effects. 13 , 14 Higher HEI scores and increased fiber intake correlate with elevated abundances of beneficial butyrate producers including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. These microbial changes may alleviate intestinal inflammation and improve metabolic outcomes in both PD and GD. 105 , 108 , 109

In summary, diet‐induced dysbiosis and metabolic dysfunction represent a modifiable risk nexus for PD and GD. Evidence‐based nutritional strategies, including reducing saturated fat and iron intake, increasing dietary fiber, and adopting Mediterranean‐style diets, may concurrently mitigate neuroinflammation and biliary cholesterol supersaturation. These dietary interventions offer promising preventive approaches for these comorbid disorders.

INFLAMMATION: A CORE PATHOLOGICAL HUB LINKING PD AND GD

Inflammation serves as a pivotal mechanistic link between PD and GD, with emerging evidence highlighting the role of TNF‐α‐mediated cross‐organ signaling and neuroimmune dysregulation in both conditions. 110 , 111 The inflammatory processes in PD and GD share remarkable similarities, centered around cytokine dysregulation, immune cell activation, and barrier dysfunction, which collectively facilitate a pathogenic crosstalk along the gut‐biliary‐brain axis.

Inflammatory mechanisms in PD

In PD, the inflammatory cascade originates from perturbations in the gut–brain axis. Early accumulation of pathological α‐synuclein in the enteric nervous system represents a fundamental initial step, as observed in colonic neurons of early‐stage PD patients. This pathological process synergizes with gut microbiota‐induced oxidative stress and barrier dysfunction to initiate systemic inflammation. 10 , 112 The propagation of neuroinflammation occurs through dual routes: (1) a systemic pathway where gut‐derived inflammatory mediators (e.g., LPS, proinflammatory cytokines) enhance BBB permeability, activating microglial responses 58 , 83 and (2) a vagal route through which α‐synuclein fibrils spread in a prion‐like manner via Lymphocyte‐Activation Gene 3 (LAG3)/neurexin1β receptors, first accumulating in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus nerve before spreading to the locus coeruleus and substantia nigra pars compacta. 21 , 113 , 114

Concurrently, gut microbiota dysbiosis amplifies intestinal inflammation by stimulating cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) + T cell differentiation into T helper 1 (Th1)/Th17 subsets, which secrete interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) and interleukin‐17 (IL‐17) to recruit macrophages and neutrophils (NEU), perpetuating chronic inflammation and disrupting epithelial tight junctions. 28 , 83 This inflammatory milieu is further characterized by elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines including IL‐1β, IL‐2, IL‐6, and TNF‐α in the brain, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood of PD patients. 33 , 115 Notably, fecal calprotectin levels, a key marker of intestinal inflammation, are significantly elevated in PD patients compared to healthy controls. 34 , 35

The sustained neuroinflammatory response in PD primarily involves microglial activation, which promotes α‐synuclein pathology through the release of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species. 7 , 62 TNF‐α emerges as a particularly crucial cytokine, triggering proapoptotic pathways in dopaminergic neurons and facilitating the pathological progression of PD. 62

Inflammatory mechanisms in GD

In GD, chronic inflammation plays an equally fundamental role in stone formation and progression. Gallbladder epithelial cells (GBECs) secrete multiple proinflammatory mediators including TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐8, and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1) in response to various stimuli (e.g., bacterial LPS, abnormal bile). 60 , 61 These cytokines drive stone formation through two primary mechanisms: (1) disruption of biliary composition ‐ TNF‐α upregulates MUC5AC/MUC2 expression via the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGF‐R) signaling pathway, inducing mucin hypersecretion that acts as a nucleation matrix for cholesterol and calcium salts 61 ; and (2) bacterial synergy ‐ LPS exposure enhances TNF‐α synthesis in GBECs, 60 while bacterial infections exacerbate local inflammation. 50

The inflammatory process in GD is further modulated by bacterial factors. Biliary microbiota (e.g., Klebsiella, Pseudomonas) persist within the gallbladder, secreting β‐glucuronidase and phospholipases that induce chronic mucosal inflammation and mucin hypersecretion, creating a nidus for stone formation. 43 , 50 Additionally, disruption of the intestinal barrier facilitates bacterial translocation into the portal circulation, promoting systemic inflammation. This process activates TLR4‐ and TLR2‐mediated immune responses, which subsequently disrupt hepatic bile acid synthesis and impair gallbladder contractility. 47 , 48 , 49

Recent evidence from large‐scale population studies has confirmed the significant mediating role of systemic inflammatory factors in obesity‐related GD. Based on NHANES 2017‐2020 data, inflammatory markers including white blood cell count (WBC), NEU count, and C‐reactive protein (CRP) significantly mediate the relationship between obesity and GD, with CRP alone mediating 12.48% of the effect. 116 This finding provides robust epidemiological support for the crucial role of inflammation in GD pathogenesis.

Convergent inflammatory pathways in GD and PD

The inflammatory mechanisms in GD and PD exhibit striking convergence, with gut microbiota dysbiosis and barrier dysfunction serving as common upstream drivers. As detailed in Section 2.3‐3, shared dysbiotic patterns (e.g., reduced SCFA producers, increased proinflammatory taxa) promote the translocation of bacterial products like LPS into systemic circulation. 18 , 47 The core inflammatory interplay between the two diseases is masterfully orchestrated by TNF‐α and LPS. These mediators activate a self‐reinforcing cycle: in GD, LPS and local cytokines stimulate GBECs to secrete TNF‐α, which upregulates mucin expression (e.g., MUC5AC) to promote stone nucleation. 60 , 61 Conversely, systemic TNF‐α originating from the inflamed biliary tree or gut can access the brain through a compromised BBB, directly activating microglia and fueling neuroinflammation and dopaminergic neuron degeneration in PD. 58 , 62 This establishes TNF‐α as a pivotal circulating cytokine functionally linking biliary and neuroinflammatory pathology.

Furthermore, sphingolipid metabolism provides another connecting pathway, as ceramide accumulation in both diseases activates proinflammatory p38 mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, exacerbating local damage in either the gallbladder or the substantia nigra (Table 2). 62 , 65 , 70

Therapeutic implications and future directions

The shared inflammatory pathways between GD and PD offer promising therapeutic targets, with strategies aimed at modulating the gut microbiota (including probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT) potentially reducing inflammation in both conditions by restoring microbial homeostasis and strengthening barrier function. 24 , 66 Similarly, anti‐inflammatory interventions targeting TNF‐α signaling may confer benefits in both diseases. 17 , 62 Recent research has identified several potential therapeutic approaches, including microbial‐based therapies to restore SCFA‐producing bacteria via probiotic supplementation or fecal transplantation, thereby enhancing anti‐inflammatory responses and improving barrier integrity 24 , 66 ; cytokine‐targeted therapies such as anti‐TNF agents, already utilized in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which warrant investigation in PD patients with comorbid GD 17 ; modulation of the sphingolipid pathway through inhibition of ceramide synthesis using compounds like myriocin or chitosan, which has shown promise in animal models of both GD and PD 65 , 71 ; and dietary interventions emphasizing anti‐inflammatory diets rich in fiber and omega‐3 fatty acids to reduce systemic inflammation and positively modulate the gut‐biliary‐brain axis (Table 3). 102 , 105

Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies to definitively establish the temporal relationship between inflammatory markers and disease progression in both conditions. Furthermore, exploring whether anti‐inflammatory therapies (e.g., anti‐TNF‐α) can concurrently mitigate the progression of both GD and PD represents a critical direction for translational research. Additionally, clinical trials evaluating these microbiome‐targeted and anti‐inflammatory strategies in patients with comorbid GD and PD are needed to validate their therapeutic potential. 24 , 66

In conclusion, chronic inflammation represents a fundamental mechanistic link between GD and PD, with shared pathways involving cytokine dysregulation, gut microbiota alterations, and barrier dysfunction. Targeting these common inflammatory mechanisms may offer novel therapeutic opportunities for patients suffering from both conditions. Future research should focus on validating specific inflammatory biomarkers and developing targeted anti‐inflammatory strategies that simultaneously address both diseases.

LIMITATIONS

Despite the converging evidence presented, this review and the field it synthesizes are subject to several important limitations. A primary constraint arises from the nature of the available clinical evidence, which is predominantly observational and cross‐sectional. This study design inherently limits our ability to infer causality or establish a clear temporal sequence between GD and PD. Consequently, the fundamental question of whether one condition predisposes the other, or whether both stem from a common origin, remains unresolved. Furthermore, many of the mechanistic insights discussed, particularly those involving the gut–brain axis and specific microbial metabolites, are extrapolated from animal models. While these models are invaluable, they may not fully capture the complexity, chronicity, and multifactorial nature of human disease pathogenesis. This is especially true for the human‐specific nuances of gut microbiota composition and neuroinflammatory responses.

An additional layer of complexity arises from the methodological heterogeneity observed across various studies. The variations in diagnostic criteria for PD and gastrointestinal disorders, coupled with the diverse techniques employed for profiling gut microbiota and measuring inflammatory markers, complicate direct comparisons and the integration of data. For instance, the techniques utilized range from 16S rRNA sequencing to metagenomics. The potential for confounding variables is also significant; factors such as pharmacotherapy, long‐term dietary patterns, and co‐existing metabolic conditions can independently influence both the gut microbiome and disease progression. It is noteworthy that the widespread use of levodopa in PD may affect biliary motility; however, such variables are often not comprehensively accounted for in existing analyses. Furthermore, while we propose an interconnected framework of pathways, robust multi‐omics data that simultaneously interrogate the microbiome, metabolome, host genetics, and clinical phenotypes in large, well‐characterized PD‐GD cohorts remain scarce. Addressing these gaps necessitates prospective, longitudinal studies with standardized protocols, which are essential for validating the proposed biological relationships and translating these findings into clinical applications.

ONGOING CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Current understanding and comorbidity background

Neurological disorders represent the a leading cause of disability worldwide, with PD being the fastest‐growing neurological condition. 2 Concurrently, GD is a highly prevalent gastrointestinal disorder, affecting up to 20% of adults in Western populations. 3 , 4 A growing body of clinical and mechanistic evidence suggests a significant comorbidity between PD and GD, potentially rooted in shared pathophysiological processes involving gut dysbiosis, lipid metabolic disruption, neuroinflammation, and genetic susceptibility. 5 , 36 , 44 , 98 This association prompts critical questions about underlying biological connections and clinical implications.

Critical knowledge gaps and research imperatives

The fundamental question of directionality remains unresolved: it is unclear whether GD predisposes to PD, PD promotes gallstone formation, or both conditions arise from common underlying mechanisms. Current evidence remains predominantly cross‐sectional, 5 inadequate for establishing causal relationships or temporal sequences.

To address these limitations, we identify five key research priorities: (1) Prospective cohort studies: Large‐scale longitudinal studies tracking GD incidence before PD diagnosis are essential. These should incorporate serial biomarker assessments (gut microbiome, metabolomic, and neuroimaging profiles) and evaluate whether cholecystectomy modifies PD progression or symptom severity. 5 , 38 (2) Multi‐omics integration: Combining genomics, metagenomics, metabolomics, and proteomics in large biobanks can distinguish causal microbial taxa from consequential changes and elucidate gene‐environment interactions (e.g., APOE ε4 and dietary factors). 74 , 75 , 101 , 108 (3) Targeted clinical trials: Preclinical evidence supports evaluating microbiota‐focused interventions (probiotics, prebiotics, FMT) and pathway‐specific treatments (anti‐TNF agents, ceramide inhibitors) in comorbid populations. Randomized trials must assess their efficacy in restoring microbial eubiosis, reducing inflammation, and slowing disease progression. 24 , 65 , 66 (4) Advanced mechanistic models: Human‐derived organoids, gut–brain axis microphysiological systems, and GD animal models with PD pathology are needed to validate molecular pathways (e.g., TNF‐α signaling, ceramide trafficking) identified in human studies. 21 , 44 , 62 And (5) precision medicine approaches: Developing integrated risk models that incorporate genetic, microbial, dietary, and inflammatory profiles could identify high‐risk individuals and enable targeted prevention strategies for PD‐GD comorbidity. 74 , 75

Concluding perspective

The PD–GD relationship represents a paradigm shift in understanding how peripheral metabolic and inflammatory conditions interact with neurodegenerative processes. Addressing these research priorities will not only clarify the nature of this association but also potentially identify novel therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target both conditions. The gut‐biliary‐brain axis emerges as a promising frontier for developing interventions that could modify disease progression in both PD and GD.
