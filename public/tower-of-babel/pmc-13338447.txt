Machine learning modeling to predict the photocatalytic degradation rate constant in wastewater using TiO2
Authors: Moges Mitku, Yunxiang Zhang, Getachew G. Kebede, Yuxin Zhang, Wang Shou, Qinfang Zhang, Fekadu Gashaw Hone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Many researchers have studied the application of titanium dioxide (TiO2​) for photodegradation, but optimization is time-consuming and resource-intensive due to the complexity of various experimental parameters. In this study we developed a machine learning model for enhancing the photocatalytic efficiency of TiO2 photocatalyst for pollutant degradation. Therefore, we compiled a dataset of 150 experimental results from published literature. This dataset was compiled based on various parameters that influence the reaction rate constant (k), such as pollutant type, catalyst particle size, surface area, pollutant concentration, TiO2 dosage, pH, light intensity, and temperature. Several machine learning models were trained and tested, among them the CatBoost model, demonstrating the prediction with R2 of 0.9044 and MAE of 0.1052. Analysis revealed that catalyst load, light intensity, and pollutant concentration were the most influential factors in the model’s predictions. The optimized CatBoost model successfully predicted a degradation rate constant of 0.1383 min− 1 for methylene blue, which was experimentally validated as equivalent to the rate constant of 0.1278 min− 1, confirming the potential of machine learning. This work confirms the potential of machine learning models for optimizing the photocatalysis efficiency of TiO2 photocatalysts for environmental applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51108-7.

Introduction

All living organisms’ life depend on water; however, its contamination poses a significant threat to public health and global environmental sustainability. Industrial processes, agricultural activities, and human waste all contribute to the pollution of water resources1. The wastewater may contain dyes, phenols, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals that are resistant to conventional wastewater treatment methods due to their complex structures and low biodegradability2. Advanced oxidation processes are effective alternative methods to decompose these complex pollutant compounds into less harmful substances like CO2, H2O, and minerals3. Among advanced oxidation process (AOP), heterogeneous photocatalysis has been extensively studied, with titanium dioxide serving as the most widely studied photocatalyst due to its chemical stability, non-toxicity, low cost, and high photoactivity4,5.

The photocatalytic performance of the TiO2 catalyst depends on several operational parameters, including particle size, surface area, catalyst dosage, type of pollutant, initial pollutant concentration, solution pH, light intensity, and reaction temperature6,7. Optimizing these parameters through conventional experimental approaches is time-intensive and fails to capture complex interactions between variables. In recent years, machine learning (ML) has proven effective in modeling complex, non-linear systems across various scientific and engineering domains8. ML algorithms can learn patterns and relationships from structured data, making them suitable for predicting the performance of intricate processes like photocatalysis9. By training ML models on datasets comprising experimental parameters and corresponding degradation efficiencies, it is possible to develop predictive tools that can rapidly estimate process performance under different conditions. We use some popular ML models like Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Regression (SVR), CatBoost, Random Forest (RF), and XGBoost to predict the photocatalytic degradation rate constant of model organic pollutants using TiO2 nanoparticles under varying experimental conditions. In this study, a dataset consisting of approximately 150 experimental data points was compiled from peer-reviewed literature, covering eight key variables: pollutant type, UV light intensity, TiO₂ particle size, catalyst surface area, TiO₂ dosage, initial pollutant concentration, pH, and reaction temperature. For model development, the dataset was randomly divided into 75% training data (≈ 112 data points) and 25% testing data (≈ 38 data points) to evaluate predictive performance. In addition, five-fold cross-validation was applied during training to further assess the robustness and generalization capability of the model. Despite extensive research on TiO2-based photocatalysis for pollutant degradation, optimizing operational parameters remains challenging due to the large number of experimental variables and the time- and resource-intensive nature of traditional trial-and-error approaches. To address this gap, this study adopts a machine learning–driven strategy to enhance the efficiency and predictability of photocatalytic processes. The objectives of this work were to: (i) generate a structured dataset of experimental results covering multiple key parameters, (ii) develop, train, and evaluate multiple ML models while comparing their predictive performance using standard metrics (R², MAE, and RMSE), and (iii) identify the main parameters influencing the degradation rate constant through feature importance analysis. Final model predictions were further validated through experimental studies on representative pollutants, ensuring reliability and practical relevance. By integrating interpretable machine learning with experimental validation, this work provides a cost-effective, rapid, and generalizable approach for optimizing photocatalytic systems, highlighting the potential of computational intelligence to guide environmental remediation strategies.

Methodology

Data collecting

In this study, we employed machine learning models using a dataset consisting of 150 experimental data points obtained from published literature sources3,10–39. All 150 experimental data points collected from ScienceDirect, PubMed, Elsevier, etc., with 96% of data points originating from Q1 and Q2 journals. The full database is listed in supplementary information. Each data point contains eight experimental parameters such as types of pollutant, particle size, surface area, initial concentration of pollutant (mg/L), TiO2 dosage (g/L), the solution of pH, light intensity (mW/cm2), and experimental temperature (°C).

Machine learning models training and evaluation

Supervised machine learning models have the ability to optimize and handle complex system factors in wastewater treatment40. ML models such as Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, CatBoost, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) are powerful tools for predicting photocatalytic activity based on experimental parameters41, and therefore we used these models in this work. All data processing and modeling were performed using Python with libraries such as Pandas for data manipulation, Scikit-learn42 for ML algorithms, model selection, and metrics, and Matplotlib and Seaborn for visualization. Categorical variables (pollutant types) were transformed using one-hot encoding to enable their use in ML algorithms43.

The final dataset comprised 150 data points. Input features (X) included type of pollutant, particle size (nm), surface area (m2/g), TiO2 dosage (g/L), initial pollutant concentration (mg/L), pH, experimental temperature (°C), and light intensity (mW/cm2). The output variable (Y) was the degradation rate constant (k). This value was converted into -log (k) to transform tiny values of k into a more manageable scale for visualization44.

Prior to modeling, preprocessing steps were performed. These included handling missing values and scaling features using StandardScaler from Scikit-learn to normalize the range and improve model convergence42. The dataset was divided into a training set (75%) and a testing set (25%). The training data were used to develop the machine learning model, while the testing data were used to evaluate its predictive performance. Model accuracy was assessed using standard statistical metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE), as presented in Eqs. (1)–(3). Model parameters were optimized using the gradient boosting approach, where each successive tree is trained to minimize the residual error from the previous iteration. In addition, the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method was applied to interpret the model by quantifying the contribution and importance of each input feature in predicting the degradation rate constant. Photo catalytic activity measurements

The photocatalytic activity was studied with the model pollutants methylene blue (MB), tetracycline (TC) and rhodamine B (RhB). In this study, we adjusted the reaction vessel (250 ml reaction vessel) is 10 cm and Cary 60 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer used for all experiments. In this process, a 50 mg sample was added to 100 ml of methylene blue (10 mg/L) solution. To establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium was magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min; subsequently, 5 ml of samples were withdrawn at time intervals of 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, and 30 min, respectively, under irradiation under a UV lamp (300 W) with continuous stirring. Similarly, 40 mg was introduced into 50 ml of RhB (10 mg/L) solution, and adsorption-desorption was established for 45 min. Then 3 ml of sample was taken at the time intervals of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 min. In the same experimental condition 50 mg catalyst was added into 100 ml of TC (10 mg/L) solution, and 5 ml of sample was withdraw at time interval of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min. Finally, each sample studied at different time intervals is analyzed by a Cary 60 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer.

Optimized ML model & photocatalytic conditions

In this study, the prediction of the photocatalytic degradation rate constant (k) was performed using the CatBoost Regressor, a gradient boosting algorithm based on an ensemble of decision trees. The model iteratively constructs sequential trees, where each new tree is trained to minimize the residual error of the previous ensemble by optimizing a mean squared error (MSE) loss function. The final prediction is obtained as the additive combination of all decision trees in the ensemble. Model training was conducted using 75% of the dataset, while 25% was reserved for testing, and model robustness was further assessed using five-fold cross-validation. Key hyperparameters were optimized with max_depth = 6, iterations = 500, and learning_rate = 0.1, while R² was used as the primary scoring metric. The interpretability of the trained model was evaluated using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to quantify the contribution and relative importance of each input feature influencing the photocatalytic degradation rate constant. The optimized experimental parameters were methylene blue (MB) concentration of 10 mg/L, TiO2 particle size of 20 nm, catalyst surface area of 65 m2/g, TiO2 dose of 0.5 g/L, pH of 7, experimental temperature of 20 °C, and light intensity of 50 mW/cm2. The main output variable (Y) for model optimization was the degradation rate constant (k). Among them, the CatBoost machine learning model predicted a degradation rate constant (k) of 0.1383 min.

Limitations and practical considerations of ML models

The developed machine learning models demonstrate high prediction within the scope of the training dataset and must acknowledge some limitations to contextualize test practical considerations. The ML models particularly the high performing CatBoost (categorical boosting) is highly efficient at pattern recognition and categorical feature handling. The dataset was organized from diverse literature source; maybe it occurred experimental noise due to variations in reactor geometry, intensity of light measurement techniques, and catalyst preparation methods across different laboratories. A critical limitation is that the machine learning algorithms are primarily trained on data derived from synthetic solution (distilled water with pollutant). The performance of ML model like CatBoost model is determined based on the quality of the literature source data. In the field of photocatalysis, there is a lack of reporting the key features like intensity of light, surface area of catalyst, and string speed.

Photocatalytic degradation analysis of methylene blue using TiO2 nanoparticles: Fig. 6 (a) & (c) Pseudo-first-order kinetic plots showing for various catalyst loadings and concentration, Fig. 6 (b) & (d) Comparison of reaction rate constants (k) of experimental and ML model predicted values at varying catalyst loading and concentration respectively.

The effect of catalyst loading on the degradation rate constant (k) is illustrated in Fig. 6a. Increasing the TiO2 dosage from 25 mg to 75 mg enhanced the degradation rate, with higher k values observed at 50 mg and 75 mg compared with 25 mg. This behavior is commonly reported in photocatalytic systems and can be attributed to the increased number of available active sites and enhanced generation of reactive oxygen species as the catalyst amount increases. However, further increasing the catalyst loading to 100 mg and 150 mg resulted in a decrease in the degradation rate constant, suggesting the presence of an optimal catalyst loading. At higher catalyst dosages, particle aggregation, increased turbidity, and light scattering may reduce photon penetration and limit the effective utilization of the catalyst surface, thereby decreasing the photocatalytic efficiency. Figure 6b compares the reaction rate constants obtained experimentally with those predicted by the machine learning (ML) model. The experimental k values were calculated from the pseudo-first-order kinetic plots derived from the degradation data shown in Fig. 6a. Overall, the ML-predicted values closely follow the experimental trend across the studied catalyst loading range, indicating that the model successfully captures the general relationship between catalyst dosage and photocatalytic activity. Similarly, the influence of pollutant concentration on the degradation kinetics is presented in Fig. 6d. The results show that the reaction rate constant decreases as the initial pollutant concentration increases. This phenomenon is widely observed in photocatalytic processes and can be explained by several factors, including increased competition for active sites on the catalyst surface, reduced light penetration due to higher solution absorbance, and the accumulation of intermediate products that may inhibit active sites.

The comparison between ML-predicted and experimental reaction rate constants at different pollutant concentrations is shown in Fig. 6c. While the model reproduces the overall decreasing trend with increasing concentration, minor deviations are observed at higher concentration levels. These discrepancies may arise from the limited number of high-concentration data points available in the training dataset and the inherent variability of experimental conditions across different literature sources used for data compilation. Additionally, photocatalytic reactions often exhibit complex non-linear behavior at higher pollutant concentrations due to mass transfer limitations and photon attenuation effects, which may not be fully captured by the model. Despite these deviations, the strong agreement between the predicted and experimental values and the consistent reproduction of the overall trends demonstrate the robustness of the ML model in predicting the photocatalytic degradation kinetics of TiO2-based systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of machine learning to predict and optimize the photocatalytic degradation rate constant of Titanium dioxide systems for multiple pollutants. A structured dataset of 150 experimental data points was compiled from literature, and several models were trained and evaluated using standard metrics (R², MAE, and RMSE), with the CatBoost model showing the best predictive performance. Feature importance and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis identified catalyst dosage, light intensity, and pollutant concentration as the most influential factors governing photocatalytic efficiency. Experimental validation using Methylene blue confirmed the reliability of the optimized model, as the predicted degradation rate constant (0.1383 min⁻¹) closely matching the measured value (0.1278 min⁻¹). Although the dataset was compiled from heterogeneous literature sources and limited parameter ranges, the results highlight the potential of integrating machine learning with experimental validation as a cost-effective strategy to guide the design and optimization of photocatalytic systems for environmental remediation.
