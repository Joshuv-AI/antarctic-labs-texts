Impact of COVID-19 pandemic on traumatic brain injury emergency department visits, interfacility transfer and mortality in the United States, 2016–2020: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sydney Gilkey, Megan Armstrong, Robin Alexander, Biche Osong, Eric A Sribnick, Rachel M Stanley, Jonathan I Groner, Lawrence J Cook, Bo Lu, Henry Xiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to determine how the COVID-19 pandemic affected patient demographics, injury mechanisms, interhospital transfers and mortality of patients with traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) treated in US emergency departments (EDs).DesignThis cross-sectional study analysed 2016–2020 Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS) data.SettingUS EDs contained in the NEDS.ParticipantsParticipants were patients with moderate and severe TBI who visited the ED.Primary and secondary outcome measuresProbability sampling design and survey weights generated nationally representative estimates of ED visits by patient demographics, hospital characteristics and COVID-19 diagnosis and the top four leading TBI causes. To assess COVID-19 impact, we calculated the per cent change of estimated TBI ED visits and disposition outcomes from 2016 to 2019 and 2019 to 2020, and proportion and 95% CI of injury severity groups and admitted/transfer by hospital type and trauma centre level. A multivariable logistic regression model identified the mortality OR by patient demographics, injury severity, hospital characteristics and COVID-19 positive diagnosis.ResultsIn 2020, there were 527 123 ED visits nationally for TBI, 4.3% higher than 2016 but 1.0% lower than 2019. Patients with TBI transferring to short-term hospitals and other facilities increased by 16.0% and 18.2%, respectively, from 2016 to 2019 and were 3.7% and 14.1% higher in 2020 than in 2019. An estimated 3317 patients with TBI died (in ED or later in hospital) in 2020, which is 9.8% higher than 2019. Firearm-related TBI proportion was 15.1% higher in 2020 than in 2019. Patients with TBI with injury severity scores 25–75 were significantly higher at nontrauma (29.4% vs 31.9 %) and level III trauma centres (34.9% vs 38.2%) in 2020 than in 2019. Patients with TBI treated at rural hospitals had significantly higher odds of mortality (OR=1.95, CI=1.58–2.40) than those at urban hospitals.ConclusionsTBI patient mortality was higher at all US hospital types and almost all trauma centre levels in 2020 than in 2019. Patients with TBI treated at rural hospitals had a significantly higher mortality risk.Keywords: COVID-19, Brain Injuries, Triage

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic was declared in the United States by the WHO on 11 March 2020.1 Soon after, governments urged people to stay home and limit contact with others. These new public safety guidelines and a public perception that hospitals were sources of COVID-19 infection led the public to avoid hospitals and seek care after injuries.2 3

As the pandemic progressed, healthcare services worked to reallocate resources to accommodate the high volume of COVID-19 patients overwhelming emergency care department (ED) capacity worldwide.4,7 Elective procedures were postponed, and surgical prioritisation guidelines were modified to provide the space and staff to care for the large influx of patients with COVID-19.8 In response, neurosurgical workflow and neurological trauma management changed within the hospital.7 9 These changes, accompanied by the widespread isolation and hospital avoidance behaviours that the pandemic incited, are hypothesised to have had a wide-ranging impact on the epidemiology, interhospital transfers and outcomes of traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) during the COVID-19 pandemic.8 10

TBIs are the most prevalent cause of neurological disability worldwide and are the leading cause of mortality in young adults.11 Several studies have reported that changes to hospital policy and public behaviours during the COVID-19 pandemic altered ED visit volume, interfacility transfers, mechanisms of injury and mortality of TBIs compared with the prepandemic period.412,14 However, these studies are largely single-centre, retrospective studies that are limited in scope and not generalisable to a national level. National representative data are needed to better understand the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on patterns of TBI ED visits, interhospital transfers and case mortality risk at different hospital types in the United States.

In this study, we used a national representative medical records data set to determine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on patient demographics, injury mechanisms, interhospital transfers and mortality of patients with TBI treated in EDs of US hospitals. Based on the limited literature,412,14 we hypothesised that (1) firearm-related injuries would cause a significantly higher proportion of patients with TBI and (2) case mortality rate at rural hospitals and urban nonteaching hospitals would be significantly higher than at urban teaching hospitals during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Data source

Data for this cross-sectional study were obtained from the 2016–2020 Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS), developed and maintained as one of the national data sets of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.15 The NEDS contains clinical and resource-use information in a typical patient discharge abstract without identifiable data to protect individual patient, physician and hospital privacy. Data elements collected by the NEDS include patient demographics, expected payment source (eg, Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, self-pay), hospital characteristics, diagnoses, mechanism and intent of the injury and ED discharge disposition, including mortality. Due to its large volume of patient records (eg, 28 million ED visit records in 2020, 84.0% of all US ED visits), the NEDS data sets enable analyses of national ED utilisation patterns. NEDS is built on a 20% stratified sample of hospital-owned US EDs. The Nationwide Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt because the database is de-identified and publicly available.

On 1 October 2015, the United States transitioned from using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) to the Tenth Revision (ICD-10-CM/Procedure Coding System) code sets for reporting medical diagnoses and inpatient procedures. Therefore, we analysed 2016–2020 NEDS data, with 2016–2019 defined as the prepandemic period and 2020 as the pandemic period.

TBI case Identification

We used the proposed ICD-10-CM surveillance definition for TBI, recommended by the US National Center for Health Statistics, to identify TBI-related ED visits.16 ICD-10-CM codes for TBI morbidity include S02.0, S02.1, S02.8, S02.91, S04.02,S04.03, S04.04, S06, S07.1 and T74.4. In this study, all diagnosis codes in the NEDS database (up to 35) were searched to identify TBI cases, and we used the last letter of ICD-10-CM codes (A or B) to ensure that only initial ED visits for TBI were included. ED visits for subsequent TBI care (ICD-10-CM last letter D, G and K) or TBI sequela (ICD-10-CM last letter S) were excluded.

Outcomes of interest and statistical analysis

Our outcomes of interest include (1) national estimates of ED visits for TBI by patient demographics (sex, age, geographic region, expected primary payer and annual household income quarter group), hospital characteristics (urban non-teaching, urban teaching, rural; trauma centre level classification), COVID-19 diagnosis (positive vs negative, 2020 only) and ED disposition; (2) top external causes of TBI in 2019 vs 2020 and (3) number of deaths and case mortality by patient and hospital characteristics.

We used survey data analysis techniques to adjust for survey design and sample weights to yield national estimates. NEDS developed survey weights using the American Hospital Association data of more than 6200 US hospitals and healthcare as the standard. The Taylor series linearisation method was used to estimate the 95% CI of the national estimates. To assess the impact of COVID-19, we calculated 2% changes (% change from 2016 to 2019 and % change from 2019 to 2020) for nationally estimated ED visits of TBIs and their outcomes. For 2019 and 2020, we calculated the proportion of patients with injury severity score groups (ISS: 0–8, 9–15, 16–24 and 25–75) and the type of ED events by hospital location/teaching status and trauma centre levels. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to identify the OR of case mortality by patient demographics, hospital characteristics and COVID-19 positive diagnosis. Weighted frequency for the missing value categories of these variables was presented.

To comply with the HCUP data use agreement, result table cell values of 1–10 were suppressed to avoid individual identification. SAS software V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) computed weighted counts and 95% CI,17 and p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement statement

There were no funds or time allocated for patient and public involvement for this secondary data analysis, so we were unable to involve patients.

From 2016 to 2019, the Northeastern and Southern regions of the United States had more ED TBI visits than other regions (7.7% and 8.1%, respectively). Publicly insured patients had the largest per cent increase in TBIs (8.8%) from 2016 to 2019 and a slight increase (1.5%) from 2019 to 2020, while privately insured patients (3.5%), self-pay (6.0%) and other payment (4.7%) decreased from 2019 to 2020.

Routine ED dispositions of patients with TBI decreased by 4.6% from 2016 to 2019 and 3.3% from 2019 to 2020. Home healthcare increased by 33.2% from 2016 to 2019 and 44.8% from 2019 to 2020. Patients with TBI transferred to short-term hospitals and other facilities increased by 16.0% and 18.2%, respectively, from 2016 to 2019, further increasing by 3.7% and 14.1% from 2019 to 2020. An estimated 3317 patients with TBI died (either in ED or later in hospital) in 2020, 9.8% higher than 2019.

Urban teaching hospitals treated most patients with TBI (391 515, 74.3% of the total 527 123 patients with TBI) in 2020. TBI volume did not significantly change in rural hospitals from 2016 to 2019 (−0.02%) or from 2019 to 2020 (−0.6%). During the 2020 pandemic period, nontrauma and trauma centre level III hospitals increased patients with TBI presenting to the ED by 3.0% and 1.8%, respectively. In comparison, trauma centre level I hospitals decreased patients with TBI by 3.9% from 2019 to 2020. In 2020, 6873 patients (1.3%) of ED visits for TBI tested positive for COVID-19.

Compared with 2019, the proportion of TBI ISS groups and ED event dispositions (admitted into the same hospital, transferred to another short hospital and other) was generally similar regarding hospital teaching status and trauma centre levels (table 2). However, the proportion of patients with TBI ISS 25–75 was significantly higher at nontrauma centres (29.4%, CI: 28.4 to 30.5% vs 31.9 %, CI: 30.8 to 32.9%) and level III trauma centres (34.9%, CI: 33.2 to 36.6% vs 38.2%, CI: 36.1 to 40.3%) in 2020.

Falls remained the most common mechanism for TBIs in 2019 (63.9%) and 2020 (64.6%) (figure 1). However, TBIs caused by falls, motor vehicle traffic crashes and struck by or against an object decreased from 2019 to 2020 (3.42%, 5.85% and 7.92%, respectively). Firearm-related TBIs (1.4%) remained the lowest proportion of US ED visits for all patients with TBI in 2020, though this was an increase of 15.1% over 2019.

The case mortality rate for TBIs presenting to the ED decreased by 10.0% from 2016 to 2019 but increased by 9.9% from 2019 to 2020 (table 3). Case mortality rates in all regions increased (the lowest increase was in the Northeast by 1.2% and the highest increase was in the West by 19.2%) during the 2020 pandemic period. Urban nonteaching and rural hospitals had higher case mortality rate increases (20.0% and 14.6%, respectively) than urban teaching hospitals (6.8%) from 2019 to 2020. Nontrauma centres and level III trauma centres had higher mortality rate increases (12.5% and 26.1%) than level I trauma centres (11.8%) from 2019 to 2020. The mortality rate was similar between those with COVID-19 positive diagnosis (5.2 per 1000 patients) and those patients with TBI without COVID-19 diagnosis (6.3 per 1000 patients).

Results from a binary multivariable logistic regression analysis of ED mortality of TBIs in the prepandemic period (2016–2019) compared with the pandemic period (2020) are shown in table 4. Sex, age, TBI ISS, hospital location, hospital type and trauma centre level classification all significantly impacted the case mortality risk of patients with TBI. When comparing patients with TBI treated at urban nonteaching hospitals, patients with TBI treated at rural hospitals had significantly higher odds of mortality (OR=1.76, CI=1.51–2.05), but those treated at urban teaching hospitals had significantly lower odds of mortality (OR=0.81, CI=0.71–0.91). The odds of ED mortality overall did not significantly differ between the prepandemic and pandemic periods. Similarly, the OR of mortality in patients with TBI diagnosed with positive COVID-19 (OR=0.73, CI=0.34–1.44) did not significantly differ.

Discussion

This study evaluated how the COVID-19 pandemic impacted the national trends of TBIs treated in US EDs and the ED disposition outcomes using data sets from 2016 to 2020. The national estimates of TBI-related ED visits were 5.3% higher in 2019 than in 2016 but 1.0% lower in the pandemic period of 2020 than 2019. More patients with TBI transferred to other hospitals/facilities during the prepandemic and pandemic periods. Overall ED mortality rate was 9.8% higher in 2020 than in 2019, and patients with TBI treated at rural hospitals had significantly higher odds of mortality, but those treated at urban teaching hospitals had significantly lower odds of mortality than patients treated at urban nonteaching hospitals. The OR of case mortality was lower for patients with TBI diagnosed with positive COVID-19, which is inconsistent with a prior study reporting a higher risk of death among patients hospitalised for COVID-19 versus seasonal influenza at the US Department of Veterans Affairs hospitals.18

Our study found higher TBI ED visits volume in 2019 than in 2016, though visits were slightly lower in 2020 than in 2019 (532 345 to 527 123 visits). This finding supports several retrospective single-centre studies that reported fewer TBI referrals and admissions during the pandemic.4 12 13 19 However, further investigation has revealed that volume levels may fluctuate depending on the lockdown phase. A single-centre study from India found lower overall TBI volume in the beginning months of the pandemic and higher volume at the end of the pandemic.13

In 2019, we found more TBI ED visits in older patient populations, especially those aged 65 to <80 years than in 2016. All other age groups, except 0–5 years, had fewer ED visits of TBI in 2020 than in 2019. Several other studies have investigated the age of patients with TBI before and during the pandemic and found no significant changes in the mean and median ages of patients with TBI.13 14 20 One study that analysed neurotrauma volume in Pennsylvania21 found that the proportion of TBIs in patients aged >60 years was higher during the pandemic than the prepandemic period. Additionally, they found that TBIs during the pandemic were significantly more likely to occur indoors in private residences.21 Because falls remained the most common TBI mechanism during the pandemic, falls might occur more frequently in the older population; however, our data suggest that the proportion of TBI caused by falls, motor vehicle crashes and struck against/by an object was lower in 2020 than in 2019.

Overall, the case mortality rate for TBIs presenting to the ED was lower in 2019 than in 2016 but higher (9.9%) in 2020 than in 2019. Higher case mortality during the pandemic occurred in male and female patients across all regions, at all types of hospitals and at almost all trauma centre levels except at the level II trauma centres. ISS of patients with TBI treated in US EDs was significantly higher in 2020 compared with prepandemic years, particularly at nontrauma centres and level III trauma centres in our study. Several other studies reported more severe TBIs and neurotrauma mortality during the pandemic.10 13 22 Concussions to mild TBIs may be lower during the pandemic due to the public perception that hospitals should be avoided in non-life-threatening situations. However, our study excluded concussion patients from our analysis. Some of the patients in our study might present to hospitals later when their condition worsened, leading to hospitals seeing a larger proportion of high-severity TBIs.13 This pattern of widespread hospital avoidance, which led to higher severity at ED presentation, was observed with various medical problems during the COVID-19 pandemic.23 24 In the future, further efforts to alter public perception of hospitals during pandemics may encourage people to present to hospitals with critical injuries and prevent these increases in severe injuries and mortality.23

Lack of resources for many hospitals during the pandemic may have led to interrupted emergency care for trauma, leading to higher mortality of trauma patients in the EDs.8 9 Patients with TBI treated at rural hospital EDs were significantly more likely to experience mortality during the pandemic than patients in urban nonteaching hospitals. The pandemic disproportionately affected under-resourced and rural healthcare systems due to a lack of funding, staff, resources and capacity.25 The overstretching of these hospitals during the pandemic period may account for the higher severity of TBIs in this area. Rural hospitals also experienced a higher mortality of COVID-19 patients than other hospitals, which further reflects the disparities experienced in mortality by rural health systems during the pandemic period.26 Revised operating procedures for under-resourced hospitals during times of strain have been suggested,27 which rural healthcare systems could use to increase efficiency and promote better patient outcomes. State and federal governments should be encouraged to consider budget shifts to increase funding for rural and under-resourced healthcare systems, especially during increased strain like COVID-19, to decrease these disparities and promote health equity.26

Before the pandemic, falls and motor vehicle accidents were the most common mechanisms for TBIs worldwide.28 29 Our study found that the proportion of motor vehicle-related TBIs was lower in 2020 than in 2019. Several other studies also found fewer motor vehicle TBIs during the pandemic.8 12 13 22 Our data showed that the proportion of ED visits of firearm-related TBIs was higher in 2020 (15.1%) than in 2019. Firearm-related TBI and assaults were also higher during the pandemic in several other studies.10 12 22 These results are congruent with other reports of firearm-related trauma and firearm sales increasing during the pandemic.30,32 The widespread negative impact of the pandemic and lockdown procedures on social and financial well-being may have contributed to more interpersonal violence during the pandemic.33

Limitations

One strength of this study was the national emergency department visit data sets, which allowed us to generalise results to the national US ED visits for nonconcussion TBI prepandemic and during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the findings reported here should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, data obtained for this study were from administrative medical records, originally collected for billing purposes rather than research. These medical records could have differed in hospital billing practices and changed over time, like including a COVID-19 code. We used common practices to identify TBIs using ICD-10-CM code recommendations by the National Center for Health Statistics. We believe the TBI case identification based on nationally recommended ICD-10-CM codes will make our results interpretable in the existing literature. Second, a start date for the pandemic period is difficult to pinpoint, given different state lockdown dates. For ease of analysis and interpretation, we used the full 2020 year as the pandemic period. Adjusting the pandemic period start date may alter our results because traumatic injuries exhibit seasonality, including TBIs, with peaks typically occurring during the summer and fall.34 35 Knowing lockdowns began in the first few months of 2020, we felt we appropriately allocated the bulk of TBI injuries. Third, the actual number of patients with TBI during the COVID-19 pandemic might be underestimated due to people preferring to stay home even when they suffered nonconcussion TBI. Finally, the results were unable to be disaggregated by both sex and gender as only sex was reported in the NEDS data sets. Further work is needed to capture gender within nationally representative medical data to determine whether results were modelled using sex hold.

Conclusions

Our study based on a nationally representative ED visits data set suggests that the COVID-19 pandemic had widespread implications on the patient demographics, interhospital transfers, external causes and case mortality of patients with TBI treated in the hospital EDs across all US regions. The national estimates of ED visits of patients with TBI were lower in 2020 than in 2019; however, TBI case mortality rates were higher in both male and female patients across all regions, at all types of hospitals and at almost all trauma centre levels except at the level II trauma centres, which reversed the general decreasing trend of mortality risk from 2016 to 2019. Patients with TBI treated at rural hospitals had a significantly higher risk of mortality during the pandemic.
