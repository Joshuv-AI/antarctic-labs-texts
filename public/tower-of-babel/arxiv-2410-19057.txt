Continuity of the solution map of some active scalar equations in Hölder and Zygmund spaces
Marc Magaña

Abstract
We prove that the solution map for a family of non-linear transport equations in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , with a velocity field given by the convolution of the density with a kernel that is smooth away from the origin and homogeneous of degree − ( n − 1 ) -(n-1) , is continuous in both the little Hölder class and the little Zygmund class. For particular choices of the kernel, one recovers well-known equations such as the 2D Euler or the 3D quasi-geostrophic equations.



1
Introduction

The study of transport equations plays a crucial role in various fields of applied mathematics, particularly in fluid dynamics and mathematical physics. In this paper, we focus on the Cauchy problem for a family of non-linear non-local transport equations given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
&v(\cdot,t)=k*\rho(\cdot,t),\\
&\rho(\cdot,0)=\rho_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(T)

where
\rho=\rho(x,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a scalar quantity usually known as the
density
,
v=v(x,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector field called the
velocity
and
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a homogeneous kernel of degree
-(n-1)
and smooth away from the origin and relates
\rho
and
v
via convolution.

This particular family of equations was studied by Cantero in
[
3
]
where he proved existence and uniqueness of Hölder regular solutions. Later, Cantero, Mateu, Orobitg and Verdera
[
4
]
proved persistence of boundary smoothness of vortex patches for the case of odd kernels. Notably, this family encompasses several well-known equations as special cases, for particular choices of
k
, serving as a generalization of these models. A prime example is the vorticity formulation of the Euler equation in the plane, where
k=\nabla^{\perp}N
being
N(x)
the fundamental solution of the Laplacian. Other remarkable examples are the aggregation equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for initial conditions being the characteristic function of some domain (see
[
2
]
for the derivation), here
k=\nabla N
, and the quasigeostrophic equation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which corresponds to
k=L(\nabla N)
for
L(x)=(-x_{2},x_{1},0)
, see
[
8
]
.

The existing literature on these equations is vast. For the Euler equation see, for instance
[
5
,
6
,
11
]
and for the aggregation
[
2
,
1
]
. The main focus in these papers was on either existence and uniqueness of Hölder regular solutions or in studying persistance of boundary smoothness of patches. The same happens in
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
. However, the question of continuity with respect to initial conditions was not explicitly addressed in these works and is rarely studied in this context, with
[
7
]
being an interesting exception.

According to Hadamard, a Cauchy problem is locally well-posed in a Banach space
X
if for any initial data in
X
there exists a unique solution that persists for some
T>0
in the space
C([0,T),X)
and depends continuously on the initial data. This notion of well-posedness is rather strong and may not always be suitable, as highlihted by Kato
[
10
]
. Recent works have explored ill-posedness in the sense of Hadamard of the Euler equations under certain initial conditions, providing a comprehensive survey on this topic
[
12
, Section 1]
.

The authors of the above mentioned article, Misiołek and Yoneda, proved well-posedness in the sense of Hadamard for the Euler equations in 2D and 3D for velocities in the little Hölder spaces
c^{1,\gamma},
with
0<\gamma<1
. They also provided an example for the 3D Euler equation (which is worth noting that is not a transport equation) that shows that the problem is ill-posed, in general, for
v\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma},
in the sense of Hadamard. In this paper, we follow and extend their work to prove well-posedness for (
T
) in the case that the initial data is in
c^{\gamma}
, for
0<\gamma<1
. It is important to point out that we are changing the focus from the velocity field to the density, and that is the reason why the space we study is
c^{\gamma}
rather than
c^{1,\gamma}
. Moreover, we also establish Hadamard well-posedness for initial data in the little Zygmund class.

The main theorem of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\};\mathbb{R}^{n})
a kernel homogeneous of degree
-(n-1).
Then

(
A
)

For
0<\gamma<1,
and
\rho_{0}\in c^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}),
there exist
T>0
and a unique weak solution
\rho
to the transport equation (
T
) such that the map
\rho_{0}\to\rho
is continuous from
c^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
C([0,T),c^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})).

(
B
)

The result in
(A)
is also true if one changes
c^{\gamma}
for the little Zygmund class,
\lambda^{*}.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the definitions of Hölder spaces and present some relevant results concerning these spaces and Calderón-Zygmund operators acting on them. In Section
3
, we prove part
(A)
of Theorem
1.1
, that is, we prove the result for the little Hölder spaces. Subsequently in Section
4
we extend this result to the little Zygmund class, thereby proving part
(B)
.

2
Preliminaries

First, we recall the definitions of Hölder and little Hölder spaces.

Definition 1
.

Given
0<\gamma<1
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
let

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)|\text{ and }\left|f\right|_{\gamma}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}.

We define the norm

\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}\coloneqq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|f\right|_{\gamma}.

For
F(x)=(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{d}(x)):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define
\left\|F\right\|_{\gamma}\coloneqq\sup_{i=1,\dots,d}\left\|f_{i}\right\|_{\gamma}
and the Hölder space

\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\;|\;\left\|f\right\|_{\gamma}<\infty\right\}.

We also define

\left|F\right|_{1,\gamma}=\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|DF\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|DF\right|_{\gamma},

and the Hölder space
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of the functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left|f\right|_{1,\gamma}<\infty
.

Let
c^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the closed subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of those functions satisfying the vanishing condition

\lim_{h\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
0<|x-y|<h\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}=0.

(1)

Likewise, let
c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the closed subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of those functions whose derivatives satisfy the vanishing condition. We call
c^{\gamma}
and
c^{1,\gamma}
little Hölder spaces. It is easy to see that
c^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the clousure of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the usual Hölder norm of
C^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Let us now recall some well known properties for elements of the Hölder spaces as well as bounds for Calderón-Zygmund operators acting
on them.

\displaystyle\leq c\left\|DX\right\|_{\gamma}^{2n-1}

\displaystyle\left|X^{-1}\right|_{1,\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq c\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{2n-1}

a kernel homogeneous of degree

That is,

such that

(10)

\displaystyle\left|Sf\right|_{\gamma}

(11)

Lemma 2.3
.

Given

3
Local well-posedness in
c^{\gamma}

In this section, we establish the main result of this paper concerning the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem (
T
) for functions in the little Hölder spaces
c^{\gamma}
with
0<\gamma<1
. That is, we prove part
(A)
of Theorem
1.1
.

As in the case of Euler equation, a good way to prove an existence and uniqueness result is by dealing with an, in some sense, equivalent equation rather than the one presented in Theorem
1.1
. To introduce this equivalent equation, we first need to define the
flow map
.

Given a velocity field
v
and a point
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define, whenever it is well-defined, the
flow map
or the
particle trajectory map
,
X(\alpha,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the solution of the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}X(\alpha,t)=v(X(\alpha,t),t),\\
&X(\alpha,0)=\alpha.\end{aligned}\right.

This map indicates the position at time t of the particle that was initially at

\alpha
and that has moved according to the velocity field at every moment. If
\rho(\cdot,t)
is sufficiently smooth, a straightforward computation shows that
\rho(X(\alpha,t),t)=\rho_{0}(\alpha).
Thus, it can be said that
\rho
is transported with the flow defined by
v
, which justifies calling (
T
) a transport equation.

We can focus then on proving existence, uniqueness and regularity for the ODE for the particle trajectory map
X
, which after a change of variables is:

\frac{dX}{dt}(\alpha,t)=\int_{R^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\det\left(DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)\right)d\alpha^{\prime}.

A standard way to prove existence and uniqueness for an ODE is to apply
Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem, which can be stated as follows:

Theorem 3.1

(Picard-Lindelöf)
.

Let
O\subseteq B
be an open subset of a Banach space
B
and let
F:O\to B
be a locally Lipschitz continuous mapping. Then, given
X_{0}\in O,
there exists a time
T>0
such that the ordinary differential equation

\frac{dX}{dt}=F(X),\quad X(\cdot,t=0)=X_{0},

has a unique (local) solution
X\in C^{1}((-T,T);O).

To apply Theorem
3.1
, we first need an equation of type
\frac{dX}{dt}=F(X)
, which we have for

F(X(\alpha,t))\coloneqq\int_{R^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\det\left(DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)\right)d\alpha^{\prime}

(12)

We also need a Banach space
B
and an open subset of
B
such that
X
belongs to it. We take, as in
[
12
]
,
B=c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the open set

U_{\delta}=\left\{X:R^{n}\to R^{n}:X=e+\varphi_{X},\varphi_{X}\in c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and }\left|\varphi_{X}\right|_{1,\gamma}<\delta\right\},

(13)

where
e(x)=x
and
\delta>0
is chosen small enough so that

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\det DX(x)>\frac{1}{2}.

(14)

Clearly,
U_{\delta}
can be identified with an open ball centered at the origin in
c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We are now ready to check the hypothesis in Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem. We split them into the two following propositions.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
U_{\delta}
as defined in (
13
). Then, the functional
F
defined in (
12
) maps
U_{\delta}
to
c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

We need to first verify that

\left\|F(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{i\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{i}}F(X)\right\|_{\gamma}<\infty.

If we consider the change of variables
x^{\prime}=X(\alpha^{\prime})
in (
12
) we get, for the
j-

F_{j}(X)(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{j}(X(\alpha)-x^{\prime})\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))dx^{\prime}=(k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})\circ X(\alpha),

(15)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{0}\coloneqq\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}.

Following
[
3
, Section 3]
and setting
R_{0}=m(supp(\rho_{0}))^{\frac{1}{n}},
one gets

\left\|F(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cR_{0}\left\|DX\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

(16)

which is bounded for
X\in c^{1,\gamma}
and
\rho_{0}\in c^{\gamma}_{c}.
Next, we focus on the norms of the derivatives of
F(X).
We write
\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{i}.}
By definition of the norm we have
\left\|\partial_{i}F(X)\right\|_{\gamma}=\sup_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\left\|\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)\right\|_{\gamma}.
Thus, we work with
\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)
for
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Applying the chain rule to
F_{j}
in (
15
), combined with Lemma
2.3
, yields

\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)(\alpha)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}(c_{rj}\rho_{0}(\alpha)+p.v.(\partial_{r}k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})(X(\alpha)))\partial_{i}X_{r}(\alpha).

(17)

Clearly, hypothesis in Lemma
2.2
are satisfied if
P=\partial_{r}k_{j},
thus we can use (
10
) to bound the

norm of the derivatives:

\begin{split}\left\|\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}&\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}(|c_{rj}|\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|p.v.(\partial_{r}k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\left\|\partial_{i}X_{r}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq\left\|DX\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{r=1}^{n}(|c_{rj}|\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|p.v.(\partial_{r}k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\\
&\leq n\left\|DX\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(C\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+c\left\{\left|\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right|_{\gamma}+\max(1,\ln{R})\left\|\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\right\}\right)\\
&\leq C(\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right|_{\gamma})\end{split}

(18)

where the last step uses that
R=m(supp(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))^{\frac{1}{n}}
is bounded and
\left\|\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

To bound the Hölder seminorm we first use (
2

\begin{split}\left|\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)\right|_{\gamma}&\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left\|c_{rj}\rho_{0}+p.v.(\partial_{r}k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})(X)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\partial_{i}X_{r}\right|_{\gamma}\\
&+\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left|c_{rj}\rho_{0}+p.v.(\partial_{r}k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})(X)\right|_{\gamma}\left\|\partial_{i}X_{r}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&=I+II\end{split}

The first term is estimated as in (

I\leq C(\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right|_{\gamma})\left|DX\right|_{\gamma}\leq C(\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right|_{\gamma})\left|D\varphi_{X}\right|_{\gamma}.

) and then (

\begin{split}II&\leq\left\|DX\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{r=1}^{n}|c_{rj}|\left|\rho_{0}\right|_{\gamma}+\left|p.v.(\partial_{r}k_{j}*\tilde{\rho}_{0})\right|_{\gamma}\left\|DX\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}\\

(19)

Combining (

), (

we get

\left|F(X)\right|_{1,\gamma}\leq C(\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\rho_{0}\right|_{\gamma}).

(20)

maps

In order to apply Picard-Lindelöf theorem one must check now that the functional

Proof of Theorem

(A)
.

Let
B=c^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})

defined in (

Since,
\rho

\rho\in c^{\gamma}_{c}
to (

). To see that the solution is unique, assume that
\tilde{\rho}\in c^{\gamma}_{c}
and
\tilde{v}\in c^{1,\gamma}
is also a solution to (

) with the same initial data. Then we can find a trajectory
\tilde{X}(\cdot,t)
associated to
\tilde{v}(\cdot,t)
such that
\tilde{\rho}
is transported by

But by uniqueness of the flow-map,

so
\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t)=\rho_{0}(\tilde{X}^{-1}(\cdot,t))=\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(\cdot,t))=\rho(\cdot,t)

We now focus on the dependence of the solutions on
\rho_{0}.
Note that
\rho_{0}
appears as a parameter on the right hand side of the ODE

\frac{dX}{dt}=\int_{R^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\det\left(DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)\right)d\alpha^{\prime}=F_{\rho_{0}}(X)(\alpha).

Moreover, since the dependence is linear on
\rho_{0}
it follows that continuity of the map
\rho_{0}\to F_{\rho_{0}}
is an immediate consequence of the estimate in (
20
). Applying the theorem of continuous dependence on initial data of ODEs for Banach spaces we find that there exist
T>0
and a unique Lagrangian flow
X\in C([0,T),U_{\delta})
which depends continuously on
\rho_{0}
.

Next, take
\rho_{0},\omega_{0}\in c^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and let
X(t)
and
Y(t)
be the corresponding Lagrangian flows solving the Cauchy problem in
U_{\delta}
. Given any
\varepsilon>0
and using he fact that smooth functions are dense in

\phi_{\varepsilon}

such that

\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)-\rho_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}<\varepsilon.

We estimate

\begin{split}\left\|\rho-\omega\right\|_{\gamma}&=\left\|\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(x,t))-\omega_{0}(Y^{-1}(x,t))\right\|_{\gamma}\\
&\leq\left\|\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(x,t))-\phi_{\varepsilon}(X^{-1}(x,t))\right\|_{\gamma}+\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}(X^{-1}(x,t))-\phi_{\varepsilon}(Y^{-1}(x,t))\right\|_{\gamma}\\
&+\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}(Y^{-1}(x,t))-\omega_{0}(Y^{-1}(x,t)\right\|_{\gamma}=I+II+III\end{split}

I
is clearly bounded by
\varepsilon
. For the second term we have

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}(rX^{-1}-(1-r)Y^{-1})(X^{-1}-Y^{-1})\right\|_{\gamma}dr

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\circ(rX^{-1}-(1-r)Y^{-1})\right\|_{\gamma}\left\|X^{-1}-Y^{-1}\right\|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\phi_{\varepsilon}\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X^{-1}(x,t)-Y^{-1}(x,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}

where on the second inequality we have used the algebra properties of Hölder functions, (
3
), and on the third one (
7
). Now it is clear that
II
converges to zero by continuity of the inversion map, see Lemma
3.4
, and the fact that
X
converges to
Y
in
c^{1,\gamma}
whenever
\rho_{0}
converges to
\omega_{0}
since the Lagrangian flows depend continuously on the initial vorticities. Finally, using (
8
) we get

\begin{split}III&\leq\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}(Y^{-1}(x,t))-\rho_{0}(Y^{-1}(x,t)\right\|_{\gamma}+\left\|\rho_{0}(Y^{-1}(x,t))-\omega_{0}(Y^{-1}(x,t))\right\|_{\gamma}\\
&\leq\left\|\phi_{\varepsilon}(Y^{-1}(x,t))-\rho_{0}(Y^{-1}(x,t)\right\|_{\gamma}+\left\|\rho_{0}-\omega_{0}\right\|_{\gamma}(1+\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma(2n-1)})\end{split}

where the first term is bounded by
\varepsilon
and the second term converges to 0 whenever
\rho_{0}
converges to
\omega_{0}
.
∎

4
Local well-posedness in the little Zygmund class,
\lambda^{*}

In this section, we address the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem (
T
) for functions in the little Zygmund class. For this case, the existence and uniqueness of solutions are also novel contributions.

The Zygmund class was introduced by Zygmund in the 1940s when he observed that the conjugate
function of a Lipschitz function in the unit circle does not need to be Lipschitz but it is in
the Zygmund class
[
14
]
.

Definition 2
.

We define the Zygmund class,
\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
, consisting of all bounded continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which

\left|f\right|_{*}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h)|}{|h|}<\infty,

We equip
\Lambda^{*}
with the norm
\left\|f\right\|_{*}\coloneqq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|f\right|_{*}<\infty
.

As in the definition of Hölder spaces, see definition
1
, one can extend the definition of the Zygmund class to functions
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We also define the little Zygmund class,
\lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
as the closed subspace of
\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of those functions satisfying the vanishing condition

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sup_{|h|<\delta}\frac{|f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h)|}{|h|}=0,

and as noted for the little Hölder spaces one can see that
\lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the clousure of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the usual Zygmund norm of
\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

It is well known that
\operatorname{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
0<\gamma<1
and, in fact, the Zygmund class is the natural substitute of the Lipschitz class in many different contexts.

We now present a lemma that will be crucial later.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f,g\in\Lambda^{*}
and let
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
, for some
0<\gamma<1,
be an invertible transformation with
|\det\nabla X|\geq c_{1}>0.
Then

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq c\left\|f\right\|_{*}\left\|g\right\|_{*},

(21)

\displaystyle\left|f\circ X\right|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq c\left|f\right|_{*}(1+\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}).

(22)

Proof.

The fact that
\Lambda^{*}
is an algebra, and therefore that the first inequality holds, is well known, see, for instance
[
13
, Chapter 4.6, Theorem 2]
. However, we give here an alternative proof that was shown to us by J.J. Donaire.

Notice that

f(x+h)g(x+h)-2f(x)g(x)+f(x-h)g(x-h)=\\
(f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h))(g(x+h)-2g(x)+g(x-h))\\
+f(x)(g(x+h)-2g(x)+g(x-h))+g(x)(f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h))\\
-(f(x+h)-f(x))(g(x-h)-g(x))-(f(x-h)-f(x))(g(x+h)-g(x))

Thus, using the definition of the seminorm and the fact that Zygmund functions are lip-log we get

|f(x+h)g(x+h)-2f(x)g(x)+f(x-h)g(x-h)|\leq\\
|h|(\left|f\right|_{*}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left|g\right|_{*})+c|h|^{2}\left|f\right|_{*}\left|g\right|_{*}\left(1+\log^{2}\frac{1}{|h|}\right)

which implies the first inequality since
\left\|fg\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

We now prove the second inequality for small
h
, since otherwise one can just use the
L^{\infty}
norm for the bounds. Notice that if we set
k=X(\alpha-h)-X(\alpha)
we have

|f(X(\alpha+h))-2f(X(\alpha))+f(X(\alpha-h))|\leq\\
|f(X(\alpha)+k)-2f(X(\alpha))+f(X(\alpha)-k)|+|f(X(\alpha+h)-f(X(\alpha)-k)|=I+II

It is clear that

I\leq\left|f\right|_{*}|k|=\left|f\right|_{*}|X(\alpha-h)-X(\alpha)|\leq\left|f\right|_{*}\left\|\nabla X\right\|_{L^{\infty}}|h|

and for
II
we use again that Zygmund functions are lip-log

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\leq C\left|f\right|_{*}|X(\alpha+h)-X(\alpha)+k|\log\frac{1}{|X(\alpha+h)-X(\alpha)+k|}

\displaystyle=C\left|f\right|_{*}|X(\alpha+h)-2X(\alpha)+X(\alpha-h)|\log\frac{1}{|X(\alpha+h)-2X(\alpha)+X(\alpha-h)|}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|f\right|_{*}|h|^{1+\gamma}\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma}\log\frac{1}{\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma}|h|^{1+\gamma}}

\displaystyle=C\left|f\right|_{*}|h||h|^{\gamma}\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma}\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma}|h|^{\gamma}}+\log\frac{1}{h}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left|f\right|_{*}|h|(1+\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma})

where in the second inequality we use that
t\log\frac{1}{t}
is increasing in
\left(0,\frac{1}{e}\right)
, together with the mean value theorem inequality and the that
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
. The final inequality holds since
h
is small enough, so that
|h|^{\gamma}\left|DX\right|_{\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{e},
and therefore, both terms
|h|^{\gamma}\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma}\log\frac{1}{\left|\nabla X\right|_{\gamma}|h|^{\gamma}}
and
|h|^{\gamma}\log\frac{1}{h}
are bounded. Putting all together and using the definition of
\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}
we get the desired inequality.
∎

Remark
.

Notice that if
f\in\lambda^{*}
and
X\in c^{1,\gamma}
for some
0<\gamma<1
the proof of (
22
) also gives that
f\circ X\in\lambda^{*}.

We finally prove
(B)
. Consider
\rho_{0}\in\lambda^{*}.
Since
\lambda^{*}\subset c^{\gamma}
for all
0<\gamma<1
by the proof of Theorem
1.1
part
(A)

. Since the solution is transported by the flow it follows by (
22
) from the previous lemma that the solution
\rho(\cdot,t)=\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(\cdot,t))
is in
\lambda^{*}.
To verify that the solution depends continuously on the initial data, one can follow the argument used for the Hölder class, replacing the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\gamma}
with
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}

4.1
one can prove analogs of Lemmas
2.1

2.2

the final part of the proof of
