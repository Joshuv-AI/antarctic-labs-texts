Effectiveness of standardized training for rural-oriented tuition-waived medical students in Yunnan Province: a multi-center cross-sectional survey
Authors: Xing Wang, Anye Lei, Qiaoyun Huang, Yingyurui Liu, Yali Li, Xiao Yan, Weiming Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal primary healthcare providers are grappling with workforce shortages, regional development imbalances, and inconsistent service delivery models. Rural-oriented tuition-waived medical students (RTMSs), who are the focus of standardized residency training (SRT), play a pivotal role in enhancing grassroots medical capabilities. This study focuses on the current situation and satisfaction level of RTMSs participation in SRT, with a particular emphasis on the deeper factors that influence their willingness to continue practicing in rural areas after completing the program.MethodsIn June 2022, a multicenter cross-sectional observational study was conducted across eight hospitals in Yunnan Province, surveying 643 RTMSs enrolled in the SRT program. Descriptive and analytical statistical methods were used to analyze the data.ResultsFindings indicated that 91.29% of the respondents endorsed the SRT, believing it significantly enhanced their core competencies, and 81% were satisfied with the training. However, only 28% expressed willingness to continue practicing in rural primary healthcare institutions after their contractual service. The low retention intention was mainly associated with expectations for higher income, positive psychological states, frequent skill training and lectures, regular psychological counseling, and team-building activities, as well as an enhanced sense of identity as RTMSs.ConclusionsThe study underscores the critical role of SRT in improving professional competences for RTMSs. Although training satisfaction has improved, their willingness to remain in rural practice after contract completion remains low. Immediate measures should include increasing rural allowances and improving housing conditions, while long-term strategies require government-level reforms to enhance remuneration, living conditions, and career development pathways.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08231-2.Keywords: Standardized training, General practitioner, Free medical education

Introduction

A global shortage of human resources for primary health care is particularly pronounced in rural and remote areas. This predicament exerts a substantial influence on the health outcomes of the local population by contributing to delays in the timely diagnosis and treatment of prevalent diseases and impeding the implementation of public health programs [1]. This, in turn, engenders the emergence of more profound and complex health complications over time. In response, many countries have undertaken efforts to optimize general medical training systems, improve standardized residency training programs, and develop talent cultivation models that encourage medical students to remain in underserved communities. For instance, the “5 + 2 + 3” model in the United Kingdom and the general practice training system in Australia represent well-established and evolving frameworks aimed at addressing such workforce disparities. Evidence suggests that focusing on trainees’ mental health improves training effect [2] while incorporating practice-based learning and continuous improvement strategies can stimulate active learning and engagement among students [3].

In China, there are three primary pathways for medical education: the general five-year clinical medicine program (non-directional), which allows graduates to seek employment independently; the eight-year “Outstanding Doctors” program, aimed at cultivating highly specialized medical professionals; the Rural-oriented Tuition-waived Medical Education (RTME) program, which is designed to mitigate the shortage of general practitioners at the grassroots level. These students receive tuition-free education in exchange for a commitment to serve in rural areas for six years following graduation. Breach of contract, including failure to assume duty, incomplete service, unauthorized leave, or failure to pass licensing exams—requires full reimbursement of waived expenses along with an additional penalty ranging from 50% to 100% of the waived amount [4]. As of 2021, the Chinese government had invested nearly 2.1 billion yuan in the Rural-oriented tuition-waived medical students (RTMSs) training program. By 2024, approximately 89,000 RTMSs students had been trained for deployment across 30,000 townships in 22 central and western provinces. Of these, around 54,000 have completed their service obligations, significantly alleviating the shortage of primary health-care personnel in these underserved regions [5]. In Yunnan Province, the annual enrollment in medical programs exceeds several thousand students, with the proportion of RTMSs ranging from 10% to 20%. After more than a decade of implementation, early cohorts of RTMSs have achieved nearly a 100% pass rate on both the standardized residency training (SRT) and the national medical licensing examinations [6, 7].

The distribution of medical resources in China remains highly imbalanced between urban and rural regions, with a severe shortage of grassroot medical personnel seriously constraining the improvement of rural medical services [8, 9]. According to national data, rural medical practitioners constitute only 14.98% of the total physician workforce, and their motivation for career advancement remains low [10]. In Yunnan Province, for instance, 85% of rural healthcare workers participate in SRT, yet the attrition rate ranges 8% to 10%. Medical students generally prioritize career development opportunities and workplace conditions, while also expressing expectations for fair compensation, manageable working hours, and a high degree of professional autonomy [11]. Moreover, training facilities for SRT are primarily concentrated in provincial capitals, resulting in high transportation costs and limited access to training opportunities for practitioners in remote areas [12]. Previous studies have shown that rural medical workers in Yunnan report significantly lower satisfaction with the SRT program compared to their counterparts in more developed eastern regions [13]. However, research examining the relationship between the satisfaction levels of RTMSs and factors such as salary, training content, psychological well-being, and self-identity remains limited. To address this gap, the present study conducted a cross-sectional survey involving eight hospitals in Yunnan Province. It systematically assessed the current status and satisfaction levels of RTMSs enrolled in the SRT program and explored the underlying mechanisms influencing their training experience.

The questionnaire for this study was carefully developed and rigorously validated, with the primary aim of assessing RTMSs’ satisfaction with various components of the SRT program and to explore their intention to continue working in rural healthcare institutions after completing their service contracts. Evaluation framework

Evaluation is a critical component of health professional education, as it allows educators to identify effective teaching strategies and serves as an essential step in curriculum planning. The Kirkpatrick Model, a widely used framework for training evaluation, organizes outcomes into four levels: Reaction, Learning, Behavior, and Results [14]. This framework assesses participants’ engagement across four dimensions: (1) students’ reactions to the training, (2) learning outcomes, (3) changes in students’ behavior, and (4) whether the training achieved its intended results. Each level provides a distinct perspective on the effectiveness and impact of the training program, informing both immediate improvements and long-term educational planning.

Methods

Design, setting, and participants

This multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted in June 2022 in Yunnan Province, China, using a stratified sampling method. Initially, 42 standardized residency training bases in Western medicine recognized by the National Health Commission were identified as the sampling pool. After excluding three bases that did not admit RTMSs, the remaining 10 provincial-level and 29 prefecture-level general practice training bases (all Class A, Grade 3 hospitals) were stratified by geographic region. Four provincial-level hospitals were selected using a simple random number table, and another four prefecture-level hospitals were chosen based on comprehensive consideration of operational capacity, historical enrollment and graduation data of RTMSs, and the availability of qualified general practice trainers. Ultimately, 643 registered medical trainees (RTMSs) from eight hospitals were included in the analysis.

This was a non-interventional observational study focusing on educational practices rather than clinical outcomes. Since it involved no direct medical interventions and no patient population, clinical trial registration was not required. To ensure participant confidentiality, the survey was administered anonymously. To ensure participant confidentiality, the survey was administered anonymously. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) RTMSs currently enrolled in SRT; (2) Participation in training programs at designated hospitals and the capacity to provide complete training records; (3) Voluntary participation in the study, with informed consent obtained.

Measures

Questionnaire design

The survey questionnaire was designed through a combination of literature review, group discussions, and expert consultations. The complete English version is provided in the supplementary materials. The purpose of the questionnaire was to evaluate both the implementation and effectiveness of SRT in Yunnan Province and participants’ satisfaction with the program and their willingness to remain employed at their training hospital after completion of the service period. Before data collection, the purpose of the study was clearly explained to participants, and verbal consent was obtained. The questionnaire covered several key dimensions, including demographic and background information of RTMSs, awareness and understanding of the SRT program, assessment and evaluation processes, core competency development, institutional satisfaction, and career intentions. The reliability coefficient was 0.887 and the validity coefficient was 0.885, indicating it is suitable for subsequent analysis.

Before the formal survey, a pilot test was conducted to assess the reliability and validity of the scale through exploratory factor analysis and reliability testing. A Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value greater than 0.5 and a Cronbach’s alpha above 0.8 were considered indicative of good reliability and validity [15, 16].

Primary outcome: identification and future job intention

The primary outcomes of this study included (1) identifying characteristics and professional identity of RTMSs, (2) evaluating their enthusiasm toward training after signing the service agreement, and (3) assessing their career plans following the completion of the mandated service period. Satisfaction with the training program was measured using a nine-item scale, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.858 after reliability testing. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied). A score of ≥ 3 on an individual item was interpreted as a satisfactory response. The total satisfaction score ranged from 9 to 45.

To categorize overall satisfaction levels, the median score of the sample was used as the cutoff point [17]. Participants scoring 27 or above were classified as generally satisfied while those scoring below 27 were considered as generally dissatisfied with SRT program.

Secondary outcome: participation in training

Training participation was assessed using an six-item index, including support for the SRT program, knowledge of training standards, evaluation of hospital’s training methods, passing rate of the physician licensing exam, and their psychological state during the training. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale, which delineated various levels of satisfaction as follows: 1= very dissatisfied, 2 = somewhat satisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = mildly discontented, and 5 = very satisfied.

Covariates

The identification of covariates was based on the following criteria: identity, intention to participate in future training, and hospital level (provincial-level vs. prefecture-level). Additional demographic and socioeconomic variables were also included: gender, age, marital status, and monthly post-tax income (< 3,000 RMB, 3,000–4,500 RMB, 4,500-6,000 RMB, and > 6,000 RMB).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. For ordinal categorical variables, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were used for description, and for nominal categorical variables, percentages were reported. Univariable analyses. χ² tests for categorical variables, independent t-tests for continuous variables, were conducted to preliminarily explore factors associated with training satisfaction with SRT. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analyses were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to assess independent predictors of training satisfaction. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), confirming no significant collinearity (VIF < 5). Missing data were excluded from final analysis. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To ensure data quality, a dual-entry method was used during data entry to minimize potential bias.

Initially, 685 students were enrolled; however, 42 were excluded due to loss to follow-up for various reasons. Of the final sample, 298 (46.35%) were based in provincial-level hospitals, while 345 (53.65%) were in prefecture-level hospitals (see Fig. 1). Self-assessment of core competencies

Participants assessed their improvements in core competencies by utilizing a five-point Likert scale. For professional ethics, the median score was 4 (IQR 3–4), with 46.97% reporting substantial improvement. Similar results were found for professional skills (median = 4, 44.45% reporting improvement), patient management (median = 4, 44.48%), and communication and collaboration (median = 4, 45.41%). Teaching ability and learning/self-improvement showed slightly lower median scores of 3 (IQR 3–4), with 48.06% and 40.76% of respondents, respectively, reporting some improvement. (see Fig. 2)

Satisfaction with training

Satisfaction with the training was also evaluated using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied). The highest satisfaction ratings were observed in two domains: assessment methods (median = 4, IQR 3–4) and content of teaching and training (median = 4, IQR 3–4), with approximately 40% of respondents being “quite satisfied” in each area. Conversely, the lowest satisfaction levels were reported for salary and benefits (median = 3, IQR 2–4), where 33.90% of students were “somewhat dissatisfied,” and for living conditions at the training hospital (median = 3, IQR 3–4), with 44.44% reporting being “basically satisfied.”(see Fig. 3).

Univariate analysis was conducted to identify potential factors associated with training satisfaction, including many variables such as gender, age, marital status, hospital level, and income. No significant associations were found between demographic characteristics and satisfaction (p > 0.05). The nine variables that showed significant associations were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. The analysis indicated that higher income, a positive psychological state, frequent clinical training and lectures, participation in hospital-organized counseling and team-building, and stronger identification as an RTMSs (reflecting the extent to which graduates internalized their rural service role and commitment to contractual obligations) were all independent predictors of greater training satisfaction (p < 0.05). Identity and future work intentions

Among the participants, 358 (55.68%) reported being willing to serve in nationally designated areas of need, which were classified as impoverished or with fewer than one physician per 1,000 population. Only 108 (16.80%) participants stated that their RTMSs identity positively influenced their training motivation. Notably, 299 (46.50%) participants expressed unwillingness to remain in rural or grassroots positions after completing their contractual service period. Among this group, 53.85% intended to seek employment in larger hospitals, among which the willingness of RTMS is stronger in prefecture-level hospitals;26.42% planned to pursue further education, and 19.73% aimed to take civil service examinations (competitive national exams for securing formal positions in government agencies or public institutions, similar to public sector entry exams in other countries). Discussion

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to systematically investigate the factors influencing the satisfaction of RTMSs with SRT across eight hospitals in Yunnan Province. The findings reveal that the majority of RTMSs reported moderate satisfaction with their training experience. A key factor enhancing both satisfaction and perceived competency was the improvement in medical skills. However, a notable concern was that nearly half of the RTMSs expressed reluctance to continue working in rural or grassroots areas after fulfilling their service obligations.

These findings align with the Kirkpatrick Four-Level Evaluation Model [18], which posits a comprehensive framework for evaluating training outcomes. At the reaction level, participants’ satisfaction was generally low, particularly regarding salary, benefits, and living conditions. Consistent with numerous previous studies, demographic factors such as gender, age, marital status, and professional title showed no significant association with satisfaction [19]. This conclusion is further supported by research such as a 2021 study in Chongqing, which reported insufficient implementation of SRT requirements [20].

The main objective in this respect is to improve satisfaction with each component of the training. Consistent with findings from Gansu Province [21] and international examples, including incentive programs in in India, Tanzania, Zambia, and the Philippines [22], our study underscores that financial incentives are critical for improving satisfaction and service retention after completion in underserved regions. With the majority of participants being unmarried and earning less than 6,000 RMB per month, financial stress related to housing, commuting, and family life likely contributed to dissatisfaction.

The following aspects aim to discuss the experience of secondary objectives in the training program. At the learning level, frequent clinical training sessions and academic lectures were significantly associated with greater satisfaction [23]. These findings suggest that high-quality, practical, and interactive learning experiences are critical for enhancing the clinical competencies of future rural healthcare professionals. Previous studies support this association, indicating that active learning not only boosts medical proficiency but also broadens academic perspectives, both of which are essential for rural medical practice. Therefore, increasing the frequency and quality of training opportunities could further enhance satisfaction and long-term engagement.

This section aims to discuss the factors that affect satisfaction.Moreover, RTMSs who maintained a positive psychological outlook were more likely to express satisfaction with their training, consistent with previous studies indicating that a constructive mindset helps trainees manage stress more effectively and improve outcomes [24]. Institutional support mechanisms—such as psychological counseling and team-building activities—also contributed to greater well-being and improved satisfaction. These findings support the importance of investing in the psychosocial support systems within training institutions [25].

This section examines the identification of secondary objectives regarding trainees’ professional identity. A critical component of trainee engagement is identity recognition. In this study, stronger identification with the RTMSs role correlated with greater satisfaction. This is consistent with existing literature, which highlights the influence of intrinsic motivation and professional identity on medical education outcomes towards rural medical services [26, 27]. The substantial difference in working conditions between rural and urban healthcare settings presents both a challenge and an opportunity: cultivating a strong rural identity among RTMSs can serve as a driver for long-term commitment and professional fulfillment [28].

Behavioral outcomes were also positive. At the behavior level, RTMSs reported notable improvements in Self-evaluation of core competencies, including professional ethics (≥ 46.97%), patient management, communication, collaboration, and teaching ability [29].The results of these self-evaluation reports suggest that learning gains may be reflected in clinical practice. These results confirm that learning is being effectively translated into clinical practice. Furthermore, this behavioral shift reflects a growing sense of responsibility and adaptability among RTMSs, qualities essential to sustainable rural medical service delivery [30, 31].

This and the following sections are intended to introduce the willingness and factors to remain in service after the completion of the contract.However, at the results level, approximately 46.50% of respondents indicated an intention to leave rural practice after completing their service period. Most of these individuals planned to transfer to higher-level hospitals (53.85%), pursue further education (26.42%), or enter civil service (19.73%). This trend suggests a misalignment between training goals and career development opportunities. A review of domestic literature indicates that medical students from rural backgrounds are significantly more likely than their urban counterparts to remain in primary healthcare positions after completing their service contracts [32, 33].Career decision-making is influenced not only by contractual obligations but also by perceived opportunities for growth and quality of life [34]. To address this, policy-makers must balance the goal of rural retention with the provision of meaningful career paths, professional development, and improved working conditions.

Multiple personal and contextual factors influence RTMSs retention, including gender, marital status, income, job satisfaction, workload, and whether individuals are only children.Additionally, provincial hospitals have stronger resources that can provide better training, counseling, team building, and better compensation, while prefecture-level hospitals are relatively weaker in all aspects, which also affects retention [35, 36]. One potential issue is that many students enter the RTMSs program directly from high school with limited understanding of its implications [37]. Therefore, medical colleges should improve policy education and prioritize students whose hometowns match designated service locations. Governments should increase funding for rural healthcare infrastructure, raise compensation levels, and enhance training environments. Furthermore, institutions should invest in early career planning, promote a sense of professional belonging, and nurture social responsibility among students.

The challenge of retaining healthcare workers in rural areas is not unique to China; it is a global issue faced by many low- and middle-income countries. According to the World Health Organization, half of the world’s population lives in rural areas, yet access to medical care remains disproportionately low [38]. International strategies, including regulatory reforms, financial incentives, and personal and professional support mechanisms, demonstrate that multifaceted, context-sensitive policies are essential for achieving Universal Health Coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals. China’s RTMSs program can benefit from these global lessons by adopting comprehensive retention strategies tailored to local realities.

This study has several strengths. It was a multicenter, cross-sectional study with a robust sample size. The use of a Likert scale allowed for nuanced assessment of satisfaction and its predictors. Additionally, the multivariate logistic regression model accounted for potential confounders, enhancing the reliability of the findings. However, there are limitations. The sample was restricted to RTMSs in Yunnan Province, limiting the generalizability of the results to other regions with different economic or healthcare policies, and leaving potential residual confounding factors for urban-level differences. Moreover, as a cross-sectional study using self-administered questionnaires, it cannot establish causality and may be subject to recall and information bias. Finally, the study’s design does not allow for long-term follow-up, limiting insights into the durability of training impacts and career choices over time.

Conclusions

This study found that most RTMSs report moderate satisfaction with SRT. However, nearly half indicated that they were unwilling to remain in rural positions after completing their service period. Improving training quality alone is insufficient to reverse this trend. Government-level policy reforms, such as enhancing compensation, improving living conditions, providing preferential staffing, and creating clear pathways for professional promotion, are critical determinants of retention after service completion. Therefore, education reform by itself cannot resolve the challenges of “unable to go, unable to stay”; coordinated, systemic policies at the provincial and national levels are urgently needed to ensure the sustainable development of the rural healthcare workforce.
