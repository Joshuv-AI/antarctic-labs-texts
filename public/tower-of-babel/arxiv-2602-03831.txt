1
Introduction

We study extremal perimeter properties of high-dimensional log-concave probability measures.
Our main result establishes an
O(n^{3/2})
upper bound for the maximal perimeter constant of isotropic log-concave probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, improving the previously best known quadratic bound.

For a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
\mu
–perimeter of
A
is defined by

\mu^{+}(\partial A)=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\big((A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\setminus A\big)}{\epsilon},

where
B_{2}^{n}
denotes the Euclidean unit ball.
This notion coincides with the Minkowski content of
\partial A
with respect to
\mu
and quantifies the first-order variation of the
\mu
-measure of
A
under infinitesimal Euclidean enlargements.
The maximal perimeter constant of
\mu
is then defined as

\Gamma(\mu)=\sup\big\{\mu^{+}(\partial A):\ A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ is a convex body}\big\},

and measures the largest possible boundary contribution among all convex bodies with respect to
\mu
.

§1.1. Main results.

A probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is log-concave if it admits a density
f
satisfying

\ln f(\tau x+(1-\tau)y)\geqslant\tau\ln f(x)+(1-\tau)\ln f(y)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \tau\in[0,1].

The measure
\mu
is called isotropic if its barycenter is at the origin and its covariance matrix is the identity.

In this work, we investigate the growth rate of

\Gamma_{n}:=\sup\{\Gamma(\mu):\mu\;\text{is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on}\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

A basic example is provided by the uniform measure on the cube
Q_{n}
in isotropic position, for which
\Gamma(\mu_{Q_{n}})\approx n
, where
\mu_{Q_{n}}
is the
associated isotropic measure. More generally, in Section
5
we show that for any isotropic convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the maximal perimeter constant of the uniform measure on
K
is of the same order as its surface area
S(K)
(see Section §5.1).
In particular, when
K=Q_{n}
, this quantity is of order
n
.

Prior to the present work, the best known general upper bound was quadratic, namely
\Gamma_{n}\leqslant Cn^{2}
, due to Livshyts
[
25
]
.
Our main result improves the bound to order
n^{3/2}
in full generality.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant Cn^{3/2},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Together with the example of the cube, Theorem
1.1
yields

cn\leqslant\Gamma_{n}\leqslant Cn^{3/2}.

The estimate of Theorem
1.1
can be improved if one restricts attention to the symmetric maximal perimeter constant of
\mu
, defined by

\Gamma^{(s)}(\mu)=\sup\big\{\mu^{+}(\partial A):\ A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ is a symmetric convex body}\big\}.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma_{n}^{(s)}:=\sup\{\Gamma^{(s)}(\mu):\mu\;\text{is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on}\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
.
Then

\Gamma_{n}^{(s)}\leqslant 4n.

In Section
5
we further show that better bounds can be obtained under additional structural assumptions.
Specifically, we prove linear
O(n)
bounds for several important classes of isotropic geometric log-concave measures,
including uniform measures on convex bodies,
1
-symmetric measures and measures with homogeneous level sets. These cases are already mentioned
in
[
25
]
. Our approach allows us to obtain sharper bounds in some of these cases and applies to more general classes:

(i) In Proposition
5.2
we show that if
K
is an isotropic convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu_{K}
denotes
the associated isotropic probability measure with density
L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{K/L_{K}}
where
L_{K}
is the isotropic constant of
K
, then

\Gamma(\mu_{K})=L_{K}S(K),

where
S(K)
is the surface area of
K
. As a consequence,
\Gamma(\mu_{K})\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2}}\,n
. This inequality is sharp: we check
that equality holds if
K
is an isotropic regular simplex.

(ii) In Theorem
5.7
we prove that if
\mu
is a
1
-unconditional isotropic log-concave probability measure,
meaning that its density
f
satisfies
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(\epsilon_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}x_{n})
for all choices of signs
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant\sqrt{2}n.

The proof is independent of the methods in
[
25
]
and covers the case of
1
-symmetric measures which is strictly smaller.

(iii) In Theorem
5.9
we show that if
\mu
is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant 2.

Moreover, the constant
2
is sharp: there exist isotropic log-concave probability measures
\mu
with

\Gamma(\mu)
arbitrarily close to
2
, with one-sided exponentials being extremal.

(iv) In Theorem
5.10
we prove that if
\mu=\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
is a product probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
where each
\mu_{k}
has a density
g_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then

\Gamma(\mu)\ \leqslant\ 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, the constant
2
is optimal. In particular, if each
\mu_{k}
is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant 2n.

It would be interesting to determine whether a linear bound
O(n)
holds in full generality.

§1.2. Elements of the proof.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on two elementary yet robust ingredients.
First, a dilation inequality for centered log-concave measures provides an upper bound on the perimeter of a convex body
A

containing the origin in terms of its measure and its inradius.
Second, for isotropic log-concave measures, the measure of any convex body admits a one-dimensional upper bound in terms of its minimal width.
In the symmetric setting, the inball of
A
is necessarily centered at the origin and the minimal width equals twice the inradius, which
immediately yields a linear bound for the symmetric maximal perimeter constant.

The proof of the general bound in Theorem
1.1
follows the same overarching strategy, supplemented with ideas introduced in
[
25
]
.
A central role is played by the super-level sets

R_{t}(\mu)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)\geqslant e^{-t}\|f\|_{\infty}\}

of the density
f
of
\mu
, which form an increasing family of bounded convex sets.
For isotropic log-concave measures, these sets exhaust the measure rapidly: for
t
of order
n
, they already carry almost full mass,
while their inradius is bounded from below by an absolute constant.
This structural information allows one to localize the perimeter problem to regions where the density is well controlled.

The starting point is a perimeter estimate for an arbitrary convex body
A
in terms of its inradius and the ratio between the maximum of the density
on
A
and its value at the center of an inball.
Combining this estimate with Steinhagen’s inequality (Theorem
4.6
), which compares minimal width and inradius up to a factor
\sqrt{n}
,
yields a bound of the form

\sqrt{n}\left(n+\ln\left(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A})\right)\right),

where
x_{A}
denotes the center of an inball of
A
.
When
A
is contained in a fixed level set
R_{6n}(\mu)
, the logarithmic term is of order at most
n
, leading to an
O(n^{3/2})
estimate.
To treat arbitrary convex bodies, the boundary is decomposed according to density levels: the contribution coming from outside
R_{6n}(\mu)

is controlled by comparison with the boundaries of the level sets themselves.
Using the structural properties of
R_{t}(\mu)
, this contribution is shown to be linear in
n
.
Combining the two regimes yields the
O(n^{3/2})
upper bound in full generality.

§1.3. Background and related work.

The Gaussian maximal perimeter constant arises naturally in several areas of high-dimensional probability, convex geometry and theoretical computer science.
Kwapień and Mushtari posed the problem of determining the order of growth of
\Gamma(\mu)
when
\mu=\gamma_{n}
is the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Simple arguments (see
[
5
]
) yield the bounds
\sqrt{\ln n}\lesssim\Gamma(\gamma_{n})\lesssim\sqrt{n}
with absolute implicit constants.
A breakthrough result of Ball
[
5
]
showed that

\Gamma(\gamma_{n})\leqslant 4n^{1/4}.

This estimate was later shown to be sharp by Nazarov
[
26
]
, who proved that for every
n\times n
symmetric positive definite matrix
T
, the Gaussian measure with density proportional to
\exp(-\langle Tx,x\rangle/2)
has maximal perimeter of order
\sqrt{\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
denotes Hilbert-Schmidt norm.
Since
\|I_{n}\|_{{\rm HS}}=n^{1/2}
for the identity matrix
I_{n}
, it follows that

(1.1)

\Gamma(\gamma_{n})\approx n^{1/4}.

These results highlight a non-classical scaling behavior of Gaussian surface area in high dimensions, in contrast to the
linear growth observed for the uniform measure on the cube. They also motivate the investigation of analogous bounds beyond the Gaussian framework.
A natural and substantially broader class of measures is given by log-concave probability measures, which arise ubiquitously in convex geometry and probability theory and include uniform measures on convex bodies as well as many exponential-type distributions.

In a series of works, Livshyts studied extensions of Gaussian perimeter bounds to the log-concave setting.
In particular, for the probability measure
\mu_{p,n}
with density proportional to
\exp(-|x|^{p}/p)
,
0<p<\infty
, it was shown in
[
22
]
that

\Gamma(\mu_{p,n})\approx C_{p}\,n^{\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{p}},

interpolating between Gaussian and uniform behavior.
Subsequently, almost sharp upper bounds for general rotationally invariant log-concave measures were obtained in terms of the expectation and variance of the Euclidean norm in
[
23
]
, while maximal perimeter estimates for convex polytopes, with logarithmic dependence on the number of facets, were studied in
[
24
]
.

More recently, Livshyts
[
25
]
derived general lower bounds for
\Gamma(\mu)
under mild regularity assumptions.
Specifically, if
\mu
is absolutely continuous and satisfies

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(|x|)}\leqslant\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(|x|)
for some
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
then

(1.2)

\Gamma(\mu)\geqslant C(\alpha)\,\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt[4]{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(|x|)}\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(|x|)}}.

If
\mu
is isotropic, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(|x|)\approx\sqrt{n}
and
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(|x|)\leqslant C
for an absolute constant
C
, the latter estimate following from the recent resolution of the thin-shell conjecture by Klartag and Lehec
[
21
]
.
Consequently,

(1.3)

\Gamma(\mu)\gtrsim n^{1/4}

for every isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, up to an absolute constant. In view of (
1.1
), this shows that within this class the Gaussian measure is essentially extremal on the lower end.

Livshyts also proved in
[
25
]
that for every log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
,
there exists a volume preserving transformation
T\in SL_{n}
such that the push-forward measure
T_{\ast}\mu
satisfies

(1.4)

\Gamma(T_{\ast}\mu)\leqslant Cn\|f_{T}\|_{\infty}^{1/n}=Cn\|f\|_{\infty}^{1/n}

for an absolute constant
C>0
, where
f_{T}(x)=f(T^{-1}(x))
denotes the density of
T_{\ast}(\mu)
and
\|f_{T}\|_{\infty}=\|f\|_{\infty}
.
Note that
\Gamma(\mu)
is not invariant under volume preserving transformations.

If
\mu
is an isotropic log-concave probability measure, then
\|f\|_{\infty}^{1/n}
coincides with the isotropic constant of
f
,
which is bounded by an absolute constant (see Section
2
).
Therefore, (
1.4
) implies that every isotropic log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits
a push-forward
T_{\ast}\mu
such that

\Gamma(T_{\ast}\mu)\leqslant Cn.

This result once again raises the natural question of whether
\Gamma_{n}
grows linearly with the dimension.

2
Notation and preliminaries

We work in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
The associated Euclidean norm is denoted by
|\cdot|
, the Euclidean unit ball by
B_{2}^{n}
, and the Euclidean unit sphere by
S^{n-1}
.
Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathrm{vol}_{n}
, and we write
\omega_{n}=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(B_{2}^{n})
for the volume of the Euclidean unit ball.
We denote by
\sigma
the rotationally invariant probability measure on
S^{n-1}
.

Throughout the paper, the symbols
C,c,c^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
denote absolute positive constants whose values may change from line to line.
Whenever we write
a\approx b
, we mean that there exist absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
c_{1}a\leqslant b\leqslant c_{2}a
.
All absolute constants are independent of the dimension
n
.

§2.1. Convex bodies.

A convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact convex set
K
with nonempty interior.
It is called symmetric if
K=-K
, and centered if its barycenter
\operatorname{bar}(K)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}\int_{K}x\,dx
is at the origin.
For every convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote by
\overline{K}
the homothetic copy of
K
scaled to have unit volume,
namely
\overline{K}:=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{-1/n}K
.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in{\rm int}(K)
.
The radial function of
K
is defined by
\varrho_{K}(x)=\max\{t>0:tx\in K\}
for all nonzero
x
, and the support function of
K
is given by

h_{K}(x)=\max\{\langle x,y\rangle:y\in K\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

If a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits a density
f
with respect to Lebesgue measure, then

\mu^{+}(\partial K)=\int_{\partial K}f(x)\,d\lambda(x),

where
\lambda
denotes the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\partial K
.
The surface area
S(K)
of
K
is defined by

S(K):=\lambda(\partial K).

A well-known inequality that will be useful in our study asserts that if
0\in{\rm int}(K)
then

(2.1)

S(K)\leqslant\frac{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}{r(K)},

where
r(K)
is the inradius of
K
with respect to the origin, that is, the largest
r>0
such that
rB_{2}^{n}\subseteq K
.
For completeness, we sketch a proof. Setting
r=r(K)
, we write

\displaystyle S(K)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}{\epsilon}\leqslant\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K+(\epsilon/r)K)-\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{(1+\epsilon/r)^{n}-1}{\epsilon}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)=\frac{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}{r}.

The (intrinsic) inradius of
K
is defined by

r_{K}=\sup\{r>0:\exists x\in K\;\text{such that}\;x+rB_{2}^{n}\subseteq K\}.

The existence of an inball attaining this supremum follows from Blaschke’s selection theorem. In general, the inball
need not be unique, as shown by the example of a circular cylinder.

Another parameter that will play an important role is the minimal width of
K
. The width of
K
in the direction of
\xi\in S^{n-1}
is defined by

w_{K}(\xi)=h_{K}(\xi)+h_{K}(-\xi)

and the minimal width of
K
is given by

w_{K}=\min\{w_{K}(\xi):\xi\in S^{n-1}\}.

Equivalently,
w_{K}
is the minimal distance between two parallel supporting hyperplanes of
K
.

A convex body
K
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called isotropic if it has volume
1
, is centered, and its covariance matrix is a multiple of the identity.
Equivalently, there exists a constant
L_{K}>0
, called the isotropic constant of
K
, such that

\|\langle\cdot,\xi\rangle\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}:=\int_{K}\langle x,\xi\rangle^{2}dx=L_{K}^{2}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all}\;\xi\in S^{n-1}.

We shall use several geometric properties of isotropic convex bodies.
First, it was shown in
[
18
]
that if
K
is isotropic, then

(2.2)

\sqrt{\frac{n+2}{n}}L_{K}B_{2}^{n}\subseteq K\subseteq\sqrt{n(n+2)}L_{K}B_{2}^{n}.

Moreover, it is known (see
[
11
, Proposition 3.3.1]
) that
L_{K}\geqslant L_{B_{2}^{n}}\geqslant c
for an absolute constant
c>0
.

Bourgain’s slicing problem
[
10
]
asks whether there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

(2.3)

L_{n}:=\max\{L_{K}:K\ \hbox{is an isotropic convex body in}\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\}\leqslant C.

An affirmative solution was recently obtained by Klartag and Lehec
[
20
]
, following an important
contribution by Guan
[
16
]
(see also
[
6
]
for an alternative proof).
Consequently,
L_{K}\approx 1
, uniformly in
n
, for every isotropic convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For further background, we refer to the survey
[
15
]
.

§2.2. Log-concave probability measures.

A Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called log-concave if

\mu(\tau A+(1-\tau)B)\geqslant\mu(A)^{\tau}\mu(B)^{1-\tau}

for any compact sets
A

and
B
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and any
\tau\in(0,1)
.
Borell
[
9
]
proved that if a log-concave probability measure
\mu
is not supported
on a hyperplane, then
\mu
admits a log-concave density
f
. Such measures will be called full-dimensional.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a log-concave function with finite, positive integral.
Its barycenter is defined by

\operatorname{bar}(f)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x\,f(x)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}.

We say that
f
is centered if
\operatorname{bar}(f)=0
.
We shall use the following result of Fradelizi
[
12
]
: if
f
is a centered log-concave density on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then

(2.4)

\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant e^{n}f(0).

The isotropic constant of a log-concave function
f
with finite positive integral is the affine-invariant quantity

(2.5)

L_{f}:=\left(\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}\right)^{1/n}\det(\operatorname{Cov}(f))^{1/(2n)},

where
\operatorname{Cov}(f)
denotes the covariance matrix of
f
with entries

\textrm{Cov}(f)_{i,j}:=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x_{i}x_{j}f(x)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x_{i}f(x)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x_{j}f(x)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}.

A log-concave function
f
is called isotropic if

\operatorname{bar}(f)=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx=1,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\operatorname{Cov}(f)=I_{n}.

In this case,
L_{f}=\|f\|_{\infty}^{1/n}
. A full-dimensional log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called isotropic if its
density
f
is isotropic. Then, we set
L_{\mu}:=L_{f}
.

Note that a centered convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)=1
is isotropic if and only if the log-concave function
L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{K/L_{K}}
is isotropic.

Let
\mu
be a full-dimensional log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
and any
k
-dimensional subspace
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the marginal of
\mu
onto
F
is defined by

\pi_{F}(\mu)(B):=\mu(P_{F}^{-1}(B)),

for every Borel set
B\subset F
. The measure
\pi_{F}(\mu)
is log-concave and admits a density

(\pi_{F}f)(x)=\int_{x+F^{\perp}}f(y)\,dy.

If
f
is centered (respectively isotropic), then so is
\pi_{F}f

(see
[
11
, Proposition 5.1.11]
).

In particular, if
\mu
is isotropic and
F_{\xi}=\{t\xi:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
for
\xi\in S^{n-1}
,
then the one-dimensional marginal

g_{\xi}(t)=(\pi_{F_{\xi}}(f))(t)=\int_{t\xi+F_{\xi}^{\perp}}f(x)\,dx

is an isotropic log-concave density on
\mathbb{R}
. Consequently,

(2.6)

\|g_{\xi}\|_{\infty}=L_{g_{\xi}}\leqslant 1.

More generally, it was shown in
[
12
]
that
{\rm Var}(X)\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant 1
for any
centered log-concave density
g
on
\mathbb{R}
and
X\sim g
. Bobkov and Chistyakov
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
proved that

\frac{1}{12}\leqslant{\rm Var}(X)\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant 1

for every log-concave density
g
on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the left inequality holds without the log-concavity
assumption. If
g
is even, then

\|g\|_{\infty}=g(0)
and

{\rm Var}(X)g(0)^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.

In particular, if
g
is even and isotropic, then
{\rm Var}(X)=1
, and hence

(2.7)

\|g\|_{\infty}=g(0)\leqslant 1/\sqrt{2}.

This result was first obtained by Hensley
[
17
]
; the symmetry assumption was further relaxed in
[
7
]
.

It is known that every centered log-concave density
f
admits an isotropic position: there exists
T\in GL_{n}
such that the push-forward density

f_{T}(x)=\frac{1}{|\det T|}\,f(T^{-1}x)

is isotropic (see
[
11
, Section 2.3]
).
Moreover,
f_{T}
is also log-concave, and
L_{f_{T}}=L_{f}
. It is also known (see
[
11
, Proposition 2.3.12]
) that
L_{f}\geqslant c
for every
isotropic log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
c>0
is an absolute constant. On the other hand, Ball
[
3
]
proved that for every
n
,

\tilde{L}_{n}:=\sup\big\{L_{f}:f\;\text{is a log-concave density on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}\big\}\leqslant C_{1}L_{n},

and hence
\tilde{L}_{n}\leqslant C_{2}
by the affirmative solution of Bourgain’s slicing problem.

We refer to
[
1
,
2
]
for asymptotic convex geometry, and to
[
11
]
for background
on isotropic convex bodies and log-concave measures.

3
Super-level sets of an isotropic log-concave density

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
. For every
t\geqslant 0
, we define the super-level set

R_{t}(\mu)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,f(x)\geqslant e^{-t}\|f\|_{\infty}\}.

Since
f
is log-concave, each
R_{t}(\mu)
is convex, and for
t>0
we have
0\in\mathrm{int}(R_{t}(\mu))
.
Moreover, using the fact that every log-concave function with finite positive integral satisfies (see
[
11
, Lemma 2.2.1]
)

(3.1)

f(x)\leqslant Ae^{-B|x|}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

for some constants
A,B>0
, we see that if
x\in R_{t}(\mu)
then

|x|\leqslant\frac{1}{B}\bigl(\ln(A/\|f\|_{\infty})+t\bigr).

In particular,
R_{t}(\mu)
is bounded. The family of bodies
\{R_{t}(\mu)\}_{t>0}
is clearly increasing, and the next lemma shows
that
\mu(R_{t}(\mu))
converges rapidly to
1
as
t\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then, for every
t\geqslant 6n
,
\mu(R_{t}(\mu))\geqslant 1-e^{-t/5}
.

Proof.

Define
S_{t}(\mu)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)\geqslant e^{-t}f(0)\}
. It is shown in
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
that
if
r\geqslant 5(n-1)
, then
\mu(S_{r}(\mu))\geqslant 1-e^{-r/4}
. Since
\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant e^{n}f(0)
by (
2.4
), we have

e^{-t}\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant e^{-t+n}f(0)

and therefore
S_{t-n}(\mu)\subseteq R_{t}(\mu)
for all
t>n
. In particular, if
t\geqslant 6n
, then
r=t-n\geqslant 5n
, and hence

\mu(R_{t}(\mu))\geqslant 1-e^{-(t-n)/4}\geqslant 1-e^{-t/5},

which proves the claim.
∎

The proof of the next lemma is essentially contained in
[
19
, Lemma 5.4]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\geqslant 3
. Then

r(R_{6n}(\mu))\geqslant 1/3.

Proof.

Let
r:=r(R_{6n}(\mu))
denote the inradius of
R_{6n}(\mu)
with respect to the origin. Then there exists
\xi\in S^{n-1}
such that

h_{R_{6n}(\mu)}(\xi)\leqslant r
, and consequently

R_{6n}(\mu)\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant r\}.

We decompose this half-space as

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant r\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant 0\}\cup\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:0<\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant r\}.

Since
\mu
is centered, Grünbaum’s lemma (see
[
11
, Lemma 2.2.6]
) yields

\mu(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant 0\})\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{e}.

Let
F_{\xi}=\{t\xi:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the one-dimensional subspace spanned by
\xi
. From (
2.6
),
the density
g_{\xi}
of the marginal
\pi_{F_{\xi}}(\mu)
satisfies

\|g_{\xi}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1.

therefore,

\mu(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:0<\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant r\})=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\int_{t\xi+F_{\xi}^{\perp}}f(x)\,dx\right)\,dt=\int_{0}^{r}g_{\xi}(t)\,dt\leqslant r.

Combining these estimates and using Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

1-e^{-6n/5}\leqslant\mu(R_{6n}(\mu))\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{e}+r.

For
n\geqslant 3
, this implies

r\geqslant e^{-1}-e^{-6n/5}\geqslant e^{-1}-e^{-18/5}\geqslant 1/3,

as claimed.∎

Remark 3.3
.

We shall use the following observation. Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
satisfying
f(0)=\|f\|_{\infty}
. For any
x\in R_{t}(\mu)
and any
\tau\in(0,1)
,

(3.2)

f(\tau x)\geqslant f(x)^{\tau}f(0)^{1-\tau}\geqslant e^{-\tau t}f(0)^{\tau}f(0)^{1-\tau}=e^{-\tau t}f(0).

This inequality relies only on log-concavity and the normalization
f(0)=\|f\|_{\infty}
. As a consequence,

(3.3)

\tau R_{t}(\mu)\subseteq R_{\tau t}(\mu),\hskip 18.49988ptt>0,\;\tau\in(0,1).

In particular, if
\mu
is isotropic, then

r(R_{n}(\mu))\geqslant r(R_{6n}(\mu))/6\geqslant 1/18.

4
Upper bound for the perimeter

In this section we prove the general upper bounds for
\Gamma_{n}
and
\Gamma_{n}^{(s)}
. Throughout,
\mu
denotes
an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
.

We begin with a simple estimate for the measure of a convex body in terms of its
minimal width. Recall that for a convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

w_{A}=\min\{w_{A}(\xi):\xi\in S^{n-1}\}
, where
w_{A}(\xi)=h_{A}(\xi)+h_{A}(-\xi)

is the width of
A
in the direction of
\xi\in S^{n-1}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then for every convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mu(A)\leqslant w_{A}.

Proof.

Fix
\xi\in S^{n-1}
and let
F_{\xi}=\{t\xi:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
By (
2.6
), the density
g_{\xi}
of the marginal
\pi_{F_{\xi}}(\mu)
satisfies

\|g_{\xi}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
.

Since

A\subseteq S_{\xi}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:-h_{A}(-\xi)\leqslant\langle x,\xi\rangle\leqslant h_{A}(\xi)\},

we obtain

\mu(A)\leqslant\mu(S_{\xi})=\int_{-h_{A}(-\xi)}^{h_{A}(\xi)}\int_{t\xi+F_{\xi}^{\perp}}f(x)\,dx\,dt=\int_{-h_{A}(-\xi)}^{h_{A}(\xi)}g_{\xi}(t)\,dt\leqslant w_{A}(\xi).

Taking the minimum over
\xi\in S^{n-1}
yields the result.∎

The next lemma is a standard dilation estimate for log-concave measures (see
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
).
Actually, we shall prove and use a more elaborate version later on, so we skip the details.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then for
every
\delta>0
and every Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mu((1+\delta)A)\leqslant e^{2n\delta}\mu(A).

Note
.

If the density of
\mu
is a geometric log-concave function, i.e.
f(0)=\|f\|_{\infty}
,
then the above estimate improves to
\mu((1+\delta)A)\leqslant e^{n\delta}\mu(A).

Using Lemma
4.2
we can control the perimeter of a convex body containing the origin in terms of its measure
and inradius with respect to the origin.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body
such that
rB_{2}^{n}\subseteq A
for some
r>0
. Then

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\frac{2n\mu(A)}{r}.

Proof.

Since
A
is convex, we have
tA+sA=(t+s)A
for any
t,s>0
. Using also Lemma
4.2
, we write

\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\leqslant\mu(A+(\epsilon/r)A)=\mu((1+\epsilon/r)A)\leqslant e^{2n\epsilon/r}\mu(A).

Hence,

\mu^{+}(\partial A)=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mu(A)}{\epsilon}\leqslant\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{e^{2n\epsilon/r}-1}{\epsilon}\mu(A)=\frac{2n\mu(A)}{r},

as claimed.
∎

The last fact that we need is the simple observation that if
A
is a symmetric convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then there exists an inball of
A
centered
at the origin, and

(4.1)

w_{A}=2r_{A},

where
w_{A}
is the minimal width and
r_{A}
is the inradius of
A
.

We can now give a one-line proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
A
be symmetric. Then
r_{A}B_{2}^{n}\subseteq A
and
w_{A}=2r_{A}
. By Lemmas
4.3
and
4.1
,

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\frac{2n\mu(A)}{r_{A}}\leqslant\frac{2nw_{A}}{r_{A}}=4n,

and the theorem follows.
∎

Note
.

If the density of
\mu
is geometric log-concave, then the improved dilation estimate yields

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant 2n
for every symmetric convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
In the general case,
A
need not be symmetric, and also the origin may not be an interior point of
A
. Recall that, given a convex body

A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
r_{A}
the radius of the largest Euclidean ball contained in
A
and by
x_{A}
the center of such a ball.
In other words,
x_{A}+r_{A}B_{2}^{n}
is an inball of
A
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
A
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and let
x_{A}+r_{A}B_{2}^{n}\subseteq A
be an inball of
A
. Then

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\frac{n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A}))}{r_{A}}\mu(A).

Proof.

Let
K=-x_{A}+A
, so that
0\in K
and
r_{A}B_{2}^{n}\subseteq K
. For
\epsilon>0
, we have

A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n}=x_{A}+K+\epsilon B_{2}^{n}\subseteq x_{A}+K+(\epsilon/r_{A})K=x_{A}+(1+\epsilon/r_{A})K.

Letting
\delta=\epsilon/r_{A}
,

\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\leqslant\int_{x_{A}+(1+\delta)K}f(u)\,du=\int_{(1+\delta)K}f(x_{A}+v)\,dv=(1+\delta)^{n}\int_{K}f(x_{A}+(1+\delta)y)\,dy.

Writing

x_{A}+y=\frac{1}{1+\delta}(x_{A}+(1+\delta)y)+\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}x_{A}

and using log-concavity of
f
, we get
f(x_{A}+y)^{1+\delta}\geqslant f(x_{A}+(1+\delta)y)f(x_{A})^{\delta}
, and hence

(4.2)

f(x_{A}+(1+\delta)y)\leqslant f(x_{A}+y)\left(\frac{f(x_{A}+y)}{f(x_{A})}\right)^{\delta}\leqslant f(x_{A}+y)\left(\frac{\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}}{f(x_{A})}\right)^{\delta}.

Combining the above, we see that

\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\leqslant(1+\delta)^{n}\left(\frac{\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}}{f(x_{A})}\right)^{\delta}\int_{K}f(x_{A}+y)\,dy\leqslant\exp\left(\delta\left(n+\ln\left(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A})\right)\right)\right)\mu(A).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(\partial A)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mu(A)}{\epsilon}\leqslant\mu(A)\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\exp\big(\epsilon/r_{A}(n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A})))\big)-1}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mu(A)\frac{n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A}))}{r_{A}},

as claimed. ∎

Remark 4.5
.

The argument above gives in fact a stronger estimate. Let
\mu_{A}
be the probability measure with density
(\mu(A))^{-1}f(y)\mathds{1}_{A}(y)
.
From (
4.2
) we see that

\displaystyle\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\delta)^{n}\int_{K}f(x_{A}+(1+\delta)y)\,dy\leqslant e^{n\delta}\int_{K}f(x_{A}+y)\left(\frac{f(x_{A}+y)}{f(x_{A})}\right)^{\delta}\,dy

\displaystyle=e^{n\delta}\mu(A)\int_{A}\left(\frac{f(y)}{f(x_{A})}\right)^{\delta}d\mu_{A}(y).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(\partial A)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mu(A)}{\epsilon}\leqslant\mu(A)\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[e^{n\epsilon/r_{A}}\left(\int_{A}\left(\frac{f(y)}{f(x_{A})}\right)^{\epsilon/r_{A}}d\mu_{A}(y)\right)-1\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(A)}{r_{A}}\left[n+\int_{A}\ln\left(\frac{f(y)}{f(x_{A})}\right)\,d\mu_{A}(y)\right].

Using Lemma
4.1
we obtain

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\frac{w_{A}}{r_{A}}\left[n+\int_{A}\ln\left(\frac{f(y)}{f(x_{A})}\right)\,d\mu_{A}(y)\right].

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
we shall use a theorem of Steinhagen
[
27
]
(see also
[
28
, Theorem 1.5.2]
)
to compare the minimal width
w_{A}
of a convex body
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with its inradius
r_{A}
.

Theorem 4.6

(Steinhagen)
.

For every convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

w_{A}\leqslant 2\sqrt{n}r_{A}.

As a first step, combining Theorem
4.6
with Lemmas
4.4
and
4.1
we obtain the following estimate.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for every
convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant 2\sqrt{n}\big(n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A}))\big),

where
x_{A}
is the center of the ball of radius
r_{A}
inscribed in
A
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.1
we have

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\mu(A)\frac{n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A}))}{r_{A}}\leqslant\big(n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A}))\big)\,\frac{w_{A}}{r_{A}}.

The claim follows from Theorem
4.6
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every
convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
A\subseteq R_{6n}(\mu)
,

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant 14n^{3/2}.

Proof.

From Proposition
4.7
we have that

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant 2\sqrt{n}\big(n+\ln(\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A}))\big),

where
x_{A}
is the center of the ball of radius
r_{A}
inscribed in
A
. Since
x_{A}\in R_{6n}(\mu)
we also have that
\|f\big|_{A}\|_{\infty}/f(x_{A})\leqslant e^{6n}
. Therefore,

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant 2\sqrt{n}\big(n+6n\big)=14n\sqrt{n}

as claimed.
∎

For the general case, where
A
may not be contained in
R_{6n}(\mu)
, and in particular
f(x_{A})
may be arbitrarily small, we combine our approach with some
of the ideas developed by Livshyts in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every
convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant Cn^{3/2},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that if
A\subseteq R_{6n}(\mu)
then the result follows from
Corollary
4.8
. Otherwise,
A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu)\neq\varnothing
.

We decompose
\partial A
as follows:

\partial A=(\partial A\cap R_{6n}(\mu))\cup(\partial A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu)).

Note that
\partial A\cap R_{6n}(\mu)\subseteq\partial(A\cap R_{6n}(\mu))
, therefore

(4.3)

\mu^{+}(\partial A\cap R_{6n}(\mu))\leqslant\mu^{+}(\partial(A\cap R_{6n}(\mu)))\leqslant 14n^{3/2}

by Corollary
4.8
applied to the convex body
A\cap R_{6n}(\mu)\subseteq R_{6n}(\mu)
.

Next, we estimate
\mu^{+}(\partial A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu))
. Note that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\|f\|_{\infty}}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)\geqslant e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\})\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{s}(\mu))\,ds.

We write

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(\partial A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu))

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu)}f(x)\,d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{(x,t):f(x)\geqslant t\}}(x,t)\,dt\,d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{e^{-6n}\|f\|_{\infty}}\lambda(\partial A\cap\{x:e^{-6n}\|f\|_{\infty}\geqslant f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{e^{-6n}\|f\|_{\infty}}\lambda(\partial A\cap\{x:f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{6n}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\lambda(\partial A\cap R_{s}(\mu))\,ds,

making the change of variables
t=e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}
. Since
\partial A\cap R_{s}(\mu)\subseteq\partial(A\cap R_{s}(\mu))
, and also
A\cap R_{s}(\mu)\subseteq R_{s}(\mu)

and both sets are convex, using the monotonicity of
Lebesgue surface area of convex bodies with respect to inclusion,
as well as (
2.1
) and Lemma
3.2
, we get

\lambda(\partial A\cap R_{s}(\mu))\leqslant\lambda(A\cap R_{s}(\mu))=S(A\cap R_{s}(\mu))\leqslant S(R_{s}(\mu))\leqslant\frac{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{s}(\mu))}{r(R_{s}(\mu))}\leqslant 3n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{s}(\mu)).

It follows that

(4.5)

\mu^{+}(\partial A\setminus R_{6n}(\mu))\leqslant 3n\int_{6n}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{s}(\mu))\,ds\leqslant 3n\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{s}(\mu))\,ds=3n\|f\|_{1}

using (
4.4
) in the end. Combining (
4.5
) with (
4.3
) we obtain

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant 14n^{3/2}+3n\,\|f\|_{1}=(14+3/\sqrt{n})n^{3/2}.

This completes the proof of the theorem. ∎

5
Improved bounds under additional structure

In this section we show that the estimate
O(n^{3/2})
in Theorem
1.1
can be improved under additional geometric
assumptions on the measure. More precisely, we establish linear bounds of order
O(n)
for several natural classes of isotropic log-concave measures.

Besides the results of the previous section, we shall exploit a general upper estimate for
\Gamma(\mu)
due to Livshyts
[
25
]
.
For any integrable function
f
whose level sets
R_{t}(\mu)
are convex, Livshyts established the following result.

Theorem 5.1

(Livshyts)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be an integrable function such that
R_{t}(\mu)
is convex for all
t\geqslant 0
.
Then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant\inf\left\{n\,\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{t}(\mu))+\|f\|_{1}}{r(R_{t}(\mu))}:t>0\right\}

where
\mu
is the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
.

For isotropic log-concave functions
f
, we have
\|f\|_{\infty}=L_{f}^{n}
and
\|f\|_{1}=1
.
Thus, to obtain an upper bound for
\Gamma(\mu)
, it suffices to choose
t>0
such that
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{t}(\mu))
is suitably bounded from above
and
r(R_{t}(\mu))
is suitably bounded from below.
In the isotropic log-concave case, Livshyts’ method yields
\Gamma(\mu)=O(n^{2})
for an appropriate choice of
t
.

§5.1. Uniform measures on convex bodies.

We begin with the case of uniform measures on convex bodies.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
K
be an isotropic convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\mu_{K}
be the corresponding isotropic probability measure
with density
f_{K}:=L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{K/L_{K}}
. Then

(5.1)

\Gamma(\mu_{K})=L_{K}S(K).

In particular,
\Gamma(\mu_{K})\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2}}\,n
.

Proof.

For any convex body
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we may write

\displaystyle\mu_{K}^{+}(\partial A)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial A\cap L_{K}^{-1}K}f_{K}(x)\,d\lambda(x)\leqslant\int_{\partial(A\cap L_{K}^{-1}K)}f_{K}(x)\,d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=L_{K}^{n}S(A\cap L_{K}^{-1}K)\leqslant L_{K}^{n}S(L_{K}^{-1}K)=L_{K}S(K),

with equality if
A=L_{K}^{-1}K
. It follows that

\Gamma(\mu_{K})=L_{K}S(K).

For the second claim, using the general bound
S(K)\leqslant n\,\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)/r(K)
from (
2.1
),
together with the normalization
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)=1
, we obtain

\Gamma(\mu_{K})\leqslant nL_{K}/r(K),

The lower bound
r(K)\geqslant\sqrt{\frac{n+2}{n}}L_{K}
from (
2.2
) completes the proof. ∎

Remark 5.3
.

The estimate
\Gamma(\mu_{K})\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2}}\,n

for uniform measures on isotropic convex bodies is sharp. It becomes an equality if
K
is an isotropic

n
-dimensional regular simplex. Indeed, let
v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy

|v_{i}|=1,\hskip 18.49988pt\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=-\frac{1}{n}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{if}\;i\neq j,\hskip 18.49988pt\sum_{i=0}^{n}v_{i}=0,

and consider the regular simplex
\Delta_{0}:=\mathrm{conv}\{v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\}
, which is centered at the origin.
Let
\Delta:=\beta\Delta_{0}
be the homothetic copy of
\Delta_{0}
with
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\Delta)=1
, so
\beta^{n}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\Delta_{0})=1
.
We compute
\Gamma(\mu_{\Delta})=L_{\Delta}S(\Delta)
.

To compute the inradius, note that for each
i
the facet
F_{i}:=\mathrm{conv}\{v_{j}:\ j\neq i\}
lies in the hyperplane

H_{i}:=\{x:\ \langle x,v_{i}\rangle=-1/n\}
, because for
j\neq i
one has
\langle v_{j},v_{i}\rangle=-1/n
. Hence, the distance from
the origin to
H_{i}
equals
1/n
, and since the simplex is regular, the origin is the incenter of
\Delta_{0}
. Therefore,

r(\Delta_{0})=1/n\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and}\hskip 18.49988ptr(\Delta)=\beta r(\Delta_{0})=\beta/n.

To compute the isotropic constant, let
X
be uniform on
\Delta_{0}
. Using barycentric coordinates, we may write

X=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\Lambda_{i}v_{i},

where
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{0},\dots,\Lambda_{n})
is uniform on the simplex
\{\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0,\ \sum\lambda_{i}=1\}
.
Then
\mathbb{E}(\Lambda_{i})=\frac{1}{n+1}
and

\mathbb{E}(\Lambda_{i}^{2})=\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbb{E}(\Lambda_{i}\Lambda_{j})=\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\hskip 9.24994pt(i\neq j).

Since
\sum_{i=0}^{n}v_{i}=0
, we have
\mathbb{E}(X)=0
and

\mathrm{Cov}(X)=\mathbb{E}\left(XX^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}v_{i}v_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Thus
\Delta
is isotropic with

L_{\Delta}=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{n(n+2)}}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and hence}\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{L_{K}}{r(K)}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2}}.

Finally, for the surface area and
\Gamma(\mu_{\Delta})
, note that for any simplex whose incenter is at the origin with inradius
r
, one has the exact identity

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\Delta)=\frac{r}{n}\,S(\Delta),

and since
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\Delta)=1
we obtain
S(\Delta)=\frac{n}{r(\Delta)}
. Therefore,

\Gamma(\mu_{\Delta})=L_{\Delta}S(\Delta)=L_{\Delta}\frac{n}{r(K)}=n\,\frac{L_{\Delta}}{r(\Delta)}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2}}\,n.

This shows that the estimate in Proposition
5.2
is optimal.

§5.2. Measures with homogeneous level sets.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a geometric log-concave density
f
satisfying

(5.2)

R_{t}(\mu)=t^{1/p}K\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }t>0,

where
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a fixed convex body containing the origin in its interior and
p\geqslant 1
.

Applying Theorem
5.1
, we obtain for any
t>0
,

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant n\,\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{t}(\mu))+1}{r(R_{t}(\mu))},

where we used
\|f\|_{1}=1
.
We choose
t
as large as possible under the constraint
\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{t}(\mu))\leqslant 1
.
Choosing
t=n/e^{p}
, we observe that
R_{n/e^{p}}(\mu)=e^{-1}R_{n}(\mu)
, and Markov’s inequality yields

\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{n/e^{p}}(\mu))=\|f\|_{\infty}e^{-n}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{n}(\mu))\leqslant\mu(R_{n}(\mu))\leqslant 1.

Since
r(R_{n/e^{p}}(\mu))=e^{-1}r(R_{n}(\mu))
and
r(R_{n}(\mu))\geqslant 1/18
(by Remark
3.3
), we conclude that

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant\frac{2n}{r(R_{n/e^{p}}(\mu))}\leqslant\frac{2en}{r(R_{n}(\mu))}\leqslant 36en.

Remark 5.4
.

A canonical example is provided by an isotropic measure
\nu_{K}
with density

f_{\nu_{K}}(x)=c_{K}e^{-\|x\|_{K}^{p}}
, where
K
is a symmetric convex body and
c_{K}=(c_{p,n}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K))^{-1}

with
c_{p,n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}pt^{n+p-1}e^{-t^{p}}dt
. Note that

R_{n}(f_{\nu_{K}})=n^{1/p}K.

Since
\nu_{K}
is even and geometric, this implies that
\overline{K}
has bounded geometric distance from an almost isotropic convex body
in the sense of
[
11
, Definition 2.5.11]
: there exists
an almost isotropic convex body
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

c_{1}D\subseteq\overline{K}\subseteq c_{2}D,

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are absolute constants (for a proof see
[
14
, Lemma 3.3]
).

§5.3.
1
-unconditional measures.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
which is
1
-symmetric, meaning that

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(\epsilon_{1}x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}x_{\sigma(n)})

for all choices of signs
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
and all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The symmetry of
f
implies that each level set
R_{t}(\mu)
is a
1
-symmetric convex body.
In particular,
R_{t}(\mu)
is in John’s position: the maximal-volume ellipsoid contained in
R_{t}(\mu)
is the Euclidean ball
r(R_{t}(\mu))B_{2}^{n}
.
Indeed, the maximal volume ellipsoid of
R_{t}(\mu)
is also
1
-symmetric, which forces it to be a centered ball.
K. Ball’s volume ratio theorem
[
4
]
then yields

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{t}(\mu))^{1/n}}{r(R_{t}(\mu))}\leqslant\mathrm{vol}_{n}(Q_{n})^{1/n}=2,

where
Q_{n}=[-1,1]^{n}
is the cube with inradius
r(Q_{n})=1
.
Consequently,

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(R_{t}(\mu))^{1/n}\leqslant 2r(R_{t}(\mu))\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }t>0.

This allows us to apply Theorem
5.1
with a suitable choice of
t
and obtain the bound

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant Cn

for an absolute constant
C>0
, already observed in
[
25
]
.

We now obtain a similar, linear-in-the-dimension bound for the maximal perimeter of any
1
-unconditional isotropic log-concave
probability measure. Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f
which is
1
-unconditional,
meaning that

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(\epsilon_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}x_{n})

for all choices of signs
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
.
For
k=1,\dots,n
, let
e_{k}
be the
k
-th standard basis vector and consider the line segment

I_{k}:=[-e_{k},e_{k}].

Recall that
B_{2}^{n}\subset B_{\infty}^{n}
, and that

B_{\infty}^{n}=I_{1}+\cdots+I_{n}

is the Minkowski sum of the segments
I_{k}
.
We begin with the following simple telescoping lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
\epsilon>0
, define

A_{0}:=A,\hskip 18.49988ptA_{k}:=A_{k-1}+\epsilon I_{k}\hskip 18.49988pt(k=1,\dots,n).

Then
A_{n}=A+\epsilon B_{\infty}^{n}
, and

\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mu(A)\leqslant\mu(A+\epsilon B_{\infty}^{n})-\mu(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu\big((A_{k-1}+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus A_{k-1}\big).

Proof.

Since
B_{2}^{n}\subset B_{\infty}^{n}
we have
A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n}\subset A+\epsilon B_{\infty}^{n}
, and hence

\mu(A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mu(A)\leqslant\mu(A+\epsilon B_{\infty}^{n})-\mu(A)
.
Moreover, since
B_{\infty}^{n}=I_{1}+\cdots+I_{n}
, we have
A+\epsilon B_{\infty}^{n}=A_{n}
by construction.
Finally, the telescoping identity

(5.3)

\mu(A_{n})-\mu(A_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(\mu(A_{k})-\mu(A_{k-1})\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu\big(A_{k}\setminus A_{k-1}\big)

gives the claim, since
A_{k}=A_{k-1}+\epsilon I_{k}
.
∎

In what follows, for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we define the
k
-th marginal density

g_{k}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}f(y,t)\,dy,

where we write
x=(y,t)
with
t=x_{k}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
collecting the remaining coordinates.

Lemma 5.6
.

Assume that
f
is unconditional and log-concave. Then for every convex set

B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, every
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and every
\epsilon>0
,

\mu\big((B+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus B\big)\leqslant 2\epsilon g_{k}(0).

Proof.

Fix
k
and write
x=(y,t)
with
t=x_{k}
. For each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, the fiber

B_{y}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\ (y,t)\in B\}

is an interval, since
B
is convex. If
B_{y}=[a(y),b(y)]
then
(B+\epsilon I_{k})_{y}=[a(y)-\epsilon,b(y)+\epsilon]
, and hence

\big((B+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus B\big)_{y}\subset[a(y)-\epsilon,a(y)]\ \cup\ [b(y),b(y)+\epsilon],

with the obvious modifications if one of the endpoints is infinite.

By Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mu\big((B+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus B\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{\big((B+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus B\big)_{y}}f(y,t)\,dt\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{a(y)-\epsilon}^{a(y)}f(y,t)\,dt+\int_{b(y)}^{b(y)+\epsilon}f(y,t)\,dt\right)\,dy.

For fixed
y
, the function
t\mapsto f(y,t)
is log-concave and even,
by unconditionality in the
k
-th coordinate. Hence it attains its maximum at
t=0
, so
f(y,t)\leqslant f(y,0)
for all
t
.
Therefore,

\int_{a(y)-\epsilon}^{a(y)}f(y,t)\,dt+\int_{b(y)}^{b(y)+\epsilon}f(y,t)\,dt\leqslant 2\epsilon\,f(y,0).

Integrating in
y
yields

\mu\big((B+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus B\big)\leqslant 2\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}f(y,0)\,dy=2\epsilon\,g_{k}(0),

as claimed.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic unconditional log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

with continuous density
f
. Then for every convex set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\epsilon>0
,

\mu\big((A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\setminus A\big)\leqslant\sqrt{2}n\epsilon.

Consequently,
\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\sqrt{2}n
for every convex set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and hence
\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant\sqrt{2}n
.

Proof.

Fix a convex set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\epsilon>0
. Define
A_{0},\dots,A_{n}
as in Lemma
5.5
.
Since
A
is convex and Minkowski sums preserve convexity, each
A_{k-1}
is convex.
By Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
,

\mu\big((A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\setminus A\big)\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu\big((A_{k-1}+\epsilon I_{k})\setminus A_{k-1}\big)\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}2\epsilon g_{k}(0)=2\epsilon\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{k}(0).

Each marginal density
g_{k}
is log-concave and even on
\mathbb{R}
.
By isotropicity,
{\rm Var}(X_{k})=1
for
X_{k}\sim g_{k}
. Applying (
2.7
) to each
g_{k}
yields
g_{k}(0)\leqslant 1/\sqrt{2}
, and therefore

\mu\big((A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\setminus A\big)\leqslant 2\epsilon n/\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{2}n\epsilon.

Dividing by
\epsilon
and letting
\epsilon\to 0
completes the proof.
∎

§5.4. Sharp one-dimensional bound.
Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with density
f
.
For an interval
I=[a,b]
and
\epsilon>0
we write

I+\epsilon B_{2}^{1}=[a-\epsilon,b+\epsilon],\hskip 18.49988ptB_{2}^{1}=[-1,1],

and we define the perimeter of
I
with respect to
\mu
by

\mu^{+}(\partial I):=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu([a-\epsilon,b+\epsilon])-\mu([a,b])}{\epsilon}.

The maximal perimeter of
\mu
is defined as

\Gamma(\mu):=\sup\big\{\mu^{+}(\partial I):I\subset\mathbb{R}\ \text{an interval}\big\}.

The next proposition provides an exact formula for
\Gamma(\mu)
in one dimension.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with density
f
, and set
M:=\|f\|_{\infty}
. Then

\Gamma(\mu)=2M.

Proof.

Fix an interval
I=[a,b]
. Then

\mu^{+}(\partial I)=f(a^{-})+f(b^{+})\leqslant 2\|f\|_{\infty}=2M,

and hence
\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant 2M
.

Conversely, let
x
be such that
f(x)
is arbitrarily close to
M
. For
\delta>0
sufficiently small, the interval

I_{\delta}=[x-\delta,x+\delta]
is finite and satisfies

\mu^{+}(\partial I_{\delta})=f((x-\delta)^{-})+f((x+\delta)^{+})\to 2f(x)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{as}\;\delta\to 0^{+}.

Thus
\Gamma(\mu)\geqslant 2f(x)
. Choosing
x
so that
f(x)\to M
we get
\Gamma(\mu)\geqslant 2M
.
∎

Proposition
5.8
implies a sharp bound for isotropic log-concave densities on
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with density
f
. Then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant 2.

Moreover, the constant
2
is sharp: there exist isotropic log-concave probability measures
\mu
for which

\Gamma(\mu)
is arbitrarily close to
2
. In fact, one-sided exponential distributions achieve equality in
the underlying extremal inequality.

Proof.

Let
M=\|f\|_{\infty}
. By Proposition
5.8
, it suffices to show that
M\leqslant 1
.

Let
X
be a random variable with distribution
\mu
. For a log-concave density on
\mathbb{R}
we have

{\rm Var}(X)\leqslant\frac{1}{M^{2}}.

Since
\mu
is isotropic,
{\rm Var}(X)=1
, and hence
M\leqslant 1
. Therefore,

\Gamma(\mu)=2M\leqslant 2.

To see that this bound is sharp, let
Y\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and define
X:=Y-1
. Then
\mathbb{E}(X)=0
and
{\rm Var}(X)=1
. The
density of
X
is given by

f(x)=e^{-(x+1)}\mathds{1}_{\{x\geqslant-1\}},

so
M=\|f\|_{\infty}=1
. Hence

\Gamma(\mu)=2M=2
, attained as a supremum by intervals shrinking to points where
f
is close to
1
.
∎

§5.5.
O(n)
bound for product measures.
Combining the results of the previous two subsections, we now derive a linear-in-the-dimension
upper bound for the maximal perimeter of log-concave product measures. We begin with the following general statement.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
be a product probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
where each
\mu_{k}
has a density
g_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for every convex set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(5.4)

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\ \leqslant\ 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}.

Consequently,

\Gamma(\mu)\ \leqslant\ 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, the constant
2
in (
5.4
) is optimal.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.5
, for
k=1,\dots,n
let
I_{k}:=[-e_{k},e_{k}]
. Given a
convex set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\epsilon>0
, define

A_{0}:=A,\hskip 18.49988ptA_{k}:=A_{k-1}+\epsilon I_{k}\hskip 18.49988pt(k=1,\dots,n).

By Fubini’s theorem, Lemma
5.5
, and the product structure of
\mu
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu(A_{k})-\mu(A_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\Big(\mu_{k}\big((A_{k-1})_{y}+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\big)-\mu_{k}\big((A_{k-1})_{y}\big)\Big)\,d\mu_{\widehat{k}}(y),

where
\mu_{k}
is the
k
-th marginal and
\mu_{\widehat{k}}:=\bigotimes_{i\neq k}\mu_{i}
.

Let
J\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval. The set
J+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
differs from
J
only near its endpoints.
Therefore,

\mu_{k}\big((J+[-\epsilon,\epsilon])\setminus J\big)\leqslant 2\epsilon\,\|g_{k}\|_{\infty},

since there are at most two endpoint neighborhoods, each of length
\epsilon
,
and the density is bounded by
\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}
. Hence,

\mu(A_{k})-\mu(A_{k-1})\leqslant 2\epsilon\,\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}.

Summing over
k
and using (
5.3
), we obtain

\mu\big((A+\epsilon B_{2}^{n})\setminus A\big)\leqslant 2\epsilon\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}.

Dividing by
\epsilon
and letting
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
yields (
5.4
).
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
be a product probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where each
\mu_{k}
is a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}

with density
g_{k}
and
{\rm Var}(X_{k})=1
, where
X_{k}\sim\mu_{k}
. Then

\Gamma(\mu)\leqslant 2n.

In view of Theorem
5.10
, the corollary follows immediately from the bound
\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
,
which holds by (
2.6
).
