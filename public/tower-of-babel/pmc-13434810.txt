Exploring anticancer drug structures through vertex based resolving parameters
Authors: Faryal Chaudhry, Mazhar Hussain, Muhammad Farhan Hanif, A K Alzahrani, Osman Abubakar Fiidow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe generation of molecular descriptors with structural information remains a major challenge in quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) studies. We introduce a new class of vertex-based topological indices based on the idea of resolving degrees stemming from metric dimension theory in chemical graph theory. In contrast to classical degree-based descriptors, the introduced indices consider vertex resolvability information, thus increasing their capacity to separate structurally similar molecular graphs. These descriptors were calculated for several anticancer drugs and correlated with spatially significant physicochemical properties, namely, boiling point, molar volume, enthalpy of vaporization, flash point, and molar refractivity. The predictive performance of the linear and cubic regression models was considered. The analysis revealed statistically significant and robust correlations between the proposed indices and all the studied properties. Although linear models present interpretable relationships, unlike cubic regression models that prioritize accuracy over interpretability, the underlying nonlinear structural effects are better represented in cubic regressions, which can consistently achieve the best accuracy. The results indicate that resolving-degree-based descriptors are more predictive of structure than their qualifying counterparts, making these new topological metrics promising candidates for the QSPR modeling framework as well as in computational drug design and cheminformatics.Keywords: Resolving degree, Metric dimension, Topological indices, Anticancer drugs, Regression analysis, Computational chemistrySubject terms: Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Graph theory has proven to be a versatile mathematical instrument in the study of complex systems in computer science, engineering, and natural sciences. More specifically, chemical graph theory has shown that graphs can be used as a valid model for molecular graphs, with graph vertices associated with individual atoms and graph edges symbolizing chemical bonds between them. This notion provides a useful tool for applying graph invariants to describe the topological properties of molecules, linking them with measurable physicochemical values of substances, which underlie all quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) models. Its applicability has made it a crucial tool for cheminformatics, drug design, and material studies1,2. Among the many graph invariants examined in the literature, distance-based invariants have been recognized as very successful in characterizing both the local and global properties of graphs. Invariants, such as the Wiener index and Padmakar Ivan index, have been widely used in different QSPR calculations to approximate the boiling points, bioactivity, and thermodynamic characteristics of chemical compounds3–5. However, these invariants sometimes fail to capture the differences in structurally different graphs. This has led to the investigation of different invariants with high discriminative powers. Chemical graph theory is a branch of mathematical chemistry that applies the principles of graph theory to the representation and analysis of molecular structures6. This abstraction allows complex molecular structures to be described mathematically, enabling the derivation of graph invariants and topological indices that characterize molecular connectivity and structure independently of geometric conformation7.

Metric dimension in graph theory was first mentioned independently by Slater in 1975 and Harary and Melter in 1976. Slater introduced a resolving set and a location number for a graph, considering the unique identification problem with respect to the vertices of an ordered graph G=(V,E) when distance information is given8, and Harary and Melter introduced the name for the metric dimension, by setting its basic properties9. The metric dimension has been extensively studied in both theoretical and applied graph theory since its introduction. Researchers have focused on computing the metric dimension for specific classes of graphs, such as trees, cycles, cartesian products, planar graphs, and chemical graphs, and on algorithmic complexity, showing that metric dimension computation falls into the class of NP-hard problems. Recently, the concept has been further generalized and extended by introducing new variants, namely the edge metric dimension, strong metric dimension, and mixed metric dimension, allowing more complex networks than the simple structural model described here. Recently, various studies have extended the application of the metric dimension and its variants in chemical and graph structures. For instance, Imran et al.10 analyzed the metric dimensions of different graphs related to chemicals and nanostructures. This article explains the use of resolving sets to determine the structural uniqueness of molecular graphs. This study proved the importance of the metric dimension in understanding the topological structures of complex chemicals, such as nanotubes and molecular lattices. Javaid et al. in their study11 concentrated on the strong metric dimension of chemical graphs and tried to establish the importance of the study in the unique identification of vertices using the maximally distant pairs. A thorough study of the metric dimension and the computational complexity of the dimension, as well as future studies related to strong and fault tolerance in the metric dimension, was presented by Seb and Tannier12.

Ali et al.13 have examined metric dimension and edge metric dimension studies for various families of chemical graphs, using these parameters to discriminate between molecular substructure and bonding patterns uniquely. For large and symmetric molecular graphs, metric-based dimensions are exceptionally useful. In a related study, Kelenc et al. proposed and comprehensively investigated the concept of edge metric dimension14, offering exact values for many graph classes and emphasizing its applications in models where an interaction between edges is dominant, as opposed to that between vertices, such as in the issue of chemical bonding. Furthermore, Baca et al.15 studied the metric dimension of cartesian product graphs, a structure that commonly arises in modeling complex chemical and crystalline systems. Mhagama et al.16 analyzed the edge metric dimension of the line graph of molecular structures of antiviral drugs like Acyclovir and Tecovirimat, demonstrating the capacity of metric invariants in the description of the molecular interaction relevant in the drug development process. To complement these studies, Kumar et al.17 investigated the edge metric dimension in various chemical graph structures, such as non-Kekule benzenoid hydrocarbons and backbone DNA topologies, demonstrating the ability of edge resolvability parameters to provide unique topological information regarding complex molecular structures. Furthermore, to enhance the graph resolvability parameters in chemical and graph structures, Batiha et al.18 analyzed the secure metric dimension and the corresponding metric-connected dimensions of novel types of graph structures. Materials and methods

The anticancer drugs investigated in this study were abemaciclib, anastrozole, belotecan, doxorubicin, exemestane, gemcitabine, ifosfamide, and lapatinib. The molecular structures of these compounds were obtained from standard chemical databases and represented as simple which are given in Fig. 2, connected, undirected molecular graphs, where vertices correspond to non-hydrogen atoms and edges represent covalent bonds. Hydrogen atoms were omitted, following the conventional practices in chemical graph theory. Using the constructed molecular graphs, several degree-based topological indices were computed, namely the atom bond connectivity (ABC), geometric arithmetic (GA), harmonic (HI), symmetric division degree (SDD), inverse sum indegree (ISI), forgotten (FI), and sombor (SI) indices, which are listed in Table 3. Each index was calculated by summing the contributions of all edges according to their mathematical definitions based on the vertex degrees. The computations were performed systematically using analytical methods and verified using computational tools to ensure accuracy. These indices were employed to characterize the structural properties of the selected anticancer drugs and to facilitate comparative topological analysis, as shown in Table 4 and all the data were obtained from ChemSpider.

Multiple linear regression (MLR) analysis was used to explore the quantitative correlation between the chosen topological descriptors and desired physicochemical properties. In the developed model, the physicochemical properties acted as dependent variables, whereas the topological descriptors represented the molecular models that explained the chemical structures of the investigated compounds. Based on the linear model concept, the effect of the molecular descriptors on the desired physicochemical property is direct and additive; thus, the model is easily interpretable in terms of how the topology of the molecular structure affects the desired compound property. QSPR analysis was performed using eight topological indices, namely ABC(G), GA, HI, FI, SSD, ISI, and SI, as independent variables, and boiling point (BP), molar volume (MV), flash point (FP), evaporation enthalpy (EV), and molar refractivity (MR) as dependent physicochemical phenomena. Linear and cubic models were used to examine the QSPR relationships and test the predictive ability of the descriptors. The goodness of the models and their statistical significance were tested using the correlation coefficient (r), coefficient of determination (), F-statistic, and p-values. In this context, the correlation coefficient r represents the strength of the relationship between the observed and predicted values, represents the percentage of variation in the physicochemical property accounted for by the equation, the F-statistic represents the overall significance of the regression equation, and the p-value indicates the reliability of the data for the calculation of the parameters.

Based on the atom bond connectivity index (ABC(G)) as the molecular variable, linear and cubic regression equations were established for each physicochemical property involved in the current study.

The data presented in Table 5 show that both the correlation coefficient r and the coefficient of determination suggest a solid relationship between the ABC index and the five physicochemical properties: BP, MV, EV, FP, and MR. The values for r were high and positive for both the linear and cubic models, lying between 0.88 and 0.95 for all models. These values reveal a positive linear relationship between the predicted and actual values of the property, establishing a systematic increase in the physicochemical properties with an increase in the value of the ABC index. The slightly higher values for the cubic models for most cases reveal a better agreement between the actual and predicted results when non-linearities are considered. Likewise, the coefficient of determination values indicate that the ABC-based models are successful in explaining a prominent portion of the variability in the physicochemical variables. In particular, for the boiling point and molar refractivity, tends towards 0.95, which is outstanding. For the molar volume, evaporation enthalpy, and flash point, values were retained above 0.77, which is categorized as an acceptable to strong performance of models in QSPR. The small increase observed in the cubic models indicates that it adequately covers the variability contained in higher-order terms but improves only marginally on the linear models.

Conclusion

In this work we presented resolving degree based topological indices as efficient molecular descriptors for QSPR analysis on a series of targeted anticancer drugs. The proposed indices add more structural information than most traditional degree-based descriptors by embedding concepts from metric dimension theory as related to vertex resolvability. The regression analysis revealed significant correlations of these descriptors with important physicochemical properties such as boiling point, molar volume, enthalpy of vaporization, flash-point and molar refractivity. Linear models provided simple interpretable relationships, but cubic models generally improved predictive accuracy by capturing nonlinear structure-property effects. In summary, the findings validate the effectiveness of resolving degree-based descriptors as viable options in molecular characterization with potential applications in computational chemistry, cheminformatics and anticancer drug design. We envisioned future work that may deploy this method on larger drug data sets and combine these descriptors into machine learning models in an effort to improve prediction accuracy.
