Autistic traits influence aesthetic judgments of abstract color works
Authors: Leyi Wang, Mengdan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorders (ASD) are characterized by atypicalities in both social and non-social behaviors, yet little is known about how autistic traits influence aesthetic judgments. This study investigated the impact of autism tendencies, measured by the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ), on aesthetic evaluations of abstract color works. We recruited 111 university students to rate the colorfulness of, and their liking for, images composed of geometric color shapes. The hues of these shapes were systematically varied in terms of both their perceptual distance and categorical membership. Results demonstrate that larger hue distances enhanced both colorfulness and liking ratings. Critically, higher AQ was associated with reduced liking for color combinations with larger perceptual distances and for those spanning different color categories. Furthermore, although AQ scores demonstrated an overall negative association with both colorfulness and liking ratings, the strength of this association was highly hue-dependent, peaking in purple-pink regions and diminishing to minimal levels in approximately yellow-green areas. Notably, the patterns of hue-dependent modulation differed between colorfulness and liking ratings. While colorfulness ratings showed a consistently negative relationship with AQ across hues, liking ratings displayed a biphasic pattern, transitioning from relatively enhanced preference in the green-cyan region to strongly reduced liking in the purple-pink region. It is suggested that autistic traits influence perceptual and affective dimensions of color experience through partially distinct mechanisms. These findings reveal the nuanced role of autistic traits in shaping aesthetic experience, highlighting dissociable effects on low-level perceptual processing versus higher-order aesthetic evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03876-6.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorders (ASD) are neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by atypicalities in social interaction, social communication, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior. Researchers have conducted substantial investigations into the atypical patterns of social and cognitive functions associated with autism [35]. A core feature of autism is the atypicality in social skills, which includes specific aspects of communication abilities and the social use of language. Though most previous research links the condition of ASD with social and communication difficulties, as well as repetitive behaviors, there is also widespread reporting of differences in sensory perception, particularly in the visual domain [8]. Autistic individuals have cognitive processing styles biased toward local and field-independent processing over global integration [9, 10, 22, 28, 29, 58]. Also, they exhibit reduced cognitive flexibility than TD (typical developing) individuals [36]. Children diagnosed with ASD have faster automatic visual orienting compared to TD children, which may hinder their exploratory behavior and lead to difficulties in complex social environments [34]. Additionally, autistic individuals exhibit distinctive visual processing characteristics, including enhanced abilities in color perception, altered visuospatial processing (e.g., challenges in body spatial awareness and locating oneself in space) and visuomotor skills (e.g., compensatory reliance on proprioception for spatial orientation) [18]. These features may be associated with their visual attention mechanisms and atypical early visual processing [18]. At the same time, autistic individuals exhibit their unique strengths. Some perceptual capacities of individuals on the autism spectrum are enhanced compared to the TD group [3, 11, 44]. For example, autistic individuals show superior processing of detailed partial information on a wide variety of perceptual tasks [25]. There are two main interpretations of their atypical perception. The weak central coherence hypothesis attributes this condition to difficulities in extracting the meaning or the gist of an input [23, 30]. On the other hand, the enhanced perceptual function model interprets this condition as an overdevelopment of low-level perceptual processing, which leads to enhanced basic perceptual abilities, such as detection and discrimination [45, 46, 51, 52].

Despite the substantial studies on individuals with autism, their aesthetic experience and its underlying mechanism have received much less attention. Human aesthetic experience is influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, cultural, objective, and subjective factors. There have been studies showing that autistic individuals have atypical aesthetic experiences, which are different from TD individuals [12, 16, 43, 49]. The difference in aesthetic perception has been linked to both cognitive [49] and emotional abilities [16]. [49] revealed that the aesthetic judgment score of autistic individuals was significantly lower than the control group for fractal images but not for landscape images. The tendency to experience the images as less beautiful was correlated with lower scores in emotional skills, measured by empathy scales. Compared to neurotypical individuals, autistic individuals exhibited reduced neural activation in anterior prefrontal regions associated with cognitive-emotional integration and positive emotional experience, alongside heightened activation in posterior regions dedicated to visuospatial analysis. It is suggested that it could reflect a compensatory recruitment of cognitive resources for detailed visual analysis, possibly due to a reduced integration of cognitive and emotional signals during the aesthetic experience. Using images of sculptures from classical and Renaissance masterpieces, [43] found that autistic individuals provided lower explicit ratings for both objective beauty (“Is it beautiful?“) and subjective liking (“Do you like it?“) compared to neurotypical individuals. A key insight from their study, however, lies in the dissociation they observed: while these explicit aesthetic evaluations differed, autistic individuals performed comparably on a proportion judgement task—a perceptual-cognitive component requiring no emotional engagement. Concerning eye-tracking data, there was a difference in visual exploration patterns between autistic and non-autistic participants only when an aesthetic evaluation was required. During the explicit aesthetic tasks, autistic participants showed reduced attention to faces in comparison to non-autistic participants. In contrast, there was no group difference in viewing patterns during the neutral proportion task. This dissociation between typical low-level perception and atypical explicit aesthetic evaluation suggests that the core difference may not lie in perceptual ability per se, but in the subsequent stages of information processing that translate perception into an explicit affective judgement. In addition, [12] suggested that individuals on the autism spectrum exhibited reduced intuitive processing and greater deliberative processing, consistent with predictions by the Dual Process Theory of Autism [4, 13, 15, 37].

According to these studies, there might not be universal and consistent differences between autistic individuals and TD individuals in aesthetic experience, which likely depends on various factors such as the stimulus type, task demands, and levels of processing. With regard to general aesthetic experience, both low-level and high-level features of the image can influence human preferences [32]. Color, among other visual features, makes a critical contribution to aesthetic preferences [2, 47, 56, 60]. There have been some attempts focusing on atypical color perception and cognition in individuals on the autism spectrum, including color discrimination, color categorization, color preference, etc. [20, 31]. It was found that children with autism were significantly less accurate than controls at color memory, color search, and chromatic discrimination [20, 21, 65], but the strength of categorical perception of color did not differ for the two groups [20]. The reduced chromatic discrimination in autistic individuals might be associated with less parvocellular (P) color pathway activity [24]. In addition, several studies have shown that adults with autism experienced atypical perceptions of color ensembles [41] while exhibiting normal color adaptation [42], which supports the theory that perceptual priors are diminished in autism [50]. In addition, color preferences in autistic individuals have been reported to differ significantly from those of TD individuals, though findings have been mixed. For example, one study of autistic boys aged 4–17 years observed a preference for cooler colors such as green over warmer ones like yellow [26]. In contrast, another study involving autistic children aged 5–12 reported a preference for warmer color combinations [63]. It should be noted, however, that the latter study did not include a non-autistic control group, making it unclear whether the observed preference for warmer colors is specific to autistic children.

However, no study to date has systematically examined how autistic traits relate to aesthetic judgments of complex color combinations. To address this gap, the present study investigated how varying levels of autistic traits predict aesthetic responses to abstract color stimuli. Autistic traits are widely recognized as existing along a continuum in the general population [5], and individual differences in these traits, as measured by instruments such as the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ), reflect a broader autism phenotype [54]. The validity of the AQ extends across diverse populations, including those with comorbid conditions such as severe behavioral problems, where it remains effective in distinguishing individuals with and without ASD [53]. Moreover, sub-threshold autistic traits are consistently distributed along a dimensional spectrum, with elevated levels predictive of autism spectrum conditions even among individuals with other psychiatric disorders [19]. In addition, higher AQ scores in neurotypical adults have been linked to core cognitive patterns observed in autistic individuals (e.g., [64], supporting their use in studying autism-related mechanisms across the spectrum. This body of evidence justifies the investigation of AQ scores in neurotypical samples as a means to explore core cognitive and perceptual mechanisms associated with the autism spectrum, even in the absence of clinical diagnosis. In our study, we designed two rating tasks in which participants with different levels of autistic trait, measured by the AQ scale, evaluated color images varying in hue composition along two dimensions: (1) perceptual distance (small vs. large) and (2) categorical membership (same vs. different color categories). Participants provided ratings of both liking and colorfulness.

Drawing on evidence from previous studies, we formulated two primary but not mutually exclusive hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that individuals with higher autistic traits would rate color stimuli as subjectively more colorful overall, a pattern that should be amplified for stimuli with large perceptual distance or cross-category combinations. This expectation stems from the enhanced perceptual functioning model [46], which posits superior processing of low-level sensory features in autism. Second, individuals with higher levels of autistic traits were expected to show lower liking ratings for color combinations that perceptually distinctive or span different color categories, but relatively higher or neutral liking for combinations that are perceptually similar or within the same color category. This prediction is grounded on the weak central coherence theory [22] that autistic perception is characterized by a focus on local details at the expense of global integration, making it harder to extract holistic aesthetic qualities like “harmony” from discordant color combinations. Consequently, perceptually or categorically coherent stimuli may be experienced as more aesthetically pleasing. Notably, given the mixed evidence and the scarcity of research on color aesthetics in autism, we treat these hypotheses as exploratory and remain open to a range of possible outcomes.

Method

Participants

We recruited 111 undergraduate and graduate students (39 males,72 females; age: 18–28 years old, M = 21.4) with normal or corrected-to-normal color vision. We conducted an a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size. Materials

Autism-spectrum quotient

In our study, we used the translated Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ; [5, 39] to measure autistic traits of the participants. The AQ is a self-report measure of autistic traits, which is widely cited to quantify autistic traits of adults with at least average intelligence. The AQ has 50 items, divided into five subscales consisting of 10 items each that measure 5 domains: communication, social skills, imagination, attention to detail and attention switching. The AQ employs 4-point Likert scale as its scoring system (definitely agree; slightly agree; slightly disagree; definitely disagree) and half of the 50 items are reverse-scored. The AQ scale is scored such that each item contributes 1 point toward the total score if the response aligns with the direction of autistic traits. Specifically, for items reflecting autistic traits, a response of “slightly agree” or “definitely agree” is scored as 1. For the remaining items, which reflect the absence of autistic traits, a response of “slightly disagree” or “definitely disagree” also receives 1 point. All other responses are scored as 0. The total AQ score is the sum of all endorsed items, with higher scores indicating a greater level of autistic traits. A review has supported the validity of the AQ in indexing autistic traits in non-clinical populations [54].

Stimuli

Stimuli for the rating sessions are images (400 × 400 pixels) of randomly distributed color circles or rectangles (Fig. 1A). The colors used to generate the color images were sampled from a color wheel in the CIELAB space (L = 70, a radius of 29), which only vary in hue (10°–355°, step size: 15°; Fig. 1B). The color pool includes a total of 24 hues. Each color image is composed of three distinct hues (H1, H2 and H3) that are 15° (small-distance condition; e.g., 10°, 25°, 40°) or 30° (large-distance condition; 10°, 40°, 70°) apart in the color wheel. The three hues are evenly distributed throughout the image. To create the color images, we used arrays of discrete geometric shapes (circles and rectangles), which are commonly found in abstract artworks, as the color carriers. For each condition, each hue from the color pool was used twice to form the color image, once in an image composed of circles, and once in an image composed of rectangles. By varying both perceptual distance (small, large) and shape (circle, rectangle), this experiment contains a total of 24 hues × 2 × 2 = 96 images.

Procedure

The study was divided into two main sessions, a measure of autistic traits and three behavioral tasks (liking rating, colorfulness rating and color naming). Both sessions were conducted online, and we used Naodao to present the experiment (https://www.naodao.com/).

Session 1: autistic trait measurement

In this session, participants were asked to complete a 50-item Autism-Spectrum Quotient with guidance, and after completing the quotient, they could move on to the next session.

Session 2: behavioral tasks

This session includes three tasks: liking rating, colorfulness rating, and color naming. All three parts set standardized instructions and practice trials. Furthermore, we arranged the order as such (liking rating–colorfulness rating–color naming) to make sure that the liking rating task was not influenced by the other two tasks.

Liking rating

In the first task, the participants were asked to evaluate the color images from the aesthetic perspective and rate how much they liked the images via key presses on a 4-point scale (1 = not at all; 2 = like a little; 3 = like; and 4 = strongly like). On each trial, a color image was displayed at the center of the screen for 1,200 ms, consistent with the validated paradigm used by Sun et al. 60, and participants were not allowed to respond until the image disappeared. Based on Sun et al. 60, we believed that the stimulus duration of 1,200 ms allows sufficient time for initial aesthetic appraisal, when color combinations are used as stimuli. The order of the color images was assigned randomly for each participant. This session consistes of 24 × 2 × 2 = 96 trials in total. Each color image was presented only once. Each color combination was presented twice.

Colorfulness rating

The procedure of the colorfulness rating task is essentially the same as the liking rating task, except that the participants were asked to evaluate the colorfulness of the color images via key presses on a 4-point scale (1 = not at all colorful; 2 = a little colorful; 3 = colorful; and 4 = very colorful).

Color naming

In the color naming task, the participants were asked to name the hues used in the rating tasks. On each trial, a square (200 × 200 pixels) filled with one of the 24 hues was presented at the screen center. On the bottom of the square, seven chromatic color terms were presented (1 = red; 2 = orange; 3 = yellow; 4 = green; 5 = blue; 6 = purple; 7 = pink). Participants were asked to select the color term that most closely described the present color. The color square and color terms remained on the screen until response. This task includes 48 trials in total, with each hue appearing 2 times.

Data analysis

For each color image used in the rating sessions, the category membership (same, different) of the hues was assigned according to subjective judgment in the color naming task. Thus, the category membership is a factor tailored to each participant.

For visual features such as color and orientation, stimulus-specific variation in visual perception and judgement has been reported [61]. It is highly plausible that there is stimulus-specific variation in preferences as well (e.g., [66], with participants favoring specific hues along the color wheel over others. Figure 2A and B plots the relationship between the hue position (H1) and ratings, revealing that the ratings are not uniformly distributed across hues along the color wheels. Given the circular nature of color space, we transformed the hue position variable using trigonometric functions to properly model its periodic relationship with ratings. Specifically, we decomposed the circular variable (hue position, H1) into its sine and cosine components, creating two orthogonal predictors: sin(H1) and cos(H1). This transformation allows linear models to capture periodic patterns in the data while respecting the circular continuity of the color spectrum. This approach is mathematically equivalent to modeling the hue angle as a circular predictor while maintaining compatibility with linear modeling frameworks. In the L*a*b* color space, from which our stimuli were sampled, the sine and cosine components of hue positions map directly onto the b* (yellow-blue) and a* (red-green) opponent channels, respectively.

In addition, we conducted exploratory analyses to test the relationship between AQ dimensions and ratings. Results

Autistic trait measurement

The distribution of Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ) scores for male and female participants is presented in Figure S1 (see Supplementary Materials). The sample had a mean AQ score of 19.81 (SD = 5.74). Female participants scored slightly higher (M = 20.42, SD = 6.00, range: 8–31) than males (M = 18.69, SD = 5.12, range: 10–33). Using the cut-off score of 30 recommended by the Chinese adaptation of the AQ [39], five participants (4.5%) in our sample scored above this threshold—comprising 4 of the 72 females (5.6%) and 1 of the 39 males (2.6%). In the original validation study [39], the mean AQ scores across groups were as follows: the AS/HFA (Asperger Syndrome/High-Functioning Autism) group scored highest (37.97), followed by the math-science gifted group (25.82), science college students (22.23), humanities college students (18.45), and the general population group (17.57). Our sample’s mean score closely aligns with the general population and humanities student groups from that study, supporting the characterization of our participants as predominantly non-autistic.

A comparison of AQ scores between genders in our sample revealed no significant difference, t(109) = 1.5, p =.131, Cohen’s d = 0.302. Although prior research suggests that gender can influence color preference (e.g., [1, 27, 62, 66], the absence of a significant gender difference in AQ scores in our sample suggests that any gender-related effects on colorfulness and liking ratings are likely consistent across the autism trait spectrum.

Colorfulness rating

For colorfulness ratings (Fig. 3; Table 1), we found significant effects of perceptual distance (b = 0.595, t (10545) = 10.55, p <.001) and sin (H1) (b = −0.188, t (10545) = −4.73, p <.001). As expected, participants perceived images with large-distance hues as more colorful than small-distance hues. In addition, the colorfulness ratings significantly varied across different hues along the color wheel.

Although the AQ did not significantly interact with distance (b = 0.005, t (10565) = 1.81, p =.070; Fig. 3A), the effect reaches marginal significance, which aligns with a trend in Fig. 3A wherein individuals with higher AQ scores tended to rate small-distance combinations as less colorful, compared to those with large-distance. Meanwhile, we found a significant AQ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:$$\end{document} category membership interaction (b = −0.006, t (10565) = −1.98, p =.048; Fig. 3B). Simple slope analysis revealed that the relationship between AQ and colorfulness ratings differed significantly across category memberships. For stimuli from the same category, AQ showed a non-significant relationship with colorfulness ratings (simple slope = −0.006, 95% CI [−0.019, 0.007]). For stimuli from different categories, AQ demonstrated a significant negative relationship (simple slope = −0.012, 95% CI [−0.024, −0.00008]). This pattern indicated that higher autistic traits were more strongly associated with reduced colorfulness ratings for stimuli that cross category boundaries compared to those within the same category.

Notably, we observed a significant interaction between AQ and the sine component of color angle (b = 0.004, t (10545) = 2.19, p =.029), while the interaction with the cosine component was not significant (b = 0.001, t (10545) = 0.28, p =.779). To properly interpret this circular relationship, we computed the simple slope of AQ across the full color spectrum by considering both trigonometric components simultaneously. As shown in Fig. 3C and visualized in the circular plot in Figure S2A (Supplementary Materials), the influence of AQ on colorfulness ratings exhibited a periodic variation across the hue spectrum. Overall, the simple slope of AQ was negative, indicating that higher autistic traits correlated with lower colorfulness ratings. The simple slope of AQ was most negative at 270° (purple-pink region; simple slope = −0.0108), indicating that individuals with higher AQ scores rated colors in this region as significantly less colorful compared to those with lower AQ scores. Conversely, the least negative effects (i.e., closest to zero) were observed at 90° (yellow-green region; simple slope = −0.0024), suggesting relatively smaller AQ-related differences in colorfulness perception for these hues. To further visualize the interaction between AQs and hue position, we plotted predicted mean colorfulness ratings across the full color spectrum (0–360°) for two representative AQ levels: high AQ (mean + SD) and low AQ (mean - SD) in Fig. 3D. The point of the most negative simple slope (270°) in Fig. 3C corresponds to the hue position where the difference in colorfulness ratings between high-AQ and low-AQ groups was expected to be the largest shown in Fig. 3D. In contrast, the point of the slope closest to zero (90°) indicates the region where the two groups were expected to be most similar in their perceptual ratings.

Liking rating

For liking ratings (Fig. 4; Table 1), we revealed that perceptual distance (b = 0.328, t (10545) = 5.74, p <.001), shape (b = 0.124, t (10545) = 2.19, p =.028) and sin (H1) (b = −0.434, t (10545) = −10.81, p <.001) significantly influenced liking ratings. Consistent with the findings from Sun et al. 60, participants preferred color images composed of large-distance over small-distance hue. The effect of sin (H1) suggested that the liking ratings significantly varied across different hues along the color wheel. Besides, the color images composed of circles were more liked by the participants than rectangles.

Discussion

Our study investigated aesthetic experience in university students, focusing on the influence of autism tendencies measured by the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ). Key findings revealed that autistic traits modulated both colorfulness perception and aesthetic liking in adults from the general population, depending on specific color features. Higher autistic traits were associated with reduced colorfulness ratings for cross-category stimuli, and reduced liking for color combinations with larger distance or from different color categories. In addition, the modulatory effect of autistic traits on both colorfulness and liking ratings varied systematically across the color spectrum, exhibiting a hue-dependent pattern. However, the specific shape of this hue-dependent modulation partly differed between the two dimensions. The dissociation between colorfulness and liking effects suggests partially separable mechanisms underlying perceptual and affective processing of color.

Our findings on perceptual distance replicated those of Sun et al. 60, reaffirming the effect of hue distance on aesthetic experience, including both colorfulness and liking ratings. However, unlike previous studies, we found that shape significantly influenced liking ratings. Compared to Sun et al. 60, we introduced the position of hues on the color wheel as a novel variable, which exhibited significant main effects on both colorfulness and liking ratings, suggesting that these ratings depend on the specific hues composing the images.

Importantly, we observed both similarity and divergence in the role of autistic traits in colorfulness and liking ratings. For colorfulness ratings, autistic traits did not directly influence overall ratings, but exhibited significant modulatory effects depending on category membership and hue position. Specifically, higher autistic traits were associated with relatively reduced colorfulness ratings for color stimuli that crossed category boundaries, but not for those within the same category. The observed pattern in colorfulness ratings closely resembled that of the significant AQ × category membership interaction in liking ratings. This convergence across perceptual (colorfulness) and affective (liking) domains suggests a shared tendency among individuals with higher AQ to exhibit diminished responsiveness to categorical discontinuities. Some may argue that this result possibly reflects differences in categorical processing or boundary sensitivity for individuals with elevated autistic traits. But it is not the case in our study, as individuals with varying levels of autisic traits demonstrated comparable consistency in color categorization (please see the detailed analysis in supplementary materials). This dissociation implies that the observed effects are not due to a failure to detect or represent category boundaries per se, but rather reflect differences in how these boundaries are subjectively experienced or affectively weighted. In other words, while AQ scores did not influence the way people categorize colors, individuals with higher AQ scores may not feel the contrast across categories as strongly, leading to reduced vividness and lower aesthetic appeal. This effect may be associated with a preference for structural predictability [6, 33], where individuals with higher AQ scores prioritize categorical coherence over diversity. The combination of hues from the same category provided higher structural consistency. The Dual Process Theory of Autism suggests that individuals on the autism spectrum exhibit stronger analytical processing and may rely more on explicit categorical rules for aesthetic judgments [14]. In our study, color categories were determined through a self-naming task, suggesting that individuals with higher AQ scores adhered more strictly to their self-defined categorical boundaries, leading to a preference for same-category hues.

In addition, the co-effects of autistic traits and perceptual distance diverged in colorfulness and liking ratings. Although the interaction between AQ and perceptual distance did not reach significance in colorfulness ratings, a visible trend suggested that the negative relationship between AQ and colorfulness was more pronounced for small-distance color combinations, while being weaker for large-distance combinations. This pattern suggested a possible reduction in the perceived intensity of fine-grained, within-category chromatic differences in individuals with higher AQ, potentially reflecting reduced discriminability for subtle perceptual variations [20, 21, 65], which would not be captured by a categorical naming task. The finding is inconsistent with the enhanced perceptual functioning model which may predict enhanced subjective colorfulness in individuals with higher autistic traits [45, 46, 51, 52]. This discrepancy may be explained by fundamental differences in the populations studied. The model is primarily supported by studies of clinically diagnosed autistic individuals, whereas our findings are based on a non-clinical sample with a broad distribution of autistic traits. In contrast, a significant AQ × distance interaction emerged in liking ratings, with a clearly divergent pattern: higher AQ was associated a markedly stronger reduction in liking for large-distance stimuli but not for small-distance stimuli. This indicates that while individuals with higher AQ might not differ substantially in how they perceived the vividness of increasingly distinct colors, they did exhibit lower liking to highly dissimilar combinations. From a neurocognitive perspective, both large distance and cross-category status mean sudden shifts in chromatic or categorical representation that may generate heightened prediction error or sensory salience in visual processing. In neurotypical individuals, such transitions may be experienced as dynamic, engaging, or even rewarding. However, in individuals with higher autistic traits, these same signals may be perceived as overstimulating, unpredictable, or affectively aversive, leading to active avoidance in preference judgments. This pattern aligns with the intense world theory [40] and predictive coding models [55] of autism, which proposed that heightened neural reactivity, particularly in response to salient or unexpected changes, leads to sensory overload and compensatory strategies such as preference for stability and predictability.

Regarding hue-specific variance, the influence of autistic traits on both colorfulness and liking ratings varied systematically across the hue spectrum. As shown in Fig. 2B, liking ratings in our sample followed a systematic pattern across the hue spectrum, with the highest ratings in the blue–purple–pink region (240°–300°) and the lowest in the orange–yellow region (0°–90°). This pattern is consistent with established literature on color preference (e.g., [48, 59] and can be explained by the Ecological Valence Theory [48], which posits that color preferences are shaped by affective responses to associated objects. The high preference for blue–purple–pink hues likely stems from their strong associations with positively-valenced objects such as clear skies, calm water, and blooming flowers. Notably, both the colorfulness and liking dimensions exhibited a shared zone of minimal AQ-related divergence around 90° (yellow-green region), suggesting a common low-level mechanism that renders this region less sensitive to individual differences in autistic traits, potentially linked to cone-opponent processes [21] Li et al. 38.

Critically, however, the functional form of the hue-by-AQ interaction differed between domains. Colorfulness ratings showed a consistently negative relationship with AQ across hues, whereas liking ratings displayed a biphasic pattern: the effect of AQ transitioned from slightly positive in the green-cyan region (~ 135°) to strongly negative in the purple-pink region (~ 315°). Our findings align with those of [26], who reported reduced preference for yellow and increased preference for green in autistic boys. We similarly observed a negative AQ slope around yellow areas, indicating reduced liking, while the slightly positive AQ effect in the green-cyan region corresponds to their reported preference for green. Furthermore, their finding of reduced preference for pink resonates with our strong negative AQ effect in the purple-pink region. We interpret this AQ-related modulation through the lens of ecological valence, focusing on how autistic traits may alter the weighting of ecological signals. The green-cyan region comprises colors with high probability in natural environments (e.g., foliage, water) and stable, positive ecological associations. The slightly enhanced preference for these hues among individuals with higher AQ may reflect a heightened affinity for such predictable, non-threatening, and structured visual inputs. In contrast, while the purple-pink region also carries positive associations in the general population (e.g., with flowers and sunsets; [48], these colors are ecologically rarer and often constitute salient, atypical signals against natural backgrounds [59]. We proposed that the pronounced aversion to these hues among high-AQ individuals does not negate their general ecological valence, but rather stems from a sensory over-responsivity or a lower tolerance for such statistically anomalous and intensely salient stimuli. Thus, the same ecological property, a color’s salience and atypicality, may be experienced as engaging by the neurotypical majority, but as overstimulating or aversive by those with heightened autistic traits.

This study has several limitations. First, the presence of a neurological or psychological diagnosis was not assessed in our participants. Therefore, it is unknown whether any participants were autistic or otherwise neurodivergent. However, based on the recommended cut-off score of 30 for the Chinese AQ [39], 95.5% of our sample scored below this threshold, suggesting that the majority of participants were likely non-autistic. Therefore, our findings cannot be directly generalized to the autistic population. Second, the stimuli consisted of abstract images composed of rectangular and circular color patches, which, while common in abstract art, may not encompass all types of artworks. Therefore, our findings on aesthetic preferences may require further validation when applied to other art forms. Third, our exploratory analyses revealed no significant associations between AQ subscales and aesthetic ratings after correcting for multiple comparisons. This null finding after correction could stem from insufficient statistical power to detect subtle subscale effects, which may be partly attributable to our use of a binary scoring method for the AQ. Alternatively, it may indicate that autistic traits shape aesthetic responses through a general perceptual-cognitive style rather than via specific AQ domains. Lastly, we conducted the study online, and thus, differences and inaccuracies in color rendering could pose a concern. However, we did not believe it undermines the current conclusion, as category membership of the color stimuli was assigned based on subjective judgment of each participant. A predefined, group-level category assignment could be vulnerable to inaccuracies arising from variations in color rendering.

Our study holds both theoretical and practical significance. Systematically examining how autistic traits shape aesthetic judgments of color addresses a critical gap in the literature on neurodiverse aesthetic experience. Moreover, by bridging low-level visual features (e.g., perceptual distance) with high-level aesthetic evaluations, this study has the potential to refine theoretical models of perception and cognition in autism. Regarding practical significance, our findings could offer a preliminary basis for guiding the design of educational materials, therapeutic tools, and user interfaces that are more attuned to the perceptual preferences associated with elevated autistic traits. However, as cultural background significantly shapes color preferences [27, 62], the generalizability of our findings to other cultural contexts requires future investigation.

Conclusion

The current study demonstrates that autistic traits, far from being incidental, play a systematic and dissociable role in shaping the aesthetic experience of color. Higher levels of autistic traits are associated with a distinct aesthetic profile: a preference for perceptual coherence over large differences and categorical purity over boundary-crossing combinations. Crucially, the influence of these traits is not uniform but is precisely tuned across the color spectrum, with the most pronounced effects observed in the purple-pink region. The divergence between the patterns for colorfulness and liking ratings underscores a fundamental distinction—autistic traits modulate perceptual intensity and affective evaluation through partially separable mechanisms.
