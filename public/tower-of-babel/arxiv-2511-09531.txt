Prophet and Secretary at the Same Time
Gregory Kehne, Thomas Kesselheim

Abstract
Many online problems are studied in stochastic settings for which inputs are samples from a known distribution, given in advance, or from an unknown distribution. Such distributions model both beyond-worst-case inputs and, when given, partial foreknowledge for the online algorithm. But how robust can such algorithms be to misspecification of the given distribution? When is this detectable, and when does it matter? When can algorithms give good competitive ratios both when the input distribution is as specified, and when it is not? We consider these questions in the setting of optimal stopping, where the cases of known and unknown distributions correspond to the well-known prophet inequality and to the secretary problem, respectively. Here we ask: Can a stopping rule be competitive for the i.i.d. prophet inequality problem and the secretary problem at the same time? We constrain the Pareto frontier of simultaneous approximation ratios ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) that a stopping rule can attain. We introduce a family of algorithms that give nontrivial joint guarantees and are optimal for the extremal i.i.d. prophet and secretary problems. We also prove impossibilities, identifying ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) unattainable by any adaptive stopping rule. Our results hold for both n n fixed arrivals and for arrivals from a Poisson process with rate n n . We work primarily in the Poisson setting, and provide reductions between the Poisson and n n -arrival settings that may be of broader interest.

Introduction

Many online problems have been studied under stochastic input models.
It is a standard assumption that in each step the input is drawn i.i.d. from a
known
distribution. Alternatively, one can assume that the distribution is
unknown

Examples include online matching and the Adwords problem for both the known

Unfortunately, algorithms designed for the known i.i.d. setting are often reliant on this input, and do not perform well when the distribution is unknown.
This invites the question of robustness: Can we design algorithms that exploit knowledge of the input distribution but are still competitive if the input is misspecified?

In this paper, we address this question for the i.i.d. prophet inequality problem.
This problem is central in the theory of optimal stopping, and has received much attention due in part to its implications for the design of online posted-price mechanisms.
This motivates both the setting where underlying distribution is known and where it is unknown.

1.2
Our Techniques

For our positive result, we design an algorithm consisting of three phases: In the first phase, from time
0
to time
\tau_{1}
, all arrivals are rejected. In the second phase, from time
\tau_{1}
to
\tau_{2}
, we accept all arrivals that are the best in the sequence so far and meet a threshold derived from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. In the third phase, from time
\tau_{2}
to
1
, we drop this latter requirement and accept any arrival that is the best in the sequence so far. The idea behind this choice is that regardless of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
we only accept arrivals that are the best in the sequence so far. This ensures robustness. At the same time, if
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is indeed the correct distribution, we mimic the
(1-\frac{1}{e})
-competitive algorithm for i.i.d. prophet inequality, which sets a fixed threshold for the entire sequence (and so also only accepts arrivals that are best so far).

In the analysis, we observe that the worst incorrect advice
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
gives a competitive ratio of
\min\{\tau_{1}\log(1/\tau_{1}),\tau_{2}\log(1/\tau_{2})\}
. This is the minimum of the performances of two different secretary algorithms: One for which only the first phase serves as the observation phase, and one for which the first and the second phases serve as the observation phase. By adjusting the phase lengths, we equalize these two bounds. Our remaining degree of freedom is then the length of the second phase. To show consistency, meaning to analyze the algorithm in the case that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is indeed the correct distribution, we simulate a “Phase 0” before the execution of the algorithm, from time
-z
to
0
, following the same Poisson arrival rate and draws from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Then, as the distributions are the same, we can view all arrivals as appearing at uniformly random times in
[-z,1]
across all phases.

For our negative result, we study an instance showing that one cannot get better than
\beta_{0}\approx 0.745
in the known-distribution setting defined by
33
. We modify it slightly to hold in the Poisson setting with finite rate. We then argue that on this instance, any robustness will lead to strictly worse than
\beta_{0}
consistency. The intuition is that, for robustness, decisions should not be too hasty because one first needs to observe the sequence. However, this instance is so tight that not accepting any arrival in the first
\varepsilon
-fraction of time will necessarily lead to a competitive ratio of
\beta_{0}-\Omega(\varepsilon)
.

Our argument is much more involved than this because we argue about every possible policy, including adaptive policies. We show that the competitive ratio of a policy is necessarily bounded away from
\beta_{0}
if it accepts the first arrival above a certain value
b
with significant probability. Our modified distribution then introduces arrivals above
b
at a much higher rate. This causes any policy that has good consistency to stop early on the modified instance. Additionally, in the modified instance, extremely high values arrive at a quite small rate. So these will be lost by stopping early. Importantly, there is no way to detect this higher rate, because we only argue about the acceptance of the first such arrival.

On a technical level, arguing about suboptimal policies is complicated. Letting
r(t)
denote the expected reward of the policy after time
t
, any policy fulfills
r^{\prime}(t)\geq-\int_{r(t)}^{\infty}n(1-\mathsf{CDF}(x))dx
, with equality for the optimal policy. This differential equation is generally quite challenging to reason about. Instead, we invoke the inverse function theorem and obtain
(r^{-1})^{\prime}(s)\leq\frac{1}{-\int_{s}^{\infty}n(1-\mathsf{CDF}(x))dx}
, thus removing the recursion. This way, for each possibly adaptive policy, we can argue about how much time it spends before the expected remaining reward reaches some particular value.

1.3
Related Work

We consider the i.i.d. prophet inequality paradigm both in the setting where the underlying distribution is known and unknown.
The known distribution setting was resolved only recently; the competitive ratio was brought above
1-\frac{1}{e}
first to
0.738
by
1
, then to
\beta_{0}\approx 0.745
by
12
, matching hard instances of
26
and establishing the optimal competitive ratio.
When the distribution is unknown,
13
show that the problem is essentially equivalent to the secretary problem, and show that the optimal competitive ratio is
\frac{1}{e}
even given
o(n)
samples from the distribution.

Since each extremal problem is well-studied, most relevant prior work falls into one of two camps: work on the i.i.d. prophet inequality and relaxations of the assumption of distributional foreknowledge, and work on the secretary problem augmented by additional information or advice.

In the first camp, a long line of work continues to assume the veracity of distributional prior information, but weakens the stopping rule’s access to it.
This includes the question of how many samples are needed to approach worst-case optimality.
Remarkably, one sample per arrival suffices for the prophet inequality
[
36
]
.
In the i.i.d. setting

1-\frac{1}{e}
is possible using one sample per arrival
[
13
, Section 4.2]
, and
O(1)
samples per arrival suffice to get arbitrarily close
[
36
]
.
In a similar vein,
32
ask how useful a single quantile query to
\mathcal{D}
is, and present a stopping rule that beats
1-\frac{1}{e}
and bears some semblance to ours (
Section

3
).
Separately,
16
consider the case that
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
differ by
\varepsilon
according to various statistical distances.
They normalize all arrival values to
[0,1]
and provide stopping rules with additive guarantees in terms of
\varepsilon
and
n

The second camp takes a perspective of algorithms with unreliable advice or predictions. The consistency-robustness paradigm for online algorithms with advice was introduced by
34
in the context of online caching. It has inspired hundreds of follow-up works for all kinds of problems; see
35
for a survey, or the Website for Algorithms with Predictions
2
2

2

https://algorithms-with-predictions.github.io
for a comprehensive list. In particular, augmenting the secretary problem with advice has been studied.
One line of recent work considers advice in the form of estimates of unseen arrival values, with advice inaccuracy parameterized by
\varepsilon
.
In the consistency-robustness framework, these works aim for competitive ratio
\alpha\rightarrow 1
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, while maintaining robustness
\beta=\Omega(1)

[

21
,
10

5
]

An exception is

-largest values to be
(1-\Omega(1))

Another line of work provides distributional information to the secretary problem.
For instance,
11
and
27
take advice to be a number of samples without replacement from an adversarially chosen collection of values, the remainder of which then arrive in random order, and
17
develop an LP-based approach to handle this and other augmentations.
These models assume that augmentations are generated as prescribed, and do not allow the possibility that this distributional side-information is adversarial or otherwise faulty.

While it is arguably more common to assume a fixed number of arrivals in optimal stopping problems, there is also quite some work assuming Poisson arrivals. In particular,

2
undertakes a careful analysis of the optimal stopping problems studied in the discrete-time setting by
31
and
26

[

It is also the natural setting for recent work on steady-state analogs of these stopping problems
[
29
]
.
We find the Poisson setting better suited to proving impossibilities, and present our results for Poisson arrivals.
However this is without loss of generality, and our positive and negative results also hold in the standard
n
-arrival model as
n
becomes large.
We formalize this via reductions between the settings in
Section

2
.
While the observation these settings are roughly equivalent is not novel, we are unaware of prior reductions that are comparably general.

Finally, in independent work,
4
also jointly consider the secretary and prophet inequality problems from the perspective of consistency-robustness tradeoffs.
Like us, they consider the expected competitive ratio, which they refer to as MaxExp, as well as MaxProb, the objective of maximizing the probability of stopping on the maximum value in the arrival sequence.
Their family of stopping rules for the expected competitive ratio is similar to ours: it accepts arrivals that are best-so-far and operates in three phases, where the first phase rejects all arrivals and the second phase applies an additional threshold that arrivals must surpass in order to be accepted.
In our work this middle phase threshold is stochastic but constant, while for
4
it is deterministic but decreasing.
Both approaches eventually interpolate with Hill and Kertz’ optimal strategy in order to attain sufficiently high consistency.
While our stopping rules are similar, our impossibility results are complementary: we show a hardness result for the expected competitive ratio, while they show hardness for the MaxProb objective.

2
Preliminaries

The policy
\mathsf{ALG}
receives some advice value distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
which is possibly distinct from the true value distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
We will assume that the value distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are non-atomic; since we consider the class of randomized stopping rules (which may implement randomized tie-breaking), this is without loss of generality.

For a given rate/number of arrivals, a policy
\mathsf{ALG}
that knows
\mathcal{D}
is
\alpha
-consistent
if

\expectation_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathsf{ALG}(\mathbf{X},\mathcal{D})]\geq\alpha\cdot\expectation_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathsf{MAX}(\mathbf{X})]

for all value distributions
\mathcal{D}
, where we use
\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}
to denote a random sequence of arrivals
\mathbf{X}
, each of which is an i.i.d. sample from
\mathcal{D}
, and the expectation is also over the randomness of
\mathsf{ALG}
.
A policy is
\beta
-robust
if

\expectation_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathsf{ALG}(\mathbf{X},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})]\geq\beta\cdot\expectation_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathsf{MAX}(\mathbf{X})]

for all value distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, where again the expectation is over
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathsf{ALG}
.

What is the process by which
\mathbf{X}
is generated from
\mathcal{D}
?
We will consider two choices.

2.1
The
n
-Arrival Model

Here we consider
n
discrete time steps
i\in[n]
and
\mathbf{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny\mathclap{\mbox{(def)}}}}{{=}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, where each
X_{i}
is an i.i.d. sample from
\mathcal{D}
.
Although distributional assumptions differ, this is the most common arrival model in which the secretary problem, the prophet inequality, and related problems are studied.

2.2
The Poisson Arrival Model

Here
\mathbf{X}
is comprised of arrivals that follow a Poisson point process with rate
\lambda=n
.
For each arrival a value sampled i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
at a given rate
\lambda
, as time ranges over
t\in[0,1]
.
In other words, in each infinitesimal time interval

\mathcal{D}
arrives with probability
\lambda\cdot dt
.
This gives rise to a sequence of arrival values and times.
We let
\mathbf{T}
denote the random set of arrival times according to the Poisson process, and
\mathbf{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny\mathclap{\mbox{(def)}}}}{{=}}(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbf{T}}
the random sequence of values at these times.

2.3
Reductions Between i.i.d. Arrival Models

We now present a pair of reductions between these arrival models.
These will enable us to state algorithmic and impossibility results for both models.
These reductions may well be folklore; however we are unaware of prior appearances, and so present them here.
Proofs appear in
Appendix

B
.

The first reduction shows that a competitive stopping rule in the Poisson setting implies a competitive stopping rule in the
n
-arrival setting.

Lemma 2.1

(
n
to Poisson)
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\lambda=n(1-\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
.
Suppose
\mathsf{ALG}_{\lambda}
is an
\alpha
-competitive Poisson stopping rule for rate
\lambda
.
Let
\mathsf{ALG}_{n}
be the
n
-arrival stopping rule that simulates
\mathsf{ALG}_{\lambda}
by first sampling Poisson arrival times and then taking the first
n
values to be
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
Then
\mathsf{ALG}_{n}
is
(\alpha-4\varepsilon)
-competitive in the
n
-arrival setting.

We can also make claims in the other direction: a competitive stopping rule in the
n
-arrival setting implies a competitive rule for the Poisson setting.
Here we again make no assumptions on the form of the
n
-arrival stopping rule, but the derived Poisson rule is for a much smaller rate.

Lemma 2.2

(Poisson to
n
)
.

Suppose
\textsc{Alg}_{n}
is stopping rule for
n

\alpha
-competitive.
Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we may use it to construct a Poisson stopping rule

that is
\alpha\cdot(1-\varepsilon/2)
-competitive on Poisson arrivals at rate
\lambda=\varepsilon n
.

Note this reduction holds for both the known-distribution and unknown-distribution settings.
If
\textsc{Alg}_{n}
is a known-distribution stopping rule, then given Poisson arrivals from
\mathcal{D}
, then
\textsc{Alg}_{\lambda}
proceeds by simulating
\textsc{Alg}_{n}
on arrivals from
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
.

For impossibilities, we will use the contrapositive of

.

Corollary 2.3
.

If no
\alpha
-competitive stopping rule exists for the i.i.d. prophet inequality problem with Poisson arrivals at rate

from a known (unknown) distribution, then no
\frac{\alpha}{1-\varepsilon/2}
-competitive stopping rule exists for the prophet inequality problem with
n=\lambda/\varepsilon
i.i.d. arrivals from a known (unknown) distribution.

Beating the Linear Frontier

How can we do better than randomly interpolating between the secretary-optimal stopping rule and the known-distribution i.i.d. stopping rule, and improve upon the blue line in
Figure

1(a)
?
Indeed, why should this be possible?
Working in the Poisson arrivals model, we now introduce an algorithm which beats the line.

3.1
A Three-Phase Algorithm

Our algorithm handles the Poisson arrivals for
t\in[0,1]
by partitioning the arrival interval into three phases, and treating arrivals differently depending on the phase in which they arrive.
This is preceded by a ‘Phase 0’ of simulated arrivals from the advice distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, which condition the algorithm with information about the advice.
Our algorithm,
ThreePhase
, does the following:

P0.

t\in[-z,\>0)
: Simulate arrivals from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
at rate
n
.

P1.

t\in[0,\>\tau_{1})
: Do not accept.

P2.

t\in[\tau_{1},\>\tau_{2})
: Accept if greater than the
\ell
-th largest arrival in P0 and all arrivals in P1.

P3.

t\in[\tau_{2},\>1]
: Accept if greater than all arrivals in P1 and P2.

The parameters
z
,
\ell(z)
,
\tau_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}
will be specified in
Section

3.2
.
We will set
\ell(z)
,
\tau_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}
in pursuit of provable guarantees, and then take the limit as
z

3.1.2
Intuition

To understand why these phases improve upon the Pareto frontier, it is helpful to informally take a Goldilocks perspective and consider three cases: when the advice
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is too low relative to the true distribution
\mathcal{D}
, when it is too high, and when it is accurate—that is, when it is just right.

•

When
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is ‘too low’, the
\ell
-th order statistic
x_{0}^{(\ell)}
is too small to serve as a meaningful threshold for selection in Phase 2. In this case Phase 2 and Phase 3 are the same, and we are running a secretary strategy with threshold
\tau_{1}
; that is, Phase 1 is the observation phase, and Phases 2 and 3 are the selection phase.

•

If
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is ‘too high’, then
x_{0}^{(\ell)}
is too large, and the algorithm makes no selections during Phase 2. In this case, we are again running a secretary strategy, where Phases 1 and 2 are the observation phase and Phase 3 is the selection phase.

•

If
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is ‘just right’, then
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}
or at least the relevant top quantiles match up. In this case,
x_{0}^{(\ell)}
represents a good static threshold. If the maximum
x_{1}^{(1)}
of Phase 1 is smaller than it, then we implement our good threshold for Phase 2 and relax to the lower ‘best so far’ threshold for Phase 3. On the other hand if
x_{1}^{(1)}>x_{0}^{(\ell)}
we use it as our threshold for Phase 2 and Phase 3.

The first two cases provide an intuitive grounding for our secretary guarantee in
Section

Section

We establish our joint impossibility in the Poisson setting for the sake of analysis, since certain conditional expectations and event probabilities are more tractable to work with under Poisson arrivals.
However we now show that this choice is without loss of generality.
In the limit as the number of arrivals becomes large, impossibility in the Poisson arrivals setting implies impossibility in the fixed
n
setting.

By
Corollary

2.3
, our impossibility (
Theorem

4.1
) transfers to the fixed
n
i.i.d. arrival setting:

Theorem 4.2

(Joint impossibility for fixed arrivals)
.

For any (possibly adaptive) policy, and in the limit as the number of arrivals
n
becomes large, the consistency-robustness guarantees must satisfy

\displaystyle\alpha\leq\beta_{0}-\frac{1}{76}\left(\varepsilon\delta-\frac{15}{4}\varepsilon^{3}\right)^{3}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta\leq 1-(1-\varepsilon)\left(\delta^{\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}}-\delta^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\right)

for all
\varepsilon\in[0,1/8]
and
\delta>0
.

The remainder of this section is dedicated to establishing impossibility in the Poisson setting.

4.2
Impossibility for the Known i.i.d. Prophet Inequality

Before coming to the proof of the consistency-robustness tradeoff, let us first discuss the limits of policies in the known-i.i.d. setting. That is, what is possible given perfect distributional knowledge.

A well-known and easily derived fact is the following structure of optimal policies.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider any policy. Let
r(t)
be the expected reward of this policy when started at time
t
. Then for the function
r\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,

r^{\prime}(t)\geq-\int_{r(t)}^{\infty}\left(1-\mathsf{CDF}(x)\right)dx.

Moreover, for an optimal policy, this holds with equality and it accepts an arrival of value
x
at time
t
if
x>r(t)
, rejects it if
x<r(t)
, and behaves arbitrarily if
x=r(t)
.

Relying on this characterization, one can show that one cannot achieve competitive ratios better than
\beta_{0}\approx 0.745
. For our argument, we will rely on a hard instance defined by
33
in the
n
-arrival setting, which we now translate to the Poisson setting with a finite rate.

To state it, let
\beta_{n}
be the solution to

\int_{e^{-n}}^{1}((\beta_{n}^{-1}-1)-y(\log y-1))^{-1}dy=1.

(4.1)

Define
\tilde{y}
as the unique solution to the following differential equation:

\tilde{y}^{\prime}(t)=\tilde{y}(t)(\ln\tilde{y}(t)-1)-(\beta_{n}^{-1}-1)\qquad\tilde{y}(0)=1.

(4.2)

Note that
\tilde{y}
is decreasing with
\tilde{y}(0)=1
and
\tilde{y}(1)=e^{-n}
. Furthermore, define
\tilde{r}^{\ast}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\tilde{r}^{\ast}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny\mathclap{\mbox{(def)}}}}{{=}}-\int_{t}^{1}\frac{1}{\tilde{y}^{\prime}(s)}ds.

(4.3)

This function is also decreasing and it holds that
\tilde{r}^{\ast}(1)=0
. Therefore, the inverse
(\tilde{r}^{\ast})^{-1}
is a well-defined decreasing function mapping
[0,\tilde{r}^{\ast}(0)]
to
[0,1]
.

We define the distribution of arriving values to be

\mathsf{CDF}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny\mathclap{\mbox{(def)}}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}0&\text{ for $x<0$}\\
1+\frac{1}{n}\ln\tilde{y}((\tilde{r}^{\ast})^{-1}(x))&\text{ for $0\leq x\leq\tilde{r}^{\ast}(q)$}\\
1+\frac{1}{n}\ln\tilde{y}(q)&\text{ for $\tilde{r}^{\ast}(q)<x\leq H$}\\
1&\text{ for $x>H$}\end{cases}

(4.4)

where
H:=\frac{1}{\tilde{y}^{\prime}(q)\ln\tilde{y}(q)}+\tilde{r}^{\ast}(q)
.
Note that this is a feasible choice of a CDF. The function
\ln\circ\tilde{y}\circ(\tilde{r}^{\ast})^{-1}
is increasing.
Furthermore, it is bounded below by
-n
and bounded above by
0
, and so
\mathsf{CDF}(x)
is an increasing function with range
[0,1]
, regardless of
q\in[0,1]
.
By this definition, we also have

\mathsf{CDF}(\tilde{r}^{\ast}(t))=1+\frac{1}{n}\ln\tilde{y}(t).

(4.5)

Theorem 4.4
.

For the instance (
4.4
), the optimal policy has a competitive ratio of
\beta_{0}+O(e^{-n})
.

Our analysis of these instances proceeds in two parts.
We first show that the competitive ratio of the optimal policy approaches
\beta_{n}
, which is defined for a fixed rate in (
4.1
).
We then confirm that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}
and that it converges at an exponential rate, which can also be derived from
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
.
These together give a bound on the rate at which the optimal competitive ratios of these hard instances approach
\beta_{0}
.
Omitted proofs appear in
Appendix

D
.

4.3
Good Prophet Policies Must Stop on Early High Values

To show impossibilities, we will reason about the hard instances introduced in (

Lemma 4.5

Let

We first prove two very useful lemmas that hold regardless of distribution, lower-bounding the effect of being constrained not to stop early.

Lemma 4.6

4.4
Good Secretary Policies Cannot Stop on Early High Values

So far, we have shown that policies that perform well on instance (
4.4
) need to have a high probability of stopping early, conditional on there being an early high arrival. Next we turn to the setting in which our distributional knowledge was indeed incorrect, and we show that the high conditional probability of stopping can lead to a poor performance on a different distribution.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
. Consider instance (
4.4
) and any policy that has the following property: Conditioned on there being an arrival of value at least
b<\tilde{r}^{\ast}(0)
before time
\varepsilon
, it stops on instance (
4.4
) on the first such value with probability at least
1-\delta
. Then there is a different distribution such that the competitive ratio of this policy is at most

1-(1-\varepsilon)\left(\delta^{\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}}-\delta^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\right).

We first show a useful fact.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
f\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
be any function, and
g\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
a differentiable non-increasing function. If
\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\>dx\geq 1-\delta
, then

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)g(x)\>dx\geq\int_{\delta}^{1}g(x)\>dx.

Proof.

Note that since
f(x)\leq 1
for all
x
, we have
\int_{0}^{x}f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{1}f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}-\int_{x}^{1}f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\geq(1-\delta)-(1-x)=x-\delta
. So, since
g^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for all
x
, applying integration by parts twice gives us

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f(x)g(x)dx

\displaystyle=g(1)\int_{0}^{1}f(x)dx-\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(x)\int_{0}^{x}f(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}dx

\displaystyle\geq g(1)(1-\delta)-\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(x)\cdot\max(0,x-\delta)dx=\int_{\delta}^{1}g(x)dx.\qed

Proof of
Lemma

4.9
.

Consider any fixed policy, and let
f(t)
be the probability that it stops conditional on the first value above
b
arrives at time
t
. We have

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f(t)\frac{\lambda^{\prime}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}t}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}}dt\geq 1-\delta,

where
\lambda^{\prime}>0
is the arrival rate of values above
b
.
(This is because the arrival time of the first arrival of a Poisson process follows an exponential distribution, and
1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}
is the probability that there is a large arrival in
[0,\varepsilon]
.)
Substituting
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny\mathclap{\mbox{(def)}}}}{{=}}\frac{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}t}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}f\left(t(x)\right)dx\geq 1-\delta,

where
t(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny\mathclap{\mbox{(def)}}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\ln\left(1-(1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})x\right)
.

In the modified instance, values above
b
arrive with rate
\alpha\lambda^{\prime}
. The (unconditional) probability of our policy stopping before time
\varepsilon
because of a high arrival is given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f(t)\alpha\lambda^{\prime}e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}t}dt

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})\int_{0}^{1}f(t(x))e^{-(\alpha-1)\lambda^{\prime}t(x)}dx

\displaystyle\geq\alpha(1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})\int_{\delta}^{1}e^{-(\alpha-1)\lambda^{\prime}t(x)}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{t(\delta)}^{\varepsilon}\alpha\lambda^{\prime}e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}t}dt

\displaystyle=e^{-t(\delta)\alpha\lambda^{\prime}}-e^{-\varepsilon\alpha\lambda^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\delta(1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})\right)^{\alpha}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\alpha\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon},

where in the last step we used that by concavity
\delta(1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})\leq 1-e^{-\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}
.

We introduce arrivals at rate
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
whose values are much larger than
\tilde{r}^{\ast}(0)
. The above calculation gives an upper bound on the probably that such a value is accepted: Namely, we say that it is accepted if the first such value arrives before time
\varepsilon
, which happens with probability
1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}
, or if it arrives after time
\varepsilon
and the policy has not stopped before time
\varepsilon
, which happens with probability
(e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}})(1-(e^{-\alpha\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}))
. So the overall probability is upper-bounded by

1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}+(e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}})(1-(e^{-\alpha\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})).

The probability of any such high-valued arrival is
1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
. Neglecting any smaller values, this gives us an upper bound on the competitive ratio of

\frac{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}+(e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}})(1-(e^{-\alpha\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}))}{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}=1-\frac{(e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}})(e^{-\alpha\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon})}{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}.

If we choose
\alpha=\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{(1-\delta)\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}
, then this becomes

e^{-\alpha\delta\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\alpha\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon}=\delta^{\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}}-\delta^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}.

Furthermore,

\lim_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}\to 0}\frac{e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon}-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}=1-\varepsilon.

This upper-bounds the competitive ratio by

1-(1-\varepsilon)\left(\delta^{\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}}-\delta^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\right).

∎

5
Prophet Sensitivity to Distribution Error

Our investigation is framed in terms of providing simultaneous guarantees for a stopping rule with advice distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
: a guarantee when
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}
is the true distribution from which arrivals are drawn i.i.d., and a guarantee when
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\neq\mathcal{D}
is incorrect.
It is natural to ask whether this dichotomy—either
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is accurate or it is not—is the right framing by which to study the i.i.d. prophet inequality problem, or whether one should instead hope for performance which depends on how accurate
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is, and degrades from the i.i.d. prophet to the secretary setting as a function of the discrepancy between
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Here we demonstrate that the i.i.d. prophet inequality problem is very sensitive to misspecification of the arrival distribution, and that such robustness guarantees would be necessarily quite weak in this setting.
We make use of our reductions in
Section

2
together with the impossibility result of
13
for the i.i.d. prophet inequality problem with unknown distribution.
We will prove this for the Poisson setting; as usual, the reductions in
Section

2
then establish the result for both the
n
-arrival and Poisson models.
We depart from the impossibility of

.

(Theorem 2 of

For all

6
Other Models and Future Directions

The most immediate avenue of future work is to improve upon our positive and negative results and further constrain the Pareto frontier for this problem.
We conclude by acknowledging a natural extension of our results and identifying possible next directions.

Advice via Samples

We remark that the three-phase algorithm
ThreePhase
is particularly amenable to sample-based prophet inequality settings. Here instead of full knowledge of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, some number of samples are revealed to the algorithm from
\mathcal{D}
but
\mathcal{D}
remains unknown.
Since
ThreePhase
uses
z\cdot n
samples from the advice distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
,
Theorem

3.2
provides guarantees for the setting where only sample access to the advice distribution is given.
In this setting the guarantee from
Lemma

3.5
holds even if the advice is an adversarial set of values.

For the prophet inequality, one sample per distribution is sufficient to match the optimal guarantee
[
36
]
.
In the i.i.d. setting,
n-1
samples suffice for a
(1-\frac{1}{e})
-competitive stopping rule
[
13
]
, and in fact for any
\varepsilon>0
a competitive ratio of
\beta_{0}-\varepsilon
is possible using
O(n)
samples
[
36
]
.
By porting this
(\beta_{0}-\varepsilon)
-competitive sample-based algorithm to the Poisson setting, we could also interpolate between it and
ThreePhase
, and attain an additive
\varepsilon
-approximation for
\varepsilon>0
to our Pareto frontier in
Figure

3
via
O(n)
samples from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Prophet Secretary

If we leave the Poisson model and consider strictly
n
arrivals with distinct distributions, i.e. the non-i.i.d. prophet inequality, consistency-robustness questions are uninteresting for reasons outlined in
Appendix

A
when arrival order is fixed.
But if we have left the Poisson model then the i.i.d. prophet problem and the prophet secretary problem are distinct.

While our algorithmic results can be straightforwardly adapted to the
n
-arrival i.i.d. prophet problem, this seems less likely for prophet secretary: stopping rules with guarantees proven via approximate stochastic dominance do not satisfy the ‘best so far’ property
[
14
]
, and a
(1-\frac{1}{e})
-competitive fixed-threshold rule with this property
[
18
]
does not seem amenable to approximate stochastic dominance arguments.
It therefore remains an intriguing open question what consistency-robustness guarantees can be attained for prophet secretary.

Stochastic Matching

A prominent generalization of the prophet inequality is to online stochastic matching, where not one but multiple goods are allocated to an arriving sequence of buyers drawn i.i.d. (say) from a known distribution over buyer types
[
20
]
.
Here the landscape is more complicated in that the optimal known-distribution competitive ratio is not known, as well as by the multiple model choices regarding vertex vs. edge weights and whether distributions over goods/right-hand side vertices are identical or distinct
[
25
,
7
]
.
But work of
30
in the random-order model implies that a
\frac{1}{e}
-approximation is possible for the i.i.d. unknown-distribution setting, and the more recent hardness for the unknown-i.i.d. prophet inequality implies this is optimal
[
13
]
.
Nontrivial joint guarantees may be possible for i.i.d. stochastic matching as well.

Appendix A
Other Settings: Prophet, Secretary, and Poisson

In the problem of optimal stopping, an unknown sequence of values arrives one-at-a-time, and each arrival must be irrevocably chosen or rejected before the next value arrives.
The offline optimum
\mathsf{OPT}
is the maximum of the sequence.
In the worst case this problem is intractable, and
\alpha=1/n
is the best competitive ratio possible.
(This is attained by precommitting to an index
i\in[n]
u.a.r., and is best possible for the correlated instance which extends the 2-arrival hard instance
X_{1}=1
,
X_{2}=1/\varepsilon
w.p.
\varepsilon
by additional correlated
X_{i}=1/\varepsilon^{i}
w.p.
\varepsilon^{i}
, where
X_{i}>0
only when
X_{i-1}>0
.)
This motivates beyond-worst-case assumptions, among them most prominently the so-called secretary problem and the prophet inequality paradigm.

For the prophet inequality, an adversary chooses a sequence of distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots\mathcal{D}_{n}
and the algorithm faces a sequence of independent samples from these distributions in a known or unknown order.
But if the knowledge of
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots\mathcal{D}_{n}
is unreliable then this problem is intractable, because if the advice is wrong, the true
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots\mathcal{D}_{n}
may deterministically encode a hard instance.

Observation A.1
.

For the prophet inequality, all attainable consistency-robustness guarantees
(\alpha,\beta)
satisfy
\alpha\leq 1/2
and
\beta\leq O(1/n)
.

However if the arrivals are independent samples from a
shared
underlying
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\mathcal{D}
, or if the values are independent samples from distinct
\mathcal{D}_{i}
but arrive in a uniformly random order (the so-called prophet secretary problem
19
), then when the distributional advice is wrong there is still hope: one can always ignore the advice and deploy the optimal stopping rule for the secretary problem, for a robustness guarantee of
\alpha=1/e
.

We consider the Poisson setting, where values arrive according to a Poisson point process with rate
\lambda=n
in the time interval
t\in[0,1]
, and each arrival value is an independent sample from an underlying distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
In this setting, the i.i.d. prophet inequality and the prophet secretary problem coincide:
n
Poisson processes with rate
\lambda=1
and values drawn from distinct
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is equivalent to one process with rate
n
and values drawn from the average distribution.

\displaystyle\qquad+\probability\left[\mathsf{ALG}(\mathbf{X})=x^{(j)}\;\middle|\;\Lambda_{i}(j)\wedge\Xi_{i}^{2}(j)\wedge x_{i}=x^{(j)}\right]\cdot\probability\left[\Lambda_{i}(j)\wedge\Xi_{i}^{2}(j)\;\middle|\;x_{i}=x^{(j)}\right]
