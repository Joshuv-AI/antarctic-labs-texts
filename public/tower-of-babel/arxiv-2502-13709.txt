Generically τ \tau -regular irreducible components of module varieties
Grzegorz Bobiński, Jan Schröer

Abstract
. In the representation theory of finite-dimensional algebras, the study of projective presentations of maximal rank is closely related to the study of generically τ \tau -regular irreducible components of varieties of modules over such algebras. We show that a module is τ \tau -regular if and only if its minimal projective presentation is of maximal rank. This is a refinement of a theorem by Plamondon. We prove that generic extensions of generically τ \tau -regular components by simple projective modules are again generically τ \tau -regular. This leads to the classification of all generically τ \tau -regular components for triangular algebras. We also show that an algebra is hereditary if and only if all irreducible components of its varieties of modules are generically τ \tau -regular. Finally, we discuss when the set of generically τ \tau -regular components coincides with the set of generically τ − \tau^{-} -regular components.

1.1.
Generic representation theory

We outline two concepts of

generic representation theory
.

For a dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
let

\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
be the affine variety of
A
-modules
with dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
.
The corresponding product
G_{\mathbf{d}}
of general linear groups
acts on
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
by conjugation.
The orbits of this action correspond to the isomorphism classes of
A
-modules with dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
.
For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
we denote its orbit by
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
.
Let
\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
be the set of irreducible components of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
it is usually impossible to describe all modules
in
\mathcal{Z}
. But one might be able to describe (or at least say something
about) the modules in a (small enough) dense open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{Z}
.
Here one usually chooses a set of upper or lower semicontinuous maps
on
\mathcal{Z}
and takes
\mathcal{U}
as the subset where these maps assume their
generic value.

For example, if
\mathcal{Z}
contains a module
M
with
\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(M,M)=0
,
then
\mathcal{U}={\mathcal{O}}_{M}
is open and therefore
\mathcal{Z}=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{M}}
.

As another important class of examples, assume that

A
is a hereditary algebra, i.e.
\operatorname{gl.dim}(A)\leq 1
.
Then
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
is an affine space for all
\mathbf{d}
.
In particular, there is just one irreducible component, namely
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
itself.
An important result by Kac
[
K82
, Proposition 1]
implies that
there is a dense open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
which consists of
modules of the form
M_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{t}
where

\operatorname{End}_{A}(M_{i})\cong K
for all
1\leq i\leq t
.

One could call this approach
generic representation theory
,
where the term
generic
refers to (small enough) dense open subsets of
irreducible components.

There is a similar idea, but from a different angle.
Namely,
each
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
has a projective presentation

P_{1}\to P_{0}\to M\to 0.

So for each pair
(P_{1},P_{0})
of projective modules we can look
at a (small enough) dense open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})
and
then try to study the modules
\operatorname{Cok}(f)
where
f
runs through
\mathcal{U}
.
So one studies
generic
projective presentations.
We refer to ground laying work by Derksen and Fei
[
DF15
]
in this direction.

For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
let

\operatorname{mod}_{M}(A,\mathbf{d}):=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})\\
M\in\mathcal{Z}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Z},

and define

\displaystyle c(M)

\displaystyle:=\dim\operatorname{mod}_{M}(A,\mathbf{d})-\dim{\mathcal{O}}_{M},

\displaystyle E(M)

\displaystyle:=\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,\tau_{A}(M)).

Here
\tau_{A}
is the Auslander-Reiten translation for
A
.
Then
c(M)\leq E(M)
by Voigt’s Lemma and the Auslander-Reiten
formula.

We call
M

\tau
-regular
if

c(M)=E(M).

(In this case, there is only one irreducible component which contains
M
.)
A detailed motivation for this terminology can be found in
Section
4.2
.
A component
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
is
generically
\tau
-regular
if
for generic
M
in
\mathcal{Z}
we have
c(M)=E(M)
.
(By upper semicontinuity, this is the case if and only if

\mathcal{Z}
contains some
M
with
c(M)=E(M)
.)
This class of irreducible components was first introduced in
[
GLS12
]
.

For any pair
(P_{1},P_{0})
of projective
A
-modules
let

\displaystyle r(P_{1},P_{0})

\displaystyle:=\max\{\operatorname{rk}(f)\mid f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle:=\{f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})\mid\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\{M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})\mid M\cong\operatorname{Cok}(f),\;f\in\mathcal{U}\}}

where
\mathbf{d}:=\underline{\dim}(\operatorname{Cok}(f))
for
f\in\mathcal{U}
.

A beautiful result by
Plamondon
[
P13
]
says that

\mathcal{Z}

is a generically
\tau
-regular irreducible component
and that all of these components occur in this way.
Plamondon also shows that
the set
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
of generically
\tau
-regular components is (roughly speaking) parametrized by
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, where

n
is the number of simple
A
-modules, up to isomorphism.
(A precise statement is in Section
5.1
.)

Thus studying generically
\tau
-regular components corresponds to studying modules which have projective presentations of maximal rank.

For hereditary algebras
A
, the two concepts of generic representation theory outlined above coincide,
i.e.
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
is a generically
\tau
-regular component for
all
\mathbf{d}
.

Dually, one defines generically
\tau^{-}
-regular components.
We denote this set by
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

1.2.
Main results

The following theorem characterizes the modules whose
minimal
projective presentation is of maximal rank.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
let

P_{1}\xrightarrow{f}P_{0}\to M\to 0

be a projective presentation.
Then
the following hold:

(i)

If
\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
, then
M
is
\tau
-regular;

(ii)

If
M
is
\tau
-regular and the presentation above is minimal,
then
\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
.

As a consequence of
Plamondon’s Theorem
5.1
,
Theorem
1.1
(i) holds
for
each
generic

f
in
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})
,
and
Theorem
1.1
(ii) holds
for each
generic

\tau
-regular module
M
.
Theorem
1.1
can be seen as a refinement of his result.

The following is proved by combining results in
[
AR77
]
and

[
APT92
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
e
be an idempotent in
A
, and let
B:=A/AeA
.
Then for
M\in\operatorname{mod}(B)
the following are equivalent:

(i)

M
is
\tau_{A}
-regular;

(ii)

M
is
\tau_{B}
-regular.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
e
be an idempotent in
A
, and let
B:=A/AeA
.
Then

\operatorname{Irr}(B)\cap\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B).

The inclusion
\subseteq
in Corollary
1.3
follows already from

[
MP23
, Proposition 5.2]
.
The proof is very short if one uses
[
AR77
, Proposition 4.2]
.

Let
S
be a simple projective
A
-module.
For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})

let

\varepsilon_{S}^{+}(\mathcal{Z})\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d}+\underline{\dim}(S))

be the
generic extension
of
\mathcal{Z}
by
S
.
For

\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d}+\underline{\dim}(S))
, let

\varepsilon_{S}^{-}(\mathcal{Z})\subseteq\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})

be the
generic quotient
of

\mathcal{Z}
by
S
.
In general,
\varepsilon_{S}^{-}(\mathcal{Z})
is just a
closed irreducible subset of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
and not
necessarily an irreducible component.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
S
be a simple projective
A
-module.
For a dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
let
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{d}+\underline{\dim}(S)
.
Then the maps
\varepsilon_{S}^{+}
and
\varepsilon_{S}^{-}
yield
mutually inverse bijections

\textstyle{\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A,\mathbf{d})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varepsilon_{S}^{+}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A,\mathbf{d}^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces.}
\scriptstyle{\varepsilon_{S}^{-}}

As an application of Corollary
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
we get a complete classification of
generically
\tau
-regular components for triangular algebras.

We can assume without loss of generality that

A=KQ/I
where
KQ
is the path algebra of a quiver
Q
and

I
is an admissible ideal in
KQ
.
Then
A
is called
triangular
if
Q
has no oriented cycles.
One can reformulate this condition by demanding that the simple

A
-modules
S(1),\ldots,S(n)
can be labelled such that

\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(S(i),S(j))=0
for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
A
be a triangular algebra as above.
Then for each dimension vector
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
there is a unique generically
\tau
-regular
component
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{d}}
in
\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
, namely

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{d}}:=(\varepsilon_{S(1)}^{+})^{d_{1}}(\varepsilon_{S(2)}^{+})^{d_{2}}\cdots(\varepsilon_{S(n)}^{+})^{d_{n}}(0).

The
0
appearing in Theorem
1.5
denotes the trivial component which contains only the zero module.

The following theorem answers the question when all
irreducible components are generically
\tau
-regular.

Theorem 1.6
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}(A)
;

(ii)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}(A)
;

(iii)

A
is hereditary.

Rougly speaking, the Auslander-Reiten translation
\tau
yields a
bijection

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)\to\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.
(The precise statement generalizes
Adachi, Iyama and Reiten’s
[
AIR14
]

bijection between the set of (isoclasses of)
\tau
-rigid pairs and

\tau^{-}
-rigid pairs and can be found in
Section
11.1
.)

It is natural to ask when
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

The next theorem follows directly from Corollary
1.3

combined with some
homological formulas from Auslander-Reiten theory.

Theorem 1.7
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.
Then
A/AeA
is a
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra for each
idempotent
e
in
A
.

As a consequence of
Theorems
1.6
and
1.7

we immediately get the following:

Corollary 1.8
.

Assume that
\operatorname{gl.dim}(A)<\infty
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
;

(ii)

A
is hereditary.

The following two propositions give a characterization for

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
A=KQ/I
be a gentle algebra such that

has no loops.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
;

(ii)

is a gentle surface algebra.

.

Let

be a cyclic Nakayama algebra.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)

(ii)

(with

In Proposition

, and

.

The next theorem is a reformulation of

Theorem 1.11

(Derksen, Weyman, Zelevinsky)

Let

For

we have

\langle g(M),[N]\rangle=-\hom_{A}(M,N)+\hom_{A}(N,\tau_{A}(M)).

3.
Varieties of modules and semicontinuity of projective presentations

3.1.
Varieties of modules

From now on we assume
that

A=KQ/I

where
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)

is a finite quiver with vertex set
Q_{0}=\{1,\ldots,n\}

and arrow set
Q_{1}
,
and
I
is an admissible ideal in the path algebra

KQ
.
(In contradiction to our global assumption, the algebra
KQ
might be infinite-dimensional. But
KQ/I
is finite-dimensional.)
Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be the paths of length
0
in
Q
.
They form a complete set of pairwise orthogonal primitive
idempotents in
A
.
An arrow
a\in Q_{1}
starts in
s(a)
and ends in
t(a)
.

Each finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field
K

is Morita equivalent to such an algebra
KQ/I
.

For
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

let

\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d}):=\prod_{a\in Q_{1}}\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(K^{d_{s(a)}},K^{d_{t(a)}})

be the affine space of
representations
of
Q
with

dimension vector

\mathbf{d}
.
Define

G_{\mathbf{d}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{GL}_{d_{i}}(K).

For
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in G_{\mathbf{d}}
and

M=(M_{a})_{a\in Q_{1}}\in\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})
let

g.M:=(g_{t(a)}M_{a}g_{s(a)}^{-1})_{a\in Q_{1}}.

This defines a
G_{\mathbf{d}}
-action on
\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})
, and the
isomorphism classes of representations with dimension vector

\mathbf{d}
correspond to the orbits of this action.
The orbit of
M\in\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})
is denoted by
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
.

For
A=KQ/I
let

\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d}):=\{M\in\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})\mid IM=0\}.

This is a closed stable subset of
\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})
.
(For a set
X
with a group
G
acting on
X
,
a subset
U
of
X
is called
stable
if
U
consists of
a union of orbits of the
G
-action.)
We call
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
the
variety of
A
-modules
with dimension
vector
\mathbf{d}
.

Let

\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})
be the affine scheme
defined by the equations given by the condition
IM=0
for all
M\in\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})
.
Then
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
can be seen as the associated reduced scheme.

We rarely use the scheme structure of
\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})

and prefer to work with the affine variety
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
The only (but important) exception is the tangent space

T_{M}
of a module
M
at the scheme
\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
We call
M

regular
if it is regular
as a closed point in
\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})
.

One can also define varieties of modules without using quivers
and admissible ideals.
Namely, for our finite-dimensional algebra
A
, and
d\in\mathbb{N}

let
\operatorname{mod}(A,d)
be the affine variety of
K
-algebra homomorphims

A\to M_{n}(K)
.
The precise relation between the varieties
\operatorname{mod}(A,d)
and

\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
is discussed in
[
B91
]
.
The definition of
\operatorname{mod}(A,d)
is more straightforward and does not need the passage from
A
to
KQ/I
, but
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
makes it
easier to work with explicit examples.
For some fundamental results on
\operatorname{mod}(A,d)
we refer to

[
G74
]
.

3.2.
Irreducible components

For
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

let
\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
be the set of irreducible components of
the affine variety
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
Let

\operatorname{Irr}(A):=\bigcup_{\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d}).

For

\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
let

\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{Z}):=\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{d}):=\{1\leq i\leq n\mid d_{i}\not=0\}

be the
support
of
\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
\underline{\dim}(\mathcal{Z}):=\mathbf{d}
, and let
[\mathcal{Z}:S(i)]:=d_{i}
for

1\leq i\leq n
.
Let

\mathcal{Z}^{\rm int}:=\{M\in\mathcal{Z}\mid M\text{ is not contained in any other
component in $\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})$}\}

be the
interior
of
\mathcal{Z}
.

For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
recall that

\operatorname{mod}_{M}(A,\mathbf{d}):=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})\\
M\in\mathcal{Z}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Z}.

Examples
:

(i)

Let
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
with
\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(M,M)=0
.
Then
\mathcal{Z}:=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{M}}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
.

(ii)

Let
A=KQ/I
where
Q
is the quiver

\textstyle{1}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{a}
\textstyle{3\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{b}

and
I
is generated by
ab
.
Let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
.
Then

\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})=\{(M_{a},M_{b})\in\operatorname{rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})\mid M_{a}M_{b}=0\}.

The irreducible components
of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
are parametrized by
the maximal
pairs
(r_{a},r_{b})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that

r_{a}\leq d_{1}
,
r_{a}+r_{b}\leq d_{2}
and
r_{b}\leq d_{3}
.
For example for
\mathbf{d}=(4,6,5)
the maximal pairs are

(4,2)
,
(3,3)
,
(2,4)
and
(1,5)
.
For a maximal pair
(r_{a},r_{b})
the corresponding irreducible component of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})

is

\mathcal{Z}_{(r_{a},r_{b})}:=\{(M_{a},M_{b})\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})\mid\operatorname{rk}(M_{a})\leq r_{a},\;\operatorname{rk}(M_{b})\leq r_{b}\}.

3.3.
Semicontinuous maps on varieties of modules

A map
\eta\colon X\to\mathbb{Z}
on an affine variety
X
is

upper semicontinuous
(resp.
lower semicontinuous
)
if

X_{\leq i}:=\{x\in X\mid\eta(x)\leq i\}\quad(\text{resp. }X_{\geq i}:=\{x\in X\mid\eta(x)\geq i\})

is an open subset of
X
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
If
\eta\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{Z}
is upper or lower semicontinuous,
then the associated diagonal map
x\mapsto\eta(x,x)
is
also upper or lower semicontinuous, respectively.
Similarly, if
\eta\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{Z}
is upper or lower semicontinuous,
then for each
x^{\prime}\in X
and
y^{\prime}\in Y
the maps

x\mapsto\eta(x,y^{\prime})
and
y\mapsto\eta(x^{\prime},y)
are
also upper or lower semicontinuous, respectively.

If
X
is irreducible and
\eta\colon X\to\mathbb{Z}
is an upper
semicontinuous map whose image has a minimum, then

\{x\in X\mid\eta(x)\text{ is minimal}\}

is a dense open subset of
X
.
We call its elements
generic
(with respect to

\eta
) in
X
, and the minimal value is the

generic value
of
\eta
on
X
.
Analogously, for lower semicontinuous maps
one needs to consider the maximal value.

In our context we study semicontinuous maps for
X=\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})

or
X=\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})\times\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d}^{\prime})
,
and we often restrict them to some

\mathcal{Z}
or
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
.

The maps

\displaystyle(M,N)

\displaystyle\mapsto\hom_{A}(M,N),

\displaystyle(M,N)

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{i}(M,N),

\displaystyle(M,N)

\displaystyle\mapsto\hom_{A}(M,\tau_{A}(N)),

\displaystyle(M,N)

\displaystyle\mapsto\hom_{A}(\tau_{A}^{-}(M),N),

are all upper semicontinuous on

\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})\times\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d}^{\prime})
,
and the maps

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{proj.dim}(M),

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{i}(M):=-\hom_{A}(M,S(i))+\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M,S(i)),

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{inj.dim}(M)

are upper semicontinuous on

\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
The map

f\mapsto\operatorname{rk}(f)
is
lower semicontinuous on
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
for all

M,N\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
.
The map
M\mapsto\dim{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
is lower semicontinuous
on
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
Note that

\dim{\mathcal{O}}_{M}=\dim G_{\mathbf{d}}-\hom_{A}(M,M).

We refer to
[
GLFS23
]
for more details on some of the maps above.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
let

\operatorname{proj.dim}(\mathcal{Z}):=\min\{\operatorname{proj.dim}(M)\mid M\in\mathcal{Z}\}.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
and
1\leq i\leq n
let

g_{i}(\mathcal{Z}):=\min\{g_{i}(M)\mid M\in\mathcal{Z}\}

be the generic value of
g_{i}(-)
on
\mathcal{Z}
.
Then

g(\mathcal{Z}):=(g_{1}(\mathcal{Z}),\ldots,g_{n}(\mathcal{Z}))

is the
g
-vector
of
\mathcal{Z}
.

3.4.
Dimension of fibres

We need the following standard result from basic algebraic geometry.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a dominant morphism of
irreducible quasi-projective varieties.
Then for each
y\in Y
we have

\dim f^{-1}(y)\geq\dim(X)-\dim(Y).

Furthermore, there is a dense open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
Y
such that

\dim f^{-1}(u)=\dim(X)-\dim(Y)

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

3.5.
Upper semicontinuity of projective presentations

The following lemma and its proof are due to
Fei
[
F23b
]
.
We use it frequently.

Lemma 3.2

(Fei)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be a closed irreducible subset of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
Let
M\in\mathcal{Z}
, and let

P_{1}\to P_{0}\to M\to 0

be
a projective presentation of
M
.
Then there is a dense open subset
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{Z}
such that

M\in\mathcal{U}
and
each
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
has a projective presentation of the form

P_{1}\to P_{0}\to M^{\prime}\to 0.

Proof.

Let us first
assume that the presentation
P_{1}\to P_{0}\to M\to 0

is minimal.
The maps
\hom_{A}(-,S(j))
and
\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(-,S(j))
are upper
semicontinuous, and the map

\hom_{A}(-,S(j))-\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(-,S(j))
is lower semicontinuous.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be the set of all
N\in\mathcal{Z}
such that

\displaystyle a_{j}^{\prime}:=\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(N,S(j))

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M,S(j))=:a_{j},

\displaystyle b_{j}^{\prime}:=\hom_{A}(N,S(j))

\displaystyle\leq\hom_{A}(M,S(j))=:b_{j},

\displaystyle\hom_{A}(N,S(j))-\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(N,S(j))

\displaystyle\geq\hom_{A}(M,S(j))-\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M,S(j))

for all
1\leq j\leq n
.
Thus
\mathcal{U}
is the intersection of three non-empty open subsets of
the closed irreducible subset
\mathcal{Z}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{U}
is open and dense in
\mathcal{Z}
.

For
N\in\mathcal{U}
let

P_{1}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{g_{1}}P_{0}^{\prime}\to N\to 0
be a minimal projective presentation.
Thus we have

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}P(j)^{a_{j}},

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}P(j)^{b_{j}},

\displaystyle P_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}P(j)^{a_{j}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle P_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}P(j)^{b_{j}^{\prime}}.

For
1\leq j\leq n
we have
a_{j}^{\prime}\leq a_{j}
,
b_{j}^{\prime}\leq b_{j}
and

b_{j}^{\prime}-a_{j}^{\prime}\geq b_{j}-a_{j}
.
The last inequality is equivalent to
a_{j}-a_{j}^{\prime}\geq b_{j}-b_{j}^{\prime}
.
Thus for

P_{1}^{\prime\prime}:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}P(j)^{a_{j}-a_{j}^{\prime}}\text{\quad and \quad}P_{0}^{\prime\prime}:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}P(j)^{b_{j}-b_{j}^{\prime}}

there is an epimorphism
g_{2}\colon P_{1}^{\prime\prime}\to P_{0}^{\prime\prime}
.
Observe that
P_{1}=P_{1}^{\prime}\oplus P_{1}^{\prime\prime}
and
P_{0}=P_{0}^{\prime}\oplus P_{0}^{\prime\prime}
.
We get a projective presentation

\textstyle{P_{1}^{\prime}\oplus P_{1}^{\prime\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g_{1}&0\\
0&g_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)}
\textstyle{P_{0}^{\prime}\oplus P_{0}^{\prime\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

Finally,
each non-minimal projective presentation
P_{1}\to P_{0}\to M\to 0

has the minimal presentation of
M
as a direct summand, see
Lemma
2.5
.
Now one argues as before and gets the result also in this case.
∎

The next lemma is well known.
It
follows from the upper semicontinuity of
the maps
\hom_{A}(-,S(i))
and
\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(-,S(i))
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
there is a unique (up to isomorphism) pair

(P_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}},P_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}})

of projective
A
-modules such that for a dense open subset

\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{Z}
each
M\in\mathcal{U}
has a minimal projective presentation of the form

P_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}}\to P_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}}\to M\to 0.

Note that it can happen that for different components
\mathcal{Z}_{1}

and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
in
\operatorname{Irr}(A)
we have

(P_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}_{1}},P_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}_{1}})=(P_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}_{2}},P_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}_{2}})
.

Here is the corresponding dual statement:

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
there is a unique (up to isomorphism) pair

(I_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}},I_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}})

of injective
A
-modules such that for a dense open subset

\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{Z}
each
M\in\mathcal{U}
has a minimal injective presentation of the form

0\to M\to I_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}}\to I_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}}.

3.6.
Maximal rank and dimension vectors

Also the following lemma is well known.
For convenience we include a proof.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
M,N\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
let
f,g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
with

\operatorname{rk}(f)=\operatorname{rk}(g)=r(M,N).

Then
\underline{\dim}(\operatorname{Im}(f))=\underline{\dim}(\operatorname{Im}(g))
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
.
For
1\leq i\leq n
we have
f(e_{i}M)\subseteq e_{i}N
.
Let
f_{i}\colon e_{i}M\to e_{i}N
denote the restriction map.
Thus we can write

f=\left(\begin{matrix}f_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&f_{n}\end{matrix}\right)\colon\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}M\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}N.

We have
\operatorname{rk}(f_{i})=[\operatorname{Im}(f):S(i)]
and

\operatorname{rk}(f)=\operatorname{rk}(f_{1})+\cdots+\operatorname{rk}(f_{n})
.
In particular,
\underline{\dim}(\operatorname{Im}(f))=(\operatorname{rk}(f_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{rk}(f_{n}))
.

Let

r_{i}:=\max\{\operatorname{rk}(f_{i})\mid f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)\}.

Thus

\operatorname{rk}(f)\leq r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}
for all
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
.
By lower semicontinuity,

\mathcal{U}_{i}:=\{f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)\mid\operatorname{rk}(f_{i})=r_{i}\}

is a dense open subset of
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
.
The intersection
\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\mathcal{U}_{n}
is
again dense open in
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
.
It follows that
r(M,N)=r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}
.
This yields the claim of the lemma.
∎

3.7.
Projective presentations of maximal rank

Lemma 3.6
.

For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
let

P_{1}\xrightarrow{f}P_{0}\to M\to 0

be a projective presentation.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
;

(ii)

\operatorname{rk}(f_{M})=r(P_{1}^{M},P_{0}^{M})
and

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{f},M)=0
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.5
we can assume that

(P_{1}\xrightarrow{f}P_{0})=(P_{1}^{M}\xrightarrow{f_{M}}P_{0}^{M})\oplus(P\xrightarrow{1_{P}}P)\oplus(P_{f}\to 0).

(i)
\implies
(ii):
Let
\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
.
Assume there is some
g^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1}^{M},P_{0}^{M})

with
\operatorname{rk}(g^{\prime})>\operatorname{rk}(f_{M})
.
Let

g:=\left(\begin{matrix}g^{\prime}&0&0\\
0&1_{P}&0\end{matrix}\right)\colon P_{1}=P_{1}^{M}\oplus P\oplus P_{f}\to P_{0}^{M}\oplus P=P_{0},

i.e.
g
is the direct sum

(P_{1}^{M}\xrightarrow{g^{\prime}}P_{0}^{M})\oplus(P\xrightarrow{1_{P}}P)\oplus(P_{f}\to 0).

Then
\operatorname{rk}(g)>\operatorname{rk}(f)
, a contradiction.

Next, let
h^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{f},M)
be non-zero.
Let
\pi\colon P_{0}^{M}\to M
be the epimorphism with kernel

\operatorname{Im}(f_{M})
.
By the projectivity of
P_{f}
there exists some

h\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{f},P_{0}^{M})
such that
h^{\prime}=\pi h
.
Define

g:=\left(\begin{matrix}f_{M}&0&h\\
0&1_{P}&0\end{matrix}\right)\colon P_{1}^{M}\oplus P\oplus P_{f}\to P_{0}^{M}\oplus P.

Then one easily checks that
\operatorname{Im}(f)
is a proper submodule of
\operatorname{Im}(g)
.
Therefore we have
\operatorname{rk}(f)<\operatorname{rk}(g)
, again a contradiction.

(ii)
\implies
(i):
Assume that
\operatorname{rk}(f_{M})=r(P_{1}^{M},P_{0}^{M})
and

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{f},M)=0
.
Since
P_{f}
is a projective module, the latter condition means that

\operatorname{Im}(g^{\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(f^{\prime})
for all
g^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{f},P_{0}^{M})
and

f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1}^{M},P_{0}^{M})^{\circ}
.
(Here we use that
\underline{\dim}(\operatorname{Cok}(f^{\prime}))=\underline{\dim}(M)
by Lemma
3.5
.)

It follows from
[
DF15
, Corollary 4.2]
and the lower semicontinuity of the rank that there exists

g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})^{\circ}
such that

(P_{1}\xrightarrow{g}P_{0})=(P_{1}^{M}\oplus P_{f}\xrightarrow{(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})}P_{0}^{M})\oplus(P\xrightarrow{1_{P}}P)

with
f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1}^{M},P_{0}^{M})^{\circ}
.
Then
\operatorname{Im}(g^{\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(f^{\prime})
, and therefore

\operatorname{rk}(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})=\operatorname{rk}(f^{\prime})=\operatorname{rk}(f_{M})
.
Thus

r(P_{1},P_{0})=\operatorname{rk}(g)=\operatorname{rk}(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})+\dim(P)=\operatorname{rk}(f_{M})+\dim(P)=\operatorname{rk}(f).

∎

3.8.
Generic extensions of components

For
i=1,2
let
\mathcal{Z}_{i}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d}_{i})
, and let

\mathbf{d}:=\mathbf{d}_{1}+\mathbf{d}_{2}
.
Let

\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}):=\min\{\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M_{1},M_{2})\mid(M_{1},M_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\mathcal{Z}_{2}\}.

Let

\mathcal{U}:=\{(M_{1},M_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\mathcal{Z}_{2}\mid\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M_{i},M_{j})=\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{j})\text{ for }\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}\}.

By upper semicontinuity,
\mathcal{U}
is a dense open subset of

\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.
Let

{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{U})
be the set of all
E\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
such that there exists
a short exact sequence

0\to M_{2}\to E\to M_{1}\to 0

where
(M_{1},M_{2})\in\mathcal{U}
.
Let

{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}):=\overline{{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{U})}

be the

generic extension
of
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
by
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.
This is a closed irreducible subset of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
However, in general
{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2})
is not an irreducible
component.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
CBS02
, Corollary 1.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
.
If
\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{2},\mathcal{Z}_{1})=0
, then

{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2})\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
.

For example, if
\mathcal{Z}_{2}=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P}}
with
P\in\operatorname{proj}(A)
,
then
the assumption of Theorem
3.7
is satisfied and we get

{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2})\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
.

4.
Generically
\tau
-regular
components

4.1.
Generically reduced components

For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
let
T_{M}^{\rm red}
be the tangent space of
M
at the affine variety
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
, and let

T_{M}
be the tangent space of
M
at the affine scheme

\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
Recall that one treats
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
as the reduced scheme associated with
\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
The module

as a closed point of

(In general, we have

Let

be the tangent space of

at the orbit

.
We have

\dim T_{M}({\mathcal{O}}_{M})=\dim{\mathcal{O}}_{M}.

The reduced tangent space

is usually quite inaccessible.
But
T_{M}
can often be computed by the following classical result.

Proposition 4.1

(Voigt’s Lemma
[
G74
, Section 1.1]
)
.

There is an isomorphism of vector spaces

We call
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})

reduced
if
\dim T_{M}={\rm dim}\,T_{M}^{\rm red}
.

An irreducible components
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
is
generically
reduced
if there exists a dense open subset of
\mathcal{Z}
which
consists only of reduced modules.

4.2.
Definition of generically
\tau
-regular components

For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
define

\displaystyle c(M)

\displaystyle:=c_{A}(M):=\dim\operatorname{mod}_{M}(A,\mathbf{d})-\dim{\mathcal{O}}_{M},

\displaystyle e(M)

\displaystyle:=e_{A}(M):=\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M,M),

\displaystyle E(M)

\displaystyle:=E_{A}(M):=\hom_{A}(M,\tau_{A}(M)).

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
let

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle:=c_{A}(\mathcal{Z}):=\min\{c(M)\mid M\in\mathcal{Z}\},

\displaystyle e(\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle:=e_{A}(\mathcal{Z}):=\min\{e(M)\mid M\in\mathcal{Z}\},

\displaystyle E(\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle:=E_{A}(\mathcal{Z}):=\min\{E(M)\mid M\in\mathcal{Z}\}.

In the same fashion, one defines
c(\mathcal{Z})
,
e(\mathcal{Z})
and

E(\mathcal{Z})
for an arbitrary closed irreducible subset of

.
Using Voigt’s Lemma
4.1
and

one gets

c(M)\leq e(M)\leq E(M)\text{\quad and \quad}c(\mathcal{Z})\leq e(\mathcal{Z})\leq E(\mathcal{Z}).

Lemma 4.2
.

For

the following are equivalent:

(i)

;

(ii)

is a regular point of the affine scheme

\operatorname{Spec}(A,\mathbf{d})
.

In this case,

is contained in exactly one irreducible component
of
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.

Proof.

This follows from Voigt’s Lemma
4.1
and from the fact that
points in the intersection of different irreducible components
are singular (= non-regular).
∎

A module
M
is called
regular
if
c(M)=e(M)
.
A component
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
is
generically regular
if
there exists a dense open subset of
\mathcal{Z}
which consists
only of regular modules.
This is the case if and only if

c(\mathcal{Z})=e(\mathcal{Z})
.

The following lemma is folklore.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{Z}
is generically regular;

(ii)

\mathcal{Z}
is generically reduced.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii):
For
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})

we have

(The equality follows from Voigt’s Lemma

.)
Thus
c(M)=e(M)
implies
\dim T_{M}^{\rm red}=\dim T_{M}
.

(ii)
\implies

.
∎

A module

\tau

5.
Plamondon’s construction of generically

5.1.
Plamondon’s theorems

We recall Plamondon’s construction and parametrization
of generically
\tau
-regular components.

Plamondon’s
[
P13
]
original proof is quite convoluted.
A more straightforward proof is due to Fei
[
F23
]
.
It is based on a bundle construction from Derksen and Fei
[
DF15
]
, see Section
5.2
.

For a given pair
(P_{1},P_{0})
of projective
A
-modules
recall that

r(P_{1},P_{0}):=\max\{\operatorname{rk}(f)\mid f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})\}

and

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})^{\circ}:=\{f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})\mid\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})\}.

By lower semicontinuity,
this is a dense open subset of the affine space
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})
.
Let

\mathbf{d}_{P_{1},P_{0}}^{\circ}:=\underline{\dim}(\operatorname{Cok}(f))

where
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})^{\circ}
.
(The cokernels of all such
f
have the same dimension vector, see Lemma
3.5
.)
Define

\mathcal{Z}_{P_{1},P_{0}}^{\circ}:=\{M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d}_{P_{1},P_{0}}^{\circ})\mid M\cong\operatorname{Cok}(f),\;f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})^{\circ}\},

and let

\mathcal{Z}_{P_{1},P_{0}}:=\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{P_{1},P_{0}}^{\circ}}.

Theorem 5.1

(Plamondon
[
P13
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

We have

\mathcal{Z}_{P_{1},P_{0}}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
, and each
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)

is
of the form
\mathcal{Z}_{P_{1},P_{0}}
for some pair
(P_{1},P_{0})
of
projective
A
-modules.

Plamondon also explains when different pairs of projectives give rise
to the same component.
We reformulate this in terms of
g
-vectors.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)

let

g^{\circ}(\mathcal{Z}):=g(\mathcal{Z})+\operatorname{null}(\mathcal{Z})

where

\operatorname{null}(\mathcal{Z}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq n\\
i\notin\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{Z})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{N}e_{i}.

Theorem 5.2

(Plamondon
[
P13
]
)
.

We have

\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)}g^{\circ}(\mathcal{Z})

and this union is disjoint.
In particular, each
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
is determined by

g(\mathcal{Z})
.

Theorem
5.2
is not stated
explicitely in
[
P13
]
, but it can be extracted from
[
P13
, Theorem 1.2]
.

A pair
(\mathcal{Z},P)
with
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
and
P\in\operatorname{proj}(A)
with

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P,M)=0
for all
M\in\mathcal{Z}
is a
\tau
-regular pair

for
A
.
(We consider
P
up to isomorphism.)

Note that
for a fixed
\mathcal{Z}
, the
\tau
-regular pairs
(\mathcal{Z},P)
are parametrized
by the elements in
g^{\circ}(\mathcal{Z})
.

Here is a reformulation of Theorem
5.2
in
terms of
\tau
-regular pairs:
There is a bijection between the set of
\tau
-regular pairs
and
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which sends such a pair
(\mathcal{Z},P)
to
g(\mathcal{Z})+[P]
.

There is an obvious dual notion of
\tau^{-}
-regular pairs
, and
of course all statements can be dualized.

We use the simpler approach from

Theorem 6.1

Then
the following hold:

(i)

\tau
-regular;

(ii)

is
\tau
-regular and if (

then
\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{d}:=\underline{\dim}(M)
.

(i):
Let
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{Z}_{P_{1},P_{0}}
.
We assume that
\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
,
i.e.
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}_{P_{1},P_{0}}^{\circ}

Thus
{\mathcal{C}}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0},\mathbf{d})=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{1},P_{0})^{\circ}
is irreducible,

\mathcal{Z}=\eta_{\mathbf{d}}({\mathcal{C}})
,
and Lemma

implies that

Since
\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})

3.6

says that
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{f},M)=0
.
By Lemma
2.6

there is an exact sequence

The alternating sum of the dimensions of these
four spaces is zero.
We have

c(M)=\dim(\mathcal{Z})-\dim{\mathcal{O}}_{M}=\dim(\mathcal{Z})-\dim G_{\mathbf{d}}+\hom_{A}(M,M).

Recall that
c(M)\leq E(M)=\hom_{A}(M,\tau_{A}(M))
.

Now an easy calculation yields that

c(M)=E(M)
if and only if

\dim(\mathcal{Z})-\dim G_{\mathbf{d}}=-\hom_{A}(P_{0},M)+\hom_{A}(P_{1},M).

Note that
these numbers only depend on
\underline{\dim}(M)

Since
\mathcal{Z}
is generically
\tau
-regular, there exists some

N\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
c(N)=E(N)
and
N
has
a projective presentation

(For the latter we used Fei’s Lemma
3.2

Again by Lemma
2.6

we get an exact sequence

\to\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(N,\tau_{A}(N))\oplus\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{g},N)\to 0.

Of course, we have
\underline{\dim}(M)=\underline{\dim}(N)
and therefore

\operatorname{rk}(g)=r(P_{1},P_{0})
.
This implies
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{g},N)=0
, see again Lemma
3.6
.
Now
c(N)=E(N)
together with the considerations above implies that

.

.
It is enough to show that

Fix

\operatorname{rk}(f)=r(P_{1},P_{0})

\operatorname{rk}(g)=1

We obtain a commutative diagram

\textstyle{S\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g}
\textstyle{P_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\scriptstyle{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\

\textstyle{P_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\scriptstyle{1}

Now

Finally, since

We call the component

of

by

.

Extensions and quotients by simple projectives

Theorem 8.3
.

\tau

9.1.
Description of
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
for triangular algebras

Let

be a triangular algebra,
i.e. we assume that the simple

A
-modules

are labelled such that

\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(S(i),S(j))=0
for all

Theorem 9.1
.

Let

be a triangular algebra as above.
Then for each dimension vector
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
there is a unique generically

\tau
-regular
component
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{d}}
in
\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{d}}:=(\varepsilon_{S(1)}^{+})^{d_{1}}(\varepsilon_{S(2)}^{+})^{d_{2}}\cdots(\varepsilon_{S(n)}^{+})^{d_{n}}(0).

Proof.

For
1\leq k\leq n
let

e(k):=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}e_{i},

and set
B(k):=A/Ae(k)A
.
Note that
e(1)=0
and therefore
B(1)=A
,
and that
B(n)\cong K
.

The module
S(k)
is a simple projective
B(k)
-module.

First, we show that
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A,\mathbf{d})\not=\varnothing
.

Let
\mathbf{d}(k):=(0,\ldots,0,d_{k},\ldots,d_{n})
.

We obviously have

\mathcal{Z}(n):=(\varepsilon_{S(n)}^{+})^{d_{n}}(0)=S(n)^{d_{n}}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B(n),\mathbf{d}(n)).

(Note that
\operatorname{mod}(B(n),\mathbf{d}(n))
is just a single point,
namely the projective
B(n)
-module
S(n)^{d_{n}}
.)

Let
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
We have

B(k+1)=B(k)/B(k)e_{k}B(k)
.

By decreasing induction on
k
we can assume that

\mathcal{Z}(k+1):=(\varepsilon_{S(k+1)}^{+})^{d_{k+1}}\cdots(\varepsilon_{S(n)}^{+})^{d_{n}}(0)\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B(k+1),\mathbf{d}(k+1)).

Corollary
7.3
implies

\mathcal{Z}(k+1)\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B(k),\mathbf{d}(k+1))
.

Now let

\mathcal{Z}(k):=(\varepsilon_{S(k)}^{+})^{d_{k}}(\mathcal{Z}(k+1)).

Then
Proposition
8.1
tells us that

\mathcal{Z}(k)\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B(k),\mathbf{d}(k))
.

In particular, we get

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{d}}=\mathcal{Z}(1)\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A,\mathbf{d})
.

Next, we show uniqueness.
Let
\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
We can assume
\mathbf{d}\not=0
.
Let
k:=\min\{1\leq i\leq n\mid d_{i}\not=0\}
.

By Corollary
7.3
we get

\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B(k),\mathbf{d})
.
Recall that
S(k)
is a simple projective
B(k)
-module.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime}:=\varepsilon_{S(k)}^{-}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})
and

\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{\prime}:=\varepsilon_{S(k)}^{-}(\mathcal{Z}_{2})
be the generic quotients of
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
by
S(k)
, respectively.
By Proposition
8.2
we get

\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B(k),\mathbf{d}-\underline{\dim}(S(k)))
.
By induction on

we have

\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{\prime}
.
If
\mathcal{Z}:=\varepsilon_{S(k)}^{+}(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime})
is the generic extension of
\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{\prime}
by

, then Theorem
8.3

implies
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.
∎

10.
When all or very few components are generically
\tau
-regular

10.1.
Algebras where all components are generically

\tau
-regular

The idea of the following construction is this:
For an arbritrary
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
we pick a suitable
projective module.
Taking the generic extension of
\mathcal{Z}
by this projective module yields a
new irreducible component
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
whose
g
-vector has no positive entries.

Let
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d})
, and let

P_{1}\xrightarrow{f}P_{0}\xrightarrow{g}M\to 0

be a minimal projective presentation where
M
is generic in
\mathcal{Z}
.
Thus we have
(P_{1},P_{0})=(P_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}},P_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}})
and

g(\mathcal{Z})=[P_{1}]-[P_{0}]
.
The sequence

P_{1}\xrightarrow{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}f\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)}P_{0}\oplus P_{1}\xrightarrow{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)}M\oplus P_{1}\to 0

is then obviously a (minimal) projective presentation of

M\oplus P_{1}
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{2}:=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{1}}}
, and let

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}:={\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Z}_{2})
be the generic extension of

\mathcal{Z}
by
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
, as defined in Section
3.8
.
Since
\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{2},\mathcal{Z})=0
, Theorem
3.7

says that

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A,\mathbf{d}^{\prime})
where

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{d}+\underline{\dim}(P_{1})
.

Since
M\oplus P_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, we get

g(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})=[P_{1}]-[P_{0}\oplus P_{1}]-[P]=-[P_{0}]-[P]

for some direct summand
P
of
P_{1}
.
(Here we used Lemmas
2.5
and
3.2
.)

Lemma 10.1
.

With
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
as above, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{proj.dim}(\mathcal{Z})\leq 1
;

(ii)

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{0}}}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii):
If
\operatorname{proj.dim}(\mathcal{Z})\leq 1
, then
there is an exact sequence

0\to P_{1}\to P_{0}\to M\to 0

for some
M\in\mathcal{Z}
with
\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(M,\mathcal{Z}_{2})=\operatorname{ext}_{A}^{1}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Z}_{2})
.
It follows that
P_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
and therefore

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{0}}}.

(ii)
\implies
(iii): Trivial.

(iii)
\implies
(i):
If
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
, then

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P_{0}\oplus P}},

since
g(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})=-[P_{0}]-[P]
and since generically
\tau
-regular components are determined by their
g
-vectors, see Theorem
5.2
.
Furthermore, there is a short exact sequence

0\to P_{1}\to P_{0}\oplus P\to M\to 0

for some
M\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Thus
\operatorname{proj.dim}(M)\leq 1
and therefore
\operatorname{proj.dim}(\mathcal{Z})\leq 1
.
∎

Note that the module
P
occuring in the proof of
Lemma
10.1
turns out to be
0
.

Theorem 10.2
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}(A)
;

(ii)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}(A)
;

(iii)

A
is hereditary.

Proof.

(iii)
\implies
(i),(ii):
If
A
is hereditary, then
\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
is an affine space for all

\mathbf{d}
.
Thus
\dim\operatorname{mod}_{M}(A,\mathbf{d})=\dim(T_{M})
for all

M\in\operatorname{mod}(A,\mathbf{d})
.
Now Voigt’s Lemma
4.1
,
Theorem
2.1
and
Lemma
2.2

imply (i).
Using the dual of Lemma
2.2
, one shows that (ii) also holds.

(i)
\implies
(iii):
Let
1\leq i\leq n
, and let

\mathcal{Z}:={\mathcal{O}}_{S(i)}
.
This is an irreducible component consisting of a single point,
namely
S(i)
.
Let
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
be the associated component appearing in Lemma
10.1
.
By (i) the component
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
must be generically
\tau
-regular.
Now Lemma
10.1
says that
\operatorname{proj.dim}(\mathcal{Z})\leq 1
.
Thus
\operatorname{proj.dim}(S(i))\leq 1
for all

.
This implies that
\operatorname{gl.dim}(A)\leq 1
, i.e. (iii) holds.

(ii)
\implies
(iii):
This is done similarly by using the dual of
Lemma
10.1
.
∎

10.2.
Algebras where very few components are generically

\tau
-regular

We have seen that hereditary algebras are the only algebras
where
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
is as big as possible, namely

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}(A)
.
The next result says that local algebras are the only algebras where

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
is as small as possible.

Proposition 10.3
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

A\cong A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{t}
where all

A_{i}
are local algebras;

(ii)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\{\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P}}\mid P\in\operatorname{proj}(A)\}
;

(iii)

\operatorname{proj}(A)=\text{$\tau$-$\operatorname{rigid}(A)$}
;

(iv)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)=\{\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{I}}\mid I\in\operatorname{inj}(A)\}
;

(v)

\operatorname{inj}(A)=\text{$\tau^{-}$-$\operatorname{rigid}(A)$}
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
A
is connected.
In particular,
in (i) we assume that
t=1
.

We clearly have
\operatorname{proj}(A)\subseteq\text{$\tau$-$\operatorname{rigid}(A)$}

and

\operatorname{inj}(A)\subseteq\text{$\tau^{-}$-$\operatorname{rigid}(A)$}
.

(i)
\implies
(ii):
If
A
is local, then there exists just one indecomposable projective

P(1)
, up to isomorphism.
We have

g(P(1)^{m})=-m
for
m\geq 0
.
Let
\mathcal{Z}_{m}:=\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{P(1)^{m}}}
.
Obviously,
\mathcal{Z}_{m}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
.
We get

g^{\circ}(\mathcal{Z}_{m})=\begin{cases}-m&\text{if $m\geq 1$},\\
\mathbb{N}&\text{if $m=0$}.\end{cases}

Thus

\mathbb{Z}=\bigcup_{m\geq 0}g^{\circ}(\mathcal{Z}_{m}).

Now Theorem
5.2
implies (ii).

(ii)
\implies
(iii):
This is clear, since for each
\tau
-rigid module
M
we have

\overline{{\mathcal{O}}_{M}}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
.

(iii)
\implies
(i):
Assume that
A
is not local.
Since
A
is connected, there exists some
P(i)
and some

j\not=i
such that
[P(i):S(j)]\not=0
.
Let
E(i)
be the largest factor module of
P(i)
such that

[E(i):S(k)]=0
for all
k\not=i
.
The module
E(i)
is non-zero and non-projective.
We get a minimal projective presentation

P_{1}\to P(i)\to E(i)\to 0

such that
P_{1}
does not have a direct summand isomorphic to

P(i)
.
This yields a minimal injective presentation

0\to\tau_{A}(E(i))\to\nu_{A}(P_{1})\to\nu_{A}(P(i)).

In particular, we have

\operatorname{soc}(\tau_{A}(E(i)))\cong\operatorname{soc}(\nu_{A}(P_{1}))\cong\operatorname{top}(P_{1}).

Thus
\tau_{A}(E(i))
does
not have a submodule isomorphic to
S(i)
.
In other words,
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(E(i),\tau_{A}(E(i))=0
.
So
E(i)
is a non-projective
\tau
-rigid module.

The equivalence of (i), (iv) and (v) is proved dually.
∎

11.1.
Correspondence of
\tau
-regular pairs and

\tau^{-}
-regular pairs

Recall from
[

that a pair
(M,P)
of

is a

\tau
-rigid pair
if
M
is
\tau
-rigid and

is projective
with
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P,M)=0

Dually, one defines
\tau^{-}
-rigid pairs
.
We consider such pairs up to isomorphism.

Let
\tau
-rigid-pairs
(A)
(resp.
\tau^{-}
-rigid-pairs
(A)
) be the
set (of isomorphism classes) of
\tau
-rigid pairs (resp.
\tau^{-}
-rigid pairs) of
A
-modules.

For

let

be a maximal projective direct summand
of

Applying the duality
D
to the first bijection in
[
AIR14

one gets the following theorem:

Theorem 11.1

(Adachi, Iyama, Reiten)
.

The map

(M,P)\mapsto(\tau(M)\oplus\nu_{A}(P),\nu_{A}(P_{M}))

defines a bijection

\tau\text{\rm-rigid-pairs}(A)\to\tau^{-}\text{\rm-rigid-pairs}(A).

Recall that for
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)
and

P\in\operatorname{proj}(A)
we call
(\mathcal{Z},P)
a
\tau
-regular pair

provided
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P,M)=0
for all
M\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Dually, one defines
\tau^{-}
-regular pairs
.

Let
\tau
-reg-pairs
(A)
(resp.
\tau^{-}
-reg-pairs
(A)
) be the
set (of isomorphism classes) of
\tau
-regular pairs (resp.
\tau^{-}
-regular pairs) for
A
.

For
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)

let
\tau(\mathcal{Z})
be the generically
\tau^{-}
-regular component

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
such that

(I_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}},I_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}})=(\nu_{A}(P_{1}^{\mathcal{Z}}),\nu_{A}(P_{0}^{\mathcal{Z}})).

(Here we are using the notation from Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.)
Dually, one defines
\tau^{-}(\mathcal{Z})
for
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Let
P_{\mathcal{Z}}
(resp.
I_{\mathcal{Z}}
) be a maximal projective (resp. injective) direct summand of
some generic
M\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Proposition 11.2
.

The map

\displaystyle\sigma\colon\tau\text{\rm-reg-pairs}(A)

\displaystyle\to\tau^{-}\text{\rm-reg-pairs}(A)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Z},P)

\displaystyle\mapsto(\overline{\tau(\mathcal{Z})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}_{\nu_{A}(P)}},\nu_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}}))

is a bijection.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{Z},P)\in\tau\text{\rm-reg-pairs}(A)
.
First, we show that

(\overline{\tau(\mathcal{Z})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}_{\nu_{A}(P)}},\nu_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}}))

is a
\tau^{-}
-regular pair for
A
.

By construction, we have
\tau(\mathcal{Z})\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Each generic
M\in\mathcal{Z}
is of the form

M_{\rm np}\oplus P_{\mathcal{Z}}
for some
M_{\rm np}
which does
not have a non-zero projective direct summand.
By
[
CLS15
, Theorem 1.3]
we have

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}},\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np}))=0
.
By construction,
a generic module in
\tau(\mathcal{Z})
is of the form

\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np})
.
(This was already observed in
[
F23
, Theorem 3.11(2)]
.)

We have

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\tau_{A}^{-}(\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np})),\nu_{A}(P))\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M_{\rm np},\nu_{A}(P))\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P,M_{\rm np})=0

and

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\tau_{A}^{-}(\nu_{A}(P)),\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np}))=0.

Now the dual of
[
CLS15
, Theorem 1.3]
says that

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}:=\overline{\tau(\mathcal{Z})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}_{\nu_{A}(P)}}\in\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A).

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np})\oplus\nu_{A}(P),\nu_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}}))

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}},\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np})\oplus\nu_{A}(P))

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}},\tau_{A}(M_{\rm np}))\oplus\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P,P_{\mathcal{Z}})=0.

Thus
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M^{\prime},\nu_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}}))=0
for all
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
.
It follows that

(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},\nu_{A}(P_{\mathcal{Z}}))
is
a
\tau^{-}
-regular pair for
A
.

For a
\tau^{-}
-regular pair
(\mathcal{Z},I)
define

\sigma^{-1}(\mathcal{Z},I):=(\overline{\tau^{-}(\mathcal{Z})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}_{\nu_{A}^{-}(I)}},\nu_{A}^{-}(I_{\mathcal{Z}})).

Now one checks easily that
\sigma^{-1}
is the inverse of

\sigma
.
∎

11.2.
When do
\tau
-regular and
\tau^{-}
-regular components coincide?

Let
m\geq 0
.
Then
A
is an

m
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra
if

\operatorname{proj.dim}(D(A_{A}))\leq m\text{\quad and \quad}\operatorname{inj.dim}({{}_{A}}A)\leq m.

In this case, one has

d:=\operatorname{proj.dim}(D(A_{A}))=\operatorname{inj.dim}({{}_{A}}A)
.
Furthermore, for all
M\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
the following are equivalent:

•

\operatorname{proj.dim}(M)\leq d
;

•

\operatorname{proj.dim}(M)<\infty
;

•

\operatorname{inj.dim}(M)\leq d
;

•

\operatorname{inj.dim}(M)<\infty
.

Theorem 11.3
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.
Then
A/AeA
is a
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra for each
idempotent
e
in
A
.

Proof.

Under the assumption we get that
each
I\in\operatorname{inj}(A)
is
\tau
-rigid, and each

P\in\operatorname{proj}(A)
is
\tau^{-}
-rigid.
Now Lemma
2.3
and its dual
imply that
\operatorname{proj.dim}(I)\leq 1
and
\operatorname{inj.dim}(P)\leq 1
, respectively.

Next, let
e
be an idempotent in
A
, and let
B:=A/AeA
.
Then
B
is also a
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra.
Otherwise
we get
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B)\not=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(B)
, and then Corollary
7.3
and its dual imply

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)\not=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
, a contradition.
∎

In general,
it remains unclear when
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
holds.
The following section
answers this question for several
classes of algebras.

11.3.
Examples

11.3.1.
A selfinjective algebra with
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)\not=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)

Let
A=KQ/I
where

\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{a}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

.

The

is
\tau
-rigid, but not

In particular,
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)\not=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Proposition 11.4
.

Assume

is local.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
;

(ii)

is selfinjective.

Proof.

This follows directly from Proposition
10.3
.

Proposition 11.5
.

Assume that

.
Then the following are equivalent:

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)

(ii)

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii):
We know from Theorem

This follows from (the obvious part of) Theorem

.
∎

11.3.4.
Generalized species of Dynkin type

For a symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix
C
,
a symmetrizer
D
and an acyclic orientation
\Omega
,
one can define a
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra

A=H(C,D,\Omega)
which is an analogue of a species, see

[
GLS17
]
for details.
The locally free
A
-modules (as defined in
[
GLS17
]
) are exactly
the
A
-modules with projective dimension at most one.

Proposition 11.6
.

For
A=H(C,D,\Omega)
we have

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Proof.

Let
A=H(C,D,\Omega)
.
It follows from
[
GLS17
, Theorem 1.2]
that for
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{Z}
is generically locally free;

(ii)

\operatorname{proj.dim}(\mathcal{Z})\leq 1
;

(iii)

\operatorname{inj.dim}(\mathcal{Z})\leq 1
.

Pfeifer

[
Pf23
, Theorem 1.1]

shows that
\mathcal{Z}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)
is generically
\tau
-regular
if and only if
\mathcal{Z}
is generically locally free.
A dual proof yields the same for generically
\tau^{-}
-regular components.
∎

11.3.5.
Jacobian algebras

For a
2
-acyclic quiver
Q
and a potential
W
for

Q
let
A=J(Q,W)
be the associated Jacobian algebra introduced in

[
DWZ08
]
.
These algebras play a crucial role in the categorification of
Fomin-Zelevinsky cluster algebras.
(Note that the algebras
J(Q,W)
can be infinite-dimensional, and for the
mentioned categorification one needs the additional assumption that
the potential
W
is non-degenerate.)

For example, all gentle algebras arising from surface triangulations
(see
[
ABCP10
]
)
are of the form
J(Q,W)
.
We call these
gentle surface algebras
.

Combining

[
DWZ10
, Proposition 7.3]
and

[
DWZ10
, Corollary 10.9]
yields the following
theorem.
Note that
[
DWZ10
, Proposition 7.3]

is stated in
[
DWZ10
, Section 7]
where the potential
W
is assumed to be non-degenerate for the whole section.
However the proposition clearly holds also under the assumptions
of
[
DWZ10
, Section 10]
.

Theorem 11.7
.

Assume that
Q
is a
2
-acyclic quiver, and let
W
be a potential
for
Q
such that

W\in KQ
, and the ideal
I
generated by the cyclic derivatives of
W

is an admissible ideal in
KQ
.
Then for
A=J(Q,W)=KQ/I
and each

M\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
we have

\hom_{A}(M,\tau_{A}(M))=\hom_{A}(\tau_{A}^{-}(M),M).

In particular, we get

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A).

11.3.6.
Gentle algebras

Our finite-dimensional algebra
A=KQ/I
is a

gentle algebra
if the following
hold:

(i)

For each
i\in Q_{0}
we have

|\{a\in Q_{1}\mid s(a)=i\}|\leq 2\text{\quad and \quad}|\{a\in Q_{1}\mid t(a)=i\}|\leq 2;

(ii)

I
is generated by a set of paths of length two;

(iii)

Let
a,b,c\in Q_{1}
with
t(a)=s(b)=s(c)
and
b\not=c
.
Then
|\{ba,\;ca\}\cap I|=1
;

(iv)

Let
a,b,c\in Q_{1}
with
s(a)=t(b)=t(c)
and
b\not=c
.
Then
|\{ab,\;ac\}\cap I|=1
.

These algebras first appeared in
[
AS87
]
.

Proposition 11.8

(Chen, Lu
[
CL16
, Proposition 3.1]
)
.

A gentle algebra
A=KQ/I
is
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein
if and only if for each path
a_{t}a_{1}
of length two with

a_{t}a_{1}\in I
there is a
path
a_{1}\cdots a_{t}
in
Q
such that

a_{i}a_{i+1}\in I
for all
1\leq i\leq t-1
.

Note that the path
a_{1}\cdots a_{t}
appearing in
Proposition
11.8
is an oriented cycle.

The proof of the following result is based on our reduction technique
from Corollary
7.3
.

Proposition 11.9
.

Let
A=KQ/I
be a gentle algebra such that
Q
has no loops.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
;

(ii)

A
is a gentle surface algebra.

Proof.

(ii)
\implies
(i):
All gentle surface algebras are Jacobian algebras
J(Q,W)
and
satisfy the assumptions of
Theorem
11.7
.
This implies (i).

(i)
\implies
(ii):
Since we asssume (i), we know from Theorem
11.3
that
A/AeA
is

1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein for each idempotent
e
in
A
.

First, we assume that there is a
2
-cycle in
Q
.
Since
Q
has no loops, there is an idempotent
e\in A

such that
B=A/AeA\cong KQ^{\prime}/I^{\prime}
is a gentle

1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra
where

is one of the following quivers:

\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{a}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{b}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

For the first quiver, only the ideal
(ab,ba)
gives a gentle
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra.
But in this case, we know already that

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(B)\not=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(B)
.
For the second and the third quiver, there is no ideal which yields a

-Iwanaga-Gorenstein algebra.

Thus we can assume that

does not have

-cycles.
If

is hereditary, we are done.
Thus assume that

Then there exists a

Thus, it remains to consider the following four quivers:

\scriptstyle{b}

\scriptstyle{b}

\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{a}
\textstyle{3\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{c}

\scriptstyle{a}
\textstyle{3\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

11.3.7.
Nakayama algebras

Let
A=KQ/I
where
Q
is the cyclic quiver

\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{n-1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{n\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

with
n
vertices,
and
I
is generated by some (non-empty) set of paths of length at least two.
In other words,

A
is a cyclic Nakayama algebra.
Since we dealt with local algebras already, we assume
n\geq 2
.
To simplify the notation, we work with indices modulo
n
.

The following three lemmas are well known and quite easy to prove.

Lemma 11.10
.

Let
A=KQ/I
be a cyclic Nakayama algebra as above.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
we have

\tau_{A}(S(i))\cong S(i+1)\text{\hskip 10.22217ptand \hskip 10.22217pt}\tau_{A}^{-1}(S(i))\cong S(i-1).

Lemma 11.11
.

Let
A
be a cyclic Nakayama algebra.
If
A
is
1
-Iwanaga-Gorenstein, then
A
is selfinjective.

Let
J
be the ideal in
KQ
generated by all arrows.

Lemma 11.12
.

A cyclic Nakayama algebra
A=KQ/I
is selfinjective if and only
if there exists some
t\geq 2
such that
I=J^{t}
.

Lemma 11.13

(Adachi
[
A16
, Proposition 2.5]
)
.

Let
A=KQ/I
be a cyclic Nakayama algebras as above,
and let
M\in\operatorname{ind}(A)
, and assume that
M
is non-projective.
Then
M
is
\tau
-rigid if and only if
\dim(M)\leq n-1
.

Proposition 11.14
.

Let
A=KQ/I
be a cyclic Nakayama algebra as above.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
;

(ii)

I=J^{(n-1)+nr}
for some
r\geq 0
(with
r\geq 1
in case

n=2
).

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii):
Under the assumption (i), combining
Theorem
11.3
and Lemma
11.11

shows that

A
is selfinjective.
Thus by Lemma
11.12
we have

I=J^{t}
for some
t\geq 2
.

First, let
n=2
.
If
t
is even, then
\operatorname{soc}(P(i))\cong S(j)
for
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
.
Thus
P(1)\oplus S(1)
is
\tau
-rigid but not
\tau^{-}
-rigid.
Thus
t
has to be odd, i.e. (ii) holds.

Next, let
n\geq 3
.
For
1\leq i,j\leq n
let
M_{ij}:=S(i)\oplus P(j)
.
Then
M_{ij}
is
\tau
-rigid if and only if
j\not=i+1
.

Let now
1\leq j\leq n
.
We choose
i
such that
\operatorname{soc}(P(j))\cong S(i-1)
.
This implies
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\tau_{A}^{-}(M_{ij}),M_{ij})\not=0
.
Thus, if
j\not=i+1
, then
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)\not=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Assume now that
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.
By the above, this implies

\operatorname{soc}(P(j))\cong S(j-2)
for each
1\leq j\leq n
.
It follows that there exists some
r\geq 0
such that

\dim(P(j))=(n-1)+nr
for all
j
.
This implies
I=J^{(n-1)+nr}
.

(ii)
\implies
(i):
First, let
n=2
.
Then (ii) amounts to assuming that
I=J^{t}
with
t
odd.
By Lemma
11.13
the only indecomposable

\tau
-rigid (resp.
\tau^{-}
-rigid)
A
-modules are
S(1),S(2),P(1),P(2)
(resp.
S(1),S(2),I(1),I(2))
.
We know that
\tau_{A}(S(i))\cong S(j)\cong\tau_{A}^{-}(S(i))

for
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
.
Since
t
is odd, we have
\operatorname{soc}(P(i))\cong S(i)
for
i=1,2
.
The
\tau
-rigid
A
-modules are
then
P(1)^{a_{1}}\oplus P(2)^{a_{2}}
,
P(1)^{a_{1}}\oplus S(1)^{a_{2}}
,

P(2)^{a_{1}}\oplus S(2)^{a_{2}}
for
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 0
, and these are also the

\tau^{-}
-rigid modules,
i.e. we have

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Next,
assume that
n\geq 3
.
We have

A=KQ/J^{(n-1)+nr}
for some

r\geq 0
.
Let
S
be a cycle of length
n
in
Q
, and set
W:=S^{r+1}
.
This is a potential in the sense of
[
DWZ08
]
.
Assume that
{\rm char}(K)\not=r+1
.
Then
J^{(n-1)+nr}
equals the
ideal generated by the cyclic derivatives of

W
. (If
{\rm char}(K)=r+1
, then the cyclic derivatives of
W

are
0
.)
Thus
J(Q,W)
satisfies the assumptions of
Theorem
11.7
.
This implies

\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)
.

Since
A
is a Nakayama algebra, the classification of
indecomposable

A
-modules and their Auslander-Reiten translates
and also
\hom_{A}(M,N)
for all
M,N\in\operatorname{mod}(A)
do
not depend on the chosen ground field
K
.
This implies that the condition
\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau}(A)=\operatorname{Irr}^{\tau^{-}}(A)

does not depend on
K
.

This finishes the proof.
∎
