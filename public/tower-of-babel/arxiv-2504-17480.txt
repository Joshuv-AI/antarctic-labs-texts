Unified attacks to large language model watermarks: spoofing and scrubbing in unauthorized knowledge distillation
Xin Yi, Yue Li, Shunfan Zheng, Linlin Wang, Xiaoling Wang, Liang He

Abstract
Watermarking has emerged as a critical technique for combating misinformation and protecting intellectual property in large language models (LLMs). A particularly promising property, known as watermark radioactivity, offers potential for preventing the unauthorized use of LLM outputs in downstream distillation pipelines. However, the robustness of watermarking against scrubbing attacks and its unforgeability under spoofing attacks in unauthorized knowledge distillation settings remain underexplored. Existing attack methods either assume access to model internals or fail to support both attack types simultaneously. In our work, we propose Contrastive Decoding-guided Knowledge Distillation ( CDG-KD ), a unified framework that enables dual-path attacks under unauthorized knowledge distillation. At the core of CDG-KD is a novel contrastive decoding mechanism with token-level constraint fusion, which integrates a learned watermark discriminator and probability-based constraint component to selectively manipulate watermark-relevant logits. This allows for fine-grained control of watermark strength during generation without compromising fluency or semantics. Our approach employs contrastive decoding to extract corrupted or amplified watermark texts via comparing outputs, followed by dual-path distillation to train new student models capable of watermark removal and watermark forgery, respectively. Extensive experiments show that CDG-KD effectively performs attacks while preserving the general performance of the distilled model. Our findings underscore critical need for developing watermarking schemes that are robust and unforgeable.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have been widely deployed across diverse domains, such as code generation

, literary creation

, news writing

, and human-robot interaction

. While these advancements have greatly enhanced productivity, they also raises concerns regarding misinformation spread
[

,

]
and the protection of intellectual property
[
10
]
. As a response, watermarking has emerged as a promising technique for distinguishing AI-generated text from human-authored content. Among existing approaches, generative watermarking is particularly appealing due to its integration during generation time, thereby avoiding the overhead of post-processing
[
11
]
.

Most generative watermarking methods operate at the token level by either perturbing token probability distributions through green-red vocabulary partitioning or modifying the sampling process
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. These watermarks have traditionally been designed for post-hoc detection, enabling model owners to verify whether a suspicious output was generated by a marked model. However, recent work uncovers a more proactive and persistent property of watermarks known as
watermark radioactivity
. This phenomenon refers to the phenomenon where watermark signals embedded in teacher models are unintentionally inherited by student models during knowledge distillation, thus enabling traceability even after unauthorized knowledge distillation. Originally proposed by
Sablayrolles et al. [17]
in the context of small CNNs, the concept was later extended to text generation by
Sander et al. [18]
, showing that watermark radioactivity is broadly applicable across diverse watermarking schemes. Notably,
Gu et al. [19]
demonstrate that both logit-based and sampling-based watermarks retain comparable detectability in the student models. While this property has been leveraged as a tool to detect and deter model theft, it simultaneously reveals a critical vulnerability: the inherited watermarks can be extracted, spoofed, or removed by adversaries, even in black-box settings.

Despite recent progress, existing watermarking schemes remain limited in two key aspects: robustness (resistance to removal) and unforgeability (resistance to imitation). Most prior work has focused on defending against watermark
scrubbing
attacks that attempt to erase ownership signals
[
20
,
21
]
, while relatively few methods address
spoofing
attacks that aim to falsely attribute harmful content to victim models
[
22
,
23
]
. Moreover, the robustness and unforgeability of watermarks under distillation-based attacks remains underexplored. In practice, many LLM providers deploy their models as APIs with limited access, making direct attacks on internal watermarking mechanisms impractical. Nevertheless, indirect attacks may still be possible. For example,
Pan et al. [11]
show that a student model trained via distillation from a watermarked API can not only inherit the watermark but also subsequently remove it. However, their method relies on altering the model’s output probability distribution at inference time, thereby incurring extra computational costs and limiting its applicability primarily to scrubbing attacks. Another line of work by
Jovanović et al. [23]
achieves both scrubbing and spoofing attacks by querying a watermarked model’s API to reverse-engineer its hidden watermark pattern. Nevertheless, this approach requires access to both the watermarked and unwatermarked versions of the model’s output probability distribution, allowing for precomputing the next-token probability distributions over prefix sequences. In summary, most existing methods either (1) address only one type of attack (scrubbing or spoofing) without providing a unified framework, or (2) rely on access to output distributions or a reference model, making them impractical for real-world black-box scenarios. These limitations significantly hinder their applicability in black-box deployment.

To overcome these limitations, we propose a unified attack strategy that leverages the inherent radioactivity of watermarks, a property that causes watermarked models to exhibit detectable generation biases even under indirect queries. Our approach is compatible with standard black-box settings and, importantly, enables both scrubbing and spoofing attacks within a single framework. Technically, we extend standard contrastive decoding
[
24
,
25
]
to support uniform attacks by dynamically estimating watermark strength at each generation step and updating the candidate token subset accordingly. Therefore, ensuring the probability mass over the watermark-critical token subset is precisely controlled, while minimizing interference from irrelevant tokens. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, our approach demonstrates that the radioactivity of watermarks can be exploited to indirectly extract and manipulate watermarking schemes, which poses a substantial threat to both the robustness and unforgeability of existing watermarking methods.

In this paper, we introduce a novel framework called contrastive decoding-guided knowledge distillation (
CDG-KD
), a dual-path attack paradigm for both watermark scrubbing and spoofing in unauthorized knowledge distillation settings. CDG-KD requires neither modifications to the decoding process nor access to internal parameters of the victim model, making it broadly applicable and practically efficient in real-world black-box scenarios. The framework consists of three sequential steps: (1) Watermark model initialization, where a medium watermark model is first distilled from a victim model, followed by the application of an existing scrubbing attack to produce a weak watermark model; (2) Contrastive decoding, which extracts corrupted or amplified watermark traces by enforcing discriminator-based and probability-based constraints during generation, enabling the separation of watermark-relevant and watermark-irrelevant content; (3) Dual-path knowledge distillation, where the student model is trained using both corrupted and amplified watermark outputs, supporting two distinct attack objectives: removal and forgery. Importantly, the entire CDG-KD pipeline functions without reliance on internal watermarking mechanisms or modifications to the model’s generation strategy, making it a versatile and scalable black-box attack framework.

\bullet

We propose
CDG-KD
, a unified framework that enables both watermark scrubbing and spoofing attacks in unauthorized knowledge distillation scenarios, without requiring access to the victim model’s internal parameters or decoding process.

\bullet

We introduce a novel contrastive decoding strategy that integrates probability-based and discriminator-based constraint components to guide token-wise manipulation over watermark-critical subset during generation.

\bullet

2
Related work

2.1
LLM Watermark

Watermarking techniques for LLMs aim to distinguish machine-generated content from human-authored content, thereby facilitating content traceability to combat misinformation
[
11
,
26
]
and protect intellectual property
[
27
]
. Current generative watermarking methods can be broadly categorized into two families: the KGW Family and the Christ Family
[
16
]
. The KGW family modifies the token probability distribution over the vocabulary during decoding. The Christ family alters the sampling process by leveraging pseudo-random sequences to guide token selection. A representative example, KGW
[
12
]
, partitions the vocabulary into green and red token sets based on n-gram statistics, biasing generation toward the green set. Detection is performed via statistical hypothesis testing using
p
-values. However, semantic paraphrasing may obscure green-red patterns, reducing detection reliability. To overcome this limitation, Unigram
[
13
]
employs a globally fixed green-red token partition that is globally consistent across generations.
Li [28]
further enhances detection robustness by introducing a dual-pole counting method that compares green token frequencies across opposing configurations, rather than relative to a fixed threshold. Additional studies focus on improving semantic robustness and resistance to paraphrasing and transformation attacks
[
29
,
30
,
21
]
.

In contrast, the Christ family introduces watermarking through manipulation of the sampling process. SynthID-Text
[
14
]
introduces a tournament-based strategy for token generation. The detection mechanism relies on three key components: statistical signature analysis, random seed reconstruction, and mean score computation over token positions and model layers.
Christ et al. [31]
propose a binary sampling method for LLMs based on pseudo-random number generation.
Similarly, EXP-Edit
[
32
]
implements a deterministic mapping between pseudo-random numbers and model outputs. In these approaches, detection requires knowledge of the secret key or seed to realign the generation with the original sampling trajectory.

2.2
Watermark safety threat

Existing attack paradigms against watermarking methods for LLMs can be broadly classified into two categories: scrubbing and spoofing
[
23
]
. In a scrubbing attack, adversaries aim to remove detectable watermark traces from generated text
[
33
,
34
]
. Conversely, spoofing attacks seek to embed watermarks into harmful or manipulated content to falsely implicate safety-aligned models or their providers
[
35
]
. Most current watermarking strategies are primarily tailored to defend against scrubbing attacks
[
20
,
21
]
, with limited work addressing spoofing resilience. Recent methods such as Bileve
[
36
]
and CRL
[
22
]
have made initial progress toward defending against spoofing. Another emerging concern is the radioactivity of watermarks, referring to their unintended transfer through model distillation.
Sander et al. [18]
show that watermark signals in teacher models can be inherited by student models, even when distillation is performed without explicit awareness of the watermark. This phenomenon, termed watermark radioactivity, enables adversaries to indirectly manipulate watermark presence via student models under black-box constraints
[
19
]
. As a result, even if a model is externally accessible only via an API, distillation-based attacks can still transfer and later remove (or spoof) watermarks.
Pan et al. [37]
recently propose the first distillation-based watermark to explore scrubbing attack, showing that the watermarks in the student models can be removed while retaining the general performance from the teacher model. However, their method involves modifying the default sampling strategy at inference time, thereby introducing additional latency and computational cost. In contrast, our work introduces a unified framework that enables both watermark scrubbing and spoofing attacks in black-box scenarios. Crucially, we do not alter the sampling mechanism. Instead, we apply contrastive decoding only during training to either enhance or weaken watermark signals. These signals are then distilled into new student models, which incur no inference-time overhead.

3
Methodology

3.1
Victim model and threat setting

Attack target.
Given a safety-aligned victim LLM
\theta_{\text{LM}}
, generative watermarking strategies
f_{w}
can be inherited by a student model
\theta^{*}
through sample-based knowledge distillation. These strategies modify either the output distribution or the sampling process during generation, such that the student naturally inherits watermarking behavior.

In a
scrubbing attack
, the attcker seeks to suppress the watermark signal in the student model. Formally, let
f_{d}
denote the detection algorithm corresponding to the watermarking scheme
f_{w}
. The attacker optimizes
\theta^{*}
to generate text
y
that minimizes the watermark detectability score:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta^{*}}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\theta^{*}(x)}\left[f_{d}(y)\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\theta^{*}=\theta_{\text{scrub}}

(1)

In contrast, a
spoofing attack
aims to falsely attributes harmful outputs to the safety-aligned teacher model
\theta_{\text{LM}}
, potentially causing reputational or legal consequences for its developers. Specifically, we maximize the watermark detectability score while generating harmful content
y
:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta^{*}}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\theta^{*}(x)}\left[f_{d}(y)\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\theta^{*}=\theta_{\text{spoof}}

(2)

Access assumptions.
We assume a black-box scenario where the attacker only has access to the outputs of the teacher model via API queries. The attacker lacks access to the model’s internals (e.g., logits, architecture, gradients), reflecting realistic deployment constraints.

3.2
Overview of CDG-KD framework

While watermark radioactivity enhances post-hoc attribution, it also introduces a new vulnerability: watermark signals can be indirectly manipulated via student models. To exploit this, we propose Contrastive Decoding-Guided Knowledge Distillation (
CDG-KD
), a unified framework that enables both scrubbing and spoofing attacks under black-box settings. As shown in Fig.
2
, CDG-KD comprises three stages:

Model initialization.
A base student model
\theta_{s}
is distilled from the watermarked teacher
\theta_{\text{LM}}
. Then, a weakly watermarked model
\theta_{a}
is obtained by fine-tuning
\theta_{s}
using existing paraphrasing-based scrubbing attacks (e.g., Parrot, Dipper).

Contrastive decoding.
Guided by both probability-based and discriminator-based constraint components, we identify tokens that carry strong or weak watermark signals, and selectively amplify or suppress them to generate two datasets: (a) corrupted (de-watermarked) text and (b) amplified (strongly watermarked) text.

Dual-Path attack.
These two datasets are used to fine-tune separate student models for scrubbing (
\theta_{\text{scrub}}
) and spoofing (
\theta_{\text{spoof}}
), respectively.

For scrubbing attack, the distilled model no longer exhibits detectable watermark traces. In contrast, the spoofing attack will amplify watermark signals while gaining the ability to generate harmful content, thereby increasing the risk of false attribution to the safety-aligned teacher model.

Figure 2:
CDG-KD
: a unified attacks framework for watermark scrubbing and spoofing in unauthorized knowledge distillation, enabled by contrastive decoding-guided knowledge distillation. This framework employs the phenomenon of radioactivity, where watermarked LLMs transfer detectable signals to student models during distillation. CDG-KD includes three steps: (1) Initializing a watermark student model through distillation and a weak-watermark model through current scrubbing attacks. The weak watermark model is trained from scratch on text generated by a known scrubbing attack, such as Dipper. (2) Contrastive decoding with token-level constraint fusion is applied to generate watermark-corrupted or watermark-amplified texts by contrasting the outputs of the student and weak-watermark models. (3) Dual-path distillation is conducted based on the corrupted and amplified watermark texts to train new student models, enabling watermark scrubbing (removal) and spoofing (forgery), respectively.

3.3
Model initialization

Leveraging the property of watermark radioactivity, watermarking strategies embedded in a safety-aligned teacher model
\theta_{\text{LM}}
can be unintentionally transferred to a student model
\theta_{s}
through sample-based knowledge distillation. To further attenuate the watermark signal, prior watermarking methods employ paraphrase-based attacks, such as Dipper
[
38
]
, Pegasus
[
39
]
, and Parrot
[
20
]
to generate weakly watermarked texts. Therefore, the original base model can be trained into an even more weakly watermarked model based on these texts, denoted as
\theta_{a}
, which serves as a low-watermark reference in subsequent contrastive decoding.

3.4
Contrastive decoding

The strength of watermark inheritance depends heavily on the distillation procedure. To enable fine-grained control over the watermark intensity, we propose a contrastive decoding mechanism that compares the output distributions of the student model
\theta_{s}
and its weakly watermarked variant
\theta_{a}
. This step enables us to either suppress or amplify watermark signals by adjusting token-level probabilities based on the observed discrepancies, thereby creating training data that exhibits either corrupted or amplified watermark characteristics.

3.4.1
Contrastive distribution modulation

To amplify or suppress watermark strength, we introduce contrastive decoding by comparing the output distributions of the student model and the weakly watermarked model. Specifically, given an input query
x
and two models,
\theta_{s}
and
\theta_{a}
, the models generate two distinct output distributions: one that exhibits a strong watermark signal and another that contains a distorted, weaker watermark. A new contrastive probability distribution is computed by leveraging the differences between these two distributions. In the scrubbing attack, the probability of a token, denoted as
P_{\theta_{\text{scrub}}}
, is formulated as:

\displaystyle P_{\theta_{\text{scrub}}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}\left[(1+\beta)\log P_{\theta_{a}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\beta\log P_{\theta_{s}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\right]

(3)

where
\beta
is a modulation parameter that controls the strength of contrastive adjustment, with
\beta=0
corresponding to standard decoding.

Conversely, in the spoofing attack, the contrastive decoding formulation is reversed to amplify the watermark signal:

\displaystyle P_{\theta_{\text{spoof}}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}\left[(1+\beta)\log P_{\theta_{s}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\beta\log P_{\theta_{a}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\right]

(4)

This contrastive decoding formulation induces a new output distribution
P_{\beta}
. In Appendix
D
, we provide a theoretical analysis showing that (i) the corresponding KL-based objective is convex in
\beta
, and (ii) if the derivative at
\beta=0
is negative, there exists a nontrivial
\beta^{*}>0
that guarantees improved alignment with the target distribution (either
P_{a}
for scrubbing or
P_{s}
for spoofing). These results justify the effectiveness of contrastively modulating the output distributions to achieve both watermark removal and forgery.

3.4.2
Token-level constraint fusion

Contrastive decoding serves as a selective correction mechanism that amplifies or suppresses watermark features by exploiting behavioral discrepancies between models. This aligns with contrastive objectives commonly employed in controllable text generation tasks, such as detoxification
[
40
,
41
]
, jailbreak prevention
[
42
]
, and hallucination mitigation
[
25
,
43
]
. However,
Li et al. [24]
highlight that applying contrastive correction uniformly across all tokens may impair output fluency and coherence, thereby degrading overall generation quality. To address this limitation, we adopt a token-Level constraint fusion for contrastive decoding strategy, which selectively adjusts only a subset of the token distribution. Specifically, we combine both probability-based constraints and discriminator-based constraints. The probability-based constraint component, denoted as
Dis(\cdot)
, is a BERT-based encoder by default, which evaluates the watermark likelihood of current sequences:

\displaystyle s_{t}=Dis(x_{\leq t})\in[0,1]

(5)

where larger values indicate a higher probability that current sequence
x_{\leq t}
carries the victim’s watermark.
We define the valid subset of tokens as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{valid}}=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle x_{t}\in\mathcal{V}\;\Bigg|\;

\displaystyle\log P_{\theta^{*}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\geq\lambda\max_{w}\log P_{\theta^{*}}(w\mid x_{<t}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{mode}}(s_{t-1},s_{t})\leq 0\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad P_{\theta^{*}}\in\{P_{\theta_{\text{scrub}}},P_{\theta_{\text{spoof}}}\}

(6)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is a truncation threshold limiting candidate token selection scope for probability-based constraints, and
\{\theta_{\text{scrub}},\theta_{\text{spoof}}\}
denotes the attacking mode. The discriminator-based gating function
\Gamma_{\text{mode}}(s_{t-1},s_{t};\theta^{*})
enforces token-level constraints:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{scrub}}(s_{t-1},s_{t};\theta^{*}))

\displaystyle=\max(s_{t},s_{t-1})-\tau_{\text{scrub}}

(7)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{spoof}}(s_{t-1},s_{t};\theta^{*}))

\displaystyle=\tau_{\text{spoof}}-\min(s_{t},s_{t-1})

(8)

For scrubbing attacks, valid tokens must satisfy
\max(s_{t-1},s_{t})\leq\tau_{\text{scrub}}
, thus consistently reducing watermark likelihood. Conversely, spoofing attack requires
\min(s_{t-1},s_{t})\geq\tau_{\text{spoof}}
, thereby maintaining a strong watermark signal. Incorporating both
s_{t-1}
and
s_{t}
encourages stability and prevents abrupt shifts in the watermark detection scores across consecutive tokens. The final decoding step is defined as follows:

P_{\theta^{*}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{P_{\theta^{*}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})}{\sum\limits_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{valid}}}P_{\theta^{*}}(w\mid x_{<t})},&x_{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{valid}}\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(9)

By restricting contrastive decoding to high-confidence regions within the token distribution, our approach mitigates unintended perturbations that could compromise grammaticality or semantic coherence. This targeted correction not only preserves the linguistic integrity of the generated text but also enables effective watermark removal or spoofing based on the attack objective.

3.5
Dual-path attack

After we obtain the contrastively decoded data
D_{u}
and
D_{w}
, where
D_{u}
is a de-watermarked dataset and
D_{w}
is a strongly watermarked dataset, we proceed to the three stage of our method. In this stage, we perform dual-path distillation using
D_{u}
and
D_{w}
to fine-tune new base models for scrubbing and spoofing attacks, respectively. For the spoofing attack, a base model can be fine-tuned on
D_{w}
, aiming to transfer the watermarking characteristics of the victim model to a new student model
\theta_{\mathrm{spoof}}
. The training objective is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spoof}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D_{w}}\sum_{t}\log P_{\theta_{\mathrm{spoof}}}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)

(10)

In contrast, for the scrubbing attack, the base model is fine-tuned on
D_{u}
. This process aims to transfer only the general generation capability of the teacher model without inheriting its watermark signal. The corresponding training objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scrub}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D_{u}}\sum_{t}\log P_{\theta_{\mathrm{scrub}}}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)

(11)

These formulations ensure that
\theta_{\mathrm{spoof}}
is trained to retain the watermark, whereas
\theta_{\mathrm{scrub}}
learns to generate responses without the watermarking scheme. This dual-path distillation strategy enables flexible and attack-specific watermark manipulation in fully black-box settings.

4.2
Baselines

To assess our method’s effectiveness, we compare against both existing watermark scrubbing and spoofing baselines. As shown by prior work
[
19
]
, watermarks can be unintentionally preserved through sampling-based knowledge distillation. We denote this setup as
SKD
, where the student model is trained on watermarked outputs from the victim model using standard cross-entropy loss, thus inheriting watermark signatures.

Scrubbing attack.
To examine watermark removal in the student model, we explore four scrubbing attacks methods.
Dipper

[
38
]
,
Pegasus

[
39
]
, and
Parrot

[
20
]
perform paraphrasing to obscure watermark features.
WN

[
37
]
modifies token logits at inference time to suppress watermark traces. We additionally introduce two variants of our proposed framework. We further introduce two variants of the proposed framework. The first variant,
CDG-KD*
, employs contrastive decoding based solely on the probability-based constraint component. The full version,
CDG-KD
, extends this approach by incorporating the discriminator-based constraint component to further enhance the effectiveness of the constraints. Specifically, our framework uses a strongly watermarked student model trained via SKD and a weakly watermarked auxiliary model
\theta_{a}
to construct training samples. By default, the auxiliary model is a newly distilled student initialized from weakly watermarked texts generated by Dipper, which effectively guides the watermark removal process.

To convert benign watermarked text into harmful content while simultaneously amplifying watermark signals, our framework can be effectively adapted for spoofing attacks. This type of attack seeks to falsely attribute malicious outputs to the owner of a safety-aligned victim model, posing potential reputational and security risks. Our spoofing strategy employs contrastive decoding with two auxiliary model variants:
Dipper+Ours*
and
Pegasus+Ours*
, where weakly watermarked models
\theta_{a}
are constructed using Dipper and Pegasus, respectively. By default, these variants use probability-based constraints to guide contrastive decoding. Additionally, we introduce
Dipper+Ours
, which incorporates both probability-based and discriminator-based constraints via token-level constraint fusion for more precise watermark amplification. All variants are evaluated against the original Purely Watermarked Victim Model
(PWTM)
to assess spoofing effectiveness. We also include comparisons with variants of the WN
[
37
]
, which originally aims to suppress watermarks by adjusting token logits based on prefix-level watermark matching. In our spoofing context, we introduce a modified version,
vWN
, which inverts this mechanism. Rather than weakening watermark signals, vWN amplifies them by manipulating the decoding logits in favor of watermark-associated tokens, guided by cues extracted from the prefix.

4.3
Datasets and metrics

Datasets.
To facilitate knowledge distillation, we follow the prompt format proposed by
Pan et al. [37]
and construct a training corpus consisting of 32K question–answer pairs generated by a watermarked victim model. Scrubbing attacks are assessed on two datasets: the RealNewsLike subset from the C4 dataset
[
46
]
for completion tasks and the Dolly-CW dataset
[
47
]
, which targets question-answering scenarios. To evaluate spoofing attacks, we adopt the HarmfulQ+AdvBench dataset
[
48
,
39
]
, which includes manually curated harmful queries, and the MaliciousInstruct dataset
[
49
]
comprising adversarial instruction inputs. To evaluate the general utility of the distilled student models, we report performance on three widely used benchmarks: ARC Challenge
[
50
]
, TruthfulQA
[
51
]
, and TinyGSM8K
[
52
]
.

Metrics.
A core aspect of our evaluation is measuring watermark detectability under both scrubbing and spoofing settings. We report the median p-value from statistical detection tests, which quantifies the presence and strength of watermark signals across generated outputs. To ensure consistency, all generations are truncated to a maximum of 512 tokens during evaluation. For scrubbing attacks, the primary objective is to minimize watermark detectability while maintaining output fluency and task relevance. Accordingly, we report task-specific utility metrics from each benchmark dataset. For spoofing attacks, the goal is to generate detailed responses to harmful prompts, thereby simulating a successful attack that falsely attributes unsafe behavior to the victim model. To evaluate this, we report the Attack Success Rate (ASR), defined as the proportion of harmful prompts that elicit non-rejecting responses from the model. ASR is measured using Llama-Guard-3
[
53
]
, a state-of-the-art safety classifier widely adopted in recent work
[
54
,

]

Watermark discriminability

Can the detection of watermarks in model-generated texts be framed as a simple classification task ?
Recent work has shown that model-specific patterns in generated text can be exploited for model attribution and identification
[
56

. Inspired by this, we formulate watermark detection as a binary sequence classification task: determining whether a given text contains a watermark. To this end, we fine-tune four representative discriminators with diverse architectural backbones: BERT
[

]
, T5
[

]
, GPT-2
[

, and LLM2vec
[

. As shown in Table

This high performance is likely attributed to its globally fixed green-red vocabulary partition, which simplifies the learning of watermark patterns. While larger models do not uniformly outperform their smaller counterparts, they generally yield improved results when detecting more complex watermarking schemes. Notably, LLM2vec achieves the highest accuracy in both KGW-2 and KGW-3 configurations, suggesting that its feature representation is particularly effective in capturing subtle token-level regularities induced by n-gram-based watermarking.

Are discriminator-based and p-value-based watermark discriminators consistent?
Our experimental analysis reveals notable differences in detection sensitivity across watermarking schemes under attack conditions. As shown in Fig.

In contrast, discriminator-based detectors demonstrate significantly greater robustness. Their accuracy remains stable across different N-gram configurations, particularly for sample-based watermarking methods such as SynthID-Text. A similar trend is observed in spoofing attack evaluations in Fig.
6

Notably, the sample-based watermark SynthID-Text maintains 80% detection accuracy in both scrubbing and spoofing attack settings. These findings collectively suggest that discriminator-based detection provides a more robust and reliable mechanism for watermark verification.

Analysis of watermark distillation at sentence level

Sentence-level watermarking methods, such as SemStamp
[
61
]
and k-SemStamp
[
62
]
, exhibit enhanced robustness against paraphrasing attacks. However, they also present two notable limitations that hinder their practicality in real-world applications. First, as illustrated in Table
11
, these methods incur significantly higher inference latency compared to token-level watermarking approaches. Specifically, the average forward pass time for a single sample using SemStamp and k-SemStamp is approximately 35.4 and 23.9 times slower than that of KGW, respectively. This substantial overhead makes sentence-level watermarking unsuitable for latency-sensitive deployment scenarios, such as real-time LLM-based services. Second, the radioactivity of sentence-level watermarks is relatively limited. We evaluate watermark inheritance using two indicators: the p-values of the watermark presence test (PWTM) and the signal strength retained after sampling-based knowledge distillation (SKD). Although SemStamp and k-SemStamp yield weak detectability (e.g., SKD values of 0.386 and 0.271, respectively), they underperform when compared to token-level methods such as KGW with
N=3
, which achieves an SKD of 0.134. Moreover, the higher PWTM values for sentence-level methods suggest fragile watermark transferability through distillation. These findings highlight the trade-offs associated with sentence-level watermarks: despite their robustness to paraphrasing attacks, their practical deployment is constrained by high computational cost and limited support for downstream watermark inheritance.

Ablation study

7.1
Hyperparameter analysis

Impact of watermarking configurations on the distilled model.
Watermark strategies are often sensitive to hyperparameter configurations. For watermarking methods based on red-green token sets, such as KGW and Unigram, the parameter
\delta
controls watermark strength. In contrast, for sampling-based methods like SynthID-Text, the number of candidate tokens plays a similar role in determining watermark detectability. As shown in Fig.
7
, increasing
\delta
or the candidate token number generally enhances watermark detectability (measured via
-\log(p)
), but it also significantly degrades the quality of generated text. This degradation leads to weaker generalization ability in the student model after sample-based knowledge distillation. To strike a balance between watermark strength and downstream performance, we adopt the following default settings:
\delta=3
for KGW (N=1),
\delta=2
for Unigram, and Candidate Num. = 2 for SynthID-Text (N=2).

Figure 7:
Trade-off between watermark detectability and downstream performance. Student models distilled from watermarked teachers are evaluated on TruthfulQA and tested for watermark on Dolly-CW dataset.

Impact of hyperparameter
\beta
and
\lambda
.

We conduct a systematic evaluation of the amplification coefficient
\beta
and the truncation threshold
\lambda
: two key hyperparameters in our contrastive decoding mechanism. These parameters directly influence the balance watermark detectability and model generalization capability. As shown in Fig.
8
(a) and (b), increasing
\beta
reduces watermark detectability under scrubbing attacks and enhances detectability under spoofing attacks. However, large
\beta
values tend to compromise downstream task performance, indicating a trade-off between attack strength and generative quality. For KGW (N=1) setting, we find optimal values at
\beta=0.5
for scrubbing and
\beta=1.0
for spoofing. Similarly, Fig.
8
(c)-(d) illustrates that moderate increases in
\lambda
initially enhance attack performance by refining token selection. However, overly large
\lambda
values reduce attack specificity and impair model coherence. We observe optimal performance at
\lambda=0.2
for scrubbing and
\lambda=0.1
for spoofing. These observations highlight the importance of careful tuning to maintain a balance between watermark detectability manipulation and task performance. Additional settings are detailed in Appendix
A
.

7.2
Qualitative analysis of failed attacks

While CDG-KD demonstrates robust performance across a broad range of watermarking schemes, we identify two primary sources of failure that expose its current limitations: (1) attacks on the KGW family tend to fail more frequently on low-entropy predictions, and (2) the inherent difficulty of distilling watermark behaviors from sample-based watermarking schemes such as SynthID-text.

First, spoofing attacks directed at the KGW strategy exhibit a pronounced decline in effectiveness when applied to low-entropy generations. As illustrated in Fig.
10
(a), attack success rates drop sharply when the output entropy falls below 0.8. In such cases, the output distribution becomes highly peaked, limiting the actionable token space for contrastive perturbation. This restrictiveness hampers the spoofing process, especially for outputs reflecting memorized or syntactically rigid content. Interestingly, scrubbing attacks demonstrate comparatively greater resilience in low-entropy regimes, potentially due to their ability to exploit localized uncertainties to disrupt embedded watermark patterns.

In the second case, sample-based watermarking schemes such as SynthID-Text embed watermarks by altering the token sampling process, leaving detectable statistical patterns in the output text. Unlike lexical watermarks that rely on surface-level token choices, these sampling-based methods require the student model to replicate more abstract behavioral patterns during distillation. As shown in Fig.
10
(b), CDG-KD encounters difficulties in both preserving these watermarks under spoofing attacks and fully removing them during scrubbing. Notably, for SynthID-Text, attack success steadily improves as the learning rate increases from
1\text{e}{-6}
to
5\text{e}{-5}
, indicating a more effective transfer of the teacher model’s sampling behavior. In contrast, the performance of the KGW watermark saturates around
1\text{e}{-5}
, with higher learning rates providing limited additional benefit.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce Contrastive Decoding-guided Knowledge Distillation (
CDG-KD
), a unified attack framework that leverages the radioactivity of watermarks under unauthorized knowledge distillation. Unlike prior approaches that either rely on internal model access or only address a single type of watermark attack, CDG-KD operates in a black-box setting and supports both scrubbing and spoofing attacks. A core of our approach is token-level constraint fusion during contrastive decoding, which leverages a discriminator-based
constraint component and probability-based constraint component to selectively manipulate watermark-critical logits. Our framework demonstrates that a student model can inherit and manipulate the watermarking behavior of a victim model, posing a significant threat to the robustness and unforgeability of existing watermarking schemes. These findings expose critical vulnerabilities in current watermarking techniques and underscore the urgent need for more effective schemes capable of withstanding dual-path attacks in black-box settings.

9
Limitations and discussion

While CDG-KD demonstrates strong empirical performance across various watermarking schemes, several limitations arise when considering practical deployment in real-world systems.

First,
query budget
may limit the practicality of distill-based attacks in commercial settings. Most production APIs enforce rate limits or charge on a per-query basis, which can render large-scale querying prohibitively expensive or slow. Given that CDG-KD requires a substantial number of queries to extract knowledge from the victim model, this dependency could pose a barrier in practice. However, it is worth noting that the distillation process is a one-time offline procedure and does not need to be repeated for each target query, thereby amortizing the cost over repeated usage.

Second,
latency and runtime overhead
are critical considerations in deployment. CDG-KD involves iterative contrastive decoding and discriminator-guided optimization steps during training, which may raise concerns about inference latency. Nonetheless, the final student model preserves the original architecture and remains as efficient as the base model at inference time, incurring no additional runtime cost. Consequently, latency is only affected during the offline attack phase and not during runtime deployment of the spoofing or scrubbing model.

Third, the current formulation does not consider
adaptive defenses
that service providers might deploy in response to attack attempts. These may include retraining or fine-tuning watermark detectors, introducing stochastic perturbations into outputs, enforcing content-based filtering, or integrating active verification protocols. Recent work by Li et al.
[
63
]
formalizes standard knowledge distillation for LLMs and introduces the broader concept of anti-distillation, which aims to inhibit the transfer of model behaviors to student models. However, the application of anti-distillation techniques to textual watermarking remains largely unexplored. In future work, we aim to investigate how anti-distillation can be adapted to defend against watermark spoofing or removal, offering a potential line of defense against advanced attacks such as CDG-KD.

10
Ethical statement

This work investigates critical vulnerabilities in existing watermarking schemes under unauthorized knowledge distillation, highlighting the urgent need to enhance the robustness and unforgeability of the watermark. While the proposed CDG-KD framework introduces dual-path attacks that could potentially be misused, our intent is to support the development of more secure and resilient watermarking techniques by identifying and analyzing realistic victim models. We acknowledge that any research involving attack methodologies carries inherent ethical risks. To address these concerns, all experiments are conducted within a controlled research environment and are not deployed on publicly accessible systems. Our work aims to inform the design of comprehensive defense mechanisms that jointly mitigate both scrubbing and spoofing threats.

Appendix C
Mathematical guidelines for
\beta
and
\lambda

We propose a principled mathematical approach for selecting hyperparameters, namely the amplification coefficient (
\beta
) used in contrastive decoding and the truncation threshold (
\lambda
) employed in probability-based constraints, separately tailored for spoofing and scrubbing attacks. Our objective is to balance the effectiveness of watermark manipulation with the preservation of generation quality.

Optimization objective
We assess two metrics to quantify the utility of watermark attack performance and generation quality.
Watermark effectiveness (W):
Measured by watermark detection strength using the negative logarithm of the p-value (
-\log(\text{p-value})
), where lower values indicate reduced detectability for scrubbing and higher values indicate enhanced detectability for spoofing.
Generation quality (Q):
Evaluated using task-specific general capability datasets such as ARC-C, TruthfulQA, or TinyGSM8K, with higher scores reflecting better text quality. To determine the optimal values of
\beta
and
\lambda
for spoofing attacks, we formulate the following optimization function:

\displaystyle(\beta^{*},\lambda^{*})

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\beta,\lambda}J_{\text{spoof}}(\beta,\lambda)

\displaystyle J_{\text{spoof}}(\beta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot W(\beta,\lambda)\cdot Q(\beta,\lambda)}{W(\beta,\lambda)+Q(\beta,\lambda)}

(1)

This function explicitly balances maximizing watermark detectability while preserving high-quality text generation.

For scrubbing attacks, the watermark effectiveness metric
W
becomes
1-W
reflecting the goal of watermark removal:

\displaystyle(\beta^{*},\lambda^{*})

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\beta,\lambda}J_{\text{scrub}}(\beta,\lambda)

\displaystyle J_{\text{scrub}}(\beta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot[1-W(\beta,\lambda)]\cdot[1-Q(\beta,\lambda)]}{[1-W(\beta,\lambda)]+[1-Q(\beta,\lambda)]}

(2)

Experimental validation
To validate our theoretical approach, we conduct a systematic grid search over a broad range of candidate hyperparameter values:

•

\beta\in\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,10.0\}

•

\lambda\in\{0.1,0.05,\ldots,1.0\}

Empirical results demonstrate that the mathematically optimized hyperparameters closely align with empirically derived optimal values, confirming the consistency between theoretical analysis and experimental outcomes. Specifically, the optimal hyperparameter settings fall within previously recommended empirical ranges: for scrubbing,
\beta\in[0.4,0.6]
and
\lambda\in[0.1,0.2]
; for spoofing,
\beta\in[1.0,2.0]
and
\lambda\in[0.05,0.1]
. These findings are consistent with the empirical results detailed in Section
7.1
.

Appendix D
Theoretical analysis

We provide a theoretical foundation for how contrastive decoding enables effective watermark scrubbing and spoofing under knowledge distillation. Our analysis builds upon the convex analytic framework proposed by
Fang et al. [64]
, and systematically extends it to cover both watermark removal (scrubbing) and watermark enhancement (spoofing).

D.1
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the vocabulary and
x_{<t}
represent the prefix at timestep
t
. We consider two models: a strongly watermarked model
P_{\theta_{s}}
and a weakly watermarked auxiliary model
P_{\theta_{a}}
. Their token-level output distributions are:

\displaystyle P_{s}(v)

\displaystyle=P_{\theta_{s}}(v\mid x_{<t})

\displaystyle P_{a}(v)

\displaystyle=P_{\theta_{a}}(v\mid x_{<t})

(3)

The contrastively adjusted distribution used for watermark scrubbing is defined as:

\displaystyle P_{\beta}^{\text{scrub}}(v)\propto P_{a}(v)^{1+\beta}\cdot P_{s}(v)^{-\beta}

(4)

where
\beta\geq 0
controls the degree of shifting from the strong watermark towards the weak reference.
For spoofing, the roles of
P_{s}
and
P_{a}
are reversed:

\displaystyle P_{\beta}^{\text{spoof}}(v)\propto P_{s}(v)^{1+\beta}\cdot P_{a}(v)^{-\beta}

(5)

D.2
Objective function and distance measure

We aim to theoretically justify that the contrastively adjusted distribution
P_{\beta}
can approach the desired target distribution (i.e., suppressing or amplifying watermark signals as needed). For
scrubbing
, the target is to match the weakly watermarked distribution
P_{a}
as closely as possible. Thus, we define the KL-divergence objective as:

\displaystyle g_{\text{scrub}}(\beta)=\mathrm{KL}(P_{a},|,P_{\beta})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P_{a}(v)\log\frac{P_{a}(v)}{P_{\beta}(v)}

(6)

A lower
g_{\text{scrub}}(\beta)
means
P_{\beta}
is more similar to
P_{a}
, indicating more effective watermark removal.
In contrast, for
spoofing
, the goal is to mimic the strongly watermarked distribution
P_{s}
. Therefore, we define the spoofing objective as:

\displaystyle g_{\text{spoof}}(\beta)=\mathrm{KL}(P_{s}|P_{\beta})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P_{s}(v)\log\frac{P_{s}(v)}{P_{\beta}(v)}

(7)

A lower
g_{\text{spoof}}(\beta)
means
P_{\beta}
is closer to
P_{s}
, reflecting stronger watermark features.
The choice of KL-divergence direction in each case reflects the goal: for scrubbing, we want the generated (student) distribution to mimic the weak reference, while for spoofing, we want it to mimic the strong watermark.

D.3
Convexity and existence of optimum

Proposition 1 (Convexity)

The objective function
g(\beta)
is convex in
\beta
for both scrubbing and spoofing scenarios. This follows from two facts: (i) the KL divergence is jointly convex in its arguments, and (ii) the contrastively adjusted distribution
p_{\beta}
is an exponential family function of
\beta
.

Theorem 1 (Existence of an optimal contrast)

If the derivative at
\beta=0
is negative, i.e.,
g^{\prime}(0)<0
, then there exists
\beta^{*}>0
such that:
g(\beta^{*})<g(0)
.
This implies that the contrastively adjusted distribution
P_{\beta^{*}}
is closer to the target distribution (either
P_{a}
or
P_{s}
) than the original unadjusted distribution.

Proof sketch

Due to the convexity of
g(\beta)
, the function is either monotonically increasing, decreasing, or possesses a unique minimum. If the derivative at the origin is negative (
g^{\prime}(0)<0
), the function initially decreases, ensuring the existence of a point
\beta^{*}>0
where
g(\beta)
achieves a strictly lower value than at
\beta=0
.

D.4
Implications for dual-path attacks

The theoretical result directly supports the design of the dual-path adjustment strategy in our
CDG-KD
framework. By examining the behavior of
g(\beta)
, we ensure that a positive contrast parameter
\beta^{*}
can always be selected to improve alignment with the desired distribution:

•

Scrubbing path:
Select
\beta^{*}>0
such that
P_{\beta}
better matches the weakly watermarked distribution
P_{a}
, thereby minimizing residual watermark signals.

•

Spoofing path:
Select
\beta^{*}>0
such that
P_{\beta}
approximates the strongly watermarked distribution
P_{s}
, effectively amplifying the watermark signal to enhance forgery success.
