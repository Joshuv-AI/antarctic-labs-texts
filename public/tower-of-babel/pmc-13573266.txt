The Use of Ambient Dictation Artificial Intelligence in Clinical Spaces in Surgery: A Scoping Review
Authors: Orli Weiss, Alyssa Steinbaum, Carly Fiest, Lacy Brame, Alfred Marc Iloreta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo determine the current scope of knowledge regarding ambient listening artificial intelligence (AI) tools for documentation in surgical clinical environments.Data SourcesLiterature search was performed using EMBASE, PubMed (MEDLINE), CINAHL, SCOPUS, and Cochrane databases. Citation searching was utilized to identify additional studies.MethodsA scoping review regarding the use of ambient dictation artificial intelligence in clinical note generation in surgery was performed per PRISMA‐ScR guidelines. Two reviewers independently performed title and abstract screening, full‐text review, and data extraction. Inclusion criteria included studies describing the use of ambient listening AI tools for the generation of clinical documentation for surgery, with full‐text available, in English or English‐language translation.ResultsOf 252 studies identified through database and citation searching, 12 met the inclusion criteria. Narrative reviews were most common (n = 8), followed by scoping reviews (n = 1), systematic reviews (n = 1), primary research (n = 1), and expert commentary (n = 1). Three studies provided original data: the primary research and expert commentary focused on the use of AI scribing technology in urology, and one narrative review also included survey data regarding the implementation of AI scribing in hand surgery. All three sources found positive clinician feedback following the implementation of AI scribing technology. All review articles meeting inclusion criteria expressed positive views regarding the implementation of AI scribing technology in surgical subspecialties, though most relied on sources describing AI scribing in nonsurgical subspecialties.ConclusionDocumentation burden is a common contributor to clinician burnout in the medical field. AI scribing technologies have the potential to promote clinic efficiency and decrease documentation burden. The current research landscape regarding the implementation of these tools in surgical specialties is sparse and warrants further primary research in the future.Keywords: AI scribe, ambient dictation, artificial intelligence, clinical documentation, surgery

Introduction

For every 8 h of scheduled patient time, otolaryngologists spend approximately 4 h performing documentation within the electronic health record (EHR) [1, 2]. These tasks include note writing, chart review, placing orders, and responding to messages [1]. The majority of this time is spent writing notes, and the 4 h of documentation tasks are performed during clinic hours, after scheduled hours, and on days when the physician is not scheduled to work [1].

Physicians across all specialties in medicine report high levels of burnout that are often attributed, in part, to administrative and documentation tasks [3, 4, 5]. In otolaryngology, top contributors to burnout include work activities outside of scheduled work time and EHR documentation [6], which often overlap [1]. Documentation burden not only affects physician workload; physicians who spend less time typing during encounters often have higher patient satisfaction scores than those who spend more time documenting [7, 8].

With the widespread adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) tools to improve efficiency in medicine, several tools present solutions targeting the documentation burden. These tools include speech‐to‐text ambient listening software that uses natural language processing or large language model AI networks to translate audio recordings of physicians during or after patient visits into clinical notes [3, 9, 10, 11]. Early literature regarding implementation of these ambient dictation tools or “AI scribes” shows decreased note‐writing time [3, 10], decreased physician engagement with the EHR after work hours [10], and higher clinician satisfaction with decreased documentation burden [10, 11].

With the increasing use of AI to improve clinical operations, there has been an expansion in literature regarding the use of AI in healthcare; however, there are limited studies assessing the use of ambient dictation and speech‐to‐text AI tools specifically in the surgical clinical environment. Surgical specialties, including otolaryngology, rely on meticulous documentation at every stage of patient care, such as clinic notes, procedure notes, operative notes, postoperative notes, discharge paperwork, and follow‐up notes, creating a significant documentation burden for clinicians [12]. This study seeks to understand the current landscape of knowledge regarding the use of ambient dictation AI tools in surgical specialties.

Methods

Search Strategy

A scoping review of the literature was conducted to assess the current status of ambient dictation and generative AI within the clinical environment in surgery, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines [13]. The literature search was performed in June 2025 using EMBASE, PubMed (MEDLINE), CINAHL, Cochrane, and SCOPUS databases.

The review protocol, including the search algorithm, was developed by the principal investigator (A.M.I.), rhinology fellow (L.B.), and medical student (O.W.) to identify studies describing ambient dictation and speech‐to‐text AI tools used in surgical clinical spaces to aid in medical documentation (Supporting Information). The review protocol was not registered online. Databases were searched on June 6, 2025, and sources were included from database inception through June 6, 2025. Citation searching of the returned studies' reference lists was also performed to identify additional studies.

Study Selection

All study types with full‐text availability describing AI‐driven documentation using ambient dictation in a surgical clinical environment, published from database inception through June 2025, were included. Studies that did not include discussion of AI ambient dictation, studies that did not include discussion of AI use in surgical specialties, and studies that described AI uses for uses other than clinical documentation were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included studies without an English translation or full‐text available.

Data Collection

Search results from EMBASE, PubMed (MEDLINE), CINAHL, Cochrane, and SCOPUS databases were uploaded into the Covidence review software [14]. The screening software removed duplicates. Three study team members (O.W., C.F., and A.S.) independently performed title and abstract screening, followed by full‐text screening. Differences at each screening stage were resolved through consensus discussions. If consensus was unable to be reached, a senior team member (A.M.I. or L.B.) was consulted for final determination.

Data extraction was performed for all studies meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria, with author, publication year, study title, country where research was conducted, study design, study aims, and data or description of AI ambient listening note generation extracted for each study. Given the variability in study design for the included studies, we were unable to perform a meta‐analysis.

Results

Database and citation searches returned 252 records (PubMed, n = 88; EMBASE, n = 75; Scopus, n = 69; CINAHL, n = 7; Cochrane, n = 5; and citation searching, n = 8). After deduplication by Covidence software, title and abstract screening was performed for 163 studies. Of these, 114 were excluded because they failed to meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Twelve studies met selection criteria after full‐text evaluation (Figure 1).

Of the 12 included studies, there were 7 narrative reviews [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21], 1 narrative review combined with a cross‐sectional survey [22], 1 systematic literature review [23], 1 scoping review [24], 1 primary research investigation [25], and 1 expert commentary article [26]. Notably, only one study represented original primary research to assess the efficacy of AI scribes in documenting a clinical encounter for a surgical specialty (urology), highlighting a significant gap in empirical investigations on this topic [23]. The majority of studies were published in the United States (n = 9) [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 23, 24], with additional studies published in Canada [25], Singapore [22], and the United Kingdom [23]. As shown in Figure 2, the surgical fields most represented in discussions of ambient AI documentation were orthopedics and urology (each with four studies), followed by plastic surgery, pediatric surgery, academic surgery, and generalized surgical applications (each with one study). A notable upward trend in the number of publications addressing ambient AI in surgical contexts was also observed, with three of the included studies published in 2023, four in 2024, and five published between January and July 2025, indicating projected significant growth in interest in this area.

Discussion

This review is, to our knowledge, the first to specifically highlight the lack of primary research regarding the use of AI scribing technologies, particularly in the surgical environment. While the included studies suggest ambient listening AI note generation can increase documentation efficiency and decrease clinician documentation burdens in surgical specialties, the majority of included studies were review articles. Out of 12 studies meeting inclusion criteria, there were only 3 studies with original data: 1 primary research article [25] and 1 expert commentary [26], both relating to the use of AI scribing technology in urology, as well as 1 narrative review with survey data regarding the implementation of AI scribes in a hand surgery department.

The vast majority of primary research relating to AI scribing technology has been conducted in nonsurgical specialties. These studies show great potential for improving clinical operations; one study found that AI scribes generate notes over two times faster than typing or standard dictation software and require minimal training for providers to implement them in the clinic [3]. The decreased time spent documenting patient encounters, both at work and after hours, led to increased satisfaction for providers using these technologies [3, 10], as well as providers being able to see more patients while also spending less time on documentation tasks each day [38]. Physicians are not the only ones who benefit from increased documentation efficiency. One study analyzing the benefits of ambient listening AI note generation found that patients were more satisfied with their physician encounters when their provider used AI note generation compared to manual documentation [10]. When AI note‐generating software was used, patients reported that their physician spent more time talking to them and less time looking at the computer screen during their encounter, leading to increased feelings of connection with their providers [10, 38].

The existing literature regarding the use of ambient dictation in nonsurgical specialties supports the use of these tools for decreasing clinician documentation burden and increasing patient satisfaction while maintaining high‐quality documentation. Comparatively, the data regarding the use of these tools in surgical specialties is sparse. The majority of studies (n = 9) [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 23, 24], describing the use of ambient listening AI tools in note generation, are review articles; however, the majority of these studies (n = 8) only utilize data regarding the use of these tools in nonsurgical environments. Thus, despite numerous studies that describe the utility of AI ambient listening note writing tools in surgical fields, there is a dearth of evidence to support these claims. Given the unique documentation requirements for surgical specialties, this study highlights a key gap in the current literature regarding the clinical utility, accuracy, and safety of ambient dictation tools in these specialties.

These considerations are particularly relevant to otolaryngology. In a study evaluating the implementation of in‐person medical scribes in an academic otolaryngology clinic, scribe use was associated with improved documentation efficiency and decreased chart lag time, supporting the feasibility of workflow interventions targeting documentation burden in ENT practice [39]. This demonstrates that documentation support interventions can meaningfully improve workflow within otolaryngology clinics and underscores the relevance of evaluating newer technologies such as ambient AI systems in this specialty.

However, it is important to consider that otolaryngology clinics present unique documentation challenges that may necessitate specialty‐specific validation of ambient AI systems. While many surgical specialties concentrate documentation complexity within operative reports, otolaryngology frequently integrates diagnostic endoscopy and in‐office procedures into routine outpatient encounters. Clinic visits frequently incorporate detailed microscopic and endoscopic examinations, including nasal endoscopy, flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy, otomicroscopy, and video stroboscopy. Documentation of these examinations requires precise anatomic descriptors, laterality, grading scales, and structured findings that are integral to clinical decision‐making and billing compliance. In office procedures such as myringotomy, nasal cautery, biopsies, and debridement may occur within the same visit and require clearly delineated procedural documentation. Whether ambient AI systems can reliably distinguish between history taking, examination narration, procedural reporting, and assessment and plan components in these complex encounters remains unknown.

In addition to documentation complexity, the acoustic environment of ENT clinics may introduce challenges distinct from those of many other outpatient specialties. Encounters frequently involve background noise from suction devices, endoscopic equipment, and procedural instrumentation. Patients may present with dysphonia, aphonia, airway compromise, or altered resonance, which can affect speech clarity. Providers themselves may speak while wearing masks or while manipulating instruments during examination. These factors may influence speech recognition performance and raise questions regarding transcription accuracy that have not yet been systematically evaluated within otolaryngology settings.

Recent evaluation of AI‐assisted speech recognition in pediatric otolaryngology demonstrated high overall documentation quality but identified recurrent omissions of clinical findings, terminology inaccuracies, and the need for human intervention to ensure procedural specificity and adherence to professional standards [40]. These findings underscore both the promise of AI documentation tools in ENT and the necessity of careful validation within this specialty.

Taken together, the current literature suggests that ambient AI documentation tools hold promise for improving efficiency and clinician experience in healthcare, yet high‐quality primary evidence within surgical specialties such as otolaryngology remains limited. Future work should prioritize prospective, specialty‐informed evaluation of ambient AI systems within surgical practice and explore both supervised implementation models and domain‐specific refinement of these technologies to ensure reliable performance across diverse surgical environments.

This study has several limitations. The included studies varied widely in their design and surgical subspecialty focus, which limited our ability to perform a meta‐analysis using this data. Furthermore, the results of this review consisted mainly of review articles rather than primary research. These narrative reviews primarily cited sources studying the use of ambient AI note‐writing tools in nonsurgical specialties, which limits our ability to draw conclusions about the use of the technology in the surgical setting.

Future research should examine the use of AI technologies in surgical fields and evaluate whether ambient listening AI note‐writing tools can accurately generate surgery‐specific documentation, including clinic notes and operative reports. Surgical specialties have different documentation needs than nonsurgical specialties, and more research is needed in the surgical space to ensure that the technology can be successful in that setting.

Within surgical disciplines, additional specialty‐specific validation may be necessary. For example, while emerging data suggest AI documentation tools may function effectively in otolaryngology, further prospective evaluation is required to confirm reliability in real‐world clinical environments. Future studies should extend beyond efficiency metrics to examine documentation accuracy and clinically meaningful error rates. In addition to supervised evaluation, further model refinement may be necessary to ensure reliable performance across distinct surgical subspecialties. General‐purpose language models may not fully capture specialty‐specific vocabulary, procedural nuances, and structured documentation standards. Incorporating domain‐specific corpora, procedural templates, and subspecialty‐specific terminology into model development and fine‐tuning may enhance documentation accuracy.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that researchers across surgical specialties believe AI scribing technologies have the potential to positively impact clinical practices in surgery; however, the overall lack of primary research regarding the use of these technologies in surgical clinics and surgical workflow reveals this to be a critically underexplored area, with only three studies citing original data in this field. As AI scribing tools become more prevalent across medicine, there is a need for standardized research methods to assess their utility in both medical and surgical subspecialties. Otolaryngologists often cite documentation tasks and work that occurs after hours as drivers of burnout. It is therefore especially important to further this area of research and assess the impact of AI scribing technologies on clinical efficiency and the satisfaction of both providers and patients in otolaryngology and other surgical specialties.
