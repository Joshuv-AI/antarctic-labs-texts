High-fidelity unfolding of 252Cf neutron spectra in EJ-301 scintillators via calibrated response matrices and Monte Carlo simulations
Authors: Songbai Zhang, Yitong Qin, Qi Liu, Rui Li, Xiaobo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate reconstruction of neutron spectra is essential for fissile material identification and nuclear safeguard applications. However, most existing studies only reconstructed neutron spectrum above 1–2 MeV, leaving the detector response in low energy region poorly constrained. This study introduces a unified experimental-simulation framework for high-fidelity spectrum unfolding in EJ-301 liquid scintillators. Detailed Geant4 Monte Carlo simulations were performed to model energy deposition and light-output processes for both gamma rays and neutrons, generating ideal response functions. To reproduce real detector behavior, energy-resolution parameters were experimentally calibrated using Compton-edge measurements from 137Cs and 22Na sources for 1-inch and 2-inch detectors. Validated by accurate reconstruction of 22Na gamma spectra, the calibrated response matrices were then applied in iterative unfolding algorithms(GRAVEL and MLEM) to reconstruct the neutron spectrum of a 252Cf source. The proposed approach achieves a lower energy threshold of 0.35 MeV and maintains an average deviation below 4.28%, with the best agreement of 1.42% from ISO 8529-1 reference data. The 2-inch detector demonstrated higher efficiency and resolution. This experimentally validated framework bridges the gap between ideal simulations and practical detector performance, providing a robust pathway for precise neutron spectroscopy and quantitative nuclear safeguards verification.Keywords: Organic scintillator EJ-301, Response matrix, Spectrum unfolding, MLEM, GRAVELSubject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Organic scintillation detectors, especially liquid scintillators such as EJ-301, have become essential instruments in diverse radiation measurement applications, particularly in nuclear safeguards, nonproliferation monitoring, and environmental radiation surveillance1–3. Their widespread adoption is driven by their fast timing response, high detection efficiency, and exceptional neutron–gamma pulse shape discrimination (PSD) capability4,5, which enables accurate distinction between neutron and gamma-ray events based on scintillation decay time characteristics. This key feature makes them invaluable in unknown complex radiation environments—such as nuclear smuggling scenarios, covert fissile material transport, mixed-source interdiction efforts, and safeguards monitoring and verification—where the radiation field may involve unknown isotopes, variable shielding, and overlapping gamma–neutron signatures6–8. In such conditions, accurate identification and quantification of radiation sources is paramount. As a result, precise energy calibration9,10 and robust spectrum unfolding techniques11,12 have become critical to advancing the utility of organic scintillators in nuclear instrumentation, safeguards verification, and radioactive source characterization2,3.

Achieving accurate spectrum unfolding in organic scintillation detectors fundamentally relies on both precise detector response matrices and robust unfolding algorithms13,14. Recent advancements have been made by integrating Monte Carlo simulations15 and experimental calibration10,16. Monte Carlo particle transport codes, such as Geant4, are extensively used to simulate energy deposition and light output processes, allowing the construction of ideal detector response functions without prior knowledge of resolution characteristics17. However, realistic applications, especially those involving recoil protons from neutron interactions, demand corrections for non-linear light output and energy resolution effects18. To address this, calibration procedures are employed through spectral alignment between simulated and measured responses, with emphasis on the Compton plateau region as the calibration anchor9,10,19. These calibrated data then guide the Gaussian Energy broadening (GEB) to yield response matrices that better reflect actual detector behavior12,20.

For spectral unfolding, iterative algorithms such as MLEM and GRAVEL12,21 have proven their robustness and stability, particularly under noisy measurement conditions11,12,20. Nevertheless, persistent challenges remain. Most earlier unfolding studies were confined to energies above 1-2 MeV, leaving the detector response at low energy weakly constrained and causing deviation from the ISO 8529-1 standard18,19. These limitations underscore the ongoing need for enhanced modeling fidelity, improved resolution calibration, and potentially hybrid unfolding approaches to ensure accurate nuclear material characterization.

Motivated by these challenges and the imperative for improved fission spectrum measurement techniques using organic scintillators, this study aims to optimize and enhance the precision of response function simulation and spectrum unfolding across a wide energy range. First, comprehensive light output models for both 1-inch and 2-inch EJ-301 detectors were developed via Geant4-based Monte Carlo simulations, accurately representing both gamma-ray and neutron interactions under ideal resolution conditions. Next, a rigorous calibration procedure was executed by precisely matching the measured gamma spectra to the simulated responses under different energy resolution conditions at the Compton plateau region, enabling accurate fit of the detector’s energy resolution across the relevant energy range. This derived resolution parameters were then robustly applied to introduce Gaussian broadening to both simulated gamma and neutron response matrices, thereby generating realistic and high-fidelity detector response functions. Finally, the unfolded neutron spectrum of a 252Cf source was accurately reconstructed using the measured detector response and the newly generated response matrices through established iterative algorithms. This comprehensive and validated framework offers significantly improved spectral fidelity for nuclear material identification and nuclear security applications. This framework enhances the reliability of spectral reconstruction, particularly in the low energy region, and improves the fidelity of fission-source characterization.

Methodology

Detector simulation and light output modeling

The energy response of EJ-301 organic scintillation detectors was modeled using the Geant4 Monte Carlo toolkit, incorporating detailed detector geometry, material configuration and particle interactions. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the detector consists of a cylindrical EJ-301 scintillator(blue), enclosed in an aluminum housing(red), with the PMT and base assembly region modeled as a void region(white) to eliminate extraneous interactions. Both 1-inch and 2-inch detector configurations were simulated to reflect common sizes used in nuclear material monitoring applications.

Energy resolution calibration

In practical applications, the intrinsic energy resolution of the detector—caused by statistical fluctuations in light production, photon transport, and electronics—broadens the ideal pulse height distributions generated via Monte Carlo simulation. To account for this, a Gaussian Energy Broadening model was applied to the simulated spectra, with the standard deviation derived from experimentally calibrated energy resolution values.

To determine the detector’s energy resolution, both 1-inch and 2-inch EJ-301 detectors were irradiated with standard gamma-ray sources, including 137Cs (662 keV) and 22Na (511 keV, 1275 keV). The Compton edge features of the measured spectra were used as the resolution calibration anchors, covering a representative range of light outputs in the detector. This multi-point calibration allowed the empirical determination of a resolution as a continuous function of energy, ensuring consistency between experimental and simulated spectra. A nonlinear least-squares fitting algorithm was employed to align the broadened simulated spectra with measured data.

Specifically, the width of the falling edge between the Compton peak and its half-height point was selected as the reference interval. This metric was consistently applied to both the simulated and experimental spectra to evaluate the agreement under different full width at half maximum (FWHM) conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 2a.

Experimental setup, data acquisition and energy calibration

To obtain high-quality experimental spectra for response matrix calibration and validation, a compact and configurable measurement system was constructed, centered on EJ-301 organic scintillation detectors. Both 1-inch and 2-inch cylindrical EJ-301 detectors (manufactured by Eljen Technology) were utilized to investigate size-dependent performance. Each detector was optically coupled to a photomultiplier tube (PMT) via optical silicone grease, ensuring optimal light transmission. The schematic layout of the experiment is shown in Fig. 3a.

Powered using a CAEN high-voltage supply. The detector outputs were connected to a PXI-based high-speed digitizer module featuring a 500 MHz sampling rate, 14-bit resolution, 32 parallel input channels. The schematic diagram of the data acquisition system is shown in Fig. 3b.

Neutron-gamma discrimination was performed using the Pulse Shape Discrimination(PSD) technique, defined as the ratio of delayed to the total integrated charge of each scintillation pulse. The optimal PSD threshold was empirically determined by scanning the PSD energy distribution and selecting the cut that maximized the Figure of Merit(FoM) between the neutron and gamma peaks. This ensured reliable neutron-gamma separation prior to spectrum unfolding.

Experimental setup for neutron-gamma response measurement.

To perform gamma-ray calibration and neutron spectrum unfolding, two types of radiation sources were employed: the same gamma sources including 137Cs and 22Nawere placed at a distance of 30 cm from the detector center to reduce pile-up and allow uniform irradiation, and a 252Cf spontaneous fission neutron source with ionization chamber.

For accurate pulse height analysis and neutron–gamma discrimination, each recorded waveform was analyzed using an offline digital pulse shape discrimination (PSD) algorithm. The charge integration method was applied, using two time windows: a short gate (24 ns) and a long gate (240 ns). and neutron/gamma clusters were separated using a dynamic threshold curve. Only the neutron events were selected for constructing the experimental neutron pulse height spectrum.

Detector response measurements were conducted separately using a 22Na gamma-ray source and a 252Cf neutron source, along with additional background measurements. Energy calibration of the raw channel spectra was performed using characteristic Compton edge features from 137Cs and 22Na source. Results

Gamma response and energy resolution validation

To validate the accuracy of the light output model, the response of the EJ-301 detector was simulated to monoenergetic gamma rays from 137Cs and fission neutrons from 252Cf under ideal energy resolution conditions. The resulting spectra are presented in Fig. 4.

Simulated Ideal Response Functions of Two Size EJ-301 Scintillators.

The simulated gamma-ray response for 137Cs exhibits a distinct Compton edge without broadening, and larger scintillator volumes yield higher response amplitudes due to increased energy deposition. Unlike inorganic NaI detectors no full-energy peak was observed because photoelectric absorption is rare in low-Z organic scintillators such as EJ-301. In contrast, the 252Cf response shown reveals a concentration of recoil proton events within the 0 ~ 2 MeVee range, with steep fall-off at higher energies.

These ideal response serve as the baseline for subsequent resolution calibration and practical response matrix construction.

Construction and validation of calibrated detector response matrices

To accurately account for the energy resolution of EJ-301 detectors, a systematic broadening procedure was applied to the ideal simulated spectra using six representative full width at half maximum (FWHM) values. For each FWHM condition, a corresponding broadened spectrum was generated using Gaussian convolution. These spectra were then compared to experimental data to identify the best-fitting resolution parameters.

By interpolating between the broadened simulation results and the measured Compton edge profiles, the effective energy resolution for both 1-inch and 2-inch detectors was determined, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Using the obtained energy resolution parameters, the ideal response spectra were modified to include realistic broadening. The adjusted spectra closely reproduced the experimental Compton-edge at 511 keV, 662 keV, and 1275 keV, confirming the accuracy of the Gaussian energy broadening(GEB) model. Furthermore, energy calibration curves were constructed by linearly fitting the measured channel positions of the Compton edges to their known electron-equivalent energies. The resulting calibration functions for both detectors, displayed in Fig. 5d, exhibit strong linearity (R2 > 0.99), enabling reliable conversion from channel number to deposited energy.

The fitted resolution parameters were then applied to generate the final gamma-ray and neutron response matrices, which covered the 0 ~ 3 MeV(50 bins), and 0 ~ 16 MeV(80 bins) respectively. These calibrated matrices, as shown in Fig. 6, serve as the foundation for the subsequent spectral unfolding procedures.

Compared with 1-inch detector, the 2-inch EJ-301 exhibits higher overall response intensity for both gamma and neutron interactions, due to its larger scintillation volume, enhanced light output collection, and reduced statistical fluctuations.

These results confirm the fidelity of the energy resolution modeling and calibration method. The calibrated response matrices closely replicate the detector’s real-world behavior and provide a basis for high-accuracy neutron and gamma spectrum unfolding .

Measured 22Na gamma response and unfolded spectrum

Using the experimentally measured and energy-calibrated response of the EJ-301 detector to a 22Na gamma-ray source, spectral unfolding was performed with both the MLEM and GRAVEL algorithms. The reconstructed spectra clearly resolved the characteristic gamma-ray peaks at 511 keV and 1275 keV.

To evaluate the accuracy of the unfolding results, the intensity ratio of the two peaks—after correction for intrinsic detection efficiency—was compared to the theoretical ratio of 1.80. The relative deviation remained below 7.78%, with the best case achieving 1.11%, confirming strong quantitative agreement.

The 2-inch detector outperformed the 1-inch variant due to its higher intrinsic efficiency and improved energy resolution. The background-subtracted detector response of 22Na and unfolded spectra are shown in Fig. 7, with results summarized in Table 2.

Measured 252Cf neutron response and unfolded spectrum

The neutron spectrum of a 252Cf source was unfolded using the calibrated neutron response matrix and experimentally acquired data. Neutron–gamma discrimination was obtained using PSD based charge-integration, with empirically optimized threshold 0.13 for 1-inch and 0.20 for 2-inch EJ-301 detector.

Figure 8 presents the unfolding process and results. Both 1-inch and 2-inch EJ-301 detectors produced spectra closely matching the ISO 8529-1 reference. Minor deviations were observed—slight underestimation at high energies and overestimation in the low-energy tail—likely due to background and statistical fluctuations.

Discussion and conclusion

The results demonstrate that proposed framework enables integrated γ–n energy spectrum unfolding with high accuracy and reproducibility using EJ-301 liquid scintillation detectors. By combining Geant4-based response modeling, detector-specific Compton edge calibration, Gaussian energy broadening, and iterative unfolding algorithms (MLEM and GRAVEL),

the method achieves reliable reconstruction of both gamma and fast-neutron spectra over a wide range. The unfolded 22Na spectra accurately reproduced the 511 keV and 1275 keV peaks with the expected intensity ratio, while the 252Cf neutron spectra closely matched the ISO 8529-1 reference, validating the quantitative fidelity and consistency of the approach.

This integrated methodology represents a substantive improvement over previous techniques by unifying simulation, calibration, and unfolding within a coherent γ–n reconstruction framework, unlike previous approaches treating gamma and neutron spectra separately, is cross-validated for correlated emissions and multiple detector geometries.

It extends the effective reconstruction range down to 0.35 MeV—below the 1 or 2 MeV limits reported in earlier EJ-301/EJ309 studies—and substantially enhances spectral fidelity in the sub-MeV region, where non-linear light output effects dominate.

Two primary factors contribute to this performance: (i)physically grounded Geant4 modeling of scintillator response, and (ii)detector-specific resolution calibration derived from Compton-edge alignment between measured and simulated spectra. Furthermore, consistent performance across 1-inch and 2-inch detectors confirms the method’s robustness and scalability.

Residual discrepancies—minor overestimation at low gamma energies and slight underestimation at high neutron energies—are attributed to scattering, background, or limitations of light output nonlinearity. Future improvements will focus on refined shielding, and enhanced nonlinearity corrections, and validation across additional scintillator types and mixed-field radiation environments. Extending this unified γ–n framework will broaden its applicability to fissile material detection, isotopic identification, and nuclear safeguards monitoring.

In summary, this study establishes an experimentally validated, simulation-driven, and detector-specific framework for γ–n spectral reconstruction across a broad energy range. The unified treatment of gamma and neutron responses improves measurement fidelity, lowers reconstruction thresholds, and provides a transferable basis for future developments in nuclear spectroscopy and nonproliferation applications.
