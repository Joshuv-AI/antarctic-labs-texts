Assessment of hepatitis B knowledge, attitudes, and practices among healthcare workers in Herat, Afghanistan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Enayatollah Ejaz, Mohammad Faisal Wardak, Ali Rahimi, Mohammad Masudi, Nasar Ahmad Shayan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis B virus (HBV) is a significant global health threat, with healthcare workers (HCWs) at high occupational risk. Despite effective vaccines, coverage among HCWs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains low. In Afghanistan, data on HCWs’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding HBV are limited, hindering targeted prevention efforts. A cross-sectional study recruited 453 HCWs from 22 health facilities in Herat City between October and December 2023 using a convenience sampling method. Participants completed a self-administered questionnaire on their KAP regarding HBV infection, prevention, and vaccination. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression with SPSS version 27. Among participants, 39.7% (n = 180) demonstrated greater knowledge, 44.6% (n = 202) had better attitudes, and 47.5% (n = 215) showed better practices toward hepatitis B. Notable disparities were observed in knowledge and practices among HCWs. Males had higher knowledge than females (n = 111, 45.3% vs. n = 69, 33.2%; p = 0.009), and participants aged ≥ 35 years showed greater knowledge than those aged 25–29 years (n = 39, 54.2% vs. n = 59, 32.1%; p = 0.008). Education was also associated with knowledge: master’s degree holders scored higher than diploma holders (n = 57, 53.3% vs. n = 49, 29.9%; p < 0.001), and among professions, medical doctors outperformed nurses (n = 88, 54.7% vs. n = 24, 25.5%; p < 0.001). In terms of practices, HCWs at Herat Regional Hospital demonstrated better performance than those at other health centers (n = 78, 53.1%; p = 0.007). Multivariate analysis confirmed these associations. Medical doctors had significantly greater knowledge compared to other professions (OR = 2.351, 95% CI = 1.081–5.112, p = 0.031). Likewise, HCWs in Herat Regional Hospital (OR = 4.744, 95% CI = 1.800–12.504, p = 0.002), Private Hospitals (OR = 3.845, 95% CI = 1.496–9.886, p = 0.005), and Governmental Clinics (OR = 4.844, 95% CI = 1.629–14.405, p = 0.005) demonstrated better practices compared to doctors’ offices. HCWs in Herat demonstrate suboptimal knowledge and preventive practices toward HBV, despite relatively high vaccination coverage. Educational, institutional, and regional factors influence KAP, while professional experience and training improve outcomes. Strengthening HBV prevention through universal vaccination, post-vaccination testing, reliable reporting, and integrated training is essential to protect HCWs and patients and support Afghanistan’s progress toward HBV elimination.Keywords: Hepatitis B, Healthcare workers, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Herat, AfghanistanSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) presents a formidable global health challenge, affecting approximately 3.3% of the world’s population1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), about 296 million people live with chronic hepatitis B, leading to 1.5 million new infections and 820,000 deaths annually, mainly from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Africa and Asia, which account for 90% of global cases, bear the greatest burden, and their high prevalence increases the likelihood that HCWs encounter infected patients and contaminated materials, heightening occupational risk2.

HBV infection is preventable through safe, effective, and accessible vaccination3. The virus primarily targets the liver, leading to both acute and chronic disease. Transmission commonly occurs from mother to child during childbirth, in early childhood, or through contact with contaminated body fluids such as blood, saliva, vaginal secretions, and semen. Infection can also spread via unsafe injections, sexual contact, or exposure to sharp instruments4.

HCWs, whether paid or voluntary, are at high risk of HBV infection through occupational exposure, particularly via needle-stick injuries and contact with infected blood or body fluids5,6. In the United States, the CDC reports that HCWs face a tenfold higher risk of HBV infection compared with the general population7. Globally, similar risks persist, with about 3 million HCWs exposed to bloodborne pathogens annually8, resulting in an annual infection rate of about 5.9%9. This rate varies widely by region, from 0.8% to 74.4%10, and In developing countries, 40–60% of HBV infections among HCWs are linked to occupational hazards11.

The cornerstone of HBV infection control resides in HBV vaccination, substantiating its high efficacy, safety, and lifelong protection12. HCWs’ vaccination coverage, particularly in developing countries, remains alarmingly low, posing significant challenges both for the affected individuals and for the broader health system. According to WHO, vaccination rates among HCWs range from 18% to 39% in LMICs, compared with 67% to 79% in high-income countries8.

While global HBV burden and occupational risks are well-documented, data from Afghanistan are limited. Available studies report prevalence rates of 1.8% chronically infected, with an HBV-related mortality of 7.4 per 100,00013,14. A study in Kabul hospitals reported a prevalence of 1.53% among the obstetric population15. These prevalence figures are important because HCWs frequently interact with patients and bodily fluids, highlighting the need to assess their KAP to prevent transmission.

Attaining adequate knowledge and fostering positive attitudes toward infection prevention are critical for minimizing occupational exposure to HBV. HCWs with better KAP are more likely to adhere to safe measures, including vaccination and proper handling of blood and bodily fluids, protecting both themselves and their patients. Studies from various LMICs consistently highlight substantial gaps in HCWs’ KAP regarding HBV, which contribute to low vaccination coverage and increased occupational risk9–11. In Afghanistan, investigations are limited, but available studies indicate similar deficiencies. One study in Kabul reported aggregate KAP scores of 86.58% for knowledge, 34.73% for attitude, and 61.22% for practice, indicating high knowledge but inadequate perception of occupational risk and moderate adherence to preventive measures16. Similarly, a cross-sectional study among medical, midwifery, dentistry, and medical technology students in Kabul found that only 63.5% of participants had correct knowledge regarding HBV prevention, transmission, diagnosis, treatment, and vaccination17. KAP among HCWs in Herat province has not yet been assessed, and without such data across Afghanistan, it is difficult to design effective prevention and vaccination programs.

Therefore, this study aims to assess KAP regarding hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, prevention, and vaccination among HCWs in Herat province, Afghanistan. Specifically, it examines awareness of HBV transmission and prevention, attitudes toward occupational risk and vaccination, and adherence to preventive practices such as standard precautions and vaccine uptake. We hypothesize that HCWs in Herat have limited KAP regarding HBV, influenced by professional background, years of experience, and vaccination status.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design and was conducted in Herat, Afghanistan, between October and December 2023. Data were collected from 22 health facilities representing a mix of public and private providers, including one regional hospital, nine private hospitals, five governmental health clinics, and eight private health clinics. The study focused on assessing KAP of HCWs regarding HBV. Participants were drawn from multiple professional categories to reflect the diversity of the health workforce, including medical doctors, nurses, midwives, dentists, anesthetists, and laboratory/medical technologists.

Study participants

The target population consisted of HCWs actively engaged in patient care in the participating facilities during the study period. Inclusion criteria were being a licensed health care professional (doctor, nurse, midwife, dentist, anesthetist, or laboratory/medical technologist), actively working in one of the selected facilities during the study period, and providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria were administrative or non-clinical staff, HCWs absent or on leave at the time of data collection, and questionnaires with incomplete or inconsistent responses that could not be analyzed. A total of 453 HCWs fulfilled these criteria and completed the study questionnaire in full.

Recruitment strategy for target population

Since there was no complete list of HCWs in Herat to sample from, we used convenience sampling. Within each facility, unit heads, supervisors, or the chief medical officer helped us by informing their staff about the study and connecting us with potential participants. All eligible HCWs who were on duty during data collection were invited to take part. The research team approached them in person, explained the study, and asked for their consent. Participation was completely voluntary. In total, we approached approximately 550 HCWs across the participating facilities. Of these, 453 completed the questionnaire in full and were included in the analysis. Some HCWs declined because they were busy and needed to attend to patients, while others began the self-administrated questionnaire but could not finish it due to workload pressures. These incomplete questionnaires had to be excluded. This resulted in a final response rate of about 82.4%.

Sample size and justification

The minimum sample size was calculated assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, a design effect of 1.0, and an expected proportion of 50%. The choice of 50% was made because no prior data on the study outcomes among HCWs in Herat were available, and this value provides the most conservative estimate, yielding the largest required sample size. The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a single proportion: n = (Z² × p × (1 − p))/d². In this study, Z was set at 1.96 for a 95% confidence level, p was taken as 0.50, and d was 0.05. Substituting these values into the formula gives: n = (1.96² × 0.50 × (1 − 0.50))/(0.05²) = (3.8416 × 0.25)/0.0025 = 384.16 ≈ 384. A design effect of 1.0 was applied since no cluster sampling was used, leaving the sample size unchanged. To account for a 10% nonresponse rate, the required sample size was increased to 422.4, which was rounded up to 424. Thus, the minimum required sample size was 424 participants. In practice, 453 HCWs completed the survey, exceeding the minimum requirement. In practice, a comprehensive sampling frame of HCWs in Herat was not available, and logistical constraints made it difficult to implement probability-based sampling methods. For these reasons, convenience sampling was used as a pragmatic approach to reach the required number of participants across different facilities. In total, approximately 550 HCWs were invited, and 453 completed the survey and were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Consenting participants completed a structured, paper-based questionnaire on-site after being approached and invited by the research team. The questionnaire included closed-ended items on sociodemographic characteristics, HBV exposure risk, HBV vaccination status, and KAP toward HBV. It was self-administered by participants in the health facilities. Members of the research team were present during completion to provide clarification if needed and to ensure that questionnaires were returned promptly. This questionnaire, crafted from KAP surveys drawing on previous studies in the field18–20, was scrutinized by three experienced doctors and validated through a pilot study involving 30 health workers, demonstrating acceptable reliability levels (Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.78 for knowledge, 0.72 for attitude, and 0.86 for practice).

Questionnaire and scoring system

The questionnaire comprised four sections: (1) a sociodemographic questionnaire (10 items), (2) a knowledge questionnaire (18 items), (3) an attitude questionnaire (11 items), and (4) a practice questionnaire (10 items). The scoring system for the knowledge and practice sections involved three response options (Yes, No, Not sure), with 0 assigned to incorrect or inappropriate responses and 1 to correct or appropriate responses. This yielded a knowledge score range of 0–18 and a practice score range of 0–10. Additionally, the attitude section employed a five-point scale (5: Strongly Agree, 4: Agree, 3: Not Sure, 2: Disagree, 1: Strongly Disagree), resulting in a score range of 11–55. The total scores were dichotomized using the median, categorizing individuals into High and Low KAP. This method of dichotomizing KAP scores using the median as the threshold has been applied in similar studies when no validated cut-offs exist and when distributions are not normal21. For example, Shrestha et al. (2023) dichotomized overall knowledge and attitude scores in their COVID-19 KAP study using the median as the cutoff22.

Study variables

The primary outcomes of this study were knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) regarding hepatitis B. Each was categorized as high or low based on pre-defined cut-off scores. High levels indicated adequate knowledge, positive attitude, or appropriate practice. Independent variables included gender (male or female), age (18–24, 25–29, 30–34, or ≥ 35 years), education level (diploma, bachelor’s degree, or master’s degree), profession (medical doctor, nurse, midwife, medical technologist, or other), economic status (self-reported as good, average, or poor), and professional experience (< 5 or ≥ 5 years). Work-related variables included health care setting (Herat Regional Hospital, Private Hospitals, Governmental Clinics, Private Clinics, or Doctors’ Offices), work hours per day (≤ 8 or > 8 h), and number of patients contacted per day (1–9, 10–19, 20–29, or ≥ 30 patients). Associations between independent variables and KAP were analyzed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Data analysis

The collected data were thoroughly checked for completeness and errors. Questionnaires with substantial missing data were excluded from the final analysis. All valid questionnaires were coded and entered into SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) for analysis. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study variables; frequencies and proportions were used for categorical data, while medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were used for quantitative variables that were not normally distributed. Bivariate analysis was conducted to assess the association between each independent variable and the outcome variable using the chi-square test. For the multivariate analysis, the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) composite score was dichotomized into ‘High KAP’ (scores ≥ median) and ‘Low KAP’ (scores < median), which served as the dependent variable21,22. An unconditional binary logistic regression model was then developed to identify the independent predictors of high KAP. The model was built using the ‘Enter’ method, where all independent variables assessed in the bivariate analysis were included simultaneously to control for potential confounders. Categorical variables were dummy coded, and a clinically and logically appropriate reference group was selected for each. Prior to the analysis, multicollinearity among the independent variables was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF < 10 considered acceptable. The overall model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed to determine the strength of association, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jami University on November 4, 2023, (J.2023.11.4). All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study. The confidentiality and privacy of the participants were protected throughout the study following the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical principles of research involving human subjects.

Among the 453 participants, males slightly outnumbered females (54.1%, n = 245 vs. 45.9%, n = 208). The largest age group was 25–29 years (40.6%, n = 184). Most reported ≤ 8 working hours per day (75.1%, n = 340), contact with 10–19 patients daily (48.6%, n = 220), and < 5 years of work experience (55.8%, n = 253). Regarding workplace, 32.5% (n = 147) were from Herat Regional Hospital, while only 6.6% (n = 30) worked in doctors’ offices. By education, 40.2% (n = 182) held bachelor’s degrees, and by profession, 35.5% (n = 161) were medical doctors. Economically, 40.6% (n = 184) reported average status (Table 1).

Overall, 39.7% (n = 180) of participants demonstrated high knowledge of HBV, with a mean overall knowledge score of 66.6% among healthcare workers. Most respondents recognized major complications: 85.9% (n = 389) knew HBV can cause cirrhosis, 76.6% (n = 347) identified it as a cause of liver cancer, and 84.8% (n = 384) acknowledged its potential fatality. Transmission routes were also well understood: 86.8% (n = 393) correctly identified spread through open wounds, 86.5% (n = 392) through unsterilized instruments, and 84.1% (n = 381) via contaminated blood or body fluids. Sexual transmission was recognized by 78.6% (n = 356), while casual contact such as handshaking was correctly rejected by 66.2% (n = 300). However, gaps remained. Only 29.6% (n = 134) were aware of early serological diagnosis, 29.1% (n = 132) knew about post-exposure prophylaxis, and 27.6% (n = 125) correctly answered that not all chronic HBV patients require medication (Table 2).

Overall, 44.6% (n = 202) of participants demonstrated a high attitude toward HBV, with an overall attitude score of 25. Concern about workplace exposure was widespread, with 60.0% (n = 272) strongly agreeing and 28.7% (n = 130) agreeing that they felt at risk. Most participants recognized the importance of vaccination, with 32.2% (n = 146) strongly disagreeing and 34.0% (n = 154) disagreeing that it is unnecessary, while 44.2% (n = 200) strongly agreed and 43.9% (n = 199) agreed that vaccination offers effective protection. Infection prevention was also highly valued: 64.5% (n = 292) strongly agreed and 30.0% (n = 136) agreed that HBV guidelines are essential, and 63.1% (n = 286) strongly agreed and 25.6% (n = 116) agreed that all patients should be tested prior to care. Negative perceptions were less common, as 42.4% (n = 192) disagreed and 19.9% (n = 90) strongly disagreed with discomfort in caring for infected patients, while 38.6% (n = 175) disagreed and 25.4% (n = 115) strongly disagreed with the statement that HBV is not a serious disease (Table 3).

Attitude towards hepatitis B among health workers in Afghanistan.

Overall, the practice score regarding HBV prevention was 50%, with 47.6% (n = 215) of participants demonstrating high practice. The majority (90.7%, n = 411) reported having a container for sharps and medical waste disposal. Vaccination coverage was moderate, with 62.7% (n = 284) having received HBV vaccination and 53.0% (n = 240) completing the full dosage regimen, but only 23.8% (n = 108) had undergone post-vaccination antibody testing. Infection control practices were suboptimal: 59.6% (n = 270) reported always changing gloves for each patient, 64.0% (n = 290) recommended infection control guidelines to relatives, and 58.3% (n = 264) reported needle-stick injuries when exposed. About one-third (35.1%, n = 159) experienced sharp injuries or exposure to blood/body fluids in the past year. Regarding post-exposure protection, 58.9% (n = 267) recommended PEP for HBV exposure. Finally, only 33.1% (n = 150) reported vaccinating their family members against HBV (Table 4).

Table 5 presents the univariate analysis of hepatitis B knowledge, attitude, and practice among healthcare workers in Herat. Knowledge was significantly higher among males (45.3%, n = 111) than females (33.2%, n = 69) (p = 0.009). Age showed significant differences, with participants ≥ 35 years reporting the highest knowledge (54.2%, n = 39) compared to those aged 25–29 (32.1%, n = 59) (p = 0.008). Education level was strongly associated with knowledge; those with a master’s degree had higher knowledge (53.3%, n = 57) than diploma holders (29.9%, n = 49) (p < 0.001). Profession also mattered, with medical doctors showing the highest knowledge (54.7%, n = 88) compared to nurses (25.5%, n = 24) (p < 0.001). Additionally, those working > 8 h per day had higher knowledge (48.7%, n = 55) than those working ≤ 8 h (36.8%, n = 125) (p = 0.025). Attitude was significantly different only by marital status, with married participants showing higher attitude (44.9%, n = 111) than singles (44.2%, n = 91) (p = 0.027). Practice differed significantly by hospital and work experience. Healthcare workers at Herat Regional Hospital demonstrated higher practice (53.1%, n = 78) compared to other facilities (p = 0.007). Participants with ≥ 5 years of experience also reported higher practice (53.0%, n = 106) than those with < 5 years (43.1%, n = 109) (p = 0.036).

Discussion

HBV infection remains a major global public health problem, particularly in LMIC countries. HCWs are at increased risk of exposure due to their frequent contact with blood and bodily fluids, making KAP toward HBV prevention critically important16,23. Adequate knowledge ensures awareness of transmission routes, positive attitudes encourage protective behaviors such as vaccination, and good practices reduce the likelihood of occupational exposure. Understanding KAP levels among HCWs is therefore essential for developing targeted interventions that safeguard both staff and patients9,10. This study assessed the KAP related to HBV among HCWs in Herat, Afghanistan. The findings revealed that only 39.7% (n = 180) of participants demonstrated high knowledge, 44.6% (n = 202) had a positive attitude, and 47.5% (n = 215) reported good practices toward HBV prevention. These results indicate considerable gaps in HCWs’ preparedness to protect themselves and their patients from HBV infection.

In this study, only 39.7% of HCWs in Herat (n = 180) demonstrated high overall knowledge, markedly lower than the 92.2% observed among Kabul HCWs (n = 463) and slightly lower than 63.5% among Kabul medical students (n = 81)16,17. Recognition of major transmission routes was high in both cities, but gaps remained in key aspects of HBV management. Kabul HCWs had better knowledge regarding diagnosis and treatment, including indications for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) and general management, whereas Herat HCWs demonstrated limited understanding in these areas. Attitudes were generally low, with 44.6% of Herat HCWs (n = 202) exhibiting high scores, slightly below the 46.0% favorable attitudes in Kabul (n = 231). Herat HCWs reported slightly less discomfort in caring for infected patients, whereas Kabul HCWs were more reluctant, though stigma persisted in both groups. Practice patterns differed notably. Kabul HCWs adhered more consistently to routine preventive measures such as glove changing, whereas Herat HCWs had better vaccination coverage and full-dose completion, with more consistent sharps disposal, while needle-stick injuries were more common in Kabul with suboptimal reporting. Post-vaccination antibody testing and PEP use were inadequate in both cities. Overall, Herat HCWs performed better in vaccination-related practices, whereas Kabul HCWs excelled in knowledge and adherence to routine preventive measures, highlighting persistent gaps in infection control16. Differences in HBV-related knowledge and practices between Herat and Kabul HCWs appear to reflect both structural and regional factors. As the capital, Kabul benefits from greater institutional resources, including tertiary hospitals and medical universities, which provide HCWs with stronger training in diagnosis and management. Herat, by contrast, shows higher vaccination coverage and safer disposal practices, influenced by immunization initiatives and its proximity to Iran, where vaccine uptake is high and hesitancy low24. Kabul’s geographical closeness to Pakistan, characterized by higher vaccine hesitancy and lower coverage, may partly account for weaker vaccination uptake despite greater educational infrastructure25. Nonetheless, persistent stigma, underreporting of needle-stick injuries, and limited post-exposure prophylaxis across both cities highlight systemic gaps in occupational health protections.

Compared with a study in Liberia, where 60.5% of HCWs demonstrated safe practices and 35.9% had completed HBV vaccination, our study revealed lower preventive practices, with only 47.6% achieving high practice scores. However, vaccination completion was higher in our cohort (53.0%, n = 240). Knowledge levels were also lower in our setting, with only 39.7% (n = 180) demonstrating high knowledge, compared to the generally higher correct responses reported among Liberian HCWs regarding HBV transmission and prevention. Attitudes toward HBV were positive in both settings, with around 75% of participants recognizing it as a serious occupational threat and supporting vaccination, while needle-stick and sharps injuries remained prevalent, affecting over 60% in Liberia and 35.1% in our cohort26.

When compared to Nigerian HCWs (n = 124), our cohort (n = 453) demonstrated lower overall HBV knowledge (39.7%, n = 180 vs. 78.2%, n = 97), particularly regarding early diagnosis, post-exposure prophylaxis, and chronic management. Awareness of major complications, such as liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, was high in both populations. Vaccination coverage was higher in our cohort, whereas perceived occupational risk remained similarly elevated across both groups. Notably, post-vaccination testing and adherence to infection control measures were suboptimal in our study27.

Compared to Ethiopian healthcare students (n = 246), our cohort (n = 453) also demonstrated lower overall HBV knowledge (39.7% vs. 81.3%), although knowledge regarding transmission and attitudes toward occupational risk and vaccination were moderately high in both groups. Vaccination coverage was markedly higher in our cohort, while adherence to practical infection control measures remained suboptimal in both populations20.

Compared with Saudi Arabian medical doctors, our cohort (n = 453) showed slightly higher overall HBV knowledge (66.6% vs. 62.9%), with both groups demonstrating strong awareness of major complications and high-risk transmission routes. Attitudes toward occupational risk and infection control were positive across both populations. Our participants reported high risk perception and valued vaccination, mirroring the Saudi doctors’ willingness to manage HBV patients and collaborate effectively with other healthcare professionals28. Nonetheless, vaccination coverage remains higher among Saudi HCWs (83.5%) compared to HCWs in Herat (62.7%)29.

The observed differences in HBV knowledge, preventive practices, and vaccination coverage across these populations appear to be shaped by a combination of training, experience, and institutional factors. Higher knowledge and safer practices among Liberian and Nigerian HCWs may reflect broader access to formal HBV training and continuous medical education, whereas lower knowledge in our cohort may stem from limited professional exposure and fewer educational opportunities. Interestingly, Saudi doctors, despite greater access to resources and training, scored slightly lower than Herat healthcare workers in the knowledge domain of their KAP study, likely because it employed more detailed and challenging questions than ours. By contrast, higher vaccination uptake in our participants likely reflects structured workplace immunization programs, greater vaccine availability, and institutional policies prioritizing HBV prevention. Persistent gaps in adherence to infection control measures across multiple settings, including our cohort, underscore challenges related to supervision, resource allocation, and reinforcement of safety protocols. Collectively, these findings highlight the critical influence of both individual and organizational factors in shaping HBV-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among healthcare workers.

In our study, medical doctors and HCWs with higher academic degrees consistently demonstrated greater knowledge, while practice improved with longer professional experience, a pattern that closely mirrors findings from Ethiopia where medical students outperformed nursing, midwifery, and anesthesia trainees in both knowledge and attitudes10. Similarly, in Liberia, age, education, and profession were significantly associated with knowledge; However, unlike in Herat, no meaningful associations were seen with attitudes or practices26. The Saudi Arabian data further illustrate that higher knowledge does not automatically translate into higher preventive behavior, as nurses and female HCWs reported greater vaccination uptake than medical doctors, suggesting that institutional policies, access, and sociocultural factors can override professional hierarchies in shaping practice28,29. Nigerian studies echo this decoupling, documenting reasonable awareness yet persistently low vaccination coverage, with education and workplace factors influencing outcomes. Collectively, these comparisons underscore a consistent gradient by profession and education across settings, but also highlight that practice behaviors are strongly mediated by system-level enablers, gender, and workplace structures rather than knowledge alone.

Implications for public health and policy

The findings of this study have important implications for both public health and occupational safety in Afghanistan and comparable LMICs. Suboptimal levels of KAP among HCWs highlight the need for systematic integration of HBV prevention into the curricula of nursing, midwifery, and allied health professions, alongside ongoing professional education for all cadres. At the institutional level, workplace vaccination programs should be expanded to ensure full-dose completion, supported by post-vaccination antibody testing, effective sharps disposal, and reliable systems for reporting needle-stick injuries. Establishing dedicated occupational health units within hospitals and clinics would further strengthen these measures. Regional differences in vaccination coverage, such as Herat’s higher uptake influenced by proximity to Iran compared with Kabul’s lower rates shaped by cross-border patterns of hesitancy, demonstrate the importance of harmonizing national policies to reduce geographic disparities. More broadly, prioritizing HBV prevention within Afghanistan’s health strategy is critical not only for protecting healthcare workers but also for advancing progress toward the World Health Organization’s target of hepatitis elimination by 203030. Tailored interventions that consider professional background, gender dynamics, and workplace structures will be essential to close existing gaps, safeguard frontline staff, and enhance the overall quality and safety of healthcare delivery. This study highlights substantial gaps in HBV-related KAP among HCWs in Herat, despite comparatively higher vaccination uptake. Strengthening training, ensuring universal vaccination and post-exposure prophylaxis, and embedding a culture of safety are critical to protect both staff and patients while advancing toward hepatitis elimination goals.

Strength and limitations

This study is the first large-scale assessment of HBV KAP among HCWs in Herat, covering diverse healthcare facilities. Strengths include the use of a validated questionnaire, pilot-tested with experienced doctors, and rigorous data checking with adherence to ethical standards. Convenience sampling enabled practical data collection across multiple sites. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. Convenience sampling and the lack of accurate data on the total number of HCWs in Herat may limit representativeness. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and self-administered, self-reported responses are vulnerable to recall and social desirability bias. In addition, restricting the study to a single city limits generalizability to all Afghan HCWs. Despite these constraints, the study provides valuable insights into HCWs’ KAP regarding HBV and establishes a foundation for future research and interventions.

Conclusion

This study provides the first large-scale assessment of HBV-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among HCWs in Herat, Afghanistan, highlighting important strengths and critical gaps. While vaccination uptake was comparatively higher than in several regional and international settings, overall knowledge and preventive practices were suboptimal, and stigma, underreporting of needle-stick injuries, and limited access to post-exposure prophylaxis persist. Comparisons with Kabul and other countries underscore the influence of educational exposure, institutional resources, and regional factors on HCWs’ preparedness. Subgroup analysis further demonstrates that higher academic training and longer professional experience improve knowledge and practice, but system-level enablers, workplace structures, and sociocultural factors remain decisive. To protect healthcare workers and patients, national health authorities and institutions must prioritize comprehensive HBV prevention strategies, including universal vaccination, post-vaccination testing, reliable reporting systems, and integration of HBV training into curricula and continuing education. Addressing these gaps is essential not only to safeguard frontline staff but also to align Afghanistan’s health system with global efforts toward HBV elimination by 2030.
