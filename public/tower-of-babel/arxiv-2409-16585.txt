Is speckle noise more challenging to mitigate than additive noise?
Reihaneh Malekian, Hao Xing, Arian Maleki

Abstract
We study the problem of estimating a function in the presence of both speckle and additive noises, commonly referred to as the de-speckling problem. Although additive noise has been thoroughly explored in nonparametric estimation, speckle noise, prevalent in applications such as synthetic aperture radar, ultrasound imaging, and digital holography, has not received as much attention. Consequently, there is a lack of theoretical investigations into the fundamental limits of mitigating the speckle noise.This paper is the first step in filling this gap. Our focus is on investigating the minimax estimation error for estimating a β \beta -Hölder continuous function and determining the rate of the minimax risk. Specifically, if n n represents the number of data points, f f denotes the underlying function to be estimated, ν ^ n \hat{\nu}_{n} is an estimate of f f , and σ n \sigma_{n} is the standard deviation of the additive Gaussian noise, then inf ν ^ n sup f 𝔼 f ​ ‖ ν ^ n − f ‖ 2 2 \inf_{\hat{\nu}_{n}}\sup_{f}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{\nu}_{n}-f\|^{2}_{2} decays at the rate ( max ⁡ ( 1 , σ n 4 ) / n ) 2 ​ β 2 ​ β + 1 \left(\max(1,\sigma_{n}^{4})/n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}} . Comparing this rate with the rate achieved under purely additive noise, namely ( σ n 2 / n ) 2 ​ β 2 ​ β + 1 \left(\sigma_{n}^{2}/n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}} , leads to the following insights: (i) When σ n = ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \sigma_{n}=\omega(1) , the additive noise appears to be the dominant component in the de-speckling problem. However, the presence of speckle noise significantly complicates the task of mitigating its effects. As a result, the risk increases from the rate ( σ n 2 / n ) 2 ​ β 2 ​ β + 1 \left(\sigma_{n}^{2}/n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}} , which characterizes the problem with only additive noise, to ( σ n 4 / n ) 2 ​ β 2 ​ β + 1 \left(\sigma_{n}^{4}/n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}} in the presence of both speckle and additive noise. (ii) When σ n = o ⁡ ( 1 ) \sigma_{n}=o(1) , the variance of the additive noise does not contribute to the risk in the de-speckling problem. This suggests that, in this regime, speckle noise is the primary bottleneck. Interestingly, the resulting risk rate matches the rate for mitigating purely additive noise with σ n = Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \sigma_{n}=\Theta(1) . (iii) When σ n = Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \sigma_{n}=\Theta(1) , the two rates coincide, suggesting that both the speckle noise and additive noise are contributing to the overall error.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation and Problem Set-up

Denoising—the task of removing additive noise superimposed on a function—is a fundamental problem in signal processing and statistics. It is mathematically formulated as the problem of estimating an unknown function
f
from noisy observations of the form

\widetilde{y}_{i}=f(x_{i})+w_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n,

(1)

where the noise terms
w_{i}
are independent and identically distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
. Due to its significance in a wide range of applications, this problem has been the subject of extensive theoretical study, with substantial contributions dating back at least seven decades
Nadaraya, 1964
;
Watson, 1964
;
Stone, 1977
;
Stone, 1980
;
Stone, 1982
;
Katkovnik, 1979
;
Cleveland, 1979
;
Fano, 1952
;
Le Cam, 1953
;
Nemirovskii, 1985
;
Nemirovskii A.S. and Tsybakov, 1985
;
Kerkyacharian and Picard, 1992
;
Donoho and Johnstone, 1994
;
Korostelev and Tsybakov, 1993
;
Donoho, 1999
;
Maleki et al., 2012
;
Maleki et al., 2013
;
Arias-Castro et al., 2012
;
DeVore et al., 2025
.
For a comprehensive summary of historical developments, we refer the reader to
Tsybakov, 2009
and
DeVore et al., 2025
and the references therein.

Despite a rich theoretical literature on the denoising problem, the related challenge of de-speckling has remained virtually unexamined from a theoretical standpoint. Speckle noise is among the most significant sources of distortion in coherent imaging systems, including Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)
(
Lopez-Martinez and Fabregas, 2003
)
, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)
(
Schmitt et al., 1999
)
, ultrasound imaging
(
Achim et al., 2001
)
, and digital holography
(
Bianco et al., 2018
)
. Although numerous practical de-speckling methods have been developed, the theoretical foundations and fundamental limits of this problem remain largely unexplored. In this work, we aim to present the first theoretical analysis of the de-speckling problem.

Towards this goal, we consider the problem of recovering a function
f
from its noise-corrupted samples in the presence of both additive and multiplicative noise. Specifically, suppose we are provided with observations, denoted as

y_{i}=f\left(x_{i}\right)\xi_{i}+\tau_{i},\ \ \ \ \ i=1,2,\ldots,n.

(2)

Here,
\xi_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
represents the multiplicative noise and
\tau_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
denotes the additive noise. The objective is to estimate the function
f
based on these observations.

Remark 1
.

In our model, we assume that the speckle noise follows a Gaussian distribution. This is motivated by the fact that speckle arises from the interference of coherent wavefronts scattered by microscopic surface variations. By the central limit theorem, physicists have shown that in most cases, speckle noise can be well-approximated by zero-mean Gaussian noise
Goodman, 1975
. In practice, this model is referred to as fully-developed speckle
Argenti et al., 2013
.

Remark 2
.

In
eq.

2
, we have fixed the standard deviation of the speckle noise to be
1
. This is due to the fact that increasing the standard deviation of the multiplicative noise is the same as decreasing the standard deviation of the additive noise. More specifically, if
\xi_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\xi}^{2})
, then we can rescale our data to obtain

\check{y}_{i}=\frac{y_{i}}{\sigma_{\xi}}=f\left(x_{i}\right)\check{\xi}_{i}+\frac{\tau_{i}}{\sigma_{\xi}},

where
\check{\xi}_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\frac{\tau_{i}}{\sigma_{\xi}}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}})
.
In other words, assuming that
\sigma_{\xi}
is known, the problem of estimating
f
from
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
is equivalent to the problem of estimating
f
from
\check{Y}_{1},\ldots,\check{Y}_{n}
in which the variance of the multiplicative noise is equal to
1
. Hence, without loss of generality we set
\sigma_{\xi}^{2}=1
.

In this paper, we aim to answer the following questions:

•

How well can we estimate
f
from the observations
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}
?

•

How is the complexity of this problem compared with the complexity of the denoising problem in (
1
).

To address these questions, following the long history of the de-noising problem (see
Tsybakov, 2009
), we adopt the minimax framework. The first step in this framework is to consider a class of functions for
f(\cdot)
. In
Tsybakov, 2009
, it is assumed that the function
f
is
\beta
-Hölder continuous, i.e. it belongs to the following class of functions:

\Sigma(\beta,L):=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\ |f^{(k)}(x)-f^{(k)}(y)|\leq L|x-y|^{\beta-k}\},

where
L
and
\beta
are two positive constants, and
k
is the largest integer strictly less than
\beta
. However, due to some specific features of the speckle noise problem, throughout this paper we consider the functions
f
to be in the following subclass of
\beta
-Hölder functions:

\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L):=\{f\in\Sigma(\beta,L):0<\mathfrak{h}\leq f(x)\leq 1\}.

(3)

In
appendix

A
, we present the rationale for the choices we have made in the definition of
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)
.

Using the minimax framework we measure the difficulty level of our estimation problem as:

R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}):=\inf_{\hat{\nu}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|f-\hat{\nu}\|^{2}_{2},

(4)

and

R_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}):=\inf_{\hat{\nu}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|f-\hat{\nu}\|_{\infty},

(5)

where in both equations
\hat{\nu}
denotes an estimator of function
f
based on the observations
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}
in
eq.

2
.

Therefore, the aim of the rest of this paper is to calculate
R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
and
R_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
defined in
eq.

4
and
eq.

5
and compare them with the corresponding results for the denoising problem.

To distinguish the classical model (with only additive noises) from ours, we shall use
\widetilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
and
\widetilde{R}_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
for the denoising problem where estimators are based on
\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{y}_{n}
. As mentioned before, the minimax risk has been used extensively in the past for the denoising problem. For instance,
Tsybakov, 2009
(Corollary 1.2, Theorem 1.8), and
Korostelev and Tsybakov, 1993
(Example 2.7.1, Example 2.7.3) lead to the following conclusion:

Theorem 3
.

For the denoising problem mentioned in (
1
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}):=&\inf_{\hat{\nu}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|f-\hat{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\\
=&\Theta_{\beta,L}\left(\min\left\{1,\max\left\{n^{-2\beta},\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right\}\right\}\right).\end{split}

(6)

Furthermore, if
\sigma_{n}^{2}=O(n^{1-\eta})
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{R}_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}):=&\inf_{\hat{\nu}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|f-\hat{\nu}\|_{\infty}\\
=&\Theta_{\beta,L}\left(\max\left\{n^{-2\beta},\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right\}\right)\end{split}

(7)

where in these two equations
\hat{\nu}
is an estimator that uses the data points from
eq.

1
.

Since the classical literature focuses only on the case where
\sigma_{n}=\Theta(1)
, we include a self-contained proof of
theorem

3
in
appendix

B
.
However, we should emphasize that our proof is a minor modification of the proof presented in Sections 1.6, 2.5 and 2.6 of
Tsybakov, 2009
.

The aim of the rest of this paper is to calculate
R_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
and
R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
defined in
eq.

4
and
eq.

5
and compare them with the corresponding results for the denoising problem.

Main contributions

As described before, our main goal is to calculate
R_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
and
R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
defined in
eq.

4
and
eq.

5
and compare them with the corresponding results for the denoising problem. Since in imaging applications the samples are equispaced, we consider the following model for the data:

Y_{i}=f\left(i/n\right)\xi_{i}+\tau_{i},

where
\xi_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\tau_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,{\sigma}^{2}_{n})
. Our first result establishes the minimax risk
R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
.

Theorem 4
.

We have

R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)=\Theta_{\beta,L,\mathfrak{h}}\left(\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{\max(1,\sigma_{n}^{4})}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right\}\right).

(9)

To compare
theorem

4
with the minimax error analysis of the classical additive noise model (cf.
theorem

3
), we discuss the following three settings:

1.

Strong additive noise,
\sigma_{n}=\omega(1)
: In this case, we can see that
R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{4}}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right)
, while
\tilde{R}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right)
.
Comparing the two cases reveals that the error decays more slowly in the de-speckling problem. This suggests that, although additive noise may appear to be the dominant component, the presence of even seemingly small speckle noise significantly complicates the task of mitigating the additive noise.

2.

Medium additive noise,
\sigma_{n}=\Theta(1)
: In this case, we can see that

R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)=\Theta(\tilde{R_{2}}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)).

In other words, order-wise, in this case, the de-speckling and de-noising problems are equally hard.

3.

Weak additive noise,
\sigma_{n}=o(1)
: In this case, we can see that

R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)\ll\tilde{R_{2}}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right).

Furthermore, the variance of the additive noise does not appear in
R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)
. This indicates that, in this case, the bottleneck in estimating the function
f(\cdot)
from
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}
is the speckle noise, and that the contribution of the additive noise is negligible.

Our second theorem aims to calculate
R_{\infty}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)
.

Theorem 5
.

We have for any
\eta\in(0,1)
and
\sigma_{n}^{4}=o(n^{1-\eta})
,

\displaystyle R_{\infty}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)=\Theta_{\beta,L,\eta,\mathfrak{h}}\left(\left(\max(1,\sigma_{n}^{4})\left(\log n\over n\right)\right)^{\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right)

(10)

All the remarks we made about
theorem

4
are also true about
theorem

5
. Hence, we do not repeat them here.

3.1
Preliminaries

In this section, we review some classical results that will be used in our main proofs.

The first lemma characterizes one of the minimax risks studied in this paper.

Lemma 6
.

All the functions
R_{\infty}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)
,
R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)
,
\tilde{R}_{\infty}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)
, and
\tilde{R}_{\infty}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)
are nondecreasing functions of
\sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

The monotonicity of the minimax risk with respect to the variance of additive Gaussian noise in denoising problems is a well-established result
Donoho and Johnstone, 1994
. However, for the sake of completeness—and because this monotonicity has not been established in the specific setting of our paper—we provide a proof for
R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})
below. Consider the two cases,

\displaystyle\begin{split}Y_{i}=f(i/n)\zeta_{i}+\tau_{i},\\
\tilde{Y}_{i}=f(i/n)\tilde{\zeta}_{i}+\tilde{\tau}_{i}.\end{split}

(11)

where
\zeta_{i}\sim N(0,1)
,
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}\sim N(0,1)
,
\tau_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}_{n})
, and
\tilde{\tau}_{i}\sim N(0,\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2})
. Furthermore, our assumption is that
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}>\sigma_{n}
. Our goal is to show that

R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})\leq R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\tilde{\sigma}_{n}).

Suppose that
\hat{f}(\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{n})
is an arbitrary estimator that is based on
\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{n}
. Based on this estimator we are going to construct an estimator that can be applied to
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
. We first generate independent random variables
\omega_{i}\sim N(0,\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2}-\sigma_{n}^{2}})
, and construct the estimate:
\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})
. By our construction we have that

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}(\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{Y}
denote the conditional expectation given the random variables
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Y}\|\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}\right]\\
\geq&\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbb{E}_{Y}\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}\right],\end{split}

(12)

where the last inequality is a result of Jensen’s inequality. Note that
\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})]
is a valid estimator that is only using information in
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
.

Hence, for every estimator
\hat{f}(\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{n})
we have

\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{E}_{Y}\hat{f}({Y}_{1}+\omega_{1},{Y}_{2}+\omega_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}+\omega_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}(\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2}

Hence,

\inf_{\hat{\nu}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\|(\hat{\nu}({Y}_{1}{Y}_{2},\ldots,{Y}_{n}))-f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\inf_{\hat{\nu}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\nu}(\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{Y}_{n})-f\|_{2}^{2},

which implies:

R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})\leq R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\tilde{\sigma}_{n}).

In our proofs, we will be using the properties of subGaussian and subexponential random variables. Recall that a variable
X
is called
sub-gaussian
with
sub-gaussian norm

\|X\|_{\rm subgau}
if

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\rm subgau}:=\inf\left\{t>0:\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}/t^{2}\right]\leq 2\right\}

is bounded. Similarly,
X
is called
sub-exponential
with
sub-exponential norm

\|X\|_{\rm subexp}
if

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\rm subexp}:=\inf\left\{t>0:\mathbb{E}\left[|X|/t\right]\leq 2\right\}

is bounded. The following lemma summarizes some of the useful properties of subGaussian and subexponential random variables.

Lemma 7
(Lemma 2.7.6, Lemma 2.7.7 and Exercise 2.7.10 of
Vershynin, 2018
).

The following is true for sub-gaussian and sub-exponential random variables

1.

A standard normal Gaussian random variable
\xi\sim N(0,1)
is sub-gaussian.

2.

Random variable
X

is sub-gaussian if and only if
X^{2}
is sub-exponential, and if this the case,
\|X^{2}\|_{\rm subexp}=\|X\|_{\rm subgau}
.

3.

Let
X
and
Y
be
sub-gaussian random variables. Then
XY
is sub-exponential with
\|XY\|_{\rm subexp}\leq\|X\|_{\rm subgau}\|Y\|_{\rm subgau}

4.

Let
X
be a sub-gaussian (resp. sub-exponential random variable), then there exists an absolute constant
C
such that
\|X-\mathbb{E}X\|_{\rm subgau}\leq C\|X\|_{\rm subgau}
(resp.
\|X-\mathbb{E}X\|_{\rm subexp}\leq C\|X\|_{\rm subexp}
)

The next two lemmas are two basic concentration results, one for simple quadratic functions of Gaussian random vectors, and the second one for the concentration of sum of subexponential random variables.

Lemma 8
.

(
Laurent and Massart, 2000
)

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}
be iid random variables distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. Then for all

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}Z^{2}_{i}-\sum_{i}a_{i}\geq 2\|a\|_{2}\sqrt{t}+2\|a\|_{\infty}t\right)\leq e^{-t},

and

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}Z^{2}_{i}-\sum_{i}a_{i}\leq-2\|a\|_{2}\sqrt{t}\right)\leq e^{-t}.

Lemma 9
.

[Berstein’s inequality for sub-exponential distributions. Theorem 2.8.1,
Vershynin, 2018
]
Let
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
be independent mean zero, sub-exponential random variables. Then, for

, we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}\right|\geq t\right\}\leq\exp\left[-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|Z_{j}\right\|_{\rm subexp}^{2}},\frac{t}{\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\|Z_{j}\right\|_{\rm subexp}}\right)\right]

(13)

The next lemma is a version of the well-known Berry-Esseen theorem.

Lemma 10
.

(non-iid Berry-Esseen theorem
(
Berry, 1941
)
)
Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}
be independent, zero mean random variables with finite second moment. Suppose that there exists a fixed number
\kappa
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{\mathbb{E}|Z_{i}|^{3}}{\mathbb{E}(Z_{i})^{2}}\leq\kappa.

If
F,\Phi
denote the CDFs of
\frac{\sum_{i}Z_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}Z_{i}^{2}}}
and
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
respectively, then there exists a constant

Lemma 12

The case

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(\xi_{12})|\leq\frac{|f(a_{1})|+|f(a_{2})|}{|a_{1}-a_{2}|}\leq\frac{2}{1/3}=6

Lemma 13

There exists a constant

By Leibnitz’s rule for derivatives, we have

\displaystyle(fg)^{(k)}(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}{k\choose r}f^{(k-r)}(x)g^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|f^{(k-r)}(x)g^{(r)}(x)-f^{(k-r)}(y)g^{(r)}(y)\right|\\

\overset{(b)}{\leq}&2c_{\beta,L}^{2}|x-y|\leq 2c_{\beta,L}^{2}|x-y|^{\beta-k},\end{split}

\displaystyle\left|f^{(k)}(x)g(x)-f^{(k)}(y)g(y)\right|

\displaystyle\left|f^{(k)}(x)g(x)-f^{(k)}(y)g(x)\right|+\left|f^{(k)}(y)g(x)-f^{(k)}(y)g(y)\right|

\displaystyle 2Lc_{\beta,L}|x-y|^{\beta-k}.

\displaystyle\left|g^{(k)}(x)f(x)-g^{(k)}(y)f(y)\right|\leq 2L|x-y|^{\beta-k}.

Proof of the upper bound of

In this subsection, to obtain an upper bound, we evaluate the performance of the modified version of the local polynomial estimator (LPE) approach presented in Section 1.6 of

When

, we can take the estimator

\displaystyle R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}\right)\leq\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1

To an obtain an estimate of

K(u)>0
for

. To simplify our calculations we consider the following kernel

K(u):=\frac{1}{2}I(|u|\leq 1).

(40)

Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(u):=

(41)

For a given bandwidth

we define the function

denotes the variance of the estimator. Our aim is to uniformly bound

Proposition 18

Under our choice of kernel function as in

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma^{2}(x)=&\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\left(\hat{g}_{n}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\hat{g}_{n}(x)\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\

, we always have
nh_{n}\to\infty
. In this case

\widetilde{h}_{n}^{2\beta}\sim\left(\frac{\max\left(1,\sigma_{n}^{4}\right)}{n}\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}.

If we plug in this choice of
\tilde{h}_{n}
with (
54
) we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left[\hat{f}_{n}(x)-f(x)\right]^{2}dx\right]=O\left(\left(\frac{\max\left(1,\sigma_{n}^{4}\right)}{n}\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right).

We can now finish the proof with the following simple steps:

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{2}\left(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)\right)=&\inf_{\hat{f}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&\inf_{\hat{f}_{n}\geq 0}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
\overset{(a)}{\leq}&\mathfrak{h}^{2}\inf_{\hat{g}_{n}\geq 0}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-f^{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&\mathfrak{h}^{2}\inf_{\hat{g}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-f^{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O_{\beta,L,\mathfrak{h}}\left(\left(\frac{\max\left(1,\sigma_{n}^{4}\right)}{n}\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right).\end{split}

(56)

where for
(a)
we have used that assumption that
f\geq\mathfrak{h}
.

3.5
Proof of the lower bound of
theorem

5

Our proof strategy for the lower bound mirrors that of
theorem

4
. As a result, we will omit some steps and focus on explaining the construction of the set
\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{m}\}
. Note that our construction is simpler than the one in
section

3.3
. For
\sigma_{n}^{4}=O\left(n^{1-\eta}\right)
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, let

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\middle/\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}.

(57)

Define
\nu_{0}:=1
, and for
j=1,2,\ldots,m,\,m=[\delta_{n}^{-1}]
, define
\nu_{j}:=f_{j,n}
, where

\displaystyle f_{j,n}(x):=1-\delta_{n}^{\beta}\phi\left(\frac{x-b_{j}}{\delta_{n}}\right),\quad b_{j}=(2j-1)\frac{\delta_{n}}{2}.

Similar to
lemma

16
, we can prove that
f_{j,n}\in\Sigma(\beta,L)
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Also, if
\phi^{*}
denotes the maximum of the function
\phi
, then we will have

\displaystyle\|\nu_{j}-\nu_{k}\|_{\infty}=\phi^{*}\delta_{n}^{\beta},

(58)

where
\phi^{*}:=\lVert\phi\rVert_{\infty}:=\sup_{x}|\phi(x)|.

Our next goal is to use
lemma

14
with
d
being the
L_{\infty}
distance. Towards this goal, first we need to calculate the likelihood ratio, which is

\displaystyle\Lambda\left(\nu_{0},\nu_{j}\right)=\prod_{i}\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma}^{2}_{n}+\nu_{j}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}}\exp\left[\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{2({\sigma}^{2}_{n}+\nu_{j}^{2}\left(i/n\right))}-\sum\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{2(1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n})}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{{\sigma}^{2}_{n}+\nu_{j}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}-\frac{1}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\log\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\nu_{j}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{2(1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n})}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\nu_{j}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}-1\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\log\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\nu_{j}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}\right].

Now to guarantee the second condition of
lemma

14
, we need to prove the following lemma:

Lemma 19
.

Let
\nu_{j},j=0,1,...,m
be defined as above, then there exists some
\lambda<1
, such that for all
j=1,\ldots,m
,

\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\Lambda\left(\nu_{0},\nu_{j}\right)>m^{-\lambda_{j}}\right)>p^{*},

whenever
\lambda_{j}<\lambda
.

The proof will be given in
section

3.5.1
. Combining the results of
lemma

14
(with
M=m
) and
lemma

19
gives us

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\|\hat{\nu}_{n}-\nu_{j}\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{\delta_{n}^{\beta}\phi^{*}}{2}\right)\geq 1/4.

Using Markov’s inequality, for any estimator
\hat{\nu}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\|\hat{\nu}_{n}-\nu_{j}\|_{\infty}\right)\geq\frac{\delta_{n}^{\beta}\phi^{*}}{8}.

Hence, in the case
\sigma_{n}^{4}=O(n^{1-\eta})
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),{\sigma}_{n})\geq\frac{\delta_{n}^{\beta}\phi^{*}}{8}=

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\middle/\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{\eta}\left(\left(\max(1,\sigma_{n}^{4})\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right).

3.5.1
Proof of
lemma

19

We need to show that there exists
p^{*}>0
independent of
j,n
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that for
j=1,\ldots,m
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+f_{j,n}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}-1\right)+\sum_{i}\log\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+f_{j,n}^{2}\left(i/n\right)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right)\\
&>p^{*}.\end{split}

(59)

If we put
s_{j,i}:=\frac{1-f_{j.n}^{2}(i/n)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1},
then
eq.

59
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left[\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{1-s_{j,i}}-1\right)+\sum_{i}\log(1-s_{j,i})>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right]>p^{*}.

(60)

Note that according to Taylor expansion, we know that for some
\epsilon_{j,i},\epsilon_{j,i}^{\prime}\in\left(0,s_{j,i}\right),
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-s_{j,i}}=1+s_{j,i}+\frac{s_{j,i}^{2}}{\left(1-\epsilon_{j,i}\right)^{3}}

\displaystyle\log(1-s_{j,i})=-s_{j,i}-s_{j,i}^{2}\frac{1}{2\left(1-\epsilon_{j,i}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}.

Using these two equations, we can simplify the problem of showing
eq.

60
to the problem of proving:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left[\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}\left(s_{j,i}+\frac{s_{j,{i}}^{2}}{\left(1-\epsilon_{j,i}\right)^{3}}\right)-\sum_{i}\left(s_{j,i}+\frac{s_{j,i}^{2}}{2\left(1-\epsilon_{j,i}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}\right)>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right]>p^{*}.\end{split}

(61)

For large enough
n
, we have

\displaystyle 0<\epsilon_{j,i},\epsilon_{j,i}^{\prime}<s_{j,i}\leq 2\delta_{n}^{\beta}\phi^{*}<\frac{1}{2}.

(62)

Hence to prove
eq.

61
it is sufficient to prove

\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left[\sum_{i}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}\left(s_{j,i}+s_{j,i}^{2}\right)-\sum_{i}\left(s_{j,i}+2s_{j,i}^{2}\right)>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right]>p^{*}\end{aligned},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left[\sum_{i}s_{j,i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1\right)+\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-2\right)>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right]>p^{*}.

(63)

Using
lemma

8
, we have with the choice
t=\log 3
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{{\sigma}^{2}_{n}+f_{j,n}^{2}{(i/n)}}\right)s_{j,i}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+f_{j,n}^{2}(i/n)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}\leq\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+f_{j,n}^{2}(i/n)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}\right.\\
&\left.-2\sqrt{\log 3}\sqrt{\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{4}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+f_{j,n}^{2}(i/n)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}.\end{split}

(64)

Recalling the definition of
s_{j,i}
, we know that
1-s_{j,i}=\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}+f_{j,n}^{2}(i/n)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+1}
. Hence
eq.

64
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{{\sigma}^{2}_{n}+f_{j,n}^{2}{(i/n)}}\right)s_{j,i}^{2}(1-s_{j,i})\leq\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}(1-s_{j,i})\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2\sqrt{\log 3}\sqrt{\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{4}(1-s_{j,i})^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Therefore to show
eq.

63
, we only need to show for some
p^{*}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{j,i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1\right)-\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}(1+s_{j,i})-2\sqrt{\log 3}\sqrt{\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{4}}>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right)\\
&>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.\end{split}

(65)

Note that
\forall r\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(1-f_{j,n}(i/n))^{r}=\delta_{n}^{r\beta}\sum_{i}\phi^{r}\left(\frac{i/n-b_{j}}{\delta_{n}}\right)=\delta_{n}^{r\beta+1}n\sum_{i}\frac{1}{n\delta_{n}}\phi^{r}\left(\frac{i/n-b_{j}}{\delta_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{n}^{r\beta+1}n\left(\|\phi\|_{r}^{r}+o(1)\right).

Moreover,
\frac{1}{1+\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left(1-f_{j,n}(i/n)\right)<s_{j,i}<\frac{2}{1+\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left(1-f_{j,n}(i/n)\right)
, and therefore we have

\displaystyle{1\over(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{r}}\delta_{n}^{r\beta+1}n\left(\|\phi\|_{r}^{r}+o(1)\right)<\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{r}<{2^{r}\over(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{r}}\delta_{n}^{r\beta+1}n\left(\|\phi\|_{r}^{r}+o(1)\right).

(66)

If we use the upper bounds for
\sum s_{j,i}^{2},\sum s_{j,i}^{3}
, and
\sum s_{j,i}^{4}
, to guarantee
eq.

65
it is enough to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{j,i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1\right)-{4\over(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\delta_{n}^{2\beta+1}n\left(\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}+o(1)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-{8\over(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{3}}\delta_{n}^{3\beta+1}n\left(\|\phi\|_{3}^{3}+o(1)\right)-2\sqrt{\log 3}\sqrt{{16\over(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{4}}\delta_{n}^{4\beta+1}n\left(\|\phi\|_{4}^{4}+o(1)\right)}>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right)

\displaystyle>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.

Now our choice of
\delta_{n}
gives
{\delta_{n}^{2\beta+1}n\over(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}=\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}
and substitute it into the above inequality, it suffices to establish

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{j,i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1\right)-4\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}+o(1)\right)\right.\\
&\left.-8\delta_{n}^{\beta}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(\|\phi\|_{3}^{3}+o(1)\right)-2\sqrt{\log 3}\sqrt{16\delta_{n}^{2\beta}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(\|\phi\|_{4}^{4}+o(1)\right)}>-2\lambda_{j}\log m\right)\\
&>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.\end{split}

(67)

Rearranging the terms and noticing that
\delta_{n}=\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\middle/\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}
is bounded and that

\log m=\log\left[\delta_{n}^{-1}\right]=\frac{1}{2\beta+1}\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}-\log\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right),

(68)

and by merging all lower order terms involving
o\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)
, it suffices to show

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{j,i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1\right)-4\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}+o\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)>-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{2\beta+1}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)\\
&>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.\end{split}

(69)

Equivalently, we need to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{j,i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1\right)>4\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}+o\left(\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{2\beta+1}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.

Since

\displaystyle\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(\|\phi\|_{r}^{r}+o(1)\right)<\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}<4\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(\|\phi\|_{r}^{r}+o(1)\right),

(70)

it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{s_{j,i}}{\sqrt{2\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}\left(1-s_{j,i}\right)^{2}}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1+s_{j,i}\right)\right.\\
&\left.>\frac{\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(9\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{2\beta+1}+o(1)\right)}{\sqrt{2\sum_{i}s_{j,i}^{2}\left(1-s_{j,i}\right)^{2}}}\right)>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.\end{split}

(71)

From
eq.

62
we have
0<s_{j,i}<\frac{1}{2}
, therefore it suffices to show

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{s_{j,i}}{\sqrt{2\sum s_{j,i}^{2}\left(1-s_{j,i}\right)^{2}}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{1+{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}-1+s_{j,i}\right)\right.\\
&\left.>\frac{\sqrt{2}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(9\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{2\beta+1}+o(1)\right)}{\sqrt{\sum s_{j,i}^{2}}}\right)>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.\end{split}

(72)

Now by
lemma

10
, the LHS in
eq.

72
is almost a standard Gaussian random variable. Hence, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1)>\frac{\sqrt{2}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(9\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{2\beta+1}+o(1)\right)}{\sqrt{\sum s_{j,i}^{2}}}\right)>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.

Using
eq.

66
and bounding
\sum s_{j,i}^{2}
, the above inequality is guaranteed by showing

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1)>\frac{\sqrt{10}\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}\left(9\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{2\beta+1}+o(1)\right)}{\sqrt{\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}}\|\phi\|_{2}}\right)>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1)>\sqrt{10\log\frac{n}{(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}}}\left(9\|\phi\|_{2}-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{\|\phi\|_{2}(2\beta+1)}+o(1)\right)\right)>p^{*}+\frac{1}{3}.

(73)

Now if
\lambda_{j}:=\frac{1}{2}
and
\phi
is such that
\|\phi\|_{2}<\frac{1}{4\sqrt{(2\beta+1)}}
(Notice that we can always make
\phi
small enough by scaling.), then we have

9\|\phi\|_{2}-\frac{2\lambda_{j}}{\|\phi\|_{2}(2\beta+1)}+o(1)<0

and
eq.

73
holds with
p^{*}=\frac{1}{6}
.
Justification of the step that we have used
lemma

10
in the above argument is the same as
section

3.3.1
and therefore skipped.

3.6
Proof of the upper bound of
theorem

5

As in
section

3.4
, we consider the estimator

\hat{g}_{n}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)\cdot W_{ni}^{*}(x),

(74)

where

Y_{i}=f(i/n)\xi_{i}+\tau_{i}.

(75)

From the bias-variance decomposition we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-f^{2}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\\
\leq&\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbb{E}\hat{g}_{n}-f^{2}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\hat{g}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\\
=&\left\|\mathbb{E}\hat{g}_{n}-f^{2}\right\|_{\infty}+\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\hat{g}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\end{split}

For the first part, as in
section

3.4
, we have uniformly in
x\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{g}_{n}(x)\right]-f^{2}(x)\right|\\
=&\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(i/n)W_{ni}^{*}(x)-f^{2}(x)\right|\\
=&O(h_{n}^{\beta}).\end{split}

(76)

For the second part,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\hat{g}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[f^{2}(i/n)(\xi_{i}^{2}-1)+(\tau_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{n}^{2})+2f(i/n)\xi_{i}\tau_{i}\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\\
\leq&\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[f^{2}(i/n)(\xi_{i}^{2}-1)\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}+\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\tau_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{n}^{2}\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\\
&+\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[2f(i/n)\xi_{i}\tau_{i}\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[f^{2}(i/n)(\xi_{i}^{2}-1)\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\tau_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}-1\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\\
&+\sigma_{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[2f(i/n)\xi_{i}\tau_{i}/\sigma_{n}\right]W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\end{split}

(77)

Note that each of the three terms above can be written as
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right|
where
\zeta_{i},i=1,2,..,n
represents everything except for
W_{ni}^{*}(x)
part. As the first step in finding an upper bound for the above three terms, we plug in
W_{ni}^{*}(x)
given in (
42
), and simplify the expression in the following way:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}W_{ni}^{*}(x)\right|&=\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\mathbf{U}^{T}(0)\mathcal{B}_{nx}^{-1}\mathbf{U}\left(\frac{i/n-x}{h_{n}}\right)K\left(\frac{i/n-x}{h_{n}}\right)\right|\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\mathbf{U}^{T}(0)\mathcal{B}_{nx}^{-1}S_{i}(x)\right|\overset{(b)}{\leq}\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\left\|\mathcal{B}_{nx}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x)\right\|_{2}\\
&\overset{(c)}{\leq}\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}nh_{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x)\right\|_{2},\end{split}

(78)

where we have used the following facts to obtain the above relations:

•

Equality (a): To obtain this equality we have defined:

\displaystyle S_{i}(x):=\mathbf{U}\left(i/n-x\over h_{n}\right)K\left(i/n-x\over h_{n}\right).

(79)

•

Inequality (b): This inequality is an application of Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

•

Inequality (c): To obtain this inequality we have used part (4) of
proposition

18
and have set
\lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{C_{*}}

We should mention that the above steps have been used in Section 1.6.2 of
Tsybakov, 2009
for the classical nonparametric regression.

Since the function
K
and all components of
\mathbf{U}
are bounded and Lipchitz on
[-1,1]
, there exists
L_{S}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|S_{i}(x)-S_{i}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\frac{L_{s}}{h_{n}}|x-x^{\prime}|

(80)

Now set
M=n^{2}
and define
x_{j}:=j/M
for
j=1,2,...,M
. We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}&\zeta_{i}W_{ni}^{*}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}nh_{n}}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x)\right\|_{2}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}nh_{n}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x_{j})\right\|_{2}+\sup_{|x-x^{\prime}|\leq 1/M}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\left(S_{i}(x)-S_{j}(x^{\prime})\right)\right\|_{2}\right)\\
\leq&\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}\sqrt{nh_{n}}}\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x_{j})\right\|_{2}+\frac{L_{s}}{\lambda_{0}Mnh_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\zeta_{i}|\\
=&\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}\sqrt{nh_{n}}}\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\left\|Z_{j}\right\|_{2}+\frac{L_{s}}{\lambda_{0}Mnh_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\zeta_{i}|,\end{split}

(81)

where to obtain the last equality we have used the definition:

\displaystyle Z_{j}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x_{j}).

(82)

Note that for our choice of
M
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{L_{s}}{\lambda_{0}Mnh_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\zeta_{i}|\right]=O\left(1\over n^{2}h_{n}^{2}\right).

(83)

Now for each
1\leq j\leq M
, define

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{j}:=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}S_{i}(x_{j})\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\mathbf{U}\left(i/n-x_{j}\over h_{n}\right)K\left(i/n-x_{j}\over h_{n}\right)\end{split}

(84)

We will show (after choosing an optimized
h_{n}
) that
\mathbb{E}(\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\|Z_{j}\|_{2})
grows at the rate of

In this paper, we presented the first minimax analysis of recovering a smooth function

Appendix A
Rationale for De-Speckling Modeling Assumptions

In this section, we describe key properties of the de-speckling problem that motivated our choice of the function class
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)
. From the model formulation,

y_{i}=f(x_{i})\,\xi_{i}+\tau_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n,

it is evident that the symmetry of the noise introduces an identifiability issue: both
f
and
-f
produce identical observations. However, the identifiability challenges extend beyond this simple symmetry. For example, the functions
f(x)=x-\frac{1}{2}
and
g(x)=\left|x-\frac{1}{2}\right|
, defined on
[0,1]
, also lead to identical observations. One natural constraint to ensure identifiability is to restrict the range of
f(\cdot)
to be a subset of
[0,\infty)
.

In addition to the positivity constraint imposed in
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)
, we also assume that
f(x)\geq\mathfrak{h}>0
. To motivate this assumption, consider a simplified setting in which we aim to estimate a scalar parameter
\theta_{0}>0
from observations of the form:

y_{i}=\theta_{0}\xi_{i}+\tau_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n.

A straightforward calculation shows that the maximum likelihood estimator of
\theta_{0}
is

\hat{\theta}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

Applying the delta method, we obtain the asymptotic distribution:

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{0})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{4\theta_{0}^{2}}\cdot 2(\theta_{0}+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2}\right).

As this expression shows, when
\theta_{0}\to 0
, the variance of the estimator diverges, and accurate estimation becomes infeasible. A similar issue arises in our setting: allowing the function
f(x)
to approach zero can significantly degrade the performance of locally polynomial regression. For this reason, we define
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)
to include functions satisfying
f(x)\geq\mathfrak{h}
for some fixed constant
\mathfrak{h}>0
.

The final point we would like to make is that, in the definition of
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)
, we also assume that the function is upper bounded by 1. This assumption is natural in image processing applications, where pixel values—corresponding to each
f(i/n)
in our model—are stored using a finite number of bits. Without loss of generality, We have scaled the function so that the upper bound is equal to
1
.

\sigma_{n}=\Omega(n^{1/2})
: In this case, by choosing the estimator function
\hat{f}_{n}=0
, it is trivial to confirm that:

\tilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})\leq\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\|0-f\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

•

\sigma_{n}=\omega(n^{-\beta})
, but
\sigma_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
: In this case, we set
h_{n}:=\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}\over n\right)^{1\over 2\beta+1}
. It is straightforward to confirm that this choice of
h_{n}
satisfies the requirements of
lemma

21
. Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n})\\
\leq&\sup_{f\in\Sigma 1(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}_{n}(x)-f\|_{2}^{2}\\
=&\int_{0}^{1}b^{2}(x)dx+\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{2}(x)dx\\
\leq&q_{1}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}\over n\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}+q_{2}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}\over n\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}=O\left(\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}\over n\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right).\end{split}

(100)

•

\sigma_{n}=O(n^{-\beta})
: Recall that in this case we established a lower bound of
\Theta(n^{-2\beta})
for the minimax MSE. Suppose that for some
\sigma_{n}=O(n^{-\beta})
we have

u_{n}:=\frac{R_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\sigma_{n}^{2})}{n^{-2\beta}}\rightarrow\infty.

Define
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}=n^{-\beta}u_{n}
. Note that
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}=\omega(n^{-\beta})
. Hence, by using the locally linear polynomial estimator we can prove that

\tilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L),\tilde{\sigma}_{n})=O\left(\left(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2}}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right)=O\left(n^{-2\beta}u_{n}^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right).

Hence, since
u_{n}\rightarrow\infty
we can conclude that

\tilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma(\beta,L),\tilde{\sigma}_{n})<\tilde{R}_{2}(\Sigma(\beta,L),{\sigma}_{n}).

However, this is in contradiction with the non-decreasing nature of the minimax risk proved in
lemma

6
.

B.2
The
L_{\infty}
-norm case

B.2.1
Proof of the lower bound

For obtaining the lower bound, again we consider the following two different cases:

•

\sigma_{n}=O((\log n)^{-1/2}n^{-\beta})
: As in the case with the
L_{2}
-norm, by monotonicity (
lemma

6
), it suffices to establish the lower bound rate
n^{-\beta}
for
\sigma_{n}=0
. In this case, we use the same hypotheses
f_{0,n}
and
f_{1,n}
as in
eq.

95
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left\|f_{1,n}-f_{0,n}\right\|_{\infty}=\Theta(n^{-\beta}),

and therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\inf_{\hat{f}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}(\beta,L)}}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{\infty}\\
\geq&\inf_{\hat{f}_{n}}\max\left\{\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f_{1,n}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f_{0,n}\right\|_{\infty}\right\}\\
\geq&\frac{1}{2}\left\|f_{1,n}-f_{0,n}\right\|_{\infty}=\Omega\left(n^{-\beta}\right).\end{split}

•

\sigma_{n}=\omega\left((\log n)^{-1/2}n^{-\beta}\right)
and
\sigma_{n}=o\left(n^{1-\eta\over 2}\right)
:

We shall follow the same approach as in Section 2.6.2 of
Tsybakov, 2009
. Construct
M+1
hypothesis
f_{j,n}
for
j=0,1,...,M
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&f_{0,n}:\equiv\mathfrak{h},\\
&f_{j,n}:=\mathfrak{h}+h_{n}^{\beta}\phi\left(x-(2j-1)/M\over h_{n}\right)\end{split}

where
\phi
is the basic function defined in
eq.

17
,
h_{n}=\left(\log\frac{n}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\middle/\frac{n}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}
and
M=\left\lceil c_{0}h_{n}^{-1}\right\rceil
. Now
f_{j,n}\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)
. For
0\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq M
, we have

\displaystyle\|f_{j,n}-f_{j^{\prime},n}\|_{\infty}=\Omega_{\beta,L}\left(h_{n}^{\beta}\right).

Now following the same argument as in the page 109 of
Tsybakov, 2009
, there exists
\alpha\in\left(0,\frac{1}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}{\rm KL}(\mathbb{P}_{f_{j,n}},\mathbb{P}_{f_{0,n}})\leq\alpha\log M

for sufficiently large
c_{0}>0
. By Theorem 2.5 from
Tsybakov, 2009
and Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{f}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\Sigma_{\mathfrak{h}}(\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\|_{\infty}=\Omega_{\beta,L}\left(h_{n}^{\beta}\right)

In the case when
\sigma_{n}^{2}=O\left(n^{1-\eta}\right)
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, the bound reduced to
\Omega_{\eta,\beta,L}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}\log n\over n\right).

B.2.2
Proof of the upper bound

•

\sigma_{n}=\omega\left((\log n)^{-1/2}n^{-\beta}\right)
and
\sigma_{n}=o\left(n^{1-\eta\over 2}\right)
:
Following the approach in Section 1.6.2 of
Tsybakov, 2009
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}+2\left\|\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{\infty}^{2}

Under the assumption that
h_{n}\to 0
and
nh_{n}\to\infty
and using the local polynomial estimator
\hat{f}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=h^{-2\beta},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{n}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=O_{\beta,L}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}\log M}{nh_{n}}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{M^{2}h_{n}^{4}}\right).

we obtain the estimate under local polynomial estimator
\hat{f}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\|^{2}_{\infty}\right]=&O_{\beta,L}\left(\max\left(2,\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\right)\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}\log M}{nh_{n}}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{M^{2}h_{n}^{4}}+h_{n}^{2\beta}\right)\\
=&O_{\beta,L}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}\log M}{nh_{n}}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{M^{2}h_{n}^{4}}+h_{n}^{2\beta}\right).\end{split}

(101)

Choose
M=(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})^{r}
. Then the estimate becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}&O_{\beta,L}\left(\frac{r\sigma_{n}^{2}\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})}{nh_{n}}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})^{2r}h_{n}^{4}}+h_{n}^{2\beta}\right)\\
=&O_{\beta,L}\left(\frac{r\sigma_{n}^{2}\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})}{nh_{n}}+\frac{\sigma_{n}^{4r+2}}{n^{2r}h_{n}^{4}}+h_{n}^{2\beta}\right)\end{split}

We first optimize the first and last terms by choosing

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{n}:=\left(r\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{1\over 2\beta+1}

In the case of
\sigma_{n}^{2}=o(n^{1-\eta})
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\omega\left((\log n)^{-1}n^{-2\beta}\right)
, we have
\tilde{h}_{n}\to 0
and
n\tilde{h}_{n}\to\infty
, respectively.

To optimize this three-term sum, we note that by plugging
\tilde{h}_{n}
into the first and the third terms together (they have the same decay rate at
\tilde{h}_{n}
) give the upper bound for the sum these two parts as

\displaystyle O_{\beta,L}\left(h_{n}^{2\beta}\right)=O_{\beta,L}\left(\left(r\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{2\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right)

By our assumption that
\sigma_{n}^{2}=o(n^{1-\eta})
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, we evaluate the middle term at this
\tilde{h}_{n}
and compare it to
\tilde{h}_{n}^{2\beta}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n}^{4r+2}}{n^{2r}\tilde{h}_{n}^{4}}\cdot\tilde{h}_{n}^{-2\beta}=\sigma_{n}^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{n}^{4r}}{n^{2r}}\cdot\frac{1}{\tilde{h}_{n}^{2\beta+4}}=\sigma_{n}^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{n}^{4r}}{n^{2r}}\cdot\left(n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\over r\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})\right)^{2\beta+4\over 2\beta+1}

Since
\sigma_{n}^{2}=O(n^{1-\eta})
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, there exists a sufficiently large
r>0
that only depends on
\eta
and
\beta
such that this quotient decays to zero, so that the middle term from
eq.

101
is negligible. Therefore in this case we have the upperbound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{f}_{n}-f\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{\beta,L}\left(\left(\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right)=O_{\eta,\beta,L}\left(\left(\log n\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right)

•

\sigma_{n}=O\left((\log n)^{-1/2}n^{-\beta}\right)
: Recall that at this noise level, we already have a lower bound at the rate of
n^{-\beta}
, and for any
\sigma_{n}=\omega\left((\log n)^{-1/2}n^{-\beta}\right)
and
\sigma_{n}=o\left(n^{1-\eta\over 2}\right)
, we have an upper bound at the rate of
\left(\log n\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{\beta\over 2\beta+1}
. Arguing in the same way as in the
L_{2}
-norm case with
lemma

6
gives the upperbound
O_{\beta,L}(n^{-\beta})
as desired.

Combining these, we have for
\sigma_{n}^{2}=O(n^{1-\eta})
the upperbound

\displaystyle\begin{split}&O_{\beta,L}\left(\max\left\{n^{-\beta},\left(\log(n/\sigma_{n}^{2})\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right\}\right)\\
=&O_{\eta,\beta,L}\left(\max\left\{n^{-\beta},\left(\log n\over n/\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{\beta\over 2\beta+1}\right\}\right).\end{split}
