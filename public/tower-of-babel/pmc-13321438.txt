Comparative evaluation of three trauma scoring systems: OTS, POTS, and MPOTS for predicting early visual outcomes in pediatric open-globe injuries
Authors: Dilber Celik Yaprak, Merve Ozbek, Sibel Oskan Yalcin, Aysin Tuba Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate and directly compare the prognostic performance of three trauma scoring systems—Ocular Trauma Score (OTS), Pediatric Ocular Trauma Score (POTS), and Modified Pediatric Ocular Trauma Score (MPOTS)—in predicting visual outcomes in pediatric patients with open-globe injuries (OGI).MethodsThis retrospective cohort included 110 pediatric patients (≤ 18 years) who underwent surgical repair for OGI between January 2018 and December 2024. Demographic, clinical, and surgical data were extracted from standardized medical records. Prognostic factors for final visual acuity (VA) were analyzed, and OTS, POTS, and MPOTS were calculated for each patient. Visual outcomes were classified as favorable (VA > 20/200) or poor (VA ≤ 20/200). Predictive performance was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsThe mean age was 7.85 ± 4.73 years, and the mean follow-up was 20.36 ± 16.70 months. Presenting VA, injury zone, and associated ocular pathologies (hyphema, traumatic cataract, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, choroidal involvement, vitreous prolapse, and iris prolapse) were significant predictors of poor outcomes (all p < 0.05), whereas age, sex, and time to surgery were not. Mean VA improved significantly from baseline (1.40 ± 0.92 logMAR) to final follow-up (0.68 ± 1.04 logMAR; p < 0.001). All three trauma scores correlated strongly with final VA, with OTS (r = − 0.822) and POTS (r = − 0.807) outperforming MPOTS (r = − 0.690). ROC analysis confirmed excellent discriminative ability for all three models, with the highest areas under the curve (AUC) observed for POTS (0.983), followed by OTS (0.974) and MPOTS (0.948).ConclusionsOTS, POTS, and MPOTS are valuable tools for predicting visual outcomes in pediatric OGI. While POTS demonstrated the best discriminative performance in our cohort, OTS remained highly reliable, and MPOTS provided a simpler framework but with slightly reduced accuracy. OTS should continue to serve as a reference standard, with pediatric-specific scores offering complementary value in challenging clinical scenarios.Keywords: Ocular trauma, Pediatric open-globe injury, OTS, POTS, MPOTS, Prognostic score, Visual outcome

Introduction

Ocular trauma is a major cause of monocular blindness worldwide, and open-globe injury (OGI) represents its most severe manifestation, often resulting in permanent vision loss despite timely intervention [1, 2]. Epidemiological data suggest that pediatric cases account for 20–35% of OGIs [3]. In population-based studies, the incidence of pediatric OGI has been reported as 7.93 per 1,000,000 children [4]. Children are particularly vulnerable to the development of amblyopia in cases of ocular trauma, emphasizing the necessity of precise early prognostication to inform surgical decision-making and provide guidance for parental counselling [2].

The Ocular Trauma Score (OTS) was introduced in 2002 and has since been extensively utilized for the purpose of predicting visual outcomes [5]. However, its reliance on initial visual acuity (VA) and relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) limits applicability in children, where these parameters are often difficult to assess. To overcome these challenges, Acar et al. proposed the Pediatric Ocular Trauma Score (POTS) in 2011, which reduced the weight of initial VA, removed RAPD, and incorporated age and injury zone as additional factors [6]. More recently, Cohen et al. developed the Modified Pediatric Ocular Trauma Score (MPOTS), intending to simplify the POTS and demonstrate superior predictive performance [7].

Although each scoring system has shown utility, there remains no consensus on which tool offers the most reliable prognostic accuracy in pediatric OGI. Previous studies have been limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous populations [2, 7]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate and directly compare the prognostic performance of OTS, POTS, and MPOTS in a large pediatric cohort with OGIs, to determine their relative clinical value.

Methods

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at Kartal Dr. Lütfi Kırdar City Hospital, a tertiary referral center for pediatric ophthalmology and vitreoretinal surgery. The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee.

The study population comprised 110 pediatric patients (aged ≤ 18 years) who underwent surgical repair for OGI between January 2018 and December 2024. Patients with incomplete records, pre-existing ocular conditions affecting visual outcomes, or follow-up shorter than three months were excluded. Although the predefined exclusion threshold was three months, all included patients had a minimum follow-up duration of six months.

The clinical and demographic data were extracted from the standardized medical records. The data set comprised demographic information such as age and sex, in addition to injury-related details including laterality, location, and the underlying cause. These causes were categorized as either organic or inorganic, and the specific anatomical zone of injury was also documented, ranging from I to III. The initial ophthalmological findings comprised the presenting VA and the presence of iris prolapse, hyphema, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis and traumatic cataract. The injury site was defined as Zone I when involving the cornea and limbus, Zone II when involving the sclera up to 5 mm posterior to the limbus, and Zone III when involving the sclera more than 5 mm posterior to the limbus, according to the Ocular Trauma Classification Group system [8]. The surgical details encompassed the temporal interval between the initial injury and the intervention, as well as the nature of the surgical repair that was performed.

VA assessment was performed using age-appropriate methods based on the child’s level of cooperation. In preverbal or uncooperative children, qualitative assessment using fix-and-follow responses was employed. In younger children, preferential looking techniques such as Teller acuity cards and Lea symbols were used, while in older and cooperative children, Snellen charts were utilized. Both initial and final VA measurements were recorded using these methods. Quantitative VA values were converted to logMAR units for statistical analysis when applicable.

For each patient, the OTS, POTS, and MPOTS were calculated according to their original descriptions. The OTS was determined based on presenting VA with point deductions for associated findings (globe rupture, endophthalmitis, perforating injury, retinal detachment, and RAPD), and patients were assigned to categories 1–5 using the raw-score ranges 0–44, 45–65, 66–80, 81–91, and 92–100 [5].

As originally described by Acar et al. [6] and Cohen et al. [7], POTS and MPOTS allow the use of estimation formulas in cases where initial VA cannot be reliably assessed. In this study, initial VA was evaluated in all patients using age-appropriate methods. In only two preverbal children, quantitative VA could not be obtained using fix and follow, and estimation formulas were therefore applied for these patients. POTS results were grouped into five prognostic categories based on the original classification [6], whereas MPOTS used three risk grades (grade I ≤ 30, grade II 35–65, grade III ≥ 70), as defined in the original study [7], with higher scores indicating a better prognosis.

All patients underwent primary wound-closure surgery, and additional procedures such as cataract extraction or intraocular lens implantation were performed when indicated. A uniform perioperative regimen, encompassing systemic antibiotics and postoperative topical therapy, was applied to the entire cohort. In routine clinical practice, pediatric patients were referred to the strabismus unit for evaluation and management of amblyopia when indicated. However, due to the retrospective design of the study, detailed data regarding amblyopia treatment protocols, adherence, and outcomes were not systematically available for analysis.

The final visual outcome was defined dichotomously as favorable (VA > 20/200) or poor (VA ≤ 20/200) at the most recent follow-up, consistent with previously published studies in pediatric OGI [7], and this classification was used as the dependent variable for group comparisons.

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between groups with favorable and poor outcomes were conducted using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The predictive validity of OTS, POTS, and MPOTS was primarily assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients, given the ordinal structure of the data. In addition, an age-stratified subgroup analysis was performed by dividing patients into those aged ≤ 8 years and > 8 years, and the relationships between trauma scores and final VA were evaluated separately within each group. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate discriminative ability, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each scoring system. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The mean age of the study population was 7.85 ± 4.73 years (range, 1–15 years). The average interval between injury and surgical intervention was 11.51 ± 7.78 h (range, 1–48 h). Initial VA was assessed in all patients using age-appropriate methods. In two patients (1.8%), both aged 1 year, only a qualitative assessment using fixation behavior was possible, and OTS could not be calculated in these cases. At presentation, the mean VA was 1.40 ± 0.92 logMAR (range, 0.2–3.0). The mean follow-up period was 20.36 ± 16.70 months (range, 6–72 months). Laterality analysis showed that injuries were slightly more common in the right eye (56.4%, n = 62) compared with the left eye (43.6%, n = 48). A marked male predominance was observed, with 74.5% (n = 82) of cases occurring in boys, whereas girls accounted for only 25.5% (n = 28). Mean VA improved significantly from baseline (1.40 ± 0.92 logMAR) to the final follow-up (0.68 ± 1.04 logMAR; p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in mean age between patients with favorable and poor outcomes (7.58 ± 4.65 vs. 8.91 ± 5.00 years, p = 0.24). Furthermore, the mean interval from injury to surgical intervention did not differ significantly between the two groups (11.39 ± 7.10 vs. 12.00 ± 10.21 h, p = 0.79). Conversely, preoperative VA exhibited a significant disparity among patients with divergent outcomes, with those exhibiting unfavorable outcomes demonstrating markedly poorer VA (2.80 ± 0.31 logMAR) compared to those with favorable outcomes (1.04 ± 0.63 logMAR, p < 0.001). This observation signifies a highly significant correlation with the ultimate visual prognosis. The location of the injury was found to be a significant predictor of visual prognosis, with zone I cases demonstrating a favorable outcome in 96.4% of cases, compared to 46.2% in zone II and 14.3% in zone III (p < 0.001). The relationship between sex and organic versus inorganic etiology was not found to be statistically significant in terms of its impact on outcome. A number of clinical findings have been identified as being associated with a poor prognosis. Hyphema was observed in 59.1% of cases in the poor outcome group, as opposed to 17.0% in the favorable group (p < 0.001). Furthermore, traumatic cataract was found to be significantly associated with poor vision (p < 0.001). The prevalence of retinal detachment (p < 0.001) and vitreous hemorrhage (p < 0.001) was found to be significantly higher in the poor outcome group. Choroidal involvement was observed in a greater proportion of eyes with poor outcomes (55.6% vs. 5.3%, p < 0.001). The prevalence of iris prolapse (81.8% vs. 54.5%, p = 0.02) and vitreous prolapse (76.2% vs. 14.8%, p < 0.001) was found to be significantly associated with poor prognosis (Table 2). As anticipated, all three trauma scoring systems demonstrated significantly higher scores in the favorable outcome group compared with the poor outcome group. The mean MPOTS score was 78.47 ± 16.48 in cases where outcomes were favorable, and 23.86 ± 27.94 in cases where outcomes were poor (p < 0.001). In addition, the mean OTS was 81.88 ± 12.93 versus 34.84 ± 16.88 (p < 0.001), and the mean POTS was 68.58 ± 13.73 versus 24.64 ± 15.39 (p < 0.001). The observed differences manifested as substantial effect sizes, thereby substantiating the robust predictive capacity of OTS, POTS, and MPOTS in distinguishing between favorable and unfavorable ultimate visual outcomes.

Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis revealed a strong correlation between the three trauma scoring systems and final VA. The OTS demonstrated the strongest correlation with the final VA (r = − 0.822, p < 0.001), followed by the POTS (r = − 0.807, p < 0.001) and the MPOTS (r = − 0.690, p < 0.001). The findings of this study indicate that higher trauma scores were consistently associated with better visual outcomes, with OTS and POTS demonstrating stronger correlations than MPOTS in this cohort.

Subgroup analysis was performed by stratifying patients into children aged ≤ 8 years (n = 66) and those aged > 8 years (n = 44). In both age groups, all three trauma scoring systems demonstrated strong and statistically significant correlations with final VA. In children aged ≤ 8 years, the correlation coefficients were r =− 0.795 for POTS, r =− 0.790 for OTS, and r =− 0.620 for MPOTS (all p < 0.001). In children aged > 8 years, the correlations were slightly stronger, with coefficients of r =− 0.847 for POTS, r =− 0.851 for OTS, and r =− 0.768 for MPOTS (all p < 0.001).

ROC curve analysis showed excellent predictive accuracy for all three trauma scoring systems. The AUC was 0.983 for POTS, 0.974 for OTS, and 0.948 for MPOTS (all p < 0.001), indicating that POTS performed best, followed closely by OTS, with MPOTS showing slightly lower discriminative ability (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this study, three prognostic scoring systems – OTS, POTS, and MPOTS – were evaluated and compared in a large cohort of pediatric patients with OGIs. The findings of the present study demonstrated that all three models were useful in predicting final visual outcomes. Among the models evaluated, POTS demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability, closely followed by OTS. MPOTS, despite its simplicity and ease of application in clinical practice, exhibited slightly lower predictive strength in the cohort under study. The findings underscore the reliability of conventional systems, while newer, streamlined approaches may offer practical benefits despite a concomitant decline in accuracy. Importantly, although these scoring systems do not directly influence immediate surgical management, they play a crucial role in prognostic stratification, parental counseling, and guiding postoperative follow-up and visual rehabilitation strategies.

The demographic distribution of the cohort under study, characterized by a strong male predominance and home-related accidents as the most frequent setting, is in line with the findings of previous epidemiological studies of pediatric OGI. Consistent findings were reported by Mir et al., who observed that boys are disproportionately affected [3]. In a large Tunisian series, Malek et al. likewise noted that 62.5% of cases occurred in males and that the home was the predominant site of trauma, with play-related activities being the leading cause [9]. These data highlight the global consistency of demographic and etiological patterns in pediatric OGI, emphasizing the need for targeted preventive strategies in domestic settings.

In the present series, age was not a significant predictor of final visual outcome, consistent with the findings of Cohen et al., who also reported no prognostic effect of age in their pediatric cohort, despite a trend toward better outcomes in the youngest children, possibly due to lower-velocity injuries [7]. In a similar vein, Lee et al. discovered that demographic variables such as age and sex were not associated with prognosis. Conversely, ocular findings and injury severity were identified as more significant determinants [10].

Regarding the timing of surgical intervention, the mean interval from injury to surgery in our study was only 11 h, and no case exceeded 48 h, indicating that prompt surgical repair was consistently achieved. Cohen et al. likewise reported that all patients in their series underwent surgery within 48 h, facilitated by accessible medical services, and emphasized that previous studies failed to establish a clear link between delayed surgery and poor visual outcomes. It has been suggested that later presentations may often be related to less severe trauma, which could explain the absence of a consistent association between surgical delay and final vision [7].

In our cohort, poor presenting VA, posterior zone involvement, hyphema, traumatic cataract, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, choroidal involvement, vitreous prolapse, and iris prolapse were significantly associated with poor visual outcomes. These findings align with the Tunisian study by Rmili et al., which similarly identified initial VA worse than 1 logMAR, zone III injuries, hyphema, cataract, vitreous hemorrhage, and retinal detachment as major predictors of poor prognosis [11]. Moreover, our results are also in agreement with those of Cohen et al. Both studies confirmed poor initial VA, posterior zone injuries, retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, hyphema, and iris prolapse as key prognostic indicators. Nevertheless, some discrepancies were observed: while our analysis highlighted traumatic cataract, choroidal involvement, and vitreous prolapse as additional significant risk factors, these parameters did not demonstrate predictive value in Cohen’s series [7]. In this context, rather than identifying novel individual predictors, our study demonstrates how these established risk factors are integrated within structured trauma scoring systems, enabling a more comprehensive, standardized, and clinically applicable assessment of visual prognosis.

The present study sought to compare three prognostic scoring systems – OTS, POTS and MPOTS – in a large pediatric cohort with OGIs. The extant literature provides equivocal evidence regarding the relative merits of OTS and POTS. Pahor et al. conducted a comparative analysis of both systems in pediatric OGI cases and concluded that there was no clear benefit of POTS, thereby determining that OTS remained a more straightforward and accurate option for clinical use [12]. Dursun et al. demonstrated similarly good correlations between both models and final visual outcomes but emphasized OTS as the “gold standard” in pediatric trauma [13]. Wong and colleagues have further supported the validity of OTS, emphasizing its robust performance across diverse populations and injury types [14]. Collectively, these findings corroborate our observation that OTS remains a highly reliable prognostic tool, even when compared against pediatric-specific alternatives. However, recent evidence suggests that OTS may underestimate visual recovery in cases of severe ocular trauma. Perez et al. reported that actual visual outcomes may exceed those predicted by OTS, particularly in eyes with initially poor prognostic indicators [15]. This limitation may be even more relevant in pediatric populations, where neuroplasticity and ongoing visual development can facilitate functional recovery beyond initial expectations.

Cohen et al., developers of the MPOTS, reported that this modified tool outperformed both OTS and POTS in their derivation and validation cohorts. The findings indicated that the elimination of less predictive factors, such as age, cataract, and organic injuries, and the subsequent focus on key variables, including the injury zone, initial VA, retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, hyphema, and iris prolapse, would result in a system that is more accurate and clinically feasible [7]. However, the findings of the present study did not replicate this advantage. Conversely, MPOTS demonstrated substandard performance in comparison to both OTS and POTS within our extensive and heterogeneous cohort. A recent study by Duarte et al. evaluated OTS, POTS, and MPOTS in a cohort of pediatric patients with open globe injuries and reported that POTS showed a significant correlation with visual outcomes, whereas MPOTS did not demonstrate strong predictive value [16]. However, the applicability of OTS was markedly limited, as initial VA was available in only a small proportion of patients, allowing calculation of OTS in just three cases. In addition, the relatively small sample size of that study may have further limited the robustness of their conclusions. In contrast, in our study, initial VA was measurable in all patients except two preverbal children, enabling the application of all three scoring systems across the entire cohort and allowing for a more comprehensive and reliable comparison of their prognostic performance.

An age-stratified subgroup analysis was performed focusing on children aged ≤ 8 years, who are at higher risk for amblyopia. All three trauma scoring systems demonstrated strong correlations with final VA in this subgroup, with patterns comparable to those observed in older children. Although slightly stronger correlations were observed in patients older than 8 years, the overall prognostic performance of OTS, POTS, and MPOTS remained robust in younger children. These findings suggest that, despite the potential influence of amblyopia and neuroplasticity, trauma scoring systems retain their clinical applicability even within the amblyogenic age group.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design inherently carries the risk of selection and information bias, despite the use of standardized medical records. Second, although our cohort of 110 patients represents one of the larger series in pediatric OGIs, the sample size may still be insufficient to detect less frequent predictors of outcome, and subgroup analyses were limited. Third, the absence of endophthalmitis and intraocular foreign body cases in our cohort restricted the ability to assess their prognostic impact, factors that have been shown to be relevant in other series. Fourth, VA assessment in children is often challenging in the acute trauma setting, which may have introduced measurement variability, particularly in younger patients. Moreover, visual outcomes in pediatric patients may be influenced by post-traumatic amblyopia and the duration of visual rehabilitation. Although the mean follow-up period in our study exceeded 20 months, this duration may still be insufficient to fully capture the long-term visual potential. Therefore, the reported visual outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as VA may continue to improve over time depending on neuroplasticity and the effectiveness of amblyopia management. Finally, this was a single-center study, and the findings may not be generalizable to different populations or healthcare systems.

Conclusion

Taken together, our results suggest that while POTS may offer theoretical advantages in children, particularly when initial VA cannot be reliably assessed, and MPOTS proposes a simplified framework, OTS continues to demonstrate comparable or superior prognostic accuracy. Therefore, OTS should still be regarded as a reference standard, with POTS serving as a useful adjunct in pediatric cases where clinical assessment is challenging. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to validate the relative performance of these models and clarify whether pediatric-specific scores consistently outperform the traditional OTS in diverse settings.
