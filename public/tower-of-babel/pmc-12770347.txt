Combined effects of red light and direct-current electric fields on neurite growth in 3D neural cell cultures
Authors: Yu-Chiu Kao, Her-Bang Huang, Chau-Hwang Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth red-light (RL) illumination and direct-current electric fields (dcEFs) have independently been shown to promote neurite outgrowth in two-dimensional (2D) neural cell cultures. However, their combined effects in a three-dimensional (3D) culture environment remain unexplored. In this study, we examined the combined effects of RL and dcEFs on neurite extension in human neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) and mouse neuroblastoma (N2a) cells embedded within a 3D collagen gel matrix. Our results demonstrated that optimized dcEF stimulation and RL exposure significantly enhanced neurite elongation and directional alignment. RNA sequencing further identified Neuropeptide Y (NPY) and its receptors as key mediators of these effects. Moreover, electrophysiological assessments revealed that neurons subjected to the combined stimulation exhibited enhanced functional maturation. These findings provide compelling evidence supporting the potential applications of RL and dcEFs in neural regeneration and repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29835-0.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Neuroscience

Introduction

Neuronal differentiation is a complex, multistep process in which precursor or progenitor cells acquire structural and functional characteristics of mature neurons. This transformation involves coordinated changes in morphology, gene expression, and electrophysiological properties that collectively enable neural connectivity and signaling. At the molecular level, key proteins and markers guide and reflect these changes: βIII-tubulin (Tuj1) and microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) are widely recognized as indicators of early neuronal identity and neurite extension, while growth-associated protein 43 (GAP-43) plays a central role in axonal growth and plasticity. These markers provide critical readouts for evaluating interventions that promote neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth.

Among various approaches to enhance neurite outgrowth and neuronal functions, photobiomodulation (PBM) and electrical stimulation have emerged as two promising, non-invasive techniques with significant potential in neural regeneration. PBM, particularly using red and near-infrared (NIR) light, has been widely recognized for its ability to modulate cellular functions by targeting mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (CCO)1, a key enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. By absorbing specific wavelengths of light, CCO undergoes activation, leading to enhanced mitochondrial activities, increased ATP production, and modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels2. These molecular events, in turn, activate various intracellular signaling pathways that promote cell survival, stimulate neuronal proliferation and differentiation, and facilitate neurite elongation and network formation1,3. Our previous research further demonstrated that neurite regrowth could be stimulated by precisely focusing a red-light (RL) spot on the soma of a neuronal cell4. The underlying mechanism was identified as RL-induced intracellular ROS production, mediated by CCO activation, which played a crucial role in driving neurite outgrowth5.

In parallel, electrical stimulation has been shown to exert profound effects on neuronal differentiation by influencing cellular polarization and intracellular signaling cascades6,7. The application of external direct-current electric fields (dcEFs) can modulate neuronal membrane potential, induce localized ion fluxes, and regulate ion channel activities, thereby affecting cytoskeletal dynamics and cellular morphology8,9. These effects contribute to directed neurite outgrowth, enhanced axonal regeneration, and improved synaptic connectivity7,10. Given the established benefits of both PBM and electrical stimulation, it is reasonable to hypothesize that combining these two physical stimuli may optimize mitochondrial activities as well as guide neurite growth and neuron differentiation through complementary molecular pathways.

Despite the promising findings associated with PBM and electrical stimulation, the majority of prior studies have been conducted in two-dimensional (2D) cell culture systems, where the physical and biochemical microenvironment differs significantly from that of three-dimensional (3D) tissues. Cells grown in 2D cultures exhibit dissimilar morphology, signaling dynamics, and extracellular matrix interactions compared to those in more physiologically relevant 3D environments11,12. Since the structural and functional characteristics of neurons in 3D models better replicate in vivo conditions, it is critical to evaluate these stimulation techniques within a 3D context to fully understand their potential for neural regeneration.

Recognizing this gap, in the present study we investigated the effects of combined RL and dcEF stimulation on neurite outgrowth in a 3D neural cell culture system using human neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) and mouse neuroblastoma (N2a) cells embedded in a collagen gel matrix. By systematically optimizing the stimulation parameters for each cell type, we aimed to elucidate the combined effects of RL and dcEF on neuronal differentiation. In addition, we assessed the functional properties of the stimulated SH-SY5Y cells through electrophysiological analysis to evaluate neuronal maturation. Our findings provide new insights into the use of combined optical and electrical stimulation strategies for promoting neural regeneration and repair across multiple neuronal cell models.

Results

Optimizing DcEF and RL stimulations for enhanced neurite growth in neuroblastoma cells

To investigate the effects of dcEFs and RL stimulation on neurite outgrowth in a 3D neural cell culture system, we embedded both human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells and mouse neuroblastoma N2a cells in a Type I collagen gel matrix. Figures 1a–c illustrate the experimental setup, including a schematic of 3D cell culture within a dish containing the collagen gel (Fig. 1a), the dcEF and RL stimulation apparatus (Fig. 1b), and a representative confocal image showing the randomly distributed collagen fibers within the 3D matrix (Fig. 1c). As shown in Fig. 1d, SH-SY5Y cells cultured in 1 mg/mL collagen for three days exhibited aggregation into neurosphere-like structures, with limited neurite extension. A comparable morphological pattern was observed in N2a cells under the same conditions, indicating that 3D culture alone does not markedly promote neurite outgrowth and that additional external stimulation may be required to facilitate neurite elongation. Corresponding 3D images of Fig. 1d are provided in the Supplementary Information. Figure 1e shows the intracellular ROS levels in SH-SY5Y cells with and without exposure to 625 nm RL for 45 h. The RL illumination markedly increased ROS generation, with fluorescence intensity elevated by approximately fourfold compared with the control group (P = 2.6 × 10⁻⁵, η² = 0.82), indicating a statistically significant effect consistent with our previous observations in 2D culture5.

To systematically evaluate the influence of dcEF stimulation, we applied an electric field using a salt bridge system to ensure a stable and uniform ionic current across the 3D culture. The cell densities were maintained at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL for SH-SY5Y cells and 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL for N2a cells across all experimental conditions to ensure consistency. Two electric field strengths—175 mV/mm and 350 mV/mm—were tested across various stimulation durations. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, SH-SY5Y cells exhibited enhanced neurite elongation in response to both field intensities, with the most pronounced effect observed at 175 mV/mm applied for three hours (P = 1.5 × 10−23, η² = 0.53). However, extending the stimulation duration to four hours resulted in reduced neurite length, suggesting that prolonged exposure may lead to overstimulation or cellular stress, thereby diminishing the beneficial effects of dcEF stimulation. In contrast, N2a cells (Fig. 2c) showed the greatest neurite outgrowth when stimulated with 350 mV/mm for two hours (P = 1.1 × 10−22, η² = 0.77), indicating that the optimal dcEF parameters are cell type–specific. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring both electric field strength and stimulation duration to the cellular context in order to effectively promote neurite extension while minimizing potential adverse effects.

Next, we optimized RL stimulation parameters, including light intensity and exposure duration, for both SH-SY5Y and N2a cells. Continuous illumination at 625 nm was applied at varying intensities and durations to identify the most effective conditions for promoting neurite elongation. Based on preliminary tests using exposure times of 21, 45, and 69 h, the most pronounced neurite outgrowth was observed with 45 h of RL illumination, whereas the 69-hour exposure resulted in decreased cell viability. As shown in Fig. 2b and d, RL stimulation at 2 mW/cm² produced the greatest enhancement in neurite extension in both cell types. Statistical analysis confirmed that this enhancement was significant in both N2a (P = 5.6 × 10⁻²⁴, η² = 0.27) and SH-SY5Y (P = 6.2 × 10⁻⁹, η² = 0.41) cells, indicating a robust effect of optimized RL exposure on neurite elongation. In contrast, higher-intensity illumination (5 mW/cm²) led to substantial cell death and diminished neurite growth, likely due to phototoxic effects.

Overall, these findings provide a systematic framework for optimizing dcEF and RL stimulation parameters, demonstrating that the 175 mV/mm dcEF for three hours and 2 mW/cm² of RL exposure for 45 h are the most effective conditions for enhancing neurite outgrowth in SH-SY5Y cells within a 3D culture system. For N2a cells, the optimal neurite extension was achieved with a 350 mV/mm dcEF for two hours, combined with the same RL exposure of 2 mW/cm² for 45 h. These optimized parameters were subsequently applied in our investigations of the combined effects of dcEF and RL co-stimulation on neuronal differentiation and functional maturation.

Co-stimulation with dcEF and RL enhances neurite growth in N2a and SH-SY5Y cells in 3D culture

To evaluate the combined effects of dcEF and RL stimulation on neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth, SH-SY5Y and N2a cells were embedded in a 3D collagen gel matrix and subjected to their respective optimized stimulation protocols. SH-SY5Y cells received a 175 mV/mm dcEF for three hours, followed by RL exposure at 2 mW/cm² for 45 h. In contrast, N2a cells were stimulated with a 350 mV/mm dcEF for two hours, followed by the same RL exposure of 2 mW/cm² for 45 h.

Confocal microscopy images in Fig. 3a and d illustrate neurite outgrowth in SH-SY5Y and N2a cells, respectively, across four experimental conditions: control (no stimulation), RL stimulation, dcEF stimulation, and combined dcEF + RL stimulation. In both cell types, the control group exhibited minimal neurite outgrowth with no observable alignment. The RL stimulation led to a moderate increase in neurite elongation, while the dcEF stimulation induced more substantial outgrowth accompanied by clear alignment along the dcEF direction. Notably, the combined dcEF + RL stimulation produced the most pronounced effects in both SH-SY5Y and N2a cells, characterized by significantly enhanced neurite elongation and strong directional alignment, suggesting a combined interaction between the two stimulation modalities.

Figure 3b presents the distribution of neurite orientations in SH-SY5Y cells relative to the direction of the applied dcEF, analyzed at an 18° angular interval. In the control group, neurites displayed random orientations with an average absolute cosine value <|cos θ|> of 0.48, indicative of isotropic growth. In contrast, cells subjected to combined dcEF and RL stimulation exhibited a significantly higher <|cos θ|> of 0.76, reflecting strong alignment along the electric field direction. Notably, no clear polarization bias was observed under the dcEF stimulation alone. Similarly, orientation analysis of N2a cells (Fig. 3e) revealed a control group <|cos θ|> value of 0.57, which increased to 0.68 under the dcEF stimulation, indicating a moderate degree of alignment. The extent of neurite orientation in response to the dcEF was less pronounced in N2a cells compared to that in SH-SY5Y cells, suggesting cell type–specific sensitivity to directional cues induced by dcEF.

Furthermore, Fig. 3c quantifies neurite lengths in SH-SY5Y cells across various stimulation conditions. Compared with the control group, combined dcEF + RL stimulation produced a highly significant increase in neurite length (P = 2.1 × 10⁻²⁹, η² = 0.54). A similar result was observed in N2a cells (Fig. 3f), where dcEF + RL treatment also induced a marked enhancement of neurite extension relative to the control (P = 2.12 × 10⁻³⁰, η² = 0.41). These results demonstrate that concurrent application of dcEF and RL maximally promotes neurite elongation in both cell types.

RNA sequencing results of SH-SY5Y cells after co-stimulation in 3D culture

Based on the observed combined effects of dcEF and RL stimulation on neurite growth and alignment, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses using the SH-SY5Y cells as a representative model to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms driving these effects. By identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and enriched signaling pathways, we aimed to uncover key molecular targets contributing to the enhanced neurite outgrowth and alignment under combined stimulation conditions. The volcano plot in Fig. 4a highlights genes that were significantly upregulated or downregulated in the dcEF + RL treatment group compared to the control, using log₂(fold change) ≥ 1 or ≤ − 1 and statistical significance (P < 0.05) as thresholds. A distinct subset of genes exhibited substantial changes in expression, suggesting their potential involvement in the neurite elongation and directional alignment induced by the co-stimulation.

Here shows the results of two independent experiments. c Fold change in Neuropeptide Y (NPY) RNA expression levels across different stimulation conditions, demonstrating the regulatory effects of dcEF and RL on NPY signaling. Differentially expressed genes were identified using RNA sequencing analysis, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed based on the KEGG database.

To further elucidate the signaling pathways influenced by dcEF and RL stimulations, we conducted the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, which revealed a significant enrichment of genes in the Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. The upregulated or downregulated genes in this pathway are plotted in Fig. 4b. This pathway plays a crucial role in neuronal functions, synaptic plasticity, and axonal guidance, indicating that the enhanced neurite growth and alignment observed in our experiments may be mediated by neuroactive signaling cascades. To refine our selection of key regulatory genes, we applied an additional filtering criterion, requiring a total exon fragment count exceeding 50 to ensure biological relevance. Through this process, we identified Neuropeptide Y (NPY) as a significantly upregulated gene across all stimulation groups (dcEF, RL, and dcEF + RL), with the highest expression observed under combined stimulation conditions (Fig. 4c).

NPY is a well-characterized neuropeptide known to interact with five G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), among which the Y1 and Y2 receptors are the most abundantly expressed in both the central nervous system and peripheral tissues. These receptors regulate various neurophysiological functions, including neuronal survival, differentiation, and axonal growth. The significant upregulation of NPY in response to dcEF and RL co-stimulation suggests its critical role as a key mediator of the observed neurite outgrowth and alignment enhancements. These findings provide strong molecular evidence supporting the efficacy of combined electrical and optical stimulation in neurite growth.

Neurite growth lengths under dcEF and RL stimulations following treatment with NPY Y1 or Y2 receptor inhibitors

Following the identification of NPY as a significantly up-regulated gene in the dcEF, RL, and combined dcEF + RL treatment groups, the potential role of NPY in mediating neurite outgrowth under the electrical and optical stimulations was further investigated. To assess the involvement of NPY receptors in neurite growth enhancement under the electrical and optical stimulations, selective inhibitors targeting NPY Y1 and Y2 receptors were utilized.

To determine the optimal experimental concentration, cell viability under different concentrations of the NPY Y1 receptor inhibitor (BIBP 3226) and Y2 receptor inhibitor (BIIE 0246) was assessed in both SH-SY5Y and N2a cells using the alamarBlue assay. A concentration of 20 µM was selected for both inhibitors based on the viability results in both cell types. The SH-SY5Y and N2a cells were cultured for one day prior to inhibitor treatment. Two hours after the application of the inhibitors, the cells were subjected to their respective optimized physical stimulation conditions: SH-SY5Y cells received dcEF stimulation at 175 mV/mm for three hours, followed by RL exposure at 2 mW/cm² for 45 h, while N2a cells were stimulated with 350 mV/mm dcEF for two hours followed by the same RL exposure.

The confocal microscopy images in Fig. 5a and c (SH-SY5Y) and Fig. 5e and g (N2a) revealed notable morphological changes in neurites following NPY Y1 and Y2 receptor inhibition. Quantitative analyses of neurite length (Fig. 5b and d for SH-SY5Y; Fig. 5f and h for N2a) indicated no significant differences between the control groups with and without the inhibitor treatment. However, in the presence of physical stimulations (dcEF, RL, or combined), a marked reduction in neurite lengths was observed in both cell types upon receptor inhibition, compared to their respective stimulated groups without inhibition. This reduction was consistent across both Y1- and Y2-receptor–inhibited groups. Notably, in SH-SY5Y cells, inhibition of the NPY Y1 receptor did not completely abolish neurite outgrowth under dcEF stimulation; however, the overall neurite length was still significantly reduced compared to the non-inhibited group, indicating that Y1 receptor signaling contributes substantially to the elongation effect. In contrast, N2a cells showed a more pronounced reduction under the same condition. These findings indicate that NPY signaling plays a critical role in mediating stimulation-induced neurite elongation, with both NPY Y1 and Y2 receptors contributing to the underlying molecular mechanisms in both SH-SY5Y and N2a cells.

Electrophysiological responses of SH-SY5Y cells after DcEF and RL co-stimulation

To further investigate the functional impacts of dcEF and RL co-stimulation on neuronal maturation, we evaluated the electrophysiological responses of SH-SY5Y cells. The SH-SY5Y cells were selected for this analysis due to their greater relevance to human neuronal physiology. Figure 6a illustrates the timeline of stimulation and imaging in the experiment. Electrophysiological recordings and corresponding confocal microscopy images were obtained for both the control group (Fig. 6b) and the co-stimulation (dcEF + RL) group (Fig. 6c). The dcEF stimulation was applied at 175 mV/mm for three hours, while RL illumination was delivered continuously at 2 mW/cm² for 45 h. Following the dcEF + RL co-stimulation, the cells were maintained in the incubator for additional four days before electrophysiological measurements were performed.

Electrophysiological responses of SH-SY5Y cells following the combined dcEF and RL stimulation. a Schematic illustration showing the temporal sequence of dcEF and RL application prior to electrophysiological recording. b Control group exhibiting limited neurite outgrowth and no detectable electrophysiological activity. c Combined dcEF + RL group displaying enhanced neurite elongation and robust electrophysiological responses characterized by repetitive spike-like discharges. Recordings were performed in the whole-cell current-clamp configuration. A small holding current of approximately − 20 pA was applied to stabilize the membrane potential near − 60 mV before stimulation, followed by stepwise depolarizing current injections (+ 5 pA per 500 msec step) to assess neuronal excitability.

In the control group, the SH-SY5Y cells exhibited no detectable electrophysiological response. The absence of spiking activity suggested that the neurons in the control group had not yet achieved the necessary functional maturity to generate action potentials. Confocal microscopy images corroborated these findings, showing limited neurite outgrowth and a predominantly simple, circular morphology, characteristics associated with immature neuronal states.

In contrast, the dcEF + RL co-stimulation group displayed a markedly different electrophysiological profile. Under identical stimulation conditions, SH-SY5Y cells exhibited a consistent repetitive spiking activity, which could be classified as regular spiking. This activity indicated enhanced neuronal excitability and the development of functional ion channel mechanisms necessary for action potential generation. The repetitive spiking observed throughout the one-second current application suggested maturation of ion-channel activity and the establishment of robust electrical signaling capabilities in the co-stimulated neurons. Corresponding confocal microscopy images further revealed distinct morphological changes, with the co-stimulated cells exhibiting significantly enhanced neurite outgrowth, increased neurite branching, and more complex cellular structures compared to the control group.

The results in Fig. 6 suggest that the combination of dcEF + RL stimulation not only induced structural changes in neuronal morphology but also enhanced functional maturation in terms of electrophysiological properties. The presence of consistent spiking activity in the co-stimulation group reflected the development of mature electrical signaling mechanisms, a characteristic of functional neurons capable of transmitting and processing electrical signals.

Discussion

This present study demonstrates that the combined application of RL and dcEF co-stimulation significantly enhances neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth in a 3D culture model. Our rationale for combining RL and dcEF stimulation lies in their complementary modes of action—RL enhances mitochondrial activity and intracellular signaling, whereas dcEF provides directional cues for neurite extension. By applying both, we aimed to test whether their combined interaction could more effectively promote neuronal differentiation and excitability. This multimodal approach not only amplifies individual effects but also offers a closer approximation of the complex, concurrent stimuli present in vivo. The 3D environment provides a physiologically relevant platform, enabling the replication of in vivo-like conditions, which are critical for studying neuronal behaviors13,14. Our findings indicate that optimized dcEF and RL parameters independently contribute to neuronal differentiation, with dcEF guiding neurite alignment along the electric-field direction and RL promoting neurite elongation through mitochondrial activation and ROS signaling15–17. It was reported that RL can increase ROS production and even induce apoptosis under certain exposure conditions18. However, specific levels of intracellular ROS induced by RL illumination are considered to be favorable for cell proliferation19,20. Our prior study also demonstrated that moderate RL stimulation induced a transient and controlled increase in ROS that activated pro-survival and growth-related signaling pathways5. In the present study, we carefully optimized the stimulation parameters to achieve effective neurite outgrowth while minimizing cytotoxic effects.

The enhanced neurite growth and alignment in the co-stimulated group suggest complementary mechanisms of action, where dcEF modulates cellular polarity and ion channel activities, while RL enhances cellular energy metabolism9,14,15. The RNA sequencing analysis identified NPY and its Y1 and Y2 receptors as critical mediators, linking physical stimulations to molecular pathways that drive neuronal repair and regeneration16,17. However, the observation that inhibition of the NPY receptors reduced RL–induced neurite growth only suggests a contributory role of these receptors. Further studies employing receptor knockdown or complementary blockade approaches are necessary to confirm a causal mechanism. In addition, given that only two biological replicates were analyzed per condition, the RNA sequencing data should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, serving primarily to identify candidate genes and pathways for subsequent functional validation.

Aligned scaffolds have been reported to enhance neurite outgrowth by 16–80% in 2D cultures21,22 and up to ~ 1050% in 3D hydrogels incorporating functionalized fibers23. In comparison, our RL + dcEF co-stimulation yielded ~ 150% increase in neurite length under 3D culture conditions, without the need for fiber fabrication and alignment. This operational simplicity, together with the absence of artificially aligned structures in real tissues, highlights the practicality and translational relevance of our approach. In the present work, we did not observe the structure of collagen gel after dcEF treatment. In the literatures we do find that AC electric fields can align the collagen building blocks and lead to microstructures with desired morphology24. Therefore, pre-alignment of the collagen fibers by electric fields could also be considered to improve neurite alignment in 3D cultures.

Beyond structural improvements, electrophysiological analyses demonstrated that combined RL and dcEF stimulation promoted neuron maturation, as the co-stimulated cells exhibited repetitive spiking activity, indicative of mature neuronal functionality14. This dual enhancement in both structural and functional developments underscores the therapeutic potential of dcEF and RL co-stimulation in neural repair strategies, providing a promising foundation for future applications in neuroregenerative medicine and bioelectronic therapies8,9. However, it is important to note that repetitive spiking in immortalized cell lines does not necessarily imply functional integration into a neural network. Moreover, such responses cannot be assumed to fully recapitulate the behavior of primary neurons or in vivo neural tissue. Thus, our results should be interpreted as evidence of functional ion-channel activity and excitability within SH-SY5Y cells after the co-stimulation of RL and dcEFs. Further studies in primary and in vivo models to establish network-level functionality are necessary.

The choice of a 3D collagen gel culture system was motivated by its ability to better approximate the extracellular environment of neural tissue compared to conventional 2D cultures. In 2D systems, neuronal cells grow on flat, rigid substrates that lack the structural complexity and biophysical cues present in vivo, which can limit the fidelity of neurite outgrowth and network formation. In contrast, 3D collagen matrices provide a supportive scaffold that more closely mimics the extracellular matrix, facilitating cell–matrix interactions, neurite extension in multiple directions, and a spatial organization that resembles native neural tissue. Thus, the use of 3D culture strengthens the translational impact of our findings. The findings of this present work are consistent with previous research on electrical stimulation in 3D neuronal cultures. Meng et al. showed that pulsed DC stimulation at 1 V/cm for 12 days significantly enhanced the differentiation of neural stem cells into neurons, demonstrating the importance of electrical cues in neuronal development25. Additionally, electrical stimulations have been reported to facilitate neurite outgrowth in 3D neuronal cultures derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells, reinforcing its role in neuronal repair and regeneration26,27. The observed alignment and elongation of neurites in response to dcEF in our study further support the hypothesis that electrical stimulation provides directional cues for neurite extension and cellular organization, which may contribute to the formation of functional neural networks.

Similarly, optical stimulation has emerged as a promising tool for neuronal activation. Our previous study utilizing RL illumination focused on the soma near the junction of retracted neurites demonstrated significant neurite re-growth, suggesting that RL PBM might activate intracellular pathways involved in axonal repair and regeneration5. These optical stimulation methods provide additional non-invasive strategies for modulating neural activity, making them attractive for therapeutic applications. Nonetheless, the application of RL as a stimulation modality presents inherent challenges for in vivo translation. The limited penetration depth of visible light in biological tissues, particularly in mammalian brain structures, may constrain its efficacy in deeper regions. Addressing this issue will likely require the development of optical fiber–based systems capable of targeting specific neural regions.

Taken together, our experimental results provide evidence that combined dcEF and RL co-stimulation can enhance neuronal differentiation and neurite extension. Neuronal cells subjected to this co-stimulation exhibited not only structural improvements but also repetitive spiking activities, suggestive of functional maturation through the development of ion-channel activity and excitability. These findings highlight the potential of dcEF and RL co-stimulation as a promising neuroregenerative approach, bridging physical interventions with molecular signaling mechanisms.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of immortalized neuroblastoma-derived cell lines (SH-SY5Y and N2a) does not fully replicate the properties of primary neurons, and our collagen-based 3D culture system lacks the complexity of native neural tissue. Future research should therefore validate these findings in primary neuronal cultures and animal models to establish their translational relevance. In addition, it will be necessary to compare dcEF + RL-treated cells with naturally matured long-term cultures to evaluate potential differences in neuronal maturation.

Conclusion

Given their high compatibility with existing clinical procedures and instrumentation, optical and electrical stimulations hold significant promise for neuronal repair. However, the optimal treatment parameters remain largely undetermined, necessitating further investigation. The 3D cell culture model employed in this study provided a simple, yet physiologically relevant platform for directly assessing the effects of optical and electrical stimuli on neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth.

Our experimental results provide evidence supporting the use of combined dcEF and RL co-stimulation to enhance neuronal differentiation and neurite extension. Notably, the neuronal cells subjected to dcEF and RL co-stimulation exhibited repetitive spiking activities, indicating functional maturation alongside structural improvements. These findings suggest that co-stimulation may serve as an effective neuroregenerative strategy, bridging the gap between physical interventions and molecular signaling mechanisms.

Future research should explore the translational applications of these findings, particularly in the context of neurodegenerative diseases and neural injury. By integrating molecular insights with innovative physical stimulation techniques, this work lays the foundation for advanced therapeutic strategies in neuroscience and regenerative medicine, paving the way for novel approaches to neural repair and functional recovery.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y (ATCC CRL-2266) and the mouse neuroblastoma cell line N2a (ATCC CCL-131) were used to investigate the effects of red-light (RL) illumination and direct-current electric field (dcEF) stimulation on neuronal differentiation. SH-SY5Y cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in Minimum Essential Medium Alpha (MEM-α, with nucleosides; Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 12571-063) and Ham’s F-12 Nutrient Mix (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 11765-054) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Biorion, Cat. No. AAFB0001-500, Lot. 24CL001) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Pen-Strep; Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 15140-122). N2a cells were obtained from Bioresource Collection and Research Center (Hsinchu, Taiwan), and cultured in MEM-α supplemented with the same concentration of 10% FBS and 1% Pen-Strep. Both cell types were kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. We maintained cultures at ~ 50% confluency prior to stimulation to avoid excessive cell–cell contact and nutrient limitation, which can confound neurite outgrowth measurements. Higher confluency often led to overlapping neurites and clustering, making quantitative analysis less reliable. Therefore, maintaining ~ 50% confluency was chosen as an optimal balance between sufficient cell viability and the ability to accurately assess neurite growth. To ensure cellular integrity and experimental consistency, only cells at passages ≤ 10 were used in this study. Cell morphology was frequently monitored to ensure phenotypic consistency. All cell cultures were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination using PCR-based assays.

Because the commercial testing services in Taiwan currently do not provide murine cell line authentication, only the human SH-SY5Y line was submitted for short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. The STR analysis was performed using the GenePrint® 24 System (Promega) for PCR amplification, and the resulting profile was compared against the Cellosaurus database. The analysis confirmed that the tested sample matched the authentic SH-SY5Y reference profile. The verification report is provided in the Supplementary Information.

Hydrogel preparation for 3D neural cell culture

A Type I collagen hydrogel was prepared to support neuronal growth in a 3D environment. The hydrogel was formulated using Corning® Collagen I (Corning, Cat. No. 354236, 3.89 mg/mL) at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. To achieve this, 257 µL of collagen stock solution was diluted on ice to a final volume of 1.0 mL, preventing premature gelation. The hydrogel mixture included 50 µL of 10× RB buffer (2.2% NaHCO₃ in 0.2 M HEPES) and 1.0 µL of 1.0 M NaOH solution to adjust the pH to ~ 7.0. To ensure a uniform cell distribution, the culture medium containing 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL was incorporated into the hydrogel mixture. A total of 200 µL of the prepared hydrogel was layered into a µ-Dish 35 mm high Glass Bottom dish (ibidi, Germany, Cat. No. 81158), forming a 3D culture environment with a thickness of approximately 640 μm, suitable for confocal imaging. Figure 1c shows the fluorescence image of the formed collagen gel.

In order to ensure homogeneous cell distribution within the hydrogel, we used the following procedure to prepare the 3D culture. The cells were uniformly mixed into the hydrogel, and 200 µL of the suspension was dispensed into the 2 cm glass-bottom well at the center of the culture dish. The dish was then left at room temperature for 3 min to allow partial surface gelation. Subsequently, the dish was inverted. Because the hydrogel layer was relatively thin (~ 640 μm) and was supported by surface tension with the partially gelled surface, inversion did not cause the hydrogel to detach. Instead, this procedure effectively prevented the cells from settling at the bottom. The dish was maintained in the inverted position for an additional 30 min to allow complete gelation, thereby ensuring a homogeneous distribution of cells throughout the hydrogel.

Direct-current electric field generation in 3D cell culture

To generate a dcEF within the 3D cell culture environment, two agar salt bridges (1.5% agar dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) were connected to the culture dish, ensuring a stable and uniform field application. The opposite ends of the salt bridges were submerged in PBS reservoirs, where Ag (anode) and AgCl (cathode) electrodes were positioned and connected to a DC power supply to establish a stable electric field. The distance between the two salt bridges was maintained at 2 cm, optimizing uniform field distribution across the 3D culture system.

To verify the distribution of electric fields within the 3D cell culture environment, numerical simulations were performed using COMSOL Multiphysics. The geometry of the culture system, including salt bridge positioning and medium properties, was incorporated into the simulation model. Boundary conditions were defined to replicate the experimental setup, with the applied electric potential at the electrodes and the resulting electric field simulated throughout the culture medium. The simulated field strength and distribution are shown in Fig. 7. The experimental observation area was confined to the central region of the hydrogel with a 5-mm diameter, ensuring that measurements were taken from areas where the electric field distribution remained nearly uniform.

Red light illumination system for 3D cell culture

A THORLABS LED625L (ThorLabs Inc., USA), an AlGaInP-type LED encapsulated in a spherical sealed glass lens designed for a narrow emission angle, was used as the RL source. The device provides illumination at a central wavelength of 625 ± 10 nm, with an adjustable irradiance range of 0–5 mW/cm². RL exposure was applied for 21, 45, or 69 h, corresponding to the different experimental conditions described in the study. The LED intensity was adjusted according to experimental requirements to ensure large-area illumination across the cell culture plane of the Petri dishes. The light intensity was precisely controlled using a light power meter and a variable voltage power supply. During the experiments, the LED and cell culture dishes were placed inside a temperature-controlled incubator maintained at 37 °C, ensuring consistent environmental conditions.

Observations were focused on a circular area with a 5-mm diameter, matching the applied electric field region, to maintain uniform experimental conditions and facilitate direct comparisons between the effects of RL stimulation and dcEF exposure. We observed that prolonged or more intense stimulations (e.g., higher RL intensity or extended dcEF duration) impaired cell viability. To mitigate this effect, we performed systematic preliminary tests across a range of stimulation intensities and durations. Based on these preliminary evaluations, the final parameters employed in this study were selected to maximize neurite outgrowth while minimizing cytotoxic effects.

Immunostaining

Fluorescence imaging of F-actin in neuroblastoma cells was performed using Alexa Fluor™ 488 Phalloidin (A12379, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to specifically label F-actin filaments. Neuroblastoma cell-embedded 3D hydrogels were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS at 4 °C overnight, followed by three washes with PBS to remove residual fixative.

Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min, after which the samples were again washed three times with PBS. To reduce non-specific binding, the samples were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature before incubation with Alexa Fluor™ 488 Phalloidin for 1 h. After staining, the samples were washed with PBS and counterstained with 1 µg/mL DAPI solution (62248, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min to visualize the cell nucleus.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA–Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam, Cat. No. ab113851, Lot. GR3346971-19). SH-SY5Y cells were first cultured in T-25 flasks and incubated with 10 µM DCFDA dissolved in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After staining, cells were gently washed with PBS, detached, and subsequently embedded in 1 mg/mL Type I collagen hydrogel at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL to establish the 3D culture. Fluorescence signals were acquired using a 488-nm excitation laser, and emission was collected at 515–550 nm.

For ROS measurements, confocal z-stack images were acquired under identical laser power, detector gain, and exposure settings for all samples. The fluorescence intensity of each DCFDA-labeled cell was quantified using ImageJ, and the mean signal was normalized to the local background intensity obtained from adjacent cell-free regions within the same image plane. This per-cell normalization effectively corrected for background fluorescence, dye leakage, and intensity variation across the 3D volume. To reduce photobleaching, imaging was completed within 10 min after sample mounting, and all groups were processed in parallel under identical acquisition parameters. The normalized fluorescence ratio (I_stimulation/I_control) was used for quantitative comparison across experimental conditions.

Image acquisition and analysis

Confocal fluorescence images of 3D SH-SY5Y neural cultures were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 880 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Z-stacks were captured over a total depth of 100 μm with 5 μm z-steps, and maximum-intensity projections were generated for quantitative analysis.

The neurite length was measured using ImageJ (NIH) with the NeuronJ plugin28, which enables semi-manual tracing of all visible neurite processes. All visible neurites extending from each soma were traced and included in the total length measurement for that cell. To ensure measurement reliability, two independent researchers performed tracing and quantification, and their results were compared. The inter-rater correlation coefficient exceeded 0.95, confirming high reproducibility and minimal operator bias.

For neurite orientation analysis, the trajectory of each traced neurite was represented as a vector connecting the soma center to the distal neurite tip. The neurite-orientation angle θ was defined as the angle (0–180°) between the neurite growth direction and the dcEF vector within the imaging plane. For each traced neurite, θ was determined from the vector connecting the soma center to the distal neurite tip relative to the dcEF direction. |cos θ| was computed and averaged across all neurites in each condition to yield <|cos θ|>. The orientation histograms were plotted with an 18° bin interval in Fig. 3b and e.

The neurite trajectories were obtained from maximum-intensity projections of confocal z-stacks (total depth: 100 μm; z-step size: 5 μm). Only clearly distinguishable, in-focus neurites were included. All visible neurites extending from each soma were manually traced in ImageJ using the NeuronJ plugin.

In all data figures, each data point represents a single, visibly distinguishable axon (neurite) traced from the images, and the total number of axons is reported as n. Measurements were performed on randomly acquired fields within the stimulation region across independently repeated experiments (N). Cells/neurites that did not meet the preset quality-control criteria—such as overlap with adjacent structures, being out of focus in the z-plane, neurite endings leaving the field of view, unclear soma boundaries, or signs of degeneration—were excluded from analysis. This filtering explains, for example, why the number of data points at 4 h under 175 mV/mm differs from other time intervals. Statistical procedures that accommodate unequal n across groups were applied.

RNA sequencing

The SH-SY5Y cells cultured in a 3D environment were harvested for RNA sequencing to analyze gene expression changes under various experimental conditions. We conducted the RNA-seq experiments in two independent batches. In each batch, cells from the same passage were simultaneously plated into four separate culture dishes corresponding to the four experimental conditions, and total RNA was independently extracted from each dish. This design yielded two biological replicates per condition across the two sequencing runs. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 15596026) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and integrity of the extracted RNA were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), ensuring that all samples had an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7 to meet sequencing quality standards.

The RNA samples were then sent to Tri-i Biotech, Inc. (New Taipei City, Taiwan) for library preparation, sequencing, and preliminary data analysis, ensuring high-quality transcriptomic profiling for further investigation of differentially expressed genes and pathway enrichment.

Evaluation of drug efficacy

To investigate the role of neuropeptide Y (NPY) in the pathway activated by optical and electrical stimulations, we utilized specific inhibitors targeting NPY receptors Y1 (NPY1R) and Y2 (NPY2R). The NPY1R inhibitor BIBP 3226 (HY-100052, MedChemExpress) and the NPY2R inhibitor BIIE 0246 (HY-101986, MedChemExpress) were selected for this study. The drug concentration range was initially tested between 1 and 100 µM, and a final concentration of 20 µM for both inhibitors was chosen based on cell viability assessments.

To evaluate cell viability, an AlamarBlue assay (DAL1025, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed. The reagent (10% v/v) was added to the culture medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2–4 h. Fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission wavelength of 590 nm, and relative viability was calculated by comparing treated and untreated groups. The maximum non-toxic concentrations of the inhibitors were used to assess the impacts of RL and dcEF stimulation on axon growth length, ensuring that observed effects were attributed to pathway modulation rather than cytotoxicity.

Electrophysiology

Whole-cell current-clamp recordings were performed to evaluate the excitability and functional maturation of SH-SY5Y neurons after stimulation. The resting membrane potential of cells was typically between − 40 and − 45 mV. A small holding current (approximately − 20 pA) was applied to stabilize the membrane potential near − 60 mV before stimulation. To assess excitability, stepwise depolarizing current injections were delivered in + 5 pA increments (500 msec per step) to evoke spike-like responses.

Recordings were conducted on an inverted optical microscope, using glass micropipettes fabricated with a Sutter Model P-97 horizontal puller. Pipette resistance ranged from 2 to 5 MΩ, and pipettes were filled with an intracellular solution containing 150 mM KCl and 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.2). The external bath solution consisted of self-prepared 1× artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF). The signals were amplified with an Axon Axopatch 200B amplifier and digitized through a Digidata 1440 A interface (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Recordings were low-pass filtered at 5 kHz, sampled at 25 kHz, and analyzed using Clampfit 10.7 software. Only recordings with a resting potential below − 40 mV and stable access resistance were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess significant differences. Post hoc Tukey’s test was applied for pairwise comparisons between specific two groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, significance levels are indicated as: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001. Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel with the XrealStats add-in to ensure rigorous and reproducible statistical evaluation.

Effect sizes (η²) were calculated for one-way ANOVA to indicate the magnitude of group differences, with η² values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 corresponding to small, medium, and large effects, respectively.
