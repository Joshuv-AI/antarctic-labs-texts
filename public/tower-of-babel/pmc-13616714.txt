Unsupervised discovery of public health subgroups associated with cognitive health vulnerability in adults: an analysis of BRFSS 2024
Authors: Ziyue Wang, Joshi Dookhy, Hossein Khaleghy, Soumyajyoti Maji, Xuejia Yang, Carmel Geoghegan, Jia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive health-related vulnerability is closely associated with multidimensional public health factors, including chronic disease burden, functional limitation, healthcare access, socioeconomic vulnerability, health behaviors, and preventive service utilization. However, latent population heterogeneity embedded in routine public health survey variables remains insufficiently explored.ObjectiveThis study aims to develop an unsupervised semantic stratification framework based on BRFSS 2024 data to identify adult population subgroups associated with cognitive health-related vulnerability from a public health perspective.MethodsRoutine public health variables were selected, cleaned, imputed, and standardized, while cognitive reference variables were excluded from model training. An autoencoder was used to learn low-dimensional latent representations, followed by MiniBatchKMeans clustering to identify semantic subgroups. Each subgroup was further analyzed through public health profiling, held-out cognitive reference validation, non-redundant feature signature analysis, and intervention priority assessment.ResultsFour interpretable semantic subgroups were identified: the low-burden subgroup, general health vulnerability subgroup, health behavior risk subgroup, and preventive service gap subgroup. These subgroups showed distinct patterns in public health burden, cognitive reference variables, key feature signatures, and intervention priorities.ConclusionRoutine public health survey data can support the identification of latent adult subgroups associated with cognitive health-related vulnerability and provide exploratory evidence for population monitoring, priority screening, resource allocation, and stratified intervention design. This framework is not a diagnostic, prognostic, or causal inference model, and further validation using weighted, longitudinal, cross-year, and external data is needed.

Introduction

Subjective cognitive decline has become an important public health concern because it reflects self-reported cognitive difficulties that may affect daily functioning, health service use, caregiving needs, and long-term cognitive health monitoring among adults. Although dementia and related cognitive disorders continue to impose substantial pressure on families, health systems, and social support structures (1, 2), the present study does not aim to diagnose dementia or directly predict clinical dementia outcomes. Instead, it focuses on cognitive health vulnerability in population-level surveillance data, where self-reported cognitive concerns can serve as meaningful signals for identifying groups that may require earlier attention, health education, and targeted public health support (3, 4).

A key challenge in studying cognitive health vulnerability is that it is unlikely to be distributed uniformly across the adult population. Adults differ substantially in chronic disease burden, functional limitation, behavioral patterns, mental health status, healthcare access, socioeconomic resources, and social support. These factors may interact with one another and produce distinct vulnerability profiles rather than a single homogeneous risk pattern. Prior studies have shown that social determinants of health, chronic conditions, metabolic risks, and behavioral factors are closely associated with cognitive aging, dementia-related outcomes, and subjective cognitive decline (5–8). However, most existing studies still rely mainly on predefined variable associations, conventional stratified statistics, or single-factor explanations, which may be insufficient for revealing latent subgroup structures within complex public health survey data.

Unsupervised learning provides a useful methodological pathway for addressing this limitation. Compared with supervised prediction models that require predefined labels, unsupervised methods can explore latent population structures directly from multidimensional public health variables. Representation learning methods can transform high-dimensional survey variables into lower-dimensional latent spaces while preserving essential information and reducing redundancy (9). Deep clustering and embedding-based methods further support the discovery of hidden population patterns from complex health data (10–12). Nevertheless, public health survey data differ from hospital records and clinical trajectories because they contain broader social, behavioral, and self-reported health indicators, often with weaker clinical labels and stronger requirements for interpretability. Therefore, subgroup discovery in this context should not only identify latent data structures but also connect them with meaningful public health and cognitive health vulnerability profiles.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an unsupervised analytical framework for semantic subgroup discovery and stratification of cognitive health vulnerability among adults using BRFSS 2024 data. The framework first standardizes and encodes multidimensional public health variables, learns low-dimensional latent representations, and then performs data-driven clustering in the latent space to identify latent adult subgroups. To improve interpretability, the identified clusters are semantically characterized using multiple public health dimensions, including demographic, behavioral, chronic disease, mental health, healthcare access, and social determinant-related features. Cognitive health-related variables are then introduced as held-out reference indicators to examine whether the discovered subgroups correspond to meaningful differences in subjective cognitive concerns and related functional limitations. This study aims to identify interpretable latent public-health subgroups among adults and examine whether these subgroups differ in cognitive health-related reference indicators without relying on clinical diagnostic labels.

Data and methods

Dataset

This study used data from the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), an annual public health survey conducted in the United States that collects information on demographic characteristics, health behaviors, chronic conditions, functional status, healthcare access, and preventive service use through telephone interviews. BRFSS is suitable for population-level public health analysis because it provides large-scale surveillance data with broad variable coverage and includes a cognitive decline module relevant to subjective cognitive decline and its functional consequences. The final analytic dataset used in this study contained 457,670 records after preprocessing. Table 1 summarizes the basic information of the dataset used in this study.

Data preprocessing

Because the original BRFSS 2024 (13) dataset includes both landline and mobile survey pathways, as well as many interview control fields, sampling identifiers, and derived statistical variables, directly using all fields would introduce irrelevant information and increase redundancy in subsequent representation learning. Therefore, this study first conducted task oriented variable selection for cognitive health related public health analysis. Specifically, interview dates, sample identifiers, telephone pathway screening fields, questionnaire version fields, sampling weights, and other process related variables with limited relevance were removed. In addition, topic specific variables with weak relevance to cognitive health analysis in adults were excluded, including cancer screening, gynecological and prostate related items, caregiver modules, and other less relevant sections. After this step, variables more closely related to demographic characteristics, health status, chronic conditions, functional limitations, health behaviors, health care access, and cognitive health were retained. Special missing codes in the retained fields were then uniformly identified and replaced, and common feature variables were further screened according to missingness. After variable selection, retained fields were divided into two groups according to their analytic roles. The first group consisted of common public health feature variables used for representation learning and subgroup discovery, while the second group consisted of held-out cognitive reference variables used only for post hoc validation and interpretation. This separation was intended to reduce information leakage by excluding cognitive reference variables from the representation-learning and clustering stages. The final dataset included 35 common feature variables and five cognitive reference variables. Special missing codes were recoded as missing values, common features with high missingness were removed, and the remaining common features were mode-imputed. Because this analysis was designed as an exploratory subgroup-discovery study, BRFSS sampling weights were not incorporated into model training or clustering. Accordingly, identified subgroup proportions should be interpreted as patterns within the analytic sample rather than direct population prevalence estimates.

Semantic subgroup number determination and interpretability analysis

To ensure that the latent semantic subgrouping results not only exhibit satisfactory statistical aggregation quality but also form stable group structures with clear public health significance, this study determined the number of subgroups by jointly considering internal clustering metrics and result interpretability. Specifically, after obtaining low-dimensional latent representations from the autoencoder, clustering was conducted under different candidate numbers of clusters, ranging from K = 2 to K = 8. All candidate solutions used the same MiniBatchKMeans settings and random seed, and clustering metrics were calculated using the same sample of 50,000 respondents to ensure comparability. On this basis, this study further screened candidate clustering schemes by incorporating their public health semantic interpretability. When the number of clusters is too small, populations with substantially heterogeneous risk characteristics may be compressed into the same subgroup, making it difficult to reveal differentiated structures related to chronic disease burden, functional limitation, barriers to healthcare accessibility, socioeconomic vulnerability, and unhealthy behaviors. In contrast, when the number of clusters is too large, the population may be excessively fragmented, causing some subgroups to reflect only local variable fluctuations and thereby weakening the stability of the overall structure and its practical interpretive value. As shown in Table 3, across five independent random seeds, the four-cluster solution achieved the highest mean silhouette score of 0.320231 ± 0.005097 and the highest mean Calinski–Harabasz index of 15101.797169 ± 146.683758, together with the lowest mean Davies–Bouldin index of 1.492168 ± 0.021467 among all candidate solutions. The consistent superiority of these three internal metrics indicates that K = 4 provides the most compact and clearly separated clustering structure. Considering both internal clustering performance and semantic profiling results, the four-subgroup solution was found to achieve a relatively appropriate balance between statistical stability and public health interpretability. On the one hand, under this partition, the subgroups showed relatively clear boundaries and good compactness in the latent space. On the other hand, these subgroups could be stably aligned with semantic structures carrying explicit public health meaning, including low burden, general health vulnerability, behavioral risk, and preventive care gap. Therefore, this study ultimately selected K = 4 as the number of semantic subgroups to support subsequent analyses of external cognitive health validation, multidimensional burden profiling, and comprehensive risk stratification.

Experimental results and analysis

Experimental setup

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method for public health semantic profiling related to cognitive health in adults, experiments were conducted on the BRFSS 2024 dataset. In the experiments, the preprocessed common public health features were used to construct the input matrix, while the cognitive reference variables were treated as external validation information rather than training signals in order to avoid information leakage during representation learning. In terms of model implementation, an autoencoder was adopted as the backbone for deep representation learning. The encoder compressed high dimensional public health variables into low dimensional latent representations, and MiniBatchKMeans was then applied in the latent space to identify semantic subgroups among respondents. After that, t-SNE was used to project the latent embeddings into a two dimensional space, and the clustering results were further interpreted and externally validated by combining public health dimension profiles with the distributions of cognitive reference variables. The experiments were implemented with Python, PyTorch, and scikit-learn. Adam was used as the optimizer, mean squared error was used as the reconstruction loss, the number of clusters was set to 4, and clustering performance was evaluated by the sampled silhouette coefficient. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments used a fixed random seed and unified data processing and parameter settings. The main experimental settings are summarized in Table 4.

These findings indicate that chronic diseases, preventive services, functional limitations, and socioeconomic factors jointly constitute an important explanatory basis for semantic stratification within the adult population.

Finally, to further enhance the public health applicability of semantic subgroup analysis, this section examines intervention priorities and potential intervention pathways for different semantic subgroups within the adult population by considering the relationships among subgroup size, composite public health burden, cognitive reference burden, and dominant intervention dimensions. This analysis aims to further translate the preceding results of representation learning, semantic profiling, and cognitive reference validation into population-level identification and intervention clues from a public health perspective, rather than interpreting them as individual-level clinical diagnoses or deterministic risk predictions.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that combining unsupervised deep representation learning with semantic profiling can identify interpretable latent subgroups from routine public health survey data and reveal differences in held-out cognitive reference variables. Unlike individual-level diagnosis or risk prediction, the present study focuses on population heterogeneity embedded in chronic disease burden, functional limitation, healthcare access, socioeconomic vulnerability, general health, health behaviors, and preventive service gaps. The learned representation space produced distinct subgroup profiles across public health dimensions, cognitive reference variables, key features, and intervention priorities, supporting its exploratory use for population stratification and public health resource planning.

The identified subgroups suggest that cognitive health-related vulnerability may accumulate through different public health pathways. The general health vulnerability subgroup was characterized mainly by poor general health, chronic disease, and functional limitation; the health behavior risk subgroup combined behavioral and chronic disease burdens; and the preventive service gap subgroup showed greater preventive-care and socioeconomic vulnerability. Although the low-burden subgroup had the lowest overall burden, “low burden” did not indicate the absence of risk. Its relatively elevated Preventive Care Gap score specifically reflected lower reported uptake of influenza and pneumococcal vaccination. Because healthcare access was represented separately, the present cross-sectional and self-reported data cannot determine whether this pattern was associated with reduced access, under-detection, or under-evaluation of other risks.

Public health strategies should therefore consider health services, chronic disease management, functional status, socioeconomic conditions, behaviors, and preventive care jointly. However, psychosocial coverage was limited. The 35 clustering variables included self-reported depressive disorder and several social or socioeconomic characteristics, but did not directly measure anxiety, loneliness, social isolation, or social support. These factors are important in multidomain dementia prevention and cognitive and cardiovascular health assessment (14–16). Accordingly, the targeted intervention signatures should be interpreted as population-level risk-stratification and resource-prioritization patterns rather than individualized clinical recommendations. In addition, because the Cognitive Decline module was limited to respondents aged at least 45 years in selected state–questionnaire-version strata, module availability, skip patterns, and missingness may influence the observed cognitive-reference patterns, which are not nationally representative prevalence estimates.

Repeated analyses across five independent random seeds supported the internal stability of the four-cluster solution, but population-split, state-level, cross-year, and independent external validation were not performed. Differences in population composition, variable definitions, questionnaire versions, and optional-module availability may therefore affect generalizability. Future studies should incorporate survey weighting, population-split and geographic validation, harmonized multi-year BRFSS data, longitudinal follow-up, and independent datasets, together with broader psychosocial, preventive-service, healthcare-utilization, and clinical diagnostic indicators.

Conclusion

Based on the BRFSS 2024 adult public health survey data, this study constructs an exploratory analytical framework that integrates unsupervised deep representation learning, semantic subgroup identification, public health profiling, and cognitive reference variable validation to reveal the latent distribution structure of cognitive health-related vulnerability embedded in routine public health variables. The results indicate that cognitive health-related problems in the adult population should not be simply understood as isolated memory or cognitive phenomena, but are jointly associated with multidimensional public health factors, including chronic disease burden, functional limitation, barriers to healthcare access, socioeconomic vulnerability, general health status, health behavior risk, and preventive service gaps. The public health significance of this framework lies in its ability to identify interpretable latent population structures from routine survey data without relying on clinical diagnostic labels, thereby providing data-driven evidence for priority population identification, public health resource allocation, preventive service optimization, and stratified intervention design. It should be noted that the findings of this study do not represent dementia diagnosis, disease prognosis assessment, or causal inference; future research should further incorporate sampling weights, alternative modeling approaches, cross-year data, and longitudinal or external data sources to examine the stability, representativeness, and generalizability of the identified subgroup structure.
