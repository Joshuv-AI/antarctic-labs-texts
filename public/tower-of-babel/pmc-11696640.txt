Hydrogen storage properties of TiFe-based composite with Ni addition
Authors: Hao Sun, Ziqiang Yan, Zhonggang Han, Jiaxin Li, Tingting Zhai, Zeming Yuan, Tao Li, Jin Xu, Yanghuan Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper presents the preparation of the parental experimental alloy, featuring a standard composition of Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2, via the vacuum induction melting technique. Subsequently, the Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 alloy, with an addition of 2 wt% Ni, underwent mechanical ball milling to yield a TiFe-based composite for experimental purposes. The results of the experimental tests indicate that the composite alloy's phase composition comprises the TiFe primary phase, with a minor quantity of ZrMn2 phase segregated on the surface of the primary TiFe phase, as well as Ni phase. The alloy, which underwent ball-milling for 15 min, displayed excellent activation characteristics, featuring a 12-s incubation period at 150 °C and a hydrogen pressure of 3 MPa. Furthermore, the hydrogen absorption capacity was 1.72 wt% at 90 °C and 33 min. The decrease in particle size and grain refinement of the ball-milled alloy resulted in an increase in the grain boundary specific surface area, which provided more nucleation sites for the hydrides of the ball-milled alloy and enhanced the hydrogen absorption and desorption kinetic performance of the alloy. The thermodynamic properties test indicated that the absolute values of enthalpy (ΔH) and entropy (ΔS) changes exhibited a decreasing trend followed by an increase as the ball milling time extended from 5 min to 180 min during the hydrogen absorption and desorption process. The grain refinement occurring in short-term ball milling and the agglomeration occurring in long-term ball milling are the primary reasons for the variations in the alloy powder. The smallest absolute values of enthalpy and entropy changes were observed for the alloy ball-milled for 15 min, at 19.1 kJ/mol and 22.3 kJ/mol, respectively.

Introduction

TiFe-based alloy are a promising hydrogen storage compound by scholars with a theoretical hydrogen storage capacity of about 1.86 wt%, which have the advantages of abundant reserves, low price and high bulk density of hydrogen storage [[1], [2], [3]]. However, due to the formation of oxide layers such as TiO2 and MnO2 on the surface of TiFe-based alloy, its full activation necessitates numerous cycles of hydrogen absorption and desorption at elevated temperatures ranging from 400 °C to 450 °C, as well as high H2 pressure levels of 6.5 MPa [4]. Although advancements have been made in enhancing the activation performance, nonetheless, TiFe-based alloys have yet to find practical usage in industrial procedures, and the principal challenge persists in enhancing the activation performance of TiFe-based alloys. Over the decades, a number of measures have been investigated to enhance the activation properties of TiFe alloys, mainly in the following aspects: elemental substitution alloying [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]], plastic deformation (high-energy ball milling, high-pressure rolling or mechanical alloying) [[11], [12], [13], [14]], non-chemometric design [15,16], surface modification (physical surface coating and chemically reactive surface deposition) [[17], [18], [19]], and new manufacturing techniques (combustion synthesis or mechanical press synthesis) [20,21].

The activation properties as well as the hydrogen storage properties of the TiFe-based alloys can be improved after the above treatment. In previous work, TiFe-based alloys have been modified using rare earth element substitution, Shang et al. reported a Ti-Fe-Mn alloy prepared using Mn elements instead of Fe, which achieved a substantial improvement in activation properties [15]. It is well known that high-energy ball milling is another very effective method to shorten the activation incubation period. Generally speaking, mechanical ball milling significantly reduces particle/grain size, allows the formation of nanocrystals, microcrystals and amorphous grains in the alloy, and increases the defect density and boundary area of the alloy [22]. There is no doubt that particle size reduction and grain refinement not only shorts the diffusion path of hydrogen atoms, but also provides more diffusion channels. In addition, grain boundaries are the active sites for nucleation of the hydrogen absorbing phase of hydrogen storage alloys [3]. In summary, it is evident that mechanical ball milling, serves as a straightforward and efficient means to enhance the activation capabilities of hydrogen storage alloys.

In recent years, a great deal of research has been conducted by scholars on TiFe-based alloys by ball milling. Bououdina et al. reported that mechanical ball milling of TiFe hydrogen storage alloys with the addition of different catalytic elements could significantly improve their activation properties and hydrogen absorption and desorption kinetics [23]. Yamashita et al. reported that ball milling of TiFe-based alloys with the addition of catalytic monomeric Pd powders in an inert atmosphere can greatly enhance the activation and hydrogen storage properties of the alloys [24]. Furthermore, when the TiFe alloy powder, to which a small quantity of Ni powder has been added, is ball-milled for 20–30 h within an argon-protected environment, the experimental samples could readily absorb H2 without any activation treatment, and it is believed that the enhanced kinetic performance is due to the formation of TiFe fine powder that is encapsulated by nanocrystalline Ni particles and becomes a catalytic centre for the decomposition of hydrogen molecules [25]. Manna et al. investigated that ball milling and cold rolling processes could activate TiFe +4 wt% Zr alloys exposed to air [26]. Smaller particles (in the range of 100 nm - 100 μm) were formed in the alloy after ball milling for 30 min and there were no sharp fringes or agglomerated large particles. It was indicated that the ball milling and cold rolling processes significantly enhanced the activation properties of TiFe-based alloys, It is recommended that the rapid hydrogen absorption capabilities of the mechanically processed samples may be attributed to a combination of grain refinement and the presence of smaller particle sizes, and that the formation of defects may also play a role. Kumar et al. synthesised single-phase TiFe intermetallic and TiFe - 3.1 wt% V alloys by vacuum arc melting [27]. The absorption rates of hydrogen for TiFe intermetallic and FeTi alloy with 3.1 wt% V were studied in a reactor, under conditions of 0.2 MPa H2 and a temperature of 327 K. It was observed that the hydrogen absorption rate of the FeTi - 3.1 wt% V alloy was significantly faster than that of the single-phase TiFe intermetallic compound, due to the presence of vanadium in the alloy which promotes the decomposition of hydrogen molecules and improves the hydrogen absorption kinetics of the V-substituted alloy. Emami et al. investigated the effect of ball milling on the activation properties of TiFe-based alloys [28]. Researchers found that the early TiFe alloy consisted of numerous large grains, and through ball milling, the grain size was decreased to approximately 7 nm. Ball milling enhanced the activation characteristics of TiFe alloys, that is capable of absorbing hydrogen directly at 303 K without any prior treatment, boasting a hydrogen absorption capacity of 1.5 wt%. The highly strained grain boundaries are described as hydrogen trapping sites, and therefore there is a strong relationship between the grain size of TiFe and the activation performance: as the grain size gets smaller, activation is easier and the hydrogen pressure for activation is lower. Lv et al. [29] reported on the microstructure and first hydrogenation properties of ball milled TiFe + 4 wt% Zr alloys and the correlation between ball milling time and grain size reduction and capacity reduction. It was disclosed that ball milling, especially at low temperatures, notably decreased the particle and grain size, where the majority of grain size reduction happened in the initial 15 min of milling. Furthermore, as milling time increased, particle agglomeration became more intense. Faster kinetics was attributed to the reduction in grain size and the formation of new grain boundaries. Borislav et al. synthesised nanocrystalline/amorphous TiFe1-xCox (x = 0.1 and 0.3) and TiFe0.7Ni0.2Co0.1 alloys by mechanical alloying techniques [30]. High-energy ball milling reduced the average particle size of the alloys to 5–7 μm and the powders obtained had nanocrystalline and amorphous structures. The results show that ball milling improved the activation properties and cyclic stability of the alloys. Shang et al. combined the Ti1.04Fe0.7Ni0.1Zr0.1Mn0.1Pr0.06 + 10 wt% Ni composite by mechanical ball milling [31]. The ball milled Ti1.04Fe0.7Ni0.1Zr0.1Mn0.1Pr0.06 + 10 wt% Ni composites have good activation properties. The particle size reduction, grain refinement, increase in crystal defect density and change in surface state due to mechanical ball milling with the addition of nickel powder are considered to be the main reasons for the improved activation properties. Furthermore, the Ti1.1Fe0.9Ni0.1 + 7.5 wt% Ni and Ti1.09Mg0.01Fe0.9Ni0.1 + 7.5 wt% Ni alloys were mechanically ball milled and the ball milling resulted in a reduction in particle size and the creation of new surfaces without oxide layers, that notably improved the activation characteristics of the composite alloys [32].

Through comprehensive scholarly literatures review, it has been found that mechanical ball milling can induce two changes in the structure and morphology of alloy powders. On the one hand, mechanical ball milling transforms the structure from a typical polycrystalline structure to a nanocrystalline structure, which offers an increased number of pathways for the dispersion of hydrogen atoms. On the other hand, mechanical ball milling significantly reduces the particle size of the alloy, which can shorten the diffusion path of hydrogen atoms. In the previous work, the study of Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 alloys synthesised by replacing Fe with the transition metals Ni and Mn and partially replacing Ti with the quantitative Zr and the variable Y showed that elemental substitution not only shortens the incubation period but also boosts the hydrogen adsorption and desorption dynamics of the alloy [3]. The Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 alloy is a very promising candidate as a solid-state hydrogen storage material. Because of the strong electrical conductivity and catalytic activity of Ni powder, therefore, composites with Ni powder coating on the surface of Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 alloy were prepared by adding 2 wt% Ni at different ball milling times. The integration of elemental substitution with mechanical ball milling is anticipated to produce a novel material that is readily activated and boasts outstanding hydrogen storage capabilities. The optimal ball milling duration was established through the examination and analysis of the specimens' microstructure, activation performance, as well as their kinetic and thermodynamic properties.

Experimental

In this investigation, Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 alloy ingots of 3 cm in diameter and 8 cm in height with standard components were obtained by vacuum induction melting furnace. The ingots with the oxide layer removed were mechanically crushed and then, after passing through a 200 mesh screen, experimental alloy powder was obtained. Experimental composite alloys were obtained by mechanical ball milling of Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni together in a QM-3SP2 planetary ball mill at different ball milling times of 5 min–180 min. Ball mill parameters: ball to material ratio: 40:1, the balls and ball grinding jars are made of CrNi stainless steel, the volume of the ball grinding jars is 250 mL, utilizing two 20 mm steel balls, twenty 10 mm steel balls, and the rest comprising 6 mm steel balls. Ball mill tank full of Ar for protection, speed 400 rpm, to prevent overheating during the ball milling process, the ball mill is stopped after 60 min for 30 min of cooling.

The phase structure and composition of the ball-milled composite was tested by XRD diffraction (D/max/2400), which can be carried out with CuKα1 rays filtered by nickel. The test procedure was carried out at an operating current of 160 mA, an operating voltage of 40 kV, a scanning speed of 2°/min, and a 2θ range of 20°–90° operating parameters. The microscopic morphology and crystal morphology of the ball-milled composite were determined by SEM (QUANTA 400) and HRTEM (JEM-2100F, operated at 200 kV), respectively, and energy dispersive spectroscopy was used to observe the elemental composition of the phases of the ball-milled composite.

The measurements of the kinetics and thermodynamics characteristics of the ball-milled composite were conducted using a Sievert-style manual PCT hydrogen storage tester, equipped with a heating furnace and featuring a temperature control precision of ±1 °C, manufactured by the Beijing Research Institute of Non-Ferrous Metals. The composite, undergoing ball milling, was subjected to three cycles of hydrogen absorption and desorption at a temperature of 150 °C and a hydrogen pressure of 3 MPa for activation. The fully activated ball-milled composite was measured at 30 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C and 90 °C for the hydrogen absorption and desorption kinetic properties, and at 3 MPa hydrogen pressure and 1 × 10−4 MPa hydrogen pressure respectively. The P-C-T curves of the ball-milled composite were performed at 30 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C and 90 °C with a sample weight of approximately 1 g per measurement.

Results and discussion

Microstructure characteristics

The XRD graphs of the Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni alloy at 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min, and 180 min of ball milling are shown in Fig. 1. The graphs indicate that alloys subjected to various ball milling durations exhibit nearly the same diffraction patterns. Prior to hydrogen absorption, the phase composition and structural attributes of the ball-milled alloy powder were ascertained through Jade 6.0 analysis. The composition of phases in the alloy subjected to ball-milling comprised a primary TiFe phase, with a minor quantity of ZrMn2 phase segregated on the phase boundary of TiFe primary phase and Ni phases. The position of the diffraction peaks of the ball-milled samples did not shift under different ball milling time conditions, indicating that ball milling did not change the phase composition of the alloy. However, the diffraction peaks of the ball-milled samples gradually broadened as the ball-milling time was gradually increased from 5 min to 180 min, indicating that ball-milling contributed to the grain refinement of the composites. Furthermore, some of the weaker peaks of the ball-milled composite were no longer observed after ball-milled 180 min, and the weaker diffraction peaks of ZrMn2 gradually disappeared. The improvement of the activation, as well as the hydrogen absorption and desorption kinetic characteristics of the hydrogen storage alloy, is not solely linked to the crystalline structure and phase composition, but also relies on the particle dimensions and surface condition. Therefore, to improve further the activation characteristics and kinetics of hydrogen absorption and desorption in the base alloy, the Ti1.08Zr0.1Y0.02Fe0.6Ni0.3Mn0.2 alloy with Ni composite was ball milled in order to reduce the particle size of the alloy. Fig. 3 represents the SEM images of the Ti1.08Y0.02Zr0.1Fe0.7Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni alloy at 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min, and 180 min of ball milling, it can be observed that the particle size of the short time ball-milled alloy is large (about 3–10 μm), and angular and irregular in shape from Fig. 3 (a). As shown in Fig. 3(b) and (c), mechanical ball milling causes the particle size of the ball-milled alloy to decrease to 2–4 μm, the angles gradually disappear, and the grain shape gradually tends to spherical, which inevitably leads to a considerable rise in the specific surface area of the alloy. In addition, as the ball milling time continues to be extended to 30 min, the alloy particles start to agglomerate, and after the ball milling time exceeds 60 min the agglomeration phenomenon becomes more serious, this results from the decrease in particle dimensions and the elevation in surface energy caused by the augmentation in surface imperfections linked with mechanical ball milling. Aggregation of particles results in a reduction of the particle refinement level and an elevation in the specific surface area. Therefore, agglomeration phenomenon should be minimised during the preparation of the material [[33], [34], [35], [36]].Fig. 3SEM morphologies of the as-milled Ti1.08Zr0.1Y0.02Fe0.6Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni alloys: (a) ball-milling 5 min alloy, (b) ball-milling 15 min alloy, (c) ball-milling 30 min alloy, (d) ball-milling 60 min alloy, (e) ball-milling 180 min alloy.Fig. 3

Fig. 5 shows the backscattering electron micrographs and elemental mapping of the Ti1.08Zr0.1Y0.02Fe0.6Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 %Ni alloy after ball milling for 5 min and 60 min. From the distribution of Ti, Fe, and Ni elements in the short-time ball milling alloy depicted in Fig. 5(a), coupled with XRD analysis, it is evident that the TiFe phase with solid-solution Ni constitutes the primary phase of the alloy. Additionally, the Zr, Mn, and Y elements within the short-time ball milled alloy preserve the distribution pattern of the as-cast alloy, with the presence of ZrMn2 and the rare-earth Y phases. As evident from Fig. 5(b), the distribution of Zr, Mn and Y in the alloy becomes uniform with the prolongation of the ball milling time, and the long-time ball milling leads to the disappearance of the amorphisation of ZrMn2 and the rare earth Y phases, which is consistent with the results of the XRD patterns and TEM analyses. In addition, the O element is evenly distributed on the surface of the alloy particles, indicating that the oxide TiO2 is formed on the alloy surface, which is why the alloy is difficult to activate. Ball milling significantly reduces the size of alloy particles and shortens the path of H atoms into the interior of the matrix alloy, which can enhance the activation characteristics and rate of hydrogen absorption and desorption of the ball-milled composites. But the agglomerates produced by long-duration ball milling inevitably induces a decrease in the hydrogen-absorbing capacity of the alloy, and Yuan et al. In general, The experimental alloys' activation attributes and hydrogen storage characteristics hinge on their microstructural compositions. The ball milling treatment can yield two effects: (a) ball milling results in the finer alloy particles and the elevated defect density, that not merely offers additional activation sites for the nucleation of TiFe/ZrMn2 hydrides, but also facilitates the nucleation and growth of TiFeH/ZrMn2H3 [3]; (b) long-time ball milling leads to the agglomeration of the alloy, which impacts adversely on the activation characteristics and hydrogen uptake and release dynamics of the tested alloy. Therefore, a suitable ball milling time is important for improving the hydrogen storage properties of the alloy. Activation behaviors and hydrogen absorption kinetics

TiFe alloys necessitate numerous rounds of hydrogen absorption and release at elevated temperatures ranging from 400 °C to 450 °C, along with high hydrogen pressure levels of 4–6 MPa H2 [4], to attain complete activation, posing a significant hurdle that impedes their practical utilization. After the initial attempt of the experiment, the activation temperature of the ball-milled composite was uniformly set at 150 °C, and the starting pressure was established at 3 MPa. The initial activation temperature (T), the initial hydrogen pressure (P), the number of activation cycles (n) and the incubation (t) were the four indicators to evaluate the activation ability. Under the same conditions of activation temperature, initial hydrogen pressure and activation period, the shorter the activation incubation, the superior the activation capability.

Fig. 7 presents the three activation hydrogen absorption curves of the Ti1.08Zr0.1Y0.02Fe0.6Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni alloy after ball milling for 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min and 180 min. According to Fig. 7(a)–(e), it is observed that there is a incubation period for the ball-milled alloy powder during the first hydrogen absorption process. In addition, the two remaining kinetic curves for hydrogen absorption of the ball-milled alloy, following the initial hydrogen absorption and desorption cycle, are essentially aligned, which indicates that the alloy is fully activated. From the first activation curves in Fig. 7 (f), it can be seen that the incubation periods of the ball-milled alloy were 80 s, 12 s, 54 s, 72 s, and 132 s for 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min, and 180 min of ball-milled alloy, respectively, and the incubation time was first shortened and then extended. Hydrogen absorption and desorption kinetics

The kinetic properties of the hydrogen storage alloy are usually expressed in terms of hydrogen absorption/desorption capacity and absorption/desorption rates. Fig. 10 exhibits the kinetic curves of hydrogen absorption for Ti1.08Zr0.1Y0.02Fe0.6Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni alloy after complete activation in ball milling for 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min, and 180 min. From Fig. 10(a)–(d), it can be observed that all ball-milled alloys show faster hydrogen absorption kinetic performance at different temperature conditions when the ball milling time is less than 60 min, which indicates that the dependence of the ball-milled alloys on temperature is very low, and the hydrogen absorption capacity of all ball milled alloys reaches more than 85 % of the maximum hydrogen absorption capacity at about 2 min even at room temperature. When the ball milling time exceeds 60 min, the hydrogen absorption rate of the ball-milled alloys experiences a slight decrease. However, the hydrogen absorption capacity diminishes more significantly. This reduction in both the hydrogen absorption rate and capacity is attributed to amorphisation and agglomeration. Therefore, It is considered that all ball milled alloys have fast hydrogen absorption rates and excellent kinetic properties, then the magnitude of hydrogen absorption capacity is an important parameter to determine the good or bad hydrogen absorption performance of ball-milled alloy. As seen in Fig. 10 (f), with the increase of ball milling time from 5 min to 180 min, the hydrogen absorption capacity of all ball milled alloys at 2000 s increases and then decreases under different temperature conditions, and the maximum hydrogen absorption capacity of the alloy with 15 min ball milling time and at 2000 s under the temperature conditions of 30 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C, and 90 °C are 1.715 wt%, 1.705 wt%, 1.713 wt%, and 1.684 wt%, respectively. From Fig. 10 (f), it is found that the hydrogen absorption rate of the alloy shows an increasing and then decreasing trend with the increase of ball milling time, and alloys ball milled for 15 min show the best hydrogen absorption kinetics. Ball milling leads to the reduction of alloy grain size, the gradual formation of nanocrystalline and microcrystalline, and the increase in the specific surface area of the grain boundaries of ball-milled alloys which offers additional sites for the formation of hydrides in TiFe alloys, which is the reason for the increase of hydrogen absorption rate and hydrogen absorption capacity of ball-milled alloys. However, as the ball milling time increases, more agglomerates are formed in the alloy, reducing the number of crystal boundaries in the alloy. When the proportion of agglomerates in the ball-milled alloy exceeds a certain amount, hydrogen absorption in the alloy becomes more difficult, which eventually results in a decrease in the rate and capacity of hydrogen absorption and reduces the hydrogen absorption kinetic performance of the ball-milled alloy. It is well known that the higher the hydrogen absorption capacity of the alloy, the higher the hydrogen desorption capacity. However, as soon as the hydrogen in the collection bottle is released in the practical application, the pressure of hydrogen in the collection bottle diminishes and the remaining hydrogen in the alloy is released quickly, which ensures a stable hydrogen airflow in the practical production application. According to Yuan et al. [22], the reduction of alloy particle size and the increase of nanocrystals due to ball milling were considered as the main reasons for the decrease in the absolute values of the enthalpy change of hydrogen absorption and desorption of the alloy. The reduction in alloy particle size and grain refinement results in an increase in the number of alloy grain boundaries and surfaces, which provides more nucleation sites for TiFe/ZrMn2 and reduces the desorption energy of the alloy, which in turn reduces the stability of the hydrides. The agglomeration of the alloy produced by excessive ball milling time leads to an increase in the absolute value of the enthalpy change, which makes the release of hydrogen from the hydrides of the ball milled alloy difficult.

TiFe-based alloys are conducive to large-scale promotion and application due to their excellent hydrogen storage characteristics and low price, compared to LaNi5 alloy [[46], [47], [48]], it has higher hydrogen absorption capacity and kinetic properties. Their main application scenarios primarily include two aspects: on the one hand, TiFe-based alloys can be initially applied to large-scale stationary hydrogen storage containers and hydrogen storage experimental benches, among others. It is widely recognized that renewable energy sources, such as wind, solar, and tidal energy, are inherently unstable and intermittent. To ensure a continuous power supply, it is essential to install energy storage devices and suitable hydrogen storage alloys that can serve as intermediate conversion mediums within these energy storage systems. The application of TiFe-based alloys for solar and wind energy was proposed two decades ago, and the energy generated from these sources can be used for water electrolysis to produce hydrogen, which can then be stored in TiFe-based alloys. On the other hand, TiFe-based alloys are applicable for use in vehicles, including electric cars, hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs), hydrogen-fueled buses, hydrogen fuel cell cars, transport vehicles, counterweights, and so on. In 2015, the sales volume of the Toyota Prius hybrid car exceeded eight million units, and hydrogen fuel cell cars can continuously drive 500 km in just 3 min when fueled with hydrogen. It is evident that TiFe-based hydrogen storage materials exhibit significant application value and potential in both stationary and mobile hydrogen storage fields.

Conclusions

(1)The phase composition of the as-milled Ti1.08Zr0.1Y0.02Fe0.6Ni0.3Mn0.2 + 2 wt% Ni alloys consists of the TiFe main phase, a minor amount of ZrMn2 phase segregated on the TiFe phase, and the Ni phase.(2)Alloy ball-milled for 15 min exhibited excellent activation performance, with an incubation period of 12 s at 150 °C under 3 MPa of hydrogen pressure. The application of reduction in particle size and crystal grain refinement of the ball-milled alloy are the primary reasons for the improved activation performance(3)The hydrogen absorption and desorption capacities of the alloys subjected to ball milling for 15 min were 1.72 wt% and 0.795 wt% at 90 °C and 33 min, respectively, which exhibits the most beneficial kinetic properties.(4)The absolute values of enthalpy changes during the process of hydrogen absorption and desorption initially decrease and then increase significantly with the extension of ball milling time. For the alloy ball-milled for 15 min, the absolute values of the enthalpy change for hydrogen absorption and desorption were the lowest, at 19.1 kJ/mol and 22.3 kJ/mol, respectively.
