Prevalence of potentially inappropriate medications in people living with HIV: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mustapha Muhammed Abubakar, Abdulmuminu Isah, Ruth Jeminiwa, Ibraheem Abioye, Ismaeel Yunusa, Mohammed Abba-Aji, Osagie Kenneth Cole, Jamila Sani, Mukhtar Ijaiya, Chukwuemeka Nwachuya, Yusuf Ibrahim, Mustapha Usman, Duke Daniels, Oluwaseun Samuel Ilugbuhi, Blessing Onyinye Ukoha-Kalu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPotentially inappropriate medications (PIM) are associated with adverse health outcomes. The use of PIM among persons living with HIV (PLHIV) could be more problematic due to peculiarities such as antiretroviral toxicity and drug-disease interactions. However, PIM among PLHIV continues to receive little attention. Therefore, we aimed to systematically review and estimate the prevalence of PIM among PLHIV.MethodsWe searched the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases to identify relevant studies from 1 January 2014 to 30 June 2024. Studies included were peer-reviewed articles that determined PIM prevalence using validated explicit or implicit tools and provided statistical information on the measured outcomes. Systematic, narrative, and literature reviews as well as case studies, commentaries, and editorials were excluded. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Prevalence Critical Appraisal Tool. Subgroup analysis was performed for the different geographic regions, PIM criteria, age, study period, and median numbers of medicines. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Sensitivity analyses, publication bias, and cumulative meta-analysis were conducted.ResultsA total of 13 articles, including 3200 PLHIV, were included for the systematic review, while meta-analysis was conducted using 8 studies comprising 2546 PLHIV. Most of the studies (46%) employed a cross-sectional study design. 11 out of the 13 studies were conducted in Europe. Most studies reported benzodiazepines as the most common PIM among PLHIV, while hypertension and dyslipidaemia were identified as the most frequent comorbidities. Overall, the pooled prevalence of PIM was 44.8% (95% CI, 32%-58%) using a random-effect model. PIM prevalence among PLHIV was higher among those exposed to polypharmacy (59.3%; CI: 48.9% – 68.9%), those aged > 70 years (44.6%; CI: 24.1–67.1), and post-COVID (48.9%; CI: 39%-59%).ConclusionThis study suggests that PIM exposure among PLHIV is a budding issue. Findings from this review advocate for mitigating measures against PIM use. Also, this review contributes data on the use of PIM across different cohorts, creating awareness for policymakers and health managers.Review registrationPROSPERO – CRD42024529411.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11996-y.

Introduction

Increased access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved life expectancy for persons living with HIV (PLHIV) [1]. There is evidence to show that PLHIV on long-term ART have nearly similar life expectancy to that of the general populace [2]. The Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS notes that 24% of PLHIV were above 50 years old as of 2022 [3]. With about 29 million out of an estimated 37 million persons on ART, the median age of PLHIV is expected to increase steadily over the years. Currently, there are predictions that the median age of PLHIV could increase from 43 years to >56 years in 2030, with the proportion of PLHIV who are >50 years increasing to 73% [4].

Generally, with longevity, there is an associated risk of comorbidity [5]. Similarly, as HIV patients age, they would contend with comorbidities associated with longevity [6–8]. Some treatment guidelines support the use of multiple medications to manage these comorbidities. Hence, polypharmacy, which is the concurrent use of 5 or more medicines, has been identified as a challenge among HIV patients, more importantly, in the aged category [9, 10]. A systematic review of 197 relevant studies, including >55,000 PLHIV, estimated the pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among PLHIV to be 33% [11]. Available literature has shown the association between polypharmacy and certain adverse outcomes, such as falls, comorbidities, hospitalizations, and mortality [12–14]. Nevertheless, some arguments focusing on the numerical threshold of medicines may not translate to rational use, as there are cases where multimorbidity could require multiple medications, hence the term, ‘appropriate polypharmacy’ [15]. While there is a high likelihood of drug-drug interactions when more medicines are prescribed, clinicians and researchers are urged to consider the appropriateness of medicines prescribed to assess if the benefits outweigh the potential risks [15, 16].

Potentially Inappropriate Medications (PIM) are generally referred to as medicines or interventions with a higher potential risk compared to their potential benefit [17]. Among the elderly, PIM poses greater risks of falls, gastrointestinal bleeding, and cardiovascular problems due to peculiar physiological changes associated with aging [18]. The use of PIM in aging PLHIV could be more problematic due to a high chance of adverse drug reactions resulting from ART toxicity and drug-disease interactions. Some of these interactions emanate from concomitant use of ARTs and other co-medications as a result of other comorbidities. For instance, drug interactions between ARTs and other drug classes, such as antihypertensives and corticosteroids, have been documented [19–21]. Incidents of PIM encounters can be reduced through implicit means, which involve a holistic assessment of the prescription to ascertain whether it corresponds to a patient’s need. In addition, PIM can be assessed explicitly by using standardised lists of medications that may be inappropriate for elderly patients. Examples of commonly used explicit tools are Beers’ Criteria and the Screening Tool of Older Persons’ Prescription (STOPP)/Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment (START) criteria. A recent scoping review reported that for studies that employed Beers’ List and the STOPP/START criteria, there was an observed overprescription of PIM among aged PLHIV, with benzodiazepines being the most prevalent [22]. Hence, the authors argued that PIM among aged PLHIV is frequent and an issue.

The scoping review by Velez-Diaz-Pallares et al. comprehensively mapped the studies conducted on the use of PIMs among PLHIV and identified directions for future research [22]. However, scoping reviews are not designed to provide quantitative estimates, which are critical to present the magnitude of a problem. Consequently, a meta-analysis will provide a pooled prevalence estimate for PIM use among PLHIV, further spotlighting the issue for clinicians and policymakers. Further, a meta-analysis of studies on PIMs among PLHIV would ensure the robustness of available evidence, given the variability in PIM criteria lists, study quality, study settings, and periods. This work is therefore positioned to provide the first quantitative synthesis in this area. This will complement the scoping review by offering more actionable recommendations for practice, policy, and further research.

Methods

Search strategy

The Search was conducted on PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to cover the period of 1 January 2014 to 30 June 2024. The following main keywords were used: potentially inappropriate medications and human immunodeficiency virus. BUK, MMA, and OKC independently developed and implemented all search strategies. We searched for peer-reviewed, published systematic reviews for potentially inappropriate medications and human immunodeficiency virus, and operationalized the keywords [11, 23]. A sample of the complete search strategy is shown in Appendix 1. Two researchers, MMA and OKC, screened the articles on Microsoft Excel by removing all duplicates and reading the papers’ titles and abstracts. Where articles met the inclusion criteria, both researchers read the full text to determine whether to finally include it. Any disagreements between the two researchers were resolved through discussion. In instances where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer, IY adjudicated after independently assessing the article. Cohen’s kappa statistic was used to evaluate inter-rater reliability. The study protocol of this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024529411) on 23 June 2024.

Eligibility criteria

This review included studies that were (1) peer-reviewed articles, (2) articles that reported on point prevalence, which is the number of HIV patients who received PIM divided by the total number of HIV patients during the study period, (3) Studies that determined PIM based on either explicit or implicit criteria or both, and (4) articles that provided statistical information on the measured outcomes. Also, we included studies conducted in the last 10 years, specifically from 2014 to 2024. Our decision to limit this work to only studies published within this period was to capture current prescribing issues, considering that the major revision of the Beers’ Criteria and the STOPP/START List were in 2012 and 2015/2023, respectively, both within the 10 years. Conversely, we excluded studies that did not report on the prevalence of PIM among PLHIV and studies that only reported on PIM prevalence for prescriptions and not patients. In addition, reviews such as systematic, narrative, and literature reviews were excluded. Further, case studies, preprint articles, commentaries, editorials, and correspondences were excluded. The study employed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) checklist (See Appendix 2).

Data extraction and quality assessment

The following data were extracted from each of the included studies: names of authors, publication year, continent, country, location, study design, sample period, age description of participants, gender description of participants, mean number of medicines, PIM criteria used, study population, number of patients using PIM, most common PIM, and prevalence of PIM. Quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Prevalence Critical Appraisal. The JBI provides authors the option to answer ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’, or ‘Not Applicable’, in assessing the quality of a study. Where a study had 3 ‘No’ or ‘unclear’, three reviewers (BUK, MMA, and YI) adjudged it as high risk, consistent with similar systematic reviews [24, 25]. Any uncertainty was resolved by contacting a third reviewer, AI.

Data synthesis and analysis

The outcome was PIM prevalence. PIM prevalence was defined as the proportion of persons with one or more PIMs. Some studies used more than one tool (PIM Criteria) to estimate the outcome, leading to dissimilarity in measurement tools across studies. Past studies have highlighted the challenges of pooling prevalence in a meta-analysis from studies reporting multiple PIM prevalence rates per study because of dissimilarity in the measurement tools [25, 26]. Therefore, in our pooled prevalence analysis, we included only studies that used a single tool to assess the prevalence of PIM. We also included studies that used multiple tools to assess PIM prevalence, provided the values obtained from these tools are similar. Also, studies that reported outcomes using explicitly validated tools were included in the meta-analysis [27]. We conducted a meta-analysis to present estimates of pooled PIM prevalence among PLHIV. To ensure the robustness of the computed pooled PIM prevalence among PLHIV, we conducted sensitivity analyses using both fixed-effects and random-effects models. The fixed-effects model assumes a common true effect across studies, while the random-effects model accounts for between-study heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis was performed for the different geographic regions, PIM criteria, age, study period, and median numbers of medicines. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding one study at a time and recalculating the prevalence to identify studies with outlying prevalence. The publication bias test was conducted by visualising the funnel plot and conducting the Egger’s regression test. All analysis was conducted on the statistical environment RStudio, version 4.2.1.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1229 studies were identified, including 771 studies from PubMed, 457 studies from Scopus, and a study from Web of Science. Following the application of our eligibility criteria, 768 studies were excluded. Thirteen studies with 3200 PLHIV were included for analysis [28–39]. Inter-rater agreement was substantial (k = 0.78). Figure 1 shows the study selection process.

Study characteristics

Out of the 13 included studies, 46% employed a cross-sectional study design [31, 33–36, 38], while 23% were observational [30, 32, 39]. Cohort studies made up 15% of the included studies [21, 42]. The included studies in this review utilised diverse data sources, with 31% of the papers using data from medical charts [28, 30, 32, 38]. Studies that used data from electronic medical records were 23% of the included studies [31, 36, 37] while 15% interviewed the study population [29, 34]. A combination of different data sources was observed in 15% of the studies [33, 39]. Per location, 85% of the studies were conducted in Europe [28, 30–36, 38, 39] while 15% were conducted in North America [29, 37].

The study population ranged from 19 to 1292 [35, 36] while the median age of the population was between 58 to 78 [37, 42]. Most participants in all the studies were males. The sampling period of the study population ranged from 2008–2021, with the earliest sampling period spanning from 2008–2011 [29] and the most recent between September – December 2021 [31]. Additionally, 4 studies were published between 2014 and 2019 [21–29, 41] while the remaining 9 articles were published after 2020. In estimating the outcome of interest, the studies used different PIM Criteria tools. 4 studies used only the Beers’ Criteria [21, 29, 35, 41] while 3 studies used the STOPP/START criteria [32, 33, 39]. Hence, 7 studies used a single explicit validated tool to measure the outcome of interest. 5 studies employed 2 or more PIM Criteria while [36] combined the STOPP and Beers’ Criteria (STOPP-Beers’) in measuring PIM prevalence. Risk-of-bias assessment

Only one study was categorised as high risk, as there was no clear information on the recruitment of the sample population, as well as the sample size calculation [30]. In contradiction to the JBI Criteria, the numerators for prevalence calculation were not provided in the assessments conducted for each of the PIM Criteria List. The sample frame of all the studies was appropriate as the target population in the included papers was PLHIV.

Eight studies appropriately sampled the study population, as either random sampling was performed or data on the target population were reported [28, 31, 32, 35–39]. All the studies extensively described the study population. Twelve studies used validated explicit tools to estimate the study outcome, while Macías et al. [36] combined the STOPP and Beers’ criteria in measuring PIM prevalence.

Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy was defined differently across all 13 studies. Nine studies defined polypharmacy as the use of five or more medications [29, 36, 39]. In defining polypharmacy among PLHIV [34], excluded ARVs from the numeric benchmark of five or more medications. Three studies defined polypharmacy as the use of six or more medications [30, 32, 33], while one study did not quantify the use of medicines [37]. The prevalence of polypharmacy among PLHIV ranged from 15.8% to 96% [21, 29]. In the study by Courlet et al., where the prevalence of polypharmacy was reported as 15.8%, participants were categorized as < 65 and >65 years. In the < 65 population, the prevalence was 12.1% and 42% in the >65 cohort [21].

Additionally, five studies provided definitions for hyperpolypharmacy among PLHIV [30–33, 39]. Hyper polypharmacy was referred to as the use of 11 or more medications [33], ] while it was referred to as the use of 10 or medications elsewhere [31]. The prevalence ranged from 10.9 to 30% among PLHIV [31, 39].

Undetected viral load among PLHIV

Twelve studies reported undetected viral load among the study population. Viral load was undetected in 6.9% [32] to 100% of PLHIV [30, 34]. In nine studies, >90% of the patients had undetected viral load [ [21, 30, 39, 42]. It is pertinent to state that the definition of undetected viral load was not consistent, as studies used different benchmarks ranging from < 20 copies/ml to < 50 copies/ml of HIV RNA.

Frequent comorbidities among PLHIV

Eleven studies reported the most frequent comorbidities among PLHIV. Frequently reported comorbidities were dyslipidaemia and hypertension, with their prevalence among PLHIV ranging from 59.1% to 70% [31, 39] and 56% – 61% [37, 42] respectively. One study reported hyperlipidaemia as the most frequent comorbidity, with a prevalence of 61% among the study population [29]. Some studies used broad terms such as cardiovascular diseases [30, 32, 33, 35]and lipid abnormalities [34] to report the most frequent comorbidities among PLHIV.

Most common PIM

Across all the studies and the explicit criteria employed to assess PIM, benzodiazepine was reported 12 out of 19 times as the most common PIM. Other common PIMs reported were opiates and testosterone. Some other studies reported on the drug class and not the medication. Two studies reported sedative-hypnotics as the most common PIM [30, 38]. Results from the individual studies are shown in Table 3.

PIM prevalence

Prevalence was reported 19 times in the 13 studies. Estimates ranged from one to three per study, depending on the number of explicit tools used. Of the explicit tools used, the Beers’ and STOPP/START Criteria were employed four and three times, respectively. The prevalence of PIM in this systematic review ranged from 16% − 71.4% [34, 41]. A meta-analysis to estimate the pooled prevalence of PIM was conducted. First, using our stated criteria, the analysis included the 7 studies that used a single tool to assess PIM prevalence. Second, despite the use of two tools – STOPP/START and Beers’ – the outcomes in the Livio et al. study [24] were same and hence the study was included. Conversely, the Macias et al. study [21] used a combination of tools which has not been validated by any available literature [28, 36]. Thus, only 8 studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising 2546 HIV patients [28, 29, 32, 33, 35, 38, 39].

The meta-analysis with a random-effects model gave a pooled PIM prevalence of 44.8% (95% CI, 32–58%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 96.7%, p < 0.001). Figure 3 is the forest plot showing the pooled PIM prevalence among PLHIV. Under a fixed-effects model, the pooled PIM prevalence was lower at 33.2% (95% CI: 31.4–35.1%). Noting the high heterogeneity observed (I² >90%), we consider the random-effects estimate more appropriate, though we reported results from both models. In the sensitivity analysis, using the leave-one-out method, the pooled PIM prevalence ranged from 41.2% to 49.8%. The pooled PIM prevalence was not significantly altered before or after the omission of each study, suggesting the stability of the results. Estimates obtained from the leave-one-out analysis are shown in Appendix 3.

The present review analysed the cumulative effect of PIM prevalence from the year 2011 (52%) to the year 2022 (45%). The cumulative meta-analysis was conducted by ranking the sample size. PIM prevalence became stable with the accumulation of data. When ranked by the sample size, PIM prevalence progressively decreased, peaked at Study 6 [42] and achieved stability again at 46% (95 CI: 31% − 62%). Appendix 6 is the forest plot of the cumulative meta-analysis.

Publication and small-study effect bias

Publication bias was assessed for this review, including only studies captured for the meta-analysis and visually inspected using the funnel plot (See Appendix 4). We observed there was no marked funnel plot symmetry, suggesting no evidence of publication bias. Further, Egger’s regression test gave an intercept of 0.52 (95% CI: 0.27–0.76; z = 1.94, p = 0.053), indicating that smaller studies tended to report higher PIM prevalence estimates (Appendix 5). However, as this result was not statistically significant, the evidence for small-study effects remains inconclusive. Figure 3 is the forest plot showing the pooled PIM prevalence among PLHIV.

Sub-group analysis

The subgroup analysis of the prevalence of PIM use by age category, study population, sampling period, different geographic regions, PIM criteria, mean number of medications, and percentage of male population was conducted. Based on the mean number of medications, the pooled PIM prevalence was 59.3% among PLHIV who took five or more concomitant medications. Conversely, the estimated pooled prevalence of PIM was 48.9% among the same population that took less than five medications. In the subgroup analysis, the sampling period was categorised into pre-COVID and post-COVID periods, taking into cognizance when the WHO pronounced the Coronavirus outbreak a pandemic. Pooled PIM prevalence pre-COVID was 43.6% while the estimate post-COVID was 48.9%. Per age category, pooled PIM prevalence was 44.6% in PLHIV aged 70 and above. Among PLHIV aged below 70, pooled PIM prevalence was 44.5%. A summary of the subgroup analysis is shown in Table 4. Also provided is a supplementary table summarising all individual study prevalence values alongside the tool used and sample sizes (See Appendix 7). The subgroup analysis based on age category, study population, sampling period and male population found no significant heterogeneity between groups in PIM use.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that has provided a pooled prevalence for PIM among PLHIV. The other existing review, which attempted to describe the use of PIM among this cohort, is a scoping review [22]. While the work by Velez-Diaz-Pallares et al. [22] mapped the literature on the use of PIMs among PLHIV, as with scoping reviews, there was the limitation of providing quantitative estimates for the study outcome. This present review analysed data from 13 studies that evaluated PIM prevalence among 3200 PLHIV. Publication years ranged from 2014 to 2024, providing an informed estimate of PIM prevalence among PLHIV. Our meta-analysis comprised 2546 PLHIV and 8 studies after the application of our eligibility criteria. Both random- and fixed-effect models were conducted to ensure transparency and assess the robustness of our results. The fixed-effects model yielded a PIM prevalence of 33.2% while the random-effects model gave a prevalence of 45%. The random-effects model was prioritized in the reporting of our findings, as it takes into account between-study variability. The meta-analysis showed high heterogeneity (I² >90), which was expected with the diversity in study designs, PIM assessment tools, and variance in sample sizes. While acknowledging that caution be taken in the interpretation of the reported pooled prevalence, we aimed to provide a single estimate so as to contextualise the burden of PIMs globally.

The random-effects estimate was higher compared to the PIM prevalence in other reviews focused on other settings. PIM prevalence was reported to be 36.7% in outpatient departments, 38% in middle-aged adults, 34.6% among elderly patients, and 33.3% in primary care settings [23–45]. However, the pooled prevalence in this study was slightly lower than the pooled prevalence of PIM in inpatient settings, where estimates provided were 46% and 56% depending on the explicit tool used [46].

This review showed that PIM prevalence was high among PLHIV. PIM use has been associated with falls, functional decline, and hospitalizations [47–49]. Further, associations between PIM and mortality have been extensively reported. A recent cohort study that followed middle-aged patients for five years showed that increasing PIM use was associated with mortality risk in a dose-response manner [50]. Similarly, a meta-analysis study provided evidence of associations between PIM use and mortality risk among older patients [51]. Even where PIM was not generally reported to be associated with mortality, certain antipsychotics classified as PIMs showed a positive relationship with mortality [47]. Studies with strong epidemiological study designs have strengthened the evidence that polypharmacy is a facilitator of PIM use [52, 53]. While our study observed that the definition of polypharmacy according to the literature varies, one study concluded that harms associated with polypharmacy begin to increase with the use of three or more medications in both PLHIV and the general population [54]. Though we reported significant heterogeneity between groups, our subgroup analysis suggests that PIM prevalence was higher among PLHIVs who were exposed to five or more medicines compared to patients on lower than five medicines. To reduce the prevalence and adverse outcomes of PIM, tackling polypharmacy, which is estimated to be around 33% among PLHIV, would be essential [11].

The systematic review showed that the most frequent comorbidities reported among PLHIV were dyslipidaemia and hypertension. This finding is consistent with studies that have assessed for comorbidities among PLHIV [8, 55]. While dyslipidaemia and hypertension are frequently reported among older patients, the proportion seems to be higher among PLHIV [7, 56]. A plausible explanation for this finding could be that exposure to certain ARVs, such as protease inhibitors (PI) and the nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors, Zidovudine and Stavudine, has been reported to lead to dyslipidaemia and hypertension. Hence, there are suggestions that HIV management protocols should incorporate specific guidelines for comorbid HIV patients [57]. For instance, ezetimibe and darunavir have shown better comparative efficacy in reducing low-density lipoprotein levels and cardiovascular events, respectively. Our study calls for management guidelines to take into cognizance drug safety issues and multimorbidity in patient management on an individual basis.

Our systematic review also reported benzodiazepine as the most common PIM among PLHIV. This finding adds to the growing literature that has shown benzodiazepines to be the most used PIM, especially among elderly patients [23, 25, 44, 46]. Generally, high rates of benzodiazepine use and misuse have been observed among adults, with retail pharmacies and family relatives among the major sources of acquisition [58]. Benzodiazepine is classified as PIM by major explicit criteria lists because of its associations with falls and hospitalizations, as well as its heightened sensitivity among geriatric patients [27, 28]. Also, long-term exposure to benzodiazepines is associated with HIV-related neurocognitive impairment [59]. Noteworthy is that withdrawal after long-term exposure does not necessarily translate to a better quality of life; thus, caution and comprehensive patient evaluation are essential before initiating patients on benzodiazepines. The major reason often cited for the high prevalence of benzodiazepine use among the geriatric population is insomnia [23]. Therefore, providing a better alternative for insomnia management could limit or reduce incidences and the prevalence of benzodiazepine use among the elderly and PLHIV. There are existing recommendations that the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and cognitive behavioural therapy would help to better manage insomnia [58].

The subgroup analysis by period showed differences in PIM prevalence before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings showed that high PIM prevalence was associated with the post-COVID period. The pandemic has been noted to have impacted drug utilization patterns [60–62]. Specifically, the outbreak was found to be associated with increased use of beta-blocking agents and antihypertensives [63]. During the six-month acute phase of COVID-19, increased administration of cardiovascular and psychotropic medications was reported among >146,000 individuals, with pandemic-associated fear and anxiety noted as possible facilitators [64]. Also, recent studies have associated the COVID pandemic with increased use of prescription medicines as a result of self-medication. While the global prevalence of self-medication had been earlier estimated to be 48%, its prevalence during the pandemic was highlighted to be even higher, ranging between 7 and 88% [65, 66]. Thus, further studies could explore the association between PIM use and the pandemic.

In this study, we could not compare PIM use geographically, as there was only one published study from North America that met the inclusion criteria for our meta-analysis. Additionally, we observed that there was a paucity of studies conducted in Africa and Asia that explored PIM prevalence among PLHIV. Conversely, several studies have established the high prevalence of PIM in both continents [60, 67–72]. Further, with both continents accounting for 82% of the global HIV burden, there are indications that PIM use among PLHIV could be an issue in both continents. Hence, this review lays a background for studies to be conducted in Africa and Asia to assess the burden of PIM among PLHIV.

Our systematic review and meta-analysis gave attention to the robustness of the methods employed, considering the limitations of previous meta-analyses of proportions focused on PIMs. Our methodological approach was to limit outcome reporting bias. First, we did not restrict our study by language, and in our review, one study was published in Spanish [32]. Second, we noted the argument of [25] that meta-analysis of proportions in PIM studies is methodologically challenging because of the heterogeneity in outcome measurements, and that future reviews should include studies that employed validated explicit tools. For this reason, in their study, the authors conducted a systematic review without meta-analysis. Similarly [46], suggested that combining more than one estimate from a single study for a particular outcome would contribute to the risk of bias. Hence, in this present review, we used only studies that have utilised validated explicit methods using the most recent review of the literature. Also, our review included only studies that used only one explicit tool to measure outcomes. Third, we observed that some studies included low- and high-quality studies for their review, leading to a low certainty of evidence. Thus, in this review, only studies assessed to have met the JBI checklist were included in the meta-analysis to improve the validity of our results. Lastly, our study adhered to the methods stated in the registered protocol of this review, thereby limiting any researcher bias.

Implications of findings on policy: evidence from the literature

This systematic review and meta-analysis showed a high burden of PIMs among PLHIV, which was higher when compared to other cohorts and settings. A review of the literature indicates that existing interventions, such as medication reviews, educational strategies, clinical decision support systems, and pharmaceutical interventions are widely implemented to reduce the burden of PIMs [73]. These interventions have been generally proven to be effective [74]. Nevertheless, studies with strong study designs have shown that the most successful interventions to reduce PIMs are medication review, with this strategy reportedly effective in 75% of the settings it was implemented [75]. Medication review is generally described as the ‘systematic assessment of the patient’s pharmacotherapeutic needs and prescriptions’, followed by recommendations for medicines’ optimization [73]. Medication reviews are not only effective in the reduction of PIMs and facilitation of medication appropriateness but have also shown a positive effect in the reduction of all-cause mortality compared to educational interventions [76]. Similarly, recent evidence suggests that when pharmacists are active in medication review interventions, there is appropriate or rational use of medicines [77]. Pharmacists’ activities documented to have contributed to PIM reduction outcomes are the identification of PIMs and the recommendation of alternatives. Further, there are also suggestions that PIM interventions are more effective when medication reviews and educational interventions are combined [74, 75]. Notably, where PIM interventions are less effective, identified barriers are usually low uptake of recommendations by physicians as well as short follow-up periods [74]. Thus, this paper recommends that policymakers and governments inculcate multifaceted PIM interventions into HIV programs. PIM intervention teams should be multidisciplinary, with pharmacists embedded in their activities. To tackle the issue of poor adherence to recommendations, HIV programs should include the number of PIM interventions resolved as part of the content of routine facility reports, just as done for drug switching incidences, pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis, and pharmacovigilance activities.

Implications of findings on practice

The implication of PIMs in clinical practice is the heightened probability of encountering the risk of drug-drug interactions among PLHIV. Most of these interactions or potential interactions are pharmacokinetic in nature, with interactions happening mostly during absorption and metabolism. While certain ARTs rely on the p-glycoprotein enzyme for absorption in the gut, metabolism by CYP3A enzymes is critical for the pharmacological actions of ARTs [78, 79]. Thus, concomitant use of ARTs and certain drugs with opposing actions on the same enzymatic pathway would lead to the inhibition and inducement of these enzymes. Such reactions, synergistic and antagonistic, have clinical implications.

In our study, we reported that the most frequent comorbidities among PLHIV were cardiovascular diseases, which aligns with findings in the literature [22]. Amlodipine, a calcium channel blocker, is metabolized by CYP3A enzyme, hence the inhibition or inducement of this pathway could affect drug metabolism [21]. These enzymes are either induced by PIs such as efavirenz and nevirapine or inhibited by boosters such as ritonavir and lopinavir [21]. The effect of PIs and boosters on these enzymes could lead to an increase in concentration; hence, dose adjustments and monitoring are required for PLHIV on amlodipine. Similarly, the inhibition of p-glycoprotein by ritonavir can increase digoxin concentrations to toxic levels [80]. As such, digoxin is potentially inappropriate for PLHIV on ART-booster combinations. Further, there is the expectation that PI-influenced enzyme inhibition could increase the levels of beta-blockers, and for this, there are corresponding suggestions for the use of beta-blockers not metabolized by CYP enzymes for PLHIV. Hence, for PLHIV who require exposure to beta-blockers, atenolol and labetolol will be more appropriate.

Clinically significant interactions have also been noted between ARTs and corticosteroids. For instance, ritonavir-mediated inhibition of the CYP3A4 enzyme has been linked to the increased concentrations of corticosteroids such as fluticasone and budesonide. Such interactions have the potential to lead to complications such as adrenal suppression and Cushing’s syndrome [19, 20, 81]. An alternative for PLHIV would be beclamethasone as it is not metabolized by the CYP3A4 enzyme [82]. Across the literature and in this study, the use of benzodiazepines among PLHIV has been widely reported. Ritonavir and cobicistat, through the inhibition of CYP-450, can increase the concentration of clonazepam and diazepam [73]. Increased concentrations of these benzodiazepines have been linked to extreme confusion and sedation, with the potential to lead to falls. Nevertheless, there are suggestions that benzodiazepines such as lorazepam and oxazepam, both not metabolized by the CYP-450 will be better alternatives [73]. Further, patients on benzodiazepines will benefit from longer follow-ups and increased outpatient visits [83].

Considering the number of potential interactions that could be encountered due to other co-medications, this paper recommends that the use of interaction checkers be integrated in the care of PLHIV at health facilities. Available open-resource interaction checkers are the Liverpool HIV Drug Interaction Checker and the MedScape Drug Interaction Checker. Also, the use of existing PIM criteria list should be encouraged to prevent the prescribing of medicines that could be potentially inappropriate, except extensive risk-benefit assessment has been done.

Implications of review on future research

In conducting this work, we observed a few areas with implications for future research. First, future drug utilization studies could assess frequently used co-medications among aged PLHIV. Such a study would provide background to quantify the type of risks PLHIV could already be exposed to. Secondly, we observed that most of the work on PIMs among PLHIV is conducted in the global North, specifically in Europe and North America. With the high burden of HIV in Africa and Asia, there is a need for PIM prevalence studies to be conducted in these regions. This would help to assess the extent of interventions needed in these locations. Hence, findings from this review should be considered as an early warning by regions underrepresented in this work. Further, there is a need for the development and validation of a PIM criteria list specific to countries in the African and Asian regions. These would help to guide clinicians on the safe use of medicines among PLHIV. It would also present as an additional tool to complement existing interventions on the rational use of medicines. Additionally, it would be valuable for further studies to comprehensively map the literature to assess the type of benzodiazepines commonly used among PLHIV. This would help inform policy recommendations on the management of insomnia among PLHIV.

Limitations

We identified some limitations in our study. First, the decision to exclude studies that used multiple explicit criteria to measure outcomes has the potential to bias the outcome of this review. However, combining estimates from the same study would have introduced the same risk as observed by earlier studies. Nevertheless, estimates from the sensitivity analysis conducted after the omission of each study were similar to the pooled PIM prevalence. Second, the literature search was conducted using three databases, and it is possible to have missed out on some studies. Future systematic reviews and meta-analyses could explore this limitation by including other databases to limit publication bias. Third, this systematic review did not include grey literature to ensure the reproducibility of this work and the validity of our findings. However, the findings from our publication bias assessment indicate that there is no evidence of publication bias. Lastly, differences in population characteristics, healthcare settings, and methodological quality could limit the comparability of findings and require cautious interpretation of pooled estimates. Future research should aim for standardised intervention protocols and consistent outcome reporting to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

Exposure to PIMs is linked to serious adverse events. PIM use could be even more problematic for PLHIV, considering the comorbidities associated with these patients coupled with a growing median age. This review aimed to estimate the prevalence of PIM among PLHIV. Our study found that PIM prevalence was 45% among PLHIV, suggesting that the use of certain inappropriate medications could already be a prevalent public health issue globally. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, noting the heterogeneity of the studies and the paucity of such research in Africa and Asia. Nevertheless, the findings of this review bring to attention the issue of PIM among PLHIV, providing a quantitative estimate of this burden, and thereby creating awareness for policymakers and health managers. Our review also contributed to the discourse on frequent comorbidities and commonly used PIMs among PLHIV, underscoring the need for management guidelines to take into consideration the comorbid conditions of HIV patients. This study also aligns with a key aim of the Global AID Strategy 2021–2026, which is “strengthening links to other integrated services such as communicable and non-communicable diseases”. Consequently, our findings indicated PIMs and comorbidities that clinicians and policymakers should consider in the management and administration of PLHIV. To reduce the burden of PIM, this paper calls for the integration of multifaceted PIM interventions in HIV management programs. We also propose that policymakers should consider the introduction of routine use of interaction checkers in these programs as an additional tool to ensure patient safety.
