Association between acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels and rheumatoid arthritis: A NHANES analysis
Authors: Yan Zhang, Chenyang Lu, Dipeng Li, Chuanzeng Wang, Mao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acrylamide is a widespread environmental and dietary contaminant, and hemoglobin adducts of acrylamide (HbAA) and its metabolite glycidamide (HbGA) serve as objective biomarkers of internal exposure. However, the association between acrylamide exposure and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) has not been well characterized at the population level. We analyzed data from 4 cycles of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2003–2006 and 2013–2016), including 1972 adults aged ≥20 years with complete information on RA status and acrylamide hemoglobin adduct measurements. Multivariable logistic regression models accounting for the complex survey design were used to examine associations between HbAA, HbGA, total adducts (HbAA + HbGA), the metabolic ratio (HbGA/HbAA), and RA prevalence. Restricted cubic spline models were applied to explore potential nonlinear exposure–response relationships. Nonlinear associations were observed between acrylamide-related hemoglobin adducts and the prevalence of RA. HbAA exhibited a J-shaped association, with higher odds observed at intermediate exposure levels. In contrast, HbGA and total adduct concentrations showed increasing associations across higher exposure ranges. The HbGA/HbAA ratio was positively associated with RA prevalence. Restricted cubic spline analyses supported these non-monotonic exposure–response patterns. Subgroup analyses showed variability in point estimates across behavioral and clinical strata, but estimates were often imprecise and should be interpreted as exploratory. In this nationally representative cross-sectional study, HbAA was nonlinearly associated with the prevalence of RA among US adults. These findings support an epidemiological association between internal biomarkers of acrylamide exposure and RA. Prospective studies with repeated exposure assessment and mechanistic measurements are needed to clarify temporal relationships and underlying biological pathways.

1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a common chronic systemic inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by diffuse polyarthritis and pro-inflammatory cytokine infiltration, affecting approximately 0.5% to 1.0% of the global population.[1–3] RA primarily affects the joints, causing pain, swelling, stiffness, and reduced mobility, and may also involve extra-articular organs such as the skin, eyes, lungs, heart, and blood vessels. This systemic inflammation can lead to symptoms like fatigue, fever, and anemia, significantly impairing the patient’s quality of life.[4–7] Although the exact etiology of RA remains incompletely understood, substantial evidence indicates that its onset and progression arise from multifactorial interactions between genetic and environmental factors.[8] Genetic factors are estimated to account for 50% to 60% of the disease risk, while the remaining proportion is attributed to environmental factors.[8,9] The association between environmental pollutants and RA is well established, with smoking recognized as a definitive risk factor. In addition, evidence implicating other pollutants, such as insecticides, pesticides, and traffic-related air pollution, continues to accumulate.[10–14] Moreover, dust exposure has been identified as a potential risk factor for RA.[15] These findings suggest a potential association between RA and environmental chemical contaminants.

Acrylamide (AA), a Class 2A carcinogen prevalent in high-temperature processed foods, cigarette smoke, and certain industrial settings, exhibits neurotoxicity, genotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, and carcinogenicity.[16–18] Following ingestion, AA undergoes partial metabolic activation via cytochrome P450 2E1 to form the more reactive metabolite glycidamide (GA).[19,20] Both AA and GA form stable hemoglobin adducts through covalent binding to the N-terminal valine residue, yielding hemoglobin adducts of acrylamide (HbAA) and hemoglobin adducts of glycidamide (HbGA), respectively.[21] These adducts serve as robust biomarkers of long-term AA exposure and have been extensively utilized as exposure indicators in epidemiological research.[22] Studies have shown that AA exposure and its hemoglobin adduct levels are associated with various health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorder markers, and all-cause mortality. The core pathogenic mechanism involves oxidative stress induction and chronic low-grade inflammation.[23–25] Notably, chronic inflammation is the core pathologic feature of RA, an autoimmune disease that leads to joint destruction. However, no study has directly examined the association between acrylamide exposure assessed via hemoglobin adducts and RA risk. Therefore, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this study aimed to investigate the potential relationship between acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels (HbAA, HbGA, HbAA + HbGA, and HbGA/HbAA) and RA risk in the general population. It is worth noting that the levels of hemoglobin adducts (HbAA and HbGA) not only reflect the degree of acrylamide exposure but may also be influenced by an individual’s clinical status, independent of the exposure amount. For instance, liver or kidney diseases may alter the metabolism and excretion of acrylamide and its metabolites. Anemia, polycythemia, hemolysis or recent blood transfusion can directly affect the total amount of hemoglobin and survival cycle, thereby interfering with the interpretation of adduct concentration. Heavy alcohol consumption may alter the conversion of acrylamide to glycidamide by affecting the activity of the metabolic enzyme cytochrome P450 2E1. In addition, conditions such as diabetes or obesity are often accompanied by changes in metabolic enzyme activity, which may also regulate the formation and accumulation of adducts. To accurately assess the association between acrylamide exposure and RA, this study will identify the above clinical conditions in data analysis and control the influence of these potential confounding factors as much as possible by excluding related cases, adjusting relevant covariates in the regression model, or conducting subgroup analyses, in order to enhance the reliability of the research results.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and data sources

This cross-sectional study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. This population-based, cross-sectional, retrospective study utilized data from the NHANES database, including noninstitutionalized adults aged 20 to 120 years with complete information on arthritis status and HbAA and HbGA. Additional participant data were obtained from NHANES and analyzed retrospectively. NHANES datasets were sourced from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). NHANES was designed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States, employing a complex, multistage sampling design to ensure nationally representative data for the noninstitutionalized population. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) approved the use of these publicly available research data. Participants completed household interviews and comprehensive examinations at mobile screening centers, including physical examinations, specialized measurements, and laboratory tests.

The NCHS Research Ethics Review Board approved the NHANES protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent for both the health screening and laboratorycomponents. All publicly released NHANES data were de-identified, ensuring participant anonymity throughout our analysis. As this study utilized only these anonymized datasets, no additional ethical approval or consent was required. Documentation of the original ethical approval is available on the NHANES website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

2.2. Study population

Data from four 2-year cycles of NHANES (2003–2004, 2005–2006, 2013–2014, and 2015–2016) were extracted for this study. Adults aged 20 to 120 years with complete information on arthritis status, HbAA levels, and their metabolite HbGA were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included a history of any type of arthritis other than RA (e.g., osteoarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, or unspecified arthritis).

2.3. Confirmation of RA

All NHANES participants aged ≥20 years were asked, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have arthritis?” Those who answered “yes” were further asked, “What type of arthritis?” Possible responses included RA, OA, other types of arthritis, unknown types, or refusal to answer. Individuals who reported a physician-diagnosed arthritis but either refused to specify the type or responded “unknown” were classified as missing data and excluded from the analysis. Participants reporting OA or non-RA arthritis were also excluded. As previously validated, self-reported RA diagnosis demonstrates acceptable accuracy in large-scale studies.[26]

2.4. HbAA and HbGA measurements

The levels of HbAA and HbGA are determined using whole blood or red blood cells. Specifically, the reaction products of hemoglobin N-terminal valine are measured to determine the concentrations of HbAA and HbGA: N-(2-carbamoylethyl) valine for HbAA and N-(2-hydroxycarbamoylethyl) valine for HbGA. The method is derived from the modified Edman reaction, which measures the ability of N-alkylated amino acids to form Edman products under neutral or basic conditions.[27] Total hemoglobin (Hb) was measured using the calibrators provided with the manufacturer’s assay kits. The detection thresholds were 2 pmol/g Hb for HbAA and 3 pmol/g Hb for HbGA. Measurements below the limit of detection (LOD: 2 pmol/g Hb for HbAA and 3 pmol/g Hb for HbGA) were treated as missing data. In our study cohort, HbGA levels were below the LOD in 1.8% of samples (approximately n = 35), while no HbAA measurements were below their LOD. Participants with HbGA levels below the LOD were excluded from the analysis. This approach minimizes potential bias introduced by value imputation and is consistent with common practice for handling low-level biomarker data when the proportion of non-detectable values is small. Detailed information on the measurementmethods is available on the NHANES website.

2.5. Demographics and covariates

Trained interviewers collected participants’ demographic data (age, gender, race, marital status, household income-to-poverty ratio, and education level) through standardized questionnaires, including household and sample demographic surveys, using face-to-face Computer-Assisted Personal Interviews (Confirmit Corp.). Data were weighted following NHANES protocols. Body mass index (BMI) was measured by the NHANES examination and was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Body weight was measured using an electronic scale, and standing height was measured using a fixed scale. Smokers were defined as people who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and reported current smoking status as “daily” or “several days.” Alcohol use was defined by responses to survey questions about alcohol use. Drinkers were defined as having at least 12 drinks of any type of alcoholic beverage in any given year. High blood pressure was defined by answering “yes” to the following question: “Have you/have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have high blood pressure, also known as hypertension?” Hypertension was also defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg (average of 4 consecutive measurements). Diabetes was defined as a “yes” answer to the following questions: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or health professional that you have diabetes or sugar diabetes (excluding during pregnancy)?,” “Are you taking insulin?,” or “Are you taking hypoglycemic medication?” (These medications are sometimes called oral hypoglycemic agents or oral antidiabetic drugs.) Diabetes mellitus was also defined as an HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or a fasting blood glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a “yes” answer to the question: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional to take a prescription medication to lower your blood cholesterol?” or a triglyceride level of ≥150 mg/dL.

2.6. Statistical analysis

NHANES uses a sophisticated survey design to ensure national representation. All analyses applied the NHANES-provided sampling weights (WTME-C2YR) and accounted for the complex survey design, including strata and primary sampling unit variables, to obtain nationally representative estimates with appropriate standard errors in accordance with NCHS guidelines. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error, acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as unweighted counts (weighted percentages). Acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels were analyzed after log-transformation. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling was used to examine potential nonlinear associations between RA and acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess relationships between study variables and RA, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the RCS models, the acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels were analyzed on the natural logarithm scale. We prespecified 4 knots (3 degrees of freedom) at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the ln-transformed distribution for each biomarker. In all spline models, the ORs were calculated with the reference point set at the median (50th percentile) of the respective ln-transformed biomarker concentration. Subgroup analyses were prespecified for hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia. This selection was based on the hypothesis that these common metabolic conditions, characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, might modify the association between acrylamide exposure and RA. Their shared pathophysiology with RA, along with the prevalent use of medications with potential immunomodulatory effects (e.g., statins, antihypertensives, and metformin), warranted investigation into whether the acrylamide-RA association differed across these clinically relevant subgroups. Furthermore, these conditions serve as proxies for integrated metabolic risk and distinct lifestyle patterns, which may influence both exposure levels and disease susceptibility. Given that RCS analysis reveals a significant nonlinear (J-type) association between HbAA and RA risk (Fig. 2), a simple linear model may not be able to fully capture its complex exposure–response relationship. Quartile grouping based on the percentile of the population helps to visually demonstrate the non-monotonic variation pattern of risk at different exposure level segments. Secondly, classification analysis facilitates the examination of potential heterogeneity in associations across exposure categories and subgroups. Despite this, we simultaneously provided effect estimates for continuous variables (converted by log) and continuous relationship visualization based on RCS to ensure the integrity of the analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of P < .05. Analyses were performed using RStudio software powered by Posit Software, PBC.

3. Results

3.1. Study cohort selection

The participant selection process for the study cohort is illustrated in Figure 1. We combined four 2-year cycles of NHANES data (2003–2004, 2005–2006, 2013–2014, and 2015–2016), which included a total of 40,616 adults aged 20 to 120 years. After excluding participants with missing arthritis information, those diagnosed with osteoarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, or other unspecified arthritis types, and those lacking data on HbAA/HbGA, BMI, or smoking status, the final study cohort comprised 1972 subjects. Applying NHANES sampling weights, this analytic sample represented a weighted population equivalent to 20,843,352 US adults.

3.2. Characteristics of participants with and without RA

The demographic, anthropometric, and clinical characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. A total of 1972 participants were ultimately included, with a mean age of 59.17 ± 0.45 years. The largest proportion (50.25%) was aged 60 to 80 years. Approximately 71.5% of participants had attained at least a high school education, and 45.69% were obese (BMI ≥ 30). Notably, the mean concentrations of HbAA, HbGA, and HbAA + HbGA in all subjects were 69.319, 56.566, and 125.88 pmol/g Hb, respectively, with an HbGA/HbAA ratio of 0.9031. To clearly define the cutoff points of the exposure quartiles in the subsequent analysis, the population distribution percentiles of each acrylamide hemoglobin biomarker are as follows: the P25, P50, and P75 of HbAA are 36.7, 47.6, and 73.1 pmol/g Hb, respectively; HbGA are 30.9, 44.3, and 66.4 pmol/g Hb respectively; HbAA + HbGA are68.1, 93.2, and 141.4 pmol/g Hb respectively; HbGA/HbAA are 0.6993464, 0.8686636, and 1.0655340 pmol/g Hb respectively (for detailed data, please refer to Table 2).

3.4. RCS analysis of hemoglobin adducts and RA risk

Figure 2 presents the RCS analysis examining the nonlinear relationships between acrylamide hemoglobin adducts and RA risk. Our findings reveal complex, non-monotonic associations rather than simple linear dose–response patterns for HbAA (Fig. 2A), HbGA (Fig. 2B), and their composite measure (HbAA + HbGA; Fig. 2C). The RCS analyses indicated non-monotonic exposure–response patterns across the range of biomarker concentrations. Notably, Figure 2D demonstrates a positive association between the HbGA/HbAA metabolic ratio and RA risk. The smoothed curve shows a progressive increase in ORs with higher HbGA/HbAA ratios, suggesting potential dose-dependent risk elevation. The shaded 95% confidence bands reveal important nuances in estimation precision: wider intervals at lower ratio values indicate reduced stability, likely reflecting either limited sample size in these ranges or greater biological variability. In contrast, the narrowing CIs at higher ratios demonstrate improved estimation precision, enhancing the reliability of our findings in clinically relevant exposure ranges.

3.5. Stratified associations between acrylamide biomarkers and RA

To explore potential heterogeneity in the associations between acrylamide hemoglobin adducts and RA, stratified analyses were conducted according to smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia (Table 4). Overall, the magnitude and precision of the estimated associations varied across strata, and several subgroup estimates were accompanied by wide CIs, indicating limited statistical power.

In analyses stratified by smoking status, differences in the association between the HbGA/HbAA ratio and RA prevalence were observed between smokers and nonsmokers. Among nonsmokers, no clear association was detected across exposure quartiles, whereas among smokers, higher point estimates were observed in the upper quartiles. However, CIs were wide, particularly in the smoking subgroup, and the estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution.

When stratified by alcohol consumption, the association between HbGA and RA appeared to differ between drinkers and nondrinkers. Higher HbGA levels were associated with increased RA prevalence among nondrinkers, whereas no statistically clear association was observed among drinkers. Given the limited sample size within strata and the absence of detailed alcohol exposure information, these findings should be regarded as exploratory.

Stratified analyses by hypertension and hyperlipidemia status also showed variability in effect estimates across exposure categories. In some strata, moderate exposure levels were associated with higher point estimates, while in others the associations were attenuated. These patterns were not consistent across all biomarkers or strata and were characterized by imprecise estimates.

Taken together, stratified analyses showed variability in point estimates across subgroups, with limited precision in several strata.

4. Discussion

This study, based on nationally representative NHANES data, systematically evaluated the association between acrylamide hemoglobin adducts (HbAA, HbGA, and their combined indicators) and the prevalence of RA. Using RCS models, we observed a nonlinear (J-shaped) association between HbAA and RA prevalence, while HbGA and total adduct levels (HbAA + HbGA) showed increasing associations across higher exposure ranges. Together, these findings indicate nonlinear epidemiological associations between acrylamide-related hemoglobin adducts and RA prevalence in US adults.

The primary contribution of this study lies in characterizing the nonlinear association patterns between internal biomarkers of acrylamide exposure and RA prevalence at the population level. These findings extend existing epidemiological evidence by incorporating objective exposure biomarkers and flexible modeling approaches, while also highlighting the need for cautious interpretation given the study design.

Previous experimental and toxicological studies have suggested that acrylamide exposure may be linked to immune regulation and oxidative stress processes relevant to inflammatory conditions. Given the cross-sectional design, the absence of direct mechanistic measurements, and the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses, the observed associations should be interpreted strictly as hypothesis-generating epidemiological findings rather than as evidence of causality, subgroup-specific effects, or specific biological pathways. Future prospective studies incorporating repeated biomarker assessments and mechanistic measurements are needed to clarify temporal relationships and biological pathways underlying these associations.

5. Strengths and limitations

This study leverages a nationally representative sample from the US NHANES, employing its complex sampling design in the analysis. Consequently, the findings are generalizable to the noninstitutionalized US adult population. To our knowledge, this represents the first large-scale population-based study to investigate the association between acrylamide hemoglobin adduct levels and RA.

However, several important limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective, cross-sectional design precludes establishing causality (e.g., determining whether acrylamide exposure precedes RA onset) and limits the generalizability of the findings to populations outside the United States or with different demographic characteristics. Although validated in previous studies, RA diagnosis based on self-reporting remains susceptible to misclassification bias. Despite adjustment for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors could still influence the observed associations. Specifically, we were unable to account for clinical conditions that may independently influence hemoglobin adduct levels or inflammatory pathways, such as liver or kidney disease, which can alter the metabolism and excretion of acrylamide and its metabolites. In addition, we did not adjust for individual hemoglobin concentrations, which, despite the use of adduct levels normalized per gram of hemoglobin, could theoretically reflect underlying conditions (e.g., anemia) associated with both altered biomarker levels and systemic inflammation. Although these factors represent potential sources of residual confounding, their direct measurement was not available in the surveyed cycles of NHANES.

In addition, several subgroup analyses were characterized by limited sample sizes and wide CIs, indicating reduced statistical precision. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of effect modification.

Fourth, in handling acrylamide biomarker data, we excluded the small proportion of participants (1.8%) with HbGA concentrations below the detection limit rather than employing imputation methods. While this approach ensures that analyses are based solely on measured values, it could theoretically introduce selection bias if excluded individuals systematically differ from those included in the analysis. However, given the very low proportion of excluded samples, the potential impact on overall estimates is likely limited. Consequently, we did not perform sensitivity analyses using imputed values, as such analyses would be unlikely to materially alter the main conclusions.

Nevertheless, because participants with HbGA concentrations below the detection limit were excluded and not formally compared with included individuals, the direction and magnitude of any resulting selection bias cannot be fully assessed.

Future research should utilize prospective cohort designs incorporating more precise exposure assessment methods (e.g., repeated biomarker measurements, validated dietary records) and rigorous RA case confirmation (e.g., clinical criteria, specific biomarkers). Further exploration of underlying biological mechanisms (e.g., DNA adduct formation, oxidative stress pathways, inflammatory mediator profiles) and social-behavioral pathways is also warranted.

6. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified nonlinear associations between HbAA and the prevalence of RA in a nationally representative sample of US adults. These findings support an epidemiological association between internal biomarkers of acrylamide exposure and RA, but do not establish causality, permit risk prediction, or indicate subgroup-specific effects. Further prospective studies with repeated exposure assessment and mechanistic investigations are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and underlying biological pathways.
