Optimizing Sparse SYK
Matthew Ding, Robbie King, Bobak T. Kiani, Eric R. Anschuetz

Abstract
Finding the ground state of strongly-interacting fermionic systems is often the prerequisite for fully understanding both quantum chemistry and condensed matter systems. The Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model is a representative example of such a system; it is particularly interesting not only due to the existence of efficient quantum algorithms preparing approximations to the ground state such as Hastings–O’Donnell (STOC 2022), but also known no-go results for many classical ansatzes in preparing low-energy states. However, this quantum-classical separation is known to not persist when the SYK model is sufficiently sparsified, i.e., when terms in the model are discarded with probability 1 − p 1-p , where p = Θ ⁡ ( 1 / n 3 ) p=\Theta(1/n^{3}) and n n is the system size. This raises the question of how robust the quantum and classical complexities of the SYK model are to sparsification. In this work we initiate the study of the sparse SYK model where p ∈ [ Θ ⁡ ( 1 / n 3 ) , 1 ] p\in[\Theta(1/n^{3}),1] . We show there indeed exists a certain robustness of sparsification. First, we show a “universality” result, which states that with high probability the ground state energy on the sparse SYK model matches the SYK model up to constant factors independent of the degree of sparsification. Additionally, we prove that the quantum algorithm of Hastings–O’Donnell for p = 1 p=1 still achieves a constant-factor approximation to the ground energy when p ≥ Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / n ) p\geq\Omega(\log n/n) . Finally, we prove that with high probability, Gaussian states cannot achieve better than a O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / p ​ n ) O(\sqrt{\log n/pn}) -factor approximation to the true ground state energy of sparse SYK. This is done through a general classical circuit complexity lower-bound of Ω ⁡ ( p ​ n 3 ) \Omega(pn^{3}) for any quantum state achieving a constant-factor approximation. Combined, these show a provable separation between classical algorithms outputting Gaussian states and efficient quantum algorithms for the goal of finding approximate sparse SYK ground states when p ≥ Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / n ) p\geq\Omega(\log n/n) , extending the analogous p = 1 p=1 result of Hastings–O’Donnell.

1.1
Quantum algorithm

One of our main results is a proof that a certain quantum algorithm outputs an explicit quantum state which achieves a constant approximation of the ground state energy in sparse SYK Hamiltonians when
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
.

Theorem 1.1

(Sparse SYK quantum algorithm)
.

In the sparse SYK model with any sparsity parameter
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
, there exists an efficient quantum algorithm outputting a state which achieves
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
energy with probability exponentially close to
1
over the draw of the Hamiltonian.

This quantum algorithm is based on the Hastings–O’Donnell variational algorithm
[
HO23
]
for optimizing the SYK model, which works as follows:

Hastings–O’Donnell algorithm overview.

Hastings–O’Donnell defines a “two-color” SYK model, which is the Hamiltonian

H^{(2)}_{SYK}(n_{1},n_{2})\triangleq\frac{i}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{2}}\tau_{m}\gamma_{m}

(3)

where
\tau_{m}
is an instance of a
3
-body SYK Hamiltonian on
n_{1}
Majorana modes. One can think of this Hamiltonian as partitioning the standard SYK Hamiltonian operators into two sets,
A
of size
3n/4
and
B
of size
n/4
, and only considering interaction terms which have three terms from
A
and one term from
B
. This represents a constant fraction of terms in the SYK Hamiltonian, and high-energy states of this model remain high-energy states of the standard SYK model.

The algorithm acts on the two-color model by first considering an additional set of
n_{2}
ancilla Majorana modes with associated operators
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n_{2}}
. The algorithm first constructs the Gaussian state
\rho_{0}
optimizing the Hamiltonian
h^{\prime}\triangleq\frac{i}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{2}}\sigma_{m}\chi_{m}
, which is the maximally entangled state on the
\sigma
and
\chi
modes. As this state is Gaussian, it is efficient to prepare by a classical computer. Our goal is to modify this Gaussian state into one that optimizes our original Hamiltonian using a quantum computer.

The next step of the algorithm is to apply a unitary transformation on our Gaussian state to get
\rho_{\theta}\triangleq e^{-\theta\zeta}\rho_{0}e^{\theta\zeta}
, where
\zeta\triangleq\sum_{m=1}^{n_{2}}\tau_{m}\sigma_{m}
. Intuitively, we can think of this unitary transformation as a “rotation” of the
\sigma
operators towards the
\tau
operators in the Heisenberg picture, where the magnitude of the rotation is parameterized by the evolution time
\theta
. To first order in
\theta
this rotation achieves a high energy, and the main technical challenge is to upper-bound the effect of higher-order terms. Hastings–O’Donnell
[
HO23
]
show that for some constant
\theta=\theta^{\ast}
this is indeed the case for standard SYK, and
\rho_{\theta^{\ast}}
achieves
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
energy on both the two-color model and the original SYK model.

The rotation is also crucially “non-Gaussian”, meaning the resulting state is not a Gaussian state, and it achieves a better energy scaling than any possible Gaussian state. Hastings and O’Donnell also show that their algorithm is efficiently implementable on a quantum computer with circuit complexity polynomial in
n
.

In
Section

4
we show that this algorithm retains its performance guarantees when applied to sparse SYK Hamiltonians when
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
. The proof of this relies on defining a sparse two-color SYK model similar to the model in
[
HO23
]
. From here we apply similar analysis as the original Hastings–O’Donnell algorithm, with special care to show that the Bernoulli random variables do not affect the tails of our distribution significantly when
p
is sufficiently large.

1.2
Gaussian state upper bound

Additionally, we show that Gaussian states cannot achieve a constant approximation ratio unless
p<C\log n/n
. We do this by showing an upper bound on the optimal energy Gaussian states can achieve on Hamiltonians with sparsity parameter
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
.

Theorem 1.2

(Gaussian state energy upper bound)
.

In the sparse SYK model with any sparsity parameter
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
, the set of Gaussian states achieve a maximum energy upper-bounded by
\widetilde{O}(p^{-1/2})
with probability exponentially close to 1 over the draw of the Hamiltonian.

We prove this in
Section

5
using the following argument: (I) We show that there exists a finite set of Gaussian states which forms an
\epsilon
-net of all Gaussian states in terms of energy with respect to typical sparse SYK Hamiltonians. (II) We use a Lovász theta function on graphs to bound the maximum variance of any fixed quantum state with respect to the distribution of sparse SYK Hamiltonians when
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
. A similar argument was shown in
[
Ans+24
]
for dense SYK. (III) With a tight enough bound on this variance, we can use standard concentration bounds to show that the supremum of the energy over all Gaussian states in the net strongly concentrates around its mean, and therefore cannot be too large. Since the supremum of the net well-approximates the supremum of all Gaussian states, with high probability no Gaussian state exists with high energy for typical sparse SYK instances.

Combining
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.2
, we show a continuous regime of sparse SYK models where we have a provable separation between states with efficient classical representations and general quantum states for the optimal achievable ratio of approximation algorithms. This separation holds for all sparse SYK Hamiltonians with
p\geq C\log n/n
, extending the analogous result shown by
[
HO23
]
for the special case of
p=1
.

Lastly, we also conjecture the following stronger Gaussian state energy upper bound, applying to a larger range of
p

Conjecture 1.3

(Gaussian state energy conjecture)
.

In the sparse SYK model with any sparsity parameter
p\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-2})
, the set of Gaussian states achieve a maximum energy upper-bounded by
O(p^{-1/4})
with probability exponentially close to 1 over the draw of the Hamiltonian.

This conjecture follows from another conjecture on the behavior of the Lovász theta function for sparse graphs, which we give numerical evidence for in
Section

5.2
.

2.2
Fermionic operators

The creation and annihilation operators are conjugate operators that add and remove, respectively, a single particle from a given mode. Fermionic operators follow the canonical anticommutation relations. Specifically, representing the fermionic creation and annihilation operators for a mode
i
as
a^{\dagger}_{i}
and
a_{i}
, respectively, we have:

\{a_{i},a_{j}\}=0,\qquad\{a^{\dagger}_{i},a^{\dagger}_{j}\}=0,\qquad\{a_{i},a_{j}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{ij},

(4)

where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the anticommutator.

Majorana fermions are a special class of fermions which are their own antiparticle. Mathematically, they have equivalent creation and annihilation operators. That is, given a Majorana annihilation operator
\gamma_{i}
we have
\gamma_{i}=\gamma^{\dagger}_{i}
. Majorana operators are also traceless, square to the identity, and form a Clifford algebra, i.e.,
\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}\mathbbm{I}
. We can represent a general set of
n
fermionic modes with
2n
Majorana operators as follows:

a_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{2i-1}+i\gamma_{2i}),\qquad a^{\dagger}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{2i-1}-i\gamma_{2i})\qquad\forall i=1,2,\ldots,n,

(5)

and for this reason we will concern ourselves only with Majorana fermions in what follows.

Canonical representation.

There exists a canonical matrix representation of the Majorana operators
\gamma_{i}
. The Weyl–Brauer matrices form a representation of the Clifford algebra, and generalize the Pauli matrices to higher dimensions

]

(Weyl–Brauer matrices)

The degree-

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=X_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{3}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{n},

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=Y_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{3}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{n},

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\otimes X_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{3}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{n},

\displaystyle\gamma_{4}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\otimes Y_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{3}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{n},

\displaystyle\gamma_{2n-1}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\otimes Z_{2}\otimes Z_{3}\otimes\ldots\otimes X_{n},

2.3
Fermionic Hamiltonians

Our problem of interest is in finding the maximal energy state of a system of
n
fermionic modes (e.g., electron orbitals) with low-degree interactions.

Problem 2.2

(Degree-
q
fermionic Hamiltonian optimization)
.

Given even integer
q
and the fermionic Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{q}\}\subseteq[2n]}c_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{q}}\gamma_{i_{1}}\gamma_{i_{2}}\ldots\gamma_{i_{q}}+\text{h.c.},\qquad c_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{q}}\in\mathbb{R},

(7)

find the maximum eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(H)=\max_{\psi}\braket{\psi|H|\psi}
.

We will particularly be interested in the case where
H
is an instance of the
sparse SYK-q (SSYK-q) model
:

H_{\text{SSYK-q}}\left(p\right)\triangleq\binom{2n}{q}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}i^{q/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{q}\}\subseteq[2n]\\
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{q}\end{subarray}}J_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{q}}X_{i_{1}\ldots i_{q}}\gamma_{i_{1}}\gamma_{i_{2}}\ldots\gamma_{i_{q}},

(8)

where
q\geq 4
is even, the
J_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{q}}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables, and the

are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i_{1}\ldots i_{q}}=1\right)=p
. When

we call this model the
(dense) SYK-q model
. For the special case
q=4
—the minimal setting beyond free fermions—we will refer to the dense and sparse models as the SYK and SSYK models, respectively. Finally, we will often denote the summation over all terms as
\sum_{I}J_{I}X_{I}\gamma_{I}
, where
I
is assumed to iterate through all strictly-increasing ordered subsets of

of cardinality

.

Note that the “interesting” range of sparsification lies between
p=\Theta(1/n^{q-1})
and
p=1
, as any value

will generally have fermionic modes with no interactions with any other modes. Thus for notational convenience, we assume some minimum value
p_{min}=\epsilon/n^{q-1}
for any arbitrarily small constant
\epsilon>0
. We implicitly assume that

, and whenever we refer to “all values of

2.4
Gaussian states

We can also consider restricting
\ket{\psi}

to be over specific classes of states. One important such class is the set of

Definition 2.3

(Fermionic Gaussian states)
.

For any orthogonal matrix
R\in SO(2n)
, we define the unitary operator
U_{R}\in U(2^{n})

n

(U_{R})^{\dagger}\gamma_{i}(U_{R})=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}R_{ij}\gamma_{j}\qquad\forall i=1,2,\ldots,2n,

(9)

which is uniquely defined up to global phase

]
. A state

\ket{\psi}=U_{R}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

All fermionic Gaussian states can also be written in the standard density matrix form:

\rho\triangleq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{I}+i\lambda_{j}\tilde{\gamma}_{2j-1}\tilde{\gamma}_{2j}\right),

(10)

where
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,1]
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{i}=(U_{R})^{\dagger}\gamma_{i}(U_{R})

\lambda_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
the state is a pure fermionic Gaussian state, otherwise it is a mixed fermionic Gaussian state. We generally use
\ket{\psi}
to denote pure quantum state vectors and
\rho
to denote density matrices of mixed quantum states.

We can also define the

\Gamma

Definition 2.4

(Gaussian state covariance matrix)
.

Given a fermionic Gaussian state
\rho

\Gamma_{ij}=\frac{i}{2}\Tr(\rho[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}])

\Gamma
can be block-diagonalized as

\Gamma=R\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda_{j}\\
-\lambda_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}R^{\top}

(11)

where

is the orthogonal matrix from

9
and
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,1]
is from

10
.

As classical mean-field methods output Gaussian states, an interesting open question is how well Gaussian states can approximate the maximum energy over arbitrary states for degree-4 Hamiltonians.

Definition 2.5

(Gaussian approximation factor)
.

Denote
\lambda_{\text{Gauss}}(H)\triangleq\max_{\psi\text{ Gaussian}}\braket{\psi|H|\psi}

\ket{\psi}
is restricted to be a Gaussian state. The

\alpha_{\text{Gauss}}\in[0,1]

\alpha_{\text{Gauss}}\triangleq\lambda_{\text{Gauss}}(H)/\lambda_{\max}(H)

When considering the sparse SYK Hamiltonian, we will often be interested in a net of Gaussian

Proposition 2.6

For any constant

\exp(O(n^{2}\log(n/\epsilon)))

\epsilon

\inf_{\ket{\phi}\in S}T(\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi},\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})\leq\epsilon.

(12)

Proof.

The set of all
n
-mode fermionic Gaussian states is generated by a circuit architecture of at most
O(n^{2})
continuously parameterized two-qubit gates

Jia+18
]
. For any Gaussian state
\ket{\phi}

which express the state as

\ket{\phi}=\prod_{j=1}^{O(n^{2})}U_{j}(\theta_{j})\ket{0},

(13)

from some fixed starting state
\ket{0}
.
We form our net by discretely parameterizing each gate parameter
\theta_{i}
with intervals of size
\epsilon^{\prime}=\Theta(\epsilon/n^{2})
so that there are
O(n^{2}/\epsilon)
discrete values each parameter can take. Given a state
\ket{\phi}
with gate parameters

\bar{\theta}_{i}
the closest parameter value in our net to

\ket{\bar{\phi}}
which is the state associated to parameters

\ket{\phi}
. Denote intermediate states for

\ket{\psi_{k}}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}U_{j}(\theta_{i})\prod_{j^{\prime}=k+1}^{|\{\theta_{i}\}|}U_{j^{\prime}}(\bar{\theta}_{i})\ket{0}.

(14)

Then, it holds that

T(\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi},\ket{\bar{\phi}}\bra{\bar{\phi}})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{|\{\theta_{i}\}|-1}T(\ket{\psi_{j}}\bra{\psi_{j}},\ket{\psi_{j+1}}\bra{\psi_{j+1}})\leq O(\epsilon^{\prime}n^{2})=O(\epsilon).

(15)

In total, this construction will have
\exp(O(n^{2}\log(n/\epsilon)))
many distinct states in the net.
∎

\big\lvert\braket{\phi|H|\phi}-\braket{\psi|H|\psi}\big\rvert\leq\frac{\epsilon}{m}\cdot\left\|H\right\|_{\text{op}}.

Thus if
\left\|H\right\|_{\text{op}}\leq m
, our
(\epsilon/m)
-net construction approximates all Gaussian states in energy with respect to
H
up to an additive
\epsilon
error.

2.5
Commutation index and Lovász theta function

Given a random Hamiltonian of the form
H=\sum_{I}J_{I}A_{I}
, where
A_{I}
are Hermitian operators which square to the identity operator and
J_{I}
are random coefficients with mean zero and unit variance, a key quantity of study is the variance of the energy of a given state
\ket{\psi}
:

\operatorname{Var}(\bra{\psi}H\ket{\psi})=\sum_{I}\bra{\psi}A_{I}\ket{\psi}^{2}.

(17)

We will later use upper bounds on the energy variance to obtain tail bounds on the probability that the energy of a state can be at a specified value. We describe here how to bound the variance quantity above using the Lovász theta function of the so-called
commutation graph
of the set
\mathcal{S}=\{A_{I}\}_{I}
. This is the graph constructed by taking
\mathcal{S}
as the vertex set, and inserting an edge between
A_{I},A_{J}\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if they anticommute.

Definition 2.7

(Commutation graph)
.

Given a finite set
\mathcal{S}
of Pauli or Majorana operators, the commutation graph
G(\mathcal{S})
of
\mathcal{S}
is a graph of
|\mathcal{S}|
nodes constructed as follows:

•

The vertices of
G(\mathcal{S})
correspond to operators
A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
.

•

There is an edge between two vertices if and only if their operators
anticommute
(i.e.,
i\sim j
if
A_{i}A_{j}+A_{j}A_{i}=0
).

We follow closely the formulation of
[
Ans+24
]
, though similar ideas have been advanced in works on studying uncertainty relations
[
GHG23
,
XSW24
]
, random Hamiltonians
[
AGK24
]
, and shadow tomography
[
Kin+25
]
.
The commutation index is defined as follows:

Definition 2.8

(Commutation index)
.

The commutation index
\Delta
of a finite set of Hermitian operators
\mathcal{S}
is

\Delta(\mathcal{S})\triangleq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sup_{|\psi\rangle}\;\sum_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}}\langle\psi|A_{i}|\psi\rangle^{2}.

(18)

It is easy to see that the commutation index bounds the energy variance of any given state. In turn, the commutation index is known to be bounded by the
Lovász theta function
of the commutation graph of
\mathcal{S}
. This fact was proven in
[
GHG23
, Equation 5]
,
[
HO23
]
, and
[
XSW24
, Proposition 3]
.

We define the Lovász theta function as follows.

Definition 2.9

(Lovász theta function)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
|V|
vertices with edges in
E
. The
Lovász theta function

\vartheta(G)
is given by a semidefinite program of dimension
|V|
:

\begin{split}\max\ \{\ &\Tr\left(\mathbb{J}X\right)\ ,\ X\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times|V|}\\
&\text{s.t.}\ X\succeq 0\ ,\ \Tr\left(X\right)=1\ ,\ X_{jl}=0\ \forall(j,l)\in E\ \},\end{split}

(19)

where
\mathbb{J}
is the all-ones matrix.

Finally, we restate a key lemma which shows that
\Delta(\mathcal{S})\leq\vartheta(G(\mathcal{S}))/|\mathcal{S}|
.

Lemma 2.10

(Bound on commutation index
[
GHG23
,
HO23
,
XSW24
]
)
.

Given a finite set
\mathcal{S}
of Pauli or Majorana operators with commutation graph
G(\mathcal{S})
, it holds that

\Delta(\mathcal{S})\leq\frac{\vartheta(G(\mathcal{S}))}{|\mathcal{S}|}.

(20)

This lemma allows us to bound the commutation index of the SYK Hamiltonian.

Lemma 2.11

(Bound on commutation index of SYK Hamiltonian
[
Ans+24
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be of the set of all degree
q
Majorana operators for
q>0
even:

\mathcal{S}=\left\{i^{q/2}\gamma_{i_{1}}\gamma_{i_{2}}\ldots\gamma_{i_{q}}\;|\;\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{q}\}\subseteq[2n],\;i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{q}\right\}.

(21)

Then
\Delta(\mathcal{S})=\Theta(n^{-q/2})
.

3.1
Maximum eigenvalue upper bound

[
HO23
]
showed that the maximum eigenvalue of the SYK-4 model can be upper-bounded by
O(\sqrt{n})
with probability exponentially close to 1 using a straightforward application of the matrix Chernoff bound, given as follows:

Proposition 3.3

(Theorem 1.2 of
[
Tro11
]
)
.

Consider a finite sequence
\{A_{k}\}
of fixed, self-adjoint matrices with dimension
d
, and let
J_{k}
be a finite sequence of independent standard
normal random variables. Then, for all
t\geq 0
,

\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\sum_{k}J_{k}A_{k}\right)\geq t\right)\leq d\cdot e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

(24)

where
\sigma^{2}\triangleq\left\|\sum_{k}A_{k}^{2}\right\|_{\text{op}}
.

We reproduce the proof of
[
HO23
]
for the dense SYK model for completeness. Since
\gamma_{I}
in the Weyl–Brauer representation is a
2^{n}
dimensional matrix and
\left\|\sum_{I}\gamma_{I}^{2}\right\|_{\text{op}}=\left\|\sum_{I}\mathbb{I}\right\|_{\text{op}}=\binom{2n}{4}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}\sum_{I}J_{I}\gamma_{I}\right)\geq C\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\sum_{I}J_{I}\gamma_{I}\right)\geq Cn^{5/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n}\cdot e^{-\Omega\left(\frac{n^{5}}{n^{4}}\right)}.

For large enough constant
C
the right hand side scales as
\exp(-\Omega(n))
.

We generalize this result to the sparse SYK model. Consider a distribution of sparse SYK Hamiltonians with sparsity parameter
p
, and consider the random vector
\vec{x}
of size
\binom{2n}{4}
where each entry is a random Bernoulli variable with
\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{x}_{i}\right]=p
. From a Chernoff bound (see, e.g., Theorem 18.4 from
[
Har23
]
):

\Pr\left(|\vec{x}|_{0}\geq(1+\delta)\binom{2n}{4}p\right)\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\binom{2n}{4}p/2}

(25)

for all
0\leq\delta\leq 1
. Thus we have that
\Pr\left(|\vec{x}|_{0}\geq 2\E[|\vec{x}|_{0}]\right)
with probability
\exp(-\Omega(n))
. Conditioned on the event
|\vec{x}|_{0}<2\E[|\vec{x}|_{0}]
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\sum_{I}X_{I}J_{I}\gamma_{I}\right)\geq C\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\sum_{I\subseteq\text{supp}(\vec{x})}J_{I}\gamma_{I}\right)\geq Cn^{3/2}p^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n}\cdot e^{-\Omega\left(\frac{pn^{3}}{pn^{4}}\right)}

For large enough constant
C
the right hand side scales as
\exp(-\Omega(n))
. Using a union bound, we can upper-bound the probability of the event that, (1) either the number of non-zero entries in our binomial vector is greater than twice the expected value, or (2) that the maximum eigenvalue is greater than
C\sqrt{n}
given the number of non-zero entries in our binomial vector is less than twice the expected value, as less than
\exp(-\Omega(n))
. Formally,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}(H_{SSYK})\geq C\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}(H_{SSYK})\geq C\sqrt{n}\;\Big|\;|\vec{x}|_{0}<2\E[|\vec{x}|_{0}]\right)\vee\left(|\vec{x}|_{0}\geq 2\E[|\vec{x}|_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}(H_{SSYK})\geq C\sqrt{n}\;\Big|\;|\vec{x}|_{0}<2\E[|\vec{x}|_{0}]\right)+\Pr\left(|\vec{x}|_{0}\geq 2\E[|\vec{x}|_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\Omega(n)\right)+\exp\left(-\Omega(n)\right)\leq\exp\left(-\Omega(n)\right).

Thus we see that the maximum eigenvalue of the sparse SYK value is
O(\sqrt{n})
for all
p
with probability exponentially close to 1.

4.1
Reduction from sparse SYK-4 model

Consider the sparse SYK-4 model

H_{SSYK}(p)\triangleq\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[2n]\\

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix some sparsification parameter
p
. Let
H^{(2)}_{SSYK}
be a sparse two-color SYK Hamiltonian with
0.1n\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq 2n
. For fixed state
\rho
, if
\Tr(H^{(2)}_{SSYK}\rho)\geq C_{1}\sqrt{n}

for some constant

\Tr(H_{SSYK}\rho)\geq C_{2}\sqrt{n}
with probability
e^{-\Omega(n)}
for some constant
C_{2}

Proof.

We partition the Majorana operators into two sets, indexed by
A=[n_{1}]
and
B=[2n]\setminus A
. Let
n_{2}=2n-n_{1}
, and assume
n_{2}\leq n_{1}
without loss of generality. Consider all Majorana operators
\gamma_{I}
in the sparse SYK Hamiltonian
Eq.

33
; we let
T
denote the subset of all
\gamma_{I}
for which
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}<i_{4}\}
with
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in A
and
i_{4}\in B

H_{SSYK}=H_{T}+H_{\overline{T}},

(34)

where
H_{T}
contains only the terms with
\gamma_{I}\in T
and
H_{\overline{T}}
the terms with
\gamma_{I}\in\overline{T}
. We can rewrite

\displaystyle H_{T}

\displaystyle=\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\sum_{j\in B}\left(\sum_{I\subset A,|I|=3}X_{I,j}J_{I,j}\gamma_{I}\right)\cdot\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2i}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{j\in B}\left(i\binom{2n-1}{3}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\sum_{I\subset[n_{1}],|I|=3}X_{I,j}J_{I,j}\gamma_{I}\right)\cdot\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{n_{2}}}{\sqrt{2n}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\binom{n_{1}}{3}}{\binom{2n-1}{3}}}\frac{i}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\sum_{j\in B}\tau_{j}(p)\cdot\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\frac{n_{2}\binom{n_{1}}{3}}{2n\binom{2n-1}{3}}}H^{(2)}_{SSYK}(p).

n_{1},n_{2}\in[0.1n,2n]
, we have
H_{T}(p)=C_{3}H^{(2)}_{SSYK}(p)
, for some absolute constant
C_{3}
. Thus if some state
\rho
has energy
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
(independent of

H^{(2)}_{SSYK}(p)

\Omega(\sqrt{n})
on

.

Now we consider the

where

with probability at least

over the draw of
H_{SSYK}^{(2)}

Proof.

Following the high-level idea from

, we calculate the energy of

\Tr\left(\rho_{\theta}H_{SSYK}^{(2)}\right)=\Tr\left(e^{-\theta\zeta}\rho_{0}e^{+\theta\zeta}H_{SSYK}^{(2)}\right)=\Tr\left(\rho_{0}e^{+\theta\zeta}H_{SSYK}^{(2)}e^{-\theta\zeta}\right)=\Tr\left(\rho_{0}\text{Ad}_{e^{+\theta\zeta}}(H_{SSYK}^{(2)})\right)

Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula,

\text{Ad}_{e^{+\theta\zeta}}(H_{SSYK}^{(2)})=H_{SSYK}^{(2)}+\theta[\zeta,H_{SSYK}^{(2)}]+\theta^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\text{Ad}_{e^{+s\theta\zeta}}\left([\zeta,[\zeta,(H_{SSYK}^{(2)})]]\right)\,dx,

and thus

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\rho_{\theta}H_{SSYK}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(H_{SSYK}^{(2)}\right)+\theta\Tr\left(\rho_{0}[\zeta,H_{SSYK}^{(2)}]\right)+\theta^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\Tr\left(\rho_{0}\text{Ad}_{e^{+s\theta\zeta}}\left([\zeta,[\zeta,(H_{SSYK}^{(2)})]]\right)\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\theta\Tr\left(\rho_{0}[\zeta,H_{SSYK}^{(2)}]\right)+\theta^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\Tr\left(\rho_{0}[\zeta,[\zeta,(H_{SSYK}^{(2)})]]\right)\,dx.

We finally get

therefore we wish to lower bound the right-hand side of

for some

Lemma 4.5

Proof.

We begin by directly calculating out the trace term similar to the dense SYK-4 case.

\displaystyle\Tr(\rho_{0}[\zeta,H_{SSYK}^{(2)}])

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\Tr(\rho_{0}[\zeta,\tau_{j}\chi_{j}])

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{2}}\Tr(\rho_{0}[\tau_{k}\sigma_{k},\tau_{j}\chi_{j}])

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\cdot 2^{-(n_{2}+n_{1}/2)}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\Tr(\tau_{j}^{2})

From here our proof differs from the dense SYK-4 case, and we use our new definition of

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\cdot 2^{-(n_{2}+n_{1}/2)}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\Tr(\tau_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n_{2}}}\cdot 2^{-(n_{2}+n_{1}/2)}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\binom{n_{1}}{3}^{-1}p^{-1}\Tr\left(\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n_{1}]\\

|S|=3\end{subarray}}X_{S}J_{S,j}^{2}

5
Optimal energy Gaussian states for sparse SYK Hamiltonians

In this section we prove upper and lower bounds on the maximum energy Gaussian states can achieve on sparse SYK Hamiltonians. Both results hold with probability exponentially close to 1 over the disorder of the Hamiltonians. We also give a conjecture on a stronger upper bound, and give numerical simulations which provide evidence for it.

5.1
Gaussian state energy upper bound

We restate
Theorem

1.2
for convenience:
See
1.2

Proof.

This result is a consequence of the following proposition:

Proposition 5.1

(Sparse SYK circuit complexity lower bound)
.

With probability exponentially close to 1 over the draw of the Hamiltonian, any family of states
S
containing a state which achieves energy
t
on the sparse SYK model with sparsity parameter
p
must have at least
|S|\geq\exp(\Omega(pn^{2}t^{2}))
distinct states.

As shown in
Proposition

2.6
, we can construct a net of all Gaussian states with
\epsilon
additive error in energy using
O(n^{2}\log(m/\epsilon))
gates with respect to Hamiltonian
H
, given that
\left\|H\right\|_{\text{op}}\leq m
. When
p>\omega(1/n^{3})
, we have
\left\|H_{SSYK}\right\|_{\text{op}}\leq O(\sqrt{n})
with probability exponentially close to 1 over the draw of the Hamiltonian (due to
Theorem

3.1
), implying we can use
O(n^{2}\log n)
gates to construct our
\epsilon
-net for any fixed constant
\epsilon
. Assuming
Proposition

5.1
, for Gaussian states to achieve energy
t
we must have
\Omega(pn^{2}t^{2})\leq O(n^{2}\log n)
, or
t\leq O(\sqrt{\log n/p})
. Thus to achieve any constant approximation to the maximum energy of
t=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, we cannot have
p\geq\Omega(\log n/n)
.

We now complete the proof of
Theorem

1.2
by proving
Proposition

5.1
. For the standard
p=1
SYK-4 model,
\braket{\psi|H_{SYK}|\psi}
is a Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}\leq O(n^{-2})
due to
Lemma

2.11
. As a reminder, we define our sparse SYK model as

H_{SSYK}\triangleq\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\sum_{B\in S^{2n}}J_{I}X_{I}B,

(52)

where
X_{I}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with
P(X_{I}=1)=p
. Note that the number of nonzero terms in the sum above is distributed binomially as
\operatorname{Bin}(\binom{2n}{4},p)
. Therefore by standard Chernoff bounds on the tails of the Binomial distribution (see, e.g., Theorem 18.4 from
[
Har23
]
), it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[2n]\\
|I|=4\end{subarray}}X_{I}-p\binom{2n}{4}\right|\geq\delta p\binom{2n}{4}\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\delta^{2}p\binom{2n}{4}\right)\right).

(53)

Therefore, when
p\binom{2n}{4}=\omega(1)
, there exists a choice of
\delta=o(1)
which suffices to guarantee that with high probability the number of terms is
\sum_{I}X_{I}=\Theta(pn^{4})
.

Finally, we note that removing a node from
G(S^{2n})
cannot increase the Lovász theta function of the graph. Therefore, denoting
G(S^{2n}_{p})
as a given sparsified graph with parameter
p
, we have the universal bound

\vartheta(G(S^{2n}_{p}))\leq\vartheta(G(S^{2n}))\leq O(n^{2})

(54)

for all
p
. By
Lemma

2.10
, noting that
\sum_{I}X_{I}=\Theta(pn^{4})
with probability
1-\exp\left(-\operatorname{\Omega}\left(pn^{4}\right)\right)
, and standard Gaussian tail bounds, we then have that for any state
\ket{\psi}
:

\Pr(\braket{\psi|H_{SSYK}|\psi}\geq t)\leq\exp(-\Omega(pn^{2}t^{2})).

(55)

Thus, by the union bound, we must have circuit complexity
N(p)\geq\Omega(pn^{2}t^{2})
to have
\Omega(1)
probability of achieving energy
t
over the set of states in the ansatz.
∎

5.3
Gaussian state energy lower bound

We prove that Gaussian states achieve—with probability exponentially close to 1 over the disorder—
\operatorname{\Omega}\left(1\right)
energy for the sparse SYK model for all values
p
:

Theorem 5.4
.

In the sparse SYK model with any sparsity parameter
p
, there exists a fermionic Gaussian state
\rho_{\varSigma}
with constant energy, i.e.,
\Tr(H_{SSYK}\rho_{\varSigma})\geq\Omega(1)
, with probability exponentially close to 1 over the draw of the Hamiltonian.

Recall the sparse SYK model

H_{SSYK}(p)\triangleq\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[2n]\\
|I|=4\end{subarray}}J_{I}X_{I}\gamma_{I},

(56)

where the
J_{I}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables and the
X_{I}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{I}=1\right)=p
. Our argument is similar to Remark 7.14 of
[
HO23
]
, though we reproduce the full details here for completeness. Note that the Gaussian state depends on the specific SSYK Hamiltonian.

Proof.

Let
\bm{g}^{0}
be the
2n\times 2n
block diagonal matrix:

\bm{g}^{0}\triangleq i\bm{\varPi}_{n/2}\otimes\bm{Y},

(57)

where

\bm{Y}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}

(58)

is the Pauli
Y
matrix and
\bm{\varPi}_{n/2}
is the
n\times n
projector with ones along the first
n/2
entries of the diagonal and zeros everywhere else.

Similarly, let
\bm{g}^{1}
be the
2n\times 2n
matrix with entries:

\bm{g}_{k,l}^{1}\triangleq\bm{1}\left\{k>n\wedge l>n\right\}\left(-1\right)^{\bm{1}\left\{k>l\right\}}\sum_{i<j\leq n}\bm{g}_{i,j}^{0}J_{\left\{i,j,k,l\right\}}X_{\left\{i,j,k,l\right\}},

(59)

where
\bm{1}\left\{\cdot\right\}
denotes the indicator function. The bottom-right
n\times n
block of
\bm{g}^{1}
defines a Wigner matrix with entries with variance
\operatorname{\Theta}\left(np\right)
. Standard results in random matrix theory (see
[
BY88
]
or Theorem 2.1.22 of
[
AGZ09
]
) then imply that
\left\|\bm{g}^{1}\right\|_{\text{op}}=\operatorname{\Theta}\left(n\sqrt{p}\right)
w.h.p.

Consider now the antisymmetric matrix

\bm{\varSigma}\triangleq\bm{g}^{0}+C\bm{g}^{1},

(60)

where
C=\operatorname{\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{p}}\right)
such that
\bm{\varSigma}
defines the covariance matrix of a valid quantum Gaussian state. Using the block diagonal structure of the covariance matrix, the fact that
\Theta\left(n^{3}\right)
terms in the Hamiltonian give a nonzero contribution to the energy, and the variance
p
of the Bernoulli random variables, we calculate the expected energy achieved by this Gaussian state
\rho_{\varSigma}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr\left(H_{SSYK}(p)\rho_{\varSigma}\right)\right]=C\binom{2n}{4}^{-1/2}p^{-1/2}\times\operatorname{\Theta}\left(n^{3}p\right)=\operatorname{\Theta}\left(1\right).

(61)

This statement is also true with probability exponentially close to 1 over the disorder by an identical argument to
Section

3.2
, taking the maximum energy over Gaussian states (
\lambda_{\text{Gauss}}
) instead of the maximum energy over all states (
\lambda_{\text{max}}
).

f(S,S^{\prime},S^{\prime\prime},j,k,l)=1
if
(|S^{\prime\prime}\cap S|\text{ is odd})\wedge(|S^{\prime}\cap(S^{\prime\prime}\triangle S)|+\delta_{k,l}\text{ is odd})
forces at least two pairs of the 12 indices to be identical in the non-zero terms of the summation. Thus, due to symmetry, we can arbitrarily fix two pairs of indices to be equivalent (
u
and
t
respectively) and sum over all remaining indices, and the set of terms in
D_{0}
in our actual expression will be a subset of the summation in
Eq.

63
. This will only increase the
2n^{th}
moment.

Let
B(f(n),p)
denote independent binomial random variables
\text{Binomial}(f(n),p)
, where
f(n)
is some integer-valued polynomial of
n
. We have:

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}\sum_{t,u}\sum_{rsa}(X_{rsta}J_{rsta})^{(1)}\sum_{wb}(X_{utwb}J_{utwb})^{(2)}\sum_{yzd}(X_{uyzd}J_{uyzd})^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}\sum_{tu}\sum_{rsa}(X_{rsta}J_{rsta})^{(1)}\sum_{wb}(X_{utwb}J_{utwb})^{(2)}\cdot B^{(3)}_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)^{1/2}\cdot L_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}\sum_{tu}\sum_{rsa}(X_{rsta}J_{rsta})^{(1)}\cdot B^{(2)}_{tu}(C_{2}n^{2},p)^{1/2}\cdot K_{tu}\cdot B^{(3)}_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)^{1/2}\cdot L_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}\sum_{tu}B^{(1)}_{t}(C_{1}n^{3},p)^{1/2}\cdot H_{t}\cdot B^{(2)}_{tu}(C_{2}n^{2},p)^{1/2}\cdot K_{tu}\cdot B^{(3)}_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)^{1/2}\cdot L_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}\sum_{tu}\left(B^{(1)}_{t}(C_{1}n^{3},p)\cdot B^{(2)}_{tu}(C_{2}n^{2},p)\cdot B^{(3)}_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)\right)^{1/2}H_{t}K_{tu}L_{u},

where
H_{t}
,
K_{t,u}
, and
L_{u}
are independent standard Gaussians;
B_{t}^{(1)},B_{tu}^{(2)},B_{u}^{(3)}
denote independent random binomial variables; and
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
are constants.

Let
B_{\text{small}}
denote the event
\left\{\forall t,u\ B^{(1)}_{t}(C_{1}n^{3},p)\leq(C_{1}+c)pn^{3}\wedge B^{(3)}_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)\leq(C_{3}+c)pn^{3}\right\}
for some sufficiently large constant
c
, and denote
B_{\text{large}}
as its complement. We have:

\left\|D_{0}\right\|_{2n}\leq\left(\E\left[\tilde{D}_{0}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{small}}\right]+\Pr(B_{\text{large}})\E\left[\tilde{D}_{0}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}\right]\right)^{1/2n}.

(64)

First term.

We can directly use
[
HO23
]
to bound the first term. As a reminder, we set
\tilde{D}_{0,\text{norm}}:=\tilde{D}_{0}/\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}
. We have:

\displaystyle\E[\tilde{D}^{2n}_{0,\text{norm}}\mid B_{\text{small}}]

\displaystyle\leq\left((C_{1}+c)pn^{3}\cdot(C_{3}+c)pn^{3}\right)^{2n}\cdot\E\left[\left(\sum_{tu}B^{(2)}_{tu}(n^{2},p)^{1/2}H_{t}K_{tu}L_{u}\right)^{2n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(Cpn^{3})^{2n}\cdot{\sum}_{b=1}^{n^{2}}\E\left[\left(\sum_{tu}b^{1/2}H_{t}K_{tu}L_{u}\right)^{2n}\right]\Pr(\max_{tu}B^{(2)}_{tu}=b)

\displaystyle\leq(Cpn^{3})^{2n}n^{3n}\cdot{\sum}_{b=1}^{n^{2}}b^{n}\Pr(\max_{tu}B^{(2)}_{tu}=b),

where
\left\|\sum_{tu}H_{t}K_{tu}L_{u}\right\|_{2n}^{2n}\leq O(n^{3/2})
is a bound due to
[
HO23
]
. By the union bound,
\sum_{b=1}^{n^{2}}b^{n}\Pr(\max_{tu}B^{(2)}_{tu}=b)\leq Cn^{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n^{2}}b^{n}\Pr(B^{(2)}_{tu}=b)
for some fixed
t,u
; the sum is just the
n^{th}
-moment of a
\text{Binomial}(C_{2}n^{2},p)
distribution. Using the general upper bound of the moments of
X\sim\text{Binomial}(m,q)
:

\E[X^{c}]\leq(mq)^{c}\exp{\left(\frac{c^{2}}{2mq}\right)},

(65)

we get:

\displaystyle(Cpn^{3})^{2n}n^{3n}\cdot{\sum}_{b=1}^{n^{2}}b^{n}\Pr(\max_{tu}B^{(2)}_{tu}=b)

\displaystyle\leq(Cpn^{3})^{2n}n^{3n+2}\left(Cn^{2}p\right)^{n}\exp{\left(\frac{Cn^{2}}{2n^{2}p}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq(Cpn^{3})^{2n}n^{5n+2}p^{n}\exp{(C/2p)}

\displaystyle\leq(Cn^{11/2}p^{3/2})^{2n}\exp{(C/p)},

with the last inequality holding for sufficiently large
n
. Since we wish to upper bound
\E[D^{2n}_{0,\text{norm}}\mid B_{\text{small}}]
by
(Cn^{11/2+O(1/n)}p^{3/2})^{2n}
, we must have
\exp{(1/p)}\leq C^{2n}
, or equivalently
p\geq\Omega(1/n)
.

Second term.

By a union bound and Chernoff bound on the binomial random variables,

\displaystyle\Pr(B_{\text{large}})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\bigcup_{t,u}\left(B_{t}(C_{1}n^{3},p)>(C_{1}+c)pn^{3}\right)\lor\left(B_{tu}(C_{2}n^{2})>(C_{2}+c)pn^{2}\right)\lor\left(B_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)>(C_{3}+c)pn^{3}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\sum}_{t,u}\Pr\left(B_{t}(C_{1}n^{3},p)>(C_{1}+cp)n^{3}\right)+\Pr\left(B_{tu}(C_{2}n^{2})>(C_{2}+c)pn^{2}\right)+\Pr\left(B_{u}(C_{3}n^{3},p)>(C_{3}+c)pn^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cn\exp\left(-\left(\frac{c^{2}}{c+2}\right)pn^{3}\right)+Cn^{2}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{c^{2}}{c+2}\right)pn^{2}\right)+Cn\exp\left(-\left(\frac{c^{2}}{c+2}\right)pn^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2}\exp\left(-\Omega(pn^{2})\right).

Thus

\left\|D_{0}\right\|_{2n}\leq\left(\E\left[\tilde{D}_{0}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{small}}\right]+n^{2}\exp\left(-\Omega(pn^{2})\right)\E\left[\tilde{D}_{0}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}\right]\right)^{1/2n}

(66)

Considering the worst case where each binomial random variable
X
appearing in our expressions is
1
, we have that:

\E\left[\tilde{D}_{0,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}\right]\leq(n^{4})^{2n}\left(\sum_{tu}H_{t}K_{tu}L_{u}\right)^{2n}\leq n^{11n}.

(67)

Taking into consideration the probability factor,

n^{2}\exp(-\Omega(pn^{2}))\E[\tilde{D}_{0,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}]\leq\exp(-\Omega(pn^{2}))\cdot n^{11n+2}.

(68)

Having
p\geq\Omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
and our constant factor
c
large enough, we can make
\exp(-\Omega(pn^{2}))=\exp(-12n\log n)=n^{-12n}
.

Putting the main term and tail term together, we have

\left\|D_{0}\right\|_{2n}\leq\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}\left(C^{2n}n^{11n}p^{3n}+C/n^{n-2}\right)^{1/2n}\leq C\sqrt{n}

(69)

for sufficiently large
n
, as desired.

A.2
Bounding
D_{1}

Recall:

D_{1}=C\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\binom{n}{3}^{-3/2}p^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n_{1}]\\
|S|=3\end{subarray}}X_{S,j}J_{S,j}^{3}.

(70)

To bound this, we can use hypercontractivity (Bonami’s lemma
[
Bon70
]
).
D_{1}
is a degree-3 Gaussian polynomial with
B=\text{Binomial}(O(n^{4}),p)
terms. Let
B_{\text{small}}
denote the event
B\leq(1+c)pn^{4}
for some constant
c
, and
B_{\text{large}}
as its complement. We have:

\left\|D_{1}\right\|_{2n}\leq(2n-1)^{3/2}\left\|D_{1}\right\|_{2}\leq(2n-1)^{3/2}\left(\E[D_{1}^{2}\mid B_{\text{small}}]+\Pr(B_{\text{large}})\E[D_{1}^{2}\mid B_{\text{large}}]\right)^{1/2}.

(71)

First term.

\displaystyle\frac{n\binom{n}{3}^{3}p^{3}}{C}\E[D_{1,\text{norm}}^{2}\mid B_{\text{small}}]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{(1+c)pn^{4}}J_{i}^{3}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{(1+c)pn^{4}}J_{i}^{6}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O(pn^{4})

Second term.

By a Chernoff bound on the binomial random variable
B
,

\displaystyle\Pr(B_{\text{large}})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(B>(1+c)pn^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\left(\frac{c^{2}}{c+2}\right)pn^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\Omega(pn^{4})).

As
B\leq n^{4}
always, we have:

\frac{n\binom{n}{3}^{3}p^{3}}{C}\E\left[D_{1,\text{norm}}^{2}\mid B_{\text{large}}\right]\leq\E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n^{4}}J_{i}^{3}\right)^{2}\right]\leq Cn^{4}.

(72)

Putting the main term and tail term together, we have:

\left\|D_{1}\right\|_{2n}\leq\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}n^{3/2}\left(pn^{4}+\exp(-\Omega(pn^{4})\right)\cdot Cn^{4})^{1/2}\leq O(p^{-1}n^{-3/2}).

(73)

Having
p\geq\Omega(1/n^{2})
gives
\left\|D_{1}\right\|_{2n}\leq O(\sqrt{n})
as desired.

A.3
Bounding
D_{2},D_{3},D_{4}

Taking into account the index constraints from
f
, we have:

D_{2}=C\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\binom{n}{3}^{-3/2}p^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,S^{\prime}\subset[n_{1}]\\
|S|=|S^{\prime}|=3\\
S\neq S^{\prime}\end{subarray}}X_{S,j}J_{S,j}^{2}X_{S^{\prime},j}J_{S^{\prime},j}.

(74)

After decoupling our Gaussian random variables, up to relabelings the norms of
D_{2},D_{3}
and
D_{4}
terms are bounded in the same way when considering the SYK model with
q=4
, so we focus on
D_{2}
WLOG.

Define
\{X^{\prime}_{S,j}J^{\prime}_{S,j}\}
as a new independent set of random variables where the
X_{S,j}^{\prime}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli and the
J_{S,j}^{\prime}
are i.i.d. Gaussian. By Proposition
A.1
, we need only bound the norm of:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{D}_{2}\right\rVert_{2n}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert C\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\binom{n}{3}^{-3/2}p^{-3/2}\sum_{j,S\neq S^{\prime}}X_{S,j}J_{S,j}^{2}X^{\prime}_{S^{\prime},j}J^{\prime}_{S^{\prime},j}\right\rVert_{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert Cn^{-5}p^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}\subset[n_{1}]\\
|S^{\prime}|=3\end{subarray}}X^{\prime}_{S^{\prime},j}J^{\prime}_{S^{\prime},j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n_{1}]\\
|S|=3\end{subarray}}X_{S,j}J_{S,j}^{2}\right\rVert_{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert Cn^{-5}p^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}\subset[n_{1}]\\
|S^{\prime}|=3\end{subarray}}X^{\prime}_{S^{\prime},j}J^{\prime}_{S^{\prime},j}\Big)\right\rVert_{2n},

where
\chi_{j}
is a chi-squared random variable with
B_{1,j}=\text{Binomial}(n^{3},p)
degrees of freedom. Conditioned on the
X_{S^{\prime},j}
,
\sum_{S^{\prime}}X_{S^{\prime},j}J_{S^{\prime},j}
is also a sum of
B_{2,j}
standard Gaussians, where
B_{2,j}=\text{Binomial}(n^{3},p)
. This culminates in:

\left\|D_{2}\right\|_{2n}\leq Cn^{-5}p^{-3/2}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}B_{2,j}^{1/2}J^{\prime\prime}_{j}\right\|_{2n},

(75)

where the
J_{j}^{\prime\prime}
variables are i.i.d. standard Gaussians. We wish to show this value is bounded above by
C\sqrt{n}
, or equivalently that:

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}B_{2,j}^{1/2}J^{\prime\prime}_{j}\right\|_{2n}^{2}\triangleq\left\|\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}\right\|_{2n}^{2}\leq Cn^{11}p^{3}.

(76)

Let
B_{\text{small}}
denote the event
\left(\forall j\in[n_{2}],S,S^{\prime}\ B_{1,j}\leq(1+c)pn^{3}\wedge B_{2,j}\leq(1+c)pn^{3}\right)
for some constant
c
, and
B_{\text{large}}
as its complement. We have:

\left\|D_{2,\text{norm}}\right\|_{2n}\leq\left(\E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{small}}]+\Pr(B_{\text{large}})\E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}]\right)^{1/2n}.

(77)

First term.

We have:

\E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{small}}]=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}B_{2,j}^{1/2}J^{\prime\prime}_{j}\right\|_{2n}^{2n}\leq C^{2n}p^{n}n^{3n}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}J_{j}^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{2n}^{2n}.

(78)

If we fix
\{\chi_{j}\}
, the expression
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}J_{j}^{\prime}
is a Gaussian with variance
\nu^{2}\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\chi_{j}^{2}
and
2n^{th}
-moment bounded by
C^{n}\nu^{2n}\cdot(2n)^{n}
. Thus it is sufficient to show that

\E[\nu^{2n}]\leq O(p^{2}n^{7})^{n}

(79)

to show that
\E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{small}}]\leq C^{2n}n^{11n}p^{3n}
. The Laurent–Massart
[
LM00
]
lower-bound for chi-squared random variables gives:

\Pr(\chi-k\geq 2\sqrt{kx}+2x)\leq\exp{(-x)},

(80)

where
k
is the degrees of freedom of
\chi
. Plugging in
k=\left(1+c\right)pn^{3}
and
x=cpn^{3}
with
c\geq 1
gives:

\Pr(\chi_{j}\geq 8cpn^{3})\leq\exp{(-cpn^{3})}.

(81)

Applying the union bound, we get (for sufficiently large
n
)

\displaystyle\Pr(\nu\geq 8cpn^{7/2})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{j}\geq 8cpn^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Pr\left(\chi_{j}\geq 8cpn^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\exp{(-cpn^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}pn^{3}\right)}^{c}\qquad\forall c\geq 1.

Setting
\nu^{\prime}\triangleq\nu/(8n^{7/2})
, we have

\Pr(\nu^{\prime}\geq cp)\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}pn^{3}\right)}^{c}\qquad\forall c\geq 1.

(82)

Finally to show the correctness of
Eq.

79
,

\E[(\nu^{\prime})^{2n}]\leq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{(j+1)2n}\cdot\Pr\left(2^{j}\leq\nu^{\prime}\leq 2^{j+1}\right)\leq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{(j+1)2n}\cdot\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}pn^{3}\right)^{2^{j}}}.

(83)

Assuming
p\geq C/n^{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\E[(\nu^{\prime})^{2n}]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{(j+1)2n}\cdot\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}pn^{3}\right)}^{2^{j}}

\displaystyle\leq O(1),

proving
\E[v^{2n}]\leq O(p^{2}n^{7})^{n}
for large enough constants. This gives us
E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{small}}]\leq C^{2n}p^{3n}n^{11n}
.

Second term.

By a union bound and Chernoff bound on the binomial random variables,

\displaystyle\Pr(B_{\text{large}})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\bigcup_{j,S,S^{\prime}}\left(B_{1,j}>(1+c)pn^{3}\right)\cup\left(B_{2,j}>(1+c)pn^{3}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j,S,S^{\prime}}\Pr\left(B_{1,j}>(1+c)pn^{3}\right)+\Pr\left(B_{2,j}>(1+c)pn^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{7}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{c^{2}}{c+2}\right)pn^{3}\right)+n^{7}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{c^{2}}{c+2}\right)pn^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{7}\exp(-\Omega(pn^{3}))

Assume every binomial variable
B
takes its maximum value. From the dense case
[
HO23
]
, we have:

\E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}]\leq n^{11n}.

(84)

Adding the probability factor,

n^{7}\exp(-\Omega(pn^{3}))\E[\tilde{D}_{2,\text{norm}}^{2n}\mid B_{\text{large}}]\leq\exp(-\Omega(pn^{3}))\cdot n^{11n+7}.

(85)

When
p\geq\Omega(\frac{\log n}{n^{2}})
and our constant factor
c
large enough, we can make
\exp(-\Omega(pn^{3}))=\exp(-12n\log n)=n^{-12n}
.

Putting the main term and tail term together, we have

\left\|D_{2}\right\|_{2n}\leq\frac{Cp^{-3/2}}{n^{5}}(C^{2n}p^{3n}n^{11n}+C/n^{n-7})^{1/2n}\leq C\sqrt{n}

(86)

for sufficiently large
n
, as desired.
