Direct Conversion of Methyl–Ethyl-Ketone into Olefins Precursors of Fuels and Chemicals over Supported Cu Catalysts
Authors: Hanzhong Shi, Yang He, Vanessa Lebarbier Dagle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As global demand for light olefins continues to increase, driven by expanding plastics production and the need for jet fuel precursors, biomass-derived oxygenates such as methyl-ethyl-ketone (MEK) have emerged as promising intermediates for olefin production. This study investigates the selective hydrodeoxygenation of MEK to butene over copper catalysts supported on Al2O3, SiO2, and SiO2–Al2O3, each offering distinct acid characteristics. Among the catalysts evaluated, 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 achieved the highest olefin selectivity (97.6%) while maintaining a high MEK conversion level (>80%) due to its acidity and favorable metal–support interactions. Further studies revealed that at temperatures above 210 °C, the reaction pathway progressively shifts from hydrodeoxygenation toward aldol-condensation, producing undesired carboxylic acids and ketones, while simultaneously accelerating deactivation through hard-coke formation. A scaled-up experiment using a 100 g catalyst batch demonstrated that 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 operated at 210 °C (1 atm, w8 h space velocity = 0.8 h–1, PMEK = 12%) sustains high conversion (∼90%) and olefin selectivity (94–97%) for more than 140 h, with full recovery of activity and selectivity after mild oxidative regeneration. These results establish 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 as a robust and scalable catalyst for MEK hydrodeoxygenation, offering mechanistic insight into activity, selectivity, and deactivation pathways critical for industrial implementation.

Introduction

Light olefinsincluding ethylene, propylene, and butenesare essential building blocks in the chemical industry and serve as precursors for polymers, solvents, and synthetic rubbers. With the growing interest in sustainable fuels, these molecules are also being explored as intermediates for producing gasoline-, jet-, and diesel-range blendstocks through oligomerization and hydrogenation. As a result, global demand for light olefins continues to rise, driven by expanding plastic production and the need for cleaner fuel components. Conventional olefin production relies on steam cracking of naphtha or natural gas liquids and on methanol-to-olefins processes. , Although these technologies are mature, they are energy-intensive and depend heavily on fossil feedstocks, highlighting the need for more reliable alternatives.

Oxygenated intermediates such as alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes have emerged as promising renewable feedstocks for olefin production. − Recent studies have demonstrated efficient conversion of ethanol, butanol, acetone, and ketones over zeolites, mixed metal oxides, and bifunctional catalysts, highlighting the importance of catalyst acidity, pore structure, and hydrogen transfer activity in determining olefin selectivity. − Despite these advances, ketone upgrading remains significantly less explored than alcohol conversion because multiple competing reaction pathways often reduce olefin yields. Methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) is particularly attractive because it can be produced from several biomass-derived intermediates, including 2,3-butanediol, 2-butanol, acetoin, and levulinic acid (Table ). Its carbonyl group located adjacent to C–C bonds enables multiple reaction pathways–dehydration, decarbonylation, hydrogenation, and C–C bond scissionmaking MEK a versatile platform molecule for generating fuels and chemicals via olefin intermediates.

Several studies have shown that ZnZrO x catalysts, which combine the acid functionality of ZrO2 with the redox properties introduced by Znare effective for the single-step conversion of ketones into olefins via the aldol condensation mechanism. − Dagle et al. demonstrated that Zn1Zr10O x can achieve high olefin selectivity (≈82.5%) at high MEK conversion (93–98%) with remarkable stable performance. , However, the formation of undesired 3-pentanone, produced via aldol condensation followed by decomposition, remains unavoidable and accounts for roughly 10% selectivity, thereby limiting olefin yields. This has motivated our interest in catalytic routes that suppress aldol condensation and instead favor ketone hydrodeoxygenation. For catalytic upgrading of ketones into olefins via hydrodeoxygenation, bifunctional catalysts consisting of a metal supported on an acid material are commonly used. ,, The metal function allows for hydrogenation of the ketone into alcohol intermediate that subsequently is dehydrated into olefin over the acidic function. Depending on the hydrogenation strength of the metal and the operating conditions, the olefin may undergo further hydrogenation into alkane, as illustrated in Figure . Copper (Cu) is well-known for its mild-hydrogenation properties, − making it particularly attractive for reactions in which olefins are the desired products and overhydrogenation to alkanes must be avoided. Al-Auda et al. showed that Cu/zeolite Y effectively converts MEK to butenes with no detectable butane formation. However, the catalyst exhibits rapid deactivation, primarily caused by coke deposition. Therefore, catalysts that combine high stability with high olefin selectivity are still lacking.

Mechanism for the hydrodeoxygenation of MEK over a catalyst containing both metal and acid functionalities under a hydrogen environment.

The goal of this work was to develop a Cu-based catalyst and operating conditions that achieve high olefin selectivity while maintaining MEK conversion. We first examined MEK conversion to olefins over Cu catalysts supported on Al2O3, SiO2, and SiO2–Al2O3 to clarify how metal dispersion and support acidity govern activity and selectivity. This screening helped identify SiO2–Al2O3 as the most effective support, and the corresponding Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 catalyst was subsequently investigated in detail. We determined how reaction temperature influences performance, reaction pathways, and catalyst stability. Finally, we assessed scalability and regenerability through 100 g reactor tests to demonstrate that the catalyst is suitable for larger-scale MEK upgrading.

Experimental Methods

Materials Preparation

Cu-based catalysts were prepared via incipient wetness impregnation using an aqueous solution of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O (Thermo Scientific; ≥98.0%). The Cu precursor solution was added dropwise to the support material–SiO2 (Davisil Grace 646), Al2O3 (Engelhard), or SiO2/Al2O3 (Grace)–under continuous stirring. The impregnated samples then were dried at 120 °C for 8 h and subsequently calcined at 550 °C for 4 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. The resulting catalysts are designated as 10Cu/SiO2/Al2O3, 10Cu/Al2O3, and 10Cu/SiO2.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES)

Bulk Cu loading was determined by ICP-OES. Approximately 20 mg of each catalyst samples was digested in concentrated HNO3 using a CEM MARS 6 microwave digestion system at 210 °C for 20 min. The resulting solutions were diluted with deionized water and analyzed using a PerkinElmer Avio 550 ICP-OES. Calibration was performed using certified standards and verified with independent quality-control standards.

Nitrogen N2-Physisorption

The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller BET surface area of the catalysts was determined by N2 physisorption using a Quantachrome Autosorb IQ3 analyzer. Prior to analysis, samples were degassed at 150 °C to remove adsorbed impurities. Isotherms were then collected at 77 K by measuring the volume of N2 adsorbed at different relative pressures (P/P0). The BET method was applied to calculate specific surface areas.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

X-ray Diffraction patterns were acquired using a Rigaku Smartlab SE X-ray diffractometer with Cu anode (Kα1 = 0.15405 nm), operating at a scan rate of 0.05° per second between 2θ = 20° and 100°. Jade 9.1 (Materials Data Inc., Livermore, CA) and the Powder Diffraction File database (International Center for Diffraction Data, Newtown Square, PA) were used to analyze diffraction patterns. Prior to analysis, the fresh catalyst was treated under 20% hydrogen (H2) /N2 for 1 h at 300 °C, while spent catalysts were examined without further treatment.

N2O Titration

Cu dispersion was determined by the N2O titration method using an Altamira AMI-300 chemisorption analyzer. About 100 mg of the calcined sample was purged with helium (He) at 180 °C for 2 h and then the sample was subjected to a temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) in 10% H2/He (50 mL min–1) from ambient temperature to 400 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min–1 to quantify the total H2 consumption corresponding to complete reduction of Cu2O to metallic Cu. Following the first TPR, the sample was purged in flowing He at 400 °C for 30 min to remove residual H2 and water, then cooled to 40 °C. A flow of 1% N2O/He (50 mL min–1) was introduced for 30 min to selectively oxidize the surface Cu0 atoms to Cu2O according to 1 N2O+2Cusurf0→N2+Cu2Osurf

The system was then purged with He for 30 min at 40 °C to remove physisorbed N2O. A second TPR was subsequently performed under identical conditions (10% H2/He, 10 °C min–1 to 400 °C) to quantify the H2 consumption associated with the reduction of surface Cu2O. The difference between the first and second H2 consumptions corresponds to the amount of Cu present at the surface. Assuming a 1:1 molar ratio between surface Cu atoms and Cu2O oxygen (O2) atoms, dispersion (D) was calculated as 2 D(%)=100×2nH2,surfnH2,total

Pyridine Adsorption–Desorption Followed by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

Pyridine adsorption–desorption measurements were performed using a Nicolet FTIR spectrometer IS50 equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride detector (resolution: 4 cm–1, 128 scans). Each sample was pressed into a pellet (ca. 20 mg for a 2 cm2 pellet) and placed in a transmission infrared cell. Prior to pyridine adsorption, the catalyst pellets were treated in situ under 5%H2/N2 at 300 °C for 1 h and then cooled to 25 °C under vacuum. Pyridine was subsequently introduced at 25 °C (Pequilibrium = 133 Pa), and infrared spectra were recorded following desorption from 50 to 350 °C. The number of Lewis acid sites titrated by pyridine was calculated using an integrated molar absorption coefficient value of ε = 2.22 cm.μmol–1 for ν19b vibration of coordinated pyridine at ca. 1450 cm–1.

Ammonia (NH3) Temperature-Programmed Desorption (NH3-TPD)

Ammonia temperature-programmed desorption measurements were conducted using an Altamira AMI-300 chemisorption analyzer. Approximately 100 mg of the catalyst was pretreated in flowing He (50 mL min–1) at 200 °C for 1 h to remove adsorbed impurities and moisture. The catalyst was then cooled to 100 °C under He, after which NH3 adsorption was carried out by switching the gas flow to 5% NH3/He (50 mL min–1) for 30 min. Following adsorption, the catalyst was purged with He (50 mL min–1) at 100 °C for 1 h to eliminate physisorbed NH3, thus ensuring that only chemisorbed species remained. Ammonia desorption was subsequently performed by heating the sample from 100 to 700 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min–1 under flowing He flow (50 mL min–1). The desorbed NH3 was monitored continuously using a mass spectrometer to record the NH3 desorption profile.

Temperature-Programmed Oxidation (TPO)

Temperature-programmed oxidation was performed using an Altamira AMI-300 chemisorption analyzer to evaluate carbonaceous species deposited on spent catalysts after reaction. Approximately 100 mg of the spent catalyst was loaded into a quartz reactor and pretreated in flowing He (50 mL min–1) at 200 °C for 1 h to remove weakly adsorbed species and residual moisture. The sample was then cooled to 50 °C under flowing He.

Following pretreatment, the gas feed was switched to an oxidizing mixture of 5% O2/N2 (50 mL min–1). Oxidation of surface carbon species was conducted by heating the sample from 50 to 800 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min–1 under continuous oxidizing flow. The reactor effluent was monitored using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) to track the evolution of oxidation products (e.g., carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide) and obtain TPO profiles. To minimize interference from water produced during oxidation, water was removed upstream of the detector using a condenser cooled with an isopropanol–liquid N2 mixture prior to the TCD.

Reactivity

Reactivity tests for the conversion of MEK into olefins were conducted in a 6.6 mm inner diameter fixed-bed packed bed reactor typically loaded with 2.0 g of catalysts. A K-type thermocouple was placed in the reactor to measure temperature in the catalyst bed. To minimize temperature gradients, an electrical resistance heating block was installed for temperature control. Prior to testing, catalysts were reduced under 100 sccm of 20% H2/N2 for 1 h at 300 °C to ensure metallic Cu formation. Methyl-ethyl-ketone (MEK) was fed into the system using an ISCO syringe pump and was converted to the gas phase using a vaporizer consisting of 6.6 mm inner diameter steel tubing filled with quartz beads. Catalyst activities were measured at temperatures between 180 °C and 340 °C, 1 atm, and w8 h space velocities (WHSV) between 0.4 and 0.8 h–1. Hydrogen was used as the carrier gas. A knockout pot placed directly downstream of the reaction zone was used to collect liquid products. The gaseous effluent was analyzed online using an Inficon micro-GC (Model 3000A) equipped with MS-5A, Plot U, alumina, and OV-1 columns and a thermal conductivity detector. Liquid samples collected from the knockout pot were analyzed separately ex situ using liquid chromatography.

Results and Discussion

Catalysts Screening

In this study, we first evaluated the performance of Cu catalysts supported on oxide materials with distinct acid characteristics, namely SiO2, SiO2–Al2O3, and Al2O3. Because these oxides were sourced from different suppliers, each using its own preparation methods, the acidity of the supports does not systematically correlate with the SiO2/ Al2O3 ratio in the resulting Cu catalysts. As shown in Figure , under identical operating conditions (T = 290 °C, P = 1 atm, WHSV = 0.8 h–1, PMEK = 12%), the MEK conversion follows the order 10Cu/SiO2 < 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 < 10Cu/Al2O3. Because the first step in converting MEK to olefins involves MEK hydrogenation over metal sites, we used N2O titration to measure Cu dispersion and the results are summarized in Table . Cu dispersion was measured at 25% for 10Cu/SiO2, 42% for 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3, and 47% for 10Cu/Al2O3, corresponding to surface Cu-site concentrations of 0.8, 1.3, and 1.5 mmol/g, respectively. The dispersion trend follows the same pattern as the MEK conversion. In fact, we observed a clear linear relationship between MEK conversion and the number of surface Cu sites (Figure ), which demonstrates that catalytic activity is directly governed by Cu dispersion. A greater number of accessible Cu sites facilitate H2 activation and carbonyl hydrogenation, thereby increasing the formation rate of the 2-butanol intermediate. , However, Cu-site availability alone does not determine olefin selectivity as the subsequent dehydration of 2-butanol depends on the nature and concentration of acid sites on the support.

Because dehydration proceeds on acid sites, we characterized the catalysts using pyridine adsorption–desorption FTIR. The resulting Lewis acid site concentrations, which are summarized in Table and derived from the spectra shown in Figure , show that 10Cu/SiO2 possesses a markedly lower acidity, only 7.9 μmol/g. Among all catalysts evaluated, 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 delivers the highest olefin selectivity (97.6%) while maintaining a high conversion level (81.6%), making it the most effective formulation in this series. On that basis, it was chosen for more detailed investigation.

The conversion exhibits a volcano-type profile, reaching a maximum of 93.1% at 210 °C. To determine whether the decline in conversion at higher temperatures is related to Cu sintering, the metallic Cu crystallite size was calculated from the XRD patterns in Figure .

As shown in Table , the metallic Cu crystallite sizes remain similar and between 30.5° and 39.8° across all catalysts, indicating that sintering is not responsible for the decrease in conversion above 210 °C. The TPO profiles presented in Figure for all spent catalysts reveal two types of coke: the low-temperature oxidation peak (100–300 °C) is attributed to weakly bound amorphous carbonaceous species or oxygenated surface intermediates, whereas the higher-temperature peak (300–500 °C) corresponds to more condensed and polymerized carbon deposits that interact more strongly with the catalyst surface. Similar TPO features have been widely reported for supported metal and oxide catalysts containing multiple carbonaceous species. − The profiles show a progressive increase in hard coke with increasing reaction temperature. Therefore, the decline in conversion at higher temperatures may be attributed to accelerated deactivation caused by hard coke formation.

The selectivity toward butene decreases markedly as temperature increases from 98.3% at 180 °C to 74.6% at 340 °C. This decline coincides with the emergence and growth of side products, particularly pentene and carboxylic acids (acetic and propanoic acid). Pentene first appears at 250 °C with a selectivity of 3.1% and continues to rise, reaching 10% at 340 °C. The selectivity toward acetic and propanoic acids also increases up to 290 °C before declining at 340 °C. The simultaneous formation of pentene and propanoic acid indicates that MEK undergoes partial self-aldol condensation followed by decomposition, as illustrated in Figure . Gas chromatography–mass spectroscopy analysis of the aqueous phase (Figure ) confirms the presence of 3-pentanone, supporting this proposed pathway. Regarding acetic acid, the presence of H2 in the reaction environment rules out oxidative decomposition of 2-butanone as a source. We therefore propose that acetic acid may originate from cross-aldol condensation between 3-pentanone and MEK, as outlined in Figure . Additional analysis will be required to fully clarify the mechanism of acetic acid formation under these reducing conditions.

Overall, while hydrodeoxygenation of MEK to butene via butanol intermediate remains the dominant olefin-forming pathway, the results demonstrate that increasing the reaction temperature above 210 °C progressively enhances the contribution of aldol-condensation-derived olefins and undesired carboxylic acids.

Evolution of the Catalytic Performance of 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 with TOS

The catalytic performance of 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 was evaluated for 72 h at each reaction temperature, and the results are shown in Figure . A pronounced decline in conversion is observed during the first 24 h for all temperatures ≥210 °C. Beyond 24 h, the loss of conversion becomes much less significant. In contrast, the selectivity toward olefins remains relatively stable throughout the entire test period at all temperatures.

To identify the origin of the initial loss in conversion, the spent catalysts were first analyzed by XRD after 6 and 72 h TOS. No measurable change in the metallic Cu crystallite size is detected, indicating that sintering is not responsible for the observed deactivation.

A progressive decrease in acidity with time on stream is evident: for all temperatures, the number of acid sites at 72 h is lower than at 6 h, which in turn is lower than that of the fresh catalyst.

To assess whether acid-site loss occurs uniformly or predominantly at specific stages of the reaction, Figure presents the percent loss of acid sites per hour during the first 6 h and during the subsequent 66 h. The rate of acid-site loss is substantially higher during the initial 6 h, demonstrating that loss of acid sites is most severe at the beginning of the reaction.

Additionally, the NH3-TPD profiles in Figure show that the strong acid sitesrepresented by the desorption feature near 500 °Cdisappear completely after only 6 h on stream. Hence, the higher rate of acid-site loss observed at the beginning of the reaction is due specifically to the loss of the strongest acid sites during the first 6 h. Furthermore, the carbon content measured after 6 and 72 h is similar (slightly higher at 6 h, likely due to experimental variability), suggesting that carbonaceous deposition occurs primarily at the start of the reaction on the strongest acid sites.

NH3-TPD profiles for the fresh 10Cu/SiO2/Al2O3 and the spent catalysts from Figure after (a) 6 h on stream and (b) 72 h on stream.

Taken together, these results indicate that the strongest acid sites are the first to interact with the reactants but are rapidly covered by tightly bound carbonaceous deposits. After approximately 6 h, only the weaker acid sites remain available to catalyze the reaction. Because these sites bind carbonaceous species less strongly, they accumulate less coke and, therefore, experience a smaller loss in conversion, resulting in a more stable catalytic performance over time. Thus, the initial drop in the conversion is attributed to the rapid deactivation of the strongest acid sites by carbonaceous deposits.

Scale-Up Evaluation of the Catalytic Performance of 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3

Given that 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 exhibits high catalytic performance while maintaining reasonable conversionat least over the first 72 h TOSthe next step was to scale up the catalyst synthesis and evaluate its performance at the 100 g scale using a 1-in.-diameter reactor to further assess its commercialization potential. Figure shows the conversion and olefins selectivity over a total of 180 h TOS. Overall, the conversion remains stable at approximately 90% during the first 140 h. Beyond this point, the conversion gradually decreases, reaching 82% after 170 h on stream. Olefin selectivity remains between 94 and 97% during the first 140 h, after which it decreases slightly to 90–92%, accompanied by a modest increase in the formation of 2-methyl-3-heptanone, which is an intermediate in the formation of pentene. Following a mild regeneration at 400 °C under 5% O2/N2, the conversion was fully restored, indicating that reversible coking was responsible for the observed decline. Selectivity for olefins likewise was fully recovered, reaching 97.8% after regeneration. These results demonstrate the regenerability and reusability of the 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 catalyst, underscoring its potential for large-scale application.

Evolution of the conversion and selectivity toward olefins for 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 tested at 100 g scale. T = 210 °C, P = 1 atm, WHSV = 0.8 h–1, PMEK = 12%.

Overall, the conversion and olefins selectivity obtained at 100 g scale are similar to those obtained at 2 g scale, demonstrating that the scale up was successful. Although some deactivation occurs with TOS, activity can be recovered through a mild regeneration treatment.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that Cu catalysts supported on oxides with balanced acidity and high metal dispersion are essential for the selective hydrodeoxygenation of MEK to butene. Among the materials evaluated, 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 provides the best combination of accessible Cu surface sites and moderate acidity, enabling high MEK conversion and exceptional olefin selectivity while minimizing undesired hydrogenation and incomplete dehydration pathways. Temperature-dependent studies reveal that 210 °C represents the preferred operating point. Below that temperature, conversion is limited, whereas higher temperatures promote aldol-condensation pathways, leading to pentene, ketones, and carboxylic acids, as well as accelerated deactivation through hard-coke formation.

Time-on-stream experiments show that the initial loss of activity arises from rapid deactivation of the strongest acid sites by tightly bound carbonaceous deposits, after which the catalyst stabilizes as weaker acid sites dominate the reaction. Notably, this deactivation is largely reversible. Scale-up to a 100 g catalyst batch confirms that 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 maintains high conversion and olefin selectivity for more than 140 h, with full recovery of performance after mild oxidative regeneration.

Overall, the results establish 10Cu/SiO2–Al2O3 as a robust, regenerable, and industrially scalable catalyst for MEK hydrodeoxygenation. The mechanistic insights gained linking metal dispersion, acidity, temperature-dependent pathways, and deactivation behavior, providing a foundation for further optimization and practical implementation of biomass-derived oxygenate upgrading to light olefins.
